# Francisco Gianotti: Research Dossier

## Scope and identification

This dossier concerns the Italian-born architect active in Argentina who is usually identified in Spanish-language sources as **Francisco Gianotti** and in Italian-language sources as **Francesco Gianotti**. It does not concern other people with the Gianotti or Giannotti surname.

The documentary record available online is uneven. Gianotti’s buildings are substantially better documented than his private life. The principal modern biographical treatments are Horacio Eduardo Caride’s 1997 study, later incorporated into the CEDODAL exhibition monograph *Francisco Gianotti: del Art Nouveau al racionalismo en la Argentina* (2000), and institutional studies of the Galería Güemes and Confitería del Molino. No digitized civil file, naturalization record, probate inventory, complete correspondence, or full manuscript of his memoir was located in this search.

Evidentiary labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a governmental, archival, institutional, contemporaneous, or scholarly source.
- **Reported fact:** consistently stated in secondary sources, but the underlying primary document was not examined.
- **Disputed or uncertain:** sources conflict or the evidence is incomplete.
- **Allegation or theory:** a claim advanced without an established adjudication.
- **Rumor or myth:** a culturally significant story without sufficient documentary support.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and origins

**Reported fact:** Gianotti was the son of **Luigi Gianotti**—usually Hispanicized as **Luis** and occasionally misspelled “Luiggi”—and **Bernardina Monaco or Mónaco**. He was born at Lanzo, a Piedmontese settlement near Turin at the foot of the Alps.

No reliable online source located in this research supplies:

- the occupations of his parents;
- the family’s social or financial position;
- grandparents or earlier ancestry;
- the family’s religious practice;
- a complete list of siblings;
- the precise household in which he was raised.

### Brother: Giovanni Battista Gianotti

**Documented or strongly reported fact:** His elder brother **Giovanni Battista Gianotti** (1873–1928) also studied at Turin’s Accademia Albertina. Giovanni became an artist, decorator, furniture designer, and proprietor of an artistic-decoration enterprise generally identified as the **Officine d’Arte**.

The brothers collaborated in Europe and maintained a professional relationship after Francisco emigrated. Decorative elements for Francisco’s Argentine buildings were designed or manufactured through Giovanni’s Milanese operation. The Galería Güemes interior and the Confitería del Molino are repeatedly connected with the brother’s decorative production.

This arrangement placed the Gianotti brothers within a transatlantic supply chain: Francisco designed buildings and interiors in Argentina, while Giovanni’s Italian workshops could provide metalwork, furnishings, ornamental components, and other applied art. The outbreak of the First World War complicated imports but apparently did not end the relationship.

### Marriage and children

**Reported fact:** In **1919**, Gianotti married **Amelia Vercesi**. Horacio Caride reports that he met her while working on the **Confitería La París**, owned by her father, **Pedro Vercesi**. The marriage therefore also emerged from one of Gianotti’s commercial commissions.

Their known children were:

- **Aníbal Gianotti**, born in 1920;
- **Pedro Gianotti**, born in 1922, also identified in some sources as **Luis Pedro**;
- **Juan Francisco Gianotti**, born in 1927.

Aníbal and Pedro later collaborated in their father’s architectural practice. Pedro or Luis Pedro is specifically credited as collaborator on the apartment building at Arenales 2080, Gianotti’s final known completed work.

No reliable material located here establishes Amelia’s birth or death dates, her later life, the professions of all three sons, or whether Gianotti had descendants through them.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1881–1903

Very little has been recovered about Gianotti’s childhood. He grew up in Piedmont during the decades following Italian unification, but existing biographies do not document childhood travel, illnesses, religious formation, early employment, or the circumstances under which he entered architectural study.

Descriptions of Lanzo as a frontier or Risorgimento-influenced environment occur in later biographical writing. They provide historical context but do not by themselves prove that Gianotti acquired a specific political outlook there.

No evidence was located of poverty, aristocratic status, political persecution, imprisonment, military service, addiction, or a formative personal crisis.

## Education and Formation, approximately 1900–1906

### Accademia Albertina, Turin

**Documented or strongly reported fact:** Gianotti studied architecture at Turin’s **Accademia Albertina di Belle Arti**, frequently called the Academy of Fine Arts of Turin in Spanish sources. He graduated around **1904**; some catalogues give the year without qualification.

His principal named teacher was **Alfredo Melani**, the Italian architect, critic, and historian of decorative art. Melani’s teaching connected architecture with applied art and decoration, an approach visible in Gianotti’s later integration of structural design, stained glass, bronze, ironwork, ceramics, furniture, and commercial interiors.

### Brussels, 1905

**Reported fact:** In **1905**, Francisco and Giovanni Battista undertook postgraduate study or professional formation in **Brussels**. The surviving accounts characterize the trip as their introduction to the anti-academic ideas and buildings of **Victor Horta** and **Henry van de Velde**.

The phrase “postgraduate study” is common in later biographies, but the institution, enrollment record, teachers, duration, and qualification have not been identified. It is safer to state that the brothers studied or observed contemporary Belgian architecture in Brussels than to assume a formal degree.

### Formative currents

The principal traceable influences were:

- northern Italian *Liberty* architecture and decorative art;
- the Milanese and Turin movements against rigid academic historicism;
- Belgian Art Nouveau;
- the total-design ideal joining architecture with furniture and ornament;
- new construction technologies, especially steel and reinforced concrete;
- the commercial architecture of European galleries and passages;
- Milan’s Galleria Vittorio Emanuele II, repeatedly identified as a precedent for the Galería Güemes.

These influences did not produce a simple stylistic progression. Gianotti continued to use academic symmetry and traditional building types while applying highly individual ornament and modern construction systems.

## Early Career in Italy and Belgium, 1905–1909

### Milan International Exhibition of 1906

**Reported fact:** After Brussels, the Gianotti brothers designed or decorated pavilions for the **1906 International Exhibition in Milan**. This was Francisco’s earliest substantial known professional work.

The presently accessible sources do not provide a complete, securely attributed list of the pavilions. Later catalogues also associate him with decorative work in Milan and with the **Restaurant Casanova**, dated about 1907.

### Applied art and collaboration

The early partnership with Giovanni Battista blurred the boundaries among architecture, interior design, furniture, exhibition architecture, and commercial decoration. This interdisciplinary practice anticipated Francisco’s later work in Buenos Aires, where a building’s identity could depend as much on its tower, stained glass, bronzes, and interior furnishings as on its plan.

## Migration to Argentina, 1909–1911

### Arrival in Buenos Aires

**Documented or strongly reported fact:** Gianotti arrived in **Buenos Aires in 1909**. His reasons are normally understood within the larger migration of Italian construction professionals to a rapidly expanding Argentine capital, though no personal letter explaining the decision was located.

He initially worked as a draftsman in the office of **Arturo Prins and Oscar Ranzenhofer**. This employment helped connect a recently arrived Italian architect with Buenos Aires building practice, regulations, contractors, and patrons.

### Centennial Exposition

In 1909–1910 Gianotti became involved in the **Italian Pavilion for Argentina’s International Centennial Exposition**. The pavilion had been designed by the prominent Italian architect **Gaetano Moretti**. Gianotti and **Mario Palanti** are credited with its construction, installation, or interior arrangement in Buenos Aires.

A contemporaneous technical-journal reference also identifies an ornamental wrought-iron kiosk by the Gianotti brothers for the Centennial’s industrial or railway exhibition. Attribution should be confined to the documented kiosk and pavilion work rather than expanded to the exposition as a whole.

### Temporary return to Italy

**Reported fact:** After the 1910 exposition Gianotti returned to Italy for several months. He came back to Buenos Aires in **1911**, apparently permanently.

### Independent studio

In 1911 he opened an independent practice at **Paraná 972**, Buenos Aires. By this point he had begun receiving commissions for private houses, apartment buildings, commercial premises, and the project initially called the **Pasaje Florida**.

## Chronological Life History

## 1911–1915: Establishment and the Galería General Güemes

Among Gianotti’s early commissions were residences or income properties for Luis Beatti, the Esteban Devoto succession, Federico Pinedo, and other clients. Some addresses and dates differ among catalogues, and buildings have been demolished or altered.

His decisive opportunity came through the Salta businessmen **Emilio San Miguel**, **Víctor San Miguel**, and **David Ovejero**. They planned a commercial passage connecting Florida and San Martín streets in central Buenos Aires.

The project was approved or initiated as the **Pasaje Florida** in 1912. Construction began on **10 March 1913**, according to the Buenos Aires heritage study. The building was executed by the **Compañía General de Obras Públicas**, commonly known as **GEOPE**, with engineer **Manuel F. Pereyra** and Nicolás Spallone among the technical personnel. Approximately 600 workers reportedly participated.

The First World War endangered the work by disrupting European materials and finance. Nevertheless, construction continued, reportedly taking approximately 32 months and costing about **10 million pesos moneda nacional**. This amount belongs to the development as a whole and should not be interpreted as Gianotti’s personal fee or investment.

The completed complex:

- joined Florida and San Martín through a passage approximately 116 metres long;
- incorporated shops, offices, residences, restaurants, a theatre, and service spaces;
- had three basement levels;
- employed reinforced concrete on a major scale;
- included a tower and rooftop lookout;
- incorporated European decorative components and work associated with Giovanni Battista Gianotti.

It was inaugurated on **15 December 1915** and named **Galería General Güemes**, honoring the Salta independence leader Martín Miguel de Güemes.

After completion, Gianotti moved his office into the gallery, making it both his most visible work and his professional address.

### Height and “first skyscraper” problem

Official and popular accounts frequently call the Galería Güemes Buenos Aires’s first skyscraper and give a height of **87 metres**. A detailed municipal heritage publication, consulting the 1916 special issue of *Revista Técnica de Arquitectura*, argues that the top was closer to **80 metres** and notes that the contemporary Railway Building was of similar height.

Accordingly:

- **Documented fact:** It was among Buenos Aires’s earliest tall reinforced-concrete commercial buildings.
- **Disputed designation:** “First skyscraper” depends on definition and competes with the Railway Building.
- **Disputed measurement:** Published figures range from roughly 76–80 metres for the structural tower and lantern to 87 metres in later accounts.

## 1914–1920: Salta patronage and Confitería del Molino

The San Miguel network brought Gianotti work in **Salta**, including:

- reconstruction or redesign of the **Mercado San Miguel**, approximately 1914;
- the **Pabellón del Centenario** or park hostelry, later associated with the provincial natural-sciences museum;
- the **Chalet or Palacio Usandivaras**;
- interior decoration for the Club 20 de Febrero or “Club 3 de Febrero,” with the name varying in derivative accounts;
- a commercial-residential commission for Adolfo San Miguel in Buenos Aires.

The Mercado San Miguel had existed since the nineteenth century. Gianotti did not found the market; he designed and executed the modern building commissioned by the founder’s descendants. Later expropriation and remodeling left only portions of his work intact, particularly parts of the central structure and the Florida-street façade.

### Confitería del Molino

The confectioner **Cayetano Brenna** commissioned Gianotti to enlarge and unify several properties at Rivadavia and Callao opposite the National Congress. A government decree dates the commission to 1916 and the inauguration to 1917; other sources state that work began in 1914 and that the new establishment opened on **9 July 1916**. The safest range is **1914–1917**, with substantial completion in 1916.

The commission required the existing confectionery to remain in operation while structures on adjoining properties were integrated. Gianotti used:

- iron framing and masonry;
- reinforced-concrete and precast components;
- stained glass;
- polychrome ceramics;
- bronze and iron ornament;
- a corner tower with rotating windmill sails;
- production and storage spaces in three basements;
- upper-floor rental accommodation.

Gianotti later wrote that he wanted a conspicuous public image. The windmill and tower became a visual trademark linked to the business’s name.

The decoration is attributed partly to Francisco and partly to the Gianotti brothers’ transatlantic operation. Claims that “all materials” were imported from Italy should be treated cautiously; important decorative elements were imported, but the structure necessarily combined local labor and materials with imported components.

The building became Gianotti’s best-known work, though the business belonged to Brenna and his successors, not to the architect.

### Other works of the period

- **Pabellón Circus**, Corrientes 480–496, approximately 1917; demolished.
- **Residencia Dellepiane**, Gaspar Campos 861, Vicente López, approximately 1917.
- **Confitería La París**, Libertad and Marcelo T. de Alvear, approximately 1918; demolished.
- **Residence Gianotti**, Ciudad de la Paz 965, approximately 1917.
- A rental and commercial property at Almirante Brown and Pinzón, developed for Gianotti himself, with catalogues variously dating its design or completion between 1916 and 1931.

In 1919 he married Amelia Vercesi. Their first son, Aníbal, was born in 1920.

## 1921–1929: Expansion, experiments, writing, and institutional commissions

### Transport design

In **1921**, Gianotti designed a raft or barge for transporting timber from Paraguay. No evidence was found that it entered serial production. The project nevertheless illustrates his interest in engineering beyond conventional buildings.

His second son, Pedro, was born in 1922.

### Residences and commercial buildings

Important projects of the decade included:

- **Residencia Brach**, Posadas 1064, approximately 1922–1923;
- office building for **Ana Ortiz Basualdo de Olazábal**, Diagonal Norte 647–651, 1925;
- rental building for **María Testi**, Corrientes 914–932, approximately 1926;
- office building associated with **Felisa Ortiz Basualdo de Alvear**, Diagonal Norte 622, approximately 1926;
- **Edificio Andes**, Corrientes 1300, for Arnaldo Arabehety, approximately 1926;
- the **Palacio Italia-América** or offices of the Compañía de Navegación Italia-América, Diagonal Norte 660, approximately 1927.

Some later sources identify the Diagonal Norte 647 building as the offices of the newspaper *El Mundo*. Addresses and client names are not consistently assigned in derivative catalogues, and the original permit files would be needed for a definitive reconciliation.

The Palacio Italia-América exemplified Gianotti’s movement away from his earlier floral Art Nouveau toward a more monumental eclecticism incorporating classical and Moorish-revival references.

His third son, Juan Francisco, was born in 1927.

### Architectural writing

Gianotti published or prepared writings explaining modern housing and his buildings. A work called **“La casa nueva”** is inconsistently dated: biographical chronologies place an article under 1927, while Caride’s bibliography identifies a publication in *El Arquitecto Constructor*, no. 526, in December 1935. These may be different versions, a republication, or a chronological error.

### Monument to the Flag competition, 1928

Gianotti participated in the controversial 1928 international competition for a national Monument to the Flag in Rosario.

Sources distinguish two projects involving him:

- the official monument website identifies the first-prize entry **“Invicta,”** with sculptor **Juan Francisco Finochiaro**;
- a historical study also lists a Gianotti–**Troiano Troiani** proposal, **“Patria de leyes justas, patria de todos,”** consisting of a monumental obelisk and horseshoe-shaped steps.

The precise division of authorship and whether Gianotti submitted or collaborated on more than one entry requires examination of the original competition files.

The competition itself became contentious. Argentine artists’ organizations protested the invitation of foreign-born architects and sculptors and questioned the jury’s composition. President **Marcelo T. de Alvear** expanded the jury, adding architect Raúl Fitte and artist **Pío Collivadino**. The enlarged jury ultimately declared the competition void. Thus, although Gianotti is commonly said to have “won first prize,” his scheme was not constructed and did not lead directly to the present monument.

This was a significant professional success followed by an institutional failure: formal recognition without execution.

### Residence and financial position

A residence for Gianotti at **Avenida del Libertador 5280** was reportedly completed in 1929. Along with other properties designed for his own account, it indicates that the Galería Güemes and subsequent commissions had given him the means to own or develop Buenos Aires real estate. No valuation, mortgage file, partnership agreement, or complete asset inventory was located.

## 1930–1939: Mature eclecticism and movement toward rationalism

### Projects

- Remodeling of **Samuel Ortiz Basualdo’s estancia** at Las Armas, Buenos Aires Province, approximately 1930.
- **Edificio Schaffhausen**, Reconquista 336–356, completed around 1932.
- Remodeling of Mendoza’s **Gran Hotel Plaza**, approximately 1933.
- Houses or apartment buildings on Riobamba and at **Juncal and Esmeralda**, 1935–1936.
- Project for the **Sociedad Comercial del Plata or Compañía Ítalo-Argentina de Electricidad**, 1936; apparently unbuilt.
- Project for a **Country Club Hotel at Villa del Lago**, Córdoba, approximately 1932; execution has not been established.

### Schaffhausen building

Gianotti issued a publication devoted to the Schaffhausen building in 1933. Its restrained massing and commercial-office character mark a departure from the exuberant ornamental surfaces of his prewar work.

### Hydroglider or hydrofoil-type project

In **1935**, Gianotti designed a passenger **hidrodeslizador**. Available biographical sources record the design but do not establish whether a prototype was built, patented, tested, or commercially adopted. No patent number was located.

### Stylistic development

By this period Gianotti was developing what he or later commentators called **“modernismo gianottiano”**: a personal modernism that retained carefully selected ornament, fine materials, and monumental composition while accepting simplified façades and rational planning.

This was not a sudden conversion from Art Nouveau to orthodox International Style. His practice moved through:

1. Italian and Belgian-derived floral modernism;
2. monumental and commercial eclecticism;
3. Art Deco geometry;
4. increasingly rationalist apartment and office design.

## 1940–1959: Business interests, family practice, and final buildings

### Mercado Monroe

Around **1940**, Gianotti designed the **Mercado Monroe**, at Monroe and 11 de Septiembre in Buenos Aires.

### Marble quarry

In **1942**, he began exploiting a marble quarry in **Córdoba Province**. This is the clearest reported non-architectural business venture of his later life. The venture may have connected his architectural interest in fine stone with control of a material supply, but no company name, concession file, capitalization, partners, production figures, or evidence of profitability was located.

### Property development

Catalogues identify a rental-apartment building belonging to Gianotti at **Avenida del Libertador 5176–5182**, designed or completed between 1942 and 1946. As with his property at Brown and Pinzón, this indicates direct participation in real-estate ownership rather than professional fees alone.

### Later work

- Mercado Monroe, approximately 1940.
- Rental apartments, Avenida del Libertador 5176–5182, approximately 1942–1946.
- Unbuilt Greek Orthodox church project for Belgrano, approximately 1952.
- Expansion or alteration of Banco Supervielle premises in the Galería Güemes, 1953–1954.
- **Edificio Vázquez Italia**, Bogotá and Bolivia, approximately 1955.
- Apartment building at **Arenales 2080**, completed in 1959 with Pedro or Luis Pedro Gianotti.

By the late period, Aníbal and Pedro were assisting their father. Arenales 2080, with exposed brick and comparatively modern detailing, is normally treated as Gianotti’s last work.

## 1960–1967: Retrospective work and death

Gianotti prepared a mimeographed professional memoir or career record titled **“El arquitecto Francisco T. Gianotti en Buenos Aires, 1909–1960.”** The document is dated **1964** in Caride’s bibliography and is sometimes described simply as Gianotti’s “memoirs.”

It appears to have been a privately circulated or unpublished typescript/mimeograph rather than a commercially published autobiography. Its current archival location was not conclusively identified online, although material used by CEDODAL came from family and architectural collections.

Gianotti died in **Buenos Aires on 13 February 1967**. *La Nación* published a notice concerning his burial on 15 February. No reliable source consulted here gives:

- the cause of death;
- a final illness;
- his place of burial;
- his estate’s value;
- a probate dispute;
- a comprehensive obituary;
- evidence of addiction, psychiatric illness, imprisonment, or accidental injury.

### Professional institutions and firms

- **Accademia Albertina di Belle Arti, Turin:** educational institution.
- **Office of Arturo Prins and Oscar Ranzenhofer:** Gianotti’s first known Buenos Aires employer.
- **Independent studio, Paraná 972:** opened in 1911.
- **Galería Güemes office:** professional address after the gallery’s completion.
- **Officine d’Arte of Giovanni Battista Gianotti:** family-linked decorative supplier and collaborator.
- **GEOPE:** contractor for the Galería Güemes; Gianotti was its architect, not established as an owner.
- **Banco Supervielle:** financier or participant in the Galería Güemes development and later client for alterations.
- **Compañía de Navegación Italia-América:** client for its Buenos Aires headquarters.
- **Sociedad Anónima Industrial Schaffhausen:** client for the Reconquista building.
- **Compañía Ítalo-Argentina de Electricidad/Sociedad Comercial del Plata:** associated with an unbuilt 1936 project.

### Technologies

Gianotti’s documented technical interests included:

- reinforced-concrete construction;
- mixed structures combining older masonry and iron framing with concrete additions;
- precast-concrete components;
- elevators and high-rise mechanical systems;
- complex basement servicing;
- electric lighting integrated with decorative glass;
- architectural metalwork;
- stained glass and polychrome ceramic cladding;
- freight-handling infrastructure within commercial buildings;
- a timber-transport raft;
- a passenger hydroglider or hydrofoil-like craft.

No verified patent record was located. The timber raft and passenger craft should therefore be treated as **documented designs**, not established inventions or patented commercial technologies.

### Property and wealth

Gianotti designed several buildings for his own account:

- a property at Almirante Brown and Pinzón;
- a residence at Ciudad de la Paz 965;
- a house at Avenida del Libertador 5280;
- apartments at Avenida del Libertador 5176–5182.

These holdings indicate accumulated professional wealth and participation in the rental-property economy. They do not establish his total net worth. The reported Córdoba marble quarry represents a further material and industrial interest.

No evidence was located of bankruptcy, insolvency, tax prosecution, hidden ownership structures, offshore accounts, or criminal financial activity.

## Architectural, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

Gianotti did not leave a large theoretical corpus. His intellectual development must chiefly be reconstructed from his projects and a small number of professional texts.

### Anti-academic position

His early work challenged strict Beaux-Arts conventions through asymmetrical decorative details, plant forms, unusual towers, theatrical entrances, and the integration of applied art. At the same time, his plans and façades often retained axial organization and established commercial or residential types.

### Total design

The collaboration with Giovanni Battista and specialist workshops allowed the architectural concept to extend into:

- furniture;
- stained glass;
- ironwork;
- bronze fittings;
- ceramics;
- signs and commercial identity;
- murals and decorative painting.

### Functionalism

The Galería Güemes and Confitería del Molino demonstrate that ornament did not exclude functional experimentation. Gianotti organized multiple uses, service circulation, basements, machinery, commercial frontage, offices, residences, performance spaces, and restaurants within technically difficult sites.

### Eclectic and Deco phases

After approximately 1918, his work became more monumental and less florally decorative. Neoclassical, Italianate, Moorish, and later Art Deco elements appeared. The Schaffhausen building and 1936 electricity-company project show an increasing preference for geometric massing and modern surfaces.

### Rationalism

His later residential works simplified ornament and used exposed brick and modern apartment planning. The CEDODAL monograph consequently frames his career as a movement “from Art Nouveau to Rationalism.” This is a useful summary but should not be interpreted as a clean break: continuity in planning, material quality, and visual distinctiveness remained.

### Political and religious outlook

No adequate evidence was found for membership in a political party, Fascist organization, Masonic lodge, Catholic movement, anarchist group, or other ideological body. His Italian nationality, professional contacts, and work for Italian-associated companies do not by themselves establish Fascist allegiance.

The unbuilt Greek Orthodox church project documents a commission or design interest, not Gianotti’s own religious affiliation.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Giovanni Battista Gianotti

Brother, collaborator, designer, and decorative supplier. The brothers’ relationship joined European artistic production with Argentine architectural commissions.

### Alfredo Melani

Teacher and formative influence at Turin. Melani’s combination of criticism, architectural history, and decorative art provides a documented educational context for Gianotti’s later practice.

### Arturo Prins and Oscar Ranzenhofer

Employers in Buenos Aires after Gianotti’s 1909 arrival. Their office gave him entry into the local profession.

### Mario Palanti

Italian émigré architect and collaborator on the Italian Pavilion at the 1910 Centennial Exposition. Later histories group Palanti, Gianotti, and Virginio Colombo as major Italian anti-academic designers in Buenos Aires, although grouping should not be confused with a continuing partnership.

### Gaetano Moretti

Designer of the Italian Pavilion whose Buenos Aires execution or installation involved Gianotti and Palanti. Moretti was an important institutional connection rather than an established long-term employer.

### Virginio Colombo

Professional contemporary and participant in the same Italian-derived anti-academic architectural scene. Sources repeatedly group their work stylistically; no specific jointly designed building between Gianotti and Colombo was established in this research.

### Emilio and Víctor San Miguel and David Ovejero

Patrons or developers of the Galería Güemes. The San Miguel family also facilitated commissions in Salta and in Buenos Aires. This was Gianotti’s most important patronage network.

### Cayetano Brenna

Owner of the Confitería del Molino and the client who gave Gianotti his second canonical commission.

### Pedro Vercesi and Amelia Vercesi

Pedro owned the Confitería La París. During that commission Gianotti reportedly met Pedro’s daughter Amelia, whom he married in 1919.

### José J. Barboni

Architect named as a collaborator on some works. The exact projects and contractual division of labor require further archival verification.

### Aníbal and Pedro Gianotti

Sons and late-career collaborators. Pedro is specifically connected with Arenales 2080.

### Troiano Troiani and Juan Francisco Finochiaro

Sculptural collaborators or co-participants in Gianotti’s 1928 Monument to the Flag competition work. Sources associate Finochiaro with the first-prize “Invicta” entry and Troiani with a separate or related Gianotti scheme.

### Pío Collivadino

Artist added to the 1928 competition jury after protests. His role was institutional and potentially adverse to Gianotti’s prospects, but no personal animosity is documented.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

### Findings of the dedicated adversarial search

Searches were conducted in Spanish, Italian, and English combining Gianotti’s name with terms corresponding to controversy, scandal, allegations, lawsuit, fraud, accusations, conspiracy, criticism, corruption, crime, money laundering, Fascism, and Mussolini, including searches constrained toward 2024–2026 material.

**Finding:** No credible evidence was located that Francisco/Francesco T. Gianotti was investigated, indicted, convicted, sued for fraud, implicated in corruption, accused of money laundering, connected to intelligence services, imprisoned, or personally involved in political violence.

Search results frequently conflated him with unrelated people named Francisco Gianotti, Francesco Gianotti, or Giannotti. Those results have no evidentiary relevance to the architect.

Absence from accessible search results is not proof that no civil dispute ever existed. Building professionals commonly generated contractual and permit records, but no personal scandal or judicial history emerged from the available evidence.

### Monument to the Flag competition controversy

**Documented controversy:** The 1928 Rosario competition was protested by Argentine artists’ bodies. Their objections included:

- the privileged invitation of several foreign-born artists and architects;
- a belief that a national monument should be entrusted to Argentine artists;
- alleged prior promises of support for national practitioners;
- concern that the jury had been constituted too late to guarantee impartiality.

President Alvear altered the jury, and the competition was declared void. Gianotti’s first-prize status therefore produced no commission.

No evidence establishes that Gianotti bribed the jury, manipulated the rules, or committed misconduct. The controversy concerned procedure, nationalism, artistic patronage, and institutional authority.

### Criticism of the Galería Güemes

A contemporaneous article by **René Villeminot**, “Algunas críticas sobre el Pasaje Güemes,” appeared in *Revista de Arquitectura* no. 8 in October 1916. Its existence demonstrates that the building was debated rather than universally acclaimed. The complete text was not available in the materials consulted here, so its arguments should not be reconstructed from title alone.

Later descriptions differ over:

- whether the gallery was the city’s first skyscraper;
- whether it was 80 or 87 metres high;
- the degree to which its design was original rather than adapted from European gallery precedents;
- whether its Art Nouveau ornament concealed or reinforced its structural modernity.

These are architectural and historiographical disputes, not allegations of wrongdoing.

### Confitería del Molino attribution and dates

Sources variously date the building to 1914–1916, 1916, or 1916–1917. Government heritage documents state that the new building was commissioned in 1916 and inaugurated in 1917; other histories give 9 July 1916 as its inauguration. The complex integration of pre-existing properties may explain why design, construction, completion, and public opening dates differ.

Decorative attribution also varies between Francisco alone and the two Gianotti brothers. The strongest formulation is that Francisco was the architect and that Giovanni Battista or his workshops contributed decorative components.

### Claims of foreign exclusivity

Statements that the Molino’s doors, windows, marble, bronze, ceramics, glass, and more than 150 square metres of stained glass were all imported from Italy are widely repeated. The family’s Milanese decorative business makes substantial importation plausible. Nonetheless, categorical claims that every important component came from Italy require bills of lading, invoices, or construction accounts not examined here.

### Fascism and political association

Because Gianotti was an Italian professional active during Mussolini’s rule and designed buildings for Italian-associated commercial organizations, it is possible to find him placed retrospectively within narratives about Italian influence in Argentina.

**Unresolved question:** No source consulted documents his membership in the Fascist Party, propaganda activity, state appointment under Mussolini, or an ideological statement supporting Fascism. Such a connection should not be inferred from nationality, style, or clientele.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## “Buenos Aires’s first skyscraper”

**Story:** The Galería Güemes was the first skyscraper in Buenos Aires or even Latin America.

**Circulation:** Municipal tourism pages, heritage descriptions, architectural tourism, and popular journalism.

**Evidence for:** It was completed in 1915, rose roughly 80 metres, used reinforced concrete, combined numerous urban functions, and was perceived as an unprecedented vertical complex.

**Evidence against or qualification:** The Railway Building was already comparable in height; contemporary technical evidence puts the Güemes closer to 80 metres than the often-repeated 87. “Skyscraper” has no stable historical threshold.

**Effect:** The claim transformed the gallery into a symbol of Buenos Aires’s metropolitan modernity and amplified Gianotti’s status as a technological pioneer.

## The 87-metre height

**Story:** The Galería Güemes reaches exactly 87 metres.

**Circulation:** Government, tourism, and popular architectural sources.

**Counterevidence:** A detailed municipal heritage volume, citing the January 1916 technical special issue, calculates approximately 76 metres to the tower plus lantern, or about 80 metres overall.

**Status:** Disputed measurement.

## “All materials came from Italy”

**Story:** Gianotti imported essentially every decorative and architectural material for the Confitería del Molino from Italy.

**Evidence for:** Documented family and commercial links with Giovanni Battista’s Italian workshops; clearly imported decorative pieces; Italian craftsmanship central to the commission.

**Qualification:** The building incorporated existing local structures, Argentine labor, and structural work performed in Buenos Aires. “All” is almost certainly rhetorical unless supported by complete construction accounts.

**Effect:** The story strengthened the Molino’s identity as a transplanted fragment of Italian *Liberty* culture.

## The windmill as a literal remnant

**Story:** The Molino’s tower windmill marked the site of an old mill.

**Evidence:** The business name referred to the historical Molino a Vapor de Lorea, once located in the wider area near Rivadavia and Rodríguez Peña. Gianotti’s sails were a later emblematic design, not preserved machinery from that mill.

**Status:** Historical association transformed into architectural symbolism.

## A direct Saint-Exupéry–Gianotti relationship

**Story:** Because **Antoine de Saint-Exupéry** lived in the Galería Güemes around 1929–1930 and reportedly wrote part of *Vol de nuit* there, he is sometimes folded into Gianotti’s personal cultural circle.

**Evidence:** Saint-Exupéry’s residence in the building is widely reported, and Gianotti maintained his office there.

**Against:** No meeting, correspondence, friendship, or collaboration between the two men was located.

**Status:** Documented shared building at different functional levels; personal relationship unverified.

## Carlos Gardel and Gianotti

**Story:** Gardel’s performance in the gallery’s theatre is sometimes presented as part of Gianotti’s personal artistic milieu.

**Evidence:** Buenos Aires heritage material records **Carlos Gardel’s performance in the Teatro Florida on 27 February 1917**.

**Against:** No personal meeting or sustained association with Gianotti is documented.

**Status:** Documented cultural event in Gianotti’s building; no documented interpersonal connection.

## “Modernismo gianottiano”

**Story or self-fashioning:** Gianotti created a distinct architectural idiom worthy of his name.

**Evidence:** The phrase appears in accounts based on his own retrospective statements and in later architectural criticism. His combination of rational planning, modern materials, historical references, and distinctive ornament is recognizable.

**Qualification:** It was not a formally organized school, manifesto-based movement, or large group of disciples.

**Effect:** The term provides a convenient bridge between the early Art Nouveau works and later rationalist buildings while reinforcing an image of Gianotti as an independent stylist.

## Significant Successes and Failures

### Successes

- Establishing an independent Buenos Aires practice within two years of immigration.
- Securing the Galería Güemes commission from a major commercial patronage network.
- Completing an unusually complex high-rise commercial passage during the disruptions of the First World War.
- Designing the Confitería del Molino while integrating multiple existing buildings and maintaining business operations.
- Developing a transatlantic architectural and decorative supply relationship with Giovanni Battista Gianotti.
- Receiving first-prize recognition in the 1928 Monument to the Flag competition.
- Maintaining a practice for approximately five decades.
- Acquiring or developing personal real estate.
- Expanding from design into transportation concepts and marble extraction.
- Adapting stylistically from Art Nouveau through eclectic, Deco, and rationalist work.

### Failures, abandoned projects, or losses

- The 1928 Monument to the Flag competition was annulled or declared void; Gianotti’s winning design was not executed.
- The Country Club Hotel, hydroglider, electricity-company headquarters, and Greek Orthodox church are not established as completed works.
- Several important commercial interiors and buildings were demolished.
- Portions of the Mercado San Miguel were altered or destroyed.
- The Galería Güemes suffered fire damage, interventions, and loss or concealment of ornament.
- The Confitería del Molino closed in 1997 and deteriorated before public acquisition and restoration.
- Gianotti’s broader output became overshadowed by two famous works, leaving much of his archive and career poorly known.

## Death, Later History of the Buildings, and Present Status

Gianotti’s death in 1967 preceded the major preservation campaigns that reshaped his reputation.

### Galería Güemes

The gallery suffered alterations, a fire affecting its Florida façade, concealment or loss of ornament, and periods of deterioration. Restoration campaigns in the 2000s uncovered or rehabilitated decorative work, including murals or frescoes attributed to the original 1915 design. Its lookout reopened to visitors in 2013, and façade restoration continued into 2014.

The gallery remains a commercial and office passage. Its continued mixed use closely resembles Gianotti’s original program, although interiors and tenants have changed.

### Confitería del Molino

The confectionery remained a major Buenos Aires institution but closed in **1997**. Deterioration and uncertainty over ownership made it a prominent preservation controversy. After national historic designation, Congress approved expropriation in **2014**, and the state acquired the property in **2017**. Extensive restoration recovered its tower, stained glass, metalwork, furniture, and production spaces.

Restoration increased public attention to Gianotti’s design methods, materials, and archival documentation.

### Mercado San Miguel

The Salta market was expropriated in 1961 amid political controversy concerning hygiene, maintenance, and public use. Later alterations removed or transformed substantial portions of Gianotti’s work. Surviving fabric retains heritage value but should not be treated as the complete 1914 design.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Gianotti’s posthumous reputation developed in several stages.

### Relative neglect

For decades, Argentine architectural histories tended to group him with other Italian “anti-academic” architects rather than examine his career separately. Galería Güemes and Confitería del Molino dominated references, obscuring his residences, commercial buildings, transport designs, industrial venture, and late rationalism.

### Scholarly recovery

Buschiazzo’s 1965 Art Nouveau study, Cacciatore’s 1986 article, Caride’s 1997 research, and the 2000 CEDODAL exhibition and monograph progressively reconstructed the range of Gianotti’s work.

### Heritage canonization

Restoration and public acquisition of his best-known buildings established him as a central figure in Buenos Aires Art Nouveau. Government and tourism narratives now regularly present:

- the Galería Güemes as an early skyscraper;
- the Confitería del Molino as a defining Belle Époque landmark;
- Gianotti as a bridge between European Art Nouveau and Argentine modern architecture.

### Reinterpretation

Recent scholarship emphasizes technological and commercial modernity rather than decoration alone. The gallery’s reinforced-concrete structure, elevators, basements, multiuse program, and freight systems and the Molino’s technically difficult integration of old and new construction complicate the older view of Gianotti as primarily an ornamental designer.

His late work also challenges a biography ending with Art Nouveau. The Schaffhausen building, electricity-company proposal, markets, and exposed-brick apartment buildings show a long transition toward modernism and rationalism.

### Limits of the legacy record

Many attributions still depend on repeated lists rather than permit files. His personal archive, political views, teaching activity, finances, family life, and Italian years remain incompletely studied. The memoir and family papers are especially important targets for future research.

A historical study of the 1928 Rosario Monument to the Flag competition attributes the proposal **“Patria de leyes justas, patria de todos”** to **Troiani–Gianotti**. The project called for a monumental obelisk and a horseshoe-shaped stepped base. The relationship between this entry and the first-prize “Invicta” scheme attributed elsewhere to Gianotti and Juan Francisco Finochiaro remains unresolved.

After protests over the 1928 Monument to the Flag competition, President Marcelo T. de Alvear added Pío Collivadino and Raúl Fitte to the jury. The enlarged jury declared the competition void, preventing Gianotti’s first-prize proposal from being constructed. No personal hostility between Collivadino and Gianotti is documented.

Saint-Exupéry resided in the Galería Güemes around 1929–1930 and is associated with writing *Vol de nuit* there. Gianotti had designed the gallery and maintained his office in it. No evidence located here proves a meeting, correspondence, friendship, or acknowledged influence between them.
