# Apostle Paul Research Dossier

**Subject:** Paul the Apostle (Greek: Παῦλος; Hebrew/Aramaic name: Saul, Σαῦλος / שָׁאוּל)  
**File prepared:** 22 August 2026  
**Historical span:** approximately the first decade CE to the 60s CE  
**Method:** documentary reconstruction, with source tiers kept distinct.

---

## Source-Critical Note

Paul is unusually well documented for a non-elite person of the first century, but the evidence is uneven.

1. **Highest-priority primary evidence:** the seven letters accepted by an overwhelming majority of critical scholars as substantially Pauline—Romans, 1 Corinthians, 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, and Philemon. They are occasional, partisan documents rather than neutral biographies, but contain contemporary first-person evidence. Second Corinthians is probably a compilation of several letters; Philippians may also be composite.
2. **Acts of the Apostles:** an anonymous narrative conventionally associated with Luke, generally dated after Paul, often c. 80–90 CE, though proposed dates range wider. Acts supplies most narrative detail—Tarsus, Gamaliel, Roman citizenship, three formal journeys, trials, voyage, and Roman house arrest—but sometimes differs from Paul's own chronology and emphases.
3. **Disputed Pauline letters:** 2 Thessalonians, Colossians, and Ephesians have divided scholarly support; 1–2 Timothy and Titus are considered post-Pauline by most critical scholars, though traditional and some contemporary scholars defend authenticity. These cannot be used uncritically as autobiography.
4. **Early reception:** 1 Clement (c. 96), Ignatius, Polycarp, Marcion's collection, the Muratorian fragment, Irenaeus, Tertullian, Origen, and Eusebius show the developing Pauline corpus and martyr tradition.
5. **Apocrypha and hagiography:** the Acts of Paul, Acts of Paul and Thecla, Acts of Peter and Paul, later martyr acts, and medieval legends preserve reception, folklore, and sometimes early local tradition, but are not eyewitness biography.
6. **External controls:** inscriptions, civic titles, Roman provincial administration, geography, roads, coins, and archaeology can test context but do not independently prove most personal episodes. The Delphi/Gallio inscription is the principal absolute chronological anchor.

Dates below are therefore approximate unless explicitly fixed. “Paul says” means an undisputed letter unless otherwise specified; “Acts says” marks the later narrative.

## Basic Identifying Information

| Field | Reconstructable information |

| Primary name | **Paul** (Greek *Paulos*, Latin *Paulus*, “small/little”), the name used in every letter bearing his name. |
| Jewish name | **Saul** (*Saulos* in Acts), likely after Israel's King Saul, a Benjaminite; Acts shifts from “Saul” to “Paul” at Acts 13:9. This is not presented as a conversion renaming. Diaspora Jews commonly used more than one culturally appropriate name. |
| Titles | “Apostle,” “apostle of Christ Jesus,” “servant/slave of Christ Jesus,” “prisoner of Christ Jesus,” “apostle to the Gentiles/nations.” Later: Saint Paul, Paul the Apostle, Apostle of the Nations/Gentiles, Doctor of the Gentiles. |

| Ethnicity and status | Self-identified Israelite, descendant of Abraham, tribe of Benjamin, Hebrew born of Hebrews, formerly a Pharisee (Rom 11:1; 2 Cor 11:22; Phil 3:5). Greek-speaking Jew of the diaspora. Acts additionally calls him a native of Tarsus and Roman citizen by birth. |

## Family and Ancestry

Paul names no parents. His own claims establish Jewish ancestry and Benjaminite tribal identity, but no genealogy. Acts' Roman-citizenship claim, if historical, implies that an ancestor or parent acquired citizenship through grant, manumission, military or civic service, or purchase; no mechanism is known. Citizenship was not automatic for all residents of Tarsus.

Acts 23:16 mentions “the son of Paul's sister,” who learns of a plot in Jerusalem and alerts the authorities. This is the sole narrative evidence for a sibling or nephew. Their names, residence history, and later lives are unknown.

Paul was unmarried during his surviving ministry (1 Cor 7:7–8; 9:5). Whether he had always been unmarried, was widowed, divorced, or separated is unresolved. The claim that membership in the Sanhedrin required marriage, sometimes used to infer a deceased wife, rests both on the uncertain claim that he was a voting Sanhedrist and on later norms. No spouse, partner, biological child, or household is securely attested. He used kinship language—Timothy and Titus as children, Phoebe as sister, congregations as children—but metaphorically.

## Childhood and Early Life

Acts 21:39 and 22:3 give Tarsus, capital of Cilicia, as his birthplace. Tarsus was a major Hellenistic city connected to Mediterranean commerce and the routes across the Taurus Mountains. It had a reputation for education in Strabo, although this does not demonstrate that Paul attended its schools.

Acts 22:3 can be punctuated to imply either upbringing in Jerusalem from youth or later education there. It says he was educated “at the feet of Gamaliel,” generally identified with Gamaliel I, a prominent Pharisaic teacher. Paul himself never names Gamaliel, Jerusalem schooling, or Tarsus. His letters nonetheless show deep scriptural formation, command of Greek rhetoric, and familiarity with Jewish interpretive methods. Direct dependence on Stoic, Cynic, or mystery-cult schooling is debated; parallels prove a shared Hellenistic environment, not enrollment in a philosophical school.

Philippians 3:5 says Paul was circumcised on the eighth day. He presents his pre-Christian life as exemplary zeal “for the traditions of my ancestors” and “as to righteousness under the law, blameless” (Gal 1:13–14; Phil 3:4–6). “Pharisee” appears in Phil 3:5 and in Acts. His later polemics should not be retrojected into a simple rejection of Judaism: he continued to call himself an Israelite and expected Israel's eventual salvation (Romans 9–11).

## Education and Formation

Paul's formation had several observable layers:

- **Jewish scripture:** he usually quotes the Greek Septuagint, interprets Abraham, Adam, Hagar and Sarah, Moses, Elijah, and Israel typologically, and deploys midrash-like argument.
- **Diaspora Greek culture:** his rhetoric includes diatribe, athletic and military metaphors, household advice, friendship conventions, and civic vocabulary. His letters follow Greco-Roman epistolary forms but reshape their greetings and thanksgivings.
- **Pharisaic zeal:** his autobiographical passages locate him within zealous ancestral practice before the revelation of Christ.
- **Jesus movement:** Paul says he “received” traditions about the Lord's Supper and resurrection appearances (1 Cor 11:23–26; 15:3–7), formulas likely older than his letters. He knew Cephas/Peter and James, “the Lord's brother.”
- **Revelation and vocation:** he grounded apostolic authority in a revelation/appearance of the risen Jesus, not appointment by human authorities (Gal 1:1, 11–17; 1 Cor 9:1; 15:8).

## Early Career: Persecutor and Convert/Apostle

### Persecution

Paul admits that he violently persecuted “the assembly/church of God” and tried to destroy it (Gal 1:13; Phil 3:6; 1 Cor 15:9). He does not specify victims, legal authority, imprisonment, or deaths. Acts expands this into guarding witnesses' garments at Stephen's stoning, entering houses, imprisoning believers, voting for executions, and obtaining high-priestly letters to Damascus. These details are possible but dependent on Acts; the high priest's authority to extradite sectarians from Roman Syria is disputed.

This is the strongest documented “criminal/political violence” issue in the dossier: Paul himself confesses coercive persecution, but the scale and acts cannot be quantified. No independent charge, trial record, or victim testimony survives.

### Revelation near/in Damascus

Paul says God revealed his Son “in” him so that he might proclaim him among the Gentiles (Gal 1:15–16). He later lists an appearance of the risen Christ to himself “as to one untimely born” (1 Cor 15:8). He gives no road, light, fall, blindness, or Ananias.

Acts narrates the event three times (Acts 9, 22, 26), with variations: companions hear a voice in one version but do not understand/hear it in another; they stand in one and fall in another. The core is a heavenly light, Jesus' voice, temporary blindness, Ananias, baptism, and commission. The event is commonly dated c. 32–36 CE, depending on Jesus' death and Paul's later intervals.

Calling it “conversion” is traditional but contested. Paul understood it as revelation, prophetic call, and commissioning; “call” better preserves his continuing Jewish identity, while “conversion” captures his radical change of allegiance and conduct.

### Arabia, Damascus, and first Jerusalem visit

Galatians 1 states that Paul did not immediately consult Jerusalem; he went into “Arabia” and returned to Damascus. “Arabia” probably means the Nabataean kingdom, but destination and activity are unknown. Three years later he visited Jerusalem for fifteen days, meeting Cephas and James but no other apostle. Then he went to Syria and Cilicia. In 2 Cor 11:32–33 he recalls escape from Damascus in a basket through a window while the ethnarch of King Aretas guarded the city. Whether Aretas IV controlled Damascus directly or acted through a Nabataean community is debated; the passage is difficult to date precisely, often before Aretas's death in 40 CE.

Acts compresses and rearranges this phase: immediate synagogue preaching, a Damascus plot, Jerusalem introduction by Barnabas, and departure to Tarsus. Acts omits Arabia.

## Chronological Life History

### c. 30s–mid-40s: Syria-Cilicia and Antioch

Paul's own brief notice places him in Syria and Cilicia after his first Jerusalem visit. Acts says Barnabas retrieved him from Tarsus for work in Antioch, where Jesus-followers were first called “Christians.” The Antioch movement was ethnically mixed and became Paul's principal launch point.

Acts records a famine-relief trip by Barnabas and Saul to Jerusalem during Claudius's reign. Galatians records a Jerusalem visit “after fourteen years” with Barnabas and Titus. Scholars dispute whether Galatians 2 corresponds to Acts 11's famine visit or Acts 15's council. Paul's wording may count fourteen years from revelation or his prior visit, inclusively or exclusively.

### Jerusalem agreement and dispute

In Galatians 2, Paul privately presented his Gentile gospel to the recognized “pillars”—James, Cephas, and John. Titus, a Greek, was not compelled to be circumcised. They acknowledged Paul's mission to the uncircumcised and requested remembrance of the poor. Paul presents this as recognition, not subordination.

Acts 15 gives a public council: Peter speaks, Barnabas and Paul report, and James issues a judgment that Gentiles need not be circumcised but should observe several prohibitions. Differences include sequence, participants, decrees, and rhetoric. Paul never mentions the Acts decree.

At Antioch, Paul publicly confronted Cephas/Peter for withdrawing from table fellowship with Gentiles when men came “from James.” Barnabas also withdrew. Paul's letter preserves his rebuke but not the outcome; the episode shows that the early movement was not institutionally uniform.

### c. 46–49: First journey in Acts

Acts 13–14 sends Barnabas, Saul/Paul, and John Mark from Antioch to Cyprus, then Perga, Pisidian Antioch, Iconium, Lystra, and Derbe, returning through the same cities and Attalia. At Paphos Paul confronts the Jewish magician Bar-Jesus/Elymas before proconsul Sergius Paulus. At Lystra, a healing leads locals to identify Barnabas as Zeus and Paul as Hermes; later opponents stone Paul and leave him for dead. These episodes lack direct Pauline corroboration, although Paul confirms severe beatings, stoning, dangers, and ministry in Galatia.

The Acts of Paul and Thecla later inserts Thecla into the Iconium/Pisidian Antioch phase; she does not appear in Acts or Paul's authentic letters.

### c. 49–52: Macedonia, Athens, and Corinth

Acts reports disagreement between Paul and Barnabas over John Mark. Barnabas takes Mark to Cyprus; Paul takes Silas through Syria and Cilicia. At Lystra Paul recruits Timothy, son of a Jewish mother and Greek father, and circumcises him—an episode sometimes seen as tension with Galatians, though defenders distinguish voluntary mission strategy from compulsory circumcision.

After a vision of a Macedonian man, the group crosses from Troas to Philippi. Lydia, a dealer in purple cloth, hosts them. Paul and Silas are beaten and jailed after Paul expels a divining spirit from an enslaved girl whose owners lose income. Acts claims their Roman citizenship makes the untried beating illegal.

They pass through Thessalonica and Beroea; Paul proceeds to Athens, where Acts places the Areopagus speech. Paul then lives in Corinth with Aquila and Prisca/Priscilla, Jewish artisans expelled from Rome under Claudius. He works at their trade, teaches, and becomes involved in a synagogue conflict.

Paul's First Thessalonians, usually dated c. 49–51 and often considered the earliest surviving Christian text, comes from this Macedonian/Achaean phase. It addresses persecution, sexual conduct, mutual labor, and anxiety over believers who died before Christ's return.

Acts 18 places Paul before Lucius Junius Gallio, proconsul of Achaia. Gallio refuses to treat the intra-Jewish dispute as a Roman crime. The Delphi inscription referring to Gallio and Claudius's acclamations anchors Gallio's tenure around 51–52 CE and therefore Paul's Corinthian stay. No Roman trial file survives.

### c. 52–57: Ephesus and the Corinthian crisis

After a short stop at Ephesus and travel via Caesarea/Antioch, Acts depicts Paul revisiting Galatia and Phrygia and settling in Ephesus for over two years. Aquila and Priscilla instruct Apollos, an Alexandrian preacher. Acts describes exorcisms, converts burning magical books, and a riot led by Demetrius and silversmiths whose Artemis-shrine trade was threatened.

Paul's letters confirm a long Ephesian-centered period, intense danger, and a network across Asia. “I fought with beasts at Ephesus” (1 Cor 15:32) is probably metaphorical; Roman citizens were normally exempt from arena execution, and no literal episode is documented. He mentions “affliction in Asia” so severe he despaired of life (2 Cor 1:8–10).

The Corinth correspondence was larger than the canonical pair:

- a lost earlier letter mentioned in 1 Cor 5:9;
- 1 Corinthians, answering reports and a letter from Corinth;
- a painful visit (inferred from 2 Corinthians);
- a severe/tearful letter, perhaps partly preserved in 2 Cor 10–13;
- one or more reconciliatory letters, perhaps 2 Cor 1–9.

Issues included factions invoking Paul, Apollos, Cephas, or Christ; a man living with his father's wife; litigation; sex work; marriage and celibacy; idol food; women's prayer and prophecy; class humiliation at the Lord's Supper; ecstatic speech; resurrection denial; apostolic finances; and rival “super-apostles.” Paul used irony, threats of discipline, paternal authority, pleading, and intermediaries.

### Macedonia, Illyricum, and Romans

Paul traveled through Macedonia, collected funds, and eventually reached Corinth. Romans, commonly dated c. 56–58, was dictated to Tertius and commends Phoebe, a *diakonos* and *prostatis* of the Cenchreae church, likely its courier and interpreter. Paul says his mission extended “from Jerusalem and as far around as Illyricum.” He planned to deliver the collection to Jerusalem, then visit Rome and proceed to Spain.

The collection for poor Jerusalem believers occupied years (1 Cor 16; 2 Cor 8–9; Rom 15). Delegates and advance procedures were designed to avoid suspicion. Paul refused support in some places and accepted it in others, especially from Philippi. The sums are unknown. His language makes the gift material aid, reciprocity for spiritual benefits, interchurch solidarity, and perhaps an eschatological offering. Whether Jerusalem accepted it is never stated.

### c. 57–60: Jerusalem arrest, Caesarea detention

Acts says Paul returned through Macedonia, Troas, Miletus, Tyre, and Caesarea. At Miletus he delivered a farewell to Ephesian elders. Despite prophecies of arrest, he went to Jerusalem and met James and elders. To counter reports that he taught diaspora Jews to abandon Moses, he joined men completing a vow and paid expenses.

Jews from Asia accused him of teaching against the people, law, and temple and of bringing Greeks into the sacred precinct. A crowd seized him; Roman troops intervened. Acts depicts successive defenses before the crowd, Sanhedrin, governor Felix, governor Festus, and Herod Agrippa II. A plot led Paul's nephew to warn the tribune, who transferred him to Caesarea.

The charges in Acts concern temple profanation and sectarian disturbance. Paul denies introducing Trophimus into the restricted court. The narrative gives no conviction. Felix keeps him for two years and allegedly hopes for a bribe; Festus succeeds him. Paul appeals to Caesar as a citizen. Whether a citizen could appeal at exactly this procedural stage is discussed, but the broad right of appeal is historically plausible.

This entire arrest-to-Rome story depends on Acts. Paul's letters written before Jerusalem express fear of hostile Judeans and uncertainty that his service will be accepted (Rom 15:30–31), lending indirect support to danger but not the trial details.

### c. 59–62: Voyage, shipwreck, and Rome

Acts 27–28 provides a detailed maritime narrative: transfer under centurion Julius; stops along the Levantine and Anatolian coast; storm after leaving Crete; fourteen days adrift; shipwreck on Malta; winter there; arrival through Syracuse, Rhegium, and Puteoli; then Rome. On Malta, Paul survives a viper and heals Publius's father and others.

Acts ends with Paul under guarded house arrest for two years, receiving visitors and preaching “with all boldness and without hindrance.” It does not state trial outcome or death. The abrupt ending has produced competing theories: composition before trial, a literary open ending, missing sequel, or the author's lack of later information.

Philippians and Philemon attest a real imprisonment and possible execution, but the location is debated among Rome, Caesarea, and Ephesus. Ephesus fits proximity and coworker movements; Rome fits later tradition and Acts. Colossians and Ephesians, if Pauline, belong to a prison phase. The Pastorals, if authentic, require release and further activity; if pseudonymous, they construct a final Pauline itinerary.

### Spain and a second career: unresolved

Romans proves an intention to travel to Spain, not fulfillment. 1 Clement says Paul reached “the limit of the West,” which may mean Spain or simply Rome from an eastern perspective. The Muratorian fragment mentions departure from the city for Spain; later writers make the journey explicit. No Spanish community, inscription, or contemporary letter independently confirms it.

## Institutions, Projects, Technologies, and Financial Interests

Paul did not establish a modern company or hold known property. His durable “institutional” project was a network of urban assemblies connected by letters, visitors, collections, shared ritual, and mutual recognition.

### Mission network

The network included Antioch, Galatian communities, Philippi, Thessalonica, Corinth/Cenchreae, Ephesus and Asian communities, and Rome, with possible activity in Illyricum. Households supplied meeting rooms, patronage, lodging, food, introductions, and communications. Named hosts or patrons include Lydia, Jason, Aquila and Prisca, Gaius, Phoebe, Nympha (in disputed Colossians), and Philemon.

### Communications technology

Letters were collaborative performances, probably dictated to an amanuensis, carried by trusted agents, read aloud, copied, and exchanged. Tertius identifies himself as scribe of Romans; Paul marks some letters with a final greeting in his own hand. Co-senders include Sosthenes, Timothy, and Silvanus. There is no evidence Paul invented a cipher, patent, or proprietary technology.

### Manual work and patronage

Paul repeatedly says he worked “night and day” to avoid burdening communities (1 Thess 2:9; 1 Cor 4:12). Acts links him to Aquila and Priscilla through *skenopoios* work. He defended a right to material support while sometimes renouncing it. Philippi sent aid more than once; he praises their “partnership.” His refusal of Corinthian patronage may have protected independence but also became a source of conflict and perceived insult.

### Jerusalem collection and safeguards

Paul requested weekly setting aside of funds, used approved delegates, and stressed honorable administration “not only in the Lord's sight but also in the sight of others” (2 Cor 8:20–21). Accusations of exploiting Corinthians appear in his defensive rhetoric (2 Cor 12:16–18), but no evidence establishes embezzlement. The defensive procedure shows reputational risk, not proven fraud.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

Paul held no known civic office. His preaching had political implications: “Jesus is Lord,” an alternative assembly (*ekklesia*), a coming divine kingdom, and a crucified man as God's exalted ruler could collide symbolically with imperial claims. Acts repeatedly portrays Roman magistrates as finding no actionable sedition, while local economic and communal conflicts generate violence.

### Central convictions

- Israel's God raised the crucified Jesus and would complete cosmic restoration.
- Gentiles could join Abraham's family through Christ and the Spirit without circumcision or full Torah conversion.
- Baptism incorporated believers into Christ's death and new life.
- The Spirit distributed gifts within an interdependent body.
- Christ would return; earliest letters expect an imminent transformation, while later passages contemplate death before it.
- The cross overturned conventional honor, wisdom, and power.
- Communities must practice mutual support, sexual discipline, reconciliation, and care for the weak.

### Law, Judaism, and Gentiles

Paul's statements range from harsh polemic against circumcision for Gentile converts to praise of the law as holy and Israel's gifts as irrevocable. Major interpretive schools include:

1. **Augustinian/Lutheran and Reformation readings:** justification by faith/grace opposed to human works or merit.
2. **New Perspective:** E. P. Sanders, James D. G. Dunn, and others emphasize Second Temple Judaism's covenantal framework and see “works of law” especially as boundary practices marking Jews from Gentiles.
3. **Paul within Judaism:** argues Paul remained Torah-observant as a Jew and directed law-free teaching specifically to Gentiles.
4. **Apocalyptic readings:** emphasize God's invasive deliverance in Christ rather than a human progression from law to grace.

No single synthesis has closed the debate.

### Women

Authentic letters name women as coworkers and leaders: Phoebe (*diakonos*, *prostatis*), Prisca, Junia (“prominent among the apostles” or “well known to the apostles,” grammatically disputed), Mary, Tryphaena, Tryphosa, Persis, Euodia, Syntyche, and Chloe's people. Women pray and prophesy in 1 Cor 11, with head covering. 1 Cor 14:34–35 orders women to be silent; many scholars regard the verses as an interpolation or a limited instruction because they conflict with 1 Cor 11 and float in manuscript order, while others accept Pauline authorship. 1 Timothy 2's broad restriction is in a letter most critical scholars consider post-Pauline. Reception frequently treated all restrictions as Paul's.

### Slavery

Paul did not demand abolition. He used slavery metaphors and instructed enslaved and free believers within existing structures. In Philemon he returns/sends Onesimus, an enslaved man, to Philemon, urging reception “no longer as a slave but more than a slave, a beloved brother,” without explicitly requiring manumission. Interpretations range from tactful pressure to free Onesimus to accommodation of slavery. Later pro- and antislavery readers both invoked Paul.

### Sexuality and marriage

Paul preferred celibacy as a gift and because “the present form of this world is passing away,” but allowed marriage and insisted on mutual conjugal obligations. He condemned porneia/adultery and male same-sex relations in 1 Cor 6:9–10 and Rom 1:26–27; translation, ancient categories, exploitation, desire, and applicability to modern identities remain heavily debated. His texts became foundational to later Christian sexual ethics.

### Government

Romans 13 urges submission to governing authorities as servants of God who punish wrongdoing; Paul himself experienced beatings and imprisonment and invoked legal rights. The passage has supported order, taxation, resistance debates, and authoritarian uses. Its immediate Roman context and relation to Romans 12's nonretaliation remain contested.

## Controversies, Allegations, Violence, and Legal Questions

### Documented by Paul

- **Persecution of the church:** admitted; violent character admitted; victims and acts unspecified.
- **Repeated punishments:** Paul reports five synagogue floggings, three beatings with rods, one stoning, repeated imprisonments, dangers, hunger, exposure, and shipwrecks (2 Cor 11). Exact dates and jurisdictions are mostly unknown.
- **Community discipline:** he ordered exclusion of a sexually offending member and delivered offenders “to Satan,” language whose practical consequences are uncertain.
- **Polemical abuse:** he wished Galatian circumcision advocates would castrate themselves; called opponents dogs, mutilators, false apostles, and deceitful workers. These are attested rhetorical attacks, not neutral descriptions of the opponents.
- **Apostolic authority:** rivals accused him of weakness in person, poor speech, self-commendation, financial manipulation, and deceit. Paul denied exploiting converts. With no opponent documents, findings are impossible.

### Narrated by Acts

- Participation in Stephen's killing and votes for executions: later narrative, not independently confirmed.
- Arrest warrants from the high priest to Damascus: later narrative; jurisdiction questioned.
- Exorcism affecting owners' revenue, riot at Ephesus, repeated synagogue and civic disputes: narrative claims with plausible settings but no external case files.
- Temple-profanation allegation: Acts calls it a mistaken inference about Trophimus. No verdict survives.
- Roman citizenship: asserted repeatedly by Acts, never by Paul. Some scholars accept it as explaining appeal and treatment; others note Paul's reported beatings and silence about it.
- Appeal to Nero/Caesar: only Acts; historically plausible, outcome unknown.

### Christianity's separation from Judaism

Paul's Gentile mission, circumcision polemic, and later Christian use of “law versus gospel” contributed to Christian differentiation from Judaism. Yet Paul did not call Judaism a superseded false religion in modern terms; Romans 9–11 insists on Israel's privileges and future. Anti-Jewish interpreters have used Pauline passages, while modern scholarship has recovered his Jewish framework. Historical influence must be distinguished from authorial intention.

## Health, Illness, and Psychological Questions

Paul mentions a “thorn in the flesh, a messenger of Satan” (2 Cor 12:7–10). Proposals include eye disease, epilepsy, malaria, chronic pain, speech impediment, depression, sexual temptation, persecutors, or a metaphorical opponent. None can be established. Gal 4:13–15 refers to a bodily illness during first preaching to the Galatians and hyperbolically says they would have torn out their eyes for him; this has fueled an eye-disease theory but does not prove it.

He reports extreme distress, despairing of life (2 Cor 1:8), fear, sleeplessness, and daily anxiety for churches. These are firsthand descriptions of crisis, not enough for retrospective psychiatric diagnosis. No addiction is documented. Injuries from floggings, beatings, stoning, exposure, and shipwreck are probable if his catalogue is broadly accurate; their lasting effects are unknown.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. “Saul was renamed Paul at conversion”

**Story:** Jesus changed Saul's name to Paul.  
**Appearance/circulation:** common sermons, children's Bibles, and popular retellings.  
**Evidence:** Acts uses “Saul” after the Damascus episode and only at Acts 13:9 says “Saul, who was also called Paul.” No renaming scene exists.  
**Assessment:** popular myth arising from analogy with biblical renamings.  
**Effect:** symbolizes conversion but obscures ordinary dual naming in the diaspora.

### 2. Thecla as Paul's disciple

**Story:** Thecla hears Paul preach chastity at Iconium, abandons marriage, survives fire and beasts, baptizes herself, dresses as a man, receives authority to teach, and becomes healer/saint.  
**Earliest secure setting:** Acts of Paul and Thecla, generally late second century; Tertullian says an Asian presbyter composed an Acts of Paul “out of love for Paul” and was removed after confessing.  
**Circulation:** Greek, Latin, Syriac, Armenian, Coptic, and Ethiopic traditions; major cult at Seleucia/Meriamlik and elsewhere.  
**Evidence:** no Thecla in authentic letters or canonical Acts. Some names/settings overlap Pauline traditions, but miraculous episodes are hagiographic.  
**Effect:** Thecla became a premier female apostle/protomartyr and a contested warrant for women teaching and baptizing. The story also transmitted the best-known physical “portrait” of Paul.

### 3. Beheading on the Ostian Way

**Story:** Nero condemned Paul; because he was a Roman citizen he was beheaded, traditionally at Aquae Salviae/Tre Fontane; his head bounced three times and springs arose; burial occurred on the Ostian Way at today's San Paolo fuori le Mura.  
**Development:** 1 Clement only says Paul suffered martyrdom after reaching the West. Dionysius of Corinth, reported by Eusebius, links Peter and Paul in Italy; Tertullian compares Paul's death to John the Baptist's; Eusebius locates execution under Nero. Detailed beheading and spring legends are later.  
**Evidence:** early and geographically coherent martyr tradition, but no contemporary execution record or remains authenticated by independent methods. Excavations beneath the basilica identify an ancient memorial and sarcophagus associated with the cult, not proof of identity.  
**Effect:** established Rome's joint Peter-Paul apostolic prestige and feast on 29 June.

### 4. Paul traveled to Spain

**Story:** after release in Rome, Paul completed his planned Spanish mission.  
**Sources:** intention in Romans 15; ambiguous “limit of the West” in 1 Clement; later Muratorian and patristic claims.  
**Evidence:** intention is fact; completion unverified.  
**Effect:** Spain developed Pauline sites and apostolic-memory claims.

### 5. Paul as founder or corrupter of Christianity

**Story:** in competing modern narratives Paul either faithfully systematized Jesus' gospel or invented a Gentile, divine-Christ religion unrelated to Jesus. Some conspiracy versions allege Roman intelligence control or deliberate imperial fabrication.  
**Appearance:** ancient opponents already contested Paul; modern forms grew through Enlightenment criticism, F. C. Baur, Nietzschean polemic, comparative-religion theories, and internet conspiracy culture.  
**Evidence:** Paul's letters prove a distinct, powerful interpreter operating within an already existing Jesus movement, using traditions older than himself and negotiating with Jesus' brother and earliest disciples. No evidence supports Roman-agent or intelligence-control allegations. Discontinuities and developments between Jesus traditions and Paul remain legitimate scholarly questions.  
**Effect:** made Paul a central figure in debates over “Jesus versus Christianity,” antisemitism, church authority, and historical origins.

### 6. Epilepsy or temporal-lobe seizure caused the Damascus event

**Story:** the light, fall, voice, and blindness were neurological symptoms.  
**Appearance:** modern medical retrospectives.  
**Evidence:** only Acts supplies these symptoms, in literary narratives written decades later; Paul gives no clinical description. No diagnosis is possible.  
**Effect:** offers a naturalizing counter-myth but is not medical evidence.

### 7. Paul's incorrupt body or authenticated bones

**Story:** remains beneath the Roman basilica are Paul's and sometimes possess miraculous qualities.  
**Evidence:** longstanding cult location; a sarcophagus under the altar and carbon-dated organic fragments reported as first/second century. The sampling chain and identity do not establish that the bones are Paul's.  
**Effect:** reinforces pilgrimage and Roman custodianship.

### 8. Anti-Pauline religious traditions

Some Jewish-Christian groups portrayed Paul as apostate or enemy of the law; the Pseudo-Clementines attack a figure often read as a veiled Paul, though identification is debated. Islamic tradition generally does not name Paul in the Qur'an; later Muslim polemic frequently casts him as corrupter of Jesus' monotheism. These are reception histories, not independent first-century biographies.

### Disputed/deutero-Pauline letters

- **2 Thessalonians:** divided opinion; close imitation of 1 Thessalonians alongside different eschatological sequencing.
- **Colossians:** divided; vocabulary, style, cosmic Christology, and church conception weighed against named coworkers and plausible secretary/coauthor models.
- **Ephesians:** majority critical view is post-Pauline; depends heavily on Colossians, has long periodic style and a more universalized church. Earliest manuscripts omit “in Ephesus,” suggesting a circular letter.
- **1 Timothy, 2 Timothy, Titus (Pastorals):** majority critical view dates them after Paul's death because of vocabulary, church offices, opponents, social accommodation, and itinerary; authenticity defenders invoke changing circumstances and secretaries. 2 Timothy contains vivid first-person prison/testament material that may preserve fragments.
- **Hebrews:** anonymous and almost universally non-Pauline in modern scholarship. Origen already distinguished its polished Greek and famously concluded that only God knew who wrote it. Western Christianity long hesitated; later manuscripts/canons placed it with Paul.

### Lost letters and fragments inferred from canonical letters

- Precanonical Corinthian letter (1 Cor 5:9).
- “Letter of tears” or “severe letter” (2 Cor 2:3–4; 7:8), possibly embedded in 2 Cor 10–13.
- Possible additional Corinthian correspondence behind 2 Corinthians' composite structure.
- Letter to Laodicea mentioned at Col 4:16, if Colossians reflects a real letter; may mean a letter from rather than to Laodicea.

### Ancient pseudepigrapha

- **3 Corinthians / Correspondence of Paul and the Corinthians:** part of Acts of Paul; temporarily canonical in some Syriac and Armenian settings.
- **Epistle to the Laodiceans:** short Latin cento assembled from Pauline phrases; medieval circulation.
- **Correspondence of Paul and Seneca:** fourteen letters presenting mutual admiration; generally fourth-century pseudepigrapha, historically influential.
- **Epistle to the Alexandrians:** condemned in the Muratorian fragment; lost.
- **Prayer of the Apostle Paul:** Valentinian/Gnostic text in Codex I of Nag Hammadi; pseudonymous.
- **Apocalypse of Paul / Visio Pauli:** fourth-century tour of heaven and hell based loosely on 2 Cor 12; highly influential in medieval afterlife literature.
- **Coptic Apocalypse of Paul (Nag Hammadi V):** separate Gnostic revelatory work.
- **Acts of Paul**, including Acts of Paul and Thecla, 3 Corinthians, and Martyrdom of Paul: late second-century narrative corpus, not by Paul.

### Speeches

Acts attributes numerous speeches to Paul—Pisidian Antioch, Lystra, Athens Areopagus, Miletus, Jerusalem defenses, and hearings before governors. Ancient historians commonly composed speeches appropriate to occasions; they may reflect Pauline themes but are not verbatim transcripts.

## Death and Burial Traditions

Paul's last datable undisputed letter is usually Romans c. 56–58, though prison letters may be later. Acts ends alive in Rome after two years. First Clement 5, written from Rome near the end of the first century, is the earliest extrabiblical witness: Paul endured repeated imprisonment and exile, testified before rulers, reached the “limit of the West,” and died after martyrdom. It does not name Nero, Rome, or beheading.

Dionysius of Corinth, quoted by Eusebius, says Peter and Paul taught in Italy and were martyred about the same time. Tertullian refers to Paul's death like John the Baptist's, suggesting beheading. Eusebius says Paul was beheaded and Peter crucified at Rome under Nero. A presbyter Gaius, also quoted by Eusebius, points to apostolic “trophies” at the Vatican and Ostian Way.

The conventional date range 64–67 reflects Nero's persecution after Rome's fire in 64 and Nero's death in 68. If Paul died in the 64 persecution, Tacitus' description of mass punishment supplies context but does not name him. A later execution after a second arrest depends on the Pastorals and later acts.

Traditional burial is beneath the Basilica of Saint Paul Outside the Walls on the Via Ostiensis. An inscribed marble slab “PAULO APOSTOLO MART” and a sarcophagus mark an ancient cult site. In 2009 Pope Benedict XVI announced that a probe found textile, incense, and bone fragments carbon-dated to the first or second century. This is compatible with an early burial but cannot identify an individual as Paul.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Formation of the canon

Paul's letters were collected early. 2 Peter already refers to “all [Paul's] letters” and says some passages are difficult, though 2 Peter's date and authorship are disputed. Marcion made ten Pauline letters central to his canon; proto-orthodox writers responded by claiming Paul within a larger apostolic canon. P46 shows a substantial collection by around 200.

### Theology and institutions

Paul's interpretations of Christ, Gentile inclusion, grace, faith, baptism, Eucharist, church as body, charismatic gifts, resurrection, and mission became basic Christian vocabulary. Augustine's anti-Pelagian writings, medieval scholasticism, Luther's reading of Romans and Galatians, Calvin, Wesley, Barth, liberation theology, feminist interpretation, and modern ecumenism repeatedly reconstructed Paul for new disputes.

### Liturgical cult

Paul is venerated as apostle and martyr across Catholic, Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, Anglican, and Lutheran traditions. Peter and Paul share 29 June in major calendars; 25 January commemorates the Conversion of Paul. Patronages include missionaries, theologians, writers, publicists, rope-makers, tentmakers, and the city of Rome. Churches, cities (São Paulo), orders, universities, and missions bear his name.

### Art and iconography

Early images often give Paul a narrow face, high forehead, pointed beard, and receding hairline, paired with Peter. Later Western art gives him a sword, both instrument of martyrdom and “sword of the Spirit,” and a book/scroll. Major representations include mosaics at Ravenna and Rome; Dürer's apostles; Michelangelo's *Conversion of Saul*; Caravaggio's two conversion paintings; Raphael's cartoons and *St Paul Preaching at Athens*; Rembrandt's self-identifications as Paul; and El Greco's apostles.

### Social and political reception

Pauline texts have been used to defend and challenge slavery, patriarchy, state obedience, celibacy, marriage norms, antisemitism, universal human equality, and revolutionary community. Galatians 3:28 became a resource for abolitionists and equality movements; household and silence texts were used in the opposite direction. Romans 13 served monarchies and dictatorships but was also delimited by interpreters invoking conscience and divine judgment. These effects are documented reception, not proof that every later use matches Paul's intention.

### Historical evaluation

Paul did not single-handedly found Christianity: communities, traditions, apostles, and Gentile missions predated or paralleled him. He did become the earliest Christian author whose words survive, the best-documented first-generation missionary, and the principal architect of a durable translocal Gentile network. The survival and canonization of his letters magnified his voice over coworkers and rivals whose writings were lost.

## Atlas Connections

### Abraham

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Paul interpreted Abraham as the paradigmatic believer and ancestor of both circumcised and uncircumcised faithful in Romans 4 and Galatians 3, written c. 48–58 CE. Because Abraham is the earlier figure and Paul the later writer, Paul receives the negative sign. [Galatians overview and textual references](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/intro/?search=Galatians&version=NABRE); [Romans overview](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/intro/?search=Romans&version=NABRE).

### King David

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Paul wrote that Jesus was descended from David in Romans 1:3 and quoted David as testimony concerning justification in Romans 4:6–8, c. 56–58 CE. These are scriptural interpretations, not evidence of personal contact. [Romans](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans&version=NIV).

### Moses

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Paul repeatedly interpreted Moses and the Mosaic law, notably in Romans 5–10, 1 Corinthians 10, 2 Corinthians 3, and Galatians 3–4. Romans explicitly situates Moses between Abraham and Christ in Paul's history of salvation. [Romans overview](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/intro/?search=Romans&version=NABRE).

### Jesus of Nazareth

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Paul's undisputed letters, c. 49–58 CE, repeatedly interpret the earlier life, crucifixion, resurrection, teachings, and ritual traditions of Jesus. Paul additionally reports receiving a revelation of Jesus and seeing the risen Christ (Gal 1:11–17; 1 Cor 9:1; 15:3–11); those are documented first-person claims, although their supernatural content is not independently verifiable. This is classified as T1 rather than T5 because Paul does not claim to have met Jesus during Jesus' public life. [Galatians](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Galatians%201&version=NIV).

### Simon bar Jonah (Apostle Peter/Cephas)

- **[T5]** **Documented fact:** Paul says that approximately three years after his call he stayed with Cephas in Jerusalem for fifteen days; later, Cephas joined Paul, James, and John in the Jerusalem mission agreement, and Paul subsequently confronted him publicly over Gentile table fellowship at Antioch (Gal 1:18; 2:1–14), probably during the later 30s through 40s CE. This is Paul's own near-contemporary testimony, although the exact chronology remains disputed. [Galatians introduction](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/intro/?search=Galatians&version=NABRE).

### Saint Thecla of Iconium

- **[T5]** **Rumor or myth:** The late-second-century *Acts of Paul and Thecla* places Paul and Thecla together at Iconium and Pisidian Antioch, where she hears him preach, follows him, and later receives his blessing or commission. No authentic Pauline letter or canonical Acts names her, and Tertullian reports that an Asian presbyter composed the work “out of love for Paul”; the alleged meeting is therefore hagiographic rather than documented biography. [Text and historical introduction](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/pages/frontline/shows/religion/maps/primary/thecla.html); [Tertullian, *On Baptism*](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0321.htm).

### Tertullian

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Writing at Carthage around 198–220 CE, Tertullian repeatedly discussed Paul and his letters, including Paul's baptism and apostolic standing in *On Baptism* and his death and authority elsewhere. He also supplied the earliest surviving report about the composition of the *Acts of Paul and Thecla*. Paul, as the earlier figure written about, receives the positive sign. [Tertullian, *On Baptism*](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0321.htm).

### Origen of Alexandria

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** In the early-to-mid third century, principally at Alexandria and Caesarea, Origen produced commentaries or homilies on numerous Pauline letters, including a fifteen-book *Commentary on Romans*. Much survives only in fragments or Rufinus's abbreviated Latin translation, but the literary engagement itself is securely documented. [British Library manuscript description](https://iiif.biblissima.fr/collections/manifest/206818cb8523aa11be64fe7b349a3e47943d13d9); [study of Origen's Pauline commentaries](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/heyj.12168).

### Augustine of Hippo

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Augustine engaged Paul's letters throughout his writings in Roman North Africa, especially from the 390s until his death in 430. His renewed reading of Paul around 395 materially shaped his mature doctrines of grace, predestination, and original sin. Paul is the earlier, written-about figure. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/augustine/).

### Constantine the Great

- **[T9+]** **Reported fact:** Constantine ordered a basilica erected in Rome over the site venerated as Paul's tomb on the Via Ostiensis; it was consecrated in 324 CE. The building and its foundations are documented, while identification of the underlying burial as Paul's remains traditional rather than independently demonstrated. Paul receives the positive sign as the earlier figure whose reputed burial site generated Constantine's later monument. [Papal Basilica of Saint Paul Outside the Walls](https://www.vatican.va/various/basiliche/san_paolo/en/basilica/costantino.htm).

### Thomas Aquinas

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** During the thirteenth century, Thomas Aquinas composed or delivered extensive *lecturae* on the letters attributed to Paul, treating the epistles as an interconnected theological corpus centered on Christ's grace. Paul is the earlier figure interpreted by Aquinas. [Pontifical Academy study of Aquinas's Pauline *lecturae*](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/pontifical_academies/san-tommaso/publications/dc11.pdf).

### Martin Luther

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Luther's lectures and commentaries on Romans and Galatians, beginning in the 1510s and continuing through later revisions, directly interpreted Paul and made his reading of Pauline justification central to Reformation controversy. Paul is the earlier figure written about.

### Friedrich Nietzsche

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Nietzsche wrote extensively and polemically about Paul, most explicitly in *The Antichrist* (written 1888), portraying him as a decisive transformer—and, in Nietzsche's judgment, corrupter—of Jesus' message. The existence and direction of the literary engagement are factual; Nietzsche's historical characterization is polemic, not neutral biography.

No defensible T2 prophecy/hyperstition connection was found among the roster. Mere chronological overlap, shared Mediterranean geography, later religious influence, artistic depiction, or use of a Pauline name was not treated as a crossing.
