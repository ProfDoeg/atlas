## Works by Sir Joseph Paxton

### Gardens and designed landscapes

- **1826–1846:** *Chatsworth Gardens*. A long, collaborative obra rather than a single design: Paxton and the sixth Duke of Devonshire transformed the pleasure grounds through planting, paths, waterworks, rockwork and glass architecture; individual components are separated below where the evidence permits. ([Historic England](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1000355))
- **1829 onward:** *Chatsworth Pinetum*. A living collection and designed landscape begun by Paxton in the south-east of the grounds; because trees grew, died and were replaced, it resists classification as a finished object. ([Historic England](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1000355))
- **1830s:** *Serpentine Walks, Buxton*. A riverside pleasure-ground design made for the sixth Duke of Devonshire and subsequently absorbed into the Pavilion Gardens; later work by Edward Milner makes the surviving division of authorship uncertain. ([Parks & Gardens](https://www.parksandgardens.org/places/pavilion-gardens-buxton))
- **1835 onward:** *Chatsworth Arboretum*. A living collection begun by Paxton and arranged as woodland garden; it is simultaneously horticultural experiment, landscape and continually changing body of work. ([Historic England](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1000355))
- **1840s:** *Chatsworth Rock Garden*. Monumental artificial rockwork distributed through woodland rather than a single building; surviving fabric belongs to Paxton’s broader transformation of the estate. ([Historic England](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1000355))
- **1842:** *Prince’s Park, Liverpool*. A subscription-park landscape commissioned by Richard Vaughan Yates, combining public amenity with revenue-producing residential plots. ([Parks & Gardens](https://www.parksandgardens.org/people/joseph-paxton))
- **1842–1843:** *Victoria Park, Upton/Slough*. A residential-park project traditionally associated with Paxton, but the attribution is not secure: Historic England says only that he “may have provided” its layout, while Benjamin Baud designed buildings. ([Historic England](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1001648))
- **1843–1847:** *Birkenhead Park*. A publicly funded municipal landscape laid out by Paxton with Edward Kemp supervising construction and Lewis Hornblower and John Robertson contributing architecture; its authorship is therefore collaborative. ([Historic England](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1000994))
- **1845–1847:** *London Road Cemetery, Coventry*. A municipal cemetery landscape designed as both burial ground and public promenade; its buildings were produced with other designers, so “Paxton’s cemetery” denotes the governing landscape project rather than every structure. ([Historic England research report](https://historicengland.org.uk/research/results/reports/7014/Cemeterieschurchyardsandburialgrounds_devisingandapplyingasignificanceframeworkappendix))
- **c. 1850–1854:** *Mentmore Towers Park and Gardens*. A new park, formal gardens and pleasure grounds conceived with the mansion for Baron Mayer de Rothschild; later nineteenth-century alterations complicate the surviving obra. ([Historic England](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1000319))
- **1852–1854:** *Crystal Palace Park, Sydenham*. A Gesamtkunstwerk of terraced gardens, fountains, lakes, architecture, geological displays and reconstructed prehistoric animals, devised around the relocated palace; many specialists contributed and significant parts were never completed as intended. ([Parks & Gardens](https://www.parksandgardens.org/people/joseph-paxton))
- **1852–1854:** *West End Park/Kelvingrove Park, Glasgow*. A public-park project commissioned from Paxton and subsequently developed and altered by others; attribution applies most safely to the originating layout rather than every surviving feature. ([Historic Environment Scotland](https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/designation/GDL00244))
- **1856:** *People’s Park, Halifax*. A public landscape given to Halifax by Sir Francis Crossley and designed by Paxton, with architecture by George Henry Stokes; it is a collaborative civic ensemble. ([Historic England](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1001511))
- **1856:** *South Cliff Gardens, Scarborough*. Italianate terraced pleasure grounds associated with Paxton’s work for the seaside spa; subsequent enlargement by other designers means that the present gardens exceed his design. ([Parks & Gardens](https://www.parksandgardens.org/places/south-cliff-gardens-and-valley-gardens-scarborough))
- **1857 onward:** *Queen’s Park, Glasgow*. Paxton supplied the principal public-park design, but later municipal development and alteration prevent the present landscape from being treated as an intact single-author work. ([Parks & Gardens](https://www.parksandgardens.org/places/queens-park-glasgow))
- **1861–1863:** *Baxter Park, Dundee*. A public park designed by Paxton, with its pavilion attributed to George Henry Stokes; it survives as the most complete Scottish park associated wholly with Paxton’s practice. ([Historic Environment Scotland](https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/designation/GDL00051), [Historic Environment Scotland pavilion record](https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/designation/LB24992))
- **c. 1863–1865:** *Public Park, Dunfermline*. A late public-park project by Paxton, unfinished at his death and completed or interpreted by Edward Milner; it therefore survives as a project whose final manifestation is not exclusively Paxton’s. ([Parks & Gardens](https://www.parksandgardens.org/people/joseph-paxton))
- **Undated, mid-nineteenth century:** *Holker Hall garden improvements*. Advice and possible design work for the seventh Duke of Devonshire; Historic England treats Paxton’s responsibility for particular surviving portions as conjectural, so this resists treatment as a securely bounded work. ([Historic England](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1000665))

### Glasshouses, waterworks and garden structures

- **1826–1849:** *Chatsworth glasshouse programme*. A body of experimental forcing houses, fruit houses and conservatories through which Paxton developed modular ridge-and-furrow glazing; not every component has a stable individual title or sole attribution. ([George F. Chadwick, bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Works_of_Sir_Joseph_Paxton_1803_1865.html?id=Sar40QEACAAJ), [Historic England](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1000355))
- **1836–1840:** *Great Conservatory, or Great Stove, Chatsworth*. An immense iron, timber and glass plant house designed by Paxton with Decimus Burton; demolished in 1920, it now survives principally through foundations, images and documentation. ([Historic England](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1334744))
- **c. 1840:** *Chatsworth Aqueduct*. A deliberately ruin-like stone aqueduct and waterfall devised by Paxton with the estate engineer Holmes, based on the Wilhelmshöhe waterworks. ([Historic England](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1334746))
- **1843–1844:** *Emperor Fountain*. A gravity-fed fountain and associated reservoir installed at Chatsworth for an anticipated visit by Emperor Nicholas I; the visit did not occur, but the waterwork was completed. ([Historic England](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1000355))
- **1846:** *Conservatory at Hampton Court, Herefordshire*. A glasshouse added to the south side of the house; it belongs to a larger garden repeatedly remodelled by other designers. ([Historic England](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1403731))
- **1848:** *Conservative Wall, Chatsworth*. A long heated wall covered by glazed frames: part greenhouse, part boundary and part cultivation apparatus, and therefore not adequately described simply as a building. ([Historic England](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1000355))
- **1849:** *Victoria Regia House, Chatsworth*. A purpose-built glasshouse for the giant waterlily, using a structural system developed from observations of the plant’s leaves; it was a horticultural apparatus and architectural prototype, later demolished. ([Parks & Gardens](https://www.parksandgardens.org/people/joseph-paxton))
- **1858:** *Pregny glasshouses*. Conservatories made for Adolphe de Rothschild at Pregny near Geneva and later incorporated into the Geneva botanical establishment; their design is associated with Paxton’s practice rather than securely documented as his unaided work. ([George F. Chadwick, bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Works_of_Sir_Joseph_Paxton_1803_1865.html?id=Sar40QEACAAJ))

### Exhibition architecture and integrated spectacles

- **1850–1851:** *Building for the Great Exhibition of the Works of Industry of All Nations (“Crystal Palace”), Hyde Park*. A temporary, prefabricated iron-and-glass exhibition building derived from Paxton’s sketch and developed with Charles Fox, Charles Fox Henderson and the contractors Fox, Henderson & Co.; its collective engineering authorship should not be collapsed into Paxton’s name alone. ([Historic England](https://historicengland.org.uk/images-books/publications/london-list-2010/london-list-2010/), [Victoria and Albert Museum](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O127825/facsimile-of-the-first-sketch-drawing-paxton-joseph/))
- **1852–1854:** *Crystal Palace, Sydenham*. A greatly enlarged and permanently intended reconstruction of the exhibition building, containing architectural courts, fine-art casts, natural-history displays and performance spaces; Paxton directed the project, but Owen Jones, Matthew Digby Wyatt, Charles Fox and many others made substantial parts of this Gesamtkunstwerk. ([Parks & Gardens](https://www.parksandgardens.org/people/joseph-paxton))
- **1852–1854:** *Crystal Palace waterworks and fountains*. A spectacular hydraulic system of reservoirs, cascades and innumerable jets integrated with the Sydenham terraces; it functioned as infrastructure and performance rather than as one easily bounded object. ([Parks & Gardens](https://www.parksandgardens.org/people/joseph-paxton))

### Houses and estate architecture

- **c. 1842–1843:** *Paxton’s House, Chatsworth*. A residence and working office built for Paxton within the Chatsworth estate; published accounts differ over how much architectural assistance he received, so sole authorship should not be assumed. ([George F. Chadwick, bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Works_of_Sir_Joseph_Paxton_1803_1865.html?id=Sar40QEACAAJ))
- **1851–1854:** *Mentmore Towers*. A Jacobethan country house made for Baron Mayer de Rothschild by Paxton and his son-in-law George Henry Stokes; Stokes was co-designer and clerk of works, making the conventional sole-Paxton attribution incomplete. ([Historic England](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1117863), [Buckinghamshire Heritage Portal](https://heritageportal.buckinghamshire.gov.uk/Monument/MBC16289))
- **1850s:** *Battlesden House*. A country house often described as a smaller relative of Mentmore and attributed to Paxton’s practice; it was demolished after purchase by the Duke of Bedford, and the exact division of work between Paxton and Stokes is not firmly recoverable. ([George F. Chadwick, bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Works_of_Sir_Joseph_Paxton_1803_1865.html?id=Sar40QEACAAJ))
- **1855–1859:** *Château de Ferrières*. A vast French country house commissioned by James de Rothschild and designed through the Paxton–Stokes practice; Paxton supplied the governing conception while George Henry Stokes carried substantial architectural responsibility. ([Royal Academy contributor dictionary](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/ba/The_Royal_Academy_of_Arts%3B_a_complete_dictionary_of_contributors_and_their_work_from_its_foundation_in_1769_to_1904_%28IA_royalacademyofar06grav%29.pdf))
- **1860:** *Fairlawn, Wimbledon*. A country residence designed for the dentist Sir Edwin Saunders; surviving accounts attribute it to Paxton, although—as with his other late buildings—the contribution of his architectural office is difficult to separate. ([George F. Chadwick, bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Works_of_Sir_Joseph_Paxton_1803_1865.html?id=Sar40QEACAAJ))
- **Before 1865:** *Rockhills, Sydenham*. Paxton’s own house near the Crystal Palace and the place of his death; it was connected to his Sydenham enterprise, but documentation does not justify treating every element as his personal design. ([Oxford Dictionary of National Biography bibliography and summary](https://doi.org/10.1093/ref:odnb/21624))

### Unrealized architectural and metropolitan projects

- **1832–1834:** *Plan for Forming Subscription Gardens*. A published social and landscape protocol proposing commercially supported gardens that combined recreation with productive cultivation; it is an intention expressed as a plan, not a securely identified constructed garden. ([Landscape History](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/01433768.2024.2339082))
- **1855:** *Great Victorian Way*. An unbuilt ten-mile glazed circuit proposed to Parliament, combining railway, road, shops, dwellings and covered promenade; its scale and fusion of transport, architecture and urban life make it an unrealized Gesamtkunstwerk rather than merely a railway scheme. ([Landscape History](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/01433768.2024.2339082))
- **1855–1856:** *Thames Embankment proposal*. A metropolitan infrastructure project coupling a riverside road with sewerage and land reclamation; Paxton’s advocacy helped secure parliamentary consideration, but the later constructed embankment was Joseph Bazalgette’s work and must not be listed as Paxton’s manifestation. ([Landscape History](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/01433768.2024.2339082))
- **Late 1850s–1860s:** *Thames Graving Dock project*. A commercial dock enterprise pursued by Paxton late in life; surviving summaries identify his involvement but do not establish that the engineering design was his, so it resists classification as an authored architectural work. ([George F. Chadwick, bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Works_of_Sir_Joseph_Paxton_1803_1865.html?id=Sar40QEACAAJ))

### Horticultural publications and editorial works

- **1832–1836:** *The Horticultural Register and General Magazine*. A five-volume horticultural periodical edited with Joseph Harrison and involving additional contributors; the work is the serial editorial project, not each later bound edition. ([WorldCat bibliographic record](https://search.worldcat.org/title/174147056), [Dictionary of National Biography](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionary_of_National_Biography%2C_1885-1900/Paxton%2C_Joseph))
- **1834–1849:** *The Magazine of Botany and Register of Flowering Plants*. An illustrated serial edited by Paxton, usually counted as sixteen annual volumes; older summaries sometimes give fifteen volumes or terminate it in 1848, reflecting different treatment of its final volume and successor title. ([Biodiversity Heritage Library](https://field.biodiversitylibrary.org/creator/425), [Dictionary of National Biography](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionary_of_National_Biography%2C_1885-1900/Paxton%2C_Joseph))
- **1838:** *A Practical Treatise on the Cultivation of the Dahlia*. An independent horticultural protocol: a book whose primary action is to instruct cultivation rather than to describe a literary subject. ([The Spectator archive](https://archive.spectator.co.uk/article/31st-march-1838/19/a-practical-treatise-on-the-cultivation-of-the-dah), [Dictionary of National Biography](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionary_of_National_Biography%2C_1885-1900/Paxton%2C_Joseph))
- **1840:** *A Pocket Botanical Dictionary*. A botanical and horticultural reference work prepared with John Lindley’s assistance; later revised editions are manifestations of the same work, and the posthumous 1868 revision by Samuel Hereman is not a new Paxton work. ([Biodiversity Heritage Library](https://www.biodiversitylibrary.org/bibliography/127594), [Dictionary of National Biography](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionary_of_National_Biography%2C_1885-1900/Paxton%2C_Joseph))
- **1841 onward:** *The Gardeners’ Chronicle*. A weekly newspaper co-founded by Paxton, John Lindley, Charles Wentworth Dilke and William Bradbury; it is a collaborative continuing institution, not a text wholly authored by Paxton. ([University of Oxford, Constructing Scientific Communities](https://conscicom.web.ox.ac.uk/article/gardeners-chronicle-1841-1985))
- **1849:** *Paxton’s Magazine of Gardening and Botany*. A short-lived successor or recasting of the *Magazine of Botany*; its status as a distinct work rather than a renamed continuation is bibliographically ambiguous. ([Dictionary of National Biography](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionary_of_National_Biography%2C_1885-1900/Paxton%2C_Joseph))
- **1850–1853:** *Paxton’s Flower Garden*. A three-volume illustrated serial/book made with John Lindley and illustrators including Walter Hood Fitch; later recast and reissued versions are manifestations, not additional Paxton works. ([Biodiversity Heritage Library](https://field.biodiversitylibrary.org/creator/425), [Dictionary of National Biography](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionary_of_National_Biography%2C_1885-1900/Paxton%2C_Joseph))
- **1851:** *The Cottager’s Calendar of Garden Operations*. A compact horticultural protocol arranged around recurring seasonal work; later calendars “based on” Paxton’s compilation should not automatically be treated as authored by him. ([Online Books Page](https://onlinebooks.library.upenn.edu/webbin/book/lookupname?key=Paxton%2C+Joseph%2C+Sir%2C+1803-1865))

### Cultivated organisms and horticultural manifestations

- **1830–1836:** *Chatsworth cultivation of the Cavendish banana*. Paxton successfully cultivated and propagated the banana later known as the Dwarf Cavendish from material received at Chatsworth. The plant is a biological lineage and horticultural achievement, not a work created ex nihilo by Paxton, so claims that he “invented” it overstate his authorship. ([Parks & Gardens](https://www.parksandgardens.org/people/joseph-paxton))
- **1849:** *Flowering of Victoria regia at Chatsworth*. A deliberately produced horticultural manifestation enabled by Paxton’s cultivation regime and purpose-built house; the flower appeared and was nurtured, but it resists being called an authored work in the same sense as the glasshouse. ([Parks & Gardens](https://www.parksandgardens.org/people/joseph-paxton))
