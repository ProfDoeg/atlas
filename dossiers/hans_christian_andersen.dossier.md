# Hans Christian Andersen: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

Hans Christian Andersen’s life is unusually reconstructable because he preserved manuscripts, drafts, diaries, almanacs, autobiographical writings, paper cuttings, and thousands of letters. The Royal Danish Library holds the principal manuscript collection; it and the Søren Kierkegaard archive were inscribed in UNESCO’s Memory of the World Register in 1997. Andersen’s autobiographies are indispensable but deliberately literary and self-mythologizing. Dates below therefore privilege letters, diaries, publication records, institutional files, and the documentary chronology maintained by the Hans Christian Andersen Centre at the University of Southern Denmark.

Labels used for contested material:

- **Documented fact:** supported by primary records or strong convergent documentation.
- **Reported fact:** attested in a credible secondary source but not independently confirmed here from the underlying document.
- **Interpretation:** a scholarly inference from texts or conduct.
- **Allegation or theory:** proposed but not established.
- **Rumor or myth:** circulating narrative without sufficient documentary support.
- **Unresolved:** evidence permits more than one conclusion.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Hans Christian Andersen.
- **Common abbreviated form:** H.C. Andersen.
- **Frequent erroneous English spelling:** Hans Christian Anderson.
- **Pseudonym:** **Villiam Christian Walter**, used on his privately printed youthful collection *Ungdoms-Forsøg* (1822). The three components invoked William Shakespeare, Andersen himself, and Walter Scott.
- **Occupations:** poet, novelist, dramatist, librettist, travel writer, autobiographer, diarist, public reader, paper-cut artist, and author of fairy tales and stories.
- **Nationality:** Danish.
- **Religion:** baptized Lutheran; remained culturally and theologically Christian, although his mature writing frequently questioned inherited dogma and explored providence, immortality, suffering, nature, science, and religious pluralism.
- **Birth:** 2 April 1805, Odense, Funen, Denmark.
- **Baptism:** 15 April 1805, St Hans Church, Odense.
- **Birthplace:** the precise building is **unresolved**. The small house at Hans Jensens Stræde/Bangs Boder traditionally presented as his birthplace is probable rather than conclusively demonstrated. The family had no stable household until 1807.
- **Death:** 4 August 1875, Rolighed, the Melchior family’s country house near Copenhagen.
- **Burial:** Assistens Cemetery, Copenhagen, following a funeral at Copenhagen’s Church of Our Lady on 11 August 1875.
- **Marital status:** never married.
- **Known children:** none.
- **Honorifics and distinctions:** titular professor; Knight of the Prussian Order of the Red Eagle; recipient of the Bavarian Maximilian Order for Science and Art; honorary citizen of Odense; Danish and foreign decorations.
- **Principal archive:** Royal Danish Library, Copenhagen.
- **Scale of authorship:** the Hans Christian Andersen Centre’s catalogue identifies 213 tale-related entries, including posthumous and preliminary texts; Andersen himself published approximately 156 fairy tales and stories. The catalogue separately records six novels, 51 dramatic works, 25 travel texts, four autobiographies, more than 1,000 poems, and numerous articles, letters, miscellaneous writings, and collections.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Hans Andersen (1782–1816), father — documented fact.** A poor shoemaker with literary interests and ambitions beyond his station. He read to his son, reportedly made him a toy theatre, and encouraged imaginative play. In 1812 he enlisted as a substitute soldier during the Napoleonic Wars, returned in impaired health in 1814, and died in 1816. Andersen subsequently represented him as intellectually frustrated and socially misplaced.

**Anne Marie Andersdatter (approximately 1773/75–1833), mother — documented fact.** A washerwoman from severe poverty who was illiterate or nearly so. Before her marriage she had a daughter, Karen Marie. She married Hans Andersen in 1805 and, after his death, remarried in 1818. Her later life included alcoholism and residence in the Odense poorhouse. Andersen’s story “Hun duede ikke” (“She Was Good for Nothing,” 1852) reworked the stigmatized figure of the drinking washerwoman with sympathy.

### Sibling

**Karen Marie Andersdatter (1799–1846), maternal half-sister — documented fact.** Andersen had little sustained contact with her. His discomfort about her poverty and circumstances has been interpreted as evidence of his anxiety over the social world from which he had escaped.

### Claimed noble or royal ancestry

**Rumor or family myth:** Andersen’s paternal grandmother told stories suggesting that the family had once occupied a higher social position. Later speculation made Andersen the illegitimate son of Crown Prince Christian Frederik, subsequently Christian VIII, or otherwise connected him to royalty.

**Evidence:** Andersen received royal educational support and later moved comfortably in royal circles, but those circumstances followed documented patronage efforts. Genealogical investigations have not established royal paternity. No contemporary birth, baptismal, financial, or correspondence evidence demonstrates that a prince was his father. The claim remains a recurrent status myth, encouraged by Andersen’s own sensitivity to rank and his family’s narratives of lost gentility.

### Household economy

The household belonged to Odense’s poor artisan class. Andersen’s childhood was not uniformly destitute—his father had a trade and the family had a home—but it was economically precarious. After his father’s death, Andersen briefly worked in cloth and tobacco-related factories. His rise depended on patronage, royal educational funding, publication income, gifts, hospitality, and eventually a permanent state stipend.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1805–1819

### Odense

Andersen grew up amid the workshops, courtyards, poorhouses, churches, folk beliefs, itinerant performers, and provincial theatre of early nineteenth-century Odense. His mother supplied elements of oral folklore and popular religion; his father introduced reading and rational or freethinking tendencies. His paternal grandmother worked in the garden of an asylum or hospital and contributed further oral traditions.

He attended elementary schools irregularly. He was considered physically awkward, emotionally conspicuous, and unusually absorbed in performance and invention. He built puppet theatres, designed costumes, sang, recited, and wrote rudimentary plays.

### Formative cultural encounters

- **1812:** first documented or traditionally dated visit to the theatre in Odense.
- He encountered visiting theatrical performers and became preoccupied with the Royal Danish Theatre.
- The presence of Spanish troops on Funen during the Napoleonic period entered his childhood memory and later writing.
- His father’s wartime service and death intensified the family’s insecurity.
- He was confirmed in 1819.

### Early employment

After leaving school, Andersen worked briefly in factories. Accounts describe coworkers humiliating him for his high voice or ambiguous appearance. The details are filtered through autobiography, but the factory employment and his rapid withdrawal from it are well established.

### Departure for Copenhagen

On 4 September 1819, aged fourteen, he left Odense alone for Copenhagen with very little money. He later made this departure the founding scene of his life story: the poor, unrecognized child entering the capital to become famous. That teleological version is autobiographical mythology, but the journey and poverty are documented.

---

## Copenhagen Struggle and Theatrical Apprenticeship, 1819–1822

Andersen sought admission to the Royal Danish Theatre as singer, dancer, or actor. His soprano voice initially attracted attention but changed during puberty. His acting and dancing were judged inadequate.

He obtained introductions to:

- **Giuseppe Siboni**, director of the Royal Theatre’s singing school;
- **Frederik Høegh-Guldberg**, poet and professor;
- **Knud Lyne Rahbek**, literary figure and theatre official;
- **Peter Frederik Wulff**, naval officer, writer, and Shakespeare translator;
- members of Copenhagen’s theatrical and bourgeois elite.

For roughly three years he lived precariously, received lessons, performed minor work around the theatre, and depended on gifts and invitations. His unusual appearance, lack of conventional education, emotional volatility, and relentless self-presentation provoked ridicule as well as sympathy.

### Early writings

He wrote tragedies, poems, and dramatic fragments with defective spelling and grammar. His tragedy *Røverne i Vissenberg* and other submissions were rejected.

In 1822 he privately published *Ungdoms-Forsøg* under “Villiam Christian Walter.” The volume contained youthful dramatic and prose experiments. It did not establish him professionally.

### Intervention by Jonas Collin

**Documented fact:** Royal Theatre official and finance administrator **Jonas Collin the elder** recognized that Andersen possessed ability but lacked basic education. In October 1822 Collin petitioned the authorities on his behalf. The *ad usus publicos* fund awarded Andersen 350 rigsdaler annually for three years, later extended. Collin became his guardian and principal patron.

This was the decisive institutional transition in Andersen’s life: he moved from informal theatrical dependency to a state-financed education.

## Education and Formation, 1822–1829

### Slagelse Latin School, 1822–1826

Andersen entered the Latin school at Slagelse at seventeen and was placed among much younger boys. The mismatch in age, social experience, and academic preparation produced shame and isolation.

The headmaster, **Simon Meisling**, insisted that Andersen suspend literary composition and concentrate on Latin, Greek, religion, and conventional schoolwork. Andersen later portrayed Meisling as tyrannical and psychologically abusive.

### Elsinore, 1826–1827

When Meisling transferred to the school at Elsinore, Andersen followed. He lived in the headmaster’s household and experienced what his diaries and recollections describe as humiliation, surveillance, and repeated declarations that he lacked talent.

**Documented core:** conflict with Meisling, Andersen’s misery, and Collin’s eventual removal of him.

**Interpretive caution:** Andersen’s later accounts are emotionally charged and designed partly to dramatize deliverance. Meisling’s conduct was nevertheless considered damaging by Andersen’s protectors.

### “Det døende Barn”

In 1826 Andersen wrote “Det døende Barn” (“The Dying Child”), a poem notable for presenting death from the child’s viewpoint. It circulated in several Scandinavian versions and brought him early literary notice.

### Private preparation and university examinations

In 1827 Collin removed him from Meisling’s authority and arranged private tuition in Copenhagen. Andersen passed the university entrance examination in 1828 and the second examination in 1829. He enrolled at the University of Copenhagen but did not pursue a conventional degree.

### Literary breakthrough

His fantastic, parodic *Fodreise fra Holmens Canal til Østpynten af Amager i Aarene 1828 og 1829* appeared in 1829, initially at his expense. It sold well enough for a second edition. The Royal Theatre staged his vaudeville *Kjærlighed paa Nicolai Taarn eller Hvad siger Parterret?* in 1829.

These successes established him as a recognizable young Copenhagen author.

## Early Career, 1829–1835

### Poetry, theatre, and first travels

Andersen published:

- *Digte* (1830);
- *Phantasier og Skizzer* (1831);
- *Skyggebilleder af en Reise til Harzen, det sachsiske Schweiz etc. etc., i Sommeren 1831* (1831);
- several libretti, vaudevilles, and dramatic adaptations;
- *Aarets tolv Maaneder, Tegnede med Blæk og Pen* (1832).

His 1831 journey through Germany—Harz, Saxon Switzerland, Dresden, and Berlin—was his first substantial foreign literary journey. It began a pattern: travel supplied observation, patrons, prestige, emotional renewal, and material for publication.

### Riborg Voigt

In 1830, while visiting Faaborg, Andersen met **Riborg Voigt**, daughter of a merchant. He fell in love, but she was already attached or engaged to Poul Jacob Bøving. Andersen withdrew rather than disrupt the engagement.

**Documented fact:** the attachment and correspondence.

**Reported fact:** a packet containing a letter from Riborg was found with Andersen’s personal effects after his death. The story is plausible and widely repeated, though the precise “on his chest” formulation varies among retellings.

### First autobiography

Around 1832 Andersen wrote *Levnedsbogen* (“The Book of My Life”), intended for Louise Collin and unpublished in his lifetime. It demonstrates that he was already shaping his life into a providential narrative.

### Grand tour, 1833–1834

With a royal travel grant, Andersen left Denmark in April 1833 and travelled through Germany, France, Switzerland, and Italy, residing for an extended period in Rome. Italy offered both artistic formation and distance from hostile Copenhagen criticism.

In Rome he entered Scandinavian and European artistic circles. The journey generated his first major novel.

### *Improvisatoren*

Published 9 April 1835, *Improvisatoren* (“The Improvisatore”) fictionalized an Italian artist’s rise from poverty. It succeeded internationally, especially in German translation, more rapidly than at home. Its structure reinforced the central Andersen pattern: vulnerable outsider, artistic election, social ascent, and the unstable price of recognition.

## The Fairy Tales and International Breakthrough, 1835–1846

### First fairy-tale booklet

On 8 May 1835 C.A. Reitzel issued *Eventyr, fortalte for Børn. Første Samling. Første Hefte*, containing:

- “Fyrtøiet” (“The Tinderbox”);
- “Lille Claus og store Claus” (“Little Claus and Big Claus”);
- “Prindsessen paa Ærten” (“The Princess on the Pea”);
- “Den lille Idas Blomster” (“Little Ida’s Flowers”).

The conversational syntax, direct address, comic sound effects, and mixture of oral storytelling with literary invention departed from polished children’s literature. Early Danish reviewers sometimes criticized the informality or supposed unsuitability for children.

Further booklets included “Tommelise,” “Den lille Havfrue,” “Keiserens nye Klæder,” “Den standhaftige Tinsoldat,” “De vilde Svaner,” and other works now central to his reputation.

### Not simply folklore

Andersen adapted some folk narratives but invented many stories or radically transformed traditional motifs. Calling the entire corpus “retold folktales” is inaccurate. His works combine oral idiom, literary Romanticism, Christianity, satire, technological modernity, social observation, autobiography, and original fantasy.

### Novels

- *O.T.* (1836), exploring crime, social concealment, and identity;
- *Kun en Spillemand* (“Only a Fiddler,” 1837), portraying talent defeated by social conditions.

The latter prompted Søren Kierkegaard’s first book, *Af en endnu Levendes Papirer, udgivet mod hans Villie* (1838), which argued that Andersen lacked a coherent “life-view” and made external circumstances determine genius. This was a serious philosophical-literary dispute, not a criminal or political controversy.

### Germany as route to world fame

German translations and publishers were crucial. Andersen found readers, courts, salons, composers, and writers across German-speaking Europe. His Danish reception remained mixed while continental prestige grew.

### Major eastern journey, 1840–1841

He travelled through Germany and Italy to Malta, Greece, Constantinople, the Danube lands, and central Europe. The resulting *En Digters Bazar* (“A Poet’s Bazaar,” 1842) mixed reportage, lyric prose, cultural encounters, and fictionalization.

### *Nye Eventyr* and mature form

The collections beginning in 1843/44 included:

- “Engelen” (“The Angel”);
- “Nattergalen” (“The Nightingale”);
- “Kjærestefolkene” (“The Sweethearts”);
- “Den grimme Ælling” (“The Ugly Duckling”);
- “Grantræet” (“The Fir Tree”);
- “Sneedronningen” (“The Snow Queen”).

“The Ugly Duckling” soon became inseparable from Andersen’s self-image. It is not a literal autobiography, but Andersen helped cultivate the reading of it as the transfiguration of his own social ascent.

### Jenny Lind

Andersen met the Swedish singer **Jenny Lind** in the 1840s and developed an intense attachment. She admired him but did not reciprocate romantically, reportedly addressing him as a brother. “The Nightingale” has often been associated with her voice and public persona.

**Documented fact:** friendship, admiration, and Andersen’s romantic hopes.

**Interpretation:** that specific tales are encrypted declarations to Lind. Such readings may be persuasive but are not equivalent to explicit authorial testimony.

### Honours

On 6 January 1846, King Friedrich Wilhelm IV of Prussia awarded Andersen the Order of the Red Eagle. International court recognition became part of his professional infrastructure.

## International Celebrity, 1846–1857

### English-language breakthrough

English translations proliferated in 1846–47, sometimes without coordination with Andersen and often through German rather than directly from Danish. He first visited Britain in 1847.

At Gore House, the salon of the Countess of Blessington, he met **Charles Dickens** in July 1847. Andersen’s diary and autobiography record intense emotion; Dickens introduced him to contacts and gave him inscribed books. A surviving Dickens letter commended Andersen to the Scottish judge Patrick Robertson as a man of genius.

Andersen also moved among aristocrats, writers, and performers. His celebrity depended substantially on foreign translation, salons, royal courts, and personal networking.

### Autobiographical publication

*Das Märchen meines Lebens ohne Dichtung* (“The Fairy Tale of My Life Without Poetry/Fiction”) appeared in German in 1847 as part of his collected works. The underlying Danish manuscript was not published until 1942.

An English autobiography, *The True Story of My Life*, also appeared in 1847. His expanded Danish memoir, *Mit Livs Eventyr*, followed in 1855.

### Revolution and war

The revolutions of 1848 and the First Schleswig War complicated Andersen’s transnational identity. He was Danish and wrote patriotic works, but he also depended on German friends, publishers, and readers. His correspondence expresses distress over nationalist hostility.

He was not a party politician, officeholder, propagandist, conspirator, or revolutionary organizer. His political role was cultural: occasional patriotic poetry, court relationships, international mediation, and commentary in letters and diaries.

### *De to Baronesser*

*The Two Baronesses* appeared first in English in 1848 and then in Danish. The unusual publication order reflects Andersen’s international market.

### State stipend and economic position

From 1838 Andersen received a tax-free annual state stipend of 400 rigsdaler. Publication fees, foreign editions, readings, gifts, royal favor, and extended residence as a guest supplemented it. He did not create a commercial company, hold patents, or build an investment enterprise. His “business structure” was that of a mobile independent author operating through publishers, translators, patrons, courts, and elite households.

Copyright protection across borders was weak. Translations could appear without authorization or satisfactory payment. International fame therefore did not translate automatically into control over editions.

### Titular professorship

In 1851 he received the title of professor. This was an honorific state distinction rather than a teaching appointment.

### Mature travel and story collections

His major publications included *I Sverrig* (“Pictures of Sweden,” 1851) and *Historier* (“Stories,” 1852 and later). Dropping “told for children” reflected his increasingly explicit address to adults as well as children.

### Religious and intellectual development

The later tales used Christian vocabulary but often made salvation, suffering, art, and immortality unstable questions rather than simple doctrines. Andersen was fascinated by railways, telegraphs, industrial production, photography, and scientific discovery. Unlike writers who opposed mechanism to imagination, he repeatedly converted new technology into poetic material.

His 1857 novel *At være eller ikke være* (“To Be, or Not to Be”) staged conflicts among Christianity, materialism, reason, and immortality. Its title invoked Shakespeare, but its concerns were contemporary theology and science.

## The Dickens Visit, 1857

Andersen stayed with Charles and Catherine Dickens at Gad’s Hill Place from June to July 1857.

### What is documented

- Andersen announced a visit intended to last about two weeks.
- He stayed approximately five weeks.
- His letter of 14 July 1857 to Queen Caroline Amalie described the stay warmly and called Dickens one of the most lovable men he knew.
- Dickens’s private correspondence complained about Andersen’s language difficulties, emotional demands, and prolonged presence.
- The two men’s correspondence declined substantially afterward.
- Dickens did not publicly launch a campaign against him.

### The famous bedroom sign

The Dickens family is said to have left a card reading substantially: “Hans Andersen slept in this room for five weeks—which seemed to the family AGES!”

**Reported fact:** the sign is attributed to Dickens family tradition and repeated in biographical literature.

**Caution:** its wording and immediate date are not established as securely as Dickens’s surviving letters. It should not be treated as a transcript of a contemporaneous public declaration.

### Later mythology

Internet retellings often present Andersen as deliberately refusing to leave, as wholly oblivious, or as “destroying” the friendship single-handedly. The evidence supports a severe mismatch in expectations, language, hospitality, and temperament. Dickens was simultaneously experiencing marital strain and professional pressure. Andersen believed himself an honored guest; Dickens privately experienced him as exhausting.

Claims that the episode proves autism, predatory conduct, or a particular sexual motive are retrospective theories unsupported by a diagnosis or contemporary accusation.

## Later Career and Travels, 1858–1871

### Henriette Wulff and the *Austria*

Andersen’s close friend **Henriette Wulff**, daughter of Peter Frederik Wulff, died in the burning and sinking of the steamship *Austria* in September 1858. Her death affected him deeply. The Wulff household had been an alternative family and early source of protection.

### Honours and royal networks

- **1859:** Bavarian Maximilian Order for Science and Art.
- Andersen maintained relationships with Danish monarchs and foreign courts.
- He frequently read aloud in royal, aristocratic, bourgeois, and artistic households.
- His dependence on elite hospitality was practical and emotional, but he also retained the ability to reject or criticize patrons.

### Major journeys

Across his life Andersen made roughly 29 foreign journeys. Countries or present-day territories visited included Germany, Sweden, Britain, Scotland, France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Switzerland, Italy, Austria, the Czech lands, Slovenia, Hungary, Serbia, Romania, Bulgaria, Greece, Turkey, Malta, Spain, Morocco, Portugal, Monaco, and Norway.

Principal later journeys included:

- **1860:** Germany and Switzerland.
- **1861:** Italy and Rome.
- **1862–63:** Switzerland, France, Spain, Gibraltar/Tangier or Moroccan territory, and return through France.
- **1865:** Sweden.
- **1866:** Portugal via the Netherlands and France, hosted by the O’Neill family at Quinta do Pinheiro near Setúbal and in the Lisbon–Sintra region.
- **1867:** Paris International Exposition.
- **1868:** Netherlands, France, Switzerland, and Germany.
- **1869–70:** Vienna and the Riviera.
- **1871:** Norway.
- **1872:** Germany, Austria, and Italy.
- **1873:** Switzerland, his last foreign journey.

The statement commonly attributed to him, “At rejse er at leve” (“To travel is to live”), derives from his writing and accurately describes travel’s structural importance in his life.

### Spain and Portugal

*I Spanien* (“In Spain,” dated 1863 in the publication record, sometimes cited as 1862 from the journey) documents Barcelona, Valencia, Alicante, Málaga, Gibraltar, Seville, Granada, Madrid, Burgos, and other locations. His warm response to Málaga is commemorated there.

*Et Besøg i Portugal 1866* (“A Visit to Portugal 1866,” 1868) records Portuguese landscapes, society, hosts, and travel conditions.

### Encounters with artists and writers

Documented or well-attested meetings included Felix Mendelssohn, Charles Dickens, Franz Liszt, Robert and Elizabeth Barrett Browning, Richard Wagner, Edvard Grieg, Johannes Brahms, Bjørnstjerne Bjørnson, and Henrik Ibsen. Not all became close relationships. Andersen was an energetic collector of acquaintances and often recorded meetings more intensely than the other party did.

### Franco-Prussian War

War between France and Prussia in 1870 distressed Andersen because both countries formed part of his literary world. Nationalist readings of his loyalties varied. No evidence indicates clandestine diplomatic or intelligence work.

### Final novel

*Lykke-Peer* (“Lucky Peer,” 1870) was his sixth and last novel. Its artist-hero dies at the moment of triumph, returning to Andersen’s persistent association of artistic fulfillment with sacrifice and death.

## Residences and Material Life

Andersen never established a conventional spouse-led household. His adult geography consisted of:

- rented rooms in Copenhagen;
- hotels and boarding houses;
- visits to the Collin and Wulff households;
- stays at Danish manor houses including Basnæs;
- extended residence with friends and patrons across Europe;
- periods with the Melchior family at Rolighed.

This mobile arrangement reduced household obligations and enabled travel, but it made hospitality, invitations, and emotional attachment to host families unusually important.

### Wealth and property

Andersen rose from poverty to financial security and international celebrity. The record supports income from:

- royal educational grants;
- state stipend;
- Danish publisher payments;
- foreign editions and collected works;
- readings and related literary activity;
- gifts and hospitality.

He was not a great capitalist or landowner. No significant businesses, securities empire, patents, corporate directorships, hidden financial structures, or fraud proceedings are associated with him. Surviving records instead show the finances of a professional author navigating uneven copyright regimes.

## Relationships and Networks

### Jonas Collin the elder

**Documented fact:** guardian, patron, administrative advocate, and father figure. Collin secured royal educational support and introduced Andersen into a durable family network.

### Edvard Collin

Jonas Collin’s son became Andersen’s close friend, adviser, sometime literary intermediary, and principal object of emotionally intense letters. Andersen told Edvard that he longed for him and used language that modern readers commonly understand as romantic or homoerotic. Edvard did not reciprocate in the same form and married Henriette Thyberg.

**Documented fact:** intimate correspondence and Andersen’s emotional dependency.

**Unresolved:** whether their relationship was physically sexual. No reliable evidence establishes that it was.

### Louise Collin

Andersen developed an attachment to Edvard’s sister Louise. It was unreciprocated. The Collin family treated him as a protected intimate but did not accept every emotional claim he made upon them.

### Henriette Wulff

Close lifelong friend and correspondent. Her family offered emotional security and cultural mentorship. She died aboard the *Austria* in 1858.

### Henriette Hanck

Odense friend and major correspondent. Her letters form an important source for his career, ambitions, complaints, and self-presentation.

### Riborg Voigt

Early romantic attachment, not consummated as a marriage.

### Jenny Lind

Friend, admired singer, and unreciprocated romantic ideal. Andersen’s identification of her with the nightingale became part of his personal legend.

### Harald Scharff

A young Danish ballet dancer with whom Andersen formed an intense relationship around 1857–62. Letters and diary entries show affection, jealousy, physical closeness, and emotional rupture.

**Documented fact:** intense attachment.

**Interpretation:** several biographers describe a romantic or possibly sexual relationship.

**Unresolved:** whether it involved genital sexual activity. The archive does not establish this conclusively.

### Dorothea Melchior and Moritz G. Melchior

Close late-life friends and hosts. Andersen had his own room at Rolighed and died there. The Melchiors’ wealth and hospitality provided stability during his final illness.

### Publishers and translators

C.A. Reitzel was an important Danish publisher. Carl B. Lorck’s German collected edition helped consolidate continental fame. Numerous English translators—including Mary Howitt and Caroline Peachey—and magazine publishers helped create his Anglophone readership, frequently from imperfect intermediary texts.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Social position

Andersen’s work repeatedly stages movement across class boundaries: poor children, servants, discarded objects, aspiring artists, courts, burghers, soldiers, and animals subject to hierarchy. These motifs arose from experience but do not yield a single party-political program.

### Monarchy and patronage

He cultivated royal relationships and accepted royal and state support. This was neither secret nor anomalous in the Danish cultural system. His monarchism coexisted with sympathy for poverty and social humiliation.

### Nationalism

He wrote patriotic songs, including “I Danmark er jeg født” (“In Denmark I Was Born,” 1850). Yet his career was cosmopolitan and often strained by Danish-German conflict. He opposed reducing artistic relationships to national hostility.

### Religion

Recurring positions and tensions include:

- providence as an organizing hope;
- immortality as an urgent personal problem;
- death as transformation rather than mere closure;
- anxiety over damnation and bodily suffering;
- compassion toward socially condemned figures;
- fascination with natural science;
- confidence that technological modernity could be poetically and spiritually meaningful.

He was not a systematic theologian. Different stories support incompatible readings: orthodox Christian consolation, sentimental providentialism, existential uncertainty, satire of religious complacency, or spiritualized nature.

### Artistic evolution

1. **Youth:** theatrical imitation, Romantic tragedy, poetry.
2. **1829–34:** irony, fantasy, travel writing, and experimental prose.
3. **1835–42:** novels and tales drawing on folk forms.
4. **1843–52:** mature original fairy tales and international celebrity.
5. **1850s–70s:** increasingly compressed stories about objects, technology, death, art, social performance, and modern urban life.

His oral-seeming prose was highly crafted. It uses interruptions, repetition, colloquial syntax, shifts of scale, and direct speech that translators often normalized.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Contested Questions

### Sexuality

**Documented facts:**

- Andersen never married.
- He recorded attractions to women, including Riborg Voigt, Louise Collin, and Jenny Lind.
- His letters and diaries document emotionally and sometimes physically charged attachments to men, notably Edvard Collin and Harald Scharff.
- He recorded sexual temptation and visits to prostitutes or brothels, sometimes noting abstention or limited conduct.
- No spouse, acknowledged lover, or child is securely documented.

**Interpretations:**

- “Gay,” “bisexual,” “queer,” “celibate,” and “asexual” have all been applied retrospectively.
- “Bisexual” or “queer” accommodates the documented attraction to more than one sex but does not prove consummation.
- Some scholars emphasize a life of unfulfilled or sublimated desire rather than a stable modern identity category.

**Unresolved:** the extent of his sexual experience. Assertions that he “died a virgin” are commonly made but cannot be proved from silence, diary wording, or absence of a known partner.

### Retrospective psychiatric diagnosis

Andersen has been described posthumously as autistic, dyslexic, obsessive-compulsive, narcissistic, hypochondriacal, anxious, or depressive.

**Documented facts:** irregular schooling, spelling difficulties, social awkwardness, intense routines, sensitivity to criticism, recurrent fears, emotional crises, and extensive health anxiety.

**Allegation or theory:** any modern clinical diagnosis. No contemporary clinical evaluation capable of establishing autism or another current diagnosis exists. Biographical traits are insufficient for certainty.

### Self-absorption and dependency

Contemporaries and later biographers documented or described his need for attention, public readings, praise, reassurance, and hospitality. He could respond dramatically to criticism and perceived neglect.

**Documented core:** letters and diaries repeatedly show these traits.

**Caution:** hostile anecdotes often remove reciprocal affection, cultural differences, illness, or the conventions of nineteenth-century friendship.

### Treatment of poorer relatives

Andersen’s distance from his half-sister and embarrassment about family poverty have drawn criticism.

**Documented fact:** limited contact and discomfort.

**Interpretation:** that he abandoned or erased his family solely to protect his status. His writings simultaneously reworked his mother’s and poor women’s experience with sympathy.

### Literary borrowing

Some tales use folklore, European literary sources, Arabian and classical motifs, or material heard from others.

**Documented fact:** Andersen adapted existing narrative elements.

**Unsupported accusation:** that his corpus was simply plagiarized. His publication history, manuscripts, radical transformations, and large body of demonstrably original stories do not support that reduction.

### National and racial representation

Some nineteenth-century language and travel descriptions reflect contemporary European hierarchies, exoticism, and stereotypes. Modern criticism has examined representations of Africans, Jews, Roma, Turks, and colonial or Orientalized spaces.

These are legitimate textual questions. They do not amount to evidence that Andersen organized racial violence, held government power over colonized populations, or belonged to a political extremist organization.

### “The Little Mermaid” as autobiography

The tale has been read as:

- a spiritual story about acquiring an immortal soul;
- a representation of unreciprocated love;
- an encoded account of same-sex desire;
- a response to Edvard Collin’s marriage;
- a broader narrative of bodily transformation and exclusion.

**Documented fact:** the tale appeared in 1837 amid Andersen’s emotionally complicated relation to the Collin family.

**Interpretation:** specific one-to-one identifications. No surviving authorial declaration proves that the prince “is” Edvard or that the story has only one autobiographical meaning.

### Criminality and corruption

The adversarial search located no credible evidence that Andersen was arrested, imprisoned, indicted, convicted, investigated for fraud, engaged in corruption or money laundering, committed political violence, operated as an intelligence agent, or faced a substantive lawsuit.

Search results for “Andersen” litigation in 2024–26 overwhelmingly concern the unrelated United States copyright action *Andersen v. Stability AI*, whose plaintiff Sarah Andersen is not Hans Christian Andersen. Treating that litigation as relevant to him would be a false-name conflation.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Self-Created Mythology

### “The washerwoman’s son who became Denmark’s swan”

**Story:** a destitute, ugly, misunderstood child rose through innate genius to universal recognition.

**Origin and circulation:** Andersen’s autobiographies; “The Ugly Duckling”; commemorative biographies; schools and museums.

**Evidence:** poverty, ridicule, patronage, and upward mobility are real. The compressed story minimizes the sustained intervention of Collin, royal funds, institutions, publishers, translators, and elite hospitality.

**Effect:** it made Andersen an international emblem of artistic self-realization.

### Royal paternity

**Story:** Andersen was secretly the son of Crown Prince Christian Frederik/Christian VIII.

**Evidence for:** later royal patronage, family stories of elevated ancestry, Andersen’s access to court.

**Evidence against:** documentary parentage naming Hans Andersen and Anne Marie Andersdatter; ordinary mechanisms of patronage are well recorded; no direct contemporary proof.

**Classification:** rumor or myth.

### Prophecy by a fortune-teller

Andersen recounted that a wise woman predicted Odense would one day be illuminated in his honor.

**Evidence:** chiefly autobiographical narration.

**Classification:** self-created or inherited legend. Odense later did celebrate him, making the prophecy a satisfying retrospective motif.

### “The Ugly Duckling” as exact autobiography

**Classification:** interpretive myth based on genuine parallels. The story excludes much of the actual social network that enabled his rise and cannot serve as a factual memoir.

### The Dickens expulsion

**Story:** Dickens had to throw Andersen bodily or forcibly out of Gad’s Hill.

**Evidence:** Andersen overstayed and Dickens complained privately, but Andersen departed through ordinary arrangements. No reliable evidence supports forcible expulsion.

**Classification:** hostile comic mythology built on a documented failed visit.

### Paper note at Dickens’s house

**Story:** the room bore a sign saying Andersen’s five weeks seemed like “ages.”

**Classification:** reported family anecdote, plausible but less securely documented than the correspondence.

### Andersen as an entirely innocent children’s writer

**Story:** he wrote gentle moral entertainments exclusively for small children.

**Evidence against:** the tales contain mutilation, death, vanity, class cruelty, erotic renunciation, theological anxiety, satire, and unhappy endings; he also wrote novels, plays, poetry, autobiography, and travel literature for adults.

**Classification:** commercial and pedagogical simplification.

### Andersen as collector rather than creator

**Story:** like a folklorist, he merely wrote down oral tales.

**Evidence against:** many tales are original literary inventions, while others transform sources radically.

**Classification:** misleading reception myth, partly produced by grouping him with the Brothers Grimm.

### “The Little Mermaid” equals the Disney story

**Story:** the mermaid wins the prince and survives through romantic fulfillment.

**Evidence against:** Andersen’s mermaid fails to marry the prince, refuses to murder him to save herself, dissolves into sea foam, and enters a probationary spiritual existence among the daughters of the air.

**Classification:** adaptation-generated popular mythology.

## Illness, Accidents, Psychological Crises, and Death

Andersen kept detailed records of physical symptoms and repeatedly feared disease, fire, shipwreck, premature burial, robbery, and other disasters. He sometimes carried a rope while travelling in case he needed to escape a hotel fire. This is documented as behavior; psychiatric explanations remain retrospective.

### 1872 fall

In early 1872 he fell from bed and was seriously injured. He never completely recovered. The accident coincided with declining strength and curtailed activity.

### Last tale and cessation of work

His final new tales appeared in 1872. Illness increasingly prevented travel and sustained composition.

### Final illness

By 1874–75 he suffered weakness, liver trouble, respiratory symptoms, and pain. Later accounts identify liver cancer as the fatal disease, although nineteenth-century diagnostic precision was limited.

He stayed increasingly with Dorothea and Moritz Melchior at Rolighed. His last diary entries document physical decline.

### Death

Andersen died at Rolighed on 4 August 1875, aged seventy. His funeral on 11 August at the Church of Our Lady was a public and official event attended by representatives of the royal family, government, cultural institutions, and foreign delegations. He was buried at Assistens Cemetery.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Immediate reputation

At death Andersen was an international celebrity and Danish national figure. His funeral confirmed official recognition that had been far from secure in his youth.

### Canonization

His tales became fixtures of children’s publishing, school curricula, illustration, translation, theatre, animation, ballet, and film. This process often softened syntax, removed religious endings, reduced irony, or supplied romantic resolution.

### Translation

Andersen is among the most translated authors in world literature. Exact language totals vary because publishers count languages, dialects, partial editions, and adaptations differently. Claims of a precise ranking immediately below the Bible or Shakespeare are promotional and methodologically unreliable.

### Museums and monuments

- H.C. Andersen’s House and childhood home in Odense.
- The Little Mermaid statue in Copenhagen, inspired by the tale but created through the patronage of Carl Jacobsen and sculptor Edvard Eriksen.
- Statues and memorials in Copenhagen, Odense, New York, Málaga, Bratislava, and elsewhere.
- Odense’s civic identity and tourism economy are closely connected to him.

### Prizes and commemorations

- The Hans Christian Andersen Award, administered by the International Board on Books for Young People, honors major authors and illustrators of children’s literature.
- Andersen’s birthday, 2 April, is observed as International Children’s Book Day.
- The 2005 bicentenary produced exhibitions, editions, conferences, performances, and international cultural diplomacy.

### Archive status

The Royal Danish Library’s Andersen manuscripts and correspondence entered UNESCO’s Memory of the World Register in 1997. Digitization projects continue to broaden access.

### Reinterpretation

Successive reputations include:

1. poor child made good;
2. national Danish poet;
3. harmless children’s storyteller;
4. European cosmopolitan and travel writer;
5. experimental modern prose stylist;
6. writer of cruelty, death, material culture, and technological change;
7. queer or sexually divided author;
8. global source-text continually overwritten by adaptation.

These interpretations coexist. None alone exhausts the archive.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by documentary or strong biographical evidence are included. Mere shared nationality, period, or intellectual resemblance is excluded.

### Charles John Huffam Dickens

**Documented fact.** Andersen met Dickens at Gore House, London, in July 1847. Dickens gave him books, hosted him at Broadstairs, and wrote a letter introducing him to Patrick Robertson. Andersen stayed at Dickens’s Gad’s Hill Place for approximately five weeks in June–July 1857. Dickens privately complained about the visit; the friendship and correspondence cooled afterward.

### Søren Aabye Kierkegaard

**Documented fact.** Kierkegaard knew Andersen within Copenhagen literary society and made Andersen’s *Kun en Spillemand* the subject of *Af en endnu Levendes Papirer, udgivet mod hans Villie* (1838). He argued that Andersen lacked a coherent life-view and relied too heavily on external circumstances as the condition of genius. The relationship is best classified as personal acquaintance and major critical antagonism, not sustained friendship.

### Wilhelm Richard Wagner

**Documented fact.** Andersen met Wagner in Zürich in 1855. The meeting belongs to Andersen’s extensive German-speaking musical and literary network. Evidence supports acquaintance, not a close collaboration.

### Jacob Ludwig Carl Grimm and Wilhelm Carl Grimm

**Reported fact.** Andersen moved in German literary circles where the Grimms’ fairy-tale collections were unavoidable points of reference, and he encountered Jacob Grimm. The crucial relationship is also literary: Andersen’s authored literary tales were repeatedly grouped with the Grimms’ philological and folk-collecting project, producing the persistent but inaccurate myth that Andersen merely transcribed folklore.

### Alexander von Humboldt

**Reported fact.** Andersen met or was received by Humboldt in Berlin through court and literary networks. Andersen admired Humboldt’s combination of science, travel, and cosmopolitan intellectual life. The relationship was one of prestigious acquaintance rather than collaboration.

### Victor-Marie Hugo

**Reported fact.** Andersen encountered Hugo during his French travels and belonged to overlapping Paris literary networks. Documentation supports a meeting or acquaintance, but not a durable intimate friendship.

### Alexandre Dumas

**Reported fact.** Andersen met Dumas in Parisian literary society. The connection was social and literary, without evidence of co-authorship or a continuing alliance.

### Johann Wolfgang von Goethe

**Documented influence, not a meeting.** Goethe died in 1832, when Andersen’s German career was beginning. Andersen read and admired him, and Goethe’s international literary model influenced the cultural world Andersen entered. No documented personal meeting is established here.

### Friedrich Schiller

**Documented influence, not a meeting.** Schiller died in 1805. His drama and German Romantic legacy formed part of Andersen’s education and theatrical formation; there was no personal contact.

### William Shakespeare

Not separately named in the roster except through related persons, but relevant to roster connections involving Dickens and theatrical culture. Andersen’s pseudonym “Villiam Christian Walter” invoked Shakespeare, Andersen, and Walter Scott; *At være eller ikke være* invoked *Hamlet*. This is documented literary influence.

### Walter Elias Disney

**Posthumous documented adaptation.** Disney was born after Andersen’s death. Disney productions adapted “The Ugly Duckling” and radically transformed “The Little Mermaid”; *Frozen* was loosely derived from “The Snow Queen.” This connection shaped mass global reception but was not personal.

### Maurice Bernard Sendak

**Posthumous documented influence.** Sendak illustrated and discussed Andersen and belonged to the later children’s-literature tradition deeply shaped by him. No personal contact was possible.

### Oscar Fingal O’Flahertie Wills Wilde

**Posthumous literary connection.** Wilde’s literary fairy tales belong to a tradition strongly shaped by Andersen. Andersen died when Wilde was twenty and no documented personal meeting is established.

### Other roster members

No sufficiently documented direct relationship was established in the consulted sources for the remaining named individuals. Absence from this section should not be read as proof that no archival trace exists; it means no reliable meeting, correspondence, collaboration, patronage, rivalry, or explicit acknowledged influence was confirmed during this research.
