# Pierre-Simon Laplace: Research Dossier

*Prepared 22 August 2026. Dates are Gregorian unless stated otherwise. Claims are separated into documented fact, contemporary testimony, later recollection, and legend where the evidence requires it.*

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and social origins

Laplace was the son of **Pierre Laplace** and **Marie-Anne Sochon**. Parish, tax, and local evidence examined by modern biographers does not support the once-standard picture of the boy as the child of a destitute peasant or farm laborer. His paternal family had been established in agriculture in the Beaumont area; his father also traded cider and held the municipal position of **syndic**. His mother belonged to a locally substantial family. The household was provincial and non-noble, but sufficiently secure to educate a son. The exact proportions of land, trade income, and patronal aid are not recoverable from the web-accessible record.

Older accounts—Rouse Ball is a conspicuous example—made him the son of “a small cottager or perhaps a farm-labourer” educated through wealthy neighbors. They also claimed that he later concealed or rejected his humble relations. These are **hostile biographical traditions**, not securely documented facts. Roger Hahn and Charles Gillispie reconstructed a more comfortable local milieu and warned that Laplace’s surviving personal archive is thin. The contrast probably arose from nineteenth-century genius-from-poverty storytelling, sparse evidence about his youth, and Laplace’s later aristocratic rise.

No sibling played a documented role in his Paris career. A frequently named benefactor is the Beaumont patron **the marquis d’Héricy**; the exact extent of his intervention is less certain than the broad fact that local patronage and recommendations assisted Laplace’s education.

### Marriage and descendants

On **15 March 1788** (Roger Hahn; Gillispie gives May in one account), Laplace, then thirty-eight, married at Saint-Sulpice, Paris, **Marie-Anne-Charlotte de Courty de Romanges** (1769–c.1832), about nineteen years old and from a family connected with Besançon. The date discrepancy should be checked against the original parish act for genealogical use.

Their children were:

- **Charles-Émile-Pierre-Joseph de Laplace** (5 April 1789–27 October 1874), artillery officer, veteran of the Peninsular, Russian, German, and 1814 campaigns; later lieutenant-general, hereditary marquis and peer, and senator under Napoleon III. He died without children.
- **Sophie-Suzanne de Laplace** (1792–22 September 1813), married **Adolphe-François-René, marquis de Portes**, on 8 October 1812 and died following childbirth in 1813. Their daughter **Angélique de Portes** (1813–1889) carried the direct family descent.

Sophie-Suzanne’s death was the major documented private catastrophe of Laplace’s later life. His wife managed the social household around his scientific patronage. Charles-Émile assisted with late editions and inherited the title immediately after his father’s death.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1749–1766

Laplace grew up in Beaumont-en-Auge. His father appears initially to have intended him for the Church, a normal route of advancement for an intellectually able provincial boy. He attended the local school associated with a Benedictine priory and/or the external classes of the Beaumont military school; institutional accounts phrase this differently. The surviving evidence does not permit a day-by-day childhood reconstruction, and later anecdotes about prodigious memory should be treated as retrospective character sketches.

At about sixteen he entered the **University of Caen**, formally oriented toward theology. Caen exposed him to mathematics through **Christophe Gadbled** and **Pierre Le Canu**. The common account that mathematics abruptly displaced a clerical vocation is broadly credible, though the precise moment is not documented. He absorbed scholastic philosophy as well as the newer analysis and mechanics. His early paper on integral calculus and finite differences brought him into correspondence with **Joseph-Louis Lagrange** and appeared in the Turin academy’s *Miscellanea Taurinensia*.

## Education and Formation, 1766–1769

Laplace did not take a conventional Parisian degree. Around 1768–69, aged nineteen or twenty, he went to Paris with letters of recommendation. The celebrated account says **Jean le Rond d’Alembert** at first ignored him; Laplace then sent a short memoir on the principles of mechanics, which induced d’Alembert to recognize his ability and secure him a teaching post. The core—d’Alembert’s decisive sponsorship and an appointment at the École Militaire—is well supported; the dramatic ignored-letter sequence comes from later biography rather than a surviving complete correspondence.

At the **École Militaire** Laplace taught elementary mathematics to cadets while pursuing advanced research. His post supplied income, a Paris base, and proximity to the Académie. Claims that he formally taught or examined Napoleon need precision: Laplace participated in military-school examination structures, and later tradition makes him Napoleon Bonaparte’s examiner in 1785, but the exact personal encounter is less fully documented than their later political relationship.

### Formative intellectual sources

- **Newton:** universal gravitation and the planetary problem, recast through continental analysis.
- **Euler, Clairaut, d’Alembert, Lagrange:** differential equations, perturbation theory, mechanics, and probability.
- **Thomas Bayes and Richard Price:** inverse probability. Laplace independently built a far broader analytic program; historians dispute how “Bayesian” should be applied retrospectively.
- **Daniel and Jakob Bernoulli, de Moivre:** probability, generating functions, demographic applications.
- **Buffon and natural history:** broad probabilistic and cosmogonic problems.
- **Leibnizian sufficient reason and classical determinism:** background to his later hypothetical intelligence, though the famous formulation was Laplace’s own synthesis.

## Early Career and Académie, 1769–1788

### Entry into research

Laplace presented a paper on maxima and minima to the Académie on **28 March 1770**, followed on **18 July** by work on difference equations. Between 1771 and 1773 he submitted a rapid series on differential equations, finite differences, probability, and mechanics. D’Alembert promoted him but also tried unsuccessfully to obtain a post for him in Berlin.

On **31 March 1773**, aged twenty-four, Laplace was elected an **adjoint** member of the Académie des sciences after an earlier unsuccessful candidacy. Advancement in the corporate hierarchy followed: associate and then pensionnaire in the mathematical sciences. The common Senate page’s statement “admitted in 1785” compresses or confuses rank changes; 1773 is the key first election.

### Probability and statistics

His *Mémoire sur la probabilité des causes par les événements* (read in 1773, published 1774) gave a systematic rule for inferring causes from observed effects. He developed generating functions, asymptotic approximation, inverse probability, laws of error, demographic ratios, and testimony. In 1774 he derived the double-exponential error law now called the **Laplace distribution**; he later moved toward normal approximations and characteristic-function methods.

Laplace’s “rule of succession”—if an event has occurred *s* times in *n* trials, with a uniform prior, the probability of its next occurrence is (s+1)/(n+2)—became famous and controversial. His sunrise example was illustrative, not a declaration that astronomy should be ignored. He explicitly noted that prior knowledge changes the probability.

### Celestial mechanics

In 1773 he addressed the long-term invariability of planetary mean motions, then built a program explaining apparent irregularities as periodic perturbations rather than secular collapse. Major results included:

- analysis of the Jupiter–Saturn “great inequality,” arising from their near 5:2 orbital resonance;
- explanation of the Moon’s secular acceleration, initially attributed to changes in Earth’s orbital eccentricity (later work showed tidal dissipation supplies the missing component);
- theory of planetary figures and attraction of spheroids;
- systematic use of what became spherical harmonics, Legendre/Laplace coefficients, and gravitational potential methods;
- the **Laplace resonance** of Jupiter’s moons Io, Europa, and Ganymede.

The result was not a proof of absolute, eternal stability in the modern nonlinear sense. It showed stability to the perturbative orders and model assumptions then handled. Later celestial mechanics—Le Verrier, Poincaré, twentieth-century numerical dynamics—exposed limits to the strong popular claim that Laplace proved the Solar System permanently stable.

### Collaboration with Lavoisier

From 1782 to 1784 Laplace collaborated with **Antoine-Laurent Lavoisier**. Their ice calorimeter measured heat through melted ice; experiments on respiration related animal heat to combustion; work on gases and pressure contributed to mathematical physics and physical chemistry. Authorship was genuinely collaborative. Laplace supplied mathematical analysis and Lavoisier the chemical program and laboratory. The pair’s memoir on heat (1783) is a principal primary document.

### Intellectual style and criticism

Laplace rapidly acquired a reputation for power, ambition, reserve, and scant acknowledgment. *Mécanique céleste* later absorbed results by Newton, Euler, Clairaut, d’Alembert, Lagrange, Legendre, and others, often without modern citation practice. This generated contemporary and historical criticism. **Documented issue:** predecessors were often not named at the point of use. **Interpretive dispute:** whether this was plagiarism, the conventions of synthetic treatise writing, or an imperial authorial style. Biot’s recollection that difficult steps were replaced by “Il est aisé à voir…” became part of Laplace folklore; it expresses a real feature of the text but should not be treated as a literal confession that proofs were lost in every instance.

## Chronological Life History

### 1788–1794: marriage, Revolution, and technical administration

Marriage in 1788 placed Laplace within a stable bourgeois household. During the early Revolution he served scientific commissions rather than a party. He participated in the standardization of **weights and measures**, the work that produced the metric system, and in geodetic and administrative calculations.

The Académie was suppressed in 1793. In December 1793 the Committee of Public Safety removed Laplace and several others from the weights-and-measures commission on the stated ground that it needed men worthy of republican confidence. This was a **political dismissal**, not a criminal charge. Lavoisier was executed in May 1794; Laplace was not arrested. He left Paris for **Melun** during the most violent phase of the Terror. Later portrayals range from prudent survival to cowardly political avoidance; no evidence shows participation in repression or a conspiracy.

Laplace and Lagrange were consulted over the Revolutionary calendar. Lalande’s archived notes suggest Laplace avoided directly confronting the calendar’s enthusiasts with the mathematical incompatibility between the natural day and year. This is a small but revealing instance of political caution.

### 1794–1799: rebuilding French science

Laplace briefly taught at the **École normale** established in 1795 and contributed to the organization and examination culture of the **École polytechnique**. The restored scientific body, the **Institut national**, elected him to its mathematical section in 1795. He became a founding member of the **Bureau des Longitudes**, created to improve astronomy, navigation, geodesy, and timekeeping. He exerted influence at the Paris Observatory, although Delambre and others criticized an administrator whose strength was theory rather than daily observation.

In **1796** Laplace published *Exposition du système du monde*, a largely nontechnical synthesis. Its final book advanced the **nebular hypothesis**: a hot, rotating solar atmosphere contracted and shed rings that formed the planets and satellites. **Immanuel Kant** had published a distinct nebular cosmogony in 1755, building in part on earlier suggestions by Swedenborg and others. Laplace apparently formulated his version independently and did not cite Kant; the later compound “Kant–Laplace hypothesis” can conceal substantial differences. Kant supplied a qualitative universal history; Laplace proposed a more mechanically specified solar-ring scenario.

The first two volumes of *Traité de mécanique céleste* appeared in **1799**, translating Newtonian gravitation into advanced analysis and integrating a century of perturbation results.

### 1799–1804: minister and senator

After the coup of 18 Brumaire, First Consul **Napoleon Bonaparte** appointed Laplace **Minister of the Interior** on 12 November 1799. He left office on 25 December, replaced by Lucien Bonaparte. Napoleon’s later *Mémorial de Sainte-Hélène* mocked him for carrying the “spirit of infinitesimals” into administration and seeking subtleties everywhere. This is a **hostile retrospective judgment by the ruler who dismissed him**. Historian Ivor Grattan-Guinness argued Laplace was a prestigious short-term figurehead while the new regime consolidated itself. The record supports failure to establish himself as an effective minister, but not the full caricature.

Napoleon immediately placed him in the **Sénat conservateur**. Laplace became vice-president (1803) and chancellor (often described as chancellor or arch-chancellor) and received the Legion of Honor: commander and then grand officer. He was politically useful as a scientist of international prestige and scientifically powerful because state patronage passed through institutions he influenced.

### 1802: the documented Napoleon–Laplace conversation

Sir **William Herschel’s diary entry of 8 August 1802** is the only identified eyewitness record. At Malmaison, Napoleon, Laplace, and others discussed the origin and extent of the heavens. Napoleon asked who made it all; Herschel recorded Laplace’s response as saying that a continuous chain of natural causes would account for the phenomena. The diary does **not** record “I had no need of that hypothesis.” This proves a conversation about creation occurred; it neither proves nor disproves every later phrase attributed to it.

### 1804–1814: imperial eminence and Arcueil

Laplace supported the imperial settlement and received noble status as **count of the Empire**. With the chemist **Claude-Louis Berthollet**, he formed the **Société d’Arcueil** around their neighboring country houses (c.1805–06). Members and guests included **Jean-Baptiste Biot, Siméon-Denis Poisson, Étienne-Louis Malus, Louis-Jacques Thénard, Joseph-Louis Gay-Lussac**, and the young **Alexander von Humboldt**. The society’s *Mémoires* (1807–17) institutionalized mathematical physics and exact measurement.

This was both a research network and a patronage machine. Laplace and Berthollet offered laboratories, introductions, academy votes, problems, and state access. The “Laplace school” developed capillarity, gases, optics, acoustics, heat, probability, and celestial mechanics. Critics saw concentrated influence, doctrinal pressure, and favoritism. There is no evidence of a commercial company, patents, or private industrial enterprise; his capital was institutional and political.

Laplace’s physical program favored short-range molecular forces, imponderable fluids, corpuscular light, and caloric. It achieved major results in capillarity and the speed of sound but later lost ground to **Fresnel’s wave optics** and **Fourier’s analytic theory of heat**.

The *Mécanique céleste* continued: volumes III and IV (1802, 1805), and volume V much later (1825). His *Théorie analytique des probabilités* appeared in **1812**, with a second edition in 1814 and a third in 1820. The accessible *Essai philosophique sur les probabilités* followed in **1814**, derived from the treatise’s introduction.

In 1810–11 Laplace developed characteristic-function methods and an early general central-limit theorem. Responding to **Carl Friedrich Gauss’s** *Theoria motus* (1809), he linked least squares, normal approximation, and probability. The direct priority quarrel over least squares was principally **Legendre versus Gauss**: Legendre published the method in 1805; Gauss claimed use since 1795. Laplace entered the mathematical debate, corresponded with Gauss, and supplied probabilistic justifications. Calling Gauss simply Laplace’s rival misstates a relationship that mixed independent achievement, correspondence, and overlapping programs.

### 1813–1815: family loss and regime change

Sophie-Suzanne died in childbirth in September 1813. In April 1814 Laplace voted in the Senate for Napoleon’s deposition and adhered to the Bourbon Restoration. During the Hundred Days he withdrew from Paris. Louis XVIII rewarded him with a peerage; in Marshal Ney’s trial in December 1815, Laplace voted for death.

These are **documented political acts**. Bonapartist and republican writers consequently made him a symbol of opportunism. His defenders emphasize institutional survival and the lack of a stable democratic alternative. The evidence establishes repeated accommodation—to republic, consulate, empire, and monarchy—but cannot establish an unrecorded private motive.

### 1816–1827: Bourbon peer, marquis, and last works

Laplace entered the **Académie française** in 1816 and became **marquis** in 1817. He participated in cadastral and scientific commissions and continued revising his great works. The fifth volume of *Mécanique céleste* appeared in 1825.

His scientific dominance weakened. **Joseph Fourier’s** *Théorie analytique de la chaleur* (1822) treated heat through differential equations and trigonometric series without Laplacian caloric microphysics. Laplace and allies had subjected Fourier’s 1807 memoir to demanding review; the relationship was institutional and intellectually tense, not a simple personal feud. Fourier’s framework and Fresnel’s wave optics ultimately displaced important parts of Laplace’s physical school. At the same time, Fourier analysis and the later standardized **Laplace transform** became complementary pillars of mathematical physics. Laplace had used exponential integral transformations in probability; the modern operational transform and its name were systematized chiefly in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

In 1826 Laplace declined to join an Académie protest for freedom of the press. This political conservatism alienated liberal colleagues, including the circle around Arago. It was not a legal offense or formal investigation.

Laplace became ill early in 1827. Modern summaries do not establish a precise diagnosis from clinical records. He died at his Paris house on Monday morning, 5 March. The Académie canceled its meeting, an exceptional mark of respect. A conventional reported deathbed statement has him affirming that humanity pursues phantoms; its exact wording and witness chain require caution.

Laplace rose from provincial non-nobility to a wealthy titled household. State salaries, academy pensions, senatorial office, imperial and royal honors, property, and book income all contributed. Claims of corruption, embezzlement, secret business ownership, money laundering, or fraudulent investment are not supported by the consulted record. The material controversy is instead **patronage concentration**: access to posts, publications, and academy recognition flowed through a network in which Laplace had exceptional power.

## Political, Religious, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

### Politics

Laplace left no sustained political theory comparable to his scientific works. His record is administrative and adaptive:

1. served royal scientific institutions;
2. worked for revolutionary standardization and republican education;
3. accepted the Consulate’s interior ministry and Senate;
4. accepted imperial honors;
5. voted Napoleon’s deposition;
6. accepted Bourbon peerage and marquisate;
7. voted to execute Ney and resisted the 1826 press-freedom protest.

“Ardent republican,” “Bonapartist,” “royalist,” and “pure opportunist” each capture only a phase or polemical reading. The documented constant was protection of hierarchy, scientific institutions, and his own position.

### Religion

He was raised for a possible Catholic clerical career but his mature writings explain physical phenomena through lawful causes without revealed theology. Evidence does not securely classify him as atheist. Contemporaries and later interpreters variously called him deist, skeptical, indifferent, or atheist. His wife was conventionally religious; some accounts say he received religious attention at death, but this does not settle private belief.

His *Essai philosophique* rejected miraculous suspension as a useful scientific explanation and demanded proportionately strong evidence for extraordinary claims. He nevertheless advised against denying unexplained phenomena merely because current science could not explain them. The safest description is **methodological naturalism with unresolved private metaphysics**.

### Determinism and probability

Laplace imagined an intelligence that, knowing at one instant all forces and positions and possessing sufficient analytic power, would comprehend past and future in one formula. This is a limiting statement of classical determinism. Probability, for Laplace, ordinarily measured human ignorance rather than ontological indeterminacy. The tension is deliberate: exact causation at the world level, probabilistic reasoning at the finite-knower level.

The later label **“Laplace’s demon”** was not his. Later physics qualified the model through statistical mechanics, chaos, relativity, quantum mechanics, and computational limits. It remains a philosophical benchmark, not an experimentally proposed creature.

## Scientific Work: Technical Inventory

### Mathematics and analysis

- **Laplace’s equation**, \(\nabla^2\phi=0\), central to potential theory; its history includes Euler, Lagrange, and Poisson, so the eponym should not imply isolated invention.
- **Laplace operator / Laplacian** and harmonic functions.
- **Spherical harmonics** and Laplace coefficients in gravitational analysis; Legendre independently and priorly introduced closely related functions.
- Generating functions, finite differences, continued fractions, determinants, differential equations.
- Exponential integral transforms later called the **Laplace transform**. Euler and Lagrange supplied precedents; the modern theory and operational notation postdate Laplace.
- **Laplace method** for asymptotic integrals.

### Probability and statistics

- inverse probability and the rule of succession;
- Laplace distribution and laws of error;
- central-limit approximations and characteristic functions;
- Bayesian estimation and asymptotic theory;
- probabilistic treatment of least squares;
- demographic sex ratios, juries, testimony, mortality, and population;
- philosophical codification of the classical definition: favorable equally possible cases divided by all possible cases.

Limits include the indifference principle’s dependence on parameterization, insufficiently controlled priors, and social applications that can import assumptions of equipossibility. These are technical and historical limitations, not accusations of fraud.

### Astronomy and physics

- analytic celestial mechanics and perturbation theory;
- Jupiter–Saturn inequality and satellite resonance;
- lunar theory and an incomplete explanation of secular acceleration;
- figures of rotating bodies and tides;
- nebular cosmogony;
- capillary action through molecular forces;
- correction to Newton’s speed-of-sound calculation by accounting for adiabatic heating, producing the Newton–Laplace formula;
- calorimetry and respiration with Lavoisier;
- barometric and atmospheric analysis.

### Black-hole precursor

In early editions of *Exposition du système du monde*, Laplace reasoned within Newtonian corpuscular optics that a sufficiently massive star could have escape velocity exceeding the speed of light and therefore be invisible. **John Michell** had published a related “dark star” argument in 1784. Laplace removed the discussion from later editions, plausibly as wave optics gained force. This is a precursor analogy, not general relativity’s black hole concept.

## Controversies, Allegations, and Disputed Questions

### Political opportunism

**Documented facts:** Laplace accepted posts and honors from successive regimes; voted Napoleon’s deposition; voted death for Ney; avoided Paris during the Terror and Hundred Days. **Contemporary/later accusation:** servility and opportunism. **Unresolved:** the mixture of conviction, fear, ambition, institutional protection, and family security motivating each act. No court or parliamentary body charged him with treason or corruption.

### Patronage and intellectual empire

**Documented:** he held multiple institutional offices, mentored a school, influenced appointments and evaluations, and promoted a distinct mathematical-physical program. **Criticism:** favoritism and doctrinal domination. **Qualification:** patronage was the normal mechanism of Old Regime and Napoleonic science, but Laplace’s concentration of it was exceptional. No formal misconduct finding is known.

### Acknowledgment and priority

**Documented:** the *Mécanique céleste* often incorporates predecessors’ results without point-by-point credit. Legendre, Lagrange, and others had legitimate priority interests. **Disputed classification:** plagiarism versus contemporary synthesis. The absence of modern citations is real; an accusation of wholesale theft is too broad without result-specific analysis.

### Probability disputes

Laplace’s inverse probability overlaps what is now called Bayes’ theorem. Bayes’s posthumous paper, edited by Price, preceded Laplace; Laplace independently generalized the method and created analytic machinery. “Laplace stole Bayes’s theorem” is unsupported. Conversely, calling the full field solely “Bayesian” can erase Laplace’s contribution.

### Scientific failures

- permanent solar-system stability was overstated in popular reception;
- the lunar secular-acceleration solution was incomplete because tides were omitted;
- ring-shedding details of the nebular hypothesis failed in their original form;
- caloric and corpuscular-light commitments lost to thermodynamics and wave optics;
- the Newtonian dark-star calculation was not a relativistic black-hole theory.

These are revisions in science, not fraud.

### Crimes, lawsuits, and investigations

No substantiated evidence was found of criminal activity, fraud, money laundering, sexual scandal, addiction, imprisonment, lawsuit, or intelligence-service connection. Political violence enters his record through his Senate vote for Napoleon’s deposition and his peer’s vote for Ney’s execution, not through personal command of violence.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Propaganda

### “I had no need of that hypothesis”

1. **Story:** Napoleon observed that *Mécanique céleste* did not mention the Creator; Laplace replied, “Sire, je n’avais pas besoin de cette hypothèse-là.” A coda has Lagrange say God is a beautiful hypothesis explaining many things, with Laplace answering that it predicts nothing.
2. **Appearance and circulation:** Napoleon’s physician **Francesco Antommarchi**, *Derniers moments de Napoléon* (1825), records a creation exchange without securely producing the canonical scene. Nineteenth-century scientific biography, including Rouse Ball, fixed the famous wording. It circulated among atheists as defiance and among religious writers as scientistic arrogance.
3. **Evidence for:** Herschel’s 1802 diary proves Napoleon and Laplace discussed who made the universe; Arago reportedly believed some version of the phrase existed.
4. **Evidence against/qualification:** Herschel does not record the canonical words. In 1884 **Hervé Faye**, citing Arago, said the story was strangely transformed: the rejected hypothesis was a special divine intervention to restore planetary stability, not God’s existence. Exact wording and occasion remain unresolved.
5. **Reputational effect:** it became Laplace’s defining public quotation and an emblem of scientific naturalism, eclipsing the subtler religious evidence.

**Classification:** documented underlying conversation; famous wording **unverified later formulation**; atheistic interpretation **disputed**.

### “Laplace’s demon”

The story imagines a supernatural demon knowing every particle and future. Laplace actually wrote of an **intelligence** in the 1814 *Essai*, as an epistemic ideal. The demon label arose later, commonly associated with nineteenth-century commentary. It turned an analytic limit-case into a mythic being and made Laplace a symbol of mechanistic fatalism.

### Impoverished peasant genius who denied his family

Nineteenth-century writers described extreme poverty, charitable discovery, and later ingratitude. Local-record research supports a comfortable agricultural/merchant municipal family, though not wealth or nobility. Sparse autobiographical evidence and his later title facilitated both heroic and hostile versions. **Classification:** exaggerated/partly refuted biographical legend.

### “French Newton”

This honorific compresses a real achievement—making Newtonian celestial mechanics analytic and systematic—but also suppresses the work of Euler, Clairaut, d’Alembert, Lagrange, Legendre, and observational astronomers. It has functioned as national scientific hagiography.

### The tiny preserved brain

Later accounts say physician **François Magendie** removed Laplace’s brain, which entered anatomical collections and was exhibited in Britain, reportedly below average size. The story was used to challenge crude equations of brain size with genius. The chain of custody and measurement should be verified against museum catalogs before treating the reported dimensions as established. **Classification:** plausible postmortem report, incompletely verified here; measurement folklore grew around it.

### “It is easy to see” because he forgot the proof

Biot and later mathematical writers associated Laplace’s “Il est aisé à voir” with difficult omitted derivations, sometimes saying he had lost or could not reconstruct proofs. The phrase and compressed derivations are genuine; the universal rule that it signals a forgotten proof is pedagogical folklore.

## Works by Laplace

### Principal books and separately issued works

1. **_*Exposition du système du monde*_** (Paris, 1796; substantially revised editions 1799, 1808, 1813, 1824). Popular-systematic astronomy; Book V contains the nebular hypothesis. Early editions contain the dark-star passage; later ones omit it. English versions appeared as *The System of the World*.
2. **_*Traité de mécanique céleste*_**, 5 vols. (Paris, 1799, 1799, 1802, 1805, 1825). The fifth volume incorporates supplements. Contemporary translations/adaptations include Nathaniel Bowditch’s monumental English *Mécanique céleste* (1829–39), with extensive commentary.
3. **_*Théorie analytique des probabilités*_** (Paris, 1812; 2nd ed. 1814; 3rd ed. 1820, with supplements). Genuine authored treatise.
4. **_*Essai philosophique sur les probabilités*_** (Paris, 1814; repeatedly revised). Originated as the introduction to the second probability edition; genuine authored popular/philosophical presentation.
5. **_*Précis de l’histoire de l’astronomie*_** (1821, also embedded in editions/collections). Historical exposition associated with *Système du monde*.
6. **_*Œuvres complètes de Laplace*_**, 14 vols. (Paris: Gauthier-Villars, 1878–1912), published posthumously under the auspices of the Académie des sciences. **Edited collected edition, not a new work authored in 1878–1912.** Vols. 1–5 contain *Mécanique céleste*; vol. 6 *Exposition*; vol. 7 probability works; vols. 8–12 scientific memoirs; vols. 13–14 supplements and correspondence/materials (volume organization varies in catalog descriptions).

### Major memoirs and paper clusters

Laplace published scores of memoirs, chiefly in the *Mémoires de l’Académie royale des sciences*, *Mémoires présentés par divers savants*, *Mémoires de l’Institut*, *Connaissance des temps*, and *Mémoires de la Société d’Arcueil*. A complete item-by-item scholarly bibliography should be generated from the tables of contents of all fourteen *Œuvres complètes*. The principal identifiable clusters are:

- early memoirs on maxima/minima, integral calculus, finite differences, differential equations, and mechanics (1770–73);
- **“Mémoire sur la probabilité des causes par les événements”** (read 1773; pub. 1774);
- memoirs on probability, generating functions, and laws of error (1774 onward);
- planetary secular inequalities and invariability (1773–76);
- attraction and figures of spheroids, lunar motion, Jupiter–Saturn inequalities, and satellite theory (1770s–1780s);
- **Lavoisier and Laplace, “Mémoire sur la chaleur”** (1783), genuine co-authorship;
- memoirs on capillary action, refraction, atmospheric pressure, and speed of sound;
- probability memoirs of 1810–11 establishing asymptotic and characteristic-function techniques;
- supplements to the *Théorie analytique* and *Mécanique céleste* through 1825.

### Correspondence and archival status

Laplace did not leave a large published diary or autobiography. The *Œuvres complètes* and later scholarly works print selected correspondence, including exchanges with Lagrange, Gauss, and scientific officials. The Paris Observatory holds the manuscript of the third edition of *Système du monde* and related scientific archives; Académie and French national/local repositories hold institutional records. Letters published after death are **edited primary sources**, not new authored monographs. No patents, novels, plays, artworks, recordings, or ghostwritten political memoirs are known.

### Hostile and sympathetic traditions

No single modern “hostile biography” dominates. Hostile material appears in Napoleon’s Sainte-Hélène recollections, republican/Bourbon political commentary, Legendre-centered priority narratives, and nineteenth-century stories of family ingratitude. Sympathetic institutional memorials include Arago’s éloge, Académie commemorations, and French scientific heritage pages. Both genres must be controlled against institutional records and the scientific papers.

## Films, Documentaries, Interviews, and Archival Material

Laplace died before recording media and left no interview in the modern sense. No major feature film or authoritative standalone television documentary was identified in the consulted catalogs. He appears as a historical figure in educational programs on celestial mechanics, probability, black holes, Napoleon, and the history of astronomy; these should not be confused with archival footage.

## Death, Burial, and Postmortem History

Laplace died at his rue du Bac residence on 5 March 1827. A precise cause of death is not securely stated in the consulted primary-level sources; generic claims should not be upgraded to diagnosis. He was first buried at **Père Lachaise Cemetery**. In **1888** his remains were transferred to the family estate/tomb at **Saint-Julien-de-Mailloc** near Orbec, Normandy.

The reported removal and preservation of his brain by Magendie belongs to postmortem scientific culture. Without a fully traced museum record it should remain qualified. His widow survived him by several years; Charles-Émile inherited the marquisate and peerage.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Laplace’s reputation divides into several durable identities:

1. **Architect of celestial mechanics:** the five-volume synthesis became the benchmark from which nineteenth-century authors such as Bowditch, Tisserand, Le Verrier, and Poincaré advanced.
2. **Founder of mathematical probability:** modern Bayesian history, asymptotic statistics, central-limit theory, and error analysis repeatedly return to him.
3. **Eponymic mathematician:** Laplacian, Laplace equation, transform, distribution, approximation/method, coefficients, invariant/resonance, and related objects. Many eponyms summarize collective histories.
4. **Philosophical determinist:** the later “demon” made him the canonical representative of a clockwork universe.
5. **Scientific statesman and opportunist:** French institutional history remembers both his construction of research networks and his accommodation to regimes.
6. **Naturalistic icon:** the Napoleon quotation, whether exact or not, made him a hero of secular science and a target of religious polemic.

Named memorials include lunar and Martian features, asteroid **4628 Laplace**, an asteroid/lunar eponymic tradition, his name among the **72 scientists engraved on the Eiffel Tower**, schools and streets, and proposed/working names for space-mission concepts. Mission names change; current claims should be verified against the responsible agency rather than repeated from older pages.

### Connections to the requested later figures

- **Fourier:** institutional friction over heat, followed by the complementary modern pairing of Fourier and Laplace analysis.
- **Gauss:** intersecting programs in celestial computation, potential theory, error distributions, and least squares; correspondence without a simple master-pupil relation.
- **Babbage:** personal visits and admiration; indirect transmission through French mathematical tables and analytic culture, not co-invention of computing.
- **Kant:** independent nebular hypotheses later joined under one label.
- **Kelvin:** inheritance of potential theory and participation in later thermodynamic/cosmogonic critique; no personal link due to age.

## Research Gaps and Evidentiary Cautions

- A critical edition of the complete correspondence remains the best route to refine personal relationships.
- Parish acts should settle the marriage-date discrepancy and deepen ancestry.
- A probate inventory is needed for exact wealth, property, debts, securities, and publishing income.
- The precise terminal illness and the brain’s chain of custody require medical/museum primary records.
- The Napoleon anecdote cannot responsibly be printed as verbatim fact without its variant history.
- A truly exhaustive paper bibliography requires collation of all fourteen collected-work volumes; the dossier identifies the books and principal paper clusters but does not pretend every Academy memoir title has been independently transcribed.
