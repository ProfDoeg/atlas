## Works by Hypatia of Alexandria

### Mathematical commentaries

- **Before 415 CE:** *Commentary on Diophantus*. A mathematical commentary, usually understood to concern Diophantus’s *Arithmetica*, attested only by the *Suda*; it is lost as an independently identifiable text. Proposals that parts survive anonymously within the Greek or Arabic Diophantine tradition remain disputed and cannot securely establish its contents or extent. ([*Suda* υ 166](https://www.cs.uky.edu/~raphael/sol/sol-cgi-bin/search.cgi?db=REAL&enlogin=ad4a568f20ff7b94c06403f7377f7baa&field=adlerhw_gr&login=Guest&searchstr=upsilon%2C166); [Cameron, “Hypatia: Life, Death, and Works”](https://academic.oup.com/book/10522/chapter-abstract/158433189); [Deakin, *Dictionary of Scientific Biography* article](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/DSB/Hypatia.pdf))

- **Before 415 CE:** *Commentary on the Conics of Apollonius*. A commentary on Apollonius of Perga’s geometrical treatise, likewise known from the *Suda* and lost. Attempts to identify traces of it in Eutocius’s surviving commentary are highly conjectural, so no surviving passage can confidently be called Hypatia’s. ([*Suda* υ 166](https://www.cs.uky.edu/~raphael/sol/sol-cgi-bin/search.cgi?db=REAL&enlogin=ad4a568f20ff7b94c06403f7377f7baa&field=adlerhw_gr&login=Guest&searchstr=upsilon%2C166); [Deakin, *Dictionary of Scientific Biography* article](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/DSB/Hypatia.pdf))

### Astronomical text

- **Before 415 CE:** *Astronomical Canon*. The *Suda* names this work between Hypatia’s two commentaries, but its wording is textually and grammatically uncertain: it may have been an astronomical table or canon, an edition of Ptolemy’s *Handy Tables*, or a commentary “on” such a canon. Nothing securely identifiable survives, and the title should not be silently expanded into a particular Ptolemaic work. ([*Suda* υ 166](https://www.cs.uky.edu/~raphael/sol/sol-cgi-bin/search.cgi?db=REAL&enlogin=ad4a568f20ff7b94c06403f7377f7baa&field=adlerhw_gr&login=Guest&searchstr=upsilon%2C166); [Deakin, *Dictionary of Scientific Biography* article](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/DSB/Hypatia.pdf); [Cameron, “Hypatia: Life, Death, and Works”](https://academic.oup.com/book/10522/chapter-abstract/158433189))

### Editorial collaboration

- **Before 415 CE:** *Theon’s Commentary on Book III of Ptolemy’s Mathematical Syntaxis (Almagest), in an edition associated with Hypatia*. This is an editorial manifestation rather than an independently authored book: its heading says that the edition was “prepared,” “revised,” or “proofread” by Theon’s daughter Hypatia. Scholars disagree whether she checked Theon’s commentary, established the underlying text of Ptolemy, or performed a more substantial revision; Book III survives, but her individual contribution cannot be isolated with certainty. ([Deakin, *Dictionary of Scientific Biography* article](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/DSB/Hypatia.pdf); [Cameron, “Hypatia: Life, Death, and Works”](https://academic.oup.com/book/10522/chapter-abstract/158433189); [Petkas, ed., *Hypatia of Alexandria: Her Context and Legacy*](https://cdn.mohrsiebeck.com/0d%2Cf4f6f1b6a947b4a8aa0bdf94b5dfa7.pdf))

### Conjectural editorial attributions

- **Before 415 CE?:** *An edition of Archimedes’ Measurement of a Circle*. Wilbur Knorr proposed that features of the transmitted text might descend from Hypatia’s editorial activity, but subsequent reference scholarship treats the attribution as highly speculative. It resists inclusion as an obra: no ancient source names Hypatia in connection with it. ([Deakin, *Dictionary of Scientific Biography* article](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/DSB/Hypatia.pdf))

- **Before 415 CE?:** *Anonymous material on isoperimetric figures*. This is another hypothetical textual manifestation attributed to Hypatia through modern stylistic and transmission arguments, not through an ancient title or ascription. Its authorship and even its status as a distinct Hypatian work are unproved. ([Deakin, *Dictionary of Scientific Biography* article](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/DSB/Hypatia.pdf))

- **Before 415 CE?:** *Material on the measurement of the sphere later transmitted through John of Tynemouth*. Knorr also suggested Hypatian ancestry for this material, but the proposed transmission is too indirect to identify a work by her. It belongs in the record only as a disputed attribution, not as an established item in her corpus. ([Deakin, *Dictionary of Scientific Biography* article](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/DSB/Hypatia.pdf))

### Instrumental projects and instruction

- **Before or during 402 CE:** *Instruction for constructing a silver plane astrolabe*. This was pedagogical and technical know-how, not an extant instrument demonstrably made by Hypatia: Synesius credits his teacher with the knowledge underlying an astrolabe that he himself presented to Paeonius. The evidence does not support the frequent claim that Hypatia invented the plane astrolabe. ([Synesius, *On the Gift of an Astrolabe*, discussed by Deakin](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/DSB/Hypatia.pdf); [Cameron, “Hypatia: Life, Death, and Works”](https://academic.oup.com/book/10522/chapter-abstract/158433189))

- **Probably after 402 and before 415 CE:** *Hydroscope requested by Synesius*. This is an unrealized or undocumented commission rather than a securely completed obra: Synesius asked Hypatia to have a notched brass instrument—apparently a hydrometer—cast and assembled, but no source says that she designed it, made it, or fulfilled the request. No physical object survives, and attributing its invention to her exceeds the evidence. ([Synesius, *Letter 15*](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Letters_of_Synesius_of_Cyrene/Letter_15); [Deakin, *Dictionary of Scientific Biography* article](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/DSB/Hypatia.pdf))

### Philosophical teaching as a resistant work-form

- **Late fourth–early fifth century CE:** *Alexandrian philosophical and mathematical teaching*. Contemporary and later testimony presents Hypatia’s teaching, lectures, and direction of pupils as a sustained intellectual practice, but no securely attributable philosophical treatise, lecture text, letter, or transcript survives. This body of teaching was something she made socially and pedagogically, yet it resists division into titled textual works; doctrines reconstructed from Synesius or other pupils cannot simply be reassigned to her. ([Cambridge History of Philosophy in Late Antiquity, “The Alexandrian school: Theon of Alexandria and Hypatia”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-history-of-philosophy-in-late-antiquity/alexandrian-school-theon-of-alexandria-and-hypatia/B68E81E7D3CD1782A1A4F716EE66ECED); [Petkas, ed., *Hypatia of Alexandria: Her Context and Legacy*](https://cdn.mohrsiebeck.com/0d%2Cf4f6f1b6a947b4a8aa0bdf94b5dfa7.pdf))
