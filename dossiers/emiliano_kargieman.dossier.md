## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Emiliano Kargieman.
- **Common shortened name:** “Emi.”
- **Professional initials / nickname:** “EK,” according to his public professional profile.
- **Documented hacker handle:** **Logical Backdoor**. A 2013 *La Nación* profile reports that he appeared under this name in the 1994 book *Llaneros solitarios: hackers, la guerrilla informática*. No second handle was reliably established.
- **Nationality:** Argentine.
- **Birth:** **7 May 1975**, Buenos Aires, Argentina. Fundación Konex supplies the exact date. ## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and formative household

**Reported fact:** Kargieman grew up in Palermo as the son of two psychologists or psychoanalysts. An Infobae profile names them as **Alfredo and Ana María**. His later recollections describe a household in which he began dismantling radios and other objects, initially using tools belonging to his grandfather.

His childhood accounts emphasize technical curiosity rather than formal family training in science. His parents gave him his first computer at approximately nine years old. He has said that frustration with repeatedly losing computer games induced him to inspect and alter their code.

No reliable published genealogy, immigration history, ethnic ancestry, or information about siblings was located. His surname is sometimes made the object of unsupported ethnic or ideological insinuation online, but the dedicated searches conducted for antisemitic, “golem,” propaganda, fabrication, and conspiracy material did not produce documentary evidence about his ancestry or religious identity.

### Partner and child

**Documented by direct interview:** By August 2026 his partner was the Argentine novelist **Pola Oloixarac**. Their daughter is named **Asia**. Kargieman described a close relationship with his daughter and said she then wanted to become a marine biologist and pop singer.

The date and legal status of the relationship, Asia’s birth date, any earlier marriage or partnership, and whether he has other children are not established in the consulted public record.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1975–Early 1990s

Kargieman attended **Wenceslao Posse**, a Buenos Aires public primary school. He later had a period in private education but finished secondary school at the public **Colegio Nicolás Avellaneda**. He has repeatedly described himself as a product of Argentine public education.

At about nine, he received a home computer. Sources vary slightly over whether he was nine or ten when he first learned to program. His own later account places the gift at nine. He learned low-level programming, including assembler, partly to alter games. At approximately twelve he acquired a modem.

The modem connected him to Buenos Aires BBS culture before routine household internet access. These bulletin-board systems allowed users to exchange messages, software, technical information, and telephone numbers for other systems. The scene overlapped with phreaking, copied software, modem experimentation, security research, and unauthorized entry into remote computers.

Kargieman’s retrospective description of the hacker ethos was that commercially supplied technology arrived with artificial labels defining what its owner could do with it. The hacker’s task was to turn the tool around, inspect it, and make it perform functions outside those labels. In 2026 he also associated the scene with the maxim that information “wants to be free.”

## The Hacker Years

## Buenos Aires BBS and Early-Internet Scene

In the early 1990s a small circle of young hackers met in person around **San José and Avenida de Mayo**, Buenos Aires. A detailed 2013 *La Nación* profile describes an informal status system based on demonstrated technical ability: access to difficult systems conferred prestige. The same account places Kargieman among the group **HBO — Hacked by Owls**.

HBO reportedly produced a small electronic magazine or “revistita.” No complete authenticated run of this publication was located during this research. Its contents and exact masthead therefore remain unresolved.

**Documented through retrospective quotation:** Kargieman says the group entered computers belonging to banks, universities, and telephone companies. He states that they did not steal or deliberately damage data and were motivated by learning and technical challenge. That is his account of intent; independent logs capable of proving the full scope of the activity are not publicly available.

### Logical Backdoor and *Llaneros solitarios*

The 1994 Argentine book *Llaneros solitarios: hackers, la guerrilla informática*, by **Fernando Bonsembiante and Raquel Roberti**, chronicled the local scene. According to *La Nación*, the nineteen-year-old Kargieman appears as **Logical Backdoor**, briefly abandoning an interview with the authors because he had other plans.

This is the clearest published attribution of a hacker handle to him. The book is an important contemporaneous source but also participates in the journalistic mythology of the period: hackers as clandestine electronic cowboys, simultaneously criminalized, romanticized, and treated as precocious technical elites.

### 1994 Recoleta conference intervention

At a 1994 hacker conference at the **Centro Cultural Recoleta**, HBO objected to the publicity surrounding the movement. According to the later *La Nación* reconstruction, the group altered or commandeered a public telephone at the venue to display or transmit a manifesto.

The manifesto rejected hackers becoming “rockstars,” defined curiosity as the potential basis of hacking, and treated disobedience toward unjustified technological restrictions as a form of personal autonomy. This episode is reported rather than preserved in an official proceeding. It became part of Kargieman’s later self-narrative: not the hacker as thief, but the hacker as a person who refuses the intended use of a system.

## DGI / AFIP Security Work

Accounts often say Kargieman “worked at AFIP at seventeen,” but the institution was still the **Dirección General Impositiva (DGI)** before the AFIP was created in 1997. The historically accurate formulation is that he worked in a DGI group later absorbed into AFIP.

An academic history of Core reconstructs the sequence as follows:

- **Ariel Futoransky** joined the DGI’s **Grupo de Proyectos Especiales**.
- He recruited **Jonatan Altszul, Emiliano Kargieman, and Gerardo Richarte**.
- The group’s members had backgrounds in programming and informatics competitions.
- Their work involved testing systems, finding security failures, and then spending substantial time correcting or mitigating the weaknesses.

Kargieman’s own version says the agency invited him to “break” its computers so officials could understand where the vulnerabilities were. On one occasion, he has said, the team found a problem rapidly and then spent approximately eighteen months fixing the consequences.

The exact civil-service title, dates of appointment and departure, pay, security clearance, and systems examined have not been published. “AFIP hacker” is therefore a convenient retrospective label, but “young DGI special-projects security tester” is more chronologically precise.

No evidence was found of a prosecution or disciplinary finding arising from this work.

Other profiles and Kargieman’s own descriptions say he also studied **Philosophy**, likewise without graduating. ## Core SDI / Core Security Technologies

## Founding, 1996

The best-documented founder list is **six people**:

1. Emiliano Kargieman
2. Iván Arce
3. Ariel Futoransky
4. Gerardo Richarte
5. Jonatan Altszul
6. Lucio Torre

Retrospective profiles sometimes reduce the story to Kargieman and Richarte, or to “four friends,” or date the company to 1995. The academic reconstruction and contemporaneous company history favor **1996**, when four members left the DGI and joined Arce and Torre.

The first office was reportedly established in **Iván Arce’s home in central Buenos Aires**. The original name was **Core SDI S.A.**, later expanded or rebranded as **Core Security Technologies**.

Early roles evolved informally:

- Arce became chief technology officer.
- Futoransky led research and development.
- Kargieman worked in research, product development, business building, and later served as vice-president.
- Richarte was both a technical founder and later Kargieman’s partner in Aconcagua and Satellogic.

## Early business model

Core began with security consulting. Its staff examined client networks, demonstrated weaknesses, and delivered technical reports. Banks and financial institutions were natural early customers because network failure and intrusion had directly measurable monetary implications.

The company simultaneously published vulnerability research. This was strategically important: advisories circulated on lists such as Bugtraq established technical credibility beyond Argentina and led to foreign clients.

In 1998 Core conducted its first penetration test for a US customer. In 2000 it opened a US office in **New York**. Headquarters later moved to **Boston**, while research and engineering continued in Buenos Aires.

## Notable research, 1997–2005

### CoreLabs

Core formed **CoreLabs** approximately one year after the company’s founding. The laboratory developed exploits, published advisories, communicated with vendors, and studied offensive-security automation.

### SSH insertion attack, 1998

The most historically significant early finding associated personally with Kargieman was the **SSH1 insertion attack**, credited in later Core correspondence to **Ariel Futoransky and Emiliano Kargieman**.

The June 1998 CORE-SDI-04 advisory showed that an attacker able to modify traffic could inject chosen data into an SSH1 encrypted stream, potentially causing arbitrary command execution on the client or server. Because the weakness implicated SSH protocol 1.5 semantics, a detector was introduced into SSH implementations.

In 2001 a further Core advisory disclosed a vulnerability in that detector—the CRC-32 compensation-attack detector—which could itself permit arbitrary code execution. The second flaw was not described as Kargieman’s discovery, but the advisory explicitly recalled his and Futoransky’s 1998 work.

### Other Core research

CoreLabs’ period output included work on authentication, protocol weaknesses, penetration methodology, and exploit development. Important examples include:

- the MySQL authentication-design flaw;
- Iván Arce’s 2001 SANS presentation on automating penetration tests;
- research that became the conceptual basis of CORE IMPACT;
- Kargieman, Futoransky, Carlos Sarraute, and Ariel Waissbein’s cryptographic research on secure triggers.

Because many advisories were corporate products with individual researchers named separately, they should not all be assigned to Kargieman personally.

## CORE IMPACT

In 2001 Iván Arce publicly presented the abstractions behind automated penetration testing. Approximately eight months later Core commercialized **CORE IMPACT**, described by Core as the first commercial off-the-shelf automated penetration-testing product.

IMPACT organized real exploits into repeatable attack paths so authorized testers could demonstrate how an attacker could move across a network. Its significance lay not in inventing penetration testing, but in packaging exploitation, agent deployment, privilege escalation, and reporting into a commercial framework.

Kargieman has repeatedly been credited with developing the first automated penetration-testing software. That description reflects his leadership and founding role; the surviving technical record also shows that CORE IMPACT was a collaborative CoreLabs engineering project, with Arce particularly prominent in its public technical exposition.

## Customers and government work

Published profiles and institutional biographies name customers including Apple, Cisco, Amazon, Mastercard, NASA, the US Air Force, DARPA, the Department of Homeland Security, and the NSA. Such lists demonstrate the company’s market position but do not establish that Kargieman personally performed work for every listed organization.

Claims that Core “worked for the White House” recur in profiles. No underlying procurement record was located during this research, so it should be treated as a reported client claim, not proof of a personal Kargieman relationship with a US president or intelligence officer.

## Investment, expansion, and exit

Core was selected by **Endeavor** in 1998. Its US expansion brought management, sales, and capital connections:

- New York office: 2000.
- Commercial release of CORE IMPACT: approximately 2002.
- Institutional investment from Morgan Stanley-associated funds occurred in stages or is dated differently across sources. Corporate histories cite an early **US$1.5 million** investment and board seat in 2002; Morgan Stanley’s current portfolio page dates its first institutional investment to **November 2008**. A 2005 investment transaction is also reported by deal databases. These may represent distinct rounds, but the public summaries do not fully reconcile them.
- US headquarters moved to Boston in 2003.
- By 2013 Core reportedly had more than 200 employees.
- Kargieman left operational involvement before the company’s later acquisition. The exact resignation date is not consistently reported; his transition into investing occurred around age thirty, approximately 2005–2006.
- Courion acquired Core in December 2015. Courion then adopted the Core Security name. The products later passed through SecureAuth and HelpSystems/Fortra.

Kargieman has summarized the experience as: **“Core fue mi MBA.”**

No public sale price establishing his personal proceeds was found.

## Aconcagua Ventures

After leaving Core, Kargieman and Gerardo Richarte formed **Aconcagua Ventures**, an early-stage technology-investment firm focused on Latin American companies capable of becoming global businesses.

A 2008 ITBA event still described Kargieman as a Core co-founder and prominent entrepreneur. In later interviews he treated Aconcagua as a personal misstep: he had assumed that the way to reproduce Core’s success was to raise a fund and advise other founders. He found investing less satisfying than building technology and believed he entered venture capital too young.

No complete public portfolio, fund size, limited-partner list, audited return, or dissolution record was found. “Founder and CEO” or “co-founder and director” are the usual public titles.

## Singularity University and the Turn to Space, 2010

In 2010 Kargieman attended the ten-week **Graduate Studies Program at Singularity University**, then housed at **NASA Ames Research Center / NASA Research Park**, Moffett Field, California.

Singularity University was founded by **Ray Kurzweil and Peter Diamandis** and hosted through a NASA lease arrangement. Its program asked participants to develop technology that could improve the lives of at least one billion people within a decade.

Kargieman investigated the space industry and concluded that satellite construction used comparatively old components and development methods. His hacker-style proposal was to substitute cheaper, contemporary electronics—including elements derived from consumer mobile-phone supply chains—and rapidly iterate small spacecraft.

Reports that he “worked at NASA” or completed “a NASA program” are imprecise. **Documented fact:** he attended a program hosted at NASA Ames. **Not established:** employment by NASA, a NASA academic degree, or a direct NASA appointment.

His direct later account says that discussions with people in the space industry convinced him that the field was less technologically modern than outsiders assumed.

## Satellogic: A Geographical and Corporate Journey

## Formation and Argentine prototype phase, 2010–2013

Kargieman founded **Satellogic** in 2010, again collaborating with Gerardo Richarte. The company’s early engineering base drew on Argentine software, electronics, aerospace, and scientific talent.

The early project received approximately **US$6 million** in support associated with Argentina’s Ministry of Science and obtained assistance from **INVAP** and the Argentine space ecosystem. This occurred during the presidency of Cristina Fernández de Kirchner. The relationship was institutional; no personal meeting between Kargieman and Fernández de Kirchner was established.

Satellogic’s first experimental spacecraft were open-platform CubeBugs:

- **CubeBug-1 / Capitán Beto:** launched 26 April 2013 on a Chinese Long March 2D from the Jiuquan Satellite Launch Center.
- **CubeBug-2 / Manolito:** launched 21 November 2013 on a Russian/Ukrainian Dnepr launch from Yasny/Dombarovsky, Russia.

The names drew on Argentine popular culture: “Capitán Beto” from Luis Alberto Spinetta’s song and “Manolito” from Quino’s *Mafalda*. This naming practice linked the private space project to Argentine cultural memory.

## Aleph-1 and international facilities

Satellogic progressed from CubeBug prototypes to the commercial **ÑuSat / Aleph-1** Earth-observation constellation. Its model combined relatively small satellites, multispectral and hyperspectral instruments, onboard processing, and a vertically integrated manufacturing process.

Documented facilities over time included:

- **Buenos Aires:** satellite research and development; mission operations.
- **Córdoba:** engineering or mission operations.
- **Montevideo, Uruguay:** headquarters for the pre-redomiciliation company and satellite assembly, integration, and testing. A filing described a pilot plant in the Zonamerica free-trade zone with capacity for approximately 24 satellites annually.
- **Barcelona:** product-delivery platform, software, data, and mission operations.
- **Tel Aviv:** engineering, development, and mission operations.
- **Beijing:** sales.
- **Charlotte / Davidson, North Carolina:** finance and, later, the principal US corporate address.
- **Netherlands:** a proposed high-throughput manufacturing facility or lease later terminated during cost reductions.

Kargieman chose Uruguay in part for regulatory, import/export, logistics, and free-zone advantages. He continued to argue that high-technology component movement in Argentina was costly and restrictive.

## Launch geography

Satellogic satellites have traveled to orbit through multiple national launch systems:

- China: early CubeBug and ÑuSat missions on Long March vehicles.
- Russia: CubeBug-2 and later deployments using Dnepr or Soyuz launch services.
- India: Aleph-1 satellites on PSLV missions from the Satish Dhawan Space Centre.
- United States: multiple launches on SpaceX Falcon 9 Transporter rideshare missions, including Transporter-4 in April 2022.
- Other launch arrangements have depended on third-party brokers and rideshare providers.

This geographical dispersion reflects commercial dependence on external launch providers rather than ownership of a launch system.

## Capital history

Satellogic initially raised approximately US$2 million from Latin American investors. Subsequent rounds included:

- **2015 Series A:** led by **Tencent Holdings** and Brazil’s **Pitanga Fund**.
- **2017 Series B:** approximately **US$27 million**, again involving Tencent, Pitanga, and CrunchFund.
- **2019 round:** approximately **US$50 million**, involving Tencent, Pitanga, IDB Lab, and other investors; cumulative reported private financing approached US$110–124 million.
- Individual pre-SPAC investors disclosed in the 2021 presentation included Mercado Libre founder Marcos Galperin and Facebook executive Javier Oliván.
- The 2021–2022 public transaction brought in Cantor Fitzgerald, SoftBank Latin America Fund, and **Liberty Strategic Capital**, led by former US Treasury secretary Steven Mnuchin.
- In 2024 **Tether Investments** provided US$30 million through secured convertible notes.

A contemporaneous report said Tencent no longer held shares at the time the SPAC was announced, even though it remained historically important as an investor.

## SPAC merger and Nasdaq listing, 2021–2022

On 5 July 2021 Satellogic agreed to merge with **CF Acquisition Corp. V**, a Cantor Fitzgerald-sponsored special-purpose acquisition company. The presentation assigned Satellogic an enterprise value of approximately **US$850 million** and projected rapid constellation and revenue growth.

The deal closed in New York on **25 January 2022**. Shares and warrants began trading on Nasdaq as **SATL** and **SATLW**. Satellogic later reported receiving approximately **US$168 million** in net cash or balance-sheet funds after transaction adjustments, less than the maximum headline figures circulated when the deal was announced.

The transaction involved:

- CF V trust capital;
- PIPE and related financing;
- SoftBank Latin America Fund;
- Cantor commitments;
- Liberty Strategic Capital;
- transaction fees paid in cash and stock;
- enhanced voting rights through Kargieman’s Class B shares.

### Stock performance and missed projections

Satellogic’s share price fell far below the SPAC’s nominal US$10 starting level after listing. The original investor presentation anticipated weekly remapping of Earth by 2023 and a much larger constellation. Those targets were not achieved on the proposed schedule.

This is a documented commercial shortfall, not evidence by itself of fraud. Public space companies broadly suffered post-SPAC repricing, but Satellogic also confronted company-specific revenue delays, launch requirements, continuing losses, and capital needs.

## Government and defense customers

### Albania

On **29 September 2022**, Satellogic signed a **US$6 million, three-year** agreement with Albania. Albania received priority use of two satellites, named **Albania-1** and **Albania-2**, for agriculture, border monitoring, illegal construction, wildfire response, traffic, and environmental applications. The satellites were launched on a SpaceX mission.

In January 2026 the relationship was extended for eleven months. The arrangement is documented by company filings and releases; independent assessment of operational outcomes was not located.

### United States

Satellogic was selected in 2024 as one of eight providers in NASA’s Commercial SmallSat Data Acquisition on-ramp. The cited **US$476 million** is a ceiling shared across all recipients, not a Satellogic award of that amount.

In December 2024 Satellogic entered an agreement giving **Maxar Intelligence** exclusive rights to task its high-revisit constellation for defined US-government and allied national-security missions. This marked an explicit shift toward defense and intelligence markets.

Earlier investor materials had emphasized commercial, environmental, and civil-government applications. By 2024–2026 national security had become a stated priority. The company warns investors that US defense work subjects it to export, cybersecurity, procurement, and debarment risks.

### Argentina and the 2019 CONAE tender

In 2019 Argentina’s CONAE opened an international procurement for high-resolution optical satellite imagery, identified as **Concurso Público 2/2019**, file EX-2019-37889113-APN-GPAYF#CONAE.

**Documented facts:**

- The tender sought an eight-year imagery service.
- Satellogic submitted an offer.
- Official procurement records preserve the procedure and offer evaluation materials.
- Chinese supplier Head Aerospace/Head Space also reportedly entered.

**Allegation:** CONAE personnel and journalists argued that the long duration and technical specifications appeared tailored to Satellogic’s capabilities. Critics connected the tender to Kargieman’s access to officials in Mauricio Macri’s government.

**Status:** No court judgment, corruption charge, or finding that Kargieman wrote the specifications was located. The dispute concerned procurement design and possible favoritism, not an adjudicated crime.

## Financial Retrenchment and Redomiciliation, 2023–2025

Satellogic entered a period of slower-than-projected growth and workforce reduction:

- 2023 revenue: approximately **US$10.1 million**, up 68%, but below earlier SPAC-era expectations.
- 2023 headcount reduction: approximately 25%, or roughly 110 positions.
- End-2023 workforce: 274, including approximately 150 in Argentina and 47 in Uruguay.
- Second-quarter 2024 reduction: a further 104 full-time equivalents, with approximately US$2 million in severance charges.
- 2024 revenue: **US$12.9 million**.
- 2024 year-end cash: **US$22.5 million**.
- 2024 operating cash use: approximately **US$35.9 million**.
- 2024 accounts expressed substantial doubt about ability to continue as a going concern because of recurring losses and limited liquidity.

The company also terminated its Netherlands high-throughput plant lease and narrowed strategy toward US government, national-security, and space-systems opportunities.

Satellogic discontinued its British Virgin Islands domicile and became a **Delaware corporation in March 2025**, following filings and shareholder procedures initiated earlier. Descriptions of a “2024 move” refer to the planning and authorization process; the legal continuance became effective in 2025.

Kargieman’s disclosed 2025 compensation terms included a base salary of **€442,130**, eligibility for a US$225,000 target cash bonus, and potential annual restricted-stock-unit grants with a US$1.5 million target value. Eligibility did not guarantee payment or vesting.

## Sur Energy and Stargate Argentina

## Founders and network

Sur Energy was co-founded by:

- **Emiliano Kargieman**
- **Matías Travizano**
- **Stan Chudnovsky**

Travizano was an Argentine data entrepreneur associated with GranData and an earlier security background. Reports say he died in 2025. The exact date and circumstances were not established from a primary family or civil record in the sources consulted, so the death should be treated as reported fact.

Chudnovsky is a technology executive who worked at Facebook/Meta, including as head of Facebook Messenger, and had a long-standing relationship with Kargieman and Travizano.

Sur Energy describes itself as the energy and infrastructure developer for Stargate Argentina.

## October 2025 announcement

On **10 October 2025**, Sur Energy and OpenAI announced a **letter of intent** to explore and develop **Stargate Argentina**, a data-center project in Patagonia with capacity of up to **500 megawatts** and an investment value of up to **US$25 billion at full scale**.

The announcement was followed by an OpenAI country-opportunity statement on 14 October.

The legal and financial distinctions are central:

- **Signed:** a non-final letter of intent between OpenAI and Sur Energy.
- **Announced:** an intended project, maximum capacity, maximum full-scale investment, and proposed use of Argentina’s RIGI incentive regime.
- **Not demonstrated by the announcement:** a final engineering, procurement, and construction contract; financial close; committed US$25 billion funding; land acquisition; environmental permit; water permit; grid interconnection; completed RIGI approval; or construction start.
- **OpenAI’s contemplated role:** anchor customer or buyer of computing capacity, rather than necessarily the owner or sole financier.
- **Sur Energy’s contemplated role:** developer assembling energy, infrastructure, capital, and partners.

In December 2025 Kargieman said an initial approximately **US$7 billion** construction phase was under discussion and that roughly half of construction expenditure could remain in Argentina. He emphasized that a completed data center would employ relatively few permanent workers—approximately one hundred in his formulation—and described its national value primarily as an exporter of computing services generating dollar income.

## Site and energy supply

The public LOI says **Patagonia** but does not establish a final parcel. Reporting has associated the search with **Río Negro** and energy corridors linked to Vaca Muerta gas, grid power, and renewable generation.

Kargieman said the intended system would combine renewables with dispatchable generation, potentially natural gas, because AI infrastructure requires continuous power. Statements describing the project as entirely renewable are therefore premature unless a final energy design proves otherwise.

As of the research cutoff:

- a final Río Negro site was not publicly documented;
- no verified land title or lease was located;
- no final natural-gas agreement was located;
- no final water-supply design was located;
- no environmental-impact assessment was located;
- no construction commencement was independently confirmed.

## RIGI

The project was announced for possible structuring under Argentina’s **Régimen de Incentivo para Grandes Inversiones (RIGI)**, created under Javier Milei’s government. RIGI supplies long-term tax, customs, foreign-exchange, and regulatory stability for qualifying large investments.

**Documented political connection:** Sur Energy and OpenAI worked with Milei-government officials around the announcement, and the government promoted the plan as evidence of international technology investment.

**Unresolved:** whether and when a formal RIGI application was filed or approved and what entity would be the applicant.

## Intellectual and Political Development

Kargieman’s recurring intellectual position is technological autonomy: users and builders should inspect, alter, and repurpose systems rather than accept prescribed functions.

His public-economic views include:

- technology businesses can be built globally from Argentina;
- public science should translate more research into economic value;
- Argentina underproduces technically trained people;
- public education is formally open but practically difficult for poorer students to complete;
- space data should become inexpensive and widely available;
- AI infrastructure can function as an export industry.

He participated in a large WhatsApp group of Argentine businesspeople described in reporting and academic analysis as the informal **“círculo rojo.”** Members coordinated public statements or hashtags about political and economic issues. Membership indicates participation in an elite business network; it does not by itself establish adherence to every position circulated there.

No political party membership or elected office was established.

## Autonomous Legal Entities, 2026

In April 2026 Kargieman published **“Autonomous Legal Entities: A Polycentric Framework for Machine Agency.”** It proposed legal structures through which autonomous AI systems could operate while remaining constrained by governance mechanisms such as:

- human boards or accountable committees;
- mandatory insurance;
- capital requirements;
- graded sanctions;
- distributed “kill switches”;
- competition among jurisdictions.

The framework used **Elinor Ostrom’s** polycentric-governance ideas and the **katechon** concept associated with Carl Schmitt. Kargieman also discussed Peter Thiel’s lectures about the Antichrist and concentrated sovereignty, treating Thiel’s formulation as a problem to answer with distributed governance rather than adopting a single global sovereign.

Javier Milei’s government later proposed legislation concerning companies operated by AI agents. Kargieman said his involvement was **“purely intellectual, not political.”** He discussed the subject with Minister Federico Sturzenegger and deputy Sabrina Ajmechet and commented on a preliminary draft. Government sources and reporting acknowledge inspiration from his ideas, but his paper and the bill are not identical.

The proposal produced a public argument involving Milei, Yuval Noah Harari, and Microsoft AI executive Mustafa Suleyman about autonomous companies and human control. No personal meeting between Kargieman and Harari was established.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

## Unauthorized hacking and the hacker-to-vendor transition

**Documented admission:** Kargieman acknowledges entering bank, university, and telecommunications systems without authorization as a teenager.

**His account:** the activity was exploratory; HBO did not steal or destroy information.

**Unresolved:** no comprehensive log or independent inquiry establishes every target or consequence.

Later critics of the security industry argued that companies such as Core converted hacker-developed intrusion practices into expensive corporate and government products. The factual basis is that Core employed former hackers and commercialized real exploits. The broader claim—that this was hypocrisy or a privatization of underground knowledge—is interpretive, not a legal finding.

No conviction or formal criminal charge against Kargieman was found.

## Argentine satellite procurement, 2019

**Allegation:** CONAE tender specifications and an eight-year term were designed to favor Satellogic.

**Evidence supporting concern:** unusual term length, specifications matching Satellogic’s imagery, internal criticism reported from CONAE, and Kargieman’s access to Macri-administration officials.

**Evidence limiting the claim:** the procurement was public; another bidder reportedly participated; no judicial finding, indictment, or proof of Kargieman’s authorship of the tender was located.

**Status:** unresolved procurement-favoritism controversy.

## SPAC disclosures and SEC action

On **12 December 2024**, the US Securities and Exchange Commission charged **Cantor Fitzgerald**, not Kargieman or Satellogic, with causing CF Acquisition Corp. V and another Cantor SPAC to make misleading pre-IPO disclosures.

The SEC found that Cantor personnel had already held substantive discussions with potential targets—including Satellogic—before CF V’s IPO, contrary to statements that the SPAC had not begun such discussions. Cantor agreed to pay **US$6.75 million** without admitting or denying the findings.

Important distinctions:

- **Court or agency finding:** the SEC order found securities-law violations by Cantor.
- **Not charged in that order:** Kargieman and Satellogic.
- **Relevance to Kargieman:** Satellogic was the eventual CF V target and participated in the pre-IPO discussions described.
- **Disputed regulatory interpretation:** SEC commissioner Mark Uyeda dissented, questioning materiality and the theory applied.

## Growth projections, losses, and going-concern warning

Satellogic did not reach the constellation scale, revenue, or Earth-remapping cadence projected in its 2021 SPAC materials. It later laid off hundreds of employees and disclosed substantial doubt about continuing as a going concern.

These are documented financial and execution problems. They do not alone demonstrate intentional deception. No adjudicated securities-fraud judgment against Kargieman was found in the consulted record.

## Military, intelligence, China, and Palantir connections

Satellogic received Chinese investment from Tencent, used Chinese launch vehicles, entered a large imagery arrangement with China’s ABDAS, partnered with Palantir, and later prioritized US and allied national-security customers.

**Documented fact:** these are commercial, financing, launch, or data relationships.

**Allegation/theory:** critics describe the pattern as integration with a transnational surveillance or intelligence architecture.

**Not established:** that Kargieman is an intelligence officer or agent; that Tencent directed Satellogic operations; or that Palantir controlled the company.

The shift from earlier reluctance to foreground defense applications toward explicit national-security business is real and documented. Its political meaning remains contested.

## Stargate financing

The repeated phrase “OpenAI will invest US$25 billion” overstates the October 2025 instrument.

**Documented:** up to US$25 billion was the estimated full-scale project value; a letter of intent was signed.

**Not documented:** OpenAI committing that sum from its balance sheet, financial close, or binding lender commitments.

Kargieman has described a structure in which Sur Energy and partners finance and build infrastructure while OpenAI purchases capacity. Until definitive contracts and financing disclosures appear, the payer, equity shares, debt structure, guarantees, and risk allocation remain unresolved.

## Energy, water, and environmental effects

Environmental critics argue that a 500 MW data center would:

- require power comparable to a major industrial complex;
- compete with other grid users;
- stimulate gas generation or other new infrastructure;
- consume or evaporate large volumes of water if water-cooled;
- receive extensive tax benefits through RIGI.

These are reasonable project questions but not quantified facts without a selected site and engineering design.

Kargieman’s response is that Patagonia combines energy availability, a cool climate, land, renewable resources, and access to gas for firm supply. No completed environmental-impact study was located.

## Legacy and Influence

Kargieman’s historical significance rests on four overlapping trajectories:

1. **Argentine hacker culture:** He is one of the few figures from the early Buenos Aires BBS underground whose career can be followed into major international companies.
2. **Commercial offensive security:** Core helped institutionalize public vulnerability research and automated penetration testing.
3. **Latin American private space development:** Satellogic demonstrated that satellite design, software, operations, and serial manufacturing could be distributed across Argentina, Uruguay, Spain, Israel, and global launch markets.
4. **AI infrastructure and legal theory:** Sur Energy and his autonomous-entity paper moved him from specialized aerospace entrepreneurship into national policy disputes over energy, capital, corporate law, AI autonomy, and state incentives.

His reputation contains competing images:

- hacker turned security professional;
- public-education success story;
- beneficiary and critic of the Argentine state;
- regional deep-technology builder;
- overoptimistic SPAC promoter;
- surveillance-and-defense contractor;
- advocate of decentralized AI governance;
- intermediary between Milei’s Argentina and Silicon Valley capital;
- “Argentina’s Elon Musk.”

None alone reconstructs the whole career.
