# Charles Barbier de la Serre: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

Charles Barbier left no known memoir and has never received a full conventional book-length biography based on all surviving records. His life is reconstructed principally from:

- his surviving publications;
- his military file, preserved in copy at the Musée Louis-Braille;
- his 1792 letter to George Washington;
- Kentucky newspapers and archival property papers;
- proceedings of the Académie des sciences;
- records and correspondence of the Institution royale des jeunes aveugles;
- the Barbier papers at the Association Valentin Haüy;
- contemporary institutional reports and journals;
- early histories of the Paris school for blind students;
- Philippa Campsie’s research in the Barbier and Institut archives.

The richest modern reconstruction is Campsie’s 2021 study, “Charles Barbier: A Hidden Story,” supplemented by her later article on Barbier’s First Empire inventions. Many older accounts derive from histories of Louis Braille and contain stories that cannot be reconciled with Barbier’s publications or correspondence.

Evidence labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a primary document or archival record.
- **Reported fact:** stated in serious secondary research but not independently verified here from the underlying document.
- **Disputed:** credible sources give materially different accounts.
- **Unverified claim:** repeated but without adequate documentary support.
- **Myth or legend:** a story demonstrably created or substantially elaborated after the events.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name:** Nicolas-Marie-Charles Barbier de la Serre.

Variants appearing in sources include:

- Nicolas-Marie-Charles Barbier de La Serre;
- Nicolas-Charles-Marie Barbier de La Serre;
- Nicolas Marie Charles Barbier de la Serre;
- Charles Barbier;
- C. Barbier;
- “Captain Barbier” or *capitaine Barbier* in later literature.

During his American residence he normally called himself simply **Charles Barbier**. His brother Guillaume-Arsène similarly anglicized his name as William Delaserre. Barbier generally reserved his full name for legal documents.

**Born:** 18 May 1767, Valenciennes, Hainaut/French Flanders, Kingdom of France.

**Died:** probably 22 April 1841, Paris, aged 73.

The date is disputed in accessible sources:

- the Musée Louis-Braille and modern specialist histories give **22 April 1841**;
- the present inscription on his Père-Lachaise tomb and several cemetery sources give **29 April 1841**.

The conflict probably reflects an error in either transcription or the later inscription. Without direct inspection of the Paris civil record, 22 April is the stronger specialist date, but 29 April cannot be silently discarded.

**Burial:** Père-Lachaise Cemetery, Paris, division 53.

**Nationality:** French.

**Status and occupations:**

- artillery officer, 1784–1792;
- political émigré or refugee in the United States;
- teacher of French, geography, mathematics, and surveying;
- surveyor;
- land or property owner and later *rentier*;
- inventor of shorthand, alternative alphabets, cryptographic systems, tactile writing, writing tools, and elementary-education methods;
- independent author and educational reform advocate.

**Principal historical importance:** Barbier was the first documented inventor to propose a coded system of raised points specifically enabling blind people both to read and to write. Louis Braille adopted Barbier’s raised-point principle and basic writing tools but replaced his large coordinate signs with the compact six-dot cell that became braille.

**Terms incorrectly or ambiguously attached to him:**

- **Écriture ponctuée** (“point writing”) is Barbier’s own principal term.
- **Écriture nocturne** (“night writing”) was used occasionally but was not his normal name for the system.
- **Sonographie** became common in later histories for Barbier’s phonetic version, but no known publication by Barbier uses that word.
- The familiar description “Napoleon’s night-writing code” is historically misleading.

No verified pseudonym, nickname, hereditary title used in public life, major state office, or membership in a religious order has been identified.

## Family and Ancestry

### The Barbier de La Serre family

The Barbier de La Serre family was an old office-holding family associated with Agenais and later northern France. Genealogical authorities trace it to Jean Barbier, an advocate in the Parlement of Paris who settled in Agenais and acquired the estate or designation “La Serre.” His son Claude Barbier, also an advocate, became a royal councillor in the Cour des aides of Guyenne at Agen. Long tenure in that office conferred hereditary noble status.

The family’s traditional arms are described as:

> Azure, three flames Or, accompanied in base by a star Argent.

This genealogical background should not be confused with evidence about Charles’s personal wealth or political convictions. By his birth the family belonged to the provincial service elite, but his father’s occupation also tied it directly to the fiscal administration of the monarchy.

### Parents

**Father:** Louis-Joseph Barbier de la Serre, described in institutional histories as a prosperous *fermier* or controller of the *Fermes du Roi*—the privately administered tax and customs system of the Bourbon monarchy.

**Mother:** Marie-Ernestine Polchet.

Charles was reportedly the sixth of eight children who survived infancy. The surviving reconstruction identifies five brothers, but a complete verified birth-order list has not been located in the sources consulted.

### Known or reported siblings

- **Alexis-Joseph Barbier de La Serre** (1764–1826), elder brother, naval officer and later rear admiral. He served during the American War of Independence, emigrated during the Revolution, served in British and Portuguese contexts, returned under the Restoration, and eventually directed the Royal Naval College at Angoulême.
- **Guillaume-Arsène Barbier de la Serre**, who served in the Saintonge Regiment, fought alongside the American revolutionary forces, settled at Baltimore, and became known as **William Delaserre**. Charles initially stayed with him and his American wife after leaving France.
- Two brothers reportedly followed their father into public service.
- One brother reportedly became a blacksmith.
- Another brother joined the navy; this may refer to Alexis-Joseph.
- Another joined the Saintonge Regiment; this was Guillaume-Arsène.

The occupational diversity reported among the brothers suggests that not all sons remained within the same fiscal or noble-professional path.

### Marriage, partners, and descendants

No spouse, partner, or child is identified in the surviving specialist literature or archival summaries consulted. Barbier’s correspondence and census descriptions present him as an independently supported man rather than the head of a documented household. It would be unsafe to state categorically that he never married or had children, but no evidence of either has surfaced in the sources used here.

No reliable information was found concerning intimate relationships, illegitimate children, inheritance disputes, or a personal family household in Paris or Versailles.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1767–1784

Barbier was born in Valenciennes on 18 May 1767. Valenciennes was a fortified commercial city near the northern frontier, culturally connected to both French Flanders and Hainaut. His father’s position in the royal tax system placed the family within the administrative structures of the ancien régime.

Nothing detailed is presently known about Charles’s earliest schooling, childhood health, religious formation, or relations with individual siblings. His later technical interests imply substantial training in mathematics, geometry, drawing, and written French, but the surviving record does not name childhood tutors.

His father arranged his entry into the artillery establishment. Artillery was among the more technically demanding branches of the French military and required mathematics, fortification, mechanics, surveying, and map work. This training later enabled Barbier to advertise himself in America as a teacher of mathematics and surveying.

## Education and Military Formation, 1784–1792

### Douai artillery school

**Documented fact:** In 1784, aged about seventeen, Barbier entered the military or artillery school at Douai.

His 1792 letter to George Washington explicitly cited:

- his attendance at the military school at Douai;
- eight years’ experience in French artillery;
- knowledge of fortification;
- experience concerning the movement of convoys.

These claims were made in a request for American employment and are consistent with the surviving military chronology.

The identities of his instructors have not been established. The formative intellectual influences visible in his later work are institutional rather than personal:

- artillery mathematics and geometry;
- surveying;
- fortification;
- tabular and coordinate systems;
- the military need to communicate without ordinary writing materials;
- Enlightenment projects for rational or universal language;
- contemporary shorthand, phonetic writing, and cryptography.

### Rank and resignation

Barbier served from 1784 until 1792. Although later writers routinely call him “Captain Barbier,” the title exaggerates the length of his command experience.

**Reported from the military record:** He attained the rank of captain in 1792, held it for precisely two days, then resigned his commission. He was twenty-five.

The immediate motives are imperfectly documented. The Revolution had transformed the officer corps and placed men with noble or royal-administrative associations under political pressure. Barbier’s departure is consequently described as emigration or political flight. No evidence was found that he was imprisoned, formally prosecuted, convicted, or personally accused of treason before leaving.

There is likewise no evidence that he served in Napoleon’s armies, took part in the Napoleonic Wars, or experienced the battlefield episode later credited with inspiring “night writing.”

## Emigration to the United States, 1792–ca. 1802

### Baltimore and the letter to George Washington

Barbier reached Baltimore by November 1792 and stayed initially with Guillaume-Arsène/William Delaserre and his American sister-in-law.

On 5 November 1792 he wrote in French from Baltimore to President **George Washington**. The surviving autograph letter, now in the Washington Papers, is the most important primary document for his early life.

Barbier:

- said that he had decided to leave France;
- sought employment in the military service of the United States;
- invoked his Douai education and eight years in the artillery;
- claimed expertise in fortification and convoys;
- admitted that he had forgotten much of the English he once knew;
- promised to work diligently to relearn it.

No documented reply or appointment followed. The application therefore appears to have failed. It nevertheless establishes that Barbier initially attempted to continue a military or engineering career rather than immediately devoting himself to writing systems.

### Lexington, Kentucky

After several months in Baltimore, Barbier moved west to Lexington, Kentucky, where a French immigrant community was developing.

Kentucky Gazette advertisements from 1795–1798 show “Citizen Charles Barbier” seeking pupils or employment. His advertised teaching evolved:

- first French;
- later geography;
- mathematics;
- especially the mathematics required for surveying.

A local history of early Lexington education records that “Citizen Charles Barbier” arrived in 1795 intending to establish a French school connected with Transylvania Seminary. The exact institutional outcome is unclear; there is no strong evidence that he obtained a permanent professorship.

### Surveying and property

Surveyors were in heavy demand as settler claims and speculative land transactions multiplied. Barbier’s artillery education gave him commercially useful geometry and field-surveying skills.

**Reported fact:** He found work as a surveyor and by 1800 had acquired property of his own in Kentucky.

Three documents in the Kentucky Historical Society’s Alexander Family Papers reportedly show Barbier arranging his affairs before an extended absence in 1800. Robert Alexander was a prominent Kentucky landowner. The documents indicate that:

- Barbier possessed property;
- he expected to leave Kentucky;
- he was uncertain whether he would return;
- he made legal arrangements to protect or administer his interests.

The exact acreage, purchase price, property location, partners, income, and eventual disposition have not been published in the sources consulted. Claims that he became wealthy should therefore be treated cautiously. What can be said is that he accumulated enough American property income to be described later in France as a *rentier*.

### Pittsburgh and disappearance from the American record

A notice cited in a nineteenth-century history places or advertises Barbier in Pittsburgh in 1801. No later American residence has been securely located.

His precise itinerary between Pittsburgh and France is unknown. It is not established whether he returned through Baltimore, traveled elsewhere in the United States, or visited the Caribbean.

### Claims about Indigenous writing systems

Some popular biographies say that Barbier became fascinated in America by Indigenous or “Native American” systems of communication. No primary evidence supporting this claim was located. His surviving work can be explained more directly through European shorthand, phonetics, cryptography, military field writing, and Enlightenment language reform.

**Evidentiary status:** unverified claim.

## Return to France and Independent Means, ca. 1802–1808

Napoleon Bonaparte proclaimed a general amnesty for most Revolutionary émigrés in April 1802. Barbier returned to France at approximately this time, although no exact embarkation or arrival date is known.

He may have visited Valenciennes, but settled principally in Paris. He did not rejoin the army and has not been identified as a salaried official, engineer, or schoolmaster in France.

A Versailles census in the 1820s later described him as a **rentier**, meaning a person supported by income from investments or property. Campsie concludes that income from his American properties allowed him to live independently and devote himself to experiments. The value and legal structure of those assets remain unknown.

No evidence has been found that:

- he created a commercial company;
- employed a factory workforce;
- raised outside investment;
- sold a large number of licenses;
- obtained state patronage sufficient to support his projects;
- became financially distressed.

He appears instead to have self-financed publications, devices, correspondence campaigns, and donations of equipment.

## Early Career in Language Reform, 1808–1814

### Intellectual context

Barbier entered a crowded field. Revolutionary and Napoleonic France had heightened demand for:

- accurate reporting of speeches and legislative debates;
- fast commercial and administrative writing;
- secret correspondence;
- universal languages;
- phonetic spelling;
- literacy methods less dependent on years of penmanship.

He explicitly compared his work with:

- **Tachygraphie**, associated in France with Jean-Félicité Coulon de Thévenot;
- **Sténographie**, including Théodore-Pierre Bertin’s adaptation of Samuel Taylor;
- **Okygraphie**, Honoré Blanc’s phonetic system;
- **Pasigraphie**, Joseph de Maimieux’s proposed universal written language.

Joseph de Maimieux wrote an endorsement included in Barbier’s 1809 publication. This is the clearest identifiable early intellectual association.

### Tableau d’expédiographie, 1808

In April 1808, *Le Télégraphe littéraire ou le Correspondant de la librairie* reported a single-sheet **Tableau d’expédiographie**. This is the earliest documented publication under Barbier’s name.

The sheet itself has not been fully described in the sources consulted, and no secure surviving copy was independently located. Barbier later altered the name from *expédiographie* to *expéditive*, reportedly after receiving advice from readers.

### Principes d’expéditive française, 1809

In 1809 Gillé fils printed:

*Principes d’expéditive française pour écrire aussi vite que la parole*, described as a substantially enlarged second edition.

The system used six basic marks:

- straight line;
- curved line;
- circle;
- oval;
- dot;
- tilde-like stroke.

Ninety rules addressed:

- simple and compound vowels;
- diphthongs;
- nasal sounds;
- consonants;
- meaningful accents;
- plurals;
- common endings;
- numerals;
- distinctions between homophones.

Barbier promised speed and joined writing, but the rules were numerous and the graphic system was not demonstrably easier than competitors. He offered private lessons. No evidence shows widespread adoption.

### Écriture coupée

The last twenty pages introduced the more original **écriture coupée**, “cut writing.” A traveler or officer lacking pen, pencil, or ink could make wedge-shaped cuts in folded paper using a knife.

Its construction used:

- large and small wedges;
- eight possible orientations;
- positions above, touching, below, or crossing a folded reference line;
- approximately forty to fifty possible signs;
- phonetic simplifications;
- numerals and limited punctuation.

Barbier recommended this method to military officers in the field and travelers. This genuine military application of cut marks later helped generate confusion with his entirely different raised-dot system for blind readers.

### Patent and government submissions

A nineteenth-century British patent-office catalogue lists Barbier’s shorthand under patent no. 9029. The exact jurisdiction, claims, date, commercial rights, and financial return require further examination of the patent itself.

In October 1809 the French Ministry of the Interior acknowledged receiving his work and promised to compare it with tachygraphy and stenography. The Police Générale also responded. Neither exchange produced documented adoption.

On 4 October 1809, *La Gazette nationale ou le Moniteur universel* published an extensive review signed only “T.” The reviewer treated *écriture coupée* as original and potentially useful when ordinary writing materials were unavailable.

### Commercial cards and advertisements, 1809–1810

Advertisements in the *Journal de Paris* and *Gazette de France* offered three small products:

1. *Alphabet d’écriture facile à l’usage des personnes qui savent lire et pas écrire*;
2. *Alphabet d’écriture coupée pour prendre des notes à la fois sans plume ni crayon*;
3. *Alphabet d’écriture coupée et le Calendrier du militaire et du voyageur pour l’année 1810*.

They were sold as cards at 60 centimes per card. A June 1810 advertisement offered an explanatory plate with the *Expéditive*.

No sales figures survive. Barbier evidently recognized that the cut-writing idea attracted more notice than his complicated shorthand.

## The 1815 Synthesis: Twelve Alternative Writing Methods

### Publication circumstances

On 30 June 1815—twelve days after Waterloo and nine days after Napoleon’s abdication—Barbier dated the preliminary notice to his self-published:

*Essai sur divers procédés d’expéditive française, contenant douze écritures différentes, avec une planche pour chaque procédé.*

He said printing had been rushed but did not explain why. The timing may have reflected political uncertainty, but any specific motive is unknown.

Despite the word *expéditive*, this was not simply a revised shorthand manual. It proposed a general architecture for alternative writing.

### Philosophy of language and education

Barbier argued that ordinary alphabets were defective because one sign could represent several sounds and one sound could require several letters. Conventional writing also demanded years of motor training unavailable to laborers, artisans, and rural people.

His objectives were consequently broader than military communication:

- phonetic regularity;
- speed;
- communication without conventional penmanship;
- literacy for working people;
- communication for deaf people;
- reading and writing for blind people;
- secret correspondence for diplomats and private correspondents.

The underlying philosophy was utilitarian and educational: “useful things” should be simplified so that literacy would no longer be restricted by time, disability, occupation, or manual dexterity.

No developed party-political program or religious doctrine appears in the work.

### Coordinate principle

Barbier organized letters or speech sounds into grids. A symbol could be identified by two coordinates:

- a row;
- a column.

He used:

- a conventional alphabet in a 5×5 grid;
- a phonetic or abbreviated alphabet, initially in a 5×6 grid;
- pairs of numbers, shapes, finger positions, strokes, dots, or musical-note-like symbols to represent coordinates.

The system omitted or conflated certain letters and sounds. The French alphabet of the period did not normally include W, despite Barbier’s northern background.

### Methods for deaf people and secret correspondence

One plate proposed two-handed finger communication:

- a finger of the left hand represented the row;
- a finger of the right represented the column;
- bringing the two together identified a letter or sound.

The requirement to use both hands made it less practical than established manual alphabets.

Later plates converted coordinates into disguised writing. Marks could appear as:

- dots on lines or spaces;
- joined strokes;
- apparent musical notation;
- rearranged numerical coordinates.

These were proposed for confidential correspondence. No evidence shows governmental, diplomatic, intelligence, or criminal adoption.

### Planche VII: raised-point writing

Plate VII used vertical groups of raised dots. A phonetic table eventually contained thirty-six sounds in a 6×6 arrangement. Each sound was encoded by:

- up to six dots in one column for the row;
- up to six in the other for the column.

A sign could therefore be twelve dots high.

Barbier explicitly wrote that the system was for people blind from birth and other blind people who could not trace ordinary letters. It was to be made without ink or pencil using a metal implement that impressed regular, tactile points.

This passage is decisive. The raised-dot method was not an accidental military by-product: Barbier deliberately designed it for blind users in 1815.

### Limitations

Barbier’s initial point system lacked:

- capitals;
- adequate punctuation;
- numerals;
- mathematical notation;
- musical notation;
- conventional spelling when used in its purely phonetic form.

The twelve-dot-height signs were too large to be perceived instantly under one fingertip. Readers had to move down each sign before proceeding horizontally.

Its coordinate construction also used only a small portion of the theoretical combinations available from twelve independently variable dots.

## Scientific and Institutional Evaluation, 1819–1821

### Expéditeur

Barbier built a mechanical device called the **Expéditeur**, with a wooden frame and metal mechanism for producing one of his alternative scripts. Only one is known, preserved at the Association Valentin Haüy museum.

The machine was ingenious but overly complicated and did not become a commercial product.

### Académie des sciences

On 28 June 1819 Barbier demonstrated the Expéditeur before the Académie des sciences.

A commission consisted of:

- Claude-Pierre Molard, engineer and machine specialist;
- Abraham-Louis Breguet, watchmaker and instrument maker;
- Gaspard-Clair-François-Marie Riche de Prony, engineer and mathematician.

Its May 1820 report paid particular attention to finger communication and cryptographic methods. The commissioners saw potential in Barbier’s general principles but considered the Expéditeur unnecessarily complex.

Barbier subsequently advertised the report as an academic endorsement. The description was not wholly false, but it overstated the commission’s cautious and qualified judgment.

### Société d’Encouragement pour l’Industrie Nationale

Barbier exhibited his device and writing methods at the Louvre in 1819 under the auspices of the Société d’Encouragement pour l’Industrie Nationale.

The *Annales de l’Industrie* published a seventeen-page account in 1821. Editor Jean-Gabriel-Victor de Moléon apparently reorganized some of Barbier’s difficult exposition into a clearer table.

### Earlier approach to the blind school

Pierre Henri later suggested that Barbier discovered the needs of blind pupils after attending an 1819 demonstration. Archival research instead indicates that Barbier had already designed point writing for them in 1815 and approached the school soon afterward.

The school’s earlier director did not adopt it. Accessible accounts differ over whether the rejection came from Sébastien Guillié personally or merely under the earlier administration. The decisive trial occurred only after Alexandre-René Pignier became director.

## Adoption at the Institution Royale des Jeunes Aveugles, 1821

Barbier wrote to Pignier in early 1821 and supplied equipment.

On 28 April 1821, two students demonstrated the method to the school’s governing council:

1. one student left the room;
2. another took dictation in Barbier’s point writing;
3. the first returned and read what had been written.

The demonstration succeeded. The school adopted point writing as an ancillary skill.

It did not immediately replace Valentin Haüy’s embossed Roman type for reading or framed pencil writing. Its advantage was different: blind students could now independently produce text, reread it, and exchange notes.

Barbier donated dozens and possibly hundreds of sets of tools, along with equipment for making more. The apparatus included:

- a grooved wooden ruler or *réglette*;
- a movable metal guide, *coulisse* or *guide-main*;
- a blunt punch or *poinçon*.

Dots were punched from right to left; the paper was turned over and read from left to right. This procedure remains fundamental to slate-and-stylus braille writing.

Students reportedly learned the method quickly and received it enthusiastically. They probably used both Barbier’s phonetic table and a conventional alphabetic version.

## Louis Braille and the Transformation of Point Writing

### Indirect influence, 1821–1829

Louis Braille entered the institution in 1819 and was twelve when point writing arrived. He learned it with his classmates.

Between his mid-teens and 1829, Braille retained:

- raised points;
- writing from the reverse of the sheet;
- ruler/slate and stylus;
- systematic dot patterns.

He changed the method fundamentally by:

- reducing the cell to two columns of three points;
- assigning patterns directly rather than as two long coordinate numbers;
- representing conventional orthography;
- adding punctuation;
- developing mathematical and musical uses;
- making each sign small enough to perceive under one fingertip.

### Braille’s acknowledgment

In the preface to his 1829 *Procédé pour écrire les paroles, la musique et le plain-chant au moyen de points*, Braille explicitly credited the “ingenious procedure” invented by Barbier especially for blind people. He explained that a new system was necessary because Barbier’s signs occupied too much space, provided too few characters, and could not adequately represent ordinary writing, music, and plainsong.

This is direct contemporary evidence of influence and of the differences between the systems.

### Did they meet in the 1820s?

No surviving evidence shows an early meeting. Pignier’s 1859 biography said that the young Braille identified improvements and solved difficulties that had troubled Barbier. Later writers converted this into a face-to-face confrontation.

Barbier’s 1833 letter asking Pignier for Braille’s publication states that he did not know the author. This makes an earlier personal meeting highly unlikely.

The best-supported sequence is:

- Braille learned Barbier’s method at school in 1821;
- he developed his system independently within the school;
- he published in 1829;
- Barbier first learned his identity and work in 1833;
- they corresponded and met only afterward.

### Their actual relationship, 1833–1841

Barbier requested Braille’s book from Pignier in 1833, praising its unknown author for working for his companions’ welfare. After reading it, he wrote directly to Braille.

In a letter of 31 March 1833, Barbier praised the sentiments that moved Braille to help those who shared his condition and said much could be expected from the wisdom guiding him.

The Institut National des Jeunes Aveugles preserves:

- five letters from Barbier to Braille;
- at least one from Braille to Barbier.

Their tone is cordial and mutually respectful. They met in person later in 1833.

The Association Valentin Haüy states that they cooperated on early tools for writing Braille’s raised points, derived from Barbier’s apparatus. The detailed division of labor in those tool modifications has not been fully published.

The documentary record does not support a personal rivalry, quarrel, lawsuit, priority dispute, or attempt by Barbier to suppress Braille’s system.

## Later Educational Campaigns, 1820s–1841

### Residence at Versailles

A census places Barbier in Versailles for part of the 1820s and calls him a *rentier*. The exact address and duration are not specified in the accessible research. His principal institutional activity continued to center on Paris and Versailles.

### Continuing revisions

Barbier repeatedly altered his tables:

- changing the order of sounds;
- adding and removing rows;
- developing alphabetic and phonetic variants;
- simplifying tools;
- trying applications for blind and deaf students;
- proposing methods for people unable to master ordinary handwriting.

These revisions produced no second breakthrough comparable to the 1815 raised-point principle.

### Recognition and exhibition medal

Several institutional accounts report that Barbier received a bronze medal at the 1823 Versailles industrial exhibition for writing readable without sight or light. This is plausible and widely repeated, but the original award list was not independently examined for this dossier.

**Evidentiary status:** reported fact.

### Quinze-Vingts

The Hospice des Quinze-Vingts retained connections with the blind school. A small number of books were reportedly produced there using Barbier’s phonetic raised-point system.

Residents added signs for numerals and punctuation, showing that users altered the system to meet needs Barbier had not adequately supplied. An archival poster summarizes an expanded method but was not authored by Barbier.

### Émancipation intellectuelle, 1832

In 1832 Barbier published a brochure usually cited as:

*Émancipation intellectuelle d’expéditive française.*

It advocated the broader educational use of simplified writing and presented applications for blind people, military or hospital contexts, and people denied conventional schooling. Exact subtitle and collation vary in later references and should be checked against a surviving copy.

### Salles d’asile and working-class education

Barbier concentrated increasingly on *salles d’asile*, early nursery institutions for children aged approximately two to five, especially children of poor working families.

An 18 October 1832 letter from an asylum director shows both interest and practical difficulty. The director wanted to teach Barbier’s system to children and workers but was unsure which of Barbier’s many variants to use. He also described severe personal poverty and asked Barbier for money.

Barbier corresponded with:

- the Ministry of the Interior;
- the Ministry of Public Instruction;
- the Prefecture of the Seine;
- the General Council of Paris Civil Hospices;
- the Société pour l’enseignement élémentaire;
- the commission revising primary-school books;
- Denis Cochin, a promoter of French *salles d’asile*;
- Eugénie Millet, inspector-general of the institutions.

The administrations repeatedly referred him elsewhere or declined the proposals.

A response associated with Cochin argued that asylum teachers were practical mothers, not specialists able to master methods requiring extended study. This exposed the central problem in Barbier’s reform program: methods advertised as radically simple had proliferated into systems requiring explanation and training.

On 6 April 1836 the Royal Council of Public Instruction decided that his table for deaf-mute instruction and notice on *salles d’asile* should not be introduced. On 31 August 1836 Eugénie Millet also rejected or discouraged the proposal.

### Instruction familière des classes laborieuses, 1837

Barbier’s 1837 prospectus, *Instruction familière des classes laborieuses*, appealed for an official test among working-class pupils.

Trials or demonstrations were scheduled in March 1839 at the École normale primaire de Versailles. At the time of his death Barbier was reportedly awaiting results from demonstrations in three nursery schools.

No national system, durable classroom network, or state contract followed.

### Final years

Barbier continued promoting simplified education until his death. The record depicts persistence rather than commercial success. His non-tactile systems were not adopted on a meaningful scale.

No documented illness, disability, addiction, psychiatric crisis, accident, or loss of legal capacity has been identified. No cause of death is supplied by the consulted sources.

### Institutions

- French artillery school at Douai;
- French royal/revolutionary artillery;
- Transylvania Seminary or its Lexington educational circle;
- Académie des sciences;
- Société d’Encouragement pour l’Industrie Nationale;

### Technologies and inventions

1. Expédiographie shorthand.
2. Expéditive française.
3. Écriture coupée made with a knife.
4. Simplified pen-and-line writing derived from cut writing.
5. Coordinate alphabets.
6. Phonetic coordinate tables.
7. Two-handed finger communication.
8. Cryptographic line, dot, and musical-note-like scripts.
9. Écriture ponctuée in raised dots.
10. The grooved ruler, guide, and punch for producing raised points.
11. The Expéditeur mechanical writing device.
12. Simplified scripts for early-childhood and working-class education.

### Patent and proprietary interests

A catalogue identifies British patent no. 9029 for his shorthand. No evidence was found of:

- substantial licensing revenue;
- patent litigation;

- By 1800 he owned property in Kentucky.
- Later French census evidence called him a *rentier*.

- Precise wealth, annual income, debts, estate, will, Paris property, and beneficiaries remain unknown.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Political position

Barbier’s resignation and emigration place him among those alienated or endangered by the Revolution, but no manifesto establishes a consistent royalist ideology.

His activities show pragmatic engagement with successive regimes:

- he sought employment from republican America;
- returned after Napoleon’s émigré amnesty;
- submitted inventions to Napoleonic institutions;
- continued petitioning ministries under the Restoration and July Monarchy.

No evidence was found of elected office, party organization, revolutionary conspiracy, counterrevolutionary violence, intelligence work, or political imprisonment.

### Educational and social ideas

His writings repeatedly argued that conventional literacy excluded:

- rural laborers;
- urban artisans;
- people whose work left no time for years of schooling;
- those who could read but could not form letters;
- blind people;
- deaf people;
- very young children.

He did not call for abolition of conventional writing. He sought supplementary methods adapted to different users.

His approach mixed:

- Enlightenment rational-language projects;
- phonetic reform;
- technological solutionism;
- paternal or philanthropic working-class instruction;
- faith in commissions, demonstrations, and institutional validation.

### Religion

No reliable evidence identifies Barbier’s personal devotion, irreligion, conversion, church office, or denominational conflict. His references to plainsong arose primarily through Braille’s work, not a documented theology of his own.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Louis Braille

Indirect technical influence from 1821; correspondence and personal meeting from 1833; later mutual respect and reported cooperation on writing equipment. Braille publicly acknowledged Barbier’s invention while replacing its coding structure.

### Alexandre-René Pignier

Director of the Institution Royale des Jeunes Aveugles and Barbier’s principal institutional intermediary. Pignier arranged the 1821 trial, corresponded with Barbier, and later wrote the sentence that generated the supposed youthful confrontation with Braille.

### Guillaume-Arsène/William Delaserre

Brother, American Revolutionary veteran, and Baltimore host. His American settlement gave Charles an immediate refuge and social base in 1792.

### George Washington

Recipient of Barbier’s unsuccessful 1792 employment application. No reply or meeting is documented.

### Joseph de Maimieux

Inventor of pasigraphy who endorsed Barbier’s 1809 publication. An intellectual supporter rather than a documented long-term collaborator.

### Claude-Pierre Molard, Abraham-Louis Breguet, and Gaspard de Prony

Académie des sciences commissioners who assessed Barbier’s work in 1819–1820. Their response was cautiously favorable toward the principles and critical of the machine’s complexity.

### Jean-Gabriel-Victor de Moléon

Editor connected with the 1821 *Annales de l’Industrie* presentation. He appears to have helped reshape Barbier’s difficult explanatory materials into a clearer tableau.

### Denis Cochin and Eugénie Millet

Important figures in early-childhood education who rejected or discouraged adoption of Barbier’s later methods.

### Institutional users

The blind students who tested, adopted, and modified point writing were collaborators in practice even when most remain unnamed. Residents of the Quinze-Vingts expanded the code with numbers and punctuation.

No patron comparable to a court sponsor, industrial financier, or permanent political protector has been identified.

## Successes and Failures

### Significant successes

- Completed a technical artillery education.
- Reconstructed a civilian career after political emigration.
- Worked as a teacher and surveyor in frontier Kentucky.
- Acquired American property.
- Received attention from ministries, scientific bodies, and national-industry institutions.
- Invented the first documented coded raised-point writing system deliberately intended for blind users.
- Created practical tools allowing blind students to write and reread their own work.
- Secured adoption of point writing at the Paris institution in 1821.
- Supplied equipment at his own expense.
- Provided the technological and conceptual starting point for Braille’s system.
- Received public acknowledgment from Louis Braille.

### Significant failures or limitations

- Failed to obtain the American military position sought from Washington.
- His original shorthand was too complicated to displace established systems.
- The Expéditeur was judged unnecessarily elaborate.
- Two-handed finger communication was impractical.
- His phonetic point writing was bulky and incomplete.
- His continual revisions created uncertainty about which method users should learn.
- Government and educational authorities declined his nursery-school and deaf-education proposals.
- His non-tactile systems disappeared almost entirely.
- He did not live to see braille internationally standardized or his own role widely recognized.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### Myth 1: Barbier invented raised dots after soldiers were killed while reading orders by lamplight

**Story:** During a Napoleonic battle, soldiers revealed their position by lighting a lamp to read an order and were killed. Captain Barbier then devised tactile night orders.

**Origin:** No nineteenth-century source consulted contains this episode. Pierre Henri’s 1952 biography speculated that a former artillery officer “perhaps” imagined writing in darkness. Jean Roblin’s popular 1952 *The Reading Fingers* converted the speculation into a concrete story involving Louis XVIII’s army and commands such as “Advance” and “General withdrawal.” Lennard Bickel later added the slaughtered gun post.

**Evidence against:**

- Barbier left the army in 1792.
- He did not serve in Napoleon’s army or Louis XVIII’s restored army.
- His 1815 text explicitly says raised-point writing was for blind people.
- The genuine military method in his 1809 work used knife cuts, not raised dots.
- No battle report, military adoption record, or contemporary anecdote supports the episode.

**Status:** myth.

**Effect:** It made Barbier memorable as a military cryptographer but obscured his work in disability education and mass literacy.

### Myth 2: “Night writing” was a military code later repurposed for blind students

**Story:** Barbier designed a battlefield code; only later did someone discover that blind people could use it.

**Evidence:** Barbier’s 1815 publication directly identifies blind users and explains tactile reading. The method was presented to their school because it had been designed for that purpose.

**Status:** myth based partly on conflating *écriture coupée* with *écriture ponctuée*.

### Myth 3: Barbier demonstrated his invention personally to twelve-year-old Braille

**Story:** The uniformed captain visited the classroom in 1821 and taught the method directly.

**Evidence:** Archival records show Barbier corresponded with Pignier and supplied equipment. The school conducted the trial. Barbier’s 1833 letter indicates that he did not yet know Braille or his identity as the author of the 1829 method.

**Status:** unsupported dramatization; no early personal meeting is documented.

### Myth 4: Young Braille confronted an arrogant Barbier, who rejected his improvements

**Story:** Braille immediately saw the flaws; Barbier became angry and refused to listen.

**Origin:** Pignier’s 1859 statement that Braille suggested improvements was elaborated in later children’s books, films, and biographies.

**Evidence against:**

- no contemporary record of a confrontation;
- no evidence they met before 1833;
- surviving letters are friendly;
- Barbier praised Braille’s work;
- Braille credited Barbier.

**Status:** myth or hostile dramatic legend.

**Effect:** It created a simple moral narrative of youthful genius defeating an inflexible military elder.

### Myth 5: Barbier called his system “sonography”

Later authors used *sonographie* to emphasize its phonetic operation. Barbier did not use this word in the publications examined by Campsie.

**Status:** retrospective terminology, not an authentic title.

### Myth 6: Braille merely shortened Barbier’s twelve dots to six

This understates Braille’s work. He did reduce sign height, but also abandoned coordinate encoding, expanded the character repertoire, supported ordinary spelling, and developed punctuation, mathematics, and music.

**Status:** misleading oversimplification.

### Myth 7: Barbier’s work was wholly unsuccessful

Most of his schemes failed, but point writing was successfully used by blind students and directly generated a durable technological lineage.

**Status:** misleading retrospective claim.

## Works by Charles Barbier

The following bibliography distinguishes known publications, small-format products, and uncertain or derivative titles. Barbier generally published under “Charles Barbier” or “C. Barbier,” not a pseudonym.

### Major publications

1. **Tableau d’expédiographie** (Paris, 1808).  
   Single-sheet table reported in *Le Télégraphe littéraire* on 25 April 1808. Earliest documented publication. Surviving-copy status not established here.

2. **Principes d’expéditive française pour écrire aussi vite que la parole** (Paris: Gillé fils, 1809).  
   Described as a substantially enlarged second edition. Contains shorthand rules, phonetic analysis, numerals, *écriture coupée*, and a simplified pen version.

3. **Essai sur divers procédés d’expéditive française, contenant douze écritures différentes, avec une planche pour chaque procédé** (Paris: published by the author, 1815).  
   Principal work. Dated 30 June 1815. Includes coordinate alphabets, finger communication, cryptographic scripts, and Plate VII’s raised-point method for blind people.

4. **Émancipation intellectuelle d’expéditive française** (Paris, 1832).  
   Brochure promoting simplified writing and broader educational emancipation. Exact full title and edition data require verification against the original.

5. **Notice sur les salles d’asile, le retour à la simplicité primitive de la théorie alphabétique, l’instruction familière des enfants du premier âge, des aveugles de naissance, des sourds-muets…** (Paris, 1834 or 1835).  
   Administrative references date a submission to 1834; the Académie’s printed proceedings list a Paris 1835 edition. These may represent submission and publication dates rather than separate editions.

6. **Instruction familière des classes laborieuses** (Paris: Bureau de la Société pour l’enseignement élémentaire, 1837).  
   Prospectus advocating experimental instruction among working-class pupils.

### Advertised cards and ephemera

7. **Alphabet d’écriture facile à l’usage des personnes qui savent lire et pas écrire** (advertised 1809–1810).

8. **Alphabet d’écriture coupée pour prendre des notes à la fois sans plume ni crayon** (advertised 1809–1810).

9. **Alphabet d’écriture coupée et le Calendrier du militaire et du voyageur pour l’année 1810** (1810).

10. Separate explanatory plate for *écriture coupée* and its application to pen or pencil (advertised June 1810).

### Manuscripts and correspondence

11. **Letter to George Washington**, Baltimore, 5 November 1792.  
    Autograph signed letter in French; published in *The Papers of George Washington*.

12. Correspondence with Alexandre-René Pignier, principally 1821–1830s.  
    Manuscripts in the archives of the Institut National des Jeunes Aveugles and Association Valentin Haüy.

13. Five surviving letters to Louis Braille, beginning in 1833.

14. Government submissions and correspondence with the Ministries of the Interior and Public Instruction, the Prefecture of the Seine, educational commissions, and *salles d’asile* personnel.

15. Tables, prospectuses, tool diagrams, and experimental alphabets in the Fonds Barbier, Association Valentin Haüy.

### Works not by Barbier but associated with his system

- The expanded Quinze-Vingts point-writing materials, including punctuation and numerals, were user adaptations.
- Archival posters summarizing his system were not necessarily authored by him.
- “Sonography” is a later label, not the title of a verified Barbier work.
- Braille’s 1829 and 1837 systems were independent publications acknowledging but substantially transforming Barbier’s technology.

No diaries, memoirs, literary works, paintings, films, musical compositions, recorded speeches, or published political manifestos by Barbier were identified.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Immediate recognition

In 1844 Joseph Guadet described Barbier as the first person to enter the genuinely creative path of giving blind people a tactile writing form designed for them rather than imitating sighted print. Guadet credited both:

- point coding;
- the practical writing tools.

This early institutional memory was relatively accurate.

### Eclipse by braille

Braille’s six-dot system superseded Barbier’s because it was:

- smaller;
- faster to perceive;
- orthographic rather than obligatorily phonetic;
- capable of punctuation, numbers, mathematics, and music;
- extensible to other languages.

Once braille was adopted, Barbier’s original code disappeared from ordinary instruction. His other scripts also failed to survive.

### Distortion

Twentieth-century biographies converted Barbier from a disability-education innovator into a Napoleonic battlefield cryptographer whose invention was accidentally repurposed. This produced several consequences:

- his explicit 1815 purpose was forgotten;
- his work with working-class children and deaf people disappeared;
- his American teaching and surveying career was marginalized;
- his cordial relationship with Braille became a generational conflict;
- Braille’s acknowledged debt was either overstated as mere adaptation or understated as unrelated invention.

### Reassessment

Archival work since the 2010s has restored a more differentiated account:

- Barbier invented the raised-point principle and its first dedicated tools.
- Blind students were active users and modifiers.
- Braille transformed the code’s structure and range.
- The two inventors were not rivals.
- The military origin story is modern mythology.

Barbier’s durable influence lies less in his exact alphabet than in three propositions:

1. tactile writing need not reproduce visible Roman letter shapes;
2. blind people should be able to write for themselves, not merely read embossed books made by sighted printers;
3. a simple slate and punch can encode reusable raised-point patterns.

Those propositions survive in braille and in the broader principle of designing writing around tactile perception.
