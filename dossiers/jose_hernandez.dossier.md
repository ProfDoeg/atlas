# José Hernández: research dossier

**Subject:** José Rafael Hernández (1834–1886), Argentine journalist, soldier, political operative, legislator, rural writer, bookseller, and author of *Martín Fierro*  
**Prepared:** 22 August 2026  
**Scope note:** This dossier separates reasonably documented biography from family testimony, later reconstruction, literary interpretation, and folklore. Nineteenth-century newspaper authorship is often anonymous or pseudonymous; attribution is therefore stated cautiously. Dates before the civil registration era sometimes derive from baptismal, family, or retrospective records. URLs actually consulted appear in the final section.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and lineages

- **Father:** Pascual Rafael Hernández (usually Rafael Hernández), associated with the federal political side and employed as manager or mayordomo on rural properties, including properties connected with Juan Manuel de Rosas in southern Buenos Aires Province. His work took the family toward Camarones and Laguna de los Padres. He died in 1857 according to the standard chronology.
- **Mother:** Isabel Pueyrredón, of the politically and socially prominent Pueyrredón family. She died in **1843**, when José was eight. The maternal family is conventionally described as Unitarian, creating the oft-repeated image of Hernández as the child of a federal father and a Unitarian mother.
- His parents married on **20 January 1833**. The Cervantes Virtual chronology says the match displeased José Gregorio Hernández Plata, the paternal grandfather, and that the propertyless newlyweds lived with Isabel’s sister **Victoria Pueyrredón** at Perdriel. This is a retrospective biographical account rather than a marriage-contract analysis.
- Maternal ancestry connected him to the French Pueyrredon/Pueyrredón line and, through an eighteenth-century marriage, to an Irish-descended O’Doggan line. Popular biographies sometimes turn this into an overly simple claim that he was a close nephew of the revolutionary director Juan Martín de Pueyrredón. The precise relationship is collateral and generationally more distant; “relative of” is safer than “nephew.”
- The political contrast between the two lineages is real as a broad family pattern but should not be treated as a rigid household allegory. Individual allegiance shifted in the civil wars. A paternal uncle, Colonel **Juan José Hernández**, died fighting on the Rosas side at Caseros in 1852.

### Siblings and surrogate caregivers

The securely repeated siblings are **Rafael Hernández** (later engineer, legislator, agricultural educator, and an important source for José’s life) and **Magdalena Hernández**. José was the second child in the Cervantes Virtual chronology. His maternal aunt **Victoria Pueyrredón**, whom he reportedly called **“Mamá Toto,”** cared for him during long parental absences. When the Pueyrredón household went to Montevideo around 1840 amid Rosista political pressure, José reportedly remained in Barracas with his paternal grandfather **José Gregorio Hernández Plata**.

Rafael’s 1896 biographical notice, written for *Pehuajó: nomenclatura de sus calles*, is foundational but not neutral: it is family testimony published ten years after José’s death. It supplies vivid claims—his extraordinary memory, self-taught stenography, rustic knowledge—and must be cited as testimony rather than a contemporaneous school or employment record.

### Marriage and children

Hernández married **Carolina González del Solar** in Paraná on **8 June 1863**. A surviving April 1865 letter from Nogoyá opens “Chinita querida” and documents an affectionate, homesick voice; it is stronger evidence of the marriage’s emotional register than later anecdote.

Sources disagree between **seven and eight children**. A frequently reproduced eight-child list is:

1. **Isabel Carolina**, born Paraná, 16 May 1865 (some chronologies instead place a first daughter Isabel in March 1864, creating a conflict requiring parish verification);
2. **Manuel Alejandro**, born Paraná, 6 November 1866;
3. **María Mercedes**, born Paraná, 24 September 1867;
4. **Margarita Teresa**, born San Martín, 28 May 1871;
5. **Juan José**, dates uncertain in the consulted summaries;
6. **María Sofía**, born Buenos Aires, 20 June 1876;
7. **María Teresa**, born San Martín, 24 October 1877;
8. **Carolina**, born Buenos Aires, 7 April 1879.

The Cervantes chronology introduces further inconsistency by mentioning a daughter **Josefa** in 1876 and placing María Teresa’s birth in 1875 and again in 1877. This dossier does not silently reconcile those entries. Baptismal and civil registrations, family papers, and the Hernández archive should be used for a definitive genealogy.

No reliable evidence of extramarital partners or children was found in the consulted sources. Absence from standard biographies is not proof of absence.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1834–1852

Hernández was born in a house on the Chacra de Perdriel, property of his aunt Victoria and her husband Mariano Pueyrredón. The property had earlier been associated with the 1806 resistance to the British invasion; this later allowed the birthplace museum to combine three national narratives—Perdriel, Pueyrredón, and *Martín Fierro*. The museum’s description as “Hito Nº 1 de la Argentinidad” is commemorative language, not a neutral historical category.

Family accounts place his earliest childhood between Perdriel, Buenos Aires, Barracas, and his parents’ rural workplaces. He learned reading and writing around age four. From approximately **1841 to 1845**, he attended Pedro Sánchez’s **Liceo Argentino** in San Telmo, studying literacy, Christian doctrine, grammar, arithmetic, history, drawing, and later French, geometry, and geography. Claims that Sánchez waived fees because of the boy’s merit come through biography rather than surviving school ledgers.

After his mother’s death and an illness described only as a “chest complaint,” medical advice reportedly led him in **1846** to leave formal schooling for a drier rural climate. He joined his father in the southern frontier zone. There he learned horsemanship, cattle work, rural speech, songs, customs, and the practical dependence of gauchos on military and judicial authorities. This was not formal ethnography, and the later claim that *Martín Fierro* simply transcribed an untouched popular voice is false: Hernández transformed observed speech through the established conventions of gauchesque print poetry.

The landscape was a militarized borderland. Ranching, Indigenous polities, forts, forced militia levies, fugitive movement, trade, raiding, and state expansion coexisted. Hernández’s later defense of forcibly recruited rural men grew out of this setting, as did the poem’s hostile stereotypes of Indigenous people. His sympathy was selective rather than universally anti-coercive.

## Education and Formation

Hernández had no documented university degree. His education combined:

- elementary and secondary-level instruction at Pedro Sánchez’s school;
- immersion in estancia work and frontier life;
- militia experience;
- self-education through newspapers, legislative debate, historical reading, and political correspondence;
- practical stenography. Rafael Hernández claimed José learned tachygraphy essentially without a teacher in seven months and became capable of recording Senate debates alone. The later precision may be fraternal embellishment, but his employment as stenographer is well supported;
- journalistic apprenticeship in a highly partisan press culture;
- the gauchesque literary lineage of **Bartolomé Hidalgo, Hilario Ascasubi, and Estanislao del Campo**, alongside Spanish proverbial, biblical, Romantic, and classical materials.

His intellectual antagonists were also formative. Domingo F. **Sarmiento’s** civilization/barbarism model and centralizing state policy gave Hernández a language to oppose; Bartolomé **Mitre’s** Buenos Aires liberal-national project provided another pole. Yet Hernández later entered the governing autonomist order and his 1879 sequel is more conciliatory toward social reintegration. The relationship is development, not a permanent binary.

## Early Military and Political Career, 1852–1861

After Rosas fell at **Caseros (3 February 1852)**, the young Hernández entered Buenos Aires militia politics. The exact unit histories and ranks vary in biographies.

- In **1853**, he served under **Pedro Rosas y Belgrano** and **Faustino Velazco** against the movement of Hilario Lagos. At **San Gregorio (22 January 1853, not 1854 as some summaries mistakenly state)**, Rosas y Belgrano’s force was defeated.
- He is commonly credited with participation as a lieutenant at **El Tala (8 November 1854)**, a Buenos Aires victory over Lagos’s forces.
- He joined Nicolás Calvo’s **Partido Federal Reformista**, whose supporters were derisively called *chupandinos*. From **1856**, he was linked to Juan José Soto’s newspaper *La Reforma Pacífica*. Early contributions are difficult to identify because they were unsigned.
- A frequently repeated account says that a **duel with another officer** in 1858 forced him out of the Buenos Aires army and toward Paraná. The opponent, circumstances, legal consequences, and documentary record are not consistently supplied. It should be treated as a plausible biographical report, not a proven criminal proceeding.
- He settled in **Paraná**, capital of the Confederation, by 1857–58. He worked in commerce and public administration, then as a stenographer in the Confederation Congress.
- He supported the Confederation under **Justo José de Urquiza** and is conventionally listed as a participant at **Cepeda (23 October 1859)** and **Pavón (17 September 1861)**. Individual service records should be checked; the political alignment is clearer than every battlefield detail.
- In **1860**, signed/pseudonymous “Vincha” articles make his journalism securely traceable. In 1861 he served as secretary to Vice-President **Juan Esteban Pedernera** during Santiago Derqui’s presidency.
- He and his brother Rafael were connected with the Confederation forces surprised by Mitrist troops at **Cañada de Gómez (22 November 1861)**. The event helped end the Confederation’s separate political order.

## Chronological Life History

### Paraná, journalism, family, and Peñaloza, 1862–1864

In 1862 Hernández wrote for **Evaristo Carriego’s *El Litoral*** in Paraná. Archival newspaper research by María Celina Ortale has identified signed pieces and advertisements in which he offered services as a bankruptcy syndic or representative. These notices are important evidence that he earned income beyond literature and state employment.

In 1863 he founded **_*El Argentino*_**. After the killing of the federal caudillo Ángel Vicente **“Chacho” Peñaloza**, Hernández published a series accusing the national/Sarmientine system of responsibility. Collected as **_*Rasgos biográficos del general D. Ángel V. Peñaloza*_** (1863), later retitled **_*Vida del Chacho*_**, it is biography, partisan intervention, and warning to Urquiza. It preserves facts about Peñaloza’s pursuit and killing but is not a disinterested judicial report. Its authorship predates and differs from Sarmiento’s own *El Chacho, último caudillo de la montonera de los llanos*.

He married Carolina in June 1863. In 1864 he served as secretary to the Entre Ríos constitutional convention at **Nogoyá**. He attempted to move toward **Paysandú**, where Uruguay’s Blanco defenders were besieged by Colorado and Brazilian forces; biographies differ over how close he came or whether he entered the fighting. His politics formed part of a cross-border federal/Blanco network, not a strictly Argentine arena.

### Corrientes, the Paraguayan War, and Evaristo López, 1865–1868

Hernández opposed the war against Paraguay and Mitre’s conduct of it. He did not become a major national antiwar theorist, but his journalism belongs to the federal opposition that saw the conflict and provincial interventions as connected centralizing projects.

He moved through Paraná and **Corrientes**, held teaching and administrative work, and supported Governor **Evaristo López**. Accounts identify him as fiscal, secretary, or minister-general in the governor’s field administration, and as a grammar teacher at Colegio San Agustín. After a Mitrist-aligned revolution deposed López in May 1868, Hernández lost office and moved to **Rosario**.

Masonic records, as presented by the Gran Logia de la Argentina and UNED’s museum of Freemasonry, say he was initiated in Paraná’s **Logia Asilo del Litoral No. 18 on 3 March 1865**, later worked in Corrientes’s **Constante Unión No. 23**, and presided there in 1868–69. Some older summaries instead give 28 August 1861 and describe him as secretary in 1862. The institutional Masonic date is more specific, but the conflict should be resolved against lodge minute books.

### Rosario and Buenos Aires press, 1868–1870

Ovidio Lagos brought him to Rosario’s **_*La Capital*_**. From 20 June to 21 July 1868, articles signed J. H. defended provincial legality and proposed Rosario rather than Buenos Aires as the national capital. He then moved to Buenos Aires.

In 1869 he founded and ran **_*El Río de la Plata*_**, at Victoria 202. Its program included municipal autonomy, popular election of local authorities, and—crucially—the abolition of arbitrary frontier contingents or levies. The paper connected his later poem directly to prior prose advocacy. It also defended Argentine sovereignty over the **Malvinas/Falkland Islands** and rejected exterminatory treatment of Indigenous people in principle. Such editorials complicate any simple label: Hernández could denounce expulsion or extermination while reproducing violent frontier stereotypes in the poem.

He supported **Ricardo López Jordán** as a provincial-autonomy leader. When Urquiza was murdered at San José on 11 April 1870 and López Jordán’s revolution began, Hernández publicly stated that nothing solid could be founded on blood. Yet he soon became one of López Jordán’s advisers. This is a documented political reversal or rapid accommodation; it does not prove that Hernández planned Urquiza’s killing.

### Jordanism, armed rebellion, proscription, and exile, 1870–1875

Hernández’s participation in the Jordanist rebellions is the most serious episode of political violence in his biography.

- A letter of **7 October 1870** tells López Jordán that Hernández had desired the revolution’s triumph since learning on 20 April that López Jordán led it.
- He served as adviser and propagandist and probably took part in campaign logistics. Claims that he personally fought in every listed battle are less secure than his political role.
- A memorandum attributed to Hernández proposed an autonomous or separate regional combination involving Entre Ríos, Corrientes, and potentially Santa Fe, and seeking Brazilian support. In 1874 he drafted another request for arms to the Brazilian Empire. The latter was reportedly not executed. These were insurgent foreign-policy schemes; calling them “treason” would import a legal conclusion for which no conviction has been located.
- After federal defeat, he moved through **Santana do Livramento, Brazil**, and **Montevideo**. “Exile” is accurate politically; the exact periods of legal proscription and any formal arrest warrant require archival confirmation.
- He returned to Buenos Aires in 1872 under amnesty/relaxation and published *El gaucho Martín Fierro*.
- During the second Jordanist rising in 1873, he again went to Uruguay and was associated with the invasion of Entre Ríos. In Montevideo he wrote for and eventually directed **_*La Patria*_** with **Héctor Soto**.
- On **10 March 1874**, *La Patria* printed a López Jordán manifesto drafted by Hernández. Under **“El Patagón”** he answered Chilean historian-politician Benjamín Vicuña Mackenna in nine articles. By late 1874, Hernández and López Jordán separated over strategy. This was a political break, not a public repudiation of every earlier federal position.
- *La Patria* ceased publication on 1 January 1875. President Nicolás Avellaneda’s conciliatory policy enabled Hernández to settle permanently in Buenos Aires/Belgrano.

No court judgment convicting Hernández of homicide, corruption, fraud, or treason was located. His material support for armed rebellion is nevertheless documented by correspondence and propaganda.

### Composition and publication of *El gaucho Martín Fierro*, 1872

The first part appeared in late **1872**. *La República* began printing it in installments on **28 November**; the book, printed by **La Pampa**, followed in December with a dedicatory letter to friend and publisher/bookseller **José Zoilo Miguens**. The first edition bore the title **_*El gaucho Martín Fierro*_**, not simply *Martín Fierro*.

The poem speaks through a forcibly conscripted gaucho whose family and property collapse while he serves at a corrupt frontier post. After deserting, Fierro becomes an outlaw and killer, joins Cruz, and crosses into Indigenous territory. It condemns the levy, military exploitation, police and judicial arbitrariness, electoral manipulation, and class-biased state formation. It also includes a racially charged killing of a Black man and essentializing depictions of Indigenous communities. The narrator is not mechanically identical with Hernández, but neither can the text’s racial language be wished away as mere character speech.

Sales were extraordinary by nineteenth-century Argentine standards. Cheap editions circulated through rural stores, pulperías, itinerant networks, and collective reading. Eleven editions appeared by 1878 in the standard chronology; exact print runs differ among bibliographers. The often-repeated story that the poem was written on butcher’s paper or in a pulpería notebook while Hernández hid in the **Gran Hotel Argentino**, almost opposite the government house, appears in later recollection and popular biography. The hotel stay may be historical; the picturesque writing surface is unverified folklore.

### Business, satirical press, and return to legal politics, 1875–1879

Back in Belgrano, Hernández became associated with and later proprietor of the **Librería del Plata**, at Tacuarí 17. The business gave him an urban commercial base and a book-distribution network. No balance sheets, purchase price, inheritance inventory, or reliable net-worth figure was located.

In 1875 he publicly fought with *La Tribuna* over the new edition of *Vida del Chacho*, answering in Manuel Bilbao’s *La Libertad* with “Señor Sarmiento: ¿Por qué mataron?” The dispute mixed historical responsibility, party alignment, and personal accusation. It did not result in a known defamation judgment.

He joined the **Partido Autonomista**, served on Belgrano’s school council, helped organize an industrial-products exhibition, and participated in the satirical publications **_*El Bicho Colorado*_** and possibly **_*Martín Fierro*_** (both 1876). His candidacy for provincial senator in 1877 failed.

### *La vuelta de Martín Fierro* and political accommodation, 1879

**_*La vuelta de Martín Fierro*_** appeared in 1879, illustrated by **Carlos Clérice**. It is not merely “part two” in tone. Fierro returns from Indigenous territory, recounts Cruz’s death and captivity, reunites with his sons and Picardía, debates the Black singer, and gives practical counsels before the characters separate. The sequel advocates work, family, prudence, education, and reintegration.

Interpretive dispute:

- **Continuity reading:** Hernández remained concerned with rural rights but proposed a program for surviving within the consolidated state.
- **Accommodation reading:** The former rebel, now entering official politics and commerce, domesticated the first poem’s insurgent voice.
- **Frontier-state reading:** Published during Julio A. Roca’s 1879 campaign, the sequel’s portrayal of Indigenous life indirectly legitimized conquest even if it was not commissioned as campaign propaganda.
- **Textual-genetic reading (Élida Lois and others):** revisions, suppressions, and shifts reveal changing class and political positioning between the two poems.

There is no evidence that Roca commissioned or secretly financed *La vuelta*. “Tool of extermination” is a later interpretive claim about cultural effect, not evidence of an operational propaganda contract.

### Provincial legislator and federalization, 1879–1886

Hernández was elected to the Buenos Aires provincial **Chamber of Deputies**, became its president in 1880, and supported the federalization of the city of Buenos Aires after the armed confrontation between the national government and Governor Carlos Tejedor. In the celebrated debate, **Leandro N. Alem** warned against concentrating national power; Hernández supported making Buenos Aires the federal capital. The decision aligned him with the consolidation he had earlier resisted, while also fulfilling his long-standing demand to separate the port-city’s national and provincial roles.

He became a provincial **senator in 1881** and remained politically active until 1885/86. Legislative interests included municipal institutions, agricultural and veterinary education, rural development, immigration/colonization, and the new provincial capital.

He supported **Dardo Rocha’s** plan to found **La Plata** and reportedly proposed names including “La Plata.” Institutional commemorations credit him with helping create the province’s first higher agronomy/veterinary school. The precise authorship of each statute should be assigned from legislative journals, not ceremonial summaries.

In **1881**, Casavalle published **_*Instrucción del estanciero*_**, a practical manual on livestock, labor, land, fencing, disease, accounts, and ranch management. It reveals Hernández not as a romantic opponent of modernization but as an advocate of technically improved capitalist pastoral production. He reportedly said it was more useful than *Martín Fierro*; the wording is transmitted through his brother Rafael.

On 30 December 1879 he joined Buenos Aires’s **Logia Obediencia a la Ley No. 13**. Masonic sources place him in senior offices, including First Grand Warden in 1880–81, and say he was recognized shortly before death for long service. Claims that specific verses encode a comprehensive secret Masonic doctrine are interpretive; lodge membership is documented, an all-explanatory cipher is not.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Federalism and the state

Hernández’s durable themes were provincial autonomy, hostility to arbitrary central intervention, local elective authority, and protection of rural producers from forced levies. His politics changed in method and coalition:

1. Buenos Aires federal-reform opposition;
2. Confederation service under Urquiza/Derqui;
3. dissident provincial journalism;
4. armed Jordanism;
5. reconciliation under Avellaneda;
6. autonomist provincial office and cooperation with the Roca-era state.

This is neither simple apostasy nor unbroken consistency. Federalization could be read as the fulfillment of an old anti-port grievance, while the coercive national settlement and frontier conquest contradicted the first poem’s anti-state energies.

### Religion and Freemasonry

He was baptized Catholic, used biblical and Christian proverbial language, and formulated family morality in the sequel. His organized adult affiliation was Freemasonry. Nineteenth-century Argentine Masonry was not necessarily an abandonment of every Catholic belief, though the institutional relationship was antagonistic. No private theological diary was located.

### Gauchesque art

Hernández did not invent gauchesque poetry. Educated urban writers had long ventriloquized rural voices. His innovation was the sustained first-person dramatic authority, narrative scale, memorable sextains, ethical aphorism, and fusion of individual suffering with structural critique. The poem is both representation of gaucho experience and a literate politician’s crafted intervention.

### Race, Indigenous peoples, and contradiction

Documentary presentation requires holding together four facts:

- He denounced forced recruitment and official abuse of gauchos.
- His prose could oppose expelling or exterminating Indigenous inhabitants.
- *Martín Fierro* contains dehumanizing generalizations about Indigenous people and a racist provocation preceding Fierro’s killing of a Black man.
- The poem also exposes white state violence and later stages a payada in which a Black singer intellectually challenges Fierro.

Whether the sequel “supports genocide” is an interpretive conclusion. Its imagery participated in a culture that marginalized Indigenous sovereignty; a direct policy order or financial link to the campaign has not been shown.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Political Violence, and Financial Questions

### Documented

- Service in civil wars and association with armed forces.
- Advising and propagandizing for López Jordán’s armed rebellion after Urquiza’s murder.
- Advocacy of cross-provincial separation/autonomy and attempted foreign support, including a drafted Brazilian arms request.
- Partisan attacks assigning political responsibility to Sarmiento’s system for Peñaloza’s killing.
- Published racial stereotypes and violent episodes in *Martín Fierro*.
- Later integration into the state and landholding modernization order that the poem had criticized.

### Alleged or incompletely documented

- **Duel:** reported as the reason for leaving the Buenos Aires army; no case file located.
- **Direct role in Urquiza’s assassination:** no evidence found that Hernández planned, ordered, or participated in the killing. His subsequent alliance with López Jordán generated suspicion but is not proof.
- **Every attributed battle:** political affiliation is documented; individual combat presence needs muster rolls.
- **Separatist “treason”:** memoranda support a separatist reading, but no located indictment or conviction establishes the legal label.
- **Secret Masonic encoding of the poem:** membership is documented; detailed symbolic decoding is speculative.
- **Financial misconduct:** none found. His bankruptcy-syndic advertising was an occupation, not evidence that he was bankrupt or fraudulent.

### No evidence located

No documented prosecution or conviction for corruption, fraud, embezzlement, money laundering, intelligence work, domestic abuse, addiction, or sexual scandal was found in the consulted sources. This is a research finding, not a universal negative.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Martín Fierro was a real gaucho”

1. **Story:** Hernández based the hero on a particular man named Martín Fierro, sometimes linked to a farm, soldier, or acquaintance.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** local oral histories, property lore, newspaper features, and tourism claims multiplied after the poem became canonical.
3. **Evidence:** no single prototype has been established. The name may combine culturally resonant elements; the experiences synthesize documented systems of levy and frontier abuse.
4. **Against:** the character’s literary construction and composite biography fit gauchesque convention better than a disguised one-person life.
5. **Effect:** “real Fierro” searches anchor the epic in local geography and allow towns/estancias to claim heritage.

### The hotel and butcher-paper manuscript

1. **Story:** proscribed, Hernández hid in the Gran Hotel Argentino and wrote the poem on brown wrapping paper or a pulpería ledger.
2. **Circulation:** family/popular biographies and school retellings.
3. **Evidence:** Hernández was politically vulnerable and used Buenos Aires hotels; composition in 1872 is certain.
4. **Against:** no contemporaneous manuscript or hotel record consulted proves the dramatic paper detail.
5. **Effect:** makes the national poem an improvised outlaw document analogous to Fierro’s own voice.

### “A single uneducated gaucho’s spontaneous song”

1. **Story:** the poem is direct folk speech, almost orally dictated by the pampas.
2. **Circulation:** nationalist pedagogy and popular recitation.
3. **Evidence:** Hernández knew rural life intimately and captured recognizable registers.
4. **Against:** he was a professional journalist, stenographer, legislator, and reader working in a developed literary genre; the verse is highly constructed.
5. **Effect:** authorized the poem as authentic national essence while obscuring mediation and print commerce.

### “National book” and gaucho as total Argentine essence

1. **Story:** *Martín Fierro* uniquely embodies all Argentina.
2. **Emergence:** especially powerful around the 1913 lectures of Leopoldo Lugones, Ricardo Rojas’s educational nationalism, the 1939 Buenos Aires provincial Día de la Tradición law, and later national adoption.
3. **Evidence:** exceptional circulation, quotation, translation, school use, and artistic influence.
4. **Against:** Argentina was and is Indigenous, Afro-Argentine, immigrant, urban, regional, multilingual, and class-diverse. Canonical status is historical construction, not demographic equivalence.
5. **Effect:** elevated Hernández from partisan journalist to national founder; marginalized rival cultural lineages.

### Hernández as consistent Indigenous-rights champion—or extermination propagandist

Both polarized myths extract part of the record. His editorials could reject expulsion/extermination; his poem could dehumanize Indigenous groups; the 1879 sequel appeared alongside conquest. No evidence supports either a spotless universal-rights hero or a secret salaried architect of Roca’s campaign.

### Last words: “Buenos Aires… Buenos Aires…”

This is a durable deathbed tradition reported by later biographies. Without a contemporaneous bedside account it should be labeled family/biographical testimony. It symbolically reconciles the former provincial rebel to the federal capital and therefore had unusual commemorative usefulness.

### Journalism, speeches, and attributed texts

- Articles in *La Reforma Pacífica* (from 1856): some genuine but unsigned attribution uncertain.
- “Vincha” articles in Paraná papers, 1860–61: genuine where bibliographically identified.
- Articles and notices in *El Litoral* (1862), including political reviews and professional advertisements.
- Editorial corpus of *El Argentino* (1863), including the Peñaloza series.
- Corrientes journalism and official texts, 1860s; attribution requires issue-level work.
- J. H. articles in *La Capital* (20 June–21 July 1868).
- Editorials in *El Río de la Plata* (1869–70), on frontier levies, municipal government, provincial rights, Malvinas, Indigenous policy, and national organization.
- Letter to Ricardo López Jordán, 7 October 1870; other political correspondence preserved in archives/editions.
- López Jordán manifesto published in *La Patria*, 10 March 1874: drafted by Hernández but issued politically in the caudillo’s name—best classified as commissioned/ventriloquized political authorship.
- Nine “El Patagón” articles replying to Benjamín Vicuña Mackenna, April–May 1874.
- *La Patria* editorials, 1873–74.
- “Señor Sarmiento: ¿Por qué mataron?” in *La Libertad*, September 1875.
- Satirical pieces in *El Bicho Colorado* and the weekly *Martín Fierro* (1876); “El payador Pepe José” attribution remains probable rather than fully secure.
- Legislative speeches, especially the 1880 federalization debate; genuine but published from stenographic legislative records rather than as authored books.
- Letters to Carolina and political associates; genuine manuscripts and transcriptions are dispersed.

### Posthumous editions and collections

- *Martín Fierro* in hundreds of combined, illustrated, school, critical, and translated editions. These are genuine works with editorial intervention; later punctuation and normalization should not be mistaken for authorial revision.
- *Obras completas*, six volumes, coordinated/edited by **Élida Lois and Ángel Núñez** (Universidad Nacional de La Matanza, 2001 and subsequent facsimile/reprint projects). This is an editorial reconstruction, not a lifetime collected edition.
- Newspaper-article compilations such as *Artículos periodísticos de José Hernández en “La Patria” de Montevideo (1874)*. Authorship varies item by item and should follow editorial apparatus.
- Correspondence and documentary collections edited posthumously. No diary or memoir authored by Hernández is known.

### Dubious or erroneous bibliography

- **_*Los treinta y tres orientales*_ (often dated 1878)** appears in popular work lists, but its bibliographical status is much less secure than the five core titles and may reflect attribution/confusion. It should not be treated as a major verified Hernández book without an edition record.
- Claims that he founded a newspaper **_*El Plata*_** after 1873 lack evidence of the paper’s existence in the specialist chronology.

## Films, Documentaries, Recordings, and Archival Material

### Major adaptations and screen works

- **_*Martín Fierro*_ (1923)**, silent Argentine adaptation directed by Rafael de los Llanos. Survival/access status should be checked with Argentine film archives.
- **_*Martín Fierro*_ (1968)**, directed by Leopoldo Torre Nilsson, screenplay team including Beatriz Guido and others, starring Alfredo Alcón; music by Ariel Ramírez. A major state-era national-cinema adaptation and winner of the Argentine critics’ Silver Condor for best film.

## Illness, Death, and Burial

The childhood chest illness is known only through retrospective accounts and apparently resolved enough for an active military and political life. No reliable record of chronic addiction, psychiatric crisis, or disabling injury was located.

Hernández died on **Thursday, 21 October 1886**, at his Belgrano residence. Later accounts diagnose myocarditis/cardiac arrest. He was 51. The reported final words “Buenos Aires… Buenos Aires…” are unverified bedside tradition. He was buried at Recoleta. Carolina survived him; precise probate, debts, bookstore disposition, and division of property require notarial research.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### From partisan poem to national classic

During Hernández’s life, *El gaucho Martín Fierro* was already a rural bestseller, but elite canonization developed later. Around the centenary era, **Ricardo Rojas** incorporated gauchesque writing into national literary history, while **Leopoldo Lugones’s** 1913 lectures (published as *El payador* in 1916) declared the poem an Argentine epic. This recoding muted some of the work’s partisan, class, and outlaw dimensions and made the gaucho an ancestor of national unity.

Avant-garde and later writers contested the inheritance. Borges admired the poem but rejected treating it as an unquestionable epic scripture. Martínez Estrada read it as a dark anatomy of Argentine social formation. Post-1960s criticism restored print markets, law, voice appropriation, race, gender, and frontier conquest to the analysis.

### Día de la Tradición

Buenos Aires Province established **10 November**, Hernández’s birthday, as **Día de la Tradición** in 1939 after activism associated with Francisco Timpone and the Agrupación Bases; national observance followed later. The date transformed a writer’s birthday into a recurring state ritual of gaucho display, folklore, schools, horsemanship, and craft. It honors real cultural practices while also selecting a particular rural-creole image as “tradition.”

### Places and institutions

His name is carried by the birthplace museum, libraries, schools, streets, a Buenos Aires Subway Line D station, railway locations, and the locality of José Hernández in La Plata partido. His Belgrano association survives in street nomenclature and monuments. The Chacra Pueyrredón was donated for a museum in the twentieth century, opened in **1966**, and was declared a national historic monument in **1972**.

### Continuing controversies

The poem remains simultaneously:

- testimony against class-biased conscription and frontier corruption;
- a masterpiece of crafted vernacular voice;
- a story containing murder, misogynistic assumptions, and racial hierarchy;
- a source appropriated by nationalists, anarchists, Peronists, conservatives, left critics, folklorists, advertisers, and schools;
- a work used to debate whether state incorporation civilizes, disciplines, or destroys the gaucho;
- a site of Indigenous critique because its “desert” is inhabited and its second part coincides with military conquest.

The famous aphorisms—especially “Los hermanos sean unidos”—circulate detached from the narrative’s violence and political history. Their afterlife is genuine influence, but quotation can turn a conflicted poem into generic civic wisdom.
