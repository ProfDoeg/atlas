## Works by Jane Morris

### Embroidered hangings and furnishings

- **c. 1860:** *Daisy*. A repeating-pattern wall hanging designed by William Morris and embroidered by Jane Morris for Red House; surviving sections were rediscovered at Kelmscott Manor in 1962. It is collaborative textile work, not solely Jane’s design. ([Dufty review](https://morrissociety.org/wp-content/uploads/W85-86.6.4.Reviews.pdf); [Open University research](https://oro.open.ac.uk/95915/1/E%20Hardy%20thesis%20vol.%201%20%28corrected%29.pdf))

- **c. 1860:** *Sunflower*. A repeating-pattern embroidered hanging made for Red House and catalogued with the early Morris embroideries. The design belongs to William Morris’s decorative vocabulary; the surviving scholarship associates its execution with the household workshop but does not securely divide the stitching among its participants. ([Dufty review](https://morrissociety.org/wp-content/uploads/W85-86.6.4.Reviews.pdf))

- **c. 1860–65:** *The Legend of Good Women / Red House figure embroideries*. An unfinished project for monumental figure hangings intended to encircle the Red House dining room: twelve were planned and seven completed or partly completed. William Morris designed the figures and scheme, while Jane Morris and Elizabeth Burden executed much of the embroidery; individual hands remain disputed, so the cycle resists division into securely attributable solo works. ([Dufty review](https://morrissociety.org/wp-content/uploads/W85-86.6.4.Reviews.pdf); [Society of Antiquaries](https://www.sal.org.uk/collections/explore-our-collections/collections-highlights/embroidered-wall-hanging-of-st-catherine/))

- **c. 1860–65:** *Queen with a Sword*. One of three figure embroideries later mounted as a Castle Howard screen, with a figure applied to a blue-serge ground of embroidered stems and flowers. It belongs to the collaborative Red House project and is sometimes identified with Lucretia, but its title and allocation of the stitching are not settled. ([Dufty review](https://morrissociety.org/wp-content/uploads/W85-86.6.4.Reviews.pdf))

- **c. 1860–65:** *Hippolyte*. A figure panel from the Red House project, preserved on the Castle Howard screen. Jane Morris participated in making the series, but surviving evidence does not justify assigning every stitch of this panel to her alone. ([Dufty review](https://morrissociety.org/wp-content/uploads/W85-86.6.4.Reviews.pdf))

- **c. 1860–65:** *Flamma Troiae*. A third figure panel on the Castle Howard screen, traditionally associated with Helen of Troy. Its status is that of a collaboratively designed and embroidered component subsequently remade into another object. ([Dufty review](https://morrissociety.org/wp-content/uploads/W85-86.6.4.Reviews.pdf))

- **c. 1860–65:** *Penelope*. A surviving figure hanging mounted on green serge and associated by A. R. Dufty with the Red House cycle. The design is William Morris’s; Jane’s participation belongs to the collective embroidery project rather than an uncontested solo attribution. ([Dufty review](https://morrissociety.org/wp-content/uploads/W85-86.6.4.Reviews.pdf))

- **c. 1860–65:** *Guenevere*. An unfinished figure embroidery related to an early stained-glass design by William Morris. As an incomplete component of the Red House project, it is both an object and evidence of an unrealized decorative Gesamtkunstwerk. ([Dufty review](https://morrissociety.org/wp-content/uploads/W85-86.6.4.Reviews.pdf))

- **c. 1860–65:** *Aphrodite, or Venus*. An unfinished embroidered figure corresponding to William Morris’s painted study of a nude. Its execution has also been attributed to Elizabeth Burden, making Jane Morris’s contribution uncertain rather than established. ([Dufty review](https://morrissociety.org/wp-content/uploads/W85-86.6.4.Reviews.pdf))

- **c. 1876–1880s:** *Honeysuckle*. A silk-on-linen floral embroidery worked by Jane Morris from a design dated 1876 and generally assigned to William Morris. It is an executed textile manifestation of another person’s design, now in the William Morris Gallery. ([De Morgan Foundation](https://www.demorgan.org.uk/jane-morris-muse-artists-model-and-friend-of-the-de-morgans/))

- **c. 1880:** *Acanthus-design bedcover*. A large silk-on-linen embroidered furnishing designed by William Morris and thought to have been worked collaboratively by Jane, May, and Jenny Morris. Museum Wales explicitly treats the attribution as probable rather than certain and notes that the object may later have been displayed as a curtain. ([Museum Wales](https://museum.wales/collections/online/object/6004e65a-22f7-3b0f-9eee-9e7f00ffb7a6/Bedcover/))

- **1885–86:** *Westward Ho!*. An oblong silk-on-linen mat designed by May Morris and embroidered by Jane Morris, its title apparently referring to the North Devon school for which it may have been made. It is a small furnishing rather than a pictorial “work” in the conventional fine-art sense. ([Victorian Web](https://www.victorianweb.org/art/design/embroidery/54.html))

- **1888:** *Portière*. A silk-on-linen door curtain designed by William Morris and executed jointly by Jane Morris and her daughter Jenny; it was exhibited at the first Arts and Crafts Exhibition Society exhibition. The catalogue records collective making rather than a separable individual contribution. ([BIFMO Arts and Crafts Exhibition Society index](https://bifmo.furniturehistorysociety.org/research/aces/miss-jane-morris))

- **1890s:** *William Morris’s bed embroideries*. A room-scale furnishing ensemble at Kelmscott Manor rather than one autonomous object: Jane embroidered the bedcover, while May designed the 1891 valance and May and other workers made the curtains and associated pieces. Attributions and dates vary among the individual components, so the bed is best understood as a collaborative Gesamtkunstwerk. ([Society of Antiquaries](https://www.sal.org.uk/2020/02/sal289/); [Kelmscott Manor collections](https://kelmscottmanor.org.uk/explore/about-the-collections/))

- **c. 1899–1900:** *The Homestead and the Forest*. A cot quilt designed by May Morris and embroidered in silk by Jane Morris, depicting Kelmscott Manor as a protected homestead. Its imagery is May’s, while Jane’s close translation of the drawing into thread is the material making of the surviving object. ([National Heritage Memorial Fund](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a815497e5274a2e87dbd0a0/WEB1_National_Heritage_Memorial_Fund_Annual_Report_and_Accounts_for_the_year_ended_31_March_2016.pdf); [Kelmscott Manor](https://shop.kelmscottmanor.org.uk/products/cot-quilt-1000-piece-jigsaw))

### Personal and wearable objects

- **c. 1878:** *Embroidered evening bag*. A coloured-silk embroidery fitted to a metal mount, made by Jane Morris and probably also designed by her. The design attribution is explicitly qualified because it rests on stylistic comparison with her decorated keepsake booklet rather than documentary proof. ([Victorian Web, citing V&A object T.70-1939](https://www.victorianweb.org/art/design/embroidery/22.html))

- **1899:** *Case of embroidery, bead necklaces and jewellery*. A composite exhibit entered under Jane Morris’s name at the sixth Arts and Crafts Exhibition Society exhibition. Because the catalogue groups multiple small objects without individually identifying them—and may include work by both Jane and her daughters—it resists treatment as either one work or a securely enumerable set. ([BIFMO Arts and Crafts Exhibition Society index](https://bifmo.furniturehistorysociety.org/research/aces/miss-jane-morris))

### Keepsake manuscript books

- **1878:** *“Oneglia” keepsake booklet*. A four-page manuscript booklet of copied verse, including a poem titled “Oneglia,” decorated by Jane Morris and given to Rosalind Howard. It combines transcription, drawing, hand-lettering, and gift exchange: not an authored literary work, but a deliberately made book-object. ([Victorian Web, citing the V&A production note](https://www.victorianweb.org/art/design/embroidery/22.html))

- **late 1870s–1880s:** *Four keepsake manuscript books*. Four small, individually decorated manuscript volumes assembled by Jane Morris from copied poems, carols, quotations, pencil ornaments, and multilingual fragments; three were found among May Morris’s papers. Scholarship treats them as distinct handmade works, but fixed titles and precise dates have not been established, so inventing separate titles would misrepresent them. ([Johanna Amos, “Meaning and ‘Material Reality’: Jane Morris’ Keepsake Books”](https://academic.oup.com/jdh/article-abstract/33/2/107/5643753); [Journal of William Morris Studies](https://morrissociety.org/wp-content/uploads/25.2CompleteIssue.pdf))

### Correspondence as a body of work

- **1869–1913:** *Surviving letters*. Approximately 570 known letters to numerous correspondents survive in the standard collected edition. They are individually made texts but were not conceived as a unified obra; treating the correspondence as one “work” would erase its occasional, relational character, while listing every letter as a titled work would invent titles they never possessed. ([Boydell & Brewer, *The Collected Letters of Jane Morris*](https://boydellandbrewer.com/book/the-collected-letters-of-jane-morris-pb/?v=7885444af42e))
