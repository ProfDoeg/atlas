# Farid al-Din Attar: Research Dossier

## Scope, Method, and Evidentiary Limits

Farid al-Din ʿAttar lived in twelfth- and early-thirteenth-century Khurasan. Contemporary documentation about his private life is exceptionally thin. No surviving birth certificate, family archive, personal correspondence, business account, court record, travel diary, or securely contemporary portrait is known. Most detailed biographies were written centuries after his death, when his reputation had already generated hagiography.

This dossier distinguishes:

- **Documented fact:** supported by Attar’s authentic writings, an early manuscript, or a reasonably contemporary witness.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a historical source but not independently verified.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** inferred by modern specialists from textual or chronological evidence.
- **Disputed claim:** supported and rejected by competing scholarly arguments.
- **Legend or hagiography:** a reputation-forming narrative appearing substantially later than Attar’s lifetime.
- **Unverified claim:** repeated without adequate traceable evidence.

Dates are given in Common Era, with Islamic-era equivalents where useful. Medieval names and titles appear in several transliterations.

## Historical Setting

Attar’s life belongs to the final Seljuk and post-Seljuk period in Khurasan. Nishapur was a major Persian-speaking urban center of trade, scholarship, hadith study, theology, medicine, and Sufism. Its political history was violent:

- The city suffered devastation in the Ghuzz/Oghuz disorders of the 1150s.
- Khurasan passed among Seljuk, Khwarazmian, and regional powers.
- Genghis Khan’s armies invaded the Khwarazmian territories beginning in 1219.
- Nishapur was captured and subjected to catastrophic destruction in 1221.

If the approximate birth date of 1145 is correct, Attar grew up during or immediately after the Ghuzz crisis and died during the Mongol conquest. The recurrence of ruin, suffering, displacement, mortality, and bewilderment in his poetry is historically consonant with that environment, but particular poems cannot safely be treated as eyewitness records of specific attacks.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Reported fact:** Attar’s father was associated with the apothecary profession and encouraged his son’s early interest in Sufis, their sayings, and their modes of life. The evidence is indirect and partly reconstructed from Attar’s writings.

**Reported fact:** Turkish scholarship reports that Attar lost his mother and father while relatively young. No securely identified names, dates of death, property transfers, or graves for the parents are established.

The names “Ibrāhīm” and “Abū Bakr Ibrāhīm” in expanded patronymics may identify his father, but the instability of the complete name in later sources prevents certainty.

### Ancestry and Social Position

No reliable genealogy beyond the patronymic is known. Claims of noble, sayyid, Arab, or specially saintly descent are not established by early documentation.

Attar’s pharmacy implies an urban artisanal or professional household with literacy and some economic independence. His works display access to books and an education sufficient to read religious and Sufi literature. This supports a comparatively secure urban background, but it does not establish substantial wealth.

### Spouse and Child

**Scholarly reconstruction:** Two quatrains in the *Mukhtār-nāma* have been interpreted as lamenting a son who died at age thirty-two. If correctly read as autobiographical, Attar had married or otherwise fathered a child and outlived an adult son. The wife’s identity and the son’s name and date of death are unknown. [TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi](https://islamansiklopedisi.org.tr/attar-feriduddin)

No reliable evidence identifies additional children, siblings, partners, or descendants.

## Childhood and Early Life

### Birth Date

Attar never supplies an unequivocal birth date in an unquestionably authentic passage. Modern reconstruction commonly places his birth at approximately 540 AH/1145–46. This estimate rests principally on:

1. A report that Nasir al-Din Tusi saw Attar as an old man sometime between 1215 and 1221.
2. Muhammad ʿAwfi’s placement of Attar among poets of the Seljuk period.
3. Attar’s reported advanced age at death.
4. The probable chronology of his writings.

Later biographers gave 512/1118–19, 513/1119–20, 537/1142–43, and other years. The earlier dates would make him more than a century old in 1221 and are generally rejected as hagiographic chronological inflation.

### Birthplace

Two principal versions survive:

- **Nishapur:** Some reference works state simply that he was born in the city.
- **Kadkan:** A widespread Persian tradition places his birth in Kadkan, within the historical Nishapur region and now associated with the Torbat-e Heydarieh area.

“Of Nishapur” may designate the larger cultural and administrative region, so the two statements are not necessarily incompatible. No contemporary source settles the exact house, quarter, or village.

### Formative Environment

Attar says that from childhood he was interested in saints, Sufi teachings, and their transmitted sayings, encouraged by his father. He also says that he read many books about prophets and saints and gathered Sufi poetry and stories over decades.

Nishapur exposed him to:

- Shafiʿi jurisprudence and hadith scholarship;
- Karrami, Ashʿari, and other theological currents;
- Malamati and early Sufi traditions;
- Persian courtly and didactic poetry;
- Arabic devotional, biographical, and hagiographic literature;
- A developed medical and pharmaceutical marketplace.

No specific childhood school or instructor is securely named.

## Education and Formation

### Religious and Literary Education

Attar’s writings demonstrate competence in:

- Qurʾanic narrative and symbolism;
- hadith and sayings attributed to Muhammad;
- Sufi biography and technical vocabulary;
- Persian and Arabic anecdotal literature;
- Islamic ethics;
- jurisprudential and exegetical discourse;
- Persian poetic meters and forms.

In later life he explicitly privileged jurisprudence, Qurʾanic exegesis, and hadith. He displayed skepticism toward Aristotelian philosophy and toward intellectual systems treated as substitutes for direct religious transformation. His authentic works contain little technical medicine despite his occupation. [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/attar-farid-al-din-poet/)

No madrasa enrollment, degree, license to transmit hadith, or formal medical credential is documented.

### Teachers and Spiritual Masters

The identity of Attar’s living Sufi master is unresolved.

#### Majd al-Din Baghdadi

**Documented fact:** Attar says he met Majd al-Din Baghdadi, a major Kubrawi-associated Sufi of Khwarazm.

**Possible but unproved relationship:** Later scholarship has considered Majd al-Din the only famous proposed master whose chronology and Attar’s own testimony make a direct relationship plausible. A formal master-disciple bond is not demonstrated.

#### Abu Saʿid ibn Abi al-Khayr

**Documented intellectual or spiritual affiliation:** Attar expresses a special debt to the eleventh-century Nishapuri Sufi Abu Saʿid, who had died about a century before Attar’s birth.

**Scholarly interpretation:** This has been understood as an **Uwaysi** affiliation—spiritual instruction received inwardly from a deceased saint rather than through ordinary personal discipleship.

#### Saʿd al-Din ibn Rabib

Attar praises a little-known Saʿd al-Din ibn Rabib in language that can imply discipleship. The man’s identity and Sufi standing are otherwise too uncertain to establish the relationship.

#### Rejected or weakly supported proposed masters

Later traditions named:

- Najm al-Din Kubra;
- Qutb al-Din Haydar;
- Saʿd al-Din Hamuyi;
- Rukn al-Din Akkaf.

Modern chronological and textual research, especially that summarized by Hellmut Ritter and Badiʿ al-Zaman Foruzanfar, treats these affiliations as inventions, confusions, or unsupported late constructions. [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/attar-farid-al-din-poet/)

### Conversion Legend

**Legend or hagiography:** Attar supposedly began as a prosperous, spiritually indifferent druggist. A dervish entered his shop and asked how Attar expected to die amid his possessions. Attar replied that he would die as the dervish died. The dervish lay down, placed his head on a wooden bowl, invoked God, and died. Shaken, Attar abandoned his shop, became a disciple, and devoted himself to Sufism.

The story appears in late biographical traditions, not in Attar’s secure contemporary record. Its structure resembles widespread saintly-renunciation narratives: a worldly professional witnesses voluntary holy death and undergoes immediate conversion.

**Evidence against a literal reading:**

- Authentic works indicate that Attar composed major books while continuing to work in his pharmacy.
- His father had already encouraged his interest in Sufism.
- His intellectual development appears extended rather than instantaneous.

**Reputational effect:** The tale transformed the occupational name “Attar” into a moral drama contrasting bodily medicine with medicine for the soul.

## Pharmacy, Employment, and Finances

### Professional Activity

**Documented fact from authentic writings:** Attar practiced pharmacy or an associated druggist’s trade and personally dealt with many customers. He says that he worked among his medicines and customers while composing or conceiving portions of the *Musibat-nama* and *Ilahi-nama*.

The medieval *ʿattar* could sell medicinal preparations, herbs, aromatics, perfumes, oils, and related substances. Calling Attar a modern physician overstates the evidence; “apothecary” or “pharmacist” is safer.

### Economic Independence

Attar did not depend primarily on court patronage. His poetry contains very little conventional praise of rulers, and he disparaged the profession of court panegyrist. His trade likely gave him sufficient independence to write without dedicating his oeuvre to a ruling patron.

No ledgers, shop address, business partners, employees, inheritance record, investment, waqf deed, landholding, or numerical estimate of income survives. Assertions that he treated “five hundred patients a day” or possessed enormous wealth belong to later rhetorical amplification unless tied to a specific authentic passage and interpreted cautiously.

### Patrons

No royal patron can be securely assigned. A friend reportedly asked Attar to versify a prose romance attributed to Badr Ahwazi, producing the work known as *Khusraw-nama* or *Gul u Hurmuz*. The friend is not securely identified.

### Technology and Medical Work

No medical treatise, pharmaceutical invention, device, patent, or technical discovery is securely attributable to him. Works marketed or catalogued under his name should not be presumed to record professional pharmacy.

## Travels, Residences, and Geographical Movements

### Secure Residence

Attar’s only securely anchored residence is Nishapur and its immediate region. His pharmacy, literary activity, reported meeting with Tusi, death tradition, and tomb all converge there.

### Claimed Journeys

Later sources and some modern summaries claim that he traveled through:

- Iraq;
- Baghdad;
- Syria;
- Egypt;
- Mecca and Medina;
- India;
- Turkistan or Transoxiana.

The reports sometimes connect the journeys to study with Sufi masters, pilgrimage, and manuscript collecting.

**Disputed claim:** Encyclopaedia Iranica’s critical reconstruction finds no persuasive evidence that Attar’s settled professional life was interrupted by such extensive travel. References to distant places in poetry can derive from books, stories, and conventional geography rather than eyewitness experience. Turkish biographical scholarship is more willing to accept extensive travels inferred from later sources and poems. The conflict remains unresolved, but no year-by-year itinerary can be reconstructed.

### Pilgrimage

A pilgrimage to Mecca is possible for a Muslim Sufi of his standing but is not securely documented. It should not be stated as fact.

### Retirement

Attar describes a withdrawn later existence. Modern scholarship consequently places him in increasing seclusion in or near Nishapur during his old age. “Retirement” need not mean total abandonment of pharmacy or literary work, and its precise date is unknown.

## Early Career and Literary Development

Attar’s literary chronology is reconstructed largely from cross-references in authentic prefaces.

### Lyric Poetry

He composed ghazals and a smaller number of qasidas collected in a *Divan*. His qasidas generally address ethical and mystical subjects rather than royal panegyric. His ghazals use the vocabulary of earthly love, wine, the tavern, the beautiful beloved, unbelief, and the qalandar; in the poetic system of Persian Sufism, these images often indicate ecstatic, antinomian, or mystical states.

### Courtly Romance

*Khusraw-nama*, also known as *Gul u Hurmuz*, *Gul u Khusraw*, or *Khusraw u Gul*, is unlike Attar’s mystical masnavis. It concerns the lovers Gul, daughter of the king of Ahvaz, and Hurmuz/Khusraw, an illegitimate son of the Byzantine emperor. Attar identifies an otherwise unknown Badr Ahwazi as the author of the prose model.

Two recensions may survive: an earlier version preceding *Mantiq al-tayr* and a later abridgment. Its exact relationship to Nizami Ganjavi’s *Khusraw u Shirin* remains unresolved; thematic similarity does not demonstrate borrowing. [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/attar-farid-al-din-poet/)

### Mystical Masnavis

The probable developmental sequence is:

1. *Asrar-nama*;
2. *Mantiq al-tayr*;
3. *Musibat-nama*;
4. *Ilahi-nama*;

with *Mukhtar-nama* assembled after several narrative poems but before *Ilahi-nama*. Precise dates are not secure.

A verse gives 573/1177–78 as the completion date of *Mantiq al-tayr*, but it is absent from some manuscripts. It is evidence, not conclusive proof.

## Chronological Life History

### Approximately 1142–1146: Birth

Born in Nishapur or Kadkan in greater Nishapur. His household was probably connected to the apothecary trade. No exact birthday is known.

### 1150s: Childhood amid regional crisis

If the standard dating is right, Attar was a child during the Ghuzz devastation of Nishapur. No autobiographical description securely identifies his personal experience, but the destruction formed part of his generation’s historical environment.

### 1160s–1170s: Education and pharmacy

He acquired Persian literary training, knowledge of Arabic religious sources, and practical familiarity with pharmacy. He may have inherited or entered his father’s business.

His interest in Sufi saints preceded any alleged conversion. He read extensively and later said that he gathered Sufi stories and poetry over thirty-nine years.

### Approximately 1170s onward: Composition

Attar composed ghazals, qasidas, quatrains, romances, mystical masnavis, and prose hagiography while retaining professional independence.

### 1177–78 or later: *Mantiq al-tayr*

A manuscript-dependent verse dates the poem to 573 AH. If authentic, this is the only explicit compositional date from Attar’s oeuvre. The uncertainty of its textual transmission prevents absolute reliance.

### Mature period: *Tadhkirat al-awliya*

Attar compiled Persian biographies and sayings of early Muslim ascetics and Sufis. He usually did not cite his written sources. Comparative scholarship finds that he translated some Arabic sayings closely but reshaped narratives to serve his own hagiographic and instructional purposes.

### Mature or later period: family bereavement

If the pertinent *Mukhtar-nama* quatrains are autobiographical, Attar’s thirty-two-year-old son died before him. No date can be assigned.

### 1215–1221: Reported meeting with Nasir al-Din Tusi

**Reported fact:** Tusi, then a young scholar, visited Attar at Nishapur and remembered him as an old man with a notably large poetic corpus. The report helps modern scholars estimate Attar’s age and confirms that he was alive shortly before the Mongol attack. [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/attar-farid-al-din-poet/)

### 1220 or 1221: Alleged meeting with the young Rumi

Later tradition says that Baha al-Din Walad passed through Nishapur with his young son Jalal al-Din Rumi, met Attar, and received Attar’s prediction of the boy’s future greatness. Attar allegedly gave Rumi a copy of *Asrar-nama*.

This is discussed under “Myths and Disputed Narratives.”

### April 1221: Mongol destruction and probable death

The predominant reconstruction holds that Attar died during the Mongol massacre at Nishapur in Safar 618/April 1221. The historical destruction is secure; Attar’s precise individual fate is not independently recorded by an eyewitness account.

Other proposed death years, especially 627/1230, remain in reference literature. The 1221 dating fits the Tusi report, Attar’s advanced age, and the Nishapur tradition most coherently.

## Religious, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

### Doctrinal Location

Attar’s authentic works present him as a Sunni Muslim with pronounced respect for Muhammad’s family as well as his companions. Later pseudo-Attarian works include stronger Shiʿi elements and should not be used to reclassify the historical Attar.

He was not a systematic theologian. His preferred methods were:

- narrative;
- paradox;
- saintly anecdote;
- dialogue;
- sermon;
- allegorical journey;
- ecstatic lyric;
- speech placed in the mouths of fools, beggars, sinners, rulers, animals, and saints.

### The Soul’s Estrangement and Return

A central pattern is the soul’s imprisonment in material individuality and its return toward divine origin. Purification, love, suffering, bewilderment, and self-annihilation disclose in this life what bodily death will ultimately reveal.

### Love and Reason

Attar often opposes calculating reason to transformative love. This is not a rejection of all intelligence; it is an attack on reason’s claim to master divine reality. Love breaks social respectability, doctrinal pride, possessiveness, and the autonomous self.

### The Seven Valleys

In *Mantiq al-tayr*, the birds pass through valleys conventionally rendered as:

1. Quest or Search;
2. Love;
3. Knowledge or Gnosis;
4. Detachment or Independence;
5. Unity;
6. Bewilderment;
7. Poverty and Annihilation.

Different translations vary. The final discovery of the Simurgh through the “thirty birds” (*si murgh*) is not a simple statement that individuals are literally God. It stages recognition, judgment, annihilation of separate selfhood, and the paradoxical relation of divine transcendence to the seekers’ transformed identity.

### Fana and Baqa

Attar emphasizes *fana*, the destruction or effacement of egoic separateness, followed implicitly or explicitly by *baqa*, subsistence in or through God.

### Suffering and Divine Justice

The *Musibat-nama* presents a seeker asking successive cosmic beings for relief. Each is itself powerless or afflicted. The seeker finally learns that the answer cannot be found through external traversal alone.

Attar’s “holy fools” sometimes accuse God of injustice, expose hypocrisy, or speak with startling irreverence. Their words can function simultaneously as protest, prayer, and revelation of human incapacity. Navid Kermani’s modern study *The Terror of God* places this metaphysical revolt beside the biblical Job tradition.

### Attitude toward Philosophy

Attar was hostile or skeptical toward the prestige of Greek-derived philosophy when it competed with revelation, prophetic knowledge, or lived transformation. He nevertheless inherited narrative and conceptual material that had traveled through philosophical literature. His rejection should not be confused with lack of learning.

### Ethics and Social Criticism

Recurring targets include:

- courtly flattery;
- accumulation of wealth;
- religious hypocrisy;
- pride in scholarship;
- authoritarian kingship;
- self-satisfied asceticism;
- possessive love;
- obsession with reputation;
- the use of outward piety as status.

He did not formulate a political program or leave evidence of participation in government or revolt.

## Major Works: Content and Documentary Status

### Secure or Generally Accepted Works

#### *Dīvān* — دیوان

Collection of ghazals and qasidas. The ghazals employ love, wine, tavern, qalandar, and martyrdom imagery; the qasidas are chiefly religious and ethical. The corpus has suffered contamination by poems attributed to other writers, making critical editions important.

#### *Mukhtār-nāma* — مختارنامه

A thematically arranged collection of quatrains in fifty chapters. Attar says friends had difficulty navigating the many quatrains in his *Divan*, leading him to select and reorganize them. Themes include divine unity, Muhammad and his companions, ethics, love, erotic symbolism, qalandari imagery, annihilation, and bewilderment.

#### *Asrār-nāma* — اسرارنامه, “Book of Mysteries”

Probably the earliest mystical masnavi. It lacks a continuous frame tale and comprises discourses illustrated by anecdotes. Its central concern is liberation of the spiritual substance from material and worldly bonds.

#### *Manṭiq al-ṭayr* / *Maqāmāt al-ṭuyūr* — منطق‌الطیر / مقامات طیور

Usually translated *The Conference of the Birds* or *The Speech/Language of the Birds*. The hoopoe summons the birds to seek the Simurgh. Their excuses represent spiritual attachments; the journey and seven valleys dramatize transformation, death, and self-recognition.

The frame draws on earlier “bird treatises” associated with Ibn Sina and Ahmad or Muhammad al-Ghazali, but Attar expanded the form into an extensive Persian mystical epic. [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/attar-farid-al-din-poet/)

#### *Muṣībat-nāma* — مصیبت‌نامه, “Book of Affliction”

A seeker passes through forty stations, questioning angels, cosmic structures, elements, minerals, plants, animals, prophets, faculties, and the universal soul. The answer turns the seeker inward toward annihilation in the ocean of the soul.

#### *Ilāhī-nāma* — الهی‌نامه, “Divine Book” or “Book of God”

A king or caliph asks six sons what they most desire. Their wishes—for a fairy princess, magic, Jamshid’s cup, the water of life, Solomon’s ring, and alchemy—are reinterpreted as distorted forms of spiritual longing. The poem contains many embedded tales, including gender-crossing, erotic, royal, ascetic, and conversion narratives.

#### *Khusraw-nāma* / *Gul u Hurmuz*

A courtly romance, not primarily a Sufi allegory. Its place in the authentic corpus has been more debated than the central four mystical masnavis, but influential critical scholarship accepts Attar’s authorship and the authenticity of its preface.

#### *Tadhkirat al-awliyāʾ* — تذکرة الاولیاء, “Memorial of the Saints”

Attar’s principal prose work. The earliest secure textual form contains seventy-two biographies, beginning with Jaʿfar al-Sadiq and ending with Mansur al-Hallaj.

The earliest identified manuscript discussed by Encyclopaedia Iranica was copied in 692/1293, about seventy years after Attar’s probable death, and ends with Hallaj. Sixteenth- and seventeenth-century copies add approximately twenty to twenty-five later saints. Those additions are not Attar’s work. [Encyclopaedia Iranica: *Tadhkerat al-awliya*](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/tadkerat-al-awlia-2/)

### Works Attar Says He Destroyed

Attar reports destroying:

- *Jawāhir-nāma* or *Jawāher-nāma*;
- *Sharḥ al-qalb*.

No complete authentic text is known. A later work with a similar title should not automatically be identified with the destroyed original.

### Titles Mentioned but Lost or Unidentified

Attar’s prose preface also refers to:

- *Kashf al-asrār*;
- *Maʿrifat al-nafs*.

Their content and survival are unknown.

### Major Spurious or Disputed Attributions

The enormous traditional Attarian canon—sometimes said to contain forty, 114, or 190 books—resulted from conflating several authors who used “Attar,” scribal attribution, devotional prestige, and the market for collected works.

Works rejected or strongly questioned by modern textual scholarship include:

- *Pand-nāma*;
- *Ushtur-nāma*;
- *Jawhar al-dhat*;
- *Haylaj-nama*;
- *Mansur-nama*;
- *Bisar-nama*;
- *Mazhar al-ʿajaʾib*;
- *Lisan al-ghayb*;
- *Khayyat-nama*;
- *Waslat-nama*;
- *Kanz al-asrar*;
- *Miftah al-futuh*;
- *Wasiyyat-nama*;
- *Kanz al-haqaʾiq*;
- *Bulbul-nama*;
- *Haydar-nama*;
- the independent *Haft Wadi* treatise appended to some manuscripts.

*Pand-nama*, a concise moral manual, became particularly popular in Ottoman and other educational settings. Its popularity is historically important even though its language, ideas, and manuscript history make Attar’s authorship doubtful. [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/attar-farid-al-din-poet/)

A securely dated 735/1334–35 manuscript of *Jawhar al-dhat* is part of the evidence that some pseudo-Attarian material belongs to a later author or authors.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Father

**Reported fact:** Occupational predecessor and early encourager of Attar’s interest in Sufi saints.

### Majd al-Din Baghdadi

**Documented meeting; disputed discipleship:** Attar’s own statement supports contact. Formal initiation is possible but unproved.

### Abu Saʿid ibn Abi al-Khayr

**Documented posthumous spiritual influence:** Attar treats the deceased Nishapuri saint as an especially important spiritual guide.

### Nasir al-Din Tusi

**Reported meeting:** Tusi encountered the elderly Attar in Nishapur between approximately 1215 and 1221 and noted his prolific poetry.

### Muhammad ʿAwfi

**Early reception:** The anthologist placed Attar among Seljuk-era poets and appears to have known primarily his lyric poetry. There is no proof of a personal meeting.

### Badr Ahwazi

**Textual relationship:** Named as the author of the prose source behind *Khusraw-nama*. Nothing else secure is known.

### Rumi

**Documented literary influence; legendary personal meeting:** Rumi repeatedly ranked Attar and Sanaʾi as major predecessors. The childhood encounter and gift of *Asrar-nama* are late reports.

### Hallaj

**Documented posthumous influence:** Attar made Hallaj the culmination of the authentic *Tadhkirat al-awliya*. Hallaj’s martyrdom, ecstatic utterance, and annihilated self became central to Attar’s imaginative world.

### Courts and Rulers

Attar’s avoidance of panegyric limited his demonstrable integration into court patronage networks. No dependable political patron, royal appointment, or salaried office is known.

## Political Activity, Institutions, and Offices

No evidence establishes that Attar:

- held political office;
- served as court poet;
- acted as diplomat;
- joined an army;
- participated in a rebellion;
- directed a formal Sufi order;
- founded a madrasa, hospice, or commercial company;
- was imprisoned before the Mongol attack;
- was investigated or tried by a religious or civil court.

His political significance lies principally in narrative criticism of kings, wealth, power, hypocrisy, and institutional prestige.

His later title “shaykh” does not by itself prove formal leadership of a named order. No initiatory lineage headed by him is securely documented during his lifetime.

## Successes, Failures, and Contemporary Standing

### Successes

- Produced one of the largest and most influential surviving bodies of pre-Mongol Persian mystical poetry.
- Created a durable synthesis of lyric, sermon, hagiography, folklore, and frame narrative.
- Compiled a foundational Persian prose collection of early Sufi lives.
- Maintained unusual independence from court panegyric.
- Exercised major influence on Rumi and later Persian mystical literature.

### Contemporary Limitations

Attar may not have enjoyed broad fame in his lifetime outside Nishapur. Early witnesses emphasize his *Divan* and *Mantiq al-tayr*, while his standing as a supreme mystical narrator expanded later.

His authentic works do not give an orderly autobiography. Their abundance encouraged later readers to use poetic speakers as literal historical evidence, producing false biographies.

### Literary Vulnerabilities

- Unnamed sources in *Tadhkirat al-awliya* make source criticism difficult.
- Attar freely reshaped anecdotes for instruction.
- Later manuscript expansion altered the boundaries of his works.
- The pen name “Attar” facilitated conflation with later poets.
- Translation frequently reduces doctrinal ambiguity to generalized “universal spirituality.”

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Critical Questions

### No Evidence of Personal Criminal or Financial Misconduct

A dedicated search using Attar’s name with “controversy,” “scandal,” “allegations,” “lawsuit,” “fraud,” “accusations,” “conspiracy,” and “criticism,” including searches directed at 2024–2026, produced no credible evidence that the medieval poet was involved in a lawsuit, fraud, corruption, money laundering, sexual scandal, criminal prosecution, or political conspiracy.

Modern search results frequently confuse:

- other people surnamed Attar;
- the word *attar* for perfume;
- unrelated institutions and businesses.

Such results have no evidentiary relation to Farid al-Din Attar.

### Authorship Controversy

This is the principal documentary controversy.

Late bibliographers attributed tens or hundreds of works to Attar. Modern philology separates at least three stylistic and ideological clusters. Saʿid Nafisi, Hellmut Ritter, Foruzanfar, Shafiʿi Kadkani, and others rejected many titles because of:

- vocabulary and style;
- doctrine;
- late manuscript dates;
- Shiʿi emphases absent from the secure corpus;
- historical anachronisms;
- internal contradictions;
- attribution to otherwise unknown later “Attars.”

A 2026 study of “Pseudo-ʿAttars” argues that these marginal authors were also central to how Persian readers constructed the canonical Attar. Misattribution is therefore not merely error: it is part of his reception history. [“Cheap Perfume?: Pseudo-ʿAṭṭārs at the Margins and Centers of Persian Literature”](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/408465806_Cheap_Perfume_Pseudo-Attars_at_the_Margins_and_Centers_of_Persian_Literature)

### Reliability of *Tadhkirat al-awliya*

**Documented limitation:** Attar rarely identifies sources and often molds narratives around spiritual lessons. His versions cannot be treated uncritically as biographies of saints who lived centuries earlier.

**Countervailing value:** He sometimes translates Arabic sayings closely, preserves material otherwise lost, and offers exceptional evidence for the reception of early Sufism in Persian-speaking Khurasan.

### Orthodoxy and Alleged Pantheism

Attar’s language of union, annihilation, the lover becoming the beloved, and discovering the sought reality within oneself has led some readers to describe him as pantheist or close to *hulul*—divine indwelling—or *ittihad*, ontological union.

**Documented fact:** His secure works also affirm Qurʾan, Muhammad, Islamic ritual and ethics, divine majesty, judgment, and the necessity of spiritual discipline.

**Interpretive controversy:** Whether his paradoxes constitute metaphysical monism, ecstatic rhetoric, pedagogical shock, or poetic dramatization cannot be decided by isolating individual lines.

No documented medieval heresy prosecution against Attar is known.

### Sectarian Identity

Later strongly Shiʿi works attributed to Attar led to claims that he was Shiʿi. Critical scholarship generally regards the authentic corpus as Sunni while emphasizing his devotion to the Prophet’s family. The choice between “Sunni” and “Shiʿi” cannot be made from pseudo-Attarian texts.

### Gender and Social Hierarchy

Attar’s stories reproduce some medieval patriarchal assumptions while also making spiritually authoritative women, especially Rabiʿa al-ʿAdawiyya, exemplary saints. His narratives sometimes destabilize fixed gender, religious, and social categories. This is a matter of literary interpretation; there is no recoverable evidence about his domestic conduct toward women.

### Modern Decontextualization

Short passages attributed to “Attar” circulate online without Persian originals, manuscript references, or reliable translations. Some derive from Rumi, Hafiz, modern paraphrases, or pseudo-Attarian works. Attribution should be checked against critical Persian editions.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### The Dervish Who Died in the Pharmacy

**Story:** A dervish rebukes Attar’s attachment to his prosperous shop, lies down, and dies voluntarily. Attar abandons commerce and enters Sufism.

**Appearance and circulation:** Late Persian hagiographic biography, subsequently repeated in popular biographies and Sufi teaching literature.

**Evidence:** No contemporary corroboration. Authentic writings indicate long-standing Sufi interest and composition while working.

**Effect:** Establishes Attar as a healer converted from bodies to souls.

**Label:** Legend or hagiography.

### Worldwide Initiatory Travels

**Story:** Attar left Nishapur, traveled through Iraq, Syria, Egypt, Arabia, India, and Turkistan, met many masters, collected books, and returned after decades.

**Evidence for:** Later biographies and geographical references in his writing.

**Evidence against:** No dated itinerary, travel document, or early independent witness; book learning explains much of the geographical range.

**Effect:** Casts him as an encyclopedic pilgrim and universal inheritor of Sufism.

**Label:** Disputed claim.

### Meeting the Young Rumi

**Story:** Baha al-Din Walad, traveling west with young Jalal al-Din Rumi, stopped at Nishapur. Attar recognized the child’s future greatness, said that a sea was following a river, and gave him *Asrar-nama*.

**Where it appears:** The story belongs to the later Rumi biographical tradition associated with accounts such as Aflaki’s *Manaqib al-ʿarifin*, compiled generations after the supposed event.

**Evidence for:** Chronological possibility if Attar lived until 1221 and Rumi’s family passed through Nishapur; Rumi’s strong literary debt to Attar.

**Evidence against:** No contemporary account; the *Asrar-nama* gift elegantly explains textual influence and therefore has the form of retrospective literary genealogy.

**Effect:** Makes Attar the personal transmitter of Persian mystical epic to Rumi.

**Label:** Reported tradition / probable hagiography, not documented meeting.

### Gift of *Asrar-nama* as Explanation of Rumi’s *Masnavi*

The legend likely arose partly because scholars and readers perceived affinities between *Asrar-nama* and the opening or spiritual program of Rumi’s *Masnavi*. Literary influence does not prove a physical presentation copy. [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/attar-farid-al-din-poet/)

### Death and the Sack of Straw

**Story:** A Mongol captured Attar. Someone offered one thousand silver coins for him. Attar told the captor not to sell because that was not his true price. Later a man offered a sack or bundle of straw. Attar said this was his actual worth. Enraged, the Mongol killed him.

**Source history:** Dawlatshah Samarqandi’s fifteenth-century *Tadhkirat al-shuʿara* is an important circulator of the story, more than two centuries after the event.

**Evidence for:** Attar probably died during the historical massacre.

**Evidence against:** No contemporary witness confirms the ransom dialogue. Its perfectly formed lesson in self-annihilation suggests hagiographic construction.

**Effect:** Makes Attar’s death the final enacted parable of *fana*: worldly valuation falls from silver to straw and the self accepts nothingness.

**Label:** Legendary death narrative built around a probable historical death.

### Writing a Final Poem in Blood

**Story:** Mortally wounded, Attar dipped a finger in his blood and wrote a final quatrain on a wall.

**Evidence:** No secure contemporary report; various versions supply different verses.

**Effect:** Produces the familiar martyr-poet motif in which life, blood, and text become identical.

**Label:** Legend.

### 114 or 190 Books

**Story:** Attar authored as many books as the 114 Qurʾanic suras, or approximately 190 works.

**Origins:** Late bibliographers and hagiographers magnified his prolificacy; works by other writers using “Attar” entered his corpus.

**Evidence against:** Attar’s authentic cross-references support a small core, with several lost works. Modern scholarship accepts approximately seven or eight surviving works, depending on treatment of *Khusraw-nama*.

**Effect:** Elevated him from prolific poet to miraculous encyclopedic author.

**Label:** Hagiographic numerical inflation.

### Posthumous Miraculous Power

Dawlatshah and later writers circulated stories of Attar’s spiritual efficacy after death. These accounts document his cult, not verifiable events in his biological life.

## Death, Burial, and Mausoleum

### Historical Death

The most defensible reconstruction is:

- Attar was alive as an old man between approximately 1215 and 1221.
- Mongol forces destroyed Nishapur in April 1221.
- A strong early-modern tradition places his death in that catastrophe.
- He was likely more than seventy years old.

The precise day, weapon, killer, and circumstances are unknown. “Executed by a Mongol soldier after refusing ransom” is legend rather than established fact.

### Alternative Date

A death around 627/1230 remains in some scholarly reference works. Hermann Landolt notes it as a possibility while identifying April 1221 as the prevailing opinion. [Institute of Ismaili Studies](https://www.iis.ac.uk/scholarly-contributions/farid-al-din-attar/)

### Cause of Death

**Probable:** Violent death during the Mongol capture or massacre at Nishapur.

**Not documented:** Particular wound, beheading, captivity sequence, or medical cause.

### Tomb

A mausoleum associated with Attar stands near Shadiyakh outside modern Nishapur. The surviving monument is associated with Timurid patron and poet Ali-Shir Navaʾi in the fifteenth century, when Attar’s cult and literary prestige had greatly expanded.

The structure underwent later attention and restoration, including in the Qajar and modern periods, and is recognized as Iranian national heritage. The twenty-fifth day of Farvardin is observed in Iran as Attar commemoration day. [Visit Iran](https://visitiran.ir/attraction/tomb-attar)

The monument confirms a durable burial tradition but cannot independently identify the medieval remains beneath it.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Thirteenth Century

Attar’s lyric poetry and *Mantiq al-tayr* were known near his lifetime. The earliest identified *Tadhkirat al-awliya* manuscript, copied in 1293, demonstrates significant circulation within approximately seventy years of his probable death.

Rumi’s poetry made Attar part of a lineage with Sanaʾi. A famous formulation attributed to Rumi calls Attar the spirit and Sanaʾi its two eyes, while implying that later poets followed them.

### Fifteenth Century Canonization

Attar’s reputation reached a decisive stage under Timurid literary culture. Jami, Dawlatshah, and Ali-Shir Navaʾi treated him as a saintly master:

- biographies accumulated conversion and martyrdom legends;
- the tomb was monumentalized;
- spurious works enlarged the canon;
- Attar became both poet and saint.

### Persianate and Ottoman Worlds

His works circulated across Iran, Central Asia, Anatolia, and South Asia. *Pand-nama*, though probably spurious, became an influential ethical school text and attracted commentaries and translations.

*Tadhkirat al-awliya* contributed to Persian, Turkish, and later Urdu knowledge of early Sufi saints. Its narratives often became better known than the earlier Arabic materials from which they were adapted.

### European Reception

Major stages included:

- nineteenth-century Orientalist editions and translations;
- Reynold A. Nicholson’s 1905–07 critical edition of *Tadhkirat al-awliya*;
- R. P. Masani’s 1924 abridged English *Conference of the Birds*;
- C. S. Nott’s twentieth-century adaptation;
- A. J. Arberry’s *Muslim Saints and Mystics*;
- Hellmut Ritter’s *Das Meer der Seele* (1955), later translated as *The Ocean of the Soul*;
- Dick Davis and Afkham Darbandi’s widely read English *Conference of the Birds*;
- Peter Avery’s translation;
- Sholeh Wolpé’s modern English rendering.

Ritter’s study remains the most extensive mapping of Attar’s narrative and intellectual world. [Brill](https://brill.com/edcollbook/title/8119?language=en)

### Theatre, Visual Art, and Performance

Peter Brook and Jean-Claude Carrière created *La Conférence des oiseaux*, first performed at the Festival d’Avignon on 15 July 1979 and later at the Bouffes du Nord. It transformed Attar’s poem into intercultural ensemble theatre and became one of its most influential modern adaptations. [Alternatives théâtrales](https://alternativestheatrales.be/numerique/132/lettres-persanes-et-scenes-diran/la-conference-des-oiseaux/)

Later adaptations have included:

- stage plays;
- dance theatre;
- music theatre;
- illustrated books;
- calligraphic and visual-art cycles;
- children’s retellings;
- musical compositions.

### Modern Iranian Commemoration

Attar is commemorated through:

- his Nishapur mausoleum;
- an official memorial day;
- editions by major Iranian scholars;
- conferences and literary festivals;
- school and university curricula;
- paintings and public images, including idealized portraits without historical claim to likeness.

### Contemporary Reinterpretation

Modern readers have variously presented Attar as:

- orthodox Muslim teacher;
- Sufi metaphysician;
- radical critic of religious hypocrisy;
- proto-universalist;
- psychologist of desire;
- narrator of sacred violence and protest;
- precursor to Rumi;
- source for ecological or collective readings of the birds;
- representative of Iranian cultural heritage.

These are receptions, not neutral substitutes for his historical setting.

## Works by Attar: Bibliographic Register

### Authentic or Generally Accepted Primary Works

1. **Dīvān-e ʿAṭṭār** — lyric poems, chiefly ghazals and qasidas; twelfth–thirteenth century.
2. **Mukhtār-nāma** — thematic collection of quatrains; twelfth–thirteenth century.
3. **Asrār-nāma** — mystical masnavi; probably earliest major mystical narrative poem.
4. **Manṭiq al-ṭayr**, also **Maqāmāt al-ṭuyūr** — mystical bird epic; possibly completed 573/1177–78, but the date is textually uncertain.
5. **Muṣībat-nāma** — forty-station mystical quest.
6. **Ilāhī-nāma** — framed narrative of a ruler and six sons.
7. **Khusraw-nāma**, also **Gul u Hurmuz**, **Gul u Khusraw**, or **Khusraw u Gul** — romance; survives in more than one recension.
8. **Tadhkirat al-awliyāʾ** — prose lives and sayings of saints; authentic core of seventy-two biographies.

### Lost, Destroyed, or Unidentified Works Mentioned in Authentic Texts

9. **Jawāhir-nāma / Jawāher-nāma** — reportedly destroyed by Attar.
10. **Sharḥ al-qalb** — reportedly destroyed.
11. **Kashf al-asrār** — mentioned; unidentified or lost.
12. **Maʿrifat al-nafs** — mentioned; unidentified or lost.

## Atlas Connections

Only relationships supported by sources are included. Mere contemporaneity, shared geography, or treatment of a legendary figure as a literary character is not counted as a personal meeting.

### Jalal al-Din Rumi

- **Nature:** Major documented literary and spiritual influence; alleged childhood meeting.
- **Dates and places:** Literary influence throughout Rumi’s mature career. Alleged meeting at Nishapur, approximately 1220–21.
- **Evidence:** Rumi explicitly praises Attar and places him with Sanaʾi among his great predecessors. The meeting and gift of *Asrar-nama* appear in later hagiography.
- **Label:** **Documented influence; reported tradition / probable hagiography for the meeting.**

### Baha ud-Din Walad

- **Nature:** Allegedly brought his son Rumi to Attar during the family’s westward migration.
- **Date and place:** Nishapur, approximately 1220–21, according to later tradition.
- **Evidence:** No contemporary corroboration; dependent on the Rumi meeting narrative.
- **Label:** **Reported tradition / probable hagiography.**

### Mansur al-Hallaj

- **Nature:** Central posthumous intellectual and hagiographical influence.
- **Date and place:** Hallaj died in Baghdad in 922, long before Attar’s birth. Attar made him the final and culminating saint in the authentic seventy-two-chapter *Tadhkirat al-awliya*.
- **Evidence:** Directly documented in Attar’s text and early manuscript structure.
- **Label:** **Documented posthumous influence.**

### Abu Yazid Tayfur ibn Isa al-Bistami

- **Nature:** Subject and exemplary ecstatic in *Tadhkirat al-awliya* and Attar’s broader story world.
- **Dates and places:** Bistam, ninth century; no personal overlap.
- **Evidence:** Direct textual treatment.
- **Label:** **Documented posthumous literary and spiritual connection.**

### Abu al-Qasim al-Junayd ibn Muhammad al-Baghdadi

- **Nature:** Subject in Attar’s hagiographical transmission of classical Sufism.
- **Dates and places:** Baghdad, ninth–tenth century; no personal meeting possible.
- **Evidence:** Direct treatment in *Tadhkirat al-awliya*.
- **Label:** **Documented posthumous literary connection.**

### Abu al-Qasim Abd al-Karim ibn Hawazin al-Qushayri

- **Nature:** Earlier Nishapuri Sufi author whose Arabic *Risala* preserves parallel material used in the tradition underlying Attar’s hagiography.
- **Dates and places:** Nishapur, eleventh century.
- **Evidence:** Nicholson and later scholars compare parallel passages. Direct dependence must be evaluated passage by passage.
- **Label:** **Documented textual parallel; probable literary influence.**

### Ibrahim ibn Adham

- **Nature:** Major saintly subject in Attar’s prose and verse narratives, representing renunciation of kingship and wealth.
- **Dates and places:** Eighth-century Khurasan and Islamic world; no personal overlap.
- **Evidence:** Direct textual treatment.
- **Label:** **Documented posthumous literary connection.**

### Rabia al-Adawiyya

- **Nature:** Major female saint in *Tadhkirat al-awliya*, presented as an authority of divine love.
- **Dates and places:** Basra, eighth century.
- **Evidence:** Direct textual treatment; Attar’s account is hagiography rather than contemporary biography.
- **Label:** **Documented posthumous literary connection.**

### Muhammad ibn Abdullah

- **Nature:** Prophet, theological center, and object of praise throughout Attar’s authentic works.
- **Dates and places:** Seventh-century Arabia; no personal overlap.
- **Evidence:** Direct and pervasive textual devotion.
- **Label:** **Documented religious and literary connection.**

### Abu Bakr al-Siddiq

- **Nature:** Companion of Muhammad praised and narrated in Attar’s works.
- **Evidence:** Direct textual presence.
- **Label:** **Documented posthumous religious-literary connection.**

### Aishah bint Abi Bakr

- **Nature:** Figure in the Islamic narrative and transmitted devotional world available to Attar.
- **Evidence:** References belong to textual tradition; no special individual relationship beyond literary-religious reception is established.
- **Label:** **Documented textual connection where cited; no personal relationship.**

### King Solomon

- **Nature:** Foundational symbolic figure in *Mantiq al-tayr*: the hoopoe is Solomon’s messenger in Qurʾan 27, and Solomon’s ring is one of the desires in *Ilahi-nama*.
- **Evidence:** Direct literary use.
- **Label:** **Documented scriptural and symbolic connection.**

### Nizami Ganjavi

- **Nature:** Possible literary comparison involving Attar’s *Khusraw-nama* and Nizami’s *Khusraw u Shirin*.
- **Dates and places:** Both wrote in the later twelfth century; no meeting is documented.
- **Evidence:** Thematic parallels exist, but detailed dependence has not been demonstrated.
- **Label:** **Disputed or unresolved literary relationship.**

### Ibn Arabi

- **Nature:** Frequently grouped with Attar in later histories of Sufism.
- **Evidence:** No securely documented meeting, correspondence, teacher-student bond, or explicit personal relationship was found. Similar concerns do not establish direct influence.
- **Label:** **No demonstrated Atlas connection.**

### Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani, Khwaja Muʿin al-Din Chishti, Shams al-Din Tabrizi, Abu Saʿid al-Bistami’s later interpreters, and Nizam al-Din Awliya

These figures belong to overlapping Sufi histories or later reception networks, but the sources consulted did not establish a personal meeting, correspondence, or securely acknowledged direct influence involving Attar. **Absence of a documented link is the finding.**

No defensible personal or textual connection was found to the overwhelming majority of the Atlas roster.
