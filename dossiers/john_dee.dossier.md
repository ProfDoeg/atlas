# John Dee: Research Dossier

## Scope and Evidentiary Method

This dossier concerns the English mathematician, astrologer, antiquary, imperial theorist, bibliophile, alchemist, and practitioner of ritual spirit communication John Dee (1527–1608/1609).

Evidence is classified as follows:

- **Documented fact:** supported by Dee’s manuscripts, diary, official records, surviving correspondence, contemporary printing, institutional records, or physical objects.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a contemporary or near-contemporary source but not independently confirmed.
- **Allegation:** an accusation made against Dee or an interpretation advanced without conclusive proof.
- **Disputed claim:** supported by conflicting evidence or scholarly interpretations.
- **Rumor or myth:** a later story lacking adequate contemporary documentation.
- **Inference:** a cautious reconstruction from circumstantial evidence.

Dates before England’s 1752 calendar reform may appear differently in sources, especially around January–March. Dee’s death is consequently reported as December 1608 or early 1609.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Name:** John Dee.
- **Latin forms:** *Ioannes Dee Londinensis*; *Joannes Dee*.
- **Usual historical titles:** Dr John Dee; mathematician; astrologer; antiquary; natural philosopher; “magus.”
- **Contemporary hostile designation:** “conjuror,” “arch-conjuror.”
- **Modern popular designations:** “Queen Elizabeth’s magician,” “court astrologer,” “the original 007.” The last is a modern myth.
- **Birth:** 13 July 1527, according to Dee’s own autobiographical account.
- **Birthplace:** Tower Ward, London.
- **Death:** probably December 1608 at Mortlake; some authorities use 1609 because burial or calendar evidence is uncertain.
- **Burial:** traditionally associated with St Mary the Virgin, Mortlake. The precise grave is lost.
- **Nationality and ancestry:** English, with Welsh paternal ancestry claimed by Dee.
- **Principal occupations:** mathematician, mathematical teacher, astronomer, astrologer, geographer, cartographer, navigation adviser, instrument collector, antiquary, manuscript collector, alchemical experimenter, Christian occult philosopher, and warden of the collegiate church at Manchester.
- **Religion:** outwardly Christian throughout his life. His position crossed confessional regimes, and his theology combined Christian devotion with Neoplatonic, Hermetic, Cabalistic, mathematical, apocalyptic, and angelological ideas.
- **Known institutional affiliations:** St John’s College, Cambridge; Trinity College, Cambridge; the Mercers’ Company; the Muscovy Company’s navigational milieu; Elizabeth I’s courtly and patronage networks; the Collegiate Church of Manchester.

Dee was not a salaried “royal magician” in any clearly documented formal office. He did perform astrological and intellectual services for Elizabeth I and members of her court, but the familiar title compresses an irregular patronage relationship into a nonexistent permanent post. [S1, S8, S15]

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

Dee’s father was **Rowland Dee**, sometimes described as a London vintner and member of the Mercers’ Company. Another early account calls him a gentleman sewer in Henry VIII’s household. These descriptions need not be mutually exclusive: Tudor household service, mercantile standing, and livery-company membership could overlap.

His mother was **Johanna or Jane Wild**, daughter of William Wild.

The family appears to have possessed sufficient status and resources to give John a grammar-school and Cambridge education, but it did not belong to the highest nobility.

### Welsh ancestry and royal pedigree

Dee represented himself as descended from Welsh families and ultimately from **Rhodri Mawr**, the ninth-century ruler of Gwynedd. A pedigree in his hand survives.

- **Documented fact:** Dee constructed and used an elaborate Welsh genealogy.
- **Disputed claim:** the descent from Rhodri Mawr cannot be independently established.
- **Historical significance:** the pedigree supported Dee’s British-imperial and Arthurian arguments and may also have served courtly self-presentation under the Welsh-descended Tudor dynasty.

The older *Dictionary of National Biography* called the pedigree a “pretended” descent. Modern scholarship treats it more usefully as part of Dee’s antiquarian and political program, whether or not genealogically sound. [S1, S21]

### Marriages

The marital sequence is not completely secure.

1. **Katherine Constable**, probably the widow of London grocer Thomas Constable. The marriage likely occurred in 1565 or 1566. She died in March 1575. No children are known.
2. Some reconstructions posit an unnamed second wife who married Dee around 1575 and died in 1576. The evidence is uncertain, and other accounts treat Jane Fromond as Dee’s second wife.
3. **Jane Fromond or Fromonds** (c.1555–1604/1605), married Dee on 5 February 1578. She had served in the household of Elizabeth FitzGerald, Countess of Lincoln. Jane was about twenty-two or twenty-three; Dee was fifty.

Jane managed a large and difficult “experimental household” at Mortlake and later endured the family’s lengthy migration through Central Europe. Dee’s diaries record her pregnancies, illnesses, menstruation, dreams, household labor, servants, finances, and participation in the spirit sessions. The surviving record is filtered through her husband’s writing, but it establishes that she was central to the operation of the household. [S10, S20, S21]

### Children

John and Jane Dee had eight children in the most commonly accepted reconstruction:

- **Arthur Dee** (1579–1651), physician, alchemist, and author; later served Tsar Michael I in Russia.
- **Katherine Dee**.
- **Rowland Dee**.
- **Michael Dee**, died in 1594.
- **Theodore Dee**, died in 1601.
- **Madinia Dee**, born 1590.
- **Frances Dee**, born 1592.
- **Margaret Dee**, born 1595 and apparently died in 1603.

The later fates of Katherine, Rowland, Madinia, and Frances are incompletely documented. Jane and possibly some children died during plague conditions at Manchester in 1604–1605, but surviving accounts disagree on exact dates and victims. [S20, S21]

### Household

The Mortlake establishment was not simply a private study. It included Jane, children, servants, visiting scholars, artisans, patients or astrological clients, alchemical workers, and scryers. Household instability—births, illness, debts, servant turnover, theft, travel, and the cost of books and experiments—was inseparable from Dee’s scholarship.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1527–1542

Dee was born in London’s Tower Ward on 13 July 1527. Little securely documented evidence survives about his first decade.

He learned Latin in London and attended school at Chelmsford, Essex. Traditional accounts identify Chelmsford Grammar School, although its later institutional form should not be projected uncritically backward.

The environment in which Dee grew up combined:

- London mercantile culture;
- the Tudor royal household;
- the English Reformation;
- expanding access to printed mathematical and geographical works;
- renewed study of Greek;
- maritime ventures seeking routes to Asia;
- Renaissance interest in astrology, natural magic, cryptography, and classical antiquity.

These subjects were not regarded as sharply separated disciplines. Astrology belonged to learned medicine and astronomy; alchemy could mean practical metallurgy as well as spiritual transformation; “magic” could describe illicit conjuration, mathematical technology misunderstood by observers, or a philosophical investigation of hidden natural forces.

## Education and Formation, 1542–1551

### Cambridge

In November 1542 Dee entered **St John’s College, Cambridge**. He received the BA in 1544/1545 and became a fellow.

Dee later claimed that during 1543–1545 he studied eighteen hours each day, allowing four hours for sleep and two for meals and recreation. This is an autobiographical claim demonstrating his cultivated identity as an extraordinarily disciplined scholar; it cannot be independently timed.

When **Trinity College, Cambridge**, was founded in 1546, Dee became one of its original fellows and served as an under-reader in Greek.

For a production of Aristophanes’ *Peace*, Dee devised a stage effect in which the scarab appeared to fly to Jupiter’s palace. Spectators reportedly attributed the mechanism to supernatural means.

- **Documented core:** Dee worked on the production and described its mechanical effect.
- **Reported consequence:** rumors of magical agency followed.
- **Importance:** the episode became an early model for his reputation—mathematical mechanics reclassified by outsiders as sorcery. [S1]

### First Continental journey

In 1547 Dee traveled to the Low Countries. In 1548 he enrolled at Louvain, then a major center of mathematical instrument making, cosmography, medicine, and printing.

His principal intellectual contacts included:

- **Gemma Frisius**, mathematician, astronomer, cartographer, and instrument designer;
- **Gerardus Mercator**, cartographer, globe maker, engraver, and mathematical instrument maker;
- probably other members of the Louvain cosmographical network.

Dee acquired or brought home mathematical books, maps, instruments, globes, and knowledge unavailable in comparable concentration in England. Sources associate him with two Mercator globes and Gemma Frisius’s astronomical rings. [S18, S19, S22]

### Paris lectures

In 1550 Dee lectured in Paris on Euclid. His lectures reportedly drew large audiences. He was offered or considered for an academic position, but returned to England.

His formation by this point combined Euclidean mathematics, astronomical calculation, judicial astrology, geography, optics, instrument making, textual philology, and the conviction that number provided access to the structure of creation.

## Early Career, Court Danger, and Patronage, 1551–1562

### Return to England

After returning in 1551, Dee entered networks associated with mathematics, navigation, court education, and overseas enterprise. He reportedly declined academic or ecclesiastical appointments, including a readership at Oxford. Exact details of some offers rest primarily on Dee’s later self-account.

He accumulated a substantial private library and advised navigators and merchants. Scholarship places him in the environment that became the **Muscovy Company**, chartered in 1555. His precise operational role varied: he was a consultant and intellectual resource, not the commander of voyages. [S9, S16]

### Arrest under Mary I, 1555

In May 1555 the Privy Council ordered Dee’s arrest after allegations involving horoscopes made for Queen Mary I, Philip of Spain, and Princess Elizabeth. George Ferrers and John Prideaux were among the accusers named in later reconstructions. Dee and the mathematician John Field were examined.

Astrological calculation concerning a monarch’s lifespan or succession could be treated as politically dangerous because it might encourage rebellion or imply foreknowledge of royal death. Dee was accused not merely of “calculating” but of conjuring or enchantment.

- **Documented fact:** Dee was arrested, examined, and imprisoned.
- **Allegation:** that his calculations constituted treasonous or harmful magic.
- **Outcome:** no conviction for treason or magical injury is known. He was transferred into ecclesiastical custody and examined by Bishop Edmund Bonner.
- **Dee’s position:** he maintained that his studies were lawful and Christian.
- **Unresolved question:** exactly what was calculated, for whom, and with what political intention.

The episode was a serious encounter with the Tudor state, not merely picturesque persecution of a scientist. It also shows that his relationship with Elizabeth began while she herself was politically vulnerable. [S14]

### Marian and early Elizabethan survival

Dee navigated the transition from Catholic Mary to Protestant Elizabeth without abandoning the language of Christian orthodoxy. Elizabeth succeeded in November 1558. Dee was consulted about selecting an auspicious coronation date; the often-repeated assertion that he alone “set” the date overstates the surviving evidence, but his astrological counsel to Elizabeth’s circle is well established.

He dedicated or presented learned works to powerful patrons and sought stable preferment. Despite access to court, he repeatedly failed to secure the enduring, well-funded position he believed his services deserved.

## Mathematical and Philosophical Maturity, 1558–1576

### *Propaedeumata Aphoristica*

Dee published *Propaedeumata Aphoristica* in 1558, later issuing a revised edition in 1568. It set out aphorisms on celestial influence and natural causation. The work belongs simultaneously to Renaissance astrology, optics, natural philosophy, and mathematical physics.

The text should not be reduced either to modern science or to superstition. Dee sought lawful, mathematically intelligible mechanisms through which celestial bodies affected the terrestrial world.

### Continental travels, 1562–1564

Dee traveled again in the Low Countries and Central Europe. He obtained access to Johannes Trithemius’s *Steganographia*, then circulating in manuscript. The text appeared to contain angelic invocations but also encoded cryptographic techniques. It became central to later claims that Dee’s occult work concealed intelligence operations.

In 1563 Dee visited Venice and met the longevity writer Thomas Ravenna. In 1564 he was at Antwerp and Pressburg/Bratislava, where he presented his newly printed *Monas Hieroglyphica* to Emperor Maximilian II.

### *Monas Hieroglyphica*, 1564

The *Monas Hieroglyphica* presented a composite glyph incorporating signs for the moon, sun, elements, and Aries. In twenty-four densely written theorems, Dee proposed a unifying symbolic language linking astronomy, alchemy, number, Cabala, grammar, and theology.

It was dedicated to Maximilian II and printed by Willem Silvius at Antwerp.

Interpretations differ:

- as a work of Christian Cabala;
- as an alchemical program;
- as a mathematical-symbolic reconstruction of knowledge;
- as a courtly bid for Habsburg patronage;
- as an apocalyptic reform project.

These readings are not mutually exclusive. Dee’s annotated books show that the work developed from intensive reading, including Johannes Pantheus’s *Voarchadumia*, rather than appearing as a sudden mystical revelation. [S11, S13]

### Mortlake

By the mid-1560s Dee had established himself at **Mortlake**, southwest of London. Its proximity to the Thames and court residences made it suitable for receiving visitors.

His library eventually contained approximately 3,000 printed books and about 1,000 manuscripts by his own claim. Even if cataloguing categories and totals varied, it was one of the largest private scholarly collections in England. It included mathematics, astronomy, medicine, history, geography, theology, alchemy, Cabala, chronicles, languages, and manuscripts unavailable in English institutional libraries. Dee proposed a national library to Mary I; after the project failed, he effectively built a major private research library himself. [S3, S4]

### Elizabeth I

Elizabeth visited or attempted to visit Dee at Mortlake on several occasions. In March 1575 she approached shortly after Katherine Dee’s death and viewed one of Dee’s optical or reflective instruments outside rather than entering the mourning household.

Dee advised the queen and courtiers on astrology, calendars, geography, antiquarian title, navigation, and possibly political intelligence. However:

- he was not continuously employed as a salaried privy councillor;
- he remained dependent on gifts, temporary grants, and ecclesiastical preferment;
- several promised or requested offices did not materialize.

### Euclid, 1570

Dee wrote the extensive **“Mathematicall Praeface”** to Henry Billingsley’s English translation of Euclid’s *Elements*.

The preface offered a hierarchy of mathematical sciences and argued for their practical application to navigation, architecture, mechanics, perspective, music, military engineering, and other arts. Its diagram and terminology helped establish vernacular mathematical culture in England.

Dee was not the translator of the whole Euclid. Billingsley translated the text; Dee supplied the major preface and probably technical assistance.

### The “new star” and parallax

Dee observed the 1572 nova and wrote on parallax. *Parallaticae Commentationis Praxeosque Nucleus Quidam* appeared in 1573. His work participated in the European debate over whether the new star lay below or beyond the moon.

### Calendar reform

Dee advised on calendar reform. He developed proposals more astronomically exact than the eventual Gregorian arrangement, but England did not adopt them. Religious and political suspicion of reform associated with Rome complicated adoption.

## Navigation, Exploration, and Imperial Projects, 1576–1583

### *General and Rare Memorials*, 1577

Dee’s *General and Rare Memorials Pertayning to the Perfect Arte of Navigation* was intended as the first part of a larger series. It advocated:

- a permanent royal navy;
- mathematical training;
- improved navigation;
- maritime security;
- overseas expansion;
- assertion of alleged British historical rights.

Its elaborate frontispiece depicted Elizabeth governing a divinely sanctioned maritime polity.

### The “British Empire”

Dee used the phrase and concept of a **“Brytish Impire.”** He assembled legal, geographical, genealogical, and legendary arguments asserting Elizabeth’s rights over territories in the North Atlantic and North America.

The claim that Dee simply “coined the term British Empire” is disputed. Humphrey Llwyd and earlier traditions supplied related language. Dee’s importance lies in systematizing and applying the concept to Elizabethan maritime policy.

His arguments used:

- King Arthur’s legendary conquests;
- the alleged voyage of the Welsh prince Madoc;
- English and Welsh chronicles;
- papal and imperial jurisdictional arguments;
- voyages by John and Sebastian Cabot;
- recent exploration.

Modern historical assessment recognizes Dee as an ideological contributor to expansion, but scholars disagree on his practical influence. Some regard him as an important adviser; others emphasize that he exploited exploratory networks to advance a courtly program and secure patronage. [S7, S8, S9]

### Frobisher and other navigators

Dee supplied geographical, mathematical, and navigational advice connected with Martin Frobisher’s voyages and consulted for circles involving Humphrey Gilbert, Michael Lok, the Muscovy Company, and explorers seeking Northeast or Northwest passages.

He did not personally sail on the major expeditions. His contribution lay in maps, instruction, instruments, cosmography, claims of sovereignty, and evaluation of routes.

### Economic position

Dee spent heavily on books, manuscripts, instruments, household personnel, travel, and experiments. Court access never produced stable financial security. His projects depended upon patronage from Elizabeth, William Cecil, Robert Dudley, Christopher Hatton, Edward Dyer, the Earl of Pembroke, the Marchioness of Northampton, and others.

In 1568 the Earl of Pembroke reportedly gave him £20 after presenting the revised *Propaedeumata* to Elizabeth. Such gifts were significant but episodic.

Dee was connected to mercantile ventures as an adviser, but no evidence establishes that he accumulated a large investment portfolio or major commercial fortune.

## Intellectual and Religious Development

Dee’s intellectual program sought a single divinely ordered knowledge uniting:

- arithmetic and geometry;
- astronomy and astrology;
- optics;
- mechanics;
- geography and navigation;
- alchemy;
- medicine;
- history and genealogy;
- Hebrew and Christian Cabala;
- Neoplatonism;
- angelology and apocalypse.

Influences included Euclid, Plato and later Platonists, Roger Bacon, Ramon Llull, Nicolaus Cusanus, Marsilio Ficino, Giovanni Pico della Mirandola, Johannes Reuchlin, Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa, Johannes Trithemius, Paracelsian currents, Copernican astronomy, and Louvain mathematical practice.

Dee distinguished what he considered lawful natural or divine magic from demonic sorcery. His prayers repeatedly invoked God, Christ, and biblical angels. Critics nevertheless regarded any deliberate spirit communication as forbidden necromancy.

Modern scholarship has moved away from dividing his career into a respectable scientific phase followed by irrational decline. His mathematics, astrology, imperialism, alchemy, and angelic conversations formed overlapping parts of a program of universal reform. [S12]

## Angelic Conversations and Edward Kelley, 1581–1589

### Early scryers

Dee desired knowledge he believed unaided study could not supply. By December 1581 he was systematically recording attempts at communication through a crystal or “shew-stone.”

He did not ordinarily claim to see the spirits himself. A **scryer** gazed into the stone and reported visions while Dee questioned, prayed, and wrote.

Earlier scryers included Barnabas Saul. Dee became dissatisfied with Saul’s results.

### Edward Kelley

In 1582 Dee began working with **Edward Kelley**, who initially used the name Edward Talbot. Kelley reported extraordinarily elaborate visions, angelic hierarchies, tables, seals, alphabets, and messages.

Kelley’s background is uncertain. Later writers said he had been convicted of forgery or counterfeiting and had his ears cropped.

- **Reported fact:** Kelley had a reputation for fraud and alchemical skill.
- **Disputed claim:** the ear-cropping story appears in later testimony and is not conclusively verified by surviving judicial records.
- **Documented fact:** Dee sometimes distrusted Kelley, tested his statements, and recorded disputes.
- **Unresolved question:** whether Kelley consciously fabricated all, some, or none of the visions; whether trance, improvisation, cryptographic play, psychological dissociation, religious performance, or deliberate fraud best explains them.

### Content of the sessions

The records contain:

- the *Heptarchia Mystica*, concerning seven planetary rulers;
- the *Liber Loagaeth* or “Book of the Speech from God”;
- tables of letters;
- angelic names and seals;
- an alleged sacred language later called “Enochian” by occultists;
- political and apocalyptic predictions;
- directions for ritual equipment;
- admonitions, threats, and promises;
- extensive questioning by Dee.

“Enochian magic” is mainly a later systematization. Dee did not use that phrase as the standard name for a complete magical system.

The surviving manuscript **Sloane MS 3188** contains the early conferences and precedes material published by Meric Casaubon in 1659. Elias Ashmole made a later copy, Sloane MS 3677. [S2, S5]

### Voynich manuscript theory

A recurrent theory associates Dee or Kelley with the Voynich manuscript.

- **Theory:** they created it as a cipher, magical book, or fraudulent object for sale to Rudolf II.
- **Evidence offered:** their presence in Bohemia, cryptographic interests, Kelley’s reputation, and a report that Rudolf paid a large sum for an unidentified manuscript.
- **Evidence against:** no contemporary document securely identifies the Voynich manuscript with Dee or Kelley; manuscript dating and provenance do not demonstrate their authorship.
- **Status:** speculative and unproved.

## Central European Migration, 1583–1589

### Departure

In September 1583 Dee, Kelley, their households, and the Polish nobleman **Albert Łaski** left England. The route passed through the Low Countries and German lands toward Poland and Bohemia.

Łaski had visited England and cultivated Dee. He offered access to continental patrons but lacked the wealth necessary to support the enterprise for long.

### Kraków and Prague

The group stayed in Kraków and Prague and attempted to secure hearings from rulers, including Emperor Rudolf II and King Stephen Báthory of Poland.

Dee presented religious and political messages purportedly transmitted by angels. Such claims could appear heretical, politically presumptuous, or fraudulent.

Papal nuncio **Filippo Sega** investigated or denounced the men. Dee’s beliefs attracted Catholic suspicion, while his English and Protestant associations also complicated his position.

Rudolf II received Dee but did not establish him in the permanent, exalted role Dee sought. Patronage remained insecure.

### Třeboň and Vilém of Rožmberk

From 1586 Dee and Kelley received protection from **Vilém of Rožmberk** at Třeboň in Bohemia. Alchemical experiments became increasingly prominent.

Kelley claimed or was credited with metallic transmutations. No replicable evidence establishes that he produced gold by alchemical transformation. Such demonstrations could involve genuine metallurgical experimentation, sleight of hand, concealed gold, misunderstood assaying, or sincere error.

### The wife-sharing command, 1587

In April 1587 Kelley reported that the angel Madimi commanded Dee and Kelley to hold all things in common, including their wives.

Dee’s diary records distress, resistance, prayer, and eventual acceptance of a covenant. The surviving text strongly suggests that a sexual arrangement was at least formally agreed to, and possibly enacted. Shortly afterward Jane Dee became pregnant; Theodore was born in 1588.

- **Documented fact:** the command and covenant appear in Dee’s contemporary record.
- **Probable fact:** Dee, Kelley, Jane Dee, and Kelley’s wife Joan participated in a formal agreement.
- **Unresolved question:** whether intercourse actually occurred, how freely either woman consented, whether Kelley engineered the revelation, and Theodore’s biological paternity.
- **No evidentiary basis:** confident claims that Theodore was certainly Kelley’s son.
- **Historical effect:** the episode became the most damaging evidence against Kelley and a central modern controversy concerning manipulation, sexuality, consent, and Dee’s credulity.

The episode cannot safely be described as casual consensual “wife swapping.” It emerged from a claimed supernatural command in a relationship with substantial religious and interpersonal pressure.

### Separation from Kelley

The partnership deteriorated amid disputes over visions, alchemy, money, authority, and the sexual command. In January 1589 Dee transferred ritual and alchemical objects to Kelley for Rožmberk. Kelley left Třeboň for Prague.

They never met again, although correspondence continued for a time.

### Financial scale of the return

Dee said his return journey cost £796 and involved coaches, wagons, horses, servants, children, and books. The figure is his own claim but conveys the scale of the household migration.

He left Třeboň in March 1589, traveled through German territories and Bremen, and reached Gravesend on 2 December. Elizabeth received him at Richmond on 19 December.

## Mortlake Library Loss and Return, 1589–1595

When Dee returned to Mortlake, he found his library, papers, scientific equipment, and household property dispersed or damaged.

Older accounts describe a mob destroying the collection because Dee was considered a sorcerer. The evidence is more complicated:

- **Documented fact:** substantial losses occurred during Dee’s absence.
- **Dee’s allegation:** people entrusted with the property and other intruders misused, stole, sold, or damaged it.
- **Disputed embellishment:** a single dramatic anti-witchcraft mob destroying all 4,000 volumes.
- **Result:** Dee recovered perhaps three-quarters of his books but reported losses approaching £400.

The surviving annotated books are now dispersed among the Royal College of Physicians, British Library, Bodleian Library, Cambridge libraries, and other collections. Their marginalia provide major evidence for his reading practices. [S3, S4]

### *Compendious Rehearsal*, 1592

Dee delivered or prepared an autobiographical defense commonly called the *Compendious Rehearsal*. It catalogued his services, publications, manuscripts, expenses, offers, and injuries.

It is indispensable but interested evidence: Dee was petitioning for support and constructing a record of public usefulness against the label of conjuror.

### *Discourse Apologetical*, 1599

Dee published *To the King’s Most Excellent Majestie: A Compendious Rehearsall…* and related apologetic writings, including the *Discourse Apologetical*, defending himself as a Christian philosopher.

He denied dealing with devils and asked that his life and studies be assessed from his writings.

## Warden of Manchester, 1595–1605

In 1595 Dee received appointment as warden of the Collegiate Church of Manchester. He moved north with his family in 1596.

This was a substantial office but not an uncomplicated reward. The college suffered from disorder, contested property, weak finances, and religious division. Dee’s appointment was partly intended to impose discipline.

### Institutional conflicts

Dee clashed with fellows and local interests over:

- collegiate governance;
- conformity and worship;
- revenues and tithes;
- leases and encroachments;
- parish boundaries;
- the warden’s authority.

A surviving 1597 letter to William Langley concerns parish boundaries. Dee complained to Sir Edward Dyer about the “defamed & disordered” college.

He pursued property disputes in manorial and Duchy of Lancaster courts. The case involving Richard Heape and disputed college property extended to 1602.

- **Documented fact:** Dee engaged in litigation and institutional conflict.
- **No evidence of criminal conviction:** the proceedings concerned administration and property, not proven fraud by Dee.
- **Assessment:** his wardenship was both an attempt at reform and a failure to establish harmonious control.

By 1605 he had effectively abandoned Manchester and returned to Mortlake. [S15, S17]

### Family deaths

Michael died in 1594, before the Manchester move. Theodore died in 1601 and Margaret in 1603. Jane died around 1604/1605, commonly associated with plague in Manchester. Some daughters disappear from the record during the same epidemic period.

These losses coincided with Dee’s institutional defeat, declining patronage, and advancing age.

## Later Life, Poverty, and Death, 1603–1609

Elizabeth I died in 1603. James I’s accession did not revive Dee’s fortunes. James was personally hostile to witchcraft and demonic magic, although the exact degree to which this determined Dee’s treatment is uncertain.

In June 1604 Dee petitioned James to clear him publicly of accusations that he was a conjuror. He proposed submitting himself to extreme punishment if such accusations could be proved. No effective royal rehabilitation followed.

After leaving Manchester, Dee lived at Mortlake with surviving family members. Accounts describe increasing poverty. He sold or exchanged possessions and relied substantially upon support connected with Arthur Dee.

Claims that he died in complete abandonment should be qualified: he retained family, books, and some social contacts, but had lost the courtly access, household scale, and institutional hopes of earlier decades.

He probably died in **December 1608**. The traditional year **1609** reflects uncertainty in evidence and calendrical conventions. An exact day and medically documented cause of death are unknown. No reliable evidence identifies a specific final illness.

## Professions, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Finances

### Mathematics and education

Dee promoted mathematical literacy as a foundation for learned and practical arts. His Euclidean preface influenced English mathematical vocabulary and the social legitimacy of mechanics, surveying, perspective, architecture, and navigation.

### Instruments and technology

He collected or used:

- globes;
- astronomical rings;
- astrolabes and related instruments;
- mirrors and optical devices;
- navigational instruments;
- alchemical furnaces and vessels;
- wax seals and ritual furniture;
- crystals and polished “shew-stones.”

Objects attributed to Dee survive in museum collections, but individual provenance is sometimes late or uncertain. The famous obsidian mirror associated with him is Mexican in material origin; its secure chain of ownership back to Dee is incomplete, although it has long been identified with his collection.

### Navigation and cartography

Dee instructed seamen and advised promoters of voyages. His contribution was technical and ideological rather than operational command at sea.

### Library and information infrastructure

His Mortlake library was arguably his largest material project. It functioned as:

- personal research collection;
- lending library;
- archive;
- teaching site;
- meeting place;
- laboratory household;
- repository of maps and instruments.

No English institutional collection of the period clearly matched it in combined printed and manuscript holdings.

### Alchemy

Dee read alchemical literature and participated in experiments, especially in Bohemia. He did not leave evidence of a commercially successful transmutation business. His hopes for alchemy included medicine, natural knowledge, spiritual purification, patronage, and material relief.

### Property and income

Income came from family position, patronage gifts, consultations, ecclesiastical appointments, and possibly fees or exchanges associated with astrology and scholarship. He repeatedly sought benefices and institutional support.

There is no evidence that Dee was a major capitalist investor, monopolist, or proprietor of a large commercial company. His finances were household and patronage-based, chronically strained by unusually expensive scholarly ambitions.

## Political Activity and Intelligence Questions

### Court politics

Dee served Elizabethan state interests through geographical advice, antiquarian title research, calendar proposals, navigation, and court consultation. He was connected to William Cecil, Robert Dudley, Christopher Hatton, Francis Walsingham, Edward Dyer, and Philip Sidney’s intellectual circle.

The intensity of particular relationships varied, and “friend,” “patron,” “client,” and “political associate” should not be treated as interchangeable.

### Espionage

The idea that Dee was a formal secret agent is plausible in limited form but heavily mythologized.

Evidence supporting intelligence activity includes:

- international travel and access to foreign courts;
- connections to Walsingham’s and Cecil’s networks;
- expertise in cryptography and Trithemius;
- politically useful correspondence and geographical information;
- the routine overlap between scholarship, diplomacy, patronage, and intelligence in Elizabethan Europe.

Evidence lacking:

- no appointment naming Dee as a professional spy;
- no surviving series of intelligence reports signed “007”;
- no proof that the angelic diaries were an espionage cipher;
- no evidence that his entire continental mission was directed by Walsingham.

**Assessment:** occasional intelligence reporting or informal service is likely; the image of Dee as Elizabeth’s master spy is unproved.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Elizabeth I

Patron, recipient of advice, and occasional visitor. She valued Dee’s learning but never supplied the stable position he sought.

### William Cecil, Lord Burghley

Political intermediary and recipient of Dee’s proposals. Cecil could facilitate access but did not consistently support all Dee’s ambitions.

### Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester

Court patron and intermediary, including in astrological consultation.

### Sir Christopher Hatton

Dedicatee of *General and Rare Memorials* and prospective sponsor of maritime policy.

### Sir Edward Dyer

Friend and patron. Dyer remained important during Dee’s Manchester difficulties and had interests in alchemy.

### Sir Philip Sidney

Member of an overlapping courtly intellectual network. Evidence supports shared circles more strongly than an intimate lifelong friendship.

### Gerardus Mercator

Teacher, colleague, and major influence in Louvain cosmography, maps, globes, and instruments.

### Gemma Frisius

Formative mathematical and astronomical teacher or mentor.

### Albert Łaski

Polish nobleman whose invitation helped draw Dee and Kelley into Central Europe. His inability to sustain patronage contributed to their insecurity.

### Rudolf II

Imperial audience and hoped-for patron. Rudolf received Dee but did not make him a secure court philosopher.

### Vilém of Rožmberk

Bohemian protector at Třeboň and patron of alchemical activity.

### Edward Kelley

Scryer, collaborator, rival authority, and possible manipulator. Kelley mediated nearly all of Dee’s most elaborate claimed angelic revelations.

### Jane Dee

Spouse, household manager, migrant partner, mother of eight children, and participant under pressure in the 1587 covenant.

### Meric Casaubon

Posthumous editor. His 1659 publication preserved crucial material but framed it as evidence for the reality and danger of spirits rather than as a neutral documentary edition.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### 1. Treasonous astrology, 1555

- **Allegation:** Dee calculated royal horoscopes and engaged in conjuring against Mary I.
- **Documented:** arrest, interrogation, and imprisonment.
- **Outcome:** no known treason conviction.
- **Unresolved:** political intention and exact scope of the calculations.

### 2. Necromancy and demonic magic

- **Accusation:** Dee knowingly summoned devils.
- **Documented:** he sought spirit communications using scryers, prayers, seals, and a crystal.
- **Dee’s claim:** the entities were good angels sent by God.
- **Unresolved:** subjective or objective nature of the experiences.
- **Historical legal-religious point:** even if Dee believed the spirits angelic, authorities could classify such operations as unlawful conjuration.

### 3. Kelley’s possible fraud

- **Allegation:** Kelley fabricated visions and alchemical transmutations to control Dee and attract patrons.
- **Evidence for:** Kelley alone generally saw the entities; revelations served his interests; he repeatedly tried to end sessions; the wife-sharing command especially benefited or empowered him; no verified transmutation survives.
- **Evidence against a simple fraud model:** Kelley sometimes resisted the work, reported frightening or humiliating messages, and behaved inconsistently with a single clear financial scheme.
- **Status:** deliberate fraud is plausible but unproved.

### 4. Sexual coercion and the 1587 covenant

- **Documented:** an alleged angelic command to share wives and a written covenant.
- **Probable:** severe religious pressure upon all participants, particularly Jane and Joan.
- **Unknown:** actual sexual acts, consent, paternity consequences, and Kelley’s intent.
- **Later distortion:** both sensational retellings and euphemistic “wife-swapping” accounts erase the coercive structure recorded in the diary.

### 5. Imperial expansion

- **Documented:** Dee supplied intellectual arguments for English and “British” maritime sovereignty.
- **Historical consequence:** such concepts became part of the ideological repertoire of colonization.
- **Qualification:** Dee did not create the later British Empire single-handedly, personally govern colonies, or originate every argument he used.

### 6. Fabricated genealogy

- **Documented:** Dee claimed royal Welsh descent.
- **Disputed:** the genealogical accuracy.
- **Possible motives:** sincere antiquarian belief, Tudor-Welsh identification, imperial argument, and status-seeking.

### 7. Library destruction

- **Documented:** extensive theft and dispersal.
- **Disputed:** whether a superstitious mob was chiefly responsible.
- **Likely:** cumulative appropriation, negligent custody, vandalism, and unauthorized sale during a six-year absence.

### 8. Manchester litigation and governance

- **Documented:** prolonged property and authority disputes.
- **No substantiated criminal finding:** surviving evidence does not establish that Dee embezzled college property.
- **Competing interpretations:** conscientious reformer obstructed by corrupt fellows; impractical outsider unable to manage a damaged institution. Elements of both may be true.

### 9. Psychological illness

Some older writers interpreted the angelic records as evidence of insanity.

- **Documented:** no contemporary medical diagnosis.
- **Evidence:** intense religious experiences, credulity, anxiety, dreams, fasting or prayer, and dependence on scryers.
- **Insufficient inference:** these do not establish psychosis retrospectively.
- **Status:** psychological diagnosis is speculative.

### 10. Dedicated recent adversarial-search result

Searches combining Dee’s name with *controversy*, *scandal*, *allegations*, *fraud*, *lawsuit*, *accusations*, *conspiracy*, and *criticism*, including 2024–2026 results, produced no new archival indictment, lawsuit, or historical discovery materially changing the established dossier. Recent discussion continues to center on Kelley’s possible fraud, the sexual covenant, imperial ideology, espionage mythology, the Voynich manuscript, and “007.” Many recent search results concern unrelated modern people named John or Dee and were excluded.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### Dee as “the original 007”

**Story:** Dee served as Elizabeth’s secret agent and signed reports “007,” with two circles representing his eyes and 7 as an occult lucky number. Ian Fleming supposedly adopted the designation for James Bond.

**Appearance and circulation:** the story became popular through twentieth-century biographies, especially writings presenting Dee as a secret agent, and later through press articles and Internet repetition.

**Evidence:** Dee did use an elaborate sign resembling two circles and an angular mark in some communications.

**Evidence against:** manuscript specialist Katherine Birkwood found no basis for reading it as the number 007 or as Dee’s standard espionage signature. The Ian Fleming connection is not securely documented.

**Status:** modern myth built around a misread manuscript mark. [S23, S24]

### Prospero in *The Tempest*

**Story:** Shakespeare modeled Prospero on Dee—the learned magician with a great library, political connections, command of spirits, and concern with navigation.

**Evidence for:** thematic parallels; Dee’s public reputation; the play’s date shortly after Dee’s death.

**Evidence against:** no surviving statement by Shakespeare identifies Dee as the model; Prospero also draws on literary magicians, Neoplatonism, colonial narratives, and the conventional figure of the exiled ruler-scholar.

**Status:** plausible influence or cultural resonance, not proven one-to-one identification. [S25]

### Dee as Shakespeare, editor of Shakespeare, or secret Rosicrucian controller

Later occult and authorship theories claim Dee wrote Shakespeare’s works, encoded secret geometry in them, founded Rosicrucianism, or directed a hidden intellectual fraternity.

No adequate documentary evidence establishes these claims. Similarity of imagery or shared Renaissance sources does not demonstrate authorship or organizational control.

### Dee and the Spanish Armada

Popular narratives sometimes claim Dee magically defeated the Armada by conjuring storms.

- **Documented:** storms contributed to Spanish losses; Dee had court and naval interests.
- **No evidence:** that he performed a weather ritual causing the storms.
- **Status:** later magical-nationalist legend.

### The obsidian mirror

A polished obsidian mirror in the British Museum has long been associated with Dee and is made from Mexican obsidian.

- **Documented:** the object is early modern and entered later collections with an attribution to Dee.
- **Uncertain:** an uninterrupted provenance showing Dee owned or used it.
- **Mythic importance:** it materially links popular images of Dee, New World conquest, optical wonder, and spirit vision.

### Kelley’s red powder and transmutation

Stories say Kelley found alchemical powder in a ruined abbey or tomb and used it to transform base metals into gold before witnesses.

The accounts are late, inconsistent, and tied to alchemical legend. No surviving sample or controlled documentation proves transmutation.

### Spirits as intelligence code

**Theory:** the spirit diaries concealed encrypted political reports.

Trithemius’s influence and Dee’s intelligence contacts make the theory attractive, but no accepted decryption demonstrates that the diaries constitute a systematic espionage cipher. The records also contain extensive domestic, devotional, sexual, and repetitive material poorly explained by that hypothesis.

### “Enochian” as an objectively nonhuman language

Occult traditions regard the transmitted language as primordial angelic speech.

Linguists and historians can document its vocabulary, tables, textual production, and later ritual use but cannot verify supernatural origin. Its complex generation may reflect designed language, cryptographic procedures, glossolalia-like production, collaborative invention, or processes not fully recoverable.

### Faustian Dee

Seventeenth-century and later writers increasingly assimilated Dee to the Faust legend: a proud scholar who traded orthodox faith for forbidden power. Casaubon’s edition helped define him through his spirit records. This hostile mythology preserved evidence that might otherwise have disappeared while narrowing his reputation to conjuration.

## Works by John Dee

The following distinguishes contemporary publications, manuscripts, contributions, later editions, and disputed attributions. Spelling varies between copies and catalogues.

### Works published during Dee’s life

1. **Propaedeumata Aphoristica** (London, 1558). Latin aphorisms on celestial and natural influence.
2. **Propædeumata Aphoristica**, revised edition (London, 1568).
3. **Monas Hieroglyphica** (Antwerp, 1564). Latin; printed by Willem Silvius.
4. **“Mathematicall Praeface”** to Henry Billingsley’s English translation, *The Elements of Geometrie of the Most Auncient Philosopher Euclide of Megara* (London, 1570). Genuine contribution; Dee was not sole translator.
5. **Parallaticae Commentationis Praxeosque Nucleus Quidam** (London, 1573), on astronomical parallax.
6. **General and Rare Memorials Pertayning to the Perfect Arte of Navigation** (London, dated 1577; printing history sometimes associated with 1576). First published installment of an unfinished series.
7. **A Letter… Containing a Most Briefe Discourse Apologeticall** (London, 1599), defense of his studies and religious intentions.

### Major manuscript works and tracts

8. **The British Monarchy / Brytish Impire materials** (principally 1570s), including maps, genealogical and territorial arguments.
9. **Of Famous and Rich Discoveries** (1577), surviving incompletely or through manuscript witnesses.
10. **Unto Your Majestie’s Tytle Royall to These Forene Regions and Ilands** (c.1580), manuscript argument concerning Elizabeth’s territorial title.
11. **Cathay voyage instructions** (1580), directions associated with Muscovy Company planning.
12. **Calendar-reform treatises** (1582–1583), manuscript proposals.
13. **The Limits of the British Empire**, title used for related territorial materials.
14. **Compendious Rehearsal of John Dee His Dutiful Declaration and Proof of the Course and Race of His Studious Life** (1592), autobiographical petition; manuscript, later edited.
15. **Petition to King James I** (5 June 1604), printed as a broadside or preserved through later reproduction.
16. **John Dee’s library catalogue**, compiled and revised across his life; an essential source for his collection and reading.
17. **Private diary**, principally surviving for 1577–1601, with gaps; later edited.
18. **Genealogical pedigree**, asserting Welsh royal descent.
19. **Maps and geographical papers**, numerous items surviving separately or through references.

### Angelic or “spirit-action” manuscripts

20. **Mysteriorum Liber Primus** (begun 1581).
21. **Mysteriorum Liber Secundus**.
22. **Mysteriorum Liber Tertius**.
23. **Mysteriorum Liber Quartus**.
24. **Mysteriorum Liber Quintus**.
25. **Liber Mysteriorum Sextus et Sanctus** (1583).
26. **De Heptarchia Mystica**, in evolving manuscript forms.
27. **Liber Loagaeth** (“Book of the Speech from God”), letter tables and related material.
28. **Tabula Sancta / Holy Table materials**.
29. **Action records from Kraków, Prague, and Třeboň**, 1583–1587.
30. **A True & Faithful Relation of What Passed for Many Yeers Between Dr John Dee…and Some Spirits** (London, 1659), edited posthumously by Meric Casaubon. Dee authored or recorded the underlying diaries; Casaubon selected, arranged, titled, and polemically framed the publication.

### Other attributed or lost works

Dee’s own inventories list numerous treatises on astronomy, navigation, optics, alchemy, chronology, the tides, military matters, and territorial claims. Some are lost, survive only as fragments, or cannot be matched securely to extant manuscripts.

A **Treatise of the Rosie Crucian Secrets** has been attributed to Dee in manuscript catalogues, but the title and transmission require caution: “Rosicrucian” framing may be later than Dee’s original work.

Translations of Trithemius’s *Steganographia* in Dee’s hand are copying or translation work, not original authorship of Trithemius’s text. [S4, S5, S6]

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Seventeenth century

Meric Casaubon’s 1659 *True & Faithful Relation* made the spirit diaries central to Dee’s memory. Casaubon intended the book as evidence that spirits were real and that illicit curiosity exposed learned people to demonic deception.

Elias Ashmole copied and collected Dee material, preserving manuscripts important to later occult and antiquarian study.

### Enlightenment and nineteenth century

Dee was often treated as an example of how great learning could coexist with credulity. Antiquarians nevertheless valued his diaries, library catalogue, and historical papers.

Nineteenth-century occult revivalists increasingly appropriated the angelic manuscripts.

### Golden Dawn and modern ceremonial magic

The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn and later occultists transformed portions of Dee and Kelley’s material into an organized “Enochian” magical system. This involved selection, rearrangement, new correspondences, and ritual innovation.

Consequently, what practitioners now call Enochian magic is historically related to Dee’s records but is not identical to Dee’s own procedures or theology.

### History of science

Historians first recovered Dee as an early scientist, mathematician, and navigator, sometimes treating his angelic work as an embarrassing deviation. From the late twentieth century, scholarship increasingly reconstructed the unity of his natural philosophy, religion, politics, and occult practice.

### Imperial history

Dee is now studied as an important theorist of British maritime sovereignty and expansion. His use of Arthurian and Welsh materials shows how medieval legend, cartography, law, and colonial ambition could be combined.

### Book history

His library catalogue and surviving marginalia make him one of the best-documented Renaissance readers. They reveal borrowing, annotation, cross-referencing, compilation, and the household circulation of knowledge.

### Popular occult culture

Dee’s glyph, crystal, seals, angelic alphabet, and association with Elizabeth retain strong symbolic power. His popular image is frequently a composite of:

- the historical mathematician;
- Faust;
- Prospero;
- court astrologer;
- alchemist;
- spy;
- wizard;
- imperial strategist.

No single component adequately represents the whole record.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by consulted sources are included. Mere chronological overlap is excluded.

### Edward Kelley — documented fact

Dee’s principal scryer and collaborator from 1582 to 1589. They conducted recorded spirit sessions at Mortlake and across Poland and Bohemia, lived under shared patronage, pursued alchemical experiments, entered the 1587 “common wives” covenant, and separated at Třeboň/Prague in 1589.

### Frances Amelia Yates — documented posthumous intellectual connection

Yates was not a contemporary acquaintance. Her twentieth-century scholarship substantially reshaped Dee’s historiography by placing him within Renaissance Hermeticism, occult philosophy, and the intellectual background of Rosicrucianism. Later scholars revised but did not erase her influence.

### Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa von Nettesheim — documented intellectual influence

Dee owned, read, and worked within traditions strongly shaped by Agrippa’s *De occulta philosophia*. There was no personal meeting: Agrippa died when Dee was eight. The connection is textual and intellectual.

### Johannes Reuchlin — documented intellectual influence

Reuchlin’s Christian Cabalistic works formed part of the learned tradition behind Dee’s symbolic theology and *Monas Hieroglyphica*. No meeting occurred; Reuchlin died in 1522.

### Johannes Trithemius — documented intellectual influence

Dee sought, copied, and studied Trithemius’s *Steganographia*. Its mixture of cryptographic technique and apparently angelic language influenced his interests in secret communication. No personal meeting was possible; Trithemius died in 1516.

### Nicolaus Copernicus — documented intellectual connection through texts

Dee engaged with contemporary mathematical astronomy in which Copernicus was a major reference. No personal encounter is documented. Dee’s cosmological position cannot be reduced to straightforward Copernicanism.

### Roger Bacon — documented intellectual influence

Dee regarded the medieval English Franciscan as an important predecessor in mathematics, optics, natural philosophy, and reform. The relationship is textual and retrospective.

### Ramon Llull — documented intellectual influence

Llullian combinatory and encyclopedic traditions contributed to the intellectual environment of Dee’s universal and symbolic projects. The link is textual; no personal connection was possible.

### Giovanni Pico della Mirandola — documented intellectual influence

Pico’s synthesis of Platonism, Cabala, and Christian theology formed part of the background to Dee’s thought. This was a textual, not personal, relationship.

### Francis Bacon — reported shared intellectual and court environment; direct relationship not established here

Both belonged to Elizabethan learned and courtly culture, but the consulted evidence does not securely establish a major meeting, collaboration, correspondence, or teacher-student relationship. No stronger Atlas connection is asserted.

### Giordano Bruno — alleged or theoretical connection

Dee and Bruno moved through intersecting European occult, courtly, and intellectual worlds, and later literature frequently pairs them. The sources consulted for this dossier do not establish a documented personal meeting or correspondence. The connection remains comparative rather than biographical.

### Robert Fludd — reported intellectual succession, not a documented personal relationship

Fludd’s later Hermetic and cosmological work is frequently discussed beside Dee’s. Direct personal contact is not established; the connection belongs principally to intellectual history.

### William Shakespeare — disputed influence

Dee has often been proposed as a model for Prospero. The resemblance is historically significant, but there is no documentary proof of a personal meeting or of Shakespeare’s deliberate one-person model.

### Sir Isaac Newton — posthumous field connection only

Newton later pursued alchemy, biblical chronology, and natural philosophy, leading historians to compare him with Dee. No direct personal link was possible, and no specific documentary transmission is asserted here.

### Remaining roster

No supported personal or clearly traceable intellectual connection was identified between Dee and most members of the supplied atlas roster. Their absence is a finding; shared interests in magic, mathematics, religion, imperialism, or esotericism alone were not treated as evidence.
