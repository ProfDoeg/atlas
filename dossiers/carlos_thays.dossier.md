# Carlos Thays: Research Dossier

**Subject:** Jules Charles Thays, generally known in Argentina as Carlos Thays  
**Life:** 20 August 1849–31 January 1934  
**Principal fields:** landscape design, horticulture, municipal parks administration, botany, urban planning, forestry and conservation  
**Geographical center:** France until 1889; Argentina from 1889 until his death, with projects and professional travel elsewhere in South America and Europe  
**Research status:** completed 20 August 2026

## Evidence and attribution note

The documentary record is uneven. Thays left plans, reports, photographs, articles, a major 1910 book and a large professional archive, but there is no modern, fully digitized critical edition of all of his papers. The indispensable modern scholar is Sonia Berjman, who edited his writings, catalogued his work, curated the principal retrospective exhibition and helped arrange the donation of the family archive to the City of Buenos Aires. Municipal histories, later family recollections and popular profiles often derive from her work without showing their full chain of citation.

This dossier uses the following distinctions:

- **Documented design or report:** supported by a dated plan, published report, official commission, archival catalogue or specialist research.
- **Municipal execution under Thays:** work built or altered by the Dirección de Paseos during his directorship; this does not always prove that every element was personally drawn by him.
- **Remodeling:** Thays altered an existing park; it should not be described as his original creation.
- **Attributed:** repeated in official or secondary sources, but the underlying plan or contract was not located in the sources consulted.
- **Disputed:** serious sources conflict or specialist research questions the familiar attribution.
- **Family recollection / later legend:** valuable for reputation and private life, but not equivalent to a contemporary record.

Exact project totals vary because sources count spaces, phases and lost works differently. The Buenos Aires Historical Archive says he **created, remodeled or enlarged 69** public plazas and promenades in Buenos Aires and worked on **16** provincial projects; Berjman’s later profile also estimates roughly fifty estancia parks and forty residential gardens. Tourist claims that he designed “80 percent” or “90 percent” of all present-day Buenos Aires green spaces are not a stable archival statistic and should not replace the project record.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and childhood household

- **Father:** Simon (also given as Simon Joseph) Thays, a typographer of Belgian origin established in Paris.
- **Mother:** Ernestina/Ernestine Paris, from a commercial family of Versailles.
- **Siblings:** Berjman describes Jules Charles as the couple’s **only child**. No siblings have been documented in the consulted sources.
- **Orphanhood:** His father reportedly died when Thays was seven. The wording “orphaned at seven” in later profiles can misleadingly suggest that both parents died; the available narrative specifically establishes the father’s early death but does not establish the mother’s death at that date.
- **Baptism:** reportedly baptized Jules Charles at the parish of Saint-Germain-des-Prés at age ten. The unusual delay is repeated in Berjman’s account; the actual parish register image was not located during this research.

The combination of a printing household, early paternal death and practical rather than university education is relevant to his formation. Claims that he was born into a gardening dynasty are false: the landscape-design dynasty began with him.

### Marriage to Cora Venturino

His wife was **Petrona Cora Venturino Rodríguez**, usually called **Cora Venturino**, born about 1874 into a family described as Uruguayan. Later family histories say they met at a Buenos Aires kermesse when he was about forty-one and she sixteen. Two chronological versions circulate:

1. Popular profiles say they married in **1892** at the Church of Nuestra Señora de la Piedad; this would make him forty-two or forty-three and her about eighteen.
2. A genealogical index gives **30 November 1893, Buenos Aires**. This is more precise, but the underlying civil or parish image was not independently inspected. It may identify a civil or sacramental entry distinct from the date remembered in family tradition.

The age difference—about twenty-five years—is documented at the level of birth chronology. The language of an instantaneous and idyllic “love story” belongs largely to later family and journalistic retelling. There is no located evidence of coercion, scandal, separation or another partner. Cora accompanied him on work outings, and later accounts place her on horseback with him in Misiones. Photographs document the couple together, including at the 1911 Gran Premio Pellegrini.

### Children and descendants

- **Ernestina Thays Venturino:** daughter. Her exact birth and death dates were not securely established in the sources consulted.
- **Carlos León Ernesto Thays Venturino (21 August 1894–14 December 1962):** son; engineer-agronomist and later Director General de Paseos of Buenos Aires, approximately 1922–1946. He married Luisa López Saavedra and had Carlos Julio, Julia Elena and Cora Martha according to later genealogical accounts.
- **Carlos Julio Thays (1926–2017):** grandson; private landscape practitioner and later president of Argentina’s National Parks administration.
- **Carlos Thays (born 1959):** great-grandson; agronomist and landscape practitioner, active professionally from 1978 according to the family practice.

The family’s practice of naming successive male landscape designers Carlos produced chronic confusion in catalogs, web pages and project lists. Later work by Carlos León or Carlos Julio is frequently projected backward onto the founder. The family studio’s own history distinguishes the generations ([Thays y Asociados](https://estudiothays.com.ar/historia/)).

### Domestic life

From **1892 to 1898**, Thays and his family lived in the brick, English picturesque/Gothic Revival house inside the developing Botanical Garden. The building predated his garden and had served the Department of Agriculture and National Historical Museum; it was not designed as his private “castle.” Family recollection depicts Cora and the children bringing a picnic basket while he supervised work. “Hup, hup,” supposedly Cora’s call for him to pause, became a family nickname for the grandfather. This detail is family folklore, not independently documented contemporaneous testimony.

No securely documented later residential address, real-estate holdings, probate inventory or testament was located.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1849–c. 1870

Thays was born in mid-nineteenth-century Paris during the Second Republic, shortly before the transformation of Paris under Napoleon III, Prefect Georges-Eugène Haussmann and engineer Adolphe Alphand. His childhood therefore coincided with the creation of the Bois de Boulogne, Bois de Vincennes, Parc des Buttes-Chaumont, street-tree system and neighborhood squares that made public landscape a central arm of modern municipal government.

The record of his childhood is sparse. There are no located school registers, diaries or memoirs describing his early education. Later biographers characterize him as an **autodidact**, but this means he lacked a formal professional degree, not that he was without rigorous training. He entered the professional world through practical instruction and employment.

Claims that he personally helped remake imperial Paris alongside Alphand and André need qualification. André worked in the Paris parks service and within the Alphand-Barillet-Deschamps network during the 1860s; Thays, ten years younger than André, was still a child when much of the principal Haussmann-era park system was planned. Thays inherited that professional culture through André and may have worked on later Parisian or European projects, but presenting the three men as equal co-authors of Napoleon III’s Paris is commemorative compression.

## Education and Formation

### Practical formation with Édouard François André

Thays’s decisive mentor was **Édouard François André (1840–1911)**, horticulturist, botanist, editor and landscape designer, author of *L’Art des jardins: traité général de la composition des parcs et jardins* (1879). Thays worked with him for more than a decade, described variously as pupil, disciple, employee, assistant or secretary. No dated apprenticeship contract was located, and the exact European projects on which Thays personally worked remain incompletely reconstructed.

André’s influence can be traced in method rather than merely style:

- the **mixed style**, combining geometrical/formal areas near architecture with curving picturesque treatment in the wider park;
- attention to circulation, framed vistas, water, rockwork, bridges and garden architecture;
- horticultural experimentation and acclimatization;
- integration of botanical knowledge with design;
- the belief that public parks served hygiene, recreation and civic education;
- international practice based on plans, travel, journalism and botanical exchange.

Thays also entered André’s publishing world. He wrote or collaborated for approximately ten years in the **Revue Horticole** and in the journal of the Société Nationale d’Horticulture de France. Articles collected by Berjman cover park barriers, new horticultural varieties, winter-flowering orchids, Bouvardias, *Masdevallia*, *Correa*, useful plants, exhibitions, trees, cemeteries and parks. The [CPAU catalogue of Berjman’s critical compilation](https://cpau.opac.com.ar/pergamo/documento.php?id=CPAU.1.14595&recno=14595&ui=1) is the clearest accessible guide to this corpus.

### Other formative figures

- **Adolphe Alphand (1817–1891):** chief engineer and director of works in Paris. The usual account says André recommended Thays to Alphand, who in turn recommended him to Miguel Crisol. Some versions reverse or collapse the chain. The secure point is that Thays reached the Córdoba commission through the André–Alphand professional network.
- **Jean-Pierre Barillet-Deschamps:** the principal landscape gardener of Second Empire Paris and André’s early superior; an indirect rather than documented personal mentor.
- **Haussmann:** an institutional and urban model rather than an established personal relationship.
- **French horticultural journalism and exhibitions:** provided training in plants, technologies, professional publicity and an international network outside university education.

## Early Career in France, c. 1870–1889

The European phase is the least fully reconstructed portion of Thays’s career. Available sources establish long service with André and a decade of horticultural journalism, but do not provide a reliable job-by-job itinerary. Later claims that he “built famous gardens throughout Europe” are plausible at the level of André’s studio work but often omit the distinction between André’s authorship and Thays’s assisting role.

By the 1880s, however, Thays was publishing under his own name and had sufficient standing to be chosen for an overseas public-park contract. His writings show competence in greenhouse culture, orchids, ornamental horticulture, park equipment and broader landscape questions. A “verja-barrera para parque y jardín” appears in the collected writings; no patent record was found, so it should be treated as a design or technical proposal, not a patented invention.

## Chronological Life History

### 1888–1891: Contract, migration and Parque Sarmiento in Córdoba

Argentine entrepreneur and real-estate developer **Miguel Crisol** planned the expansion called Nueva Córdoba and required a large park. In 1888 the French professional network recommended Thays. He signed a contract for roughly one year and arrived in Argentina in **June 1889**, aged thirty-nine. A surviving general plan signed **Córdoba, October 1889** documents his authorship of the park then called Parque Crisol, later **Parque Sarmiento**. Carlos A. Page’s archival study identifies this as Thays’s first Argentine work ([study record and plan discussion](https://www.academia.edu/84492583/Carlos_Thays_y_el_Parque_Sarmiento_de_C%C3%B3rdoba)).

The park was inseparable from Crisol’s speculative urban extension: landscape amenity, boulevard planning and land development operated together. Thays reportedly formed good relations with provincial officials and widened his Argentine network while executing it. Later narratives say he intended to return to France when the contract ended.

In Buenos Aires, Mayor **Francisco Bollini** sought him for the city’s parks service. Family-derived accounts say Thays refused a direct appointment and insisted on an open competition. Seven candidates reportedly entered; he won unanimously on his record and proposed program. The moral—merit over patronage—is prominent in later hagiography, but the competition and his successful candidacy are part of the municipal record.

He assumed office on **15 May 1891**.

### 1891–1895: Building a municipal system

Thays inherited work begun by earlier French professionals, especially **Eugène Courtois**, and a city expanding explosively after federalization and mass immigration. He did not introduce the idea of the green public square from nothing. His achievement was to enlarge, systematize and institutionalize the network.

His competition report—surviving in draft and later published by Berjman—treated:

- gardens in general and the “economy of vegetation”;
- organization of the municipal parks service;
- maintenance of existing plazas;
- new parks and plazas;
- destructive insects;
- public-park policy;
- trees as instruments of shade, ornament and hygiene.

Between 1891 and 1895 the Dirección de Paseos completed **twenty-two new promenades**, compared with twenty-seven existing ones in the municipal count. Thays also expanded street planting. A commonly cited figure says that 21,250 street trees were planted in 1891; Berjman later summarized a total of about **150,000** street trees during his tenure. These figures describe municipal output under his leadership, not trees personally planted by his hands.

The program served both the ceremonial center and peripheral working neighborhoods. It combined formal civic squares with large picturesque parks and neighborhood recreation. Facilities included children’s areas, music kiosks, athletic grounds, public lavatories, paths, bridges, lakes and fountains. Public greenery was simultaneously understood as beauty, sanitary infrastructure, regulated recreation, property-value enhancement and civic formation.

Important early actions included work at Parque 3 de Febrero, Barrancas de Belgrano, Plaza Constitución, Plaza de Mayo, Plaza San Martín and new southern or western parks. He also designed or supervised gardens for hospitals, military installations and public institutions.

#### Botanical Garden proposal

On **22 February 1892**, Thays formally proposed an acclimatization botanical garden for scientific, educational, recreational and landscape purposes. He recommended the present Palermo site, then occupied by national institutions. Work ran from 1892 to 1898; the family lived in the central house during this period.

#### Marriage and family

The standard family account puts his marriage to Cora in 1892; the precise genealogical index gives November 1893. Their son Carlos León was born in August 1894. The family was therefore formed in the same years that the municipal institution and Botanical Garden were taking shape.

#### Provincial commissions

He designed **Parque Santa Ana** for the Hileret sugar-estate complex in Tucumán (1894) and **Parque Urquiza** at Paraná, whose municipal history associates his plan with the 1895 inauguration. Execution involved municipal engineers and the gardener Julio Kumagae, illustrating how a “Thays park” could be a collaborative and phased work ([Municipality of Paraná](https://www.parana.gob.ar/ciudadymemoria/parque-urquiza)).

### 1895–1898: Yerba mate, Mendoza and the Botanical Garden

#### Yerba mate experiments

In **1895**, Honorio Leguizamón supplied seeds and cuttings of *Ilex paraguariensis* from Paraguay. Cuttings failed. Thays experimented with the notoriously recalcitrant seeds and achieved germination after extended treatment in hot water, imitating scarification in animal digestion. The method was published through Paraguay’s agricultural society and later endorsed and circulated by Argentine agricultural authorities. The Botanical Garden’s account connects the work to reducing dependence on imports and destructive harvesting of wild yerbales ([official Garden history](https://buenosaires.gob.ar/gcaba_historico/vicejefatura/ambiente/jardin-botanico/colecciones/el-yerbal)).

Thays did not “discover yerba mate,” invent its Indigenous or Jesuit cultivation, or single-handedly create the Argentine industry. Guaraní communities had used and cultivated mate; Jesuit plantations had operated at scale; Aimé Bonpland and others had studied the plant. Thays’s documented contribution was a successful, replicable modern germination treatment and its diffusion at a commercially important moment.

#### Parque del Oeste, Mendoza

In **1896**, after provincial legislation authorized a great western park, Mendoza commissioned Thays. The project answered multiple aims: sanitation after epidemics, alluvial defense, urban expansion, civic display and elite recreation. The official provincial history describes a 394-hectare irrigated landscape; other counts separate 307 cultivated hectares and auxiliary land. Thays’s curving roads, lake, plantations and framed Andean views relied on the much older Huarpe and colonial irrigation landscape. Brian Davis’s study explicitly places the celebrated European designer within 2,500 years of local hydrological knowledge ([study](https://thecela.org/wp-content/uploads/Landscape%20Research%20Record%20No.02/A-CASE-STUDY-IN-HYDROLOGY-AND-CULTURAL-IDENTITY-2%2C500-YEARS-OF-LANDSCAPE-MAKING-IN-MENDOZA%2C-ARGENTINA.pdf)).

The park was politically controversial at inception. Government supporters presented it as public-health infrastructure; opponents argued that an expensive monumental park served elites while sewers, drinking water and acequias required funds. This was a dispute over the project and public expenditure, not an allegation of personal corruption by Thays. The Province of Mendoza dates the park’s creation to 1896 ([official history](https://www.mendoza.gov.ar/prensa/la-historia-arquitectonica-del-parque-general-san-martin/)). It became Parque General San Martín in the twentieth century.

#### Opening of the Botanical Garden

The Buenos Aires Botanical Garden opened on **7 September 1898**. Its approximately 7–8 hectares combined:

- geographic collections representing continents and Argentine regions;
- systematic groupings by botanical family;
- useful, medicinal, food, textile and ornamental plants;
- Roman, French/formal and picturesque landscape idioms;
- nurseries, herbarium, library and greenhouses;
- a large native-flora section, unusual in elite Argentine gardening at the time.

One iron-and-glass greenhouse had been exhibited in Paris in 1889 and reassembled in Buenos Aires. It was not designed by Thays, though he incorporated it. The site later became the principal public monument to him. Its current official historical account emphasizes the combination of design and scientific acclimatization ([National Monuments Commission](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/jardin-botanico-carlos-thays)).

### 1898–1904: National reputation

Thays directed the agronomy thesis of **Benito Javier Carrasco**, *Fitogeografía de varios árboles indígenas cultivados en el Jardín Botánico Municipal* (1900). Carrasco became his most important disciple, later directed the Buenos Aires parks service and established academic landscape instruction. Distinguishing their works matters: Carrasco, not Thays, created the Rosedal in Palermo in 1914.

In 1900 Thays worked on Parque 20 de Febrero in Salta. In 1901 *Caras y Caretas* published José María Cao’s caricature of him (issue 169, 28 December), giving wide currency to **“el jardinero de la Nación.”** The image is direct evidence that Thays had become a recognizable public figure, not merely a municipal technician ([caricature record](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archivo:Carlos_Thays_Caras_y_caretas.jpg)).

In **1901**, the mayor of São Luís do Maranhão, Brazil, reportedly received from Thays a proposed embellishment plan for the Largo do Palácio/Praça Pedro II and waterfront ramp. A contemporary *Diário do Maranhão* notice said the plan was offered by the director of the Buenos Aires Botanical Garden and displayed publicly. Brazilian scholarship has found evidence of partial implementation but stops short of treating the entire later plaza as securely his work ([USP study](https://revistas.usp.br/paam/article/view/85694/88454)).

#### First Iguazú expedition, 1902

The national government and Misiones governor **Juan José Lanusse** commissioned Thays to survey Iguazú in 1902. He arrived on 6 April and reportedly remained about two months. He mapped access, tourist facilities, viewpoints, footbridges and a settlement while arguing for preservation of the falls and forest. This was both conservation and territorial development: the project sought tourism, national presence and integration of a sparsely controlled border region.

The National Parks administration confirms the sequence: 1902 study; 1909 legal reservation of surrounding lands; a renewed extended report in 1912; purchase of 75,000 hectares in 1928; and statutory creation of Parque Nacional Iguazú in 1934 ([official National Parks history](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/parquesnacionales/que-hacemos-en-parques-nacionales/historia)).

In Buenos Aires, **Parque Patricios** opened in 1902 as part of the transformation of a district marked by slaughterhouses, refuse and epidemic burial grounds. The park system carried sanitation, land-use change and social-order aims. Its construction in a working district complicates the notion that Thays worked only for the oligarchy.

Rosario’s **Parque de la Independencia** also opened in 1902. Municipal tourism attributes its plan to Thays ([Rosario tourism](https://rosario.tur.ar/donde-ir/rio-y-naturaleza/parque-de-la-independencia)); specialist urban history has questioned this, noting the absence of documentary support and emphasizing Mayor Luis Lamas, municipal engineering, land purchases and mass tree planting. The attribution should remain **disputed**, not silently accepted or rejected.

### 1904–1910: Honors, regional practice and the Centennial city

Thays was made an honorary member of the **Sociedad Central de Arquitectos** in 1904. In 1906 France reportedly made him a **Chevalier of the Ordre du Mérite Agricole**; he also received the Tunisian **Order of Nichan Iftikhar**, commonly given as commander. The exact award instruments were not located, though the honors are repeated in specialist biographical literature.

#### Mar del Plata

He worked on Plaza Colón, Paseo General Paz and the seafront/Boulevard Marítimo, and later on Parque Camet and private grounds. Much of the earlier seafront landscape was demolished when the Casino and Hotel Provincial complex was built in the late 1930s. Conflicting web histories give 1903 or 1938 for its “disappearance”; the former cannot describe demolition for the 1930s complex and is evidently erroneous or refers to a different alteration.

#### Montevideo and Uruguay, c. 1905–1912

Uruguayan government and municipal clients commissioned a group of plans and interventions:

- Parque Urbano, now **Parque José Enrique Rodó**, with Carlos Racine involved in execution or development;
- **Parque Central**, now Parque Batlle y Ordóñez;
- Plaza Independencia and Plaza Libertad/Cagancha;
- Bulevar Artigas and other planting/ornamentation;
- gardens of the Idiarte Borda residence;
- later the picturesque plan for the resort suburb of **Carrasco**.

The University of the Republic repository dates the principal group to 1905–1912 ([La memoria de Thays en Montevideo](https://repositorio.fadu.edu.uy/items/67328ff4-135b-49ba-82ad-9a42eebda7ad)); Montevideo itself commemorates his intervention in Carrasco, Parque Rodó, Parque Batlle and Plaza Independencia with Plaza Thays ([municipal page](https://municipioe.montevideo.gub.uy/node/200)). Authorship is often collaborative and phased, especially with Racine and municipal offices.

#### The 1908 city-plan commission

On **6 July 1908**, Mayor Manuel Güiraldes appointed Thays to the **Comisión del Plano de Buenos Aires**, charged with a comprehensive study of traffic, beautification and sanitation. This placed him within formal urban planning, not merely park design. He participated in debates over a system of parks, forested avenues and urban extension during preparations for the 1910 centennial.

#### Tucumán

Thays designed the great **Parque Centenario**, now Parque 9 de Julio. Sources variously date the commission or design to 1908–1910; the foundation stone was laid in 1910, and the northern half opened for the 1916 centennial of independence. Only part of the full plan was built. The design used an oval center, curved circulation, native and exotic planting, and sanitary reclamation of a malarial eastern lowland. An official monument description provides the plan and chronology ([Argentina.gob.ar](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/capital-humano/cultura/monumentos/parque-9-de-julio)).

#### 1910 book

For the national centennial, the municipality published **_El Jardín botánico de Buenos Aires_** through Jacobo Peuser. The 180-page illustrated volume describes the institution’s foundation, plan and collections. It is both an administrative-scientific report and Thays’s principal book. A complete public-domain scan is held by the [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/eljardnbotni00buen) and the [Biodiversity Heritage Library](https://www.biodiversitylibrary.org/item/55995).

### 1910–1913: Conservation, urbanism and final municipal years

Thays traveled across Argentina to examine vegetation and landscapes: Córdoba and San Luis; Entre Ríos and Corrientes; Santa Fe, Chaco and Formosa; Buenos Aires Province; Misiones; Salta, Tucumán and Jujuy; and the Nahuel Huapi region, sometimes extending into Chile. These journeys supplied plants, photographs and comparative observations. The exact date and company of every expedition remain unreconstructed.

He advocated native street and park trees including tipa, jacarandá, lapacho, ceibo, palo borracho, yuchán, aguaribay and others, while continuing to use exotics. His “native” planting program was not ecological restoration in the current sense: species from Argentina’s subtropical north were exotic to the Pampas ecology of Buenos Aires, though native to the nation-state. It simultaneously served horticulture, visual seasonality and the construction of national botanical identity.

#### Palermo Chico and garden-suburb plans

In **1912**, Thays designed the irregular, curved street plan of **Barrio Parque/Palermo Chico**. It departed from the relentless Buenos Aires grid through small blocks, curves and landscaped spaces. He also worked on Carrasco in Montevideo, the Luro Roca garden-city project in San Vicente and an unbuilt preliminary plan for Pueblo Chovet in Santa Fe. These demonstrate his adoption of picturesque urbanism and contemporary garden-suburb ideas.

#### Scouts

On **4 July 1912**, Thays joined Francisco P. Moreno and other figures in founding the Asociación de Boy Scouts Argentinos. The association joined landscape, outdoor education, citizenship and national formation. No evidence was found that Thays held a party-political office.

#### Second Iguazú report

In March 1912 he submitted the extensive illustrated report **_Parque Nacional del Iguazú_** to Agriculture Minister Adolfo Mujica. Accounts describe 103 photographs, comparative discussion of Iguazú and Niagara, and detailed proposals for roads, bridges, viewpoints, lodging and a radial settlement. Historian Ximena Carreras Doallo locates this work within sovereignty, tourism and the visual construction of national territory, not only nature protection ([study](https://revistas.unc.edu.ar/index.php/anuarioceh/article/download/22083/21699/)).

#### Paris, 1913

At the International Forestry Congress in Paris, Thays delivered a lecture on 19 June 1913 and published **_Les forêts naturelles de la République Argentine: projets de parcs nationaux_**, a 47-page French pamphlet issued by L. Pochy for the Touring-Club de France. The work describes Argentine forests and advocates national parks, particularly Iguazú and Nahuel Huapi ([WorldCat record](https://search.worldcat.org/title/Les-forets-naturelles-de-la-Republique-argentine-projets-de-parcs-nationaux.-Conference-faite-...-le-19-juin-a-l%27Hotel-des-Societes-Savantes-../oclc/39063498)).

He left the Buenos Aires directorship at the **end of 1913**, after twenty-two years. The sources consulted describe the end of tenure but do not document a dismissal, scandal or political purge. “Retirement from office” is safer than asserting a particular personal motive.

### 1914–1934: Private practice, counsel and later life

Thays continued private and institutional work after leaving municipal office. Dated plans in the family archive extend well into the 1920s, including:

- Federico Pinedo’s property at Glew (1914–1920);
- urban residential gardens in Buenos Aires and suburbs;
- Saint property on an island in Tigre (1917);
- Guillermo Morgan at Estación Ruiz (1918);
- Estancia Un Durazno (1918–1919);
- Estancia La Ventana in several phases through 1919;
- La Ruina at Moreno and San Alberto projects (around 1920);
- Luis Chico at Verónica (1921);
- a Quinta Dorrego project (1922);
- Carlos Molina Salas at La Cumbre (1923).

His son’s professional rise made the name “Thays” both a family and institutional brand. Carlos León’s later municipal career, including later work on Plaza San Martín, Costanera Norte, Avenida 9 de Julio and other spaces, is often incorrectly added to the father’s oeuvre.

Information on the elder Thays’s health is remarkably thin. No reliable diagnosis, addiction, major accident, psychiatric crisis or terminal illness was located. Photographs show him active into old age. Family memory presents him as cheerful and sociable. He died in Buenos Aires on 31 January 1934; the cause is not given in the accessible records consulted.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects and Financial Interests

### Municipal Dirección de Paseos

This was the material base of his largest accomplishment. It included design, nurseries, tree production, planting crews, maintenance, pest control, park structures and coordination with other municipal departments. The notion that he drew and directed every project “without a team” is biographical hyperbole: a city-scale parks system necessarily involved gardeners, laborers, engineers, draftsmen and administrators. What the recollection conveys is his reputation for direct, intense supervision.

### Botanical Garden as laboratory and supply system

The Garden was simultaneously:

- a public display garden;
- a municipal nursery and acclimatization station;
- a herbarium and library;
- a site for instruction and thesis research;
- a botanical-exchange node;
- a testing ground for street trees, ornamentals and useful plants.

Thays exchanged material internationally. A 1912 United States Department of Agriculture introduction bulletin records cuttings of several *Opuntia* species and yerba mate presented by “Dr. Carlos Thays, director, Jardin Botanico” ([USDA bulletin](https://www.ars-grin.gov/npgs/pi_books/scans/pi033.pdf)). “Dr.” was an honorific in that record, not evidence of an earned doctorate.

### Design methods and technical repertoire

- mixed formal/picturesque composition;
- curves balanced by axial or rectilinear elements;
- manipulation of topography and water;
- lakes, ponds, canals, cascades and fountains;
- framed views and clearings;
- bridges, grottoes, rockwork, pergolas, kiosks, bandstands and statuary;
- nurseries, greenhouses and acclimatization;
- coordinated flowering calendars in urban street trees;
- radial or irregular urban plans in selected settings;
- sanitary reclamation and shade as municipal infrastructure.

No patents, incorporated company, industrial plant or equity interest have been identified. The family practice known today as Thays y Asociados represents professional continuity, not proof that the elder Thays operated a modern corporation under that name.

### Private commissions and patronage network

Thays’s private clients included many of the landowning and political elite: Julio Argentino Roca, Ernesto and Eduardo Tornquist, the Unzué, Anchorena, Alvear-related, Güiraldes, Pereda, Piñero, Hileret, Díaz Vélez and other families. Landscape commissions made estates productive displays of capital, botanical collecting and social status. The same professional stood at the center of municipal public works and elite estate design.

### Wealth and financial questions

No reliable salary series, fee book, banking records, investment portfolio, property inventory, probate valuation or net-worth estimate was found. ## Political, Religious, Intellectual and Artistic Development

### Politics and civic institutions

Thays did not pursue elected office and no party membership was located. His political importance was administrative and spatial. He worked for mayors appointed within Argentina’s conservative order, provincial governments, national ministries and elite private patrons. His parks participated in the state’s projects of sanitation, beautification, immigration management, civic ritual, territorial integration and property development.

He remained active in the French community:

- vice-president, Société Philanthropique du Río de la Plata;
- president, Club Français;
- president, Comité des Sociétés Françaises;
- vice-president of a centennial committee;
- president of a patriotic committee;
- member of the Allied committee during the First World War.

He was also associated as member or honorary member with the Sociedad Rural Argentina, Sociedad Central de Arquitectos, horticultural and forestry societies, and the Boy Scouts. Exact dates and legal titles for all these roles were not recovered.

### Hygienism, recreation and reform

His professional texts treated vegetation as urban metabolism: shade, air, dust control, temperature, health and relief from dense urban life. “Hygiene” in this setting was not only medical; it was a late-nineteenth-century program that linked environment, conduct and municipal order. Parks were meant to give structured leisure to workers and families while also staging elite ceremony and national monuments.

Adrián Gorelik’s *The Grid and the Park* places the park within a larger contest over the metropolitan grid, reform, center and suburb ([open-access publisher record](https://lasapress.org/books/m/10.25154/book8)). In that interpretation, public space was never simply decorative: it could integrate neighborhoods, support land development, perform citizenship and discipline conduct. This context is not evidence of a secret political program by Thays; it is the institutional environment in which his designs functioned.

### Landscape philosophy

Thays favored a mixed composition rather than doctrinaire formalism. Near a residence he often used geometrical terraces and axial beds; farther away, curving drives, woodland masses, irregular water and open lawns. He sought designed “nature,” not untouched wilderness.

The statement “Para ser feliz, es preferible vivir en una cabaña dentro de un bosque que en un palacio sin jardín” is ubiquitously attributed to him. It accurately summarizes the public image of his philosophy, but a contemporaneous first publication was not located. It should be cited as a traditional attribution.

### Native flora and national imagination

Thays’s use of native trees challenged a fashion that privileged European species. Yet his practice remained cosmopolitan and acclimatizing, not nativist in an exclusionary sense. He assembled plants by continent, imported ornamentals and combined them with species from across Argentina. The Botanical Garden made the republic visible as a collection of regional floras. The Iguazú and forest writings similarly converted remote landscapes into objects of science, tourism, conservation and sovereignty.

### Religion

Aside from baptism, church marriage and participation in civic organizations in a nominally Catholic society, the record reveals no developed religious ideology. His conservation arguments were scientific, aesthetic, economic and patriotic rather than explicitly theological.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption or Financial Questions

### Findings

No substantiated record was located of:

- criminal charge or conviction;
- fraud, bribery, embezzlement, money laundering or corrupt procurement;
- lawsuit against him personally;
- professional disciplinary proceeding;
- imprisonment, exile or intelligence-service relationship;
- direct participation in political violence or abuse;
- addiction or documented psychiatric crisis.

Absence from the sources consulted is not proof that no minor civil or administrative dispute ever occurred. It does mean that importing the standard categories of a scandal dossier would manufacture a story unsupported by the known Thays record.

### Issues that require contextual rather than criminal treatment

#### Public office and elite private practice

Thays accepted numerous private commissions from politically connected landowners while directing the municipal parks service. This creates a modern appearance of conflict of interest. The consulted sources do not show a legal complaint, secret municipal transfer, procurement case or finding of self-dealing. The documented fact is simultaneous public authority and elite patronage; any stronger claim requires contracts and municipal finance records not found here.

#### Park expenditure and class

Mendoza’s Parque del Oeste provoked opposition that public money was being spent on an elite amenity instead of sewers and water. Buenos Aires parks also raised enduring questions: whether monumental northern landscapes received disproportionate resources; whether southern parks were sanitary reform, social provision or instruments of discipline; and how greenery increased adjacent land values. These are documented political and historiographical controversies about urban priorities, not charges that Thays stole funds.

#### Conservation and territorial power

Iguazú conservation also advanced state sovereignty, tourism and colonization in Indigenous Guaraní territory. Later national-park policy could restrict traditional occupation and recast inhabited lands as pristine nature. Thays’s reports are part of the genealogy of that state landscape. The sources consulted do not document him personally ordering expulsions or violence. The distinction between his conservation plan and later coercive administration must be preserved.

#### Erasure of collective labor and earlier landscapes

Heroic authorship can obscure:

- Indigenous irrigation knowledge in Mendoza;
- Guaraní knowledge of yerba mate and Iguazú;
- earlier designers such as Courtois and Wysocki;
- engineers, horticulturists and municipal crews;
- later designers such as Carrasco and Carlos León Thays.

This is a problem of attribution and historical memory, not evidence that Thays himself fraudulently claimed every contribution.

#### Marriage-age issue

The later story says Thays met Cora when she was sixteen and he was forty-one. If the 1874 birth and 1892/1893 marriage chronology is correct, she was approximately eighteen or nineteen at marriage. No contemporaneous accusation or legal proceeding was found. The facts should neither be suppressed nor converted into an allegation unsupported by the period record.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda and Disputed Narratives

### 1. “Thays designed 80 or 90 percent of Buenos Aires’s green spaces”

1. **Story:** nearly every park or 80–90 percent of current green space was drawn by him.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** modern city tourism pages and popular journalism; the percentages vary even between Spanish and English versions.
3. **Evidence:** the municipal archive supports 69 created, remodeled or enlarged spaces and a massive street-tree program, but the denominator and survival criteria behind 80/90 percent are not published.
4. **Assessment:** **unverified promotional statistic** built on a real, exceptional scale of influence.
5. **Effect:** turns a complex municipal system into the work of a solitary founder.

### 2. “He created Parque 3 de Febrero”

1. **Story:** Thays designed the Bosques de Palermo from the beginning.
2. **Circulation:** travel writing, captions and compressed biographies.
3. **Evidence:** the park originated under Domingo F. Sarmiento and opened before Thays arrived; earlier plans involved other professionals. Thays performed the decisive modern enlargement and remodeling.
4. **Assessment:** **false if ‘created’ means originated; correct if stated as major transformation.**
5. **Effect:** magnifies his authorship and erases Sarmiento-era planning.

### 3. “Thays designed the Rosedal”

1. **Story:** the Palermo rose garden is one of his signature works.
2. **Circulation:** image captions and tourist lists attach every feature within Parque 3 de Febrero to him.
3. **Evidence:** Benito Javier Carrasco created the Rosedal in 1914, after Thays’s municipal tenure.
4. **Assessment:** **misattribution**; Carrasco was Thays’s disciple, which explains the association but not authorship.
5. **Effect:** absorbs a student’s major independent work into the master’s legend.

### 4. “He discovered how to grow yerba mate and created the industry”

1. **Story:** Thays solved an ancient mystery, allowing Argentine mate to exist.
2. **Circulation:** municipal commemorations and popular profiles; sometimes embellished with chickens or accidental discovery.
3. **Evidence:** Guaraní and Jesuit cultivation long preceded him. His 1895 experiments produced and publicized a useful modern germination protocol based on hot-water treatment; agricultural authorities diffused it.
4. **Assessment:** **documented technical contribution, exaggerated into sole discovery.** The precise role of animal digestion in his actual procedure requires the original report rather than later retelling.
5. **Effect:** makes a national beverage part of the benevolent immigrant-genius narrative while minimizing Indigenous knowledge.

### 5. “He invented Argentina’s first national park”

1. **Story:** Thays single-handedly invented the national-park concept and Iguazú was Argentina’s first park project.
2. **Circulation:** biographies celebrating his 1902 and 1912 reports.
3. **Evidence:** his Iguazú plan was genuinely early and influential. Francisco Moreno donated Nahuel Huapi land in 1903; a national reserve expanded in 1907 and Parque Nacional del Sud was created in 1922. Iguazú lands were reserved in 1909, purchased in 1928 and the park created in 1934.
4. **Assessment:** **pioneer, not sole founder.** “First plan” depends on whether one means designed tourist-conservation plan, donated reserve or statutory national park.
5. **Effect:** concentrates a multi-person, multi-decade institutional history in one heroic figure.

### 6. “He stayed in Argentina because he fell in love”

1. **Story:** a temporary French visitor met sixteen-year-old Cora and abandoned his return voyage for love.
2. **Circulation:** family profiles, tourism copy and the 2018 “amor de película” article.
3. **Evidence:** he did meet and marry Cora; he also received an unusually powerful municipal appointment and a continent-wide field of commissions. Chronology differs on whether the relationship began before or after the decisive employment opportunity.
4. **Assessment:** **romantic family legend around documented marriage and migration.** Professional opportunity and attachment to the country are at least equally explanatory.
5. **Effect:** humanizes migration and removes institutional patronage from the story.

### 7. “He was an academically trained architect”

1. **Story:** he is routinely called architect, engineer, agronomist or even doctor.
2. **Circulation:** official pages, honorific records and later professional usage.
3. **Evidence:** Berjman explicitly says he lacked formal academic training and learned through more than a decade with André. “Dr.” appears as an honorific in a USDA exchange record.
4. **Assessment:** **professionally a landscape architect; no located degree.**
5. **Effect:** makes his status legible to later credentialed professions while obscuring apprenticeship culture.

### 8. “He remodeled imperial Paris with Haussmann”

1. **Story:** Thays, André and Alphand together transformed Paris under Napoleon III.
2. **Circulation:** official commemorative biographies and the family studio.
3. **Evidence:** Thays was born in 1849 and was a child during the foundational projects. He later worked in André’s orbit and inherited the system’s methods.
4. **Assessment:** **genealogical association exaggerated into co-authorship.**
5. **Effect:** supplies direct Haussmannian prestige and supports the “Paris of South America” mythology.

### 9. The solitary superhuman worker

1. **Story:** Thays worked twenty hours a day, had no team and personally directed everything.
2. **Circulation:** family recollection and popular biography.
3. **Evidence:** his output and direct supervision were extraordinary, but municipal parks required large organizations and named collaborators appear outside Buenos Aires.
4. **Assessment:** **reputation-based exaggeration with a factual core of intense work.**
5. **Effect:** erases laborers and turns bureaucracy into personal genius.

### 10. The universally loved, controversy-free benefactor

1. **Story:** aristocrats, workers, students and neighbors all loved him; his funeral united the city.
2. **Circulation:** obituaries, family testimony and later civic hagiography. The large funeral cortege is consistently reported.
3. **Evidence:** the 1901 caricature and funeral record support exceptional popularity. Project-level controversy, class conflict and attribution disputes nonetheless existed.
4. **Assessment:** **reputation strongly grounded, but later writing idealizes it.**
5. **Effect:** makes Thays a rare nonpartisan civic saint and personification of benevolent public space.

### Posthumous edited collection

- **Carlos Thays: _Sus escritos sobre jardines y paisajes_**, compiled and introduced by Sonia Berjman. Buenos Aires: Ciudad Argentina with support of the French Embassy/Foreign Ministry, 2002, 375/376 pages; ISBN 987-507-226-5. ### No evidence found

No novel, memoir, diary, patent corpus, political manifesto, musical work, film, sound recording or ghostwritten autobiography was located. His photographs from surveys are part of the professional corpus, but not all images in later Thays archives were necessarily taken by him.

- Diego Roldán and Sebastián Godoy, “Antes del espacio público: una historia de los espacios verdes y libres de la ciudad de Rosario, 1900–1940” (2017): specialist context for the disputed Rosario attribution ([record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/318892732_Antes_del_espacio_publico_una_historia_de_los_espacios_verdes_y_libres_de_la_ciudad_de_Rosario_1900-1940)).
- Florencia Rolla, “Los discursos y narrativas de las ideas de Carlos Thays…” (2023): analysis of his written ideas and later narratives ([PDF](https://www.publicacionescientificas.fadu.uba.ar/index.php/actas/article/download/3307/3220)).
- “La memoria de Thays en Montevideo,” University of the Republic repository: survey of Uruguayan work.

### Popular and family-history profiles useful as evidence of reception

- Sonia Berjman’s 2018 illustrated *La Nación* profile is concise and grounded in the family archive.
- Susana Ceballos’s “La curiosa historia de amor…” in *La Nación* records the full romantic/family legend, including “Hup, hup,” the picnic basket and twenty-hour workdays ([article](https://www.lanacion.com.ar/sociedad/la-curiosa-historia-de-amor-del-genial-paisajista-carlos-thays-nid2105385/)). It should be used as reception and family narrative, not alone for contested dates.
- Municipal and tourism histories are useful for present-day commemoration but sometimes contain date, naming and percentage errors.

### Gaps in the literature

No full critical biography was located that combines French civil and employment archives, Argentine municipal personnel and finance files, private account books, family correspondence and plan-by-plan attribution. The French career, retirement motive, later residence, finances, health and cause of death remain particularly underdocumented in accessible literature.

## Death and Funeral

Thays died in Buenos Aires on **31 January 1934**. He had completed eighty-four years and five months. The accessible municipal and biographical sources do not state a cause of death or final illness.

Accounts consistently describe a large funeral procession to Chacarita Cemetery attended by officials, aristocrats, students and workers. Architect **René Karman** delivered a farewell. The exact full text of Karman’s oration was not located in the accessible sources; later pages often introduce a quotation but omit it because of defective transcription. The funeral narrative is plausible and well established, but its language became part of the civic saint legend.

His widow Cora survived him, reportedly until 1959. His son Carlos León was already positioned to continue the family’s landscape profession and municipal influence.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Institutional legacy

- A citywide system of parks, plazas, promenades and street trees rather than isolated gardens.
- The Botanical Garden as public space, scientific institution and principal Thays memorial.
- Native northern Argentine species made ordinary elements of Buenos Aires streetscape.
- A professional lineage through Carrasco, Carlos León Thays and later family practitioners.
- Early national-park planning joining conservation, tourism and territorial administration.
- Transfer of mixed-style French landscape practice into varied Argentine ecologies.

### Physical survival and loss

His landscapes are living works and therefore unusually vulnerable to false claims of “original” condition. Plants die, views close, lakes are filled, sculptures move, paths change, recreation facilities accumulate and administrations redesign. Important works such as the Mar del Plata seafront and Parque Colón were demolished or radically transformed. Surviving parks may preserve the overall armature while containing few original plantings.

### Memorialization

- The Botanical Garden received his name in 1937 and contains his bust/monument.
- Plaza Thays in Carrasco commemorates his Uruguayan work.
- Parque Carlos Thays in Recoleta commemorates him but is a later park on the former Italpark site.
- The 2009 retrospective and archive donation shifted memory from anecdote toward documentation.
- City walking circuits, centennial restorations and replanting campaigns repeatedly revive him as the personification of “green Buenos Aires.”

### Historiographical reinterpretation

Earlier memory emphasized benevolent French genius, beauty and a grateful public. Later scholarship adds:

- predecessors and collective municipal labor;
- the park as sanitary and disciplinary infrastructure;
- real-estate development and elite patronage;
- Indigenous landscape knowledge;
- conservation’s link to sovereignty, tourism and colonization;
- the distinction between national-native species and local ecology;
- collaborative, phased and disputed authorship.

These interpretations do not negate his documented output. They replace the solitary-creator myth with a more complete account of how designs, governments, workers, plants, water systems, patrons and later administrations produced the surviving landscapes.

## Atlas Connections

### Joseph-Antoine Bouvard

- **[T3] Documented fact:** Thays and Bouvard participated in the municipal planning process that produced Buenos Aires’s 1910 comprehensive plan. Bouvard served as international consultant, while Thays sat on the municipal technical commission as Director de Paseos. This establishes institutional and written professional engagement, although no private correspondence between them was located. [Observatorio Metropolitano](https://dev.observatorioamba.org/planes-y-proyectos/caba/planes/1910-nuevo-plan-para-buenos-aires/ficha)

### Julio Argentino Roca

- **[T3] Reported fact:** Roca commissioned Thays to redesign the grounds of his summer residence, Estancia La Paz at Ascochinga, Córdoba, around the beginning of the twentieth century. Thays laid out the park and artificial lake. The commission documents a direct patron–designer relationship, but the sources consulted do not independently establish a particular face-to-face meeting or friendship. [Estancia La Paz history](https://arquitecturapanamericana.com/club-house-estancia-la-paz/)

### Domingo Faustino Sarmiento

- **[T9-] Documented fact:** Parque 3 de Febrero connects the two men across time. Sarmiento promoted its creation, headed its auxiliary commission and oversaw its formative period; after Sarmiento’s death in 1888, Thays assumed management in 1891 and substantially remodeled and expanded it. This is a successive relationship to the same landscape, not a personal meeting. [Buenos Aires Historical Archive](https://archivodelaciudad.org/index.php/Detail/fonds/8)

### Jules Dormal

- **[T9-] Documented fact:** Dormal belonged to the team responsible for the original Parque 3 de Febrero landscape opened in 1875. Thays later inherited, enlarged and extensively reshaped that same park during his municipal tenure. The evidence supports successive authorship at one site, not documented personal collaboration. [Buenos Aires Legislature, *La ciudad verde*](https://pergamo.legislatura.gob.ar/pgmedia/biblioteca/LIBROS%20DIGITALIZADOS/039262_D.pdf)

### Auguste Rodin

- **[T9+] Documented fact:** Rodin’s Monument to Sarmiento was inaugurated in 1900 inside Parque 3 de Febrero while that landscape was under Thays’s management and transformation. Contemporary photographic documentation explicitly juxtaposes “el Sarmiento de Rodin” with “la jardinería de Thays.” No evidence was found that the two men met or corresponded; the securely documented connector is the park-monument ensemble. [Buenos Aires park history](https://buenosaires.gob.ar/areas/med_ambiente/parque_3_de_febrero/resena_historica.php?menu_id=22933), [historical landscape study](https://www.fundacionazara.org.ar/img/libros/buenos-aires-la-historia-de-su-paisaje-natural.pdf)

### Juan Manuel de Rosas

- **[T9-] Documented fact:** Thays’s remodeling of Parque 3 de Febrero incorporated the former Palermo estate of Rosas. During Thays’s tenure the surviving Caserón de Rosas was demolished in 1899, and the vacated location became the setting for Rodin’s Sarmiento monument in 1900. Rosas and Thays therefore connect through successive, ideologically charged uses of the same site, without shared occupancy. [Buenos Aires park history](https://buenosaires.gob.ar/areas/med_ambiente/parque_3_de_febrero/resena_historica.php?menu_id=22933)

### Agustí Querol i Subirats

- **[T9+] Documented fact:** Querol’s Monument to the Magna Carta and the Four Argentine Regions—commonly called the Monumento de los Españoles—was conceived for and ultimately installed within the Parque 3 de Febrero landscape that Thays had enlarged and reshaped. Querol died in 1909 and the monument was completed and inaugurated only in 1927, so this is a shared-site connection rather than evidence of meeting or collaboration. [Buenos Aires Legislature, *La ciudad verde*](https://pergamo.legislatura.gob.ar/pgmedia/biblioteca/LIBROS%20DIGITALIZADOS/039262_D.pdf)

### Francisco Salamone

- **[T9+] Documented fact:** At Parque Municipal Domingo F. Sarmiento in Azul, the landscape was laid out from Thays’s design and inaugurated in 1918; Salamone subsequently created its monumental entrance during the later 1930s. Their works consequently occupy successive layers of the same park. No personal encounter is supported, and Salamone’s intervention postdated Thays’s death. [National University of La Plata repository](https://sedici.unlp.edu.ar/bitstream/handle/10915/139385/Documento_completo.%20Comportamiento%20de%20los%20consumidores%20de%20festivales%20culturales.pdf-PDFA.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=1)

No sufficiently documented crossing was found with Aimé Bonpland: Bonpland’s earlier yerba-mate research forms an important intellectual precedent for Thays, but the located evidence does not show that Thays wrote about, cited or otherwise engaged Bonpland in a manner meeting T1 or T3. Other apparent roster overlaps based only on commemorative place names, broad contemporaneity, shared nationality or residence in Buenos Aires have likewise been omitted.
