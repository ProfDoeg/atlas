# Harold Thomas Finney II: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

This dossier concerns the American software engineer, cryptographic activist, cypherpunk, reusable-proof-of-work designer, and early Bitcoin participant usually known as **Hal Finney**.

Evidence is classified as follows:

- **Documented fact:** supported by primary records, Finney’s own authenticated writings, contemporaneous correspondence, software records, blockchain data, or institutional documentation.
- **Reported fact:** stated by family members, colleagues, or reputable reporting but not independently established here from a primary record.
- **Allegation or theory:** a claim advanced with some argument or circumstantial evidence but not proved.
- **Rumor or myth:** folklore, conjecture, or symbolic narrative unsupported by adequate evidence.
- **Unresolved:** available evidence does not permit a firm conclusion.

Research included a dedicated adversarial search combining Finney’s name with controversy, scandal, allegations, accusations, lawsuits, fraud, conspiracy, and criticism, including material published or recirculated in 2024–2026.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and siblings

**Reported fact:** Finney’s father was a longtime employee of Union Oil of California. His work produced several family relocations during Finney’s childhood. Finney’s mother was principally a homemaker. Reliable public accounts consulted for this dossier do not consistently supply the parents’ full names; they should therefore not be reconstructed from unsourced genealogy sites.

Finney was the third of four children. His surviving siblings named in the *Los Angeles Times* obituary were:

- **Pat Wolf**, sister, then of Willis, Texas;
- **Kathy Liu**, sister, then of Temple City, California;
- **Mike Finney**, brother, then of Bend, Oregon.

A family profile describes Hal and Mike as sufficiently close in age and appearance that they were sometimes mistaken for twins. It characterizes Mike as the more mischievous brother and Hal as earnest, academically oriented, and attracted to codes and puzzles. These are family recollections rather than independently recorded childhood observations.

No responsibly documented, detailed ancestral pedigree was located. Claims about deeper ethnic or national ancestry should be treated as **unestablished**.

### Marriage and children

Finney married **Fran Finney**, whom he met at Caltech. A later Alcor family profile says they lived in the same student residence and quickly became close. Fran became an important partner in his running, ALS fundraising, medical care, and cryonics arrangements.

Their children were:

- **Jason Finney**, son;
- **Erin Finney**, daughter.

Family photographs published by Alcor show Finney, Fran, and their children together by the mid-1980s. The family participated in swimming, travel, running, and other physical activities.

On **October 15, 1992**, Hal and Fran reportedly completed Alcor cryonics membership paperwork in Riverside, California. This decision preceded his ALS diagnosis by almost seventeen years and reflected a longstanding interest in life extension rather than a last-minute response to terminal illness.

## Childhood and Early Life

Finney was born in Coalinga, an oil-producing California town, while his father worked for Union Oil. The family subsequently moved several times. By Finney’s adolescence they were established in **Arcadia**, in the San Gabriel Valley east of Los Angeles.

He attended high school in Arcadia. The precise school is not firmly established in the strongest sources consulted, although later biographical accounts describe him as an Arcadia high-school graduate.

Family recollections portray him as interested in puzzles, formal systems, and homemade codes from elementary-school age. This material fits his later interests but survives principally through commemorative family accounts and should be read as **reported recollection**, not proof of an inevitable path toward cryptography.

Finney also developed a durable interest in physical fitness. At Caltech he participated in twenty-four-hour relay races and informal steeplechases. In later life he ran long distances and pursued marathon qualification.

## Education and Formation

### California Institute of Technology

Finney entered the **California Institute of Technology** in Pasadena and graduated in **1979** with a BS in engineering. Sources frequently identify the field as electrical engineering.

At Caltech he encountered an unusually concentrated computing and engineering culture. Later accounts connect him with classmates and alumni involved in **APh Technological Consulting**, a Pasadena engineering concern founded in 1974 by Caltech associates **Glenn Hightower** and **John Denker**.

Finney met Fran at Caltech. Their relationship joined his technical life with a household that remained strongly oriented toward physical activity, experimentation, and futurist ideas.

No documentary evidence was found for a single professor functioning as his decisive teacher or formal graduate mentor. His formation is better reconstructed through:

- Caltech’s engineering culture;
- early microprocessor and game-console programming;
- the public-key-cryptography revolution;
- David Chaum’s writing on anonymous electronic money;
- Phil Zimmermann’s release of PGP;
- the cypherpunk and Extropian mailing-list communities.

### Intellectual influences

Finney explicitly engaged with or discussed:

- **David Chaum’s** digital cash and blind signatures;
- public-key cryptography and digital signatures;
- cryptographic remailers and traffic-analysis resistance;
- Adam Back’s Hashcash;
- Wei Dai’s b-money;
- Nick Szabo’s digital-money and proof-of-work thinking;
- libertarian, civil-libertarian, and Extropian thought;
- cryonics and technological life extension.

He was neither simply a technological determinist nor an advocate of electoral politics as the primary answer to surveillance. In “Politics vs Technology” he argued that technology alone could not guarantee privacy and that political and institutional conditions continued to matter.

## Early Career: APh and Video Games

### APh Technological Consulting

Finney began working for **APh Technological Consulting** while still finishing his Caltech education, reportedly in summer 1978. One account says his first assignment involved software for cash-register systems. APh was simultaneously doing substantial engineering work for Mattel’s emerging Intellivision console.

The precise boundaries between work done for APh, Mattel Electronics, and Mattel’s **M Network** publishing label are sometimes blurred in secondary biographies. The defensible formulation is that Finney worked at APh on console software commissioned or published by Mattel.

## PGP and the Crypto Wars

### Entry into PGP

In **1991**, while working for another software company, Finney learned that **Philip R. Zimmermann** was developing Pretty Good Privacy. PGP sought to make strong public-key encryption available to ordinary computer users.

Finney contacted Zimmermann and volunteered. He became one of the central programmers responsible for the transition from PGP 1.0 to **PGP 2.0**, released in 1992. In 2014 Finney told Andy Greenberg that he wrote “the bulk of the changes” between those versions. The PGP 2.0 documentation credits Hal Finney among contributors.

His work included cryptographic implementation and mechanisms associated with PGP’s decentralized **web of trust**, through which users could sign one another’s public keys rather than depend exclusively on a central certificate authority.

### Federal investigation

The United States government investigated whether PGP’s international distribution violated export-control laws treating strong cryptographic software as a munition.

**Documented fact:** Zimmermann was investigated; the investigation ended in 1996 without charges.

**Reported fact:** Zimmermann said Finney’s role was deliberately kept comparatively quiet so that Finney would be less exposed to the investigation. That protective strategy also reduced contemporary recognition of his work.

No evidence was found that Finney was indicted, charged, convicted, imprisoned, or formally accused by the government of a PGP-related crime.

### Corporate history

After the investigation ended, Zimmermann formed **PGP Inc.** in 1996. Finney joined as one of its first employees and served as a senior software engineer.

The operation passed through several corporate forms:

1. PGP Inc.;
2. acquisition by Network Associates;
3. re-formation as PGP Corporation;
4. acquisition by Symantec in 2010.

Finney worked principally from Santa Barbara, including through telecommuting, and developed cryptographic library components. He retired in early **2011** as ALS increasingly limited his movement and speech.

No public evidence located here shows that Finney had a major founder’s equity position, controlled PGP’s corporate structure, or obtained extraordinary wealth through its acquisitions.

## Cypherpunk Activity

### Mailing-list participation

Finney joined the Cypherpunks mailing list in 1992 and became one of its most prolific technically substantive participants. A surviving archive indexes hundreds of messages under his name.

His first widely quoted message stated that the computer could be used “as a tool to liberate and protect people, rather than to control them.” The sentiment became central to his posthumous reputation.

### Anonymous remailers

Finney implemented and operated early **Type I cypherpunk anonymous remailers**. A remailer stripped identifying headers from email and forwarded the content; messages could be encrypted and passed through several remailers in succession.

He operated services associated with addresses including:

- `hal@alumni.caltech.edu`;
- `hfinney@shell.portal.com`.

He published source code, instructions, and practical analysis of remailer chaining. Eric Hughes and Finney are regularly credited with the earliest cypherpunk-remailer implementations, Hughes’s initial Perl implementation being followed and extended by Finney’s work.

Finney also confronted the abuse problem: anonymous systems could protect dissidents and whistleblowers but could also be used for harassment. His writings explored payment, rate-limiting, reputational systems, and other mechanisms rather than denying the conflict.

### Electronic cash

Finney repeatedly analyzed Chaumian electronic cash, blind signatures, double spending, pseudonymous accounts, and privacy. His 1993–1994 writings anticipated several questions later central to cryptocurrencies:

- how to detect double spending;
- whether private issuers might issue competing monies;
- how electronic tokens might acquire collector or commodity value;
- how anonymity and auditability could coexist;
- whether computational work could discourage network abuse.

### SSL challenge

In August **1995**, Finney published a captured Netscape Secure Sockets Layer session using weak, export-approved encryption and challenged cryptographers to recover the session key.

Damien Doligez broke the 40-bit key in approximately eight days using distributed computing. The exercise demonstrated that export-grade encryption deliberately permitted by US policy was not meaningfully secure against organized attack.

### Extropianism and cryonics

Finney participated in Extropian mailing lists and wrote for *Extropy*. He accepted neither biological mortality nor existing institutional limits as necessarily permanent. His interests included:

- life extension;
- cryonics;
- artificial intelligence and future technology;
- individual autonomy;
- technological self-transformation;
- market or decentralized coordination.

“Transhumanist” and “Extropian” are accurate affiliations, but surviving writings show a practical engineer more often than a systematic ideological theorist.

## Reusable Proofs of Work

### Design

On **August 15, 2004**, Finney announced **RPOW—Reusable Proofs of Work**.

Hashcash required a computer to perform costly computation and produce a token, but its ordinary anti-spam use did not make a token permanently transferable. RPOW allowed a valid proof-of-work token to be submitted to a server and exchanged for a freshly signed token of equal value.

Its architecture combined:

- Hashcash-style proofs of computational expenditure;
- RSA-style signed tokens;
- a server designed to prevent double spending;
- IBM 4758 secure cryptographic hardware;
- remote attestation allowing users to check what software the server was running;
- published source code.

### Importance and limitation

RPOW was an operational prototype and an important predecessor to cryptocurrency, but it was not Bitcoin.

Its central limitation was its reliance on a particular trusted server and trusted-computing hardware. Remote attestation reduced the necessary trust but did not eliminate a central point of operation. Bitcoin later replaced that architecture with a peer-to-peer proof-of-work ledger and economic consensus.

Claims that Finney “invented proof of work” are inaccurate. Earlier foundations include Cynthia Dwork and Moni Naor’s computational pricing and Adam Back’s Hashcash. The narrower documented claim is that Finney created the first implemented **reusable** proof-of-work token system and explicitly explored its monetary possibilities.

RPOW did not achieve broad commercial adoption. No substantial revenue, investment round, or company formed around it has been documented. Technically influential, it was economically a failed or dormant prototype.

## Bitcoin: 2008–2009

### Initial announcement

On October 31, **2008**, Satoshi Nakamoto circulated *Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System* on the Cryptography mailing list.

Finney responded more positively than most early participants. On November 7 he wrote that Bitcoin seemed a promising idea and analyzed the possibility that a limited number of coins might one day become extremely valuable.

In a January 2009 calculation, he imagined Bitcoin becoming a major worldwide payment system and divided an estimate of global household wealth by the eventual 21-million-coin supply. The resulting hypothetical valuation—approximately **$10 million per bitcoin** in then-current dollars—was a scenario analysis, not an investment promise or demonstrated price prediction.

### Running Bitcoin

Bitcoin 0.1 was announced on January 8–9, 2009. Finney downloaded and ran it shortly thereafter. On January 10 he posted the two-word message:

> Running bitcoin

His surviving correspondence with Nakamoto documents genuine interaction between two apparent correspondents. Finney reported:

- crashes under Windows;
- missing Microsoft runtime components;
- differences between launching from Windows and Cygwin;
- a disk-full problem;
- observations about syncing and block files.

Nakamoto replied with technical explanations and patched builds, frequently very quickly.

### Mining and first transfer

Finney said he mined a block in the “seventy-something” range. Blockchain reconstruction generally identifies **block 78** with his early activity, although identification of every early mined block with a named person is not always cryptographically conclusive.

On **January 12, 2009**, Satoshi sent Finney **10 BTC** in transaction `f4184fc596403b9...`. It is recognized as the first recorded Bitcoin transfer between two people, as distinct from coinbase rewards created by mining.

Finney recalled that he turned the software off after several days because it made his computer run hot and the fan noise bothered him. He later forgot about it for a period and was surprised that Bitcoin survived.

### Contributions and limits

Finney:

- tested the earliest public Windows builds;
- identified and reported bugs;
- received and tested the first transfer;
- discussed monetary and security implications;
- later contributed ideas and experimental wallet-security code.

Available records do **not** justify saying that Finney co-authored the Bitcoin white paper, jointly designed the entire protocol, or was publicly identified as a Bitcoin “co-founder.” Those are later extrapolations.

## Illness, Running, and Adaptation

### Onset and diagnosis

During preparation for the 2009 Los Angeles Marathon, Finney experienced:

- slurred speech;
- weakness in his hands;
- injuries and unusually slow recovery;
- deteriorating athletic performance.

He was diagnosed with ALS in **August 2009**.

ALS progressively destroys voluntary motor control while often leaving cognition largely intact. Finney initially continued running. Fran organized a relay and fundraiser at the inaugural Santa Barbara International Marathon on **December 6, 2009**. Hal and Fran crossed the finish line together with their South Coast Fights ALS team.

### Disease progression

By 2010–2011 he required increasing assistance. Eventually he:

- used a wheelchair;
- lost ordinary speech;
- depended on a feeding tube;
- required assisted ventilation;
- communicated through eye-tracking equipment and speech synthesis;
- depended heavily on Fran and professional caregivers.

In 2013 he described himself as essentially paralyzed but cognitively intact. Using eye tracking, he continued programming at a fraction of his former speed—he estimated approximately fifty times slower.

### `bcflick`

Finney’s final substantial software project, generally called **bcflick**, investigated protecting Bitcoin private keys with trusted-computing hardware. It aimed to make wallet secrets harder to extract even if the host computer were compromised.

He did not finish the system before his death. It is best understood as an experimental continuation of ideas already present in RPOW: using auditable secure hardware to protect cryptographic state.

## Finances, Bitcoin Holdings, and Property

Finney mined bitcoins when they had no established market price. The complete number he mined and the total retained by his estate are not public.

In “Bitcoin and Me,” Finney said:

- he transferred his Bitcoin wallet to an offline computer;
- he expected the holdings to have value for his heirs;
- he believed his heirs were technologically capable;
- he had sold some bitcoins at approximately **$100 per BTC** to help pay medical expenses.

Reports that Finney left a precisely known fortune—sometimes calculated by multiplying presumed early blocks by later peak prices—are **unsupported**. Early mining attribution is uncertain, coins were sold for care, wallet ownership is incomplete, and neither Finney nor his family publicly disclosed a final balance.

Andy Greenberg reported seeing wallet records and the early transfer while investigating the Satoshi theory. Those records supported Finney’s account of receiving coins from Nakamoto but did not disclose a comprehensive estate.

No evidence was found of:

- a cryptocurrency company owned or controlled by Finney;
- securities fraud;
- investor solicitation;
- a token sale;
- exchange manipulation;
- money laundering;
- bankruptcy;
- tax prosecution;
- civil judgments over Bitcoin ownership;
- hidden corporate vehicles attributable to him.

## Political, Religious, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

### Political orientation

Finney was associated with cypherpunk, libertarian, civil-libertarian, and Extropian circles. His central political concerns were:

- freedom of speech;
- privacy of communication;
- limits on state and corporate surveillance;
- free availability of strong encryption;
- protection for dissidents, journalists, and ordinary users;
- decentralized or pseudonymous exchange.

He did not hold elected office and no significant party-political campaign role was found.

His approach was not reducible to the claim that code automatically overrides government. “Politics vs Technology” warned that technical safeguards operated inside a political world and could be prohibited, weakened, or bypassed.

### Religion

No reliable source consulted establishes a formal religious affiliation. His commitment to cryonics reflected confidence in possible future technology, not by itself a documented theological position. Claims that he was an atheist, religious believer, or member of a specific faith remain **unestablished** unless supported by additional personal records.

### Intellectual temperament

Colleagues and family consistently described Finney as optimistic, technically curious, restrained in personal conflict, and willing to implement systems rather than merely theorize. These descriptions are testimony from sympathetic witnesses and form part of his posthumous image; they are not objective psychological diagnoses.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Fran Finney

Spouse, running partner, caregiver, ALS fundraiser, custodian of personal records, and representative of his legacy. Fran authorized journalists to inspect relevant email and wallet materials and later supported commemorative projects including the Finney Freedom Prize.

### Philip R. Zimmermann

PGP creator, early collaborator, and later employer. Zimmermann credited Finney with extensive PGP work and said the federal investigation caused that work to remain under-recognized.

### Satoshi Nakamoto

Documented remote correspondent and software collaborator during Bitcoin’s launch. No verified in-person meeting is known. Finney repeatedly denied being Nakamoto.

### Eric Hughes and the cypherpunks

Hughes’s remailer work and the Cypherpunks mailing list provided the setting for Finney’s early privacy engineering. Finney interacted with a wide network including Tim May, John Gilmore, Nick Szabo, Wei Dai, Adam Back, and others.

### David Chaum

An important intellectual influence rather than a documented close personal associate. Finney repeatedly analyzed Chaum’s blind signatures and electronic-cash architecture.

### Nick Szabo

A mailing-list contemporary and fellow digital-money theorist. Their writings intersected around digital cash and proof of work. Szabo later named Finney among the very small number of people who pursued such ideas seriously before Bitcoin.

### John Denker and Glenn Hightower

Caltech/APh connections associated with Finney’s early professional entry into software engineering.

### Dorian Prentice Satoshi Nakamoto

A Japanese-American engineer who lived in Temple City and was incorrectly identified by *Newsweek* in 2014 as Bitcoin’s creator. Finney had grown up and maintained family connections in the same San Gabriel Valley area. The two men reportedly lived only a few blocks apart during part of their lives, but no documentary evidence shows that they met or knew one another.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, or Financial Questions

### PGP export investigation

**Status: documented federal investigation; no charge against Finney.**

The investigation concerned the international dissemination of PGP and US cryptography-export regulations. Zimmermann was its central target. Finney’s participation was deliberately kept quieter. The inquiry closed without charges in 1996.

### Extortion and swatting

**Status: documented victimization; perpetrator unresolved in the sources consulted.**

During the final period of Finney’s life, an unknown extortionist demanded **1,000 BTC**, then worth more than $400,000. After the Finneys refused, the perpetrator used spoofed telephone information to make a false emergency report, causing armed police to respond to the family home.

The assailant also circulated private information. Fran Finney reported that the FBI believed the same extortionist targeted other early Bitcoin users.

No evidence indicates that Hal or Fran committed wrongdoing in this episode. They were victims. Publicly available reporting does not establish a conviction or definitive identification of the perpetrator.

### Satoshi Nakamoto allegation

**Status: persistent identity theory; unresolved in an absolute sense but contradicted by substantial evidence.**

The theory that Finney was Satoshi arose from:

- his technical ability;
- his RPOW work;
- his unusually early enthusiasm;
- his being the first transfer recipient;
- stylistic similarities alleged between his and Nakamoto’s prose;
- the coincidence that Dorian Satoshi Nakamoto lived nearby;
- Finney’s retirement occurring near the end of Satoshi’s public activity;
- assumptions that ALS could explain Nakamoto’s withdrawal.

Evidence against the theory includes:

- an extensive two-sided email exchange with distinct technical roles;
- contemporaneous debugging in which Finney reported unfamiliar failures and Nakamoto supplied modified builds;
- timestamps showing Satoshi activity while Finney was participating in a publicly recorded ten-mile race;
- Finney’s wallet history and first incoming transaction;
- Finney’s direct denial, communicated through eye-tracking software;
- Fran Finney’s denial;
- Greenberg’s inspection of emails and wallet data;
- differences in code style and technical habits identified by later investigators;
- the absence of evidence that Finney’s estate controlled the large early-mining hoard often attributed to Nakamoto.

Scheduled messages, a collaborator, or deliberately staged correspondence can be imagined, so the historical proposition cannot be disproved mathematically. No cryptographic proof identifying Finney as Nakamoto has appeared.

The appropriate classification is therefore **unproved theory with substantial counterevidence**, not established fact.

### Dorian Nakamoto-name theory

**Status: speculation.**

A more specific story says Finney borrowed his nearby resident’s birth name, Satoshi Nakamoto, to construct the pseudonym.

Evidence for it is geographic coincidence. Evidence against it is that no meeting, correspondence, or other connection between Finney and Dorian Nakamoto has been documented. The residential proximity became public only after *Newsweek*’s separate and widely disputed identification of Dorian in 2014.

### Craig Wright’s claims

**Status: unsupported allegation by a judicially discredited claimant.**

Craig Wright at various times suggested that Bitcoin was a group project involving figures including Finney and Dave Kleiman. Wright produced no verified Finney evidence establishing this.

In 2024 the High Court of England and Wales found that Wright was not Satoshi Nakamoto and that documents advanced in support of his claim had been forged. Wright’s association of Finney with a supposed secret Bitcoin authorship should not be treated as reliable evidence.

### 2026 Finney–Len Sassaman joint-Satoshi theory

**Status: documentary theory; unproved and publicly contested.**

The 2026 documentary *Finding Satoshi* argued that Finney and privacy technologist **Len Sassaman** jointly constituted Satoshi Nakamoto.

The theory drew on:

- their cryptographic backgrounds;
- cypherpunk networks;
- complementary alleged skills;
- activity-timing and stylistic arguments;
- both men’s deaths before later identity investigations could question them fully.

Critics noted:

- race-day overlaps between Finney’s documented physical activity and Satoshi’s communications;
- Sassaman’s recorded skepticism about Bitcoin;
- a lack of primary correspondence showing secret joint development;
- no demonstrated access by either estate to Nakamoto-associated early reserves;
- the capacity of flexible team theories to explain away contradictory timestamps without independently proving collaboration.

As of September 2026, the documentary has not supplied a private key, authenticated development archive, financial trail, or contemporaneous witness sufficient to establish its conclusion.

### Environmental and monetary criticism

Finney’s work contributed to proof-of-work systems later criticized for energy consumption. In surviving discussions he considered efficiency, emissions, layered payment systems, and banking-like overlays. There is no evidence that he controlled Bitcoin’s later energy profile or profited by concealing it.

Some later critics apply general accusations against Bitcoin—speculation, crime facilitation, concentration of wealth—to Finney personally. No source located in the adversarial pass documents his involvement in exchange fraud, theft, ransomware, laundering, market manipulation, or illegal securities sales.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Hal invented Bitcoin”

- **Story:** Finney created Bitcoin and used Satoshi Nakamoto as a disguise.
- **Appearance:** recurring Bitcoin forums, social media, Satoshi-investigation books, videos, and documentaries from 2013 onward.
- **Circulators:** amateur investigators, cryptocurrency enthusiasts, stylometric analysts, some documentary producers, and Craig Wright-adjacent narratives.
- **Evidence for:** capability, chronology, RPOW, early participation, geographic coincidence, alleged style similarities.
- **Evidence against:** two-sided emails, wallet history, race timestamps, code differences, denials, lack of Nakamoto-key proof.
- **Effect:** raised Finney from early collaborator to quasi-founder in Bitcoin folklore, while making his family a target of invasive investigation and extortion.

### “The first Bitcoin user”

- **Story:** Finney was Bitcoin’s first user.
- **Assessment:** essentially accurate if qualified as the first publicly identified user after Satoshi and probably the first outside person to run the released software. Calling him the literal first user erases Nakamoto’s prior operation.

### “The first Bitcoin transaction”

- **Story:** Finney received the first bitcoin transaction.
- **Assessment:** accurate for the first recorded person-to-person transfer. Mining rewards are technically transactions too, so an unqualified formulation can be misleading.

### “He predicted a $10 million bitcoin”

- **Story:** Finney precisely forecast Bitcoin’s future price days after launch.
- **Assessment:** exaggerated. He offered a hypothetical upper-value calculation based on Bitcoin becoming the dominant global payment system and the fixed supply being divided into global wealth. It was scenario analysis, not a dated forecast.

### “Bitcoin was named after his neighbor”

- **Story:** Finney took Dorian Satoshi Nakamoto’s name because he lived nearby.
- **Assessment:** possible only in an abstract sense; no evidence establishes that Finney knew Dorian or even knew his birth name.

### “Cryonically frozen”

- **Story:** Finney was “frozen” immediately after death so that he can be revived.
- **Assessment:** partly factual, technically imprecise. Alcor performed cardiopulmonary support, medication, cooling, perfusion, and vitrification procedures, then placed him in long-term liquid-nitrogen storage. Whether future revival will ever be possible is unknown; current science cannot revive a cryopreserved human.

### “Halvening/Halfinning”

Bitcoin’s approximately four-year block-subsidy reduction is often called the “halving.” Admirers punningly call it the **Hal Finney** or “halfinning.” This is commemorative folklore, not terminology created by Finney.

### “Saint Hal” and hagiography

Online memorial culture often presents Finney as uniquely prescient, wholly selfless, or almost saintly. The image draws on genuine testimony concerning his conduct during illness and his technical generosity, but it tends to:

- compress collective cypherpunk work into an individual story;
- overstate his role in Bitcoin’s design;
- describe speculative valuations as prophecy;
- omit the trusted-server limitations and lack of adoption of RPOW.

The hostile counter-myth—that he covertly manipulated history by pretending to correspond with himself—likewise rests on speculation rather than documentary proof.

## Death and Cryopreservation

By August 2014 Finney depended on mechanical ventilation. He and Fran traveled to Arizona on **August 26**, allowing Alcor personnel to prepare close to the hospital.

According to Alcor’s case summary:

1. Finney was admitted to an intensive-care unit near Alcor;
2. the family said goodbye;
3. ventilatory support was withdrawn under medical supervision;
4. caregivers ensured that he lacked conscious awareness of distress;
5. legal death was pronounced on August 28;
6. Alcor immediately restored circulation and ventilation for preservation purposes;
7. medications and external cooling were administered;
8. the body underwent cryoprotective perfusion and long-term storage preparation.

He was cryopreserved as Alcor patient **A-1436**.

“Cause of death” is conventionally reported as complications of ALS. Withdrawal of ventilatory support occurred in the context of terminal neurodegenerative disease and planned end-of-life care; no credible source treats the death as homicide or suspicious.

His legal death is final under present law. Cryonics advocates call preserved members “patients,” but this terminology does not mean he is medically alive or capable of communication.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Finney’s posthumous reputation rests on four linked achievements:

1. helping turn PGP into usable mass-market cryptographic software;
2. implementing privacy infrastructure rather than merely advocating it;
3. building RPOW as a functioning bridge between Hashcash and cryptocurrency;
4. recognizing, testing, and improving Bitcoin during its first days.

His preserved emails have become important primary documents in Bitcoin historiography. The January 2009 correspondence records the software as a small, fragile experiment rather than an already inevitable financial system.

Later commemorations include:

- recurring “Running Bitcoin” events and ALS fundraisers;
- the **Hal Finney Cryonics Research Fund**, established at Alcor in 2018;
- artworks and memorials based on his January 2010 tweet;
- the Human Rights Foundation’s **Finney Freedom Prize**, established in 2024 with Fran Finney’s approval;
- a posthumous inaugural Finney Prize recognizing the 2009–2012 era.

The prize is planned across Bitcoin halving eras and combines a physical award with **1 BTC**.

Finney is frequently invoked in debates over:

- open-source software;
- state access to encrypted communications;
- privacy-enhancing technology;
- trusted hardware;
- decentralized money;
- the ethics of energy-intensive proof of work;
- cryonics;
- treatment of severely disabled technologists;
- historical credit in collaborative software projects.

His significance does not depend on proving that he was Satoshi. The documentary record independently establishes his position in the history of PGP, anonymous remailers, reusable proof of work, and Bitcoin.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by actual evidence are included. Shared chronology, geography, or membership in a broad intellectual tradition is not by itself treated as a connection.

### Philip R. Zimmermann

**Evidentiary label: documented fact.**

Finney contacted Zimmermann after learning of PGP in 1991, helped develop PGP 2.0, and later became one of the first employees of PGP Inc. Their collaboration continued through later corporate incarnations until Finney’s retirement in 2011. Zimmermann publicly credited Finney with major technical contributions and explained why his role had initially been kept quiet.

### David Lee Chaum

**Evidentiary label: documented intellectual influence; personal meeting not established.**

Finney repeatedly analyzed Chaum’s blind-signature protocols and digital-cash system from at least 1992–1994. “Protecting Privacy with Electronic Cash,” “Digital Cash & Privacy,” and his related technical notes explicitly develop problems posed by Chaumian money. No direct meeting or private correspondence was established from the consulted sources.

### Nick Szabo

**Evidentiary label: documented shared network and intellectual exchange.**

Finney and Szabo participated in overlapping cypherpunk/Extropian digital-cash discussions in the 1990s and 2000s. Finney’s RPOW and Szabo’s bit-gold thinking addressed related proof-of-work and scarcity problems. In 2011 Szabo identified himself, Wei Dai, and Finney as the few people who pursued these ideas substantially before Nakamoto. This documents intellectual recognition and shared discussion spaces, not joint authorship of Bitcoin.

### Bailey Whitfield Diffie

**Evidentiary label: documented institutional/intellectual connection; close personal relationship not established.**

Diffie’s public-key cryptography was foundational to PGP and thus to Finney’s work. Cypherpunk archives also record Diffie and Finney within the same cryptographic discussion environment. The consulted material does not establish a sustained personal collaboration.

### Martin Edward Hellman

**Evidentiary label: documented intellectual connection; personal relationship not established.**

Hellman’s public-key work with Diffie formed part of the technical foundation used by PGP. Mailing-list archives place Hellman and Finney in the broader cryptographic network, but no evidence was found of a teacher-student bond or major bilateral project.

### Ralph Charles Merkle

**Evidentiary label: documented intellectual influence; personal relationship not established.**

Merkle’s public-key and hash-tree work belongs to the cryptographic lineage underlying PGP and Bitcoin. Finney’s systems used related cryptographic concepts. The evidence examined does not establish a close personal or organizational relationship.

### Friedrich August von Hayek

**Evidentiary label: reported intellectual association, not a personal connection.**

Finney moved in Extropian and digital-cash circles where Hayekian competing-currency and decentralized-market ideas were frequently discussed. His own writings contemplated privately issued electronic monies. No direct meeting was possible in the relevant period and no explicit claim that Hayek was Finney’s singular formative influence was found.

### Alan Mathison Turing

**Evidentiary label: symbolic and disciplinary influence only.**

Turing is part of the intellectual history of computation relevant to Finney’s profession, but no source located documents a specific biographical connection beyond that general inheritance. This is not counted as an interpersonal Atlas crossing.

### Other roster names

No adequately documented personal crossing was established with the many other people on the Atlas roster. In particular, the research did not support claims of direct relationships with Adi Shamir, Ronald Rivest, Leonard Adleman, Claude Shannon, John von Neumann, Dennis Ritchie, Donald Knuth, Linus Torvalds, Peter Thiel, Elon Musk, Sam Altman, Sam Bankman-Fried, or Marc Andreessen. Mere use of related technology or presence on a large mailing-list archive was not treated as a relationship.
