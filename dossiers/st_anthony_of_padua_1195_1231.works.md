## Works by Saint Anthony of Padua

### Sermonaries: written instruments for preaching

- **1227–1228 (probable final redaction):** *Sermones dominicales* (“Sunday Sermons”). An obra comprising a general prologue, 53 liturgically ordered sermons, seven short sectional prologues, and an epilogue; it is not a transcript of Anthony’s preaching but a learned theological and exegetical instrument for training preachers. The dating follows the 1979 critical edition, but scholars recognize several compositional layers and continuing uncertainty about the chronology of individual sermons; the manuscripts supply no stable collective title, though Anthony’s epilogue calls it an “opus of the Gospels through the yearly cycle.” [Meirinhos, pp. 147–52](https://ojs.letras.up.pt/index.php/mediaevalia/article/viewFile/894/863)

- **1227–1228 (probable final redaction):** *Sermones in honorem et laudem beatissimae Mariae virginis* (“Sermons in Honor and Praise of the Blessed Virgin Mary”). A coherent Marian cycle of four sermons with its own prologue, transmitted as part of the *Sermones dominicales* rather than as an unequivocally independent book. Its prologue announces five sermons, but the promised sermon for the Nativity of the Lord is absent, so the project is textually incomplete or was never completed in the announced form. [Meirinhos, pp. 148–49](https://ojs.letras.up.pt/index.php/mediaevalia/article/viewFile/894/863)

- **Disputed: traditionally 1230–1231; alternatively 1225–1227:** *Sermones festivi* or *Sermones in solemnitatibus* (“Festival Sermons”). An unfinished project of twenty sermons for feasts, without an authorial prologue or epilogue and without the literary finish or concordance method of the Sunday collection. The early hagiographical tradition and the critical edition place it in Anthony’s last year and explain its incompleteness by his death; Agostinho Frias and João S. Costa instead argue that its rougher state may represent an earlier compositional stage in France. [Meirinhos, pp. 149, 151–52](https://ojs.letras.up.pt/index.php/mediaevalia/article/viewFile/894/863)

### Finding protocol

- **Probably 1227–1228 or shortly afterward:** *Tabula sermonum dominicalium* (“Table of the Sunday Sermons”). A protocol-like retrieval instrument listing the beginnings of the authorities explained in the Sunday sermons so that a preacher could locate material without rereading the whole collection. Only seven manuscripts preserve it, sometimes fragmentarily or repositioned; the table attached to the festive sermons in one manuscript is not considered authorial. [Meirinhos, pp. 147–49, 162–64](https://ojs.letras.up.pt/index.php/mediaevalia/article/viewFile/894/863)

### Oral manifestations that resist cataloguing as texts

- **Before 1231:** *Oral sermons and preaching campaigns*. Anthony indisputably made sermons as performances, but these manifestations are not identical with the surviving written sermonaries, which were composed as aids to other preachers rather than as transcripts. No complete, critically established catalogue of his delivered sermons survives; consequently individual occasions cannot responsibly be supplied with invented titles or dates. [Meirinhos, pp. 146–47](https://ojs.letras.up.pt/index.php/mediaevalia/article/viewFile/894/863); [Centro Studi Antoniani](https://www.centrostudiantoniani.it/sermoni-di-santantonio-0)

- **Dates unknown:** *Orationes* (“Prayers”) attributed through oral tradition. Medieval sources attach several freestanding prayers to Anthony, but their transmission is oral and their authorship cannot be established; they therefore resist inclusion as authenticated obras. They must also be distinguished from the prayers and summarizing doxologies integral to the genuine sermons. [Meirinhos, pp. 177–78](https://ojs.letras.up.pt/index.php/mediaevalia/article/viewFile/894/863)

### Attributed things excluded from the authenticated corpus

- **Shortly after 1231:** *Miscellanea of the “Treasure Codex”*. A compilation of 103 short texts, including 38 sermons, once entertained as Anthony’s notes because it shares a manuscript and scribal hand with his sermonaries. Its critical editors instead locate it in Anthony’s Paduan circle several years after his death; its anonymous author or authors remain unknown, so it is a related manifestation rather than his obra. [Meirinhos, pp. 174–75](https://ojs.letras.up.pt/index.php/mediaevalia/article/viewFile/894/863)

- **Date unknown; attribution rejected:** *Expositio vel Interpretatio mystica in Sacram Scripturam*. An anonymous biblical compilation apparently derived from Anthony’s sermons, possibly through the 1520 printed edition; it is a posthumous construction from his work, not something he made. [Meirinhos, pp. 176–77](https://ojs.letras.up.pt/index.php/mediaevalia/article/viewFile/894/863)

- **Thirteenth century; attribution rejected:** *Expositio*, *Moralitates*, or *Sermones in Psalmos*. A set of moral sermons on the Psalms formerly presented as Anthony’s autograph and sometimes dated 1224, but manuscript evidence identifies its author as Cardinal John of Abbeville. [Meirinhos, p. 177](https://ojs.letras.up.pt/index.php/mediaevalia/article/viewFile/894/863)

- **Mid-thirteenth century; attribution rejected:** *Concordantiae morales Sacrorum Bibliorum*. A biblical concordance first printed under Anthony’s name in 1624; the numerous manuscripts do not attribute it to him, and scholarship assigns it to an unidentified Franciscan of the mid-thirteenth century. [Meirinhos, pp. 179–80](https://ojs.letras.up.pt/index.php/mediaevalia/article/viewFile/894/863)

- **Fourteenth century or later; attribution rejected:** *Sermones quadragesimales et de tempore*. A sermon collection attributed to Anthony by Bartholomew of Pisa around 1385 and repeatedly printed under his name, but substantially or wholly attributable to Henry of Montejardim, who lived about a century later. It must not be confused with the genuine Lenten sermons contained within the *Sermones dominicales*. [Meirinhos, pp. 178–79](https://ojs.letras.up.pt/index.php/mediaevalia/article/viewFile/894/863)

- **Later medieval; attribution rejected:** *Sermones de sanctis in communi*. Thirty-six sermons for the common feasts of saints, largely attributed by the scholarly repertory to Peter of Reims rather than Anthony. Other late *Sermones de sanctis* and *Sermones de diversis* printed under Anthony’s authority are likewise much later compositions. [Meirinhos, pp. 177, 179](https://ojs.letras.up.pt/index.php/mediaevalia/article/viewFile/894/863)

- **Date and authorship disputed, but not Anthony’s:** *Sacrum commercium sancti Francisci cum domina Paupertate*. This Franciscan allegory was assigned to Anthony in two relatively late manuscripts; its actual author and date remain debated, but the attribution to Anthony is considered deceptive. [Meirinhos, pp. 178–79](https://ojs.letras.up.pt/index.php/mediaevalia/article/viewFile/894/863)

- **Dates unknown; attribution rejected:** *Epistulae duae*. Two letters published as Anthony’s in 1899 are judged certainly apocryphal; no authentic correspondence by him belongs to the critically accepted corpus. [Meirinhos, p. 177](https://ojs.letras.up.pt/index.php/mediaevalia/article/viewFile/894/863)

- **Date unknown; lost and unauthenticated:** *Scholia in Sacram Bibliam*. Biblical scholia reportedly preserved in a now-unlocated manuscript at Forlì and locally described as written in Anthony’s own hand. Because the witness is lost and the attribution rested on relic-like claims about the manuscript, the work cannot be authenticated or securely described. [Meirinhos, p. 177](https://ojs.letras.up.pt/index.php/mediaevalia/article/viewFile/894/863)

- **Date unknown; attribution rejected:** *Incendium amoris*. A lost manuscript reported in 1732 attributed this “Fire of Love” to Anthony, but modern scholarship considers the attribution certainly apocryphal. [Meirinhos, p. 180](https://ojs.letras.up.pt/index.php/mediaevalia/article/viewFile/894/863)

- **Fifteenth century; attribution rejected:** *Imitatio Christi*. Its occasional association with Anthony is unfounded and may derive from confusion with a later Portuguese translator named António de Pádua e Belas; it is generally attributed to Thomas à Kempis. [Meirinhos, pp. 180–81](https://ojs.letras.up.pt/index.php/mediaevalia/article/viewFile/894/863)

### Sources

- José Francisco Meirinhos, [“S. António de Lisboa, escritor: a tradição dos *Sermones*”](https://ojs.letras.up.pt/index.php/mediaevalia/article/viewFile/894/863), *Mediaevalia: Textos e Estudos* 11–12 (1997), pp. 139–182. This study analyzes the manuscript tradition and the 1979 critical edition and concludes that only the Sunday sermons, festive sermons, and their table belong securely to Anthony.

- Sancti Antonii Patavini, *Sermones dominicales et festivi*, ed. Beniamino Costa, Leonardo Frasson, Giovanni Luisetto, with Paolo Marangon, 3 vols. (Padua: Edizioni Messaggero, 1979). The standard critical edition; its corpus, dating arguments, manuscript stemmata, and section “De scriptis dubiis ac spuriis” are reported and assessed by Meirinhos above.

- Centro Studi Antoniani, [“Sermoni di Sant’Antonio”](https://www.centrostudiantoniani.it/sermoni-di-santantonio-0). Specialist research portal describing the sermonary as Anthony’s principal literary-theological obra and as a doctrinal treatise in sermon form.

- Basilica of Saint Anthony, [“Sermons”](https://basilicadisantantonio.com/en/content/sermons). Overview based on the critical-edition corpus, emphasizing that the written sermons were composed as instruments for the formation of fellow friars and preachers.
