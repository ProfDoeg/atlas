# Antoinette de Saint Léger: Research Dossier

## Scope, Method, and Evidentiary Labels

Antoinette de Saint Léger left no readily accessible autobiography, and much of her surviving personal archive remains unpublished or dispersed. This dossier therefore gives priority to:

- Eva Frassi’s archival research, which cites civil registers, municipal correspondence, notarial deeds, death records, auction papers, lawyers’ files, private diaries, and museum archives;
- Antoinette’s own published horticultural paper;
- surviving correspondence and diary references by contemporaries;
- institutional histories and public records concerning the Brissago Islands;
- specialist art-historical and biographical studies.

Popular biographies frequently repeat picturesque claims without supplying evidence. Those claims are retained where historically influential but labeled accordingly.

Labels used:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a contemporary record, primary document, or specifically identified archival item.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a credible secondary source but not independently verified here from the underlying document.
- **Allegation or theory:** a claim advanced by a source without conclusive proof.
- **Rumor or myth:** folklore, gossip, literary embellishment, or a story for which no adequate documentary foundation has been located.
- **Unresolved:** available records conflict or remain insufficient.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Usual name:** Antoinette de Saint Léger.

**Documentarily preferred spelling of married surname:** Antoinette or Antonietta **Saint Leger**, without the acute accent. Eva Frassi observes that surviving records use *Saint Leger*, consistent with the Irish family’s spelling. *Saint Léger* became common in French, Italian, and later publicity.

**Other names and forms:**

- Antonietta Bayer
- Antoinette Bayer
- Beyer, Beger, or Bayer in variant or erroneous records
- Antonietta Saint Leger
- Antoinette de Saint-Léger
- Baronessa di Saint Leger
- Baroness de Saint Léger
- “Signora delle Isole” / “Lady of the Islands”
- “Dame du Lac” / “Lady of the Lake”
- “La Sirena” / “the Siren,” a later reported nickname
- “Principessa di Saint Leger,” principally the title attached to Daniele Ranzoni’s portrait rather than a securely established rank
- Antonietta Tzikos, a name associated with her relationship to Perikles Tzikos and used in the title or identification of her portrait
- “Madama,” James Joyce’s reported name for her

**Date of birth:** 20 June 1856. This date appears in the Brissago civil register and is broadly accepted.

**Place of birth:** **Unresolved.**

- The standard account gives **St Petersburg, Russian Empire**.
- A 12 December 1949 letter from the Intragna hospital to collector Giacomo Jucker stated that she had been born in **Königsberg, Prussia**—present-day Kaliningrad—and that this information had passed through third parties who said they had received it from Antoinette herself.
- No birth certificate establishing either location was located in the sources consulted. St Petersburg remains the dominant but not conclusively proven version.

**Date and place of death:** 24 January 1948, Ospedale or Ospizio San Donato, Intragna, Canton Ticino, Switzerland.

**Age at death:** 91.

**Occupations and activities:** Pianist; horticulturist and garden creator; art patron and hostess; collector; doll designer or manufacturer; writer on horticulture; investor and serial business promoter.

**Nationality or civic status:** Born within either the Russian Empire or Prussia; later resident in Italy and Switzerland. A reported chronology states that she obtained Swiss citizenship in 1934, but the precise naturalization instrument was not located in the accessible sources.

## Family and Ancestry

### Recorded parents

The Brissago civil register and Antoinette’s municipal correspondence identify her parents as **Wilhelmina** and **Marian Beyer/Bayer**. The reading of her mother’s surname or additional name in one 1914 letter is uncertain—Frassi transcribes possible forms such as “Cohui” or “Cosui.”

Some later accounts describe Wilhelmina as a German-born dancer connected with the Russian court. This detail is **reported but not securely documented** in the sources presently accessible.

No reliable information was located about siblings or earlier generations.

### Alleged Romanov paternity

A long-standing story identifies Antoinette as an illegitimate daughter of **Tsar Alexander II of Russia**.

**What the story says:** Wilhelmina Bayer supposedly had a relationship with Alexander II, and Antoinette was their unacknowledged daughter. Her education, command of Russian, reported court connections, and later social self-presentation have been used as circumstantial support.

**When and where it appears:** The claim appears in twentieth-century local histories and journalistic retellings and remains central to tourist literature, a 2008 biographical novel, a 2017 documentary, and reporting as recent as 2025.

**Evidence for it:** No birth record, acknowledgment, imperial correspondence, financial provision, DNA evidence, or contemporary court document establishing Alexander II’s paternity was found. Antoinette’s reported aristocratic education and knowledge of court society are compatible with—but do not prove—the story.

**Evidence against or limiting it:** Her parents are named as Wilhelmina and Marian Beyer in civil material. The unresolved St Petersburg/Königsberg birthplace further weakens attempts to build a precise Romanov genealogy.

**Assessment:** **Unverified allegation and identity myth.** It should not be presented as established ancestry.

**Reputational effect:** It transformed a socially mobile, multilingual woman of uncertain origins into a “Russian princess,” supplied an explanation for her self-confidence and aristocratic circle, and gave her eventual death in an institution for the poor the structure of a fall-from-royalty legend.

## Childhood and Early Life

Little independently verifiable information survives about Antoinette’s first sixteen years.

She is said to have grown up in cultivated or aristocratic surroundings and to have attended the **Smolny Institute for Noble Maidens** in St Petersburg. The Smolny claim is repeated by local historians, but no enrollment record was located in the material consulted. It is therefore a **reported fact requiring archival verification**.

Her later linguistic abilities are consistently described as extraordinary. Municipal and biographical sources credit her with spoken and written Russian, Italian, German, French, English, Polish, and Greek. Her letters and international relationships demonstrate multilingual competence, although mastery of every listed language has not been separately tested.

At approximately sixteen, around **1872**, she moved to Naples.

Two explanations circulate:

- **Reported medical account:** she was sent south because of pulmonary tuberculosis, then commonly called *mal sottile*.
- **Alternative reported account:** the move was intended to further her education and language study.

The two explanations are not mutually exclusive. Her long life provides no basis for denying a youthful pulmonary illness, but no medical record has been located.

In Naples she stayed with **Giulio Evardo/Edoardo Ewald Jaeger** (approximately 1827–1882 or 1828–1883), a German-born, naturalized Italian consular official. Sources disagree over his exact dates and office:

- Frassi describes him as United States consul in Naples.
- Other accounts call him German consul at Messina.
- A later Max Emden study identifies him as German federal consul in Messina from 1868.

This may reflect different appointments during his career, but the sequence requires further consular-archive research.

## Education, Teachers, Mentors, and Formation

### Smolny Institute

**Reported fact:** Antoinette studied at the Smolny Institute, a prestigious school for girls of the Russian nobility. If verified, it would account for her languages, music, drawing-room manners, and familiarity with elite networks.

### Franz Liszt

Between **1877 and 1878**, Antoinette reportedly met **Franz Liszt** at the thermal baths of Albano near Rome.

Eva Frassi’s research states that:

- Liszt frequently visited the Jaeger household;
- Antoinette became his pupil and admirer;
- he gave English lessons to her son Giulio;
- he dedicated a work to her.

The precise work supposedly dedicated to her is not identified in the accessible excerpt, and no Liszt catalogue or manuscript dedication was found during this research. The pupil relationship is therefore a **strongly reported fact**, but the dedication remains **unresolved**.

### Artistic and intellectual formation

Her mature interests combined:

- piano performance and chamber music;
- botanical acclimatization and landscape composition;
- painting and collecting;
- multilingual correspondence;
- spiritualism and occult or magical ideas;
- mechanical, chemical, and commercial schemes;
- literary sociability.

No formal scientific degree or horticultural qualification has been found. Her botanical knowledge appears to have developed through practice, travel, exchange with gardeners and collectors, and the long experimental cultivation of the Brissago Islands.

## Marriages, Partnerships, and Children

The number and legal validity of Antoinette’s marriages are unusually uncertain. Modern accounts generally identify three formal marriages and one later partnership; local folklore sometimes raises the number to four or seven.

### 1. Friedrich or Federico Stolte

Around **1873**, Antoinette married or formed a marital union with **Friedrich/Federico Stolte**, a German or German-associated landowner at Portici near Naples.

Children attributed to this relationship:

- **Maria Grazia Vera Stolte**, born Naples in 1874; died on the Brissago Islands in 1893.
- **Peppinuccio/Peppino Stolte**, born in 1875; Frassi gives death in 1889, while later summaries sometimes give 1892 or identify his birth as 1876.

After the parents separated, Peppinuccio reportedly remained with his father in Naples.

Stolte is variously described as a Portici landowner or German consul-general and is reported to have died in 1878. The exact marriage and death documentation was not located.

### 2. Giulio Evardo/Edoardo Ewald Jaeger

Antoinette subsequently married—or was regarded as married to—the older consular official who had first hosted her.

Child:

- **Giulio Antonio Jaeger**, born Naples in 1876; died at Rolle in 1950.

The dating creates an evident overlap with the Stolte relationship. Frassi explicitly notes uncertainty over the juridical status of Antoinette’s unions. Giulio Antonio later assisted his mother and worked for Max Emden as keeper or “admiral” of a fleet of as many as thirteen boats.

### 3. Richard Arthur Flemyng/Fleming Saint Leger

Antoinette met the Anglo-Irish **Richard Arthur Flemyng Saint Leger** in Milan. Their marriage is recorded in the Brissago civil register and marriage index as occurring on **1 October 1881**.

Richard, born in 1858, is described as:

- an Anglo-Irish officer;
- a member or claimant of the Saint Leger family of Doneraile;
- wealthy through family property or inheritance in Dublin, Cork, and Tipperary;
- later an employee of the British consulate at Naples.

The popular title “Baron de Saint Leger” was used socially. Later sources also associate Richard with the earldom or viscountcy of Doneraile. The precise legal basis of his title and succession was not established here. Stories that the title had descended from a grant by William the Conqueror belong to family legend rather than demonstrated peerage history.

Children:

- **James Saint Leger**, born in Milan in approximately April 1882. The Ghiffa death record states that he died at Cargiago at 8 p.m. on **5 September 1882**, aged five months. Later accounts giving death in 1884 are erroneous or based on faulty secondary material.
- **Joan Saint Leger**, born on the Brissago Islands in 1886; died at Rolle in 1955. She later appears under the name Joan Müller St. Leger.

An oddity in James’s death record is that Antoinette was described as *celibe*—unmarried. Frassi regards this as one reason not to assume that every purported marriage was juridically straightforward.

Richard left the island and effectively separated from Antoinette in **1897**. He lived at Naples, reportedly worked at the British consulate, and died there in 1922.

### 4. Perikles Tzikos

After the collapse of her third marriage, Antoinette formed an intimate and financial partnership with **Perikles Tzikos** (1851–21 July 1906), an industrial entrepreneur involved in railway construction and operation.

A surviving unpublished 1898 diary by Perikles’s son **Paolo Tzikos**, held in the Archivio di Stato del Cantone Ticino, records the developing connection between Antoinette and Perikles. The precise surviving passage was not available in full, but Frassi treats the relationship as documentarily grounded.

Perikles reportedly left his entire estate to Antoinette when he died. Sources sometimes call him her “fourth husband”; the legal existence of a marriage has not been demonstrated.

The surname **Tzikos** consequently appears in relation to Antoinette, including in art-historical titles. It was not her birth name on the stronger documentary evidence.

## Chronological Life History

### 1856–1872: Russia or Prussia

Antoinette was born on 20 June 1856. Her conventional birthplace is St Petersburg; a later hospital communication records Königsberg. She grew up under circumstances sufficiently privileged to acquire languages, music, and elite social confidence.

Her supposed Smolny education and imperial paternity became the foundational mythology of her later identity.

### Circa 1872–1881: Naples, Portici, Rome, and Italy

At sixteen she moved to Naples, either for treatment of a suspected pulmonary condition, further education, or both.

She lived initially in the household of Giulio Jaeger. During the next several years she:

- married or partnered Federico Stolte;
- had Maria Grazia Vera and Peppinuccio;
- separated from Stolte;
- married or partnered Jaeger;
- had Giulio Antonio;
- traveled to Rome, Florence, and Sanremo;
- met Liszt at Albano in 1877–78;
- moved to Milan after the Jaeger relationship ended;
- met Richard Saint Leger.

The closeness of these pregnancies and unions, imperfect civil registration, and inconsistent later accounts produced the legend of a woman married four or even seven times.

### 1881–1883: Cargiago and the Troubetzkoy circle

After marrying Richard on 1 October 1881, the couple rented a chalet at **Cargiago**, now part of Ghiffa, from **Prince Pietro Troubetzkoy and Princess Ada Troubetzkoy**.

The Troubetzkoy estate, Villa Ada, contained heated and unheated greenhouses, bamboo groves, eucalyptus, and exotic plants. This environment was an identifiable precursor to Antoinette’s later island garden.

Richard and Antoinette’s infant James died at Cargiago in September 1882.

Their relationship with the Troubetzkoys continued after they left Cargiago. A cultivar, *Chamaecyparis obtusa* ‘Troubetzkoyana,’ associated with the Troubetzkoy garden, was reportedly grown on the Brissago Islands.

### 1883–1884: Minusio and Locarno

The Saint Legers moved to **Villa Baronata** at Mappo, Minusio, in 1883–84. The property had previously been purchased in Mikhail Bakunin’s name with money supplied by Carlo Cafiero and had served Bakunin’s revolutionary circle.

Antoinette did not meet Bakunin there: he died in 1876, years before her residence. The relationship is one of successive occupancy and symbolic inheritance of a site, not a personal connection.

During approximately six months of 1884, the couple lived in Locarno in the home of **Emilio Balli**. Balli’s brothers included Federico and Francesco Balli, the latter mayor of Locarno and a cultivated amateur musician. Francesco later performed with Antoinette and Filippo Franzoni.

### 1885: Acquisition of the Brissago Islands

Contemporary notarial records establish two transactions:

- **18 March 1885:** deed no. 873, purchase of San Pancrazio/Isola Grande from Carlo Tomaso Borrani for **15,000 Swiss francs**, paid by Antoinette with Richard’s consent.
- **7 July 1885:** deed no. 878, purchase of Sant’Apollinare/Isolino from the parish church and municipality of Brissago for **5,001 francs**.

Thus the documentarily supported total was **20,001 francs**, not the 10,000 or 25,000 francs often given in later accounts.

The islands had previously supported a medieval Humiliati community and later contained ruined religious buildings. In the nineteenth century they were largely neglected and were sometimes called the Rabbit Islands. Plans to use them in connection with explosives manufacture had generated official concern before Antoinette’s acquisition.

### 1885–1897: Construction of the island estate

Antoinette and Richard established a villa and began transforming the larger island.

The work reportedly included:

- transporting large quantities of soil, manure, and building material across the lake by boat or barge;
- grading and constructing paths;
- planting exotic and subtropical specimens;
- establishing conservatories or protected cultivation;
- testing plants from the Mediterranean, East Asia, the Americas, southern Africa, and Oceania;
- assembling a library and art collection;
- creating facilities for guests, musicians, and artists.

Popular accounts divide credit differently. Some emphasize Antoinette as the principal creator; others stress Richard’s botanical interests. A later witness, Bruno Spertini, claimed that she had done “very little or nothing” and credited Max Emden with the surviving garden. That statement concerns the garden’s later form and conflicts with Antoinette’s 1913 detailed account of decades of planting. The most supportable conclusion is that Antoinette initiated and substantially developed the exotic garden, while Richard, professional gardeners, and later Emden-era work also shaped it.

### Artistic circle, 1886 onward

The island became a meeting place for the Lombard and Swiss art worlds.

Documented or strongly evidenced participants include:

- **Daniele Ranzoni**, who painted Antoinette’s celebrated portrait;
- **Filippo Franzoni**, who worked in a studio on the islands in 1886–87;
- **Prince Pietro Troubetzkoy**;
- **Vittore Grubicy de Dragon**;
- **Ferdinand Hodler**, who began frequenting the circle in 1886;
- **Marianne Werefkin**, supported by surviving correspondence;
- **Gordon McCouch**, likewise connected through correspondence;
- **Lilia Slutskaya**, who painted Antoinette’s dolls;
- **Richard Seewald** and **Ernst Geiger**, named in Frassi’s broader study;
- composer **Ruggero Leoncavallo**.

On **26 February 1888**, Antoinette performed on piano in a chamber concert in which Ferdinand Hodler played cello. This is unusually concrete evidence of her musical activity.

Claims that Giovanni Segantini, Cosima Wagner, Erich Maria Remarque, Hermann Hesse, and other celebrities visited are common, but the accessible sources did not provide comparable primary documentation for every name. They should not all be treated as equally established.

### Family deaths

Antoinette’s daughter Maria Grazia Vera died on the islands in 1893. Her son Peppinuccio had died several years earlier, probably in 1889, although 1892 appears in later summaries. Infant James had died in 1882.

These deaths preceded or accompanied the breakdown of her marriage to Richard.

### 1897–1906: Separation and Tzikos partnership

Richard left in 1897. Later writers attributed the separation to Antoinette’s commercial and erotic “escapades,” but the phrasing is interpretive and often rests on hostile gossip. Separation itself is established; its private causes are not.

By 1898 Antoinette was closely involved with Perikles Tzikos. Through him she entered railway and industrial investment networks. She is reported to have invested in:

- Transcaucasian railways;
- Romanian tramways;
- other railway schemes;
- industrial or chemical processes.

Tzikos died on 21 July 1906 and reportedly left her his estate. The amount and legal form of the inheritance have not been established.

### 1906–1914: Mature island period

Antoinette continued enlarging the garden and operating the islands as an artistic and intellectual salon.

She maintained a heavy international correspondence. Because of the amount of mail and the transmission of plants, samples, and research material, a postal service was established on the islands. Swiss stamps canceled **ISOLE S. LEGER** later became collectible. Claims that she issued wholly independent stamps should be distinguished from the documented use of Swiss postage with a local cancellation.

She built a workshop for artistic dolls. Surviving examples are reported, and Lilia Slutskaya painted dolls for her. No comprehensive catalogue or business ledger was located.

She also experimented with unorthodox technical ideas. Projects attributed to her include:

- extracting alcohol from peat;
- producing lubricating oil from grasshoppers;
- other chemical and industrial processes;
- railway and tramway finance.

Some proposals may have been impractical, but they were not merely fantasies: the peat-alcohol project later generated a written contract and multi-year experiments at Collegio Papio.

### 1913: Horticultural publication

Antoinette published **“The Vegetation of the Island of St. Leger in Lago Maggiore”** in the *Journal of the Royal Horticultural Society*, new series, volume 38, pages 503–514, in 1913.

The paper records:

- her plant selections;
- acclimatization successes and failures;
- climatic observations;
- architectural and landscape interventions;
- the construction of the island garden.

It is her only securely identified published work and is important primary evidence that she—not merely Richard or Max Emden—played a knowledgeable and sustained role in the garden.

### 1914–1916: War and financial pressure

Letters Antoinette sent to Brissago on 9 and 10 August 1914 survive in the municipal archive and contain information about her family identity.

By 1916 her finances were sufficiently precarious that a sale of the islands was under discussion. Archival evidence includes:

- a 13 March 1916 letter from socialist politician and newspaper associate **Guglielmo Canevascini** asking lawyer **Francesco Borella** to handle her affairs and a possible sale;
- Antoinette’s 3 April 1916 letter to Borella, inviting him to inspect the estate, describing its improvements and value, and discussing prospective purchasers.

She resisted selling and sought mortgages instead.

One prospective buyer, a singer named **Gürtler**, reportedly proposed breeding Angora cats on the island. Antoinette suspected that he wanted a secluded site for other reasons. Her suspicion is recorded but the underlying purpose remains unknown.

### 1917 and 1919: James Joyce

**Documented or strongly evidenced fact:** James Joyce visited the Locarno area twice, in 1917 and 1919, and met Antoinette on both occasions.

Frassi argues that the encounters were relevant to Joyce’s work on *Ulysses*, especially “Sirens” and “Circe.” Antoinette reportedly gave Joyce books and letters belonging to Tzikos.

The further proposition that Antoinette inspired **Bella Cohen**, **Circe**, or aspects of **Ellen Bloom** is a **literary theory**, not a demonstrated statement of Joyce’s intention. Frassi notes visual and mystical parallels; later journalism has amplified these into direct-model claims.

### 1919–1920: Rilke and Kessler

**Rainer Maria Rilke:** A letter dated 22 October 1919 records his wish to move to Antoinette’s island despite describing her through local gossip as an ugly “Bluebeard” who buried one of her men every third year. This proves knowledge of her, interest in the island, and circulation of hostile stories. It does not by itself prove a stay on the island. Later tourist accounts routinely call Rilke her guest, but the evidence cited by Frassi is more limited.

**Harry Graf Kessler:** His diary establishes that he was in Brissago from 7 April 1920 and was received by Antoinette on 12 April. This is a documented visit.

### 1920s: Decline of the island estate

Antoinette lived increasingly alone, though servants, boatmen, advisers, artists, and visitors remained in her network.

Her position deteriorated through a combination of:

- risky investments;
- inherited and newly contracted debts;
- multiple mortgages;
- costs of maintaining the islands;
- failed commercial projects;
- litigation and legal fees;
- declining health and age.

Some summaries call the outcome “bankruptcy.” The accessible documents establish heavy indebtedness, creditor pressure, mortgaging, forced asset dispositions, and later auction proceedings. Whether there was a formal bankruptcy judgment in the technical legal sense was not independently verified.

### 1927–1928: Sale to Max Emden

The eventual purchaser was **Max James Emden**, a Hamburg department-store entrepreneur and art collector.

The precise price varies:

- the islands were reportedly first offered by the administrator for 150,000 francs;
- previously undisclosed mortgages caused the practical price to rise;
- one detailed study says Antoinette ultimately received **340,000 francs**;
- the same account says Emden’s acquisition cost became approximately **350,000 francs**.

The difference likely reflects gross price, mortgages, and transaction structure.

Other prospective purchasers reportedly included a Turkish pasha and violinist **Bronisław Huberman**. Huberman and Emden initially considered a joint acquisition, with one taking each island, but Huberman withdrew after the mortgages emerged.

The contract required Antoinette to vacate by 15 February 1928. Sources disagree on her precise departure:

- the Max Emden study gives **28 January 1928**;
- a declaration made by Giulio Antonio Jaeger shows that she still anticipated leaving between **9 and 13 February 1928** and requested an inspection regarding allegations that she had ordered plants cut or uprooted.

The safest conclusion is that the transfer and physical removal unfolded between late January and mid-February 1928.

Her son Giulio, the Locarno firm Quadri, household worker Georg Fett, and boatman Dionisio Costantini reportedly assisted in moving her property. The image of her garden being carried away by boat has a documentary basis in the removal operation, although later retellings make it a more theatrical final voyage.

Emden demolished the Saint Leger house and remaining church fabric to build the present neoclassical Villa Emden, bath, orangery, and boathouse. He also restored, altered, and extended the planting.

### 1928–1940: Casa Moscia

Antoinette moved to **Casa Moscia** near Ascona, a former mill and pasta or pastry factory of Bolongaro-Pisani S.A. Emden purchased the building for her as part of the settlement.

She remained commercially active despite age and illness.

#### Peat-alcohol project

On 31 January 1934, Antoinette contracted with **Collegio Papio** in Ascona concerning experiments to extract alcohol from peat. Father **Geroldo Mallepell**, a chemistry teacher, worked on the process for about four years.

On 29 May 1938, her lawyer Francesco Borella informed Rector Father Leonardo Hugener that, using wording supplied by Antoinette, she considered the contract null and void.

No evidence was found that the process reached profitable production, obtained a patent, or generated a sustainable business. It was a documented experiment and failed commercial relationship, not evidence of fraud.

#### Loss of Casa Moscia

Debts led to the property being offered at auction:

- first auction: **18 November 1936**, unsuccessful;
- final award: **1 October 1937**, to Pietro Sasselli of Locarno for **80,500 francs**.

Antoinette apparently remained there for some time after the sale but no longer controlled the property.

### 1940–1948: Intragna

In November 1940, gravely ill and without funds, Antoinette entered Ospedale/Ospizio San Donato in Intragna. She occupied **room 32**.

Historian Romano Broggini, who knew her, later described her as intellectually alert but living within a private mental world. This is a personal recollection rather than a clinical diagnosis. No psychiatric or medical records were located.

She died there on **24 January 1948**. The consulted sources do not provide a specific medical cause of death.

## Residences, Migrations, and Travels

Known or reported movements include:

1. St Petersburg or Königsberg, 1856–approximately 1872.
2. Naples and Portici, from approximately age sixteen.
3. Albano near Rome, 1877–78.
4. Travel through Rome, Florence, and Sanremo.
5. Milan, by 1881.
6. Cargiago/Ghiffa, 1881–83.
7. Villa Baronata, Mappo-Minusio, 1883–84.
8. Emilio Balli’s house in Locarno, approximately six months in 1884.
9. Brissago Islands, 1885–early 1928.
10. Casa Moscia, Ascona, 1928–40.
11. Ospizio San Donato, Intragna, November 1940–24 January 1948.

Sources mention extensive travel and the acquisition of plants from several continents. No sufficiently documented itinerary of personal voyages to Asia, Africa, the Caucasus, or the Americas was located. The garden’s global flora must not automatically be treated as proof that Antoinette personally visited every source region.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Brissago garden

Antoinette’s principal project was the conversion of San Pancrazio into an acclimatization garden. Her 1913 paper makes it possible to distinguish this sustained horticultural work from later mythology.

The garden now contains roughly 1,600–1,700 taxa, but the present collection is cumulative. Not every surviving plant was introduced by Antoinette; Emden-era gardeners and the public botanical park added and replaced material.

### Postal service

She maintained a postal facility using Swiss stamps and the **ISOLE S. LEGER** cancellation. Its establishment was connected to unusually heavy correspondence and shipment activity. Later philatelic value belongs to the history of the postmark, not to proof that she operated a sovereign postal authority.

### Doll workshop

Antoinette had a workshop constructed for dolls and new models. Surviving examples are reported. Lilia Slutskaya painted some of them. The scale, revenues, employees, trademarks, and distribution network remain unknown.

### Investments

Reported interests include:

- Transcaucasian railway construction;
- Romanian tramways;
- other railway enterprises associated with Perikles Tzikos;
- peat-alcohol extraction;
- lubricants from grasshoppers;
- mortgages and real-estate financing.

No patent numbers, corporate filings, share certificates, or audited accounts were found. Assertions that she “invented” the processes should therefore be avoided.

### Wealth and financial structure

Her wealth appears to have come from several sources:

- Richard Saint Leger’s family inheritances and Irish real estate;
- Antoinette’s own funds or settlements;
- Perikles Tzikos’s reported bequest;
- the value of the island estate, artworks, and collectibles;
- borrowing against real property.

The sources do not permit a reliable reconstruction of her peak net worth.

Known figures:

- 15,000 francs for San Pancrazio in 1885.
- 5,001 francs for Sant’Apollinare in 1885.
- Approximately 340,000–350,000 francs in the 1927 sale structure.
- 80,500 francs paid for Casa Moscia at the 1937 auction.
- 7,000 francs in a 1949 private auction record for the Ranzoni portrait, followed by documentation of a 20,000-franc payment in its onward transaction. The difference indicates dealer or intermediary resale, not money received by Antoinette, who was already dead.

## Political, Religious, Philosophical, and Artistic Development

### Politics

No evidence was found that Antoinette held office, joined a political party, stood for election, or directed a political movement.

Her network included:

- socialist politician and newspaper figure Guglielmo Canevascini;
- lawyer Francesco Borella;
- people connected to the anarchist and reform milieu around Locarno and Monte Verità;
- émigré Russian artists.

Her residence at Bakunin’s former Villa Baronata and proximity to Monte Verità are culturally significant but do not establish that she was an anarchist or political revolutionary.

### Religion and spiritualism

Antoinette maintained links with Catholic institutions, including Collegio Papio, and an inventory-like literary parallel in *Ulysses* evokes an Agnus Dei and Sacred Heart invocation. These details do not establish formal religious orthodoxy.

She was also interested in **spiritualism**. Frassi reports writings in which Antoinette expressed the belief that she possessed magical power or could cast the evil eye on opponents.

These statements are evidence of her expressed beliefs or self-mythologizing, not evidence that supernatural events occurred.

### Artistic identity

Antoinette was:

- an accomplished pianist active in chamber performance;
- the subject of major portraits;
- an organizer of artistic encounters;
- a collector and promoter;
- a correspondent who sought opportunities for artist friends;
- a designer or patron of artistic dolls.

Surviving letters document her promotion of Marianne Werefkin and Gordon McCouch. Her patronage was therefore more than passive hospitality.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Richard Arthur Flemyng Saint Leger

Husband, co-resident, and source of name and social title. His inherited resources helped make the island project possible. He departed in 1897. Claims that her sexuality or speculative behavior drove him away remain interpretive.

### Perikles Tzikos

Partner and financial associate. His railway interests shaped her investment activity, and his 1906 bequest reportedly replenished her wealth. His papers may also have entered Joyce’s hands through Antoinette.

### Giulio Antonio Jaeger

Son, later practical assistant. He helped with her removal from the islands and became boatman or fleet manager for Max Emden.

### Pietro and Ada Troubetzkoy

Landlords and formative horticultural contacts at Cargiago. Their greenhouse landscape provided a model for Antoinette’s later work.

### Daniele Ranzoni

Painter and close artistic associate. His portrait, usually titled *La principessa di Saint Leger* or *Ritratto di Antonietta di Saint Leger*, became the defining visual image of Antoinette.

### Filippo Franzoni

Painter, island resident or studio user in 1886–87, and musical collaborator. He and Antoinette performed in the same cultural circle.

### Francesco Balli

Locarno mayor and musician who performed with Antoinette and Franzoni.

### Ferdinand Hodler

Frequented the island circle beginning in 1886 and played cello in a chamber concert with Antoinette on 26 February 1888.

### Marianne Werefkin and Gordon McCouch

Artists whom Antoinette actively promoted. Letters survive in the Fondazione Marianne Werefkin and Christoph Geiger archive.

### James Joyce

Met Antoinette during visits in 1917 and 1919. She reportedly supplied Tzikos books or papers. Later claims that she directly inspired *Ulysses* characters remain theories.

### Rainer Maria Rilke

Knew of her island and considered staying there. His 1919 letter also preserves hostile folklore about her. An actual island stay was not established from the evidence cited.

### Harry Graf Kessler

Documented visitor on 12 April 1920.

### Francesco “Nino” Borella

Lawyer, adviser, and long-term associate who dealt with her mortgages, sales, contracts, debts, and artwork. The relationship later became contentious.

### Max James Emden

Purchaser of the islands and buyer of Casa Moscia for Antoinette. The transaction ended her island project but provided a temporary financial settlement and residence.

## Controversies, Allegations, Litigation, and Financial Questions

### Validity and number of marriages

**Documented uncertainty:** At least three marital relationships and one later partnership are identifiable, but the legal termination and validity of the early marriages are obscure. James Saint Leger’s death record calls Antoinette unmarried despite the recorded 1881 ceremony.

**Folklore:** Claims of seven marriages appear in recent Swiss reporting without identification of the supposed additional husbands.

### Sexual reputation

Later accounts describe “erotic escapades,” numerous lovers, or scandalous island parties.

- Her relationship with Tzikos during or after separation from Richard is historically grounded.
- A broader pattern of unnamed lovers is poorly documented.
- The allegations were shaped by gendered local gossip about an independent female property owner.
- They should be recorded as **reported reputation**, not an enumerated factual sexual history.

### “Bluebeard” killings

Rilke’s 1919 letter repeats a story that Antoinette buried one of her men every third year.

There is no evidence of homicide, suspicious death, criminal investigation, charge, or concealed burial. Known deaths of children and partners have ordinary documentary traces. The story is **hostile rumor or macabre joke**.

### Litigation

Antoinette had a reputation for frequent lawsuits, and legal expenses contributed to her difficulties.

A specific dispute concerned the Ranzoni portrait, which had been pledged to lawyer Borella. Documents show that another lawyer, acting for Antoinette, contested the pledge. The available material proves a civil property dispute, not criminal misappropriation.

### Financial failure

Her losses are associated with railway, tramway, and industrial speculation, mounting mortgages, legal costs, and expensive maintenance.

No evidence of embezzlement, fraudulent securities, money laundering, corruption, or criminal conviction was located.

### Garden removals

Near her departure, allegations arose that plants had been or were to be cut down or uprooted. Giulio Antonio Jaeger, acting for his mother, denied that she had ordered such action and requested an official inspection before her departure.

The outcome of that inspection, if held, was not located. This remains a **documented dispute with unresolved facts**.

### Conflict with fishermen or Ronco

A later anecdote says Antoinette quarreled with the municipality of Ronco over fishing too close to the islands. No municipal file was located in the accessible material. It is a **reported local anecdote**.

### Adversarial search findings, including 2024–2026 material

Dedicated searches combined her name with terms for scandal, controversy, fraud, accusation, lawsuit, criticism, and conspiracy in English, Italian, German, and French.

The search found:

- repeated discussion of debt, speculative failure, mortgages, and litigation;
- gossip about lovers and multiple marriages;
- the Bluebeard rumor;
- disputes concerning pledged artwork and removal of plants;
- claims of magical power or the evil eye;
- contested credit for the botanical garden;
- inconsistent accounts of parentage, birthplace, title, and marital status.

It found **no credible evidence** of:

- criminal prosecution or conviction;
- fraud findings;
- corruption proceedings;
- money laundering;
- political violence;
- intelligence-service activity;
- abuse allegations supported by named complainants or judicial records.

Recent 2025 reporting largely re-circulates the Romanov, Joyce, Hesse, and erotic-island narratives. It does not supply new primary evidence.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### “Daughter of Tsar Alexander II”

**Status:** Unverified allegation and foundational identity myth.

No direct documentary proof has surfaced. It explains her in popular memory as a displaced princess and intensifies the drama of her poverty.

### “Born in St Petersburg”

**Status:** Dominant reported account, challenged by a posthumous Königsberg report.

The exact birthplace remains unresolved pending discovery of a birth or baptismal record.

### “Seven husbands”

**Status:** Folklore.

Three recorded or reported marriages and a fourth major partnership can be reconstructed. Seven cannot.

### “Princess of Saint Leger”

**Status:** Social and artistic styling.

Ranzoni’s painting bears the “princess” title. Her more usual title was baroness. Neither title’s exact legal status has been conclusively demonstrated in the consulted records.

### “Murderous Bluebeard”

**Status:** Hostile rumor documented as rumor by Rilke in 1919.

No supporting criminal evidence exists. Its importance lies in showing her contemporary reputation as sexually dangerous, domineering, and uncanny.

### “Sorceress” or possessor of the evil eye

**Status:** Partly self-created mythology.

Unlike purely external gossip, Frassi reports that Antoinette’s own writings expressed belief that she could exercise magical power against adversaries. The underlying writings remain unpublished.

### “James Joyce modeled Bella Cohen/Circe on her”

**Status:** Literary theory.

Joyce met her and may have received Tzikos papers. Parallels exist, but no explicit statement by Joyce proving direct characterization was located.

### “Hermann Hesse was her protégé or guest”

**Status:** Unverified in the consulted primary evidence.

Recent journalism places Hesse among artists she supported, but Frassi’s archival roster and evidence excerpts do not document a personal meeting. Proximity within the wider Monte Verità environment is insufficient.

### “Rilke stayed on the island”

**Status:** Unresolved and probably overstated.

Rilke’s correspondence proves that he considered moving there and knew her reputation. It does not, in the cited evidence, prove residence.

### “She single-handedly created the present garden”

**Status:** Simplification.

Her initiative and decades of horticultural experimentation are strongly established. Richard, employees, Max Emden, later directors, and the public botanical park also shaped the present landscape.

### “The garden was carried away by boat”

**Status:** Based on a real event, subsequently poeticized.

Plants, possessions, and collections were removed by boat during her forced departure. It is unclear how much living botanical material she successfully transferred to Casa Moscia.

### “From imperial court to pauper’s grave”

**Status:** Composite legend.

Her final poverty is documented; imperial parentage is not. The phrase became the narrative structure of biographies and film.

## Illness, Psychological Condition, and Death

At sixteen Antoinette may have suffered tuberculosis or another pulmonary complaint. This comes from later local history rather than a medical record.

During her final decades she was described as elderly, increasingly ill, and financially exhausted. No documented addiction was found.

Romano Broggini remembered her in Intragna as lucid and intellectually active but inhabiting a personal world. This does not justify a retrospective psychiatric diagnosis.

She died on 24 January 1948. No specific cause of death was established.

Her remains were reportedly transferred to the Brissago Islands in **1972**, where they now rest in a wooded area of San Pancrazio. This is a reported posthumous commemoration; the exhumation and reinterment record was not consulted directly.

## Portraits and Material Representations

### Daniele Ranzoni

**Ritratto della principessa di Saint Leger**, also called *Ritratto di Antonietta di Saint Leger* or identified with Antonietta Tzikos, approximately 1886.

The portrait became Antoinette’s principal icon. It was reportedly displayed above her fireplace, later pledged in connection with debts, and contested through lawyers. ## Death, Burial, and Estate

Antoinette died without significant liquid resources in the Intragna institution on 24 January 1948.

Her remaining artworks and possessions became subjects of posthumous inventory, pledge, and auction proceedings. The Ranzoni portrait was sold through a 1949 transaction involving Francesco Borella and Giacomo Jucker.

The fate of her reported sixty-plus diary volumes is unknown. Their disappearance is one of the principal obstacles to a complete biography.

Her remains were reportedly returned to San Pancrazio in 1972. The transfer symbolically reversed her forced departure, turning the garden into both her memorial and burial place.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Antoinette’s reputation has passed through several phases.

### Immediate posthumous period

When the islands entered public ownership in 1949–50, accounts recognized her role in establishing the exotic garden but often contrasted her indebtedness and eccentricity with the administrative order of later owners.

### Romantic reconstruction

Later local histories portrayed her as:

- an illegitimate Romanov princess;
- a multilingual femme fatale;
- a patroness of Europe’s artistic elite;
- a visionary botanist;
- an impractical inventor;
- a woman ruined by lovers, lawsuits, and speculation;
- an abandoned aristocrat dying among the poor.

This version preserves many real events but often converts uncertainties into dramatic facts.

### Feminist and cultural recovery

Recent presentations emphasize:

- female property ownership;
- her authorship of the garden;
- artistic patronage;
- intellectual independence;
- entrepreneurship in fields dominated by men;
- the gendered character of gossip about her sexuality and temperament.

These reinterpretations restore neglected evidence but can create a different simplification if they minimize failed investments, creditor disputes, autocratic behavior, or her role in generating her own occult mythology.

### Botanical legacy

Her clearest surviving achievement is the initial creation of a subtropical acclimatization garden on San Pancrazio. The public park opened on **2 April 1950**.

The islands were purchased in 1949 by a consortium involving:

- the Canton of Ticino;
- Ascona;
- Brissago;
- Ronco sopra Ascona;
- Swiss heritage and nature-protection organizations.

Since 1 January 2020, the Canton of Ticino has held full ownership.

Modern annual attendance is reported around 100,000, with higher peak years. Present biodiversity and infrastructure result from several eras, but the core idea of a global garden on the lake belongs to Antoinette’s period.

### Artistic legacy

Ranzoni’s portrait preserves her public image. The islands’ association with Franzoni, Hodler, Werefkin, Joyce, Rilke, and Kessler places her within the cultural geography connecting the Lombard Scapigliatura, Swiss modernism, Russian émigré art, and Monte Verità.

### Historiographical problem

The central difficulty is that the legend is better circulated than the archive. The two should not be collapsed:

- The garden, property purchases, several relationships, children, financial decline, Joyce meetings, Kessler visit, Rilke letter, peat contract, auctions, and death are documentarily recoverable.
- Romanov paternity, seven marriages, criminal Bluebeard stories, universal celebrity guest lists, and direct literary-model claims remain unverified or mythic.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are included. Mere presence in the Lake Maggiore or Monte Verità region has not been counted.

### James Augustine Aloysius Joyce

**Label:** Documented or strongly evidenced fact.

Joyce visited the Locarno area in **1917 and 1919** and met Antoinette on both occasions. She reportedly gave him books and letters connected with Perikles Tzikos. Eva Frassi associates the encounters with Joyce’s work on the “Sirens” and “Circe” episodes of *Ulysses*.

The claim that Antoinette directly inspired Bella Cohen, Circe, or Ellen Bloom is a **literary theory**, not an established authorial fact.

### Mikhail Aleksandrovich Bakunin

**Label:** Documented indirect connection; no meeting.

Antoinette and Richard lived in **1883–84** at Villa Baronata in Mappo-Minusio. The estate had earlier been purchased in Bakunin’s name in 1873 using Carlo Cafiero’s money and had hosted Bakunin’s family and revolutionary fugitives.

Bakunin died in 1876, so no personal meeting is possible on the known chronology. The connection is successive occupation of a politically significant property.

### Ferdinand Hodler

**Label:** Documented fact.

Hodler began frequenting Antoinette’s artistic and intellectual circle on the Brissago Islands in **1886**. On **26 February 1888**, he played cello in a chamber concert with Antoinette at the piano and other musicians.

### Hermann Hesse

**Label:** Unverified claim.

Recent journalism and broader Monte Verità narratives describe Hesse as an artist supported or hosted by Antoinette. The archival study used here does not supply a letter, diary entry, or dated meeting. Shared participation in the cultural geography of the Locarno–Ascona region is not sufficient evidence of a personal connection.

### Other roster names examined

No adequately documented personal connection was established with Gabriele d’Annunzio, Cosima’s husband Richard Wagner, Carl Gustav Jung, Isadora Duncan, Paul Klee, Rudolf Steiner, Otto Gross, Max Weber, Mary Wigman, Erich Mühsam, Gustav Landauer, or the other Monte Verità figures appearing on the atlas roster.

Their presence in the broader cultural history of the region should not be converted into an acquaintance with Antoinette without direct evidence.
