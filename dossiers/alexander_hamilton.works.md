## Works by Alexander Hamilton

### Pamphlets, manifestos, and public interventions

- **1772:** *Letter on the St. Croix Hurricane*. A published eyewitness account and fund-raising appeal prompted by the hurricane of August 1772; the text survives through its appearance in the *Royal Danish American Gazette*, not through Hamilton’s manuscript. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-01-02-0042))
- **1774:** *A Full Vindication of the Measures of the Congress*. An anonymous revolutionary pamphlet answering “A.W. Farmer,” the pseudonym of Samuel Seabury; it is argument used as political action and thus fits the category manifesto. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-01-02-0054))
- **1775:** *The Farmer Refuted*. A second anonymous pamphlet against Seabury, considerably larger than the *Full Vindication* and concerned with natural rights, sovereignty, and colonial resistance. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-01-02-0057))
- **1775:** *Remarks on the Quebec Bill*. An anonymous newspaper essay traditionally attributed to Hamilton; the collected edition prints it with reservations because the external evidence for authorship is not conclusive. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-01-02-0059))
- **1778:** *The Publius Letter*. An anonymous public letter attacking Samuel Chase and alleged wartime profiteering; it is an early use by Hamilton of “Publius,” later the shared signature of *The Federalist*. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-01-02-0683))
- **1781–1782:** *The Continentalist*. A six-number newspaper series advocating stronger continental fiscal and governing powers. Its proper object is the serial intervention rather than a later book assembled from it. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton, introductory note](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-02-02-1166))
- **1784:** *A Letter from Phocion to the Considerate Citizens of New-York*. A pamphlet opposing punitive treatment of Loyalists and defending legal restraint; its pseudonymous public argument functions as a manifesto of postwar reconciliation. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-03-02-0333))
- **1784:** *A Second Letter from Phocion*. A sequel defending the first pamphlet and continuing the argument over citizenship, confiscation, and treaty obligations. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-03-02-0341))
- **1784:** *The Case of Rutgers v. Waddington*. Hamilton’s surviving legal briefs and argument for the defendant constitute a forensic work rather than a book; the final judgment was made by Mayor James Duane, not Hamilton. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton, case materials](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-03-02-0428))
- **1789:** *Address to the Electors of the State of New-York*. A public defense of the Federalist record and an electoral intervention issued under Hamilton’s name. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-05-02-0206))
- **1792:** *An Explanation*. A newspaper defense of Treasury policy published through a sequence of numbered pieces; the serial form and partially reconstructed publication history resist treatment as a single fixed pamphlet. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton, introductory note](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-12-02-0207))
- **1792:** *The Vindication of the Funding System*. An unfinished project answering criticism of Hamilton’s financial program. It survives as drafts and fragments and therefore is a project, not a completed publication. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-13-02-0236))
- **1793:** *Pacificus*. A seven-essay newspaper series defending Washington’s Neutrality Proclamation and an expansive conception of executive power. Its contemporary identity was pseudonymous and serial. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton, introductory note](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-15-02-0038))
- **1793:** *No Jacobin*. Two published essays and an unfinished third attacking Edmond-Charles Genet and his American supporters; because the projected series stopped, it is both a manifestation and an incomplete project. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton, introductory note](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-15-02-0124))
- **1794:** *Americanus*. A newspaper series on American relations with Britain and France. The collected papers identify three numbers, while surviving drafts show that Hamilton contemplated a broader intervention. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton, introductory note](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-16-02-0039))
- **1794:** *Tully*. A four-number manifesto supporting the federal government’s suppression of the Whiskey Rebellion. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton, introductory note](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-17-02-0130))
- **1795–1796:** *The Defence*. A newspaper defense of the Jay Treaty published under the signature “Camillus.” Hamilton wrote most of the essays, but Rufus King wrote several numbers; the series is therefore a collaborative obra whose individual boundaries and numbering vary in later editions. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton, introductory note](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-18-02-0282))
- **1795:** *Philo Camillus*. A short sequel to *The Defence*, answering objections to the Jay Treaty under a related pseudonym. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-19-02-0150))
- **1796:** *The Stand*. A newspaper series warning against French political influence. Hamilton initiated the project, but numbers were also written by John Jay and Rufus King, so the whole cannot be assigned to Hamilton alone. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton, introductory note](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-20-02-0075))
- **1797:** *The Warning*. A newspaper polemic against French policy and its American defenders; it appeared as numbered pseudonymous essays rather than as a book. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton, introductory note](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-21-02-0104))
- **1797:** *The Answer*. A brief newspaper series continuing Hamilton’s dispute with Republican critics of the administration’s foreign policy. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton, introductory note](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-21-02-0148))
- **1797:** *Observations on Certain Documents Contained in No. V & VI of “The History of the United States for the Year 1796”*. The pamphlet commonly called the *Reynolds Pamphlet*: a public evidentiary defense against charges of official corruption that disclosed Hamilton’s adultery. It is at once apologia, dossier, and reputational act. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-21-02-0138-0001))
- **1798:** *Titus Manlius*. A projected newspaper intervention during the crisis with France. Only a draft survives, making it an unfinished project rather than a completed series. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-21-02-0300))
- **1800:** *Letter from Alexander Hamilton, Concerning the Public Conduct and Character of John Adams, Esq., President of the United States*. A privately printed intra-party indictment that escaped its intended restricted circulation and became a public election pamphlet. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-25-02-0110-0001))
- **1801–1802:** *The Examination*. A newspaper series opposing the Jefferson administration’s fiscal changes and defending the Federalist financial system. The series is an extended political intervention, not a retrospectively authored book. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton, introductory note](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-25-02-0285))
- **1802:** *Lucius Crassus*. Three newspaper essays opposing alteration of New York’s constitution through an irregularly empowered convention. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton, introductory note](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-26-02-0002))
- **1804:** *Statement on Impending Duel with Aaron Burr*. A private declaration explaining Hamilton’s intention to reserve his first fire. It was written for posthumous discovery, so it acts simultaneously as testament, moral defense, and protocol for interpreting his conduct. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-26-02-0001-0241))

### Constitutional and governing designs

- **1782:** *Resolutions for a General Convention*. A congressional proposal for revising the Confederation. It is a constitutional project whose operative significance lies in what it sought to convene, not merely in its wording. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-03-02-0006))
- **1783:** *Continental Tax Plan*. Hamilton’s resolutions and reports in Congress proposing permanent continental revenue. This is a governing design distributed across official documents rather than a single autonomous text. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-03-02-0186))
- **1786:** *Report of the Annapolis Convention*. Hamilton drafted the convention’s call for a broader meeting at Philadelphia, but it was issued collectively by the commissioners and should not be treated as his sole-authored work. Its action—calling another convention—is inseparable from its textual form. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-03-02-0553))
- **1787:** *Plan of Government*. Hamilton’s plan presented to the Constitutional Convention on 18 June, favoring a powerful national government and executives and senators holding office during good behavior. It remained a proposal and was never the Constitution adopted by the convention. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-04-02-0090))
- **1787:** *Conjectures About the New Constitution*. Notes evaluating the probable operation and political prospects of the completed Constitution; a memorandum rather than a public work. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-04-02-0148))
- **1787–1788:** *The Federalist*. An eighty-five-essay ratification project made collaboratively with James Madison and John Jay under the shared persona “Publius”; Hamilton planned the series and wrote fifty-one essays under the attribution accepted by the modern scholarly edition. The authorship of several essays was historically disputed because Hamilton’s and Madison’s later lists conflicted, although statistical and editorial scholarship assigns the disputed numbers to Madison. ([Library of Congress](https://guides.loc.gov/federalist-essays-in-historic-newspapers/about-the-authors); [Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-04-02-0152))
- **1788:** *Speech to the New York Ratifying Convention*. A sustained oral and political performance delivered through multiple speeches rather than one fixed text. The surviving versions are reporters’ notes and editorial reconstructions, so the “work” cannot be identified straightforwardly with an authorial manuscript. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-05-02-0012))
- **1796:** *Draft of Washington’s Farewell Address*. Hamilton produced a substantial draft after exchanging plans and revisions with George Washington; Washington selected, revised, and issued the final address under his own name. Hamilton’s draft is a collaborative contribution, not authorship of the final presidential manifestation, and the extent of Washington, Hamilton, and James Madison’s respective contributions generated a long controversy. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-20-02-0176); [Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/resource/mgw2.024/?sp=229))
- **1801:** *The Constitutionalist*. A projected set of political essays on constitutional construction. Surviving outlines and fragments show an intention that Hamilton did not complete, so the project itself—not a nonexistent finished treatise—is the obra. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-25-02-0310))

### Treasury reports and financial protocols

- **1790:** *Report Relative to a Provision for the Support of Public Credit*. Hamilton’s first major Treasury report, proposing federal funding of the national debt and assumption of state debts. It is both analysis and protocol: Congress was meant to turn its recommendations into a working fiscal order. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-06-02-0076-0002-0001))
- **1790:** *Report on a National Bank*. A Treasury proposal for a federally chartered bank that supplied the design for the First Bank of the United States. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-07-02-0229-0003))
- **1790:** *Report on the Establishment of a Mint*. A design for a national coinage, decimal monetary units, denominations, and administrative machinery. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-07-02-0334-0002))
- **1790:** *Report on the Petition of the Pennsylvania Society for the Abolition of Slavery*. An official Treasury response concerning the slave trade and federal revenue law; it is administrative work, not an abolitionist manifesto authored on Hamilton’s private initiative. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-06-02-0099-0002))
- **1791:** *Opinion on the Constitutionality of a National Bank*. A cabinet opinion arguing for implied federal powers and answering Thomas Jefferson and Edmund Randolph. It is constitutional advocacy made to enable an executive decision. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-08-02-0060))
- **1791:** *Report on Manufactures*. A comprehensive program for industrial development through credit, tariffs, bounties, infrastructure, immigration, and technological promotion. It is less a description of existing manufacture than a project for making a manufacturing economy. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-10-02-0001-0007))
- **1792:** *Report on a Plan for the Further Support of Public Credit*. A Treasury design for managing and extinguishing public debt through dedicated revenues and a sinking fund. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-11-02-0094-0002))
- **1792:** *Report on the Public Debt*. A detailed official accounting and policy report on the funded debt and the means available for its reduction. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-12-02-0287-0002))
- **1793:** *Report on the Privileges and Restrictions on the Commerce of the United States in Foreign Countries*. A comparative commercial report prepared for the House of Representatives; its tables, evidence, and policy argument form an administrative knowledge-object rather than a conventional literary work. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-14-02-0177-0002))
- **1795:** *Report on a Plan for the Further Support of Public Credit*. Hamilton’s final major report as Treasury secretary, proposing completion of the funding system and permanent provision for the debt. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-18-02-0052-0002))
- **1789–1795:** *Treasury circulars, forms, instructions, contracts, revenue opinions, and administrative reports*. These hundreds of documents collectively made a repeatable federal fiscal protocol: customs collection, debt service, accounting, procurement, taxation, and supervision. Treating every form or instruction as an autonomous “work” would falsify their bureaucratic and frequently clerical or collaborative production; the scholarly edition records them document by document. ([National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/nhprc/projects/catalog/alexander-hamilton); [Papers of Alexander Hamilton Digital Edition](https://www.upress.virginia.edu/title/arhn/))

### Military designs and command papers

- **1777–1781:** *Washington’s Headquarters Drafts*. As Washington’s aide-de-camp, Hamilton drafted letters, orders, intelligence communications, and policy papers for Washington’s signature. These texts resist being called Hamilton’s works: manuscript evidence may show his hand, but official authorship and final authority belonged to Washington, and other aides or clerks sometimes participated. ([Example in the Papers of George Washington](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Washington/03-23-02-0474))
- **1780:** *Plan for a Corps of Light Infantry*. A military organizational proposal designed for operational use during the Revolutionary War. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-02-02-0825))
- **1780:** *Letter to James Duane*. A long private memorandum diagnosing the Confederation’s failures and proposing national executive, fiscal, military, and banking institutions. Although formally a letter, its scale and program make it a constitutional project. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-02-02-0899))
- **1798:** *Plan for the Organization of the Army*. A protocol for organizing, staffing, training, and deploying the expanded army during the Quasi-War crisis. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-22-02-0108))
- **1798–1800:** *Military letters, general orders, regulations, tables, and tactical plans for the Additional Army*. These documents form a command apparatus made with James McHenry, George Washington, Charles Cotesworth Pinckney, subordinate officers, and clerks. The apparatus is a collective administrative obra, while attribution of any individual document depends on manuscript and office evidence. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton Digital Edition](https://rotunda.upress.virginia.edu/founders/ARHN.html); [Alexander Hamilton Papers, Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/collections/alexander-hamilton-papers/about-this-collection/))

### Institutional obras

- **1784:** *Bank of New York*. Hamilton helped design its constitution, promoted subscriptions, served as a director, and supplied legal guidance. The bank is an institutional obra rather than a text, but it was made collectively and cannot be credited to Hamilton alone. ([Bank of New York constitution in the Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-03-02-0371))
- **1785:** *New-York Society for Promoting the Manumission of Slaves*. Hamilton was a founding member and officer of this collective organization, not its sole maker. Its petitions, rules, legal assistance, and educational activity are manifestations of an institutional project rather than one bounded work. ([New-York Historical Society](https://www.nyhistory.org/blogs/the-new-york-manumission-society))
- **1787–1788:** *New York ratification campaign*. Hamilton’s convention speeches, newspaper writing, correspondence, coalition-building, and *Federalist* project together form a multimedia political campaign—a limited political Gesamtkunstwerk—but the campaign was made with many collaborators and resists conversion into a single authored text. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/about/Hamilton); [Library of Congress](https://guides.loc.gov/federalist-essays-in-historic-newspapers))
- **1789–1795:** *United States Treasury system*. Hamilton organized a department, reporting hierarchy, customs service, revenue administration, public-credit system, and standardized flow of information. This institutional obra was authorized by Congress and made by many legislators, officers, clerks, collectors, and creditors; “Hamilton made it” is defensible only if that collective and statutory authorship remains visible. ([National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/nhprc/projects/catalog/alexander-hamilton); [U.S. Treasury](https://home.treasury.gov/about/history/prior-secretaries/alexander-hamilton-1789-1795))
- **1790–1791:** *Revenue-Marine*. Hamilton proposed and administratively organized a fleet of revenue cutters to enforce customs law. The service is a governmental project and material system—vessels, officers, instructions, and patrols—not merely the text of Hamilton’s proposal. ([U.S. Coast Guard Historian’s Office](https://www.history.uscg.mil/Browse-by-Topic/History-Heritage-Traditions/Revenue-Cutter-Service/))
- **1791–1792:** *Society for Establishing Useful Manufactures*. Hamilton devised and promoted the industrial-development project associated with Paterson, New Jersey, but William Duer, corporate managers, investors, engineers, and laborers made its material manifestations. Hamilton’s report, prospectus, and corporate design should not be confused with sole authorship of the society or city. ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-10-02-0041); [National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/pagr/learn/historyculture/alexander-hamilton-and-the-sum.htm))
- **1791:** *First Bank of the United States*. Hamilton designed and defended the federal banking project, but Congress enacted its charter and its subscribers and officers created the corporation. It is an institutional manifestation of his plan, not an object made by him alone. ([National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/act-establishing-first-bank); [Papers of Alexander Hamilton](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-07-02-0229-0003))
- **1801:** *New-York Evening Post*. Hamilton organized investors and helped establish the Federalist newspaper later known as the *New York Post*. The newspaper is an ongoing collective institution, while William Coleman was its founding editor; calling the resulting publication Hamilton’s personal work would erase that distinction. ([New-York Historical Society](https://www.nyhistory.org/blogs/alexander-hamilton-and-the-new-york-evening-post))

### Private, legal, and documentary bodies that resist “work” status

- **1769–1804:** *Correspondence*. Hamilton made thousands of letters, drafts, copies, memoranda, endorsements, and enclosures, ranging from intimate messages to instruments of government. They are separately catalogued documents, but not every surviving note is meaningfully an autonomous obra; the standard edition consequently preserves them as papers rather than declaring them all “works.” ([Papers of Alexander Hamilton Digital Edition](https://www.upress.virginia.edu/title/arhn/); [Founders Online](https://founders.archives.gov/about/Hamilton))
- **1782–1804:** *Legal papers*. Pleadings, opinions, case notes, accountings, agreements, and briefs survive from Hamilton’s law practice. Many were made within adversarial proceedings and offices involving clients, clerks, opposing counsel, judges, and copyists, so their documentary existence is clearer than their status as individually authored works. ([Alexander Hamilton Papers, Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/collections/alexander-hamilton-papers/about-this-collection/); [Papers of Alexander Hamilton Digital Edition](https://rotunda.upress.virginia.edu/founders/ARHN.html))
- **1789–1795:** *Treasury account books and financial papers*. Ledgers, estimates, warrants, schedules, calculations, and office copies materialized Hamilton’s fiscal system. Many bear institutional rather than personal authorship, and some were prepared by Treasury clerks for his approval; they resist classification as Hamilton’s individual creations. ([National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/nhprc/projects/catalog/alexander-hamilton); [Library of Congress finding aid](https://findingaids.loc.gov/exist_collections/ead3pdf/mss/2017/ms017143.pdf))
- **undated and 1770s–1804:** *Notes, reading extracts, calculations, outlines, and fragments*. The collected papers include items without secure dates, completed titles, or evidence of intended publication. They are manifestations of Hamilton’s working process, but assigning invented titles or treating every fragment as a finished work would misrepresent them. ([Founders Online](https://founders.archives.gov/about/Hamilton); [Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/collections/alexander-hamilton-papers/))
