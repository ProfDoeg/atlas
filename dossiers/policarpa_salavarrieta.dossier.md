# Policarpa Salavarrieta: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

Policarpa Salavarrieta left no known memoir, diary, securely preserved personal correspondence, portrait from life, complete trial transcript, baptismal certificate, marriage record, or estate. Much of the familiar biography was reconstructed decades later from family documents, official notices, chronicles, testimony collected from survivors, nineteenth-century histories, and patriotic literature.

The dossier therefore uses these labels:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a contemporary record or identifiable archival document.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a later witness or reputable historical reconstruction but not independently established by a surviving contemporary record.
- **Disputed:** credible sources give materially different versions.
- **Tradition / legend / myth:** culturally influential but not demonstrable from contemporary evidence.
- **Unsupported:** repeated without an identifiable evidentiary foundation.

The name itself is an archival problem. Her father’s 1802 testamentary documentation reportedly calls her **Polonia**. Her brother Bibiano, Andrea Ricaurte de Lozano, and conspirator Ambrosio Almeyda called her **Policarpa**. A false passport from 1817 reportedly names her **Gregoria Apolinaria**. Contemporary or near-contemporary writers José María Caballero, José Hilario López, Francisco Mariano Fernández, and Almeyda used **La Pola**. The military record concerning the execution calls her “Policarpa o Pola Salabarrieta.”

The familiar “María Policarpa” and “Gregoria Polonia” forms are later attempts to harmonize incompatible records. The [Museo Nacional](https://www.museonacional.gov.co/colecciones/Pieza_del_mes/pieza-del-mes-2010/Paginas/489_2010-01-19.aspx) explicitly states that neither her exact name nor birth date is certain.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

Her parents were:

- **Joaquín Salavarrieta**, associated with agriculture, trade, and possibly the postal service.
- **Mariana Ríos**, also encountered as Mariana Díaz or Mariana Ríos Chamorro in later genealogical accounts.

Their marriage is reported as having occurred at Moniquirá on 16 September 1786, but this dossier did not locate the parish image itself.

Family testamentary material describes them as property holders of moderate means rather than impoverished peasants or titled elites. Joaquín’s estate reportedly reflected agricultural and commercial activity; Mariana possessed clothing, jewelry, and substantial household furnishings. The preserved Guaduas house traditionally associated with the family is modest but inconsistent with the later image of utter destitution.

A historical reconstruction published by the Real Academia de la Historia describes Joaquín and Mariana as descendants of Spaniards, residents of Guaduas, and owners of some property. The family appears best characterized as respectable provincial householders without demonstrated hidalgo status.

### Ancestry

An older investigation in the *Boletín de Historia y Antigüedades* identifies:

- Paternal grandparents: **Francisco Salavarrieta** and **Eulalia Morales**.
- Maternal grandparents: **Francisco Díaz** and **Bárbara Chamorro**.

These identifications are **reported facts**, not independently checked here against parish originals.

### Joaquín Salavarrieta and the Comunero rebellion

A widely repeated account says Joaquín participated with José Antonio Galán in the 1781 Comunero uprising, passed through Guaduas during Galán’s Magdalena campaign, and later fled persecution.

**Evidentiary status: disputed or weakly documented.** Later biographies repeat the story, but the older Academy investigation found no proof that Joaquín was Guaduas’s corregidor and warned that several family claims lacked documentary support. The Comunero connection may have helped later writers provide Policarpa with an inherited revolutionary vocation.

### Siblings

Sources disagree on whether the couple had seven, eight, nine, or ten children. Names appearing in different reconstructions include:

- María Ignacia or Clara
- Catalina, also Catarina
- José María, later associated with the Augustinians
- Manuel, also described as an Augustinian
- Ramón
- Francisco Antonio
- Joaquín
- Eduardo
- José Ignacio or José Antonino
- Vicente Bibiano María, normally **Bibiano**

The discrepancies arise from variant names, children who died young, conflation of baptismal names, and incomplete later lists.

The most consequential siblings in Policarpa’s life were:

- **Catalina/Catarina**, the older sister who cared for Policarpa and Bibiano after the parents’ deaths.
- **Bibiano**, her younger brother, later her companion in clandestine activity and imprisonment.
- **José María** and probably **Manuel**, the religious brothers credited with reclaiming her body after execution.

### Brother-in-law

Catalina married **Domingo García**. He reportedly served in Antonio Nariño’s southern campaigns and died as a patriot soldier, commonly dated to 1813. Bibiano also served in or accompanied those campaigns and was reportedly imprisoned until about 1815.

These family experiences are plausible formative influences on Policarpa, although claims about her inner reaction remain inferential.

## Childhood and Early Life

### Birthplace and birth date dispute

The traditional official formulation is **Guaduas, 26 January 1795**. Other published dates include 1791, 1793, 1795, and 1796; proposed birthplaces have included Guaduas, Bogotá, Mariquita, Villeta, Tabio, Tenjo, Tuta, and El Socorro.

No accepted baptismal record for Policarpa has been found.

A baptismal entry from Tenjo for a **María Polonia Solavarrieta**, daughter of Ignacia Solavarrieta, was once offered as evidence. An Academy commission concluded that this was a different person: Policarpa’s parents are independently identified as Joaquín Salavarrieta and Mariana Ríos, and family testamentary documents place a daughter called Polonia among their legitimate children. In 1991 the Colombian Academy of History favored Guaduas.

The strongest reconstruction is therefore:

- **Guaduas is highly probable.**
- **1795 or 1796 is probable.**
- **26 January 1795 is traditional, not conclusively documented.**

### Move to Santafé

The family moved from Guaduas to Santafé around **1797 or 1798**, settling in a low adobe-and-tile house in the **Santa Bárbara** parish. The chronological variation reflects differing later reconstructions.

### The 1802 smallpox catastrophe

In 1802 a smallpox epidemic struck Santafé. Joaquín and Mariana died, as did two children. The children named as victims vary: some accounts give Joaquín and María Ignacia; others Eduardo and María Ignacia.

The deaths are supported by the parents’ testamentary proceedings in the Bogotá notarial records and by subsequent family dispersal. Exact victim identification remains disputed.

The infected family house was reportedly closed. Surviving children separated:

- José María and Manuel entered the Augustinian religious community.
- Ramón and Francisco Antonio went to work on a property at Tena.
- Catalina assumed care of Policarpa and Bibiano.
- Around 1804 Catalina took the two younger children back to Guaduas.

They reportedly stayed with Catalina’s godmother **Margarita Beltrán** before joining Catalina and Domingo García’s household.

## Education and Formation

Evidence for Policarpa’s education is largely retrospective.

She was literate enough to participate in clandestine communications and prepare or transmit written recommendations. This makes some schooling probable.

Later accounts say that:

- Margarita Beltrán arranged for her attendance at a school connected with the Franciscan **Convento de la Soledad**.
- She learned reading, writing, Catholic religion, Spanish history, singing, and guitar.
- A woman identified as **Señorita Cañizares** taught her sewing and dressmaking.
- Policarpa may later have taught children in Guaduas.

**Evidentiary status:** reported tradition. These details appear in pedagogical and biographical reconstructions, but no contemporary school register or named teacher’s record was located. “Schoolteacher” should not be stated with the same confidence as her work in sewing.

Guaduas itself was formative. It stood on the road between Santafé, Honda, and the Magdalena River, exposing residents to travelers, mail, military movements, commerce, and political news. That geography later made the town useful for clandestine communication.

---

## Political and Intellectual Development

Policarpa did not leave a political treatise. Her ideology must be reconstructed from conduct and from statements attributed to her.

The family’s political environment included:

- The reported Comunero association of her father.
- Domingo García’s death in patriot military service.
- Bibiano’s participation and imprisonment.
- Guaduas’s role as a communications corridor.
- The collapse of the first republican governments and the Spanish reconquest.
- Royalist executions and confiscations during the “pacification” regime.

Her demonstrable political position by 1816–1817 was not merely sympathy. She participated in an armed insurgent support network seeking to overthrow restored royal authority. Her work supported guerrillas and fugitives attempting to reach patriot forces in the Llanos.

Whether she held a developed republican constitutional philosophy cannot be established. Later speeches assigned to her use the vocabulary of liberty, tyranny, patriotism, and popular dignity, but their exact wording is not securely contemporary.

## Chronological Life History

### c. 1795–1802: Guaduas and Santafé

Born into the Salavarrieta-Ríos family, probably at Guaduas, Policarpa moved as a small child to Santafé. Her parents and two siblings died in the 1802 smallpox epidemic.

### c. 1804–1810: Return to Guaduas

Catalina brought Policarpa and Bibiano back to Guaduas. Policarpa apparently learned sewing, gained basic literacy, and may have assisted in teaching. No securely documented adolescent diary, romance, employment contract, or school record survives.

### 1810–1815: First independence period

Santafé established a revolutionary junta on 20 July 1810, beginning a politically fragmented first republican period.

A recent Bogotá council document states that Policarpa participated personally in the events of 20 July 1810. This is **unsupported in the sources consulted**. The traditional narrative places her in Guaduas, and no contemporary document was found placing her at the uprising.

Family members joined the patriot cause. Domingo García reportedly died in 1813 during Nariño’s southern campaign; Bibiano served and later returned after imprisonment or severe hardship.

Policarpa’s own activities in this phase are poorly recorded. Later writers often project her documented 1817 commitment backward into these years.

### 1815–1816: Spanish reconquest

The expedition led by Pablo Morillo restored royal rule across much of New Granada. Military tribunals, executions, imprisonment, confiscation, and surveillance followed. Juan Sámano became the central royalist military authority in Santafé and later viceroy.

Patriot survivors fled toward Casanare and the Llanos, where resistance continued. Urban and provincial networks supplied intelligence, money, weapons, clothing, recruits, passports, and recommendations.

### 1816 or 1817: Move to occupied Santafé

Policarpa and Bibiano moved to Santafé. The move is usually connected to letters of introduction from the guerrilla leader **Ambrosio Almeyda** to **Andrea Ricaurte de Lozano**, a patriot widow who maintained a clandestine household network.

Some narratives say Policarpa entered the city using a forged passport under the name **Gregoria Apolinaria**. The false passport is reported in museum-based chronologies, although its physical archival location is not consistently cited online.

She lived in Andrea Ricaurte’s house. Her status as a seamstress or dressmaker allowed her access to royalist and elite households and created an ordinary reason to hear conversations, observe uniforms, learn military dispositions, and carry messages.

### Clandestine work in Santafé

The best-supported outline of her work includes:

- Acting as liaison between urban patriots and guerrilla groups.
- Gathering information about Spanish troops and officials.
- Carrying or arranging correspondence.
- Securing money, weapons, clothing, and supplies.
- Helping fugitives and recruits travel toward the Llanos.
- Encouraging soldiers in royal service to desert.
- Producing or transmitting letters of recommendation for travelers.
- Working within, rather than alone above, a wider household and kinship network.

The romantic image of Policarpa as the solitary “master spy” exaggerates the centralized nature of the network. The surviving evidence points to overlapping cells involving Andrea Ricaurte, the Almeyda brothers, clergy, soldiers, artisans, relatives, couriers, and guerrillas.

### Alejo Sabaraín

**Alejo Sabaraín**—also Zabaraín or Sarabaín—was a patriot officer or operative trying to reach the Llanos.

Later biography and popular culture present him as Policarpa’s fiancé or lover. Their operational connection is documented more securely than their romance. He carried papers or recommendations connected to her, and his arrest exposed her. No engagement record, correspondence proving a romance, or marriage plan survives.

### Arrest of Sabaraín and companions

In the autumn of 1817, Sabaraín and companions were intercepted while traveling toward the Llanos. Papers found with them included recommendations associated with Policarpa and information naming royalists and patriots.

The intercepted group is variously described, and the names and military status of the final condemned vary by source. The official report following the executions listed:

- José Manuel Díaz
- Antonio Galeano/Galiano
- José María Arcos
- Antonio or Joaquín Suárez
- Jacobo Marufú/Manrufuo
- Alejo Sabaraín/Zabaraín
- Francisco Arellano
- Manuel Díaz
- Policarpa/Pola Salabarrieta

The report distinguished five military men and four civilians.

### Search and capture

Royalist sergeant **Anselmo or José Iglesias** was tasked with locating her. Popular accounts add that he tricked a local shopkeeper into revealing her residence by claiming that an officer wished to court her. That detail is a **reported anecdote**, not established here from the official proceeding.

Policarpa and Bibiano were arrested in Andrea Ricaurte’s house. Andrea and others reportedly destroyed incriminating papers before or during the search. Policarpa was confined in the **Colegio Mayor de Nuestra Señora del Rosario**, then being used as a prison.

The precise date of arrest remains uncertain. A University of Rosario reconstruction proposes a compressed sequence:

- arrest and imprisonment shortly before 10 November;
- council of war on 10 November;
- placement “en capilla” on 13 November;
- execution on 14 November.

### Trial and legal status

On **10 November 1817**, a military council condemned Policarpa and eight men to death for conspiracy and assistance to insurgents.

The extant post-execution communication is important because it records concern within the Royal Audiencia that civilians had been tried and executed by military jurisdiction without ordinary judicial separation or prior notice to the court. Secretary Francisco José de Aguilar y Contreras reported that the nine had been captured while attempting or assisting passage to the Llanos and that papers of recommendation connected them to Policarpa.

This was a **military conviction under Spanish royal authority**, not merely an allegation. At the same time, the jurisdictional complaint indicates procedural controversy even within the royal administration.

No evidence was found for torture, a full adversarial civil trial, appellate review, or a verbatim interrogation transcript. Claims that she refused to reveal any names may be true but belong principally to patriotic tradition unless tied to a specific record.

### 13–14 November 1817: “En capilla”

The condemned were placed “en capilla”—spiritual preparation for death—at the Rosario on 13 November according to one contemporary-style relation.

Tradition says Policarpa spent the night denouncing Spain, rejected wine or water from a guard, and declared that she would accept nothing from her enemies. These episodes are **legendary or weakly documented**.

### Execution

On **14 November 1817**, eight men and one woman were taken to the Plaza Mayor of Santafé, now Plaza de Bolívar. Sources give the execution time as approximately 9:00 a.m., 11:00 a.m., or noon.

They were shot from behind, the punishment assigned to traitors. Several of the men were afterward suspended from gallows for public display.

Accounts say Policarpa objected to turning her back, then asked to kneel because this was more fitting for a woman. In the best-known version, she faced or turned toward the firing party as far as possible and continued addressing the crowd.

The fact of execution, date, place, method, and collective nature are documented. Her exact posture and every sentence attributed to her are less certain.

## Death, Body, and Burial

Policarpa died from the firing squad’s volley. No medical report or autopsy is known.

Unlike some male companions, her corpse was not hung publicly. Contemporary custom and gendered rules concerning exposure of a woman’s body are usually offered as the reason.

A contemporary accounting and later descriptions indicate that her religious brothers obtained authorization to remove the body. It was buried in the church or convent church of **San Agustín**, while other executed prisoners went to the Veracruz cemetery.

Claims that identifiable remains still repose at a precise place in San Agustín should be treated cautiously. The documentary tradition supports burial there, but this research did not locate a modern forensic identification or uninterrupted exhumation history.

She left no documented spouse, children, business, investment portfolio, landholding, will, or estate of her own.

## Employment, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Sewing and teaching

Her principal civilian occupation was dressmaking, sewing, or domestic textile work. This was both income-producing labor and a means of access to politically useful households.

Possible teaching in Guaduas is repeatedly reported but not securely documented.

### Intelligence network

Policarpa did not belong to a modern intelligence agency or incorporated political organization. Her “espionage” occurred through an informal insurgent network built from:

- kinship;
- women’s households;
- sewing clients;
- clergy;
- military deserters;
- travelers;
- guerrilla leaders;
- written recommendations and false travel papers.

### Money and property

The family possessed moderate property before the 1802 deaths, but no reliable evidence establishes Policarpa’s personal wealth. She appears to have supported herself through skilled labor and depended partly on family and political associates.

No businesses, securities, patents, inventions, commercial companies, debts, investments, financial crimes, or independent real estate holdings are documented.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Andrea Ricaurte de Lozano — documented associate

Andrea housed Policarpa and Bibiano in Santafé and participated in the patriot clandestine network. Her later testimony is an important source for Policarpa’s appearance, name, and activities. Andrea reportedly continued espionage after Policarpa’s death.

### Bibiano Salavarrieta — documented sibling and associate

Bibiano shared the family’s displacement, patriot experience, Santafé residence, arrest, and imprisonment. His 1824 declaration explicitly called Policarpa his sister, helping resolve some identity disputes.

### Ambrosio Almeyda — documented political associate

Almeyda was connected to guerrilla resistance and provided recommendations or introductions facilitating Policarpa’s work in Santafé. He later referred to her as Policarpa.

### Alejo Sabaraín — documented operational associate; disputed romantic partner

Sabaraín carried papers that exposed the network and was executed beside her. The fiancé or lover designation is common but lacks surviving personal correspondence or legal documentation.

### Catalina/Catarina Salavarrieta — documented family protector

Catalina assumed responsibility for Policarpa and Bibiano after 1802 and brought them back to Guaduas.

### Domingo García — reported familial and political influence

Her brother-in-law’s death in patriot service linked family bereavement to the independence struggle.

### José María and Manuel Salavarrieta — reported/documented religious brothers

They are credited with reclaiming and burying her body. José María also appears in accounts of patriot clerical networks.

### Anselmo/José Iglesias — royalist adversary

The sergeant assigned to locate and arrest her.

### Juan Sámano — institutional and political adversary

Military commander and senior royal authority under whose regime the permanent council of war operated. Popular retellings often say Sámano personally “ordered her execution”; the more precise formulation is that a military council convicted her under his command structure.

### Francisco José de Aguilar y Contreras — official recorder

Secretary of the Royal Audiencia who documented the executions and the jurisdictional issue concerning military trial of civilians.

## Physical Appearance and Portraiture

Andrea Ricaurte reportedly described Policarpa as young, attractive, lively, intelligent, and having a light or “pearly” complexion.

No portrait reliably produced from a sitting is known. José María Espinosa, born in 1796, became the principal creator of her canonical face decades after her death. His miniature and *La Pola en capilla* helped establish later iconography. Other artists repeatedly copied or transformed his model.

An anonymous or later-attributed painting, *Policarpa Salavarrieta marcha al suplicio*, conventionally dated around 1825–1830, is the earliest major visual narrative. It is a memorial reconstruction, not documentary reportage.

Consequently, modern images—including banknotes, stamps, statues, schoolbook portraits, films, and AI reconstructions—represent posthumous national iconography, not a verified likeness.

## Controversies, Charges, and Adversarial Findings

### Royalist criminal charge

**Established contemporary status:** the restored Spanish government regarded Policarpa as an insurgent conspirator and traitor. A military council convicted and executed her.

**Modern description:** her actions included espionage, procurement, recruiting, facilitating desertion, false documentation, and support for armed rebellion. These were political and military crimes under royal law, although the victorious republican state later recoded them as patriotic service.

### Military jurisdiction over civilians

The Royal Audiencia’s post-execution reaction suggests that civilians, including Policarpa, were executed by military jurisdiction without the ordinary separation or notification expected by the civil court. This is a documented procedural controversy. It does not erase the conviction but qualifies portrayals of the proceeding as an ordinary civilian trial.

### Was she a “spy”?

The label is broadly justified, but it can mislead if interpreted as formal employment in a centralized secret service. She was a clandestine insurgent courier, intelligence collector, recruiter, and facilitator embedded in a decentralized network.

### Was she the network’s supreme leader?

Claims that she was the “center and soul of a vast conspiracy” are characteristic of later patriotic commemoration. Available evidence establishes an important role but does not permit a reliable organizational ranking.

### Birthplace and identity controversy

The absence of an accepted baptismal record produced rival municipal claims. The Tenjo María Polonia appears to have been another person. Guaduas remains the strongest conclusion because of family residence, testamentary evidence, sibling testimony, and the Academy’s 1991 ruling.

### Unsupported claim of participation on 20 July 1810

Some commemorative documents place her at the Santafé uprising. No supporting contemporary evidence was found. This should not be presented as established biography.

### Romance with Sabaraín

The relationship is possible and became central to drama and television. The evidentiary record securely supports political association, not engagement or a fully reconstructable love affair.

### No substantiated financial or personal scandal

A dedicated adversarial search combined her name with controversy, scandal, accusations, fraud, lawsuit, conspiracy, criticism, and recent years 2024–2026. It identified disputes over historical accuracy, identity, commemoration, and representations, but no credible evidence that Policarpa engaged in corruption, fraud for personal gain, theft, abuse, money laundering, or private financial misconduct.

Modern lawsuits and scandals involving schools, hospitals, neighborhoods, or organizations named “Policarpa Salavarrieta” concern later institutions—not the historical person.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Pueblo indolente”

The most famous attributed speech begins in variants with:

> “¡Pueblo indolente! ¡Cuán diversa sería hoy vuestra suerte si conocieseis el precio de la libertad!”

It continues by declaring that, although young and a woman, she had courage to suffer death and “a thousand deaths.”

- **What the story says:** she rebuked passive spectators, foretold Spanish defeat, and transformed her execution into revolutionary oratory.
- **Evidence:** near-contemporary memoirists report her defiance, but surviving versions differ considerably. No stenographic or official verbatim transcript exists.
- **Circulation:** nineteenth-century histories, patriotic biographies, school texts, theatre, radio, monuments, and political speeches.
- **Status:** the general fact of vocal defiance is plausible and repeatedly attested; the polished canonical wording is likely literary reconstruction.
- **Effect:** it made her simultaneously martyr, prophet, and critic of civic passivity.

### Refusal of wine or water

- **Story:** on the eve of death she rejected a drink, declaring she would accept nothing from enemies.
- **Evidence:** no contemporary documentary support located.
- **Status:** legend.
- **Effect:** reinforced an image of absolute, incorruptible resistance.

### Facing the firing squad

- **Story:** ordered to die with her back toward the guns as a traitor, she resisted, turned, or knelt so that she would face the executioners.
- **Evidence:** several later narratives contain versions of the incident.
- **Status:** reported tradition; exact posture unresolved.
- **Effect:** became a key visual motif of courage and refusal of dishonor.

### The doomed romance

- **Story:** Policarpa and Alejo Sabaraín were engaged lovers who died together.
- **Evidence:** political association and joint execution are documented; romance is not securely established.
- **Circulation:** biography, theatre, film, radio drama, and RCN’s 2010–2011 telenovela.
- **Effect:** converted a clandestine political network into a tragic love narrative readily adapted for mass culture.

### Prophecy by the vicereine

Some stories say the vicereine María Francisca Villanova visited the Salavarrieta home at Guaduas and predicted Policarpa’s fate.

- **Status:** legend without adequate documentary support.
- **Function:** supplies the biography with supernatural foreshadowing.

### Father as Comunero revolutionary

- **Story:** Joaquín fought beside José Antonio Galán and passed an inherited rebel tradition to his daughter.
- **Evidence:** repeated in later biographies; primary proof remains unclear.
- **Status:** disputed family-political tradition.
- **Effect:** constructs a multigenerational genealogy of rebellion.

### “La Pola” as beer

Bavaria introduced a low-priced beer called **La Pola** around 1910–1911, using an image associated with Policarpa during centennial nationalism. “Pola” subsequently became Colombian slang for beer.

There is no evidence that Policarpa created, endorsed, or had a special personal association with beer. The connection is twentieth-century commercial appropriation.

### Patriotic and feminist recoding

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century writers presented her as a chaste, Catholic, sacrificial republican heroine. Twentieth-century women’s history and feminist commemoration foregrounded her political agency and the obscured work of women in clandestine networks.

Both traditions selectively emphasize evidence:

- the first often domesticates her as virtuous martyr;
- the second sometimes makes her a modern feminist before the relevant political vocabulary existed.

Her documented conduct supports female political agency. Her adherence to a later feminist program cannot be established.

### Comparison with Joan of Arc and Mariana Pineda

Later literature compares her to Joan of Arc or to Spanish liberal martyr Mariana Pineda. These are symbolic analogies, not documented influence or contact.

## Works by or Attributed to Policarpa Salavarrieta

No formal book, essay, manifesto, diary, artwork, or independently published work by Policarpa is known.

### Operational papers — attributed, 1817

Letters or notes of recommendation supplied to travelers heading toward the Llanos were found among captured insurgents and attributed to her in official reporting. The full authenticated corpus has not survived in a readily identifiable modern edition.

### Lists of royalists and patriots — attributed, 1817

Later accounts state that Sabaraín carried a list prepared or delivered by Policarpa. It helped lead authorities to her. Whether she personally wrote every item, dictated it, or merely transmitted it is unresolved.

### False passport under “Gregoria Apolinaria” — associated document, 1817

A false travel document bearing this name is repeatedly cited. It was issued for her use rather than necessarily authored by her.

### Final speech — orally attributed, 14 November 1817

Multiple variants survive through witnesses, memoir, and later literary transmission. It is not a securely fixed authored text.

### Other sayings

Her imprecations against Spain, rejection of a drink, exchanges with priests, and encouragement of fellow prisoners belong to oral and literary tradition. They should not be treated as a stable collected *oeuvre*.

## Films, Television, Radio, Interviews, and Archival Material

### Film and screenplay

- **Policarpa Salavarrieta / La Pola** — an early Colombian silent historical film is generally associated with the Di Domenico film enterprise and the 1910s, but the consulted online sources did not yield a complete verified production record or surviving print.
- *Libreto para la filmación de la película “Policarpa Salavarrieta: yace por salvar la patria”* (Bogotá: Imprenta de La Luz, 1925). The screenplay contains 56 tableaux across two parts and openly combines historical events with dramatization.

### Television

- **La Pola** (RCN Televisión, 2010–2011), directed by Sergio Cabrera, starring Ana María Estupiñán as the younger Policarpa and Carolina Ramírez as the adult.
- The production generated public controversy because it dramatized undocumented childhood scenes, retained parents beyond their documented 1802 deaths, and greatly expanded the romance with Sabaraín. It is historical fiction, not a documentary reconstruction.

### Radio

- **Por salvar la patria**, a Radio Sutatenza/Acción Cultural Popular radionovela. The title adapts Joaquín Monsalve’s anagram. Its 32nd chapter dramatizes the last hours, official report, burial accounts, and continuation of Andrea Ricaurte’s network.
- Numerous Colombian patriotic radio plays and educational broadcasts have represented the execution.

### Archival and museum collections

- Archivo General de la Nación: Bogotá notarial protocols, testamentary material, and published documentary studies.
- Museo Nacional de Colombia: paintings, miniatures, lithographs, sculpture records, and the *Policarpa 200* iconographic inventory.

- Biblioteca Digital de Bogotá: Radio Sutatenza recordings.
- Church of San Agustín, Bogotá: traditional burial site.
- Casa Museo Policarpa Salavarrieta, Guaduas: preserved commemorative birthplace house; the exact room or structure’s uninterrupted authenticity should not be assumed without architectural documentation.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Immediate revolutionary memorialization

Her execution quickly became patriot propaganda. Fellow prisoner or patriot **Joaquín Monsalve** is credited with the anagram:

> “Policarpa Salavarrieta — Yace por salvar la patria.”

It reportedly circulated clandestinely after her death and became openly publishable following republican victory. The exact first circulation is difficult to verify, but the phrase became foundational to her cult.

In January 1820 Francisco Antonio Zea invoked her in republican commemoration. By the 1820s she was already being painted as a national martyr.

### Nineteenth-century national heroine

Her memory was cultivated in chronicles, paintings, poems, public ceremonies, and histories. Unlike most female independence participants, she obtained a durable place in the official national pantheon.

This memorial prominence also narrowed her story: artistic works concentrated on prison, chapel, procession, and execution, often omitting the collective clandestine network.

### Monuments

- A national law in 1894 appropriated funds for a monument at Guaduas.
- Dionisio Cortés modeled a statue of Policarpa in 1899.
- A version was installed in Bogotá’s Plazuela de Las Aguas in 1910 for the independence centennial.
- The bound, seated figure has been interpreted both as dignified resistance and as a gendered image of submission.

### Stamps and currency

Policarpa appeared in Colombia’s 1910 independence-centennial stamp series and is frequently described as the first Latin American woman portrayed on a postage stamp.

A 10,000-peso banknote bearing her portrait and a view of Guaduas entered circulation on **30 November 1995**.

Under Law 1903 of 2018, the Banco de la República issued a commemorative 10,000-peso coin:

- copper-nickel alloy;
- 35 mm diameter;
- approximately 21.75 g;
- mintage of 20,000;
- released to the public in 2023 and subsequently sold out.

### Public holidays and official honors

Colombia’s **Law 44 of 8 November 1967** associated the nation with the sesquicentennial of her death and proclaimed 14 November “Día de la Mujer Colombiana.”

Her name has been given to:

- the municipality of Policarpa, Nariño;
- neighborhoods;
- schools;
- hospitals and former public entities;
- streets, plazas, libraries, women’s organizations, awards, and governmental decorations.

Institutional controversies involving these entities are not biographical controversies about her.

### Historiographical reinterpretation

Her reputation has passed through several overlapping forms:

1. **Royalist traitor and insurgent:** the legal classification under which she died.
2. **Republican martyr:** early national memory.
3. **Catholic and feminine exemplar:** nineteenth- and early twentieth-century moral biography.
4. **Popular romantic heroine:** theatre, cinema, radio, and television.
5. **Female political actor:** modern women’s history.
6. **National allegory:** art-historical readings of her body, sacrifice, and posthumous image.
7. **Commercial icon:** beer, stamps, currency, tourism, souvenirs, and branding.

The documentary core—clandestine service, capture through intercepted papers, military conviction, and execution—is substantial. The seamless childhood-to-martyr narrative is not.
