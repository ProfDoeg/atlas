# Simone de’ Bardi: Research Dossier

**Prepared:** 25 August 2026  
**Subject:** Florentine knight, officeholder, landholder, and member of the Bardi mercantile-banking consorteria; husband of Bice/Beatrice Portinari  
**Geographical scope:** principally Florence and its contado, Volterra, Prato, Orvieto, San Miniato, Arezzo, and Montecatini  
**Period:** late thirteenth to early fourteenth century

> **Evidence warning.** Simone’s biography is a prosopographical reconstruction, not a continuous medieval life. Several contemporary Bardi men were named Simone. Nineteenth-century historians repeatedly fused their careers. This dossier uses **Simone di Geri di Ricco de’ Bardi** as the best-supported identification of Beatrice Portinari’s husband, following Isidoro Del Lungo’s reconstruction and Arnaldo D’Addario’s review in the *Enciclopedia Dantesca*. Even then, particular offices and acts remain more or less secure. A set of claims publicized by Domenico Savini and Samuele Masi in 2026—especially a 1321 codicil, a third wife, a daughter of Beatrice, and Simone’s death in September 1321—has not yet received the kind of independent scholarly edition or archival verification available for the older evidence. Those claims are labeled accordingly.

The principal modern biographical notice is Arnaldo D’Addario’s [“Bardi, Simone”](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/simone-bardi_(Enciclopedia-Dantesca)/), published in the *Enciclopedia Dantesca* in 1970. Its central contribution is not merely a list of events but an explanation of why earlier biographies conflict: surviving official documents, notarial instruments, and Bardi commercial books contain numerous contemporary Simones—Simone di Nello di Simone, Simone di Giuliano di Ricco, Simone di Geri di Ricco, Simone del fu Cino di messer Iacopo, Simone di messer Iacopo, and others.

## Source Base and Identification Problem

### What is directly documented

1. **Folco Portinari’s will, 15 January 1288.** The most secure identification of the couple is the clause giving Bice fifty lire in small florins and calling her the wife of *dominus Simon de Bardis*. The [Archivio di Stato di Firenze’s exhibition page](https://archiviodistatofirenze.cultura.gov.it/asfi/dante-tra-le-carte/le-donne-della-vita-di-dante/beatrice) supplies the archival description (ASFi, ex Mostra 55) and the fuller clause:

   > *Item domine Bici filie sue, uxori domini Simonis de Bardis, iure institutionis legavit de bonis suis quinquaginta libras ad florenos, quibus simul et dote quam pro ea dedit cum nubsit eam iussit esse contentam.*

   In substance: Folco left his daughter Bice, wife of messer Simone de’ Bardi, fifty lire, with which sum and the dowry already given at her marriage she was to be content. This proves Bice’s marriage to a titled Simone Bardi by January 1288; it does **not** by itself name Simone’s father.

2. **Early Dante commentary.** A damaged marginal gloss associated with Graziolo de’ Bambaglioli identifies Beatrice as a Portinari and the wife of a Bardi “of Geri”; Andrea Lancia’s autograph gloss, dated about 1341–43, gives the more explicit identification “monna Biatrice … moglie … di messer Simone di Geri de’ Bardi.” Boccaccio likewise says that Beatrice was the wife of a Bardi knight named Simone and claimed information from a trustworthy person closely related to her. D’Addario summarizes these witnesses in the [Treccani notice](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/simone-bardi_(Enciclopedia-Dantesca)/); Maria Paola Zanoboni’s [DBI entry on Beatrice Portinari](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/beatrice-portinari_(Dizionario-Biografico)/) assesses the early-commentary tradition.

3. **The 26 February 1280 Florentine-style instrument (26 February 1281 modern style).** Savini and Masi report a seventeenth-century copy of a now-missing medieval parchment in Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale di Firenze, Magliabechiana XXV.591, p. 310. It describes Mone, son of the late Geri di Ricco di Bardo de’ Bardi, selling land and a house at “al Buco” in the parish of San Lorenzo, pieve of Ripoli, to his brother Cecchino, with the consent of Mone’s wife Bice. Their [2026 presentation of the document](https://www.adhocnews.it/messer-simone-e-madonna-bice-nuovi-e-inediti-documenti-su-beatrice-portinari/) gives its chain of custody and witnesses. The original parchment is reported missing; the evidence is therefore a later transcription, not an extant original.

4. **Public records and notarial acts attributed to Simone di Geri.** These furnish the offices, peace guarantees, military service, surety obligations, and property transactions assembled by Del Lungo and D’Addario.

### Why the identification remains qualified

Del Lungo’s decisive criterion was the honorific **dominus**. The Simone married to Bice is called *dominus* in Folco’s will, and among the namesakes the Simone di Geri found in Bardi business books and public documents was the one consistently marked with the title. Medieval Florentine usage reserved it especially for knights, jurists, and ecclesiastics. On this basis Del Lungo separated Simone di Geri’s career from that of Simone di Iacopo and other kinsmen. D’Addario judged the method persuasive but did not convert every similarly named record into certainty.

Earlier scholars had produced incompatible biographies:

- G. A. Scartazzini identified the husband as Simone di Geri but assigned him acts that Del Lungo attributed to Simone di Iacopo.
- Del Lungo’s own earlier notes had called Simone di Iacopo the husband, before he revised the identification after examining Bardi business books.
- A researcher named Randi proposed Simone di Giuliano.
- Passerini conflated Simone di Iacopo and Simone di Geri.

Accordingly, “Simone de’ Bardi did X” is not enough. A claim is strongest when the record supplies his patronymic, his title, and an associative network consistent with Geri’s branch.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal line

The 1281 property text as reported in 2026 styles him **Mone, son of the late Geri di Ricco di Bardo de’ Bardi**. The reconstructed line is therefore:

- **Bardo** — earlier eponymous ancestor in the reported patronymic chain.
- **Ricco di Bardo** — grandfather.
- **Geri di Ricco** — father, dead by the 1281 act.
- **Simone (Mone) di Geri di Ricco** — subject.

Known close agnates include:

- **Cecchino di Geri**, brother. He bought property from Simone in 1281, acted as Simone’s procurator in 1305, appears in the Donati political network, and later participated in arranging the 1313 marriage of a niece Francesca.
- **Iacopo di Ricco**, paternal uncle in Marco Santagata’s reconstruction. The branch descended from Iacopo was more directly visible in high Florentine finance and the priorate; Geri’s branch appears more active in the consorteria’s external, diplomatic, and military affairs.

The Bardi were a large **consorteria**, a kinship corporation whose cohesion supported mercantile, banking, political, military, and patronage activity. The [Archivio di Stato di Firenze](https://www.archiviodistato.firenze.it/asfi/istituto/progetti/le-carte-dei-granduchi-lorenesi-a-praga-iniziative-di-ricerca-e-di-inventariazione/progetto-bardi-archivio-bardi-archivio-bardi-serzelli-e-archivio-preunitario-della-contea-di-vernio-inventariazioni-pubblicazioni-e-messa-on-line/) holds Bardi and Bardi-Serzelli fonds received in 1890 and 1961; together with the Prato material they comprise about 1,500 archival units spanning the thirteenth through twentieth centuries. This abundance coexists with severe identification problems because the family was large, names recurred, and the surviving corporate archive is uneven.

### Marriages

#### 1. Gemma dei Pulci — newly reported first wife

Savini and Masi’s 2026 reconstruction says that Simone first married **Gemma dei Pulci**, who died about 25 September 1280. They cite a later dowry-restoration proceeding involving the couple’s daughter Bartola: ASFi, Notarile Antecosimiano, notary Bonaiuto di Nolfo, vol. 2950, pp. 90 ff. Their published article contains typographical date corruption in the narrative (“1235” and “1203”), while its archival note and argument clearly require a fourteenth-century proceeding referring back to Gemma’s death in September 1280. Until the record receives a diplomatic edition, the first marriage should be called **reported from an archival act, not independently verified here**.

Proposed child:

- **Bartola de’ Bardi**, described as the only daughter of Simone and Gemma dei Pulci; said to have married Francesco Beccarugi. Her later dowry claim is the reported source for her maternity.

#### 2. Bice/Beatrice Portinari — documented wife

Bice was a daughter of **Folco di Ricovero Portinari** and **Cilia di Gherardo Caponsacchi**. Zanoboni gives her birth as Florence, April 1266, while other reconstructions use about 1265; both depend partly on the numerological chronology of Dante’s *Vita Nova* rather than a baptismal record. Her father was a prominent merchant-financier, a partner of the Cerchi, prior in 1282, 1285, and 1287, and founder of Santa Maria Nuova hospital in 1285–86. Her mother came from the old Caponsacchi elite.

The marriage date has been repeatedly misstated as 1287 or 1288 merely because Folco’s will proves the marriage then. The reported 1281 act places it earlier. Savini and Masi narrow it to January–25 February 1281, after Gemma dei Pulci’s death and before Bice’s consent on 26 February. That tight window is an **argument**, not a marriage record: it depends on the identification of Gemma, assumptions about mourning and Advent, and the inference that the couple were newly married. The safe formulation is: **married by 26 February 1281 if the copied act is authentic and correctly identified; certainly married by 15 January 1288**.

Children of this marriage are disputed:

- The older consensus found **no documented child** of Bice and Simone. Del Lungo inferred childlessness from Folco’s will because it named a grandchild through a dead daughter but no Bardi grandchild; Randi replied that Bice was alive, so Folco could provide for her directly. D’Addario in 1970 left the question open but reported no proof of children by Beatrice.
- **Dada de’ Bardi** is proposed by Savini and Masi in 2026 as a daughter of Bice. They reason from the age of Dada’s husband, notary Ser Paniccia di Duccio da Poggibonsi (active from 1292), and from the adult activity of their children in the 1330s. This is chronological inference; the public article does not quote a record naming Dada’s mother. “Almost certain” is the proponents’ assessment, not a demonstrated filiation.
- Older secondary accounts assign **Francesca**, **Bartolo**, and **Gemma** to Simone but cannot agree on their mother. A 1313 marriage arrangement for Francesca, mediated by uncle Cecchino, is real enough to enter the discussion, but does not identify her mother.

#### 3. Later wife or wives — conflicting reconstructions

Older accounts often name **Bilia/Sibilla di Puccio Deciaioli** as Simone’s later wife and leave Francesca, Bartolo, and Gemma between Bice and Bilia. The 2026 Savini–Masi reconstruction instead calls **Lisa degli Ubaldini** Simone’s third wife and attributes to that union several children. The two schemes are not reconciled in the consulted literature. Possible explanations include an additional marriage, variant or mistaken identification, or renewed confusion between Bardi namesakes.

Children named in the 2026 scheme:

- **Giorgio**, said to be the only legitimate son.
- **Costanza**, unmarried and apparently young in the proposed 1321 codicil; assigned a dowry.
- **Francesca**, wife in 1313 of a Strozzi variously called Francesco di Pierozzo, Pinaccio, or Pierozzo Strozzi in modern accounts.
- **Dada**, wife of Ser Paniccia di Duccio da Poggibonsi; maternity disputed as above.
- **Bartola**, wife of Francesco Beccarugi; assigned by Savini–Masi to Gemma dei Pulci.
- **Bistruffo** and **Casentino**, described in the 2026 account as acknowledged nonmarital sons provided for in the codicil.

Older accounts additionally name:

- **Bartolo**.
- **Gemma**, said in different summaries to have married a Baroncelli or Ardingo de’ Medici.

These lists should not be combined into a single certain family tree. They are competing reconstructions derived from different documents and, possibly, different Simones.

## Childhood and Early Life

No record of Simone’s birth, childhood, mother, schooling, or personal formation was located. His later title and offices indicate formation appropriate to a Florentine magnate and knight: household instruction, weapons and horsemanship, practical knowledge of communal government and law, and immersion in the Bardi kin-corporation. None of this identifies a teacher or formal school.

His father was dead by the reported February 1281 act. Simone was already legally able to transact real property and, according to the 2026 reconstruction, had already been widowed and remarried. A birth no later than the 1250s or early 1260s is plausible, but **no exact range can be proved**.

## Education and Formation

No university attendance, legal degree, apprenticeship contract, literary education, or named mentor is documented. The honorific *dominus* could apply to jurists, knights, or clergy, but early testimony specifically calls Beatrice’s husband a **cavaliere**. Thus the title need not imply legal training.

His formation was probably institutional rather than scholastic:

- the extended Bardi household and business network;
- the Guelf political order of Florence;
- the military obligations and privileges of magnate status;
- the officeholding circuit through which outsiders served as podestà or captain of the people in other communes;
- property and surety practices visible in his notarial acts.

No writings reveal his religious, philosophical, or artistic views.

## Early Career and Marriage, 1280–1290

### 1280–81: property and household transition

On 17 June 1280 Simone and Cecchino reportedly bought land and a house from their cousin Bartolo. On 26 February 1281 modern reckoning, Simone sold related land and a house at “al Buco” in the pieve of Ripoli to Cecchino, with his wife Bice consenting. Witnesses named in the later copy include Gualterotto and Ruggero, sons of the late Rosso, and Maffeo, son of the late Tommasino di Gualterotto de’ Bardi; the notary was Giunta di Spigliato di Brunetto of Santa Lucia dei Magnoli.

The act establishes several features of Simone’s position: patrimonial land in the Florentine contado, intra-family conveyancing, a brother with whom he repeatedly did business, and a wife whose consent was required because marital property or dowry rights could be affected.

After marrying, Bice probably moved from the Portinari neighborhood east of central Florence to the Bardi houses in Oltrarno. Marco Santagata places the family residences along the artery now called Via de’ Bardi, near Santa Lucia dei Magnoli and the road toward Rome. This spatial movement is historically plausible and socially significant, but no deed naming the couple’s exact house is supplied.

### 1288: podestà of Volterra and Folco Portinari’s will

Simone di Geri served as **podestà of Volterra in 1288**. A podestà was an external chief magistrate, normally charged with justice, public order, and executive authority for a fixed term. The appointment demonstrates regional standing beyond Florence.

On 15 January 1288 Folco Portinari’s will documented the marriage and the financial settlement. Bice received only fifty lire in addition to the dowry already supplied, whereas unmarried sisters received much larger dowry provisions. This is evidence of a completed marital settlement, not evidence that Simone received only fifty lire or that the couple’s wealth was limited.

### 1290: captain of the people at Prato; death of Bice

Simone served as **captain of the people at Prato in 1290**, another external communal magistracy oriented toward the institutional interests of the *popolo*. This juxtaposition—a magnate knight serving a popular office outside Florence—was normal within the professional circuit of communal government.

Dante’s *Vita Nova* encodes Beatrice’s death through three calendars and the number nine. Modern conversions vary between 8 and 19 June 1290; the Archivio di Stato exhibition and the DBI entry use 19 June, while Santagata calculates an hour after sunset on 8 June. The discrepancy is calendrical and textual, not evidence of two deaths.

The cause of Bice’s death is unknown. Childbirth is a recurrent modern conjecture; no medical or mortuary record supports it. A 2026 “Masi theory” reads a seventeenth-century Bardi family tree as placing Bice’s death in 1295, but this conflicts with the literary chronology and is not yet established by a contemporary record. Simone’s conduct or location at her death is unknown.

## Chronological Life History, 1290–1321/30

### 1295: peace guarantor and surety

On **17 July 1295**, in San Piero Scheraggio, Simone appeared among guarantors of a peace between the Mannelli and Velluti, in the presence of magistrates, magnates, and popolani. The same year he acted as surety for the condottiere **Inghiramo of the counts of Biserno** before the commune. Both roles fit a knight with wealth, credit, and connections: a guarantor put reputation and potentially assets behind another’s compliance.

### 1300: Donati/Black Guelf conspiracy

Marco Santagata identifies Simone, husband of Bice, among the main conspirators in the Donati faction’s plan after the May Day violence of 1300. The plot proposed outside armed intervention led by Guido di Battifolle against the Cerchi-dominated government. It was discovered; Simone and Guido were fined, while Corso Donati, then in Rome and treated as the instigator, was sentenced to death in absentia and suffered confiscation and demolition of his Florentine houses.

This is the clearest serious political controversy attached to Simone. It should be described as a **discovered conspiracy followed by a communal fine**, not as a modern criminal conviction and not as rumor. The precise surviving judicial record is not reproduced in the consulted online source, and the general homonym caveat remains relevant; Santagata’s identification rests on the reconstructed Simone di Geri biography.

The episode placed Simone on the opposite side from Dante’s Cerchi/White-aligned political network. Santagata observes that Dante nevertheless never names the Bardi family in the *Commedia*, despite attacking many enemies. Whether this silence expresses restraint, family sensitivity, indifference, or *damnatio memoriae* cannot be determined.

### 1304: campaign against the Stinche

Simone took part in the 1304 expedition against the **castle of the Stinche** in the Val di Greve, a Cavalcanti possession. This was armed communal/factional service, not the later Florentine prison of the same name. His role is reported as participation; a command title is not supplied.

### 1305–07: Mozzi surety dispute and Moriano estate

Notarial records studied by Del Lungo show a *messer Simone del fu Geri de’ Bardi* involved under the Ordinances of Justice as one of those who had provided security for **Andrea, son of messer Tommaso de’ Mozzi**. Andrea was condemned for beating a popolano, and Simone had to pay under the surety. On **22 May 1305**, seeking recourse against Andrea, Simone appointed Cecchino as procurator to take possession of assets ceded by Mozzi.

On **20 April 1306**, Simone bought from Vanni de’ Mozzi the **castle of Moriano** in the Val di Greve and other possessions. He leased the property a few months later, showing active estate management and rental income. On **7 December 1307** he sold the castle to the inquisitor of heretical depravity. D’Addario’s online text breaks off after the buyer’s title and does not supply the price; no amount should be invented.

The sequence is important financially. It documents real estate, leasing, and creditor recovery more directly than it documents personal participation in the Bardi bank.

### 1308–10: war against Arezzo; captain of the people at Orvieto

Simone participated in military operations against **Arezzo in 1308 and 1310**. In 1310 he also served as **captain of the people of Orvieto**. The exact relationship between the office term and the military operations is not specified in the summary source.

### 1313: opposition to Henry VII; Francesca’s marriage

In **1313** Simone was among Florentines firmly opposing Emperor **Henry VII**. This fits the dominant Florentine Guelf posture, though “Guelf” did not erase the violent division between Blacks and Whites.

A separate 1313 record concerns the marriage of Simone’s daughter or niece **Francesca** to a Strozzi, arranged with the involvement of uncle Cecchino. The record does not identify Francesca’s mother and therefore cannot prove descent from Beatrice.

### 1315: Montecatini

Simone is reported **in arms at Montecatini** against Uguccione della Faggiola in 1315. Florence and its Guelf allies suffered a major defeat on 29 August. The notice proves presence in the military mobilization if correctly attributed; it does not state whether Simone was killed, captured, wounded, or returned safely.

### 1321: proposed codicil and death

Savini and Masi announced a codicil dated **7 September 1321**, dictated in a Florentine hospital under Bardi patronage and written by notary Michele Mangiadori. Their popular account alternately calls the institution San Bonifacio and Sant’Eusebio/the hospital of the Incurables, an inconsistency that requires archival clarification. They say the lost main testament had already disposed of much of the estate, while the codicil:

- assigned a dowry to the still-unmarried Costanza;
- provided for nonmarital sons Bistruffo and Casentino;
- implied prior provision for other children;
- identified Giorgio as the legitimate male heir.

They infer that Simone was dying and died on 7 or 8 September 1321, less than a week before Dante’s death at Ravenna on 14 September. A codicil made in hospital supports illness or perceived danger but **does not by itself prove death that day**. The article does not publish the full Latin, shelfmark, or image of the codicil. The 1321 death must therefore remain a recent, uncorroborated proposal.

### 1329–30: possible captaincy at San Miniato

Del Lungo found an ASFi *Provvisioni* entry (25, fol. 88v) appointing a **dominus Symon de Bardis** captain of the people of San Miniato on **18 December 1329**, for six months beginning 10 February 1330. D’Addario explicitly treated attribution as conditional. If this is Beatrice’s husband, he was alive after 1321; if the codicil and near-immediate-death reconstruction are correct, the San Miniato office belongs to a namesake. No consulted source resolves the conflict.

Dates such as “after 1346” found in derivative web summaries should not be accepted without a patronymic record; they likely perpetuate the homonym problem.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### The Bardi company

The Bardi consorteria controlled one of medieval Europe’s largest merchant-banking companies. Its activities included commodity trade, remittance, sovereign and papal finance, tax and tithe collection, and branch operations across Italy, western Europe, the Mediterranean, and the Levant. Zanoboni notes that the Bardi and Peruzzi had European branches and collected papal tithes. An economic-history survey, [“Italian banking companies of the XIIIth and XIVth centuries”](https://jeeh.it/article/view/2654/2659), situates the Bardi among the great Florentine merchant-banking societies.

The company’s later scale and its collapse in the 1340s must not be retrojected mechanically onto Simone. Santagata’s reconstruction says Geri’s branch handled something like the family’s “foreign policy,” whereas Iacopo’s branch was more visible in high finance and Florentine priorates. No consulted account-book entry calls Simone a partner, factor, branch manager, or depositor. Thus:

- **Documented:** he belonged to the Bardi; held valuable property; acted as surety; leased estates; had the standing to hold office and conduct military/diplomatic business.
- **Reasonable but unproved:** his resources and credit were entangled with the Bardi kin-corporation.
- **Not established:** a precise partnership share, investment account, managerial role, or personal loan book in the Compagnia dei Bardi.

### Property and income

Known or reported assets and transactions include:

- land and a house at **al Buco**, pieve of Ripoli, conveyed to Cecchino in 1281;
- unspecified assets in the Mozzi surety settlement;
- **castle of Moriano** and associated holdings, acquired in 1306, rented, and sold in 1307;
- property rights sufficient to require spousal consent where Bice’s dowry interests might be implicated.

No comprehensive inventory, tax return, balance sheet, or net-worth estimate survives in the consulted material. Descriptions such as “one of Florence’s richest men” should be avoided unless tied to a specific assessment.

### Institutions

Simone’s documented or reconstructed institutional connections include:

- Commune of Florence;
- offices of podestà and captain of the people in Volterra, Prato, and Orvieto;
- possibly San Miniato;
- Bardi consorteria and its houses around Santa Lucia dei Magnoli;
- Guelf and Donati/Black political networks;
- military mobilizations against Stinche, Arezzo, Henry VII’s cause, and Uguccione della Faggiola;
- a Bardi-patronized hospital at the proposed 1321 codicil.

No technologies, inventions, patents, independent companies, or authored projects are known.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Political trajectory

Simone’s career combines three identities:

1. **Magnate knight.** The title *messer/dominus* placed him above ordinary merchants in Florentine status hierarchies.
2. **Guelf officeholder and military agent.** His service in other communes and wars shows participation in the intercommunal Guelf system.
3. **Black/Donati partisan.** His alleged central role in the 1300 plot and association with the Bardi-Della Tosa hard line place him against the Cerchi-aligned government in which Dante became prior.

The marriage to Bice has sometimes been interpreted as a calculated truce between a Portinari family close to the Cerchi and Bardi relatives close to the Donati. Del Lungo advanced that view; Zingarelli considered it unnecessary and warned against inventing additional family enmity. Political alliance was a normal function of elite marriage, but no marriage contract or correspondence states this motive.

### Religion

Simone lived within Latin Catholic civic society. His sale of Moriano to an inquisitor, Bardi ecclesiastical patronage, and the proposed hospital codicil document institutional connections, not personal doctrine. No evidence was found of unusual devotion, heresy, religious office, pilgrimage, or theological writing.

### Intellectual and artistic life

No text by Simone survives, and no record makes him a patron of Dante, Giotto, or another artist. The Bardi chapel frescoes by Giotto belong to wider family patronage and postdate Beatrice’s death; attaching their commission personally to Simone would be unsupported.

### Dante: what can and cannot be said

No letter, legal action, meeting record, quarrel, duel, payment, or conversation connects Dante directly with Simone. Dante does not name Simone or the Bardi in the *Vita Nova* or *Commedia*. He does not state that his Beatrice married, and no text establishes that Simone knew of Dante’s poetic devotion.

The historical triangle is therefore asymmetrical:

- Simone’s marriage to Bice is documentary fact.
- Dante’s poetic Beatrice is textual fact.
- The identification of Bice Portinari with Dante’s Beatrice rests on near-contemporary and fourteenth-century testimony plus biographical fit.
- A reciprocal romance, adultery, rivalry, jealousy, persecution, or personal hostility among the three is not documented.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Violence, and Financial Questions

### 1. The 1300 conspiracy

**Classification:** documented/reconstructed political conspiracy; communal punishment.  
**Claim:** Donati partisans planned an armed overthrow of the Cerchi government using outside forces; Simone was among the leaders.  
**Outcome:** plot discovered; Simone and Guido di Battifolle fined; Corso Donati sentenced more severely in absentia.  
**Caution:** the online synthesis does not reproduce the trial roll; attribution follows Santagata’s prosopography.

### 2. Surety for assault by Andrea de’ Mozzi

**Classification:** documented civil-financial consequence of another man’s offense.  
**Claim:** Andrea de’ Mozzi was condemned for beating a popolano; Simone and other kin had guaranteed him under the Ordinances of Justice and therefore paid.  
**Aftermath:** Simone pursued reimbursement and authorized Cecchino to seize ceded assets.  
**Caution:** this does not show Simone participated in the beating.

### 3. Political and military violence

Simone participated in campaigns against the Stinche/Cavalcanti stronghold, Arezzo, and Uguccione’s forces. These were public or factional military acts, not individually charged private crimes in the consulted evidence. No evidence was found of torture, murder, imprisonment, exile, or personal atrocities committed by him.

### 4. Wealth and banking claims

Popular biographies call Simone a “rich banker.” His family’s banking power is undeniable, but his **individual** banking position is not documented in the consulted sources. The safer classification is “Bardi knight and officeholder from a merchant-banking dynasty, with documented land and credit dealings.”

### 5. No modern categories without evidence

No evidence was found of money laundering, intelligence service, formal corruption proceedings, fraud, sexual scandal, addiction, or abuse. Applying those modern categories to routine medieval kin-credit and factional politics without records would be anachronistic.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### Myth 1: Simone as the obstructive husband in a consummated Dante–Beatrice romance

1. **Story:** Dante and Beatrice mutually loved one another; Folco forced her to marry Simone for money or politics; Simone becomes the worldly obstacle, sometimes a Black Guelf villain.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** romantic biographies, historical novels, opera, theatre, and film. Mario Caserini’s 1913 *Dante e Beatrice* depicts reciprocal love and marriage to Simone despite it; the surviving synopsis even has Beatrice die in Dante’s arms. Francesco Fioretti’s twenty-first-century fiction turns “ser Mone” into the obstacle to an impossible love.
3. **Evidence for:** marriage to Simone is real; Dante’s poems express intense devotion to Beatrice; the families occupied different political networks.
4. **Evidence against:** no evidence of reciprocal love, an affair, forced marriage, Simone’s jealousy, or Dante at Bice’s deathbed. Dante’s narrative is stylized and withholds her civil identity and marriage.
5. **Effect:** Simone’s independent public career disappears; he becomes a stock “husband of the muse.”

### Myth 2: the marriage occurred in 1287

1. **Story:** Bice married Simone in 1287 and died three years later.
2. **Origin/circulation:** derivative biographies confused the date of Folco’s will with the wedding.
3. **Evidence:** the will proves she was married by January 1288.
4. **Counterevidence:** the reported February 1281 act already calls Bice Mone’s wife; institutional sources now place the marriage about 1280–83.
5. **Effect:** it compresses the marriage and supports a romantic “brief forced union” narrative unsupported by the documents.

### Myth 3: Simone was simply “the banker”

1. **Story:** Simone personally ran the great Bardi bank.
2. **Circulation:** short accounts of Dante and Beatrice use occupational shorthand.
3. **Evidence:** he was a Bardi, had property, and engaged in surety and rental transactions.
4. **Counterevidence:** no consulted source supplies his partnership share or banking office; his documented profile is knightly, political, military, and landed.
5. **Effect:** it ties him anachronistically to the Bardi company’s later loans to Edward III and its 1340s failure, events possibly after his death.

### Myth 4: Beatrice died in childbirth and bore Simone’s children

1. **Story:** Bice died giving birth, sometimes after producing several Bardi children.
2. **Circulation:** later biographies and family-tree literature; childbirth fills a documentary void and makes the young death legible.
3. **Evidence:** young married women did face high obstetric risk; Simone had children in one or more marriages.
4. **Counterevidence:** no contemporary source states a cause of death or names Bice as mother. The Dada attribution is a new chronological inference.
5. **Effect:** it humanizes Bice but is often repeated as fact; it also changes Simone’s descendant line without proof.

### Myth 5: Bice died in 1295—the “Masi theory”

1. **Story:** a seventeenth-century Bardi genealogy places a date “1295” beside Bice, meaning she survived five years beyond Dante’s date.
2. **Appearance:** promoted by Samuele Masi and Domenico Savini in March 2026.
3. **Evidence for:** the family tree was reportedly compiled from older household papers; a neighboring 1280 date appears consistent with the marriage evidence.
4. **Evidence against:** the tree is three centuries later and the meaning of the dates is not demonstrated; Dante’s *Vita Nova* and the early biographical tradition place the death in 1290; the full genealogy and apparatus have not been critically published in the consulted sources.
5. **Effect:** if accepted, it would separate the historical Bice’s death from Dante’s purported autobiographical chronology and reopen the identification question. At present it is an **unverified revisionist hypothesis**.

### Myth 6: Simone and Dante died within a week of one another

1. **Story:** Simone dictated a deathbed codicil on 7 September 1321 and died almost immediately; Dante died at Ravenna on 14 September.
2. **Appearance:** Savini and Masi’s 2026 account.
3. **Evidence for:** the proposed codicil’s date and hospital setting.
4. **Evidence against/uncertainty:** a codicil does not prove death; the full instrument is not published; an older record may place a *dominus Symon de Bardis* in office in 1329–30.
5. **Effect:** it produces a powerful symbolic “two men of Beatrice dying together” ending, making especially strict source separation necessary.

### Myth 7: Santa Margherita de’ Cerchi is certainly Beatrice’s tomb

The tourist tradition places Bice in Santa Margherita de’ Cerchi with the Portinari. Zanoboni notes that the modern marker is not reliable and records Savini’s rival proposal of burial in the Bardi chapel at Santa Croce. Santagata argues that Santa Lucia dei Magnoli or nearby Santa Maria Sopr’Arno are more geographically probable because a married woman normally belonged to her husband’s parish and burial network. No secure grave of Bice is known, and Simone’s burial is likewise unproved.

## Works by Simone de’ Bardi

No literary, theological, political, or technical work authored by Simone is known. No speeches, letters, account books, memoirs, artistic works, patents, or dictated narrative are securely attributed to him.

Documentary acts in which he participated are **not authored works** in the modern sense; they were drafted by notaries. They nevertheless form his documentary corpus:

- **17 June 1280 property purchase** with Cecchino from cousin Bartolo — extant/reported archival parchment.
- **26 February 1281 modern style conveyance to Cecchino**, with Bice’s consent — medieval original reported lost; seventeenth-century transcription by Carlo Strozzi survives.
- **15 January 1288 will of Folco Portinari** — not Simone’s act, but the secure marital identification.
- **17 July 1295 peace guarantee** between Mannelli and Velluti.
- **1295 surety** for Inghiramo of Biserno.
- **1305–07 notarial acts** concerning Andrea de’ Mozzi, Cecchino’s procuratorship, and Moriano.
- **7 September 1321 proposed codicil** — dictated by Simone if the 2026 identification is correct; main testament lost; full text not yet available in the consulted public account.

## Illness, Later Life, and Death

No securely documented illness, injury, addiction, psychological crisis, or cause of death is known. The 1315 battlefield appearance does not tell whether Simone was hurt. The proposed 1321 hospital codicil suggests illness or mortal anxiety but does not name a disease in the public summary.

Three end-of-life positions must remain separate:

1. **Older conservative position:** alive through the securely attributed activities ending in or after 1315; death date unknown.
2. **2026 Savini–Masi proposal:** codicil in hospital on 7 September 1321; presumed death that day or the next.
3. **Conditional Del Lungo extension:** if the San Miniato appointment is his, alive in 1329–30.

No secure burial site was established. Assertions that he lies in Santa Croce or a particular Bardi cloister tomb require a published inscription or burial record.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Simone left no identifiable school, text, invention, or cult. His reputation developed indirectly through three structures:

### 1. Dante’s silence and Beatrice’s fame

Dante made Beatrice one of the central figures of European literature but never named her husband. The result is paradoxical: Simone is perpetually present as “the husband” in biography and absent from the works that generated the fame. His historical life—offices, military service, property, faction—is normally discarded.

### 2. The romantic triangle

Later culture transformed an asymmetric documentary situation into a conventional triangle: inspired poet, reciprocal beloved, obstructive husband. Film, opera, drama, and fiction supplied scenes for which no medieval evidence exists. Simone’s Black Guelf alignment made him especially available as a political villain.

### 3. Prosopographical rehabilitation

Del Lungo and D’Addario shifted attention from “Beatrice’s husband” to a distinguishable Florentine knight with a public career. The ongoing Bardi archival projects and Savini–Masi announcements continue that recovery, while also demonstrating the danger of building a smooth biography from homonyms and late genealogies.

The most historically defensible legacy statement is therefore narrow: Simone was a substantial Florentine magnate whose marriage placed the historical Bice Portinari within the Bardi kin-world; his later fame is overwhelmingly a by-product of the contested identification of that Bice with Dante’s Beatrice.
