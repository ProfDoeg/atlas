## Works by Aaron the Levite

### Material obra

- **Undated; wilderness narrative:** *The molten calf*. A cult image fashioned from Israelites’ gold: Exodus 32:4 assigns the forming or casting to Aaron, while Exodus 32:24 has Aaron claim that the calf simply emerged from the fire, and Deuteronomy 9:16 attributes its making collectively to Israel. Its authorship and even its original narrative function therefore remain contested; historical-critical readings often understand the episode as a later polemic involving northern calf worship rather than a securely datable artifact by a recoverable historical Aaron ([Exodus 32:1–24](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus%2032%3A1-24&version=NRSVUE); [Deuteronomy 9:15–21](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy%209%3A15-21&version=NRSVUE); [Watts, “Aaron and the Golden Calf”](https://surface.syr.edu/rel/14/); [Collins, *Introduction to the Hebrew Bible*, discussion of Exodus 32](https://www.sbl-site.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/IntrotoHebrewBible_FP.pdf)).

- **Undated; immediately after the calf’s fabrication:** *Altar before the molten calf*. An altar explicitly built by Aaron, distinct from the image and apparently intended to accommodate sacrifice during the festival he announced. Unlike the calf, its construction is not contradicted by Aaron’s subsequent account, although its historical existence cannot be verified outside the literary narrative ([Exodus 32:5–6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus%2032%3A5-6&version=NRSVUE); [Watts, “Aaron and the Golden Calf”](https://surface.syr.edu/rel/14/)).

### Manifestations mediated through ritual objects

- **Undated; before the confrontation with Pharaoh:** *Signs before the Israelite people*. Exodus 4:30 credits Aaron with performing an unspecified set of signs first entrusted to Moses. These resist classification as Aaron’s works: they are divine manifestations reenacted by a human spokesman, and the passage neither enumerates them nor represents Aaron as their inventor ([Exodus 4:27–31](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus%204%3A27-31&version=NRSVUE); [Findlay, *From Prophet to Priest*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/jj.29556551)).

- **Undated; Egyptian-plague narrative:** *The staff-serpent sign*. Aaron casts down his staff and it becomes a serpent or great snake before Pharaoh; Pharaoh’s specialists produce comparable manifestations. Aaron performs the gesture, but the transformation belongs to the narrative’s divine agency rather than to an independently authored object ([Exodus 7:8–13](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus%207%3A8-13&version=NRSVUE); [Findlay, *From Prophet to Priest*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/jj.29556551)).

- **Undated; Egyptian-plague narrative:** *The transformation of Egyptian waters into blood*. At Moses’ instruction Aaron extends his staff over Egypt’s waters, after which they become blood. It is a large-scale manifestation mediated by Aaron, not a stable artifact, and source-critical scholarship disputes whether Aaron belonged to the earliest form of the plague tradition ([Exodus 7:19–24](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus%207%3A19-24&version=NRSVUE); [“Aaron,” *Encyclopaedia Judaica*](https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/philosophy-and-religion/biblical-proper-names-biographies/aaron)).

- **Undated; Egyptian-plague narrative:** *The emergence of frogs*. Aaron extends his hand and staff over the waters, and frogs cover Egypt. As with the other plague signs, the text makes him the ritual operator rather than the autonomous creator, while critical reconstructions differ over whether his role is original or a priestly addition ([Exodus 8:1–7](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus%208%3A1-7&version=NRSVUE); [Findlay, *From Prophet to Priest*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/jj.29556551); [“Aaron,” *Encyclopaedia Judaica*](https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/philosophy-and-religion/biblical-proper-names-biographies/aaron)).

- **Undated; Egyptian-plague narrative:** *The emergence of gnats*. Aaron strikes the earth with his staff and its dust becomes gnats. This, too, is a narrated manifestation produced through Aaron’s action but attributed ultimately to divine command, not an obra that survives independently of the story ([Exodus 8:16–19](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus%208%3A16-19&version=NRSVUE); [Findlay, *From Prophet to Priest*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/jj.29556551)).

- **Undated; wilderness narrative:** *The flowering of Aaron’s staff*. Aaron supplies the staff bearing his name, but the overnight production of buds, blossoms, and almonds is represented as a divine sign confirming his priestly status. It therefore resists attribution as something Aaron made; his contribution is the inscribed staff, while the living manifestation is explicitly miraculous ([Numbers 17:1–11](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Numbers%2017%3A1-11&version=NRSVUE); [Findlay, *From Prophet to Priest*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/jj.29556551)).

### Manifesto and public speech-act

- **Undated; after construction of the calf and its altar:** *Proclamation of a festival to YHWH*. Aaron publicly declares a festival for the following day. This is a manifesto in the literal performative sense: the utterance institutes an observance rather than merely describing one, although its wording and preservation belong to the much later literary composition of Exodus ([Exodus 32:5–6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus%2032%3A5-6&version=NRSVUE); [Nocquet, “Pourquoi Aaron n’a-t-il pas été châtié?”](https://www.revue-etr.org/article/pourquoi-aaron-na-t-il-pas-ete-chatie-apres-la-fabrication-du-taurillon-dor-essai-sur-les-mentions-daaron-en-exode-321-336/)).

### Ritual works

- **Undated; inauguration of the sanctuary cult:** *Inaugural offerings, atonement, and blessing*. Leviticus 9 presents Aaron’s first priestly service as a composite ritual work: he offers sacrifices for himself and Israel, makes atonement, and blesses the people, after which divine glory and fire appear. The sacrifices and blessing are Aaron’s enacted work, but the fire is a manifestation rather than his creation; historically, the scene is generally treated as priestly literature idealizing the establishment of Aaronide worship ([Leviticus 9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Leviticus%209&version=NRSVUE); [Findlay, *From Prophet to Priest*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/jj.29556551); [Oxford Bibliographies, “Aaron”](https://academic.oup.com/reference/62341/reference-article-abstract/554109232)).

- **Undated; prescribed annually rather than securely reported as a completed event:** *Day of Atonement rite*. Leviticus 16 casts Aaron as the intended performer of a multipart purification and atonement protocol involving sacrifices, incense, blood, lots, and the dispatched goat. It resists being called a completed work by Aaron because the chapter is chiefly an instructional protocol—what Aaron “shall” do—not an unambiguous report that the named individual actually performed it ([Leviticus 16](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Leviticus%2016&version=NRSVUE); [“A Literary and Ritual Analysis of Leviticus 16”](https://scielo.org.za/scielo.php?pid=S2305-445X2023000100003&script=sci_arttext)).

- **Undated; Korah-rebellion narrative:** *Incense atonement during the plague*. Aaron places altar fire and incense in a censer, runs into the assembly, stands between the living and the dead, and thereby “makes atonement”; the plague stops. This is an emergency ritual performance rather than a material object, and scholarship notes that it is the Pentateuch’s exceptional narrative of incense itself functioning as the means of atonement ([Numbers 16:41–50](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Numbers%2016%3A41-50&version=NRSVUE); [Davies, *Numbers*, pp. 180–81](https://biblicalstudies.org.uk/pdf/e-books/davies_eryl-w/numbers_new-century-bible-commentary_davies.pdf)).

- [James D. Findlay, *From Prophet to Priest: The Characterization of Aaron in the Pentateuch* (Peeters, 2017)](https://www.jstor.org/stable/jj.29556551). The principal full-length literary and form-critical study used to delimit Aaron’s Pentateuchal dossier.

- [Mark Leuchter, “Aaron,” *Oxford Bibliographies in Biblical Studies*](https://academic.oup.com/reference/62341/reference-article-abstract/554109232). Scholarly bibliography used to check the scope and major critical literature.

- [John J. Collins, *Introduction to the Hebrew Bible*, excerpt supplied by the Society of Biblical Literature](https://www.sbl-site.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/IntrotoHebrewBible_FP.pdf). Used for the critical interpretation of Exodus 32 and its relationship to later Israelite calf traditions.
