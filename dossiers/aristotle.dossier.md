# Aristotle: Research Dossier

## Scope and Evidentiary Note

Aristotle lived in the fourth century BCE. No contemporary biography, personal diary, securely authenticated correspondence, financial account, court record, or complete publication archive survives. The principal biographical witness is Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of Eminent Philosophers* 5.1–27, written roughly five centuries after Aristotle’s death but drawing on earlier authors, including Hermippus. Later Greek, Latin, Syriac, and Arabic lives preserve additional material of uneven reliability.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by Aristotle’s surviving writings, his transmitted will, contemporary or near-contemporary evidence, or several substantially independent ancient sources.
- **Reported fact:** credible ancient testimony, but not independently demonstrable.
- **Disputed:** ancient or modern sources conflict, or attribution remains contested.
- **Allegation:** a charge made by an identifiable source without adequate proof.
- **Rumor or myth:** late, folkloric, propagandistic, miraculous, or demonstrably embellished tradition.
- **Unresolved:** available evidence does not permit a reliable conclusion.

Dates before the Common Era are approximate unless otherwise indicated.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full Greek name:** Ἀριστοτέλης, *Aristotélēs*.
- **Latin form:** Aristoteles.
- **English form:** Aristotle.
- **Common identifying title:** Aristotle of Stagira; the Stagirite.
- **Later Arabic and scholastic titles:** *Arisṭūṭālīs* or *Arisṭū*; “the First Teacher” (*al-muʿallim al-awwal*) in Islamic philosophical tradition; *Ille Philosophus*, “the Philosopher,” in medieval Latin scholastic usage.
- **Birth:** conventionally 384 BCE.
- **Birthplace:** Stagira, a Greek city in Chalcidice, near the eastern side of the Chalcidic peninsula, then within the Macedonian sphere.
- **Death:** 322 BCE, conventionally aged sixty-two.
- **Place of death:** Chalcis on Euboea.
- **Cause of death:** ancient testimony reported an illness of the stomach or digestive organs; the precise diagnosis is unknown.
- **Nationality/status:** a free Greek male from Stagira, politically connected to Macedon but not an Athenian citizen. During his Athenian years he was a resident alien, or metic.
- **Professions:** philosopher, lecturer, scientific investigator, organizer of collaborative research, political theorist, rhetorician, logician, literary theorist, and tutor.
- **Institutions:** Plato’s Academy; the court of Hermias at Assos; Philip II’s educational establishment for Alexander; the Lyceum or Peripatos in Athens.
- **Secure likeness:** none. Surviving busts are Roman-period copies or idealized portraits derived from lost Greek models.

No patents, incorporated companies, modern investments, or commercial enterprises existed in the relevant sense.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and ancestry

**Documented or strongly reported fact:** Aristotle’s father was **Nicomachus**, a physician associated with King Amyntas III of Macedon. Diogenes Laertius, citing Hermippus, calls Nicomachus both physician and friend of Amyntas. Medical practice was frequently hereditary in Greek families, and Aristotle’s sustained attention to anatomy and living organisms may reflect this environment, although direct instruction by his father is not documented.

Ancient genealogical tradition traced Nicomachus through a namesake to **Machaon**, physician-son of **Asclepius**. This is best treated as **prestige genealogy or family legend**, not verifiable descent.

Aristotle’s mother was **Phaestis** or **Phaestias**, reportedly from Chalcis. Her family connections or property probably explain Aristotle’s final refuge there, but the details are poorly preserved.

Both parents apparently died while Aristotle was young. **Proxenus of Atarneus** reportedly became his guardian. Proxenus’s son **Nicanor** later occupied an important place in Aristotle’s household and will.

### Siblings and collateral relatives

The evidence for siblings is fragmentary. **Arimnestus** is reported in the biographical tradition as a brother and **Arimneste** as a sister, but the exact family reconstruction is not secure. The statues commissioned in Aristotle’s will for relatives bearing these names support their existence more strongly than later narrative alone.

The historian **Callisthenes of Olynthus** was related to Aristotle, conventionally called his great-nephew or nephew through Aristotle’s family. The exact degree of kinship is not perfectly preserved.

### Pythias

Around 347–345 BCE Aristotle married **Pythias**, a relative—usually described as niece, adopted daughter, or both—of **Hermias of Atarneus**.

Competing descriptions exist:

- Ancient hostile sources sometimes called her Hermias’s concubine.
- The mainstream biographical reconstruction identifies her as Hermias’s niece or adopted daughter.
- Aristotle’s will treats her memory with marked respect and directs that her remains be placed with his own, according to her wish.

They had a daughter, also named **Pythias**. The wife probably died before Aristotle, but the date and place are unknown.

### Herpyllis and Nicomachus

After Pythias’s death Aristotle lived with **Herpyllis**, probably a woman from Stagira. Ancient sources call her a concubine; some modern scholars prefer “partner,” since Aristotle’s will provides generously for her and records that she behaved worthily toward him.

They had a son, **Nicomachus**, traditionally associated with the title *Nicomachean Ethics*. Whether that title means the work was dedicated to him, edited by him, named after Aristotle’s father, or acquired the name through another editorial circumstance is unresolved. Nicomachus was apparently still a minor in 322 BCE and was reportedly killed later in military service.

### Household, dependents, and enslaved people

Aristotle’s will is the principal evidence for his domestic economy. It names children, Herpyllis, guardians, executors, servants, and enslaved persons. He directed that several enslaved people be freed, that some young enslaved persons not be sold, and that others continue in service until old enough to merit freedom. He arranged a dowry or marriage provision for **Ambracis**, an enslaved woman who was to be freed.

This shows both personal provision for household dependents and Aristotle’s participation in slaveholding. The will does not provide a complete inventory or monetary valuation of the estate.

## Childhood and Early Life, 384–367 BCE

Aristotle was born in Stagira during the reign of Amyntas III. The city stood in a region where Greek city culture met expanding Macedonian power. His father’s court connection placed the family closer to the Macedonian monarchy than most Greek philosophical households.

Almost nothing securely documented survives about Aristotle’s childhood. Claims that he was personally raised at the Macedonian court, trained formally as a physician, squandered an inheritance, became a drug seller, or first joined a military expedition occur in later hostile or anecdotal traditions and are not reliable.

The medically connected family, proximity to Macedonian institutions, and guardianship by Proxenus form the strongest reconstructable background.

## Education and Formation at Plato’s Academy, approximately 367–347 BCE

At about seventeen Aristotle moved to Athens and entered **Plato’s Academy**. He remained for approximately twenty years, until Plato’s death in 348/347 BCE.

### Teachers and intellectual environment

- **Plato:** principal teacher and defining philosophical interlocutor.
- **Eudoxus of Cnidus:** mathematician and astronomer associated with the Academy; his theories of proportion, planetary spheres, and pleasure became objects of Aristotelian engagement.
- **Speusippus:** Plato’s nephew and successor as head of the Academy.
- **Xenocrates:** fellow Academician who later traveled with Aristotle to Asia Minor.
- Other important intellectual predecessors repeatedly analyzed by Aristotle include Socrates, Parmenides, Heraclitus, Empedocles, Anaxagoras, Democritus, and the Pythagoreans.

Aristotle adopted Plato’s conviction that philosophy should seek systematic explanations, but increasingly rejected separately existing Platonic Forms. He developed an account in which form is ordinarily immanent in concrete substances, knowledge begins from experience, and explanation requires attention to material, formal, efficient, and final causes.

### Activity within the Academy

Later tradition says Plato called Aristotle “the mind” or “intellect” of the school and contrasted his eagerness with Xenocrates’s slowness. These are **admiring anecdotes**, not contemporary records.

Aristotle probably wrote dialogues and other “exoteric” works during this period. Surviving fragments suggest early works such as *Eudemus, or On the Soul*, *Protrepticus*, and *On Philosophy* were more overtly Platonic in literary form than the technical treatises now preserved.

The claim that Aristotle left because Plato selected Speusippus rather than him is **speculative**. Aristotle was not an Athenian citizen, and the succession may not have been open to him. Political hostility toward Macedonian connections may also have contributed.

## Assos, Atarneus, and Lesbos, approximately 347–343 BCE

After Plato’s death Aristotle and Xenocrates traveled to **Assos** in the Troad. There Aristotle joined a philosophical circle supported by **Hermias**, ruler of Atarneus and Assos and a former associate of Academicians.

### Hermias

Hermias was a politically ambitious local ruler navigating relations among Greek cities, Macedon, and the Persian Empire. Aristotle became his friend and relative by marriage.

In approximately 341 BCE the Persian commander Mentor captured Hermias through deception or negotiation and sent him to Artaxerxes III, who had him executed. Tradition attributes to Hermias the final declaration that he had done nothing unworthy of philosophy.

Aristotle composed:

- a memorial inscription for Hermias at Delphi;
- a hymn or paean beginning with an invocation to virtue.

The hymn later supplied the formal basis for an impiety allegation against Aristotle.

### Lesbos and biological research

Aristotle moved to **Mytilene on Lesbos**, probably around 345/344 BCE. There he worked with **Theophrastus**, a native of Eresus.

The lagoons and marine environments of Lesbos are often connected with Aristotle’s observations of fish, cephalopods, crustaceans, reproductive processes, and animal anatomy. Particular observations in *History of Animals*, *Parts of Animals*, and *Generation of Animals* plausibly derive from Aegean fieldwork, though the surviving texts do not provide a travel diary.

Aristotle and Theophrastus founded systematic zoology and botany respectively in the sense of sustained, organized classification joined to causal explanation. Aristotle described hundreds of animals, distinguished blooded from bloodless animals, investigated reproduction and embryology, and sometimes relied on fishermen, hunters, beekeepers, breeders, and other practitioners.

## Macedonian Period and Alexander, approximately 343–335 BCE

King **Philip II of Macedon** invited Aristotle to educate his son **Alexander**, then approximately thirteen or fourteen. The traditional location is **Mieza**, near modern Naousa, where archaeological remains have been identified with a nymphaeum used for instruction.

Other elite youths associated with Alexander’s circle included Hephaestion, Ptolemy, Cassander, and perhaps future commanders, but evidence that all were formally Aristotle’s pupils is uneven.

### What Aristotle taught Alexander

Later sources say Aristotle taught ethics, politics, medicine, literature, and restricted or “acroamatic” philosophy. Plutarch reports that Alexander possessed an Aristotle-edited copy of Homer’s *Iliad*, the “casket Iliad,” which he carried on campaign.

Evidence levels:

- **Reported fact:** Aristotle served as Alexander’s tutor.
- **Probable:** Homer, rhetoric, ethics, politics, and Greek literature formed part of the curriculum.
- **Disputed:** Aristotle prepared a special recension of the *Iliad*.
- **Unresolved:** the content of supposed secret or esoteric instruction.
- **Unsupported inference:** Alexander’s later imperial policy directly implemented Aristotle’s political philosophy.

Aristotle’s surviving political theory often privileges Greek civic institutions and draws a Greek/barbarian distinction. Alexander later incorporated Persian personnel and court practices and promoted a composite empire. The relationship between those policies and Aristotle’s views cannot be reconstructed from surviving correspondence.

### Stagira

Philip had destroyed or depopulated Stagira during his campaigns. Ancient tradition says Aristotle obtained its restoration and new laws in return for tutoring Alexander. The city later commemorated Aristotle, but the exact scale of his intervention is not securely documented.

### Callisthenes

Aristotle recommended his relative and pupil **Callisthenes** as historian of Alexander’s Asian expedition. Callisthenes produced a strongly propagandistic campaign history. He later opposed Alexander’s effort to introduce *proskynesis*, or court obeisance, among Macedonians and Greeks.

After the Pages’ Conspiracy in 327 BCE, Callisthenes was arrested. Ancient accounts disagree whether he was executed, tortured, hanged, or died in confinement. His guilt remains unproved. Alexander reportedly blamed or threatened those who had sent Callisthenes—language understood as including Aristotle. This likely damaged the Aristotle-Alexander relationship.

## Athens and the Lyceum, approximately 335–323 BCE

Aristotle returned to Athens after Alexander’s accession and founded his school at or near the **Lyceum**, a gymnasium and sanctuary of Apollo Lykeios outside the city walls.

He could not own Athenian land as a metic. “Founded the Lyceum” therefore means established a teaching and research community in facilities associated with that public or sacred precinct, not necessarily acquired a privately owned campus.

### The Peripatos

The school became known as the **Peripatos**, and its members as Peripatetics. The name is traditionally associated with a covered walkway and, secondarily, with teaching while walking. The familiar image of Aristotle constantly pacing while lecturing is plausible but not independently documented.

### Teaching organization

Ancient reports distinguish:

- morning lectures for advanced students on difficult or “acroamatic” subjects;
- more accessible afternoon lectures for a broader audience.

The exact timetable may be later systematization. Aristotle’s surviving works read largely like compressed lecture materials, working treatises, research dossiers, or texts repeatedly revised within the school—not polished books intended for a general reading public.

### Collective research

The Lyceum assembled material across many fields:

- constitutions of 158 Greek and non-Greek communities;
- records of dramatic competitions;
- lists of Olympic victors;
- studies of customs and laws;
- zoological and botanical observations;
- histories of earlier philosophy, mathematics, medicine, and rhetoric.

Alexander is said to have supplied Aristotle with substantial money and ordered hunters, fishermen, and officials throughout the empire to collect specimens. The very large sums in later accounts are **unverified royal-patronage traditions**. Macedonian connections may have aided research, but no financial account proves the scale.

### Colleagues and pupils

Prominent associates included:

- **Theophrastus of Eresus:** closest collaborator and successor.
- **Eudemus of Rhodes:** philosopher and historian of science.
- **Aristoxenus of Tarentum:** music theorist and philosopher; later tradition portrays him as resentful that Theophrastus received the succession.
- **Dicaearchus of Messana:** geographer, historian, and political writer.
- **Meno:** associated with a history of medicine.
- **Demetrius of Phalerum:** statesman and Peripatetic associate.
- **Phanias of Eresus**, **Clearchus of Soli**, and others.
- **Neleus of Scepsis:** later inherited Theophrastus’s library according to one transmission narrative.

### Intellectual program

Aristotle divided disciplined inquiry broadly into:

- **Theoretical sciences:** first philosophy or metaphysics, mathematics, natural philosophy.
- **Practical sciences:** ethics and politics.
- **Productive sciences:** rhetoric, poetics, crafts, and productive knowledge.

He developed or systematized:

- categorical predication;
- syllogistic logic;
- demonstrative science;
- substance, form, matter, actuality, and potentiality;
- four-cause explanation;
- teleological natural science;
- soul as the form of a living body;
- virtue as a stable disposition formed by habituation;
- practical wisdom;
- political constitutions and their corruptions;
- rhetorical proof through character, emotion, and argument;
- tragedy as structured imitation producing pity and fear.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Politics

Aristotle was not an elected Athenian official. His political activity consisted principally of teaching, advising, constitutional research, personal association with Macedonian rulers, and reputed legislative work for Stagira.

His *Politics* classifies correct constitutions as kingship, aristocracy, and polity, with corresponding deviations of tyranny, oligarchy, and democracy in its narrowly critical sense. Elsewhere he uses “democracy” descriptively and analyzes mixed institutions pragmatically.

He regarded the polis as natural and held that human beings achieve distinctive flourishing in political community. Citizenship in his fullest sense involved sharing in deliberative and judicial office.

He did not advocate modern political equality. His ideal arrangements generally exclude women, enslaved persons, resident foreigners, manual laborers, and sometimes farmers from full citizenship.

### Ethics

In the *Nicomachean Ethics* and *Eudemian Ethics*, the human good is *eudaimonia*: flourishing or living well through rational activity in accordance with virtue over a complete life. Moral virtue is acquired through habituation and ordinarily seeks an appropriate mean relative to the person and circumstances; it is not a formula requiring mathematical moderation in every act.

The highest life is presented as contemplative, although political virtue, friendship, household relations, law, and external goods remain necessary elements of human flourishing.

### Theology and religion

Aristotle participated in Greek civic religious forms and made vows and dedications in his will. His philosophical theology argued for eternal motion ultimately dependent on an unmoved mover, described as actuality and self-thinking thought.

This is not straightforwardly the personal creator God of later Judaism, Christianity, or Islam. Medieval interpreters integrated Aristotle into monotheistic systems, often through concepts Aristotle did not himself formulate.

### Natural philosophy

Aristotle held that the cosmos was eternal, geocentric, finite, and divided between terrestrial change and celestial regularity. He denied a vacuum and analyzed motion in terms of natural places and causes.

These theories dominated long traditions but were substantially displaced by early-modern mechanics, astronomy, chemistry, and experimental science. His empirical zoology has fared better historically than his cosmology and mechanics, although it too contains errors, reports based on hearsay, and teleological assumptions rejected or reformulated in modern biology.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Institutions

- Plato’s Academy, approximately 367–347 BCE.
- Hermias’s Assos circle, approximately 347–345 BCE.
- Educational establishment at Mieza, approximately 343–340 BCE.
- Aristotle’s Lyceum/Peripatos, approximately 335–323 BCE.

### Research projects

- Comparative collection of approximately 158 constitutions.
- Compilation of dramatic and athletic victor lists.
- Zoological survey and classification.
- Historical surveys of philosophy, mathematics, medicine, and rhetoric.
- Collection of legal, political, poetic, and cultural examples.

Authorship of collaborative Lyceum products cannot always be assigned to Aristotle personally. *Constitution of the Athenians* is commonly treated as a product of his school and possibly supervised or revised by him.

### Property and wealth

The will indicates a prosperous household containing real property or rights in property, furnishings, dependent labor, and enslaved persons. It refers to houses at Stagira and arrangements for Herpyllis to live either there or in Chalcis. It also orders tombs, statues, dedications, marriages, manumissions, and transfers of property.

No reliable total wealth figure survives. Claims of enormous payments from Alexander are not documentary facts.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Plato

Teacher for approximately twenty years and Aristotle’s decisive philosophical predecessor. Aristotle criticizes Platonic Forms extensively but preserves many Platonic problems, methods, and aspirations. The phrase “Plato is dear, but truth is dearer” is a later paraphrastic tradition rather than a securely verbatim personal declaration.

### Theophrastus

Student, collaborator, biological counterpart, intimate associate, and successor. Aristotle’s will named Theophrastus among temporary guardians if willing and able. Ancient tradition says Aristotle selected him over Eudemus and bequeathed or transmitted books to him.

### Hermias

Friend, political host, patron, and relative by marriage. His execution produced Aristotle’s memorial inscription and hymn. Hostile ancient accounts used Hermias’s allegedly servile origins and Pythias’s supposed sexual status to attack Aristotle.

### Philip II

Royal patron who employed Aristotle as Alexander’s tutor. The degree of earlier personal familiarity through Nicomachus’s service to Amyntas is unknown.

### Alexander the Great

Pupil and later king. Their connection is documented by ancient tradition, but later literature magnified it into a master-conqueror partnership. Their continuing correspondence, Alexander’s financing of Aristotle’s research, and a final rupture are plausible but not securely measurable.

### Antipater

Macedonian regent and political associate. Aristotle addressed or was credited with works concerning kingship or colonies to him and named him in connection with execution of the will. His proximity later helped generate the poisoning allegation involving Alexander.

### Callisthenes

Relative, pupil, and recommended court historian. His arrest and death created political danger and perhaps estrangement between Aristotle and Alexander.

### Xenocrates and Speusippus

Academic colleagues and, in later tradition, rivals. Aristotle reportedly traveled with Xenocrates. There is no good evidence of a personal feud with Speusippus, although they differed philosophically.

### Isocrates

Intellectual and educational competitor rather than documented personal enemy. Aristotle’s teaching of rhetoric developed in an Athenian environment shaped by Isocrates’s school. The saying that it was shameful to remain silent and let Isocrates speak is a later report.

### Diogenes of Sinope

Later anecdotal collections pair Aristotle and Diogenes as opposing court philosopher and Cynic outsider. No securely documented meeting or sustained relationship exists.

## Departure from Athens, 323–322 BCE

Alexander died in Babylon in June 323 BCE. Athens and other Greek states revolted against Macedonian power in the Lamian War. Aristotle’s Macedonian relationships made his position dangerous.

**Eurymedon**, a hierophant, and/or **Demophilus** reportedly brought an accusation of impiety. The grounds were Aristotle’s hymn to Hermias and honors allegedly excessive for a mortal.

Aristotle left Athens for Chalcis. A famous saying attributes to him the wish not to let Athens “sin twice against philosophy,” meaning that after executing Socrates it should not destroy another philosopher. The remark is **late and likely apocryphal**.

The departure was political exile in practical effect, although surviving evidence does not establish a completed trial, conviction, formal sentence, or imprisonment.

Theophrastus assumed leadership of the school.

## Death, Will, and Burial

Aristotle died in Chalcis in 322 BCE. Apollodorus’s chronology, preserved through Diogenes Laertius, gives an age of sixty-three in ancient inclusive counting, corresponding approximately to sixty-two modern years.

### Cause

- **Reported fact:** illness or disease of the stomach.
- **Unresolved:** modern medical diagnosis.
- **Rumor:** suicide by poison.
- **Myth:** drowning himself in the Euripus because he could not explain its tides.

### Will

The transmitted will provides for:

- guardianship of Pythias and Nicomachus;
- marriage of daughter Pythias to Nicanor, if circumstances permitted;
- residence, money, servants, and possible remarriage for Herpyllis;
- co-burial with his deceased wife Pythias;
- dedications to Zeus Soter and Athena Soteira;
- memorial statues;
- freedom or eventual freedom for named enslaved persons;
- prohibition on selling certain young household slaves.

Recent scholarship has argued that two forms of the will circulated and that one was manipulated to minimize Herpyllis’s status and deny that she was Nicomachus’s mother. The transmitted document is therefore valuable but not textually transparent.

### Burial and Stagira cult

Later tradition says Aristotle’s remains were returned to Stagira, where citizens established a tomb, altar, festival, and civic gathering place called the Aristoteleion. This is **reported posthumous commemoration**, not securely contemporary archaeological documentation.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Contested Material

### Impiety prosecution

- **Status:** documented ancient accusation; no surviving indictment or verdict.
- **Accusers:** Eurymedon and/or Demophilus.
- **Basis:** the hymn and honors for Hermias.
- **Political setting:** anti-Macedonian reaction after Alexander’s death.
- **Outcome:** Aristotle departed Athens; no trial result survives.

### Relationship with Macedonian imperial power

- **Documented fact:** Aristotle’s father served Amyntas; Aristotle was employed by Philip; he taught Alexander; he was associated with Antipater.
- **Unresolved:** whether he advised specific imperial policies.
- **Unsupported simplification:** that he was either architect of Alexander’s empire or a consistent philosophical opponent of it.
- **Political consequence:** Macedonian associations exposed him to hostility in Athens.

### Natural slavery

In *Politics* I Aristotle argues that some people are slaves “by nature” when they can apprehend reason in another but lack deliberative authority sufficient for autonomous rule. He also distinguishes natural slavery from enslavement merely by war or law and concedes that legal enslavement can be unjust.

Evidence and classification:

- **Documented doctrine:** natural hierarchy can make mastery and slavery mutually advantageous and just.
- **Documented qualification:** not every actual slave is naturally a slave; conquest does not by itself establish justice.
- **Historical consequence:** later writers used Aristotelian language in debates over conquest and enslavement, including the Spanish controversy over Indigenous Americans.
- **Scholarly dispute:** whether the argument is internally inconsistent, partly critical of existing slavery, or an attempted philosophical justification of it.
- **Documented household practice:** Aristotle owned enslaved people.

### Women and household hierarchy

Aristotle states that the male is naturally fitted to rule and that women possess deliberative capacity “without authority.” His biological writings describe the female as, in one context, a “mutilated” or incomplete male and assign active/formal reproductive causality primarily to male semen.

- **Documented doctrine:** political and household hierarchy based partly on sex.
- **Textual complexity:** Aristotle recognizes women’s moral virtues and indispensable household functions but differentiates their authority and virtues from men’s.
- **Modern controversy:** whether translations exaggerate particular formulations, and how biological and political claims relate.
- **No evidence:** that Aristotle advocated civic equality of women.

### Ethnocentrism and Greek/barbarian distinction

Aristotle sometimes treats Greeks as naturally positioned to rule “barbarians” and quotes the sentiment that Greeks should rule non-Greeks. Elsewhere he makes natural aptitude rather than ethnicity the formal criterion for slavery.

- **Documented tension:** universal-sounding functional criteria coexist with Greek cultural superiority.
- **Historical misuse:** later imperial and colonial writers sometimes presented this as blanket authorization for conquest.
- **Unresolved:** how Aristotle would have classified specific later populations; counterfactual claims are not evidence.

### Errors in science

Documented errors include geocentric cosmology, denial of a vacuum, incorrect mechanics, claims about sex difference, and reports about animals that modern investigation rejects. Other observations—notably aspects of cephalopod anatomy and embryology—were unusually accurate.

The common story that Aristotle claimed women have fewer teeth than men is based on *History of Animals* 2.3. The claim is present in the transmitted corpus, although whether based on his own count, inherited report, textual corruption, or sex-biased sampling cannot be determined.

### Alexander poisoning allegation

Six years after Alexander’s death, according to Plutarch, **Hagnothemis** reported that **Antigonus** had said Aristotle procured poison from the Styx at Antipater’s request; Antipater’s son Iollas supposedly administered it in a mule’s hoof.

- **Status:** ancient political allegation and conspiracy narrative.
- **Evidence for:** later testimony preserved by Plutarch and Arrian; possible motive after Callisthenes’s death; Aristotle’s Antipater connection.
- **Evidence against:** late emergence, politically interested circulation, inconsistent ancient accounts, no physical or documentary evidence, and uncertainty that Alexander was poisoned at all.
- **Finding:** not established; generally treated as hostile or Successor-era political mythology.

### Plagiarism and “stolen African knowledge” claims

Modern polemical narratives sometimes assert that Aristotle stole Egyptian books from the Library of Alexandria or appropriated knowledge gathered during Alexander’s conquest.

- **Chronological evidence:** Aristotle died in 322 BCE; the royal Library of Alexandria developed under the Ptolemies after Alexander’s death.
- **Documented fact:** Greek philosophy and science interacted over centuries with Egyptian, Babylonian, Persian, and other knowledge traditions.
- **No supporting evidence:** Aristotle raided the Library of Alexandria or personally accompanied Alexander into Egypt.
- **Unresolved broader question:** the exact pathways by which non-Greek scientific knowledge entered particular Aristotelian texts.
- **Classification:** the library-theft story is anachronistic; claims of wider cross-cultural intellectual debt require case-by-case study.

### Adversarial current-search finding, 2024–2026

A dedicated search combining Aristotle’s name with “controversy,” “scandal,” “allegations,” “lawsuit,” “fraud,” “accusations,” “conspiracy,” and “criticism” found no new primary evidence of personal criminal or financial misconduct. Recent discussion chiefly revisits:

- natural slavery;
- hierarchy of women;
- Greek/barbarian ethnocentrism;
- the Alexander poisoning legend;
- contested authorship and transmission;
- claims of Egyptian plagiarism;
- Aristotle’s authority in debates over artificial intelligence and formal logic.

Modern “lawsuit,” “fraud,” and “money laundering” categories produce no historically applicable dossier entries for Aristotle.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Propaganda

### The Euripus death

**Story:** Unable to solve the tides of the Euripus Strait, Aristotle threw himself into the water, allegedly saying that if he could not comprehend it, it would comprehend him.

**Appearance and circulation:** Christian authors including Gregory of Nazianzus and later medieval and early-modern compilers circulated versions. Sir Thomas Browne examined and rejected it in *Pseudodoxia Epidemica*.

**Evidence:** Diogenes Laertius instead places his death at Chalcis after illness or poison; no contemporary source supports drowning.

**Effect:** The tale turned Aristotle into a symbol of reason defeated by nature or punished for intellectual pride.

**Classification:** late hostile myth.

### Suicide by aconite

Diogenes records a version in which Aristotle drank aconite after the impiety charge.

**Evidence:** it conflicts with illness traditions; there is no medical or contemporary corroboration.

**Classification:** ancient disputed report, not established fact.

### “Athens will not sin twice against philosophy”

**Story:** Aristotle fled so Athens would not repeat its execution of Socrates.

**Evidence:** absent from the earliest secure account and rhetorically polished.

**Effect:** cast Aristotle as a second Socrates while rationalizing flight rather than trial.

**Classification:** apocryphal saying.

### Aristotle ridden by Phyllis

**Story:** A woman named Phyllis or Campaspe seduced Aristotle and forced him to crawl on all fours while she rode him, demonstrating love’s domination over wisdom.

**Appearance:** medieval European exempla, literature, sculpture, ivories, prints, and church decoration; often called “Aristotle and Phyllis.”

**Evidence:** no ancient historical basis.

**Effect:** became a major misogynistic and anticlerical image of the wise man humiliated by sexuality and female power.

**Classification:** medieval folklore.

### Secret books for Alexander

Arabic and Persian traditions attributed to Aristotle the *Sirr al-asrār* or *Secret of Secrets*, framed as advice to Alexander on rule, medicine, astrology, alchemy, physiognomy, and occult knowledge.

**Evidence:** composed centuries after Aristotle, probably from multiple Arabic sources; not authentic correspondence.

**Effect:** transformed Aristotle into an occult sage, royal counselor, astrologer, and master of hidden sciences.

**Classification:** pseudepigraphic wisdom tradition.

### Aristotle as magician or necromancer

Medieval Islamic, Jewish, and Christian texts sometimes connected Aristotle with talismans, astrology, alchemy, or spirits. Works such as *Theology of Aristotle* and *Secret of Secrets* encouraged an image far removed from the extant Greek corpus.

**Classification:** pseudonymous reception and legendary accretion.

### Alexander’s casket Iliad

**Story:** Aristotle edited Homer for Alexander, who kept the volume in a precious casket taken from Darius.

**Source:** Plutarch’s *Life of Alexander*.

**Evidence:** possible literary instruction by Aristotle, but no manuscript or early independent confirmation.

**Effect:** made Aristotle the editor of the conqueror’s personal heroic scripture.

**Classification:** reported ancient anecdote, unverified.

### The hidden library

Strabo and Plutarch relate that Aristotle and Theophrastus’s books passed to Neleus, whose heirs hid them underground at Scepsis to prevent seizure by Attalid kings; damaged manuscripts were later sold to Apellicon, taken to Rome by Sulla, and edited in the environment associated with Andronicus of Rhodes.

**Evidence:** ancient testimony supports a transmission episode, but the strong claim that Aristotle’s technical works vanished entirely for two centuries is difficult to reconcile with Hellenistic evidence.

**Effect:** created the enduring rediscovery narrative of a lost intellectual treasure.

**Classification:** ancient transmission report with legendary exaggeration.

## Works by Aristotle

### General authorship warning

Ancient lists attribute approximately 150 titles and hundreds of books to Aristotle. Most polished dialogues and public works are lost. About thirty-one works or work-clusters survive substantially. The modern *Corpus Aristotelicum* includes authentic, school-produced, disputed, and spurious texts.

Original publication dates are generally unknown. Many surviving texts reached their present form through lectures, revisions, student copying, editorial combination, and posthumous arrangement. Standard references use **Bekker pagination**, derived from Immanuel Bekker’s nineteenth-century Greek edition.

### Logic: the later *Organon*

Generally accepted as substantially Aristotelian unless noted:

1. **Κατηγορίαι — *Categories***: disputed by some scholars but conventionally included.
2. **Περὶ ἑρμηνείας — *On Interpretation***: authorship occasionally disputed.
3. **Ἀναλυτικὰ πρότερα — *Prior Analytics***.
4. **Ἀναλυτικὰ ὕστερα — *Posterior Analytics***.
5. **Τοπικά — *Topics***.
6. **Σοφιστικοὶ ἔλεγχοι — *Sophistical Refutations***.

The grouping and title *Organon* are later editorial constructions, not Aristotle’s own single-volume publication.

### Natural philosophy, cosmology, psychology, and biology

7. **Φυσικὴ ἀκρόασις — *Physics***.
8. **Περὶ οὐρανοῦ — *On the Heavens***.
9. **Περὶ γενέσεως καὶ φθορᾶς — *On Generation and Corruption***.
10. **Μετεωρολογικά — *Meteorology***; parts, especially Book IV, have disputed authorship.
11. **Περὶ ψυχῆς — *On the Soul / De Anima***.
12. **Μικρὰ φυσικά — *Parva Naturalia***, conventionally comprising:
    - *On Sense and Sensibilia*;
    - *On Memory and Recollection*;
    - *On Sleep and Waking*;
    - *On Dreams*;
    - *On Divination in Sleep*;
    - *On Length and Shortness of Life*;
    - *On Youth and Old Age, Life and Death*;
    - *On Respiration*.
13. **Περὶ τὰ ζῷα ἱστορίαι — *History of Animals***; Books VII/X and portions of the transmission are disputed.
14. **Περὶ ζῴων μορίων — *Parts of Animals***.
15. **Περὶ ζῴων κινήσεως — *Movement of Animals***.
16. **Περὶ πορείας ζῴων — *Progression/Locomotion of Animals***.
17. **Περὶ ζῴων γενέσεως — *Generation of Animals***.

### Metaphysics

18. **Τὰ μετὰ τὰ φυσικά — *Metaphysics***.

The title probably arose from editorial placement “after the Physics.” The fourteen-book work is composite, contains repetitions and tensions, and likely incorporates materials from different periods.

### Ethics and politics

19. **Ἠθικὰ Νικομάχεια — *Nicomachean Ethics***.
20. **Ἠθικὰ Εὐδήμεια — *Eudemian Ethics***.
21. **Ἠθικὰ Μεγάλα — *Magna Moralia / Great Ethics***: disputed, frequently regarded as a Peripatetic compilation rather than Aristotle’s own text.
22. **Πολιτικά — *Politics***.
23. **Ἀθηναίων πολιτεία — *Constitution of the Athenians***: recovered from papyrus in the nineteenth century; generally assigned to Aristotle’s school, with personal authorship disputed.
24. **Constitutions** of approximately 158 states: lost except for the Athenian work and fragments.

Books IV–VI of the *Eudemian Ethics* substantially overlap *Nicomachean Ethics* V–VII. Their original location and editorial history remain disputed.

### Rhetoric and poetics

25. **Τέχνη ῥητορική — *Rhetoric***.
26. **Περὶ ποιητικῆς — *Poetics***: only the portion principally treating tragedy and epic survives; a second book on comedy is often inferred but not extant.

### Surviving disputed or spurious corpus works

27. **Mechanics / Mechanical Problems** — probably Peripatetic rather than directly Aristotelian.
28. **Problems** — layered school compilation.
29. **On Indivisible Lines** — probably spurious.
30. **The Situations and Names of Winds** — doubtful.
31. **On Melissus, Xenophanes, and Gorgias** — spurious or heavily corrupt.
32. **On Colors** — attributed in antiquity to Aristotle or Theophrastus; likely school work.
33. **On Things Heard** — doubtful.
34. **Physiognomonics** — composite or school text.
35. **On Plants** — not Aristotle; surviving form derives from Nicolaus of Damascus through Arabic and Latin transmission.
36. **On Marvellous Things Heard** — later compilation.
37. **On the Cosmos / De Mundo** — spurious; date disputed.
38. **On Virtues and Vices** — spurious.
39. **Economics / Oeconomica** — multiple books of differing authorship; not wholly Aristotle.
40. **Rhetoric to Alexander** — usually attributed to Anaximenes of Lampsacus, not Aristotle.

### Pseudonymous works with major historical influence

Not genuine Aristotle:

- **Theology of Aristotle**: Arabic adaptation of Plotinus, especially *Enneads* IV–VI, produced in the ninth-century translation movement and associated with al-Kindi’s circle.
- **Liber de causis / Book of Causes**: derived principally from Proclus; long attributed to Aristotle in Latin Europe.
- **Secret of Secrets / Sirr al-asrār**: Arabic pseudepigraphic advice literature attributed to Aristotle for Alexander.
- **Book of the Apple**: Neoplatonic death dialogue attributed to Aristotle.
- Numerous medieval works on astrology, alchemy, physiognomy, stones, talismans, and sexual or medical secrets.

## Textual Transmission

Theophrastus inherited or controlled Aristotle’s books and reportedly left them to Neleus. The later Scepsis narrative says the manuscripts were concealed, damaged, purchased by Apellicon, seized by Sulla after Athens fell in 86 BCE, and brought to Rome.

**Andronicus of Rhodes**, active in the first century BCE, is the only ancient editor explicitly named. Porphyry says Andronicus arranged Aristotle’s and Theophrastus’s writings by subject, collecting related materials together. The exact extent to which he created current treatises, merely ordered them, or edited already existing works remains disputed.

Aristotle was transmitted through:

- Greek Peripatetic and Neoplatonic commentary;
- Syriac translation;
- the Abbasid Arabic translation movement;
- Islamic philosophers and commentators, especially al-Kindi’s circle, al-Farabi, Avicenna, and Averroes;
- Hebrew translation and Jewish philosophy;
- twelfth- and thirteenth-century Latin translations;
- Byzantine manuscripts;
- Renaissance Greek editions and translations;
- Bekker’s *Aristotelis Opera* and modern critical editions.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Antiquity

Theophrastus expanded the Peripatetic school. Later Peripatetics specialized in ethics, biography, literary history, science, and commentary. Hellenistic Epicureans and Stoics competed with Aristotelian positions.

From the first century BCE onward, editorial and commentary traditions increasingly treated the technical corpus as a systematic philosophy.

### Late antiquity

Alexander of Aphrodisias became the most influential ancient Aristotelian commentator. Neoplatonists, including Porphyry, sought harmonies between Plato and Aristotle. Christian authors both used Aristotelian logic and criticized doctrines such as the eternity of the world.

### Islamic worlds

Arabic translators and philosophers made Aristotle foundational to *falsafa*. He became the “First Teacher,” while al-Farabi was sometimes the “Second Teacher.”

The Arabic Aristotle was not identical with the Greek corpus: Plotinian and Proclean texts circulated under his name. Al-Kindi, Avicenna, Averroes, and others combined commentary, criticism, and reconstruction rather than simple repetition.

### Jewish philosophy

Maimonides interpreted Scripture and theology in sustained engagement with an Aristotelianized philosophical tradition mediated by Islamic thinkers. He accepted much natural philosophy while distinguishing demonstrable knowledge from claims he considered unproved, including the eternity of the world.

### Latin scholasticism

Translations of Aristotle transformed European universities. Parisian prohibitions in the early thirteenth century were followed by curricular incorporation; by 1255 the arts curriculum required major Aristotelian works.

Albertus Magnus commented broadly on the corpus. Thomas Aquinas integrated Aristotelian metaphysics, psychology, ethics, and natural theology into Christian thought while rejecting or modifying doctrines inconsistent with creation and personal immortality. Aristotle became “the Philosopher.”

### Renaissance and early modern criticism

Humanists produced new Greek texts and Latin translations. Galileo, Descartes, Bacon, Hobbes, and others attacked scholastic Aristotelianism or specific physical doctrines. The displacement of Aristotle’s cosmology did not eliminate his influence on logic, metaphysics, rhetoric, ethics, biology, and political theory.

### Modern science and philosophy

Darwinian evolution rejected fixed-species and strongly teleological readings of nature, but historians of biology continue to study Aristotle’s empirical and comparative methods. Modern logic surpassed syllogistic logic in expressive power, while recognizing Aristotle’s foundational formalization of valid inference.

Twentieth- and twenty-first-century revivals include:

- virtue ethics;
- neo-Aristotelian metaphysics;
- philosophy of action;
- philosophy of biology;
- rhetoric and literary theory;
- political constitutional analysis;
- work on causation, essence, powers, and teleology.

His writings remain contested because their analytical power coexists with defenses of hierarchy, slavery, patriarchy, ethnocentrism, and obsolete natural science.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by historical evidence or explicit documented influence are included. Mere appearance in the same intellectual tradition is excluded.

### Plato — documented fact

Aristotle studied in Plato’s Academy at Athens for approximately twenty years, c. 367–347 BCE. Plato was his principal teacher and the central philosophical interlocutor throughout Aristotle’s work. Aristotle rejected separate Forms while retaining and transforming Platonic problems concerning knowledge, soul, ethics, politics, and first principles.

### Alexander the Great — reported fact with disputed extent

Philip II appointed Aristotle tutor to the adolescent Alexander, conventionally c. 343–340 BCE at Mieza in Macedonia. The teaching relationship is ancient and broadly accepted. The precise curriculum, duration, later correspondence, degree of political influence, and extent of estrangement are disputed. The allegation that Aristotle helped poison Alexander is an unsupported ancient conspiracy narrative.

### Diogenes of Sinope — rumor or myth

Later anecdotes contrast Aristotle, associated with Macedonian court patronage, with Diogenes the Cynic. No reliable source establishes a personal meeting, correspondence, collaboration, or rivalry between them. The connection belongs to moralizing anecdotal literature.

### Archimedes of Syracuse — documented intellectual succession, no personal contact

Archimedes was born late in Aristotle’s lifetime or around the time of his death. No meeting is documented. Archimedes’s works belong to the Greek mathematical and mechanical tradition that engaged problems also treated in the Aristotelian or Peripatetic *Mechanics*, but a direct personal relationship cannot be shown.

### Ctesias of Cnidus — documented textual use, no meeting established

Aristotle cites or draws on Ctesias’s reports concerning animals and eastern regions. This is a source relationship; no personal encounter is known.

### Homer — documented literary influence; legendary editorial connection

Aristotle analyzed Homer throughout the *Poetics*, *Rhetoric*, and lost *Homeric Problems*. Plutarch’s story that Aristotle prepared Alexander’s personal “casket” edition of the *Iliad* is an unverified ancient anecdote.

### Hesiod of Ascra — documented literary and intellectual influence

Aristotle cites and discusses Hesiod as an authoritative early poet and source of cosmological, ethical, and political sayings. This is textual influence across centuries, not personal contact.

### Herodotus of Halicarnassus — documented textual relationship

Aristotle knew and cited Herodotus. His use is critical and evidentiary; no personal contact was chronologically possible.

### Pindar of Thebes — documented textual relationship

Aristotle quotes and discusses Pindar in rhetorical, ethical, and poetic contexts. There was no personal relationship.

### Marcus Tullius Cicero — documented reception

Cicero read Aristotle’s now-lost dialogues and praised their style, describing a “golden stream” of eloquence. His evidence is important because the polished Aristotle he knew differs from the terse technical corpus that survives.

### Strabo of Amaseia — reported transmission connection

Strabo preserved the major ancient account of Aristotle and Theophrastus’s library passing through Neleus’s heirs at Scepsis to Apellicon. He had no personal connection to Aristotle.

### Plinio el Viejo — documented reception

Pliny the Elder used Aristotelian zoological and natural-historical material, directly or through intermediaries, in the *Natural History*. This is textual reception, not a personal link.

### Abu Yusuf Yaʿqub ibn Ishaq al-Kindi — documented reception and editorial network

Al-Kindi and his ninth-century Baghdad circle sponsored or revised Arabic translations and adaptations of Greek philosophy. Texts attributed to Aristotle—including the pseudonymous *Theology of Aristotle*—shaped al-Kindi’s metaphysics. This is a major transmission connection, though Aristotle did not author all works circulated under his name.

### Maimonides — documented intellectual influence

Maimonides engaged an Aristotelian philosophical curriculum mediated by Arabic thinkers, particularly in the *Guide of the Perplexed*. He accepted, modified, or rejected Aristotelian positions on demonstration, causality, soul, providence, and cosmic eternity.

### Albertus Magnus — documented intellectual influence

Albertus wrote extensive paraphrases and commentaries on the Aristotelian corpus in the thirteenth century and helped establish Aristotle’s natural philosophy and metaphysics in Latin university culture.

### Thomas Aquinas — documented intellectual influence

Aquinas wrote close commentaries on numerous Aristotelian works and reconstructed Christian theology using Aristotelian concepts of act and potency, form and matter, causation, virtue, and intellect. He also rejected or revised doctrines incompatible with Christian creation and personal immortality.

### Dante Alighieri — documented literary and intellectual reception

Dante calls Aristotle “the master of those who know” in the *Inferno* and structures ethical and cosmological material partly through Aristotelian and Thomistic traditions.

### Aurelius Augustinus — documented indirect reception

Augustine knew Aristotelian categories and dialectical materials, though his philosophical formation was more strongly Platonic and Neoplatonic. Later Latin readers frequently negotiated contrasts between Augustinian and Aristotelian frameworks.

### Roger Bacon — documented reception and criticism

Roger Bacon studied Aristotle intensively while criticizing defective translations and excessive reliance on authority without linguistic, mathematical, and experimental competence.

### Galileo Galilei — documented critical reception

Galileo’s mechanics and astronomy were framed partly against university Aristotelian physics. His dispute was primarily with inherited scholastic systems and specific physical claims, not with every aspect of Aristotle’s method.

### Francis Bacon — documented critical reception

Francis Bacon criticized Aristotelian syllogistic and scholastic methods as inadequate for discovery and promoted a new inductive program. His attacks helped construct the image of Aristotle as an obstacle to experimental science.

### René Descartes — documented critical reception

Descartes rejected major Aristotelian-scholastic accounts of substance, qualities, matter, and explanation while working in an intellectual vocabulary partly formed through them.

### Thomas Hobbes — documented critical reception

Hobbes attacked Aristotle’s metaphysics and politics, especially the naturalness of the polis and classical constitutional language. The connection is intellectual opposition.

### John Locke — documented reception

Locke criticized scholastic substance terminology and syllogistic education while retaining philosophical problems transmitted through Aristotelian traditions.

### Immanuel Kant — documented philosophical reception

Kant treated Aristotle’s logic as historically foundational and engaged categories, teleology, virtue, and metaphysics in transformed form. No personal connection existed.

### Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel — documented philosophical reception

Hegel lectured extensively on Aristotle and regarded him as a central predecessor in logic, metaphysics, psychology, and actuality.

### Karl Marx — documented philosophical reception

Marx studied Aristotle closely, especially on exchange, value, money, slavery, and the political character of human beings. In *Capital* he praised Aristotle’s analysis of value while locating its limits in Greek slave society.

### Friedrich Nietzsche — documented critical reception

Nietzsche engaged Aristotle’s ethics, tragedy, rhetoric, and poetics, often critically. His account of tragedy opposed later Aristotelian interpretations of catharsis.

### Martin Heidegger — documented philosophical reception

Heidegger’s early lectures and major concepts developed through close readings of Aristotle on being, truth, motion, practical understanding, temporality, and rhetoric.

### Hannah Arendt is not on the supplied roster

Her major Aristotelian reception is therefore not entered as an Atlas connection.

### Paul-Michel Foucault — documented intellectual reception

Foucault’s histories of ethics and practices of the self engage the ancient ethical tradition, including Aristotle, though usually less centrally than later Hellenistic and Roman sources. This is a documented interpretive relationship, not a personal link.

### Gilles Deleuze — documented philosophical reception

Deleuze repeatedly engages Aristotle’s categories, difference, actuality, potentiality, and representation, often as a framework to be displaced.

### Jacques Derrida — documented philosophical reception

Derrida analyzed inherited metaphysical concepts shaped partly by Aristotle, including presence, time, voice, metaphor, friendship, and animality.

### Jürgen Habermas — documented intellectual reception

Habermas engages Aristotelian practical philosophy and later neo-Aristotelian ethics as historical alternatives and interlocutors in theories of communicative rationality and modernity.

### Charles Robert Darwin — documented historical comparison, limited direct dependence

Darwin’s theory transformed biological thought by replacing species fixity and intrinsic teleology with descent and natural selection. Historians compare him extensively with Aristotle’s biology, but claims that Aristotle anticipated Darwinian evolution overstate the evidence.

### Carl Gustav Jung — documented reception, peripheral

Jung’s psychological and alchemical writings occasionally use Aristotelian concepts transmitted through scholastic and alchemical traditions. No direct foundational relationship comparable to Plato, Aquinas, or medieval Aristotelianism is established.

### Jorge Luis Borges — documented literary reception

Borges repeatedly invoked Aristotle’s classifications, logic, poetics, and the lost second book of the *Poetics*. The latter became a major fictional device in later literature, most famously outside the roster in Umberto Eco’s *The Name of the Rose*.

### Yuval Noah Harari — reported modern interpretive reference

Harari discusses Aristotle as an example of historically influential hierarchy, particularly sex hierarchy. This is modern commentary rather than scholarly or personal connection.

No securely supported personal or direct textual connection was found with most of the remaining atlas roster. Absence from this section means the searches did not establish more than remote inheritance, broad cultural influence, coincidental geography, or unsupported speculation.

## Chronology

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 384 BCE | Born at Stagira to Nicomachus and Phaestis/Phaestias. |
| Before c. 367 | Parents reportedly die; Proxenus becomes guardian. |
| c. 367 | Moves to Athens and enters Plato’s Academy. |
| 367–347 | Studies, teaches, and writes within the Academy. |
| 348/347 | Plato dies; Speusippus succeeds him. Aristotle leaves Athens. |
| c. 347 | Travels with Xenocrates to Assos under Hermias’s patronage. |
| c. 347–345 | Marries Pythias; works in Assos and Atarneus. |
| c. 345–343 | Resides at Mytilene on Lesbos; conducts biological research with Theophrastus. |
| c. 343/342 | Invited by Philip II to educate Alexander. |
| c. 343–340 | Teaching period at Mieza or the Macedonian court. |
| 340s | Stagira reportedly restored with Aristotle’s intervention. |
| 336 | Alexander becomes king of Macedon. |
| c. 335 | Aristotle returns to Athens and establishes the Peripatetic school at the Lyceum. |
| 335–323 | Main Lyceum teaching, research, and compilation period. |
| 334 | Alexander begins Persian campaign; Callisthenes accompanies him as historian. |
| 327 | Callisthenes arrested after opposition to *proskynesis* and the Pages’ Conspiracy; later dies in custody or is executed. |
| 323, June | Alexander dies in Babylon. |
| 323/322 | Anti-Macedonian revolt; Aristotle accused of impiety and leaves Athens for Chalcis. |
| 322 | Dies at Chalcis, probably from illness. Theophrastus succeeds him. |
| Late fourth century BCE | Family and school execute or preserve provisions of Aristotle’s will. |
| First century BCE | Andronicus of Rhodes edits and arranges Aristotle’s and Theophrastus’s works. |
| Ninth century CE | Major Arabic translation and adaptation activity; Aristotle becomes the “First Teacher.” |
| Twelfth–thirteenth centuries | Broad Latin recovery; corpus enters European university curricula. |
| 1255 | Paris arts curriculum requires study of major Aristotelian works. |
| 1831–1870 | Bekker’s *Aristotelis Opera* establishes standard pagination. |
| 1890–1891 | Papyrus text of *Constitution of the Athenians* recovered and published. |
| 1984 | Jonathan Barnes’s revised Oxford translation consolidates the English corpus. |
| 2025 | Major updated scholarly synthesis of the corpus’s textual transmission published by the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. |
