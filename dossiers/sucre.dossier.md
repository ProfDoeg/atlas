# Antonio José de Sucre: Research Dossier

## Scope, method, and evidentiary conventions

This dossier reconstructs the documented life of Antonio José de Sucre from birth through assassination and posthumous commemoration. It draws on Sucre’s correspondence and official documents, archival catalogues, contemporary and near-contemporary narratives, later documentary collections, and modern historical studies. Spanish-language sources were prioritized.

Evidentiary labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary correspondence, an official act, archival record, or multiple strong historical sources.
- **Reported fact:** widely repeated in reputable scholarship but not independently verified here from the original record.
- **Allegation:** an accusation made by an identified person or document.
- **Disputed claim:** supported and challenged by competing evidence or interpretations.
- **Rumor or myth:** a story whose documentary foundation is inadequate, but which has affected historical memory.
- **Unresolved question:** available evidence does not justify a firm conclusion.

No meaningful “recent scandal” can arise from Sucre’s own conduct after 1830, but an adversarial search through 2024–2026 found continuing disputes about the Pasto campaign, his role in creating Bolivia, his government’s treatment of Indigenous communities and the Catholic Church, Freemasonry, extramarital children, and competing theories of his assassination.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name:** Antonio José Francisco de Sucre y Alcalá. Some catalogues use Antonio José de Sucre y de Alcalá or shortened forms such as Antonio José de Sucre.

**Common name:** Antonio José de Sucre.

**Titles and styles:**

- General in Chief of the Colombian army.
- Liberator of Ecuador, a title used in patriotic literature.
- **Gran Mariscal de Ayacucho**—Grand Marshal of Ayacucho—conferred after the victory of 9 December 1824.
- President of Bolivia, 1825/1826–1828.
- “Abel of America” or *Abel de América*, a posthumous martyr-image particularly associated with Bolívarian historiography.
- “The most accomplished of Bolívar’s generals,” a recurrent historical characterization rather than an official title.

**Reported military nicknames:** “Mulei” and “Mulengue.” Their origin and prevalence are uncertain; they appear in discussions of correspondence surrounding his death and should not be treated as universally used names.

**Birth:** 3 February 1795, Cumaná, Province of Cumaná, Captaincy General of Venezuela, Spanish Empire.

**Death:** 4 June 1830, in the Berruecos mountain region, traditionally identified with La Jacoba, on the road between Popayán and Pasto, in the contemporary Republic of Colombia; now in Nariño Department, Colombia.

**Cause of death:** Gunshot wounds inflicted in an ambush. Contemporary descriptions differ over whether three or four shots struck or were fired.

**Nationality and political identity:** Born a Spanish colonial subject; became a Venezuelan republican and then an officer and officeholder of the Republic of Colombia—retrospectively “Gran Colombia.” He governed Bolivia but was not Bolivian by birth.

**Principal professions:** Military engineer, army officer, general, diplomat, administrator, constitutional president, legislator, and political negotiator.

**Religion:** Raised Roman Catholic. His marriage was solemnized through Catholic rites. His Bolivian government sharply reorganized ecclesiastical institutions, but documentary evidence does not establish atheism or rejection of Christianity.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and social background

Sucre was born into the colonial provincial elite of eastern Venezuela.

His father was **Vicente de Sucre y Urbaneja**—also rendered Vicente de Sucre Pardo y García de Urbaneja—a militia officer and political figure. His mother was **María Manuela de Alcalá y Sánchez**. She died while Antonio José was still a child, reportedly when he was approximately seven.

The Sucre family possessed military standing, local offices, and property. Later patriotic genealogy emphasized Flemish or Belgian roots in the Sucre line and Spanish-American ancestry through the Alcalá and Urbaneja families. The exact European genealogy has been repeatedly elaborated in commemorative works and is less securely documented online than the immediate parentage.

Antonio José was usually described as the fifth of nine children of his father’s first marriage. Vicente subsequently remarried and had additional children, producing a large blended family sometimes summarized as eighteen children in all.

### War losses in the family

**Documented general pattern; individual totals vary by source:** numerous members of the Sucre family joined the republican cause, suffered exile, imprisonment, or death, and lost property during Venezuela’s wars.

Older biographies report that royalist forces killed or caused the deaths of several of Sucre’s siblings and close relatives during the collapse of the eastern republic in 1814. One brother was reportedly murdered while ill in a hospital. Exact lists differ, and later patriotic accounts sometimes compress distinct deaths into a single family martyrdom narrative.

His father survived until 3 July 1824. Sucre’s correspondence shows attachment to Cumaná and continuing responsibility toward relatives despite long absence.

### Marriage

Sucre married **Mariana Carcelén de Guevara y Larrea**, Marquesa de Solanda and Villarocha, a wealthy Quito heiress.

The marriage was contracted **by proxy on 20 April 1828** in Quito while Sucre remained in Bolivia. Colonel Vicente Aguirre represented him. The couple met as husband and wife later that year, usually dated to 28 September 1828 at the Chisinche estate near Machachi.

The marriage linked Sucre to prominent Quito aristocratic families and to substantial urban and rural property, including estates associated with the Carcelén inheritance. Surviving correspondence indicates that Sucre hoped to retire to private life with Mariana in Ecuador.

### Children and attributed children

The documentary record does not support the simplified claim that Teresa was Sucre’s only child. At least three extramarital children have been attributed to him in biographical and genealogical research, with unequal levels of proof.

1. **Simona de Sucre Bravo**

   - Reportedly born 16 April 1822.
   - Mother: **Tomasa Bravo**.
   - **Documented evidence:** in a letter of 11 October 1825 to Vicente Aguirre, Sucre asked that a girl identified as Simona be collected and educated at his expense, reporting the deceased mother’s assertion that he was the father.
   - The wording supports acknowledgment of responsibility but leaves some uncertainty about formal paternity.
   - Her later fate is unclear.

2. **José María Sucre Cortés**

   - Reportedly born in La Paz on 15 January 1826.
   - Mother: **Rosalía Cortés Silva**.
   - Recognized in later genealogical and biographical literature as Sucre’s son. The online sources consulted did not expose the original baptismal record, so the exact status remains a reported fact rather than independently verified here.

3. **Pedro César de Sucre Rojas**

   - Reportedly born in Chuquisaca on 7 June 1828.
   - Mother: **Manuela de la Concepción Rojas Íñiguez**, associated with Tarija.
   - Treated in later family research as Sucre’s natural son. The child’s conception occurred shortly before Sucre’s proxy marriage; it became part of modern challenges to the image of Sucre as a domestically spotless hero.

4. **Teresa Sucre y Carcelén**

   - Legitimate daughter of Antonio José and Mariana.
   - Born 10 June 1829 in Quito and baptized the following day at El Sagrario.
   - Named for her grandmothers; Juan José Flores and Mercedes Jijón were reportedly her godparents.
   - Died in childhood, generally dated to 1831.

A later archival study lists records concerning Sucre’s inheritance, Teresa’s death, Mariana’s remarriage, and the descendants of her second marriage.

### Teresa’s death: competing accounts

- **Documented or reported medical account:** Teresa died of a gastrointestinal or childhood illness, a common cause of early death.
- **Rumor or hostile legend:** Isidoro Barriga, Mariana’s second husband, killed Teresa by throwing or dropping her from an upper floor while playing with her.
- **Evidence assessment:** no judicial finding or contemporary medical record establishing homicide was found in the consulted material. Members of the Carcelén family rejected the story. It survives because Teresa’s death affected the inheritance and because Barriga’s marriage to Mariana had already attracted suspicion.
- **Historical effect:** the story enlarged a conspiracy narrative in which Barriga and Mariana supposedly benefited from both Sucre’s and Teresa’s deaths.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1795–1808

Sucre grew up in Cumaná, one of the oldest Spanish-founded cities on the South American mainland. His family environment combined provincial landed status, military service, Catholic upbringing, and experience of the Caribbean commercial world.

After his mother’s death, relatives helped direct his upbringing. His brother José Manuel reportedly persuaded their father to send Antonio José to Caracas. There he lived under the supervision of his godfather or maternal relation, **Antonio Patricio de Alcalá**, archdeacon of Caracas Cathedral.

No evidence supports later romanticized depictions of a destitute childhood. He was a motherless child in a prominent but increasingly endangered Creole family.

## Education and Formation

### Military engineering

In approximately 1808 Sucre entered military-engineering studies in Caracas under the Spanish engineer **José—often called Tomás—Mires**. The curriculum reportedly included:

- mathematics;
- geometry and surveying;
- fortification;
- artillery;
- military drawing;
- practical engineering.

The name of his teacher varies in popular biographies, but José Mires is the historically identifiable officer and later served with Sucre in the Ecuadorian campaign.

Engineering training shaped Sucre’s later reputation for careful logistics, route planning, fortification, artillery placement, disciplined staff work, and written administrative precision.

### Intellectual influences

Sucre did not leave a systematic philosophical treatise. His writings show several recurring commitments:

- American independence from Spain;
- republican legality and constitutional government;
- civilian institutions rather than permanent personal military rule;
- administrative rationalization;
- public education;
- religious reform under state authority;
- humanitarian regulation of warfare;
- loyalty to the Colombian union and to Bolívar personally.

His political outlook was strongly influenced by **Simón Bolívar**, but they were not identical. Sucre sometimes acted ahead of Bolívar—most conspicuously in convening the Upper Peruvian assembly—and repeatedly sought retirement from command.

## Early Military Career, 1810–1819

### 1810–1812: First Venezuelan Republic

After the Caracas revolution of 19 April 1810, Cumaná adhered to the new political order. On 12 July 1810 the provincial junta appointed the fifteen-year-old Sucre a sublieutenant in the regulated militia of the East.

He served in engineering and staff capacities. In 1811 he was sent to Margarita and received engineering responsibilities; some biographies style him commander of engineers there. He served during the First Republic under the wider authority of **Francisco de Miranda**.

After Miranda’s capitulation in July 1812 and the republican collapse, Sucre returned east. He did not become a major public figure in the First Republic, but the episode gave him experience of defeat, broken capitulations, royalist repression, and the fragility of republican armies.

### 1813–1814: Mariño and the eastern campaigns

In early 1813 Sucre joined **Santiago Mariño’s** campaign from the eastern Caribbean littoral. He served alongside or within the military network of Mariño, Manuel Piar, José Francisco Bermúdez, and Manuel Valdés.

He participated in the campaigns that recovered Cumaná, Barcelona, and parts of eastern Venezuela. As Mariño’s aide or staff officer, he was present when the eastern and western republican forces tried to combine.

The republican disaster of 1814 was formative. José Tomás Boves’s royalist forces destroyed much of the Second Republic; civilians fled eastward, battles became exceptionally violent, and Sucre’s family suffered deaths and dispossession. Sucre escaped through the eastern theater and Caribbean routes.

### 1815–1816: Caribbean displacement

Accounts place him at different times in the Antilles, including Trinidad, Haiti, or nearby islands, amid the dispersed republican forces. Exact movements are difficult to reconstruct from short biographies.

He returned to the mainland with renewed patriot operations in 1816 and rejoined Mariño’s forces. The period exposed him to chronic factional rivalry among republican commanders.

### 1817: Guayana and Bolívar’s command structure

Sucre served under **Manuel Piar** during the campaign that opened Guayana to the republicans, then became integrated into Bolívar’s centralized command.

Piar’s subsequent rebellion, arrest, trial, and execution in October 1817 demonstrated the consequences of regional and personal military rivalry. No evidence suggests Sucre participated in the decision to execute Piar, but his career thereafter developed under Bolívar rather than the independent eastern command.

### 1818–1819

Sucre became a trusted staff officer, served in the lower Orinoco and eastern campaigns, and gained responsibilities beyond his age. By 1819 he had reached general officer rank—usually brigadier general—and held senior staff and administrative functions.

His ascent depended on demonstrated technical competence, disciplined obedience, and ability to reconcile or manage officers in a revolutionary army frequently fractured by regionalism.

---

## Armistice and Regularization of War, 1820

In 1820 Bolívar employed Sucre in negotiations with the Spanish commander **Pablo Morillo**. Sucre played a central drafting and negotiating role in the **Treaty of Armistice** and the **Treaty for the Regularization of War**, signed at Trujillo on 25–26 November 1820.

The agreements:

- temporarily suspended operations;
- established procedures for prisoners and exchanges;
- required humane treatment of wounded and captured soldiers;
- restricted the retaliatory “war to the death” practices that had marked the Venezuelan conflict;
- treated the opposing forces as organized belligerents rather than criminals without rights.

Later patriotic histories sometimes credit Sucre alone with founding modern international humanitarian law. That is a **hagiographic overstatement**: the agreements emerged from negotiation between Colombian and Spanish representatives and belonged to a wider law-of-war tradition. Nevertheless, Sucre’s role in drafting and implementing them is well documented and constitutes an important part of his career.

The later violence at Pasto is therefore especially significant: it stands in tension with the humanitarian reputation built around Trujillo.

## Ecuadorian Campaign, 1821–1823

### Guayaquil and command of the Army of the South

In 1821 Bolívar sent Sucre to Guayaquil, where a local revolution had created the Free Province of Guayaquil. His assignments were both military and political:

- negotiate cooperation between Guayaquil and Colombia;
- organize local and Colombian troops;
- prevent royalist recovery of the coast;
- advance toward Quito;
- promote Guayaquil’s incorporation into Colombia.

Sucre concluded an agreement with the Guayaquil authorities in May 1821. The political status of the province remained contentious among local independence advocates, Colombian unionists, and Peruvians associated with José de San Martín.

### Yaguachi and Huachi

Colonel José Mires, Sucre’s former teacher, served in the campaign. Patriot forces won at Yaguachi on 19 August 1821, but Sucre suffered a serious defeat at the **Second Battle of Huachi**, 12 September 1821.

The defeat cost men, equipment, and momentum. It was one of Sucre’s most important military failures. He subsequently negotiated an armistice and rebuilt his mixed force with Colombian, Guayaquilean, Peruvian, Argentine, Chilean, and other troops.

### Cuenca and Pichincha

Early in 1822 Sucre advanced from the south, moved through Cuenca and the central highlands, and used an arduous night march to place his army on the slopes of Pichincha above Quito.

At the **Battle of Pichincha**, 24 May 1822, his army defeated the royalist forces of Melchor de Aymerich. Quito capitulated. The victory secured the central territory of the former Audiencia and made the incorporation of Quito into Colombia effective.

The battle produced enduring national legends, especially that of the mortally wounded **Abdón Calderón**, whom Sucre promoted and memorialized. Later accounts embellished Calderón’s wounds and actions; the death and Sucre’s commendation are historical, while details vary.

### Guayaquil and San Martín

Sucre was not one of the two principals in the closed Guayaquil Conference of 26–27 July 1822. Bolívar’s secretary José Gabriel Pérez sent him a report of the Bolívar–San Martín discussions. Sucre’s operations had, however, created the military situation in which Bolívar controlled Quito and held decisive influence in Guayaquil.

There is insufficient evidence in the consulted sources to claim a substantive personal meeting between Sucre and **José de San Martín**. Their documented connection is institutional and military: troops associated with San Martín’s Peruvian expedition fought in Sucre’s army, and Sucre later assumed the decisive Peruvian command.

### Pasto revolt and “Black Christmas,” 1822

After Pichincha, the strongly royalist and locally autonomous Pasto region remained resistant. Benito Boves and Pastuso militias rebelled. Sucre advanced from Quito, defeated resistance at Taindalá on 22 December and the Guáitara on 23 December, and entered Pasto on 24 December.

**Documented fact:** republican troops, notably units identified with the Rifles Battalion, carried out killings, robbery, rape or sexual violence, and an extended sack. Contemporary and later accounts describe soldiers and civilians—men and women, including defenseless inhabitants—being killed indiscriminately.

**Disputed quantities:** estimates range from roughly 200 to 500 or more dead. The surviving evidence does not support a precise total.

**Command responsibility:** Sucre commanded the invading force. Some accounts state that Pasto was delivered to sack for three days; others emphasize loss of control by troops embittered by combat. A letter from Sucre to Santander dated 27 December 1822 acknowledged violent excesses in language later used both to condemn him and to argue that he did not personally order every act.

**Bolívar’s role:** later narratives quote or paraphrase exterminatory language attributed to Bolívar concerning the Pastusos. The campaign followed Bolívar’s strategic policy, but specific claims that Sucre merely executed a direct order to massacre every civilian require scrutiny of the exact letter and context.

**Evidentiary conclusion:** the massacre and sack are historical facts; Sucre’s operational command is a historical fact; the exact death toll and degree to which he explicitly ordered, tolerated, failed to prevent, or belatedly stopped particular atrocities remain contested.

**Reputational effect:** Ecuadorian and Venezuelan heroic narratives long minimized the event. Pasto and Nariño memory preserved it as the *Navidad Negra*. Twenty-first-century scholarship increasingly treats local royalism and Indigenous Pastuso agency seriously rather than depicting the population simply as fanatical enemies of liberty.

## Peru and the Final Royalist War, 1823–1825

### Arrival and political-military crisis

In 1823 Sucre went to Peru as Bolívar’s representative and military commander amid factional conflict, competing presidents and congresses, a still-powerful royalist army, and disputes between Colombian auxiliaries and Peruvian authorities.

He negotiated, organized reinforcements, and tried to preserve the Colombian expeditionary army. His relationship with Peruvian leaders included cooperation, mistrust, and jurisdictional conflict.

### Junín

At the Battle of Junín, 6 August 1824, Bolívar commanded the patriot army. The engagement was fought largely by cavalry. Sucre was part of the senior command but was not the sole battlefield architect.

### Command before Ayacucho

As Bolívar moved away from direct field command, Sucre assumed command of the United Liberating Army. The royalist commander José de Canterac maneuvered through the Andes under Viceroy José de la Serna. Sucre managed retreats, crossings, supply difficulties, multinational units, and disputes among senior officers.

### Battle of Ayacucho, 9 December 1824

At La Quinua near Ayacucho, Sucre commanded approximately 5,700–6,000 patriot troops against a larger royalist army often estimated at 9,000 or more. His principal subordinates included José María Córdova, Jacinto Lara, José de La Mar, Guillermo Miller, and other Colombian, Peruvian, Argentine, Chilean, and European officers.

The battle ended with the defeat and capture or wounding of the royalist leadership, including Viceroy La Serna. Córdova’s division delivered the most celebrated charge.

Sucre and Canterac signed the **Capitulation of Ayacucho**, dated 9 December. Its eighteen articles provided for:

- surrender of the royalist army and territorial positions;
- release or regulated treatment of prisoners;
- permission for Spanish personnel to return to Europe;
- recognition of ranks and, under controversial terms, state responsibility for certain expenses and debts;
- handover of fortresses and military material.

### Controversy over the capitulation

Peruvian critics later considered the terms excessively generous, financially burdensome, or favorable to Spanish officers. Supporters argued that generosity secured a rapid end to organized royalist resistance and avoided further bloodshed.

The capitulation did not instantly end every Spanish position: the Callao fortress held out until January 1826, and scattered resistance persisted. Ayacucho nevertheless destroyed the principal royalist field army and effectively ended Spanish continental power in South America.

After the victory Sucre received the title **Gran Mariscal de Ayacucho**.

### Women and camp followers

Manuela Sáenz’s precise battlefield role remains disputed. A later-published letter attributed to Sucre praised her conduct, including organizing support, tending wounded soldiers, and rescuing combatants, and recommended promotion. Some historians accept her direct presence at Ayacucho; others question later embellishment or textual authenticity. Sucre’s reported recommendation became central to Sáenz’s military legend.

## Creation of Bolivia, 1825

### Advance into Upper Peru

Sucre entered Upper Peru after Ayacucho. Royalist general Pedro Antonio Olañeta continued resistance, but his forces fractured; Olañeta was mortally wounded at Tumusla on 1 April 1825. A surviving Sucre letter to Carlos Medinaceli states that Olañeta’s death ended the war.

### Decree of 9 February 1825

At La Paz, Sucre issued the decree of 9 February convening representatives of the Upper Peruvian provinces to decide their political future.

This was politically delicate because Upper Peru had historical and legal connections to the former Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata, while Peru also claimed interests, and Bolívar initially opposed or sought to constrain an immediate separate state.

Sucre did not unilaterally “invent” Bolivia. Local elites—including Casimiro Olañeta and representatives of Chuquisaca, La Paz, Potosí, Cochabamba, and Santa Cruz—actively pressed for a separate assembly. Nevertheless, his control of the occupying army and his decree made deliberation possible under Colombian military authority.

The assembly declared independence on 6 August 1825 and named the country Bolivia in honor of Bolívar. Chuquisaca was later renamed Sucre.

### Competing interpretations

- **Bolivian national account:** Sucre enabled free self-determination and protected the new republic.
- **Peruvian or unionist criticism:** he helped detach Upper Peru from Peru or obstructed a larger Peruvian union.
- **Argentine legal-historical view:** the Río de la Plata renounced claims and allowed the provinces to decide.
- **Revisionist claim:** a narrow Upper Peruvian elite, protected by a foreign army, chose separation without broad popular participation.
- **Evidence assessment:** the assembly was representative by early nineteenth-century elite suffrage standards, not by universal democratic standards. Both local agency and Sucre’s military power were indispensable.

## Presidency of Bolivia, 1825/1826–1828

### Dates and constitutional position

Bolívar was designated the new republic’s first president but remained only briefly. Sucre exercised practical executive power from late 1825 and became constitutional president in 1826. Sources variously begin his presidency on 29 December 1825 or with constitutional formalization in 1826.

The Bolivian Constitution of 1826 was primarily drafted by Bolívar, not Sucre. It provided a strong, effectively life-tenured presidency and a complex legislative structure. Sucre administered under it but repeatedly expressed aversion to indefinite personal rule.

### Administration and public finance

The new state inherited:

- a devastated mining economy;
- depleted population and livestock;
- military expenses;
- colonial debts and administrative fragmentation;
- weak customs revenue;
- dependence on the silver economy;
- powerful regional and ecclesiastical corporate interests.

Sucre sought budgetary accounting, reorganized customs and treasury offices, reduced unnecessary military expenditure where possible, and attempted to establish public credit. The government remained financially precarious and depended heavily on revenues extracted from rural Indigenous communities.

### Indigenous taxation and land policy

Bolívar and Sucre initially attempted to abolish the colonial Indigenous tribute and replace it with a general direct contribution based on property or income.

**Documented outcome:** the replacement system failed to produce adequate revenue. Administrative weakness, lack of property information, rural resistance, and fiscal crisis led the government to restore the levy under the name “Indigenous contribution.”

Measures aimed at converting communal *ayllu* land into individual property were suspended or reversed. The reforms threatened collective landholding without creating an effective egalitarian tax system.

**Historical assessment:** claims that Sucre permanently abolished Indigenous tribute are misleading. He attempted abolition, but his administration restored a structurally similar tax. Liberal equality before taxation remained more programmatic than real.

### Slavery

The Bolivarian constitutional project proclaimed gradual emancipation principles, but slavery did not disappear immediately in practice. Claims that Sucre completely and immediately ended slavery throughout Bolivia overstate the enforceable result. The legal transition involved free-birth, manumission, and later constitutional developments rather than a single conclusive act.

### Education and Simón Rodríguez

Sucre’s government founded or reorganized schools and colleges, appropriated buildings and revenues for education, and sought foreign or specialized instructors.

**Simón Rodríguez**, Bolívar’s former teacher, was appointed to direct public education and responsibilities involving agriculture, mines, and roads. His plan emphasized practical and popular education, including poor and socially marginalized children.

The project encountered resistance from local elites, clergy, administrators, and parents; financing and management disputes followed. Rodríguez left amid conflict. Sucre supported educational reform but did not consistently sustain Rodríguez’s full experimental program.

### Church reforms

On 29 March 1826 the government decreed a large-scale reorganization of monasteries and religious communities. Measures included:

- suppressing or consolidating small convents;
- transferring members to authorized houses;
- converting some churches to parish use;
- placing convent property, income, accounts, and archives under state control;
- redirecting revenues to schools, hospitals, or other public institutions;
- regulating ecclesiastical staffing and tithes.

**Documented controversy:** clergy and conservative property-holders regarded these acts as confiscatory and hostile to the Church. The government presented them as rationalization of underpopulated institutions and redirection of corporate wealth toward public needs.

Claims that Sucre intended to abolish Catholicism are unsupported. His policy was state-directed ecclesiastical reform, not documented religious eradication.

### Military presence and nationality conflict

Bolivia depended on Colombian troops. Their pay, conduct, and privileged position generated resentment. Peruvian officers and local politicians attacked what they considered foreign domination. Sucre increasingly found himself caught between Colombian soldiers, Bolivian factions, and the Peruvian government.

### Foreign relations

Principal issues included:

- recognition and borders with Peru and the Río de la Plata;
- the status of Tarija;
- access to the Pacific through Cobija;
- Arica and proposals for territorial exchange;
- fear that Bolivia would remain a Colombian or Bolivarian satellite;
- maintenance and eventual withdrawal of Colombian troops.

Some anti-Sucre accounts accuse him of planning to partition Peru or annex territory to Bolivia. Correspondence shows that several territorial and federal schemes were discussed in the fluid postwar environment, but no single conspiracy narrative captures the shifting proposals.

### Opposition and failure of the constitutional project

The life-presidency constitution never acquired stable legitimacy. Opponents attacked centralized power, foreign troops, taxation, ecclesiastical reform, and Bolívarian influence. Peru under José de La Mar and Agustín Gamarra regarded Colombian influence in Bolivia as a strategic threat.

Sucre repeatedly asked to resign. His own later explanation blamed weak political foundations, factionalism, administrative inexperience, and social disintegration.

## Chuquisaca Mutiny, Wounding, and Resignation, 1828

On 18 April 1828 Colombian troops mutinied at Chuquisaca, reportedly over pay and political grievances. Sucre went personally to confront or calm them.

He was shot and seriously wounded in the right arm; accounts describe a fractured arm or elbow. The injury impaired the limb permanently. He took refuge under protection while the government disintegrated.

Peruvian general **Agustín Gamarra** invaded Bolivia, officially presenting his intervention as liberation from Colombian control. Under the Treaty of Piquiza, Colombian troops were to leave and a new political arrangement followed.

Sucre resigned and delivered his farewell message to the Bolivian Congress on 2 August 1828. He rejected the option of maintaining power by terror, declaring that his wounds would remind him that in forming Bolivia he had preferred the rule of laws to acting as tyrant or executioner.

The statement is both an authentic political defense and a foundation of later hagiography. It did not erase coercive taxation, ecclesiastical seizure, foreign military occupation, or the Pasto violence.

---

## Marriage, Private Life, and Return to War, 1828–1829

After leaving Bolivia, Sucre joined Mariana in Ecuador and initially intended to retire on her estates.

The attack on Bolívar in Bogotá in September 1828 and the outbreak of war between Colombia and Peru drew him back into public service. He accepted command in the south.

### Colombian–Peruvian War and Tarqui

Peruvian forces invaded southern Colombia—modern Ecuador. Sucre organized Colombian defenses and defeated the Peruvian army at the **Battle of Tarqui**, 27 February 1829.

He negotiated the Convention of Girón. The aftermath became contentious:

- Colombian accounts emphasized a decisive victory against a larger force.
- Peruvian writers challenged Colombian casualty claims and accused Sucre of humiliating or triumphalist inscriptions.
- The settlement did not resolve all border questions.
- Renewed tensions led to further negotiation, including the Guayaquil treaty of September 1829.

Tarqui restored Sucre’s military prestige but further deepened hostility among some Peruvian and anti-Bolivarian circles.

---

## Final Political Mission, 1829–1830

Sucre returned to Quito and family life, but the Republic of Colombia was breaking apart.

He was elected a delegate for Cumaná to the **Admirable Congress**, which convened in Bogotá in January 1830 in a final attempt to preserve constitutional union.

Sucre became president of the congress. He favored a constitutional compromise and continued union rather than permanent military dictatorship.

The congress sent him with other commissioners to negotiate with Venezuelan representatives after José Antonio Páez’s separatist movement rejected Bolívar’s authority. The negotiations failed: Venezuelan representatives refused to treat with delegates lacking recognition of Venezuela’s separation.

Sucre returned to Bogotá. Bolívar resigned. On 8 May 1830 Sucre wrote his famous final letter to Bolívar, stressing that personal friendship rather than Bolívar’s power had inspired his affection.

Sucre then traveled south toward Quito, intending to reunite with Mariana and Teresa.

## Assassination at Berruecos, 4 June 1830

### Established facts

Sucre left Bogotá and traveled through Popayán toward Pasto with a small party. On the morning of 4 June, in the wooded Berruecos region, concealed gunmen fired on him. He fell from his mount and died at the scene.

His companions fled or continued before the body was recovered. A local man, often identified as a servant or resident, helped bury or guard the remains until transfer could be arranged.

A hostile Bogotá newspaper, **El Demócrata**, had printed a warning or insinuation shortly before the killing, portraying Sucre as advancing to attack Pasto and Obando as moving to resist him. The item became evidence in later claims that the murder had been politically prepared.

### Material perpetrators

The later proceedings implicated:

- **Apolinar Morillo**, a Venezuelan or Colombian officer;
- **José Erazo**, a guerrilla, local operative, or bandit;
- **Juan Gregorio Sarria**;
- Andrés Rodríguez;
- José Cruz;
- José Gregorio Rodríguez;
- other local accomplices named inconsistently across proceedings.

Erazo was arrested approximately a decade later and made incriminating statements. Morillo was tried and sentenced to death in 1842; he accused José María Obando before execution. The process occurred amid intense civil conflict and partisan pressure.

### José María Obando theory

**Allegation:** General **José María Obando**, military commander in the Cauca/Pasto region and opponent of Bolívarian centralism, ordered the assassination through Morillo and Erazo.

**Evidence cited for it:**

- testimony by Erazo and others;
- Morillo’s statements;
- alleged written or verbal orders;
- Sucre’s route passed through Obando’s sphere of command;
- Obando’s political hostility to Bolívarian power;
- the anticipatory newspaper attack.

**Evidence against or limiting it:**

- witnesses were interrogated years later under coercive and partisan conditions;
- Morillo alleged that investigators pressured him to accuse Obando in exchange for clemency;
- relevant documents were missing, destroyed, or contested;
- Obando denied involvement and published or inspired defenses;
- later governments and historians used the charge in partisan struggles.

**Status:** no reliable modern consensus converts the case into an uncontested judicial fact. Obando was the most persistently accused intellectual author, but the evidentiary record is compromised.

### Juan José Flores theory

**Allegation:** General **Juan José Flores**, who became Ecuador’s first president, feared Sucre as a more prestigious rival and either commissioned or knowingly facilitated the assassination.

**Arguments advanced:**

- Flores benefited politically from Sucre’s removal;
- he had contacts in both Quito and the southern Colombian military world;
- some suspects or intermediaries were linked to his circle;
- later testimony attributed the order to Flores.

**Counterpoints:**

- political benefit is not proof of prior direction;
- Flores was Sucre’s associate and godfather to Teresa;
- no conclusive order from Flores has been authenticated;
- the accusation was circulated by Obando and Flores’s political enemies.

**Status:** unresolved allegation.

### Mariana Carcelén–Isidoro Barriga theory

**Allegation:** Mariana and Sucre’s subordinate **Isidoro Barriga** were lovers and arranged the assassination so they could marry and control the Carcelén fortune.

This theory was circulated by Obando and repeated in later scandal literature. Morillo was also reported to have alleged a connection to Flores and “the wife of the marshal.”

**Evidence against:**

- Mariana wrote a furious letter accusing Obando after Sucre’s death;
- no surviving contemporary judicial proof establishes her participation;
- accusations of adultery were shaped by the rapidity of her later remarriage and nineteenth-century gender expectations;
- Barriga and Mariana’s eventual marriage proves a later relationship, not complicity in Sucre’s murder.

**Status:** allegation and hostile mythology, not established fact.

### Santanderist or wider anti-Bolivarian conspiracy

Later conspiratorial narratives attribute the assassination to Santander’s political followers, a coordinated anti-Bolivarian network, Peruvian interests, or an alliance of Obando, José Hilario López, Flores, and other commanders.

The political logic is intelligible: Sucre was a plausible successor to Bolívar and a symbol of Colombian unity. But no consulted primary document demonstrates a centralized conspiracy extending to Santander or all named beneficiaries.

**Status:** theory without conclusive documentary proof.

### Who benefited?

Sucre’s death removed:

- the most prestigious young Bolivarian general;
- a possible constitutional successor to Bolívar;
- a rival to Flores in Ecuador;
- a potential military obstacle to southern separatists;
- a symbol of Colombian unity.

This breadth of benefit helps explain why multiple conspiracy theories remain plausible but also makes beneficiary-based reasoning unreliable as proof.

## Burial, Removal, and Identification of the Remains

Sucre’s body was initially buried near the place of death. It was later transported secretly toward Quito, reportedly through arrangements connected to Mariana’s properties.

Accounts describe concealment at the El Deán estate and eventual burial in the Carmelite church known as Carmen Bajo. A false report that he had been buried at San Francisco may have been spread to protect the grave.

Venezuela and Bolivia later sought the remains. Venezuelan commissions searched San Francisco in 1876 and 1894 without success. Remains identified as Sucre’s were found in Quito in 1900 and transferred with ceremony to the Metropolitan Cathedral.

### Dispute over authenticity

- **Official Ecuadorian position:** the remains in Quito Cathedral are Sucre’s.
- **Alternative theory:** Lucila Luciani and others argued that the recovered skeleton could not be securely authenticated and that Sucre might still lie in Colombia.
- **Evidence problem:** repeated secret transfers, misleading burial stories, incomplete chain of custody, and the limitations of pre-DNA identification leave room for doubt.
- **Current status:** official identification is accepted by the relevant states and commemorative institutions, but the chain of custody is not beyond historical challenge.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

Sucre did not operate companies, issue patents, or build a private commercial empire.

### Institutions associated with him

- Venezuelan republican militias and armies.
- Army of the East.
- Colombian Army and its General Staff.
- Army of the South.
- United Liberating Army of Peru.
- Government and Congress of Bolivia.
- Admirable Congress of Colombia.
- Schools, colleges, military institutions, and public-benefit bodies created or reorganized under the Bolivian presidency.

### Technologies and technical expertise

His engineering education informed:

- fortification;
- artillery use;
- surveying;
- route and terrain analysis;
- bridge, road, and logistical planning;
- formal staff systems.

No invention or patent is attributable to him.

### Property and finances

Sucre came from a property-owning family whose holdings were damaged during the Venezuelan wars. He received military salary, honors, and awards but repeatedly complained about costs, arrears, and family responsibilities.

His marriage placed him in a wealthy Ecuadorian landholding network. Properties associated with Mariana included Chisinche and El Deán and urban holdings in Quito. These were primarily her inherited assets, not enterprises created by Sucre.

Documents referenced in later archival publications include Sucre’s assignment of a third of his inheritance to siblings. His testamentary arrangements became important after his assassination and Teresa’s early death.

There is no credible evidence found of fraud, embezzlement, secret corporate wealth, money laundering, or illicit commercial structures.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Republicanism

Sucre’s republicanism was institutional but not modern-democratic. He accepted restricted suffrage, strong executive government, military emergency powers, and Bolívar’s highly centralized constitutional project.

He nevertheless repeatedly maintained that military victory did not authorize permanent personal tyranny and voluntarily left Bolivia rather than attempt to hold office through a domestic reign of terror.

### Colombian unionism

He supported the political union of Venezuela, New Granada, Quito, and Panama in Colombia. By 1830 he still sought constitutional compromise, even as Venezuela and Ecuador moved toward separation.

### Bolivarian loyalty

Sucre’s relationship with Bolívar combined subordination, friendship, mentorship, and occasional disagreement. Bolívar trusted his competence and relative lack of personal faction. Sucre’s final letter explicitly distinguished affection for Bolívar from attachment to Bolívar’s power.

### Social reform

His Bolivian program combined Enlightenment administrative reform with coercive state-building:

- expansion of education;
- reorganization of the Church;
- attempted fiscal equality;
- reform of communal landholding;
- gradual emancipation principles;
- centralization of state accounts and offices.

Its limitations were substantial: Indigenous tribute returned, local participation was narrow, communal institutions were disrupted, and public projects relied partly on appropriated corporate property.

### Warfare and humanitarian restraint

The Treaty for Regularization of War and the generous Ayacucho capitulation support Sucre’s reputation for restraint toward defeated regular armies.

Pasto demonstrates that this restraint was not consistently extended to a rebellious civilian population or irregular opponents. Both elements belong in the record.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Simón Bolívar

Commander, mentor, political patron, collaborator, and close friend. Their relationship began during the Venezuelan wars and became central after 1817. Bolívar entrusted Sucre with negotiation at Trujillo, the southern campaigns, Peru, and Bolivia.

### Santiago Mariño

Early commander and patron in eastern Venezuela. Sucre learned staff warfare within Mariño’s army before attaching himself to Bolívar’s centralized command.

### José Mires

Engineering teacher in Caracas and later subordinate or collaborator in the Ecuadorian campaign.

### Manuel Piar

Senior commander during the eastern and Guayana campaigns. Sucre later remained with Bolívar after Piar’s fall.

### José Francisco Bermúdez

Eastern Venezuelan commander and family-war associate. Their relationship formed part of the contentious eastern military network.

### José María Córdova

Principal divisional commander at Ayacucho. Córdova’s charge was decisive. Later legend often merges Sucre’s overall plan and Córdova’s battlefield action into a single heroic narrative.

### Andrés de Santa Cruz

Peruvian and later Bolivian commander and statesman. Collaborator in Upper Peru, later an independent center of Bolivian power.

### Agustín Gamarra

Peruvian rival and invader of Bolivia in 1828. His intervention helped terminate Sucre’s government.

### José de La Mar

Peruvian general at Ayacucho and later president of Peru; political and military opponent in the 1828–1829 war.

### José María Obando

Southern Colombian commander, political opponent of Bolivarian centralism, and principal alleged intellectual author of Sucre’s murder. The accusation remains historically disputed.

### Juan José Flores

Former military associate, godfather to Teresa, leading figure in Ecuador, and alleged beneficiary or planner of the assassination. No conclusive proof of complicity has been established.

### Mariana Carcelén

Wife, intended partner in retirement, and administrator of the family and estate world Sucre hoped to join. Later made a target of adultery and murder allegations.

### Vicente Aguirre

Trusted friend and correspondent; represented Sucre at his proxy marriage and handled sensitive family matters.

### Simón Rodríguez

Educational reformer appointed in Bolivia. Their collaboration produced ambitious but short-lived institutional experiments and ended amid administrative conflict.

## Controversies, Allegations, Political Violence, and Financial Questions

### Pasto massacre

**Status:** documented mass violence under Sucre’s command; disputed exact orders and death toll.

The event is the strongest documented negative episode in his career. It cannot be reduced either to fabricated royalist propaganda or to a proven personal order to kill every inhabitant.

### Foreign occupation of Bolivia

**Status:** documented political controversy.

Sucre governed with Colombian troops and under Bolívarian strategic influence. Critics reasonably described the arrangement as foreign military tutelage. This does not prove Bolivia lacked local independence advocates.

### Indigenous tribute

**Status:** documented policy reversal.

The government’s original abolition failed and the contribution was restored. Heroic claims of permanent fiscal emancipation omit this reversal.

### Church property

**Status:** documented state seizure and institutional suppression.

Monasteries were consolidated or closed and assets redirected. Whether described as modernization or confiscation depends on perspective; the acts themselves are documented.

### Authoritarian constitution

**Status:** documented constitutional structure.

Sucre administered Bolívar’s life-presidency constitution. He did not originate all its provisions, and he resigned rather than perpetuate himself, but he cannot be separated from its implementation.

### Ayacucho capitulation

**Status:** documented policy dispute.

The settlement was criticized as overly generous and expensive. No evidence indicates bribery or personal enrichment.

### Tarqui narrative

**Status:** disputed national historiography.

Colombian/Ecuadorian and Peruvian sources disagree over the scale and decisiveness of the victory and the propriety of triumphal claims. Sucre won the principal engagement and negotiated afterward, but patriotic casualty figures require caution.

### Extramarital relationships and children

**Status:** partly documented, partly reported.

The acknowledgment or support of Simona is grounded in correspondence. José María and Pedro César are accepted in later family literature but deserve verification from church registers. These relationships conflict with later celibate or saint-like portrayals but do not constitute a public corruption case.

### Freemasonry

**Status:** unverified claim.

Masonic writers have retrospectively enrolled Sucre among prominent Masons, and institutions or lodges have used his name. The consulted literature expressly notes the absence of decisive initiation or membership records. Association with Masonic officers and Enlightenment republicanism is not proof of lodge membership.

### Fraud, corruption, or private enrichment

No substantiated case was located. Sucre’s administration faced fiscal disorder and contentious property transfers, but no reliable evidence establishes personal embezzlement, fraud, money laundering, or corrupt business structures.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Abel of America”

**Story:** Sucre was the innocent, virtuous Abel murdered by the political Cains of republican fragmentation.

**Origin and circulation:** Bolívarian mourning language, nineteenth-century patriotic histories, schoolbooks, monuments, and state commemoration.

**Evidence:** Sucre was murdered and was less visibly factional than many contemporaries.

**Limitation:** the biblical image suppresses his exercise of coercive military and executive power, especially at Pasto and in Bolivia.

**Effect:** it made assassination, not administrative controversy, the organizing fact of his public memory.

### Bolívar’s perfect successor

**Story:** Bolívar had definitively selected Sucre to inherit continental leadership, so conspirators killed him to destroy the union.

**Evidence:** Bolívar admired Sucre and he possessed unmatched prestige. His age, victories, and presidency made him a plausible successor.

**Against:** Colombia’s constitution imposed age and political constraints; regional leaders controlled armed constituencies; Sucre repeatedly sought retirement. No binding succession designation existed.

**Status:** plausible counterfactual elevated into myth.

### The flawless humanitarian commander

**Story:** author of humane warfare at Trujillo, merciful victor at Ayacucho, incapable of cruelty.

**Evidence:** his treaty work and treatment of defeated regulars.

**Against:** mass violence at Pasto occurred under his command.

**Status:** hagiographic simplification.

### The butcher of Pasto

**Story:** Sucre personally ordered an extermination of Pasto’s population.

**Evidence:** he commanded the operation; troops killed civilians and sacked the city.

**Against:** exact orders, casualty totals, and his attempts or failures to restore discipline are disputed.

**Status:** based on a real atrocity but often expanded beyond demonstrable evidence.

### Sole creator or “father” of Bolivia

**Story:** Sucre created Bolivia through individual vision.

**Evidence:** his decree enabled the assembly and his army controlled the territory.

**Against:** Upper Peruvian political actors drove the separatist movement; Bolívar, Peru, Buenos Aires, local assemblies, and military facts all shaped the outcome.

**Status:** national heroic compression.

### Foreign destroyer who amputated Peru

**Story:** Sucre conspiratorially fragmented Peru to build a Bolivarian satellite.

**Evidence:** he advanced separate Upper Peruvian statehood and governed with Colombian troops.

**Against:** Upper Peru had distinct institutions and local separatist advocates; Buenos Aires renounced claims; political options were genuinely contested.

**Status:** polemical national interpretation, not a proven secret conspiracy.

### Mariana and Barriga murdered him

**Story:** the wife and lover arranged Sucre’s death for marriage and inheritance.

**Appearance:** circulated in accusations associated with Obando and in later scandal narratives.

**Evidence:** Mariana later married Barriga; Barriga benefited socially; Morillo was reported to have named them or their allies.

**Against:** no conclusive judicial or documentary evidence; Mariana accused Obando; remarriage does not prove prior murder.

**Effect:** transformed a geopolitical assassination into domestic melodrama and subjected Mariana’s conduct to lasting suspicion.

### Barriga killed Teresa

**Story:** Barriga threw Sucre’s daughter from a balcony or upper story to control the inheritance.

**Evidence:** motive inferred from inheritance; persistent family lore.

**Against:** illness is the competing reported cause; no established prosecution or finding.

**Status:** rumor or myth.

### Obando’s proven guilt

**Story:** Morillo’s conviction and confession conclusively proved Obando ordered the murder.

**Evidence:** trial testimony, alleged orders, repeated accusations.

**Against:** coercion allegations, partisan judicial setting, delayed testimony, missing records, and Obando’s defense.

**Status:** formally alleged and historically plausible, but not an uncontested finding against Obando.

### Flores’s proven guilt

**Story:** Flores eliminated Sucre to become ruler of Ecuador.

**Evidence:** motive, opportunity through networks, subsequent benefit.

**Against:** absence of authenticated command; former comradeship and family-godparent tie.

**Status:** unresolved allegation.

### Confirmed Freemason

**Story:** Sucre belonged to revolutionary Masonic lodges.

**Evidence:** association, symbolism, retrospective Masonic lists.

**Against:** lack of definitive lodge documentation.

**Status:** unverified.

### Authenticity of the Quito remains

**Story:** the remains in Quito Cathedral are definitively Sucre’s—or, conversely, are certainly those of someone else.

**Evidence for official identification:** recovery in a place connected to the secret-burial tradition and state investigation.

**Against:** broken custody and earlier false burial sites.

**Status:** officially accepted identification with unresolved historical doubts.

## Health, Injuries, and Psychological Strain

Sucre survived repeated campaigns, tropical disease environments, hunger, exhaustion, and battlefield danger. The best-documented lasting injury was the gunshot wound sustained during the Chuquisaca mutiny of 18 April 1828, which fractured or badly damaged his right arm.

His correspondence repeatedly expresses fatigue, disgust with political command, desire for private life, and emotional strain. These statements support political exhaustion but do not establish a diagnosable psychiatric illness.

No reliable evidence was found of addiction, chronic alcoholism, or substance dependence.

## Works by Antonio José de Sucre

Sucre was not primarily a book author. His authentic corpus consists of letters, military reports, proclamations, decrees, treaties, official messages, administrative memoranda, and testamentary papers. Most “books by Sucre” are posthumous edited collections.

### Authentic contemporary documents

- **Treaty of Armistice**, Trujillo, 25 November 1820. Negotiated and signed by representatives of Colombia and Spain; Sucre was a principal Colombian negotiator, not sole author.
- **Tratado de Regularización de la Guerra**, Trujillo, 26 November 1820. Joint diplomatic text with major Sucre participation.
- Communications and agreements with the Free Province of Guayaquil, 1821.
- Military reports concerning Yaguachi, Huachi, Cuenca, Pichincha, and Pasto, 1821–1823.
- Correspondence from Peru, 1823–1824.
- Military orders and reports for the Ayacucho campaign, 1824.
- **Capitulación de Ayacucho**, 9 December 1824. Signed by Sucre and José de Canterac; jointly negotiated official instrument.

- **Mensaje de despedida del general Sucre al Congreso de Bolivia**, 2 August 1828.
- Military documents of the Colombian–Peruvian war and Tarqui campaign, 1828–1829.
- Speeches and papers of the Admirable Congress, 1830.
- Correspondence with Bolívar, Mariana Carcelén, Vicente Aguirre, Santander, military officers, and civil officials.
- Testamentary and inheritance papers.

- Aphorism collections isolate sentences from letters and official papers; they are not independent works.

- Titles such as “memoirs” occasionally applied to Sucre compilations do not mean that he wrote a continuous autobiography.

## Death, Estate, and Immediate Aftermath

Mariana learned of the assassination after a delay caused by distance and communications. She wrote to Obando accusing him of responsibility.

Bolívar learned of Sucre’s death while on the Caribbean coast. His reaction became a central text of the Sucre cult: he treated the murder as proof that Colombia had become unlivable for its liberators.

Mariana observed the conventional mourning period and married Isidoro Barriga in 1831. The timing generated accusations of “moral adultery” and later murder theories but was not proof of criminal conduct.

Teresa’s death extinguished Sucre’s legitimate direct line. Estate questions then became entangled with Mariana’s second marriage and descendants.

The assassination accelerated the symbolic collapse of Colombia. Venezuela and Ecuador separated in 1830; Bolívar died on 17 December of the same year.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### State cults and national appropriation

Five countries claim major parts of Sucre’s historical identity:

- **Venezuela:** birthplace, family sacrifice, republican officer.
- **Colombia:** general of the Colombian army, congressional leader, victim of the union’s collapse.
- **Ecuador:** liberator at Pichincha, defender at Tarqui, husband and resident-in-intention of Quito.
- **Peru:** commander at Ayacucho.
- **Bolivia:** convener of the independence assembly and formative president.

The result is not one cult but several overlapping national Sucre traditions.

### Place names and material commemoration

His name appears in:

- Sucre, constitutional capital of Bolivia;
- Chuquisaca Department’s capital and institutions;
- Sucre State, Venezuela;
- municipalities and parishes in several countries;
- airports, squares, military academies, schools, streets, and hospitals;
- orders and decorations;
- statues in Cumaná, Quito, Ayacucho, Bogotá, and Bolivian cities;
- historical currencies and banknotes.

### Military reputation

Ayacucho remains his central international achievement. Military histories credit:

- disciplined staff organization;
- maneuver in difficult terrain;
- effective use of divisional commanders;
- ability to hold multinational forces together;
- restrained settlements after conventional victory.

Pasto qualifies any undifferentiated reputation for humanitarian command.

### Political reputation

Sucre’s voluntary departure from Bolivia and his distaste for ruling by terror support an image of unusual restraint among independence-era generals. At the same time, his administration participated in centralized government, foreign military occupation, coerced taxation, and ecclesiastical property transfer.

### Modern reinterpretation

Recent scholarship has shifted attention:

- from “liberation” alone to the experiences of royalist, Indigenous, and local communities;
- from abolitionary proclamations to failed implementation;
- from heroic founders to the institutional weaknesses of early republics;
- from a solved Obando murder case to a compromised evidentiary record;
- from a monogamous saintly image to a more complex family history;
- from unquestioned remains to problems of provenance and identification.

The bicentenaries of Pichincha in 2022, Ayacucho in 2024, and Bolivian independence in 2025 renewed both commemoration and criticism. The adversarial search found no credible newly discovered criminal or financial case from 2024–2026, but it did find renewed public argument over assassination responsibility, Pasto, the creation of Bolivia, and the domestic-conspiracy legends.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted record are included. Mere contemporaneity has been excluded.

### Antonio José de Sucre

**Documented fact:** subject of this dossier and member of the atlas roster.

### Simón Bolívar

**Documented fact.** Commander, political patron, mentor, collaborator, and intimate friend, approximately 1817–1830. Bolívar assigned Sucre the Trujillo negotiations, Army of the South, Peruvian command, and Upper Peruvian responsibilities. They corresponded extensively. Sucre’s final surviving letter to Bolívar is dated Bogotá, 8 May 1830.

### Francisco de Miranda

**Documented institutional connection.** Sucre served in the forces of the First Venezuelan Republic during Miranda’s general command in 1811–1812. A close personal mentor-student relationship is not documented.

### José de San Martín

**Documented indirect political-military connection; personal meeting unverified.** San Martín’s Peruvian and auxiliary military project supplied forces later operating with Sucre. Bolívar’s secretary sent Sucre a report of the July 1822 Guayaquil Conference. The sources consulted do not establish a significant face-to-face relationship between Sucre and San Martín.

### Bernardo O’Higgins Riquelme

**Documented institutional connection only.** Chilean forces produced by the O’Higgins–San Martín independence coalition formed part of the wider military apparatus whose officers and soldiers participated in Peru and the Ecuadorian campaign. No direct meeting or correspondence with Sucre was established.

### Manuela Sáenz de Vergara y Aizpuru

**Reported/documented correspondence connection, with disputed battlefield details.** Sáenz traveled within the Bolívarian military network. A letter attributed to Sucre praised her conduct during the Ayacucho campaign and recommended promotion. Her exact battlefield role and the provenance of some later-published documents remain disputed.

### Simón Rodríguez

**Documented fact.** Sucre’s Bolivian government appointed Rodríguez to direct public education and responsibilities involving agriculture, mines, and roads in 1825–1826. They collaborated and corresponded, but the educational experiment collapsed amid administrative and political conflict.

### Juana Azurduy de Padilla

**Reported fact supported by historical studies.** Bolívar, accompanied by Sucre and other officials, visited or formally honored Azurduy in Chuquisaca in 1825–1826. Sucre’s administration granted or increased a pension in recognition of her independence service. Details of the encounter are partly shaped by later patriotic narration.

### Juan Martín de Pueyrredón y O’Doggan

**Documented institutional connection only.** Pueyrredón’s Río de la Plata government had recognized Azurduy and supported the earlier southern independence effort. Upper Peru’s legal relationship to the Río de la Plata formed part of the diplomatic setting for Sucre’s 1825 assembly. No direct Sucre–Pueyrredón meeting was established.

### Martín Miguel de Güemes

**Documented institutional connection only.** Güemes’s northern war helped sustain resistance in the Upper Peruvian frontier before Sucre’s arrival. Juana Azurduy served under Güemes before later receiving recognition under Sucre. No direct personal relationship is documented; Güemes died in 1821.

### Napoleon Bonaparte

**Documented historical influence, not a personal connection.** Napoleon’s invasion of Spain and overthrow of the Bourbon political order helped trigger the Spanish-American sovereignty crisis that formed the setting of Sucre’s career. No correspondence, meeting, or specifically acknowledged personal intellectual influence was found.

### Alexander von Humboldt

**No documented personal connection found.** Humboldt had visited Cumaná before Sucre’s birth and influenced later representations of Spanish America, but the consulted sources do not establish meeting, correspondence, or acknowledged influence.

### Abraham Lincoln and the remaining roster

**No connection found.** No documentary crossing with the remainder of the supplied atlas roster was identified in the sources consulted. Given the roster’s breadth, absence here means that no support was found in this research; it is not a claim that no undiscovered archival connection could exist.

## Chronology

|---|---|
| 3 Feb. 1795 | Born in Cumaná to Vicente de Sucre and María Manuela de Alcalá. |
| c. 1802 | Mother dies; exact date varies in secondary accounts. |
| 1808 | Sent to Caracas; studies military engineering under José Mires. |
| 19 Apr. 1810 | Caracas revolution begins the Venezuelan republican crisis. |
| 12 July 1810 | Appointed sublieutenant by the Cumaná junta. |
| 1811–1812 | Serves the First Republic in engineering duties. |
| July 1812 | First Republic collapses after Miranda’s capitulation. |
| 1813 | Joins Santiago Mariño’s eastern campaign. |
| 1814 | Serves through the collapse of the Second Republic; family suffers severe losses. |
| 1815–1816 | Period of Caribbean displacement and return to patriot service. |
| 1817 | Serves in Guayana; becomes integrated into Bolívar’s command after Piar’s fall. |
| 1818–1819 | Senior staff service; reaches general officer rank. |
| 25–26 Nov. 1820 | Helps negotiate the Trujillo Armistice and Treaty for Regularization of War. |
| May 1821 | Reaches agreement with Guayaquil authorities; organizes southern campaign. |
| 19 Aug. 1821 | Patriot victory at Yaguachi. |
| 12 Sept. 1821 | Defeated at Second Huachi. |
| 24 May 1822 | Commands victory at Pichincha; Quito capitulates. |
| 24 Dec. 1822 | Takes Pasto; republican troops commit the *Navidad Negra* massacre and sack. |
| 1823 | Sent into the Peruvian political-military crisis. |
| 6 Aug. 1824 | Junín victory under Bolívar’s overall command. |
| 9 Dec. 1824 | Sucre commands at Ayacucho and signs the capitulation. |
| 10 Feb. 1825 | Receives or is confirmed in the title Grand Marshal of Ayacucho. |
| 9 Feb. 1825 | Issues decree convening the Upper Peruvian assembly. |
| 1–3 Apr. 1825 | Olañeta is defeated and dies; organized Upper Peruvian royalism ends. |
| 6 Aug. 1825 | Upper Peru declares independence as Bolivia. |
| Late 1825 | Sucre assumes practical executive government in Bolivia. |
| 29 Mar. 1826 | Major decree reorganizes monasteries and ecclesiastical property. |
| 1826 | Becomes constitutional president under the Bolivarian Constitution. |
| 1826–1827 | Fiscal, educational, military, land, and Church reforms; Indigenous contribution restored after attempted abolition. |
| 18 Apr. 1828 | Wounded in the Chuquisaca military mutiny. |
| 20 Apr. 1828 | Marries Mariana Carcelén by proxy. |
| May–July 1828 | Gamarra intervenes; Treaty of Piquiza ends Colombian military predominance. |
| 2 Aug. 1828 | Delivers farewell message and leaves the Bolivian presidency. |
| 28 Sept. 1828 | Reported first meeting with Mariana as husband and wife at Chisinche. |
| 27 Feb. 1829 | Commands Colombian victory at Tarqui. |
| 10 June 1829 | Birth of legitimate daughter Teresa in Quito. |
| Jan.–May 1830 | Represents Cumaná and presides over the Admirable Congress in Bogotá. |
| 8 May 1830 | Writes final celebrated letter to Bolívar. |
| 4 June 1830 | Assassinated at Berruecos while traveling to Quito. |
| 1831 | Mariana marries Isidoro Barriga; Teresa dies in childhood, exact date and cause disputed. |
| 1840–1842 | Erazo and Morillo proceedings revive the murder case; Morillo is executed after accusing Obando. |
| 1876, 1894 | Venezuelan commissions unsuccessfully search Quito’s San Francisco church for the remains. |
| 1900 | Remains officially identified as Sucre’s are recovered in Quito and transferred to the cathedral. |
| 1995 | Bicentennial commemorations and major documentary/bibliographical publications. |
| 2022–2025 | Bicentenaries renew debate over Pichincha, Pasto, Ayacucho, and Bolivia’s creation. |
