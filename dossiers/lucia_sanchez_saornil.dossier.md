# Lucía Sánchez Saornil: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

Lucía Sánchez Saornil left no known autobiography, comprehensive diary, or deposited personal archive sufficient to reconstruct every period of her life. Her public activity is documented through literary and anarchist periodicals, CNT and Mujeres Libres publications, organizational records, photographs, correspondence involving Solidaridad Internacional Antifascista, municipal registers, her Telefónica employment file, French administrative records cited by later researchers, and testimony collected from relatives and former militants.

The fullest modern reconstruction is Ignacio C. Soriano Jiménez’s *Lucía Sánchez Saornil, entre mujeres anarquistas* (2022), supplemented by the earlier biography of Antonia Fontanillas Borràs and Pau Martínez Muñoz, literary scholarship, and contemporary archival work. Some widely repeated biographical details predate those investigations and are demonstrably uncertain or wrong. This dossier distinguishes documentary facts from later interpretation.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Lucía Sánchez Saornil.
- **Principal literary pseudonym:** **Luciano de San-Saor**, formed by masculinizing *Lucía* and combining or transforming her surnames.
- **Other reported signatures or pseudonyms:** **Vigía**, **El Observador**, **Un Confederado**, **La Compañera X**, **La Compañera**, and **Otra compañera**. Anonymous journalism makes a complete attribution impossible.
- **Birth:** 13 December 1895, Madrid, Spain.
- **Death:** 2 June 1970, Valencia, Spain.
- **Cause of death:** Most detailed recent accounts identify breast cancer; one secondary publisher’s page says lung cancer. The breast-cancer account is better supported in the modern biographical literature, but the medical record has not been published.
- **Burial:** Cementerio General de Valencia.
- **Nationality:** Spanish.
- **Occupations:** Telephone operator; poet; short-story writer; journalist; editor; war correspondent; propagandist; lecturer; anarcho-syndicalist organizer; humanitarian administrator; commercial representative; painter and decorative artist.
- **Political affiliations:** Briefly associated with José Antonio Balbontín’s Partido Radical Socialista Revolucionario; subsequently the Sindicato Nacional de Teléfonos and Confederación Nacional del Trabajo (CNT); co-founder and national secretary of Mujeres Libres; press-and-propaganda secretary and later general secretary of Solidaridad Internacional Antifascista (SIA).
- **Known partner:** América Barroso García, commonly called **Mery** and sometimes identified in sources as América Barroso Mery (1908–1977).
- **Children:** None documented.
- **Marriage:** None documented.
- **Religion:** No personal religious adherence is documented in adulthood. Her mature politics were anti-clerical and anarchist, opposing the institutional power of church, state, capitalism, and the patriarchal family.
- **Sexual identity:** She is now commonly described as lesbian. The long domestic partnership with América Barroso and the female-directed eroticism in poems signed “Luciano” are documented; no surviving declaration in which Sánchez Saornil labels herself lesbian has been found.

The Biblioteca Nacional de España gives the established dates and places and summarizes her literary and political career. The most detailed recent literary chronology is maintained by the [Biblioteca Virtual Miguel de Cervantes](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/portales/lucia_sanchez_saornil/semblanza/).

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and origins

Lucía was born into a working-class Madrid family. Her parents were:

- **Eugenio Sánchez Rodríguez**, born into a family associated with Pozal de Gallinas, Valladolid.
- **Gabriela Saornil Serrano**, likewise from Pozal de Gallinas.

Madrid municipal-register research cited in recent biographies identifies the family’s first known residence as Calle del Labrador in the Peñuelas district. They subsequently lived on or near Calle Hortaleza, Pedro Unanue, Calvo Asensio, and Paseo de Santa María de la Cabeza. The latter remained Lucía’s Madrid base into the 1930s.

Eugenio is described in municipal records as a **jornalero**, or day laborer. Between approximately 1915 and 1922 he was employed by the Compañía Madrileña de Teléfonos. The recurrent story that he operated the private switchboard in the Duke of Alba’s household may preserve a real employment connection, but recent researchers have not located evidence establishing his exact duties. He later belonged to the UGT-affiliated building workers’ society El Progreso.

### Siblings and childhood losses

Known siblings included:

- **José**, an elder brother, who died in 1900.
- **Concepción or Concha Sánchez Saornil**, born in 1897, who lived until early 1970.
- **Eleuteria**, born around 1900 and dead in 1901.

Gabriela died in 1908, when Lucía was twelve. Lucía thereafter assumed a substantial share of domestic work and care for her chronically ill sister Concha. Rafael Cansinos Assens later described Lucía as the eldest surviving child who supported a household of younger children through her telephone work, calling her a constantly occupied “madrecita.” His numerical picture of a “caterva” of children appears exaggerated in light of recovered civil records, but the underlying burden of wage work and care is supported.

Concha suffered a chronic intestinal or digestive illness and remained part of Lucía’s household through much of her life. Her death, approximately four months before Lucía’s, was a major final bereavement.

A family library supposedly inherited from a paternal aunt appears in biographical tradition. The evidence may derive partly from Lucía’s 1914 story “El breviario de tía Isabel”; it cannot safely be treated as literal autobiography.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1895–1913

Lucía grew up in the working-class south of Madrid amid high childhood mortality, financial insecurity, and repeated changes of address. Her later poem “Calle de la infancia” recalled the urban environment and her dead mother.

No primary-school enrollment record has yet established exactly where she received elementary education. By 1913 she was attending the **Centro de Hijos de Madrid**, popularly called the Casa de los Gatos. This charitable and recreational institution offered classes, a library, newspapers, lectures, and artistic instruction. She may have studied drawing, painting, and other subjects there, but no formal credential is known.

Numerous biographies say that she studied at the Real Academia de Bellas Artes de San Fernando. More cautious recent research finds no enrollment evidence. She may have attended open instruction connected with the Escuela de Pintura de San Fernando, or the claim may have grown from her documented artistic training elsewhere. It should therefore be labeled **reported but unverified**, not an established academy degree.

Her education was substantially autodidactic. Literary journals, institutional libraries, drawing instruction, and her own reading mattered more than formal qualifications.

## Education and Formation

### Artistic formation

Painting and drawing preceded or accompanied her literary activity. She made portraits, later painted copies of existing pictures, decorated fans, and accepted commercial art work. No securely catalogued early corpus survives, and she never achieved the institutional standing of a professional gallery painter.

### Literary influences

Her first poems reflect late Spanish Modernismo:

- musical and regular verse;
- gardens, twilight, fountains, autumn, moonlight, flowers, and melancholy;
- the influence of Juan Ramón Jiménez;
- erotic and sentimental symbolism.

From 1918–1919 she moved toward Ultraísmo, adopting free or irregular verse, typographic experiment, compressed metaphor, and modern imagery: aircraft, factories, electric light, cinemas, gramophones, telephone and telegraph cables, jazz, and urban streets.

Important literary figures in the surrounding network included Rafael Cansinos Assens, Guillermo de Torre, Adriano del Valle, Isaac del Vando Villar, César A. Comet, and the Borges siblings. Her inability or unwillingness to attend the male-dominated café circuit regularly distinguished her position from that of many male Ultraists.

### Political formation

Working conditions at the telephone company, women’s economic dependence, the repression of organized labor, and the limitations of supposedly egalitarian male militants were formative. By the early 1930s she had moved from avant-garde experimentation to revolutionary journalism.

She came to argue that:

1. women’s subordination was not merely an automatic by-product of capitalism;
2. male domination persisted inside revolutionary organizations and homes;
3. women therefore required autonomous organization and education;
4. emancipation had to be achieved by women themselves, not conferred by male leaders;
5. motherhood was one possible life choice, not woman’s defining destiny;
6. legal marriage could become an institution of dependence;
7. sexual and domestic relations belonged within the anarchist critique of authority.

## Early Literary Career, 1913–1930

### First prose and poetry

Her earliest located prose dates from 1913. In January 1914 the Ciudad Rodrigo weekly *Avante* published “Nieve,” her first known poem. She continued contributing to *Avante* into 1915 and then appeared in *Cádiz-San Fernando*, *Los Noveles*, *El Progreso* of Lugo, and other provincial publications.

From late 1916 she became a regular contributor to the Madrid magazine *Los Quijotes*, often as **Luciano de San-Saor**. Her poem “Madrigal de ausencia” appeared there on 10 December 1916. Other early pieces included “Crepúsculo sensual,” “Motivos galantes,” “Motivos triunfales,” “Tigresa,” “Autorretrato,” and “Madre primavera.”

### The pseudonym

Three explanations recur:

- **Publishing strategy:** a masculine name improved her chances of being read seriously.
- **Literary persona:** “Luciano” was a formal or aesthetic mask rather than an attempt to deceive.
- **Sexual camouflage:** a nominally male speaker could address a female beloved without openly presenting female-female desire.

The evidence supports elements of all three but proves none as her exclusive motive. Cansinos Assens and Guillermo de Torre knew Luciano was Lucía, and some works appeared under both names. The pseudonym was therefore not a successfully concealed legal identity. Nor was it limited to erotic poems. Recent scholarship consequently treats it as a flexible authorial strategy rather than a simple closet device.

### Ultraísmo

Her transitional “El madrigal de tus sortijas,” published in *Grecia* on 1 January 1919, represented marriage through constricting jewelry. “Balada de las viudas,” “Poema de la vida,” and “Poema de las vírgenes de treinta años” likewise moved social constraints on women into the foreground.

In June 1919 *Cervantes* included “Cuatro vientos” in a selection of “Los poetas del Ultra.” The poem turned Madrid’s military aerodrome, aircraft, and radiotelegraph tower into avant-garde imagery. Further work appeared in:

- *Cervantes*
- *Grecia*
- *Ultra*
- *Tableros*
- *Vértices*
- *Cosmópolis*
- *Plural*
- *Manantial*
- *La Gaceta Literaria*

Her Ultraism was comparatively restrained: less playful than that of some colleagues, often shadowed by labor and social concern. She was nevertheless recognized by male contemporaries as part of the movement. Guillermo de Torre profiled her in *Grecia* in 1920. Jorge Luis Borges later discussed her work in *Manantial*. Norah Borges dedicated her engraving “Maternidad” to Sánchez Saornil; Lucía answered with “Caminos de arco-iris,” “por una deuda contraída,” in *Ultra* on 1 March 1921. The documentary evidence for this artistic exchange is analyzed in [Carmen Medina Puerta’s study](https://repositori.udl.cat/bitstreams/d5aa578b-76c8-487a-a817-125bc2063555/download).

By the middle 1920s her literary output decreased. She later repudiated the social passivity of much avant-garde writing, accusing former colleagues of producing “Literatura, nada más.”

## Employment at the Telephone Company, 1916–1931 and 1936–1939

An extract from her employment file provides a firmer chronology than older memoirs:

- **1916:** Entered the Compañía Madrileña de Teléfonos as an operator.
- **1924:** The enterprise became part of the Compañía Telefónica Nacional de España.
- **March 1927:** Promoted to first-class operator at Madrid-Alcalá’s interurban center.
- **September 1927:** Transferred to Valencia “for service needs.”
- **29 January 1931:** Removed from the company payroll.
- **October 1936:** Readmitted during the Civil War.
- **Final termination:** Not established; some researchers suggest formal purge in May 1939.

Older accounts say her 1927 transfer was punishment for labor militancy. The surviving employment summary calls it a service transfer, so retaliatory motive remains **possible but unproven**.

Although no longer formally employed in July 1931, she played an active role in the national telephone strike, spoke at assemblies, and was briefly imprisoned. This is the one clearly documented imprisonment in Spain. It arose from labor conflict, not an ordinary criminal charge.

She joined the CNT-affiliated Sindicato Nacional de Teléfonos with membership number 26. The claim that she was “dismissed in 1931 for anarchism” is plausible shorthand but oversimplifies a poorly documented personnel decision.

---

## Political Development and Organizing, 1931–1935

In the opening phase of the Second Republic Sánchez Saornil briefly belonged to José Antonio Balbontín’s **Partido Radical Socialista Revolucionario**. She left it in 1932 as her commitment to anarcho-syndicalism deepened.

Between 1931 and 1936 she wrote for anarchist and labor publications including:

- *El Libertario*
- *Tierra y Libertad*
- *Solidaridad Obrera*
- *CNT*
- *Avance Marino*

She addressed meetings in Madrid, Cuenca, Manzanares, León, Burgos, La Horra, Talavera de la Reina, and other towns. During 1933–1934 she served on the editorial staff of the daily *CNT*, frequently described as secretary or chief of editorial staff. Attribution of unsigned pieces is incomplete.

### “La cuestión femenina en nuestros medios”

In autumn 1935 *Solidaridad Obrera* published her six-part intervention “La cuestión femenina en nuestros medios,” written partly in response to Mariano Rodríguez Vázquez, “Marianet.”

Her central dispute with male anarchists was documentary, not retrospective mythology. She maintained that theoretical declarations of equality had not transformed behavior:

- male militants expected service and obedience at home;
- women’s lack of education impeded political participation;
- economic emancipation alone would not erase male privilege;
- men could not act as tutors of women’s liberation;
- autonomous women’s organization was compatible with, and necessary to, libertarian revolution.

A modern edition collects these articles and related documents: [Editorial Eleuterio](https://eleuterio.noblogs.org/inicio/21-lacuestion/).

## Mujeres Libres, 1936–1939

### Foundation

In Madrid, Sánchez Saornil, lawyer and educator **Mercedes Comaposada Guillén**, and physician **Amparo Poch y Gascón** planned a women-produced periodical. The first issue of *Mujeres Libres* appeared in May 1936. The initial Madrid group later joined the Barcelona **Grupo Cultural Femenino**.

The military rebellion of July 1936 transformed the journal project into a mass wartime organization. Regional groups federated at a national conference in Valencia beginning on 20 August 1937. Sánchez Saornil served as national secretary or secretary of the national committee.

Membership was reported at more than 20,000 by 1938, but surviving wartime statistics are not sufficiently complete for an exact audited total.

### Program and institutions

Mujeres Libres described women’s predicament as a “triple slavery” of ignorance, womanhood, and wage labor. Its practical work included:

- literacy and general education;
- vocational and technical training;
- transport and industrial instruction;
- childcare and maternity services;
- nursing and health education;
- communal dining and wartime relief;
- political formation;
- sexual education;
- assistance to refugees and combatants;
- programs intended to offer women alternatives to prostitution.

The organization did **not normally call itself feminist**. Its members associated contemporary “feminism” with middle-class electoral and legal reform and called their own project libertarian and revolutionary. Later historians reasonably classify it as anarchist feminism or anarcofeminism, but that is a retrospective analytical label.

### Conflict within the libertarian movement

Mujeres Libres sought recognition as a fourth autonomous component of the organized libertarian movement beside the CNT, FAI, and Federación Ibérica de Juventudes Libertarias. At the October 1938 libertarian plenum, its delegation did not obtain that status. The question was deferred and never resolved before defeat.

This episode is a documented institutional rejection. Members interpreted it as evidence that male comrades remained unwilling to accept women’s autonomy.

The group also resisted absorption into the communist-influenced **Agrupación de Mujeres Antifascistas**, despite appeals associated with Dolores Ibárruri. Sánchez Saornil argued that the umbrella organization’s political character was insufficiently candid and that Mujeres Libres needed to preserve its own revolutionary identity.

## Civil War Activity, 1936–1939

At the beginning of the war Sánchez Saornil remained in Madrid. CNT memorial accounts say she participated in the revolutionary mobilization around the Cuartel de la Montaña; the precise nature of any armed role is not documented. Her firmly established wartime work was journalism, propaganda, organization, and relief.

She traveled to fronts and social institutions as a reporter. Her poetry became direct public verse concerning combatants, workers, revolutionary heroines, and the dead. Subjects included:

- Buenaventura Durruti;
- María Silva Cruz, “La Libertaria”;
- Encarnación Giménez, the laundress of Guadalmedina;
- the Asturian revolutionary struggle;
- unnamed soldiers and working women.

A surviving state-archive catalogue records a CNT-FAI-FIJL poster printing of her poem “Durruti”: [PARES catalogue record](https://pares.mcu.es/ParesBusquedas20/catalogo/description/120384/imprimir).

### Valencia, *Umbral*, and SIA

In spring 1937 the CNT sent her to Valencia to help establish **Solidaridad Internacional Antifascista**. She led press and propaganda work and became editor-in-chief or a senior editor of the illustrated anarchist weekly *Umbral*.

Her reports documented collectivized industry, hospitals, childcare, maternity facilities, rural transformation, and refugees. In August 1937 she traveled with photographer **Kati Horna** to Vélez Rubio, Almería, to report on a maternity home created in a former convent. Horna supplied the photographs and Sánchez Saornil the text for “La maternidad bajo el signo de la revolución,” published in issue 12 of *Umbral*. The collaboration and surviving negatives have been reconstructed in [El País’s archival report](https://elpais.com/cultura/2025-10-28/asi-descubri-la-identidad-de-la-mujer-de-una-de-las-imagenes-iconicas-de-la-guerra-civil-tomada-por-la-fotografa-kati-horna.html).

She attended the Second International Congress of Writers for the Defense of Culture in Valencia in July 1937. Attendance establishes presence at the congress but does not by itself prove personal acquaintance with every famous delegate.

### América Barroso

Sánchez Saornil met América “Mery” Barroso García in Valencia, probably through *Umbral* or SIA work, in 1937. They formed a household and remained together until Lucía’s death.

The archival record documents co-residence, joint work, migration, and family integration. Later acquaintances and historians describe them as a couple. Surviving family discourse sometimes called Mery a cousin or intimate friend, language that also protected them under Francoism. No marriage or explicit public declaration survives.

### Barcelona and international work

Late in 1937 the couple moved toward Barcelona. In May 1938 Sánchez Saornil became general secretary of SIA’s Spanish or international secretariat. She coordinated:

- children’s colonies;
- hospitals and medical supplies;
- soldiers’ dining facilities;
- refugee evacuation;
- foreign donations;
- relations with SIA branches abroad.

She traveled to France on organizational business. She also worked closely with **Emma Goldman**, who visited Republican Spain and promoted SIA abroad. A photograph dated 20 October 1938 shows Sánchez Saornil, Goldman, and interpreter Christine Kon-Rabe together. Archival listings record an October 1938 memorandum from Sánchez Saornil concerning Goldman’s proposed Canadian speaking work: [UAM study and document inventory](https://libros.uam.es/tfm/catalog/download/1118/2000/2222?inline=1).

Her wartime books were *Horas de revolución* (1937) and *Romancero de Mujeres Libres* (1938).

## Defeat, Exile, and France, 1939–1942

Following the fall of Catalonia, Lucía and Mery crossed into France through Le Perthus in February 1939 and proceeded through Perpignan to Paris. As SIA officers, they initially continued relief and organizational work rather than entering the biographical record simply as anonymous camp inmates.

French pressure on anarchist organizations led them from Paris to Orléans. There they helped publish **España Expatriada**, a newspaper serving dispersed Republican refugees by publishing practical information, messages, and family-location notices.

The outbreak of the European war ended the newspaper. After the German invasion in 1940 they moved to **Montauban**, in unoccupied southern France, and cooperated with Quaker relief services assisting Spanish refugees.

By late 1941 Lucía had reportedly been ordered to enter an accommodation center at Cazals because she was unemployed. She avoided doing so. Mery received mail under an assumed name; police construed this as a security matter. Both women were detained for several days in early 1942 on suspicion of subversive activity.

**Evidentiary classification:** brief French detention is a reported fact based on administrative and biographical research. No evidence located for this dossier indicates an indictment, trial, conviction, espionage, violence, fraud, or common crime. The assumed-name correspondence was dangerous in wartime France but does not itself establish clandestine criminal activity.

Facing possible internment, forced placement, or deportation, they chose clandestine return to Francoist Spain. Mery’s sister **Electra**, an Argentine citizen living near La Jonquera, helped them cross.

Some older summaries place their return or flight from Madrid in 1944. More recent register-based reconstruction establishes a Madrid rental contract in Lucía’s name in July 1942. **1942 is therefore the stronger date.**

---

## Clandestine Life in Madrid, 1942–1947

Lucía and Mery settled on Calle Donoso Cortés in Madrid’s Argüelles district. Lucía’s father and sister Concha joined them.

The 1945 municipal register listed Lucía as head of household and Mery as her cousin, both working as commission agents. They sold or represented knitwear and supplemented their income by making hair nets. The fictive cousin relationship gave their household a socially and legally legible form under the dictatorship.

Evidence for resistance activity is limited:

- Sisters Carmen and Visitación Lobo later testified that Lucía tried to reorganize Mujeres Libres.
- A clandestine Mujeres Libres manifesto from 1946 survives.
- Another report places her at a confederal meeting.

These are **reported facts or testimony**, not enough to reconstruct a continuous clandestine command role. There is no evidence that she was arrested, prosecuted, or convicted by the Franco regime.

The severe repression of anarchist networks in 1947 likely contributed to the couple’s departure from Madrid. Personal security and the presence of Mery’s family in Valencia both mattered.

---

## Valencia, 1948–1970

Lucía and Mery moved to Valencia in January 1948. Mery’s niece **Elena Samada**, who had previously worked with SIA, helped them obtain an apartment in the Ruzafa district. Lucía taught Samada’s children.

For a period the couple operated **Gabriren**, a small commission business representing knitwear. Its name reportedly combined **Gabriela** and **Irene**, the names of their mothers. No incorporation papers, accounts, capital figures, real-estate holdings, or significant investments have been identified. This was a modest household enterprise, not a large company.

Lucía received her first documented national identity card in June 1953, listing her occupation as **painter of fans**. A 1954 occupational-union card used the same description. Her documentation suggests eventual administrative regularization without revealing whether the dictatorship ever discovered the full extent of her wartime offices.

She painted fans, copied paintings to order, and undertook decorative work. Artists from her earlier Valencia years—including Juan Lacomba, Genaro Lahuerta, and especially Pedro de Valencia—renewed contact; Pedro apparently helped obtain copying commissions.

Private gatherings in the couple’s home included poetry readings. Lucía continued writing but avoided overtly political publication. In 1955 the Burgos journal *Estrofa*, associated with Falangist youth cultural circles, published “Quiero en mi ley cumplirme.” Publication there does not demonstrate ideological conversion; no evidence shows that she joined Falangism or repudiated anarchism.

Her late poems turned from the collective “we” of revolutionary verse toward solitude, memory, mortality, and persistence. “Yo siempre he dicho nosotros” became a retrospective expression of the gulf between her former collective public identity and postwar obscurity.

Her father’s death date is not firmly established in the accessible sources. Concha remained dependent on Lucía’s care and died early in 1970.

---

## Illness and Death

Lucía developed cancer, most often identified as breast cancer. She died in Valencia on 2 June 1970, aged seventy-four, not seventy-five. Her sister Concha had died roughly four months earlier.

Mery arranged the grave inscription:

> Pero… ¿es verdad que la esperanza ha muerto?

The line derives from Lucía’s late poetic vocabulary of hope and mortality. Mery died of a heart attack in 1977.

No evidence has been located of substance addiction, psychiatric institutionalization, suicide attempt, major accident, or violence causing Lucía’s death.

## Political, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

### From republican household to anarchism

Cansinos Assens called Eugenio Sánchez a republican. Lucía’s own early political path moved through radical republicanism to anarcho-syndicalism. Her mature position combined:

- opposition to state authority;
- class struggle and worker self-organization;
- libertarian communism;
- cultural and technical education;
- anti-clericalism;
- autonomous women’s organization;
- skepticism toward marriage and inherited family authority;
- refusal of male tutelage.

### Gender and sexual politics

She rejected the proposition that economic revolution alone would liberate women. Her phrase “the female question in our circles” located the problem within anarchism itself.

She differed from anarchists, including Federica Montseny in some writings, who treated “the woman problem” mainly as an aspect of individual formation, love, or economic dependency. Sánchez Saornil emphasized structural male privilege and collective organization by women.

She defended free union over compulsory legal marriage and treated motherhood as optional. Her writings also criticized prostitution as a product of economic and sexual domination, while Mujeres Libres experimented with “liberatorios de prostitución.” The surviving record does not justify attributing every Mujeres Libres policy personally to her.

### Art and revolution

Her move away from Ultraísmo was not a rejection of artistic innovation as such. She came to regard aesthetic revolution without material social change as inadequate. Wartime poetry became intentionally accessible—romances, elegies, hymns, reportage, and direct exhortation.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### América Barroso García (“Mery”)

- **Nature:** Lifelong domestic and probable romantic partner from 1937 to 1970.
- **Evidence:** Co-residence, shared exile and return, joint economic activity, family presentation as “cousins,” testimony, grave arrangements.
- **Limit:** No located self-identification by either woman using a modern sexual label.

### Mercedes Comaposada Guillén

- Co-founder of *Mujeres Libres* and close political collaborator.
- Worked on women’s education, editorial production, and national coordination.
- Their complete private correspondence has not been published.

### Amparo Poch y Gascón

- Physician, sex educator, and co-founder of *Mujeres Libres*.
- Her medical and educational expertise complemented Sánchez Saornil’s journalism and organizational work.

### Mariano Rodríguez Vázquez (“Marianet”)

- CNT leader and polemical interlocutor in the 1935 debate.
- Sánchez Saornil used his argument about women as a “revolutionary factor” to expose paternalism and male inconsistency.

### Emma Goldman

- International anarchist ally and SIA advocate.
- Correspondence, administrative records, and photographs document collaboration in 1938.

### Kati Horna

- Photographer and *Umbral* colleague.
- Documented joint reporting trip to the Vélez Rubio maternity home in August 1937.

### Rafael Cansinos Assens

- Critic and central figure in the Madrid avant-garde.
- Knew that “Luciano” was Lucía and described her poetry and family burdens in his memoirs.
- His recollections are valuable but sometimes impressionistic.

### Guillermo de Torre

- Ultraist writer and promoter.
- Publicly recognized Sánchez Saornil’s movement from conventional lyricism to the avant-garde.

### Norah Borges

- Artist in the transnational Ultraist network.
- Their reciprocal dedications document admiration and intellectual exchange, probably maintained chiefly through print or correspondence.

### Federica Montseny

- Anarchist leader and wartime minister.
- Shared the libertarian field and contributed to *Mujeres Libres*, but differed from Sánchez Saornil over the conceptual treatment of women’s oppression.
- Later reportedly called Lucía the best writer among the women militants.

### Relatives and postwar protectors

Electra Barroso facilitated the clandestine crossing into Spain. Elena Samada provided accommodation and family support in Valencia. Carmen and Visitación Lobo preserved testimony about attempted postwar reorganization.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Compañía Madrileña de Teléfonos / Telefónica

Her principal stable early employment, 1916–1931, with wartime readmission in 1936. No shares, patents, managerial ownership, or investment interest is documented.

### *CNT*

Editorial employment or organizational appointment in 1933–1934. She wrote signed and pseudonymous commentary and likely unsigned editorial matter.

### *Mujeres Libres*

She was co-founder of both the magazine and organization, not sole founder or proprietor. The organization developed education, health, vocational, childcare, and relief programs. It did not operate as her private financial vehicle.

### *Umbral*

She was a leading editor and reporter. It combined anarchist reporting with professional photography and graphic design.

### Solidaridad Internacional Antifascista

A humanitarian and propaganda network founded within the libertarian movement. Her office administered foreign aid and relief projects. No evidence of personal enrichment, embezzlement, or diversion of funds has been located.

### *España Expatriada*

Refugee-information newspaper produced in Orléans. It helped reconnect separated families and disseminated practical information.

### Gabriren

Small Valencia-era sales and representation venture operated with Mery. No balance sheets or lasting corporate structure are known.

### Inventions, patents, and technologies

No invention or patent is documented. Technology entered her life through telephone work and Ultraist representations of aviation, radio, electricity, cinema, and urban machinery.

### Wealth and property

She was never demonstrably wealthy. Her life was supported by wages, journalism, organizational work, commission sales, hair-net production, teaching, fan painting, and picture copying. No substantial property portfolio, investment account, inheritance, corporate holding, or patron-funded fortune has been identified.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, and Financial Questions

### Telephone strike and imprisonment

- **Documented fact:** She participated in the 1931 telephone strike and was briefly jailed.
- **Unresolved:** Whether her 1927 transfer and 1931 termination were formally imposed as anti-union punishment.
- **No evidence located:** ordinary criminal conviction.

### Wartime propaganda

- **Documented fact:** She wrote revolutionary propaganda, war poetry, organizational appeals, and memorial verse for anarchist figures.
- **Classification:** political propaganda openly issued by the CNT, Mujeres Libres, or related bodies, not covert disinformation.
- Some texts employed heroic imagery and simplified battlefield or revolutionary narratives. That literary function does not independently verify every implied historical claim.

### SIA funds and administration

- **Documented fact:** She held high administrative responsibility for international relief.
- **Adversarial-search finding:** No credible allegation of fraud, corruption, embezzlement, money laundering, misuse of donations, or personal enrichment was found.

### French detention

- **Reported fact:** Lucía and Mery were detained for several days in 1942 after correspondence under an assumed name drew police suspicion.
- **No located evidence:** indictment, trial, conviction, intelligence recruitment, or violent conspiracy.
- **Unresolved:** Exact wording and disposition of the French police file.

### Francoist repression

- **Documented context:** Her CNT, SIA, Mujeres Libres, and Republican activities exposed her to severe risk.
- **Reported fact:** She lived cautiously, used protective kinship descriptions, and reduced public political activity.
- **No located evidence:** a completed Francoist prosecution or prison sentence against her.

### Relationship with América Barroso

- **Documented:** Thirty-three years of shared life.
- **Strong historical interpretation:** romantic partnership.
- **Not documented:** self-applied identity label, marriage, or explicit surviving declaration of sexual relationship.
- Describing her as “openly lesbian” overstates the surviving evidence if “openly” implies public self-identification.

### “Feminist” versus “Mujer Libre”

- **Documented:** She organized specifically against women’s subordination.
- **Documented:** Mujeres Libres generally rejected the contemporary label *feminist*, which members associated with bourgeois reformism.
- **Historical classification:** “anarcha-feminist” accurately describes much of the program but is partly retrospective.

### 2024–2026 adversarial search

Searches pairing her name with Spanish and English terms for controversy, scandal, accusation, lawsuit, fraud, corruption, conspiracy, criticism, police files, and criminal investigation produced no credible recent allegation of financial or personal misconduct. The current controversies concern historical representation:

1. overconfident claims about her education at San Fernando;
2. birth-year mistakes;
3. incompatible return dates from exile;
4. the motive for “Luciano de San-Saor”;
5. whether “lesbian” and “openly lesbian” are supportable formulations;
6. whether she should be called feminist despite her historical vocabulary;
7. image misidentification in public memorial art.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### Myth 1: Born in 1896 or 1901

- **Story:** Older accounts give 1896; Lola Iturbe reportedly gave 1901.
- **Origin:** Early memorial writing and repeated secondary summaries.
- **Evidence:** Civil registration supports 13 December 1895.
- **Effect:** Public commemorations and captions have occasionally carried the wrong year.

### Myth 2: Formal student of the Real Academia de Bellas Artes de San Fernando

- **Story:** Lucía enrolled and studied painting at the academy.
- **Circulation:** Numerous popular biographies.
- **Evidence for:** Documented artistic interests and some form of art instruction.
- **Evidence against or limiting:** No enrollment or degree record located; newer accounts distinguish the academy from open or associated classes.
- **Classification:** Unverified.

### Myth 3: The masculine pseudonym completely hid her sex

- **Story:** Editors and readers believed Luciano was a man.
- **Evidence against:** Cansinos Assens and Guillermo de Torre explicitly knew her identity; she sometimes used both signatures.
- **Historical significance:** The story dramatizes barriers faced by women writers but simplifies her actual, semi-transparent literary masquerade.

### Myth 4: The pseudonym proves lesbian identity

- **Story:** “Luciano” existed solely so Lucía could write openly to women.
- **Evidence for:** Female-addressed erotic poetry and her later partnership with Mery.
- **Evidence against or limiting:** The pseudonym also appears on non-erotic texts; some erotic texts appeared under her own name; contemporaries knew who she was.
- **Classification:** Plausible partial interpretation, not proven sole motive.

### Myth 5: “Openly lesbian”

- **Story:** She publicly proclaimed lesbian identity within Mujeres Libres.
- **Evidence:** No such proclamation has been located.
- **What is established:** Long partnership with a woman and poetry compatible with lesbian desire.
- **Reputational effect:** She has become an important predecessor in Spanish lesbian and queer cultural history, sometimes through claims stronger than the archive permits.

### Myth 6: Armed militia combatant

- **Story:** She fought as an armed miliciana during the Madrid battles.
- **Evidence:** She participated in revolutionary mobilization and visited fronts; photographs, reportage, and organizational work are secure.
- **Limitation:** No detailed combat record has been established.
- **Classification:** Possible participation in street defense; sustained combatant career unverified.

### Myth 7: Return to Spain in 1944

- **Story:** She returned in 1944 and fled Madrid after recognition.
- **Evidence against:** A July 1942 Madrid housing contract and subsequent register evidence.
- **Best reconstruction:** Clandestine return in 1942, Madrid until January 1948, then Valencia.

### Myth 8: A famous portrait shows Lucía

A widely reproduced photograph or mural image was identified as Sánchez Saornil but was reportedly based on an image of anarchist Antonia Fontanillas. The controversy illustrates the substitution of generic revolutionary iconography for verified portraiture. The misidentification is discussed in [“Lucía Sánchez Saornil y el error que no cesa”](https://amanecemetropolis.net/lucia-sanchez-saornil-y-el-error-que-no-cesa/).

### Myth 9: Complete postwar political silence

- **Story:** Lucía abandoned all politics immediately upon return.
- **Evidence complicating it:** Testimony about a reorganization effort, the 1946 manifesto, and a reported CNT meeting.
- **Best conclusion:** Her visible mass-organizational career ended, but limited clandestine contact probably continued.

### Myth 10: “Thirty years of being nobody”

This formulation captures her enforced anonymity and historiographical disappearance after 1939, but she did not literally cease producing work or maintaining networks. She ran businesses, painted, taught, hosted literary gatherings, wrote poems, and sustained old friendships. It is a symbolic description, not a factual social condition.

## Successes, Failures, and Institutional Outcomes

### Significant successes

- Built a literary reputation despite limited formal education and heavy family responsibilities.
- Became the most clearly established woman poet within Spanish Ultraísmo.
- Developed a distinct critique of male authority inside anarchism.
- Co-founded *Mujeres Libres*, which grew into a national organization with thousands of participants.
- Managed major press and humanitarian responsibilities during wartime.
- Helped refugees in Spain and France.
- Sustained a long partnership and household through war, exile, occupation, clandestine return, and dictatorship.
- Continued creative work into her final illness.

### Failures and defeats

- Never published a comprehensive prewar poetry volume.
- The Ultraist movement dissolved without securing her durable canonical status.
- Mujeres Libres failed to gain recognition as the fourth branch of the libertarian movement.
- The Republican and revolutionary cause suffered military defeat.
- SIA’s Spanish operations and *España Expatriada* were disrupted by war and occupation.
- Attempts at postwar reorganization could not restore Mujeres Libres in Spain.
- Her political and literary identity remained largely suppressed during her last three decades.
- Much prose, correspondence, art, and organizational documentation was lost or remains unidentified.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Lucía was remembered after 1970 chiefly inside libertarian exile circles. The broader recovery unfolded in stages:

- Mary Nash and other historians reintroduced Mujeres Libres in the 1970s.
- The 1996 collected *Poesía* restored Sánchez Saornil as a significant avant-garde poet.
- Feminist historians examined her critique of male anarchism.
- Lesbian and queer scholarship reread her erotic poems and partnership with Mery.
- Recent archival research corrected errors concerning her birth, education, employment, exile, and return.
- Literary historians increasingly place her near the Generation of 1927 while noting that she did not belong to its best-known social and institutional circle.
- Anarchist organizations reclaimed her as a founder and theoretician.
- Public murals, streets, school programs, and commemorations transformed her into a civic symbol—sometimes producing iconographic or biographical simplification.

Her legacy now operates in at least four partly overlapping fields:

1. Spanish Ultraísmo and avant-garde poetry.
2. Anarcho-syndicalism and Civil War culture.
3. Autonomous working-class women’s organization.
4. Lesbian and queer literary genealogy.

These recoveries are historically important but sometimes turn uncertainty into emblematic certainty. Her surviving record supports a radical anarchist organizer, innovative poet, working woman, refugee-relief administrator, and woman who shared her adult life with another woman. It does not support every later slogan or identity formulation placed upon her.

## Atlas Connections

Only documented or specifically reported links are included. Mere contemporaneity, attendance at the same broad historical event, or membership in the same literary generation is excluded.

### Jorge Luis Borges — documented literary connection

Borges and Sánchez Saornil belonged to the transnational Ultraist print network. Their poems appeared in the same periodicals, including *Grecia*. Borges later wrote a brief critical characterization of “Luciano de San-Saor” in *Manantial*, recognizing Sánchez Saornil’s development from Modernismo to the avant-garde. Lucía’s better documented personal-artistic exchange was with Borges’s sister Norah. A face-to-face meeting between Lucía and Jorge Luis Borges has not been established.

### José Buenaventura Durruti Dumange — documented commemorative and political connection

Both operated within the Spanish anarchist movement during the Civil War. After Durruti’s death in Madrid on 20 November 1936, Sánchez Saornil wrote “Romance de Durruti,” which was printed by CNT-FAI-FIJL propaganda bodies and read at commemorative events. She spoke at a Durruti homage in Valencia’s Teatro Apolo on 21 November 1937. No source consulted establishes a personal meeting between them.

### Federico García Lorca — no established personal connection

Sánchez Saornil is now sometimes grouped with women surrounding the Generation of 1927 and attended the 1937 Valencia writers’ congress after Lorca’s death. No correspondence, meeting, collaboration, or explicitly acknowledged influence between them was located.

### George Orwell, André Breton, Albert Camus, Pablo Picasso, and other rostered contemporaries — no supported direct connection found

Their names arise in the wider histories of the Spanish Civil War, anarchism, or European modernism, but the sources consulted do not document a personal or working relationship with Sánchez Saornil. Absence of evidence is therefore recorded rather than converting shared context into a connection.
