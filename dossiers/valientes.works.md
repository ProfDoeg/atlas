## Works by Eustaquia de Souza & Ana de Urinza

### Texts and protocols

- **1653:** *Note to Diego Melgarejo*. A jointly authored, now-untraced message mocking Melgarejo as cowardly and summoning him to accompany their second nocturnal expedition; as a text intended to compel action, it is closer to a manifesto than correspondence. Its existence is reported only by Bartolomé Arzáns, whose late chronicle attributes the story to a lost history by Juan Pasquier and to oral testimony; no independent document confirms it. ([Arzáns in *The Bolivia Reader*, pp. 80–81](https://doi.org/10.1215/9780822371618))

- **1653:** *Message requesting four mules*. A practical protocol sent through their servant to her son in the Mataca valley, instructing him to obtain four equipped mules for the flight from Potosí; Melgarejo, rather than Eustaquia or Ana, physically wrote the message. No copy survives, and its existence rests solely on Arzáns’s uncorroborated narrative. ([Arzáns in *The Bolivia Reader*, pp. 84–85](https://doi.org/10.1215/9780822371618))

### Performances and self-fashioned personae

- **1653:** *First nocturnal appearance in men’s dress*. A jointly constructed social persona—costume, armor, weapons, concealed faces, speech, and comportment—made for walking through Potosí while being read as two young noblemen. The garments were assembled and worn by the pair, not necessarily fabricated by them; the episode therefore resists classification as a discrete obra and survives only as Arzáns’s literary-historical account. ([Arzáns in *The Bolivia Reader*, pp. 78–80](https://doi.org/10.1215/9780822371618); [Navia Antezana, pp. 17–19](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/servlet/articulo?codigo=10931128))

- **1653, eve of Saint John:** *Second nocturnal appearance and musical performance in the Plaza del Gato*. A manifestation combining another elaborate masculine costume, armed street performance, and guitar-playing and singing led by Eustaquia, with Ana participating; the songs are unnamed and described as chosen by Eustaquia, not composed by her, so no musical works can safely be attributed to either woman. ([Arzáns in *The Bolivia Reader*, pp. 80–83](https://doi.org/10.1215/9780822371618))

- **1653–c. 1658:** *Five-year masculine persona and journey through Charcas and Peru*. A sustained life-project encompassing flight from Potosí, travel through La Plata/Chuquisaca, Lima, Trujillo, and unnamed additional cities, followed by a return to Lima and then Chuquisaca; costume, mobility, reputation, and public conduct formed an inseparable whole rather than a conventional authored “work.” The chronology and itinerary come solely from Arzáns, while modern scholarship expressly classifies the account as fictionalized or undocumented and says its historical truth cannot be proved. ([Arzáns in *The Bolivia Reader*, pp. 84–86](https://doi.org/10.1215/9780822371618); [Navia Antezana, pp. 17–22](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/servlet/articulo?codigo=10931128))

- **Between 1655 and c. 1658:** *Ana’s bullfight in Lima*. An embodied performance attributed to Ana alone, undertaken while publicly appearing as a man; the fall from her horse was later said to have caused her final illness. Arzáns admits that there were additional alleged causes he would not report because he was uncertain, and no independent record establishes the event. ([Arzáns in *The Bolivia Reader*, p. 86](https://doi.org/10.1215/9780822371618))

### Unfinished project

- **c. 1658:** *Plan to enter the convent of Santa Monica*. Eustaquia and Ana made arrangements to become nuns, but Ana’s prolonged illness prevented the project from being realized. It is an intention with practical preparation—not a completed foundation, profession, or surviving text—and is known only through Arzáns. ([Arzáns in *The Bolivia Reader*, p. 86](https://doi.org/10.1215/9780822371618))

### Collaborative manifestations not securely their works

- **c. 1658:** *Portrait of “The valiant Doña Eustaquia de Souza”*. An untraced portrait showing Eustaquia in her preserved men’s clothing, apparently made by an unnamed painter at Juan de Itulaín’s request and bearing an inscription ordered by him. Eustaquia supplied the pose, clothing, and performed persona, but neither authorship nor commission was hers; it therefore resists inclusion as a work “by” her and is better treated as a collaborative manifestation known only from Arzáns’s claim to have seen it. ([Arzáns in *The Bolivia Reader*, pp. 86–87](https://doi.org/10.1215/9780822371618); [Navia Antezana, pp. 18–19](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/servlet/articulo?codigo=10931128))

- **c. 1658:** *Portrait of “The valiant Doña Ana de Urinza, Peruvian lady of Potosí”*. The companion portrait, likewise untraced, represented the ill Ana after she reluctantly put on her retained men’s garments at Itulaín’s request. Its maker is unnamed and its attribution to Ana would be misleading: her contribution was the embodied persona represented, not the painted object or its inscription. ([Arzáns in *The Bolivia Reader*, pp. 86–87](https://doi.org/10.1215/9780822371618); [Navia Antezana, pp. 18–19](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/servlet/articulo?codigo=10931128))

### Final dispositions

- **c. 1660–1661:** *Disposition of their estate*. Arzáns reports that the pair directed their property to be divided among the poor, freed their enslaved servant, provided money for her and her son, and gave Melgarejo one thousand pesos and their men’s clothing. This acts like a testamentary manifesto, altering property and legal status, but Arzáns neither identifies a written will nor reproduces one; no surviving jointly authored instrument can therefore be asserted. ([Arzáns in *The Bolivia Reader*, p. 87](https://doi.org/10.1215/9780822371618))
