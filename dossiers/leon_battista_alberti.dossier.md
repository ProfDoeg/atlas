# Leon Battista Alberti: Research Dossier

## Scope and method

This dossier reconstructs Alberti's life from surviving contemporary and near-contemporary testimony, his own writings and correspondence, institutional records summarized in modern documentary scholarship, architectural evidence, and specialist reference works. It distinguishes between (a) documented events; (b) reasonable scholarly reconstruction; (c) traditional attribution; and (d) legend or later reputation. Dates attached to manuscript works are dates of composition or circulation, not dates of first print, unless stated otherwise.

The principal biographical control is Cecil Grayson and Giulio Carlo Argan's *Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani* entry, which itself rests on Alberti manuscripts, the fifteenth-century anonymous *Vita*, Girolamo Mancini's archival biography and publication of Alberti's testament, and later philology. The anonymous *Vita* has often been treated as disguised autobiography. That view remains influential but is not uncontested; its first-person access, self-fashioning, and factual errors require it to be used as testimony rather than transparent fact.

## Family and Ancestry

### The Alberti commercial and political dynasty

The Alberti del Giudice were among late-medieval Florence's major merchant-banking families. Their firms operated through multiple Italian commercial centers. Wealth brought political office and factional exposure. In the struggle among Florentine oligarchic houses, Alberti relatives were proscribed and banished; Lorenzo di Benedetto Alberti consequently conducted business outside Florence. The family's exile was not a romantic literary condition but a legal and economic regime affecting residence, property, civic identity, marriage strategy and succession.

Alberti later converted family history into a moral and political archive. *I libri della famiglia* stages conversations among Alberti relatives in Padua in 1421, just before Lorenzo's death. It treats education, marriage, household management, thrift, wealth, friendship and relations with rulers. The speakers are literary constructions, not stenographic transcripts, but the setting turns family exile, commercial experience and threatened continuity into a theory of domestic government.

### Parents, sibling and maternal identity

- **Father:** Lorenzo di Benedetto Alberti, a Florentine merchant-banker living under banishment. He worked at Venice after Genoa and died at Padua on **28 May 1421**. The anonymous *Vita* and later accounts credit him with encouraging Battista's mathematical formation.
- **Mother:** **Bianca Fieschi** in the Grayson–Argan DBI reconstruction. The University of Bologna describes her as a Genoese/Bolognese-linked widow of a Grimaldi. Older or derivative biographies sometimes call her unknown. Her precise biography and status are not fully recoverable from the material consulted, but “unknown mother” is no longer the strongest account.
- **Brother:** **Carlo di Lorenzo Alberti**, also a natural son of Lorenzo. Alberti dedicated *De commodis litterarum atque incommodis* to Carlo. Works once assigned to Battista—notably *Amiria* and *Ephoebia*—are now assigned to Carlo; *Ephoebia* is related to Battista's Latin *Amator*.
- **Extended family:** His dialogues name or evoke Lorenzo, Adovardo, Ricciardo, Lionardo, Giannozzo, Carlo and other Alberti. Francesco d'Altobianco Alberti received the dedication of Book III of *Della famiglia*. Late dialogues include young Alberti nephews. The exact complete sibling and cousin tree exceeds what the surviving biographical sources permit with confidence.

### Illegitimacy, inheritance, and family conflict

After Lorenzo's death, Battista entered a prolonged dispute with relatives over patrimony and inheritance. The DBI describes “contestazioni patrimoniali ed ereditarie”; the University of Bologna says relatives repeatedly tried to disown him. Alberti's *Pupillus* gives literary form to the injured ward/orphan. The anonymous *Vita* goes further and says relatives plotted or attempted his murder. Mancini believed he found a document lending some support, but no consulted source establishes a judicial finding, named perpetrator, or completed prosecution. The responsible classification is:

- **Documented:** illegitimate status; father's death; patrimonial conflict; later papal dispensation; economic strain followed by salaried office and benefices.
- **First-person/biographical allegation:** relatives withheld his inheritance and tried to have him killed.
- **Unresolved:** the precise estate, sums, legal actions, and whether an attempted murder occurred.

The conflict is biographically important even if its most dramatic form cannot be proven: exile, dependency and hostile kin recur throughout Alberti's ethics of *virtù* against *fortuna*.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1404–1421

### Genoa and Venice

Alberti was born in Genoa while his father lived outside Florence under ban. The household moved to Venice, where Lorenzo continued commerce. Some accounts connect the move to plague in Genoa, but the exact date and causal sequence are less secure than the move itself. The family's resources still permitted elite education despite political displacement.

### Padua and Gasparino Barzizza

After 1416 Battista was in Padua. Probably between about 1415/16 and 1418 he attended the school of **Gasparino Barzizza**, a major teacher of classical Latin style. Fellow pupils or contacts included **Francesco Barbaro**, **Francesco Filelfo** and **Antonio Beccadelli (“Il Panormita”)**. Lapo da Castiglionchio's testimony supports some study of Greek, perhaps with Filelfo, though Alberti's Greek competence should not be inflated to the level of his Latin.

His formation was literary and rhetorical rather than workshop-based. He did **not** receive a conventional apprenticeship as painter, sculptor or master mason. That fact later shaped his distinctive role: he supplied learned designs, proportional programs, models and written directions while experienced builders executed projects.

## Education and Formation, 1421–1432

### University of Bologna

By 1421 Alberti was at Bologna studying canon law. His father's death on 28 May intensified economic and psychological pressure. Between roughly 1424 and 1428 he suffered recurrent illness and what the anonymous *Vita* represents as collapse under overwork and family hostility. It says legal study damaged his memory; he turned toward mathematics and physics because demonstrative reasoning seemed less dependent on rote recall. No modern diagnosis is possible.

He completed a doctorate in **canon law in 1428**. The degree later qualified him for chancery office and ecclesiastical income. His Bolognese intellectual world also produced friendships with the physician-mathematician **Paolo dal Pozzo Toscanelli** and contacts extending across Italian humanist circles.

### *Philodoxeos* and the classical-authorship deception

In 1424 Alberti completed a draft of the Latin comedy *Philodoxeos fabula* (“The Lover of Glory”). Panormita obtained the text and circulated defective copies. Alberti then encouraged the belief that it was an ancient work by “Lepidus,” supposedly recovered from an old manuscript. Its success as an antique and loss of prestige when he later claimed it illuminate the prestige economy of humanism.

This was a deliberate literary deception, but not a commercial forgery or criminal fraud in the modern legal sense. It tested whether humanists judged Latin by textual quality or by the authority of antiquity. Alberti revised the play and acknowledged authorship about a decade later, dedicating it to Leonello d'Este. The work was printed at Salamanca in 1500 under Alberti's name; Aldus Manutius's 1588 Italian edition still attributed it to Lepidus. The episode itself became part of Alberti's mythology as a youth capable of fooling expert philologists.

### Early works, sickness and intellectual direction

The late 1420s produced *De commodis litterarum atque incommodis*, dedicated to Carlo: a severe audit of the costs and rewards of scholarship. It attacks a society organized around money and professional utility while promising the scholar wisdom, virtue and possible immortality rather than income. Early erotic and anti-erotic dialogues—*Amator*, *Deifira*, *Ecatonfilea* and related pieces—examine desire as a threat to autonomy. These works contain generalized suspicions of women and marriage; they are evidence of Alberti's gender ideology, but not proof of a specific failed affair.

### Disputed northern-European journey

Older biographies place Alberti after graduation in the service of Cardinal Louis Aleman and then Cardinal Niccolò Albergati, traveling through France and Germany on peace diplomacy and returning via Basel. Grayson and Argan reject this as unverified: Alberti does not appear in the documented suite at the Peace of Arras, was not at the Council of Basel, the anonymous *Vita* is silent, and supposed eyewitness allusions in *De re aedificatoria* are insufficient. It is possible that he traveled outside Italy, but the dossier treats the journey as a **later biographical hypothesis, not fact**.

## Early Career in Rome and the Papal Curia, 1431/32–1434

By 1432, and possibly in 1431, Alberti was at Rome as secretary to **Biagio Molin**, patriarch of Grado and regent of the papal chancery. Molin helped secure his appointment as an **apostolic abbreviator**, an official who drafted and prepared papal documents. Before October 1432 Pope **Eugene IV** issued a dispensation removing the canonical disability attached to Alberti's illegitimate birth, allowing him to take orders and enjoy ecclesiastical benefices.

He became prior of **San Martino a Gangalandi** near Florence and, in 1448, pievano of **Borgo San Lorenzo** in Mugello. These offices and chancery income ended his acute financial insecurity. They also embedded him in a transregional institution whose court moved with the pope.

Alberti's papal work has sometimes been described loosely as “secretary to several popes.” More precisely, he served within chancery and curial networks under Eugene IV, Nicholas V and Pius II. There is no evidence that he held the office of principal papal secretary or directed papal policy.

### Hagiography, antiquarian measurement and optical experiment

For Molin he wrote the *Vita Sancti Potiti* (1433), intended as the first of a series of saints' lives. In a letter to Leonardo Dati, Alberti expressed historical doubts about legendary material. He apparently abandoned the larger project. The episode shows not irreligion by itself but friction between humanist source criticism and inherited hagiography.

Rome's ruins prompted measurement, mapping and reconstruction. *Descriptio urbis Romae* later encoded the city's plan using polar/radial coordinates, enabling a map to be regenerated from numerical data rather than copied pictorially. The anonymous *Vita* also describes optical demonstrations with mirrors and chamber-like devices, sometimes exaggerated into an invention of the camera obscura or stereoscopy. Alberti certainly experimented with controlled visual representation; priority claims for the camera obscura are not warranted.

## Chronological Life History

### 1433–1434: *I libri della famiglia* and flight from Rome

The anonymous *Vita* says Alberti drafted the first three books of *Della famiglia* in ninety days in his thirtieth year. He revised them later; Book IV followed around 1440/41. In June 1434 Eugene IV fled Rome amid Colonna and Fortebracci pressure, and Alberti traveled with the papal court to Florence.

### 1434–1436: Florence, artists and *De pictura*

In Florence he renewed or developed relations with **Filippo Brunelleschi**, **Donatello**, **Lorenzo Ghiberti**, **Luca della Robbia**, and the artistic world transformed by **Masaccio**. He also interacted with Leonardo Bruni, Carlo Marsuppini, Poggio Bracciolini, Giannozzo Manetti, Vespasiano da Bisticci, Marco Parenti, Burchiello, Leonardo Dati and Piero di Cosimo de' Medici.

In 1435 he composed *De pictura/Della pittura*, in Latin and Tuscan versions. The exact sequence of the two redactions is debated: traditional accounts made Latin first and vernacular second; Lucia Bertolini's philological work argues for priority of the vernacular version. The Tuscan prologue praises Brunelleschi, Donatello, Ghiberti, Luca della Robbia and Masaccio. The Latin version was dedicated to Gianfrancesco Gonzaga.

The treatise:

- defines the picture as an intersection of the visual pyramid, famously comparable to an open window;
- provides a geometrical construction for pavement and recession;
- treats light, color, contour and composition;
- makes **historia**, a coherent multi-figure action, the painter's highest task;
- treats painting as an intellectual, mathematical and socially dignified activity.

Alberti did not invent linear perspective ex nihilo. Brunelleschi's experiments and Florentine practice preceded him. Alberti's achievement was to formulate, generalize and circulate a learnable theory.

Around this period he also produced *Elementa picturae* and *De statua*. Dating *De statua* as late as 1464, common in popular summaries, is disputed; the DBI places it near *De pictura*. It describes proportional measurement of bodies and mechanical means of transferring three-dimensional form.

### 1436–1439: Bologna, Ferrara and the ecumenical council

Alberti accompanied Eugene IV to Bologna in April 1436. Works include a 1437 letter to Pietro Codagnello against erotic entanglement, *Sofrona*, and *De iure*, reportedly improvised in twenty hours at Francesco Coppini's request—an achievement claim originating in the self-fashioning tradition and not independently timed.

In January 1438 he followed the papal court to Ferrara for the council seeking reunion between the Latin and Greek churches, then to Florence when plague forced its transfer in 1439. At Ferrara he renewed his connection with **Leonello d'Este**. His court relationships were cultural and advisory, not evidence that he held territorial political power.

### 1439–1443: Florence, vernacular advocacy and moral crisis

This was one of Alberti's most productive literary periods. *Teogenio* (sent to Leonello d'Este in 1441) confronts adversity and the ruin of arrogant prosperity. *Della tranquillità dell'animo* seeks an equilibrium among passion, fortune, injury and contemplative freedom. *Villa* considers rural estate life. *De equo animante* (1441) treats the education and management of horses; *Canis* praises Alberti's dog; *Musca* mock-praises the fly in Lucianic style. The *Intercenales*, composed over years and gathered in ten books, use grotesque, dreamlike and satirical dialogues to expose avarice, vanity, scholarly misery, clerical weakness and social disorder.

In October 1441 Alberti and **Piero di Cosimo de' Medici** organized the **Certame Coronario**, a public poetic competition in Tuscan on friendship. Alberti attempted classical meters in the vernacular and offered Book IV of *Della famiglia* to the commune. The judges awarded no crown. A second contest on envy was planned but not held. Later accounts treat the non-award as a defeat inflicted by conservative Latinists; the event nevertheless made a public institutional claim for vernacular literary dignity.

### 1443–1455: Rome, Nicholas V and architecture

The papal court returned to Rome in June 1443, after a stop at Siena. Alberti's center of gravity shifted from vernacular moral literature toward architecture, antiquity, mathematics and engineering. Under **Nicholas V** (pope 1447–1455), humanist rebuilding became papal policy. Alberti advised rather than personally supervised most Roman works. Projects and discussions associated with him include new St Peter's, urban routes, fortification, the Acqua Vergine, and restoration of San Teodoro, Santa Prassede, Santa Maria Maggiore and Santo Stefano Rotondo. The documentary boundary between Alberti's advice, Nicholas's program and Bernardo Rossellino's execution is often impossible to draw.

He attempted to recover ancient Roman ships from **Lake Nemi**. The operation failed to raise them. Later Renaissance and modern recoveries make it an important episode in engineering archaeology, but contemporary technical drawings of Alberti's apparatus do not survive.

#### *De re aedificatoria*

Alberti worked on the ten-book *De re aedificatoria* from the 1440s and presented a version to Nicholas V around 1450/52; he continued revising it. Modeled structurally on Vitruvius but not a paraphrase, it addresses design, materials, construction, public and private building types, temples, ornament, streets, tombs, theaters, houses, water, roads, repairs and the architect's education. Its key concept of **concinnitas** describes beauty as the fitted correspondence of parts such that nothing can be added, removed or altered without worsening the whole.

The work remained manuscript during Alberti's life and was printed at Florence in **1485**, the first printed modern architectural treatise. Its addressee is the learned patron and governing elite as much as the craft practitioner.

#### Ferrara

At the Este court from about 1443, Alberti advised on the monument to Niccolò III (the “Arco del Cavallo”) and the cathedral campanile. The exact division between Alberti's conception and local execution remains debated.

#### Tempio Malatestiano, Rimini

**Sigismondo Pandolfo Malatesta** initially employed Matteo de' Pasti and Agostino di Duccio to alter chapels in the Gothic church of San Francesco. Around 1450 Alberti supplied a larger project to encase and transform it into the structure later called the **Tempio Malatestiano**. A medal by Matteo de' Pasti preserves the uncompleted façade and dome scheme. Alberti worked largely from Rome, sending designs and a detailed 1454 letter that corrected Matteo's proposed departures.

Classical triumphal arches and aqueduct arcades inform the exterior. The planned upper façade and great dome were never completed. Sigismondo's military and financial collapse and excommunication in 1460 curtailed the project. Because the building commemorated Sigismondo, Isotta degli Atti and Malatesta culture with extensive classical symbolism, hostile papal rhetoric called it pagan or a “temple” to its patron. It remained a Christian Franciscan church and is now Rimini's cathedral. “Pagan temple” is therefore a historically consequential polemical description, not a literal change of cult.

#### Stefano Porcari's conspiracy

After the failed 1453 attempt by **Stefano Porcari** to overthrow papal government and establish a Roman republic, Alberti wrote *De Porcaria coniuratione*. The DBI finds a measure of sympathy for Porcari's ambitious republican gesture. Alberti was present at the aftermath, not shown to have joined the plot. No charge, investigation or punishment of Alberti for conspiracy is documented.

### 1455–1464: Mantua, Florence and expanding patronage

Under **Pius II**, Alberti traveled with the papal court to the **Congress of Mantua (1459)**, intended to organize a crusade after the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople. There he strengthened ties to **Ludovico III Gonzaga**, who commissioned San Sebastiano and later Sant'Andrea.

In Florence, merchant and patron **Giovanni di Paolo Rucellai** became central to Alberti's architectural network. Works traditionally or securely linked to Alberti include:

Alberti's relationship with **Lorenzo de' Medici** began when Lorenzo was young. He dedicated *Trivia senatoria* (c. 1460), on public speaking, to him. Cristoforo Landino's later *Disputationes Camaldulenses* depicts an Alberti–Lorenzo encounter, probably reflecting a real 1468 meeting but assigning Alberti Platonizing speeches that cannot be treated as transcripts.

### 1460–1472: Mantuan churches

#### San Sebastiano

Ludovico Gonzaga commissioned **San Sebastiano** in 1460 as part of Mantuan renewal after the 1459 congress. **Luca Fancelli** directed construction. The church's elevated, centralized Greek-cross conception and crypt/mausoleum level are Albertian; construction changes, noncompletion and twentieth-century restoration complicate any reconstruction of his façade. A 1470 Gonzaga letter confirms continuing consultation about the portico.

#### Sant'Andrea

In 1470–71 Ludovico asked Alberti for a design to replace the church of Sant'Andrea, repository of Mantua's relic of Christ's blood and a major pilgrimage site. Alberti argued in a surviving letter for a design that would be “more capacious, more eternal, more worthy, more joyful, and less expensive.” The plan combines a colossal triumphal-arch façade, a single barrel-vaulted nave, lateral chapels functioning as buttressing masses, and ancient basilica/mausoleum references. Construction under Fancelli began in 1472, after Alberti's death; later centuries altered and completed it, including the eighteenth-century dome. It is Alberti's design, not a building he supervised to completion.

### 1464: Loss of chancery office

When **Paul II** abolished or reorganized the College of Abbreviators in 1464, Alberti lost his office. Other humanists protested; some were later imprisoned and accused of conspiracy or heterodoxy. Alberti is not documented as arrested, tried or charged. He remained in Rome and was financially able to do without the salary, probably because of benefices and accumulated resources.

### 1466/67: *De componendis cifris* and the cipher disk

At the prompting of his friend **Leonardo Dati**, a papal secretary, Alberti composed *De componendis cifris* (also *De cifris* or *La cifra*) about **1466**, with 1467 often given for completion or circulation. The text was not printed in the fifteenth century; it appeared in Cosimo Bartoli's *Opuscoli morali* in Venice in 1568.

Alberti's innovations were threefold:

1. **Cryptanalysis:** he tabulated letter frequencies in different Latin text types, one of the earliest Western expositions of frequency analysis.
2. **Polyalphabetic substitution:** instead of a single fixed substitution alphabet, the encipherer changes alphabets during the message.
3. **Mechanical aid:** his *formula* consists of two concentric disks joined by a pin. A fixed outer ring carries capital plaintext letters plus four numerals; a movable inner ring carries a mixed lowercase alphabet. Turning the inner disk changes the substitution.

The four numerals could connect to a nomenclator/codebook of prearranged expressions. Indicator letters or enciphered numerals told the recipient when and how to rotate the disk. Changing alphabets at irregular intervals dispersed ordinary letter frequencies and made contemporary monoalphabetic attacks ineffective. The device is best described as the **first known European mechanical polyalphabetic cipher device**, not the first cipher or first cryptanalysis in world history. Arabic cryptanalysts had developed frequency analysis centuries earlier.

There is no surviving patent; Renaissance Italy had privilege systems, but no evidence consulted shows Alberti seeking one. No original physical disk securely attributable to him survives; reconstructions follow the treatise.

#### Relationship to Trithemius, Bellaso and Vigenère

The line is evolutionary, not a simple succession of identical ciphers:

- **Alberti, 1466/67:** mixed alphabet on a rotating disk; alphabet changes signaled within the cryptogram and not necessarily periodic.
- **Johannes Trithemius, *Polygraphiae* (written c. 1508; printed 1518):** a progressive tabula recta of shifted alphabets.
- **Giovan Battista Bellaso, *La cifra* (1553; developments in 1563):** keyed polyalphabetic systems. The repeating-key system commonly called the “Vigenère cipher” belongs historically to Bellaso.
- **Giambattista della Porta, 1563:** further reciprocal and keyword-related systems.
- **Blaise de Vigenère, *Traicté des chiffres* (1586):** synthesized earlier literature and described a stronger autokey system. Nineteenth-century usage attached Vigenère's name to Bellaso's repeating-key cipher.

Thus Alberti is Vigenère's predecessor in the European polyalphabetic tradition, but the modern “Vigenère cipher” is not simply Alberti's disk written as a table. Alberti's irregular alphabet changes may in some operational respects be less vulnerable than a short repeating keyword.

### Final writings and final journeys

Alberti wrote *Cena familiaris*, the *Sentenze pitagoriche* (1462), seven letters styled to Diogenes, and in 1468 *De iciarchia*, a late dialogue on the household ruler or first citizen. It revisits moderation, education, authority and civic action without producing a program for state office.

In 1471 he guided **Lorenzo de' Medici**, **Bernardo Rucellai** and **Donato Acciaiuoli** through the Roman Forum. This documented antiquarian role is a fitting late example of how he connected ruins, texts, patrons and political elites.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Employment and institutional affiliations

- Alberti family merchant-banking network: source of birth status and early support, but not an enterprise he is documented as managing.
- University of Bologna: canon-law student and graduate, 1428.
- Papal chancery/College of Abbreviators: c. 1432–1464.
- Benefices: San Martino a Gangalandi; Borgo San Lorenzo from 1448; the documentary record may include additional lesser revenues, but the consulted sources do not support a complete income ledger.
- Courts and patronage networks: papal court; Este of Ferrara; Malatesta of Rimini; Gonzaga of Mantua; Montefeltro of Urbino; Medici and Rucellai circles in Florence.

### Wealth and property

No audited net worth survives. The following trajectory is supportable:

1. born into a very wealthy merchant lineage;
2. legally vulnerable as an illegitimate son in an exiled family;
3. experienced real inheritance conflict and youthful economic insecurity;
4. gained stable curial salary and benefice income after 1432;
5. by 1464 could survive loss of his chancery salary;
6. remained dependent on elite patronage for large architectural projects but was not merely a paid building contractor.

His writings praise moderate wealth, thrift and active household management while criticizing both sterile avarice and aristocratic display. *Della famiglia* is valuable evidence for patrician economic mentality, not a literal balance sheet of the Alberti firms.

### Technologies, mathematical work and engineering

- linear-perspective construction and instruments for painters;
- proportional measuring devices for sculpture;
- polar-coordinate topographic description of Rome;
- *Ludi rerum mathematicarum*: practical methods for measuring heights, distances, areas and depths;
- mechanical ideas involving weights, levers, hauling and movement;
- attempt to recover the Nemi ships;
- hydraulic, road, fortification and conservation discussions in *De re aedificatoria*;
- optical demonstrations using mirrors and controlled viewing;
- cipher disk and frequency tables.

Claims that Alberti invented every device mentioned in the anonymous *Vita* are unprovable: most drawings and machines are lost, and some mathematical procedures were inherited. His distinctive contribution was often to turn artisanal or ancient procedures into reproducible rules and literary theory.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Politics

Alberti held no elected Florentine magistracy or sovereign office. His politics operated through household theory, papal service, court advice, architecture and literary reflection. Exile made him skeptical of civic triumphalism. *Della famiglia* locates durable agency in kinship and disciplined household government; *De iciarchia* imagines ethical primacy without a straightforward constitutional program. *Momus* satirizes rulers, counselors and court adaptation. *De Porcaria coniuratione* shows limited sympathy for republican ambition while Alberti remained a papal employee.

He was politically networked across rival regimes—papacy, Medici Florence, Este Ferrara, Gonzaga Mantua, Malatesta Rimini and Montefeltro Urbino. That does not prove ideological loyalty to all of them. It reflects the mobile patronage ecology of a curial humanist.

### Religion and possible heterodoxy

Alberti depended on the Church institutionally, wrote a saint's life, designed Christian churches and treated temples as the highest architectural type. Yet his moral and literary works are often classical, ironic and short on explicit Christian doctrine. *Momus* and the *Intercenales* expose clerical and divine-court absurdity through Lucianic satire.

After his death, Cardinal Pietro Barozzi reportedly considered Alberti irreligious; Pietro Dolfin defended the copying of his works by arguing that reticence about piety did not equal hostility to Christ. This is evidence of a **reputational controversy**, not a heresy trial or formal condemnation. Alberti was not charged with heresy in the records consulted.

### Philosophy

His recurrent vocabulary is *virtù*, *fortuna*, labor, moderation, *commoditas*, dignity, utility and *concinnitas*. Stoic strategies are clearest in *Teogenio* and *Della tranquillità dell'animo*, but his thought is not a closed Stoic system. He knew Aristotelian and Platonic traditions and participated in conversations around Marsilio Ficino. Landino's posthumous portrayal of him as a Platonic spokesman is literary and should not override the relatively independent, eclectic character of his surviving works.

### Art and architecture

Alberti's art theory recast painting, sculpture and architecture as intellectual arts accessible to the educated patron and theorist. This social elevation could also distance design from manual execution. His architectural practice typically involved letters, drawings, models, on-site masters and patron negotiation. The resulting authorship is distributed: “Alberti” may mean conceptual scheme, proportional control and façade design rather than personal stonecutting or daily site direction.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, and Financial Questions

### Evidence register

| Matter | Classification | Evidence and limits |
|---|---|---|
| *Philodoxeos* presented as ancient | Documented literary hoax | Alberti encouraged attribution to “Lepidus,” later acknowledged authorship. No known court action or financial victim. |
| Panormita “stole” *Philodoxeos* | Documented unauthorized circulation in biographical tradition | “Stole” means took/circulated the manuscript; the precise legal ownership framework is unknown. |
| Inheritance withheld | Strongly documented family dispute | Exact assets and judgments not reconstructed. |
| Relatives attempted to murder Alberti | Allegation in anonymous *Vita*; some archival circumstantial support claimed by Mancini | No conviction, named defendant or complete proceeding established. |
| Misogyny | Textually documented ideological pattern | Anti-marriage and anti-woman claims recur in erotic/moral dialogues. Context and speaker irony vary; the label describes texts, not a criminal allegation. |
| Porcari conspiracy | No evidence of participation | Alberti wrote sympathetically after the event. He was neither indicted nor punished in evidence consulted. |
| Heterodoxy/irreligion | Posthumous accusation | Barozzi's reported view; rebutted by Dolfin. No heresy prosecution. |
| Papal “intelligence” work | Unsupported if understood as espionage | Chancery service and cipher design had obvious diplomatic-security uses, but no evidence identifies Alberti as a spy or operator of an intelligence network. |
| Architectural authorship inflation | Modern historiographical controversy | Several buildings lack contracts or autographs proving sole authorship; execution was collaborative. |
| “First” claims in cryptography | Partly valid, often overstated | First known European polyalphabetic cipher device and foundational Western treatise; not first cryptanalysis globally. |
| Camera obscura invention | Unverified priority claim | Optical demonstrations are reported; the device and principle predate him in broader history. |
| Plagiarism from Vitruvius | Misleading polemic | *De re aedificatoria* consciously models and transforms Vitruvius; it is neither concealed copying nor a modern-style original research claim. |

No evidence consulted indicates money laundering, bribery, embezzlement, prosecution for corruption, imprisonment, addiction, political violence committed by Alberti, or a criminal conviction.

## Illness, Psychology, Injuries, and Physical Life

Between 1424 and 1428 Alberti suffered illnesses associated in the *Vita* with overwork, memorization and family conflict. *Musca* was supposedly composed during a fever. His writings repeatedly stage anxiety, grief, envy, humiliation, unstable fortune and the desire for mental tranquility. These support an account of recurrent psychological distress but not a retrospective psychiatric diagnosis.

The anonymous *Vita* celebrates exceptional physical ability: riding difficult horses, throwing objects to great height, walking and exercising, standing with feet together and springing over a man, and strong musical sensitivity. Jacob Burckhardt used such passages to construct Alberti as the “all-sided man” of the Renaissance. Because the *Vita* is self-promotional and possibly author-shaped, exact feats belong in the borderland between autobiography and heroic self-myth.

No disabling accident, addiction or chronic terminal disease is documented in the consulted evidence. Cause of death is unknown.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. Alberti as the perfect “universal man”

**Story:** He possessed equal mastery of every art and science, an athletic body, beauty, wit, musical gifts and moral composure—the prototype Renaissance man before Leonardo.

**Appearance and circulation:** Rooted in the anonymous fifteenth-century *Vita*, reinforced by Vasari, canonized by Jacob Burckhardt's *The Civilization of the Renaissance in Italy* (1860), and repeated in museums and survey histories.

**Evidence:** His extraordinary range of authentic writings and projects is real. Individual athletic and mnemonic feats depend largely on encomiastic biography. His practical authorship in painting, sculpture and building is thinner than his theoretical authorship.

**Effect:** The myth preserved his fame but flattened the darker Alberti of exile, family injury, satire, skepticism and unfinished projects.

### 2. The anonymous *Vita* as autobiography

**Story:** Alberti wrote his own life in the third person, producing the first postclassical autobiography.

**Appearance:** The fifteenth-century life survives in manuscripts and was printed in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century collections. Burckhardt and much later scholarship treated it as autobiographical.

**Evidence:** Intimate access, rhetorical self-fashioning and overlap with Alberti's concerns strongly suggest authorial involvement; Janitschek, Pellegrini, Renee Watkins and others raised objections. Recent editors often speak of autobiographical writing while retaining qualifications.

**Effect:** It is the main source of the athletic, psychological and family legends and has made Alberti an unusually self-fashioned Renaissance subject.

### 3. Murderous relatives

**Story:** Alberti's kin, angered by inheritance and perhaps by his writing, plotted his death.

**Appearance:** Anonymous *Vita*; repeated by Mancini and modern biographies.

**Evidence:** Family/property hostility is secure; Mancini cited a potentially supporting document. No complete legal case proves an attempted assassination.

**Effect:** It frames the family theorist as excluded by the very lineage he idealized.

### 4. “Father of Western cryptography” and inventor of an unbreakable cipher

**Story:** Alberti single-handedly founded cryptography and made an unbreakable system.

**Appearance:** Modern cryptographic histories, especially through David Kahn, museums and educational sites.

**Evidence:** The disk, polyalphabetic substitution, code superencipherment and Western frequency discussion are major innovations. “Western” is essential: al-Kindi and later Arabic scholars developed cryptanalysis earlier. No finite historical cipher is absolutely unbreakable; security depends on procedure, keys, message volume and user discipline.

**Effect:** It restored a technical Alberti often missing from art history and linked him to modern information security.

### 5. Alberti invented the Vigenère cipher

**Story:** His 1467 disk is the first version of the cipher later called Vigenère.

**Appearance:** Simplified cipher histories and popular diagrams.

**Evidence:** He founded the European polyalphabetic branch, but the tabular/repeating-key genealogy runs through Trithemius and especially Bellaso; Vigenère published an autokey system in 1586. The systems are related but operationally distinct.

**Effect:** The simplification gives Alberti priority but obscures Bellaso and the misnaming of the “Vigenère cipher.”

### 6. Alberti personally built every attributed monument

**Story:** He functioned as a modern architect-contractor and directly built Palazzo Rucellai, the Tempio, Santa Maria Novella and the Mantuan churches.

**Appearance:** Vasari, guidebooks, survey textbooks and monument labels.

**Evidence:** Alberti often worked through letters, drawings, models and masters such as Rossellino, Matteo de' Pasti and Fancelli. Some major attributions lack contemporary contracts. Sant'Andrea began after his death.

**Effect:** It established the architect as an individual author while concealing collaborative construction and uncertain evidence.

### 7. A Florentine tomb in Santa Croce

**Story:** Alberti lies in Santa Croce among famous Florentines.

**Appearance:** Modern popular biographies and memorial websites.

**Evidence against:** His testament asked for initial burial at Sant'Agostino in Rome and eventual transfer to his father's tomb at Sant'Antonio in Padua. The Roman tomb disappeared after rebuilding; no document proves the transfer to Padua. The DBI explicitly says no trace remains and no transport is documented. A Santa Croce burial should be treated as unsupported confusion with later commemoration.

**Effect:** It symbolically repatriates the exile into Florence's pantheon.

### 8. Alberti in Mantegna's *Camera degli Sposi*

**Story:** The older man in dark red whispering to Ludovico Gonzaga is Alberti.

**Appearance:** Twentieth-century iconographic proposals, notably Eugene Johnson.

**Evidence:** The age, court connection and intellectual role make the identification possible; there is no identifying inscription. It remains disputed.

**Effect:** It supplies a vivid late-life portrait and embeds Alberti in the Gonzaga court image.

## Death and Testament

Alberti made his will in Rome on **19 April 1472**, “on the eve of death” in the DBI's phrasing. A replacement at San Martino a Gangalandi was presented by 25 April because the benefice was vacant through his death. The exact day is variously printed as 20 or 25 April; the surviving administrative bracket is more secure than either popular date.

He requested initial burial in **Sant'Agostino, Rome**, followed by transfer to his father's tomb in **Sant'Antonio, Padua**. Sant'Agostino was rebuilt from 1479, and no trace of his tomb remains. No document proves the body reached Padua. Modern claims of burial in Santa Croce, Florence, conflict with the testamentary record and should not be repeated as fact.

No documented cause of death was found. He was about sixty-eight.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Fifteenth and sixteenth centuries

Alberti's works first circulated in manuscripts among courts, chancery officials, artists and humanists. *De re aedificatoria* appeared in 1485; a Latin *Opera* in 1499; *Momus* in 1520; architectural translations and editions followed; Cosimo Bartoli's 1568 *Opuscoli morali* printed *De cifris*. Print redistributed a corpus that Alberti himself had designed for elite manuscript circulation.

His architectural vocabulary shaped Bramante and the classical High Renaissance, while Santa Maria Novella's volutes offered a durable church-façade solution. Sant'Andrea's monumental single nave and chapel-buttress system became important for later church design. His concept of the architect as learned designer reinforced the separation of intellectual authorship from craft execution.

### Vasari to Burckhardt

Vasari included Alberti among artists but emphasized learning and virtue. In the nineteenth century Burckhardt made the anonymous *Vita* a central exhibit in his portrait of Renaissance individualism: Alberti became the physically, intellectually and aesthetically complete modern person. This canonical image helped recover him but treated self-presentation as social fact.

### Modern disciplinary afterlives

- **Art history:** foundational theorist of perspective, composition and the dignity of painting.
- **Architecture:** author of the first printed Renaissance architectural treatise and designer of a small group of exceptionally influential monuments.
- **Literary history:** major bilingual prose writer, satirist and experimental dialogist; the rediscovery of *Momus* and the *Intercenales* displaced the uniformly serene “humanist” portrait.
- **Linguistics:** author or strongest candidate for the earliest surviving grammar of Tuscan/Italian; defender and experimenter of the vernacular at the Certame Coronario.
- **History of science:** practical geometry, optics, surveying, mapping, instruments and controlled representation.
- **Cryptology:** founder of the European polyalphabetic-device tradition; predecessor, but not simple inventor, of the Bellaso/Vigenère family.
- **Urban thought:** architecture as a technology of civic order, memory and government.

Recent scholarship stresses exile, illegitimacy, family conflict, irony, pessimism, material practice and distributed authorship. Alberti remains a “universal man,” but now more often as an unstable, strategic self-construction than as a frictionless list of accomplishments.

## Atlas Connections

### Aristotle

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Alberti repeatedly invoked and discussed Aristotle as an authority in works including *De re aedificatoria*, while adapting Aristotelian ideas about civic life, ethics, causation, nature, and the organization of knowledge to his own arguments. The connection is textual, not personal: Aristotle died more than seventeen centuries before Alberti’s birth.

### Cicero

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Alberti wrote extensively in dialogue with Cicero’s rhetorical, ethical, and philosophical works. Ciceronian models inform such texts as *De commodis litterarum atque incommodis*, *I libri della famiglia*, *Della tranquillità dell’animo*, and *De re aedificatoria*. Alberti’s engagement involved adaptation and occasional criticism, not merely imitation.

### Diogenes of Sinope

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** During the 1460s Alberti composed seven Latin letters presented as addressed to Diogenes, the *Epistulae septem Diogeni inscriptae*, which he sent to Francesco Griffolini. These pseudo-Diogenic letters use the ancient Cynic as an imagined interlocutor for moral reflection; they do not document a transmitted ancient correspondence.

### Lorenzo de’ Medici (“Il Magnifico”)

- **[T3]** **Documented fact:** Around 1460 Alberti dedicated *Trivia senatoria*, his instruction on public speaking and civic rhetoric, to the young Lorenzo de’ Medici. This is direct, one-sided written engagement between two roster members.
- **[T5]** **Documented fact:** Alberti and Lorenzo had a personal relationship in Alberti’s later years. Alberti guided Lorenzo, Bernardo Rucellai, and Donato Acciaiuoli through the Roman Forum in 1471. Cristoforo Landino’s *Disputationes Camaldulenses* also places them together at Camaldoli in 1468; modern scholarship considers an underlying meeting probable, although Landino’s polished speeches are literary compositions rather than transcripts.

### Plato

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Alberti cited and discussed Plato in *De re aedificatoria* and elsewhere, drawing upon Platonic political, cosmological, and aesthetic arguments while incorporating them into an eclectic rather than doctrinally Platonist philosophy. Landino later made Alberti a principal Platonic speaker in the *Disputationes Camaldulenses*, but the specific doctrines placed in his mouth there are a **reported fact/literary representation**, not secure evidence of Alberti’s exact spoken words.

### Virgil

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Alberti invoked Virgil as an ancient literary authority in his writings. A more elaborate connection appears in Landino’s posthumously composed *Disputationes Camaldulenses* (c. 1472–1473), which represents Alberti expounding allegorical meanings in Virgil’s *Aeneid* during a gathering set at Camaldoli in 1468. That Virgilian exposition is a **reported fact/literary representation**: it may preserve the subject of a real discussion, but it cannot be treated as a verbatim record.

No secure direct crossing was found with other tempting roster candidates. In particular, shared intellectual themes do not by themselves establish personal contact between Alberti and Nicholas of Cusa; Alberti’s cipher does not establish that he knew al-Kindi’s earlier Arabic cryptanalysis; and the later sequence from Alberti through Trithemius to Vigenère is a technological genealogy, not documented correspondence, meeting, or passage of one identifiable physical object among those men.
