# Manuela Sáenz de Vergara y Aizpuru: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

Manuela Sáenz left no authenticated autobiography and published no work during her lifetime. Her surviving record is dispersed among correspondence, legal and notarial files, government papers, newspapers, memoirs, and later recollections. Many intimate letters and personal papers were reportedly destroyed after her death. Modern editions have also mixed authentic documents with disputed or demonstrably anachronistic material.

This dossier uses the following labels:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary records or mutually corroborating primary evidence.
- **Reported fact:** found in a credible contemporary or scholarly source but not independently confirmed here.
- **Allegation:** a contemporary accusation whose truth was not judicially established.
- **Disputed claim:** substantial disagreement exists among sources.
- **Rumor or myth:** unsupported or primarily legendary material.
- **Literary representation:** fiction, drama, film, poetry, or ideologically shaped commemoration rather than biographical evidence.

Pamela S. Murray’s archival biography, *For Glory and Bolívar: The Remarkable Life of Manuela Sáenz* (2008/2009), is the principal modern synthesis consulted. It draws on Ecuadorian and Peruvian notarial records, diplomatic correspondence, newspapers, litigation, and authenticated letters. Its conclusions are identified where they correct entrenched popular stories.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name:** Manuela Sáenz de Vergara y Aizpuru.

**Common forms:**

- Manuela Sáenz
- Manuela Sáenz Aizpuru
- Manuela Sáenz de Thorne
- Manuelita Sáenz
- Señora Sáenz de Thorne
- “La Libertadora del Libertador”
- “La Libertadora”
- “La Generala”
- “La Coronela”
- “La Caballeresa del Sol”
- “La amable loca” or “querida loca,” derived from Bolívar’s affectionate language
- “La insepulta de Paita,” principally a posthumous poetic designation associated with Pablo Neruda
- “La difunta,” in Paita memory and Neruda’s poetic treatment
- “Amazon,” “new Amazon,” “masculine woman,” “marimacho,” “mujer-hombre,” “loca,” “prostitute,” and “libertine”: hostile, satirical, or gendered descriptions circulated by political opponents and later writers, not formal names

**Birth:** Usually given as 27 December 1797, Quito, Presidency/Audiencia of Quito, Viceroyalty of New Granada, Spanish Empire.

**Birth-date qualification:** The archival literature regularly uses 1797, sometimes with a question mark. Some catalogs give 1798. The precise evidentiary basis for 27 December is less secure than its repetition suggests because she entered the record as an *expósita*, or foundling, and the circumstances of her birth were deliberately concealed.

**Death:** 23 November 1856, approximately 6:00 p.m., Paita, Peru.

**Cause of death:** Probably an infectious disease during a regional epidemic, traditionally identified as diphtheria. Her June 1856 letter reported severe illness affecting her face, sinuses, or throat. No medical certificate has been located. “Diphtheria” is therefore a **probable but not medically proven diagnosis**.

**Burial:** According to Paita tradition, she was placed in a common epidemic grave outside the regular parish cemetery. The precise burial site is unknown. A late-twentieth-century Peruvian investigation did not locate identifiable remains.

**Nationality and political identity:** Born a Spanish imperial subject in Quito; subsequently associated with independent Peru and Gran Colombia. She increasingly identified politically as a Colombian and Bolivarian patriot. Later national traditions have claimed her as Ecuadorian, Peruvian, Colombian, Venezuelan, and broadly Latin American.

**Occupations and functions:**

- Elite household and commercial administrator
- Attorney-in-fact for James Thorne
- Trader and retail buyer
- Property owner
- Patriot organizer, fundraiser, supplier, and probable recruiter
- Member of the female section of Peru’s Order of the Sun
- Personal archivist and political confidante of Simón Bolívar
- Political intermediary and intercessor
- Informant and correspondent
- Political hostess
- Exiled political partisan
- Retailer, tobacco seller, boarding-house operator, and translator or letter writer in Paita

She held no securely documented, salaried senior civil office. Descriptions of her as an official “first lady” of Colombia, Peru, or Bolivia are retrospective constructions and should not be treated as nineteenth-century appointments.

## Family and Ancestry

### Maternal family

Manuela’s mother was **María Joaquina de Aizpuru**, also rendered Joaquina Aizpuru. She was the unmarried daughter—and second-youngest of ten children—of:

- **Mateo José de Aizpuru y Montero** (1717–1803), born in Panama, an attorney or *relator* attached to the Audiencia of Quito.
- **Gregoria Sierra Pambley**, a native of Quito.

The Aizpurus were a respectable urban Creole family living in an old and prestigious quarter of Quito.

**Documented fact:** Joaquina became pregnant outside marriage through a relationship with Simón Sáenz de Vergara. The pregnancy and birth were managed privately to protect her and her family’s public honor. Manuela was consequently recorded and treated as an *hija expósita*.

Joaquina entrusted the infant to a nun at the Convent of La Concepción after an earlier effort to find a household placement. Surviving evidence indicates that Joaquina was dead by 1804 and may have died during or soon after childbirth. No evidence establishes that Manuela consciously knew or met her mother.

In 1821 Manuela initiated legal steps to prove this maternity and obtain her share of the estate of Mateo Aizpuru. The testimony of the Mercedarian friar **Mariano Ontañeda**, who knew both parents and had assisted in placing the infant, disclosed the previously concealed maternal identity. Manuela eventually negotiated with her maternal aunt **Ignacia Aizpuru** and secured part of the inheritance.

### Paternal family

Her father was **Simón Sáenz de Vergara y Yedra** (1755–1825), born in Villasur de Herreros, Burgos, Spain. He reached Spanish America around 1780.

In 1781 he married by proxy **Juana María del Campo Larrahondo y Valencia**, an aristocratic woman of Popayán. The marriage connected him to:

- **Francisco del Campo y Larrahondo**, magistrate, administrator of Popayán’s royal aguardiente monopoly, and knight of the Order of Charles III.
- **María Ignacia Valencia Fernández del Castillo**
- The prominent and wealthy Valencia family of Popayán.

Simón became a successful merchant in the trade between New Granada and Quito. His holdings included:

- A textile mill at Guaytacama
- Four houses in Quito
- A retail shop near the main plaza, on or near present-day Guayaquil Street
- Connections to regional import and export commerce

He also held public and bureaucratic positions under the colonial order.

Although Manuela was born outside his marriage, he supported her privately and financially. He supplied the 1,000-peso minimum dowry required for her admission and maintenance at La Concepción and later gave 8,000 pesos toward her marriage settlement.

### Half-siblings

Simón Sáenz and Juana María del Campo had at least six legitimate children. The best documented in Manuela’s life include:

- **José María Sáenz del Campo**, an officer in the royalist Numancia Regiment who moved toward the patriot side, later joined the Colombian army, became a lieutenant colonel, and served as an aide-de-camp to Bolívar. He participated in Ecuadorian opposition politics and died after the failed conflicts surrounding the Sociedad del Quiteño Libre.
- **María Josefa Sáenz del Campo**, mentioned in family records.
- Other legitimate half-siblings are less visible in the surviving materials consulted.

Manuela’s political enemies later claimed that she returned toward Quito in 1835 to avenge José María’s death. That was an **official allegation**, not a fact established by trial.

### Household dependents and enslaved people

Manuela grew up and lived in a slaveholding social order. Her father’s household included enslaved people and Indigenous servants.

As an adult in Lima, she possessed authority to buy, sell, rent, or manumit enslaved persons under James Thorne’s power of attorney. Surviving documentation records that:

- In 1820–1821 she bought and sold several enslaved household workers, including **Trinidad** and **Manuel Solís**.
- In March 1822 she emancipated **Juana Rosa**, then approximately one year old, the daughter of a woman in her household.

Later national and fictional narratives often describe **Jonathás** and **Nathán** as enslaved childhood playmates whom her father gave her. Documentary support is uneven. Jonathás is better attested as an adult servant and close companion; Nathán is much less visible in authentic records. Statements that both accompanied Manuela throughout her entire life are therefore **reported or traditional claims**, not equally documented facts.

A different **Juana Rosa**, or possibly the same person later represented imprecisely in the sources, became a trusted attendant in Paita. She read letters with Manuela, assisted her after disability, and died two days before her in November 1856.

### Marriage and children

Manuela married **James Thorne**, also called Jaime Thorne or Thornet, on 27 July 1817 at the parish church of San Sebastián in Lima.

No children of the marriage are documented. Claims that she was sterile, suffered abortions, bore a secret child, or had a child with Bolívar are unsupported in the reliable record consulted.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1797–1817

Manuela’s birth outside marriage shaped her early circumstances but did not condemn her to poverty. Her parents belonged to prosperous urban families, and her father furnished material support.

She was placed under the protection of a nun in **La Concepción**, Quito’s oldest, wealthiest, and most socially prestigious female convent. The complex included living quarters, workshops, gardens, a bakery, and educational spaces. Its residents reproduced the social hierarchy of colonial Quito: elite women and girls occupied a different position from servants, enslaved workers, and poorer dependents.

### Education

No school curriculum or list of named teachers has been found. Her education probably included the customary elite female curriculum:

- Catholic doctrine and devotional practice
- Reading and writing
- Needlework and household administration
- Music or other genteel accomplishments
- Social discipline appropriate to an elite household

Her later letters demonstrate literacy, political acuity, command of correspondence, and familiarity with accounting and property business. Claims that she received formal military training, mastered several languages in childhood, or studied philosophy in a systematic academy are not established.

She apparently spent time outside the convent in paternal or family environments and may have visited family properties such as Cataguango. Later biographies supply elaborate childhood adventures with Jonathás and Nathán, but the archival basis for many of these scenes is weak.

### The alleged convent escape

A frequently repeated story states that at approximately seventeen Manuela fled the Convent of Santa Catalina with a Spanish army officer, **Fausto D’Elhuyar**, was expelled, and returned after abandonment or scandal.

**Status: disputed claim or legend.** Versions differ on the convent, officer, date, circumstances, and whether any sexual relationship occurred. Some versions confuse La Concepción with Santa Catalina. No decisive contemporary documentation establishing the elopement was identified in the sources consulted. It became important in hostile biographies as an explanation for alleged precocious promiscuity and in romantic biographies as proof of rebellion against convention.

## Marriage, Lima, Commerce, and Political Formation, 1817–1822

### James Thorne

Thorne was born in **Aylesbury, England**, and was roughly twenty years older than Manuela. He was not securely documented as a physician, despite frequent popular descriptions of him as “Doctor Thorne.” He was principally a shipping merchant, commercial agent, and estate administrator.

His business interests included:

- Pacific trade between Lima–Callao, Valparaíso, Guayaquil, and Panama
- British cloth and manufactured goods
- At least one or two vessels, including the brigantine *Columbia*
- Commercial relations with Lima’s Creole elite
- Administration of **Hacienda Huaito**, one of Domingo Orué y Mirones’s estates and a highly productive sugar plantation in Chancay Province

Manuela’s father probably regarded the marriage as beneficial to his commercial network. The union also offered Manuela public marital status and elite respectability.

### Financial arrangements

Her father supplied an 8,000-peso marriage settlement. This was substantial but close to the average Lima dowry later calculated for the social sector around 1820.

The money became the subject of decades of financial difficulty. It was held or used by Thorne, and Manuela later sought its recovery with interest.

### Commercial work

In March 1820 Thorne gave Manuela a broad general power of attorney. It authorized her to:

- Collect debts
- Employ accountants
- Buy, sell, or rent property
- Buy, sell, or lease enslaved people
- Bring lawsuits
- Act as executor if Thorne died
- Conduct his business during his absences

She supervised business in Lima and undertook transactions on her own account. A May 1822 receipt records her purchase of merchandise valued at 3,643 pesos, including damask, brocade, tulle dresses and shawls, imitation pearls, cashmere scarves, and stockings, probably for resale through Quito or Guayaquil connections.

She also opened proceedings to recover her maternal inheritance. These facts complicate the later image of a woman wholly dependent on Bolívar: before meeting him, she already possessed substantial experience in commerce, litigation, credit, and household management.

### Patriot activity

Lima remained a major royalist center when Manuela arrived. By 1818–1820 she was moving among pro-independence circles.

**Documented or strongly supported activity:**

- Association with the patriot **Rosa Campuzano**
- Probable participation in efforts to “seduce”—politically recruit—soldiers of the approximately 700-man royalist Numancia Regiment
- Connection to the regiment through José María Sáenz
- Material support for José de San Martín’s patriot government
- Collection and donation of cloth and clothing for patriot troops
- Participation in neighborhood donation drives

Claims that she single-handedly converted the Numancia Regiment or directed its defection are exaggerations. The campaign involved a network of officers, conspirators, and women.

### Order of the Sun

San Martín’s government founded the Order of the Sun as a republican honor society. Its women’s section recognized 112 “worthy patriots.” The *Gaceta del Gobierno del Perú Independiente* of 23 January 1822 listed Manuela among women distinguished for patriotic sentiment.

Recipients wore a red-and-white silk sash and a gold medal bearing the government’s arms and a dedication to female patriotism. Female members did not receive the offices and pensions available to men, although their male relatives could receive preference for appointments.

The date is sometimes given as 8 October 1821; the official published roll appeared in January 1822. “Caballeresa del Sol” is historically grounded, while later military rank claims require separate analysis.

## Return to Quito and Relationship with Simón Bolívar, 1822–1823

Manuela returned to Quito in 1822, partly to settle the Aizpuru inheritance and probably in association with her father or half-brother. She and Thorne were not yet necessarily irretrievably separated; surviving correspondence shows that he continued to address her affectionately in December 1822 and asked her to return.

### First meeting with Bolívar

After Antonio José de Sucre’s victory at Pichincha, Bolívar entered Quito ceremonially on **16 June 1822**. The city held official and private festivities, including a ball at the Larrea family home.

Manuela probably met Bolívar during his June visit and may have obtained an audience concerning her inheritance. José María Sáenz, newly attached to the Colombian army and later one of Bolívar’s aides, may have introduced them.

The exact date and setting of the first romantic encounter cannot be proved. The familiar story that she threw a laurel wreath from a balcony and that it struck Bolívar is **romantic legend**, not securely documented contemporary fact.

The relationship became sexual and emotionally intense during 1822. Bolívar was a widower who had maintained numerous relationships. Manuela was married, and adultery exposed her to legal, religious, and reputational risks.

### Separation from Thorne

Thorne attempted to recover the marriage. His letter of 11 December 1822 called her his beloved wife and “chichita,” insisted his love was firm, and sought reunion. Manuela declined.

In a later letter she told him that she could not return because her relationship with Bolívar constituted a profound emotional commitment. The marriage was never formally dissolved. She continued to use her married status when legally advantageous, and Thorne continued at times to recognize her rights.

The simple formula that she “abandoned her husband for Bolívar” is broadly accurate but incomplete: their relationship also involved commercial incompatibilities, separations, Thorne’s absences, and later allegations that Thorne maintained other women.

### Inheritance

On 31 July 1823 Manuela reached a transaction with her aunt Ignacia Aizpuru over her maternal rights. Part of the settlement included or helped establish her interest in **Hacienda Cataguango**, a property that later became central to her finances.

## Bolívar’s Staff and the Peruvian Campaign, 1823–1826

Around October 1823 Manuela returned to Peru. By December she had become a member of Bolívar’s permanent entourage and his **personal archivist**.

### Archivist and political associate

Her work likely included:

- Receiving, preserving, and ordering papers
- Protecting confidential correspondence
- Forwarding information and petitions
- Acting as a link between Bolívar and supporters in Quito, Lima, and elsewhere
- Interceding for deserters, prisoners, petitioners, and acquaintances
- Maintaining social and political contacts

Bolívar sometimes denied that she “meddled” in government, but his own correspondence acknowledged exceptions and her activity as an intercessor. The available record supports **informal political influence**, not control of state policy.

### Travel with the army

In 1824 she traveled through the Andes in the wake of Bolívar’s forces. Her movements included Lima, the northern and central Peruvian highlands, Huaraz or its region, and Jauja.

**Battle of Junín, 6 August 1824:** Her precise location is uncertain. There is no conclusive evidence that she fought in the engagement.

**Battle of Ayacucho, 9 December 1824:** Murray’s reconstruction finds that Manuela had stopped following the army after Junín, turned toward the coast, stayed at Jauja, and rejoined Bolívar later. She was back in or near Lima when news of Ayacucho arrived.

Accordingly:

- Her physical presence in combat at Ayacucho is a **disputed claim unsupported by known archival evidence**.
- Stories that she commanded cavalry, cared for wounded soldiers on the battlefield, or received a battlefield promotion from Sucre depend chiefly on later anecdote and disputed documents.
- Boussingault’s much later memoir states that she showed military courage with Sucre at Ayacucho, but his account is isolated, retrospective, and demonstrably prone to sensationalism.

### Alleged military ranks

She is variously called captain, colonel, or hussar officer in later accounts. Some purported correspondence presents Sucre recommending her promotion and Bolívar authorizing it.

The secure record establishes attachment to Bolívar’s staff and association with the Colombian army, but not a conventional commission commanding combat troops. “Colonel” and “Generala” became commemorative realities later; their use as precise ranks in 1824–1825 remains disputed.

### Political context

Bolívar governed Peru with extraordinary authority granted by Congress. His government defeated remaining royalist forces but became increasingly controversial because of:

- Preference for Colombian officers
- Executions of alleged traitors
- Pressure on electoral and constitutional processes
- The proposed Bolivian Constitution
- A lifetime presidency and restricted indirect suffrage
- Plans for an Andean federation

Manuela aligned herself with Bolívar’s centralist project and his loyalists. She was not simply a nationalist independence supporter; she became a partisan of a specific post-independence political formation.

### Personal tensions

Their relationship included repeated separations, jealousy, and Bolívar’s other affairs. In 1824 Manuela heard reports of his involvement with the young Manuelita Madroño. Her letters complain about neglect and infrequent correspondence. The surviving exchange nevertheless shows sustained attachment, political cooperation, humor, quarrels, and mutual reliance.

## Lima Crisis, Quito, and Bogotá, 1826–1828

### Anti-Bolivarian reaction in Peru

By 1826 resentment of Bolívar’s rule had intensified. In January 1827 Colombian troops stationed in Lima rebelled, and the Peruvian regime associated with Bolívar collapsed.

Manuela actively opposed the anti-Bolivarian movement. Accounts describe her distributing propaganda, monitoring enemies, and confronting soldiers. The particularly vivid story that she rode armed into a rebellious barracks in male uniform is **legendary or weakly corroborated**.

She was arrested or detained and expelled from Peru. The episode demonstrated that authorities regarded her as a political actor, regardless of whether later heroic descriptions exaggerated her methods.

### Quito, 1827

She returned to Quito and dealt with family property. Cataguango required capital, labor, tools, and cattle; she authorized investment in it but later complained of poor administration and missing revenues.

Her public behavior—riding, wearing military-style clothing on some occasions, maintaining a politically active household, and refusing conventional marital seclusion—continued to attract admiration and hostility.

### Bogotá

By 1828 she was established in Bogotá, close to Bolívar and deeply embedded among the Bolivarian network. Her house or rooms served as:

- A social center
- A site for political conversation
- A channel for petitions and information
- A place where military officers, officials, clergy, and foreign visitors encountered one another

Associates included Daniel Florencio O’Leary, Luis Perú de Lacroix, Rafael Urdaneta, José París, Juan José Flores, Bolívar’s nephew Fernando, and numerous Colombian officers and political supporters.

Her opponents were principally Santander-aligned liberals, journalists, lawyers, and student activists who opposed Bolívar’s centralization and dictatorship.

### Clothing and gender presentation

Contemporaries reported that she sometimes wore trousers, a military jacket, cape, or officer’s uniform and rode astride. These practices are credible and were politically symbolic, though later representations often imply she lived continuously as a man or held a male identity. The record supports strategic or recreational cross-dressing and military presentation, not a definite conclusion about modern gender identity.

## The September Conspiracy, 1828

### Background

Opposition to Bolívar intensified after the failure of the Convention of Ocaña and his assumption of dictatorial powers under the Organic Decree of 27 August 1828. Radical clubs discussed tyrannicide.

On the night of **25 September 1828**, conspirators gathered at Luis Vargas Tejada’s home and divided into groups. One group attacked the presidential palace in Bogotá.

### Manuela’s actions

**Documented fact:** Manuela was with Bolívar and helped him escape. Contemporary accounts, including those of Bolívar’s nephew Fernando and British diplomatic correspondence, acknowledge that she saved his life.

She:

- Awakened or warned Bolívar
- Urged him not to confront the attackers
- Helped direct him toward a window or escape route
- Delayed or misdirected the assailants
- Endured blows or rough treatment during the intrusion

Bolívar escaped and hid beneath or near a bridge until loyal forces regained control.

The official government account issued immediately afterward omitted or minimized her role. Later Bolivarian memory expanded it.

### “Libertadora del Libertador”

Bolívar is traditionally said to have called her “the Liberator of the Liberator” after the attack. The phrase accurately expresses the historical outcome and became her defining title. Its exact first wording and documentary appearance are less certain than the underlying rescue.

### Repression and responsibility

The government responded by banning secret societies, restricting movement, removing suspected teachers, trying conspirators, and executing several defendants.

Admiral José Prudencio Padilla was executed despite a lack of evidence tying him directly to the palace attack. Bolívar’s government also pursued Francisco de Paula Santander.

**Documented qualification:** Santander knew members of the opposition and was aware of conspiratorial activity, but direct proof that he masterminded the assassination attempt was lacking. He was sentenced to death, and Bolívar commuted the sentence to exile.

Manuela remained a committed enemy of Santander and interviewed or monitored persons connected to the conspiracy. Her political hostility was active, not merely romantic loyalty.

## Political Radicalization and Monarchist Project, 1829–1830

Manuela supported the Bolivarian regime as Gran Colombia fractured. She participated in loyalist publicity and political mobilization and used her household as a Bolivarian center.

### Monarchy

By 1829 some Bolívar supporters considered a constitutional monarchy as a solution to regional disintegration. Manuela was involved in circles supporting or discussing this project.

This is a **documented but often omitted controversy**. Her politics cannot be reduced to modern democratic republicanism. She favored strong central authority, distrusted many liberal opponents, defended Bolívar’s exceptional powers, and was open to monarchical or quasi-monarchical arrangements intended to preserve union.

Bolívar never crowned himself and publicly distanced himself from kingship, but his lifetime-presidency model and his associates’ negotiations created credible fears among opponents.

### Propaganda conflict

Santanderist and liberal newspapers portrayed Manuela as:

- A prostitute
- An intrusive mistress
- An “Amazon”
- A masculine and disorderly woman
- An alien manipulator of the president
- A threat to republican liberty

Bolivarian responses portrayed her as:

- A loyal patriot
- A victim of factional hatred
- The courageous savior of Bolívar
- A model of devotion

Neither pole adequately describes her: she exercised real political agency but held no evidence-supported power equivalent to cabinet or military command.

### Bolívar’s resignation and departure

Bolívar resigned in March 1830 and left Bogotá on 8 May, intending to reach Cartagena and possibly leave the country. Manuela remained in Bogotá, apparently expecting his political return.

She publicly defended him and confronted symbolic attacks. One incident involved an effigy of Bolívar or Santander, depending on the episode and source. She was associated with mock executions and demonstrations. On Corpus Christi in June 1830, armed authorities anticipated that she might attack an anti-Bolivarian display.

### Deaths of Sucre and Bolívar

Antonio José de Sucre was assassinated at Berruecos on 4 June 1830. Bolívar died near Santa Marta on 17 December 1830.

Luis Perú de Lacroix wrote to Manuela on 18 December describing Bolívar as near death, apparently before final confirmation reached her. Rafael Urdaneta officially announced the death in Bogotá in January 1831.

Claims that Manuela attempted suicide by allowing a snake to bite her or by another method are repeated in popular biographies but are **not firmly established by contemporary evidence**. The authentic record supports severe grief, withdrawal to Guaduas, rheumatic illness, and increasing cigar smoking.

## Life in New Granada after Bolívar, 1831–1834

Manuela remained a conspicuous Bolivarian after the collapse of the union. Her political and financial position deteriorated.

She lived in Bogotá and, from September 1832, rented a *quinta* outside the city. She maintained correspondence, preserved Bolívar papers, watched political developments, and received allies.

### Earlier indictment

A criminal indictment dated 17 July 1830 accused her in connection with public disorder and political defiance. Its enforcement was delayed or suspended amid regime changes.

### Santander’s return

Francisco de Paula Santander returned from exile and became president of New Granada. Newspapers allied with his government revived Manuela’s case.

The paper *El Cachaco*, secretly associated with Lorenzo María Lleras and Florentino González, published the 1830 indictment in September 1833 and demanded that police expel her. It accused her of mocking government and allowing troublemakers to meet at her home.

### Sardá conspiracy

In 1833–1834 General **José María Sardá** led or was associated with a Bolivarian conspiracy against the Santander government. Manuela knew members of Bolivarian opposition circles.

**Allegation:** Officials and hostile newspapers linked her to subversive activity.

**Evidentiary result:** No completed public trial established her role. Her expulsion functioned as preventive political policing as much as criminal punishment.

### Arrest and expulsion

In early 1834 authorities moved against her. A United States diplomat reported that a guard of twenty soldiers spent an entire day arresting her without killing her and called her “as brave as Caesar.” British minister William Turner interpreted the affair as evidence of the Santander government’s intolerance and its willingness to deny opponents judicial process.

Around early April 1834 she was forced aboard a vessel for **Kingston, Jamaica**. The order was executive and political; she did not receive a full adjudication of the allegations.

---

## Jamaica and Attempted Return to Ecuador, 1834–1835

In Kingston she was isolated, short of money, and dependent on correspondence. Her financial survival required access to properties and debts in Ecuador.

She appealed to **Juan José Flores**, Ecuador’s former or outgoing president and an old Bolivarian associate. Flores had earlier helped secure her maternal inheritance. He arranged papers and assistance for her return.

She reached Guayaquil around October 1835. Flores received her and issued a safe-conduct and letters supporting her journey to Quito.

### Rocafuerte’s exclusion order

President **Vicente Rocafuerte** revoked or refused her permission to proceed. His correspondence supplies unusually explicit evidence of official fear and misogynistic hostility.

Rocafuerte described her in terms including:

- Proud
- Insubordinate
- Brazen
- Ambitious
- Audacious
- Associated with “prostitution”

He feared that she would revive the **Sociedad del Quiteño Libre**, avenge José María, or organize opposition to his administration. A minister alleged that the Quiteño Libre conspirators had rejoiced at news of her return and regarded her as the leader capable of mobilizing them.

These were **government allegations and preventive-security assessments**, not findings from a trial. The surviving record does not establish that she arrived with an operational insurrection plan.

She was turned back toward Guayaquil and prevented from returning to Quito. She then went to **Paita**, a port in northern Peru.

## Paita Exile, Political Correspondence, and Finances, 1835–1856

Paita became her longest continuous residence. It was a Pacific port serving merchants, naval traffic, whalers, sailors, travelers, and nearby Piura.

### Initial political activity

Manuela remained politically informed and communicated extensively with Juan José Flores. She:

- Supplied news from Peru
- Reported military and political developments
- Used contacts in Lima
- Followed Andrés de Santa Cruz, Agustín Gamarra, Manuel Ignacio de Vivanco, and other regional leaders
- Supported Flores and his family
- Discussed Ecuadorian opposition networks
- Served as an informal intelligence and correspondence node

Her letters reveal political skepticism as well as loyalty. She mocked Peru’s cycles of civil war as a “farce” or “madmen’s dance” and regarded many caudillos as unimpressive or contemptible.

After Flores’s overthrow in Ecuador’s March Revolution of 1845, her active political role diminished. Around 1850 she wrote that no one spoke to her of politics and that she no longer intervened, although she continued corresponding with Ecuadorian political figures.

### Property and income

Her finances were not a simple descent from aristocratic wealth to absolute destitution. They consisted of shrinking assets, unpaid debts, credit, litigation, and small business income.

Assets and claims included:

- **Hacienda Cataguango**, inherited through her maternal family
- A Quito house and retail shop, probably inherited from her father
- Goods, furniture, paintings, carpets, dishes, and textiles
- Debts owed to her by buyers and administrators
- Her 8,000-peso marriage settlement
- Possible marital property and widow’s rights in Thorne’s estate

She sold Cataguango in or around 1837 for approximately 6,000 pesos. By 1843 she had received only about 4,000, leaving a balance of roughly 1,700–2,000 pesos depending on expenses and accounting. The buyer, identified as Señora Gangotena, delayed payment.

Her estate administrators were unreliable. Manuela repeatedly complained that she received no revenue and could not make debtors answer.

### Work in Paita

To support herself, she reportedly:

- Operated a small retail concern or tobacco shop
- Sold tobacco, cigars, sweets, foodstuffs, or imported goods
- Took in boarders
- Used servants to assist with household and commercial activity
- Translated or wrote letters for English-speaking and North American sailors and whalers
- Assisted travelers with correspondence and local contacts

The often-reproduced sign “Tobacco. English spoken. Manuela Sáenz” is evocative but its exact documentary origin is uncertain.

She was frequently short of cash and relied on loans and credit, but “beggar” or wholly destitute descriptions exaggerate. She maintained a household, correspondents, social influence, and some property claims.

### James Thorne’s death and estate

Thorne continued living at Hacienda Huaito and apparently had intimate relationships with other women, including **Buenaventura Concha**. He was murdered in February 1847 during rural violence in Chancay Province.

Manuela learned of the death in July 1847 and experienced significant distress. On 29 November she granted power of attorney to Lima lawyer **Cayetano Freyre** to:

- Pursue those responsible for the killing
- Claim maintenance
- Recover her 8,000 pesos and interest
- Assert rights to marital gains and the estate

Thorne’s executor **Manuel Escobar** resisted. In 1848 litigation he attacked Manuela’s reputation and alleged marital or sexual misconduct.

Freyre replied that:

- Thorne himself had respected her rights.
- Had he believed her adultery legally actionable, he could have sought separation.
- Thorne’s own infidelities and relationship at Huaito contributed to the marriage breakdown.
- The 8,000 pesos constituted a debt owed to her, whether or not technically classified as dowry.

The final outcome is not established in the surviving evidence reviewed by Murray. Thus, claims that Manuela recovered the full money—or received nothing at all—cannot be confirmed.

### Disability and health

By January 1842 she reported inability to move normally. Freyre later testified that she had dislocated a hip and spent much of the day in a hammock.

The cause of the injury is unknown. Later accounts attribute it to:

- A fall down stairs
- A riding accident
- Rheumatic disease
- Complications from an earlier injury

None is conclusively documented. The result—permanent impaired mobility—is well supported.

Other health information includes:

- Recurrent rheumatism
- Weight gain in later life
- Regular cigar smoking beginning or increasing after Bolívar’s death
- Final facial, sinus, or throat illness in 1856

No reliable evidence establishes alcoholism, narcotics addiction, diagnosed mental illness, or a chronic sexually transmitted disease. “Madwoman” rhetoric was principally affectionate or political invective, not a diagnosis.

### Social network in Paita

Her correspondents and visitors included:

- Juan José Flores and members of his family
- Roberto Ascásubi
- Mercedes Jijón de Flores
- Cayetano Freyre
- Daniel Florencio O’Leary
- Pedro Moncayo
- Gabriel García Moreno
- General Antonio de la Guerra
- Simón Rodríguez
- Giuseppe Garibaldi
- Sailors, merchants, officials, local women, servants, and godchildren

She developed ties of *compadrazgo* with local families. Paita memory preserves her as a socially connected resident rather than an entirely isolated exile.

### Simón Rodríguez

Bolívar’s former teacher **Simón Rodríguez** spent time in or around Paita during his later wanderings. Sources associate him with Manuela in her final years, though descriptions that he continuously “accompanied” her until his own death in 1854 overstate what is known.

### Giuseppe Garibaldi

Garibaldi stopped in Paita in 1851 while traveling toward Lima. According to his autobiography, Manuela invited him to her home. He recalled spending approximately half a day reclining on her sofa and listening to her stories about Bolívar.

This meeting is **documented by Garibaldi’s retrospective first-person account** and is among the best-supported encounters between Manuela and a later international revolutionary celebrity.

### Gabriel García Moreno

The future Ecuadorian president passed through Paita while in exile and knew or visited Manuela. Correspondence between him and the Ascásubi family refers to her warmly. Her late network overlapped with emerging Ecuadorian conservatism: wealthy highland families, former Floreanos, and opponents of José María Urbina’s anticlerical liberal government.

This late alignment complicates retrospective efforts to classify her straightforwardly as a modern leftist or liberal feminist. Her central commitments remained personal loyalty, continental union, order, friendship, and Bolivarian memory.

### Herman Melville

A popular story says the American novelist **Herman Melville** met her in Paita, sometimes in 1841 and sometimes in 1856.

**Status: unverified claim and later literary device.** Melville sailed the Pacific and used Paita in *Moby-Dick*, but no reliable primary evidence of a meeting has been located. Diego Rísquez’s film uses a Melville-like visiting framework, helping popularize the connection. Murray’s archival biography substantiates Garibaldi’s visit but does not substantiate Melville’s.

## Death and Burial

In her last known letter, dictated to Roberto Ascásubi on 29 June 1856, Manuela said she was very ill “of the face,” possibly referring to the sinuses or throat.

Juana Rosa, her attendant, died on 21 November. Manuela died two days later, on **23 November 1856**, around six in the evening. General Antonio de la Guerra reported the death in a letter dated 5 December.

The traditional account states that public-health authorities burned her clothes, furniture, and papers because of contagion. Later narratives often say the house itself burned. Critical examination suggests that belongings were destroyed; the claim that the entire house burned is doubtful, particularly because a building long identified as her residence remained standing.

Her grave was unmarked. Parish records that might have helped locate it were reportedly lost in a later church fire. Paita tradition situates the epidemic grave on a hill south of town.

No authenticated physical remains have been recovered.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Religion

Manuela was educated in a Catholic convent and remained conversant with Catholic practice. She maintained friendships with clergy and requested religious articles, including scapulars, in later life.

She also participated in anti-clerical humor: Jonathás’s imitation of a sermon at a Bogotá gathering reportedly amused guests. Such episodes do not establish atheism.

Her mature political network included conservative Catholics and opponents of secularization. No systematic theological writings survive.

### Political development

Her political trajectory can be reconstructed as follows:

1. **Colonial elite formation:** Daughter of a prosperous Spanish merchant and a Creole mother; beneficiary of slaveholding and estate society.
2. **Sanmartinian patriotism in Lima:** Recruitment, donations, and the Order of the Sun.
3. **Bolivarian identification:** Service as archivist, intermediary, and partisan of Gran Colombia.
4. **Centralist and authoritarian alignment:** Support for Bolívar’s exceptional powers and resistance to Peruvian and Santanderist liberal opposition.
5. **Possible constitutional-monarchist sympathies:** Participation in circles considering monarchy as a defense against fragmentation.
6. **Post-Bolívar opposition:** Continued Bolivarian activism against the Santander government.
7. **Floreano alignment:** Political and personal support for Juan José Flores.
8. **Late conservative affinities:** Friendship with Ascásubi and García Moreno’s circle, coupled with opposition to Urbina-era liberalism.
9. **Withdrawal:** Reduced direct intervention after 1845, though continued intellectual engagement through correspondence.

### Women’s rights and feminism

She defied conventional gender restrictions in practice:

- Exercised commercial power
- Managed property and litigation
- Rejected cohabitation with her husband
- Chose a public adulterous relationship
- Traveled with armies
- Entered political spaces
- Used masculine or military clothing
- Interceded in state affairs

No authenticated manifesto by her calling for universal women’s political or legal equality has been located. Describing her as a feminist is a **retrospective interpretation**, meaningful in reception history but not a documented self-description.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Simón Bolívar

**Nature:** Lover, political ally, employer or staff superior, confidant, and central emotional attachment, 1822–1830.

Their connection was neither solely romantic nor equivalent to co-rule. Manuela preserved his papers, relayed information, defended his politics, served as intercessor, and saved him during the 1828 palace attack.

### James Thorne

**Nature:** Husband, financial partner, estranged spouse, and continuing legal connection, 1817–1847.

Their marriage combined elite respectability and commerce. It failed as a domestic union but persisted legally. Both sides were accused of infidelity. Thorne’s estate became the subject of litigation.

### Rosa Campuzano

**Nature:** Friend and fellow patriot in Lima.

Campuzano participated in pro-San Martín circles and was also admitted to the Order of the Sun. Later popular culture frequently pairs the two women.

### José María Sáenz del Campo

**Nature:** Half-brother, military intermediary, and political associate.

His service in Numancia and later Bolívar’s army linked Manuela to soldiers and probably facilitated her entry into Bolívar’s circle.

### Antonio José de Sucre

**Nature:** Senior patriot commander and political associate within Bolívar’s network.

She knew and supported his cause. Claims that he personally commanded or promoted her at Ayacucho depend on disputed evidence.

### Daniel Florencio O’Leary

**Nature:** Bolívar aide, correspondent, and custodian of historical memory.

Manuela later supplied him with recollections of the September attack and remained interested in his documentary work on Bolívar.

### Luis Perú de Lacroix

**Nature:** Bolívar associate and correspondent.

He wrote to her about Bolívar’s final illness.

### Rafael Urdaneta

**Nature:** Senior Bolivarian general and ally.

He formally announced Bolívar’s death in Bogotá and remained part of the network with which Manuela identified.

### Francisco de Paula Santander

**Nature:** Principal political enemy.

Manuela regarded him as ungrateful and dangerous; Santanderist governments and journalists treated her as a subversive. Her possession of Bolívar–Santander correspondence gave her a potentially useful political archive.

### Vicente Rocafuerte

**Nature:** Executive opponent.

He barred her return to Quito in 1835, explicitly citing fear of her character, influence, and political associations.

### Juan José Flores

**Nature:** Friend, patron, political ally, and correspondent.

Flores aided her inheritance claims and attempted return, received intelligence from her, and served as her principal political connection during early Paita exile.

### Roberto Ascásubi and family

**Nature:** Friends, financial helpers, correspondents, and late-life conservative network.

Ascásubi helped pursue Quito debts and property matters.

### Jonathás, Nathán, and Juana Rosa

**Nature:** Enslaved or formerly enslaved servants, household companions, and—in later memory—friends and political collaborators.

Their lives are obscured by an archive centered on elite actors. Hagiographic accounts sometimes erase their legal subordination by calling them only “friends”; hostile accounts erase their agency by calling them only servants. Available evidence supports close household bonds within a structure of slavery and dependency.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### Adultery

**Documented fact:** She maintained an intimate relationship with Bolívar while legally married to Thorne.

**Legal result:** No completed divorce or adultery conviction is documented.

**Historical effect:** Opponents used the relationship to discredit her politics; admirers transformed it into sacrificial romance.

### Ownership and sale of enslaved people

**Documented fact:** She participated in slave transactions under Thorne’s authority and emancipated the infant Juana Rosa in 1822.

This record contradicts simplified portrayals of her as uniformly emancipatory in the modern sense. It also places her within the routine economic practices of her class and period.

### Military record inflation

**Disputed claim:** Participation in combat at Junín and Ayacucho and formal promotion to colonel.

**Evidence against:** Her reconstructed itinerary places her away from Ayacucho; no secure contemporary commission or battle report establishes combat service.

**Evidence cited in favor:** Boussingault’s later memoir and disputed letters attributed to Sucre or Bolívar.

### September repression

**Documented context:** Manuela supported the regime responsible for executions and political restrictions after the 1828 attack.

No evidence establishes that she ordered executions. She did, however, favor vigorous action against opponents and assisted Bolivarian intelligence and political efforts.

### Effigies and public disorder

She participated in or was accused of provocative symbolic politics involving effigies, pistols, demonstrations, and insults against rival factions.

Some incidents are documented through hostile newspapers or memoirs; precise details vary. These actions contributed to her indictment and expulsion.

### Sardá conspiracy

**Allegation:** Participation in a Bolivarian plot against Santander.

**Formal status:** Political accusation and basis for removal; no conclusive trial finding.

### 1835 insurrection plan

**Allegation:** She intended to avenge her brother and lead the Quiteño Libre opposition.

**Formal status:** Official suspicion expressed in correspondence; no proven conspiracy and no trial.

### Sexual relationships beyond Bolívar

Jean-Baptiste Boussingault alleged or insinuated affairs with:

- A physician called Cheyne or Cheyme
- A young Englishman named Wills
- Boussingault himself, or at least flirtation with him
- Women called Policarpa and Baltasara

**Status:** Uncorroborated retrospective allegations. Boussingault knew Manuela but wrote decades later, employed sensational and gendered descriptions, and included questionable details. His memoir remains historically valuable as evidence of reputation and elite male attitudes, not automatic proof of every sexual claim.

### Bisexuality or lesbian relationships

**Unresolved question.** Warm letters to female friends and Boussingault’s insinuations have led modern writers to describe her as bisexual. No unambiguous authenticated evidence proves a sexual relationship with a woman.

Her later status as an LGBTQ symbol is a documented fact of reception history, distinct from a definitive biographical diagnosis.

### Thorne estate dispute

**Documented litigation:** Manuela pursued the 8,000-peso settlement and widow’s claims; executor Manuel Escobar resisted and attacked her reputation.

**Outcome:** Uncertain.

### Wealth and poverty

Popular accounts alternate between “wealthy aristocrat” and “destitute tobacco seller.”

The record supports both at different stages, with qualifications:

- She began adulthood with a substantial settlement, commercial authority, and inherited property.
- Assets were tied up in marriage, estates, and unpaid debts.
- Exile cut her off from income.
- She sold Cataguango but did not receive full payment promptly.
- She relied on retail activity and credit in Paita.
- She was financially constrained but maintained a household and influential network.

### Criminal activity, fraud, corruption, and money laundering

No evidence was found of:

- A conviction for fraud
- Embezzlement
- Money laundering
- Organized criminal enterprise
- Personal corruption for financial gain
- A formal criminal conviction of any kind

Her alleged offenses were chiefly political: conspiracy, disorder, propaganda, and resistance to authorities.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### The balcony wreath

**Story:** Manuela threw a wreath at Bolívar during his triumphal entry into Quito; it landed on him, initiating their romance.

**Appearance and circulation:** Romantic biographies, popular histories, plays, and films.

**Evidence:** No secure contemporary account.

**Effect:** Converted their political and personal meeting into a scene of predestined romance.

### “She fought at Ayacucho”

**Story:** She rode with Sucre, cared for wounded soldiers, fought the Spanish, and received promotion to colonel.

**Evidence for:** Boussingault’s later memoir; letters published in disputed collections; twentieth-century nationalist biography.

**Evidence against:** Her reconstructed movements indicate that she turned back after Junín, stayed in Jauja, and rejoined Bolívar before returning to Lima; no contemporary battle record confirms her presence.

**Effect:** Established “La Coronela” and “La Generala” as central heroic identities.

### The complete lifelong companionship of Jonathás and Nathán

**Story:** Two enslaved girls were given to Manuela as childhood companions and remained beside her through campaigns and exile.

**Evidence:** Jonathás is attested in adult recollections and household episodes; Nathán is much less securely documented. The continuous childhood-to-death narrative is largely biographical reconstruction.

**Effect:** Added a multiracial female trio to revolutionary mythology. Some recent novels have restored the Black women’s agency; others use them mainly as picturesque attendants.

### Sexual libertine or “Messalina”

**Story:** Manuela was indiscriminately promiscuous, bisexual, drunken, and manipulative.

**Circulators:** Political enemies, Boussingault, moralizing biographers, and anti-Bolivarian polemicists.

**Evidence:** Adultery with Bolívar is documented. Other affairs and same-sex relationships are not securely corroborated. She drank and smoked in a social world where both could be reputational weapons; no addiction is established.

**Effect:** Reduced political agency to sexual influence and supplied the “bad girl” school of historiography.

### Chaste sacrificial heroine

**Story:** Every action after 1822 was a pure sacrifice for Bolívar, with no independent ambition, property interest, political thought, or contradictory allegiance.

**Evidence against:** Her commercial work, inheritance litigation, intercession, correspondence, political intelligence, and post-Bolívar alliances demonstrate autonomous interests.

**Effect:** Rehabilitated her honor while continuing to define her through a male hero.

### The snakebite suicide attempt

**Story:** After learning of Bolívar’s death, she deliberately allowed a snake to bite her but survived.

**Evidence:** No strong contemporary corroboration located.

**Effect:** Reinforced the tragic-romantic narrative.

### Meeting Herman Melville

**Story:** Melville visited Manuela in Paita and heard her account of Bolívar.

**Evidence:** No known first-person documentation. The story is absent from the strongest archival reconstruction and is amplified by film.

**Effect:** Connects Latin American independence memory with the canonical author of *Moby-Dick*.

### The burned house and rescued trunk

**Story:** After her death, health officials burned her house, but General Antonio de la Guerra rescued a trunk containing diaries and unknown Bolívar papers.

**Evidence against:** The surviving report of her death is brief; critics identify anachronisms and false documentary claims in the supposedly rescued material. Her belongings may have been burned, but the house itself probably was not.

**Effect:** Supplied provenance for the “lost diaries” and supported major books, plays, and films.

### The “lost diaries”

The corpus appeared in several related forms:

- *Patriota y amante de usted: Manuela Sáenz y el Libertador: diarios inéditos* (1993), coordinated by Heinz Dieterich.
- Carlos Álvarez Saá’s *Manuela: sus diarios perdidos y otros papeles* (1995; later editions).
- *Los diarios perdidos de Manuela Sáenz y otros papeles* (2005).
- Later collections of “love letters” incorporating substantially the same material.

**Reasons for rejecting or doubting authenticity:**

- A supposed 1864 notarial appearance by Santander, who died in 1840.
- Reference to Joaquín Mosquera as a general when he was not.
- Implausibly rapid transmission of news from Pasto to Quito.
- Material attributed to the *Diario de Bucaramanga* that is absent from the authentic work.
- Ideological and linguistic anachronisms.
- A purported long death letter inconsistent with the brief known report.
- Weak or inaccessible provenance and lack of independent archival authentication.

Historian Pilar Moreno de Ángel publicly called the 1993 volume a crude apocryphal montage. Pamela Murray and other researchers also treat the diaries as apocryphal or highly suspect.

**Historical importance:** The texts have nevertheless shaped twenty-first-century education, feminist recovery, drama, cinema, and state commemoration. They must be studied as modern cultural artifacts, not quoted silently as Manuela’s authenticated words.

## Works by or Attributed to Manuela Sáenz

### Authenticated writings

Manuela published no known book, essay, or manifesto during her lifetime. Her genuine literary and historical corpus consists principally of letters, petitions, business instruments, powers of attorney, and legal statements.

Important documentary groupings include:

- Letters to Simón Bolívar, principally 1822–1830.
- Letters from Bolívar to Manuela.
- Letters to Juan José Flores, especially from Paita, approximately 1835–1846.
- Letters to Roberto Ascásubi, approximately 1846–1856.
- Letters to Daniel Florencio O’Leary, including her 10 August 1850 recollection of the September 1828 attack.
- Correspondence with Mercedes Jijón de Flores, Pedro Moncayo, Pedro Sanz, Cayetano Freyre, and other political or business contacts.
- Legal filings concerning maternal filiation and inheritance, 1821–1823.
- Commercial receipts and slave-sale or manumission instruments, 1820–1822.
- Powers of attorney, including the 1847 instrument for litigation after Thorne’s death.
- Final known dictated letter to Roberto Ascásubi, 29 June 1856.

### Major documentary editions

- Felipe Luis Borja, ed., “Epistolario de Manuela Sáenz,” *Boletín de la Academia Nacional de la Historia* 29 (1946), pp. 228–246. Posthumous scholarly compilation.
- Vicente Lecuna, “Papeles de Manuela Sáenz” (1945). Documentary and historiographical publication containing legal material and correspondence.
- Jorge Villalba Freire, ed., *Manuela Sáenz: Epistolario* (Quito: Banco Central del Ecuador, 1986). Major posthumous scholarly selection, principally covering 1829–1853.
- Jorge Villalba Freire, ed., *Manuela Sáenz en la leyenda y en la historia* (Caracas: Sociedad Bolivariana de Venezuela, 1988). Documentary and interpretive compilation.
- Apolo M. Espinosa, ed., *Simón Bolívar y Manuela Sáenz: correspondencia íntima* (Quito, 1996). Posthumous joint correspondence edition; individual documents should be checked against archival provenance.
- Juan P. Llinás, ed., *Simón Bolívar visto por Manuela Sáenz* (Barranquilla, 2008). Posthumous selection.

### Disputed or apocryphal writings

- *Patriota y amante de usted: Manuela Sáenz y el Libertador: diarios inéditos* (1993): substantially disputed and commonly judged apocryphal.
- *Manuela: sus diarios perdidos y otros papeles* (1995): disputed/apocryphal diary corpus mixed with some genuine documents.
- *Los diarios perdidos de Manuela Sáenz y otros papeles* (2005): later edition of the same disputed material.
- “Diario de Quito”: disputed attribution.
- “Diario de Paita”: disputed attribution.
- Alleged continuation or fragment of the *Diario de Bucaramanga*: apocryphal attribution; Luis Perú de Lacroix, not Manuela, authored the authentic diary.
- Collections marketed as the “most beautiful love letters” may combine authentic correspondence with documents from the disputed corpus and require item-by-item verification.

No patents, technical papers, recorded speeches, paintings, musical compositions, films, or commercial publications authored by her are known.

- Archivo General de la Nación, Lima: Thorne’s will, estate records, and related proceedings.
- Archivo Histórico del Banco Central or successor Ecuadorian repositories: Jijón y Caamaño papers and correspondence.
- British Foreign Office records: diplomatic correspondence from Bogotá, Jamaica, Lima, and Callao.
- O’Leary papers and published *Memorias*.
- Contemporary newspapers including the *Gaceta del Gobierno del Perú Independiente*, *El Cachaco*, and *El Comercio* of Lima.
- Biblioteca and Archivo de la Casa de la Cultura Ecuatoriana: copies and editions of her epistolary record.
- Museo Manuela Sáenz, Quito: commemorative and purported documentary material; disputed items require independent authentication.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Nineteenth century

Her earliest posthumous images came through:

- Political enemies who described her as immoral and masculine
- Bolivarian loyalists who remembered the 1828 rescue
- Travelers and memoirists such as Boussingault
- Ricardo Palma’s literary reminiscences
- Paita oral memory

She was neither wholly forgotten nor securely incorporated into national histories.

### Twentieth-century division

Pamela Murray identifies two broad historiographical schools:

- The **“bad girl” school**, emphasizing adultery, eccentricity, sexuality, cross-dressing, and meddling.
- The **“heroine” school**, emphasizing sacrifice, battlefield courage, continental patriotism, and rescue of Bolívar.

Ecuadorian and Venezuelan nationalism, the Bolívar cult, socialism, feminism, and cultural decolonization strengthened the heroic interpretation. Archival scholarship subsequently challenged both extremes.

### Feminist and LGBTQ reception

From the late twentieth century, she became:

- A symbol of women’s political participation
- A precursor invoked by feminist movements
- An example of resistance to marital and gender constraints
- An LGBTQ and gender-nonconforming icon because of same-sex speculation and military clothing

These are documented uses of her image. They do not resolve the historical questions of her sexual orientation or personal gender identity.

### State commemoration

- Statues, streets, schools, cultural organizations, and women’s associations bear her name.
- In May 2007 Ecuador awarded her the posthumous honorary rank of **Generala de la República**.
- On 5 July 2010 a symbolic casket containing soil from Paita was received at Venezuela’s National Pantheon. The material was symbolic; it did not consist of authenticated remains.
- Her image has been incorporated into Bolivarian state rituals in Venezuela and continental-integration rhetoric in Ecuador.
- A Peruvian proposal indexed in 2025–2026 sought formal recognition of her contribution to Peru and Latin American integration.

### Historical significance

Documentary reconstruction supports her importance in four distinguishable fields:

1. Women’s participation in independence networks.
2. Informal political work surrounding Bolívar and Gran Colombia.
3. Political exile and partisan communication after independence.
4. The construction of national and transnational historical mythology.

Her verified rescue of Bolívar in 1828 was consequential. Had the attack succeeded, the immediate political history of Gran Colombia would have been different, although counterfactual consequences cannot be established.

## Atlas Connections

Only source-supported links to names in the supplied atlas roster are included.

### Simón Bolívar — documented fact

Manuela met Bolívar in Quito in June 1822 and became his lover, archivist, political confidante, intermediary, and defender until his death in 1830. She traveled through Ecuador, Peru, and New Granada in connection with his entourage. On 25 September 1828 in Bogotá she helped him escape an assassination attempt.

### Antonio José de Sucre — documented association; disputed battlefield story

Sucre was Bolívar’s senior commander, victor at Pichincha and Ayacucho, and a member of Manuela’s political-military network. Her support for his and Bolívar’s campaign is documented. Claims that she fought beside him at Ayacucho and received promotion on his recommendation are disputed.

### José de San Martín — documented fact

Manuela supported the independence movement in Lima under San Martín’s protectorate, contributed material assistance, and was listed among 112 women honored through the female section of the Order of the Sun in 1821–1822. A private personal meeting is possible but was not established in the materials consulted.

### Simón Rodríguez — reported/documented acquaintance

Bolívar’s former teacher encountered Manuela in the Paita–Piura region during their later lives. Sources associate him with her social circle before his death in 1854. Claims of uninterrupted co-residence or continuous companionship are stronger than the available evidence.

### Giuseppe Maria Garibaldi — documented by retrospective first-person report

Garibaldi stopped at Paita in 1851 while traveling toward Lima. His autobiography records that Manuela received him and spent part of a day telling him about Bolívar.

### Gabriel José García Márquez — documented posthumous literary connection

García Márquez incorporated Manuela into *El general en su laberinto* (1989). This is a posthumous literary representation and part of her cultural afterlife, not a lifetime connection.

### Pablo Neruda — documented posthumous literary connection

Neruda published *La insepulta de Paita* in 1962, transforming her missing grave and Paita exile into a major poetic symbol.

### Policarpa Salavarrieta — no personal connection established

Both participated separately in women’s patriot networks, and scholars compare their activities. Policarpa was executed in Bogotá in 1817, before Manuela entered Gran Colombian political life. No meeting or correspondence is documented.

### Alexander von Humboldt — no direct connection established

Jean-Baptiste Boussingault, who later knew and wrote about Manuela, had scientific connections to Humboldt’s intellectual world. This does not establish that Humboldt met, corresponded with, or directly influenced Manuela.

### Francisco Pizarro González — geographical and historical background only

Pizarro founded Spanish Lima centuries before Manuela lived there. This is not a personal or documented influence and is excluded as a substantive atlas connection.

No other names in the supplied roster were found to have a securely documented personal, institutional, intellectual, or posthumous representational connection substantial enough for inclusion.
