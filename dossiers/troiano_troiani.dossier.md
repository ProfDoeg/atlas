# Troiano Troiani: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

Troiano Troiani left a substantial body of public sculpture but a comparatively thin accessible biographical record. The strongest biographical source located is Giuseppe Bergamini’s entry in the *Dizionario Biografico dei Friulani*, based partly on Pietro Menis’s posthumous study and the 2006 Comune di Buja monograph. Argentine government, municipal, museum, academic, and historic-building records supply corroboration for individual works and appointments.

No digitized civil birth, marriage, immigration, naturalization, death, probate, military, school, employment, or cemetery record was located in the accessible sources consulted. Consequently:

- “Documented fact” below means supported by a specialist institutional, governmental, archival, or contemporaneous source.
- “Reported fact” means repeated by credible secondary sources but not independently verified against the underlying record.
- “Disputed” identifies incompatible published accounts.
- “Legend” or “myth” identifies stories whose circulation is historically demonstrable but whose factual basis is absent, incomplete, or contradicted.
- Absence of documentary evidence is not treated as proof that an event did not occur.

The dedicated adversarial search combined Troiani’s full name in Spanish, Italian, and English with terms including *controversia*, *escándalo*, *acusación*, *fraude*, *corrupción*, *coima*, *demanda*, *fascismo*, *controversy*, *scandal*, *allegations*, *lawsuit*, *fraud*, and *criticism*, including searches limited to 2025–2026. It found no criminal case, civil lawsuit, corruption investigation, accusation of personal fraud, or documented scandal involving Troiani himself. The principal contested material concerns interpretations and attribution of his public sculpture.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and household

**Documented fact:** Troiani was born to Giobatta Troiani and Regina Martina and was the fourth of thirteen siblings. The size of the household helps explain, though does not independently prove, the economic necessity behind his very early employment.

The consulted sources do not establish:

- his parents’ birthplaces or occupations;
- the names, dates, or later lives of the other twelve children;
- grandparents or earlier ancestry;
- the family’s property, income, political affiliation, or religious practice.

Buja’s artisanal culture—especially stone cutting and medallic art—formed part of Troiani’s environment. Later Italian writers place him within the distinctive tradition of “medaglisti buiesi,” but this is a regional artistic lineage, not evidence of blood kinship with other medallists.

### Marriage

**Documented fact:** After his first period in Argentina, Troiani returned to Italy to marry **Eugenia Celotti** and departed again for Argentina in 1914. The exact wedding date and place are not supplied by the specialist biography. No information was found about Celotti’s parentage, occupation, death, or whether she accompanied him immediately on the 1914 voyage.

### Children and descendants

**Reported fact:** Vanna Troiani was the sculptor’s daughter. A modern account of a posthumous bronze casting says *Figura alada* was cast with her authorization and under the supervision of Troiani’s former assistant Antonio Pujia. This supplies evidence that at least one child survived him and exercised or was recognized as exercising authority over an artistic model.

No reliable source consulted identifies further children, descendants, or heirs. Claims about a larger family should therefore remain unverified unless supported by civil or probate documentation.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1885–1907

### Buja and child labor in Carinthia

**Documented fact:** Troiani’s childhood ended early. In **1894**, aged nine, he was sent to work in a furnace or kiln in **Carinthia**, then part of Austria-Hungary. In **1895** he became an assistant to **Domenico Conchin**, a stonecutter from Buja, working with him at **Sankt Veit near Klagenfurt**.

This was not an artistic Grand Tour but migrant child labor within the seasonal economic networks connecting Friuli to the Habsburg lands. The surviving biography does not reveal:

- the type of furnace;
- his wages or working conditions;
- whether a family member accompanied him;
- the duration of each stay;
- the language he used in Carinthia.

### First training

During periods back in Buja, he attended the local **school of drawing and music** and worked in the shop of the altar-maker **Bartolomeo Rizzotti of Artegna**.

This combination—manual stone work, ecclesiastical ornament, drawing, music, and altar production—provides the firmest basis for understanding his formation. It also complicates later descriptions of him as an “autodidact.” He was not initially the product of a continuous academy course, but neither was he wholly self-taught.

Early surviving or reported works include:

- *Bondanze*, a small sculpture representing **Giovanni Durì**, a characteristic local personality whose nickname was Bondanze; preserved by the Civic Museums of Udine.
- A bust of **Bartolomeo Rizzotti**.
- Sculptural work for the high altar of the parish church at **Risano**.
- Sculptures for the altar of the small church of **San Rocco at Tonzolano di Buja**.

The exact dates, materials, division of workshop labor, and present condition of the church works are not fully established in the online sources.

## Education and Formation, 1907–1910

### Military service and Florence

**Documented fact:** Troiani performed Italian military service in **Florence**. There he encountered the sculpture and architecture of the Tuscan Renaissance. No regiment, rank, barracks, or exact service dates have been recovered.

### Venice Academy

In **1908**, he was admitted to the **Accademia di Belle Arti di Venezia**, where the specialist Friulian biography identifies **Antonio Dal Zotto** as his teacher.

A municipal Argentine museum instead calls him an autodidact and says he studied in Venice with “Anibal de Latto,” followed by Florence, Rome, Austria, and Romania. That name is probably a corruption of Antonio Dal Zotto, but it should not be silently accepted as equivalent. The Romanian study or travel claim was not corroborated by the strongest Italian biography.

### Resolving the “self-taught” contradiction

- **Documented fact:** Troiani received workshop instruction, attended a drawing-and-music school, and was admitted to the Venice academy.
- **Reported characterization:** Argentine summaries sometimes call him *autodidacto* or “of non-academic formation.”
- **Best reconstruction:** His foundational training was artisanal and irregular rather than a conventional uninterrupted academic education. He nevertheless received formal academy instruction in Venice. Whether he graduated is unclear: one medallist directory says he emigrated after obtaining a diploma, while the main biographical entry says only that he was admitted in 1908.

### Artistic influences

The principal specialist account identifies:

- **Leonardo Bistolfi** as an influence on Troiani’s youthful, elegant modelling.
- **Émile-Antoine Bourdelle** as a probable source for monumentality, heroic tension, and exaltation of the human body.
- **Auguste Rodin**, indirectly through Bourdelle and through the modelled surface treatment attributed to works such as *El Arquero*.
- **Ivan Meštrović** as a major admired sculptor whose combination of monumentality and popular human feeling interested Troiani.

Troiani remained fundamentally figurative. His style combined nineteenth-century academic foundations, classical and Renaissance subjects, symbolist or Bistolfian elegance, simplified modern volumes, and—in major architectural commissions—Art Deco monumentality.

## Migration to Argentina

### The 1910 journey

**Documented fact:** After winning a competition reserved for young artists, Troiani went to **Argentina in 1910**.

Some later accounts convert this competition into an Argentine government scholarship intended to bring European artists to Argentina. The accessible specialist source confirms a competition but does not describe its organizer, terms, or funding. The stronger wording “Argentine government scholarship” therefore remains a **reported but incompletely verified claim**.

### 1910 versus 1914 as the date of settlement

Sources repeatedly give two dates:

- **1910:** first journey to Argentina after the competition.
- **1914:** permanent settlement after returning to Italy to marry.

Both can be correct if “arrival” and “permanent establishment” are distinguished. A University of La Plata record and the Zurbarán gallery identify 1914 as the date he settled in Argentina; Bergamini records his first arrival in 1910 and final departure from Italy in 1914.

### Return, marriage, and permanent emigration

Troiani returned to Italy to marry Eugenia Celotti. In **1914** he departed again for Argentina, where he spent the remainder of his working life, apart from a documented return visit in 1952.

Passenger ship, port, immigration-category, and first Buenos Aires address have not been recovered.

## Early Argentine Career, 1910–1921

### Entry into the Buenos Aires art world

Troiani began exhibiting at the **Salón Nacional de Bellas Artes** by **1914**, according to the Museo Municipal de Arte de Junín. He later served repeatedly as both exhibitor and jury member.

He also participated in official salons at:

- Rosario;
- Santa Fe;
- Bahía Blanca.

The online sources state that he received prizes and medals, but do not provide a complete year-by-year award register.

### Buenos Aires Stock Exchange, 1916

**Documented fact:** In **1916**, working with a sculptor identified as **maestro Riganti**—usually given in secondary accounts as Rodolfo Riganti—Troiani produced decorative sculptures for the **Bolsa de Comercio de Buenos Aires**.

This commission increased his visibility and led to work from:

- Italian-Argentine community organizations and patrons;
- public bodies;
- private clients seeking plaster and bronze portraits;
- commissioners of plaques and medals.

The exact contractual and financial relationship between Troiani and Riganti is not known. No fee or workshop account was located.

### Italian patriotic commission: General Armando Diaz, 1919

In 1919 the Buenos Aires newspaper *Il Giornale d’Italia* organized a subscription for a gift to Italian general **Armando Diaz** following the victory of Vittorio Veneto. Troiani was commissioned to make:

- a Roman-form **sword of honor**;
- a presentation casket.

The gold hilt represented four women: **Mother Italy** and the “redeemed” territories **Trento, Trieste, and Istria**. The work was illustrated in *L’Illustrazione Italiana*, no. 36, 7 September 1919, p. 241.

This was an explicitly nationalist Italian diaspora commission. It documents Troiani’s participation in the post–First World War patriotic culture of Argentina’s Italian community. It does not, by itself, establish adherence to fascism, which had not yet taken governmental power in Italy.

## Mature Career, 1922–1942

### Rio de Janeiro, 1922

**Documented fact:** Troiani made sculptural groups and bas-reliefs for the **Argentine Pavilion at the 1922 International Exposition in Rio de Janeiro**.

An architectural study describes the pavilion’s façade as containing classical mouldings, garlands, laurels, vessels, and sculptural groups worked by Troiani. This placed his sculpture within Argentina’s official international self-presentation.

### First solo exhibition and *Arquero*

In **1925**, Troiani held his first individual exhibition in Buenos Aires. One exhibited work, *Arquero* or *Arciere*, later received a prize at the Municipal Exhibition of Industrial Arts.

The work now generally known as **El Arquero Divino** is dated to **1924**. It was acquired by the Municipality of La Plata but installed in **Plaza Moreno only in 1970**, seven years after Troiani’s death.

The sculpture presents a tense male archer; an actual bow and arrow are absent from its current appearance. Eduardo Migo describes it as modelled “in the style of Rodin.” Other commentary recognizes a close formal relationship to Bourdelle’s *Héraklès archer*.

In 2019 it was included, with Plaza Moreno, under Argentina’s national designation as a **Bien de interés artístico** and **Bien de interés histórico**.

### Naturalization: 1925 or 1929

The date of Argentine citizenship is **disputed in published sources**:

- Bergamini’s specialist biography says **1925**.
- The Junín municipal museum and the Buenos Aires Legislature say **1929**.

No naturalization decree, court record, or citizenship file was located to resolve the discrepancy. The fact of naturalization is well supported; the year remains unsettled.

### Philadelphia, 1926

Troiani was invited to represent Argentina at the **1926 Philadelphia International Exposition**. A scholarly reconstruction of prizes for foreign artists at Argentina’s National Salon lists Troiani and the work *El karma* under 1926, but the available extract does not establish whether this was the work sent to Philadelphia or a separate salon entry.

### Banco Tornquist, 1927–1928

In **1927**, Troiani executed monumental decorative sculpture and refined bas-reliefs for the **Banco Tornquist** building in Buenos Aires. The city’s heritage record dates the building’s inauguration to **1928**, identifies architect **Alejandro Bustillo**, and confirms that four Troiani sculptures stand above the Doric entrance portico at Bartolomé Mitre 531.

The commission illustrates the connection between Troiani’s workshop and elite Argentine banking and commercial architecture. No evidence was found that he held shares in the bank or had a financial relationship beyond artistic commission work.

### Plaza del Congreso lamps, 1929

Troiani designed the artistic streetlamps around the **Plaza de los Dos Congresos** in Buenos Aires. A city historical publication identifies him as their designer and dates the work to approximately **1929**.

### Monument to Thomas Edison, 1930

**Documented fact:** In **1930**, Troiani made bas-reliefs for a monument to **Thomas Alva Edison**. The consulted online sources do not provide the monument’s full commissioning history, unveiling details, or current location.

### Palacio del Concejo Deliberante / Buenos Aires Legislature, 1931–1932

In **1931**, Troiani executed four large allegorical sculptures for Buenos Aires’s municipal council building, now the Palace of the Legislature.

He also made the **Fuente del Tritón**, a bronze figure on a granite base at Perú and Diagonal Sur. The Legislature records that it was inaugurated in **1932** and was still being completed in Troiani’s workshop after the palace’s 1931 opening.

Some secondary compilations credit him with a much wider program, including numerous façade caryatids. The official sources consulted directly confirm the Triton fountain and the specialist biography confirms four allegories; a complete attribution catalogue for every façade figure was not recovered.

### Ministry of Public Works, 1936

For the new **Ministerio de Obras Públicas** building on what became Avenida 9 de Julio, Troiani produced major decorative sculpture, including:

- two monumental figures conventionally identified as **Architecture** and **Engineering**;
- bronze entrance columns or reliefs representing electrical forces through dynamically moving figures;
- additional relief decoration.

A peer-reviewed architectural study describes the works as modern in architectural character and designed to harmonize with the rationalist building.

One façade figure later became famous as the **“Monumento a la Coima”**, discussed separately below.

### Córdoba Palace of Justice, 1936

**Documented fact:** Troiani executed:

- the sculptural group for the fronton of the **Palacio de Justicia de Córdoba**;
- four statues for its **Salón de los Pasos Perdidos**.

Individual titles and iconographic details were not recovered from the online material consulted.

### Museo Histórico Provincial de Rosario, 1938–1939

Troiani produced three monumental sculptures for the first façade of Rosario’s provincial historical museum, designed by **Ángel Guido**:

- *América India*;
- *América Colonial*;
- *Historia Patria*.

The museum explains that the figures expressed an “Eurindian” historical program: Argentine nationality was represented as emerging from Indigenous and Spanish-colonial matrices rather than appearing spontaneously in the May Revolution. The sculptures remain important evidence of Troiani’s role in institutional historical symbolism.

### International exhibitions, 1939

A municipal museum biography reports that Troiani participated in exhibitions in **New York and San Francisco in 1939**. These presumably relate to the major international fairs of that year, but the exact exhibition catalogues and submitted works were not located.

### Teaching, from 1939

In **1939**, Troiani began teaching sculpture at the national fine-arts institution in Buenos Aires, generally identified as the **Escuela Nacional de Bellas Artes Prilidiano Pueyrredón**.

His documented pupils or workshop assistants include:

- **Antonio Pujia**, who studied with and worked as an assistant to Troiani;
- **Ricardo Roberto Giannetti**, who took Troiani’s sculpture courses.

Pujia later recalled his teachers, including Troiani, as masters from whom he deliberately sought practical studio experience. Troiani’s influence consequently extended beyond his own monuments into postwar Argentine sculpture.

### Alta Gracia, 1940

For a monument marking the **350th anniversary of the grant or foundation history of Alta Gracia**, Troiani produced four allegorical figures:

- *El misionero*;
- *El indio*;
- *El conquistador*;
- *El gaucho*.

The ensemble condensed a conventional four-stage narrative of Argentine history. The accessible sources do not provide the commission’s full political context, payment, or reception.

### Caja Nacional de Ahorro Postal, 1942

In **1942**, Troiani produced decorative bas-reliefs for the **Caja Nacional de Ahorro Postal**. Academic work on Argentina’s postal and telecommunications architecture confirms that Troiani’s sculpture formed part of a wider state program connecting monumental art, labor, national resources, and institutional identity.

## Later Career, 1943–1963

Troiani continued:

- private portrait commissions;
- funerary sculpture;
- medals and plaques;
- teaching;
- salon jury service;
- small bronzes and drawings.

A 1942 Salon catalogue lists him among sculptors chosen by exhibitors for the sculpture jury. Buenos Aires municipal salon documentation also places him on sculpture juries in the postwar period, including the 1950s.

### Religious and funerary work

Troiani’s religious production included:

- *Cristo contra los profanadores*, a patinated-plaster sculpture in the Junín municipal collection;
- *San Francisco*;
- *San Giovanni Battista*, donated to the parish at Avilla di Buja;
- *Fernando III*, on the fronton of the historic church at San Fernando de Maldonado;
- early altar sculptures at Risano and Tonzolano;
- figures, reliefs, and memorial plaques in the Recoleta and Chacarita cemeteries.

At Recoleta, documented or reported works include:

- the **Peirano family mausoleum**, designed by architect Aldo Antonio Flándoli, with a sculptural pediment and door reliefs signed by Troiani;
- reliefs and an interior Christ for the **D’Onofrio family vault**;
- sculpture for the **Manuel Cerini family tomb**;
- memorial plaques on other vaults.

The luxurious Peirano mausoleum has prompted modern speculation about whether its occupants were related to the later Uruguayan banking family of the same surname. The cemetery researcher presenting the question explicitly lacked proof. No evidentiary connection to later Peirano financial crimes has been established, and none should be imputed to Troiani.

### Return to Italy, 1952

**Documented fact:** In **1952**, Troiani returned briefly to Italy and Buja, then went back to Buenos Aires.

Later Friulian reporting says that in these years he:

- renewed ties with his birthplace;
- donated *Il Visionario* to the Comune di Buja;
- donated *Il figlio* to the Civic Museums of Udine;
- gave a *San Giovanni Battista* to the parish at Avilla di Buja;
- experimented with new artistic forms.

The exact sequence and dates of these donations require verification against municipal and museum accession registers.

### Honorary recognition

A Croatian cultural notice reports that the **Accademia di Udine** made Troiani an honorary member in **1957**. The exact institution—possibly the historic Accademia di Scienze, Lettere e Arti di Udine—and the original appointment record were not independently located.

### Death

Troiani died in **Buenos Aires on 24 May 1963**, eleven days before his seventy-eighth birthday.

A Friulian retrospective account describes the death as sudden. No medical cause, hospital, final address, death certificate, funeral report, estate inventory, or burial place was located. Claims specifying a cause would therefore be unsupported.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Political affiliations

No political-party membership, candidacy, office, imprisonment, exile, intelligence relationship, or participation in political violence was found.

Troiani nevertheless worked inside politically charged programs:

- Italian postwar nationalism in the 1919 Diaz sword;
- Argentine civic-national allegory;
- public architecture during the authoritarian government installed after the September 1930 coup;
- state building during the “Década Infame”;
- the Rosario museum’s Eurindian historical program.

Participation in these commissions documents professional involvement, not necessarily personal endorsement of every regime or ideology attached to them.

### Fascism question

The main Friulian biography explicitly addresses the proximity of Troiani’s monumental idiom to European art of the Italian Fascist period. It states that, although distant from Italy and having few contacts with contemporary Italian artists, he absorbed something of the period’s *grandeur*—colossal, heroic, proud figures—without subordinating his rhetoric to the regime’s ideology.

Evidence located:

- heroic monumental form;
- an Italian nationalist commission in 1919;
- continued cultural connections with Italy.

Evidence not located:

- Fascist Party membership;
- public support for Benito Mussolini;
- fascist organizational activity in Argentina;
- antisemitic or racist writings;
- regime office or subsidy tied to political loyalty.

The responsible evidentiary conclusion is that stylistic parallels and nationalist subject matter exist, while personal fascist affiliation remains undocumented.

### Religion

Troiani produced Catholic altar sculpture, saints, Christs, and funerary works and donated a Saint John the Baptist to an Italian parish. These are documented artistic relations with Catholic institutions. No source establishes the content or intensity of his personal religious belief.

### Artistic trajectory

Rather than moving through a simple progression, Troiani alternated between:

- delicate early symbolist or Bistolfian modelling;
- realistic portraiture;
- monumental heroic anatomy;
- flattened medallic relief;
- simplified and geometrized Art Deco figures;
- popular, devotional, and human-scale subjects;
- patriotic and historical allegory.

Bergamini emphasizes returns, changes of material, and shifts driven by a commission’s intended setting.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Teachers and early mentors

- **Domenico Conchin — documented:** employer and stonecutting mentor in Sankt Veit near Klagenfurt from about 1895.
- **Bartolomeo Rizzotti — documented:** altar-maker in whose Artegna workshop Troiani trained; Troiani made his portrait bust.
- **Antonio Dal Zotto — documented:** teacher at the Venice academy from 1908.
- **Leonardo Bistolfi — documented artistic influence:** no personal meeting established.

### Collaborators and architectural partners

- **Riganti, probably Rodolfo Riganti — documented collaborator:** joint decorative sculpture for the Buenos Aires Stock Exchange in 1916.
- **Alejandro Bustillo — documented project relationship:** architect of the Banco Tornquist building carrying Troiani’s four façade sculptures.
- **José Hortal and Alberto Belgrano Blanco — reported commissioners/project officials:** associated with the Ministry of Public Works sculpture program.
- **Ángel Guido — documented project relationship:** architect of the Rosario historical museum whose façade carries Troiani’s three historical allegories.
- **Aldo Antonio Flándoli — documented project relationship:** architect of the Peirano mausoleum, with Troiani responsible for sculpture.

### Students and assistants

- **Antonio Pujia — documented student and assistant:** worked with Troiani during his training; later supervised a posthumous casting authorized by Vanna Troiani.
- **Ricardo Roberto Giannetti — documented student:** attended Troiani’s sculpture courses at the Prilidiano Pueyrredón institution.

### Admired artists

- **Ivan Meštrović — documented intellectual/artistic admiration:** Troiani wrote a remembrance after Meštrović’s death in 1962. No evidence of a personal meeting was located.
- **Émile-Antoine Bourdelle and Auguste Rodin — documented influence, personal contact unproved.**

### Patrons, enemies, and rivals

Troiani had public and private patrons, especially among Italian-Argentine institutions, banks, and government bodies. No named enemy, sustained personal rival, hostile critic, or patronage dispute was located.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

### No located personal criminal or civil case

The dedicated adversarial search found no evidence that Troiani was:

- arrested, imprisoned, indicted, or convicted;
- investigated for fraud, bribery, corruption, or money laundering;
- sued in a documented civil case;
- accused of abuse or political violence;
- connected to an intelligence service;
- dismissed from an office for misconduct.

This is a finding about the accessible record, not proof that no undiscovered archival dispute exists.

### The “Monumento a la Coima”

One of the large figures on the Ministry of Public Works building holds a box or coffer while extending an open hand. It is popularly known as Argentina’s **“Monumento a la Coima”**—the monument to the bribe.

#### What is documented

- Troiani worked on the Ministry building’s sculptural program.
- The figure exists and its open-hand-and-box iconography permits a bribery reading.
- The nickname is unofficial but widely circulated.
- Architectural scholarship identifies Troiani’s official figures as Architecture and Engineering and describes other elements as allegories of electrical forces.

#### The legend

The story says architect or official **José Hortal**, frustrated by contractors or suppliers demanding illicit payments to speed or delay construction, secretly commissioned or inserted the figure as a permanent denunciation of corruption. Some versions say the sculpture was absent from the original plans.

#### Evidentiary status

**Urban legend / unresolved attribution of intention.** Later journalism and an engineering ethics compendium repeat the account, but the latter itself says the story is a legend and that the statue does not appear in archival plans. No located contemporary letter, contract, diary, testimony by Troiani, or authenticated statement by Hortal proves that either man intended a bribery monument.

Some later writers have attributed the sculpture to **Luis Perlotti** or **Alfredo Bigatti**, creating an attribution controversy. The specialist Troiani biography and architectural scholarship connect Troiani to the building, but a definitive object-level commission document for the popularly named figure was not located.

#### Later political use

The image has been repeatedly used as a symbol of Argentine public-works corruption. Its notoriety revived internationally in 2023 when it was discussed in connection with Tucker Carlson’s interview with Javier Milei. This modern circulation concerns the statue’s symbolic use, not wrongdoing by Troiani.

### The Peirano mausoleum and financial-crime speculation

A modern cemetery site wondered whether the Peirano family buried in a Troiani-decorated Recoleta mausoleum belonged to the later Uruguayan Grupo Velox banking family, members of which faced fraud and money-laundering convictions.

**Unverified theory:** The site posed the relationship as an unanswered question and supplied no genealogy. Even if kinship were established, Troiani’s role was that of sculptor to a funerary commission; no evidence connects him to later financial activity.

### Restoration, damage, and altered context

*El Arquero Divino* has experienced public intervention and vandalism, including blue spray paint reported in 2020. These are controversies affecting the work, not allegations against Troiani.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### The archer attacking La Plata Cathedral

#### Story

A persistent La Plata legend says *El Arquero Divino* was positioned by Freemasons so that its absent arrow points at the cathedral cross or rose window, symbolizing Masonic hostility toward the Catholic Church.

#### Circulation

The story appears in local lore, newspapers, tourism commentary, blogs about La Plata’s supposed esoteric design, and discussions of the city’s Masonic founders.

#### Evidence against literal history

- Troiani made the sculpture in **1924**.
- The Municipality acquired it, but it was placed in Plaza Moreno only in **1970**.
- Therefore it was not part of La Plata’s 1880s foundational plan.
- No source located documents a Masonic commission, instructions to aim it at the cathedral, or Troiani’s membership in a lodge.
- Its close relationship to Bourdelle’s archer supplies a conventional art-historical explanation for the subject.

#### Evidentiary label

**Urban myth, contradicted as a foundation-era conspiracy and unproved as a 1970 Masonic intervention.**

#### Historical effect

The story has made the sculpture one of La Plata’s most discussed public artworks. Even reports rejecting it repeat and preserve the association between the archer, Freemasonry, and the cathedral.

### The bribery statue as a secret protest

As discussed above, the “Monumento a la Coima” story is a durable **urban legend with a plausible visual basis but no located contemporary proof of intent**. Its effect has been to detach one element from the building’s official allegorical program and transform it into an independent anti-corruption emblem.

### “Entirely self-taught”

The description of Troiani as wholly self-taught is a **simplifying biographical myth or shorthand**. His early career was artisanal, but sources document attendance at a drawing school, workshop apprenticeships, and academy study.

### The scholarship story

Later summaries say the Argentine government awarded Troiani a scholarship specifically to attract European artists. The main specialist biography says only that he won a competition for young artists and traveled to Argentina. Until the competition record is found, the more elaborate state-recruitment account remains a **reported but unverified elaboration**.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Troiani’s death did not produce an immediate international canonization. His reputation persisted through three partially separate channels:

1. **Argentine public space:** monumental works remained embedded in banks, government buildings, courts, museums, cemeteries, plazas, and churches.
2. **Teaching lineage:** pupils and assistants, especially Antonio Pujia, carried forward his studio knowledge.
3. **Friulian commemoration:** Buja reclaimed him as an important emigrant artist and member of its medallist tradition.

### Institutional reassessment

- His work at the Rosario museum is now read within debates over Indigenous, colonial, and nationalist historical narratives.
- *El Arquero Divino* received national heritage recognition in 2019.
- Buenos Aires restoration programs identify Troiani among the important sculptors represented in Recoleta Cemetery.
- The Comune di Buja mounted the 2006–2007 exhibition *Troiano Troiani scultore* and published the Bergamini–Pauluzzi monograph.

### Market

Signed bronzes and reliefs continue to appear in Argentine and Italian auctions. Auction records demonstrate market survival but do not establish a stable price history or authenticate every attributed object.

### Popular afterlife

Two legends—the Masonic archer and the bribery monument—now occupy more public attention than many of Troiani’s formally commissioned works. They have turned him into an unwitting participant in narratives about:

- La Plata’s allegedly esoteric city plan;
- Catholic–Masonic conflict;
- Argentina’s “Década Infame”;
- enduring public-works corruption.

Neither legend establishes Troiani’s own political intention.

## Atlas Connections

Only source-supported intersections with people on the supplied atlas roster are included. Shared residence in Buenos Aires, contemporaneity, or inclusion in the same broad artistic field has not been treated as a connection.

### Auguste Rodin

**Evidentiary label: documented artistic influence; personal meeting unproved.**

Eduardo Migo describes *El Arquero* as modelled “in the style of Rodin.” Troiani’s more direct formal model appears to have been Bourdelle, Rodin’s pupil and collaborator. No correspondence, studio visit, or personal meeting between Troiani and Rodin was located.

### Dante Alighieri

**Evidentiary label: documented commemorative/artistic connection, not a personal relationship.**

Troiani made a bust of Dante, reportedly donated to Buenos Aires by the Asociación Latinum in connection with a Dante centenary and placed in the Palermo gardens. The work documents Troiani’s participation in Italian cultural commemoration in Argentina.

### Thomas Alva Edison

**Evidentiary label: documented commemorative/artistic connection, not a personal relationship.**

Troiani produced bas-reliefs for a monument to Edison in 1930. No meeting or correspondence is documented.

### Antonio Pujia

Antonio Pujia is not on the supplied atlas roster and therefore is not an atlas connection, but he was one of Troiani’s clearest documented personal-professional links: pupil, studio assistant, and later supervisor of an authorized posthumous casting.

### Other roster names

No reliable evidence was found of a meeting, correspondence, collaboration, rivalry, patronage relationship, family tie, or acknowledged influence involving the other listed atlas figures. In particular:

- Troiani worked on the Banco Tornquist building designed by **Alejandro Bustillo**, not rostered architect **Alejandro Christophersen**.
- His Rosario museum collaboration was with **Ángel Guido**, not rostered **Ángel della Valle**.
- The Ministry of Public Works building later acquired a major association with **Eva Perón**, but this arose after Troiani’s sculpture was installed and does not establish a personal encounter.
- He worked in a sculptural culture affected by European modernism, but no documentary connection to Pablo Picasso, Filippo Tommaso Marinetti, Benito Mussolini, Le Corbusier, or other rostered contemporaries was located.

Absence of a documented connection is the finding.
