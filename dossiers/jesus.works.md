## Works by Jesus of Nazareth

### Corpus and attribution

- **c. AD 27–30, if historical:** *The oral teaching of Jesus*. This is an orally performed body of Aramaic teaching, not a book or authorial manuscript; it survives only through differently shaped Greek traditions in Paul, the canonical Gospels, reconstructed Q, and later noncanonical witnesses, so the wording, boundaries, dates, and authorship of every individual unit remain disputed (1 Cor. 7:10–11; 9:14; 11:23–25; Mark–John; Gospel of Thomas).
- **No securely identifiable date:** *Sayings preserved outside the canonical Gospels*. These include sayings attributed to Jesus in Acts, Paul, the Gospel of Thomas, and fragmentary or patristic witnesses. Critical editions catalogue them, but whether any preserves an independent saying of the historical Jesus is decided case by case and remains controversial (Acts 20:35; 1 Thess. 4:15–17; Thomas 1–114).

### Proclamation, prayers, and protocols

- **c. AD 27–30:** *Proclamation of the Kingdom of God*. An itinerant oral project rather than a fixed text: the Synoptic tradition makes the approaching or present divine kingdom the organizing subject of Jesus’s preaching, but scholars disagree about its precise content and apocalyptic horizon (Mark 1:14–15; Matt. 4:17; Luke 4:43).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Lord’s Prayer*. A communal prayer-protocol attributed to Jesus; Matthew and Luke preserve significantly different forms, and neither can simply be identified with his exact words (Matt. 6:9–13; Luke 11:2–4; Didache 8.2).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Beatitudes*. A performative series of blessings rather than a descriptive treatise; Matthew and Luke transmit different selections, wording, and settings (Matt. 5:3–12; Luke 6:20–23).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Antitheses*. A sequence of legal and ethical pronouncements conventionally grouped under the repeated contrast between received teaching and Jesus’s own ruling. Matthew alone preserves the sequence in this form, so its unity and degree of Matthean composition are disputed (Matt. 5:21–48).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Golden Rule*. An ethical maxim transmitted in positive form by Matthew and Luke and paralleled in other ancient traditions; its precise formulation and originality are therefore not securely recoverable (Matt. 7:12; Luke 6:31).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Double Commandment of Love*. A halakhic synthesis joining love of God to love of neighbor; Mark, Matthew, and Luke frame it differently, while the constituent commandments come from Deuteronomy and Leviticus (Mark 12:28–34; Matt. 22:34–40; Luke 10:25–28).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *Mission instructions*. A protocol for itinerant emissaries concerning travel, hospitality, proclamation, rejection, and dependence on hosts. Mark, Matthew, Luke, and reconstructed Q preserve incompatible versions, indicating adaptation in transmission (Mark 6:7–13; Matt. 10; Luke 9:1–6; 10:1–16).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *Community-discipline instructions*. A protocol concerning reconciliation, correction, forgiveness, and communal authority; its developed ecclesial vocabulary makes the extent of direct historical-Jesus authorship disputed (Matt. 18).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The woes*. Performative judgments directed, in differing traditions, against the rich, complacent towns, and scribes or Pharisees. Their several arrangements resist treatment as one fixed speech (Matt. 11:20–24; 23; Luke 6:24–26; 10:13–15; 11:37–54).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The temple saying*. A compact prediction or threat involving destruction and rebuilding of the temple; its wording varies sharply, and the Gospels dispute whether Jesus spoke literally, metaphorically, or was misquoted (Mark 14:58; 15:29; John 2:19–21; Thomas 71).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The apocalyptic discourse*. An attributed prophetic complex about war, persecution, Jerusalem, cosmic upheaval, and the Son of Man. Mark 13 is normally treated as the earliest extant literary form, not as a verbatim transcript; Matthew and Luke extensively revise it (Mark 13; Matt. 24–25; Luke 21).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *Farewell and consolatory discourses*. John presents long first-person speeches concerning departure, mutual love, the Paraclete, and unity. Most historical-Jesus research treats their surviving literary form as Johannine theological composition, even where earlier tradition may underlie it (John 13–17).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The sayings from the cross*. Seven distinct sayings are conventionally assembled from four incompatible Passion narratives; no Gospel contains the later seven-part compilation, and historical attribution varies by saying (Mark 15:34; Matt. 27:46; Luke 23:34, 43, 46; John 19:26–27, 28, 30).

### Parables and narrative thought-objects

- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Sower*. A spoken agricultural parable transmitted by Mark, Matthew, Luke, and Thomas; the attached allegorical interpretation may represent a separate layer of tradition (Mark 4:3–20; Matt. 13:3–23; Luke 8:5–15; Thomas 9).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Mustard Seed*. A miniature kingdom-parable with substantially different versions in the Synoptics and Thomas (Mark 4:30–32; Matt. 13:31–32; Luke 13:18–19; Thomas 20).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Leaven*. A miniature domestic parable independently shaped in Matthew, Luke, and Thomas (Matt. 13:33; Luke 13:20–21; Thomas 96).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Seed Growing Secretly*. A kingdom-parable preserved only by Mark; its historical attribution consequently rests on a single extant literary witness (Mark 4:26–29).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Weeds among the Wheat*. A parable and appended allegorical interpretation preserved only by Matthew; their relation to one another and to Jesus is disputed (Matt. 13:24–30, 36–43).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Hidden Treasure*. A brief kingdom-parable preserved in Matthew and a differing Thomasine version (Matt. 13:44; Thomas 109).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Pearl*. A brief kingdom-parable preserved in Matthew and, differently, Thomas (Matt. 13:45–46; Thomas 76).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Net*. A fishing parable preserved in Matthew and Thomas; Matthew adds an eschatological interpretation (Matt. 13:47–50; Thomas 8).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Lost Sheep*. A recovery parable variously made to concern vulnerable disciples, repentant sinners, or a favored sheep (Matt. 18:12–14; Luke 15:3–7; Thomas 107).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Lost Coin*. A recovery parable preserved only by Luke among the canonical Gospels (Luke 15:8–10).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Two Debtors*. A parable of forgiveness embedded in Luke’s account of anointing; it should not be confused with Matthew’s Unforgiving Servant (Luke 7:40–43).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Good Samaritan*. A narrative ethical parable preserved only by Luke; its familiar title is editorial convention, not an ancient title supplied by Jesus (Luke 10:25–37).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Friend at Midnight*. A petition-and-hospitality parable preserved only by Luke (Luke 11:5–8).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Rich Fool*. A mortality-and-possession parable preserved by Luke, with a related but not identical Thomasine form (Luke 12:16–21; Thomas 63).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Watchful Servants*. An eschatological vigilance parable whose boundaries overlap several transmitted sayings (Luke 12:35–40; cf. Mark 13:33–37; Matt. 24:42–44).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Faithful and Unfaithful Slave*. A household-management parable in Matthew and Luke, probably inherited from shared tradition but differently expanded (Matt. 24:45–51; Luke 12:42–48).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Barren Fig Tree*. A reprieve-and-judgment parable preserved only by Luke, distinct from the enacted cursing of a fig tree (Luke 13:6–9).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Great Banquet*. An invitation-and-refusal parable transmitted in Luke, Matthew’s more violent royal-wedding form, and Thomas (Matt. 22:1–14; Luke 14:15–24; Thomas 64).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Tower Builder and the Warring King*. Two brief deliberation parables joined by Luke to the cost of discipleship; whether Jesus originally paired them is unknown (Luke 14:28–33).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Prodigal Son, or the Two Sons*. A family narrative about loss, restoration, and resentment preserved only by Luke; both conventional titles foreground only part of its structure (Luke 15:11–32).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Dishonest Manager*. A deliberately troubling prudential parable preserved only by Luke; the several moralizing conclusions may be secondary applications (Luke 16:1–13).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Rich Man and Lazarus*. An afterlife reversal narrative preserved only by Luke and drawing on wider ancient folktale motifs (Luke 16:19–31).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Unjust Judge and the Widow*. A persistence-and-justice parable preserved only by Luke, who supplies its explicit interpretive frame (Luke 18:1–8).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Pharisee and the Tax Collector*. A prayer-and-status reversal parable preserved only by Luke (Luke 18:9–14).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Workers in the Vineyard*. A wage-and-generosity parable preserved only by Matthew (Matt. 20:1–16).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Two Sons*. A terse obedience parable preserved only by Matthew and surviving in significantly variant textual forms (Matt. 21:28–32).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Wicked Tenants*. A vineyard-conflict parable transmitted by all three Synoptics and Thomas; its allegorical features and violent conclusion have generated substantial debate over its earliest form (Mark 12:1–12; Matt. 21:33–46; Luke 20:9–19; Thomas 65–66).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Ten Virgins*. An eschatological delay-and-readiness parable preserved only by Matthew (Matt. 25:1–13).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Talents or Minas*. A stewardship-and-judgment parable in sharply different Matthean and Lukan versions; its original plot and application cannot be recovered with certainty (Matt. 25:14–30; Luke 19:11–27).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Sheep and the Goats*. An eschatological judgment tableau that functions as parable, prophetic vision, and ethical manifesto; it is preserved only by Matthew (Matt. 25:31–46).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Strong Man*. A compact metaphorical parable concerning the binding or defeat of a strong householder, transmitted in Mark, Matthew, Luke, and Thomas (Mark 3:27; Matt. 12:29; Luke 11:21–22; Thomas 35).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Children in the Marketplace*. A comparison directed at an unreceptive generation, transmitted by Matthew and Luke in their shared sayings material (Matt. 11:16–19; Luke 7:31–35).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Returning Unclean Spirit*. A cautionary miniature transmitted by Matthew and Luke; Matthew adds an explicit application to his generation (Matt. 12:43–45; Luke 11:24–26).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Burglar*. A vigilance image independently transmitted in several contexts and in Thomas, making its original application uncertain (Matt. 24:43–44; Luke 12:39–40; Thomas 21, 103).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The New Patch and New Wine*. Paired material metaphors transmitted by the Synoptics and Thomas, with variation in wording and application (Mark 2:21–22; Matt. 9:16–17; Luke 5:36–39; Thomas 47).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The House on Rock or Sand*. A concluding wisdom-parable about hearing and acting, preserved in Matthew and Luke in different forms (Matt. 7:24–27; Luke 6:47–49).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Lamp*. A cluster of sayings treating a lamp as a figure for disclosure, witness, or perception; their differing placements resist classification as one stable composition (Mark 4:21–22; Matt. 5:14–16; Luke 8:16–17; 11:33; Thomas 33).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Eye as Lamp*. A compact bodily metaphor preserved in Matthew and Luke; its original context is uncertain (Matt. 6:22–23; Luke 11:34–36).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Blind Leading the Blind*. A proverbial image found in Matthew, Luke, and Thomas rather than a securely bounded narrative parable (Matt. 15:14; Luke 6:39; Thomas 34).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Speck and the Log*. A comic ethical image transmitted in Matthew, Luke, and Thomas; its exact wording changed in oral and literary transmission (Matt. 7:3–5; Luke 6:41–42; Thomas 26).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Wise and Foolish Bridesmaids, servants, stewards, farmers, fishers, and householders*. Beyond the separately named narratives above, the sources contain numerous short comparisons and metaphorical fragments whose boundaries are editorially unstable; they resist being catalogued as individually titled “works.”

### Manifestations and transformed matter attributed to Jesus

- **c. AD 27–30:** *Healings*. The sources attribute a large, multiply attested practice of bodily restoration to Jesus, including individually narrated healings and collective summaries. Historical scholars commonly regard Jesus as having had a reputation as a healer, while diagnoses, supernatural causation, individual identifications, and narrative detail remain disputed (Mark 1:29–34, 40–45; 2:1–12; 3:1–6; 5:21–43; 7:31–37; 8:22–26 and parallels).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *Exorcisms*. These are enacted liberations rather than objects: the Synoptic traditions repeatedly represent Jesus expelling spirits, whereas John narrates none. Historical occurrence, ancient diagnosis, and the literary shaping of each episode must be distinguished (Mark 1:21–28; 5:1–20; 7:24–30; 9:14–29 and parallels).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The transformed water at Cana*. John attributes the creation or manifestation of wine from water to Jesus; it is absent from the Synoptics and cannot be independently established historically (John 2:1–11).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The multiplied loaves and fish*. A provision or manifestation narrated in all four Gospels, with Mark and Matthew also preserving a second feeding; whether these are separate events, duplicated tradition, symbolic narrative, or miracle is disputed (Mark 6:30–44; 8:1–10; Matt. 14:13–21; 15:32–39; Luke 9:10–17; John 6:1–15).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The stilled storm*. An enacted transformation of weather and water attributed to Jesus by the Synoptics; it is a narrated manifestation rather than a surviving work, and its historicity is disputed (Mark 4:35–41; Matt. 8:23–27; Luke 8:22–25).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *Walking upon the sea*. A manifestation attributed by Mark, Matthew, and John but omitted by Luke; historical, symbolic, and theological readings compete (Mark 6:45–52; Matt. 14:22–33; John 6:16–21).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The coin in the fish*. A material provision attributed to Jesus only in Matthew, though the story does not explicitly narrate him making either coin or fish (Matt. 17:24–27).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The withered fig tree*. An enacted prophetic sign in Mark and Matthew, with different chronologies; Luke instead supplies the Barren Fig Tree parable (Mark 11:12–14, 20–25; Matt. 21:18–22).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *Persons restored to life*. The Gospels attribute the restoration of Jairus’s daughter, a widow’s son, and Lazarus to Jesus; the three cases have different and partly independent textual pedigrees, and their historicity is disputed (Mark 5:21–43 and parallels; Luke 7:11–17; John 11:1–44).
- **After the crucifixion, according to the sources:** *Resurrection appearances and signs*. These are manifestations attributed to the risen Jesus, not straightforward products made by the historical person; Paul and the Gospels preserve divergent lists and narratives, and their historical and ontological status is disputed (1 Cor. 15:3–8; Matt. 28; Luke 24; John 20–21).

### Enacted signs, institutions, and social projects

- **c. AD 27–30:** *The Twelve*. A deliberately constituted group symbolizing restored Israel: an institutional and social creation rather than a text. The existence of the group is multiply attested, but membership lists differ and the role of individual members is uncertain (Mark 3:13–19; Matt. 10:1–4; Luke 6:12–16; 1 Cor. 15:5).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The itinerant disciple movement*. Jesus is represented as gathering women and men into a mobile community of proclamation, shared resources, hospitality, and altered kinship. Its exact organization cannot be reconstructed, and the later church should not simply be equated with this historical project (Mark 1:16–20; 3:31–35; 10:28–31; Luke 8:1–3).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *Open or controversial table fellowship*. Repeated meals with followers, tax collectors, and people labeled sinners form a social manifestation of Jesus’s message rather than a discrete authored object; individual scenes and their interpretation are variously redacted (Mark 2:15–17; Matt. 11:19; Luke 7:34, 36–50; 15:1–2).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The enacted entry into Jerusalem*. A prophetic street action combining procession, animal, crowd, and scriptural allusion—something close to a brief Gesamtkunstwerk. All four Gospels narrate it, but disagree on details and chronology (Mark 11:1–11; Matt. 21:1–11; Luke 19:28–40; John 12:12–19).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The temple action*. An enacted protest involving merchants or money changers; the Synoptics place it in Jesus’s final week, while John places it near the beginning of his ministry. Its scale, meaning, words, and relation to Jesus’s execution remain disputed (Mark 11:15–19; Matt. 21:12–17; Luke 19:45–48; John 2:13–22).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The last meal tradition*. A ritualized meal in which bread and wine become interpretive signs of Jesus’s death: simultaneously performance, protocol, and the germ of a continuing communal rite. Paul supplies the earliest written account; the Synoptics vary, and John has a meal and foot-washing but no institution formula (1 Cor. 11:23–26; Mark 14:22–25; Matt. 26:26–29; Luke 22:14–20; John 13).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *Foot-washing as example*. John attributes to Jesus an enacted service and a protocol for repetition; no Synoptic Gospel records it, and its historical relation to Johannine community practice is debated (John 13:1–17).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *Baptizing activity*. John says Jesus and his followers made and baptized disciples, then qualifies that the baptizing was performed by the disciples. It therefore resists attribution as Jesus’s own act or institution (John 3:22–26; 4:1–2).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *The church*. Matthew alone attributes to Jesus a prospective act of “building” an assembly; historians dispute whether this wording belongs to Jesus or to the post-Easter Matthean community, and no institutional church designed by Jesus survives as a separately documentable object (Matt. 16:18; 18:17).

### Material objects and disputed craft

- **Before c. AD 27:** *Unidentified products of a tekton*. Mark calls Jesus a *tekton*, a construction craftsman or carpenter, while Matthew transfers the designation to his father. No object can be securely identified as something Jesus manufactured, so this possible material oeuvre has no recoverable catalogue (Mark 6:3; Matt. 13:55).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *Mud paste used on a blind man’s eyes*. John explicitly represents Jesus making a paste from saliva and earth; it is an ephemeral healing material rather than a durable work, and the episode lacks Synoptic attestation (John 9:1–7).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *A whip of cords*. John says Jesus fashioned this temporary implement during the temple action. The Synoptics mention no whip, and even in John its targets and use are grammatically debated (John 2:15).
- **c. AD 27–30:** *Writing on the ground*. The story of the accused woman twice depicts Jesus writing, but neither the marks nor their meaning is preserved. The passage is absent from the earliest and best manuscripts of John and circulates in several locations, so it cannot establish a surviving written work by Jesus (John 7:53–8:11).
- **No surviving date or object:** *Books, letters, inscriptions, furniture, or buildings by Jesus*. No extant ancient source identifies a recoverable example authored or fabricated by him; later relics and legends therefore cannot supply a critical catalogue of material works.
