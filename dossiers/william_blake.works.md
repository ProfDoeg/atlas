## Works by William Blake

### Illuminated books and plates — Gesamtkunstwerke

- **c. 1788:** *All Religions are One*. A sequence of aphoristic propositions made by relief etching, joining words, lettering, ornament, and image; known from a single incomplete copy and later impressions.
- **c. 1788:** *There is No Natural Religion*. Two related series of relief-etched propositions; no complete contemporary copy survives, so its intended order and completeness remain uncertain.
- **1789:** *Songs of Innocence*. A self-published collection of lyric, image, typography, printing, and hand coloring; individual copies are materially different manifestations, not interchangeable editions of a purely verbal text.
- **1789:** *The Book of Thel*. An illuminated poem printed in several states and at several dates; its surviving copies differ in coloring, plate order, and occasionally contents.
- **c. 1790–93:** *The Marriage of Heaven and Hell*. An illuminated satire, prophecy, and manifesto made through relief etching; copies vary, and Blake issued some concluding plates separately.
- **1793:** *For Children: The Gates of Paradise*. An emblem book of engraved images with terse captions; Blake substantially revised it decades later for another audience.
- **1793:** *Visions of the Daughters of Albion*. An illuminated prophetic poem whose text and designs form one composite object.
- **1793:** *America a Prophecy*. An illuminated history-myth; its copies have variant coloring, and some plates also circulated outside bound copies.
- **1794:** *Europe a Prophecy*. An illuminated prophecy whose famous frontispiece, commonly called *The Ancient of Days*, also exists as a separately finished print.
- **1794:** *Songs of Experience*. A companion yet contrary collection to *Songs of Innocence*, made in relief etching and normally hand colored.
- **1794 onward:** *Songs of Innocence and of Experience, Shewing the Two Contrary States of the Human Soul*. Blake’s recombination of two previously distinct books; plate order and coloring differ among copies, so the obra is also a family of manifestations.
- **1794:** *The First Book of Urizen*. A deliberately pseudo-biblical illuminated book; later copies sometimes omit “First” from the title, and the surviving copies differ substantially.
- **1795:** *The Book of Ahania*. A small illuminated prophecy known from only one complete copy and one separate impression.
- **1795:** *The Book of Los*. An illuminated prophecy printed intaglio rather than in Blake’s usual relief process, making it a technical exception within the illuminated books.
- **1795:** *The Song of Los*. A composite illuminated work containing the sections “Africa” and “Asia.”
- **c. 1804–11:** *Milton a Poem*. An illuminated prophetic book conceived in two books; copy lengths and plate sequences vary, and its production extended across several years.
- **c. 1804–20:** *Jerusalem: The Emanation of the Giant Albion*. Blake’s longest illuminated book, made as one hundred plates but surviving in differently printed, ordered, and colored copies; it is a paradigmatic Gesamtkunstwerk rather than merely an illustrated poem.
- **1818:** *For the Sexes: The Gates of Paradise*. A revised and expanded manifestation of the 1793 emblem book, with new texts and a changed title.
- **c. 1818–20:** *On Homer’s Poetry [and] On Virgil*. Two brief illuminated critical manifestos conventionally treated as the recto and verso of one plate.
- **1822:** *The Ghost of Abel*. A single-sheet illuminated drama addressed to Lord Byron; it is at once play, print, polemic, and pictorial object.
- **c. 1826–27:** *Laocoön*. A densely inscribed engraving transformed from an earlier commercial plate into a manifesto on art, religion, commerce, and imagination.

### Typographically printed and manuscript literary obras

- **written c. 1769–77; printed 1783:** *Poetical Sketches*. Blake’s first printed collection, privately produced by friends; the printing is conventional typography rather than illuminated printing.
- **c. 1784–85:** *An Island in the Moon*. An unfinished satirical prose manuscript with songs and sketches; leaves appear to be missing, and it resists classification as either completed novella, play, or notebook.
- **c. 1789:** *Tiriel*. An unpublished manuscript poem accompanied by a projected series of drawings; the verbal and pictorial components were never made into a finished book.
- **1791:** *The French Revolution*. A typographically printed prophetic poem announced as the first of seven books; only page proofs of Book I survive, and whether the published issue ever appeared remains disputed.
- **c. 1793:** *The Song of Liberty*. A concluding illuminated proclamation attached to *The Marriage of Heaven and Hell* in some copies; it can be read as part of that book or as a distinct manifesto.
- **c. 1795–97:** *The Mental Traveller*. A manuscript poem preserved in Blake’s Notebook rather than issued as an independent work.
- **c. 1795–1804:** *Vala, or The Four Zoas*. A repeatedly revised, unfinished manuscript epic written over proofs for Edward Young’s *Night Thoughts*; its unstable layers resist reconstruction as a single final text.
- **c. 1800–03:** *The Pickering Manuscript*. A fair-copy manuscript containing ten poems, including the group often called the “Ballads”; the collective title is modern, and the manuscript may represent a projected collection rather than a completed book.
- **1801–03:** *The Four Zoas* revisions. Extensive rewriting of *Vala* that changed its title, structure, and mythology; these layers are a continuing project rather than a separately completed poem.
- **1809:** *A Descriptive Catalogue of Pictures, Poetical and Historical Inventions*. A printed catalogue for Blake’s exhibition and a critical manifesto asserting his theory of art.
- **1810:** *Public Address*. An unfinished manuscript manifesto on engraving, patronage, artistic originality, and Blake’s dispute with Robert Cromek; it survives among Notebook drafts.
- **c. 1810:** *A Vision of the Last Judgment*. A prose description and aesthetic manifesto derived from Blake’s lost or unexecuted exhibition picture; multiple manuscript versions survive.
- **c. 1810:** *The Everlasting Gospel*. An unfinished constellation of manuscript verses rather than a settled poem; editors differ over the order and boundaries of its fragments.
- **c. 1811:** *An Appeal to the Public*. An unfinished prose defense of Blake’s engraving of *Chaucer’s Canterbury Pilgrims*.
- **c. 1818:** *The Accusers of Theft, Adultery, Murder*. A late satirical manuscript poem surviving in Blake’s Notebook.
- **c. 1787–1818:** *The Notebook of William Blake* (the Rossetti Manuscript). A working object containing poems, prose, drawings, engravings, revisions, and accounts; it is not a single work in the ordinary sense but the material site from which many works emerged.
- **c. 1770s–1827:** *Letters*. Blake’s surviving correspondence forms a scattered body of made texts, including discussions of commissions, visions, technique, and aesthetics; survival is accidental and incomplete.
- **c. 1780s–1820s:** *Annotations and marginalia*. Blake wrote extensive responses in books by Reynolds, Swedenborg, Lavater, Bacon, Watson, Thornton, and others; these interventions resist being called independent works because their material support and argumentative occasion belong to another author’s book.

### Independent prints and print series

- **1780:** *Joseph of Arimathea among the Rocks of Albion*. An engraving after a figure attributed to Michelangelo; Blake later reworked the plate and added a personal inscription.
- **1782:** *Morning Amusement*. A reproductive engraving after Watteau, made as commercial printmaking rather than as a Blakean pictorial invention.
- **1782:** *Evening Amusement*. The companion reproductive engraving after Watteau.
- **1784:** *A Breach in a City, the Morning after the Battle*. A separately issued engraving after Henry Fuseli.
- **1784:** *The Fall of Rosamond*. A separately issued stipple engraving after Thomas Stothard.
- **1784:** *The Witches of Shakespeare*. A separate engraving after Fuseli’s design.
- **1785:** *The Approach of Doom*. A separate engraving after Fuseli.
- **1788:** *The Accusers*. A small engraved emblem generally associated with Blake’s early experiments in combining text and design.
- **1790:** *The Beggar’s Opera*. An engraving after William Hogarth’s painting.
- **1793:** *Job*. A large line engraving based on Blake’s own design, distinct from his later Job watercolor and engraving cycles.
- **1793:** *Edward and Elenor*. A separate print designed and engraved by Blake.
- **1793:** *The Accusers of Theft, Adultery, Murder* (“The Accusers”). A separate relief-etched print; its relationship to later Notebook verses bearing the same conventional title is associative rather than a simple edition relationship.
- **1795:** *The Large Book of Designs*. A group of color-printed impressions drawn from plates for Blake’s illuminated books and separately finished; surviving copies differ and the title is bibliographical rather than Blake’s.
- **1796:** *The Small Book of Designs*. A companion portfolio of separately printed and colored illuminated-book designs; it too is a constructed group rather than a conventional authored book.
- **1796:** *George Cumberland’s Card*. A small engraved calling card designed and engraved by Blake.
- **c. 1796–1800:** *Albion Rose*. A color-printed relief etching, also known by the descriptive title *Glad Day*; the popular title is not securely Blake’s.
- **c. 1803–04:** *The Man Sweeping the Interpreter’s Parlour*. A separate engraving after an emblem in Bunyan, accompanied by Blake’s own verse.
- **1804:** *Chaucers Canterbury Pilgrims*. A painting and, subsequently, an independently published engraving; the two are related but materially distinct obras.
- **1805:** *Rev. John Caspar Lavater*. A portrait engraving after Henry Fuseli.
- **1809:** *The Canterbury Pilgrims*. Blake’s tempera painting made for exhibition and as the basis of his print; it competed directly with Thomas Stothard’s treatment of the subject.
- **1810:** *Chaucer’s Canterbury Pilgrims*. Blake’s large engraved manifestation of the composition, issued by subscription and accompanied by polemical advertising.
- **1819–20:** *Visionary Heads*. A large body of rapid portrait drawings, principally made during séances with John Varley and representing historical, legendary, and visionary sitters; it resists being treated as a fixed series because the sheets are dispersed and its boundaries are uncertain.
- **1823–26:** *Illustrations of the Book of Job*. A title page and twenty-one engraved plates designed and engraved by Blake; this is a complete print cycle, distinct from the earlier Job watercolors from which it developed.
- **1825–27:** *Illustrations to Dante*. Seven engravings were begun from Blake’s watercolor project but remained in various unfinished states at his death; the print series is therefore an incomplete project.

### Large color-printed drawings

- **1795:** *God Judging Adam*. A large color print finished by hand; impressions are individually worked manifestations rather than mechanically identical copies.
- **1795:** *Satan Exulting over Eve*. A large color-printed drawing, conventionally grouped with eleven related designs.
- **1795:** *The House of Death*. A large color print after imagery from Milton.
- **1795:** *Nebuchadnezzar*. A large color print finished in ink and watercolor.
- **1795:** *Newton*. A large color print later reworked in at least one impression; its hybrid technique lies between monotype, print, drawing, and painting.
- **1795:** *Naomi Entreating Ruth and Orpah to Return to the Land of Moab*. A large color-printed biblical design.
- **1795:** *Pity*. A large color print derived from an image in Shakespeare’s *Macbeth*.
- **1795:** *Hecate, or The Night of Enitharmon’s Joy*. A large color print whose familiar titles are conventional and vary among catalogues.
- **1795:** *Lamech and His Two Wives*. A large color-printed biblical design.
- **1795:** *Elohim Creating Adam*. A large color print finished by hand.
- **1795:** *Christ Appearing to the Apostles after the Resurrection*. A large color-printed drawing.
- **1795:** *The Good and Evil Angels Struggling for Possession of a Child*. A large color print related to a design in *The Marriage of Heaven and Hell*; catalogues also call it *The Good and Evil Angels*.

### Original paintings, drawings, and pictorial cycles

- **c. 1774–79:** *Westminster Abbey drawings*. Blake’s antiquarian studies made while apprenticed to James Basire; they are documentary exercises and workshop products, and precise attribution among surviving sheets is sometimes uncertain.
- **c. 1780–85:** *Early historical and literary drawings*. A dispersed body of works on British history, Shakespeare, Ossian, and the Bible; many titles are modern descriptions rather than names supplied by Blake.
- **c. 1789:** *Designs for Tiriel*. Twelve surviving monochrome drawings for the unpublished poem, with further designs presumed lost or never executed.
- **c. 1793–96:** *Illustrations to the Bible for Thomas Butts*. An evolving body of biblical watercolors rather than a single fixed commission.
- **1795–97:** *Illustrations to Edward Young’s Night Thoughts*. Blake made 537 watercolor designs around the printed text; only a small selection was engraved, making the surviving volumes an enormous illustrated project rather than a published Blake book.
- **1797:** *The Sea of Time and Space*. A watercolor drawing of disputed literary subject, often linked to Milton but not securely identified by Blake.
- **1797–98:** *Illustrations to Thomas Gray’s Poems*. A set of 116 watercolor designs made around the leaves of a printed edition; the unique copy is a Gesamtkunstwerk combining Gray’s typography with Blake’s pictorial response.
- **c. 1799–1803:** *Biblical temperas for Thomas Butts*. Approximately fifty small paintings on biblical subjects were projected or made; survival is incomplete, and catalogue boundaries and dates remain debated.
- **c. 1799–1805:** *Biblical watercolors for Thomas Butts*. A large, dispersed series whose individual sheets include Old and New Testament subjects; it is a commissioned body of work rather than a formally published cycle.
- **c. 1800:** *The Nativity*. A tempera painting, one of Blake’s biblical “frescoes”—his own term for a glue-based tempera technique, not true wall fresco.
- **c. 1800:** *The Last Supper*. A biblical tempera whose surviving state has been affected by the fragility of Blake’s materials.
- **c. 1800:** *The Christ Child Asleep on a Cross*. A tempera painting also known through related versions and designs.
- **c. 1800:** *The Virgin Hushing the Young Baptist*. A tempera painting; titles for such biblical subjects are commonly assigned by cataloguers.
- **c. 1800:** *The Flight into Egypt*. A biblical tempera.
- **c. 1800:** *The Agony in the Garden*. A biblical tempera made for Thomas Butts.
- **c. 1800:** *Christ in the Sepulchre, Guarded by Angels*. A biblical tempera.
- **c. 1800:** *The Resurrection*. A biblical tempera from the Butts group.
- **c. 1800–03:** *Illustrations to Milton’s Comus*. Two principal watercolor series survive, one made for Thomas Butts and another for the Rev. Joseph Thomas; they are related manifestations, not duplicates.
- **c. 1801–05:** *Illustrations to Milton’s Paradise Lost*. Multiple watercolor series were made for different patrons, including Thomas Butts, the Rev. Joseph Thomas, and John Linnell; their designs overlap but each series is a distinct obra.
- **c. 1801–09:** *Illustrations to Milton’s “On the Morning of Christ’s Nativity”*. Two related watercolor series survive; their dating and sequence have been debated.
- **c. 1801–09:** *Illustrations to Milton’s L’Allegro and Il Penseroso*. A paired watercolor project interpreting Milton’s companion poems.
- **c. 1801–09:** *Illustrations to Milton’s Paradise Regained*. A watercolor series made for Thomas Butts.
- **1805:** *Illustrations to Robert Blair’s The Grave*. Blake made finished watercolor designs and preliminary studies; the publisher transferred the engraving commission to Louis Schiavonetti, so Blake’s pictorial project and the published plates are not the same made thing.
- **1805–06:** *The Grave watercolors*. Nineteen finished watercolors were rediscovered in 2001, while others are known separately or presumed lost; the surviving set is incomplete relative to Blake’s project.
- **1805–09:** *The Ancient Britons*. A monumental painting exhibited in 1809 and now lost; its title, scale, and subject survive through Blake’s catalogue and contemporary evidence, but the object itself does not.
- **1805–09:** *Sir Jeffery Chaucer and the Nine and Twenty Pilgrims on Their Journey to Canterbury*. Blake’s painting of the Canterbury pilgrimage, usually shortened to *The Canterbury Pilgrims*.
- **1805–09:** *The Spiritual Form of Nelson Guiding Leviathan*. A tempera painting presented as a visionary history picture.
- **1805–09:** *The Spiritual Form of Pitt Guiding Behemoth*. A companion visionary history painting.
- **1805–09:** *The Bard, from Gray*. A painting exhibited in 1809; its identification with surviving versions has generated cataloguing debate.
- **1805–09:** *The Bramins*. A drawing exhibited in 1809 and now untraced; it survives as a documented but materially absent obra.
- **1805–09:** *The Goats*. A drawing exhibited in 1809 and now untraced.
- **1805–09:** *The Last Judgment*. A major exhibition composition known through related drawings, watercolors, and Blake’s prose descriptions; which surviving design most directly represents the exhibited picture remains uncertain.
- **c. 1809–10:** *A Vision of the Last Judgment*. The pictorial project corresponding to Blake’s prose text; its several designs and descriptions do not resolve into one securely surviving final painting.
- **c. 1811–20:** *Illustrations to Milton for Thomas Butts*. A later group of watercolors that overlaps several Miltonic texts; scholarly catalogues distinguish component series, but Blake did not publish them as a named collection.
- **c. 1815–20:** *Illustrations to John Milton’s L’Allegro and Il Penseroso for John Linnell*. A later watercolor cycle commissioned by Linnell.
- **c. 1816–20:** *Illustrations to the Book of Enoch*. A small group of drawings and watercolors; titles and intended sequence are partly conjectural.
- **c. 1816–20:** *Enoch Walked with God*. A watercolor biblical design that also functions as a self-sufficient picture.
- **c. 1817–20:** *Illustrations to Bunyan’s Pilgrim’s Progress*. A set of twenty-eight watercolor drawings; whether Blake planned publication is uncertain.
- **c. 1819–20:** *The Visionary Head of the Ghost of a Flea*. A drawing made during the Blake–Varley visionary sessions.
- **c. 1819–20:** *The Ghost of a Flea*. A small tempera painting developed from the visionary-head drawing.
- **c. 1819–20:** *The Man Who Built the Pyramids*. A visionary-head drawing; the identification records the vision claimed by Blake and Varley, not a historically grounded portrait.
- **c. 1819–20:** *Socrates*. A visionary-head drawing.
- **c. 1819–20:** *Caractacus*. A visionary-head drawing.
- **c. 1819–20:** *Wat Tyler*. A visionary-head drawing whose subject identification comes from the séance context.
- **c. 1819–20:** *King Edward III*. A visionary-head drawing.
- **c. 1819–20:** *William Wallace*. A visionary-head drawing.
- **c. 1819–20:** *Richard Coeur de Lion*. A visionary-head drawing.
- **c. 1819–20:** *The Head of a Damned Soul in Dante’s Inferno*. A visionary drawing later associated with Blake’s Dante project.
- **c. 1821:** *Illustrations to Virgil’s Pastorals*. Blake made watercolor designs and seventeen wood engravings for Robert John Thornton’s school edition; the tiny blocks constitute a distinct graphic cycle despite being embedded in another author’s book.
- **c. 1821–22:** *Job watercolors for John Linnell*. A set of twenty-one finished watercolors developed from an earlier Butts series and used for the engraved *Illustrations of the Book of Job*.
- **c. 1824–27:** *Illustrations to Bunyan’s Pilgrim’s Progress*. Blake returned to the subject in a late watercolor sequence; the project remained unpublished.
- **1824–27:** *Illustrations to Dante’s Divine Comedy*. A project of 102 surviving watercolors and related sketches commissioned by John Linnell; it remained unfinished at Blake’s death and therefore survives as a project rather than a completed cycle.
- **1824–27:** *The Genesis Manuscript*. Eleven pages of pencil designs and text for an illustrated Genesis; unfinished and unpublished at Blake’s death.

### Designs and engravings made for other people’s books

- **1774–79:** *Richard Gough, Sepulchral Monuments in Great Britain*. Antiquarian drawings and engravings made within Basire’s workshop; because plates were conventionally signed by the master, the extent of Blake’s hand is not always demonstrable.
- **1780:** *Thomas Commins, An Elegy Set to Music*. Blake engraved the frontispiece after Thomas Stothard.
- **1782:** *John Scott, Poetical Works*. Commercial engravings by Blake after Stothard and other designers.
- **1783:** *John Flaxman, A Letter to the Committee for Raising the Naval Pillar*. Blake supplied reproductive engraving work associated with Flaxman’s designs.
- **1784:** *The New and Complete English Traveller*. Blake contributed commercial engraved plates after other artists.
- **1786:** *Memoirs of Mrs. Sophia Baddeley*. Blake engraved plates after Stothard.
- **1787–89:** *The Works of the English Poets*. Blake contributed reproductive engravings to John Bell’s publishing project.
- **1788:** *Johann Caspar Lavater, Aphorisms on Man*. Blake engraved the frontispiece after Fuseli and extensively annotated his own copy; the engraving and marginalia are distinct interventions.
- **1791:** *Mary Wollstonecraft, Original Stories from Real Life*. Blake designed and engraved six illustrations; the pictures are his creations inside Wollstonecraft’s book, not a book authored by Blake.
- **1791:** *Joseph Priestley, The History and Present State of Electricity*. Blake contributed a commercial scientific plate after another designer.
- **1792:** *Erasmus Darwin, The Botanic Garden*. Blake engraved several plates after Fuseli and others.
- **1793:** *John Gay, Fables*. Blake engraved plates after Stothard and other designers.
- **1793:** *Gottfried August Bürger, Leonora*. Blake designed and engraved illustrations for the English translation.
- **1794:** *Joseph Ritson, A Select Collection of English Songs*. Blake engraved plates after Stothard.
- **1796:** *John Gabriel Stedman, Narrative of a Five Years’ Expedition against the Revolted Negroes of Surinam*. Blake engraved sixteen plates after Stedman and other sources; several graphically record torture and enslavement, and authorship of the images is divided between designer, witness, engraver, and publisher.
- **1796–97:** *Edward Young, The Complaint, and the Consolation; or, Night Thoughts*. Blake designed and engraved forty-three published plates around the typography; the commercial edition realizes only a fraction of his 537-watercolor project.
- **1797:** *Henry Fuseli, The Botanic Garden plates and related works*. Blake’s reproductive engravings transmit Fuseli’s designs and should not be silently reclassified as wholly Blake-invented images.
- **1798:** *Thomas Allen, A New and Complete History of the County of York*. Blake contributed commercial engraving.
- **1798:** *Thomas Gray, Poems*. Blake’s unique illustrated copy for John Flaxman contains 116 original watercolors, although the underlying printed book and verbal text are Gray’s.
- **1800:** *William Hayley, Little Tom the Sailor*. Blake designed and engraved the illustrated broadside and participated in its production.
- **1802:** *William Hayley, The Triumphs of Temper*. Blake engraved plates for the thirteenth edition after Maria Flaxman.
- **1803–04:** *William Hayley, The Life and Posthumous Writings of William Cowper*. Blake engraved portraits and other plates after several designers.
- **1805:** *William Hayley, Ballads Founded on Anecdotes Relating to Animals*. Blake designed and engraved illustrations; the larger projected series was curtailed.
- **1807:** *William Hayley, Designs to a Series of Ballads*. Blake’s surviving designs and engravings represent an incomplete publishing project rather than a wholly realized book.
- **1808:** *Robert Blair, The Grave*. The published book contains engravings by Louis Schiavonetti after Blake’s designs; Blake made the images but did not engrave the issued plates, despite earlier expectations that he would.
- **1813:** *William Hayley, The Life of George Romney*. Blake engraved plates after Romney and other artists.
- **1817:** *John Flaxman, Compositions from the Works, Days, and Theogony of Hesiod*. Blake engraved Flaxman’s designs.
- **1819–20:** *John Varley, A Treatise on Zodiacal Physiognomy*. Blake supplied visionary-head drawings, one of which was engraved for the published book; the projected publication was incomplete.
- **1820:** *The Cyclopaedia; or, Universal Dictionary of Arts, Sciences, and Literature*. Blake contributed commercial engravings after other designers.
- **1821:** *Robert John Thornton, The Pastorals of Virgil*. Blake supplied designs and seventeen wood engravings; contemporary criticism of their roughness helped define their later reputation as radically original.
- **1822:** *Robert John Thornton, The Lord’s Prayer*. Blake engraved an illustration after his own design.
- **1824:** *John Linnell, portrait and commission work*. Blake made engravings and drawings within Linnell’s patronage network; individual objects remain separately catalogued rather than forming a Blake-authored publication.

### Works known only through documents, lost works, and resistant objects

- **c. 1780s–1820s:** *Untraced paintings, drawings, sketches, and impressions*. Butlin’s catalogue records works known from sales, early descriptions, copies, or related designs whose present locations are unknown; they exist as documented obras, but a complete title-and-date list cannot be recovered without inventing identities.
- **1784–85:** *Print-shop productions made with James Parker*. Some commercial plates emerged from a partnership or workshop, making an exclusively individual attribution to Blake unsafe.
- **1790s–1820s:** *Hand coloring by Catherine Blake and workshop collaboration*. Many illuminated books and prints were printed, bound, or colored with Catherine Blake’s assistance; “made by William Blake” does not always mean made by him alone.
- **1790s–1827:** *Separate proofs, rejected states, posthumously assembled copies, and loose impressions from illuminated books*. These are genuine material manifestations, but treating every impression as a separately titled work would confuse object history with the catalogue of intellectual and pictorial obras.
- **1809:** *Works in Blake’s exhibition whose present identities are uncertain*. Blake’s *Descriptive Catalogue* documents sixteen exhibits, several now lost or disputed; scholarly catalogues preserve the uncertainty rather than assigning convenient modern objects to every entry.
- **1827:** *The final Dante drawings*. Blake was reportedly working on the Dante project shortly before his death; there is no defensible single “last work,” and the unfinished sheets resist a terminal ordering.
