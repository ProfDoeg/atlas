## Works by Mary Wigman

### Solo dances and solo cycles

- **1914:** *Hexentanz I*. Early solo choreography, apparently performed without conventional musical accompaniment; it is a different work, not merely an early performance of the substantially remade *Hexentanz II*.
- **1914:** *Lento*. Solo choreography from Wigman’s first public programme.
- **1914:** *Ein Elfentag*. Early solo dance; the catalogue evidence is sparse, and the title is sometimes omitted from shorter work lists.
- **1914:** *Groteske*. Early solo dance documented in the repertory catalogue but poorly preserved.
- **1916:** *Ägyptisches Lied*. Solo dance made during Wigman’s years with Rudolf von Laban.
- **1916:** *Die bildenden Künste*. Tripartite dance conception comprising *Die Malerei*, *Die Plastik*, and *Die Grafik*; its precise division between Wigman’s authorship and Laban-school ensemble practice is not consistently specified.
- **1916–1918:** *Maskentänze*. A changing cycle of masked solos rather than one stable text; catalogues associate it with such figures as *Der Tod*, *Die Qual* or *Die Wahl*, *Der Wahn*, and *Der Schrei*, with titles and dating varying among sources.
- **1917:** *Der Tänzer unserer lieben Frau*. Solo dance whose title invokes the medieval legend; it is a danced manifestation, not an adaptation preserved as a dramatic text.
- **1917:** *Träume*. Solo choreography, now known principally through programmes and archival documentation.
- **1917:** *Gespenstertänze*. A cycle or grouping of “ghost dances”; its contents were not fixed consistently enough to treat every surviving title as an autonomous work.
- **1917:** *Ekstatische Tänze*. Cycle comprising dances catalogued as *Gebet*, *Opfer*, *Götzendienst*, and *Tempeltanz*; individual components circulated in changing programmes.
- **1918–1919:** *Marche orientale*. Solo choreography from Wigman’s early independent repertory.
- **1918–1919:** *Scherzo*. Solo dance; surviving catalogues provide little evidence of a fixed choreographic text.
- **1918–1919:** *Serenade* (also catalogued as *Serenada*). Solo dance whose title spelling varies in the documentation.
- **1918–1919:** *Yaravi*. Solo dance, sometimes grouped under *Tanzlieder* rather than listed independently.
- **1918–1919:** *Vier ungarische Tänze*. Cycle of four solos to music by Brahms; abbreviated catalogues sometimes call it simply *Ungarische Tänze*.
- **1918–1919:** *Eroica*. Solo choreography documented in Wigman’s early touring repertory.
- **1918–1919:** *Walzer*. Solo dance; its generic title makes identification across programmes uncertain.
- **1920:** *Tanzsuite*. Four-part solo suite—*Auftakt*, *Spiel*, *Walzer*, and *Allegro con brio*—to music associated in the catalogue with Dvořák.
- **1920:** *Vier Tänze nach orientalischen Motiven*. Solo suite comprising *Arabeske*, *Der Schwung*, *Das Zeichen*, and *Die Mitte*.
- **1920:** *Tänze der Nacht*. Solo cycle including *Schatten* and *Traum*; it survives as documentation of an ephemeral choreography rather than as a fully recoverable work-text.
- **1920:** *Vision*. Solo dance performed with noise or percussion rhythm; it should not automatically be conflated with the later numbered *Visionen*.
- **1920:** *Phantasie*. Solo choreography to music by Saint-Saëns.
- **1920–1923:** *Tanzrhythmen I*. Pair of solos catalogued as *Triste* and *Der Ruf*.
- **1920–1923:** *Tanzrhythmen II*. Cycle comprising *Schwertlied*, *Klage*, and *Zamacueca*; the components were also programmed separately.
- **1920–1923:** *Der Schrei*. Solo dance whose relationship to the similarly titled figure in *Maskentänze* is not consistently resolved in catalogues.
- **1920–1923:** *Der Weg*. Solo choreography, distinct from the large 1932 group work of the same title.
- **1920–1923:** *Variationen über ein heroisches Thema*. Solo variations; little of the choreography survives beyond documentary traces.
- **1921:** *Suite im alten Stil*. Four-part solo suite comprising *Polonaise*, *Gavotte*, *Sarabande*, and *Rigaudon*; some sources date it 1922.
- **1923–1924:** *Appassionato*. Solo dance from Wigman’s mid-1920s repertory.
- **1923–1924:** *Zwei Tanzlieder*. Pair of solo dances rather than a literary song text.
- **1923–1924:** *Zwei Tanzrhythmen*. Pair of solo choreographies.
- **1923–1924:** *Schlußtanz der Rhapsodie*. Solo choreography to Liszt, articulated as *Alla marcia*, *All’improvvisato*, and *Allegretto zingarese*.
- **1924:** *Die abendlichen Tänze*. Cycle of three elegiac solos, also described simply as *Drei Elegien*.
- **1925:** *Vision I: Zeremonielle Gestalt*. Masked or transformational solo belonging to the evolving *Visionen* cycle.
- **1925:** *Vision II: Verhüllte Gestalt*. Solo in the *Visionen* cycle.
- **1925:** *Vision III: Spukhafte Gestalt*. Solo in the *Visionen* cycle; later catalogues also use *Gespenstische Gestalt*, creating possible title overlap.
- **1925–1928:** *Visionen*. An evolving project or cycle rather than a single immutable choreography: its numbered dances were added and revised over several years.
- **1926:** *Festliches Präludium*. Solo choreography.
- **1926:** *Rhapsodischer Tanz*. Solo dance from the same period as the second *Hexentanz*.
- **1926:** *Zwei Monotonien*. Pair of solos, *Verhalten* and *Drehend*; the latter is commonly known as *Drehmonotonie* and remained in Wigman’s repertory for many years.
- **1926:** *Vision IV: Hexentanz* (*Hexentanz II*). A new, masked choreography rather than a straightforward revival of the 1914 dance; only a fragmentary film record survives, so modern reconstructions cannot claim a complete choreographic text.
- **1926:** *Russische Suite II*. Solo suite based on Russian songs or rhythms; numbering presupposes an earlier suite whose documentation is not uniformly presented.
- **1927:** *Tanzlied für die Feier II*. Solo extracted from or made in connection with the group work *Die Feier II*, demonstrating that the boundary between independent solo and component is unstable.
- **1927:** *Helle Schwingungen*. Three-part solo cycle: *Im großen Schwung*, *Zart fließend*, and *Leicht spielend*.
- **1927:** *Vision V: Traumgestalt*. Solo in the expanding *Visionen* cycle.
- **1927:** *Vier Tänze nach Balkanliedern*. Cycle of four solos.
- **1927–1928:** *Ballade I*. Solo choreography; catalogues differ between 1927 and 1928.
- **1927:** *Vision VI: Feierliche Gestalt*. Solo in the *Visionen* cycle; *Zeremonielle Gestalt* is sometimes used in derivative lists, making the nomenclature uncertain.
- **1927:** *Vision VII: Gespenstische Gestalt*. Solo in the *Visionen* cycle; its title overlaps semantically with the 1925 *Spukhafte Gestalt*.
- **1928:** *Vision VIII: Raumgestalt*. Solo conceived as a preliminary study for *Totentanz II*; as a study it resists a clean division between project, experiment, and finished obra.
- **1929:** *Schwingende Landschaft*. Seven-part solo cycle comprising *Anruf*, *Seraphisches Lied*, *Gesicht der Nacht*, *Pastorale*, *Festlicher Rhythmus*, *Sommerlicher Tanz*, and *Sturmlied*.
- **1929:** *Zigeunerweisen*. Three solo “dance songs”; the historical catalogue title is retained although its ethnic terminology is now recognized as derogatory.
- **1930:** *Tanz des Leides*. Solo choreography, sometimes translated as *Dance of Grief* or *Lamentation*.
- **1931:** *Das Opfer*. Six-part solo dance sequence comprising *Schwertlied*, *Tanz für die Sonne*, *Todesruf*, *Tanz für die Erde*, *Klage II*, and *Tanz in den Tod*; it is a cycle, not the identically titled 1917 component of *Ekstatische Tänze*.
- **1934:** *Abschied* (also *Mütterlicher Tanz*). Solo choreography whose alternate titles suggest either revision or recontextualization; the available catalogues do not settle the relationship conclusively.
- **1935:** *Schicksalslied*. Masked choreography for three female figures, sometimes treated as a solo-program work and sometimes as a small-group dance within *Tanzgesänge*.
- **1935:** *Mondlied*. Dance made for the *Tanzgesänge* cycle.
- **1935:** *Tanzgesänge*. Cycle incorporating at least *Schicksalslied* and *Mondlied*; it is not a collection of written songs.
- **1937:** *Herbstliche Tänze*. Five-part solo cycle: *Tanz der Erinnerung*, *Segen*, *Windsbraut*, *Jagdlied*, and *Tanz in der Stille*.
- **1938–1941:** *Ballade II*. Late solo dance; the broad date reflects repertory documentation rather than a securely established premiere.
- **1938–1941:** *Spiel*. Solo choreography with no more specific fixed title in the catalogue.
- **1938–1941:** *Tanz der lichten Königin*. Solo character dance.
- **1938–1941:** *Tanz der dunklen Königin*. Companion solo to *Tanz der lichten Königin*.
- **1938–1941:** *Der Ruf*. Late solo; it may reuse a title already present in *Tanzrhythmen I*, and the catalogue does not justify silently identifying the two.
- **1938–1941:** *Drei Tänze nach polnischen Liedern*. Cycle of three dances; no stable individual titles are supplied by the consulted catalogue.
- **1938–1941:** *Zwiesprache*. Solo conceived as a dialogue, although no second embodied performer is implied by its classification.
- **1939:** *Danksagung*. Solo choreography to music by Hanns Hasting.
- **1942:** *Sei stille, mein Herz*. Late solo dance.
- **1942:** *Freu dich, mein Herz*. Late solo dance.
- **1942:** *Tanz der Brunhilde*. Character solo made near the end of Wigman’s performing career.
- **1942:** *Tanz der Niobe*. Character solo from the same final repertory.
- **1942:** *Abschied und Dank*. Wigman’s farewell solo and therefore both choreography and performative leave-taking; it marked the end of her career as a solo dancer.

### Group dances, movement choirs, and dance dramas

- **1920:** *Slawische Tänze*. Group dance suite, also described in some catalogues simply as a *Tanzsuite*.
- **1920–1921:** *Danse macabre*. Small-group choreography to Saint-Saëns; sources disagree between 1920 and its documented Dresden performance in January 1921.
- **1921:** *Totentanz*. Small-group work presented by Wigman’s chamber dance group; it should not be conflated with the larger and differently composed 1926 *Totentanz II*.
- **1921:** *L’Arlésienne*. Group choreography to music by Bizet.
- **1921:** *Farandole*. Group dance to Bizet; it may have formed part of the same programme or musical complex as *L’Arlésienne*, so its autonomy is not entirely clear.
- **1921:** *Die Feier I*. Group choreography comprising *Der Gruß*, *Das Lied*, *Weihe*, *Der Bann*, and *Der Ausklang*; percussion and movement, rather than a conventional score-led ballet structure, organize the work.
- **1921:** *Totenrhythmen I*. Group dance or study in death rhythms; its relation to the various *Totentanz* works is not fully resolved by abbreviated catalogues.
- **1921:** *Die sieben Tänze des Lebens*. Seven-part group work—*Sehnsucht*, *Liebe*, *Lust*, *Leid*, *Dämon*, *Tod*, and *Leben*—to music by Heinz Pringsheim.
- **1923:** *Skizzen zu einem Tanzdrama*. Choreographic sketches or project preceding *Szenen aus einem Tanzdrama*; they resist classification as a wholly independent finished work.
- **1923–1924:** *Szenen aus einem Tanzdrama*. Large group choreography whose sections are catalogued as *Aufruf*, *Wanderung*, *Kreis*, *Dreieck*, *Chaos*, *Die Wende*, *Vision*, *Begegnung*, and a final group scene; sources variously date the work 1923, 1924, or 1923/24.
- **1925:** *Gruppengesang*. Group dance—not a written choral song—known chiefly through repertory documentation.
- **1925:** *Das Tanzmärchen* (also *Ein Tanzmärchen*). Dance drama with individualized roles and music by Will Goetze; the article varies between definite and indefinite forms of the title.
- **1926:** *Raumgesänge*. Group choreography whose sections are *Feierlicher Auftakt*, *Schwingende Reihe*, *Der Strahl*, and *Rhythmus*; erroneous transcriptions sometimes render the title as *Rauhnäsege*.
- **1926:** *Totentanz II*. Masked group choreography to music by Will Goetze, distinct from the 1921 work. Later reconstructions draw on photographs, notation, programmes, and other remnants and therefore are scholarly manifestations, not the intact original.
- **1927:** *Die Feier II*, first version. Three-part ceremonial group work: *Der Tempel*, *Im Zeichen des Dunklen*, and a concluding complex of *Polonaise*, *Tanzlied*, and *Tarantella*.
- **1928:** *Die Feier II*, second version. Substantial revision of the 1927 choreography; the catalogue treats the versions separately, although they share a title.
- **1928:** *Chorische Bewegung: Drei Studien*. Three studies for a movement choir; as pedagogical-performance studies they resist a strict distinction between protocol, exercise, and public obra.
- **1932:** *Der Weg*. Major group choreography to music by Hanns Hasting, distinct from the earlier solo of the same title.
- **1934:** *Frauentänze*. Group cycle for women to music by Hanns Hasting.
- **1936:** *Totenklage*. Mass choreography for approximately eighty dancers, created for the Olympic Youth festival. Its commission and performance belong to the cultural programme of the Nazi state, a political context inseparable from the work’s status.
- **1946:** *Reigen*. Group choreography to Schubert; some catalogues date the postwar repertory broadly rather than identifying a secure premiere.
- **1946–1947:** *Aus der Not der Zeit*. Postwar group work to music by several composers; sources differ between 1946 and 1947.
- **1948:** *Auf der Suche*. Group choreography to music by Ursula Kessler.
- **1950:** *Drei chorische Studien*. Movement-choir work to music by Ursula Kessler; its title indicates studies, but it was also presented as repertory.
- **1952:** *Beschwingt*. Choreography to music by Debussy.
- **1952:** *Chorische Studien*. Group studies made with the Mary Wigman Studio; catalogues do not always distinguish them clearly from the 1950 *Drei chorische Studien*.
- **1952:** *Die Wartenden*. Choric study, sometimes catalogued as a component of *Chorische Studien* rather than an independent work.
- **1953:** *Chorische Studien II* (also reported as *Chorische Szenen*). Studio group work to music by Ursula Kessler; the competing titles in secondary sources make its exact identity uncertain.
- **1953:** *Die Seherin*. Choric scene in which Wigman made her final stage appearance; it may belong to *Chorische Studien II* rather than constitute a wholly separate choreography.

### Staged musical works and Gesamtkunstwerk-like collaborations

- **1924 onward:** Operatic choreographies for productions directed by Hanns Niedecken-Gebhard. The archival biography records this continuing collaboration but does not provide a complete, securely titled list; it is therefore a body of work whose individual boundaries remain unresolved.
- **1930:** *Das Totenmal*. Albert Talhoff’s multimedia memorial drama, for which Wigman choreographed and danced; authorship is collaborative, so it is not solely “a work by Wigman,” but her choreography is an integral part of a Gesamtkunstwerk combining speech, music, mass movement, light, and architecture.
- **1936:** *Olympische Jugend*. Mass spectacle for the opening festivities of the Berlin Olympics, jointly choreographed by Wigman, Gret Palucca, Harald Kreutzberg, and Dorothee Günther. It is a collaborative state commission, not exclusively Wigman’s obra, and its Nazi ceremonial function must be stated rather than treated as incidental.
- **1943:** *Orpheus*. Stage choreography; the short title in the catalogues may refer to preparatory or related work for Gluck’s opera and should not be silently identified with the documented 1947 production.
- **1943:** *Carmina Burana*. Choreographic staging of Carl Orff’s work in Leipzig; this is distinct from Wigman’s 1955 Mannheim production.
- **1947:** *Orpheus und Eurydike*. Choreographic staging of Gluck’s opera in Leipzig, made with Wigman’s students.
- **1954:** *Saul*. Choreographic staging of Handel’s oratorio at Mannheim’s Nationaltheater; one music reference dates the production 1957, while the German biographical and work sources give 1954.
- **1955:** *Catulli Carmina*. Choreographic staging of Carl Orff’s scenic cantata at Mannheim.
- **1955:** *Carmina Burana*. New Mannheim staging of Orff’s work, not merely a revival of the 1943 Leipzig manifestation.
- **1957:** *Le Sacre du printemps* (*Frühlingsweihe*). Wigman’s final new dance work, made to Stravinsky for the Berlin Festival. Later productions reconstructed it from surviving documents and testimony; those reconstructions are new manifestations rather than the wholly preserved 1957 choreography.
- **1958:** *Alkestis*. Choreographic staging of Gluck’s opera.
- **1961:** *Orpheus und Eurydike*. New staging made with director Gustav Rudolf Sellner; collaborative authorship and its relation to Wigman’s 1947 version should be preserved rather than collapsed into a single work.

### Books, dance-poems, theoretical texts, and protocols

- **1921:** *Die sieben Tänze des Lebens: Tanzdichtung*. Published dance-poem associated with the choreography of the same title; it is a textual manifestation or verbal counterpart, not the dance itself.
- **1925:** *Komposition*. Short theoretical text on choreographic composition; it operates partly as a protocol for making dance rather than merely describing completed works.
- **1933:** Contributions to Rudolf Bach’s *Das Mary Wigman-Werk*. Essays or statements by Wigman embedded in a collaboratively authored volume; the volume as a whole is not hers, and its constituent texts should not be converted into a separate Wigman book.
- **1935:** *Deutsche Tanzkunst*. Book-length statement on dance and German cultural identity. Its rhetoric bears visible accommodations to National Socialist cultural ideology, so its manifesto-like action cannot be separated from its political moment.
- **1963:** *Die Sprache des Tanzes*. Collection of autobiographical and theoretical writings on dance; later translations and reprints are manifestations of this work, not additional works.
- **1930–1961:** *Choreographisches Skizzenbuch*. Working notebooks containing choreographic sketches, diagrams, and notes, published posthumously in 1987. They are a process archive and practical protocol rather than a retrospectively finished book by Wigman.
- **Various dates:** Letters to Hanya Holm. Correspondence later selected and edited as *Liebe Hanya / Mary Wigman’s Letters to Hanya Holm*; the individual letters are made texts, but the 2003 collection is an editorial construction rather than a book Wigman herself composed.
- **Various dates:** Lectures, articles, interviews, programme notes, diaries, and correspondence. Wigman continued producing such texts through the end of her life, but no consulted scholarly source supplies a complete item-level bibliography; claiming an exhaustive list would therefore invent completeness.

### Institutions and pedagogical projects

- **1920–1942:** *Mary-Wigman-Schule Dresden*. School and continuing pedagogical project founded with Berthe Trümpy. It is an institutional obra—curriculum, community, performance group, and method—rather than a single authored object.
- **1921–1928; 1930–1933; 1934–1936:** *Mary-Wigman-Tanzgruppe*. Repeatedly reconstituted performing ensemble through which many choreographies came into being; it is both an institution and a collective creative medium, not simply one work.
- **From 1930:** Wigman-school branches, including New York. An international pedagogical network developed through collaborators, especially Hanya Holm; authorship is distributed, so it should not be represented as Wigman’s unaided creation.
- **1945–1949:** Leipzig dance school and projected *Mary-Wigman-Tanzstudio*. Postwar pedagogical project that was reduced from plans for a nationally significant dance academy and then overtaken by nationalization and Wigman’s departure; the unrealized academy remains a project, not a completed institution.
- **1949–1967:** *Mary-Wigman-Studio Berlin*. Teaching and performance institution founded with Marianne Vogelsang. Its choric studies make it both a school and a production environment, resisting separation between pedagogy and artwork.
