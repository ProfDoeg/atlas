## Works by Allen Welsh Dulles

### Books, pamphlets, and authored obra

- **1902:** *The Boer War: A History*. A privately printed, thirty-one-page childhood history, written when Dulles was eight; its youthful authorship is part of the object’s identity, not a reason to exclude it. The CIA bibliography records a third edition, so the dates and form of the earlier printings remain uncertain.

- **1916:** *The Power of the President over Foreign Affairs*. A legal essay, originally published in the *Michigan Law Review* and also issued separately; it argues for broad presidential authority in foreign affairs.

- **1927:** *Some Misconceptions About Disarmament*. A policy essay in *Foreign Affairs*, not a book, examining the practical limits of international arms reduction.

- **1929:** *New Uses for the Machinery for the Settlement of International Disputes: Discussion*. A published conference intervention: part argument, part recorded discussion, and therefore less self-contained than a conventional article.

- **1932:** *Progress Toward Disarmament*. A *Foreign Affairs* policy essay assessing the Geneva disarmament process.

- **1934:** *The Cost of Peace*. A fourteen-page Council on Foreign Relations pamphlet reprinted from *Foreign Affairs*; it is a policy argument rather than a monograph.

- **1936:** *Can We Be Neutral?* Co-authored with Hamilton Fish Armstrong, this book analyzes American neutrality law and policy; shared authorship must be retained.

- **1939:** *Can America Stay Neutral?* A second, substantially reworked book with Hamilton Fish Armstrong, written after the international situation had changed; it is a new obra, not merely another edition of *Can We Be Neutral?*

- **1946:** *The United Nations*. A Foreign Policy Association “Headline Series” explanatory pamphlet, issued with a statement by Edward R. Stettinius Jr.; Dulles’s text belongs to public-policy education rather than scholarly monograph writing.

- **1947:** *Germany’s Underground*. A historical-political account of German resistance to Hitler, constructed partly from Dulles’s wartime intelligence contacts. Its closeness to secret reporting makes it both a book and a public manifestation of previously clandestine knowledge.

- **1957:** *Revising National Intelligence Estimates: The Case of Soviet Airpower*. A two-page Harvard Defense Policy Seminar paper reproducing Dulles’s letter to Senator Stuart Symington; it is an intervention in an official controversy rather than a freestanding study.

- **1963:** *The Craft of Intelligence*. A public exposition of intelligence history, practice, and institutional doctrine. Its status as solely “by Dulles” is disputed: a CIA bibliographical review reports that retired officers led by Howard Roman drafted the book, although Dulles reviewed successive manuscripts; it is therefore best treated as an authorized, collaboratively produced obra under his name.

- **1966:** *The Secret Surrender*. A memoir-history of Operation Sunrise and the German capitulation in northern Italy. It transforms an earlier clandestine project into a retrospective narrative and inevitably reflects Dulles’s own contested interpretation of the negotiations.

### Introductions and framing texts

- **1947:** *Introduction to To the Bitter End*. Dulles’s introduction to Hans Bernd Gisevius’s resistance memoir is a framing text that authenticates and politically situates another person’s testimony; it also appeared in the 1948 London edition.

- **1947:** *Introduction to The Problem of Germany*. An introductory essay for Hoyt Price and Carl E. Schorske’s Council on Foreign Relations study, reprinted from Dulles’s *Foreign Affairs* article “Alternatives for Germany.”

- **1949:** *Introduction to The Unknown Warriors*. Dulles’s introduction to Pierre Guillain de Bénouville’s account of the French Resistance functions as testimonial endorsement rather than an independent book.

### Edited and assembled works

- **1968:** *Great True Spy Stories*. An anthology selected and presented by Dulles. His obra here is editorial—the making of a sequence and interpretive frame from texts written by others—not authorship of the collected stories.

### Articles and published statements

- **1946:** *Goodby, Berlin!* A magazine article in *Collier’s*, combining recollection and political interpretation.

- **1947:** *Disarmament in the Atomic Age*. A *Foreign Affairs* essay on the altered problem of arms control after the advent of atomic weapons.

- **1947:** *Alternatives for Germany*. A *Foreign Affairs* policy essay that also supplied the introduction to *The Problem of Germany*.

- **1948:** *Germany: A Formula*. A *New York Times Magazine* article proposing a political settlement for postwar Germany.

- **1955:** *Foreign Language Specialists*. A published letter entered into the *Congressional Record*, advocating language competence as a national resource; it is an official intervention, not a conventional article.

- **1956:** *The Communist Attack upon Parliamentary Government*. A published statement included in the House Committee on Un-American Activities compilation *Soviet Total War*; its governmental setting makes it testimony-like political analysis.

- **1956:** *Signs of Weakness in Soviet Dictatorship*. A public article by the serving Director of Central Intelligence. It sits uneasily between personal authorship and institutional communication because its authority and evidentiary posture depended upon Dulles’s office.

- **1961:** *Exchange of Letters Between the President and Mr. Dulles in Connection with the Latter’s Resignation as Director*. A jointly constituted public document made from Kennedy’s and Dulles’s letters; Dulles authored only his half, and the exchange as an obra belongs to neither participant alone.

- **1965:** *My Answer on the Bay of Pigs*. An unpublished article surviving in three draft manuscripts. Dulles abandoned the project because, according to an archival note supplied by Clover Dulles, secrecy prevented him from presenting what he considered a sufficient defense; its resistance to publication is essential to its status as an unfinished project.

### Speeches, lectures, interviews, and broadcasts

- **10–13 August 1925:** *Political Reporting*. A four-lecture instructional series prepared for Foreign Service officers. It is a protocol-like text: its purpose is to teach how political information should be observed, evaluated, and reported.

- **6 May 1936:** *Address on Neutrality and International Sanctions*. A symposium address published by the American Bar Association; it survives as part of a collective discussion rather than as an autonomous book.

- **16 June 1947:** *Brown University Commencement Address*. A public address known through contemporary reporting; the CIA bibliography supplies no fixed title, so none should be invented.

- **20 June 1947:** *Address before the New York State Bar Association on Human Rights*. A public speech whose identifying title is descriptive rather than authorial.

- **19 November 1947:** *Address before the World Council of Churches on the Marshall Plan*. A policy speech reported in the press; no fixed title is established by the standard bibliography.

- **7 January 1948:** *Address before the New York Export Managers Club*. An untitled public address, traceable through contemporary newspaper coverage.

- **17 May 1948:** *What’s on Your Mind?* A contribution to an NBC Radio Forum broadcast advocating four measures for peace. It is an ephemeral broadcast manifestation as well as a spoken text.

- **29 May 1948:** *The Responsibility of Power*. A commencement address delivered to the School of Advanced International Studies.

- **23 February 1951:** *Address to Princeton Alumni*. An untitled alumni address documented by press coverage.

- **16 November 1951:** *Finding the Facts: The Role of the Central Intelligence Agency*. An address to the New England Governors’ Conference explaining and publicly legitimating the still-new agency.

- **12 June 1952:** *Temple University Address*. A public speech listed in the CIA’s official speech index; the consulted list does not establish a fixed authorial title.

- **10 April 1953:** *Brain Warfare*. A summary of remarks delivered at Princeton’s National Alumni Conference. Published excerpts appeared as “Brain Warfare, Russia’s Secret Weapon,” but that journalistic title should not silently replace the speech’s documented title.

- **1 June 1955:** *Education in the Soviet Union*. A Columbia University commencement address that uses intelligence-derived observation in a ceremonial public form.

- **1955:** *Interview on “Let’s Look at Congress”*. A televised interview with Representative Kenneth B. Keating, subsequently circulated as an eight-page transcript. Newspaper excerpt titles differed, demonstrating that the interview itself—not those derivative headlines—is the obra.

- **17 June 1956:** *Television Interview with Representative Harold C. Ostertag*. A broadcast conversation whose published excerpts were titled “Soviet Lead in Missiles Discounted”; it is co-produced speech, not a solely authored essay.

- **23 October 1959:** *Address at the Conclusion of Project USEFUL*. A classified address to a military orientation exercise. It occupies an intermediate category between institutional briefing and speech, and its surviving text is an official record rather than a publication conceived for readers.

- **31 December 1961:** *Meet the Press Interview*. A televised interview after Dulles’s resignation. Because the questions shape the resulting text, authorship is dialogic rather than exclusively his.

- **1964:** *Conversation with Ian Fleming*. A recorded and published exchange between Dulles and Fleming concerning intelligence and spy fiction; it is a collaboratively produced interview-object.

### Intelligence reports and institutional texts

- **1942–1945:** *Bern OSS Intelligence Reports*. More than a thousand wartime telegrams and numerous reports were produced by Dulles’s station and issued under his authority. They resist treatment as individually authored literary works: agents, informants, translators, secretaries, and headquarters editors contributed to them, although later editors selected some as *From Hitler’s Doorstep*.

- **1945–1948:** *The Marshall Plan Papers*. Reports and memoranda concerning European recovery later edited by Michael Wala and published in 1993 as *The Marshall Plan*. The book is a posthumous scholarly manifestation of earlier official texts, not a book co-written by Dulles and Wala during Dulles’s lifetime.

- **1948–1949:** *The Central Intelligence Agency and National Organization for Intelligence*. Usually called the Dulles–Jackson–Correa Report, this 163-page classified survey was collectively produced by Allen Dulles, William H. Jackson, Mathias F. Correa, and their staff. As a protocol for reorganizing American intelligence, it acts upon the institution it describes; sole attribution to Dulles would be misleading.

- **1950–1961:** *Director’s Memoranda, Correspondence, and Briefing Papers*. An extensive official corpus preserved in CIA and Princeton collections. These documents cannot responsibly be itemized as personal “works” without examining drafting files: many bear Dulles’s signature but were composed or revised by staff, so signature, authorization, and authorship must not be treated as synonyms.

- **1960:** *U-2 Overflights of the Soviet Bloc*. A classified official memorandum associated with Dulles’s directorship. It is an administrative-intelligence text whose personal authorship cannot be inferred merely from its attribution in later citations.

### Intelligence projects and institutional creations

- **1942–1945:** *OSS Bern Station*. An operational assemblage—people, safe houses, communications, informant networks, reports, and negotiations—built and directed by Dulles. It is an obra in an institutional sense, but resists being called his individual work because its existence depended upon numerous named and unnamed collaborators.

- **1945:** *Operation Sunrise*. The secret-contact and negotiation project that helped produce the surrender of German forces in northern Italy. Dulles was a principal maker and negotiator, but the project was neither his alone nor a “text”; German, Swiss, British, American, and resistance participants all shaped its outcome.

- **1948–1949:** *Intelligence Survey Group*. A temporary investigative project chaired by Dulles that generated the Dulles–Jackson–Correa Report. The project—the inquiry, interviews, institutional criticism, and recommendations—is broader than its surviving report.

- **1950–1961:** *The Dulles-era Central Intelligence Agency*. Dulles helped construct the CIA’s enduring combination of intelligence collection, analysis, technical surveillance, propaganda, and covert action. Calling the agency his obra would overstate individual authorship, but excluding this institutional construction would omit the made thing for which he was historically most consequential.

- **1953:** *Project MKULTRA authorization*. A program initiated under Dulles’s authority to investigate behavioral control and interrogation techniques. It resists designation as a personal work: Sidney Gottlieb and a dispersed institutional network designed and conducted it, while Dulles’s role was authorization and executive responsibility.

- **1953:** *Operation TPAJAX*. The covert project against Iran’s government was executed by CIA personnel under Dulles’s directorship. It is an historical action rather than a conventional work, and operational authorship is collective and disputed; responsibility at the level of authorization should not be confused with sole design.

- **1954:** *Operation PBSUCCESS*. The CIA project that overthrew Guatemala’s government combined intelligence, paramilitary preparation, psychological warfare, and diplomacy. It is a collective and coercive institutional creation, not a personally authored text, though it belongs to Dulles’s directed body of work.

- **1955–1960:** *U-2 Reconnaissance Program*. A technological-operational system developed by the CIA, Air Force, contractors, scientists, pilots, and policymakers under Dulles’s sponsorship. It is best understood as a collaborative project or technological Gesamtkunstwerk rather than an object “made by” Dulles alone.

- **1960–1961:** *Bay of Pigs Project*. The invasion project was inherited, reshaped, and advanced by a large CIA and exile apparatus under Dulles. Its authorship and responsibility remain contested across institutional histories; the failed operation is nevertheless part of the directed project-work of his tenure.

- **1963–1964:** *Report of the President’s Commission on the Assassination of President Kennedy*. Dulles was one of seven commissioners responsible for the collective report commonly called the Warren Report. It is not properly an Allen Dulles work, but his participation in questioning witnesses, evaluating evidence, and approving the report makes it part of his institutional corpus.

### Posthumous manifestations of earlier material

- **1993:** *The Marshall Plan*. Edited by Michael Wala from Dulles’s 1947–1948 documents. This is a posthumous documentary manifestation: Wala made the edition, while Dulles and his collaborators made the underlying reports.

- **1996:** *From Hitler’s Doorstep: The Wartime Intelligence Reports of Allen Dulles, 1942–1945*. Edited by Neal H. Petersen with Nancy Petersen, this is a posthumous selection from Dulles’s OSS reporting. The title’s attribution compresses the collaborative production of a wartime station into the name of its chief.

- **2003:** *That Was Then: Allen W. Dulles on the Occupation of Germany*. A posthumously published *Foreign Affairs* text drawn from Dulles material. Its late appearance is a new manifestation, not evidence that Dulles created a new work in 2003.
