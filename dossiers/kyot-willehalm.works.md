## Works by Kyot-Willehalm

### Oral epics attributed only under Greub’s identification

- **No fixed date; placed by Greub in the ninth century:** *Willehalm*. An oral heroic-historical epic, supposedly Kyot-Willehalm’s autobiographical account of the battle of Alischanz in 818 and later translated or re-created in Middle High German by Wolfram von Eschenbach. No ninth-century text or independent witness survives: the attribution depends entirely on Werner Greub’s rejected identification of Wolfram’s Kyot with William of Gellone; standard scholarship treats Wolfram’s early-thirteenth-century *Willehalm* as Wolfram’s own unfinished adaptation of French Guillaume material.

- **Late in Kyot-Willehalm’s life, according to Greub; no independently recoverable date:** *Parzival*. Greub’s account makes this a French oral epic composed after the events it narrates and transmitted for roughly 350 years before Wolfram rendered it in Middle High German; within Wolfram’s poem, Kyot is said to have combined a Grail notice found in Toledo with genealogical information from chronicles. No manuscript, title in Kyot’s own language, or independent trace of this supposed work exists, and the prevailing scholarly interpretation treats “Kyot” and his source as parts of Wolfram’s fiction of transmission rather than as an identifiable author and lost obra ([University of Konstanz, “Wolfram, Kyot, frou âventiure”](https://wiki.uni-konstanz.de/mediaewiki/index.php/Wolfram,_Kyot,_frou_%C3%A2ventiure_%E2%80%94_Wer_erz%C3%A4hlt_im_Parzival%3F); Joachim Bumke, *Wolfram von Eschenbach*, pp. 244–46).

- **No fixed date; placed by Greub in the ninth century:** *Titurel*. Greub’s reception describes this as the third of Kyot-Willehalm’s orally committed epics, beside *Willehalm* and *Parzival*. Nothing establishes a distinct ninth-century *Titurel*: the surviving *Titurel* consists of fragments by Wolfram, while the claim of a Kyot original is a Greubian reconstruction rather than a catalogued medieval work ([Ellen Schalk, “Kyot and the Stellar Script of Parsifal,” pp. 1–2](https://sophiafoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/04/kyotandthestellarscriptofparsifal.pdf)).

### Monastic and architectural obra associated with the historical Guilhem

- **Shortly before 804–15 December 804:** *The monastery or cella of Gellone, including its Basilica of the Saviour*. This is an institutional, architectural, and liturgical obra rather than a book: contemporary-near evidence says Count Guilhem had the cella built with the advice and assistance of Benedict of Aniane; its basilica was dedicated on 14 December and the establishment endowed the following day. The later *Vita Guillelmi* magnifies Guilhem into sole planner and builder, but the documentary reconstruction retains Benedict and monks from Aniane as collaborators; the present Romanesque church is not simply Guilhem’s original building ([Vincent Challet, “Guilhem de Toulouse…,” §§209–24](https://www.etudesheraultaises.fr/publi/guilhem-de-toulouse-les-premiers-carolingiens-et-l-integration-du-midi-languedocien-au-royaume-franc/)).

- **804 or earlier; attribution uncertain:** *The monastic foundation at Casanova, in the diocese of Uzès*. Some modern historical reconstructions credit William with founding and endowing this second Benedictine house, perhaps under Aniane, but the evidence is later and less secure than for Gellone. It therefore resists classification as a firmly attributable obra and should be retained only as a disputed foundation ([Nathaniel L. Taylor, “Saint William, King David, and Makhir,” pp. 9–10 and notes](https://www.nltaylor.net/pdfs/a_Makhir.pdf)).

### Juridical manifestations

- **15 December 804:** *Dotation of the abbey of Gellone*. A dispositive charter—a legal act that transfers property rather than merely describing it—and therefore close to a manifesto in the vocabulary of the atlas. The charter tradition is exceptionally contested: one parchment dated 14 December is judged a late-eleventh-century forgery, while Pierre Chastang has defended the version dated 15 December as substantially original; the cartulary text was nevertheless altered during Gellone’s dispute with Aniane ([Challet, §§210–24](https://www.etudesheraultaises.fr/publi/guilhem-de-toulouse-les-premiers-carolingiens-et-l-integration-du-midi-languedocien-au-royaume-franc/); [municipal historical dossier on the two charters](https://www.st-guilhem-le-desert.fr/eglise_de_gellone_saint_guilhem_le_desert-p50.htm)).

### Attributions that do not resolve into works

- **No recoverable date or object:** *Urban planning and creative art at Montpellier*. Greub’s reconstruction calls Kyot-Willehalm an urban planner and “creative artist” there, but identifies no securely bounded plan, building, image, or other surviving object. This is an asserted activity, not presently a nameable work, and listing an invented title would turn conjecture into a false catalogue entry ([Schalk, “Kyot and the Stellar Script of Parsifal,” p. 1](https://sophiafoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/04/kyotandthestellarscriptofparsifal.pdf)).

- **No recoverable date or object:** *Astronomical and agricultural practice at Le Verdus*. Greubian literature credits Kyot-Willehalm with observing the stars and applying that knowledge to terracing, irrigation, animal husbandry, medicine, and cultivation near Saint-Guilhem-le-Désert. No individual terrace, protocol, instrument, or written astronomical work is securely attributable to him, so this remains a speculative body of practice rather than a catalogue of obras ([“Starlight Christmas 2015,” pp. 28–29](https://sophiafoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/04/Starlight_Christmas_2015_corrected_SM.pdf)).
