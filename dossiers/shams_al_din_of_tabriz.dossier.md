# Shams al-Din Muhammad of Tabriz: Research Dossier

## Scope and Evidentiary Note

Shams al-Din Muhammad of Tabriz is historically attested, but his biography is unusually difficult to reconstruct. Almost no independent contemporary administrative, legal, or narrative record survives. The evidence falls into four principal strata:

1. **Shams’s recorded speech:** the Persian *Maqālāt-e Shams-e Tabrīzī*, a discontinuous collection of conversations and discourses, apparently written down or compiled by members of Rumi’s circle rather than composed as a book by Shams.
2. **Near-contemporary testimony:** poems and prose by Jalal al-Din Rumi; the *Ibtidā-nāma* of Rumi’s son Sultan Walad; and the *Risāla* attributed to Faridun Sipahsalar.
3. **Fourteenth-century Mevlevi tradition:** especially Shams al-Din Ahmad Aflaki’s *Manāqib al-ʿārifīn*, begun roughly seventy-five years after Shams’s disappearance and containing both historical recollections and miracle stories.
4. **Later hagiography and modern reconstruction:** Jami, Dawlatshah, Mevlevi chronicles, shrine traditions, novels, films, and modern scholarly arguments.

Dates before Shams’s arrival in Konya and accounts of his death therefore require caution. “Documented fact” below means supported by comparatively early evidence, not necessarily by an autograph or independent government record.

## Family and Ancestry

### Father and grandfather

**Reported fact:** Aflaki gives Shams’s father as **ʿAli** and grandfather as **Malikdād**, producing the conventional full name Muhammad ibn ʿAli ibn Malikdad. A different early Mevlevi report associated with Sipahsalar calls him a son of **Imam ʿAla al-Din**. The discrepancy is unresolved and may reflect honorific naming rather than two identifiable fathers.

In the *Maqālāt*, Shams speaks of his father as a good and affectionate man who nevertheless did not understand him. He employs the image of a duck’s egg incubated by a hen: although raised in the household, he belonged inwardly to another element. This is autobiographical speech, but it supplies no name, occupation, or genealogy.

**Later claim:** Some modern biographies describe his father as a cloth merchant, or as a migrant merchant established in Tabriz. No early independent documentation establishing that occupation was located.

### Mother and siblings

No dependable source names Shams’s mother or any sibling. Statements supplying a detailed household generally derive from modern imaginative biography or unsourced shrine literature.

### Ismaʿili genealogy

**Disputed theory:** Later Ismaʿili-oriented narratives identify Shams with, or make him a close relative of, the Nizari imam **Shams al-Din Muhammad**, sometimes portraying him as a concealed prince who worked as an embroiderer.

Evidence against straightforward identification includes:

- conflicting genealogies;
- chronological problems;
- the different institutional and geographical careers attributed to the two men;
- no explicit Nizari pedigree in Shams’s recorded discourses;
- likely transfer of the epithet *zardūz* from the concealed Ismaʿili imam.

Mainstream Rumi scholarship treats the identity as unproved or as a conflation. It remains historically important because it underlies modern claims that Shams secretly initiated Rumi into Ismaʿili esotericism.

### Marriage and children

**Reported fact in later Mevlevi tradition:** During his second stay in Konya, Shams married **Kimya/Kimiyā Khātūn**, a young woman raised in Rumi’s household. Sources differ over whether she was Rumi’s ward, foster daughter, servant, or a daughter of Rumi’s wife Kira Khātūn by an earlier marriage.

Aflaki says she died after returning ill from an outing; hostile modern retellings say Shams beat or killed her. The surviving medieval narrative does not establish homicide. The allegation is therefore best classified as an **unsupported modern accusation built around an ambiguous late source**.

No child of Shams is securely recorded. A story that he had previously married and fathered a son appears in some late popular accounts but lacks corroboration.

## Childhood and Early Life

No contemporary source supplies a childhood chronology.

Shams’s speech suggests:

- a materially adequate or respectable household;
- alienation from his family’s expectations;
- early religious intensity;
- dissatisfaction with conventional piety and social rank;
- periods of fasting, vigils, solitude, and ecstatic experience.

**Hagiographic story:** As a child, Shams supposedly went without food for thirty or forty days and experienced visions incomprehensible to his father. This belongs to saintly biography rather than independently documented childhood history.

An exact birth year cannot be calculated securely. Aflaki’s statement that he was sixty when he reached Konya in 1244 yields approximately 1184–85. Sahih Ahmad Dede’s much later chronicle gives 590 AH/1194 and an age of about fifty-two. The frequently repeated “1185” or “1186” is therefore an inference, not a recorded birth date.

## Education and Formation

### Formal learning

The *Maqālāt* demonstrates substantial acquaintance with:

- Qurʾanic exegesis;
- hadith and hadith criticism;
- Arabic grammar and religious vocabulary;
- jurisprudence;
- Persian literature and storytelling;
- major Sufi sayings and biographies;
- theological controversies;
- public preaching and madrasa culture.

Shams criticized bookish scholars, but this should not be mistaken for illiteracy. He repeatedly insisted that knowledge was indispensable while distinguishing transformative knowledge from status-seeking scholarship.

### Abu Bakr Sallebaf

**Reported fact:** Shams names **Shaykh Abu Bakr Sallebāf/Salabāf of Tabriz** as an early master. The epithet means basket-weaver or basket-maker. Later tradition describes Sallebaf as a *malāmatī* or futuwwa-oriented Sufi who earned his living manually and did not distribute initiatory cloaks.

Shams revered him but said that something within Shams exceeded what the teacher could perceive. This supplied a recurring pattern in his life: gratitude to a master without permanent institutional subordination.

### Other alleged teachers

Later sources connect Shams to:

- **Baba Kamal al-Din Jundi**, associated with the Kubrawi line;
- **Baba Faraj Tabrizi**;
- **Rukn al-Din Sujasi**, connected through Qutb al-Din Abhari and Abu Najib al-Suhrawardi;
- occasionally the Suhrawardi, Kubrawi, or Kalenderi traditions.

These affiliations are unevenly evidenced. Shams’s own voice resists presentation as a conventional successor within one institutional order.

### Formative Sufi figures

His discourses engage Muhammad, ʿAli, Bayazid Bistami, Junayd of Baghdad, Mansur al-Hallaj, Ibrahim ibn Adham, Abu Saʿid ibn Abi’l-Khayr, Sanai, Ahmad Ghazali, and others. Engagement ranges from praise to correction. These are intellectual or devotional relationships, not personal meetings.

## Early Career and Livelihood

Shams appears to have avoided permanent office, discipleship establishments, and the conventional insignia of a Sufi shaykh.

**Reported activities:**

- travelling under conditions of chosen anonymity;
- staying in caravanserais rather than Sufi lodges;
- teaching children;
- copying manuscripts or performing minor learned work;
- weaving baskets or selling girdles;
- undertaking manual labor while concealing his learning;
- joining learned and Sufi conversations, then departing abruptly.

Aflaki’s epithet **Shams-e Paranda**, “Flying Shams,” reflects this mobile existence.

No reliable financial accounts, property registers, inheritance documents, business partnerships, or waqf holdings belonging to Shams have been identified. His recorded ethos favored paid work and independence from patrons. Later portrayals of destitution may exaggerate an intentional refusal of prestige.

## Chronological Life History

### Tabriz: uncertain beginnings, c. 1180s–early thirteenth century

Shams was born and initially formed in Tabriz. There he reportedly studied with Abu Bakr Sallebaf. No documentation fixes the length of this relationship. A later claim that he entered Sallebaf’s service at twenty-two and remained fourteen years is not independently verified.

Tabriz was a major commercial and intellectual city, exposed to Persian, Arabic, Turkic, Armenian, Christian, and Sufi currents. The diversity provides context but cannot establish particular childhood encounters.

### Itinerant decades

The principal scholarly reconstruction places Shams in some combination of:

- Tabriz;
- Baghdad;
- Damascus;
- Aleppo;
- Kayseri;
- Aksaray;
- Sivas;
- Erzurum;
- Erzincan;
- eventually Konya.

The order and dates are only partly recoverable.

#### Baghdad and Awhad al-Din Kirmani, 1237

**Reported fact:** Shams encountered the Sufi **Awhad al-Din Kirmani** in Baghdad around 1237. The *Maqālāt* and later retellings portray Shams criticizing Kirmani’s contemplative practice of observing divine beauty in attractive youths, conventionally called *shāhid-bāzī*.

In the famous dialogue, Shams asks what Kirmani is doing; Kirmani says he sees the moon in a basin of water; Shams replies that unless he has an abscess on his neck, he should look directly at the moon.

The encounter is plausible and rooted in early material, but its polished wording may reflect oral transmission. It became a symbolic contrast between Shams’s claim to immediate vision and Kirmani’s mediated aesthetics.

#### Damascus and Ibn ʿArabi, c. 1240

**Documented in Shams’s recorded discourses:** Shams knew a “Shaykh Muhammad,” at one point explicitly “Muhammad ibn ʿArabi,” normally identified as **Muhyi al-Din Ibn ʿArabi**. Ibn ʿArabi lived in Damascus until his death in 1240, so a meeting is chronologically possible.

Shams praises Ibn ʿArabi’s learning and graciousness but criticizes aspects of his spiritual claim and, in some passages, his conformity to Muhammad’s example. This was neither simple discipleship nor wholesale rejection.

#### Aleppo

Shams reportedly spent **fourteen months** in a madrasa cell in Aleppo, engaged in austerity. This duration comes from biographical tradition and cannot be independently dated.

#### Erzurum

Aflaki says Shams taught the children of Erzurum’s ruler. He reportedly set an intentionally low fee, then demanded a higher one once the students had learned, explaining that the family previously paid for his own labor but now should pay for the knowledge embodied in the children.

This anecdote supports the image of Shams as both teacher and provocateur, though it is preserved in a hagiographic source.

#### Kayseri

Aflaki or associated tradition connects Shams with a signature on the **Hacılar water-channel waqf deed** at Kayseri. This is sometimes treated as rare documentary evidence for his travels. The identification and document require specialist palaeographical confirmation; the dossier therefore classifies it as **reported documentary evidence**, not a fully verified autograph.

### Possible first sight of Rumi in Syria

Later sources say Shams and Rumi briefly encountered one another in Damascus or Aleppo before 1244. In Aflaki’s version, a young Rumi takes Shams’s hand and says, “O assayer of the world, understand me,” then vanishes into the crowd.

No near-contemporary account confirms this first encounter. It is a **late reported story**, possibly created to represent predestined recognition.

### Arrival in Konya, 29 November 1244

Aflaki supplies the precise date **26 Jumada II 642 AH**, conventionally converted to **29 November 1244**.

The caravanserai is variously named:

- **Şekerciler Hanı** (“Inn of the Sugar Merchants”) by Aflaki;
- **Pirinççiler Hanı** (“Inn of the Rice Merchants”) in another tradition.

Shams reportedly presented himself as a merchant and placed a valuable-looking lock on his room while possessing little inside—an emblematic hagiographic expression of concealed spiritual wealth.

### The meeting with Rumi: competing accounts

The historical core is secure: Shams and Rumi met in Konya late in 1244 and entered an intense period of companionship. The dramatic circumstances are disputed.

#### Muhammad and Bayazid question

Aflaki’s version has Shams stop Rumi’s mount and ask who was greater, Muhammad or Bayazid Bistami. Rumi answers Muhammad. Shams contrasts Muhammad’s confession that God had not been known adequately with Bayazid’s ecstatic “Glory be to me.” Rumi explains that Bayazid’s small vessel overflowed, while Muhammad’s capacity continually expanded.

This is the best-known medieval version, but its elaborate theological form may be literary construction.

#### Books thrown into water

**Legend:** Shams throws Rumi’s books into a pool. When Rumi protests, Shams retrieves them dry and says that this is knowledge Rumi does not possess.

#### Books burned by a glance

**Variant legend:** Shams causes Rumi’s books to burst into flame, or asks what they are and receives “something you do not understand”; after the miracle Rumi asks what happened and Shams repeats the answer.

#### Silent recognition or collapse

Other accounts emphasize a gaze, question, or overwhelming recognition, after which Rumi falls unconscious.

These stories were circulated by Mevlevi hagiographers and later popular writers. They shaped Shams into the archetypal wild mystic who destroys sterile learning. The *Maqālāt*, however, reveals Shams himself as learned and often protective of disciplined study.

### The retreat or seclusion, 1244–45

Shams and Rumi withdrew for prolonged conversation. Accounts variously place them in:

- Rumi’s house;
- Salah al-Din Zarkub’s room;
- a madrasa cell.

Durations include forty days, three months, and six months. The exact duration is not recoverable.

Rumi reduced or suspended normal teaching, preaching, and relations with students. Shams challenged his prestige, habits, and spiritual assumptions. Rumi introduced Shams to music and *samāʿ* in some accounts; other traditions make Shams the catalyst who intensified Rumi’s use of music and turning. Reciprocal influence is more credible than a one-directional initiation.

### Opposition in Rumi’s household and circle

Rumi’s students and relatives resented lost access to their teacher and Shams’s abrasive manner. Sultan Walad’s account acknowledges hostility but places blame upon jealous disciples.

Sources name or implicate, with varying reliability:

- disgruntled madrasa students;
- members of Rumi’s family;
- Rumi’s younger son **ʿAla al-Din Muhammad**;
- unnamed “seven men” in the later murder story.

No contemporary criminal proceeding or confession survives.

### First departure, 1245 or early 1246

Shams secretly left Konya, apparently for Damascus, after escalating hostility.

Rumi responded with grief, poems, and messages. He rebuked disciples who had driven Shams away. Sultan Walad says Rumi sent him to Damascus with companions and gifts to persuade Shams to return.

### Return from Damascus, 1246

**Near-contemporary reported fact:** Sultan Walad found Shams in Damascus and escorted him back to Konya, walking respectfully beside Shams’s horse for much of the journey.

Shams’s return produced a reconciliation. Rumi’s household and disciples reportedly asked forgiveness. The stability was temporary.

### Marriage to Kimya Khatun

Shams married Kimya during the second Konya residence. The marriage may have been intended to integrate him into Rumi’s household.

Aflaki’s narrative reports tension involving Kimya’s movements and Shams’s possessiveness. She went into a garden or vineyard without his permission, returned ill, and died after several days.

**Documented fact:** Kimya’s death is reported.

**Unresolved question:** Its date and medical cause are unknown.

**Unverified allegation:** Shams beat her to death. No known early source supplies a judicial finding, witness deposition, or unambiguous description of lethal assault. Modern novels have greatly amplified the allegation.

### Final disappearance, December 1247

The often-cited date is **5 Shaʿban 645 AH**, usually rendered **December 1247**. The exact Gregorian conversion varies.

The strongest historical conclusion is limited: Shams disappeared from Konya and did not re-enter the securely documented life of Rumi’s community.

#### Version 1: voluntary departure

Sultan Walad’s relatively early *Ibtidā-nāma* says Shams disappeared so completely that no trace remained. Its wording permits deliberate departure. Rumi initially believed Shams was in Damascus and travelled or sent inquiries there.

#### Version 2: assassination in Konya

Aflaki, writing in the fourteenth century, preserves an assassination story:

- someone called Shams outside at night;
- seven conspirators attacked him with knives;
- he cried out;
- the attackers became insensible or found that he had vanished;
- only drops of blood remained.

A second Aflaki report, attributed through Rumi’s descendants, says the body was thrown into a well. Sultan Walad learned the location in a dream, recovered the corpse, and secretly buried it near the founder of the madrasa.

This is a **late reported allegation with miraculous elements**, not a demonstrated murder.

#### Version 3: ʿAla al-Din’s participation

Later tradition implicates Rumi’s son ʿAla al-Din, motivated by resentment of Shams and sometimes by affection for Kimya. No early judicial or documentary evidence proves his participation. Claims that Rumi refused to attend ʿAla al-Din’s funeral because of guilt over Shams belong to contested later tradition.

#### Version 4: escape and death elsewhere

Khoy traditions say Shams left Konya, returned east, and died at Khoy. Other traditions send him to Tabriz, Damascus, or Multan. None establishes a continuous post-1247 itinerary.

### Rumi’s searches

Rumi reportedly travelled to Damascus, probably more than once, looking for Shams. Eventually the external search became an identification of Shams within Rumi’s transformed self. Sultan Walad says Rumi later recognized Shams’s spiritual manifestation in **Salah al-Din Zarkub**.

Rumi continued composing lyrics addressed or signed with Shams’s name. This does not prove Shams was alive; it demonstrates his continuing poetic and spiritual presence.

## Political, Social, and Financial Context

Shams held no documented government office. No evidence establishes membership in a court, military organization, intelligence network, commercial corporation, or political faction.

He moved through the Seljuk Sultanate of Rum after the Mongol victory at Köse Dağ in 1243, when Anatolian elites were negotiating Mongol supremacy. This setting encouraged later theories that religious networks functioned politically, but context alone cannot demonstrate espionage.

His relationship network included religious scholars, artisans, students, urban notables, and Rumi’s household rather than a documented court party.

No evidence has been found for:

- investments;
- real estate;
- accumulated wealth;
- patents or inventions;
- a formal business;
- litigation;
- imprisonment;
- taxation disputes;
- financial fraud;
- money laundering;
- military activity.

A report that he signed the Kayseri Hacılar water-channel endowment would indicate participation as witness, not ownership.

## Religious, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

### God, prophecy, and Muhammad

Shams’s spirituality is emphatically theistic and centered on Muhammad. He measures Sufi claims against prophetic example and attacks ecstatic language when it becomes self-satisfaction. His comparison of Bayazid with Muhammad concerns spiritual capacity, not rejection of Bayazid.

### Love and companionship

For Shams, transformative knowledge occurs through **sohbet**, living companionship and speech. A mature seeker requires another human being capable of exposing self-deception. This helps explain his search for a “companion” rather than an ordinary disciple.

### Knowledge

He distinguished:

- memorized or prestige-producing scholarship;
- knowledge that changes conduct;
- direct spiritual recognition;
- the unique knowledge embodied by saints and prophets.

He did not simply reject books. His discourses display close reading, textual memory, and arguments over interpretation.

### Religious law

Popular portrayals often make Shams antinomian. The *Maqālāt* is more complex. He criticized ostentatious jurists, conventional Sufis, and empty ritual, but continued to invoke prayer, fasting, Qurʾan, hadith, lawful conduct, and Muhammad’s model. His position is better described as an insistence that law, path, and realized truth must remain connected.

### Malāmat and provocation

His behavior resembles *malāmatī* concealment and blame-seeking:

- refusing institutional prestige;
- speaking harshly;
- hiding learning beneath a laborer’s role;
- unsettling admirers;
- rejecting automatic reverence for famous shaykhs.

The classification “Kalenderi” is plausible at the level of social style but is not a proven formal affiliation.

### Samāʿ

Shams and Rumi participated in musical audition or *samāʿ*. Later Mevlevi memory made their companionship foundational for the order’s ritual culture. The fully regulated Mevlevi ceremony developed after both men; it should not be projected intact into 1244.

### Attitudes toward other Sufis

Shams could be severe:

- Awhad al-Din Kirmani was accused of mediated or morally risky beauty contemplation.
- Ibn ʿArabi was praised yet criticized.
- Hallaj’s celebrated “I am the Truth” was not beyond scrutiny.
- Bayazid’s ecstatic claims were measured against Muhammad’s inexhaustible ascent.
- Rumi himself was challenged rather than flattered.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Jalal al-Din Rumi

**Documented fact:** The central relationship of Shams’s surviving life. Shams became Rumi’s companion, challenger, teacher, object of poetic address, and spiritual mirror. Rumi was not an unknown novice: he was already a mature jurist, preacher, and Sufi teacher.

The relationship’s emotional intensity is indisputable. Its precise category is debated: teacher–student, two-way friendship, mystical love, or identification of the perfected beloved.

### Sultan Walad

**Documented or near-contemporary reported fact:** Rumi’s son supported the relationship, travelled to Damascus to recover Shams, and later recorded the episode in verse. His account is the earliest connected biography, though filial and institutional loyalties shape it.

### ʿAla al-Din Muhammad

**Contested relationship:** Rumi’s other son is portrayed in later sources as hostile to Shams and sometimes as participant in his murder. The accusation is unproved.

### Kimya Khatun

**Reported fact:** Shams’s wife during his second Konya stay. Her early death became the foundation of later abuse and murder narratives.

### Salah al-Din Zarkub

A Konya goldsmith and Rumi disciple. Later Mevlevi narrative says Rumi found Shams’s spiritual quality manifested in Salah al-Din after the disappearance. No evidence shows a major independent Shams–Salah al-Din partnership before then.

### Husam al-Din Chalabi

A later chief companion of Rumi and stimulus for the *Mathnawi*. He belonged to the wider circle affected by Shams but is not securely documented as Shams’s close associate.

### Awhad al-Din Kirmani

A documented or strongly reported encounter marked by criticism, not lasting collaboration.

### Ibn ʿArabi

A probable documented acquaintance in Damascus, evidenced by Shams’s own recorded comments.

### Patrons and rulers

No securely named personal patron of Shams is known. The alleged employment by an Erzurum ruler remains anonymous in accessible accounts.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Adversarial Findings

### Was Shams murdered?

**Status: unresolved.**

Evidence supporting murder:

- persistent later Mevlevi tradition;
- known hostility in Rumi’s circle;
- Aflaki’s named pattern of seven conspirators and a well;
- finality of disappearance.

Evidence against treating murder as established:

- the earliest connected account, Sultan Walad’s, does not plainly report a killing;
- Rumi searched for Shams in Damascus;
- Aflaki wrote decades later and included supernatural details;
- no body, grave, contemporary inquest, confession, or legal record survives.

### Did ʿAla al-Din kill him?

**Status: allegation.** Later sources implicate him; contemporary proof is absent. The story may explain both Shams’s disappearance and later tension in family memory.

### Did Shams kill or abuse Kimya Khatun?

**Status: unverified accusation.** Aflaki depicts anger and Kimya’s illness after an outing, but does not supply the evidentiary basis needed to find homicide. Modern fiction and polemic frequently convert temporal sequence into murder.

### Was Shams a Mongol agent?

**Status: modern theory unsupported by direct documentation.**

Modern Turkish arguments, particularly those associated with Mikail Bayram and polemical extensions of his work, portray Shams as:

- a Kalenderi intermediary;
- an agent sent through eastern Anatolia;
- a link between Rumi and Mongol authority;
- a participant in ideological pacification after the Mongol conquest.

The evidence offered is generally circumstantial: his travels through recently invaded regions, selected passages regarded as favorable to submission, Rumi’s later relationships with Seljuk-Mongol political elites, and speculative identification of persons in unrelated texts.

The principal specialist rebuttal is that no contemporary document identifies Shams as a Mongol emissary, agent, paid informant, or political officer. Even if Rumi’s later circle accommodated Mongol rule, that does not retrospectively prove Shams’s recruitment.

The search of 2024–26 material located renewed Turkish and Persian online circulation of the claim, but no newly discovered primary document.

### Was Shams a Zoroastrian or concealed non-Muslim?

**Status: hostile polemic or rumor.** Modern accusations calling him *Majusi* conflict with the intensely Qurʾanic and Muhammadan content of the *Maqālāt*. No primary evidence supports secret Zoroastrian identity.

### Was the Shams–Rumi relationship sexual?

**Status: modern interpretive claim; not documented as fact.**

The sources contain passionate love language, seclusion, jealousy, and spiritual identification. Classical Persian Sufi discourse routinely uses erotic and gender-ambiguous imagery. Nothing in the surviving record establishes a sexual act or partnership. Conversely, categorical claims about private conduct cannot be proved from silence. Modern heterosexualizing and homosexualizing narratives both go beyond the documents when stated as biographical fact.

### Did Shams reject Islam or Muhammad?

**Status: quotation controversy.** Isolated translations from the *Maqālāt* circulate online as evidence that Muhammad’s message was “of no use” to him. The broader textual pattern shows Shams testing second-hand reception of prophetic teaching and attacking imitation without realization, while repeatedly treating Muhammad as the supreme standard. Extraction of a sentence without dialogic context materially alters its force.

### Criminal, civil, or financial proceedings

No lawsuit, indictment, criminal conviction, imprisonment, formal investigation, fraud proceeding, or financial scandal involving the historical Shams is documented.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### The miraculous books

**Story:** Shams throws Rumi’s books into water and returns them dry, or burns them without ordinary fire.

**Appearance and circulation:** Later Mevlevi hagiography and popular retellings.

**Evidence:** No contemporary corroboration.

**Effect:** Established the opposition “living mystical knowledge versus dead book learning,” obscuring both men’s deep scholarship.

### The single question that instantly created the poet Rumi

**Story:** One theological question transformed a dry jurist immediately into an ecstatic poet.

**Evidence:** Their meeting was transformative, but Rumi already had Sufi training under his father and Burhan al-Din Muhaqqiq Tirmidhi. The instantaneous conversion motif compresses a longer formation.

**Effect:** Made Shams a supernatural catalyst and reduced Rumi’s previous intellectual history.

### The seven assassins

**Story:** Seven men stabbed Shams; his cry made them collapse; afterward only blood was visible.

**Source:** Aflaki’s fourteenth-century hagiography.

**Evidence:** Hostility is credible; miraculous disappearance and exact conspiracy remain unverified.

**Effect:** Produced the cult of Shams as martyr and supplied later accusations against ʿAla al-Din.

### The body in the well

**Story:** Sultan Walad saw the burial place in a dream, retrieved Shams’s body from a well, and secretly buried it.

**Evidence:** Late family-chain report; no identified excavated remains.

**Effect:** Supports the Konya tomb tradition while preserving the language that Shams “disappeared.”

### Survival at Khoy

**Story:** Shams escaped Konya, travelled east, died at Khoy, and lies beside the surviving minaret there.

**Evidence:** Khoy possesses a longstanding shrine tradition but no conclusive contemporary inscription or identified remains.

**Effect:** Shams became a major heritage figure of Iranian Azerbaijan. The Khoy monument has been promoted for international heritage recognition.

### Multan tomb

**Story:** The celebrated shrine of Shah Shams Tabrez in Multan contains Rumi’s Shams.

**Evidence against:** The Multan saint is commonly identified within a different Ismaʿili missionary tradition and chronology. Shared name and title encouraged conflation.

**Effect:** Expanded Shams’s cult across South Asia and merged Persian, Ismaʿili, and local devotional histories.

### Ismaʿili prince and gold embroiderer

**Story:** Shams was the hidden Nizari imam Shams al-Din Muhammad, or his son, living as a *zardūz*.

**Evidence:** No firm support in early Mevlevi sources; genealogical and chronological contradictions.

**Effect:** Reinterprets the Rumi–Shams relationship as concealed Ismaʿili initiation.

### “Forty Rules of Love”

**Story:** Shams formulated forty numbered universal rules reproduced in modern quotation culture.

**Origin:** Elif Shafak’s novel *The Forty Rules of Love*/*Aşk*.

**Evidence:** The numbered rules are a modern literary creation, not a medieval work by Shams.

**Effect:** They are among the most widely attributed pseudo-quotations under his name.

### Predestined search for Rumi

**Story:** Shams prayed for a companion capable of receiving his secrets; God named Rumi in exchange for Shams’s life.

**Evidence:** Elements occur in *Maqālāt* and Mevlevi tradition, but the polished bargain narrative is hagiographic.

**Effect:** Frames disappearance or murder as voluntary sacrificial completion.

## Health, Psychological Crises, and Cause of Death

No medical record exists.

The sources describe:

- prolonged fasting;
- sleeplessness;
- isolation;
- ecstatic states;
- intense anger;
- abrupt departures;
- severe ascetic discipline.

These descriptions belong to medieval mystical vocabulary and do not permit a retrospective psychiatric diagnosis.

There is no documented addiction.

No accident or bodily injury is securely recorded.

**Cause of death:** unknown. Murder, natural death after escape, and an undocumented later life remain competing possibilities.

## Works by or Attributed to Shams

### 1. *Maqālāt-e Shams-e Tabrīzī*  
Persian: مقالات شمس تبریزی  
Alternative traditional titles: *Khirqa-ye Shams*; *Asrār-e Shams al-Dīn Tabrīzī*

**Status:** Authentic repository of Shams’s speech, but not an autograph book.

The work consists of conversations, sermons, arguments, anecdotes, autobiographical fragments, Qurʾanic commentary, criticism of Sufis, and exchanges involving Rumi and his circle. Notes were probably made by more than one listener and compiled in the Mevlevi community.

Its repetitions, abrupt transitions, mixed voices, and occasional attribution problems show that “authored by Shams” requires qualification. It is closest to dictated or reported speech.

Important editions:

- Ahmad Khushnivis, ed., *Maqālāt-e Shams-e Tabrīzī*, Tehran, 1970/71.
- Muhammad ʿAli Movahed, ed., *Maqālāt-e Shams-e Tabrīzī*, 2 vols., Tehran: Khwarazmi, 1990; the principal comprehensive critical edition.
- Jaʿfar Modarres Sadeqi, ed., *Maqālāt-e Shams*, Tehran: Nashr-e Markaz, 1994.
- Mehmet Nuri Gençosman, trans., *Makalât*, Turkish, originally 1974–75; later editions.
- William C. Chittick, trans., *Me & Rumi: The Autobiography of Shams-i Tabrizi*, Louisville: Fons Vitae, 2004. This translates roughly two-thirds of the discourses, arranging selections into a readable autobiographical portrait; “autobiography” is an editorial description, not Shams’s original genre.

At least one catalogued manuscript is a fourteenth-century copy of 191 folios, 23 lines per page. Multiple manuscripts survive in Istanbul and Ankara collections.

### 2. *Marghūb al-qulūb* / *Marātib al-qulūb*

Persian/Arabic title forms: مرغوب القلوب / مراتب القلوب

**Status:** Falsely or dubiously attributed.

A ten-section didactic mystical *mathnawi*, often described as 138 couplets, circulated under the name “Shams Tabriz” and was lithographed with Niʿmat Khan ʿAli’s *Ḥusn o ʿIshq* at the Munshi Nawal Kishore Press in 1890.

Muhammad ʿAli Movahed concluded that it has no connection to Rumi’s Shams. South Asian confusion with the Multan saint likely assisted attribution.

### 3. *Pand-nāma* or counsel treatise

**Status:** Attributed, not authenticated.

Movahed mentions a manuscript counsel text in Istanbul attributed to Shams but rejects its connection with him.

### Poetry attributed directly to Shams

**Status:** Generally doubtful.

Later anthologies assign Persian mystical poems, quatrains, and ʿAlid devotional verse to “Shams Tabrizi.” The name may refer to other poets or to the Multan saint.

### Rumi’s *Dīvān-e Shams-e Tabrīzī*

**Status:** Not written by Shams.

The *Dīvān-e Kabīr*, commonly called the *Dīvān of Shams of Tabriz*, is Rumi’s lyric collection. Rumi often uses “Shams,” “Shams-e Tabriz,” or related names as signature, addressee, or identity. Catalogues and popular editions sometimes misleadingly list Shams as poet or co-author.

## Death, Burial, and Shrine Traditions

### Konya

A shrine or *maqām* associated with Shams stands in Konya. One tradition places his body beneath or near it; another calls it an empty commemorative station intended not to distress Rumi by declaring Shams dead.

### Khoy

Khoy in West Azerbaijan, Iran, possesses the most prominent rival tomb tradition. A cylindrical minaret and burial site are associated with Shams. The monument’s architecture and later shrine history do not by themselves prove that the missing Shams was interred there.

### Tabriz

A memorial or grave tradition exists in or near Tabriz, unsurprising given his nisba. No verified contemporary grave inscription establishes it.

### Multan

The monumental shrine at Multan belongs to a saint called Shah Shams Tabrez. Historical conflation with Rumi’s companion is widespread but not genealogically or chronologically secure.

### Other claims

Damascus and scattered Anatolian or Iranian localities preserve weaker traditions. The multiplication of tombs reflects the cult of the absent saint: an unresolved disappearance enabled communities to localize his presence.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Within Rumi’s work

Shams became:

- the dominant name of Rumi’s lyric corpus;
- the absent beloved;
- a manifestation of divine radiance;
- a poetic identity into which Rumi speaks;
- the catalyst remembered as turning a jurist-preacher toward ecstatic lyricism.

The historical Shams and Rumi’s poetic “Shams” overlap but are not identical.

### Within the Mevlevi order

Shams was foundational to Mevlevi sacred history but did not leave a separate durable institutional order. Mevlevi lineage construction usually centered on Rumi, Sultan Walad, and later successors. Shams remained the disruptive, non-institutional saint whose disappearance preceded institutional consolidation.

### Persianate and Islamic reception

His *Maqālāt* has increasingly been studied as an independent contribution to Persian prose and Sufi thought, correcting the older image of Shams as merely Rumi’s mysterious catalyst.

### Turkish national and cultural reception

Konya’s heritage economy places Shams beside Rumi, while scholarly and political arguments dispute whether the pair represent universal humanism, Islamic orthodoxy, Persian culture, Turkish-Anatolian culture, heterodoxy, or accommodation to empire.

### Iranian and Azerbaijani reception

Tabriz and Khoy claim Shams as a major Azerbaijani-Iranian cultural figure. Restoration and international recognition efforts around the Khoy monument have strengthened the survival-and-burial narrative.

### South Asian reception

The Multan conflation made “Shams Tabrez” part of devotional song, shrine pilgrimage, qawwali, and Ismaʿili memory. Poems and miracles belonging to different saints have circulated under the same name.

### Modern global reception

English-language popular spirituality frequently detaches Shams and Rumi from their Islamic vocabulary, presenting them as universal teachers of love. Conversely, recent scholarship emphasizes the Qurʾanic, prophetic, and jurisprudential framework of both men.

Modern novels transformed Shams into:

- author of the fictional “Forty Rules”;
- wandering social rebel;
- romantic partner;
- feminist critic;
- victim or perpetrator in the Kimya story;
- secret Ismaʿili;
- political agent;
- martyr murdered by religious conformists.

Each image has public influence but must be separated from the medieval record.
