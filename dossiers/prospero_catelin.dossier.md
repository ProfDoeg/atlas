# Próspero Catelin: Research Dossier

## Scope, Method, and Evidentiary Limits

Próspero Catelin is documented principally through government decrees, architectural history, surviving buildings and plans, and later studies of early nineteenth-century Buenos Aires. His private life is exceptionally obscure. No authenticated portrait, personal archive, diary, correspondence collection, memoir, or securely identified body of writings was found.

The documentary record supports his public activity much more strongly than it supports the standard biographical dates repeatedly attached to him. In particular:

- The often-repeated birth year **1764** remains unverified.
- A recent account identifies him as **Prosper Jean Catelin Rozan**, but no underlying French civil or parish record was located.
- Proposed arrival dates range from **1817** to **1821**.
- The commonly reported death year **1842** is plausible but was not verified through a contemporaneous civil, parish, probate, or burial record in the accessible sources.
- The frequently reproduced alternative—**San Fernando, 1 July 1870**—appears to refer to his son, Próspero Benjamín Catelin Garay.
- Attribution of the Metropolitan Cathedral façade is complicated by the probable participation of Pierre Benoît, Juan/José Pons, contractors, craftsmen, government officials, and French architectural precedents.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact**: supported by an official, institutional, contemporaneous, or closely archival source.
- **Reported fact**: stated by a credible secondary source but not independently verified here from the underlying record.
- **Disputed attribution**: competing claims exist concerning authorship, date, model, or identity.
- **Unverified claim**: repeated without an accessible underlying document.
- **Myth or legend**: a reputation-making story whose historical circulation is significant even where its factual basis is weak.

## Names, Spellings, and Possible Identity Confusion

### Documented and reported forms

- **Prosper Catelin**: French form.
- **Próspero Catelin**: standard Spanish-language form.
- **Próspero Catelín**: Spanish spelling with an accent, frequent in Argentine architectural literature.
- **Prosper Jean Catelin Rozan**: expanded form reported in *Los sabios de Rivadavia* in 2026.

No evidence was found of a pseudonym, nickname, hereditary title, military rank, or honorific beyond professional forms such as *ingeniero*, *arquitecto*, *ingeniero arquitecto*, and *ingeniero en jefe*.

### Father-and-son conflation

The architect had a son of essentially the same name, **Próspero Benjamín Catelin Garay**. This produced a persistent biographical error:

- The architect is commonly said to have died in **1842**.
- Some online authority records and derivative biographies instead give **San Fernando, 1 July 1870**.
- José Sellés-Martínez’s biographical notice states that the 1870 date belongs to the son.
- Later members of the family lived in or were associated with San Fernando, making the conflation geographically plausible.
- An 1880s English-language handbook lists “Catelin” among the largest property owners in San Fernando, but this is necessarily a later-generation Catelin rather than the architect if the 1842 death date is correct.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents, ancestry, and siblings

No reliable record identifying Catelin’s parents, grandparents, siblings, social class, or precise regional origin in France was found.

The surname and French nationality are consistently reported, but the available evidence does not justify assigning him to a particular French Catelin family. No French parish or civil registration, military-service file, architectural-school register, or immigration file was located.

### Marriage

**Reported fact:** Catelin married **María Dionisia Garay** in Buenos Aires.

A 2026 biographical account gives her fuller name as **María Dionisia Antonia del Corazón de Jesús Garay Garaño** and dates the marriage to **1825**. These details should be checked against the relevant Buenos Aires parish marriage register before being treated as fully established.

Nothing reliable was found concerning:

- her parents;
- her birth or death;
- her property or dowry;
- whether this was Catelin’s or Garay’s first marriage;
- the family’s residence at the time of marriage.

### Child

**Reported fact:** The marriage produced one known son:

- **Próspero Benjamín Catelin Garay**, reportedly born in Buenos Aires on **11 January 1826**.

The later Catelin family became associated with San Fernando. The 1904 *Guía biográfica argentina* identifies two younger Catelins:

- **Jorge Catelín**, merchant, born in Buenos Aires in 1864, son of Próspero Catelín and Angélica Terrada; married Enriqueta Basavilbaso; educated at the Colegio Inglés, Colegio San José, and Instituto Mercantil; member of the Rowing Club and Club del Progreso.
- **Alfredo Catelín**, landowner born in San Fernando, likewise a son of Próspero Catelín and Angélica Terrada; married Carmen Villatte; educated in England; member of the Rowing Club.

Their father was apparently the architect’s son, not the architect. This later evidence helps explain the erroneous extension of the elder Catelin’s life to 1870.

No other securely documented children of the architect were identified.

## Childhood and Early Life

The first decades of Catelin’s life remain unrecorded in the accessible literature.

### Birth-year problem

Three main versions circulate:

1. **1764** — the conventional date in Argentine reference literature.
2. **1762 or thereabouts** — mentioned as one of several possibilities in a recent biographical reconstruction.
3. **As late as 1790** — another possibility acknowledged in the same recent account.

If 1764 is correct, Catelin was about fifty-seven when appointed to public office in 1821. If born near 1790, he was approximately thirty-one. His apparent marriage in 1825 and fatherhood in 1826 do not decide the question: either chronology is biologically possible.

No exact date, town, département, baptism, or civil birth certificate was found.

### Early French career

No securely attributed building, employment, military appointment, examination, patent, publication, or professional membership in France was located.

It is often inferred from his professional title and fluency in contemporary French neoclassicism that he received formal architectural or engineering training. That inference is reasonable but not documentary proof of a particular institution.

## Education and Formation

### Unverified educational claims

A few derivative teaching materials claim that Catelin attended an “Escuela Militar Politécnica de París,” apparently meaning the École Polytechnique or a related military-technical institution. No enrollment record or reliable scholarly citation supporting this claim was found. It should therefore be treated as **unverified**.

His demonstrated professional competencies included:

- architectural planning;
- preparation and review of plans;
- urban and rural street alignment;
- public-health regulation;
- cemetery planning;
- construction supervision;
- public-building design;
- administrative direction of engineers, draftsmen, and trainees.

These abilities are compatible with formal technical education, military-engineering experience, apprenticeship, or some combination of them.

### Architectural influences

Catelin worked within a French neoclassical vocabulary. Sources and models associated with his buildings include:

- the chamber of the French **Palais Bourbon** for the Sala de Representantes;
- the Palais Bourbon façade and other French monumental porticoes for the cathedral;
- classical architectural orders;
- contemporary theories of legislative visibility and public access associated with Jeremy Bentham;
- Enlightenment and post-Enlightenment ideas about hygiene, burial outside churches, rational administration, and spatial ordering.

Some teaching materials claim that Catelin referred to the architectural authorities **Vignola** and **Durand**. This is plausible in the period’s professional culture, but the underlying Catelin report was not recovered.

### Mentors and teachers

No named teacher or personal mentor is documented.

Bernardino Rivadavia was Catelin’s principal political sponsor in Buenos Aires, but should be described as a patron and ministerial superior rather than an educational mentor.

## Migration to the Río de la Plata

Two arrival chronologies compete.

### Version A: arrival in 1817

An academic discussion published in *Investigaciones y Ensayos* places Catelin among a group of European artists and technicians who reached the Río de la Plata in **1817**, some characterized as political exiles or displaced professionals after Napoleon’s fall. The same group included Jacques Boudier, José Rousseau, Pierre Benoît, José Guth, and Felipe Senillosa.

This version is compatible with evidence that Catelin may have worked on the ceremonial apparatus for Manuel Belgrano’s reinterment in 1821 before the administrative reorganization of October.

### Version B: arrival in 1821

A 2026 newspaper biography says Catelin reached the Río de la Plata in **1821**, approximately when Rivadavia returned and the new technical administration was organized.

### Assessment

The 1817 account comes from an academic source citing Ramón Gutiérrez and Alberto de Paula’s historical work and is currently the stronger published version. Nevertheless, a passenger list, passport, immigration record, or first employment document was not found. The exact arrival date remains unresolved.

No evidence was found that Catelin returned to France or lived elsewhere after settling in Buenos Aires.

## Early Career in Buenos Aires

### Possible 1821 catafalque for Manuel Belgrano

**Reported fact:** Daniel Balmaceda states that Catelin was contracted to construct the catafalque used when Manuel Belgrano’s coffin was ceremonially transferred for funeral observances in 1821.

This is important for two reasons:

- it may be Catelin’s earliest identifiable commission in Buenos Aires;
- it places him in the city before, or at least contemporaneously with, the creation of his permanent public office.

The commission concerned temporary ceremonial architecture, and no surviving drawing has been identified.

### Creation of the engineer-architect post

A law or decree of **22 August 1821** created the provincial post of *ingeniero arquitecto*. Another source places the creation of the city’s engineer-architect position on **2 October 1821**.

On **23 October 1821**, the government of Governor Martín Rodríguez, with Bernardino Rivadavia as minister of government, formally created or organized the **Departamento de Ingenieros Arquitectos** under the Ministry of Government.

Its proposed staff included:

- an *ingeniero en jefe*;
- a second officer, associated in the decree with the University’s mathematics teaching;
- two inspectors;
- two draftsmen, drawn from artillery officers;
- a clerk;
- four students distinguished in mathematics.

Catelin became the department’s first chief.

### Institutional mandate

The department’s remit extended far beyond monumental architecture. It was responsible for:

- roads, streets, and bridges;
- running and stagnant water;
- sanitation and public cleanliness;
- street and town alignments;
- review and licensing of private buildings;
- markets, shops, and dwellings;
- public construction;
- the preparation of topographical and statistical information;
- attempts to standardize the plans of provincial towns.

A decree of **7 December 1821** reportedly prohibited building without submitting a plan to the department and obtaining a license. A further regulation of **14 December 1821** addressed buildings and street alignments.

Catelin was therefore one of the founders of professionalized public building control in post-revolutionary Buenos Aires.

## Chronological Life History

## 1821–1822: Sala de Representantes

In 1821 Catelin was commissioned to create a permanent meeting chamber for the Junta de Representantes of Buenos Aires Province.

### Site

The room was inserted into one of the revenue-producing structures in the former Jesuit complex later known as the **Manzana de las Luces**, between Perú and Moreno streets. Parts of earlier eighteenth-century fabric were demolished or altered.

Later accounts attached symbolic significance to the location, claiming that the new legislature rose where the “calabozos de Oruro,” associated with colonial repression of Peruvian independence sympathizers, had stood.

### Design

The room was semicircular, with stepped hemicycles for representatives, a raised presidential platform, and galleries for public attendance.

The official Manzana de las Luces history describes the semicircular arrangement as an attempt to materialize a new republican ideal. Academic sources connect it with:

- the French legislative chamber in the Palais Bourbon;
- Benthamite ideas about legislative deliberation, visibility, and public supervision;
- the post-revolutionary rejection of Spanish-colonial architectural symbolism.

The strongest evidence supports inspiration by contemporary French legislative space. Direct personal consultation between Catelin and Jeremy Bentham is not documented. Rivadavia corresponded with Bentham, providing a plausible channel for the ideas.

### Opening and later history

The Sala opened on **1 May 1822**.

It later housed:

- the Buenos Aires provincial legislature;
- the General Constituent Congress of 1824–1827;
- the Buenos Aires State legislature;
- provincial conventions;
- sessions of the national Congress in the 1860s;
- the Buenos Aires municipal deliberative council;
- the University of Buenos Aires Faculty of Architecture’s Aula Magna.

The nineteenth-century room was substantially altered, nearly ruined, demolished or reconstructed in stages, and reconstructed again in 1981. The present interior is therefore a historical reconstruction rather than Catelin’s untouched original.

The original working plans are not known to survive. Evidence includes:

- a small illustration in the 1826 *Diario de Sesiones del Congreso General Constituyente*;
- an 1840 sketch by Felipe Senillosa;
- archaeological and architectural investigations used in the twentieth-century reconstruction.

## 1821–1827: Metropolitan Cathedral façade

Buenos Aires’s cathedral had undergone repeated collapses, rebuildings, and unexecuted façade proposals. In November 1821 the government reportedly ordered that the church be completed according to the plan approved through the technical department. Work on the present front began in **January 1822**.

### Design

The façade consists of a monumental neoclassical portico with twelve giant-order columns. The official national heritage description calls it a **dodecastyle neoclassical portico** whose appearance is more civic than ecclesiastical.

The front differs sharply from earlier Spanish and colonial church façades:

- it originally lacked overt religious sculpture;
- it resembles a civic or legislative building;
- its monumental column screen masks an older church fabric not designed together with it.

### Attribution

Several levels of attribution must be distinguished.

- **Official attribution:** Próspero Catelin designed the façade in 1822.
- **Frequent joint attribution:** Catelin and Pierre Benoît.
- **Reported workshop participation:** Pierre Benoît, Juan or José Pons, the contractor Paulier, Italian craftsmen, and imported materials.
- **Political-programmatic influence:** Rivadavia’s government selected and promoted the project.
- **Later intervention:** Carlo Zucchi prepared drawings and alternative proposals but did not originate the 1822 façade.

Pierre Benoît worked under Catelin in the Department of Engineers-Architects and is often considered a co-designer. Firm division of labor cannot be reconstructed because the decisive original documentation is incomplete.

### Model controversy

Competing claims include:

- the **Palais Bourbon** in Paris, especially its monumental Napoleonic façade;
- the **Madeleine** in Paris;
- a broader family of French neoclassical church and civic porticoes;
- direct classical or Parthenon-derived composition.

The Madeleine claim is weak if presented as literal copying: its façade and completion chronology do not correspond neatly with Catelin’s 1822 project. Fernando Aliata’s historiographical analysis reportedly treats the façade as deriving from several contemporary French precedents rather than one simple prototype.

### Construction and incompletion

The façade was erected between approximately **1822 and 1827**, but remained incomplete:

- capitals and details were unfinished for a time;
- no cathedral towers were constructed;
- the pediment initially lacked its present sculpture.

Between **1860 and 1863**, long after Catelin’s death, sculptor Joseph Dubourdieu added the pediment relief representing the reunion of Jacob and Joseph. It is therefore incorrect to attribute that sculpture to Catelin.

## 1822–1823: Cementerio del Norte, now Recoleta Cemetery

Buenos Aires’s reform government sought to move burials out of churches for reasons of sanitation, public order, and ecclesiastical reform. The garden and burial ground of the Recollect friars beside Nuestra Señora del Pilar was converted into a public cemetery.

### Establishment

The **Cementerio del Norte** opened on **17 November 1822**. It is generally described as Buenos Aires’s first public cemetery.

The provincial/city historical account attributes the original layout to Catelin. Another municipal historical study says the Ministry assigned the work to **Catelin and Felipe Bertrés**, with Bertrés responsible for a plan. The precise division of responsibility deserves further archival examination.

### Layout

Catelin’s plan imposed a regular, rationalized structure on the former convent garden. Sources describe:

- a geometrically ordered burial ground;
- main walks and diagonals;
- differentiated burial lots;
- a central or privileged zone;
- a system linking placement to price and civic distinction.

A decree dated **3 September 1823** established a scale of burial-lot prices according to location. It also reserved preferred ground for people whose virtues or public services merited commemoration, while formally declaring that wealth alone should not provide access to those places.

The plan embodied both republican commemoration and social differentiation.

### Later alteration

The cemetery reached approximately its present perimeter in **1828**. Its original plan was not followed consistently. Carlo Zucchi later made expansion proposals, and Juan Antonio Buschiazzo redesigned the entrance and other elements during the administration of Torcuato de Alvear in the 1880s.

The present cemetery is consequently a layered work, not an intact Catelin composition.

## 1822: Mercado del Centro

A municipal architectural history credits Catelin with the first purpose-built public market building in Buenos Aires:

- **Name:** Mercado del Centro.
- **Date:** 1822.
- **Location:** block bounded by present-day Perú, Moreno, Chacabuco, and Alsina streets.
- **Character:** initially a precarious or modest structure.
- **Significance:** the first Buenos Aires building intended exclusively for a market function, as distinct from open markets and the stalls of the Recova.

The building no longer survives in its original form.

## 1822: San José de Flores inspection

Shortly after becoming director, Catelin traveled to **San José de Flores**, then a small settlement west of Buenos Aires.

Fernando Aliata reports that Catelin found the settlement’s privately generated layout irregular and problematic. The extended, dispersed street pattern produced:

- unclear boundaries;
- repeated surveys;
- litigation between private owners and public authorities;
- encroachment on public space.

This inspection illustrates the department’s attempt to impose regular plans on settlements created through private subdivision.

## 1822–1823: Government House, fort, barracks, and urban works

Municipal architectural histories attribute the following to Catelin or his department:

- modification of entrances or construction of porticoes at the **Fuerte de Buenos Aires** and Casa de Gobierno;
- work on a **public market**;
- a **Cuartel de Artillería**;
- possible completion planning for the **Coliseo** or theatre after other public works.

The strongest account says Catelin added two porticoes intended to dignify the civil government inside the inherited fortification. Precise surviving drawings and contractual divisions were not located.

Claims that Catelin designed the **Teatro de la Victoria** are less consistently supported and should not be treated as established without further archival evidence.

## 1823–1824: Provincial planning and administrative expansion

Catelin’s department received additional assignments:

- prepare a model for parish churches in provincial towns;
- mark the line beyond which flour mills or *tahonas* could be established;
- prepare topographical and statistical collections;
- make plans of campaign towns, reportedly beginning with San Nicolás de los Arroyos;
- regulate public and private construction.

By 1824 the department reportedly included:

- Próspero Catelin, first chief;
- Juan Ponce/Pons, second chief;
- Teodoro Schuster, auxiliary officer;
- Pedro/Pierre Benoît, auxiliary officer.

The scale of the territorial mapping assignment exceeded the capacity of the new and poorly resourced institution. Some functions migrated to the Comisión Topográfica and later Departamento Topográfico.

## 1825–1826: Marriage and renewed national office

**Reported fact:** Catelin married María Dionisia Garay in 1825.

**Reported fact:** Their son Próspero Benjamín was born on 11 January 1826.

On **5 May 1826**, during Rivadavia’s national presidency, the Department of Engineers-Architects was re-established or reorganized as a national institution, and Catelin was again named *ingeniero en jefe*. A surviving transcription identifies him directly as the department’s chief.

This appointment confirms that his professional standing survived the transition from provincial reform to Rivadavia’s national government.

## 1827–1828: Political reversal and abolition of the department

Rivadavia resigned in 1827 amid opposition to centralization, the constitutional conflict, and the war with Brazil.

The Department of Engineers-Architects was abolished on **14 February 1828**. Its functions overlapped with:

- the provincial engineer;
- the Department of Hydraulic Engineers;
- the Topographical Department;
- police and municipal regulatory functions.

The abolition ended Catelin’s documented leadership of the institution.

It was an institutional rather than personal criminal dismissal. No evidence was found of misconduct proceedings, corruption charges, or professional discipline against him.

## Later Life, 1828–1842

Catelin’s life after the abolition of the department is poorly documented.

There is no securely reconstructed record of:

- a subsequent permanent government office;
- private architectural commissions;
- teaching;
- political exile;
- military service;
- imprisonment;
- bankruptcy;
- illness;
- return to France.

A study of civic festivities quotes a late-1820s episode in which a carpenter, Juan Vernon, referred to viewing or failing to view a plan “in the house of Señor Catelin.” This suggests that Catelin continued to hold and distribute official or ceremonial working drawings after being reinstated in his functions, but the evidence does not establish a separate private practice.

Later Catelin property and commercial references in San Fernando generally appear to concern his son or descendants.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Departamento de Ingenieros Arquitectos

Catelin’s principal institutional achievement was administrative. The department helped establish:

- professional review of building plans;
- government construction licensing;
- standardized urban alignments;
- public-health oversight of buildings and drainage;
- a documentary archive of plans;
- technical employment for engineers, architects, draftsmen, and mathematically trained students.

It was an early predecessor of later provincial and municipal public-works and building-control agencies.

### Other institutional associations

Catelin worked in or through:

- the Ministry of Government of Buenos Aires Province;
- the provincial government of Martín Rodríguez;
- Rivadavia’s national administration;
- the Manzana de las Luces complex;
- the cathedral works administration;
- the organization of the Cementerio del Norte;
- emerging topographical and statistical agencies.

### Technologies and methods

No invention or patented technology is associated with Catelin.

His technical practice involved:

- measured plans;
- geometric layouts;
- monumental masonry porticoes;
- application of classical orders;
- construction licensing;
- survey and delineation;
- functional seating geometry;
- hygienic separation of burial grounds from dense habitation;
- differentiated cemetery plotting.

### Wealth and property

No probate inventory, salary ledger, tax return, bank record, investment portfolio, partnership, company, or land grant belonging securely to the architect was found.

A historic planning source says that the engineer-architect and hydraulic-engineer posts created in 1821 were each assigned a salary of **2,000 pesos monthly**, but the monetary denomination and whether Catelin actually received the full amount require confirmation from the original fiscal record.

Later identification of “Catelin” as a substantial San Fernando landowner cannot safely be assigned to the architect because his son and grandsons were established there.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Political position

Catelin held appointments under reformist governments led by Martín Rodríguez and Bernardino Rivadavia. His buildings gave physical form to aspects of their program:

- centralized technical administration;
- secular or state-directed control of burial;
- replacement of colonial symbolism;
- public legislative deliberation;
- modernization based on imported European expertise.

No evidence was found that Catelin:

- held elective office;
- belonged formally to the Unitarian Party;
- published political doctrine;
- fought in civil wars;
- was exiled by Federalist authorities;
- swore allegiance to a secret society;
- engaged in political violence.

His identification with the Rivadavian project is institutional and architectural, not proof of a fully recoverable personal ideology.

### Religion

Catelin designed or worked on Catholic religious architecture and a cemetery created partly through ecclesiastical confiscation, but his personal religious beliefs are unknown.

His cathedral façade’s civic appearance has been interpreted as evidence of the Rivadavian state’s ascendancy over the church. That interpretation concerns the commission’s political meaning and does not establish Catelin’s own faith or irreligion.

### Intellectual influences

The Sala de Representantes has been related to Jeremy Bentham’s *Tactique des assemblées législatives*. The connection probably ran through Rivadavia and the government’s intellectual network.

Catelin’s cemetery plan reflects contemporary European and Spanish-American thinking on:

- miasma and public health;
- ending burial inside churches;
- civic commemoration;
- administrative classification;
- market pricing and differentiated property rights in graves.

### Artistic development

The accessible works show a consistent movement toward:

- sober geometric form;
- monumental classical orders;
- French rather than Spanish-colonial models;
- architecture as a representation of state authority;
- functional planning for new republican institutions.

No evidence of a later stylistic evolution was found.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Bernardino Rivadavia — patron and political superior

**Documented relationship:** Rivadavia, as minister and later president, promoted the technical institutions in which Catelin served and backed the cathedral, legislature, cemetery, and urban-reform projects.

Rivadavia’s European contacts and political program created the demand for Catelin’s expertise. The relationship was professionally decisive, although private correspondence between the two was not located.

### Martín Rodríguez — appointing governor

**Documented relationship:** As governor of Buenos Aires Province, Rodríguez’s administration created the department and appointed Catelin to senior office.

### Pierre Benoît — subordinate and collaborator

**Documented employment relationship; disputed co-authorship:** Benoît served as an auxiliary architect/draftsman in Catelin’s department. Later historians frequently credit him with collaborating on the cathedral façade and possibly other works signed or officially presented under Catelin’s authority.

The degree of Benoît’s authorship remains unresolved.

### Juan/José Pons or Ponce — departmental colleague

**Documented administrative relationship:** Listed as second chief or departmental officer. Some sources associate Pons with cathedral construction.

Name forms vary between Juan Ponce, Juan Pons, and José Pons in derivative literature; the official personnel record should be used to settle the identity.

### Felipe Bertrés — collaborator or parallel planner

**Reported collaboration:** A municipal study names Catelin and Bertrés together in the planning of the Cementerio del Norte, specifying that Bertrés had responsibility for a plan.

### Felipe Senillosa — parallel technical official

Senillosa belonged to the same post-revolutionary technical-administrative network. He later sketched Catelin’s legislative chamber and became important in the Topographical Commission. No close personal friendship is documented.

### James/Santiago Bevans — parallel department head

Bevans headed hydraulic-engineering work while Catelin headed architect-engineer work. Their mandates were complementary and sometimes institutionally overlapping.

### Carlo Zucchi — successor-generation architect

Zucchi worked in the later Rivadavian and post-Rivadavian technical environment. He drew Catelin’s cathedral project in 1834 and developed alternative designs and Recoleta expansion proposals. The evidence establishes professional succession and reinterpretation more clearly than direct collaboration.

### Jeremy Bentham — indirect intellectual connection

No meeting or correspondence between Catelin and Bentham was found. Bentham’s relationship to Catelin’s Sala is mediated through Rivadavia, government discourse, and architectural typology.

### Jacques Boudier — predecessor in anti-colonial architectural discourse

Boudier’s 1817 governmental report argued that public buildings should abandon the “style of the Goths” and represent the new political spirit. Catelin later implemented this program in permanent public architecture. A direct personal relationship is possible but not demonstrated.

## Successes and Failures

### Successes

- First chief of a foundational public architectural and building-regulation office.
- Designed or directed three of the most symbolically important surviving public spaces of early independent Buenos Aires.
- Produced the first purpose-built legislative chamber in Buenos Aires and, according to contemporary claims, one of the earliest of revolutionary Latin America.
- Created the original plan of the city’s first public cemetery.
- Established a cathedral façade that remains one of Buenos Aires’s best-known architectural images.
- Helped institutionalize plan submission, construction licensing, street alignment, and public-health regulation.

### Failures and incomplete projects

- The Department of Engineers-Architects remained short-lived, underfunded, and partly redundant.
- Its province-wide topographical and statistical mandate exceeded its capacity.
- The cathedral façade remained incomplete for decades; its pediment sculpture was added only in 1860–1863, and intended towers or campaniles were never built.
- The Recoleta plan was not followed consistently and was heavily transformed.
- Numerous public projects of the period were delayed or abandoned because of fiscal and political instability.
- Little personal documentation or original design material survives, leaving Catelin’s individual contribution difficult to separate from that of his staff.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

## Findings of the dedicated adversarial search

Searches were conducted in Spanish, English, and French-name forms combining Catelin’s name with terms for:

- controversy;
- scandal;
- accusations;
- fraud;
- corruption;
- lawsuit;
- criticism;
- conspiracy;
- allegations;
- denunciation;
- criminal activity;
- 2025 and 2026 material.

### Criminal and financial misconduct

**Finding:** No credible evidence was found that Catelin was investigated, indicted, prosecuted, convicted, imprisoned, accused of fraud, implicated in corruption, involved in money laundering, or connected to political violence or intelligence activity.

This is an absence-of-evidence finding, not proof that every aspect of his official finances was unproblematic. The surviving biographical record is thin and much administrative material remains undigitized.

### Authorship controversy

**Disputed attribution:** The main controversy concerns authorship of the cathedral façade.

- Official and heritage sources name Catelin.
- Numerous historians name Catelin and Pierre Benoît jointly.
- Other accounts suggest Benoît worked on projects formally signed by Catelin because Catelin was departmental chief.
- Pons and contractors also participated in construction.
- No decisive document located here permits a precise allocation of design authorship.

### European-model controversy

**Historiographical dispute:** Scholars and popular histories have identified the Palais Bourbon, the Madeleine, the Parthenon, and other French neoclassical churches as the cathedral’s model.

The best-supported conclusion is that the façade belongs to a wider French monumental-neoclassical design culture and strongly resembles the Palais Bourbon. Claims of exact copying from the completed Madeleine are chronologically and formally problematic.

### Cemetery attribution and chronology

Some sources say Catelin planned Recoleta in 1822; a 2026 article instead places his plan “toward 1828.” Municipal and academic evidence supports an original 1822 plan, with later expansion and alteration by others. The 1828 date likely refers to extension to approximately the present perimeter rather than initial creation.

Another source assigns Catelin and Felipe Bertrés joint responsibility. This should be treated as an unresolved division of labor.

### Birth, arrival, and death controversies

These are record problems rather than scandals:

- birth proposed anywhere between 1762 and 1790;
- arrival placed in either 1817 or 1821;
- death reported as 1842 or 1870;
- 1870 apparently belongs to his son.

### Political criticism of the Rivadavian program

Later architectural history has described the importation of European forms as:

- a deliberate effort to erase Spanish-colonial symbolism;
- a largely formal or “uncritical” transplantation of European historicism;
- an expression of Buenos Aires’s political and cultural centralization;
- a state-driven modernization program whose ambitions exceeded its financial means.

These are interpretations of the government program and Catelin’s architectural role. They are not allegations of personal illegality.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The architect who erased Buenos Aires’s Spanish past”

**Story:** Catelin was imported by Rivadavia to “liberate Buenos Aires from its Spanish past.”

**Origin and circulation:** The formulation derives from post-revolutionary architectural discourse, especially Jacques Boudier’s 1817 call for public buildings to abandon the style associated with Spanish or “Gothic” rule. It was later applied to Catelin’s neoclassical work.

**Evidence:** Catelin’s principal designs demonstrably replaced colonial forms with French neoclassical ones. What is not established is that he personally used the quoted formulation.

**Reputational effect:** It made Catelin a symbol of both cosmopolitan modernization and elite cultural displacement, depending on the historian’s perspective.

### The Madeleine as the single source of the cathedral

**Story:** Catelin copied the Paris church of La Madeleine.

**Evidence against a simple version:** The buildings differ in column number and composition; the Madeleine’s final construction chronology postdates Catelin’s design. Both belong to the same broad monumental neoclassical environment.

**Reputational effect:** The story made the façade seem like a direct Parisian import but oversimplified its architectural genealogy.

### The Palais Bourbon as an exact template

**Story:** Both Catelin’s legislative chamber and cathedral front were copied directly from the Palais Bourbon.

**Evidence:** The chamber and façade have persuasive typological relationships to the Palais Bourbon. Exact copying is harder to establish. Catelin adapted models to inherited sites, available labor, materials, and local political purposes.

### “Twelve columns for the twelve apostles”

**Story:** The cathedral’s twelve-column front was selected to symbolize the apostles.

**Evidence:** This symbolism is repeated in popular accounts, but no contemporaneous design statement by Catelin was found. The façade originally lacked explicit religious sculpture and was often criticized or interpreted as unusually civic.

**Label:** Unverified symbolic interpretation.

### A Bonapartist or Napoleonic exile

**Story:** Catelin was one of several European political exiles displaced after Napoleon’s fall.

**Evidence:** An academic source describes the 1817 immigrant technical group as including political exiles, but does not demonstrate Catelin’s own political service, Bonapartist affiliation, proscription, or exile order.

**Label:** Plausible group-context claim; unverified as an individual biography.

### The death at San Fernando in 1870

**Story:** Catelin lived until 1 July 1870 and died at San Fernando.

**Evidence against:** A local historical study states that this was his son’s death, while the architect died in Buenos Aires in 1842. Later Catelin descendants and property in San Fernando likely reinforced the error.

**Reputational effect:** The conflation added twenty-eight years to the architect’s life and created the appearance of an undocumented late career.

### The solitary genius

**Story:** Catelin alone created the cathedral façade, cemetery, and legislative hall.

**Evidence against:** He worked as chief of a government department within a network of draftsmen, builders, contractors, engineers, politicians, and collaborators. Benoît’s role is particularly significant.

**Reputational effect:** Official attribution compressed collective public works into a single-author biography.

### Lost portrait

No authenticated portrait of Catelin is known. Images attached to web biographies should not be accepted without provenance. The absence of a portrait contributes to his status as a name attached to monuments rather than a visually remembered public figure.

## Works by Próspero Catelin

No books, essays, manifestos, patents, memoirs, diaries, or literary works securely authored by Catelin were found.

His recoverable oeuvre consists mainly of architectural and administrative designs.

### Surviving or reconstructed works

1. **Sala de Sesiones de la Junta de Representantes de la Provincia de Buenos Aires**  
   - Design/construction: 1821–1822  
   - Opened: 1 May 1822  
   - Location: Manzana de las Luces, Buenos Aires  
   - Status: repeatedly altered and reconstructed; current room is a historical reconstruction  
   - Authorship: officially Catelin  

2. **Façade/portico of the Catedral Metropolitana de Buenos Aires**  
   - Project: 1821–1822  
   - Construction: principally 1822–1827

   - Authorship: Catelin officially; frequent joint attribution to Pierre Benoît  

3. **Original plan of the Cementerio General del Norte/Cementerio de la Recoleta**  
   - Date: 1822, with lot regulation in 1823  
   - Status: underlying geometry partly retained; extensively altered  
   - Authorship: Catelin; one source associates Felipe Bertrés with the plan  

### Lost, altered, temporary, or incompletely documented works

4. **Catafalque for funeral ceremonies of Manuel Belgrano**  
   - Reported date: 1821  
   - Temporary work; no surviving plan located

   - Date: 1822  
   - First purpose-built market structure in Buenos Aires  
   - Lost or superseded  

6. **Porticoes or access alterations, Fuerte and Casa de Gobierno**  
   - Approximate date: early 1820s

7. **Cuartel de Artillería**  
   - Attributed in municipal architectural history  
   - Precise plan and execution history not recovered  

8. **Model plan for parish churches in provincial towns**  
   - Ordered: 7 January 1823  
   - Survival and implementation unknown  

9. **Line for the permitted location of tahonas/flour mills**  
   - Ordered: 18 January 1823  
   - Administrative/urban plan; survival unknown  

10. **Plans or surveys of provincial towns**  
    - Commissioned from 1823, reportedly beginning with San Nicolás de los Arroyos  
    - Extent of Catelin’s direct authorship uncertain  

11. **Topographical and statistical collection for Buenos Aires Province**  
    - Departmental commission rather than a known signed publication  
    - Scope exceeded available resources  

12. **Plans for civic festivities or temporary structures**  
    - At least one late-1820s source refers to a plan kept at Catelin’s house  
    - Individual works remain incompletely identified  

### Questionable attribution

- **Teatro de la Victoria:** attributed to Catelin in some derivative sources, but stronger confirmation was not located.
- **Later Recoleta buildings:** the present entrance and many architectural features belong to Juan Antonio Buschiazzo and later architects, not Catelin.
- **Cathedral pediment sculpture:** not Catelin; it was added by Dubourdieu after Catelin’s death.

## Death and Burial

### Most likely reconstruction

- **Death:** Buenos Aires, **1842**.
- **Burial:** reportedly Cementerio de la Recoleta, the cemetery whose original plan he had prepared.

### Problems

- No exact 1842 date was found.
- No burial-register image, grave inscription, or probate record was located.
- Some sources give **San Fernando, 1 July 1870**, apparently because they have confused him with his son.
- The location and present condition of Catelin’s own grave were not established.
- A Recoleta “familia de Catelín” tomb is documented in cemetery guides, but the accessible description does not prove that the architect’s remains are there.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Catelin’s reputation rests on three architectural survivals—or, more precisely, three heavily layered survivals:

1. the reconstructed Sala de Representantes;
2. the original geometry of Recoleta Cemetery;
3. the cathedral’s monumental portico.

He is generally positioned as:

- one of the first major public architects of independent Argentina;
- a founder of professional public-works administration in Buenos Aires;
- an exponent of French neoclassicism;
- an architectural agent of Rivadavia’s political modernization;
- a designer whose works translated new ideas of state, public health, civic commemoration, and legislative representation into physical form.

His personal reputation has remained secondary to the symbolic careers of his buildings. The lack of a portrait, writings, and private archive has encouraged several distortions:

- solitary authorship substituted for collective departmental practice;
- uncertain dates hardened into fact through repetition;
- his son’s death was assigned to him;
- French precedents were reduced to a single “copied” building;
- governmental ideology was sometimes treated as if it were a documented personal manifesto.

Modern conservation has further complicated his legacy. The Sala de Representantes is a reconstruction based on fragmentary evidence. Recoleta was extensively redesigned. The cathedral was finished by later builders and sculptors. Catelin’s legacy is therefore not a preserved individual oeuvre in the conventional sense, but a set of foundational plans repeatedly altered by successive governments.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are included. Shared geography or mere contemporaneity has not been treated as a connection.

### Manuel Belgrano — reported fact

Catelin was reportedly contracted in Buenos Aires in **1821** to construct the catafalque for ceremonies surrounding Belgrano’s coffin and funeral commemoration. Belgrano had died in 1820, so this was a posthumous commemorative commission rather than a documented personal meeting.

### Pierre/Pedro Benoît and Pedro Simón del Corazón de Jesús Benoit — distinction required

The collaborator documented in Catelin’s department was **Pierre Benoît (1794–1852)**, also called Pedro Benoît. The atlas roster contains **Pedro Simón del Corazón de Jesús Benoit**, Pierre’s son, born in 1836 and later a prominent engineer.

- **Pierre Benoît:** documented subordinate and collaborator of Catelin; probable cathedral co-designer.
- **Pedro Benoit the son:** no direct relationship with Catelin was found. He was a child when Catelin reportedly died. Any influence would have been mediated through his father and the Buenos Aires technical tradition.

The rostered Pedro Benoit therefore should not be silently conflated with Catelin’s adult collaborator.

### Juan Antonio Buschiazzo — documented posthumous architectural succession

Buschiazzo remodelled Recoleta Cemetery in the **1880s**, decades after Catelin’s death. He transformed the entrance and other features of the cemetery laid out by Catelin.

This is a documented relationship between their works, not a personal meeting.

### Torcuato Antonio de Alvear — documented posthumous administrative connection

As Buenos Aires’s first mayor, Torcuato de Alvear commissioned the late nineteenth-century modernization of Recoleta Cemetery, with Buschiazzo as architect. His administration altered Catelin’s earlier plan.

No personal contact was possible if Catelin died in 1842.

### Domingo Faustino Sarmiento — documented use of Catelin’s institutional space; no personal relationship established

Sarmiento’s political generation used institutions housed in the Sala de Representantes and later public buildings shaped by Catelin’s work. No source consulted documented a meeting, correspondence, commission, or direct influence between the two men.

### Juan Manuel de Rosas — documented political and architectural afterlife, not a clear personal tie

During Rosas’s government, Catelin’s Sala de Representantes continued in use and was redecorated in Federalist red with political inscriptions. Later accounts sometimes mistakenly place Catelin’s Recoleta design under Rosas rather than Rodríguez and Rivadavia.

No direct personal meeting or commission from Rosas to Catelin was established.

### Juan Martín de Pueyrredón — contextual but unproven

One academic source places Catelin’s arrival in 1817, during Pueyrredón’s directorship. No document consulted establishes that Pueyrredón personally recruited, met, patronized, or corresponded with Catelin. This is therefore not counted as a substantive Atlas connection.

### Absence finding

No supported direct connection was found between Catelin and the other atlas figures, including Jeremy Bentham—who is not on the supplied roster—or the numerous later Argentine architects and political figures listed there. The principal documented network instead consisted of Bernardino Rivadavia, Martín Rodríguez, Pierre Benoît, Felipe Bertrés, Felipe Senillosa, James Bevans, Juan/José Pons, Jacques Boudier, and Carlo Zucchi.
