# Vera Skoronel: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

Vera Skoronel died at twenty-five. Her career occupied approximately eight years, and dance is an ephemeral art: no verified moving-image record of her performances was located. The documentary base consists principally of:

- her surviving letters, manuscripts, choreographic sketches, photographs, programs, reviews, and memorial material in the Deutsches Tanzarchiv Köln;
- papers inherited through her brother Boris Lämmel and her collaborator Berthe Trümpy;
- records in the Palucca and other dance archives;
- contemporary press, especially *Vorwärts* and the *Gladbecker Anzeiger*;
- Rudolf Lämmel’s 1928 account of his daughter’s technique and choreography;
- writings by Skoronel collected posthumously by René Radrizzani;
- retrospective testimony by Mary Wigman, Yvonne Georgi, Boris Lämmel, and Berthe Trümpy;
- modern dance-historical studies.

A further body of Skoronel material remained in the privately held Rudolf Lämmel archive and was described by the Deutsches Tanzarchiv as inaccessible to researchers. Exact performance dates, cast lists, musical collaborators, and first-publication details therefore remain incomplete.

Evidentiary labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** established by archival material, contemporaneous publication, institutional record, or agreement among strong sources.
- **Reported fact:** later testimony or a reputable secondary reconstruction that has not been independently confirmed here.
- **Allegation or disputed claim:** a consequential assertion for which the available evidence is incomplete or conflicting.
- **Rumor or myth:** anecdotal or reputation-forming material not securely verifiable.

### Origin of the name “Skoronel”

**Reported fact:** In 1989 her elder brother Boris Lämmel told dance historian Frank-Manuel Peter that “Skoronel” began as a childhood name. A Russian relative—possibly her maternal grandfather Pavel Axelrod—supposedly addressed the fast-moving child with “Vera, skoro,” using the Russian word for “quickly.” The children contracted the phrase into “Skoronel.”

This is plausible family testimony, but the precise speaker and date were already uncertain in Boris’s account. It should not be treated as a formally documented legal name change.

## Family and Ancestry

### Father: Rudolf Lämmel

**Documented fact:** Vera’s father was **Rudolf Lämmel** (1879–1962), born in Vienna into a Jewish family. He earned a doctorate in 1904 with a dissertation on methods of determining probabilities. His work crossed mathematics, popular science, educational reform, literature, and dance theory.

He established or directed progressive educational ventures, including:

- a Reformgymnasium in Zürich, founded in 1902;
- an attempted girls’ educational home connected with rhythmic gymnastics at Hertenstein on Lake Lucerne;
- work as a visiting teacher at Paul Geheeb’s Odenwaldschule in 1915;
- the Schillerheim country boarding school at Mettmenstetten, founded in 1917;
- later work as a Studienrektor.

He published under his own name and used the pseudonym **Heinrich Inführ**. His books included popular treatments of relativity and *Der moderne Tanz* (1928), whose chapter on Vera is among the most extensive contemporary descriptions of her choreography.

Rudolf’s interest in dance preceded Vera’s professional life. He recalled being strongly impressed by Isadora Duncan’s Zürich appearance in 1902 and was connected with the Semmler-Rinke rhythmic-gymnastics school by 1912. His educational interests gave Vera unusually early access to movement training.

**Financial history:** Rudolf’s Hertenstein project failed after the outbreak of the First World War deprived it of foreign pupils. Family finances were consequently unstable. Later accounts say Vera experienced serious poverty during her Dresden training, although the details are anecdotal.

### Paternal grandparents

The *Neue Deutsche Biographie* identifies Rudolf’s parents as:

- **Heinrich Laemmel** (1850–1933), an Oberoffizial in Villach;
- **Johanna Kiczales**.

Little reliable public material was located concerning Vera’s relationship with them.

### Mother: Sonja Axelrod

**Documented fact:** Vera’s mother was **Sonja, Sofia, or Sophia Axelrod** (1881–1917), daughter of the Russian revolutionary Pavel Axelrod. She suffered a prolonged illness and died in 1917, when Vera was eleven.

Surviving childhood letters from Vera to her grandfather describe the grief caused by her mother’s illness and death. The loss is one of the best-documented emotional crises of her childhood, although claims that it directly produced particular later behavior are retrospective interpretation.

### Maternal grandfather: Pavel Borisovich Axelrod

**Documented fact:** Vera’s maternal grandfather was **Pavel Borisovich Axelrod** (approximately 1850–1928), a major Russian Marxist organizer and theorist. He participated in the Emancipation of Labour group and became a leading Menshevik. He lived for long periods in Switzerland, returned to Russia in 1917, opposed the Bolsheviks, and subsequently emigrated again.

Vera corresponded with him as a child. Her descent from Axelrod was repeatedly invoked in later portrayals of her as innately rebellious or “revolutionary.” The kinship is documented; the conversion of that ancestry into an explanation of her temperament is biographical mythology.

Some secondary references identify writer and physician **Isaac Kaminer** as her maternal great-grandfather. That ancestry was not independently established through a primary genealogical document in the sources consulted.

### Siblings and stepfamily

- **Boris Lämmel**, born in 1904, was Vera’s elder full brother. He later supplied the family account of her professional name and contributed material to the dance archive. The *Neue Deutsche Biographie* records him as missing from 1992; the exact circumstances were not established.
- In or after 1917 Rudolf married **Luise Frank** (1896–1991), called “Lu” in Vera’s correspondence.
- Reference sources record **six step-siblings**, but their names and Vera’s relationships with them were not reconstructed from the publicly accessible record.

### Marriage, partners, and children

**Documented negative finding:** Skoronel never married, and no children are documented. Berthe Trümpy is consistently described as her friend, collaborator, and co-director. No reliable source consulted establishes a sexual partnership between them. The intensity of their personal and professional bond should not be converted into a claim about sexuality without evidence.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1906–1918

Vera grew up principally in Zürich within an unconventional reform-educational household.

### First movement training

**Documented fact:** From approximately 1912, when she was six, she took rhythmic-gymnastics courses at the Semmler-Rinke school in Zürich. One dance-archive reference also places her in the children’s ballet school of the Zürich municipal theatre.

Her father’s educational projects connected artistic movement, physical culture, and progressive schooling. Vera therefore encountered dance not merely as entertainment but as a possible system of education and cultural reform.

### Family instability

Rudolf Lämmel’s educational enterprises were vulnerable to wartime economics. The Hertenstein project became insolvent after 1914. Later recollections imply that changes in family fortune contributed to Vera’s material insecurity.

Her mother’s prolonged illness and death in 1917 were central childhood events. The surviving letters documenting Vera’s sorrow are stronger evidence than later psychological interpretations of the loss.

## Education and Formation, 1919–1924

### Zürich Laban school, 1919–1921

At Easter 1919 Skoronel entered the Zürich Laban school, which was then principally run by **Suzanne Perrottet** and **Katja/Käthe Wulff**.

Her education included modern movement, rhythmic study, improvisation, music, and the spatial theories associated with Rudolf von Laban. Rudolf Lämmel later said that his thirteen-year-old daughter also had several lessons directly with Laban. The formulation suggests limited direct instruction rather than a long teacher-pupil relationship.

The Laban environment supplied foundations she later transformed:

- analysis of direction and space;
- differentiation of movement qualities;
- ensemble or choric movement;
- the idea that dance could be systematically described;
- a preference for structural composition rather than narrative ballet.

### Early writing: *Asja und Skule*

**Reported archival fact:** Dance historian Karl Toepfer describes an unpublished youthful novel, *Asja und Skule*, dated 1919 in his source. It concerns two female friends who seek ecstatic power through an intensely intellectual commitment to dance.

A chronological problem should be noted: a 1919 composition would have been written when Skoronel was thirteen, although Toepfer calls her fifteen. Either the date or age in the reconstruction may be imprecise. The manuscript has not been commercially published and its current archival status is not fully clear.

### Loheland, 1921

In 1921 Skoronel spent approximately three months at the **Loheland school** in Künzell in the Rhön. Loheland combined women’s education, physical culture, handicraft, communal life, and anthroposophically influenced ideas.

The brevity of her stay cautions against making her an orthodox anthroposophist. No evidence located documents membership in the Anthroposophical Society or adherence to Rudolf Steiner’s religious-philosophical system.

### Mary Wigman’s school, Dresden, 1922–1924

From 1922 to 1924 Skoronel studied at Mary Wigman’s Dresden school and belonged to Wigman’s early dance group. Her classmates or close dance-scene associates included:

- Gret Palucca;
- Yvonne Georgi;
- Hanya Holm;
- Berthe Trümpy;
- Aenne Grünert;
- Tina Flade;
- Margarete Wallmann.

Wigman’s work emphasized intensity, space, rhythm, percussion, group dynamics, and expression beyond classical ballet. Skoronel completed her training as one of Wigman’s early students but soon developed an aesthetic more geometric, polycentric, and mechanically formal than Wigman’s.

### Recalled behavior in Dresden

The principal colorful stories come from retrospective testimony and require careful labels.

- **Reported fact:** Yvonne Georgi recalled sharing a furnished room with Skoronel.
- **Reported anecdote:** Skoronel allegedly jumped through an open studio window and left a Wigman class after declaring there was nothing further for her to learn.
- **Reported anecdote:** She allegedly took Aenne Grünert’s brown velvet dress and defended herself by saying it suited her.
- **Reported anecdote:** During inflation and poverty she was said to have lived partly on bread crusts left in dressing rooms.
- **Reported anecdote:** She reportedly wore an outfit assembled from safety pins and her father’s school flag.
- **Reported allegation:** Georgi said Skoronel drank her eau de cologne when ordinary alcohol was unavailable.

These anecdotes recur in the archive’s biographical account, but they derive from memory and oral testimony rather than contemporaneous disciplinary or medical records. Collectively, they shaped the image of Skoronel as brilliant, uncontrollable, impoverished, and self-destructive.

## Early Career: The Ruhr Theatres, 1924–1925

### Appointment at eighteen

**Documented fact:** On the recommendation of Mary Wigman and Gret Palucca, Skoronel obtained the post of ballet mistress or dance director at the **Vereinigte Bühnen Oberhausen, Hamborn und Gladbeck** in 1924.

At eighteen, she was given unusually extensive control over a modern-dance group that was substantially independent of the opera and operetta ensemble. It has been described as one of the first modern-dance companies attached to a German theatre but not routinely subordinated to opera production.

Members and collaborators included:

- **Aenne Grünert**, an experienced Wigman dancer;
- **Tina Flade**, Skoronel’s assistant;
- **Dr. Rosenberg**, house composer for the dance group;
- **Heinz Porep**, designer of settings and figures;
- **Carletto Thieben** and a brother of Ellinor Tordis, who assisted with practical affairs.

### Letter of 11 September 1924

A surviving letter to her stepmother Luise is one of the clearest primary documents for Skoronel’s temperament, conditions of employment, finances, and health.

She wrote that everyone else at the theatre was a “slave,” whereas she was free and a king over her own little world. She claimed to have gained the director’s confidence after producing three difficult group dances in one week. Her salary was **500 marks**, which she understood as exceptional for a first appointment at her age.

She described a formidable workload:

- movement sketches and calculations;
- constructing group dances “absolutely mathematically”;
- developing technique and solos;
- specifying costumes;
- acquiring scores and instruments in Düsseldorf;
- teaching classes;
- creating pantomimes;
- preparing theatrical productions;
- finding accommodation and clothing herself.

The same letter records episodes of “pathological, leaden fatigue” and fear that her health would not sustain the work. This is contemporaneous evidence that significant illness or exhaustion preceded her death by several years.

### Financial conduct

A separate quoted passage has her telling her stepmother that she needed eighty marks to have her room painted and could charge it to the theatre, adding that one could “cheat nicely” there.

**Documented statement, not documented fraud:** The letter supports that Skoronel joked or boasted about improper expense billing. No account, investigation, repayment demand, lawsuit, or criminal proceeding was found. It would be unwarranted to convert the sentence into an established fraud.

### Choreography and reception

#### *Kriegsrhythmus* / *Kriegrhythmus* (1924)

A solo characterized by independent, cutting, throwing arm movements. Rudolf Lämmel described Balinese and Sinhalese associations while insisting that the work’s warrior quality was not militarist. The title and spelling vary in later sources.

#### *Das böse Quadrat* / *Das Quadrat* (1924)

Skoronel began with angular, fractured movement and then collapsed. After darkness, a rigid wall of dancers appeared and advanced with heavy waltz steps before sinking around her inert body.

The work established several recurring principles:

- opposition between solo body and group;
- geometric spatial construction;
- independent arm articulation;
- abrupt alternation of motion and stillness;
- the group as an impersonal force rather than a collection of characters.

#### Three-part dance pantomime / early *Tanzspiel*

A contemporary *Gladbecker Anzeiger* review credited Skoronel, Dr. Rosenberg, and Heinz Porep with a three-part dance pantomime premiered in Essen. The critic described it as a break even from Wigman-derived movement, reducing bodily form into dynamic linear components and “planimetric” forces.

### Dissolution

Despite strong reviews, the theatre dance group was dismantled before the end of the season because of financial difficulties. This was an institutional and economic failure, not evidence that Skoronel’s work had failed artistically.

## Darmstadt, 1925–1926

Skoronel next served for approximately six months as ballet mistress or dance director at the **Landestheater Darmstadt**, generally assigned to the 1925–26 season.

The accessible sources preserve far fewer details about this appointment. No firm evidence was located for a dismissal, dispute, or scandal. She subsequently moved to Berlin, where her collaboration with Berthe Trümpy became the center of her career.

## Berlin: School, Company, and Mature Work, 1926–1932

### The Trümpy-Skoronel school

In Berlin Skoronel joined **Berthe Trümpy**, a Swiss colleague from the Wigman circle, in directing a major school of modern artistic dance.

Trümpy supplied administrative stability, systematic teaching, and institutional respectability; Skoronel supplied much of the school’s experimental artistic profile. Sources describe the purpose-designed or specially arranged school building, associated with architect **Alfred Gellhorn**, as exceptionally modern.

The school trained amateurs, professionals, and “master pupils.” Its teaching emphasized:

- differentiated movement of arms, legs, torso, face, eyes, fingers, and breath;
- counter-rhythm between body parts;
- multiple simultaneous directional vectors;
- rapid changes of dynamic quality;
- individual movement inside polyphonic group structures;
- composition, rather than imitation of a teacher’s emotional style.

Known pupils and company members included:

- **Hanna Berger**;
- **Lisa Czóbel**;
- **Ilse Meudtner**;
- **Oda Schottmüller**;
- **Gertrud Wienecke**;
- Leah Bergstein;
- other dancers whose participation is preserved mainly in programs and photographs.

Dore Hoyer studied in the Trümpy school’s broader institutional environment, but the precise extent of direct instruction by Skoronel was not established.

### *Tanzspiel* / *Trilogisches Tanzspiel* (1926)

Skoronel prepared a detailed written scenario and arranged specially composed music. The work lacked a conventional protagonist even though she danced in it.

Its abstract figures included such entities as:

- “the Id”;
- “the Mirror Being”;
- “the Two Lengths”;
- “the Dancing Stage Wings.”

Lines and rows transformed into spirals, triangles, diagonals, circles, quartets, double duets, canons, and fugue-like patterns. Different sections of the body or different subsections of the group could move to contrasting rhythms. Skoronel specified minute details such as eye movement, facial expression, finger vibration, breathing, and whether an action was soft, mocking, violent, sweet, grotesque, or melancholy.

A notation of the choreography, prepared after Skoronel’s sketches and attributed to Berthe Trümpy, survives under the title *Trilogisches Tanzspiel*, probably from 1932.

### *Verkündigung* (1927)

In Trümpy’s medieval-Christian-inflected *Verkündigung*, Trümpy appeared as a restrained Madonna and Skoronel as an angular, abstract, partly “Oriental” angel. The juxtaposition illustrates the aesthetic division of labor between the two women.

### *Der gespaltene Mensch* (1927–1928)

Skoronel and Trümpy choreographed **Bruno Schönlank’s** spoken-chorus work *Der gespaltene Mensch* with the speech-and-movement chorus of the Berlin Volksbühne, directed by **Karl Vogt**.

The production was presented at the first German Dancers’ Congress in Magdeburg on **26 June 1927**, before an audience reported as approximately four thousand, and at the Berlin Volksbühne on **12 February 1928**.

The chorus was divided into antiphonal groups. Hammering speech, stamping, and cutting, thrusting, or pendular arm actions created a combined vocal and bodily mechanism. The work resisted the familiar mass-chorus narrative of inevitable progress “from night into light.” Recent scholarship interprets it as a significant experiment in choreographed collective voice.

### Magdeburg Dancers’ Congress, 1927

Skoronel’s solo and group presentations at Magdeburg brought her national recognition. Contemporary critics called her a twenty-year-old genius and placed her among the leading modern dancers.

Her former Wigman associates reportedly reacted with astonishment to the independence of her mature style. This reaction is recorded retrospectively by Trümpy and should be treated as testimony rather than a verbatim contemporary consensus.

### *Legende des weißen Waldes* (1927)

This four-scene dance fairy tale complicated Skoronel’s professed abstraction with a semi-pantomimic narrative.

Figures included:

- the Child of the White Forest;
- a Sorceress;
- creatures of the forest;
- twelve black-and-white knights;
- four water spirits;
- prophets;
- red flowers;
- a Demon.

The isolated Child awakens; knights and prophets fail to protect it; the Sorceress intervenes; and the Child ultimately attempts to dance across a black lake without sinking.

Lighting became choreographic material. At times spotlights revealed only dancers’ arms as branches; at other moments light changed without bodies being visible. Sudden illumination and darkness made it impossible for spectators—or symbolically the dancers—to possess a complete view of the dance.

### Berlin Volksbühne, 1927–1928

The Trümpy-Skoronel ensemble worked at the Volksbühne during 1927 and again in 1928. Skoronel supervised choreography involving adult workers, children, young people, and dramatic groups.

This work placed her inside the culture of worker choirs and Social Democratic performance institutions. It did not necessarily mean that she was an SPD member.

### *Erweckung der Massen* / *Erweckung der Masse* (1928)

The title appears in singular and plural forms. It was performed as part of a dance matinée and continued Skoronel’s investigation of mass movement. Documentation is sufficient to establish the work but not to reconstruct a definitive full score or cast.

### Essen Dancers’ Congress, 1928

A well-known congress photograph shows Skoronel with leading dance figures, wearing a knee-length white dress and open coat, resting an arm on pianist **Alfred Schlee**, leaning against critic **Fritz Böhme**, and extending one foot in a deliberately dancerly pose.

The image conveys her integration into the modern-dance network while also contributing to the later mythology of her irreverence.

### Ascona, 1928

A Swiss cultural-research document places Skoronel in **Ascona in 1928**. The purpose and duration of the visit were not established from the accessible source. It may have been professional or connected to the wider modern-dance and body-culture milieu, but further inference would be speculative.

### Free companies and touring

Skoronel twice organized companies from advanced pupils. The *Neue Deutsche Biographie* reports successful tours that helped establish the Trümpy school’s reputation. The exact itineraries and complete programs were not reconstructed from the sources available online.

### Munich Dancers’ Congress, 1930

Skoronel participated in the third German Dancers’ Congress in Munich in 1930. She argued that the public needed systematic education and advocated collaboration between dancers and practitioners in other arts to maintain artistic quality.

American critic John Martin noted the Munich festival in *The New York Times*, but the full article was not accessible in the consulted sources.

## Artistic and Intellectual Development

### From Ausdruckstanz to “abstract dance”

Skoronel emerged from Laban’s and Wigman’s modern-dance systems but resisted being reduced to expressionism.

Her terminology included:

- **abstrakter Tanz** — abstract dance;
- **absoluter Tanz** — absolute dance;
- **formaler Tanzstil** — formal dance style;
- **Vielstrahligkeit** — multiple rays or vectors;
- **neue Polyphonie** — new polyphony;
- **Mechanisierung** — mechanization.

### Polycentric body

A central innovation was treating limbs as partly independent rhythmic centers. Arms did not have to follow the torso’s momentum; they could strike, cut, swing, or oppose the rest of the body. Legs, torso, face, eyes, fingers, and breathing could participate in different rhythmic layers.

A contemporary critic likened the broken, angled configuration of her body to a hieroglyph.

This anticipates later concepts of bodily isolation and polycentrism, although claims that she directly originated later jazz-dance technique would require a demonstrable line of transmission that has not been established.

### Group composition

For Skoronel, group dance offered the richest opportunity to combine movement categories. She created:

- canonic and fugal structures;
- simultaneous opposing rhythms;
- geometric transformations;
- independent bodily and spatial lines;
- alternation between leaderless order and conflict between soloist and mass.

Her 1924 letter confirms that she consciously used sketches and calculations and regarded her ensemble compositions as mathematically built, while denying that they were merely intellectual exercises.

### Abstraction and expression

Her theory contains a genuine tension.

In one formulation, absolute dance abolished the distinction between form and content and sought a superhuman or cosmic ecstasy in abstraction. In an unpublished 1932 manuscript she described the body as an instrument stripped of ordinary human features, moving in “pure harmony” without content or expression.

Yet works such as *Das Quadrat* and *Legende des weißen Waldes* plainly carried emotional, dramatic, and narrative force. She also assigned qualities such as tenderness, mockery, violence, sweetness, melancholy, and grotesquerie to movement.

The record therefore supports neither a purely cold formalism nor conventional emotional expressionism. Her work repeatedly used formal abstraction to intensify affect.

### Music, voice, and silence

Skoronel did not simply illustrate music. She used:

- specially commissioned or selected scores;
- conflicting rhythmic layers;
- periods of silence;
- speech chorus;
- stamping and percussive action;
- musical analogies such as fugue, counterpoint, crescendo, and syncopation.

A posthumous text by Adda Heynssen specifically addressed Skoronel’s relationship to music.

### Ballet

Skoronel regarded Russian ballet as the product of a specific historical culture and considered conventional ballet in Germany artistically dead. She also criticized efforts to renew dance through generalized “healthy sensuality” and pantomime.

Her opposition was to ballet as a dominant compositional system, not proof that she lacked exposure to elementary ballet training.

### Religion and metaphysics

Christian imagery appeared in Trümpy-associated works such as *Verkündigung*, while Skoronel’s own writing invoked cosmic infinity and superhuman ecstasy. No evidence establishes formal religious observance, conversion, or church membership.

Her three-month Loheland stay exposed her to anthroposophically influenced culture, but the evidence does not justify calling her an anthroposophist.

### Politics

Documented political contexts include:

- descent from Menshevik leader Pavel Axelrod;
- work with the Berlin Volksbühne;
- choreography for worker, youth, and speech choruses;
- mass-dance works such as *Der gespaltene Mensch* and *Erweckung der Massen*;
- coverage in the Social Democratic newspaper *Vorwärts*.

No source consulted establishes membership in the SPD, KPD, Menshevik movement, or another political party. Her interest in collectivities was also aesthetic and technical; it cannot automatically be read as party doctrine.

She died before the Nazi seizure of power. Claims about how she might have responded to National Socialism are counterfactual.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Berthe Trümpy

**Documented fact:** Trümpy was Skoronel’s closest sustained professional collaborator, friend, school co-director, and an important custodian of her legacy. Their complementary abilities—Trümpy’s administration and teaching, Skoronel’s composition and theory—made the Berlin institution possible.

After Skoronel’s death, Trümpy preserved programs, texts, photographs, biographical notes, and choreographic material, including the notation of *Trilogisches Tanzspiel*.

### Mary Wigman

Teacher, ensemble leader, and early sponsor. Wigman and Palucca recommended Skoronel for the Oberhausen appointment. Skoronel later published analyses titled *Mary Wigmans Führertum* and *Mary Wigmans Kompositionsstil*.

Their relationship combined indebtedness, rivalry with the teacher’s aesthetic, and Skoronel’s need to establish an independent formal system.

### Gret Palucca

Fellow member of the Wigman circle and co-sponsor of the Oberhausen recommendation. Skoronel’s technique and Palucca’s movement language diverged, but both became prominent representatives of the generation after Wigman.

### Suzanne Perrottet and Katja Wulff

Principal Zürich teachers. They supplied the early Laban-derived foundation that Skoronel later revised.

### Rudolf von Laban

Limited direct teacher, major theoretical predecessor, and later subject of Skoronel’s writing. She valued his analysis of movement but believed his categories did not by themselves produce adequate compositional syntax.

### Yvonne Georgi

Fellow Wigman student and Dresden roommate. Georgi later became a major source for stories concerning Skoronel’s poverty, alcohol use, theft of clothing, instability, and exceptional talent.

### Aenne Grünert and Tina Flade

Members of the 1924 theatre group. Grünert’s Wigman experience allowed Skoronel to measure her new technique against advanced Wigman practice; Flade served as assistant.

### Karl Vogt and Bruno Schönlank

Vogt directed the Volksbühne speech-and-movement chorus; Schönlank authored *Der gespaltene Mensch*. Their collaboration with Skoronel and Trümpy brought modern dance into organized choral speech and worker culture.

### Pupils

- **Hanna Berger:** later dancer, choreographer, communist, and resistance figure.
- **Lisa Czóbel:** later joined Kurt Jooss’s company and danced in the premiere of *Der grüne Tisch* after Skoronel’s death.
- **Ilse Meudtner:** began study with Trümpy and Skoronel in 1929 and danced in the chamber group.
- **Oda Schottmüller:** trained in the school and later combined dance and sculpture; executed in 1943 by the Nazi regime for her association with the resistance network called the Red Orchestra.
- **Gertrud Wienecke:** advanced pupil and member of Skoronel’s chamber group.
- **Leah Bergstein:** performed in Skoronel’s company before becoming important in the Palestinian and Israeli folk-dance movement.

These later political and artistic careers show the range of the school’s influence, but they do not prove that Skoronel shared every pupil’s later ideology.

### Visual artists and photographers

- **Georg Kolbe** made wash drawings of Skoronel’s poses between approximately 1920 and 1924.
- **Charlotte Rudolph** photographed Skoronel, including a well-known jumping study around 1925.
- **Suse Byk**, **Robertson**, and **Steffi Brandl** photographed her or her ensemble.
- **Milly Steger** made a portrait bust, now lost.
- **Julie Wolfthorn** painted her portrait, also lost.
- **Fritz Cremer** took a cast of Skoronel’s right hand on her deathbed. A portrait or death mask attributed to him is lost, but the painted plaster hand survives in the Deutsches Tanzarchiv Köln.

## Health, Alcohol, Psychological Descriptions, and Death

### Chronic illness

The strongest evidence is Skoronel’s own 1924 letter describing pathological fatigue, weakness, and fear that her health would fail under professional demands.

The Deutsches Tanzarchiv catalogue also notes illness and “lability” in Trümpy’s biographical material.

### Psychological characterization

In 1973 Mary Wigman and Yvonne Georgi reportedly agreed that Skoronel was charming, extraordinarily gifted, and “genial,” but also ill, not accountable for her actions, and unable to fit into a community because she disrupted collective life.

**Reported retrospective opinion:** This is testimony about behavior, not a clinical diagnosis. Terms such as “krank” and “unzurechnungsfähig” were informal retrospective judgments. No psychiatric diagnosis, hospitalization record, guardianship proceeding, or medical case file was located.

### Alcohol

**Reported allegation:** Georgi’s account that Skoronel drank eau de cologne when alcohol was unavailable suggests hazardous drinking. Secondary summaries sometimes say alcohol abuse may have complicated the illness that killed her.

No medical record consulted identifies alcoholism, poisoning, cirrhosis, or alcohol as a contributory cause of death. The allegation belongs in her dossier but should not be stated as a medically established cause.

### Death

**Documented fact:** Skoronel died in Berlin on 24 March 1932, aged twenty-five.

Reference works state that she died of a **blood disease**. The exact diagnosis—leukemia, aplastic anemia, another hematological disorder, or a complication—is not provided in the authoritative biographical sources consulted.

“Blood disease” is therefore the strongest established description, but it is nonspecific. Claims that alcohol caused or materially accelerated her death remain unresolved.

She was buried in the Wilmersdorf Cemetery in Berlin. A death notice dated 24 March 1932 survives in the Deutsches Literaturarchiv Marbach.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Reputation Narratives

### “The untamable genius”

**Story:** Skoronel was a natural genius whom no teacher or institution could control.

**Origins and circulation:** The narrative appears in memories by Wigman-circle dancers, Trümpy’s biographical materials, memorial texts, and Frank-Manuel Peter’s archive-based biography.

**Supporting evidence:** Her appointment at eighteen, contemporary critical acclaim, rapid composition, distinctive technique, and letters expressing unusual confidence.

**Limitations:** “Genius” is an evaluative category. Later remembrance was shaped by her early death and the absence of a long, uneven career.

**Effect:** It made Skoronel a symbol of unrealized Weimar modernism.

### The window-jump

**Story:** Bored in a Wigman class, Skoronel asked what the point was, jumped through an open window into the garden, and disappeared.

**Evidence:** Retrospective dance-world recollection; no contemporary diary or school record located.

**Status:** **Rumor or myth grounded in testimony.**

**Effect:** It dramatizes her supposed precocity and rejection of authority.

### The stolen dress

**Story:** After Aenne Grünert reported her dress missing, Georgi found Skoronel wearing it and heard her justify the act because it looked good on her.

**Evidence:** Later testimony.

**Status:** **Reported allegation**, not adjudicated fact.

**Effect:** Used to portray Skoronel as charming but indifferent to ordinary rules.

### The perfume-drinking dancer

**Story:** During inflation Skoronel drank Georgi’s eau de cologne when alcohol was unavailable.

**Evidence:** Georgi’s recollection.

**Status:** **Reported allegation.**

**Effect:** It has become the principal basis for modern suggestions of alcoholism and sometimes for speculation about her death, although the latter connection is not medically documented.

### The school flag costume and bread crusts

**Story:** After the loss of family money, Skoronel dressed herself with safety pins and her father’s school flag and survived on bread crusts left by fellow dancers.

**Evidence:** Later recollection reproduced by the dance archive.

**Status:** **Rumor or myth with a plausible basis in documented family financial instability.**

**Effect:** The stories cast her as an impoverished bohemian whose invention arose from deprivation.

### Revolutionary blood

**Story:** Her rebelliousness was inherited from her grandfather Pavel Axelrod.

**Evidence:** The family relationship is documented; hereditary interpretation is not.

**Status:** **Biographical mythology.**

**Effect:** It connects artistic revolt to Russian revolutionary ancestry and makes personality appear genealogically predetermined.

### “The greatest lost talent after Wigman”

**Story:** Skoronel was the outstanding talent of the generation following Mary Wigman and would have transformed modern dance had she lived.

**Evidence:** Strong contemporary reviews, original theoretical writings, and later dance historians’ assessments.

**Status:** **Critical interpretation and counterfactual reputation**, not falsifiable fact.

**Effect:** Her death before 1933 has made her a figure for a possible modern dance history interrupted before Nazism and exile reorganized the field.

## Works by Vera Skoronel

### Choreographies and performance works

Dating and titles sometimes vary between programs, archives, and later studies.

- *Kriegsrhythmus* or *Kriegrhythmus* (1924) — solo.
- *Das böse Quadrat* / *Das Quadrat* (1924) — solo-and-group choreography.
- Three-part dance pantomime or early form of *Tanzspiel* (1924–1926) — made with music by Dr. Rosenberg and designs by Heinz Porep; exact relation to the later *Tanzspiel* requires archival clarification.
- *Tanzspiel* (1926) — abstract ensemble work with detailed scenario and specially prepared music.
- *Trilogisches Tanzspiel* — title of the choreography as posthumously notated by Berthe Trümpy from Skoronel’s sketches, probably 1932.
- *Verkündigung* (1927) — a Trümpy-associated work in which Skoronel performed the angel; authorship should not be assigned solely to Skoronel.
- *Der gespaltene Mensch* (1927; Berlin performance 1928) — movement and speech-chorus production by Skoronel and Berthe Trümpy, using Bruno Schönlank’s text and Karl Vogt’s Volksbühne chorus.
- *Legende des weißen Waldes* (1927) — dance fairy tale in four scenes.
- *Erweckung der Masse* / *Erweckung der Massen* (1927 or 1928) — group work presented in a dance matinée.
- *Auferstehung* — listed in the Deutsches Tanzarchiv holdings; date and authorship details require consultation of the physical archive.
- *Die Former* — described in contemporary congress reception as a large “dance symphony”; its relationship to other titled works is not fully clear from accessible documentation.
- Additional solo, group, comic, lyrical, ballet-parody, and “Apache dance” items appear in contemporary reviews and programs but cannot all be securely titled or dated from online sources.

## Death and Immediate Aftermath

Skoronel’s death abruptly ended the Trümpy-Skoronel partnership in March 1932. Memorial programs characterized her work as a decisive movement toward an objectified, teachable dance governed by strict formal laws.

The memorial interpretation emphasized that her geometric and mechanical forms were not empty intellectual ornaments: they were supposed to be internally experienced and emotionally adequate to the dancer.

Fritz Cremer’s deathbed hand cast transformed a bodily fragment into a relic of an artist whose principal medium could not be preserved intact. The subsequent disappearance of several portraits intensified the sense of a fragmentary legacy.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Immediate reputation

Contemporaries remembered:

- unusually early authority;
- a forceful solo presence;
- mathematical group composition;
- angular, independent arm action;
- rapid rhythmic changes;
- an unstable or disruptive personality;
- a career cut short before consolidation.

### Historical position

Later scholars place her between Ausdruckstanz and later forms of formal, objectified, and postmodern dance. Her work challenged the assumption that German modern dance was defined solely by individual emotional expression.

Her importance lies particularly in:

- polycentric and isolated limb movement;
- group polyphony;
- fugue-like composition;
- treatment of light as movement;
- integration of spoken chorus and dance;
- theoretical insistence on abstraction;
- the body conceived as an instrument rather than a psychological portrait.

### Limits of influence claims

Direct stylistic descent is difficult to prove. Many works survive only in photographs, programs, descriptions, and partial notations. Similar later techniques may represent independent development rather than transmission from Skoronel.

Her influence is most securely documented through:

- students who continued professional careers;
- the reputation of the Trümpy school;
- preserved writings and teaching concepts;
- later dance scholarship;
- explicit acts of commemoration.

### Tanztheater Skoronel

In 1984 Judith Kuckart and collaborators founded **Tanztheater Skoronel** in West Berlin, explicitly naming it after Vera. The company toured during the 1980s and 1990s and was revived decades later.

The company did not simply reconstruct Skoronel’s repertory. Its name functioned as an act of recovery and identification with a neglected woman of Weimar modern dance.

### Exhibitions and recent reinterpretation

The 2021 Georg Kolbe Museum exhibition *Der absolute Tanz: Tänzerinnen der Weimarer Republik* placed Skoronel among twelve women central to Weimar dance. It emphasized the interaction of choreography, photography, sculpture, costume, and visual modernism.

Recent scholarship has examined:

- her relationship with Charlotte Rudolph’s photography;
- the voice-body mechanics of *Der gespaltene Mensch*;
- workers’ choruses and collective rhythm;
- how archival photography substitutes for lost movement;
- her place between human expression and posthuman or mechanized bodies.

A 2025 musical/performance project by LIGNA and Felix Kubin included a piece titled “Raumstunde Vera Skoronel,” demonstrating continued symbolic use of her name in work concerning collective movement and machine culture.

## Atlas Connections

Only source-supported intersections with names in the supplied atlas roster are listed. Shared nationality, contemporaneity, or residence alone was not counted.

- **Rudolf Laban — documented fact.** Skoronel received several lessons from Laban at approximately thirteen and trained at the Zürich school conducted by his associates. His spatial and movement analysis was a principal foundation she later criticized and extended. She wrote an essay titled “Laban” and an archival fiftieth-birthday essay about him.

- **Suzanne Perrottet — documented fact.** Principal teacher at the Zürich Laban school, 1919–1921. Perrottet gave Skoronel the basic movement education from which her later formal technique developed.

- **Katja Wulff — documented fact.** Co-teacher with Perrottet in Zürich, 1919–1921. Sources alternatively spell her first name Katja or Käthe.

- **Mary Wigman — documented fact.** Teacher and company leader in Dresden, 1922–1924; later recommended Skoronel for the Oberhausen appointment. Skoronel subsequently analyzed Wigman’s leadership and compositional style in print. Their aesthetic relationship combined formation, departure, and implied rivalry.

- **Gret Palucca — documented fact.** Fellow member of the Wigman circle and co-sponsor of Skoronel’s appointment in Oberhausen. Both attended major dancers’ congresses and became leading figures of Wigman’s successor generation.

- **Yvonne Georgi — documented fact.** Fellow Wigman pupil and Skoronel’s Dresden roommate. Georgi later circulated several of the principal anecdotes about Skoronel’s behavior, poverty, and drinking.

- **Hanya Holm — documented fact.** Member of the same early Wigman school and performance network in Dresden. Sources list Holm and Skoronel among Wigman’s first important pupils; no close private friendship has been established.

- **Berthe Trümpy — documented fact.** Although not independently named elsewhere in the roster excerpt as a separate entry, she is indispensable to Skoronel’s life: friend, co-director, collaborator, and posthumous custodian. She is included here because the atlas instruction asks for genuine crossings and her connection is the most substantial one in the record.

- **Oda Schottmüller — documented fact.** Studied at the Trümpy-Skoronel school in Berlin and belonged to the institutional circle shaped by Skoronel. Schottmüller’s later anti-Nazi resistance activity occurred after Skoronel’s death and should not be projected backward onto her teacher.

- **Dore Hoyer — reported institutional connection.** Hoyer trained within the Trümpy school environment associated with Skoronel. The extent of direct teacher-pupil contact with Skoronel is not firmly established in the sources consulted.

- **Valeska Gert — documented shared professional scene.** Both participated in the Weimar modern-dance world and are represented in congress and exhibition records. The sources consulted support shared institutions and professional gatherings, but not a close friendship or collaboration.

- **Isadora Duncan — documented indirect influence.** Rudolf Lämmel explicitly recalled Duncan’s 1902 Zürich performance as a major influence on his interest in modern dance, which in turn shaped Vera’s early environment. No direct meeting between Duncan and Vera was established.

- **Rudolf Steiner — documented institutional influence, not a personal meeting.** Skoronel spent three months at the anthroposophically influenced Loheland school in 1921. No direct encounter with Steiner or formal adherence to Anthroposophy was found.

- **Albert Einstein — no direct connection established.** Rudolf Lämmel reportedly studied alongside Einstein and later wrote about relativity. No source consulted documents a meeting, correspondence, collaboration, or acknowledged influence between Einstein and Vera herself. The family association was therefore not treated as a personal atlas connection.
