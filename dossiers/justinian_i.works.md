## Works by Justinian I

### Legal projects and enacted texts

- **528–529:** *Codex Iustinianus* (first edition). An official compilation of imperial constitutions prepared by a commission under Justinian and promulgated in 529; this edition no longer survives independently because the revised Code superseded it. It is an imperial project rather than Justinian’s personal composition.

- **530–533:** *Digesta* or *Pandectae*. A fifty-book compilation of extracts from classical Roman jurists, commissioned by Justinian and produced principally under Tribonian’s direction. Justinian made it law, but the constituent writings belong to earlier jurists, so it resists attribution to him as an author.

- **533:** *Institutiones*. A four-book protocol for legal education that simultaneously possessed statutory force, composed by Tribonian, Theophilus, and Dorotheus under Justinian’s commission. Its speaking “we” is imperial, not evidence that Justinian personally wrote every sentence.

- **534:** *Codex repetitae praelectionis*. The surviving twelve-book revision of the *Codex Iustinianus*, incorporating Justinian’s intervening legislation and replacing the lost 529 edition. It is both compilation and enacted law.

- **535–565:** *Novellae constitutiones*. The individual “new constitutions” issued after the revised Code, mostly in Greek and sometimes in Latin. Justinian never promulgated a single official book called the *Novellae*: the standard Schoell–Kroll Greek collection contains 168 numbered items, but only 159 come from Justinian’s reign and even that count includes texts whose status as imperial novels is debatable.

- **554:** *Pragmatica sanctio pro petitione Vigilii*. An imperial protocol for reorganizing government, property, taxation, and legal authority in reconquered Italy. Although transmitted separately and commonly named as an individual work, it belongs materially to Justinian’s continuing legislation.

- **527–565:** *Justinian’s imperial constitutions, edicts, rescripts, pragmatic sanctions, administrative forms, and official correspondence*. This is an open body of enacted and administrative texts rather than a closed authored book: items survive within the Code and Novels, conciliar acta, papal collections, inscriptions, and documentary fragments. Chancery drafting and collective government make personal authorship impossible to assign consistently.

### Theological manifestos, treatises, and letters

- **533:** *Edictum de fide* or *Expositio rectae fidei*. A doctrinal manifesto on the Trinity and Incarnation, preserved as *Codex Iustinianus* 1.1.6. It acts simultaneously as confession of faith and imperial law, illustrating why Justinian’s theology cannot be cleanly separated from legislation.

- **536:** *Constitutio ad Menam* and the associated condemnation of Anthimus, Severus, Peter of Apamea, and Zoaras. An imperial-theological instrument addressed to Patriarch Menas and issued in connection with the Constantinopolitan synod; its exact division into letter, edict, and conciliar dossier varies between editions.

- **c. 536–543:** *Tractatus contra Monophysitas*. A substantial Christological polemic, probably addressed to Alexandrian monks, defending Chalcedonian doctrine against Severian Miaphysitism. It is one of the three principal dogmatic writings edited by Eduard Schwartz, although composition by imperial theological advisers cannot be excluded.

- **Before c. 550:** *Epistula dogmatica ad Zoilum*. A doctrinal letter to Zoilus, patriarch of Alexandria, preserved only fragmentarily. Its surviving state prevents certainty about its original extent and relationship to the anti-Monophysite treatise.

- **543:** *Edict against Origen* or *Epistula ad Menam contra Origenem*. A theological-legal manifesto addressed to Menas, combining an extensive refutation of doctrines attributed to Origen with anathemas. The separate set of fifteen anti-Origenist propositions associated with the controversies of 553 has a disputed textual and conciliar relationship to Justinian’s edict and should not silently be treated as the same document.

- **543 or 544:** *First edict against the Three Chapters*. An imperial condemnation of Theodore of Mopsuestia, certain writings of Theodoret of Cyrrhus, and the letter attributed to Ibas of Edessa. The complete text is lost and must be reconstructed from fragments and hostile responses, so neither its exact title nor its full contents are secure.

- **c. 545–550:** *Letter to defenders of the Three Chapters*. A doctrinal address to unnamed opponents—sometimes identified conjecturally as Scythian monks—defending the proposed condemnation. Its recipients and precise date remain uncertain.

- **551:** *Confessio rectae fidei adversus tria capitula*. A long edict and confession of faith containing thirteen anathemas against the Three Chapters. It is at once treatise, manifesto, and coercive imperial act, not merely a description of Justinian’s beliefs.

- **553:** *Letter or address to the bishops of the Second Council of Constantinople concerning Origenist monks*. An imperial summons directing the assembled bishops to judge teachings circulating among Palestinian monks. Its documentary boundaries and relation to the anti-Origenist anathemas remain disputed.

- **553:** *Address to the Second Council of Constantinople concerning the Three Chapters*. A theological and procedural manifesto submitted to the council, arguing for condemnation of Antiochene writings and authors. Like other imperial conciliar documents, it was probably produced with advisers and chancery assistance.

- **553:** *Order concerning Pope Vigilius and the ecclesiastical diptychs*. An operative letter asking the council to remove Vigilius’s name from communion lists while preserving communion with Rome. It is a text that performs an ecclesiastical and political action rather than a freestanding theological “work.”

- **Before 565:** *Treatise against John Philoponus*. A polemic against the theologian’s Tritheist teaching, known principally through references and fragments. Much or all of the work is lost, and its original title and extent are unknown.

- **564–565:** *Edictum de aphthartodocetismo*. Justinian’s final doctrinal edict, reported as requiring assent to the incorruptibility of Christ’s body before the Resurrection. The text itself is lost, and both its precise theology and the claim that Justinian adopted full Aphthartodocetism remain disputed.

- **527–565:** *Letters to Hormisdas, John II, Agapetus I, Vigilius, bishops, monks, and imperial officials*. A dispersed corpus of doctrinal and administrative correspondence preserved in such collections as the *Collectio Avellana*, conciliar acta, the *Codex*, and later canonical dossiers. These are chancery-mediated imperial acts; treating every letter as an independently authored literary work would misrepresent how they were made.

### Liturgical creation of disputed authorship

- **Before the mid-sixth century; traditionally associated with 535–536:** *Ho Monogenēs Huios* (*Only-Begotten Son*). A short Christological hymn incorporated into the Byzantine liturgy and traditionally credited to Justinian, but also attributed to Severus of Antioch or to an unidentified author. Justinian may have composed it, commissioned it, revised it, or merely ordered its liturgical use; the evidence does not permit a secure choice.

### Architecture, infrastructure, and urban Gesamtkunstwerk

- **527–565:** *The Justinianic building programme*. Procopius’s six-book *Buildings* attributes no fewer than 1,128 churches, palaces, hospitals, monasteries, fortifications, bridges, roads, aqueducts, cisterns, harbours, and restored cities to Justinian across the empire. This is best understood as an imperial body of work or Gesamtkunstwerk of rule—not 1,128 objects personally designed by Justinian: Procopius wrote panegyric, sometimes credits Justinian with repairs or earlier projects, omits other works, and cannot always be reconciled with archaeology.

- **532–537; dome rebuilt 558–562:** *Hagia Sophia, Constantinople*. A church, ceremonial environment, and imperial-theological Gesamtkunstwerk commissioned by Justinian after the Nika revolt and designed by Anthemius of Tralles and Isidore of Miletus; the younger Isidore directed the dome’s reconstruction. Justinian is its patron and political maker, not its sole architect or artisan.

- **After 532:** *Hagia Eirene, Constantinople*. A rebuilt church conventionally assigned to Justinian’s post-Nika reconstruction. Later rebuilding, especially after the eighth-century earthquake, makes the surviving fabric an incomplete witness to his project.

- **c. 527–536:** *Church of Saints Sergius and Bacchus, Constantinople*. A centrally planned domed church made under Justinian and Theodora’s patronage, probably adjoining their earlier palace of Hormisdas. Its architect is not securely known, and the familiar attribution to the designers of Hagia Sophia is conjectural.

- **536–550:** *Church of the Holy Apostles, Constantinople*. Justinian’s replacement of Constantine’s apostolic church, designed by Anthemius and Isidore according to Procopius. The building was demolished after the Ottoman conquest, so it survives only through texts, images, and indirect architectural comparison.

- **Sixth century, principally Justinian’s reign:** *Great Palace and Augustaion reconstruction*. A connected project of halls, porticoes, gates, baths, senate house, ceremonial space, and imperial approaches rebuilt after the Nika destruction. Because successive emperors repeatedly altered the palace complex, its individual Justinianic components cannot all be securely isolated.

- **543:** *Column of Justinian, Augustaion*. A monumental column supporting an equestrian imperial statue and functioning as an urban manifesto of victory. It is known through written and visual testimony but was dismantled in the Ottoman period.

- **532–542:** *Basilica Cistern, Constantinople*. A vast covered water reservoir conventionally identified with the cistern Procopius says Justinian constructed beneath the “Basilica.” Its reused columns and capitals make it a composite infrastructural object rather than a wholly new architectural composition.

- **c. 540s–550s:** *Church of the Theotokos at the Source, Constantinople*. A sanctuary and hydraulic complex credited by Procopius to Justinian. Later destruction and rebuilding make the precise form and date of the Justinianic work uncertain.

- **c. 530s–550s:** *Hebdomon churches of Saint John the Baptist and Saint John the Evangelist*. Sanctuaries outside Constantinople that Procopius assigns to Justinian’s patronage. Their remains and textual identifications are incomplete, so their architectural histories cannot be reconstructed with confidence.

- **c. 530s:** *Justinianianae and associated harbour works on the Golden Horn*. A group of harbour, landing, and commercial improvements credited to Justinian. The named facilities resist treatment as one building because Procopius describes an infrastructural and administrative ensemble.

- **527–565:** *Fortification system of the Balkans*. Hundreds of new or restored forts, walls, towers, and refuges catalogued in Book IV of Procopius’s *Buildings*. The catalogue’s names, repetitions, and uncertain modern identifications prevent a reliable conversion into hundreds of separately titled works.

- **527–565:** *Fortification and urban works at Dara*. Repairs and extensions to walls, waterworks, channels, and defensive structures in the frontier city. Procopius’s claim of Justinianic making includes both new construction and reconstruction of the earlier Anastasian fortress.

- **c. 530:** *Zenobia-Halabiye fortifications and urban works*. A fortified Euphrates settlement extensively rebuilt under Justinian. Surviving masonry supports major sixth-century intervention, but the division between Justinianic, earlier Roman, and later work remains debated.

- **527–565:** *Euphrates flood-control and river-engineering works at Edessa*. Dikes, channels, and related works described by Procopius as Justinian’s response to destructive flooding. They constitute an infrastructural project rather than a single readily bounded object.

- **c. 559–562:** *Bridge over the Sangarius*. A monumental stone bridge in Bithynia commissioned by Justinian, possibly replacing a failed canal or river-diversion project. Its construction belongs to imperial engineering, while its individual designer is unknown.

- **c. 535–565:** *Justinian’s Long Walls and frontier works in North Africa*. A network of town walls, forts, and defensive restorations following the imperial reconquest. Attribution is shared among the emperor, generals, governors, engineers, soldiers, and local labour; Procopius’s imperial catalogue suppresses much of that collective making.

- **c. 535–565:** *Churches and civic rebuilding in reconquered North Africa*. A regional patronage programme accompanying Byzantine administration, including sanctuaries and urban repairs. No surviving source supplies a complete, uncontested catalogue, and attribution to Justinian often means construction during his reign rather than demonstrable personal commission.

- **c. 535–565:** *Italian reconstruction and fortification programme*. Walls, public works, and ecclesiastical patronage undertaken during and after the Gothic War. Famous contemporary monuments such as San Vitale in Ravenna display Justinianic imperial imagery but were substantially made by bishops and local patrons, so they should not simply be relabelled “works by Justinian.”

- **c. 540s–560s:** *Monastery of the God-trodden Mount Sinai*, later called Saint Catherine’s. A fortified monastic complex and basilica commissioned in Justinian’s reign at Sinai. Its conventional attribution rests on literary and epigraphic evidence, but the emperor was patron rather than architect or builder.

- **c. 543:** *Nea Church of the Theotokos, Jerusalem*. A vast basilica whose completion and dedication are associated with Justinian, although Cyril of Scythopolis indicates that construction began under Patriarch Elias decades earlier. It is therefore a completed and transformed earlier project, not an uncontested creation ex nihilo by Justinian.

- **c. 535–550:** *Justinianiana Prima*. A newly monumentalized episcopal and administrative city near Justinian’s birthplace, generally identified with Caričin Grad in Serbia. The identification is strong but not absolutely uncontested, and the city’s churches, streets, aqueduct, and fortifications form an urban project rather than one object.

- **527–565:** *Restored and newly founded cities bearing Justinian’s name*. Procopius records settlements renamed or refounded as Justinianopolis, Justiniana, or related forms. Renaming, legal elevation, fortification, and physical rebuilding do not always coincide, so some are political manifestations rather than wholly made cities.

### Monetary and representational manifestations

- **538–539 onward:** *Dated imperial coinage reform*. Justinian’s bronze issues began displaying regnal years and enlarged denomination marks, creating a new public protocol of value, chronology, and imperial authority. The types were products of imperial mints and officials rather than objects personally designed or struck by Justinian.

- **c. 542 onward:** *Frontal portrait coinage*. Gold and bronze types increasingly presented Justinian frontally with military and Christian attributes. These are serial manifestations of the imperial image, not unique authored artworks; individual dies were made anonymously across multiple mints.
