## Works by Cosimo di Giovanni de' Medici

### Letters and documentary texts

- **c. 1410s–1464:** *Surviving correspondence*. This is an open, dispersed body of mercantile, diplomatic, political, familial, and patronage letters rather than a book with a fixed title or contents; many letters were written by secretaries, and the surviving corpus is only a fraction of what once existed. Its principal archival home is the *Mediceo avanti il Principato*, not a collected-works edition ([Archivio di Stato di Firenze](https://archiviodistatofirenze.cultura.gov.it/asfi/strumenti/inventari-on-line/inventari-m/mediceo-avanti-il-principato); Kent, pp. 15–20).

- **Various dates before 1464:** *Mercantile and household ricordi*. Account entries, memoranda, instructions, and fragmentary records associated with Cosimo survive among the Medici family papers. They resist classification as individually authored works: some are autograph, some dictated, and others are products of the Medici banking and household apparatus ([Archivio di Stato di Firenze, inventory of the Mediceo avanti il Principato](https://archiviodistatofirenze.cultura.gov.it/asfi/fileadmin/risorse/allegati_inventari_on_line/s13_inventario.pdf)).

### Architectural projects

- **c. 1420s:** *Villa del Trebbio*. A project, attributed to Michelozzo, that converted and enlarged the Medici family’s fortified Mugello residence; it is properly Cosimo’s commission rather than his architectural authorship, and the exact chronology and division between inherited fabric and his intervention remain uncertain (Kent, pp. 165–70; [Medici Villas and Gardens in Tuscany, UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/175/)).

- **c. 1420–1427 and later:** *Church and convent of San Francesco at Bosco ai Frati*. Cosimo financed a long reconstruction customarily assigned to Michelozzo, although the surviving complex is the result of multiple campaigns and later alteration; the project therefore resists being treated as a single, securely dated object ([Italian Ministry of Culture catalogue](https://catalogo.beniculturali.it/Agent/018f1cc1a572c004c0d571f72e78d0b8); Kent, pp. 171–79).

- **1433–1434:** *Library at San Giorgio Maggiore, Venice*. During and after his exile Cosimo furnished the Venetian Benedictine house with books and a purpose-built library attributed to Michelozzo. The room no longer survives in its original form, so this obra is recoverable chiefly as a documented architectural and bibliographical project (Kent, pp. 86–87, 174–75; de la Mare, pp. 115–18).

- **From 1429; principally 1442–1464:** *Basilica of San Lorenzo*. Cosimo and his brother Lorenzo inherited their father Giovanni’s involvement, commissioned the completion and ornament of Brunelleschi’s Old Sacristy, and in 1442 assumed responsibility for the church’s unfinished rebuilding. This is a multigenerational, incomplete project rather than an exclusively Cosimian building ([Palazzo Medici Riccardi Mediateca](https://mediateca.palazzomediciriccardi.it/Personaggi/cosimo-il-vecchio-detto-cosimo-di-giovanni-de-medici/); Elam, pp. 157–80; Kent, pp. 179–97).

- **c. 1434–1445:** *Novitiate and Chapel of Saints Cosmas and Damian, Santa Croce*. Michelozzo designed this Franciscan complex for Cosimo; subsequent rebuilding and dispersal of its furnishings mean that the surviving architecture and paintings are fragments of the original ensemble ([Uffizi Galleries](https://www.uffizi.it/en/online-exhibitions/st-francis); Kent, pp. 243–51).

- **1436–c. 1452:** *Church and convent of San Marco*. Cosimo commissioned Michelozzo’s rebuilding and financed the complex, including its library and much of its painted and liturgical equipment. Because architecture, fresco, panel painting, books, worship, and Cosimo’s own retreat cell were conceived together, it is best understood as a patronal Gesamtkunstwerk rather than as one building ([Museo di San Marco](https://cultura.gov.it/luogo/museo-di-san-marco); Kent, pp. 197–235).

- **From 1444; substantially complete by the late 1450s:** *Palazzo Medici in Via Larga*. Michelozzo’s palace was Cosimo’s principal domestic and dynastic project, but its fabric, decoration, and functions were created collaboratively with his sons and were repeatedly altered after his death ([Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/es/toah/ht/08/eustc.html); Kent, pp. 305–42).

- **Before 1451:** *Villa di Cafaggiolo*. Cosimo commissioned Michelozzo to transform the family’s Mugello castle into a country residence; later changes obscure the limits of the fifteenth-century campaign ([Italian Ministry of Culture catalogue](https://catalogo.beniculturali.it/Agent/018f1cc1a572c004c0d571f72e78d0b8); Kent, pp. 165–71).

- **c. 1453–1459:** *Villa di Careggi*. The remodeling conventionally attributed to Michelozzo created Cosimo’s principal suburban residence and later setting for Marsilio Ficino’s activity. Its patronage was familial, however, and neither the date nor Michelozzo’s precise contribution is completely documented (Kent, pp. 342–55; [Medici Villas and Gardens in Tuscany, UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/175/)).

- **c. 1455–1459:** *Medici Bank palace in Milan*. Cosimo arranged for Michelozzo to remodel and decorate the Sforza-provided premises of the Milan branch; Vincenzo Foppa painted the now largely lost fresco decoration. It was simultaneously a commercial office, diplomatic instrument, and representational artwork (Kent, pp. 357–66).

- **1456–1464:** *Badia Fiesolana*. Cosimo financed an extensive Augustinian monastery comprising church, cloister, refectory, infirmary, meeting rooms, and library. No architect is securely documented—Michelozzo and Brunelleschi have both been proposed—and the project remained unfinished at Cosimo’s death ([Palazzo Medici Riccardi Mediateca](https://mediateca.palazzomediciriccardi.it/Personaggi/cosimo-il-vecchio-detto-cosimo-di-giovanni-de-medici/); Robinson, pp. 181–96; Kent, pp. 366–82).

- **No fixed date; before 1464:** *Hospice for pilgrims in Jerusalem*. Contemporary and early biographical testimony credits Cosimo with financing a hospice or restoration for Western pilgrims. The building’s precise identity, extent, and chronology are insufficiently secure for it to be treated as a firmly bounded architectural work (Jenkins, pp. 162–70; Kent, pp. 175–76).

- **No fixed date; before 1464:** *Collegio degli Italiani in Paris*. Cosimo reportedly financed repairs or rebuilding at the Italian college; the structure has disappeared and the evidence does not define a single design or construction campaign. It survives as a patronal project rather than an extant object (Jenkins, pp. 162–70; Kent, pp. 175–76).

### Commissioned visual and liturgical ensembles

- **From 1429:** *Sculptural decoration of the Old Sacristy, San Lorenzo*. Donatello and his workshop made the bronze doors, stucco reliefs, lunettes, and roundels under Medici patronage. Cosimo initially shared the undertaking with his brother Lorenzo, and the individual decisions cannot always be assigned to one brother ([Palazzo Medici Riccardi Mediateca](https://mediateca.palazzomediciriccardi.it/Personaggi/cosimo-il-vecchio-detto-cosimo-di-giovanni-de-medici/); Kent, pp. 179–97).

- **c. 1434–1437:** *Annalena Altarpiece*. Dale Kent argues that this Fra Angelico altarpiece was an early Cosimo commission, but its original setting, date, and patronage remain disputed; it should not be presented as securely documented (Kent, pp. 235–42; Harrold, p. 176 n. 505, [University of Warwick thesis](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/38150/1/WRAP_THESIS_Harrold_2007.pdf)).

- **c. 1438–1443:** *San Marco Altarpiece and predella*. Fra Angelico’s panel ensemble for the high altar joined Dominican devotion to the Medici saints Cosmas and Damian. It was commissioned by Cosimo and his brother Lorenzo, later dismantled, and now survives in separated components ([Uffizi Galleries](https://www.uffizi.it/en/online-exhibitions/miracolous-healings); Kent, pp. 213–28).

- **c. 1438–1446:** *San Marco fresco programme*. Fra Angelico and assistants painted the cloister, corridors, chapter spaces, and friars’ cells as part of Cosimo’s larger San Marco project. Although Cosimo financed the programme and had a cell reserved for his use, the degree to which he specified each image is unknown ([Museo di San Marco](https://cultura.gov.it/luogo/museo-di-san-marco); Kent, pp. 197–235).

- **Mid-1440s:** *Medici Altarpiece for the Santa Croce novitiate*. Fra Filippo Lippi’s altarpiece of the Virgin and Child with saints was commissioned by Cosimo for the chapel he had built; panels now in the Louvre originally belonged to this dispersed architectural-liturgical ensemble ([Uffizi Galleries](https://www.uffizi.it/en/online-exhibitions/st-francis); Kent, pp. 243–51).

- **c. 1440s:** *David*. Donatello’s bronze statue is traditionally treated as a Medici commission and was installed in the Medici palace, but no contract or direct commission document survives; its date, original intended site, subject’s precise identification, and whether Cosimo personally ordered it remain debated ([National Gallery of Art](https://www.nga.gov/content/dam/ngaweb/research/gallery-archives/PressReleases/2009-2000/2003/14A11_45329_20031123.pdf); Kent, pp. 284–96).

- **c. 1450–1452:** *Bosco ai Frati Altarpiece*. Fra Angelico painted this altarpiece for the high altar of the Franciscan convent under Medici patronage. Its association with Cosimo’s larger convent project is strong, although surviving evidence does not preserve a commission contract in his name (Kent, pp. 251–57; [Bosco ai Frati exhibition catalogue](https://www.piccoligrandimusei.it/wp-content/uploads/2014/04/18_imp_Bosco-ai-Frati.pdf)).

- **c. 1457–1464:** *Judith and Holofernes*. Donatello’s bronze group belonged to the Medici palace and was almost certainly made within Medici patronage, but scholars disagree about its exact date, initial placement, and whether Cosimo or Piero was the effective patron. It therefore resists secure assignment to Cosimo alone (Kent, pp. 296–304; [Palazzo Medici Riccardi Mediateca](https://mediateca.palazzomediciriccardi.it/Personaggi/cosimo-il-vecchio-detto-cosimo-di-giovanni-de-medici/)).

- **1459–c. 1461:** *Painted ensemble of the Chapel of the Magi*. Benozzo Gozzoli’s mural cycle and the Filippo Lippi workshop’s altar image completed the private chapel of Palazzo Medici. The commission is officially catalogued under Cosimo, but surviving letters show Piero supervising imagery and materials, making it a family work rather than Cosimo’s unaided creation ([Palazzo Medici Riccardi Mediateca](https://mediateca.palazzomediciriccardi.it/works_and_environment/la-veglia-dei-pastori/); [Palazzo Medici Riccardi](https://www.palazzomediciriccardi.it/en/the-magi-chapel/)).

### Libraries, manuscript corpora, and intellectual projects

- **From the 1430s; opened in the 1440s:** *Library of San Marco*. Cosimo acquired responsibility for Niccolò Niccoli’s books, paid his debts, supplied additional manuscripts, and housed the collection in Michelozzo’s library at San Marco. It was a public-use institutional obra assembled by collectors, scribes, architects, donors, and friars, not a text authored by Cosimo (de la Mare, pp. 115–55; Kent, pp. 95–128).

- **c. 1462–1464:** *Library of the Badia Fiesolana*. Under Cosimo’s direction, Vespasiano da Bisticci organized an intensive copying campaign to furnish the new monastery with a systematic theological and humanist collection. Cosimo saw the completed library and its inventories shortly before his death, but the individual manuscripts were the work of numerous scribes and illuminators ([Vespasiano da Bisticci Letters Project](https://vespasianodabisticciletters.unibo.it/lettere/lettera26.html); Kent, pp. 366–82).

- **c. 1462–1464:** *Latin Plato translation project*. Cosimo encouraged Marsilio Ficino to translate Plato and supplied him with Greek manuscripts; Ficino completed several dialogues for him, but the complete Latin Plato appeared only after Cosimo’s death. This is an unfinished project initiated by Cosimo, not a translation he authored ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Marsilio Ficino”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ficino/); Hankins, pp. 69–94).

### Institutional and political creations

- **From 1442:** *Support system for the Buonomini di San Martino*. Cosimo became the leading material supporter of this confraternity for the “shamed poor,” helping give durable form to its charitable operation and commemorative identity. Because Archbishop Antonino founded the confraternity and its members administered it, it resists being called Cosimo’s work; it is better classified as an institution materially co-created through patronage (Kent, “The Buonomini di San Martino,” pp. 49–67; Kent, *Cosimo de’ Medici and the Florentine Renaissance*, pp. 46–50).

- **1434–1464:** *The Medicean political network in republican Florence*. Cosimo created no formal principality or constitution; instead he maintained an informal system of alliances, electoral management, credit, patronage, and controlled access to office. Calling it an obra is deliberately uneasy: it was a collectively enacted political arrangement rather than a bounded object, manifesto, or protocol (Kent, *The Rise of the Medici*, pp. 217–68; Rubinstein, pp. 5–20).
