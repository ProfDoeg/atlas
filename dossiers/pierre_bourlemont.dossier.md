# Pierre Bourlémont: Research Dossier

## Scope, Identification, and Evidentiary Method

The name **Pierre de Bourlémont** does not identify one uniquely documented person. At least four members of the medieval Brixey-Bourlémont lineage bore the name:

1. **Pierre de Brixey/Bourlémont**, active in the mid-twelfth century, husband of Ide de Froville, reportedly dead without issue.
2. **Pierre II de Bourlémont**, active around 1200–1222 and dead approximately 1225–1230, husband of Félicité de Joinville.
3. **Pierre III de Bourlémont**, active approximately 1269–1310, son of Joffroi/Geoffroy de Bourlémont and Sibylle de Saulxures, husband of Jeanne de Choiseul.
4. **Pierre de Bourlémont**, lord of part of Domrémy in the early fifteenth century, dead without descendants on 4 April 1412 and subsequently attached to the legend of the Fairy Tree.

This dossier principally concerns **Pierre III de Bourlémont**, because:

- the Institut de recherche et d’histoire des textes identifies him as “Pierre III de Bourlémont,” alias “Pierre de Bourlaymont,” with an active sigillographic record in 1284–1289;
- archival records concerning Mureau Abbey, feudal homage, land sales, tithes, royal intervention, violence, exactions, sequestration, and arbitration form a reconstructable career extending from the 1270s to 1310;
- regional historical literature explicitly identifies this Pierre as the son and successor of Joffroi and the husband of Jeanne de Choiseul.

Events belonging to the other Pierres are separated below. In particular, the Fairy Tree tradition known from Joan of Arc’s trials concerns the Pierre who died in 1412, not Pierre III.

Evidentiary labels used in this dossier:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a surviving or catalogued medieval act, seal, judicial record, royal mandate, or archival inventory.
- **Reported fact:** stated in modern historical or genealogical literature but not independently established here from an original act.
- **Allegation:** conduct asserted in a complaint, royal order to investigate, or litigation, without treating the assertion itself as proof.
- **Later tradition / myth:** a post-medieval literary, genealogical, folkloric, or devotional account not demonstrably contemporary with Pierre III.
- **Unresolved:** surviving evidence permits more than one interpretation.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Primary name:** Pierre de Bourlémont  
**Numbering in modern scholarship:** Pierre III de Bourlémont  
**Dynastic name:** Pierre de Brixey de Bourlémont  
**Documented or indexed variant:** Pierre de Bourlaymont  
**Latin form likely encountered in charters:** Petrus de Bourlemont or equivalent orthographic variants  
**Sex:** male  
**Status:** lay nobleman  
**Titles:** sire or seigneur de Bourlémont; chevalier in multiple archival descriptions  
**Religion:** Latin Christianity, inferred from his institutional setting, donations to ecclesiastical bodies, and dealings with the cathedral of Toul and Premonstratensian Mureau Abbey  
**Birth:** uncertain; modern databases give approximately **1240** or approximately **1255**  
**Birthplace:** undocumented; probably within the family’s lordships in the Bassigny/upper Meuse region, but no consulted primary record establishes a birthplace  
**Death:** between the last securely attributed records of 1309–1310 and the succession of his son; usually given as **1309–1310** or simply **1310**  
**Place and cause of death:** undocumented  
**Burial:** undocumented  
**Principal residences or seats:** the castles or fortified lordships of Bourlémont and Rorthey; the family also possessed interests at Domrémy, Greux, Saulxures, and elsewhere  
**Known spouse:** Jeanne de Choiseul  
**Children:** regional historical literature reports eight; Jean is securely identified as heir, while the complete list and the placement of Thomas and another Pierre remain disputed or incompletely documented in accessible sources  
**Principal institutional adversary:** Notre-Dame de Mureau Abbey  
**Known feudal relationships:** connected at different moments to the counts of Champagne, Bar, and Burgundy and to the bishop of Toul; royal French protection of Mureau eventually displaced part of Pierre’s seigneurial guardianship

## Namesake Separation

### Pierre of the twelfth century

Regional histories identify an early Pierre, son of a Brixey lord and associated with Bourlémont after the family adopted the territorial name. He reportedly married **Ide de Froville** and died without children. Some unsourced online family trees instead make him the father of “Pica de Bourlémont,” alleged mother of Francis of Assisi. The conflict is substantial: the regional Bourlémont history says the marriage was childless, while devotional genealogies manufacture or repeat a daughter.

No consulted medieval source establishes that Francis of Assisi’s mother belonged to the Lorraine Bourlémont family.

### Pierre II, active 1200–1222

This Pierre was the husband of **Félicité de Joinville** and father of Joffroi. A February 1222 charter preserved as **Archives départementales de Meurthe-et-Moselle, G 58** records him confirming a donation and sale of land to the cathedral chapter of Toul by his vassal and brother-in-law Ancelme. He was reportedly a vassal of the count of Champagne for Bourlémont and Rorthey and in 1220 declared himself the liege man of the count of Bar for another fief.

He died around 1225–1230. These acts must not be assigned to Pierre III.

### Pierre III, principal subject

Pierre III was Joffroi’s son and successor. His documented career belongs chiefly to 1273–1310 and includes the Chermisey sale, the pledging of Saulxures, homage to Othon IV, and the prolonged Mureau litigation.

### Pierre who died in 1412

The early fifteenth-century Pierre was a descendant of the same house and lord of part of Domrémy. He died without descendants on 4 April 1412. Joan of Arc’s 1431 trial referred to an oak or beech called the Ladies’ or Fairies’ Tree that “used to belong” to a knight named Pierre de Bourlémont. Testimony collected in 1456 associated a lord Pierre, his wife Béatrice, his mother, and their attendants with customary outings near the tree.

That Pierre’s chronological proximity to Joan, ownership at Domrémy, and death in 1412 distinguish him from Pierre III, dead about a century earlier.

## Family and Ancestry

### The Brixey-Bourlémont lineage

Pierre belonged to a noble lineage originating in the lords of **Brixey**, near Toul. Regional historian Georges Poull’s reconstruction, summarized by ecriVOSGES, traces the territorial family through:

- an early Pierre de Brixey and his wife Hersende;
- their sons Pierre and Simon and daughter Hersende, recorded together in 1149;
- Simon, who after approximately 1172 regularly used the title sire de Bourlémont;
- Pierre II;
- Joffroi;
- Pierre III.

The family’s power derived from a network of castles, fiefs, mills, ovens, tithes, vineyards, village dues, rights of justice, guardianship over religious houses, and control over strategically situated territory near the upper Meuse.

### Paternal grandparents

**Reported fact:** Pierre’s paternal grandparents were:

- **Pierre II de Bourlémont**, dead approximately 1225–1230;
- **Félicité de Joinville**, a member of the Joinville family.

Through Félicité, the Bourlémonts were related to **Jean de Joinville**, seneschal of Champagne and chronicler of Louis IX. The precise degree is usually described as nephew or cousin relationships within the Joinville kindred.

### Parents

**Father:** **Joffroi, Joffroy, Geoffroy, or Jeoffroi de Bourlémont**, lord of Bourlémont, dead before or during 1268.

Joffroi was an important regional lord. Sources associate him with the title **seneschal of Navarre**, probably within the political world of the counts of Champagne, who also ruled Navarre. He was a liege man of the bishop of Toul. In 1235, with the consent of Bishop Roger, his mother Félicité, and his wife Sibylle, he granted Mureau Abbey his share of the tithes of Domrémy and the common oven of Greux and confirmed earlier family donations. In 1263–1265 he acquired Bermont and attached it to the hospital of Gerbonvaux, an institution serving poor people, invalids, travelers, and pilgrims.

**Mother:** **Sibylle or Sibille de Saulxures**, lady associated with Saulxures-les-Vannes, reportedly dead in March or April 1275.

Her marriage brought or consolidated Bourlémont claims at Saulxures. An Études touloises study states that Saulxures entered the Bourlémont family through her marriage to Joffroi.

### Siblings

The family reconstruction is not fully stable.

- **Thomas de Bourlémont:** Some modern accounts call him a son of Joffroi and Sibylle and therefore Pierre III’s brother. Other phrasing in the regional biographical notice can be read as making Thomas a brother of Pierre’s son Jean, and therefore Pierre’s son. Thomas became bishop of Toul from 1330 to 1353. The older-brother hypothesis is chronologically possible but would require an unusually long interval between his birth before 1268 and his episcopate. The son-of-Pierre hypothesis is also chronologically plausible. A consulted popular account gives a manifestly impossible alternative descent from Jean Saladin d’Anglure and Jeanne de Bourlémont, whose marriage occurred around 1400. The exact relationship therefore remains **unresolved in the accessible material**.
- **Isabel or Isabelle:** A memoir of the Société des lettres, sciences et arts de Bar-le-Duc indexes an agreement between a Pierre de Bourlémont and his sister Isabel. Without examination of the full act and date, it is unsafe to assign the document conclusively to Pierre III rather than a namesake.
- Other siblings are not securely reconstructable from the consulted sources.

### Marriage

Pierre married **Jeanne de Choiseul**, apparently before February 1275. She was a member of the powerful Choiseul-Aigremont lineage.

Later genealogical reconstructions identify her parents as **Jean I de Choiseul** and **Alix or Bartholomette d’Aigremont**, but accessible summaries of Choiseul genealogy contain acknowledged chronological and generational inconsistencies. Her noble Choiseul affiliation is well established; the precise parental reconstruction should be checked against a critical edition of Choiseul charters.

Jeanne participated in Pierre’s legal and property arrangements. The 1288 Mureau record explicitly states that Pierre and his wife acknowledged their obligation to guarantee the abbey’s enjoyment of half the great and small tithes of Bréchainville.

She was still living after Pierre according to sources that place her death after 1311, although no exact death record was located.

### Children

Georges Poull’s regional family history reports that Pierre and Jeanne had **eight children**. The complete list is not recoverable from the open sources consulted.

Children or probable children include:

- **Jean de Bourlémont:** eldest son and successor; later married Isabelle-Jeanne de Grancey. His death is variously given as 15 or 23 November 1337.
- **Pierre de Bourlémont:** a secondary regional reconstruction identifies a son Pierre, lord of Rorthey, dead in 1316. This requires confirmation from the charter catalogue.
- **Thomas de Bourlémont:** possibly their son and brother of Jean, later bishop of Toul; alternatively identified as Pierre III’s brother.
- The identities of the other reported children remain uncertain.

No evidence found supports modern claims of illegitimate children, extramarital relationships, or dynastic exclusion proceedings.

## Childhood and Early Life

No narrative source describes Pierre’s childhood. His approximate birth date is inferred genealogically rather than recorded contemporaneously.

If the IRHT date of approximately 1240 is accepted, he was born during his father Joffroi’s mature lordship. If the frequently repeated date of approximately 1255 is preferred, he was still a minor or young adolescent when Joffroi died in 1268. The latter chronology may imply a period of maternal, familial, or seigneurial guardianship, but no guardianship record was located.

His likely formative environment included:

- the castle of Bourlémont at Frebécourt;
- Rorthey, a fortified outpost overlooking local routes;
- Domrémy and Greux in the upper Meuse valley;
- the intersecting jurisdictions of Champagne, Bar, the bishopric of Toul, and the Empire;
- family patronage of Mureau Abbey and Gerbonvaux hospital;
- practical instruction in feudal law, mounted warfare, household administration, seigneurial justice, revenue collection, and sealing documents.

No source records a tutor, clerical school, university education, literacy level, or formal military apprenticeship.

## Education and Formation

Pierre’s ability to issue charters and conduct prolonged litigation does not establish that he personally drafted or read the documents. Noble acts were commonly prepared by clerks, notaries, ecclesiastical officials, or chancery personnel.

His surviving seals demonstrate participation in the documentary culture of noble government. Sigilla records:

- a principal seal used in 1284–1289;
- a secret seal dated 1289, approximately 25 millimeters in size;
- a counterseal dated 1284, also approximately 25 millimeters.

No securely documented teacher or mentor is known. His likely political formation came through:

- his father Joffroi;
- the Joinville kinship network;
- relations with the counts of Champagne and Bar;
- the bishop of Toul;
- local castellans, bailiffs, knights, abbots, and cathedral officials.

## Early Career and Succession

Joffroi died by 1268. Pierre appears as lord soon afterward.

The inherited estate was geographically dispersed and legally layered. Different assets were held from different overlords. This was not a unitary sovereign territory: individual castles, villages, revenues, tithes, and rights could be held from Champagne, Bar, Burgundy, or Toul or be encumbered by older religious grants.

Regional historical literature characterizes Pierre as having “dilapidated” the patrimony of his predecessors. That is a retrospective interpretation based on sales, pledges, disputes, and the alienation of guardianship rights. It should not be treated as a contemporary legal finding of incapacity. His transactions may reflect indebtedness, political realignment, liquidity needs, ordinary aristocratic finance, or a combination.

## Chronological Life History

### 1268–1273: assumption of lordship

Following Joffroi’s death, Pierre succeeded to the central Bourlémont lordship.

In **April 1273**, Pierre issued a charter concerning an obligation of thirty résaux of flour or milling revenue from the mill of Domrémy. Willaume, an esquire and son of the lord Ménissié, pledged the revenue to Mureau Abbey pending his wife’s ratification of her portion of a sale involving the Bréchainville tithes.

**Documented significance:** Pierre exercised superior seigneurial authority over a vassal’s transaction and over revenue connected with the Domrémy mill.

### February 1275: sale of Chermisey

Pierre sold his fief of **Chermisey** to **Huon Tripotel**, a knight and former bourgeois of Neufchâteau, for **1,400 livres**.

This was a large nominal sum. It cannot be converted responsibly into modern currency without detailed assumptions about coin, silver content, wages, and purchasing power.

Regional historians interpret the sale as part of the erosion of the family estate. The buyer’s description as a former bourgeois illustrates the movement of urban wealth into land and noble status.

The transaction also occurred near the time of Pierre’s marriage to Jeanne de Choiseul and the death of his mother Sibylle. Whether the sale was related to succession costs, marriage settlements, debt, or another purpose is undocumented.

### 1280: pledge of Saulxures and other lordships

An Études touloises study states that in **1280**, Pierre pledged or mortgaged several lordships, including **Saulxures**, to the bishop of Toul. Saulxures was held as a fief from the bishop.

This supplies additional evidence for financial pressure or deliberate use of landed assets as security. The conditions, principal sum, redemption terms, and eventual disposition were not available in the consulted summary.

### 1282: Bruley ecclesiastical patronage

A local historical study reports that in **1282** Pierre gave the cure or patronage of **Bruley** to the cathedral chapter of Toul.

This is consistent with the family’s ecclesiastical relationships but should be distinguished from an online claim assigning a similar donation to a Pierre as early as 1202 or 1214. Those earlier acts belong to Pierre II or may involve erroneous dating.

### 1284–1289: seals and documentary activity

Sigilla records Pierre as an active sealer in **1284–1289**. Surviving or catalogued seal impressions substantiate his identity and title independently of later genealogy.

The existence of a secret seal and counterseal indicates a developed household documentary practice. No complete heraldic description was recovered from the accessible notice.

### April 1288: Bréchainville tithe litigation

Pierre and Jeanne acknowledged, following a lawsuit with Mureau Abbey, that they were obliged to guarantee the abbey’s enjoyment of **half the great and small tithes of Bréchainville**.

The agreement imposed substantial consequences:

- they had received **100 livres of petits tournois**;
- if they failed to provide the guarantee, they were to restore that sum;
- refusal could lead to seizure of their movable and immovable property.

This is a documented settlement or acknowledgment, not merely a hostile monastic accusation.

### March 1289: additional payment for the guarantee

Pierre issued another charter acknowledging receipt of **50 livres of petits tournois** from Mureau Abbey in connection with the Bréchainville guarantee.

A separate bailiff’s record from the same period records a guarantee by a Joffroi and his wife. The identity of that Joffroi and his relationship to Pierre require examination of the original act.

### 1289: homage to Othon IV of Burgundy

An original and vidimus catalogued by the Archives départementales du Doubs state that **Pierre de Bourlaymont** rendered homage to **Othon IV, count of Burgundy**, in return for a grant of **200 livres tournois**.

The spelling and date correspond to Pierre III’s alias and Sigilla identity.

This transaction demonstrates that Pierre’s political-financial network extended eastward beyond Champagne, Bar, and Toul. The homage was compensated, making it both a political and financial relationship.

### July–August 1291: settlement over Mureau’s protection

In **July 1291**, according to Poull’s reconstruction, Pierre renounced his seigneurial right of guard or protection over Mureau Abbey and its dependencies. The relevant properties extended across:

- Avranville;
- Mont-lès-Neufchâteau;
- Bourlémont;
- Frebécourt;
- Greux;
- Domrémy;
- Bréchainville;
- Housselmont;
- Charmes-la-Côte;
- Pargny-devant-Toul;
- Toul;
- Moncel;
- Gouécourt;
- Rouceux;
- Chermisey;
- Les Roises.

The abbey’s fiefs and rear-fiefs formerly under Pierre’s protection passed under the protection of the king of France. Pierre reportedly received **900 livres** in compensation.

This was a major political change. Royal protection penetrated a frontier zone in which local lordship, the bishopric of Toul, Bar, Champagne, and imperial interests overlapped.

In **August 1291**, bailiff Ginart de la Porte recorded that Pierre could not compel Mureau Abbey to pay him **500 livres of strong Provins currency** until he had given the abbey lawful and effective possession of half the Bréchainville tithes then held by Gautier de Roche.

This was a specific restriction on Pierre’s recovery of a debt. It confirms that the tithe dispute remained unresolved in practical possession even after financial arrangements had been made.

### 1292–1301: royal consolidation

Royal records from 1292–1293 concern payments for amortization of Mureau’s property. In June 1298, Philippe IV and Queen Jeanne approved and confirmed related quittances and possessions.

In **January 1301**, Philippe IV formally amortized the half of the Bréchainville tithes sold by Pierre to the abbey. Amortization allowed ecclesiastical ownership despite the withdrawal of land or revenue from ordinary feudal circulation.

The royal act confirms the substance of Pierre’s earlier transaction and the crown’s participation in settling its legal status.

### March 1305: allegations of violence and exactions

On **6 March 1305**, Philippe IV ordered the bailli of Chaumont to investigate Pierre for **“violences and exactions”** allegedly committed against Mureau Abbey.

Evidentiary status:

- the royal mandate is a **documented fact**;
- the violent and extortionate acts were **formal allegations requiring investigation**;
- the surviving catalogue summary does not identify victims, dates, methods, amounts, injuries, or the evidentiary result of the investigation.

On **21 March 1305**, the king commissioned the bailli to compel Pierre to execute or comply with a sentence already rendered against him.

This establishes that the dispute had produced a formal decision. The accessible record does not reproduce the sentence and therefore does not establish every alleged act as proven.

### September 1310: arbitration

A Mureau cartulary description records an agreement made before **Gaucher de Châtillon**, count of Porcien and constable of France, acting as arbitrator between Mureau Abbey and Pierre, lord of Bourlémont. It concerned reciprocal “injuries, violences, and undertakings” committed in the past. The act is dated to the Friday after the Nativity of the Virgin in September 1310.

This record complicates simple accounts stating that Pierre died before July 1310. Possibilities include:

- the death date in secondary genealogy is too early;
- the act was finalized or copied after Pierre’s death;
- the “Pierre” in the arbitration was another family member;
- the archival abstract’s date or identification requires checking against the original cartulary.

Until the underlying act is examined, Pierre’s terminal date should be given as **1309–1310**, with possible activity in September 1310.

### 1309: final religious donation

A local history of Charmes-la-Côte reports that in **1309**, Pierre, Jeanne, and their family gave the church of Toul a rent of **sixty sous tournois**, charged on their property at Charmes-la-Côte. Another archival inventory describes a donation by Pierre, knight, in memory of **Jean de Bourlémont, grand archdeacon and his brother**, of an annual sixty-sous rent at Charmes-la-Côte for an anniversary at Toul Cathedral.

The exact relationship between these descriptions requires examination of the act. If Jean was Pierre’s brother, this adds an ecclesiastical sibling to the family reconstruction.

## Residences, Lordships, and Geographical Sphere

### Château de Bourlémont

The principal castle stands at Frebécourt in the Vosges, on a high promontory overlooking the confluence region of the Meuse and Saônelle.

The fortress was substantially developed under Pierre’s father Joffroi. Pierre inherited it rather than building it. It served as:

- dynastic seat;
- military strongpoint;
- administrative center;
- symbol of territorial lordship.

A later historical account says that in 1285 the castle’s Champagne-funded garrison consisted of four mounted sergeants and five foot soldiers. If correctly attributed, this illustrates a relatively small professional garrison supplemented when necessary by household retainers and feudal levies.

### Rorthey

Rorthey was another family castle, positioned as an advanced defensive point. Pierre’s family held it within the Champagne relationship. Later genealogies associate a younger son Pierre with Rorthey, but the succession requires confirmation.

### Domrémy and Greux

Pierre exercised rights affecting the Domrémy mill and inherited family interests originating in Joffroi’s lordship. The family’s later control of part of Domrémy made the name Bourlémont prominent in Joan of Arc historiography.

Pierre III never met Joan of Arc: he died about a century before her birth.

### Saulxures-les-Vannes

Saulxures entered or was consolidated in the family estate through Sibylle. Pierre held it as a fief of the bishop of Toul and pledged it in 1280.

### Other interests

The 1291 settlement places Pierre’s seigneurial or protective rights in a network extending across the upper Meuse and toward Toul. The exact kind of right differed by locality: direct lordship, fief, rear-fief, tithe, rent, milling revenue, ecclesiastical patronage, or guardianship should not be conflated.

No documented travel outside the wider Lorraine-Champagne-Burgundy zone was found. No evidence places Pierre on crusade; stories about a sire de Bourlémont advising Joinville before crusade concern his father Joffroi or another earlier lord.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

The categories of company ownership, shares, patents, and industrial technology are anachronistic for Pierre’s period.

His economic structure consisted of seigneurial rights and landed revenue.

### Castles and military infrastructure

- Château de Bourlémont
- Château de Rorthey
- family interests in a fortified house at Domrémy
- dependent military and administrative households

### Agricultural and customary revenues

- great and small tithes of Bréchainville;
- milling revenue at Domrémy;
- wine or vineyard rents at Charmes-la-Côte;
- ovens, mills, woodland, pasture, village dues, and judicial rights in associated lordships;
- guardianship rights over Mureau’s properties.

### Recorded financial transactions

- **1,400 livres:** sale of Chermisey, 1275.
- **100 livres petits tournois:** amount received in connection with the Bréchainville guarantee, subject to restitution if Pierre and Jeanne failed to perform.
- **50 livres petits tournois:** additional payment acknowledged in 1289.
- **500 livres strong Provins currency:** debt Pierre could not collect from the abbey before providing effective possession of the tithes.
- **200 livres tournois:** grant from Othon IV in return for homage, 1289.
- **900 livres:** reported compensation for renouncing protection over Mureau and its dependencies.
- **Sixty sous tournois annually:** reported cathedral anniversary rent at Charmes-la-Côte in 1309.

These figures demonstrate substantial cash and credit relationships. They do not establish Pierre’s net wealth. No balance sheet, household account, debt inventory, testament, or postmortem estate inventory was found.

### Mureau Abbey

Mureau was a Premonstratensian abbey and both beneficiary and adversary of the Bourlémonts. Earlier family members had endowed it. Under Pierre, relations became litigious and at times allegedly violent.

The dispute illustrates a larger medieval pattern:

1. a noble house grants or sells religious property;
2. possession, warranty, protection, and immunity remain contested;
3. the abbey seeks protection from a stronger prince;
4. royal officers and courts intervene;
5. local lordship loses jurisdiction or revenue in exchange for compensation.

### Toul Cathedral and bishopric

Pierre’s family interacted with Toul through:

- feudal dependence for Saulxures;
- donations and rents;
- burial or anniversary commemorations;
- ecclesiastical relatives;
- transfer of patronage rights.

## Political Activity

Pierre held no known municipal, royal, or ecclesiastical office. His politics were expressed through feudal allegiance, territorial rights, patronage, and resistance to external jurisdiction.

### Multipolar allegiance

His family’s holdings lay in a frontier environment. Pierre’s known or inherited relationships included:

- the count of Champagne;
- the count of Bar;
- the bishop of Toul;
- Othon IV, count of Burgundy;
- Philippe IV’s officers at Chaumont;
- Gaucher de Châtillon, constable of France.

His homage to Othon IV for payment shows that allegiance could be negotiated financially. His loss of Mureau’s guardianship to the French crown shows royal expansion at the expense of local authority.

### Relationship with Philippe IV

Pierre was neither simply an ally nor simply an enemy of the French king.

- The king’s protection facilitated Mureau’s independence from Pierre.
- Royal amortization confirmed the abbey’s acquisition.
- Philippe ordered an investigation of Pierre.
- Philippe ordered enforcement of a sentence against him.
- Royal intervention nevertheless arose from legal and political mechanisms rather than a documented rebellion by Pierre.

No source found accuses Pierre of treason, armed revolt against the king, or participation in a dynastic conspiracy.

## Religious, Ideological, and Intellectual Development

No personal statement of faith, theological writing, confession, devotional diary, or intellectual treatise survives.

His observable religious activity included:

- inherited involvement in family donations to Mureau;
- property transactions with an abbey;
- patronage or transfer of a parish cure;
- a commemorative rent to Toul Cathedral;
- participation in a culture in which aristocratic remembrance was maintained through masses and anniversaries.

The prolonged conflict with Mureau does not establish irreligion. Medieval noble families routinely combined donations to monasteries with litigation or violence over jurisdiction and revenue.

No evidence was found of heresy, excommunication, conversion, pilgrimage, crusade participation, occultism, or heterodox belief.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Joffroi de Bourlémont

Father, predecessor, and source of Pierre’s inherited position. Joffroi’s charitable foundations and monastic grants also created obligations Pierre later disputed.

### Sibylle de Saulxures

Mother and source of family rights at Saulxures.

### Jeanne de Choiseul

Wife, dynastic partner, and party to the Bréchainville guarantee. Her participation shows that her marital or dower rights affected the alienated property.

### Jean de Bourlémont

Eldest son and successor. He inherited both the lordship and unresolved obligations. Later proceedings concerning wine owed to Mureau refer to grants or arrears originating with predecessors.

### Huon Tripotel

Buyer of Chermisey in 1275. He represents the connection between urban wealth at Neufchâteau and noble landholding.

### Gautier de Roche

Holder of the disputed Bréchainville tithes in 1291. His possession prevented Pierre from demanding payment from Mureau until the abbey obtained effective enjoyment of what it had purchased.

### Othon IV of Burgundy

Received Pierre’s homage in 1289 and granted him 200 livres.

### Philippe IV of France

Confirmed and amortized Mureau’s acquisitions and ordered investigation and enforcement against Pierre.

### Ginart de la Porte

Bailli of Chaumont whose act restricted Pierre’s collection of the 500-livre debt.

### Guillaume de Hangest

Bailli of Chaumont associated with the 1289 warranty proceedings.

### Gaucher de Châtillon

Count of Porcien and constable of France; arbitrator in the 1310 settlement between Pierre and Mureau.

### Abbot and canons of Mureau

Collectively Pierre’s most persistent institutional counterpart. The relationship included sales, guarantees, debts, jurisdictional rivalry, accusations, litigation, and arbitration.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### Retrospective charge of dissipating the family estate

**Status: reported historical judgment.**

The regional notice states that Pierre “dilapidated the patrimony of his predecessors.” Supporting circumstances include:

- sale of Chermisey;
- pledge of Saulxures;
- repeated receipt of money from Mureau;
- compensated homage to Othon IV;
- renunciation of monastic guardianship;
- disputes over warranty and possession.

There is no located medieval declaration that Pierre was legally prodigal, incompetent, bankrupt, or under guardianship. “Dilapidation” remains an interpretation.

### Bréchainville warranty failure

**Status: documented civil dispute and acknowledged obligation.**

Pierre and Jeanne accepted money in return for guaranteeing Mureau’s possession of tithes. The abbey did not obtain peaceful enjoyment, apparently because Gautier de Roche held the property. Pierre’s right to collect 500 livres was suspended pending performance.

This is evidence of nonperformance of a property warranty. It is not enough to classify the transaction as fraud because fraudulent intent is not established.

### Violence and exactions against Mureau

**Status: formally alleged and investigated; later subject to enforcement and arbitration.**

The king’s 6 March 1305 mandate explicitly ordered an investigation into Pierre’s alleged violence and exactions. The 21 March order refers to enforcement of a sentence. The 1310 arbitration refers to injuries and violence on both sides.

What is established:

- accusations reached the king;
- royal inquiry was ordered;
- a sentence existed;
- enforcement was ordered;
- the conflict continued into arbitration.

What is not established from the accessible summaries:

- precise acts;
- number or identity of victims;
- whether Pierre acted personally or through retainers;
- whether physical injury occurred;
- whether money, crops, livestock, or property were seized;
- which allegations were judicially proved;
- the sanctions imposed.

### Sequestration

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### The Fairy Tree and a sire de Bourlémont

#### The story

A lord Pierre de Bourlémont supposedly frequented the Ladies’ or Fairies’ Tree near Domrémy with his wife and ladies. A more romantic version makes the tree a meeting place between Pierre and a fairy whom he loved.

#### Earliest relevant records

At Joan of Arc’s trial in 1431, she described a great beech near Domrémy called the Ladies’ Tree or Fairies’ Tree. Sick people visited a nearby fountain. Children made garlands there. Older people said fairies had once frequented it. Joan stated that the tree had belonged to a knight, Pierre de Bourlémont.

Rehabilitation testimony in 1456 described customary outings by a Pierre de Bourlémont, his wife Béatrice, his mother, and their attendants.

#### Evidentiary assessment

- Ownership or lordship association with the early fifteenth-century Pierre: **documented in trial testimony**.
- Community celebrations and garland customs: **documented testimony**.
- Actual fairies: **folklore**.
- A sexual or romantic liaison between Pierre and a fairy: **later literary tradition**, not established fact.
- Identification of this Pierre with Pierre III: **false conflation**, because Pierre III died around 1310.

#### Reputation effect

The story tied the Bourlémont name to Joan of Arc and ensured its continued appearance in trial editions, biographies, plays, folklore studies, and regional tourism.

### The “romance of chivalry”

A regional notice says Pierre III’s conduct impressed public opinion enough to make him the hero of a romance of chivalry. No title, manuscript, date, textual quotation, or attribution was supplied in the accessible notice.

Possible explanations include:

- a lost or unidentified romance;
- confusion with the later Fairy Tree story;
- literary transformation of a generic sire de Bourlémont;
- a statement developed in Georges Poull’s 1965 article, *Le Sire de Bourlémont et la fée*.

Until the cited study and manuscript evidence are examined, the claim is a **reported literary tradition**, not established contemporary reception.

### Pierre’s ancestor and Francis of Assisi’s mother

#### The story

Francis of Assisi’s mother is frequently called **Pica, Joanna Pica, or Pica de Bourlémont**, allegedly a Provençal or French noblewoman descended from the Lorraine Bourlémont house. Online family trees sometimes make her the daughter of the twelfth-century Pierre de Bourlémont and Ide de Froville.

#### Development

An Italian investigation notes that the only known source explicitly linking Pica to “Bourlemont” is a seventeenth-century commentary by Franciscan theologian **Claude Frassen**, who claimed support from an old manuscript in the family archive. Later devotional writers repeated the claim. Nineteenth-century scholars already criticized repetition of it without evidence.

#### Evidence

- Francis’s mother was called Pica in later Franciscan tradition: widely reported.
- Her French or Provençal origin: later tradition, not securely contemporary.
- Her belonging to the Bourlémont family: unsupported by a produced medieval document.
- Her being the daughter of Pierre and Ide: contradicted by the regional lineage stating that the couple died without issue and chronologically difficult in several online trees.
- “Bourlémont in Provence”: geographically problematic for the Lorraine house.

#### Reputation effect

The claim connects the Bourlémonts genealogically to one of medieval Christianity’s most famous saints. It has been circulated by devotional publications, diocesan websites, genealogy platforms, and visual culture. It remains a **genealogical legend**, not a documented atlas connection for Pierre III.

### Crusading advice to Jean de Joinville

Jean de Joinville’s later literary reception includes a sire de Bourlémont warning him not to abandon poor crusaders overseas. The relevant Bourlémont was Joffroi or another earlier lord, not Pierre III, who was too young or unborn before the Seventh Crusade began.

No source places Pierre III in Egypt, Palestine, or a crusading army.

### “Last of the line” confusion

Modern castle histories often state that Pierre de Bourlémont was the last male representative and died in 1412. That is correct only for the later Pierre. Applying it to Pierre III is erroneous: Pierre III had a son and successor, Jean.

## Works by Pierre de Bourlémont

Pierre was not an author in the modern literary sense. The following are documentary acts issued, sealed, acknowledged, or made in his name. Their wording was probably prepared by clerks.

### Surviving or catalogued acts

- **April 1273 — Charter concerning the Domrémy mill and Bréchainville tithes.** Pierre notified the pledge of thirty résaux of milling revenue made to Mureau by Willaume pending his wife’s ratification.
- **February 1275 — Sale of the fief of Chermisey.** Sold to Huon Tripotel for 1,400 livres; known through regional historical reconstruction.
- **1280 — Pledge of Saulxures and other lordships to the bishop of Toul.**
- **1282 — Grant of the cure or patronage of Bruley to the cathedral chapter of Toul.**
- **1284–1289 — Sealed acts represented by Pierre’s principal seal, secret seal, and counterseal.**
- **April 1288 — Charter acknowledging the obligation to guarantee Mureau’s possession of half the Bréchainville tithes.** Issued with Jeanne.
- **March 1289 — Charter acknowledging receipt of fifty livres from Mureau in connection with the guarantee.**
- **1289 — Act of homage to Othon IV of Burgundy in return for 200 livres tournois.** Original and vidimus catalogued in Archives départementales du Doubs, 1 B 47.
- **July 1291 — Renunciation of guardianship over Mureau Abbey’s properties in favor of royal protection.**

- **1309 — Donation or annual rent of sixty sous at Charmes-la-Côte to Toul Cathedral, reportedly in memory of Jean de Bourlémont.**
- **September 1310 — Arbitral settlement with Mureau Abbey before Gaucher de Châtillon.** Attribution requires checking because of uncertainty over Pierre’s death date.

## Later Reception and Legacy

Pierre III’s independent historical reputation is limited. He survives primarily through:

- archival documentation of transactions;
- legal conflict with Mureau;
- seals;
- genealogy of the Brixey-Bourlémont and Choiseul houses;
- the history of the château;
- the later prominence of the Bourlémont name in Joan of Arc studies.

### Territorial legacy

Pierre transmitted the lordship to Jean. In the mid-fourteenth century the estate was divided among branches. Through heiresses and marriages, important portions eventually passed to the house of Anglure.

The castle remained associated with only a few major families over approximately nine centuries. That continuity has encouraged simplified owner lists in which the multiple Pierres are often compressed into one.

### Institutional legacy

The settlement with Mureau represents a local episode in the extension of French royal protection into a politically fragmented frontier. Pierre’s loss of guardianship is therefore relevant beyond family history.

### Documentary legacy

His seals provide rare material evidence of his authority and self-presentation. His charters reveal the mechanics of aristocratic property, credit, warranty, patronage, and litigation.

### Mythic legacy by contamination

The later Pierre’s Fairy Tree story and the alleged Pica de Bourlémont descent have projected supernatural and saintly associations backward over the whole dynasty. Neither should be attached to Pierre III without qualification.
