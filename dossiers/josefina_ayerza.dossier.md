# Josefina Ayerza: Research Dossier

## Scope and Evidentiary Note

This dossier concerns the Argentine writer, art critic, editor, psychoanalyst, former tarot practitioner, and translator Josefina “Finita” Ayerza, born in Buenos Aires in 1950 and principally associated with *Lacanian Ink* and Lacan.com.

The public record is uneven. Ayerza’s work after 1990 is extensively documented, but much of the available biographical narrative comes from her own essays, interviews, and institutional websites. Information about her parents, siblings, formal academic degrees, marriages, children, finances, and clinical credentials is either unavailable or cannot be separated safely from records concerning namesakes.

Evidentiary labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a contemporaneous record, publication, institutional listing, or Ayerza’s published work.
- **Reported fact:** asserted by a credible secondary source but not independently established here.
- **Self-report:** stated retrospectively by Ayerza.
- **Allegation or characterization:** a claim or label whose literal accuracy is not independently established.
- **Unresolved:** evidence is insufficient or contradictory.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Principal name:** Josefina Ayerza.
- **Full legal name:** Not securely established. A June 2026 Argentine obituary concerns a “Josefina María Ayerza,” but identification with the psychoanalyst remains unresolved.
- **Nickname:** **Finita**. This identification is secure: Ayerza’s own Lacanian archive reproduces Germán García’s dedications to “Finita,” while independent Argentine cultural sources use the same nickname for the tarot practitioner and later Lacanian editor. [S3, S12, S13]
- **Pseudonym:** **Yeshira**, used for tarot consultations or divinatory columns advertised in Argentine newspapers during the 1970s. The spelling appears consistently in recent scholarship and histories of Argentine esotericism. [S11, S14]
- **Birth:** 1950, Buenos Aires, Argentina. No reliable source located gives a day or month. [S1, S2]
- **Nationality:** Argentine.
- **Languages:** Spanish and English are documented through her publications; she worked closely with French-language psychoanalytic material. Her precise proficiency in French is not independently documented.
- **Occupations:** Writer, editor, art critic, interviewer, publisher, psychoanalyst, lecturer, cultural organizer; earlier tarot reader, occultist, designer, and translator.
- **Principal residence and workplace:** New York City, especially SoHo, from at least the late 1970s or 1980s onward. [S4, S17]
- **Known institutional affiliations:** Founder, publisher, and editor of *Lacanian Ink*; creator and editor of Lacan.com; member of the World Association of Psychoanalysis and associated with the New Lacanian School; consultant or participant in Freudian Field communications. [S5, S20, S31]
- **Present status:** A June 2026 obituary for a Josefina María “Finita” Ayerza raises a serious possibility that she died on 25 June 2026. The obituary does not mention New York, psychoanalysis, *Lacanian Ink*, tarot, or publishing, and the family information cannot presently be reconciled with the professional record. Her death therefore remains **unconfirmed for the subject of this dossier**. [S32, S33]

## Family and Ancestry

The surname Ayerza belongs to a long-established Argentine family that included physicians, landowners, photographers, diplomats, and business figures. The most prominent historical bearers include physicians Toribio and Abel Ayerza, photographer Francisco Ayerza, and sculptor-diplomat Hernán Cullen Ayerza. No consulted source establishes Josefina’s exact descent from any of them.

One history of Argentine esotericism described Finita Ayerza as coming from “a family of powerful businessmen.” That is a **reported characterization**, not a reconstructed genealogy. [S14]

No reliable source located identifies:

- Her parents.
- Her siblings.
- A spouse or legal partner.
- Her children.
- Her exact place within the extended Ayerza family.
- Inherited property, trusts, or family business interests.

The 2026 obituary for Josefina María “Finita” Ayerza names relatives or mourners including Josefina, Coco, Antonio, María, Miguel and Elena Tezanos Pinto, Bruno and Mariel Quintana, Helena and Eduardo Escasany, and residents of the condominium at Avenida Alvear 1402. Another notice says acquaintances were “acompañando a Toni.” These notices may concern the subject, but the relationship cannot be treated as established without corroboration. [S32, S33]

## Childhood and Early Life

Ayerza was born in Buenos Aires in 1950. No documentary material located describes her childhood home, schools, parents, religious upbringing, or early illnesses.

A later profile connected her with the socially prominent but countercultural Buenos Aires milieu of the 1960s and early 1970s. A 2000 *Página/12* profile of the designer and actress Chunchuna Villafañe grouped “Finita Ayerza” with Villafañe, Rosita Bailón, and Felisa Pinto as women who devised aesthetic gestures and practical design solutions that became fashionable only later. [S15]

Adrian Dannatt subsequently described Ayerza as having designed plastic furniture. That detail is plausible in light of the *Página/12* account but remains poorly documented: no company name, catalogue, surviving object, patent, or dated exhibition has been located. [S16]

## Education and Formation

### Esoteric and tarot formation

The origins of Ayerza’s occult training are not securely documented. By the 1970s she was practicing tarot publicly under the name Yeshira. A later practitioner’s curriculum vitae states that she studied “Tarot Kabalístico” with Finita Ayerza in 1977, indicating that Ayerza was already teaching, not merely consulting, by that year. [S18]

Accounts connect her with:

- Tarot and qabalah.
- Ceremonial magic.
- Aleister Crowley and Thelema.
- New York’s Tahuti Lodge, then an operating body of Ordo Templi Orientis.
- Susana Lippschitz, an Argentine translator and teacher of Crowley’s tarot.

Writer Matías Bragagnolo reports that Ayerza developed an interest in Crowley early in the 1970s and met members of the Tahuti Lodge in New York in 1977. He identifies her traveling companion as Susana Lippschitz. His account draws in part on James Wasserman’s memoir *In the Center of the Fire*, which reportedly contains a photograph of Ayerza and describes her as an accomplished tarot adept. [S12]

### Lacanian formation

Ayerza’s account of her psychoanalytic formation is unusually specific but largely autobiographical:

- She met the Argentine Lacanian theorist Oscar Masotta and discussed Lacan with him in Buenos Aires bars.
- Germán García became her teacher.
- In New York she undertook six years of analysis with Stuart Schneiderman.
- She studied with Schneiderman at Barnard College.
- The final phase of her analysis took place in Paris with Éric Laurent. [S3]

No consulted source supplies the dates of these phases. Masotta left Argentina in the mid-1970s and died in 1979, placing their contact no later than that period.

Ayerza has not publicly identified a conventional university degree in psychology, medicine, or social work. Professional directories call her a psychoanalyst, but one independent directory expressly says its list is not exhaustive and does not endorse the credentials of those included. [S30] Her authority appears to derive primarily from training and institutional recognition within the Lacanian schools rather than a publicly documented Anglo-American clinical degree.

## Early Career: Design, Tarot, Journalism, and the Counterculture

### Buenos Aires cultural milieu

During the early 1970s Ayerza moved in Buenos Aires rock, design, and countercultural circles. Designer Juan Gatti recalled that David Lebón—then involved with the band Pescado Rabioso—was “with Finita Ayerza,” while Luis Alberto Spinetta was with Mercedes Robirosa. The wording supports a romantic association between Ayerza and Lebón but does not establish its duration or legal character. [S19]

### Tarot under the name Yeshira

In the 1970s, Ayerza advertised tarot consultations in *La Opinión* and *La Nación* under the pseudonym Yeshira. Later histories called her one of Buenos Aires’s leading card readers. [S11, S14]

A scholarly study of Argentine mass-circulation journalism documents María Moreno invoking “el libro de vaticinios de Finita Ayerza” in an article about tennis player Guillermo Vilas. The surviving quotation presents a flamboyant, metaphorical prediction involving pregnancy, crown, scepter, and the founding of a kingdom. This establishes that Ayerza’s divinatory persona circulated beyond private consultations and entered celebrity journalism. [S11]

### European travel and the Institute of Contemporary Arts

Research on Latin American cultural programming in Europe documents a “Latin American Week” at London’s Institute of Contemporary Arts at which Ayerza, traveling from Buenos Aires, performed a tarot reading. An event press release promoted her exotically as a “Third World female magician” with contacts in Western and Eastern esoteric circles.

The precise year is not explicit in the accessible extract, but the surrounding research places it in the 1970s. The wording should be understood as the organizer’s promotional framing, not an objective description of ethnicity, magical powers, or institutional rank. [S21]

### New York and Tahuti Lodge

In 1977 Ayerza reportedly visited New York with Susana Lippschitz and met members of Tahuti Lodge. James Wasserman, later a prominent American Thelemite and publisher, reportedly remembered her as a genuine adept of tarot. [S12]

This episode links Ayerza’s Argentine countercultural work with the American ceremonial-magic revival. No evidence located establishes formal initiation, degree, office, or continuing membership in Ordo Templi Orientis.

## Crowley Translation and Thelemic Activity

Ayerza translated Aleister Crowley’s *Liber AL vel Legis*—*The Book of the Law*—into Spanish as *El libro de la ley*. The translation is credited to **Finita Ayerza**.

Bragagnolo reports:

- Publication in Buenos Aires on 22 September 1981.
- A nearly private or very limited edition.
- Assistance from Adriana Rosenberg and Renato Rita.
- Continued ritual use of Ayerza’s Spanish translation by Latin American Thelemites for at least fifteen years of initiations. [S12]

Digital copies of the translation continue to circulate under her name. [S22, S23]

This is the only firmly identified book-length translation by Ayerza. No evidence located supports Adrian Dannatt’s broader phrase “Spanish translator of Aleister Crowley” beyond this work, although other translations may have circulated privately.

## Migration: Buenos Aires, Paris, and New York

Short institutional biographies state that Ayerza moved from Buenos Aires to Paris during the 1970s and subsequently settled in New York. [S1, S2]

The surviving evidence suggests a more complicated circuit:

1. Buenos Aires remained her base during her tarot career.
2. She appeared in European cultural programming.
3. She visited New York and Tahuti Lodge in 1977.
4. Her 1981 Crowley translation was published in Buenos Aires.
5. She later established a lasting home and professional practice in New York.
6. Paris remained important through her work with Éric Laurent, Jacques-Alain Miller, and the institutions of the Freudian Field.

No source establishes a single definitive emigration date. A permanent New York presence is securely documented by 1988, when the *Newsletter of the Freudian Field* listed her as the contact for a New York study group and gave telephone numbers and a SoHo mailing address. [S7, S8]

## Psychoanalytic Career in New York

By the late 1980s Ayerza was participating in Stuart Schneiderman’s New York Lacanian milieu. The 1988 *Newsletter of the Freudian Field* listed her as a contact for meetings and gave 133 Wooster Street, apartment 7R, as the mailing address later used by *Lacanian Ink*. [S7]

She established a private psychoanalytic practice in New York. Both the journal’s early web edition and later professional directories identify her as a practicing analyst. [S4, S30]

Her institutional affiliations include:

- World Association of Psychoanalysis.
- New Lacanian School.
- Freudian Field networks.
- Consultant to *Lacan Quotidien* or associated international communications. [S5, S31]

There is no public case history, disciplinary finding, malpractice action, licensing proceeding, or patient complaint identified in the research conducted for this dossier.

## Teaching

In a retrospective interview, Ayerza reported teaching Lacan at:

- New York University’s literature department.
- NYU’s video department.
- NYU Steinhardt’s art department.
- Deutsches Haus at NYU.
- Cooper Union.
- Barnard College, in the context of study with Stuart Schneiderman. [S3]

She credited Rena Grant with bringing her into the literature department and Jan Avgikos with introducing her to Nancy Barton in the art department. At NYU Steinhardt, she said she taught for five consecutive years, successively using Lacan’s Seminar XI, Seminar XX, and Seminar X.

These appear to have been seminars, courses, or invited teaching rather than a tenure-line academic appointment. No university catalogue or personnel record establishing title, exact years, or employment status was located.

## Founding of *Lacanian Ink*

Ayerza founded *Lacanian Ink* in New York in fall 1990. Its first issue was dated 20 October 1990. [S6, S24]

Her immediate model was Ellie Ragland’s *Newsletter of the Freudian Field*. Ayerza later wrote that the newsletter rejected or declined her writing because her English was considered inadequate. She recalled Stuart Schneiderman, then both her analyst and a member of the newsletter’s board, suggesting that she publish in Miami because many people there spoke Spanish. Ayerza described feeling offended and angry; the rejection helped precipitate her decision to establish her own journal. This is **self-report**, with no response from Schneiderman located. [S6]

The first issue contained:

- “To Begin With…”
- “Jacques-Alain Miller’s Perversion.”

Ayerza recalled that Germán García harshly criticized the Miller article, reducing her to tears. [S3]

The journal combined:

- Lacanian clinical and theoretical writing.
- Philosophy.
- Political theory.
- Contemporary art criticism.
- Poetry and experimental writing.
- Interviews and visual portfolios.

Ayerza served as publisher and editor. The early web edition identified her unequivocally as both. [S4]

The publication made English translations of Jacques-Alain Miller and other French or European Lacanian thinkers accessible to American readers. Its recurring contributors included Miller, Slavoj Žižek, Alain Badiou, Éric Laurent, Joan Copjec, Bruce Fink, Renata Salecl, Richard Foreman, Adrian Dannatt, Raphael Rubinstein, John Yau, and others. [S4, S24]

## Lacan.com and Digital Publishing

Ayerza created Lacan.com in 1997. [S1]

The site developed into an extensive, independently maintained archive containing:

- Texts by and about Jacques Lacan.
- Jacques-Alain Miller’s seminars and essays.
- Contributions by Žižek, Badiou, Laurent, and other analysts or philosophers.
- Art criticism and artist bibliographies.
- *Lacanian Ink* archives.
- The online journal *The Symptom*.
- Video and photographic materials.

Žižek later called Lacan.com the best of the numerous websites devoted to Lacan and described Ayerza as “indefatigable.” [S25]

The site’s editorial structure reflects Ayerza’s characteristic merging of psychoanalysis, fashion, photography, gossip, philosophy, and contemporary art rather than the architecture of a conventional university archive.

## New York Art World and Cultural Events

Ayerza lived in SoHo during the neighborhood’s late gallery era. In a 2009 interview she described walking home from shopping and entering galleries as part of ordinary neighborhood life, then witnessing the art market’s shift toward Chelsea. [S10]

From 1999 onward she organized public events tied to new issues of *Lacanian Ink*. Venues included:

- The New Museum of Contemporary Art.
- The Drawing Center.
- Deitch Projects.
- Jack Tilton Gallery.
- Pratt Institute.
- Other New York galleries and academic venues.

Documented events include:

- **14 October 1999, New Museum:** Žižek spoke at the journal’s first public launch event.
- **30 November 2000, Deitch Projects:** Launch of issue 17, with Badiou reading “Art and Philosophy.”
- **14 November 2001, Tilton Gallery:** Žižek spoke on “Passions of the Real: Violence in the Twentieth Century.”
- **10 March 2003, Deitch Projects:** Žižek presented “Love Without Mercy.”
- **4 December 2003, Drawing Center:** Badiou presented “Fifteen Theses on Contemporary Art.”
- **1 April 2005, Deitch Projects:** Launch of issue 24/25, with Badiou on “The Subject of Art.”
- **16 November 2006, Pratt Institute:** Ayerza introduced Badiou’s “Truth: Art as a Place for Politics without Place.”
- **18 November 2007, Jack Tilton Gallery:** Badiou spoke about Marcel Duchamp.
- **28 November 2007:** Ayerza introduced Žižek’s “Ecology—A New Opium for the Masses.”
- **23 April 2009, Jack Tilton Gallery:** Launch associated with issue 33. [S9, S17, S26]

One Žižek event at Deitch Projects became so crowded that police dispersed or restricted the audience. Adrian Dannatt presented this as colorful art-world reportage, not as evidence of wrongdoing by Ayerza. [S16]

## Intellectual and Artistic Development

Ayerza’s career can be divided into overlapping phases rather than a simple conversion from occultism to psychoanalysis:

1. **1960s–early 1970s:** design, fashion, rock culture, and Buenos Aires counterculture.
2. **1970s:** tarot, qabalah, ceremonial magic, journalism, European travel, and contact with Thelema.
3. **Late 1970s–1980s:** transition into Lacanian study and analysis while maintaining art-world connections.
4. **1990 onward:** synthesis of psychoanalysis, contemporary art, philosophy, publishing, and event production.

Her editorial program emphasized the Lacanian concepts of the gaze, symptom, objet *a*, desire, sexuality, hysteria, perversion, anxiety, and jouissance.

Ayerza described *Lacanian Ink* through the metaphor of the stain or scotoma: an object meant to disturb American culture and to operate like the analyst’s silence. [S3, S29] She also acknowledged both perseverance and obstinacy as elements of her editorial character.

In a 2009 interview she opposed the reduction of Lacan to a tool of American cultural studies, arguing that Lacanians rather than cultural-studies academics should control the presentation of Lacanian theory. [S10] This is an intellectual and institutional position, not a neutral description of the field.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Oscar Masotta

**Self-reported teacher and formative influence.** Ayerza met Masotta in Buenos Aires and discussed Lacan with him in informal bar settings. She later described this as “a punk thing to do.” [S3]

### Germán García

**Documented teacher, critic, friend, and institutional intermediary.** Ayerza called García her teacher. He criticized her first *Lacanian Ink* essay severely, but their later relationship was affectionate and durable. García wrote dedications calling her “Finita” and describing New York’s door as Finita. Jacques-Alain Miller stated that García had recommended Ayerza to him in Buenos Aires. [S3, S13, S34]

### Stuart Schneiderman

**Documented analyst and teacher; later source of conflict.** Ayerza underwent six years of analysis with him and studied with him at Barnard. Her account of his Miami remark locates him indirectly in the origin story of *Lacanian Ink*. [S3, S6]

### Éric Laurent

**Documented analyst and collaborator.** Ayerza said Laurent conducted the concluding phase of her analysis in Paris. His work became prominent in *Lacanian Ink*. [S3]

### Jacques-Alain Miller

**Documented intellectual patron, contributor, and ally.** Miller’s writings were central to *Lacanian Ink*. Miller later described Ayerza as a loyal friend and said she had hosted Badiou and Žižek in New York for years with his agreement. [S34]

### Slavoj Žižek

**Documented contributor, speaker, and public ally.** Ayerza interviewed Žižek for *Flash Art* and *Lusitania*, published his work regularly, and repeatedly hosted his New York lectures. Žižek publicly praised Lacan.com. [S17, S25, S35]

### Alain Badiou

**Documented contributor, editorial-board member, speaker, and guest.** Badiou became closely associated with the journal around 2000 and spoke at numerous Ayerza-organized events. Ayerza introduced his 2006 Pratt lecture at his invitation. [S17, S24]

### David Lebón

**Reported romantic partner in the early 1970s.** Juan Gatti recalled that Lebón was “with Finita Ayerza” during the Pescado Rabioso period. No further details are established. [S19]

### Susana Lippschitz

**Reported friend, traveling companion, and fellow Crowley translator.** They reportedly traveled to New York together and encountered Tahuti Lodge in 1977. [S12]

### Guillermo Kuitca

**Documented artist-critic relationship.** Ayerza interviewed, promoted, and wrote about Kuitca. Dannatt credited her with his New York emergence, though that claim is best treated as art-world testimony rather than exclusive attribution. [S16, S27]

### Rena Grant, Jan Avgikos, and Nancy Barton

**Self-reported teaching network.** Grant facilitated Ayerza’s NYU literature teaching; Avgikos introduced her to Barton, who invited her into NYU’s art department. [S3]

### Adriana Rosenberg and Renato Rita

**Reported publishing collaborators.** They assisted the limited 1981 Buenos Aires edition of Ayerza’s Crowley translation. [S12]

An online psychoanalytic and cultural journal hosted within Lacan.com. Ayerza contributed writings and oversaw or participated in its editorial environment.

### Galleries and museums

Her events depended on partnerships with Deitch Projects, Jack Tilton Gallery, the Drawing Center, New Museum, and academic institutions. No evidence located shows equity ownership, investment, or employment in these institutions.

Secondary accounts call Ayerza an art dealer, “marchand,” or patron. [S14, S16] Her documented activity includes criticism, introductions, event production, and advocacy. No gallery company, dealer registration, sales records, or financial arrangements have been located. Accordingly, specific claims about art-market income or commissions remain unresolved.

The SoHo address at 133 Wooster Street functioned as both residence or contact point and journal address. Ownership versus tenancy is unknown.

A 2026 obituary for a possible namesake mentions the condominium at Avenida Alvear 1402 in Buenos Aires. Identification with the subject and any ownership interest are unresolved. [S33]

### Patents and inventions

No patent under Ayerza’s name was located. Her reported plastic-furniture designs remain unidentified.

## Political Activity

Ayerza is not documented as holding political office, belonging to a political party, financing a campaign, or standing for election.

Her publishing network disseminated writers associated with Marxism, post-Marxism, psychoanalytic political theory, feminism, and critiques of capitalism. Publishing these authors does not by itself establish identical personal beliefs.

A Freudian Field bulletin records Ayerza circulating information in New York concerning Rafah Nached, a Syrian psychoanalyst detained by the Syrian government, as part of an international advocacy effort. Žižek was reported as signing the campaign. This is documented professional-political advocacy. [S38]

Her events often treated war, ideology, political violence, ecology, capitalism, and communism, but the available record presents her primarily as editor and organizer rather than political activist or theoretician.

### English-language rejection and Schneiderman dispute

**Self-reported dispute.** Ayerza said that her English was judged inadequate by the *Newsletter of the Freudian Field* and that Schneiderman told her to publish in Miami. She treated the remark as insulting. No response from Schneiderman or the newsletter’s editors was found. [S6]

### Germán García’s criticism

**Self-reported professional conflict.** García strongly criticized her essay “Jacques-Alain Miller’s Perversion,” causing her to cry. The substantive basis of his criticism has not been recovered. [S3]

### Crowding and police intervention

**Reported event-management controversy, not criminal conduct.** Police dispersed or controlled a severely overcrowded Žižek event at Deitch Projects. The surviving account treats the intervention humorously and does not allege charges or wrongdoing. [S16]

### Clinical credentials and psychoanalytic status

Ayerza is consistently identified within Lacanian institutions as a psychoanalyst. Publicly accessible sources do not state whether she held a New York license as psychologist, psychiatrist, clinical social worker, or licensed psychoanalyst. Absence of a located license is not evidence that she practiced unlawfully; the matter remains unresolved.

### Intellectual controversy by association

*Lacanian Ink* published Jacques-Alain Miller’s presentations of Lacan and operated within a field marked by disputes over control, editing, and authorization of Lacan’s seminars. No source located accuses Ayerza personally of altering Lacan’s work, misrepresenting authorization, or violating copyright.

### Editorial partiality

Ayerza explicitly rejected the authority of cultural-studies academics over Lacanian interpretation and centered Miller’s orientation in the journal. [S10] Critics may regard that as sectarian or partisan, but the present research found no developed published polemic directed specifically against Ayerza.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Self-Created Mythology

### “Black Magick witch”

- **Story:** Adrian Dannatt called Ayerza a “Black Magick witch,” “one-time Tarot goddess,” Crowley translator, plastic-furniture designer, and the woman who launched Kuitca in New York.
- **Appearance:** Art-world reportage reproduced on Lacan.com in connection with an overcrowded Žižek event.
- **Evidence:** Her tarot work and Crowley translation are documented. No evidence establishes supernatural practice, efficacy, or a formal identity as a witch.
- **Assessment:** A flamboyant characterization mixing fact, publicity, and art-world mythology. [S16]

### “Third World female magician”

- **Story:** A London ICA press release promoted her as a “Third World female magician” connected to Occidental and Oriental esoteric circles.
- **Circulation:** Cultural-event publicity during the 1970s.
- **Evidence:** She did perform tarot and had transnational esoteric contacts. The ethnic-geographic framing was promotional and exoticizing.
- **Effect:** It converted an Argentine tarot practitioner into a spectacle suitable for European “Latin American Week” programming. [S21]

### Yeshira, celebrated seer of Buenos Aires

- **Story:** Under the name Yeshira, Ayerza was among the foremost card readers of 1970s Buenos Aires.
- **Evidence:** Newspaper advertisements, later historical accounts, testimony of a student, and María Moreno’s use of her predictions.
- **Assessment:** The existence and reputation of the persona are documented; divinatory accuracy is not historically testable. [S11, S14, S18]

### Miller and “lalangue”

Ayerza recalled Miller smiling and saying that *Lacanian Ink* was what he called “lalangue,” then immediately added, “I must have made it up.” [S3]

This is a rare explicit example of Ayerza identifying uncertainty within her own founding mythology. It should not be quoted as a securely documented Miller statement.

### “New York is big, but the door is Finita”

Germán García’s reported dedication became a frequently repeated description of Ayerza as the gate or host through which Latin American and European Lacanians entered New York. The actual dedication is reported by Ayerza and echoed by Miller’s account of her hospitality. [S13, S34]

### Host to Badiou and Žižek

Miller stated that Ayerza hosted Badiou and Žižek for years with his agreement, until they chose to end the arrangement. His published remark breaks off before explaining the circumstances. [S34] Any theory of a quarrel, political split, or financial dispute would therefore be speculation.

### Ayerza as Kuitca’s discoverer

Dannatt’s claim that she “launched Guillermo Kuitca in New York” contributed to her reputation as an art-world patron. Her early writing and advocacy are documented, but Kuitca’s international career involved numerous dealers, curators, and institutions. The exclusive version is best classified as **promotional mythology built around documented advocacy**.

## Death, Later Life, and Present Status

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

If the 2026 death notice concerns another person, Ayerza’s status is that of a living but publicly inactive or less active figure. If it concerns her, institutional assessment of her posthumous reputation has barely begun.

Her established historical significance lies in four areas:

1. **Transmission of Lacanian thought in English.**  
   *Lacanian Ink* gave sustained English-language space to Miller, Laurent, Žižek, Badiou, and other figures at a time when Lacanian clinical and philosophical material was less accessible in the United States.

2. **Joining psychoanalysis to contemporary art.**  
   Ayerza did not operate only as a clinical editor. She placed psychoanalytic theory beside artist interviews, gallery criticism, fashion, photography, poetry, and event culture.

3. **Independent digital archiving.**  
   Lacan.com became a major non-university repository of Lacanian and adjacent theoretical writing.

4. **Continuity between esotericism and psychoanalysis.**  
   Her earlier life as Yeshira, tarot teacher, Thelemic traveler, and Crowley translator complicates the official short biography of the sober Lacanian editor. The two phases share interests in language, interpretation, symbolic systems, ritual, desire, and privileged forms of reading, even though no source shows Ayerza publicly theorizing the continuity.

Her reputation varies by milieu:

- In Lacanian networks: editor, institutional ally, translator of theory, host, and organizer.
- In the New York art world: eccentric intellectual impresario and critic.
- In Argentine esoteric history: Yeshira, Crowley translator, and influential tarot reader.
- In hostile or comic mythology: “Black Magick witch” and theory-world celebrity.
- In bibliographic systems: editor of numbered journal-volumes sometimes misleadingly catalogued as co-authored books.

## Chronology

- **1950:** Born in Buenos Aires; exact date and parents unknown.
- **1960s:** Later associated with a Buenos Aires circle experimenting with fashion, interiors, and design.
- **Early 1970s:** Connected with the Buenos Aires rock milieu; reportedly partnered with David Lebón.
- **1970s:** Practices tarot under the name Yeshira; advertises consultations in *La Opinión* and *La Nación*.
- **1970s:** Travels to Europe; performs tarot during Latin American programming at London’s ICA.
- **1970s:** Meets Oscar Masotta; studies Lacan with Germán García.
- **1977:** Reportedly travels to New York with Susana Lippschitz, meets members of Tahuti Lodge, and teaches Kabbalistic tarot.
- **Late 1970s:** Moves through Paris and New York; exact sequence remains uncertain.
- **22 September 1981:** Reported publication date of her Buenos Aires Spanish translation of Crowley’s *El libro de la ley*.
- **1980s:** Establishes herself in New York; undergoes analysis with Stuart Schneiderman and studies with him at Barnard.
- **1988:** Listed as a contact for New York Lacanian meetings.
- **Late 1980s:** Concludes her analysis with Éric Laurent in Paris; timing uncertain.
- **1989:** Attends or reports on a Paris–New York workshop and Jacques-Alain Miller’s lecture on perversion.
- **20 October 1990:** First issue of *Lacanian Ink* appears.
- **1991–1996:** Develops the journal while publishing extensive art criticism and interviews.
- **1993:** Interviews Richard Rorty and writes about Kuitca, Galán, Delvoye, and Sue Williams.
- **1994:** Participates in Wolfgang Staehle’s THE THING online-art community in Tribeca.
- **1997:** Creates Lacan.com.
- **14 October 1999:** Organizes first *Lacanian Ink* public launch event at the New Museum with Žižek.
- **2000:** Hosts Badiou at the issue 17 launch; Badiou becomes closely associated with the journal.
- **2001:** Organizes Tilton Gallery event with Žižek.
- **2003:** Organizes “Love Without Mercy” with Žižek and “Fifteen Theses on Contemporary Art” with Badiou.
- **2005:** Organizes Deitch Projects launch on eroticism with Badiou.
- **2006:** Publishes “Planning Stages: Guillermo Kuitca” in *Artforum*; introduces Badiou at Pratt Institute.
- **2007:** Organizes further Badiou and Žižek lectures in New York.
- **2008:** Publishes on art and waste in *Le Nouvel Âne*.
- **2009:** Gives extended interview on psychoanalysis, cultural studies, SoHo, and the journal.
- **2010s:** Continues publishing *Lacanian Ink*, Lacan.com, art criticism, and Freudian Field materials.
- **2015:** Her online “Short Bibliography” ends, though the journal continues.
- **2017 or later:** Issue 50 marks the journal’s fiftieth number; Ayerza publishes a retrospective account of its origins.
- **Later years:** Issues 51–56 continue under her editorship.
