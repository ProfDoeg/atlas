# Albertus Magnus: Research Dossier

**Prepared:** 24 August 2026  
**Scope:** Chronological life history; institutions, intellectual program, political and ecclesiastical activity; genuine and attributed works; myths; reception through Thomas Aquinas, Nicholas of Cusa, and Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa.  
**Method note:** Dates before Albert's Paris career are frequently reconstructed from late vitae rather than contemporary records. This dossier marks secure documentation, scholarly inference, hagiography, and legend separately. Medieval titles are retained in Latin where useful. URLs consulted are deduplicated at the end.

## Source Conditions and Limits

Albert left an enormous Latin corpus but no autobiography. The early record is sparse. The first extended lives were written generations after his death, especially within Dominican campaigns to build his cult. Consequently:

- **Secure:** Paris teaching and degree, Dominican offices, Cologne school, arbitration, Regensburg episcopate, papal crusade commission, late Cologne activity, testament, death.
- **Probable but not exact:** birth near 1200 at Lauingen; schooling in northern Italy; Dominican entry in the 1220s; early lector circuit.
- **Late hagiography:** noble Bollstädt parentage; Marian vision preventing him from abandoning study; precocious or miraculous learning; incorrupt corpse.
- **Early-modern legend:** philosopher's stone, transmutation secrets, magical automaton or talking brazen head, occult manuals supposedly written by him.

The principal modern editorial control is the **Editio Coloniensis**, begun in 1951 by the Albertus-Magnus-Institut. It is replacing the more inclusive but text-critically unreliable complete editions of Pierre Jammy (Lyon, 1651) and Auguste/Émile Borgnet (Paris, 1890–1899). The edition plan encompasses more than seventy authentic or critically investigated works in forty-one volumes.

## Family and Ancestry

### What can be established

No contemporary record securely names Albert's parents, siblings, or a noble house. As a Dominican friar and bishop he had no spouse or documented children. “Albert of Lauingen” may be geographical or familial.

### The Bollstädt claim

Later biographies made him a son of a count or knight of **Bollstädt/Bollstadt** and sometimes styled him *Albert von Bollstädt*. Modern scholarship generally treats this as fifteenth-century hagiographic genealogy, not established ancestry. It served two reputational purposes: giving the saint a socially eminent origin and making his choice of mendicant poverty more dramatic. The more cautious hypothesis is a family in the ministerial or urban elite, but evidence is insufficient to choose securely.

### Financial position and testament

Dominican profession required personal poverty, so Albert did not operate a business, estate, or investment structure in a modern sense. Episcopal office controlled diocesan revenues institutionally, not as private capital. His surviving last will, dated **January 1279**, shows that he could dispose of funds or claims within ecclesiastical norms. He bequeathed money that assisted completion of the large choir of the Cologne Dominican church. Later Dominican architecture historians note that this ambitious choir exceeded the order's normal restrictions and that Albert supported it against internal opposition. This is the clearest documented financial/property episode associated with him.

## Childhood and Early Life (c. 1200–1220s)

The traditional place is Lauingen, a Danube town. Exact childhood circumstances, household, literacy training, and first school are unknown. No reliable childhood anecdote survives independently of later sanctity narratives.

Albert likely received the Latin preparation required for advanced study. Later accounts place him at **Padua**, an important northern Italian center for liberal arts, medicine, and Aristotelian learning. They sometimes name **Jordan of Saxony**, the charismatic second master general of the Dominicans, as the recruiter who drew him into the order. The broad outline—Italian study followed by Dominican entry—is plausible; exact dates and the claim that he formally studied at the University of Padua remain debated because the sources are late.

## Education and Formation

### Dominican entry

Albert joined the Order of Preachers probably in the **1220s**, traditionally 1223 or 1229. He then underwent Dominican philosophical and theological formation, possibly at Bologna and other houses. A later story says the Virgin Mary appeared when he feared intellectual failure or planned to leave, promised him exceptional learning, and foretold that it would fail near the end of his life. This is hagiography, not contemporary testimony, but later became a narrative explanation of both his encyclopedic achievement and reported final cognitive decline.

### Intellectual sources

Albert's formation cannot be reduced to “Christianizing Aristotle.” His writings assimilate and criticize:

- Aristotle in the Latin translations then available;
- Greek commentators transmitted in Latin;
- Avicenna (Ibn Sīnā), Averroes (Ibn Rushd), al-Ghazālī, and other Arabic philosophical/scientific authorities;
- the *Liber de causis*, then thought Aristotelian but derived from Proclus;
- Augustine, Pseudo-Dionysius, Boethius, Gregory the Great, and Latin theological traditions;
- Jewish thinkers, including material transmitted from Maimonides and other sources;
- empirical reports from hunters, fishers, miners, artisans, physicians, travelers, and his own observation.

He distinguished the methods of philosophy and revealed theology without treating them as rival sovereignties. Philosophy should examine natural causes by reason and observation; theology begins from revelation. His program made a very large body of Greek-Arabic learning usable inside Latin Dominican education.

## Early Career and Teaching Circuit (c. 1230s–1241)

Albert served as a Dominican lector in German-speaking houses. Traditional itineraries include **Cologne, Hildesheim, Freiburg im Breisgau, Regensburg, and Strasbourg**, though exact order and dates vary. His first datable writing, *De natura boni* (On the Nature of the Good), belongs to the first half of the 1230s. Early theological works include *De sacramentis*, *De incarnatione*, *De resurrectione*, *De quattuor coaequaevis*, and *De homine*, subsequently related to the unfinished Parisian theological synthesis often called *Summa de creaturis* or *Summa Parisiensis*.

The circuit made him both teacher and institutional organizer before his university credential. It also exposed him to the natural environments later described in his zoological, botanical, geographical, and mineralogical writing.

## Paris: University Master and the Encounter with Aquinas (c. 1241–1248)

Around **1241**, the order sent Albert to the University of Paris. He lectured on Peter Lombard's *Sentences*, the normal route to the theology doctorate, under or in the academic milieu of **Guerric of Saint-Quentin**. He obtained the mastership in theology in **1245**, commonly described as the first German Dominican to do so, and occupied a Dominican chair at Saint-Jacques.

Paris was the principal arena for disputes over the new mendicant orders and the rapidly expanding Aristotelian curriculum. Albert wrote biblical and theological commentaries while developing a program of philosophical paraphrase: not merely literal commentary, but reordering, explaining, supplementing, and sometimes correcting Aristotle.

### Thomas Aquinas

Thomas Aquinas studied with Albert in Paris and followed him to Cologne in 1248. The familiar story that classmates called Thomas the “Dumb Ox,” after which Albert predicted that his bellow would fill the world, first survives in later Aquinas hagiography. The teacher-pupil relationship itself is secure; the exact aphorism is not.

Albert supplied Thomas with:

- an institutional place within Dominican study;
- a model for engaging Aristotle, Avicenna, Averroes, and natural philosophy;
- the distinction and traffic between philosophical demonstration and theology;
- a research culture in which inherited authorities could be tested.

Thomas did not simply reproduce Albert. Their metaphysics, psychology, cosmology, and theories of intellect diverged in important places. “Albert founded Thomism” is therefore too simple; he created much of the intellectual and institutional environment within which Thomas formed his own synthesis.

## Cologne *Studium Generale* (1248–1254)

In **1248**, Albert returned to Cologne with Thomas and other students to establish or head a general house of studies for the German Dominican province. This was not the University of Cologne, which was founded only in 1388. Calling Albert its literal founder is anachronistic; calling him a spiritual or institutional ancestor of Cologne's university culture is defensible.

The Cologne years produced major philosophical and scientific work. Albert began the vast project announced in his *Physica*: to give Dominican brothers a complete and intelligible account of natural philosophy based on Aristotle, supplemented where texts were absent or inadequate. The sequence eventually covered logic, physics, cosmology, generation and corruption, meteorology, soul, animals, plants, minerals, metaphysics, ethics, and politics.

### Natural inquiry

Albert valued *experimentum*—experience, skilled report, repeated observation—while remaining a thirteenth-century Aristotelian rather than a modern experimental scientist. In *De animalibus* he incorporated translated Aristotelian zoology, Thomas of Cantimpré and other sources, plus northern European observations. He disputed some inherited marvels, described fish, birds, falconry, reproduction, and anatomy, but also transmitted erroneous reports and the gender assumptions of medieval medicine.

In *De vegetabilibus et plantis*, he treated plant form, growth, grafting, habitat, and classification. *De mineralibus* discussed stones, metals, intermediates, causes, mines, and the possibility of artificial transformation. *De natura locorum* linked geography, climate, latitude, and local qualities. His science combined observation with causal theory, astrology, humoral medicine, and inherited lore.

## Provincial Prior, Mendicant Conflict, and Educational Policy (1254–1259)

Albert became provincial prior of **Teutonia** in **1254**, responsible for a geographically large Dominican province. He visited houses, governed personnel, preached, and represented the order.

### Paris mendicant controversy

Secular masters led by **William of Saint-Amour** attacked the friars' university privileges and even their religious legitimacy. In **1256**, Albert, **Bonaventure**, and Dominican master general **Humbert of Romans** represented the mendicants before the papal curia at Anagni. Pope Alexander IV condemned William's *De periculis novissimorum temporum* on 5 October 1256. This was an institutional and ideological victory for the mendicant orders, not a criminal proceeding against Albert.

### Dominican curriculum

After resigning as provincial in June **1257**, Albert returned to Cologne. At the Dominican General Chapter at **Valenciennes in 1259**, Albert, Thomas Aquinas, Bonushomo Britto, Florentius, and Peter of Tarentaise (later Innocent V) helped formulate a *ratio studiorum*. The program gave philosophy a structured preparatory role in Dominican education. It institutionalized the intellectual turn with which Albert is associated.

## Cologne Politics and the “Great Arbitration” (1258)

Cologne was divided between archiepiscopal lordship and an increasingly self-governing citizenry. Albert acted repeatedly as mediator. The best-known settlement, the **Großer Schied** (“Great Arbitration”) of **1258**, addressed conflict between Archbishop **Konrad von Hochstaden** and the city. Albert's role was juridical and political: using learned categories of authority, custom, jurisdiction, and the common peace to specify rights rather than abolish conflict permanently.

He was not a modern democrat and did not oppose episcopal government as such. He sought *pro bono pacis*—settlement for the good of peace—within the corporate and feudal order. Later civic and Catholic memory turned this work into a central image of Albert as peacemaker.

## Bishop of Regensburg (1260–1262)

Pope **Alexander IV** appointed Albert bishop of Regensburg on **5 January 1260**, against the preference of Dominican leadership and apparently against Albert's own inclination. The diocese was financially and administratively troubled. His episcopal actions included visitation, ordination, adjudication, management of obligations, and attempts at fiscal repair.

Hagiographic biographies call him *Bundschuh* (“hobnailed shoe”) because he allegedly traversed the diocese on foot rather than with episcopal display. His austerity is plausible within Dominican identity, but the picturesque nickname rests on later tradition.

Albert soon sought release. In December 1260 he traveled toward the papal curia at Viterbo. **Urban IV** accepted the resignation around November **1261**; a successor was confirmed in May **1262**. Accounts that he served a full three years are rounded or imprecise. The short tenure can be read as institutional mismatch rather than scandal: a scholar-friar was placed over a difficult prince-bishopric and exited after obtaining papal permission. No evidence establishes embezzlement, corruption, or canonical misconduct.

He retained episcopal rank and could later conduct confirmations, consecrations, and other para-episcopal functions.

## Papal Service, Crusade Preaching, Würzburg and Strasbourg (1262–1269)

Urban IV commissioned Albert as papal preacher for a crusade to the Holy Land throughout German-speaking territories from roughly **February 1263 to October 1264**. This directly connected him to the political-religious machinery of crusading: authorization, fundraising, vows, and mobilization for armed expedition. Surviving summaries do not support attributing particular battlefield violence to Albert personally, but the office was advocacy for a military-religious campaign and belongs in the record without euphemism.

After Urban's death ended the commission, Albert lived and taught in **Würzburg** and **Strasbourg** (approximately 1264–1269), while continuing major works including the *Metaphysica*. His movements across the Empire were substantial for an elderly friar and help explain the geographic observations scattered through his natural works.

## Final Cologne Period (1269–1280)

In **1269**, Dominican master general **John of Vercelli** called Albert back to Cologne as *lector emeritus*. He wrote, preached, consecrated churches and altars, arbitrated disputes, and advised religious communities.

### Aquinas's death and the 1277 condemnations

Aquinas died in **1274**. Later narratives say Albert wept whenever Thomas's name was mentioned. In **1277**, Bishop Étienne Tempier of Paris condemned 219 propositions associated with radical Aristotelianism; some formulations touched positions connected, fairly or unfairly, with Thomas. A long-lived story says the aged Albert traveled to Paris to defend his former pupil. Modern biographers regard the journey as unconfirmed. It is secure that Albert remained engaged with disputes over intellect and Aristotelian interpretation; it is not secure that he staged a personal Paris defense.

### Council of Lyons

Church tradition credits him with an important part in the **Second Council of Lyons (1274)**. The details and scale of participation are less clear than devotional summaries imply; he was an eminent bishop-theologian in the relevant network, but claims should not outrun specific conciliar documentation.

### Testament, illness, and death

Albert's will of **January 1279** describes him as sound in mind and body. Sources suggest decline beginning later that year or around 1278–79, variously characterized as loss of memory, senility, or general physical and cognitive deterioration. No modern diagnosis is possible. The Marian prophecy story retrospectively made this decline the announced end of a supernatural gift of learning.

He died at the Cologne Dominican convent on **15 November 1280**. No specific acute disease is documented. He was buried prominently in the Dominican church choir.

### Technologies and alleged inventions

No patent system existed, and no physical instrument can be securely attributed to him. Later credits for discovering elemental arsenic, inventing chemical processes, creating navigation instruments, or building a robot often convert textual discussion or legend into modern “invention.” Albert discussed arsenical substances and metallurgical transformations, but the claim that he discovered arsenic as a modern chemical element is anachronistic. The automaton is legend, addressed below.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Theology and philosophy

Albert defended the legitimacy of philosophical inquiry while subordinating ultimate theological truth to revelation. His paraphrases sometimes report a philosophical conclusion without adopting it theologically. This layered voice has caused recurrent attribution problems: an expositor's account of Aristotle or Averroes is not automatically Albert's final position.

### Metaphysics and intellect

He developed theories of being, causality, universals, emanation, abstraction, and the human intellect through a complex synthesis of Aristotle, Avicenna, Averroes, the *Liber de causis*, and Dionysian Neoplatonism. He rejected a single separate intellect shared by all human beings while treating intellectual perfection as the human goal. His *De unitate intellectus contra Averroistas* became part of the anti-Averroist controversy.

### Natural philosophy

Albert's naturalism allowed investigators to ask what secondary causes do. He often demanded observation and criticized implausible authority; he also accepted astrology, occult properties, spontaneous generation, and many reports now known false. “First modern scientist” is celebratory anachronism. “Mere compiler” is also inadequate: he organized, compared, supplemented, and sometimes experimentally tested a vast archive.

### Ethics and politics

His ethical works interpret Aristotelian happiness, virtue, friendship, household, and civic order within a Christian horizon. His practical arbitration shows engagement with jurisdiction and political peace, but he wrote no modern theory of popular sovereignty.

### Gender and reproduction

Albert's biological and medical discussions transmit Aristotelian and Galenic hierarchies in which the male is treated as normative and the female sometimes as deficient in generative heat. Conversely, pseudo-Albertine sexual manuals later magnified misogynistic claims under his authority. The genuine and spurious materials must not be conflated, but both influenced European gender lore.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Thomas Aquinas

Securely pupil, Dominican colleague, and independent intellectual heir. Albert taught Thomas at Paris/Cologne and helped create the curriculum supporting his work. The Dumb Ox prophecy, the automaton-smashing episode, and the 1277 Paris defense are later or uncertain narratives.

### Dominican leadership

- **Jordan of Saxony:** traditional recruiter; exact encounter uncertain.
- **Humbert of Romans:** master general; collaborator in defense of mendicants and educational reform.
- **John of Vercelli:** later master general who recalled Albert to Cologne.
- **Peter of Tarentaise (Innocent V), Bonushomo Britto, Florentius:** partners in the 1259 study program.

### University and ecclesiastical associates/opponents

- **Guerric of Saint-Quentin:** associated with Albert's Paris formation.
- **Bonaventure:** Franciscan master and ally in the 1256 mendicant defense, not simply a philosophical rival.
- **William of Saint-Amour:** institutional opponent of mendicant university privileges.
- **Alexander IV:** supported mendicants and appointed Albert bishop.
- **Urban IV:** accepted the episcopal resignation and commissioned crusade preaching.
- **Konrad von Hochstaden and Cologne civic leaders:** parties in Albert's arbitration environment.
- **Averroes:** deceased intellectual interlocutor, frequently criticized but deeply important as interpreter of Aristotle.

### Nicholas of Cusa (1401–1464): posthumous intellectual line

No personal relationship was possible. Cusanus studied at Cologne in an environment shaped by late medieval **Albertism**. Scholarship identifies Albert as one of his sources, alongside Proclus, Dionysius, Thierry of Chartres, and Ramon Llull. The influence includes Neoplatonic metaphysics, intellectual ascent, causality, and approaches to the coincidence and participation of finite things in the divine. It is mediated through Albert's texts and the Cologne school, not a simple master-pupil succession.

### Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa (1486–1535): posthumous occult line

Agrippa studied in Cologne, where Albertist traditions and pseudo-Albertine occult works circulated. His *De occulta philosophia* recovered the medieval natural-magic legacy “of Albertus Magnus, above all,” especially occult virtues of stones, plants, animals, celestial causation, and the boundary between natural effects and illicit demonic operations. Agrippa inherited both **authentic Albert** and **Pseudo-Albert**. Treating Albert himself as the author of all texts Agrippa could read under his name reproduces Renaissance misattribution.

## Controversies, Accusations, and Institutional Conflicts

### Documented conflicts

1. **Mendicant-secular university conflict (1250s):** Albert defended Dominican and Franciscan privileges; William of Saint-Amour's attack was condemned by the papacy. This was a struggle over chairs, preaching, corporate jurisdiction, and religious legitimacy.
2. **Cologne church-city struggles:** Albert served as mediator rather than neutral outsider; his settlements operated within episcopal and corporate power structures.
3. **Crusade preaching:** documented papal commission in 1263–64. It associated him with recruitment for religious war.
4. **Dominican architectural dispute:** Albert supported the unusually large Cologne choir against leading members' objections about order norms.
5. **Aristotelian/Averroist controversy:** he appropriated Arabic philosophy while refuting positions he considered incompatible with individual immortality and Christian doctrine.

### Matters for which no evidence was found

No documentary basis was found for criminal charges, imprisonment, exile, corruption, fraud, money laundering, secret intelligence service, sexual scandal, addiction, or personal political violence. His magical and alchemical “crimes” belong primarily to attribution and legend, not a recorded prosecution.

### Scientific errors and retrospective controversy

Albert combined unusually attentive observation with inherited falsehoods: astrology as causal science, occult mineral/organic virtues, humoral physiology, sexual and gender stereotypes, marvelous animals, and mistaken reproductive biology. These are documented intellectual positions or transmissions, not evidence of fraud. He sometimes explicitly tested reports and rejected them; elsewhere he copied or rationalized them.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. Noble son of Bollstädt

- **Story:** Albert was born to a count or noble knight of Bollstädt.
- **Appearance/circulation:** late medieval Dominican vitae, especially fifteenth-century cult-building.
- **Evidence:** no contemporary genealogy securely connects him to that house.
- **Effect:** made mendicant renunciation more exemplary and supplied the enduring surname “von Bollstädt.”
- **Status:** **hagiographic conjecture**.

### 2. The Virgin grants and withdraws learning

- **Story:** discouraged by study, Albert intended to leave the order; Mary appeared, granted him encyclopedic wisdom, and foretold that before death he would revert to childlike forgetfulness.
- **Appearance/circulation:** late Dominican hagiography; popular devotional lives.
- **Evidence:** no contemporary record; constructed neatly around reports of final cognitive decline.
- **Effect:** converted intellectual achievement into Marian grace and illness into fulfilled prophecy.
- **Status:** **hagiographic legend** resting on a possibly factual late decline.

### 3. The Dumb Ox prophecy

- **Story:** when students mocked silent Thomas Aquinas as a dumb ox, Albert said his bellow would fill the world.
- **Appearance/circulation:** later Aquinas vitae, notably the hagiographic tradition associated with William of Tocco.
- **Evidence:** teacher-pupil relation is documented; the scene and words are not contemporary.
- **Effect:** iconic parable of a teacher recognizing hidden genius.
- **Status:** **later hagiographic anecdote**.

### 4. The speaking automaton or brazen head

- **Story:** Albert spent many years constructing a brass man/head capable of speech or reasoning; Thomas smashed it because its chatter interrupted study. Some versions invoke astrology, spirits, or a demon.
- **Appearance/circulation:** developed in late medieval/early-modern magic literature and became prominent in Renaissance and Baroque retellings; demonological writers such as Martín del Río helped circulate variants.
- **Evidence:** no contemporary artifact, description, workshop account, or authentic text supports it. The motif belongs to a broader family attached to Gerbert, Virgil, Robert Grosseteste, and Roger Bacon.
- **Effect:** transformed *Magnus* into *magus*, made Albert an ancestor of robotics/artificial intelligence in popular histories, and dramatized Thomas as destroyer of autonomous artificial speech.
- **Status:** **legend/folklore**, historically important but not a documented invention.

### 5. Philosopher's stone and gold transmutation

- **Story:** Albert discovered the philosopher's stone, made gold, or transmitted the secret to Thomas.
- **Appearance/circulation:** alchemical manuscripts and printed pseudo-Albertine corpus, expanding especially from the fourteenth through sixteenth centuries.
- **Evidence:** authentic *De mineralibus* seriously analyzes alchemy and reports claims/observations about transmutation, but no secure proof shows he produced gold or possessed a stone. The story that he passed it shortly before his own death is chronologically awkward because Thomas died six years earlier.
- **Effect:** made Albert a canonical master for European alchemists and helped texts sell under his name.
- **Status:** **legend built around genuine interest in mineral transformation**.

### 6. Discovery of arsenic

- **Story:** Albert discovered elemental arsenic around 1250.
- **Evidence:** he discussed arsenical materials and metallurgical operations; isolation in a modern chemical sense is not securely documented, and “element” is anachronistic for his conceptual scheme.
- **Effect:** common science-timeline claim presenting him as proto-chemist.
- **Status:** **overstated retrospective attribution**.

### 7. *De secretis mulierum* (“Secrets of Women”)

- **Story:** Albert wrote an influential account of female sexuality, conception, menstruation, and reproductive secrets.
- **Appearance:** a widely copied and printed medieval text circulated under his name.
- **Evidence:** modern textual scholarship rejects Albert's authorship; it is **Pseudo-Albertus**, though influenced by scholastic natural philosophy.
- **Effect:** Albert's authority lent weight to misogynistic and sexual-medical lore for centuries.
- **Status:** **spurious attribution with major reception history**.

### 8. Incorrupt body

- **Story:** when the tomb was opened three years after death, Albert's body was incorrupt; a later opening found a skeleton.
- **Appearance/circulation:** sanctity tradition; later devotional compilations.
- **Evidence:** modern canonization research says a supposed early opening is legendary or uncertain. A ceremonial reburial on **11 January 1483** is documentable.
- **Effect:** supported claims of sanctity and local cult.
- **Status:** **unverified hagiographic claim**; 1483 translation/reburial is documented.

### 9. Founder of the University of Cologne

- **Story:** Albert founded Germany's medieval University of Cologne.
- **Evidence:** he founded/headed the Dominican *studium* in 1248; the civic university dates from 1388.
- **Effect:** makes him a civic-academic patron and namesake of university honors.
- **Status:** **symbolic ancestry, not literal institutional founding**.

## Works by Albertus Magnus

### Editorial warning

Medieval works rarely have “publication dates” in the print sense. Dates below are approximate composition dates or periods. The most reliable guide is the evolving *Editio Coloniensis*. The 1651 Jammy and 1890–99 Borgnet editions include works whose authenticity is doubtful or false. Anonymous continuators may have worked on Book II of the late *Summa theologiae*.

### Authentic or substantially authentic works (organized by field)

#### Early and systematic theology

- *De natura boni* (first half of 1230s).
- *De sacramentis* (c. 1240s).
- *De incarnatione*; *De resurrectione*; *De quattuor coaequaevis*; *De homine*; *De bono*—parts of the early Parisian theological complex often called *Summa de creaturis* / *Summa Parisiensis* (c. 1240s).
- *Commentarii in quattuor libros Sententiarum* (Commentary on Peter Lombard's Four Books of Sentences, Paris period, c. 1243–49).
- *Summa theologiae sive de mirabili scientia Dei* (late, unfinished; Book II has possible anonymous contributions).

#### Scripture and preaching

- Commentaries on or treatments of the Psalms, Job, the Prophets, Gospels, and Pauline/Dionysian materials, including *Super Iohannem* (On John), *Super Matthaeum*, *Super Lucam*, *Super Marcum*, and *Enarrationes in Psalmos* in the transmitted corpus. Exact authenticity and dating vary by item and edition.
- *Sermones* and collections of sermons: mixed manuscript transmission; individual sermons require critical authentication.

- *De animalibus* (26 books; largely 1250s–c. 1260; translation/paraphrase, additions, and lexicon).
- *De vegetabilibus et plantis* (On Vegetables and Plants).
- *De mineralibus* (On Minerals; five books on stones, metals, and intermediates).
- *De natura locorum* (On the Nature of Places).
- *De causis proprietatum elementorum*.

#### Metaphysics and causal theory

- *Metaphysica* (major work, substantially 1260s).
- *De causis et processu universitatis a prima causa*.
- Commentaries/paraphrases engaging the *Liber de causis* and Neoplatonic causal traditions.

#### Ethics, household, and politics

- *Super Ethica* (earlier commentary/lecture tradition on Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics*).
- *Ethica* (later paraphrase).
- *Politica*.
- *Oeconomica* (transmission/authorship details require edition-level checking).

#### Mathematics and astronomy/astrology

- *Super Euclidem* / commentary on Euclid: survives incompletely or in difficult transmission; attribution and reconstruction are specialist matters.
- *Speculum astronomiae* (Mirror of Astronomy): influential defense/classification of licit astrological literature; authorship has been debated, though important scholarship has defended attribution to Albert or his immediate circle. Mark as **disputed/contested**, not unqualified fact.

### Major pseudo-Albertine or doubtful works

- *De secretis mulierum* — spurious.
- *Liber secretorum* / *Secretum secretorum* traditions — variously spurious or confused with other textual families.
- *Secreta Alberti*, *Experimenta Alberti* — pseudo-Albertine recipe/occult traditions.
- *Speculum alchemiae* — generally treated as pseudo-Albertine.
- *Semita recta* (“The Straight Path”) — pseudo-Albertine alchemical tradition.
- *Libellus de alchimia* / *Compositum de compositis* and numerous alchemical tracts — attribution varies; most are not accepted as authentic.
- *De mirabilibus mundi* — generally pseudo-Albertine/doubtful.
- *De virtutibus herbarum, lapidum et animalium* and the *Liber aggregationis* — pseudo-Albertine magic/secrets complexes.
- *De adhaerendo Deo* — traditional attribution, disputed in modern scholarship.

## Death, Burial, Cult, and Canonization

Albert's prominent Cologne burial reflected episcopal and intellectual status, but immediate canonization proceedings are not documented. The cult grew slowly.

- **11 January 1483:** documented ceremonial reburial/translation.
- **1484:** Innocent VIII permitted Dominican churches at Cologne and Regensburg to dedicate an altar and celebrate an annual feast; Dominican historiography often calls this “beatification,” though the juridical category is not identical to later centralized procedure.
- **Seventeenth century:** liturgical permissions expanded; **1622** is commonly given as beatification/recognition under Gregory XV, but the long process is more complicated than a single modern ceremony.
- **1804:** medieval Dominican church demolished; relics preserved and transferred to St. Andreas.
- **1927–1931:** renewed German/Dominican campaign. Bavarian official Albert Decker, envoy Otto von Ritter, Cardinal Andreas Frühwirth, Angelus Walz, Heribert Christian Scheeben, and Melchior Weiß assembled materials. The 1930 documentation fed an approximately 830-page *Positio*. Petitions presented Albert as an answer to modernism, a German national intellectual, a patron of Christian science, and a model of peace. These were historically situated advocacy arguments, not neutral biography.
- **16 December 1931:** Pius XI approved equipollent canonization/extension of cult and Doctor of the Church status. The bull appeared in 1932.
- **1941:** Pius XII named him patron of students/cultivators of the natural sciences.
- **1954:** relics placed in the third-century Roman sarcophagus now in St. Andreas's crypt.

The canonization was thus both recognition of a long cult and a twentieth-century institutional project involving Dominican revival, German Catholic national representation, neo-scholasticism, and debates over science and faith.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Thirteenth to fourteenth centuries

Albert was renowned as philosopher and encyclopedist, sometimes more than as theologian. Thomas's later dominance both preserved Albert as “teacher of Aquinas” and obscured his distinct positions. His natural works circulated widely.

### Fifteenth-century Albertism and Cusanus

An **Albertist** school emerged in Paris and German universities as one branch of the *via antiqua*, often opposed to Thomist and nominalist positions. Cologne became a major center. Nicholas of Cusa absorbed Albertine and Dionysian/Proclean materials through this environment, transforming them within his own doctrine of learned ignorance and Neoplatonic metaphysics.

### Magus and occult authority

Pseudo-Albertine books of secrets, women's medicine, talismans, plant/stone/animal virtues, and alchemy turned Albert into “Albert the magician.” His authentic willingness to discuss occult properties and astrology made the attribution culturally plausible. Agrippa later incorporated Albertine natural magic into a Renaissance synthesis. The brazen-head story made him a figure in automaton and artificial-life lore.

### Early modern science and print

Natural histories used, excerpted, and mocked Albert. As philology and experimentation changed, he became alternately a symbol of scholastic credulity and a precursor of empirical science. Both stereotypes select real features but distort the mixed method of his work.

### Modern Catholic and academic recovery

Canonization, patronage of natural scientists, and the critical edition repositioned him as a bridge between faith and reason. Modern scholarship increasingly treats him independently of Thomas: as an organizer of knowledge, interpreter of Arabic and Greek philosophy, theorist of nature, and political mediator.

### Names and institutions

Schools, colleges, science buildings, parishes, academic prizes, and professorships carry his name. The University of Cologne's **Albertus Magnus Professorship** explicitly commemorates his 1248–1254 leadership of the Dominican *studium*. His statue on the Cologne campus marks symbolic ancestry, not literal university foundation.

### Balanced historical assessment

The durable legacies are institutional (Dominican curriculum, Cologne studies), textual (large Aristotelian-scientific corpus), relational (Aquinas), philosophical (Albertism, Cusanus), occult-pseudonymous (Agrippa and early-modern magic), civic (arbitration), ecclesiastical (bishop, crusade preacher, saint), and mythic (alchemist, robot-maker). None should be substituted for the others.
