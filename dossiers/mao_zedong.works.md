## Works by Mao Zedong

### Early essays, journalism, and editorial projects

- **1912–1920:** *Early Manuscripts of Mao Zedong*. An obra comprising surviving school essays, marginalia, letters, journalism, and political writings rather than a single book; the official collection itself warns through its date-bounded, archival character that the youthful corpus is incomplete. [[1]](https://book-sp.kodansha.co.jp/content/topics/mao/Mao%20Bibliographies.pdf) [[2]](https://www.routledge.com/Maos-Road-to-Power/book-series/MRP)
- **April 1917:** *A Study of Physical Education*. An argumentative journal essay presenting physical training as moral, intellectual, and national practice. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **April 1917:** *To Miyazaki Tōten*. A brief commemorative address or inscription, not a book. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **July–August 1919:** *To the Glory of the Hans*. An early journalistic text whose surviving English title is editorial rather than demonstrably Mao’s fixed title. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **1919:** *Xiang River Review*. A short-lived periodical project founded and edited by Mao; the newspaper as a collective platform is the made thing, although individual articles within it have separate authorship. [[2]](https://www.routledge.com/Maos-Road-to-Power/book-series/MRP)
- **July–August 1919:** *The Great Union of the Popular Masses*. A serialized manifesto arguing that dispersed social groups should organize themselves into a revolutionary union. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **1919:** *Toward a New Golden Age*. A political newspaper article; its status is that of an occasional intervention rather than a freestanding treatise. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **16 November 1919:** *Miss Zhao’s Suicide*. A sequence of newspaper commentaries turning a particular death into an indictment of coercive marriage and the surrounding social system. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **November 1920–January 1921:** *Communism and Dictatorship*. An early polemical essay marking Mao’s public adoption of communism. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **11 July 1923:** *The Role of the Merchants in the National Revolution*. A tactical political article. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **29 August 1923:** *The Chinese Government and the Foreigners*. A journalistic political intervention. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **1925–1926:** *Political Weekly*. An editorial project directed by Mao for the Kuomintang’s propaganda department; it resists treatment as his individual work because production and authorship were collective. [[2]](https://www.routledge.com/Maos-Road-to-Power/book-series/MRP)

### Investigations and social analyses

- **March 1926:** *Analysis of the Classes in Chinese Society*. A diagnostic political essay classifying social forces by their likely relation to revolution. The familiar text was revised for the official collected edition, so it is not an untouched reproduction of the 1926 publication. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm) [[4]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/sw-in-pdf/sw-flp-1965-v1.pdf)
- **25 November 1926:** *The Bitter Sufferings of the Peasants in Jiangsu and Zhejiang, and Their Movements of Resistance*. A report-like political article. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **March 1927:** *Report on an Investigation of the Peasant Movement in Hunan*. A field report made into a manifesto for recognizing peasant revolutionary agency. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **May 1930:** *Oppose Book Worship*. A methodological manifesto, also circulated under the opening proposition *No Investigation, No Right to Speak*; its surviving version was recovered after having been unavailable when the first four-volume selection was prepared. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **1930:** *Report from Xunwu*. A lengthy social and economic investigation assembled from local inquiry; substantial passages depend on informants, making it a Mao-directed research object rather than purely solitary authorship. [[1]](https://book-sp.kodansha.co.jp/content/topics/mao/Mao%20Bibliographies.pdf)
- **1930–1941:** *Rural Surveys*. An investigative corpus and editorial project gathering Mao’s inquiries into rural society. Mao later supplied a preface and postscript, but asked that the surveys themselves be omitted from the canonical *Selected Works*, so the corpus resists identification with that authorized selection. [[4]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/sw-in-pdf/sw-flp-1965-v1.pdf)
- **March–April 1941:** *Preface and Postscript to Rural Surveys*. Two framing texts that turn field investigation into a protocol for political knowledge. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)

### Revolutionary strategy and military protocols

- **5 October 1928:** *Why Is It That Red Political Power Can Exist in China?* A party report and strategic argument about maintaining revolutionary base areas. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **25 November 1928:** *The Struggle in the Jinggang Mountains*. A strategic report on the revolutionary base area. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **December 1929:** *On Correcting Mistaken Ideas in the Party*. A party resolution drafted by Mao; it functions as a disciplinary protocol and collective institutional act, not merely an essay. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **5 January 1930:** *A Single Spark Can Start a Prairie Fire*. A letter transformed through revision and publication into a general strategic essay; the familiar title was not the original letter’s title. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **1936:** *Problems of Strategy in China’s Revolutionary War*. A systematic military lecture or treatise derived from instruction at the Red Army Academy. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **1937:** *On Guerrilla Warfare*. A military protocol explaining the political and operational principles of guerrilla war. English editions sometimes make it look like a separate Western-style book, but it belongs to Mao’s larger body of military instruction. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **1937:** *Basic Tactics*. A compact military manual attributed to Mao and transmitted through translation; because the early translation history did not rest on a readily verifiable Chinese original, its textual history is less secure than that of the officially edited works. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **May 1938:** *Problems of Strategy in Guerrilla War Against Japan*. A strategic treatise and operational protocol. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **May 1938:** *On Protracted War*. A sequence of lectures made into a strategic treatise predicting the stages and political conditions of the war against Japan. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **October 1938:** *The Role of the Chinese Communist Party in the National War*. A Central Committee report functioning simultaneously as analysis, program, and organizational directive. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **1947:** *Manifesto of the Chinese People’s Liberation Army*. A manifesto issued in the army’s institutional voice; Mao drafted it, but the public speaker is a collective military organization. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **10 October 1947:** *On the Reissue of the Three Main Rules of Discipline and the Eight Points for Attention*. A conduct protocol issued by headquarters under Mao’s authority; its rules evolved collectively over earlier decades and cannot be treated as an entirely solitary composition. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)

### Philosophy, political theory, and cultural doctrine

- **July 1937:** *On Practice*. A philosophical lecture turned essay, presenting knowledge as a movement between practice and cognition. Its canonical wording passed through later editorial revision. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm) [[4]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/sw-in-pdf/sw-flp-1965-v1.pdf)
- **August 1937:** *On Contradiction*. A philosophical lecture turned treatise on dialectical contradiction; like *On Practice*, it survives canonically in a later revised form. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm) [[4]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/sw-in-pdf/sw-flp-1965-v1.pdf)
- **April–June 1938:** *Dialectical Materialism*. Lecture material of which only part survives or has been published securely; its overlap with Soviet philosophical sources and its incomplete transmission make it resist treatment as a finished, self-contained book. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **December 1939:** *The Chinese Revolution and the Chinese Communist Party*. A party textbook project: Mao is credited with the first chapter and with revising the second, while other collaborators participated in the latter, so undivided individual authorship should not be assumed. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **January 1940:** *On New Democracy*. A political-theoretical treatise defining a transitional revolutionary order and acting as its programmatic manifesto. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **May 1941:** *Reform Our Study*. A rectification manifesto prescribing a new relationship among Marxist theory, Chinese history, and empirical investigation. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **1 February 1942:** *Rectify the Party’s Style of Work*. A speech functioning as organizational diagnosis and disciplinary protocol. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **8 February 1942:** *Oppose Stereotyped Party Writing*. A polemical lecture attacking formulaic political language while itself acting as a protocol for revolutionary writing. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **May 1942:** *Talks at the Yan’an Forum on Literature and Art*. Two spoken interventions subsequently edited into a foundational cultural manifesto. Different published texts embody different moments of revision, so “the text” is not a single unchanging transcript. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **December 1942:** *Economic and Financial Problems*. A policy study and administrative protocol for the Communist base areas. Only its opening chapter entered the authorized *Selected Works* at Mao’s request, although the larger work also treated agriculture, livestock, handicrafts, cooperatives, salt, industry, military production, institutional production, and grain. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm) [[4]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/sw-in-pdf/sw-flp-1965-v1.pdf)
- **1 June 1943:** *Some Questions Concerning Methods of Leadership*. An organizational protocol presenting the “mass line” as a repeated movement between leaders and the people. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **29 November 1943:** *Get Organized!*. A speech and mobilizing manifesto for cooperative production. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **8 September 1944:** *Serve the People*. A funeral speech transformed through circulation and study into a compact ethical manifesto. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **24 April 1945:** *On Coalition Government*. A congress report that combines historical narrative, political program, and prospective design for the state. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **11 June 1945:** *The Foolish Old Man Who Removed the Mountains*. A closing congress speech using a traditional fable as a manifesto of revolutionary persistence. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **25 December 1947:** *The Present Situation and Our Tasks*. A political and military report announcing a program for nationwide victory and postwar policy. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **30 June 1949:** *On the People’s Democratic Dictatorship*. A state-founding political treatise and manifesto written for the CCP’s twenty-eighth anniversary. [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **February 1957:** *On the Correct Handling of Contradictions Among the People*. A speech revised for publication into a philosophical and governmental treatise; the published text therefore differs from the original oral event. [[5]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-5/index.htm)
- **March 1957:** *Speech at the Chinese Communist Party’s National Conference on Propaganda Work*. A policy speech prescribing the place of intellectual and cultural activity under socialism. [[5]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-5/index.htm)
- **18 November 1957:** *All Reactionaries Are Paper Tigers*. A retrospective condensation assembled from remarks made on several occasions, rather than one originally unified text. [[5]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-5/index.htm)
- **1958–1960:** *Reading Notes and Talks on Soviet Political Economy*. A series of annotations and seminar remarks, later published as *A Critique of Soviet Economics*. It is an unfinished intellectual project reconstructed by editors, not a book completed and issued by Mao. [[1]](https://book-sp.kodansha.co.jp/content/topics/mao/Mao%20Bibliographies.pdf)
- **1963:** *Where Do Correct Ideas Come From?* A philosophical memorandum extracted from a longer party document; its later independent life as an essay obscures its original institutional setting. [[6]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/index.htm)

### Speeches, conversations, letters, orders, and institutional texts

- **1921–1976:** *Letters and Telegrams*. A vast epistolary obra extending from private notes to military commands and diplomatic communications. No complete public corpus exists: official selections omit much, wartime records were destroyed or dispersed, and many texts were drafted or transmitted through secretarial and institutional processes. [[1]](https://book-sp.kodansha.co.jp/content/topics/mao/Mao%20Bibliographies.pdf) [[4]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/sw-in-pdf/sw-flp-1965-v1.pdf)
- **1927–1949:** *Orders, circulars, resolutions, proclamations, and reports*. A body of operative texts that made party and military decisions rather than merely describing them. Many speak in the name of the CCP Central Committee, a revolutionary military commission, a government, or an army, so Mao’s drafting role must not be confused with exclusive institutional authorship. [[2]](https://www.routledge.com/Maos-Road-to-Power/book-series/MRP) [[3]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/date-index.htm)
- **1936:** *Interviews with Edgar Snow*. An oral autobiographical project mediated by translation, transcription, selection, and Snow’s editing; later Chinese publications call it Mao’s “autobiography,” but it is not an independently written autobiography in the ordinary sense. [[1]](https://book-sp.kodansha.co.jp/content/topics/mao/Mao%20Bibliographies.pdf)
- **1949–1976:** *Manuscripts of Mao Zedong Since the Founding of the People’s Republic*. A thirteen-volume documentary corpus containing speeches, annotations, directives, correspondence, and drafts. It is evidence of an enormous textual practice, not a thirteen-volume work designed by Mao, and editorial selection prevents it from being called complete. [[1]](https://book-sp.kodansha.co.jp/content/topics/mao/Mao%20Bibliographies.pdf)
- **1949–1976:** *Conversations and speeches*. An oral-political oeuvre preserved in official records, participants’ notes, unofficial Cultural Revolution compilations, and later scholarly editions. Dating, wording, and even authorization vary among witnesses; these manifestations resist the fiction of a single stable authorial text. [[1]](https://book-sp.kodansha.co.jp/content/topics/mao/Mao%20Bibliographies.pdf) [[7]](https://www.bannedthought.net/China/Individuals/MaoZedong/Books/WritingsOfMaoZedong-1949-76-V1-Alt%28Fixed%29-sm.pdf)
- **16 May 1966:** *Circular of the Central Committee of the Chinese Communist Party*. A collective party document substantially shaped by Mao and acting as a protocol for the Cultural Revolution. Because it was issued institutionally and revised through leadership politics, it should not be assigned to Mao as an uncomplicated solitary composition.
- **5 August 1966:** *Bombard the Headquarters—My Big-Character Poster*. A very short manifesto presented in the medium of the handwritten public poster; its form and political action are inseparable.
- **1966–1976:** *Instructions, comments, inscriptions, and “latest directives” of the Cultural Revolution*. A dispersed body of brief utterances turned by newspapers, posters, loudspeakers, badges, and mass recitation into political acts. Many versions were selected, authenticated, or paraphrased by others, so the corpus is partly Mao’s language and partly a manifestation produced by the surrounding apparatus.

### Poetry

- **c. 1915–1976:** *Poems of Mao Zedong*. An obra in classical Chinese verse forms, encompassing youthful pieces, poems of revolutionary travel and war, exchanges with fellow poets, and late occasional compositions. The authoritative 1996 collection contains sixty-seven formally published poems, while later “complete” compilations count more by admitting drafts, fragments, doubtful attributions, and poems not authorized for publication; completeness is therefore disputed. [[8]](https://www.zywxpress.com/yss/mzdscj/) [[9]](https://jp.tfswufe.edu.cn/opac/book/ef53ce8548a75aa1d6e7b8cdf3520ff4)
- **1925:** *Changsha*. A lyric in the *Qinyuanchun* form joining landscape, memory, and revolutionary self-fashioning. [[10]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/poems/)
- **1927:** *Yellow Crane Tower*. A lyric in the *Pusaman* form made amid revolutionary defeat and movement. [[10]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/poems/)
- **1928:** *Jinggangshan*. A lyric in the *Xijiangyue* form turning a military defense into compressed landscape poetry. [[10]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/poems/)
- **1929:** *The Warlords Clash*. A lyric in the *Qingpingle* form responding to conflict among Nationalist military factions. [[10]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/poems/)
- **1929:** *The Double Ninth*. A lyric in the *Caisangzi* form recasting the traditional festival through revolutionary time and landscape. [[10]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/poems/)
- **1930:** *New Year’s Day*. A lyric in the *Rumengling* form registering military movement through difficult terrain. [[10]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/poems/)
- **1930:** *On the Guangchang Road*. A lyric in the *Jianzi Mulanhua* form combining marching, weather, and campaign geography. [[10]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/poems/)
- **1930:** *From Tingzhou to Changsha*. A lyric in the *Dielianhua* form made from the movement of a military campaign. [[10]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/poems/)
- **1931:** *Against the First Encirclement Campaign*. A lyric in the *Yujia’ao* form transforming battle into historical spectacle. [[10]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/poems/)
- **1931:** *Against the Second Encirclement Campaign*. A companion lyric in the *Yujia’ao* form. [[10]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/poems/)
- **1933:** *Dabodi*. A lyric in the *Pusaman* form revisiting a battlefield as a scene of color and memory. [[10]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/poems/)
- **1934:** *Huichang*. A lyric in the *Qingpingle* form composed near the beginning of the Long March period. [[10]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/poems/)
- **1934–1935:** *Three Short Poems*. A small cycle in the *Shiliuziling* form on mountains; it is one compound poetic thing made of three miniatures. [[10]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/poems/)
- **1935:** *Loushan Pass*. A lyric in the *Yiqin’e* form made from defeat, renewed attack, and passage through a mountain gate. [[10]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/poems/)
- **1935:** *The Long March*. A regulated verse retrospective on the Red Army’s journey. [[10]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/poems/)
- **1935:** *Kunlun*. A lyric in the *Nianujiao* form addressing the mountain range as a world-historical object. [[10]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/poems/)
- **1935:** *Mount Liupan*. A lyric in the *Qingpingle* form linking ascent, distance, and military purpose. [[10]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/poems/)
- **1936:** *Snow*. A lyric in the *Qinyuanchun* form whose landscape becomes an argument about history and political greatness; composed in 1936, it became publicly famous after publication in 1945. [[10]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/poems/)
- **1949:** *The People’s Liberation Army Captures Nanjing*. A regulated verse turning the fall of the Nationalist capital into revolutionary history. [[10]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/poems/)
- **1949:** *Reply to Liu Yazi*. An exchange poem whose occasion and addressee are integral to the object. [[11]](https://www.ucpress.edu/books/the-poems-of-mao-zedong/paper)
- **1950:** *Beidaihe*. A lyric in the *Langtaosha* form joining coastal weather to historical recollection. [[10]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/poems/)
- **1956:** *Swimming*. A lyric in the *Shuidiaogetou* form made from Mao’s Yangtze swim and the transformation of the river through engineering. [[10]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/poems/)
- **1957:** *Reply to Li Shuyi*. An exchange lyric in the *Dielianhua* form commemorating dead revolutionaries. [[10]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/poems/)
- **1959:** *Shaoshan Revisited*. A regulated verse returning to Mao’s birthplace after an absence of decades. [[10]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/poems/)
- **1959:** *Ascent of Lushan*. A regulated verse whose mountain journey becomes political and historical reflection. [[10]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/poems/)
- **1961:** *Reply to a Friend*. A lyric in the *Qilü* form, conventionally associated with Mao’s response to a photograph of the “immortal cave” at Lushan. [[10]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/poems/)
- **1961:** *Ode to the Plum Blossom*. A lyric in the *Busuanzi* form explicitly written in response to Lu You’s poem in the same form: imitation, answer, and political allegory at once. [[10]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/poems/)
- **1961:** *Reply to Comrade Guo Moruo*. A lyric in the *Manjianghong* form participating in a poetic-political exchange. [[10]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/poems/)
- **1965:** *Two Birds: A Dialogue*. A lyric in the *Niannujiao* form staged as an allegorical dialogue. [[10]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/poems/)

### Calligraphy and inscriptions

- **c. 1915–1976:** *Calligraphic oeuvre*. Thousands of surviving handwritten letters, drafts, copied classical poems, fair copies of Mao’s own verse, dedications, and public inscriptions form an obra in brush, ink, and handwriting. It cannot be reduced to the verbal texts: cataloguers report more than one thousand published manuscript objects and note that many others were lost, remain unpublished, or survive only photographically. [[12]](https://lib1.ahnu.edu.cn/info/1010/2201.htm)
- **1949–1950:** *People’s Daily masthead*. A public calligraphic inscription reproduced mechanically as the newspaper’s visual identity; the handwritten model, not every printed impression, is Mao’s made object.
- **1950:** *Xinhua News Agency masthead*. A calligraphic commission made into an institutional sign and endlessly reproduced.
- **1950:** *Renmin University of China inscription*. A calligraphic nameplate whose function lies between artwork, signature, and institutional architecture.
- **1950s:** *The Forbidden City inscription*. A public calligraphic inscription associated with the museum’s name; its repeated architectural and printed manifestations are reproductions of the brush-written model.
- **1950s–1960s:** *Public dedications and title inscriptions*. A dispersed corpus of brush-written names for newspapers, schools, monuments, memorial halls, and institutions. Individual catalogues disagree over dates, authentic exemplars, and the distinction between Mao’s hand, authorized tracings, and later copies, so no defensible complete itemization is presently possible. [[12]](https://lib1.ahnu.edu.cn/info/1010/2201.htm) [[13]](https://en.cnpubg.com/book/article/index/id/105.html)

### Compilations and manifestations not straightforwardly authored by Mao

- **1951–1960:** *Selected Works of Mao Zedong, volumes I–IV*. An authorized self-representation assembled by editors from earlier reports, articles, speeches, resolutions, and directives, all reviewed and sometimes revised by Mao. It is therefore both an editorial project by Mao and the party apparatus and a transformation of earlier works; its own publication note explicitly says it is incomplete. [[4]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/sw-in-pdf/sw-flp-1965-v1.pdf)
- **1977:** *Selected Works of Mao Zedong, volume V*. A posthumously issued selection of texts from 1949–1957. Mao made the constituent speeches and writings but did not complete this published volume as an authorial object, and its subsequent withdrawal from official circulation reflects political editorial judgment rather than disappearance of the underlying texts. [[5]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-5/index.htm)
- **1964:** *Quotations from Chairman Mao*. A portable anthology—later known internationally as the “Little Red Book”—compiled from Mao’s earlier writings for political study and recitation. Mao supplied the source language but did not author the compilation as a new continuous book; its editors and political distributors made the object. [[6]](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/index.htm) [[14]](https://marxists.info/reference/archive/mao/works/red-book/index.htm)
- **1966–1976:** *Mao’s words as posters, songs, badges, murals, dances, and recitation rituals*. A multimedia political manifestation approaching a Gesamtkunstwerk but lacking a single maker: Mao’s sentences and handwriting were recomposed by designers, musicians, performers, printers, Red Guards, and state institutions. These objects belong to Mao’s reception and cult more securely than to his personal authorship.
- **1991–1999:** *Collected Writings of Mao Zedong*. An eight-volume posthumous scholarly-official construction from previously published and archival texts. Its contents are Mao’s textual remains, but the collection as an ordered work was made by later editors and is neither a catalogue raisonné nor a complete works. [[1]](https://book-sp.kodansha.co.jp/content/topics/mao/Mao%20Bibliographies.pdf)
- **1992–2023:** *Mao’s Road to Power: Revolutionary Writings, 1912–1949*. A ten-volume scholarly corpus edited principally by Stuart R. Schram and collaborators. It is the broadest critical English-language reconstruction of Mao’s pre-1949 writings, but the translations, titles, ordering, annotations, and boundaries are the editors’ work; it should be used as a catalogue of surviving texts, not mistaken for a ten-volume book Mao designed. [[2]](https://www.routledge.com/Maos-Road-to-Power/book-series/MRP)
- **1986–1992:** *The Writings of Mao Zedong, 1949–1976*. A posthumous scholarly compilation edited by Michael Y. M. Kau and John K. Leung from official and unofficial sources. It establishes the existence of many later speeches, letters, comments, and directives, while its editors’ source criticism also demonstrates why the post-1949 corpus cannot honestly be called complete or textually uniform.
