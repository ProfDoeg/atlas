## Works by Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi

### Unfinished and early texts

- **1888–91:** *The Vegetarian*. A sequence of articles on Indian diet, customs, and religion written for the London Vegetarian Society’s journal; the individual articles survive within Gandhi’s collected writings rather than as a book conceived by him. ([CWMG, vol. 1](https://www.gandhiheritageportal.org/downloadPDF/MQ%3D%3D))
- **1893–94:** *Guide to London*. An unfinished practical handbook for Indians intending to study in Britain. Gandhi never published it, its exact date is uncertain, some appendix pages are missing, and the surviving copy was made by a clerk rather than being Gandhi’s original manuscript. ([CWMG, vol. 1](https://www.gandhiheritageportal.org/downloadPDF/MQ%3D%3D))
- **1895:** *The Indian Franchise: An Appeal to Every Briton in South Africa*. An advocacy pamphlet assembled from a petition against Indian disfranchisement; it is both documentary brief and political intervention. ([CWMG, vol. 1](https://www.gandhiheritageportal.org/downloadPDF/MQ%3D%3D))
- **1896:** *The Grievances of the British Indians in South Africa: An Appeal to the Indian Public* (“Green Pamphlet”). A dossier-manifesto intended to make South African Indian grievances actionable in India; Gandhi began writing it at Rajkot on 9 July 1896. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritageportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D))

### Translations, paraphrases, and adaptations

- **1907:** *Niti Dharma* (*Ethical Religion*). Gandhi’s Gujarati adaptation of William Mackintire Salter’s ethical writings—not a literal translation and therefore neither wholly Salter’s book nor wholly an independent Gandhi treatise. Its later English back-translation was sometimes mistakenly attributed to Gandhi as an English original. ([CWMG catalogue](https://www.publicationsdivision.nic.in/system/storage/upload/pdf/Gandhi_Catalogue_en-gb_000-merged_compressed_en-gb.pdf))
- **1908:** *Sarvodaya*. A Gujarati paraphrase of John Ruskin’s *Unto This Last* whose new title—“the welfare” or “uplift of all”—became the name of a Gandhian social project; its status lies between translation, condensation, and political appropriation. ([CWMG](https://www.gandhiheritageportal.org/), [publication history](https://www.mkgandhi.org/ebks/gandhiebooks.php))
- **1929:** *Anasaktiyoga: Shrimad Bhagavad Gita*. Gandhi’s Gujarati translation and interpretation of the *Bhagavad Gita*, supplied with an introduction completed on 27 June 1929. The scripture is not his obra, but the translation and doctrine of action without attachment are. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal key texts](https://www.gandhiheritageportal.org/mahatma-gandhi-books), [chronology](https://gandhiheritageportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D))

### Books, serial books, manifestos, and protocols

- **1909:** *Hind Swaraj or Indian Home Rule*. A dialogic manifesto written aboard the *Kildonan Castle* on 13–22 November and serialized in *Indian Opinion* on 11 and 18 December; Gandhi himself produced the 1910 English rendering after the Gujarati text was proscribed. It acts as a critique of modern civilization and a programme for self-rule rather than merely describing either. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritageportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D), [key-text archive](https://www.gandhiheritageportal.org/mahatma-gandhi-books))
- **1924–25:** *Dakshin Africana Satyagrahano Itihas* (*Satyagraha in South Africa*). A Gujarati serial history beginning in *Navajivan* on 6 April 1924, subsequently issued as a book and translated under Gandhi’s supervision. It is simultaneously history, memoir, and a protocol for a method whose collective making cannot be credited to Gandhi alone. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritageportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D), [key-text archive](https://www.gandhiheritageportal.org/mahatma-gandhi-books))
- **1925–29:** *Satyana Prayogo athava Atmakatha* (*An Autobiography; or, The Story of My Experiments with Truth*). An experimental autobiography serialized in Gujarati in *Navajivan* from 29 November 1925 and in Mahadev Desai’s English translation in *Young India* from 3 December. “Experiment” is literal here: the obra treats a life, diet, body, marriage, religion, and politics as its working medium. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritageportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D), [key-text archive](https://www.gandhiheritageportal.org/mahatma-gandhi-books))
- **1930–32:** *From Yeravda Mandir: Ashram Observances*. A sequence of prison letters explaining the ashram vows, first gathered in English in 1932. It is less a conventional book than a rule of life—a protocol sent from prison to a community. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal catalogue record](https://www.gandhiheritageportal.org/datalink/files/gandhiheritagesite/AccessionRegisterHSS.pdf), [key-text archive](https://www.gandhiheritageportal.org/mahatma-gandhi-books))
- **1941:** *Constructive Programme: Its Meaning and Place*. A manifesto and operational programme for building swaraj through communal unity, removal of untouchability, khadi, village industries, education, sanitation, and related work; the chronology records publication on 13 December 1941, and Gandhi later revised it. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritageportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D), [key-text archive](https://www.gandhiheritageportal.org/mahatma-gandhi-books))
- **1942–48:** *Aarogyani Chavi* (*Key to Health*). A health protocol written principally during Gandhi’s Aga Khan Palace imprisonment and first published in English by Navajivan in October 1948, after his death. Its medical prescriptions document his experiments but should not be mistaken for current clinical guidance. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal book record](https://gandhiheritageportal.org/bookdetail//MTE%3D), [key-text archive](https://www.gandhiheritageportal.org/mahatma-gandhi-books))
- **posthumously assembled; 1955:** *Ashram Observances in Action* (*Satyagraha Ashramno Itihas*). A retrospective account of the ashram and its practices, published as a book after Gandhi’s death. The named volume is therefore partly an editorial manifestation of earlier Gandhi material, not an object he completed and issued in this form. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal book record](https://www.gandhiheritageportal.org/es/mahatma-gandhi-books/ashram-observances-in-action), [key-text archive](https://www.gandhiheritageportal.org/mahatma-gandhi-books))

### Periodical Gesamtkunstwerke

- **1903–14 under Gandhi’s direction:** *Indian Opinion*. A multilingual South African weekly founded on 4 June 1903 and made through editors, translators, compositors, printers, correspondents, and readers; Gandhi owned, edited, financed, printed, and wrote for it, but did not singly author the journal. It joined text, press, settlement, and political organization into one continuing work. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritageportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D), [journal archive](https://www.gandhiheritageportal.org/journals-by-gandhiji))
- **1919–31:** *Navajivan*. Gandhi’s Gujarati weekly, first issued under his direction on 7 September 1919; it served as serial book, instructional forum, political instrument, and institutional basis for Navajivan Press and Trust. Authorship belongs separately to its many signed and unsigned contents, while the periodical as a whole is a collaborative obra. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritageportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D), [journal archive](https://www.gandhiheritageportal.org/journals-by-gandhiji))
- **1919–31:** *Young India*. An English weekly whose first Gandhi-directed issue appeared on 8 October 1919. It was simultaneously a newspaper, serial publishing platform, manifesto engine, and instrument of mass organization; Gandhi was prosecuted in 1922 for three of its articles, but the whole periodical is not solely authored by him. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritageportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D), [journal archive](https://www.gandhiheritageportal.org/journals-by-gandhiji))
- **1919 onward:** *Navajivan* (Hindi). The Hindi counterpart to the Gujarati journal, included by the Gandhi Heritage Portal among periodicals Gandhi owned, edited, or published. It is a translated and collaborative publishing project, not simply another title for his Gujarati articles. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal journal archive](https://www.gandhiheritageportal.org/journals-by-gandhiji))
- **1933–48 under Gandhi’s direction:** *Harijan*. An English weekly inaugurated on 11 February 1933, created as the principal organ of Gandhi’s campaign against untouchability and later of his constructive and political work. Editors and contributors other than Gandhi prevent the run from being treated as a single-authored book; he transferred responsibility for the journals in November 1946. ([first issue](https://gandhiheritageportal.org/journals-by-gandhiji/harijan), [chronology](https://gandhiheritageportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D))
- **1933–48 under Gandhi’s direction:** *Harijan Sevak*. The Hindi companion to *Harijan*, begun on 23 February 1933; it is a related but distinct linguistic manifestation with its own editorial history. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritageportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D), [journal archive](https://www.gandhiheritageportal.org/journals-by-gandhiji))
- **1933–48 under Gandhi’s direction:** *Harijanbandhu*. The Gujarati companion to *Harijan*, begun on 12 March 1933. Like the other two journals, it is best classified as a collaborative campaign-medium rather than a Gandhi-authored book. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D), [journal archive](https://www.gandhiheritageportal.org/journals-by-gandhiji))

### Settlements, institutions, and constructive projects

- **1894:** *Natal Indian Congress*. A political organization founded by Gandhi with South African Indian merchants and other residents; calling it his work must not erase its members, funders, and collective agency. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritageportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D))
- **1899–1900:** *Natal Indian Ambulance Corps*. A volunteer medical-service formation organized by Gandhi during the South African War. It resists celebratory classification: it joined humanitarian service to participation on the British imperial side and relied on a large collective of Indian stretcher-bearers. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritageportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D))
- **1904 onward:** *Phoenix Settlement*. A communal settlement, press site, farm, school, and experiment in simplified living established near Durban. It is a Gesamtkunstwerk in the literal sense that publishing, architecture, labour, diet, education, religion, family life, and politics were designed not to remain separate. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritageportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D))
- **1910–13:** *Tolstoy Farm*. A collectively built residence, school, workshop, and training ground for South African satyagrahis, established by Gandhi on land supplied by Hermann Kallenbach. Attribution is necessarily shared with Kallenbach and the residents whose labour physically made it. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritageportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D))
- **1915–17:** *Satyagraha Ashram, Kochrab*. Gandhi’s first Indian ashram, established on 20 May 1915 as a vowed community and laboratory for collective service. The community, rather than its buildings alone, is the made thing. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritageportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D))
- **1917–33:** *Satyagraha Ashram, Sabarmati*. The relocated ashram established on 17 June 1917, combining residence, school, spinning, sanitation, worship, publishing, and political preparation. Gandhi’s authorship is conceptual and disciplinary, while the physical and social obra belongs to its residents as well. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritageportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D))
- **1920 onward:** *Gujarat Vidyapith*. A national university established on 18 October 1920 as part of the non-co-operation project and an alternative to colonial education. Gandhi was its founding chancellor, but it is an institutional and collective creation rather than a personally authored object. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritageportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D))
- **1925 onward:** *All-India Spinners’ Association*. An organization established on 22 September 1925 to bind hand-spinning, village livelihood, anti-industrial critique, and the independence movement. Its obra was a distributed economic practice—khadi—not a single product made by Gandhi. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D))
- **1932 onward:** *Harijan Sevak Sangh*. An organization established on 30 September 1932 during the campaign against untouchability. Gandhi inspired and directed the project but did not make it alone; moreover, both his terminology and his strategy were contested by B. R. Ambedkar and Dalit movements. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D))
- **1934 onward:** *All-India Village Industries Association*. A constructive-work organization established on 14 December 1934 to coordinate decentralized rural production. Its products and practices were made by many artisans and organizers, so “work by Gandhi” applies to its programme and institutional design, not to every object it produced. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D))
- **1936–48:** *Sevagram*. Gandhi’s village settlement at Segaon, occupied from April 1936 and renamed Sevagram in 1940. Like Phoenix and Sabarmati, it was an inhabited experiment joining hut-building, education, craft, health, sanitation, prayer, correspondence, and politics rather than a separable architectural work. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D))
- **1937 onward:** *Nai Talim* (“Basic Education”). A pedagogical project developed with Zakir Husain, Aryanayakam, Asha Devi, and other educators around productive craft, intellectual learning, and communal responsibility. It has no single finished object or uncontested author: Gandhi supplied the initiating conception, while committees and schools made its curricula and practice. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal fundamental works](https://www.gandhiheritageportal.org/fundamental_worklist))

### Collective actions, enacted manifestos, and bodily works

- **1906–14:** *South African Satyagraha*. A sustained campaign inaugurated by a collective pledge at Johannesburg’s Empire Theatre on 11 September 1906. Gandhi helped name, theorize, and direct it, but Maganlal Gandhi proposed the linguistic root of “satyagraha,” and thousands of resisters made the action; it resists being called Gandhi’s individual work. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritageportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D))
- **1917:** *Champaran inquiry and satyagraha*. An investigation, civil-disobedience action, and constructive programme undertaken with Rajkumar Shukla, local cultivators, lawyers, teachers, and volunteers. Its documentation, schools, sanitation work, and political result form a collective project rather than one bounded text. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D))
- **1918:** *Ahmedabad mill-workers’ satyagraha*. A labour intervention combining arbitration, strike discipline, public communication, and Gandhi’s fast. It was made with mill workers, Anasuya Sarabhai, and others, and its use of a bodily fast as political leverage remains ethically disputed. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D))
- **1918:** *Kheda satyagraha*. A collective revenue-resistance campaign organized with cultivators and colleagues including Vallabhbhai Patel. Gandhi helped formulate its pledge and tactics, but treating the campaign as his individual obra would erase its local makers. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D))
- **1919:** *Rowlatt Satyagraha*. A nationwide hartal and civil-resistance project built around Gandhi’s February pledge and the action of 6 April. Gandhi suspended it after violence, so incompletion and acknowledged tactical failure belong to the work’s status. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D))
- **1920–22:** *Non-co-operation movement*. An enacted manifesto of boycott, resignation, spinning, alternative education, and refusal of colonial institutions, developed through the Indian National Congress and the Khilafat alliance. Gandhi was its principal designer but halted it after the Chauri Chaura killings; it is an unfinished collective project, not his proprietary creation. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D))
- **1930:** *Salt March and Salt Satyagraha*. A 24-day procession from Sabarmati beginning 12 March and culminating in Gandhi’s breach of the salt law at Dandi on 6 April, followed by mass civil disobedience. It made walking, salt, publicity, disciplined bodies, and law-breaking into a single performative manifesto, but its innumerable participants were co-makers. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D))
- **1932:** *Fast against the Communal Award and the Poona Pact*. A bodily-political intervention begun on 20 September and ended after the pact of 24 September. It resists classification as a “work” because Gandhi placed his threatened death inside negotiations over Dalit political representation; Ambedkar and later Dalit scholarship have disputed both its ethics and its consequences. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D))
- **1933–34:** *Harijan tour*. A nationwide fundraising, speaking, temple-entry, and anti-untouchability campaign beginning on 7 November 1933. It was a mobile institution and public performance made with local organizers and audiences, while its paternal terminology and reform programme remain contested. ([Sabarmati Ashram chronology](https://www.gandhiashramsabarmati.org/en/the-mahatma/life-chronology.html/))
- **1942:** *Quit India*. A mass-insurrectionary project authorized by the Congress resolution of 8 August and condensed by Gandhi into the performative call “Do or Die.” Gandhi helped formulate and launch it but was imprisoned the next day; decentralized participants, including actors who used violence, made most of what followed. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D))
- **1946–47:** *Noakhali peace mission*. A village-to-village project against communal violence, culminating in Gandhi’s barefoot march beginning 2 January 1947. It joined walking, prayer, residence, mediation, and bodily vulnerability, and resists evaluation as a completed or demonstrably successful work. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal chronology](https://gandhiheritportal.org/eventcontentdetail/OA%3D%3D/NzQxOQ%3D%3D))
- **1913–48:** *Fasts as political, penitential, and communal interventions*. Gandhi repeatedly made his own body into a medium that was at once manifesto, vow, pressure, and claimed self-purification. These episodes cannot responsibly be collapsed into one triumphant œuvre: their purposes differed, their effects are disputed, and critics have understood some as coercive. ([Gandhi Heritage Portal event chronology](https://www.gandhiheritageportal.org/chronology/event-chronology))
- **c. 1906–48:** *Experiments with truth*. Gandhi’s continuing project of vows, celibacy, diet, naturopathy, sanitation, dress, spinning, manual labour, prayer, imprisonment, and nonviolent action. It is a proposed Gesamtkunstwerk—“life” offered as the work—but it resists a catalogue boundary because many experiments involved family members, disciples, patients, opponents, and communities whose consent and agency cannot be absorbed into Gandhi’s authorship. ([Gujarat Vidyapith, CWMG description](https://gujaratvidyapith.org/extension/cwmg?lang=en), [Gandhi Heritage Portal key texts](https://www.gandhiheritageportal.org/mahatma-gandhi-books))

### The dispersed textual corpus

- **1884–30 January 1948:** *Letters, articles, speeches, interviews, petitions, resolutions, telegrams, notes, prayers, and messages*. These are thousands of separately occasioned things rather than one book and cannot be honestly reduced to a short list of titles. Their standard scholarly container is *The Collected Works of Mahatma Gandhi*: volumes 1–90 are chronological, volumes 91–97 add subsequently recovered material, volumes 98–99 are indexes, and volume 100 collects editorial prefaces; completeness remains provisional because the supplementary volumes exist precisely because new material continued to surface. ([Government of India catalogue](https://www.publicationsdivision.nic.in/system/storage/upload/pdf/Gandhi_Catalogue_en-gb_000-merged_compressed_en-gb.pdf), [Gandhi Heritage Portal CWMG catalogue](https://www.gandhiheritageportal.org/datalink/files/allbooks/images/Catalogue_CWMG_GHP.pdf), [Gujarat Vidyapith CWMG account](https://gujaratvidyapith.org/extension/cwmg?lang=en))
- **1958–94, with later digital manifestations:** *The Collected Works of Mahatma Gandhi*. This hundred-volume edition is an editorial obra made after Gandhi’s death by the Government of India’s CWMG team, not a hundred-volume book authored or designed by Gandhi. It is listed here because it is the indispensable catalogue of the dispersed things he did make, while its selection, titles, translations, dating, and apparatus belong partly to its editors. ([Government of India catalogue](https://www.publicationsdivision.nic.in/system/storage/upload/pdf/Gandhi_Catalogue_en-gb_000-merged_compressed_en-gb.pdf), [Gandhi Heritage Portal](https://www.gandhiheritageportal.org/))
