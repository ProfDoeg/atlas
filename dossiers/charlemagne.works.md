## Works by Charlemagne

### Royal acts, laws, and protocols

- **768–814:** *Diplomas of Charlemagne*. The charters, privileges, grants, confirmations, immunities, and judicial instruments issued in Charlemagne’s name form a chancery corpus rather than a personally handwritten oeuvre; scribal authorship varies, many acts survive only as later copies, and some are forged or disputed. The standard edition is Mühlbacher’s *Diplomata Karolinorum I*, supplemented by the MGH’s continually updated authenticity database, which also records sixteen subsequently identified diplomas. ([MGH](https://data.mgh.de/databases/dderg/bin/dderg_main.html))

- **779:** *Capitulare Haristallense* (“Capitulary of Herstal”). A royal legislative and administrative protocol concerning ecclesiastical discipline, tithes, justice, crime, and public order; it is MGH *Capitularia* I, no. 20, but, like other capitularies, represents government through assembled advisers rather than solitary authorship. ([MGH edition](https://www.dmgh.de/mgh_capit_1/))

- **c. 782–785:** *Capitulatio de partibus Saxoniae*. A conquest-era protocol imposing Christian observance and Frankish authority in Saxony, including severe capital penalties; its precise date is disputed, and the surviving text is an administrative product of Charlemagne’s regime rather than demonstrably his wording. ([MGH edition](https://www.dmgh.de/mgh_capit_1/))

- **789:** *Duplex legationis edictum*. Instructions for royal agents and an oath-and-governance protocol, preserved as MGH *Capitularia* I, no. 23; its composite title and textual form are editorial, not necessarily those of a single promulgated document. ([MGH edition](https://www.dmgh.de/mgh_capit_1/))

- **789:** *Admonitio generalis*. A programmatic capitulary—simultaneously manifesto and protocol—for correcting clergy, worship, education, discipline, and royal government. It was issued under Charlemagne’s authority, but its composition was collaborative and drew heavily on canon law and court scholars, especially Alcuin. ([MGH edition](https://www.dmgh.de/mgh_capit_1/))

- **794:** *Capitulare Francofurtense* (“Capitulary of Frankfurt”). The record and enactments of the Frankfurt assembly, addressing theology, ecclesiastical government, coinage, prices, and other matters; it is a conciliar-royal composite, not a text securely attributable to Charlemagne’s own hand. ([MGH edition](https://www.dmgh.de/mgh_capit_1/))

- **797:** *Capitulare Saxonicum*. A revised governance protocol for Saxony that replaced part of the earlier regime of terror with monetary penalties and procedures negotiated with Saxon representatives. Its status is legislative and political, not conventionally literary. ([MGH edition](https://www.dmgh.de/mgh_capit_1/))

- **c. 794–800:** *Capitulare de villis*. A detailed protocol for managing royal estates, personnel, production, livestock, tools, gardens, and accounts. Its date, immediate circumstances, and even whether it was intended for the whole realm or a more restricted group of estates remain disputed. ([MGH edition](https://www.dmgh.de/mgh_capit_1/))

- **802:** *Capitulare missorum generale*. A programmatic statement for the *missi dominici* concerning justice, loyalty, clerical conduct, and imperial order. Modern scholarship cautions that the received text may be an amalgam of material arising from the Aachen deliberations rather than one securely promulgated decree. ([MGH edition](https://www.dmgh.de/mgh_capit_1/), [textual-status discussion](https://spicilegium.net/03_tsuda.html))

- **802–803:** *Capitularies of Aachen*. A cluster of legislative, judicial, and administrative protocols associated with assemblies at Aachen, including instructions to royal agents and additions to existing laws; the manuscripts do not always preserve clean boundaries between separate enactments. ([MGH edition](https://www.dmgh.de/mgh_capit_1/))

- **803:** *Capitulare legibus additum*. Additions approved for the written laws of Charlemagne’s peoples, concerned especially with judicial procedure and penalties. It is legislation made through ruler, assembly, and learned redaction rather than a personally composed text. ([MGH edition](https://www.dmgh.de/mgh_capit_1/))

- **805:** *Capitulare missorum in Theodonis villa datum primum* and related Thionville capitularies. Instructions addressing justice, oaths, military obligations, trade, and frontier controls; their division into distinct texts reflects both medieval transmission and modern editorial judgment. ([MGH edition](https://www.dmgh.de/mgh_capit_1/))

- **806:** *Divisio regnorum*. A succession settlement dividing prospective rule among Charlemagne’s sons Charles, Pippin, and Louis. It is a constitutional project and performative dynastic text—one overtaken by the deaths of Charles and Pippin—rather than a description of an accomplished partition. ([MGH edition](https://www.dmgh.de/mgh_capit_1/))

- **811–813:** *Late capitularies and reform memoranda*. The final legislative cluster includes military, fiscal, ecclesiastical, and general reform texts associated with Aachen and other assemblies. Several are drafts, questionnaires, records of discussion, or collections of chapters whose completeness and status as formally issued law cannot be assumed. ([MGH edition](https://www.dmgh.de/mgh_capit_1/))

- **late eighth–early ninth century:** *Revisions and written redactions of the peoples’ laws*. Charlemagne ordered the laws administered within his realm to be collated, corrected, and committed to writing, producing or reshaping versions of the *Lex Salica*, *Lex Ribuaria*, *Lex Saxonum*, *Lex Frisionum*, and *Ewa ad Amorem*. These are governmental redaction projects with layered customary and scribal authorship, not “books by Charlemagne.” ([Einhard, *Vita Karoli Magni*, ch. 29](https://web.mit.edu/aorlando/www/ChurchHistory/Church%20History%20Readings/Life%20of%20Charlemagne.pdf), [MGH *Leges*](https://www.mgh.de/en/publications/leges))

### Letters, manifestos, and commissioned books

- **c. 780–800:** *Epistola de litteris colendis* (“Letter on the Cultivation of Learning”). A circular letter and educational manifesto urging monasteries and bishoprics to cultivate accurate language so that sacred texts could be properly understood. It speaks in Charlemagne’s royal voice, but its date is unsettled, two recensions survive, and Alcuin’s role in its composition has long been debated. ([MGH study and edition discussion](https://www.mgh-bibliothek.de/dokumente/0/086370.pdf), [MGH *Epistolae* record](https://geschichtsquellen.de/werk/803))

- **c. 768–814:** *Letters of Charlemagne*. The surviving correspondence issued in his name—orders, recommendations, diplomatic communications, theological interventions, and personal or court letters—is edited chiefly in MGH *Epistolae* III–IV. These texts belong to a ruler’s secretarial and collaborative voice; few warrant an uncomplicated claim of personal composition. ([MGH *Epistolae* III](https://www.dmgh.de/mgh_epp_3/), [work and edition record](https://geschichtsquellen.de/werk/803))

- **791:** *Codex epistolaris Carolinus*. A collection commissioned by Charlemagne of papal letters sent to his grandfather Charles Martel, his father Pippin, and himself; the surviving codex contains ninety-nine items. It is an archival and ideological compilation made at his order, not a collection of letters written by him. ([MGH Clavis Canonum](https://data.mgh.de/databases/clavis/wiki/index.php/Codex_epistolaris_Carolinus))

- **790s:** *Opus Caroli regis contra synodum*, conventionally *Libri Carolini*. A four-book theological manifesto against both iconoclasm and the formulation of image-veneration attributed to the Second Council of Nicaea. It was authorized, corrected, and presented in Charlemagne’s name, but modern scholarship strongly associates its composition with Theodulf of Orléans; calling Charlemagne its straightforward author would therefore be misleading. ([edition and attribution overview](https://www.mgh.de/de/bibliothek/digitale-angebote), [authorship discussion](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/27469/1/Ross_108044487_CorrectedThesisClean.pdf.pdf))

- **c. 796:** *Epitaph for Pope Hadrian I*. A forty-line monumental inscription commissioned by Charlemagne, composed probably by Alcuin, cut in stone near Aachen, and transported to St Peter’s in Rome. It is simultaneously poem, political manifesto, memorial, and architectural object, but not a poem securely written by Charlemagne himself. ([Oxford University Press study](https://academic.oup.com/book/46073), [review and attribution summary](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/tmr/article/view/42070))

### Linguistic and antiquarian projects

- **before 814:** *Grammar of the Frankish vernacular*. Einhard reports that Charlemagne began a grammar of his native language. Nothing identifiable survives, and even its extent is unknown: it is therefore an unfinished project known only from testimony, not an extant book. ([Einhard, *Vita Karoli Magni*, ch. 29](https://web.mit.edu/aorlando/www/ChurchHistory/Church%20History%20Readings/Life%20of%20Charlemagne.pdf))

- **before 814:** *Collection of ancient vernacular heroic songs*. Einhard says Charlemagne had old songs recounting the deeds and wars of earlier kings written down. The collection is wholly lost, its contents and title are unknown, and “Charlemagne’s heroic-song book” is a modern descriptive label rather than a transmitted title. ([Einhard, *Vita Karoli Magni*, ch. 29](https://web.mit.edu/aorlando/www/ChurchHistory/Church%20History%20Readings/Life%20of%20Charlemagne.pdf))

- **before 814:** *Frankish names for the months*. A lexical system assigning vernacular names to the twelve months. Einhard preserves the names, but the work resists classification as a text: it is better understood as an act of linguistic standardization. ([Einhard, *Vita Karoli Magni*, ch. 29](https://web.mit.edu/aorlando/www/ChurchHistory/Church%20History%20Readings/Life%20of%20Charlemagne.pdf))

- **before 814:** *Frankish names for the winds*. A corresponding nomenclature expanding the customary four winds to twelve vernacularly named directions. Like the month names, it is a linguistic construction transmitted inside another author’s biography rather than an independently surviving work. ([Einhard, *Vita Karoli Magni*, ch. 29](https://web.mit.edu/aorlando/www/ChurchHistory/Church%20History%20Readings/Life%20of%20Charlemagne.pdf))

### Architectural and engineering projects

- **from c. 789:** *Palace complex at Aachen*. A royal and ceremonial ensemble joining audience hall, residences, administrative spaces, galleries, baths, and chapel. Charlemagne was its patron and political author, while design and construction belonged to court architects and craftspeople; much of the secular complex is lost or incorporated into later buildings. ([Aachen municipal history](https://rathaus-aachen.de/wp-content/uploads/2013/08/The-Town-Hall_E_web.pdf), [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/3))

- **c. 790s–805:** *Palatine Chapel of St Mary at Aachen*. The octagonal chapel, now the nucleus of Aachen Cathedral, is the principal surviving material obra of Charlemagne’s court. It combines architecture, imported columns and marbles, metalwork, liturgy, royal ceremony, and imperial symbolism so thoroughly that it approaches a *Gesamtkunstwerk*, although Charlemagne commissioned rather than personally designed or built it. ([UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/3))

- **late eighth–early ninth century:** *Royal palace at Ingelheim*. A monumental palace begun under Charlemagne and used for assemblies and royal residence. It survives archaeologically and through later alterations; Einhard attributes the palace project to Charlemagne without naming its designers. ([Einhard, *Vita Karoli Magni*, ch. 17](https://web.mit.edu/aorlando/www/ChurchHistory/Church%20History%20Readings/Life%20of%20Charlemagne.pdf))

- **late eighth–early ninth century:** *Royal palace at Nijmegen*. A palace overlooking the Waal, likewise named by Einhard among Charlemagne’s major building projects. Its Carolingian fabric is fragmentary and its appearance must be reconstructed from archaeology and later evidence. ([Einhard, *Vita Karoli Magni*, ch. 17](https://web.mit.edu/aorlando/www/ChurchHistory/Church%20History%20Readings/Life%20of%20Charlemagne.pdf))

- **before 813:** *Rhine bridge at Mainz*. A large timber bridge commissioned across the Rhine and reportedly destroyed by fire shortly before Charlemagne’s death. Einhard says Charlemagne intended to rebuild it in stone, but that second form remained only a project. ([Einhard, *Vita Karoli Magni*, ch. 17](https://web.mit.edu/aorlando/www/ChurchHistory/Church%20History%20Readings/Life%20of%20Charlemagne.pdf))

- **792–793:** *Fossa Carolina* (*fossatum magnum*, “Charlemagne’s Canal”). An attempted navigable connection between the Rhine–Main and Danube drainage systems, physically begun in 793 and abandoned unfinished that year. Archaeology confirms approximately three kilometres of digging and sophisticated water-control planning, while scholarship continues to distinguish the project conceived in 792 from construction in 793. ([Lukas Werther, “792 or 793?”](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/emed.12422), [geoarchaeological study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4177111/))

- **late eighth–early ninth century:** *Construction and restoration program for churches*. Einhard credits Charlemagne with ordering the repair of churches throughout his dominions, especially St Peter’s in Rome, while capitularies required episcopal and monastic buildings to be maintained. This is a dispersed patronage project, not a single securely bounded work, and its individual sites cannot all be assigned to his direct initiative. ([Einhard, *Vita Karoli Magni*, chs. 17 and 27](https://web.mit.edu/aorlando/www/ChurchHistory/Church%20History%20Readings/Life%20of%20Charlemagne.pdf))

### Systems, institutions, and collective creations

- **late eighth century:** *Carolingian monetary reform*. The replacement of the older gold-oriented and variable coinages by a silver system organized around the pound, shilling, and penny was a royal standardization manifested in many mints and coins. It is a system made under Charlemagne rather than a single object, and individual dies and surviving pennies are products of anonymous mint workers.

- **late eighth–early ninth century:** *Royal educational and textual-correction program*. Schools for clergy, improved Latin instruction, correction of biblical and liturgical books, and the multiplication of reliable manuscripts formed an extensive court-and-church project. “Charlemagne invented school” and “Charlemagne invented Carolingian minuscule” are untenable simplifications: scholars such as Alcuin, Theodulf, scribes, bishops, abbots, and local scriptoria made its individual manifestations. ([*Epistola de litteris colendis* study](https://www.mgh-bibliothek.de/dokumente/0/086370.pdf), [*Admonitio generalis* in MGH](https://www.dmgh.de/mgh_capit_1/))

- **800–814:** *Renewed western empire*. Charlemagne’s imperial rule created a political and ceremonial formation joining Frankish kingship, Roman imperial language, Christian reform, law, coinage, ritual, buildings, and conquest. It resists being called his “work” because it was also made by popes, aristocrats, armies, administrators, subject peoples, and later interpreters; *creation* or political project is more accurate than personal obra.

### Body, burial, and posthumous manifestations

- **814:** *Burial of Charlemagne at Aachen*. His body was placed in the church he had commissioned, but a corpse and burial are not works made by their occupant, and the original grave’s form and exact location remain contested. Its inclusion marks the boundary of the category rather than extending authorship beyond death.

- **1000:** *Opening of Charlemagne’s tomb under Otto III*. This was a posthumous manifestation produced by Ottonian ritual and memory, not by Charlemagne; later accounts disagree about what Otto found and removed. It belongs to the reception and remaking of Charlemagne’s body rather than to his oeuvre. ([tomb-history study](https://www.persee.fr/doc/bulmo_0007-473x_1965_num_123_2_8085), [forensic historical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30544097/))

- **1165:** *Elevation and translation of Charlemagne’s remains*. Frederick Barbarossa’s court opened the burial during Charlemagne’s canonization by the antipope Paschal III and transformed the imperial corpse into relics. The act created “Saint Charlemagne” as a political-sacral manifestation, but it was emphatically not Charlemagne’s own making. ([Cambridge study](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/items/61c51d1e-e2d3-4347-949a-553464c0d9e0), [tomb-history study](https://www.persee.fr/doc/bulmo_0007-473x_1965_num_123_2_8085))

- **c. 1182–1215:** *Karlsschrein* (“Shrine of Charlemagne”). The gold reliquary completed under Frederick II received Charlemagne’s remains in 1215. It is a later artwork, reliquary, and political manifesto made around his body—not a work by him—and therefore closes rather than enlarges the list of his own creations. ([University of Reading study](https://www.reading.ac.uk/gcms/-/media/project/functions/research/graduate-centre-for-medieval-studies/documents/rms201201-d-rollason-from-tintagel-to-aachen.pdf), [historical and forensic review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1570677X18301825))
