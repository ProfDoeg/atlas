## Works by George Washington

### Copied and compiled texts

- **c. 1745–1747:** *School Copybooks*. Three surviving exercise books containing Washington’s handwriting exercises, calculations, surveying and legal material, poems, notes, and practical formulas. They are compilations and exercises rather than wholly authored works. ([Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/collections/george-washington-papers/articles-and-essays/series-notes/series-1-school-copy-books))
- **c. 1745–1747:** *Rules of Civility and Decent Behaviour in Company and Conversation*. A manuscript copy of 110 pre-existing conduct rules, preserved in one school copybook; Washington made the copy, not the underlying text, so it resists classification as an authored work. ([Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/collections/george-washington-papers/articles-and-essays/series-notes/series-1-school-copy-books))

### Surveys and maps

- **1749–1752 and undated:** *Professional Surveys*. A corpus of plats, field notes, warrants, calculations, and drawings produced during Washington’s work as a public surveyor; the surviving record is incomplete and includes material preserved outside his own papers. ([Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/collections/george-washington-papers/articles-and-essays/series-notes/series-1-school-copy-books))
- **1749–1799:** *Maps, plans, and annotated maps*. A cartographic body of work encompassing survey plats, maps of Alexandria and Mount Vernon, western-land maps, military maps, and maps made or annotated for agricultural and speculative projects. Authorship varies: some are Washington’s drawings, while others are maps he commissioned, copied, assembled, or marked, and therefore not every object is an independent Washington obra. ([Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/collections/george-washington-papers/articles-and-essays/george-washington-survey-and-mapmaker/))

### Journals and records

- **1748–1799:** *Diaries*. Thirty-six surviving manuscript diaries, edited as a six-volume scholarly series, recording travel, weather, agriculture, plantation management, military and political affairs, and social life. Large chronological gaps and lost volumes mean that this extensive diary project is not complete. ([Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/collections/george-washington-papers/articles-and-essays/series-notes/series-1-school-copy-books); [University of Virginia Press](https://upress.virginia.edu/series/pgw/))
- **1751–1752:** *Barbados Diary*. The record of Washington’s only journey outside mainland North America; it survives in a mutilated manuscript whose obscured text was recovered as far as possible by scholarly editors. It is simultaneously a travel narrative and part of the larger diary project. ([University of Virginia Press](https://upress.virginia.edu/series/pgw/))
- **1754–1799:** *Letterbooks*. A deliberately retained manuscript archive of outgoing letters, official instructions, orders, and related papers. Many entries are clerk-made copies, so the letterbooks are an administrative obra directed by Washington rather than uniformly autograph texts. ([Library of Congress finding aid](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/gdc/gdcfindingaidpdfs/ms008068/ms008068.pdf))
- **1744–1799:** *Correspondence and official papers*. The overarching textual corpus of letters, military orders, presidential papers, memoranda, addresses, reports, and enclosures attributed to or received by Washington. The scholarly *Papers of George Washington* divides it into Colonial, Revolutionary War, Confederation, Presidential, and Retirement series; many documents were drafted or copied by aides, secretaries, cabinet members, or other collaborators, making institutional authorship part of the work’s identity. ([University of Virginia Press](https://www.upress.virginia.edu/title/gewn/); [National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/nhprc/projects/catalog/george-washington))
- **1750s–1799:** *Financial and plantation papers*. Account books, invoices, orders, inventories, crop records, lists of enslaved people, and other household and business records. These are operative records rather than literary works, and many were physically entered by clerks or overseers under Washington’s system of management. ([National Endowment for the Humanities](https://www.neh.gov/about/awards/national-humanities-medals/the-papers-george-washington); [National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/nhprc/projects/catalog/george-washington))
- **1780s–1799:** *Agricultural journals and experimental records*. An increasingly systematic record of weather, planting, harvests, crop rotations, labor, and experimental results. This quasi-scientific project documents both Washington’s agricultural innovation and a plantation economy dependent upon enslaved labor. ([Mount Vernon](https://www.mountvernon.org/library/digitalhistory/digital-encyclopedia/article/george-washington-and-agriculture))

### Published narrative

- **1753–1754:** *The Journal of Major George Washington*. A journey report on Washington’s diplomatic mission to the French commandant in the Ohio country, printed at Williamsburg in 1754 and rapidly reprinted in London. It is both a travel narrative and an official colonial document, and its first publication was undertaken at Governor Robert Dinwiddie’s direction rather than conceived as an autonomous literary book. ([Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/collections/george-washington-papers/articles-and-essays/george-washington-survey-and-mapmaker/); [University of Virginia Press](https://upress.virginia.edu/series/pgw/))

### Military and civic manifestos

- **15 March 1783:** *Newburgh Address*. A speech delivered to Continental Army officers that intervened in the Newburgh crisis and helped prevent military resistance to civilian authority. It is a manifesto in the vocabulary of this atlas because its delivery was itself a consequential political act.
- **8 June 1783:** *Circular Letter to the States*. A farewell communication to the state governors setting out Washington’s conditions for national survival after the Revolution. Recipient copies bear dates between 11 and 21 June, while Washington’s retained copy is dated 8 June, so “8 June” identifies the retained text rather than every manifestation. ([Mount Vernon](https://www.mountvernon.org/library/digitalhistory/digital-encyclopedia/article/circular-letter-to-the-states))
- **2 November 1783:** *Farewell Orders to the Armies of the United States*. A general order formally taking leave of the Continental Army; it is simultaneously an administrative order, public manifesto, and ceremonial act.
- **4 December 1783:** *Farewell Address to the Officers of the Army*. A brief spoken leave-taking at Fraunces Tavern, known principally through contemporary accounts rather than a settled autograph text; the event resists treatment as a securely recoverable written work.
- **23 December 1783:** *Address Resigning the Commission of Commander in Chief*. A speech and performative civic text whose delivery to Congress accomplished Washington’s surrender of military command. The deed and the words are inseparable manifestations of the work.

### Presidential protocols and addresses

- **30 April 1789:** *First Inaugural Address*. The first presidential inaugural speech, delivered to Congress after Washington took the oath. It established an enduring civic protocol even though the Constitution did not require an address; surviving drafts also make its composition a documentary process rather than a single unproblematic object. ([National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/president-george-washingtons-first-inaugural-speech); [U.S. Senate](https://www.senate.gov/artandhistory/senate-stories/treasures-from-the-senate-archives-legislative-executive-relations.htm))
- **1789–1796:** *Annual Messages to Congress*. Eight presidential addresses that reported on public affairs and recommended policy, beginning on 8 January 1790. These operative state papers helped create the protocol now called the State of the Union; they belong to a collaborative executive writing system rather than to solitary literary authorship. ([U.S. Senate](https://www.senate.gov/artandhistory/senate-stories/evolution-of-the-response-to-the-state-of-the-union.htm))
- **3 October 1789:** *Thanksgiving Proclamation*. A presidential proclamation designating a national day of thanksgiving and prayer after Congress requested one. As a manifesto, it did not merely describe observance but attempted to bring a public observance into being.
- **1789–1797:** *Presidential proclamations, messages, nominations, commissions, orders, and memoranda*. The administrative body of texts issued in Washington’s name as president. Cabinet officers and secretaries frequently supplied drafts, so these works have executive or institutional authorship even when the documentary edition assigns them to Washington. ([University of Virginia Press](https://www.upress.virginia.edu/title/gewn/))
- **4 March 1793:** *Second Inaugural Address*. A 135-word ceremonial protocol delivered before Washington’s second oath, conventionally regarded as the shortest United States presidential inaugural address. ([Joint Congressional Committee on Inaugural Ceremonies](https://www.inaugural.senate.gov/2nd-inaugural-ceremonies/))
- **19 September 1796:** *Farewell Address*. A newspaper-published manifesto announcing Washington’s retirement and advising the nation. Authorship is deliberately composite: Washington controlled and authorized the final text, but James Madison contributed to its earlier conceptual ancestry and Alexander Hamilton drafted and revised substantial portions, making sole-authorship labels misleading. ([National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/files/nhprc/annotation/2004/fall-04.pdf); [U.S. Senate](https://www.senate.gov/art-artifacts/fine-art/paintings/31_00015_000.htm))

### Estate, landscape, and architectural Gesamtkunstwerk

- **1754–1799:** *Mount Vernon*. A decades-long architectural, agricultural, and landscape project incorporating the enlarged Mansion, outbuildings, gardens, roads, vistas, farms, fields, and industrial structures. It is best understood as a Gesamtkunstwerk directed and repeatedly redesigned by Washington, but executed by artisans, hired workers, overseers, and enslaved people; the original house predated him, so neither “building” nor sole authorship adequately describes it. ([Mount Vernon: architecture](https://www.mountvernon.org/library/digitalhistory/digital-encyclopedia/article/washington-and-architecture); [Mount Vernon: development](https://www.mountvernon.org/library/digitalhistory/digital-encyclopedia/article/development-of-mount-vernon); [Approaching Mount Vernon](https://catalog.mountvernon.org/digital/api/collection/p16829coll4/id/1317/download))
- **1773–1775 and later alterations:** *Mount Vernon Mansion enlargement and piazza*. Washington’s project extended and transformed an inherited house, giving it its principal late-eighteenth-century form and river façade. Pattern-book sources, builders, artisans, and enslaved labor complicate the familiar description of Washington as its “architect.” ([Mount Vernon](https://www.mountvernon.org/library/digitalhistory/digital-encyclopedia/article/washington-and-architecture))
- **1783–1789, with later development:** *Mount Vernon landscape plan*. A Picturesque composition of lawns, serpentine walks, gardens, groves, roads, framed views, and symmetrically arranged dependencies. Washington adapted published British models and natural conditions rather than producing an entirely unprecedented design. ([Mount Vernon](https://www.mountvernon.org/library/digitalhistory/digital-encyclopedia/article/landscape-and-gardens))
- **completed 1787:** *Greenhouse and adjoining quarters*. A combined plant house and residential structure designed under Washington’s direction for subtropical cultivation while also housing enslaved workers. The original burned in 1835; the present building is a twentieth-century reconstruction, not Washington’s surviving fabric. ([Mount Vernon](https://www.mountvernon.org/the-estate-gardens/location/greenhouse))
- **from 1786:** *Five-farm reorganization of Mount Vernon*. A large-scale agricultural design dividing the estate into Mansion House, Union, Dogue Run, Muddy Hole, and River farms, with numbered fields, rotations, roads, barns, housing, and management systems. It is a project imposed upon land and labor—including enslaved bodies—and therefore resists being treated as a neutral landscape work. ([Mount Vernon](https://www.mountvernon.org/library/digitalhistory/digital-encyclopedia/article/development-of-mount-vernon))
- **late 1780s–1790s:** *Union Farm barn complex*. Washington combined Arthur Young’s supplied barn plan with his own large stable design. It is a collaborative adaptation rather than an invention attributable to Washington alone. ([Mount Vernon](https://edit.mountvernon.org/library/digitalhistory/digital-encyclopedia/article/union-farm))
- **1792–1790s:** *Dogue Run sixteen-sided barn*. A polygonal, two-story threshing barn conceived by Washington as both architecture and grain-processing machine. Carpenter Thomas Green and enslaved carpenters constructed it; the extant Mount Vernon barn is a reconstruction, and Washington’s innovation lay in integrating the treading process with the building rather than inventing polygonal architecture. ([Mount Vernon](https://www.mountvernon.org/library/digitalhistory/digital-encyclopedia/article/16-sided-barn))
- **1790s:** *River Farm barn project*. The third of Washington’s major grain-barn schemes, developed as part of his differentiated farm system. It belongs to the larger agricultural project, and the documentary evidence supports direction and planning more securely than an autonomous architectural authorship. ([Mount Vernon](https://www.mountvernon.org/library/digitalhistory/digital-encyclopedia/article/george-washington-and-agriculture))

### Adapted machines and production systems

- **1765:** *Modified Rotherham plow*. A commissioned adaptation of an existing English swing-plow design, specified by Washington for lighter colonial soils and weaker draft animals. It is an improvement protocol embodied in a machine, not an original invention made with his own hands. ([Mount Vernon](https://edit.mountvernon.org/library/digitalhistory/digital-encyclopedia/article/agricultural-equipment))
- **1780s–1790s:** *Mount Vernon agricultural machinery program*. A practical assemblage including plows, rollers, threshing machinery, and other imported or locally constructed devices tested under Washington’s direction. Because most component machines derived from other designers, the made thing is the experimental and managerial system rather than a catalogue of Washington inventions. ([Mount Vernon](https://edit.mountvernon.org/library/digitalhistory/digital-encyclopedia/article/agricultural-equipment))
- **1790s:** *Automated gristmill system*. An installation of Oliver Evans’s patented continuous-milling technology in Washington’s existing mill. Washington made a productive system through purchase, adaptation, capital, and coerced and hired labor; he did not invent the underlying machinery.
- **1797–1799:** *Mount Vernon whiskey distillery*. A commercial production complex established on James Anderson’s proposal and under Washington’s ownership and direction. Its products and profits depended upon the labor of enslaved and hired distillers, so calling the whiskey simply “made by Washington” would conceal the actual makers.

### Legal and posthumous project

- **9 July 1799:** *Last Will and Testament*. A legal protocol governing Washington’s estate and directing, among other provisions, the emancipation of the enslaved people he personally owned after Martha Washington’s death. It could not free dower slaves belonging to the Custis estate, and its emancipation was deliberately deferred, so the document is both a posthumous project and an incomplete act rather than a simple declaration of liberation.
