# Charles Martel: Research Dossier

## Scope, Method, and Evidentiary Limits

Charles Martel lived in a period for which documentation is sparse, politically interested, and frequently retrospective. No contemporary biography survives. The fullest narrative source, the *Continuations of the Chronicle of Fredegar*, was written under Carolingian patronage: its first continuation was commissioned by Charles’s brother Childebrand, and its later portions by Childebrand’s son Nibelung. It is indispensable but openly dynastic.

Other important evidence includes:

- the near-contemporary Mozarabic or *Chronicle of 754*, composed in Iberia;
- Frankish annals, especially the *Annales Mettenses priores* (“Earlier Annals of Metz”), written much later in a strongly Carolingian and pro-Pippinid environment;
- the *Liber Historiae Francorum*, completed in 727 from a Neustrian perspective;
- papal letters preserved in the *Codex epistolaris Carolinus*;
- charters, episcopal lives, monastic records, necrologies, and later Carolingian polemic;
- modern source criticism, especially Paul Fouracre’s *The Age of Charles Martel*.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported directly by contemporary or near-contemporary evidence, allowing for ordinary chronological uncertainty.
- **Reported fact:** stated in a historical source but not independently verifiable.
- **Disputed reconstruction:** a modern interpretation of incomplete or conflicting evidence.
- **Later tradition / myth:** a story appearing substantially after the event or unsupported by contemporary evidence.
- **Unresolved:** the evidence does not permit a confident conclusion.

Dates before the mid-eighth century are sometimes annalistic rather than calendrically exact. The famous battle conventionally dated 732, for example, has also been assigned to 733.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Principal name:** Charles Martel.

**Contemporary forms:** *Carolus*, *Karl*, *Charles*.

**Later Latin epithet:** *Martellus*.

**Modern-language forms:** Karl Martell in German; Charles Martel in French and English; Carlos Martel in Spanish; Carlo Martello in Italian.

**Meaning of epithet:** “the Hammer.” The epithet is not securely contemporary. Paul Fouracre notes that *Martellus* first appears in ninth-century evidence. Stories that Charles received it immediately after the battle near Tours or because he personally fought with a war hammer are later explanations, not documented facts.

**Titles and political styles:**

- mayor of the palace, initially in Austrasia and later throughout the Frankish kingdoms;
- *dux* or duke;
- *princeps Francorum*, “prince of the Franks”;
- effective ruler of the Frankish polity, although never king;
- sometimes called “vice-king” or “de facto king” by modern historians, but neither was his regular formal title.

**Dynasty:** Pippinid-Arnulfing, retrospectively called Carolingian. The dynasty itself came to be named after Charles.

**Birth:** probably between approximately 685 and 690. About 688 or 689 is the safest conventional estimate.

**Exact birth date:** unknown. The frequently repeated date 23 August 686 has no adequate contemporary foundation.

**Birthplace:** unknown. Herstal, in present-day Belgium, is traditional but not securely documented.

**Death:** 22 October 741, according to Frankish annalistic and necrological tradition.

**Place of death:** the royal villa or palace at Quierzy-sur-Oise, now Quierzy, Aisne, France.

**Burial:** the royal abbey of Saint-Denis, north of Paris.

**Principal offices:** mayor of the palace and effective ruler from the civil wars of 715–718/719 until 741. He ruled without installing a Merovingian king after Theuderic IV died in 737.

## Family and Ancestry

### Father: Pepin of Herstal

Charles’s father was Pepin II, conventionally called Pepin of Herstal or Pepin the Middle, mayor of the palace of Austrasia and, after his victory at Tertry in 687, the dominant political figure in Francia.

Pepin belonged to the union of two powerful Austrasian families:

- the Pippinids, descended from Pepin of Landen;
- the Arnulfings, descended from Arnulf of Metz.

Pepin’s parents were Ansegisel, son of Arnulf of Metz, and Begga, daughter of Pepin of Landen. Charles therefore inherited a dense network of landholding, ecclesiastical patronage, kinship, and aristocratic allegiance concentrated between the Meuse, Moselle, Rhine, and lower Maas regions.

Precise genealogies extending this ancestry farther backward are largely speculative. Medieval Carolingian genealogy was not neutral: it was used to legitimize a family that displaced the Merovingians.

### Mother: Alpaida

Charles’s mother is named as Alpaida, Alpais, or Chalpaida. Her origin is uncertain. Later sources sometimes connect her to the region of Liège.

Older writing regularly calls her Pepin’s “concubine” and Charles “illegitimate.” These terms can mislead. Pepin was married to Plectrude, but aristocratic unions in seventh- and eighth-century Francia did not always conform to later canonical distinctions between a single valid marriage and concubinage. Charles’s weaker initial succession position is documented; his exact legal status is not.

### The Lambert of Maastricht legend

Later hagiography made Alpaida responsible for the murder of Lambert, bishop of Maastricht-Liège. In developed versions, Lambert condemned Pepin’s union with Alpaida; Alpaida or her brother Dodo then arranged Lambert’s killing.

**Evidentiary status:** later hagiographical tradition. Lambert was killed, probably around 705, in an aristocratic feud. Alpaida’s responsibility cannot be established. The tale expressed ecclesiastical opposition to irregular aristocratic unions and later hostility to Charles’s maternal line.

### Plectrude

Plectrude, Pepin’s principal wife, was not Charles’s mother. She came from a wealthy aristocratic family with strong property interests around Cologne and the Moselle. Her sons Drogo and Grimoald died before Pepin. When Pepin died in 714, she attempted to preserve power for her grandson Theudoald.

Plectrude’s treatment of Charles as a rival—most clearly his confinement—shows that he was a serious adult claimant but not her chosen heir.

### Siblings and half-siblings

The exact sibling structure is imperfectly known.

- **Childebrand I:** usually regarded as Charles’s full or half-brother. He became a count and military commander, participated in the southern campaigns, and commissioned the continuation of Fredegar’s chronicle. This was one of Charles’s most important familial and political collaborations.
- **Drogo of Champagne:** son of Pepin and Plectrude; therefore Charles’s paternal half-brother. He died before Pepin.
- **Grimoald the Younger:** son of Pepin and Plectrude; paternal half-brother. He was murdered in 714.
- Other proposed brothers, including an Arnulf, appear in genealogical reconstructions but are not equally secure.

### Wives and partners

#### Rotrude

Charles’s first principal wife or partner was Rotrude, also called Chrotrud, Chrodtrudis, or Rotrudis. She died in 724.

Her parentage is unknown. Attempts to make her a daughter of Lambert of Hesbaye or otherwise assign her a complete genealogy are reconstructions rather than established facts.

Children generally attributed to Charles and Rotrude include:

- **Carloman**, born approximately 706–713; mayor of the palace after Charles; retired to monastic life in 747 and died in 754.
- **Pepin III**, “Pepin the Short,” born approximately 714; succeeded Charles jointly with Carloman and became king of the Franks in 751.
- **Hiltrude**, who married Odilo, duke of Bavaria, apparently against her brothers’ wishes after Charles’s death.
- Possibly **Landrada**, though her maternity and even placement in the immediate family are disputed.

#### Swanachild

After the Bavarian campaigns of the 720s Charles formed a union with Swanachild, also written Suanachild, Sunnichild, or Sonichild. She belonged to or was closely connected with the Bavarian Agilolfing ducal house.

Whether she was captured, brought away under compulsion, or entered a negotiated dynastic marriage is unresolved. The continuator’s language reflects the perspective of the victorious Frankish court and does not reveal her agency.

Their securely attested son was:

- **Grifo**, born approximately 726. Charles assigned him a portion of the inheritance in 741, but Carloman and Pepin dispossessed and imprisoned him. He continued to contest their authority until his death in 753.

A daughter sometimes identified as **Oda, Auda, or Aldana**, later associated genealogically with the family of William of Gellone, has uncertain maternity and rests on later evidence.

#### Ruodhaid

Some sources or reconstructions identify a partner named Ruodhaid or Chrothais. Children attributed to this union include:

- **Bernard**;
- **Hieronymus** or Jerome;
- **Remigius**, later bishop or archbishop of Rouen.

The maternity, legitimacy, and exact status of these children are not uniformly documented. Medieval genealogies often reordered Charles’s offspring according to later political importance.

### Descendants

Through Pepin III, Charles was the grandfather of:

- **Charlemagne**;
- **Carloman I**;
- Gisela and other children of Pepin and Bertrada.

The later Carolingian royal and imperial line descended from Charles, making him a foundational ancestor of rulers in Francia, Italy, and the Carolingian successor kingdoms.

## Childhood and Early Life

Virtually nothing is known about Charles’s childhood.

His birth into Pepin’s household placed him near the center of Austrasian power, but Plectrude’s sons initially had stronger succession prospects. Charles likely grew up amid:

- Pepin’s armed household;
- royal and aristocratic assemblies;
- estates in the Meuse-Rhine-Moselle zone;
- ongoing campaigns against Neustrian opponents, Frisians, Saxons, and peripheral dukes;
- a political culture in which treasure, landed patronage, kinship, hostages, marriage, and personal military followings were inseparable.

No reliable source records his education, literacy, teachers, childhood residence, or youthful military service. Assertions that he received a specific clerical education, trained under a named warrior, or displayed extraordinary strength in childhood belong to imaginative reconstruction.

His later conduct shows familiarity with:

- aristocratic coalition-building;
- the geography and seasonal requirements of Frankish campaigning;
- episcopal and monastic patronage;
- allocation of estates and offices;
- diplomacy with popes, Lombards, Bavarians, Aquitanians, and missionary bishops.

These capacities do not prove formal schooling.

## Education and Formation

No school or tutor is documented.

Charles’s formation was probably practical and household-based. Frankish aristocratic sons learned through participation in the lord’s household, hunting, weapons training, estate management, assemblies, litigation, gift exchange, and military expeditions.

Likely formative influences included:

- **Pepin of Herstal:** the model of an Austrasian mayor controlling several Frankish kingdoms while retaining Merovingian kings.
- **Plectrude and the succession crisis:** her effort to exclude Charles demonstrated the political value of treasure, fortified cities, church alliances, and recognized descent.
- **Austrasian military elites:** Charles’s recovery after his first defeat depended on their support.
- **Childebrand:** brother, military collaborator, and later sponsor of dynastic historical writing.
- **Willibrord and Boniface:** missionary churchmen whose work intersected with Frankish expansion, although Charles’s relationship with Boniface was less intimate than later Carolingian narratives sometimes suggest.
- **Liutprand of the Lombards:** an important diplomatic ally and, in later accounts, ritual patron of Charles’s son Pepin.

## The Frankish Political Setting

The Merovingian kingdom was divided conventionally into:

- **Austrasia:** the eastern Frankish lands, centered on the Rhine, Meuse, and Moselle;
- **Neustria:** western and northwestern Francia;
- **Burgundy:** the Rhône-Saône region and related territories;
- **Aquitaine:** increasingly autonomous under its own dukes;
- peripheral or semi-autonomous regions including Frisia, Saxony, Alemannia, Bavaria, Provence, and Septimania.

A mayor of the palace had developed from a household officer into the chief broker of royal administration, aristocratic patronage, and military power. The Merovingian kings remained sacred and legally important. Charles did not simply abolish them: he ruled through kings until 737 and only then allowed the throne to remain vacant.

## Chronological Life History

## The Succession Crisis, 714–715

Pepin of Herstal died on 16 December 714. His designated dynastic arrangement had already been destabilized by the death of his son Grimoald earlier that year. Plectrude attempted to govern for the child Theudoald, Grimoald’s son.

Charles was confined, probably at Cologne.

**Documented core:** Plectrude controlled Pepin’s treasury and attempted to exclude Charles; Charles escaped or was released amid the political collapse.

The Neustrians rejected Austrasian control. They selected Ragenfrid as mayor and raised Chilperic II, a Merovingian who had previously lived as the monk Daniel, as king. At Compiègne in 715 they defeated Theudoald’s supporters.

Theudoald’s subsequent fate is uncertain. He disappears from effective politics.

## First Defeat and Recovery, 716

The Neustrian coalition, allied with the Frisian ruler Radbod, attacked Austrasia and reached Cologne. Charles opposed them but suffered a defeat, conventionally placed near Cologne.

Plectrude bought off the attackers with part of Pepin’s accumulated treasure. As they withdrew, Charles attacked near Amblève.

The ambush or battle of Amblève was Charles’s first major success. The Fredegar continuation presents it as a turning point. Exact troop numbers and tactical descriptions in later retellings are not reliable.

Charles’s ability to rebuild after defeat indicates that he already commanded an independent Austrasian following.

## Vincy and the Struggle for Kingship, 717

On 21 March 717, according to the accepted chronology, Charles defeated Chilperic II and Ragenfrid near Vincy, close to Cambrai.

He pursued his enemies toward Paris and then returned to Cologne, where Plectrude surrendered political control and treasure. Her subsequent life is obscure; she was not necessarily subjected to the dramatic punishments found in later retellings.

Charles installed his own Merovingian king, Chlothar IV. This is important: Charles’s early program was not the abolition of Merovingian monarchy but control of it. Rival mayors each required a king through whom lawful authority could be expressed.

## Soissons and Frankish Supremacy, 718–719

Chilperic II and Ragenfrid sought support from Eudo, duke of Aquitaine. Charles defeated their coalition near Soissons, generally dated 718.

Eudo withdrew, taking Chilperic with him. A settlement followed. Eudo surrendered or recognized Chilperic, whom Charles accepted as king after Chlothar IV’s death. Chilperic II died in 721 and was succeeded by Theuderic IV.

By approximately 718–719, Charles had become the dominant mayor throughout Francia, although regional dukes and aristocratic groupings retained substantial independence.

## Frisia, Saxony, and the Northern Frontier, 718–724

After Radbod’s death in 719, Charles intervened in Frisia. Frankish political and missionary expansion advanced together but were not identical projects.

Willibrord’s church received support from the Pippinid family. Charles confirmed or enlarged ecclesiastical protection, while military campaigns weakened Frisian resistance.

Charles also campaigned against the Saxons, including expeditions conventionally dated 720 and 724. The annals’ claims of “subjection” must be treated cautiously. Frankish rulers repeatedly declared peripheral peoples subdued, only to campaign against them again. Such operations often obtained tribute, hostages, plunder, or temporary recognition rather than permanent administration.

In 724, Charles finally neutralized Ragenfrid, who retained Angers or a limited territorial base. The arrangement illustrates Charles’s willingness to leave defeated rivals locally established when total removal was impractical.

## Bavaria and Alemannia, 725–730

Charles campaigned in Bavaria in 725 and again around 728. Duke Hugbert was brought into dependence on Frankish power.

During or after these interventions, Charles brought Swanachild into his household. She belonged to the Agilolfing network. The relationship served as a political connection with Bavaria but later gave her son Grifo a dynastic claim threatening Carloman and Pepin.

Charles intervened in Alemannia, where Duke Lantfrid died around 730. The evidence does not support a simple picture of complete annexation. Charles dismantled or weakened autonomous ducal power while using local counts, bishops, monasteries, and military clients.

## Conflict with Eudo of Aquitaine, 731

Eudo had ruled Aquitaine with considerable independence. He also confronted Umayyad expansion from al-Andalus. At an uncertain date he allied his daughter—or a woman described as his daughter—with the Berber commander Uthman ibn Naissa, called Munuza in Latin sources.

Munuza rebelled against the Andalusi governor. Abd al-Rahman ibn Abd Allah al-Ghafiqi defeated and killed him. The story of Eudo’s daughter being captured and sent east appears in later or ideologically shaped accounts and cannot be reconstructed confidently.

In 731 Charles crossed the Loire and campaigned against Eudo, accusing him in the Frankish account of breaking a treaty. Charles ravaged Aquitanian territory and carried away plunder.

The conflict weakened Eudo immediately before Abd al-Rahman’s major northern expedition.

## The Umayyad Campaign and Battle Near Tours-Poitiers, 732 or 733

### Background

Muslim armies had entered Iberia in 711 and occupied most of the former Visigothic kingdom. They crossed the Pyrenees repeatedly, taking Narbonne and establishing power in Septimania. Raids or campaigns reached Toulouse, Autun, the Rhône valley, and Aquitaine.

In 721, before Charles’s intervention, Eudo had defeated an Umayyad army near Toulouse. This victory is often minimized in later Frankish and nationalist narratives.

In 732—possibly 733—Abd al-Rahman led a large force across the western Pyrenees. He defeated Eudo near Bordeaux, sacked or burned churches and settlements, and advanced toward the Loire, possibly attracted by the wealth of the shrine of Martin at Tours.

Eudo sought Charles’s help. Later accounts that he formally surrendered Aquitaine as the price of aid are not securely documented, although Charles emerged politically stronger.

### The battle

The armies met somewhere between Poitiers and Tours. The exact site is unknown. Moussais-la-Bataille is traditional, not proven. The exact day is also unknown; 10 October 732 is conventional but not securely established.

The *Continuations of Fredegar* report that Charles formed his battle line, attacked, killed Abd al-Rahman, defeated the army, and returned victorious. The *Chronicle of 754*, written in Muslim-ruled Iberia, provides the famous image of the northern peoples standing “like a wall” or “a glacier” and says the battle lasted into the night.

Abd al-Rahman was killed. The surviving Andalusi force withdrew during the night, abandoning its camp.

### What can and cannot be established

**Documented facts:**

- Abd al-Rahman campaigned north of the Pyrenees.
- Eudo was defeated before the main battle.
- Charles commanded the Frankish force.
- Abd al-Rahman was killed.
- The Umayyad army withdrew.
- The battle enhanced Charles’s prestige.

**Unresolved or unsupported:**

- troop numbers;
- casualty totals;
- the exact site and exact date;
- detailed tactical formations;
- claims that Charles invented heavy cavalry for the battle;
- claims that the Franks fought entirely as infantry or entirely as armored cavalry;
- the story that plundering in the Muslim camp alone decided the battle;
- assertions that Charles personally killed Abd al-Rahman;
- assertions that one afternoon conclusively determined whether all Europe would become Muslim.

### Significance

The battle checked this particular expedition and reinforced Charles’s position north of the Loire. It did not expel Muslim power from Gaul: Narbonne remained under Andalusi rule until Pepin III’s reign. Further campaigns occurred in Provence and Septimania.

Edward Gibbon and later European writers elevated the battle into a decisive civilizational turning point. Modern scholarship generally treats it as important without accepting the strongest counterfactual claim that it alone “saved Christianity” or permanently halted all Islamic expansion.

## Consolidation in Burgundy, 733–735

Charles turned toward Burgundy, replacing or subordinating local officeholders and distributing strategic positions to loyal followers. His methods combined:

- military occupation;
- appointment of counts and bishops;
- confiscation or reassignment of estates;
- use of relatives and household men;
- negotiated survival of cooperative elites.

These interventions were not modern centralized administration. They were a reorganization of aristocratic power around Charles’s household.

In 734 Charles campaigned against the Frisian leader Bubo or Poppo. A fleet transported Frankish forces into northern Frisia. Bubo was killed at the battle of the Boarn. Pagan sanctuaries were destroyed and booty taken, according to the Frankish account.

The campaign advanced both Frankish dominance and Christian mission, but the violence and destruction should not be collapsed into a purely religious narrative: control of trade routes, tribute, and coastal power was also involved.

Eudo died around 735. Charles entered Aquitaine and confronted Eudo’s son Hunald. Hunald ultimately retained the duchy while acknowledging some form of Charles’s superior position. The degree of subordination is disputed.

## The Southern Wars, 736–739

### Provence and Avignon

A coalition of Provençal magnates, associated particularly with Maurontus, sought Andalusi support against Frankish expansion. Muslim forces occupied Avignon.

In 736 Charles and Childebrand campaigned down the Rhône. Avignon was stormed. Later accounts describe ladders, siege engines, and a massacre; the basic capture is secure, but detailed reconstructions are less certain.

### Siege of Narbonne

Charles besieged Narbonne, the chief Andalusi base north of the Pyrenees. He could not take it. This was a significant failure and demonstrates the limits of his power.

A relief force arrived by sea. Charles defeated it near the River Berre, probably in 737. The Fredegar continuation presents an emphatic Frankish victory and describes fugitives drowning or being killed.

### Destruction in Septimania

Charles’s forces devastated several cities or their fortifications, including Nîmes, Agde, Béziers, and Maguelonne, according to the pro-Carolingian narrative itself. Nîmes’s amphitheatre and gates are said to have been burned.

These were not simply defensive battles against an invading army. They were campaigns for control of contested cities whose Gothic, Christian, and Muslim-aligned elites did not necessarily welcome Frankish rule. Charles withdrew without conquering Narbonne.

### Renewed Provençal intervention

Maurontus and his allies remained active. Charles returned or sent forces south in 738–739.

He coordinated with Liutprand, king of the Lombards. When Lombard support entered Provence, Maurontus withdrew into the Alpine region. Charles and Childebrand imposed a stronger Frankish settlement.

## Saxon Campaign, 738

Charles mounted another campaign against the Saxons in 738, obtaining tribute or submission.

The recurrence of such expeditions shows that “conquest” was provisional. Charles did not incorporate Saxony as comprehensively as Charlemagne later attempted to do.

## Papal Appeals and Lombard Diplomacy, 739–740

Pope Gregory III faced pressure from Liutprand’s Lombards and sought Frankish assistance. Two papal letters to Charles survive in the *Codex epistolaris Carolinus*. Gregory invoked Saint Peter, Roman loyalty, and Charles’s role as protector.

One translated letter calls on Charles not to prefer friendship with the Lombard kings over attachment to Saint Peter and offers or invokes extraordinary honors and responsibilities.

Charles did not launch the requested invasion of Italy. Reasons likely included:

- his existing alliance and personal relationship with Liutprand;
- Lombard assistance in Provence;
- illness and age;
- the practical burden of a transalpine campaign;
- the novelty of direct Frankish intervention in papal-Lombard affairs.

Later tradition says Charles sent his son Pepin to Liutprand, who cut the boy’s hair in a ritual of adoptive or paternal sponsorship. The relationship is plausible and reported in Carolingian sources, although its precise legal meaning is debated.

Charles’s restraint contrasts with Pepin III’s later Italian campaigns and the eventual Carolingian-Papal alliance.

## Rule Without a King, 737–741

Theuderic IV died in 737. Charles did not appoint another Merovingian king.

He continued to issue commands and control the realm as mayor and *princeps*. This was an extraordinary constitutional condition, but the evidence does not show that he formally declared the monarchy abolished or attempted to crown himself.

The absence of a king may have reflected:

- Charles’s overwhelming personal authority;
- difficulty finding or agreeing upon an acceptable Merovingian;
- an experimental interregnum;
- preparation for dynastic change;
- no more than pragmatic postponement.

The claim that Charles asked the pope for permission to become king is unproven. That narrative more securely belongs to Pepin’s deposition of Childeric III in 751.

## Government, Institutions, and Political Structure

Charles did not possess a modern state apparatus. His power rested upon overlapping resources:

- dynastic estates;
- royal fisc and palace treasure;
- military followings;
- counts, dukes, bishops, and abbots;
- annual assemblies;
- hostages and oaths;
- negotiated tribute;
- confiscated or reassigned property;
- marriage and kinship;
- control of Merovingian kings and royal legitimacy.

### The mayorship

As mayor, Charles stood between the king, aristocracy, church, and army. After 737 the office’s practical authority survived even without a king.

### Counts, bishops, and abbots

Charles placed allies in strategic secular and ecclesiastical offices. Some appointees were laymen or warriors controlling abbeys. Such arrangements later drew criticism from reformers, but they were also mechanisms for maintaining garrisons, provisioning followers, and controlling local wealth.

### Childebrand’s position

Childebrand commanded in Burgundy and Provence and became an important regional power. His sponsorship of the Fredegar continuation helped fix an official family memory in which Charles appeared as the victorious restorer of Frankish unity.

### Political violence

Charles’s rule was created and maintained through warfare, ravaging, confiscation, forced submission, hostage-taking, and the destruction of towns and sanctuaries. These actions are reported even in favorable sources.

No surviving judicial record permits a systematic count of executions, massacres, or dispossessions. Medieval victory formulas frequently exaggerate devastation, while dynastic chronicles often omit internal repression.

## Companies, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

Modern categories such as companies, securities, patents, or commercial corporations do not apply.

### Property and wealth

Charles’s economic base included:

- inherited Pippinid-Arnulfing estates;
- royal fiscal lands;
- treasure seized from or surrendered by Plectrude;
- tribute and campaign booty;
- confiscated aristocratic property;
- control or redistribution of ecclesiastical estates;
- revenues attached to counties, bishoprics, and abbeys.

No comprehensive inventory of his wealth survives. Exact amounts are unrecoverable.

### Military resources

Charles is sometimes credited with creating a mounted feudal army funded through confiscated church land. This became influential through nineteenth- and twentieth-century theories about the origins of feudalism.

The strong version is not demonstrable. Mounted elite warfare predated Charles; the relationship between stirrups, cavalry, land grants, and “feudalism” was neither instantaneous nor uniform.

More cautious conclusions are possible:

- Charles rewarded followers with access to land and office.
- Church estates were sometimes placed under lay control or used to sustain military clients.
- His repeated long-distance campaigns required substantial logistical and aristocratic coordination.
- There is no evidence that he invented a new universal cavalry system in a single reform.

### Fortifications and sieges

His armies captured fortified places and used siege operations, notably at Avignon and Narbonne. No invention or specific military technology can be securely attributed to Charles personally.

## Religious Policy and Intellectual Development

No writing by Charles explains his religious beliefs. He operated as a Christian Frankish ruler and patron while also appropriating ecclesiastical resources.

### Missions

Charles supported or protected missionary work associated with:

- Willibrord in Frisia;
- Boniface in Germanic territories;
- foundations and churches serving Frankish expansion.

Boniface acknowledged Charles’s political protection, but surviving correspondence also shows frustration with the Frankish church’s condition. Boniface’s closest reform alliance developed under Carloman and Pepin after Charles’s death.

### Church appointments

Charles placed relatives, allies, or lay administrators over ecclesiastical institutions. Examples commonly discussed include:

- Hugh, a relative who accumulated episcopal or abbatial authority;
- Milo, associated with Trier and Reims in controversial circumstances;
- lay control over monasteries and their revenues.

The records are incomplete, and later reformers had reasons to portray the preceding generation as disordered.

### Church property

The traditional accusation is that Charles systematically confiscated church land to fund cavalry and thereby despoiled the Frankish church.

The underlying facts are more complex:

- church property was appropriated or reassigned;
- bishops and abbots lost control of some estates;
- lay military supporters held ecclesiastical land;
- some transfers may have taken the form of *precaria*, grants retaining a formal ecclesiastical interest;
- monasteries also received protection and patronage from Charles;
- much of the fiercest rhetoric appears in later reform and property disputes.

The *Visio Eucherii*, used by Hincmar of Reims in 858, became the most influential text in constructing Charles as a sacrilegious despoiler condemned to hell. It is not contemporary evidence for his eternal fate or even an impartial record of his property policy.

### Relations with Rome

The 739–740 correspondence proves direct papal recognition of Charles as the decisive Frankish ruler. It does not prove a completed alliance. Charles declined or failed to give Gregory III the military intervention sought.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Pepin of Herstal

Father and political predecessor. Pepin’s accumulation of power supplied Charles with both a model and a contested inheritance.

### Plectrude

Stepmother and initial enemy. She controlled the treasury and imprisoned or confined him but was overcome after his military recovery.

### Childebrand

Brother and principal collaborator. He commanded southern campaigns, administered strategic territory, and sponsored dynastic historiography.

### Ragenfrid

Neustrian mayor and long-term rival. Charles defeated him repeatedly but eventually allowed him a reduced base around Angers.

### Chilperic II

Merovingian king initially aligned with Ragenfrid and Eudo. After Charles defeated their coalition, he accepted Chilperic as king, demonstrating political accommodation rather than simple dynastic extermination.

### Eudo of Aquitaine

Rival, temporary adversary, and eventual military partner. Charles invaded Eudo’s lands in 731; Eudo then sought Charles’s assistance after defeat by Abd al-Rahman. Their relationship was strategic and unequal but not reducible to friendship or permanent submission.

### Hunald of Aquitaine

Eudo’s son. Charles campaigned in Aquitaine after Eudo’s death and obtained acknowledgment while leaving Hunald in power.

### Abd al-Rahman al-Ghafiqi

Governor of al-Andalus and battlefield opponent. They encountered one another in the campaign of 732/733. No negotiation or personal meeting outside battle is documented.

### Maurontus

Provençal leader and political enemy. Maurontus aligned with Andalusi forces against Frankish expansion. Charles and Childebrand campaigned repeatedly to break his coalition.

### Liutprand

King of the Lombards, ally, and diplomatic partner. His support helped Charles in Provence. Their relationship complicated Pope Gregory III’s appeal for Frankish action against the Lombards.

### Pope Gregory III

Correspondent and would-be ally. Gregory sought Charles’s military protection against Liutprand. Charles did not provide the requested intervention.

### Willibrord

Missionary bishop associated with the Frankish expansion in Frisia. Charles continued family patronage of his mission.

### Boniface

Missionary bishop operating east of the Rhine. Charles supplied political protection, but claims of a close reform partnership should be qualified. Boniface’s program obtained stronger institutional support from Charles’s sons.

### Carloman, Pepin, and Grifo

Charles’s sons and intended heirs. His final division recognized all three, but the unequal territorial and maternal bases of their claims produced immediate conflict after his death.

## Death and Succession

Charles became ill in his final years. The exact illness is not documented. No reliable evidence establishes poisoning, battle wounds, addiction, or a specific chronic disease.

He died at Quierzy on 22 October 741.

His burial at Saint-Denis was politically significant. The abbey was closely associated with Merovingian royalty; interment there placed the uncrowned mayor among royal dead and anticipated his descendants’ appropriation of Frankish kingship.

### Partition

Before his death Charles divided authority principally between:

- **Carloman:** Austrasia, Alemannia, Thuringia, and related eastern zones;
- **Pepin:** Neustria, Burgundy, and Provence;
- **Grifo:** a territorial share drawn from lands within or between the others’ portions, according to the dynastic narrative.

The exact geography of Grifo’s inheritance is uncertain.

After Charles died, Carloman and Pepin moved against Grifo and Swanachild. Grifo was besieged at Laon and imprisoned; Swanachild was confined in the abbey of Chelles.

This outcome suggests that Charles’s final settlement did not have enough institutional support to prevent his elder sons from revising it.

Regional revolts followed in Aquitaine, Alemannia, Bavaria, and elsewhere. Carloman and Pepin restored a Merovingian king, Childeric III, in 743. The restoration indicates that Charles’s personal rule without a king could not automatically be inherited.

Carloman retired in 747. Pepin became sole mayor, deposed Childeric III with papal approval, and was made king in 751. Charles thereby became the father of the first Carolingian king and grandfather of Charlemagne.

## Controversies, Accusations, and Unresolved Questions

## Legitimacy and Birth

**Claim:** Charles was Pepin’s illegitimate son by a concubine.

**Evidence:** He was Alpaida’s son, not Plectrude’s; Plectrude attempted to exclude him. Later terminology labels Alpaida a concubine.

**Assessment:** Charles’s disadvantaged dynastic position is documented. Applying later canon-law legitimacy categories to his birth is problematic.

## Imprisonment by Plectrude

**Claim:** Plectrude imprisoned Charles immediately after Pepin’s death.

**Evidence:** Supported by Frankish narrative tradition.

**Uncertainty:** location, length, prison conditions, and whether he escaped unaided are unknown.

## Usurpation of Merovingian Authority

Charles defeated rival mayors, controlled successive kings, and ruled without a king after 737. He never formally took the royal title.

Calling him a “usurper” is an interpretation, not a contemporary judicial finding. His position combined inherited office, military conquest, aristocratic acceptance, and control of established royal structures.

## Church Despoilment

**Documented core:** ecclesiastical property and offices were placed in lay or military hands; later churchmen regarded some transfers as wrongful.

**Later charge:** Charles conducted a general spoliation of the church and was damned for it.

**Assessment:** appropriation occurred, but the image of a single systematic confiscation program is overly neat. Some evidence reflects ninth-century disputes and reform rhetoric.

## Expulsion of Eucherius of Orléans

Later tradition says Bishop Eucherius opposed Charles’s seizure of church property and was exiled to Cologne, then confined at Saint-Trond.

The bishop’s displacement may have a historical basis, but the developed moral narrative is bound to the later damnation legend. Exact causation is not independently secure.

## Destruction in Provence and Septimania

The favorable Fredegar continuation admits that Charles’s forces burned or ruined towns and fortifications. These were acts of political and military coercion against communities aligned with or occupied by Andalusi power.

Terms such as massacre and genocide require evidence not available for these campaigns. The sources provide neither reliable population data nor casualty totals.

## “Saving Europe” at Tours

**Fact:** Charles defeated Abd al-Rahman’s expedition.

**Later counterfactual:** without Charles, Islam would inevitably have conquered all Christian Europe.

**Assessment:** unprovable. The battle was significant, but Umayyad logistical capacity, internal conflicts, Aquitanian resistance, Byzantine power, and political fragmentation prevent deterministic conclusions.

## Foundation of Feudalism

**Claim:** Charles confiscated church land, distributed it to mounted warriors, and thereby invented feudalism.

**Assessment:** disputed and largely rejected in its strongest form. The theory compresses long-term changes in land tenure, vassalage, military organization, and political authority into a single reform for which no decree survives.

## War-hammer Weapon

No contemporary source says Charles carried a hammer. The nickname is later than his lifetime. Visual depictions of him wielding a hammer are iconographic interpretations of the epithet.

## Exact Birthplace and Date

Unknown. Herstal and 23 August 686 are traditional or genealogical claims, not securely documented facts.

## Exact Battle Date

732 remains the conventional date. Some chronological arguments favor 733. Neither 10 October nor 25 October is established beyond dispute.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## “The Hammer”

### Story

Charles earned the name Martel because he hammered the Muslims at Tours, personally struck enemies like a hammer, or used a literal war hammer.

### Appearance and circulation

The epithet is first securely recorded in the ninth century. Later chronicles and popular histories linked it retrospectively to Tours.

### Evidence

No contemporary battle account calls him Martel or gives him a hammer. The epithet may reflect his general military reputation rather than a specific battle.

### Effect

It converted a complex mayor and dynastic warlord into an instantly recognizable martial hero. It also gave later artists a weapon-based visual attribute.

## Charles as the Savior of Christian Europe

### Story

Tours was one of history’s decisive battles; Charles prevented the replacement of Christianity by Islam throughout Europe.

### Development

Medieval Christian sources celebrated divine assistance but did not uniformly give the battle the later global significance. Early modern and Enlightenment historians, especially Edward Gibbon, developed the counterfactual image of Muslim armies reaching Britain. Nineteenth-century nationalist history popularized Charles as defender of France and Christendom.

### Evidence

The victory and Abd al-Rahman’s death are secure. The predicted conquest of Europe is not demonstrable. Muslim rule remained at Narbonne, and Charles’s later southern campaigns were indecisive at that city.

### Effect

Charles became a symbol successively for monarchy, French nationalism, European Christian identity, and modern anti-immigration or anti-Muslim politics. Opponents of those movements have sometimes reacted by minimizing the battle; that reaction does not negate the event’s military importance.

## The Damnation of Charles Martel

### Story

Bishop Eucherius of Orléans had a vision of Charles suffering in hell because he seized church property. When Charles’s tomb was opened, witnesses found it blackened or empty, with a dragon or serpent emerging.

### Source history

Hincmar of Reims invoked the story in his 858 letter associated with the Council of Quierzy. Versions of the *Visio Eucherii* circulated independently in multiple manuscripts. The story appeared more than a century after Charles’s death during disputes about royal use of ecclesiastical property.

### Evidence

There is no contemporary report of the vision, tomb opening, dragon, or infernal judgment. Hincmar had a direct institutional interest in discouraging rulers from appropriating church property.

### Effect

The vision became the chief hostile mythology surrounding Charles. It preserved memory of genuine disputes over ecclesiastical resources while transforming them into supernatural proof.

## Alpaida and Saint Lambert

### Story

Lambert condemned Pepin’s adultery with Alpaida; Alpaida incited her brother to murder him.

### Evidence

Lambert’s violent death is historical. Alpaida’s role appears in later hagiographical elaboration and cannot be established.

### Effect

The story stained Charles’s maternal ancestry and linked his family to sacrilege before his birth or youth.

## Charles Offered the Roman Consulate

Papal correspondence employs exalted language and appeals to Charles as protector of Saint Peter. Later accounts sometimes say the Romans formally offered him the consulship or transferred sovereignty.

The exact constitutional meaning of the papal offers is disputed. The surviving letters document an urgent request for protection, not Charles’s coronation or acceptance of Roman government.

## Founder of the First French Order of Knighthood

Seventeenth-century antiquarian tradition sometimes credited Charles with creating an early chivalric order. No eighth-century evidence supports it. The story projected later institutions of knighthood backward onto an ancestral military hero.

## Founder of the Carolingian Empire

Charles did not become king or emperor and did not establish the later empire’s institutions in finished form. Yet his consolidation of the Frankish aristocracy, his sons’ succession, and his dynasty’s control of the palace made Pepin’s kingship and Charlemagne’s empire possible. “Founder” is therefore a retrospective political characterization, not an office he claimed.

---

## Works by Charles Martel

Charles is not known to have authored books, treatises, poems, memoirs, or chronicles.

### Surviving or attributed documentary material

1. **Papal correspondence addressed to Charles, 739–740**  
   Two letters from Gregory III survive in the *Codex epistolaris Carolinus*. These are not works by Charles, and no reply from him survives.

2. **Charters and diplomas issued in Charles’s name**  
   Individual grants, confirmations, and notices are preserved or known through later copies. Their authenticity and textual integrity must be assessed separately. They were products of clerical drafting and institutional preservation, not personal literary composition.

3. **Reported commands and speeches**  
   Chronicles sometimes paraphrase decisions or battlefield conduct. No verbatim speech can confidently be assigned to Charles.

4. **Legal or administrative acts**  
   Property transfers, appointments, protections, and settlements are attributable to his government. No coherent personal law code or capitulary securely authored by Charles survives.

### Genuine authorship assessment

There is no evidence that Charles personally composed any surviving text. Documents “of Charles Martel” are acts issued under his authority, copies of grants, or later reports.

## Principal Primary Sources

### *Continuations of the Chronicle of Fredegar*

The primary narrative for Charles’s career. Its account is close to the dynasty: Childebrand commissioned the initial continuation and Nibelung continued the project. It supplies battles, campaigns, successions, and the southern wars but celebrates Charles in formulaic victorious language and omits inconvenient dynastic conflicts.

Key modern edition and translation:

- J. M. Wallace-Hadrill, ed. and trans., *The Fourth Book of the Chronicle of Fredegar with Its Continuations* (1960).

### *Chronicle of 754*

A near-contemporary Latin chronicle written in Iberia under Muslim rule. It provides a non-Frankish account of the campaign of Abd al-Rahman and the battle with Charles. Its famous description of the northern fighters as an immovable formation is important but still literary.

Modern translation:

- Kenneth Baxter Wolf, *Conquerors and Chroniclers of Early Medieval Spain*.

### *Liber Historiae Francorum*

A Neustrian Frankish history completed in 727. Especially important for the succession crisis, Neustrian politics, and the rise of Charles, although it ends before Tours.

### *Annales Mettenses priores*

The Earlier Annals of Metz provide a fuller Carolingian narrative but were compiled around 805, long after Charles’s death, in a political environment favorable to his descendants. They often retroject later dynastic legitimacy.

### *Codex epistolaris Carolinus*

A collection ordered under Charlemagne in 791, preserving papal letters to Carolingian rulers. Its first letters contain Gregory III’s appeals to Charles.

Modern critical translation:

- Dorine van Espelo, Richard Pollard, Richard Price, and Rosamond McKitterick, *Codex Epistolaris Carolinus: Letters from the Popes to the Frankish Rulers, 739–791* (2021).

### Boniface’s correspondence

The letters of Boniface and his circle illuminate mission, church discipline, aristocratic protection, and the condition of the Frankish church, though they do not constitute a biography of Charles.

### *Visio Eucherii* and Hincmar of Reims

Ninth-century evidence for the hostile ecclesiastical memory of Charles as a despoiler. Valuable for reception history, not as contemporary verification of supernatural claims.

## Books and Major Works About Charles Martel

### Modern biographies and monographs

- Paul Fouracre, *The Age of Charles Martel* (2000). The principal modern English-language monograph; source-critical and attentive to political structures, property, church relations, and later memory.
- Richard Gerberding, *The Rise of the Carolingians and the Liber Historiae Francorum* (1987). Essential for the sources and political environment of Charles’s ascent.
- Pierre Riché, *The Carolingians: A Family Who Forged Europe* (French original, *Les Carolingiens: une famille qui fit l’Europe*, 1983). Broad dynastic synthesis.
- Rosamond McKitterick, *The Frankish Kingdoms under the Carolingians, 751–987* (1983). Begins formally after Charles but provides essential institutional and dynastic context.
- Bernard S. Bachrach, *Early Carolingian Warfare: Prelude to Empire* (2001). Detailed military interpretation; some reconstructions are debated.
- Hugh Kennedy, *The Great Arab Conquests* (2007). Context for Umayyad expansion.
- Roger Collins, *The Arab Conquest of Spain, 710–797* (1989). Iberian and Septimanian context.
- Philippe Sénac, *Les Carolingiens et al-Andalus: VIIIe–IXe siècles* (2002).
- Philippe Sénac, *Charlemagne et Mahomet en Espagne (VIIIe–IXe siècles)* (2015).
- William Blanc and Christophe Naudin, *Charles Martel et la bataille de Poitiers: de l’histoire au mythe identitaire* (2015). Study of the battle’s later political memory.
- Alessandro Barbero, *Charlemagne: Father of a Continent* (Italian original 2000). Useful for Charles’s dynastic legacy.
- Josef Semmler and associated scholarship on Boniface, Frankish reform, and early Carolingian church structures.

### Older biographies and historiography

- Heinrich Brunner, “Der Reiterdienst und die Anfänge des Lehnwesens” (1887). Influential thesis connecting cavalry service, Charles’s land policy, and feudalism; historically important but heavily revised by later scholarship.
- Lynn White Jr., *Medieval Technology and Social Change* (1962). Popularized the stirrup-cavalry-feudalism model; now treated cautiously.
- Edward Gibbon, *The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire*. Highly influential in constructing Tours as a world-historical civilizational turning point.
- François-René de Chateaubriand and nineteenth-century French historical writing contributed to the Christian-national image of Charles.
- Henri Pirenne, *Mohammed and Charlemagne* (posthumously published, 1937). Not a biography, but central to debates over Islam, Mediterranean disruption, and Carolingian Europe.

### Genealogy and family studies

- Christian Settipani, *Les ancêtres de Charlemagne*.
- Eduard Hlawitschka’s studies of early Carolingian genealogy.
- Karl Ferdinand Werner’s work on Frankish aristocracy and Carolingian political structures.

Genealogical reconstructions should be distinguished from directly attested parentage; some widely copied internet pedigrees convert hypotheses into certainty.

Charles has generated fewer major screen biographies than Charlemagne. Most appearances occur in general programs on the Carolingians, medieval France, or the Battle of Poitiers.

### Screen and audio subjects to consult

- French and European television documentaries titled variants of *La bataille de Poitiers*, *Charles Martel*, or *732: la bataille de Poitiers*.
- Episodes of historical documentary series on the rise of the Carolingians.
- Radio France and France Culture broadcasts discussing Charles Martel, Poitiers, and the political uses of medieval history.

## Portraiture and Material Representation

No authenticated contemporary portrait of Charles survives.

Later imagery includes:

- manuscript miniatures showing him dividing his territories;
- royal and genealogical cycles;
- nineteenth-century paintings and engravings of the Battle of Poitiers;
- statues portraying him with sword, mace, or hammer;
- modern political cartoons and internet imagery.

These images reveal the period that produced them rather than Charles’s physical appearance. His height, hair color, facial features, armor, and preferred weapon are unknown.

His tomb at Saint-Denis was incorporated into the royal necropolis’s developing memory. Later tomb sculpture and representations are not contemporary likenesses.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

## Dynastic Legacy

Charles’s most direct achievement was the survival and consolidation of his family’s supremacy.

Pepin III converted the family’s effective rule into kingship in 751. Charlemagne extended that kingship into rule over the Lombards and imperial coronation in 800. Charles’s name became dynastic: “Carolingian” derives from *Carolus*.

His burial at Saint-Denis, division of the realm among his sons, and court-sponsored historical memory all helped transform a contested mayor into the recognized ancestor of kings.

## Ecclesiastical Memory

Two contrasting memories developed:

- protector of missionaries and Christian frontiers;
- despoiler of church property, condemned in the *Visio Eucherii*.

Both derived from genuine features of his rule. He protected Christian missions and negotiated with the pope, while also placing church resources under lay and military control.

## Military Reputation

The Fredegar continuation celebrated Charles as consistently victorious, despite known setbacks:

- defeat near Cologne;
- inability to take Narbonne;
- repeated need to reconquer supposedly subdued regions;
- incomplete control of Aquitaine.

The epithet “Hammer” reduced these complexities to an image of invincible force.

## French and European National Memory

Although Charles ruled a multi-regional Frankish polity predating modern France and Germany, later national traditions claimed him.

French royal and republican histories presented him variously as:

- founder of national unity;
- defender of Gaul;
- military genius;
- precursor of Charlemagne;
- protector of Christianity.

German scholarship emphasized his Austrasian base, eastern campaigns, and the origins of the Carolingian empire.

## Modern Political Appropriation

From the nineteenth century onward, and especially in the late twentieth and twenty-first centuries, Charles became a symbol in arguments about:

- Europe and Islam;
- immigration;
- Christianity and secularism;
- French national identity;
- colonial and postcolonial memory;
- the political use of medieval history.

Far-right and identitarian groups have invoked “Charles Martel” as a slogan of resistance to Muslims. Conversely, some polemical responses have tried to reduce Tours to an insignificant raid or deny Charles’s victory any importance.

A source-driven position distinguishes three propositions:

1. A major Andalusi campaign occurred.
2. Charles defeated it and Abd al-Rahman died.
3. The claim that the battle inevitably determined the religious future of an entire continent is a later counterfactual.

The first two are historically well supported; the third is mythic and political.

## Historiographical Reassessment

Modern scholarship has shifted attention from a single battle toward:

- the civil wars that created Charles’s authority;
- regional aristocratic networks;
- the survival of Merovingian legitimacy;
- the role of women such as Plectrude and Swanachild;
- property and ecclesiastical patronage;
- the negotiated character of peripheral “submission”;
- Carolingian control over the writing of history;
- Aquitanian and Andalusi perspectives;
- the distinction between raids, conquests, tribute, and durable government.

Charles remains central not because every later legend is true, but because his household became the hinge between Merovingian and Carolingian political orders.

## Atlas Connections

Only intersections supported by the supplied atlas roster and the historical evidence are listed. Mere contemporaneity has not been treated as a connection.

### Abd al-Rahman ibn Abd Allah al-Ghafiqi

**Evidentiary label:** documented fact.

**Nature:** military enemies and opposing commanders.

**Date:** conventionally 732; possibly 733.

**Place:** between Poitiers and Tours, exact battlefield unknown.

Abd al-Rahman, governor of al-Andalus, led the army that defeated Eudo of Aquitaine and advanced toward the Loire. Charles commanded the Frankish coalition that defeated it. Abd al-Rahman died in the battle. No correspondence, negotiation, or personal encounter apart from the battlefield is documented.

### Charlemagne

**Evidentiary label:** documented fact for family relationship; reported fact for any meeting.

**Nature:** grandson and dynastic successor.

**Dates and places:** Charlemagne was probably born in 747 or 748, six or seven years after Charles’s death; therefore they never met.

Charlemagne was the son of Pepin III and grandson of Charles Martel. He inherited the dynasty and political order Charles helped establish. In 791 Charlemagne ordered the compilation of the *Codex epistolaris Carolinus*, preserving Gregory III’s letters to Charles. Charlemagne’s court also fostered historical narratives that shaped his grandfather’s reputation.

### Clovis

**Evidentiary label:** documented institutional and dynastic influence; no personal connection.

Clovis died about two centuries before Charles’s birth. Charles governed a polity whose Merovingian monarchy descended from Clovis’s dynasty. He ruled through Merovingian kings until 737 and never personally assumed their royal title. Later Carolingian legitimacy was constructed partly through appropriation of the Frankish and Christian royal traditions associated with Clovis.

### Paul the Deacon

**Evidentiary label:** reported historical influence; no documented meeting.

Paul the Deacon was born around the time of Charles’s death and could not have had a meaningful personal relationship with him. As a Lombard and Carolingian historian, Paul helped transmit the historical world of Liutprand and the early Carolingians. His works belong to Charles’s reception context rather than his personal network.

### Charlemagne-related roster figures without direct contact

The roster includes numerous persons influenced indirectly by the Carolingian imperial tradition, but no connection has been recorded solely on that basis. For Charles himself, the only direct lifetime intersection identifiable in the roster is Abd al-Rahman al-Ghafiqi. Charlemagne is a documented descendant born after Charles’s death. Clovis and Paul the Deacon are institutional or historiographical connections, not acquaintances.
