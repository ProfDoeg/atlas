# San Te / San-De (三德和尚): Research Dossier

**Subject:** San Te, San-De, Sande, 三德和尚  
**Dossier date:** 28 August 2026  
**Geographical focus:** Guangzhou and Guangdong; the legendary “Southern Shaolin” of Fujian; later Hong Kong, Taiwan, and global media  
**Historical-status finding:** No contemporaneous Qing document, monastic register, genealogy, inscription, legal record, or independently datable biography establishing San Te as an eighteenth-century person was located. The first securely datable appearance found is as a fictional monk in the anonymous late-Qing novel *Shengchao dingsheng wannianqing* (《聖朝鼎盛萬年青》, first printed in parts beginning in 1893). The civil identity “Liu Yude” (劉裕德), childhood, father, first teacher, homicide, flight, ordination, and anti-Qing career appear in the much later novel *Sande heshang san tan Xichan si* (《三德和尚三探西禪寺》), written by Chen Jin/Chen Lujin under the pen name I Am a Mountain Man and published in the late 1940s. The best-known “36 chambers” life is a still later cinematic construction, principally Lau Kar-leung’s 1978 film.

This dossier therefore reconstructs four distinct objects:

1. the **unverified historical person** allegedly active in the late Yongzheng or early Qianlong period;
2. the **1893 literary character**, the earliest securely recoverable San Te;
3. the **1947–48 literary biography** that made him Liu Yude and an anti-Qing hero; and
4. the **film, television, performance, and martial-arts culture figure** created from 1977 onward.

These strata are not treated as interchangeable evidence.

### The name “Three Virtues”

Ding Fubao’s *Great Dictionary of Buddhist Studies* (《佛學大辭典》) records several Buddhist senses of 三德. The most familiar Mahayana triad is **法身、般若、解脫**—dharma-body, prajñā/wisdom, and liberation. Other contexts use wisdom, the severing of afflictions, and beneficence. The Fo Guang Shan and Digital Database of Buddhist Terminology entries likewise translate the expression as “Three Virtues.” These lexicographical facts make the monastic name plausible as a Buddhist name; they do not authenticate the individual.

The 1947–48 novel supplies a name-bestowal verse attributed to Zhi Shan: “無邊佛法，助我功成，靈光一點，三德其名”—approximately, “The boundless Buddha-dharma aids the completion of my work; one spark of numinous light: Three Virtues shall be his name.” This is a literary etymology. It is not found in the earlier 1893 episodes examined.

## Source Criticism and Identification

### What can and cannot be reconstructed

The documentary record supports a detailed **history of a character** but not a continuous, independently verified life of an eighteenth-century monk. Three findings control the remainder of the dossier:

1. **The earliest located textual witness is late and fictional.** *Shengchao dingsheng wannianqing*, an anonymous Cantonese chivalric/court-case novel about the Qianlong emperor’s southern travels, began appearing in lithographed parts in Shanghai in 1893—roughly a century and a half after the setting assigned to San Te. It contains San Te as an established Xichan abbot but supplies no civil name, childhood, parentage, or ordination story in the relevant chapters.
2. **The full biography is a post-1945 invention or systematization.** I Am a Mountain Man’s *Sande heshang san tan Xichan si* gives the civil name Liu Yude, Huiyang origin, merchant father, first teacher, homicide, flight, three-year probation, ordination by Zhi Shan, and anti-Qing role. Its author explicitly described his project as popular writing intended to promote Chinese martial arts and erase the humiliation of the “Sick Man of East Asia.” A scholarly study of postwar Taiwanese puppetry shows that the author deliberately reversed the older novel’s political rhetoric, turning figures condemned in *Wannianqing* into anti-Qing heroes.
3. **The most internationally familiar life is cinematic.** *The 36th Chamber of Shaolin* (1978) takes the name Liu Yude/San Te and builds a new training narrative around thirty-five monastic chambers and a reformist thirty-sixth chamber for lay students. Those chambers are a film structure, not a documented Qing curriculum or a feature of the earliest San Te text.

### Earliest-publication problem

Bibliographic descriptions identify the anonymous *Shengchao dingsheng wannianqing* as a 76-chapter work. One publication history reports that Shanghai’s Wucai Company issued the first two parts in 1893, Shanghai Shuju continued parts three and four in 1896, and a complete edition appeared in 1904. It also circulated as *Wannianqing qicai xinzhuan* (《萬年青奇才新傳》), *Qianlong xunxing Jiangnan ji* (《乾隆巡幸江南記》), *Huitu Qianlong you Jiangnan* (《繪圖乾隆游江南》), and *Qianlong you Jiangnan* (《乾隆遊江南》). Edition title and pagination therefore vary.

This publication history establishes only a **terminus ante quem for the story’s circulation**, not the age of any oral tale that may have preceded it. No pre-1893 text naming San Te was verified in this research.

### Absence of contemporary corroboration

No San Te/Liu Yude entry was located in:

- an eighteenth-century Buddhist ordination or lineage register;
- a Guangzhou Xichan Temple inscription or abbatial list;
- a Fujian or Henan Shaolin register;
- a Qing memorial, criminal file, wanted notice, military report, execution record, or local gazetteer entry demonstrably contemporary with the alleged events;
- a family genealogy or merchant record linking a Liu Yude of Huiyang to a Guangzhou tea/paper shop; or
- a datable eighteenth-century martial manual.

This is an **absence in the located evidence**, not proof that no human prototype existed. The proper status is “unverified,” not “disproved.” The scale of the later claims—multiple homicides, a hundred-tael bounty, an armed rebel monastery, and a large military assault—makes the lack of a located contemporary administrative trace significant.

## Family and Ancestry

### Historical record

No parent, grandparent, sibling, spouse, partner, or child can be historically identified. No ancestry or genealogy was found.

### Late-1940s literary version

The opening chapters of *Sande heshang san tan Xichan si* state:

- Liu Yude came from **Huiyang**.
- His unnamed father conducted a **tea and paper business** in Guangzhou.
- The shop stood at **Shuangmendi**, near Hui’ai Street, in an area the narrator describes as inhabited by Banner people.
- Liu traveled with his father on business to Fujian.
- By the time of Liu’s later flight through Zhangzhou, his father had died in the preceding autumn.
- A family friend, tea grower **Lin Boping**, sheltered him for approximately three months and eventually gave him **500 taels** for his escape.

No mother, sibling, wife, lover, or child is mentioned in the opening biographical chapters examined. His Buddhist ordination makes later celibacy part of his represented vocation, but the sources do not document an actual household or descendants.

### Evaluation

These details have often migrated from the novel into martial-arts profiles without their literary origin being stated. They should be cited as **the I Am a Mountain Man version**, not as recovered family history.

## Childhood and Early Life

### Historically reconstructable childhood

None. There is no authenticated birth date, household registration, schooling record, or childhood testimony.

### The Liu Yude childhood constructed in 1947–48

The novel places Liu Yude in the final years of the Yongzheng reign. At sixteen he is already unusually strong and drawn to martial arts. His arms are said to possess “four hundred jin” of strength, earning the epithet **Iron Arm/Iron Arms (鐵臂膀)**. A Qing jin varied by time and place; the phrase functions as heroic hyperbole rather than a measurable modern weight claim.

His first martial teacher is **Lin Tiegui (林鐵拐, “Iron-Crutch Lin”)**. The novel also says Liu is illiterate. These traits—physical genius, limited formal education, volatile temper, and exposure to ethnic/political friction around Banner residents—prepare the story’s conversion arc.

The precipitating conflict occurs around the family shop. Liu confronts the Banner martial artist **Bai Feilong** and his associates. In the second chapter, Liu defeats Bai and at one point uses a shopkeeper’s body as a weapon; the shopkeeper dies. In the third chapter, Liu kills Yellow Banner deputy commander **Shang Hu** with a chair. He is consequently treated as a homicide fugitive.

This sequence is a postwar martial-arts novel’s origin story. No corresponding police or military record was found.

### The three-year probation at Fujian Shaolin

In chapter 3 of the late-1940s novel, Yuankong conducts the fugitive to Fujian Shaolin. Zhi Shan accepts him provisionally. The training is a labor-and-endurance sequence rather than the later film’s thirty-five specialized chambers:

- **Year one:** menial temple work;
- **Year two:** daily hauling described as 300 jin of firewood and fifty loads of water;
- **Year three:** repeatedly striking a tree until he can break it.

Liu then passes an admission test with Hong Xiguan and Tong Qianjin, is tonsured, and receives the monastic name San Te. The narrator gives three motives for the conversion: concealment from homicide prosecution, the softening influence of Buddhism on Liu’s temper, and Zhi Shan’s desire to build an anti-Qing base.

The sequence fuses Buddhist discipline, martial ordeal, political recruitment, and fugitive refuge. It is valuable evidence for postwar popular ideas about Shaolin formation; it is not a monastic curriculum.

### The 1978 cinematic training

*The 36th Chamber of Shaolin* discards most of this probationary scheme. Gordon Liu’s Liu Yude becomes a young anti-Qing activist whose associates and family suffer Qing violence. He enters Shaolin, works through thirty-five visually differentiated chambers, and proposes a thirty-sixth chamber to train laypeople. The film’s training technologies—water carrying with wrist-bound blades, head-strength drills, balance devices, weapons progression, and the three-section staff—became the most influential version of his formation.

The **thirty-sixth chamber is a cinematic and symbolic institution**, not an evidenced project founded by an eighteenth-century monk.

## Early Career

### Before ordination

Only the postwar novel gives a secular career context: Liu assists or accompanies his father’s tea/paper trade. It does not establish salaried employment, property ownership, a guild position, or an independent business.

### Flight and migration in the late-1940s novel

After the Guangzhou killings, Liu’s movement is:

1. Guangzhou to **Huiyang**;
2. onward to **Zhangzhou**, where Lin Boping shelters him for roughly three months;
3. renewed flight after a reward of **100 taels** is posted;
4. encounter with Yuankong; and
5. entry into the legendary **Fujian Shaolin** establishment.

The 500-tael assistance from Lin Boping and 100-tael bounty are fictional monetary details. No account book, warrant, or legal notice corroborates either sum.

### From disciple to Xichan authority

The 1893 novel introduces San Te after this supposed formation, already residing at Guangzhou’s **Xichan Temple** and functioning as its abbot. It does not narrate how he left Shaolin or received the appointment.

The postwar cycle fills that gap differently: Zhi Shan learns that the Guangzhou temple’s monks violate discipline and dispatches San Te and Tong Qianjin to rectify it. Later summaries turn Xichan into a Shaolin-aligned base and, still later, an anti-Qing headquarters.

## Chronological Life History

### I. Alleged eighteenth-century life: minimum common legend

Across later retellings, the minimum common sequence is:

- a Guangdong youth becomes a disciple of the Southern Shaolin master Zhi Shan;
- he receives the name San Te;
- he leaves or is sent from the Shaolin monastery to Guangzhou;
- he resides at or controls Xichan Temple;
- he associates with other Southern Shaolin heroes, particularly Hong Xiguan, Tong Qianjin, and Hu Huiqian;
- conflict involving Shaolin, Wudang/Emei-aligned fighters, Banner/Qing authorities, and Guangzhou guild or workshop communities escalates; and
- San Te dies violently in a siege or purge, or in adaptations survives long enough to become a teacher.

Every element in this sequence is documented as **story**, not as eighteenth-century event.

### II. The 1893 *Wannianqing* character

#### Xichan Temple and Hu Huiqian

In chapter 14, Hu Huiqian reaches Xichan Temple, where San Te, Hong Xiguan, Tong Qianjin, and others reside. San Te is designated the temple’s **住持**, abbot or resident head. Hu’s feud with weaving workers escalates into the killing of thirteen men. San Te and Hong are detained in the aftermath but released pending inquiry.

The chapter does not make San Te the instigator. On the contrary, he later urges Hu to stop, warning that continued violence will damage the monastery’s reputation. Chapter 16 repeats the pattern: San Te counsels restraint while Hu continues the feud. This is important because later short biographies sometimes flatten San Te into a simple gang leader.

#### Network mobilization

When **Lei Dapeng** arrives at Xichan and threatens San Te, the abbot sends letters summoning Hong Xiguan, Tong Qianjin, and other associates. The temple functions as a node in a martial network. The text’s political and moral alignment remains hostile to that network: it belongs to a novel celebrating Qianlong’s order and depicting the Southern Shaolin party as criminal or disorderly.

#### Imperial suppression

By chapter 61, Governor **Zeng Bizhong** orders a major force—one thousand soldiers and one thousand archers in the novel’s numbers—to surround Xichan. An imperial order sends **Gao Jinzhong** to capture San Te and Hu Huiqian. The numbers are literary, and no historical operation matching them was found.

In chapter 62, the temple is surrounded at night. San Te tells Hu that they cannot defeat massed troops. He advocates escaping toward Fujian Shaolin and consulting Zhi Shan. The advice fails to prevent battle.

#### Death in chapter 63

The earliest located death narrative is unusually explicit. Fang De and allied fighters overwhelm San Te. Gao Jinzhong kills Hu Huiqian, decapitates him, and throws the head toward San Te. San Te accidentally splits Hu’s head during the fighting. A thrown knife wounds San Te’s right hand; Fang De strikes his left shoulder. After he falls, the attackers hack his body to pieces—rendered by the text as reducing him to a “meat patty.”

The narrator and crowd approve the destruction of the “criminal” monks. This is **hostile literary framing**, not a neutral death report. The death gives no calendar date, age, burial, remains, last rites, or successor.

### III. The postwar *Three Visits to Xichan Temple* reconstruction

#### Political reversal

I Am a Mountain Man’s novel reworks the same cultural field after the Second World War. Its author’s March 1948 preface states that popular prose should promote national martial arts and wash away the shame of the “Sick Man of East Asia.” A scholarly reconstruction of the novel’s use in Taiwanese puppetry notes the author’s explanation that the Qing-period writer of *Wannianqing* could not safely praise anti-Qing Shaolin heroes under conditions imagined as literary persecution. The new novel therefore “corrects” the old moral alignment: Qing/Banner power becomes oppressive, and San Te’s violence and concealment become part of a patriotic rebel career.

This explanation is itself **postwar authorial rhetoric**, not evidence about the anonymous 1893 author’s actual motives.

#### Civil youth and homicides

As described above, Liu Yude is a Huiyang merchant’s son, martial enthusiast, and extraordinarily strong adolescent. He defeats Bai Feilong, causes a shopkeeper’s death, and kills Banner deputy commander Shang Hu. These killings are represented as occurring amid abuse by powerful Banner residents. A bounty forces his flight.

#### Conversion and ordination

Yuankong brings him to Zhi Shan. Labor, Buddhist discipline, and martial testing reshape him. He is tonsured as San Te after three years. The narrator explicitly makes the monastery a political school as well as religious refuge.

#### Guangzhou missions and Xichan

The surviving table of contents and digital text identify a long sequence of Guangdong episodes: San Te’s first descent to Guangzhou; conflict at Xichan; fights involving iron paired weapons and the “door-blocking staff”; Tong Qianjin’s battles; Wudang rivals; the “blood-dripping” weapon or assassins; Li Bashan’s challenge to San Te; Zhi Shan’s arrival in Guangzhou; Hu Huiqian’s revenge; and conflict involving Feng Daode.

A later-volume synopsis describes the Guangzhou Xichan abbot as corrupt. Zhi Shan sends San Te and Tong Qianjin to impose discipline. Wudang leader Feng Daode and Emei’s White Eyebrow become antagonists; Gao Jinzhong denounces Southern Shaolin as a rebel base; the Qianlong emperor authorizes its destruction. Zhi Shan, Hong Xiguan, and Fang Shiyu survive the burning in that summary.

This is a greatly expanded serial universe, not a single stable “life.” Editions and sequels package episodes differently.

### IV. Later lineage and popular summaries

Martial-arts and fan retellings commonly add that:

- San Te stood very tall;
- he wielded a **sixty-jin iron monk’s spade or staff**;
- he established an explicitly “Restore the Ming, oppose the Qing” base at Xichan;
- he trained or sheltered Hu Huiqian and “Rice-Pounding Six” (舂米六);
- Wudang fighters and Qing troops jointly destroyed the base; and
- he died in the final defense.

These details are traceable to the postwar novelistic tradition and its summaries. They should not be retroactively inserted into the 1893 text without citation.

### Which Xichan Temple?

The San Te narratives concern **Guangzhou’s Xichan Temple**, associated in local historical writing with the area around present-day Xihua Road, Taibao Straight Street, Xingqi Lane, and Guangzhou No. 4 Middle School. It is not the famous surviving Xichan Temple in Fuzhou. Local-history accounts describe Guangzhou Xichan as a major western-gate monastery with earlier names such as Guifeng or Lingfeng and a history extending to the late Tang/Five Dynasties and Northern Song. These local claims require direct epigraphic verification before being used as an abbatial history; no inscription naming San Te was located.

### “Main Shaolin” versus “Southern Shaolin”

The user-supplied formulation that San Te left the “main Shaolin Monastery” risks combining two traditions. The detailed postwar biography sends him to a **Fujian/Southern Shaolin** under Zhi Shan, not securely to the historically documented Henan monastery at Songshan. Later adaptations sometimes blur or deliberately merge the institutions.

Meir Shahar’s history of Henan Shaolin distinguishes documentary history from the Triad/Southern Shaolin destruction myth. Henan Shaolin was devastated in **1641**, before the Qing conquest, in warfare associated with Li Zicheng and local forces. Qing rulers later patronized and repaired it while also restricting martial activity; Yongzheng sponsored reconstruction in 1735. Shahar concludes that the familiar story in which loyal Shaolin monks served the Qing and were then burned by the Qing is spurious as a history of the Henan institution. He leaves open the separate archaeological/local-history dispute over Southern Shaolin sites and identifies the late-Qing *Wannianqing* as probably the earliest novel to celebrate the Southern Shaolin story-world.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### 1893 politics: guardian of a disorderly network

The anonymous late-Qing novel is broadly pro-Qianlong. It makes San Te part of a martial network that shelters Hu Huiqian and resists state force. San Te often urges restraint, but the narrative finally places him among the enemies whom Qing-aligned heroes and troops destroy. He is not developed as a theorist of rebellion, and no “Restore the Ming” manifesto by him appears in the examined episodes.

### 1947–48 politics: anti-Qing and nationalist rehabilitation

The postwar novel transforms Southern Shaolin into an underground anti-Qing institution. Zhi Shan’s acceptance of Liu is partly political, and Xichan becomes an operational base. Banner abuses supply the young man’s grievance; Buddhist discipline redirects, rather than extinguishes, his violence. The author’s stated contemporary purpose—popularization of national arts after war and national humiliation—links the eighteenth-century setting to Chinese postwar nationalism.

The novel’s anti-Qing content should not be treated as evidence that a historical San Te held that ideology. It documents how late-1940s Cantonese popular culture recoded an older pro-Qing story.

### Religion

San Te’s represented religion includes:

- tonsure and receipt of a Buddhist name;
- obedience to an abbot/master;
- labor as ethical and physical formation;
- concern for temple reputation and restraint in the 1893 novel; and
- conflict between monastic rules and militarized political activity.

No sermon, commentary, ordination lineage, ritual manual, meditation teaching, temple donation, doctrinal controversy, or authenticated religious office survives under his name. The fiction uses Buddhism primarily as ethical discipline, institutional belonging, and a framework for martial transformation.

### Martial philosophy and technology

The postwar text emphasizes strength, repetitive labor, tree striking, and loyalty. The 1978 film shifts toward progressive mastery: balance, vision, head strength, wrist/arm control, speed, weapons, and pedagogical access for laypeople. Modern audiences often attribute the film’s educational philosophy directly to San Te. Historically, it belongs to Lau Kar-leung, screenwriter Ni Kuang, choreographers, performers, and the Shaw Brothers production system.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

There is no modern legal case or independently verified historical prosecution. The following are **narrative accusations and acts**, arranged by source:

### 1893 novel

- San Te presides over Xichan while Hu Huiqian conducts a lethal feud with weaving workers.
- San Te and Hong Xiguan are detained after Hu kills thirteen men, then released pending inquiry.
- San Te advises Hu to stop, which weighs against depicting him as the initiator.
- He later summons armed associates when threatened.
- The government characterizes the Xichan group as criminals and orders a large armed suppression.
- San Te resists arrest violently and kills or incapacitates attackers before being killed.

**Status:** fictional acts in a pro-Qing popular novel. They are not indictments, convictions, or court findings.

### 1947–48 novel

- Liu Yude causes a shopkeeper’s death during a fight.
- He kills Banner deputy commander Shang Hu with a chair.
- He flees across provincial/local jurisdictions and hides under monastic protection.
- Zhi Shan’s monastery is represented as an anti-Qing organization.
- Xichan is represented as a base for violent resistance.

**Status:** fictional acts reframed as resistance to abuse. No primary criminal file was found.

### Corruption and institutional abuse

The postwar cycle says the Guangzhou Xichan abbot and monks violate monastic discipline, prompting Zhi Shan to send San Te and Tong Qianjin. That is a story accusation against unnamed/fictionalized clergy, not a documented ecclesiastical investigation.

### Ethnic and political rhetoric

Later versions use hostile depictions of “Banner people” and Manchu/Qing authority. The 1893 work, conversely, stigmatizes Shaolin-aligned monks and validates Qing suppression. Both are political-literary framings. Neither should substitute for evidence about actual ethnic groups, officials, monks, or events.

### Fraud, business crime, intelligence, and money laundering

No evidence was found for fraud, corruption for personal gain, money laundering, espionage/intelligence service, business wrongdoing, or a financial structure attributable to a historical San Te.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. “San Te was a documented early-eighteenth-century monk”

**Story:** San Te/Liu Yude was a real Guangdong martial artist who trained under Zhi Shan and led Xichan Temple.  
**Where and when located:** San Te is securely visible in the 1893 *Wannianqing*; the Liu Yude biography appears in the late 1940s. Modern martial-arts profiles frequently remove those source dates.  
**Circulators:** Cantonese novelists, publishers, storytellers, martial lineages, filmmakers, television producers, fan sites, and reference summaries.  
**Evidence for:** persistent, geographically specific tradition; the existence of real Guangzhou and Shaolin institutions; the character’s presence by 1893.  
**Evidence against/limits:** no contemporary Qing record or pre-1893 named source located; the first detailed life is openly fictional and postwar.  
**Reputational effect:** converts a literary network figure into a biographical ancestor of Southern Chinese martial arts.

### 2. “Liu Yude was his birth name”

**Story:** The monk was born Liu Yude in Huiyang.  
**Earliest located form:** the I Am a Mountain Man novel, late 1940s; it becomes central in *The 36th Chamber of Shaolin*.  
**Evidence:** direct in those works; absent from the examined 1893 chapters.  
**Assessment:** documented name of a later character-version; unverified civil identity of a historical monk.  
**Effect:** gives San Te a complete conversion biography and lets cinema contrast lay student Liu with monk San Te.

### 3. “Three Harmonies”

**Story:** San-De means “Three Harmonies.”  
**Circulation:** modern English martial-arts and biographical summaries.  
**Evidence:** no supporting Chinese lexicographical source located. Buddhist dictionaries consistently treat 三德 as “Three Virtues.”  
**Assessment:** unsupported or loose English gloss.  
**Effect:** detaches the name from its specifically Buddhist doctrinal vocabulary.

### 4. The Qing burning of Shaolin

**Story:** Qing rulers destroyed Shaolin because its monks were anti-Qing rebels; survivors created Southern lineages and secret societies.  
**Appearance/circulation:** Triad initiation mythology, Southern Shaolin folklore, late-Qing novels, Republican-era martial manuals and fiction, postwar Cantonese novels, opera/puppetry, and kung-fu cinema.  
**Evidence for:** abundant evidence that the story circulated and shaped organizations and arts; multiple claimed Southern Shaolin sites.  
**Evidence against as Henan history:** Henan Shaolin’s major seventeenth-century devastation occurred in 1641 before Qing rule; Qing emperors also patronized and reconstructed it. Shahar judges the familiar loyal-service-then-Qing-burning narrative spurious.  
**Unresolved portion:** whether particular Fujian monasteries contributed to later Southern Shaolin traditions is a separate archaeological and local-history question. Putian, Quanzhou, and Fuqing claims compete.  
**Effect on San Te:** supplies the political catastrophe that explains his migration, network, rebellion, or martyrdom.

### 5. Xichan as a formal anti-Qing headquarters

**Story:** San Te established a Restore-the-Ming base in Guangzhou’s Xichan Temple.  
**Earliest located evidence:** the 1893 novel makes Xichan a martial network and object of Qing suppression but does not develop San Te as an ideological spokesman in the examined chapters. The explicit anti-Qing institution belongs to the postwar rewrite.  
**Evidence:** literary only; no temple, police, or military record located.  
**Effect:** moves the story from a guild feud and public-order narrative to national revolution.

### 6. Four-hundred-jin arms and a sixty-jin iron weapon

**Story:** Sixteen-year-old Liu had four-hundred-jin arm strength; adult San Te used a sixty-jin iron monk’s spade/staff.  
**Where:** the strength epithet comes from the postwar origin story; the weapon detail is prominent in later profiles.  
**Evidence:** heroic narrative, not measurement.  
**Effect:** establishes the “Iron Arms” iconography later associated with actor Gordon Liu’s physical performance.

### 7. The thirty-six chambers

**Story:** Shaolin contained thirty-five established chambers; San Te founded a thirty-sixth to teach laypeople.  
**Appearance:** Lau Kar-leung’s 1978 film *The 36th Chamber of Shaolin*.  
**Circulators:** Shaw Brothers distribution, dubbed television and home video, international martial-arts fandom, Wu-Tang Clan, and later film culture.  
**Evidence:** fully documented as a film plot; no evidence found for such an institutional program in the eighteenth century.  
**Effect:** became the globally dominant San Te myth and a metaphor for democratizing knowledge.

### 8. Competing death stories

**1893 version:** San Te is wounded by Gao Jinzhong and Fang De, falls, and is hacked apart during the Xichan siege.  
**Postwar-cycle version:** commonly summarized as death when Qing troops and Wudang/Emei forces destroy Xichan or Southern Shaolin; editions and sequels shift the location and survivors.  
**1978 film:** the Liu Yude/San Te story ends with pedagogical and resistance success rather than the gruesome Xichan death.  
**1986 television examples:** one TVB serial blinds and kills San Te in episodes 12–13; an ATV series embeds him in a longer ensemble anti-Qing story.  
**Assessment:** no historical death version can be selected. The differences reveal each adaptation’s purpose: punitive closure, martyrdom, educational victory, or serialized tragedy.

### 9. “Master of” every Southern hero

Later charts variously call San Te the fellow disciple, senior brother, protector, or teacher of Hu Huiqian, Rice-Pounding Six, Yan Wing-chun, Fang Shiyu, and others. The 1893 text places him as senior Xichan authority and Zhi Shan disciple but does not validate every later master-disciple link. Each relationship requires source-specific citation.

## Works by the Person

### Authenticated works

**None located.** There is no authenticated book, essay, sermon, martial manual, poem, letter, diary, inscription, oral testimony, dictated memoir, artwork, patent, interview, or recording by an eighteenth-century San Te/Liu Yude.

### Works attributed to or published under his name

No premodern work was located under his name. Modern titles that contain “San Te” or 三德和尚 are works **about a character**, not works authored by him.

### Why the absence matters

Modern descriptions sometimes discuss “San Te’s system,” “his thirty-sixth chamber,” or techniques “created by San Te.” In the evidence located, these are attributions within film or lineage tradition. Authorship belongs to novelists, screenwriters, directors, choreographers, performers, and transmitting schools unless an earlier source is produced.

## Books and Major Works About the Person

### Primary literary and performance sources

1. **Anonymous, 《聖朝鼎盛萬年青》 (*Shengchao dingsheng wannianqing*)**, first issued in parts beginning 1893; later titles include 《萬年青奇才新傳》 and 《乾隆巡幸江南記》. Chapters 14–16 establish San Te at Xichan and his relationship with Hu Huiqian; chapters 61–63 narrate the siege and death. This is the earliest securely dated San Te source located.
2. **我是山人 [I Am a Mountain Man; Chen Jin/Chen Lujin], 《三德和尚三探西禪寺》 (*Sande heshang san tan Xichan si*)**, serialized in *Guangdong Shangbao* in the late 1940s and issued in book form in 1947 or 1948 depending on edition/citation. It creates/systematizes the Liu Yude biography.
3. **I Am a Mountain Man, Shaolin cycle**, including 《洪熙官大鬧峨嵋山》, 《洪熙官三建少林寺》, 《洪熙官三破白蓮觀》, and 《洪熙官血戰羅浮山》. San Te’s story is embedded in a larger Southern Shaolin genealogy. Later Taiwan editions were grouped as 《少林演義》.
4. **Postwar Taiwanese glove-puppetry repertoires**, including *Rebuilding Shaolin Temple* (《再建少林寺》, documented from 1950) and *Three Hundred Years of the Qing Palace* (《清宮三百年》, from 1951). Chen Longting shows how the postwar Cantonese Shaolin novels entered and were recombined within performance traditions. The relationship is adaptation and myth transmission, not eyewitness biography.

### Publication-date dispute for *Three Visits*

- Chen Longting’s 2017 scholarly article cites a **Hong Kong Dazhong Book Company, 1947** edition.
- A later profile quotes a **March 1948** preface.
- A collector’s examination of copyright pages argues for a **five-volume Xiongming Publishing House first edition in 1948**, with volume 1 around May, volume 2 on 15 July, and volume 5 on 18 October; the collector rejects an earlier attribution to Guangfu Printing.
- Chen Dawei’s 2023 study describes newspaper serialization from **July 1947 to June 1948** and book publication in 1948.

These may describe serialization, preface composition, and different editions rather than a single contradiction, but the currently accessible evidence does not fully reconcile them. The safe bibliographic form is **serialized 1947–48; book editions cited as 1947 and 1948**.

### Important scholarship and historical context

1. **Meir Shahar, *The Shaolin Monastery: History, Religion, and the Chinese Martial Arts* (University of Hawai‘i Press, 2008).** Essential separation of Henan Shaolin history from Southern Shaolin/Triad destruction mythology; discusses *Wannianqing* as an early literary vehicle.
2. **Dian H. Murray, in collaboration with Qin Baoqi, *The Origins of the Tiandihui: The Chinese Triads in Legend and History* (Stanford University Press, 1994).** Foundational study of Tiandihui documents and the distinction between organizational history and foundation legend.
3. **Barend J. ter Haar, *Ritual and Mythology of the Chinese Triads: Creating an Identity* (Brill, 1998; later editions/cataloging may show 2000).** Detailed study of how ritual narratives, Shaolin destruction, and collective identity interacted.
4. **John Christopher Hamm, *Paper Swordsmen: Jin Yong and the Modern Chinese Martial Arts Novel* (University of Hawai‘i Press, 2005).** Situates late-Qing/Republican martial fiction and Southern Shaolin materials in the development of modern wuxia.
5. **陳龍廷 [Chen Longting], 〈政治修辭．歷史虛擬：戰後臺灣布袋戲的少林寺「延異」〉, *Taiwan Historica* 68.3 (September 2017).** Directly traces I Am a Mountain Man’s ideological rewriting and its transmission into Taiwanese puppetry.
6. **陳大為 [Chen Dawei], 〈天下武宗：武學敘事視野下的少林譜系（622–2018）〉, *Wen yu Zhe* 42 (June 2023), pp. 317–344.** Genealogical analysis of Shaolin’s literary/media image; identifies the postwar Cantonese novelists and the importance of San Te’s narrative.
7. **劉歡萍 [Liu Huanping], 〈別樣的想象：《乾隆巡幸江南記》的「南巡」書寫探析〉, *Ming Qing Xiaoshuo Yanjiu* 2016.1, pp. 191–203.** Study of the Qianlong southern-tour representation in the novel also known as *Wannianqing*.

### Modern digital editions and audio

The full late-1940s novel circulates in segmented digital editions on Qimao and in a table-of-contents reader on WeRead. Modern audiobook versions also circulate. These editions increase access but do not establish historical authenticity; segmentation, punctuation, simplified characters, and volume titles may differ from first editions.

## Death, Later Life, and Present Status

### Historical answer

No birth date, death date, age at death, grave, memorial tablet, relic, obituary, succession record, or cause-of-death document was located. It is not possible to state where or when an actual San Te died.

### Earliest narrative answer

The 1893 novel kills him at **Guangzhou Xichan Temple** during a combined armed suppression. Immediate causes within the story are a thrown knife wound to the right hand, a blow to the left shoulder, collapse, and massed cutting blows. His body is mutilated. Hu Huiqian has just been decapitated. There is no funeral or post-siege monastic succession in the examined death chapter.

### Later answers

The postwar legend often makes the death a patriotic last stand against Qing and rival-school forces. The 1978 film declines this ending and leaves San Te as a successful teacher/resister. Television uses blindness, assassination, or ensemble war according to serial needs.

### Illness, injury, and psychological history

No historical illness, disability, addiction, psychological crisis, accident, or medical treatment is documented. Injuries are confined to fictional combat. The postwar story describes a volatile adolescent temperament moderated by Buddhist training, but this is characterization, not clinical evidence.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Late Qing: condemned martial monk

In the earliest located text, San Te is a comparatively restraining voice inside a network the novel ultimately condemns. The state’s victory and the crowd’s approval make his death punitive. His abbacy and dramatic end nevertheless give him narrative stature.

### Postwar Cantonese culture: patriotic rehabilitation

I Am a Mountain Man changes the moral polarity. Liu Yude’s violence originates in resistance to Banner abuse; Shaolin becomes a national-martial and anti-Qing institution; Xichan becomes a revolutionary base. The author’s colloquial mixture of literary Chinese, vernacular, and Cantonese (“three-in-one” prose) rooted the story in Lingnan speech and everyday geography. Newspaper serialization, inexpensive books, radio/storytelling, film, and Taiwanese puppetry multiplied its audience.

### 1970s–1980s cinema: model student and teacher

Chan Sing’s 1977 San Te is an “iron-fisted” Shaolin figure, while Gordon Liu’s 1978 performance makes bodily training, endurance, and educational reform central. The film retains anti-Qing violence but gives San Te a constructive project: opening martial knowledge beyond monastery walls. Sequels turn him from fugitive student into institutional master and comic cultural reference.

### Global culture

Dubbed television, repertory screenings, VHS/DVD releases, and later restoration carried *The 36th Chamber of Shaolin* beyond Hong Kong. RZA’s childhood encounter with the film helped feed the Wu-Tang Clan’s Shaolin vocabulary; the 1993 album title *Enter the Wu-Tang (36 Chambers)* made “chambers” a global hip-hop metaphor. RZA’s later live re-scoring of the film makes the line of influence explicit.

This legacy belongs most directly to **the cinematic San Te**, not to an evidenced eighteenth-century author or political leader.

### Martial-arts lineage reputation

San Te is sometimes presented as a link in Southern Shaolin, Hung Gar, or other Guangdong genealogies. These charts can be historically important as claims of identity and transmission even where biographical proof is missing. No single lineage chart was found that resolves his dates, teacher, disciples, monastery, and death through independent contemporary records.

### Historiographical reinterpretation

Recent scholarship has shifted attention from deciding whether every hero “really existed” to tracing how Shaolin genealogies were manufactured across secret-society ritual, late-Qing fiction, Republican martial culture, postwar Cantonese serials, puppetry, film, television, and music. San Te is a particularly clear case because the accretion can be observed:

- **1893:** named abbot and network member, no civil biography;
- **1947–48:** Liu Yude origin, family trade, flight, ordination, politics;
- **1977–78:** screen hero and chamber curriculum;
- **1980s:** master, franchise figure, and serial martyr;
- **1990s onward:** global “36 chambers” symbol.

## Unresolved Questions and Research Priorities

1. **Pre-1893 witnesses:** Does a datable manuscript, opera script, storyteller’s promptbook, lineage text, or local gazetteer name 三德和尚 before the first lithographed *Wannianqing* parts?
2. **Early edition collation:** Do the 1893, 1896, and 1904 editions differ in chapters 14–16 or 61–63? A page-by-page collation could show whether San Te’s role expanded.
3. **Postwar serialization:** A complete run of *Guangdong Shangbao* from July 1947 to June 1948 could settle dates, installment boundaries, authorial revisions, advertisements, and reader reception.
4. **First book edition:** Examination of all copyright pages could reconcile Dazhong, Guangfu, Xiongming, Shengli, and later reprint claims.
5. **Guangzhou Xichan archives:** Surviving stele rubbings, local gazetteers, property records, temple lists, and municipal archaeology should be checked for an eighteenth-century abbot named 三德 or a violent clearance.
6. **Huiyang and Guangzhou merchant records:** Liu genealogies and tea/paper guild records might test the Liu Yude family claim, though the name may be fictional.
7. **Qing legal archives:** A coordinated search of Grand Council memorials, palace memorials, Guangdong judicial files, Banner records, and local yamen material for Liu Yude, San De, Xichan, Bai Feilong, Shang Hu, Hu Huiqian, and Gao Jinzhong could produce positive evidence or strengthen the negative finding.
8. **Southern Shaolin site claims:** Archaeological and epigraphic evidence for Putian, Quanzhou, and Fuqing should be evaluated separately from novelistic genealogy.
9. **Oral transmission:** Interviews with lineages that claim San Te should record the earliest dated chart, manual, altar tablet, or teacher testimony rather than beginning with modern websites.
10. **Screen credits:** Surviving studio call sheets or full Chinese credit frames could resolve minor San Te roles in 1980s films and the TVB actor identification.
