## Works by Mansa Musa

### Journey as sovereign manifestation

- **1324–1325:** *The pilgrimage to Mecca*. A deliberately organized royal journey comprising a large caravan, diplomatic encounters, almsgiving, trade, and public displays of Malian sovereignty; it resists classification as an obra because it survives only as an event reconstructed from reports collected by al-ʿUmarī and later historians, not as a discrete authored object. ([Levtzion](https://doi.org/10.1017/S002185370000428X))

### Royal architectural commissions

- **After 1325:** Untitled domed audience hall at the imperial capital. This square, plastered and ornamented reception structure is the commission most securely connected to Musa: Ibn Khaldūn preserves an eyewitness-derived report that Abū Isḥāq al-Sāḥilī executed Musa’s project. Its traditional location at Niani is plausible but not certain, because the medieval account says only “the capital of Mali.” ([Aradeon](https://doi.org/10.3406/jafr.1989.2279))

- **Beginning c. 1325:** *Djingareyber Mosque* (Great Mosque of Timbuktu). A congregational mosque commissioned under Musa’s patronage after his pilgrimage; the surviving building is a continuously repaired and substantially altered communal structure, including rebuilding and enlargement in 1570–1583, rather than an untouched fourteenth-century object. Later tradition assigns its design to al-Sāḥilī, but critical scholarship finds no contemporary evidence for that attribution and rejects claims that he introduced earthen architecture to West Africa. ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/119/); [Aradeon](https://doi.org/10.3406/jafr.1989.2279))

- **1325 or 1326:** Untitled mosque outside Gao, conventionally called the Kankou Moussa Mosque. The seventeenth-century *Taʾrīkh al-Sūdān* says Musa built a mosque with a mihrab outside Gao and prayed Friday prayer there; excavators have proposed particular ruins as its remains, but the archaeological identification is not conclusive, and no early source says that al-Sāḥilī designed it. ([Takezawa and Cissé](https://doi.org/10.4000/etudesafricaines.17030); [Aradeon](https://doi.org/10.3406/jafr.1989.2279))

- **1324–1326:** Untitled Friday mosques erected along Musa’s route. The *Taʾrīkh al-Sūdān* reports a repeated royal protocol of commissioning a mosque wherever Friday overtook the travelling court; because the chronicle supplies neither a count nor dependable locations, this is a documented building project rather than a list of recoverable individual monuments. ([Aradeon](https://doi.org/10.3406/jafr.1989.2279))

### Buildings preserved only as disputed attributions

- **After 1325:** *Madugu*, a royal residence traditionally placed at Timbuktu. Leo Africanus and later tradition associate a palace there with Musa and al-Sāḥilī, but the stronger Ibn Khaldūn account locates only an audience structure at Mali’s unnamed capital; consequently, the Timbuktu palace may be a displaced or expanded version of that report rather than a separate securely attested obra. ([Aradeon](https://doi.org/10.3406/jafr.1989.2279))

- **During Musa’s reign, exact date unknown:** Mosque at Goundam. Later historical summaries credit Musa with this mosque, but no contemporary testimony, secure construction date, or identifiable original fabric establishes the attribution; it should be retained as tradition rather than treated as a catalogued commission. ([UNESCO, *The Different Aspects of Islamic Culture*, vol. 6, pt. 1](https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark:/48223/pf0000244974))

- **During Musa’s reign, exact date unknown:** Mosque at Diré. Like the Goundam mosque, this is a retrospective attribution without a secure contemporary record or demonstrable fourteenth-century building phase, and therefore resists treatment as a confirmed work. ([UNESCO, *The Different Aspects of Islamic Culture*, vol. 6, pt. 1](https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark:/48223/pf0000244974))
