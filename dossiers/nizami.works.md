## Works by Nizami Ganjavi

Nizami's reputation rests almost entirely on five long narrative poems in Persian, later grouped under the collective title *Khamsa* ("Quintet") or *Panj Ganj* ("Five Treasures") — a title consolidated by later manuscript tradition rather than one Nizami himself used for the set. Each poem was written for, and dedicated to, a different regional ruler, and each is formally independent; only in retrospect, and partly through the imitation cycle it inspired, did they come to be read and copied as a unified cycle. A separate lyric collection, now lost as an intact book, is treated afterward.

### Makhzan al-Asrar — "The Treasury of Mysteries"

The earliest of the five, probably composed sometime in the 1160s to 1170s (the exact date is disputed; older scholarship favored the mid-1170s, while the modern authority François de Blois argues for the mid-1160s), dedicated to Fakhr al-Din Bahramshah, the Mengüjekid ruler of Erzincan in eastern Anatolia. Unlike the four romances that follow, it is not a story-poem but a didactic and mystical work of roughly 2,250 to 2,300 couplets, modeled on the earlier Persian poet Sanai's *Garden of Truth*. It consists of twenty discourses on ethical and spiritual themes — justice, the vanity of worldly ambition, the oppression of the weak by the powerful, the discipline of speech, the nearness of death — each illustrated with a short exemplary tale. It functions as a manual of right rule and right living addressed as much to princes as to the pious, and its dedication shows that Nizami's reputation and patronage network extended well beyond Ganja even in his early career.

### Khosrow o Shirin — "Khosrow and Shirin"

A historical romance, begun around 1175–76 and apparently revised across more than a decade, down to about 1191, as patrons changed. It retells the story — already known from the Persian national epic tradition associated with Ferdowsi — of the Sasanian king Khosrow II's long, difficult courtship of the Armenian princess Shirin, with the devoted stonemason and engineer Farhad as his unsuccessful rival for her affection. Nizami's version is distinguished by the psychological depth and agency he gives Shirin, who is not a passive prize but a ruler and negotiator in her own right who insists on legitimate marriage rather than simply submitting to a king's desire. The poem is also the site of Nizami's most personal autobiographical intrusion: a passage added around 1180–81 mourns the death of his first wife and compares her to Shirin, while addressing his young son by name. Composition and revision together make this one of the textually most layered of the five works, with manuscripts preserving different patronage dedications from different stages of its life.

### Leyli o Majnun — "Layla and Majnun"

Completed quickly, in about four months, and dated within the poem itself to the autumn of 1188, on commission from the Shirvanshah ruler Akhsatan I, who had asked for a Persian treatment of material until then transmitted mainly in scattered Arabic anecdote and poetry. Nizami says he was initially reluctant, feeling the austere desert setting of the old Arabian story offered little scope for the courtly elaboration his style depended on, and that his teenage son urged him to accept the commission anyway. The result, roughly 4,600 couplets, reorganizes the fragmentary Arabic tradition about the poet Qays — nicknamed Majnun, "the possessed" or "the madman," for his obsessive devotion — into a continuous tragic narrative: forbidden by tribal convention from marrying the woman he loves, he is driven to wandering madness in the wilderness, and the two lovers are reunited only briefly, near death. The poem became the most influential Persian telling of the story and generated centuries of further retellings and adaptations across Persian, Ottoman Turkish, and other literatures. It is also where Nizami commemorates his own mother and the maternal uncle who apparently helped raise him, in passages whose exact wording is itself subject to manuscript dispute.

### Haft Peykar — "The Seven Beauties" (also known as Haft Gonbad, "Seven Domes," or Bahram-nama)

Dated within the text itself to the summer of 1197 and dedicated to Ala al-Din Körpe Arslan, a local ruler of Maragha. Its central figure is the historical Sasanian king Bahram V, known in Persian tradition as Bahram Gur for his skill at hunting onager (wild ass). The poem builds a frame narrative: Bahram commissions seven domed pavilions, each associated with a different color, planet, day of the week, and climate, and in each one he is entertained by a princess who tells him a story. The nested tales range widely in tone and subject, but together they form an education: Bahram eventually confronts the damage a corrupt minister has done to his kingdom in his absence and reforms his rule. The poem's elaborate color-and-cosmos symbolism, and its structure of stories within a story, made it an especially rich subject for manuscript illustration in later centuries, and distinct rulers across Persian, Central Asian, and Mughal courts had themselves depicted in its central role.

### Eskandar-nama — "The Book of Alexander"

The last and longest of the five, probably composed mostly in the 1190s and into the early 1200s, though its precise chronology relative to *Haft Peykar* is disputed because manuscripts disagree on the order. It is really two linked books rather than one, meaning the *Khamsa* can be counted as either five works or, by a stricter physical count, six:

- **Sharaf-nama** ("The Book of Glory"), covering Alexander the Great's conquests, his dealings with the many peoples and rulers he encounters, and his growth as a king who seeks knowledge as well as territory.
- **Iqbal-nama**, also called **Khird-nama-ye Eskandari** ("The Book of Fortune" / "Alexander's Book of Wisdom"), which follows Alexander in a more philosophical register: consulting sages, probing the limits of the known world, and finally confronting his own mortality. In this half, Alexander is reshaped from conqueror into something closer to a seeker after wisdom, and in places a quasi-prophetic figure, in a way that departs significantly from the historical Alexander and reflects the figure's long absorption into Islamic legendary tradition rather than any Greek source Nizami could have read directly.

Together the two parts run to roughly ten to ten and a half thousand couplets, and their patronage dedications are unusually hard to pin down, since different manuscripts name different rulers — a sign that sections may have been rededicated as patrons died or fortunes changed.

### The lost Divan

Nizami states that he had assembled a divan, a collected volume of shorter lyric poems (qasidas, ghazals, and other short forms), by 1188. That original collection does not survive as such. Later anthologies and manuscript collections preserve a body of lyric verse attributed to him, but the corpus is acknowledged to be contaminated by confusion with other poets who also used "Nizami" as a pen name, and individual poems require case-by-case manuscript scrutiny before they can be accepted as genuinely his. Modern "complete" editions of his divan are therefore scholarly reconstructions rather than a recovered original book.

### Textual history and authenticity more broadly

No autograph manuscript of any work by Nizami survives, and the gap between his death (conventionally 1209) and the earliest surviving copies is substantial — the earliest known manuscript witness to *Leyli o Majnun*, for instance, is dated 1318, more than a century later. This gap matters: autobiographical passages (about his wives, his mother's possible Kurdish origin, his birthplace) appear inconsistently across manuscripts, and modern critical scholarship, especially François de Blois's manuscript-based work, has had to reconstruct dates, dedications, and even the authenticity of specific passages rather than take any single printed edition at face value. Verses in Turkic occasionally attributed to Nizami in later tradition lack secure manuscript support and are generally regarded as confusions with other poets of the same name. The *Khamsa* nonetheless became, alongside Ferdowsi's *Shahnameh*, one of the two great vehicles of later Persianate manuscript painting, with lavishly illustrated copies produced for Jalayirid Baghdad (1386–88), the Timurid courts, the Mughal emperor Akbar in the 1590s, and Akbar's descendants into the seventeenth century, and it generated a long chain of formal imitations by poets across Persian, Ottoman Turkish, Chagatai, and South Asian literary traditions, beginning with Amir Khusrow Dehlavi's own five-poem response in Delhi roughly a century after Nizami's death.
