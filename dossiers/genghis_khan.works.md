## Works by Genghis Khan

### Spoken and dictated texts

- **c. 1203:** The speeches conventionally reconstructed as *The Indictment of Ong Khan*. This manifesto-like denunciation survives only as passages dispersed among later chronicles; Christopher Atwood’s reconstruction suggests that the speeches may go back to Genghis Khan, while the linking narrative may be the work of a Kereyid author. ([Atwood’s research summarized here](https://www.medievalists.net/2022/11/WRITING-MONGOL-EMPIRE/))

- **c. 1206–1227:** The *bilig*, or precepts attributed to Genghis Khan. These are an open corpus of aphorisms, verse speeches, political counsel, and practical wisdom preserved in chronicles and later manuscript collections—not a book he wrote; Igor de Rachewiltz judges much of the content potentially thirteenth-century but identifies interpolations and later additions. ([de Rachewiltz, “On a Recently Discovered MS. of Činggis-Qaṭan’s Precepts”](https://eah.anu.edu.au/sites/default/files/article-content/42/pdfs/EAH42-Whole-issue.pdf))

- **1222–1223:** Conversations with Qiu Chuji concerning government, warfare, health, and Daoist teaching. They were oral exchanges mediated by interpreters and recorded by other people; their later manifestation as *Xuanfeng qinghui lu* (*Record of Auspicious Meetings with the Mysterious School*, dated 1232) is therefore not a book authored by Genghis Khan. ([*Encyclopedia of Taoism*](https://forum.freemdict.com/uploads/short-url/6lPVeyaC6Fobyu0r2lNDzv6bCW8.pdf))

### Letters, decrees, and documentary acts

- **1218:** An untitled message proposing trade and peaceful relations with the Khwarazmshah. Its words are known through later Persian narrative sources rather than an extant original document, so it is best classified as a diplomatic act, probably formulated through envoys and secretaries, rather than a personally written letter. ([Morgan, “Čengīz Khan”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/cengiz-khan/); [Morgan, “Mongols”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/mongols-2/))

- **1219:** The invitation or summons to the Daoist master Qiu Chuji. A Chinese text traditionally presented as Genghis Khan’s letter survives in the textual tradition surrounding Qiu’s journey, but its elaborate literary form and transmission have generated doubts about its authenticity and drafting; at most it is a chancery document issued in his name. ([Acta Mongolica study](https://journal.num.edu.mn/actamongolica/article/download/5839/4676/10502); [discussion of the authenticity dispute](https://en.sonin.org/index.php/archives/30/))

- **1220:** The follow-up rescript directing Qiu Chuji to continue westward for an audience. Like the initial summons, it survives through Li Zhichang’s account rather than as Genghis Khan’s autograph; it is a dictated or authorized chancery text. ([Li Zhichang, *Travels of Ch’ang Ch’un to the West*](https://depts.washington.edu/silkroad/texts/changchun.html))

- **1223:** The protection and tax-exemption decree for Qiu Chuji and the Quanzhen Daoists. This is a performative protocol granting privileges rather than an expository composition, and its surviving wording belongs to a mediated Chinese documentary tradition. ([Li Zhichang, *Travels of Ch’ang Ch’un to the West*](https://depts.washington.edu/silkroad/texts/changchun.html); [Chinese Text Project overview](https://ctext.org/datawiki.pl?if=gb&res=6365815))

### Law and administrative protocol

- **1206–1227:** The individual *jasaq/yāsā* decrees of Genghis Khan. They formed a developing body of orders concerning rule, military discipline, hunting, diplomacy, and other matters; scholarship no longer securely accepts the traditional image of a single, systematic book called the *Great Yāsā*, and the original corpus is largely lost. ([Jackson, “Yāsā,” *Encyclopaedia Iranica*](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/yasa-law-code/); [Morgan, “The ‘Great Yāsā of Chingiz Khān’”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bulletin-of-the-school-of-oriental-and-african-studies/article/abs/great-yasa-of-chingiz-khan-and-mongol-law-in-the-ilkhanate/C8F325C310969434E9B09D8BF600A633))

- **1206 onward:** The judicial register entrusted to Šigi Qutuqu, sometimes called the “Blue Register” from the *Secret History*’s description of its writing. This was a continuing official record of allocations and judicial decisions made in consultation with Genghis Khan, not necessarily a law code or a work physically composed by him; no original survives. ([de Rachewiltz, *The Secret History of the Mongols*, §203](https://altaica.ru/LIBRARY/rachewiltz/Rachewiltz_The%20Secret%20History%20of%20the%20Mongols_I-II%202004.pdf); [Jackson, “Yāsā”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/yasa-law-code/))

### Political and institutional obra

- **1206:** The *Great Mongol State*. This political obra joined previously independent and defeated peoples under Genghis Khan’s imperial authority; it was neither a solitary object nor solely his personal production, but a state made through assemblies, alliances, conquest, incorporation, and collective administration. ([Morgan, “Čengīz Khan”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/cengiz-khan/); [Biran, Kim et al., *The Cambridge History of the Mongol Empire*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-history-of-the-mongol-empire/339475953C6AECE567FA50F1AED951A7))

- **1206:** The reorganized Mongol imperial army. Genghis Khan’s most consequential institutional creation was a cavalry army arranged in decimal units and deliberately incorporating members of defeated groups; because steppe decimal organization predated him and implementation depended on commanders and soldiers, it resists attribution as an individual invention. ([Morgan, “Čengīz Khan”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/cengiz-khan/?generate_pdf=1); [de Rachewiltz, *The Secret History of the Mongols*, §§202–225](https://altaica.ru/LIBRARY/rachewiltz/Rachewiltz_The%20Secret%20History%20of%20the%20Mongols_I-II%202004.pdf))

- **1206:** The expanded *kešig*, or imperial guard and household establishment. Regulations attributed to Genghis Khan transformed the guard into a military, domestic, and administrative institution; it is an institutional protocol rather than a conventional work. ([de Rachewiltz, *The Secret History of the Mongols*, §§224–234](https://altaica.ru/LIBRARY/rachewiltz/Rachewiltz_The%20Secret%20History%20of%20the%20Mongols_I-II%202004.pdf); [*Encyclopedia of Mongolia*, “Secret History of the Mongols”](https://turkistanilibrary.com/sites/default/files/encyclopedia_of_mongolia_and_the_mongol.pdf))

- **c. 1204–1206:** The Mongolian imperial scribal and chancery system using the Uighur-derived script. Genghis Khan adopted personnel and practices from conquered literate societies rather than personally designing an alphabet; its classification as his “creation” therefore overstates an act of institutional appropriation and adaptation. ([Morgan, “Čengīz Khan”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/cengiz-khan/); [study of Tata-tonga’s administrative role](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/pub/uygur/article/1281924))

- **1206–1227:** The nucleus of the imperial administration. Genghis Khan recruited Uighur, Khitan, and other specialists and assembled institutions from several political traditions; this was a collaborative, evolving administrative project, not a finished constitutional design. ([Morgan, “Čengīz Khan”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/cengiz-khan/))

- **1206–1227:** The early imperial courier arrangements. Sources ascribe to Genghis Khan orders for stocked relay stations, but other scholarship distinguishes his period’s ad hoc requisitioning from the regular *yam* system established under Ögedei in 1234; it is consequently an incomplete precursor rather than the finished postal network sometimes credited to him. ([“Čāpār,” *Encyclopaedia Iranica*](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/capar-or-capar-turk/); [Morgan, “Elčī”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/elci/))

### Architectural project and court monument

- **1220:** *Karakorum*. Historical tradition credits Genghis Khan with selecting or founding the imperial center, but substantial urban construction and its establishment as a built capital belong to Ögedei’s reign from 1235; for Genghis Khan it is best classified as a site and project, not the completed city. ([Franken, “Imported Landscapes”](https://publications.dainst.org/books/dai/catalog/view/2094/3027/5775); [Bemmann et al., “Mapping Karakorum”](https://doi.org/10.15184/aqy.2021.153))

- **1224–1225:** *The Stone of Chinggis*, or Yisüngge Stele. This five-line court inscription commemorates Genghis Khan’s nephew Yisüngge winning an archery contest held during the ruler’s return from the Khwarazm campaign; it belongs to his courtly world, but neither authorship nor a direct commission by Genghis Khan is established, so it resists inclusion as his personal work. ([de Rachewiltz, “Sino-Mongolica Remota”](https://eah.anu.edu.au/42/rachewiltz-sino-mongolica-remota/index.html); [survey of early Mongolian written monuments](https://orient.sav.sk/wp-content/uploads/aas/1974.pdf))

### Works wrongly or insecurely assigned to him

- **No secure date:** *The Great Yāsā* as a single written code. Individual enactments attributable to Genghis Khan existed, but David Morgan’s analysis found no adequate evidence that he made the unified written code conventionally bearing this title; later writers converted a fluid body of rulings into the image of a book. ([Morgan, “The ‘Great Yāsā of Chingiz Khān’”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bulletin-of-the-school-of-oriental-and-african-studies/article/abs/great-yasa-of-chinghis-khan-and-mongol-law-in-the-ilkhanate/C8F325C310969434E9B09D8BF600A633); [Jackson, “Yāsā”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/yasa-law-code/))

- **1228 or later:** *The Secret History of the Mongols*. It preserves speeches, judgments, and institutional acts attributed to Genghis Khan, but the work itself is anonymous and was compiled after his death, with its date, stages, and authorship disputed; it is therefore a principal source for his creations, not one of them. ([de Rachewiltz, standard annotated edition](https://altaica.ru/LIBRARY/rachewiltz/Rachewiltz_The%20Secret%20History%20of%20the%20Mongols_I-II%202004.pdf); [Morgan, “Čengīz Khan”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/cengiz-khan/))
