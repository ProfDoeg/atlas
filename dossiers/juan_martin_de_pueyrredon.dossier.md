# Juan Martín de Pueyrredón y O'Doggan: Research Dossier

## Scope, method, and evidentiary conventions

This dossier reconstructs the life of Juan Martín de Pueyrredón from archival catalogues, published correspondence, government and museum sources, academic studies, contemporary documents, historical monographs, and later biographical traditions.

Evidence is labeled as follows:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary records, correspondence, official acts, or archival descriptions.
- **Reported fact:** stated in reputable secondary literature but not independently confirmed here from an original record.
- **Disputed:** materially conflicting versions exist.
- **Allegation:** an accusation made by a contemporary or later writer without a conclusive adjudication.
- **Inference:** a conclusion drawn from documented circumstances, identified as such.
- **Rumor or myth:** a story important to reputation but inadequately documented.

Names and dates retain period uncertainty. In particular, sources disagree over whether Pueyrredón was born in 1776 or 1777, whether he died on 12 or 13 March 1850, how many children belonged to him, and whether every person conventionally listed as a sibling was a surviving child of the same marriage.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal ancestry

Pueyrredón’s father was **Jean or Juan Martín de Pueyrredón y de la Boucherie/Labrucherie**, born in 1738 at Issor in Béarn, now Pyrénées-Atlantiques, France. His parents are given as Pierre de Pueyrredón and Marie de la Broucherie or Labrucherie.

The elder Pueyrredón went first to Spain, then settled in Buenos Aires in the 1760s. He became a substantial wholesale merchant, livestock owner, and exporter of regional products. A biographical study based on family and ecclesiastical records states that he married in the Church of La Merced on 22 June 1766. He sought and eventually received Spanish naturalization after demonstrating long residence, service in royal militia, marriage to a Buenos Aires-born woman, and ownership of valuable property.

Claims that the family belonged to an old noble or seigneurial line in southwestern France occur frequently in family histories. The connection to the **Fayolle de Puyrredon** marquisate is a genealogical claim rather than a demonstrated title personally possessed by Juan Martín or his father.

### Maternal ancestry

His mother was **María Rita Damasia O’Doggan y Martínez de Soria**, usually dated 1747–1808, born in Buenos Aires.

Two competing traditions exist:

1. **Irish-descendant version:** Rita descended through the O’Doggan/Dogan line from Irish immigrant Dionisio O’Doggan or Dogan. This is the dominant family and commemorative account.
2. **Galician or Iberian version:** some genealogical writers dispute or qualify the Irish origin and identify an Iberian military ancestor.

The surviving online evidence does not definitively resolve the remote ancestry. Rita herself was indisputably a member of the Buenos Aires colonial elite.

### Parents’ economic position

The family’s mercantile network connected Buenos Aires with Cádiz and other Atlantic ports. The father’s death in 1791 forced the younger Juan Martín to abandon or curtail formal education and enter the family business. This commercial background shaped his later logistical competence, access to credit, European contacts, and pragmatic political outlook.

### Siblings

Sources variously call him the fifth of eight sons, the sixth of eleven children, or one of eight surviving children. Named siblings include:

- **Feliciano José Pueyrredón** (1767–1826), priest and military chaplain.
- **Diego José Pueyrredón** (1769–1812), soldier, Jujuy official, and briefly governor-intendant of Córdoba.
- **María Magdalena Pueyrredón**, who married Juan Bautista Ituarte.
- **Juana María Pueyrredón O’Doggan** (1775–1812), later remembered as one of the “Patricias Argentinas”; married merchant and regidor Anselmo Sáenz Valiente.
- **José Cipriano Andrés de Pueyrredón** (1779–1827), soldier and political associate of Juan Martín.
- **Juan Andrés Pueyrredón** (c.1786–1842), soldier and provincial official.
- **Isabel Pueyrredón**, who married into the Albarellos family.

Differences in totals probably reflect infant deaths, incomplete family lists, and the tendency of later biographies to count only sons or surviving children.

### Marriages and children

#### María Dolores Pueyrredón

**Reported fact:** In Cádiz around 1802–1803, Pueyrredón married **María Dolores Pueyrredón**, apparently a relative or cousin. A letter dated 26 December 1802 refers warmly to the woman with whom he had lived for six years, although the precise legal status and marriage date require examination of the published *Correspondencia: 1802–1806*.

They sailed to Buenos Aires aboard the frigate *Humildad* in early 1804. Dolores reportedly miscarried during the 58-day passage and never fully recovered. She died in Buenos Aires on 27 May 1805 and was buried at the Recoleta church.

Some summaries call María Calixta his second wife, while others call her his third. The discrepancy arises partly from uncertainty about an additional union and partly from whether nonmarital relationships are counted.

#### Proposed marriage to María Ventura Marcó del Pont

**Documented proposal, uncompleted marriage:** On 18 September 1806, before leaving for Europe as the Cabildo’s representative, Pueyrredón reportedly granted power to his brother-in-law Ruperto Albarellos to contract a marriage with **María Ventura Marcó del Pont**, then about fifteen. The marriage did not occur. She later married Manuel Muñoz Casabal in 1813.

#### María Calixta Tellechea y Caviedes

Pueyrredón married **María Calixta Tellechea y Caviedes** on 27 May 1815. She was approximately fourteen or fifteen; he was thirty-seven or thirty-eight. Her father, **Francisco de Tellechea**, had been executed in 1812 in the repression of the alleged Álzaga conspiracy by the government of which Pueyrredón was a member.

That circumstance became a persistent and documented biographical irony. The records presently consulted do not establish that Pueyrredón personally ordered Tellechea’s execution; Bernardino Rivadavia and Feliciano Chiclana are generally identified as the principal advocates of the repression, while Pueyrredón reportedly doubted the case and tried to halt further killings.

Calixta inherited an interest in the **Chacra del Bosque Alegre** at San Isidro, later known as the Quinta Pueyrredón. Their son was:

- **Prilidiano Paz Pueyrredón** (24 January 1823–3 November 1870), engineer, architect, portraitist, and painter. He left no legally recognized descendants.

#### Other children

- **Virginia Pueyrredón Sánchez** (usually 1813–1871), daughter of **Juana Sánchez**, born during or near Pueyrredón’s San Luis confinement. She later married Colonel José María Pelliza. **Reported fact**, supported principally by genealogical literature.
- **María de los Ángeles Pueyrredón** (1798–1863), who married Antonio Antonini Tagliafico at La Merced in 1815. Some genealogists identify her as Pueyrredón’s extramarital daughter, raised by one of his sisters. **Disputed attribution:** the marriage record reportedly shows Pueyrredón as witness but is not, by itself, proof of paternity.

There is no reliable evidence that Pueyrredón formally acknowledged every child later attributed to him.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1777–1795

Pueyrredón grew up in a wealthy mercantile household in late-colonial Buenos Aires. Family commerce connected cattle, hides, agricultural goods, imported merchandise, Cádiz credit, and the wider Bourbon Atlantic.

Sources say he began studies locally, sometimes described as attendance at the “Royal College” or equivalent colonial instruction. No complete school register or curriculum was located. His education was interrupted after his father’s death in 1791.

Later descriptions portray him as cultivated, multilingual or at least comfortable in France and Spain, socially polished, and financially competent. These traits are consistent with his family situation and travels, but retrospective character sketches should not be treated as objective psychological records.

Vicente Fidel López described him as tall, erect, grave, self-controlled, observant, occasionally imperious, and unfailingly courteous across social ranks. This is a later personal-historical portrait, not a contemporaneous medical or physical record.

---

## Commercial Formation and European Years, 1795–1805

In 1795 Pueyrredón was sent to Cádiz to manage the family’s commercial affairs. He traveled repeatedly through Spain and France and spent much of the following decade engaged in Atlantic trade.

The surviving **1802–1806 correspondence**, published by the Argentine National Academy of History, is the strongest source for this period. It shows a practical merchant managing shipments, credit, kinship obligations, and family business. A scholarly review emphasizes that the letters reveal the commercial pragmatism often missing from patriotic biographies.

His first marriage or domestic union with María Dolores belongs to these years. The pair returned to Buenos Aires in April 1804. Dolores died in May 1805. By the time of the British invasion in 1806, Pueyrredón had returned permanently or semi-permanently to the Río de la Plata with significant mercantile experience and private means.

Claims that he returned with a “considerable fortune” are plausible but imprecise. No consolidated balance sheet has been located.

## The British Invasions and Rise to Public Prominence, 1806–1807

### British occupation of Buenos Aires

In June 1806 British forces under **William Carr Beresford**, supported by Commodore **Home Riggs Popham**, captured Buenos Aires.

Pueyrredón left the occupied city and helped assemble mounted volunteers from the countryside. He coordinated with Martín Rodríguez, Cornelio Zelaya, and other local leaders.

### Combat of Perdriel

On 1 August 1806 his irregular cavalry confronted British troops at **Perdriel**. The poorly armed militia was defeated by disciplined infantry and artillery. Militarily, Perdriel was a failure; politically, it enhanced Pueyrredón’s reputation as an early organizer of resistance.

He then joined the reconquest forces led by **Santiago de Liniers**, which retook Buenos Aires on 12 August 1806.

### Húsares de Pueyrredón

Pueyrredón helped organize the mounted corps commonly called the **Húsares de Pueyrredón**. He became one of the best-known locally born commanders produced by the crisis.

Archival holdings include:

- certificates of his role in the Reconquest;
- correspondence with the Cabildo;
- communications with Liniers;
- documents on cavalry organization;
- reports on the loss, reconquest, and defense of Buenos Aires;
- correspondence with his mother, siblings, Anselmo Sáenz Valiente, Cornelio Saavedra, Domingo French, and Domingo Matheu.

### Mission to Spain

The Buenos Aires Cabildo selected him to present the city’s case at the Spanish court, seek recognition and rewards, and explain the conduct of local forces. He departed in November 1806, traveling overland through Portuguese Brazil because British naval power made a direct departure hazardous.

His mission placed him in contact with metropolitan political circles during the collapse of Bourbon authority caused by Napoleon’s invasion of Spain.

---

## Spain, France, and the Independence Suspicion, 1807–1810

Pueyrredón was in Europe during the crisis of 1808: the abdications of Charles IV and Ferdinand VII, French occupation, the rise of Spanish juntas, and a widening debate over American sovereignty.

He returned toward the Río de la Plata in 1809. At Montevideo, Governor **Francisco Javier de Elío** detained him or attempted to send him to Spain on suspicion of separatism. He escaped and reached Buenos Aires.

When Viceroy **Baltasar Hidalgo de Cisneros** arrived, Pueyrredón joined those who reportedly urged Liniers not to surrender authority. Cisneros regarded him as a leader of the independence party and ordered his arrest on 24 July 1809.

### Escape from custody

**Documented core, embellished details possible:** Juana Pueyrredón and José Cipriano, assisted by Cornelio Saavedra and sympathetic guards or officials, intervened on his behalf. Pueyrredón escaped from an upper room on the night of 26 July, traditionally through a window, hid at Francisco Mariano de Orma’s quinta, and fled to Rio de Janeiro.

The window escape became part of family and patriotic folklore. The detention and flight are well supported; later dramatic dialogue and minute details are harder to verify.

He remained in Brazil until the May Revolution allowed his return to Buenos Aires in June 1810.

## Revolutionary Government and the Interior, 1810–1812

### Governor-intendant of Córdoba

The new Buenos Aires junta appointed Pueyrredón governor-intendant of **Córdoba del Tucumán** on 3 August 1810; other sources give 16 August as the date he effectively assumed office.

The appointment followed the defeat of the counterrevolution led by Santiago de Liniers, Juan Gutiérrez de la Concha, Santiago Allende, Victorino Rodríguez, and Joaquín Moreno. Most were executed at Cabeza de Tigre on orders from the Junta before Pueyrredón consolidated his administration.

He sought to pacify Córdoba, reportedly using amnesty and administrative conciliation after the executions. He supported the northbound revolutionary army and organized or supervised the local political process that selected Dean **Gregorio Funes** as Córdoba’s representative.

### Charcas/Chuquisaca

Late in 1810 or in January 1811, Pueyrredón became governor-intendant or president of the Audiencia at **Chuquisaca/Charcas**. Titles vary because revolutionary authorities adapted colonial jurisdictions while the military situation changed rapidly.

He served behind the army led politically by **Juan José Castelli** and militarily by **Antonio González Balcarce**.

### Huaqui and the Potosí treasury

The patriot army was defeated at **Huaqui** on 20 June 1811. Authority collapsed across much of Upper Peru. Pueyrredón withdrew from Chuquisaca to Potosí and then south.

His best-known act was removing the royal treasury’s coined and uncoined silver from Potosí before royalist forces could seize it. With help from men including **Martín Miguel de Güemes**, he conveyed the funds by a difficult route toward Tarija, Orán, Jujuy, and Salta.

The episode was celebrated as “saving the treasure of Potosí.” The core fact is documented. Exact amounts vary and should not be repeated without reference to treasury accounts.

### Commander of the Army of the North

In September 1811 the central government appointed him commander of the demoralized **Army of the North**.

He attempted to reorganize it but lacked men, supplies, horses, and stable local authority. Forces under Eustoquio Díaz Vélez were defeated at Nazareno. Pueyrredón withdrew to Yatasto.

On 26 March 1812 he transferred command to **Manuel Belgrano**. The later patriotic pairing of the two men should not conceal the poor military state of the army or Pueyrredón’s limited success in rebuilding it.

---

## First Triumvirate, Repression, and Overthrow, 1812

Pueyrredón returned to Buenos Aires and replaced Juan José Paso in the **First Triumvirate**, serving with Manuel de Sarratea and Feliciano Antonio Chiclana. Bernardino Rivadavia, as secretary, exercised exceptional influence.

### San Martín and the Grenadiers

The government received **José de San Martín**, Carlos María de Alvear, and other officers recently arrived from Europe. Pueyrredón and his colleagues authorized San Martín to organize the **Mounted Grenadiers**.

### Alleged Álzaga conspiracy

In July 1812 the government announced discovery of a conspiracy associated with **Martín de Álzaga** and members of the Spanish merchant community. Secret proceedings led to rapid executions, including Álzaga and Francisco de Tellechea.

The evidentiary status remains contested:

- **Documented fact:** arrests, secret proceedings, executions, and confiscations occurred under the Triumvirate.
- **Disputed:** whether a coherent plot existed, what its object was, and whether the evidence justified the sentences.
- **Reported fact:** Pueyrredón doubted the reality or extent of the conspiracy and intervened to stop the killings after the repression began.
- **Responsibility:** as a triumvir, he belonged to the government that authorized the process. Claims that he alone ordered the executions are unsupported. Rivadavia and Chiclana are more often identified as driving the prosecutions.

### Fall of the Triumvirate

The government ordered Belgrano to retreat rather than defend Tucumán. Belgrano disobeyed and won the Battle of Tucumán on 24 September 1812. The victory magnified criticism that the Triumvirate was overcautious, centralizing, and reluctant to proclaim independence.

On 8 October 1812, military units led by San Martín and Alvear, together with the Lautaro network and the Sociedad Patriótica, forced the government’s removal. Pueyrredón and Rivadavia were exiled from Buenos Aires.

---

## Confinement in San Luis and Formation of the San Martín Alliance, 1812–1816

Pueyrredón was confined in **San Luis**, where he engaged in agriculture, commerce, and local relationships. His daughter Virginia was reportedly born there to Juana Sánchez in 1813.

In September 1814 **San Martín**, then governor-intendant of Cuyo, visited him. Pueyrredón was subsequently permitted to visit Mendoza. Their conversations established the political basis of their later partnership: abandoning repeated frontal campaigns through Upper Peru in favor of liberating Chile, acquiring maritime power, and striking Peru from the Pacific.

Pueyrredón returned to Buenos Aires early in 1815. He married María Calixta Tellechea in May.

Late in 1815 or early in 1816, San Luis chose him as its deputy to the **Congress of Tucumán**. One account says Governor **Vicente Dupuy** pressured the local cabildo to select him. Thus, although legally a provincial deputy, he was not simply an organic local representative.

## Supreme Director, 1816–1819

### Election

The Congress elected Pueyrredón Supreme Director on **3 May 1816**, nearly unanimously. He assumed effective command after traveling through the north and Córdoba; official term dates vary between May, June, 3 July, and 9 July depending on whether election, assumption, or arrival in Buenos Aires is used.

He met **José Rondeau** at Trancas, **Martín Miguel de Güemes** near Salta, and San Martín at Córdoba. He confirmed Güemes in defense of the northern frontier and approved San Martín’s trans-Andean project.

The Congress declared independence on 9 July 1816 while Pueyrredón was traveling or taking up executive responsibilities.

### Administrative structure

His government relied on:

- **Gregorio Tagle**, secretary of government and a central political operator;
- treasury, customs, and war officials in Buenos Aires;
- Congress, transferred from Tucumán to Buenos Aires in 1817;
- provincial commanders and the Army of the North;
- confidential agents in Chile, Europe, Brazil, Spain, and the United States;
- the reorganized **Lautaro Lodge**, whose exact membership and command structure remain partly secret and disputed.

Pueyrredón favored a strong central authority. Labels such as “Unitary Party” are anachronistic if understood as a fully organized later party, but his policy was recognizably centralist and anti-federal.

### Support for the Army of the Andes

Pueyrredón’s most consequential policy was material support for San Martín’s campaign.

His government supplied or helped obtain:

- arms, powder, cannon, saddles, uniforms, food, and pack animals;
- drafts of troops and skilled personnel;
- treasury funds and credit;
- secret instructions and intelligence arrangements;
- authority to cross the Andes, liberate Chile, and prepare operations against Peru.

Pueyrredón’s correspondence repeatedly describes the scarcity of resources and the extraordinary measures needed to meet San Martín’s requests. The often-quoted letters in which he lists supplies and says he is left without further “arbitrio” have become a central element of his patriotic reputation.

The campaign crossed the Andes in January–February 1817 and defeated royalist forces at Chacabuco. After the temporary reversal at Cancha Rayada, victory at Maipú on 5 April 1818 secured Chilean independence and the strategic base for the expedition to Peru.

### Relationship with Bernardo O’Higgins

Pueyrredón recognized and worked with the Chilean government headed by **Bernardo O’Higgins**. The relationship was mediated principally by San Martín, diplomatic agents, and shared military planning. Buenos Aires and Chile coordinated financing, naval procurement, propaganda, and the intended expedition to Peru.

### Political ideology and monarchical projects

Pueyrredón was committed to independence but not necessarily to a democratic republic. Like several members of Congress and leading revolutionaries, he considered a constitutional monarchy a possible means of securing international recognition, internal order, and European protection.

Projects discussed during his administration included:

- an Inca or Andean monarchy;
- a European prince;
- connections with the Portuguese-Braganza court;
- French-backed dynastic alternatives.

The **Constitution of 1819** did not declare a monarchy, but its centralized executive and aristocratic Senate could operate under either a republic or constitutional monarchy. Provinces favoring federation rejected it.

There is no basis for the simplistic claim that Pueyrredón sought restoration of Spanish absolutism. His monarchical diplomacy was a proposed institutional form for an independent state.

### Press, dissent, and political policing

His administration restricted political opposition and treated hostile newspapers, pamphlets, expatriate networks, and alleged conspiracies as security threats.

Opponents included:

- **Manuel de Sarratea**;
- **Carlos María de Alvear** and his supporters;
- Chilean exiles associated with **José Miguel Carrera**;
- federalist leaders and sympathetic Buenos Aires politicians;
- editors such as **Vicente Pazos Kanki**.

Pazos’s attacks on the government contributed to his exile in 1817. Opponents were arrested, deported, confined, or tried through special procedures. Recent scholarship studies Pueyrredón’s language of “false news” as a political instrument against unofficial print networks.

### The Carrera controversy

José Miguel Carrera opposed O’Higgins and San Martín, denounced the Lautaro Lodge, and used a printing press and international contacts to attack their governments.

His brothers **Juan José and Luis Carrera** were executed at Mendoza in April 1818 after proceedings for conspiracy. Pueyrredón’s government belonged to the political coalition against the Carreras, but the executions occurred under Cuyo authorities. Assertions that Pueyrredón personally ordered the deaths require more specific documentary proof than the sources consulted provide.

Carrera propaganda portrayed Pueyrredón and San Martín as tyrants, monarchists, lodge conspirators, and usurpers of Chilean liberty. Some charges reflect actual censorship and political exclusion; others were partisan claims produced during civil and international war.

### Artigas and the Portuguese invasion

This is the central controversy of Pueyrredón’s directorship.

In 1816 Portuguese forces from Brazil invaded the **Banda Oriental**, whose principal resistance was led by **José Gervasio Artigas** and the League of Free Peoples.

#### Documented facts

- Pueyrredón did not declare full war on Portugal.
- His government insisted that Portuguese forces not cross the Uruguay River into the western provinces.
- He conducted negotiations and correspondence with Portuguese commanders.
- While Artigas fought Portugal, directorial forces also campaigned against Artigas’s allies in Entre Ríos and Santa Fe.
- Buenos Aires permitted or participated in commercial relations with Portuguese-held Montevideo.
- Artigas repeatedly accused Pueyrredón of assisting the invasion.
- Correspondence published in the *Archivo Artigas* records Artigas’s charges and Pueyrredón’s communications about containing both Portuguese expansion and the federal movement.

#### Disputed interpretation

- **Complicity thesis:** Pueyrredón privately welcomed Portuguese destruction of Artigas’s power, supplied or facilitated the invaders, and sacrificed the Banda Oriental to defeat federalism.
- **Strategic-neutrality thesis:** he distrusted Portugal but lacked the military and financial means to fight Spain, Portugal, and the federal provinces simultaneously; neutrality preserved the Andean campaign.
- **Qualified interpretation:** his policy was deliberately ambiguous. He opposed unlimited Portuguese expansion but treated the destruction of Artigas as strategically useful and directed more immediate force against federal rivals than against Portugal.

No judicial finding ever adjudicated “treason.” The dispute concerns state strategy, secret diplomacy, and the territorial meaning of the still-unstable United Provinces.

### War against Santa Fe and Entre Ríos

Pueyrredón ordered repeated operations to bring the littoral provinces under the central government.

In 1818 a force under **Juan Ramón Balcarce**, numbering approximately 3,000–5,000 depending on the source, invaded Santa Fe. A second column drawn from the Army of the North advanced under **Juan Bautista Bustos**. Governor **Estanislao López** used mobile warfare to isolate and exhaust them.

Balcarce temporarily occupied Santa Fe but withdrew. Directorial forces destroyed livestock, crops, and property; Rosario was burned during the retreat in January 1819. An academic legal-history study reproduces or discusses exceptionally severe instructions contemplating devastation if resistance continued.

San Martín resisted orders to redirect the Army of the Andes from the liberation campaign into the civil war. This produced a late strain in the Pueyrredón–San Martín relationship.

The campaigns failed politically and militarily. They deepened provincial opposition, consumed resources, and helped discredit the central government.

### Spain’s expedition and the Riego movement

In 1819 Spain prepared a large expedition intended for the Río de la Plata. Pueyrredón commissioned agents **Andrés Arguibel** and **Tomás Antonio de Lezica** to encourage resistance or mutiny in the expeditionary army and authorized necessary expenditures.

In 1820 Colonel **Rafael del Riego** led the revolt that prevented the expedition’s departure and inaugurated Spain’s Liberal Triennium.

Pueyrredón later claimed, in *Refutación a una atroz calumnia* (1829), a major role in financing or instigating the movement. Argentine historians have linked his agents to payments among Cádiz officers or Masonic networks. Spanish historiography remains more cautious: the mutiny had extensive domestic causes, and Pueyrredón’s precise causal contribution is unresolved.

### Constitution, resignation, and failure of the regime

The Constitution of 1819 created a centralized national government while leaving the monarchical or republican form open. Federal provinces rejected it.

Pueyrredón submitted his resignation in April 1819; Congress accepted it effective **9 June 1819**. José Rondeau succeeded him.

His administration’s principal success—the liberation of Chile—coexisted with:

- failure to reconcile the federal provinces;
- destructive civil campaigns;
- ambiguous Portuguese policy;
- increasingly restrictive control of dissent;
- monarchical diplomacy lacking broad legitimacy;
- a fiscal and military apparatus unable to sustain both external and internal wars.

## Expulsion, Exile, and Political Defense, 1819–1821

After leaving office, Pueyrredón remained a target of federal, Carrera, Alvear, and anti-directorial opposition.

On **31 January 1820**, the Congress in Buenos Aires ordered the expulsion of Pueyrredón and Gregorio Tagle. The decision came immediately before the collapse of the Directory at the Battle of Cepeda.

Manuel de Sarratea’s new government detained or threatened him, but Sarratea reportedly also facilitated his escape. Pueyrredón reached Portuguese-controlled Montevideo.

He published a **Manifiesto** in May 1820 defending his administration and accusing former associates of betrayal. A contemporary or near-contemporary response by unnamed former allies survives in his archive. Historian Joaquín Pérez interpreted the episode as an internal collapse of the Lautaro political network.

He spent time in Montevideo and later returned to Buenos Aires in March 1821.

## Business, Land, Investment, and Financial Questions

### Family commerce

Pueyrredón’s first profession was merchant. His 1802–1806 correspondence documents Atlantic commercial activity involving Cádiz, Buenos Aires, credit, family agents, shipments, and agricultural goods.

### Agriculture and property

During exile or confinement in San Luis he participated in agriculture and commerce.

The **Chacra del Bosque Alegre**, inherited through the Tellechea family and developed by Pueyrredón and Prilidiano, combined residence with productive rural activity. The landscape included orchards, grain, vines, livestock, river access, and associated laborers. It later became the Museo Histórico Municipal Brigadier General Juan Martín de Pueyrredón.

The surviving building is not an untouched relic. Archaeological research shows that restoration in the 1940s substantially modified it to produce an idealized colonial and “heroic” appearance.

### Buenos Aires real estate

A local architectural history reports that in 1819 Pueyrredón bought a large central Buenos Aires property for **17,500 pesos**. The fact merits verification in notarial records before drawing conclusions about enrichment while in office.

### Bermejo River and Famatina enterprises

In the 1820s Pueyrredón participated in unsuccessful commercial projects involving navigation of the **Bermejo River** and development connected with **Famatina**. Scholarly literature describes proposed companies intended to open inland transport, connect Salta and the Chaco with Atlantic markets, and stimulate mining and regional commerce.

These projects show that he remained an entrepreneur after leaving national office. They appear not to have produced sustained success.

### Tax declaration accusation, 1826

**Documented accusation, no conviction located:** In July 1826 the Controller General accused Pueyrredón of making a false declaration and concealing assets to evade taxation, ordering him to appoint a representative for his defense within four days.

The accusation arose amid poor relations with President Bernardino Rivadavia. The same administration soon appointed Pueyrredón to a military reform commission. No court judgment or conviction establishing tax fraud was found in the sources consulted. It must therefore remain labeled an official accusation, not proven fraud.

### Wealth assessment

He was born into substantial mercantile wealth, owned or controlled urban and rural property, maintained European commercial connections, and could reside abroad with his family. Nevertheless:

- no reliable comprehensive net-worth figure is available;
- wartime public finance and private finance sometimes overlapped through loans and credit;
- accusations of personal enrichment require documentary separation from ordinary family property and inherited assets;
- no established money-laundering or modern corporate-fraud case exists.

## Later Political Activity and Rosas Era, 1821–1849

Pueyrredón did not regain executive power after 1819.

He served on a **Military Commission** concerned with army reform. His relations with Rivadavia remained strained despite the appointment.

In **1829**, following Juan Lavalle’s overthrow and execution of Manuel Dorrego, Pueyrredón tried unsuccessfully to mediate between Lavalle and **Juan Manuel de Rosas**. This placed him between the emergent Unitary and Federal camps rather than neatly inside either.

As political intolerance increased and Rosas consolidated power, Pueyrredón withdrew again. In 1835 he left Argentina with Calixta and Prilidiano.

### European and Brazilian residences

The family’s movements are generally reconstructed as:

- **1835–c.1840:** Bordeaux, with visits to Cádiz or Jerez and continued commercial connections.
- **1841–c.1844:** Rio de Janeiro, after tension between France and Rosas complicated their position.
- **Later 1840s:** Paris, where Prilidiano completed advanced training in engineering and pursued art.

Descriptions of this period often center on Prilidiano and do not precisely locate Juan Martín every year.

Pueyrredón remained in correspondence with old associates, including San Martín. There is no evidence that he participated directly in armed anti-Rosas expeditions.

He returned to Buenos Aires and San Isidro in **October 1849**, already elderly and in declining health.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Political development

His political evolution can be summarized without forcing later party categories:

1. **Colonial merchant and militia leader:** loyal-language service to the monarchy during the British invasions.
2. **Suspected autonomist or separatist, 1808–1810:** opposition to peninsular officials and flight from arrest.
3. **Revolutionary administrator, 1810–1812:** service to the Buenos Aires junta and central government.
4. **Centralist executive, 1816–1819:** independence combined with strong national authority, military mobilization, constrained dissent, and willingness to consider constitutional monarchy.
5. **Retired notable, 1820–1850:** intermittent mediation, business activity, opposition to political extremism, and expatriation under Rosas.

### Religion

Pueyrredón was raised and married within Roman Catholic institutions. Family baptisms, marriages, burials, and the religious language of the period indicate conventional Catholic affiliation. No evidence was found of a conversion, theological authorship, formal anticlericalism, or unusual personal religious practice.

His suspected or asserted involvement in the Lautaro Lodge should not automatically be equated with doctrinal Freemasonry or atheism. The precise ritual, affiliation, and continuity between Cádiz rational lodges and Río de la Plata political societies remain debated.

### Intellectual outlook

His surviving writings are predominantly letters, official instructions, manifestos, and defenses rather than philosophical works. They suggest:

- political pragmatism;
- concern with credit, supply, secrecy, and administrative feasibility;
- belief in disciplined executive authority;
- fear of “anarchy,” a term he applied to autonomous federal movements;
- openness to monarchy as an instrument rather than loyalty to dynastic absolutism;
- preference for elite-managed constitutional order.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### José de San Martín

The central partnership of Pueyrredón’s public life. Their alliance crystallized in San Luis and Mendoza in 1814 and became state policy in 1816. Pueyrredón supplied the Army of the Andes; San Martín provided the strategic project and military execution.

The partnership was not permanently harmonious. San Martín resisted using his forces against the federal provinces, and by 1819 their political paths had diverged. Their friendship and correspondence nevertheless continued.

### Martín Miguel de Güemes

Güemes helped during the retreat with the Potosí treasury and later defended the northern frontier. Pueyrredón confirmed him in that strategic role in 1816, allowing resources to be concentrated on the Andes.

### Manuel Belgrano

Belgrano replaced Pueyrredón in command of the Army of the North in March 1812. During Pueyrredón’s directorship, Belgrano supported central authority and commanded forces ultimately ordered toward the civil conflict with Santa Fe.

### Carlos María de Alvear

Initially part of the returning European revolutionary-military network, Alvear became Pueyrredón’s rival and exile opponent. Alvearist and Carrera circles in Montevideo coordinated propaganda against his government.

### Bernardino Rivadavia

They served in the First Triumvirate government, with Rivadavia as dominant secretary. Relations later deteriorated. In 1826 Pueyrredón faced an official asset-concealment accusation under Rivadavia, followed paradoxically by appointment to a military commission.

### José Gervasio Artigas

The principal federal opponent of his directorship. Their correspondence documents mutual distrust and failed negotiations. Artigas charged him with complicity in the Portuguese invasion; Pueyrredón portrayed Artigas’s movement as destructive “anarchy.”

### Bernardo O’Higgins

An allied head of government in Chile within the San Martín–Pueyrredón strategic system. Their cooperation concerned Chile’s independence and the planned expedition to Peru.

### Manuel de Sarratea

Colleague in the First Triumvirate, later opponent. Sarratea’s 1820 government moved against former directorial leaders, although he reportedly helped Pueyrredón escape.

### Gregorio Tagle

Minister, close political associate, and fellow target of the January 1820 expulsion order.

### José Miguel Carrera

A major propagandistic and political enemy. Carrera’s press accused Pueyrredón, San Martín, and O’Higgins of secret-lodge tyranny and monarchist betrayal.

### Juan Manuel de Rosas and Juan Lavalle

Pueyrredón tried to mediate between them in 1829. He later lived abroad during Rosas’s second government.

### Family network

His mother, brothers, sisters, brothers-in-law, and merchant correspondents were essential to his finances, escapes, political lobbying, and international movements. Juana Pueyrredón and José Cipriano were particularly active in securing his release or escape in 1809.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Coercive Acts

### The Álzaga executions

- **Established:** executions occurred after secret proceedings in July 1812 under the First Triumvirate.
- **Unresolved:** whether a substantial conspiracy existed.
- **Pueyrredón’s position:** member of the responsible government, but reportedly skeptical and opposed to continuing the executions.
- **Personal dimension:** he later married the adolescent daughter of executed Francisco de Tellechea.

### Portuguese invasion and abandonment of the Banda Oriental

- **Established:** strategic neutrality toward Portugal; warfare against Artigas’s allies; secret communications with Portuguese officials.
- **Allegation:** deliberate collaboration amounting to betrayal.
- **Not established:** a judicial finding that Pueyrredón sold or ceded the Banda Oriental.
- **Strongest documentary conclusion:** he considered Artigas a more immediate political threat, tolerated Portuguese pressure against him, and simultaneously sought limits on Portuguese expansion.

### Destruction in Santa Fe and Rosario

- **Established:** Pueyrredón ordered invasions; directorial troops conducted destructive retreats; Rosario was burned.
- **Command responsibility:** Pueyrredón and Tagle set strategy and issued severe instructions; field commanders executed operations.
- **Disputed:** whether devastation was authorized only conditionally or represented the government’s intended policy from the outset.

### Censorship and political repression

- **Established:** opponents were confined, deported, prosecuted, and deprived of presses or political space.
- **Interpretive dispute:** wartime security enforcement versus authoritarian suppression.
- **Recent research:** unofficial newspapers and pamphlets were labeled “false news,” while Carrera and Alvear networks used exaggeration, leaks, and propaganda of their own.

### Carrera brothers

- **Established:** Juan José and Luis Carrera were executed after conspiracy proceedings at Mendoza in 1818.
- **Political responsibility:** Pueyrredón was part of the coalition hostile to the Carreras.
- **Unresolved:** whether he personally directed the sentences or executions.

### Monarchical conspiracy claims

Opponents accused Pueyrredón of betraying republican independence through secret monarchism.

- **Established:** he supported or entertained constitutional-monarchical diplomacy.
- **False simplification:** this did not mean restoring colonial dependence on Spain.
- **Unresolved:** how far he was willing to subordinate local autonomy to a foreign-backed dynasty.

### Asset concealment and tax evasion

- **Established accusation:** the Controller General accused him in 1826 of a false property declaration and concealed assets.
- **No conviction found:** it is inaccurate to call him a convicted tax fraudster.
- **Context:** antagonism with Rivadavia may have shaped the proceeding but does not itself disprove the accusation.

### Secret societies and “Masonic” control

- **Established:** he operated within a secretive revolutionary political network commonly called the Lautaro Lodge.
- **Disputed:** its exact rituals, formal Masonic status, membership, hierarchy, and degree of control over government.
- **Propaganda:** Carrera publications portrayed the lodge as an assassination and dictatorship conspiracy.
- **Historical caution:** secrecy and coordinated patronage are documented; sweeping claims of a single international occult command are not.

### No evidence located

No credible evidence was found of:

- conventional criminal conviction;
- modern money laundering;
- patent theft;
- narcotics or addiction;
- intelligence employment by Britain, France, Portugal, or another foreign state;
- personal participation in torture;
- a medically documented psychiatric disorder.

Absence of evidence in the consulted materials is not proof that every allegation is impossible, but such claims should not be introduced without documentation.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Savior of the Potosí treasure”

**Story:** Pueyrredón rescued the entire treasure of Potosí from the royalists through a daring mountain escape.

**Evidence:** Removal of major treasury funds and transport south are documented. “Entire treasure” is rhetorical; precise content and amount require treasury records.

**Circulation and effect:** Patriotic histories used it to transform a disastrous retreat into proof of courage and administrative reliability.

### The perfect civilian partner of San Martín

**Story:** San Martín conceived liberation and Pueyrredón unconditionally supplied everything required.

**Evidence:** Their cooperation was indispensable, and the supply correspondence is extensive. The formula hides bargaining, scarcity, competing fronts, local levies, Chilean contributions, and their later disagreement over civil war.

**Effect:** This became the dominant Argentine commemorative image of Pueyrredón.

### Sole betrayer of Artigas

**Story:** Pueyrredón invited Portugal to conquer the Banda Oriental and secretly sold Uruguay.

**Evidence for:** tolerance of Portuguese operations, negotiations, trade, simultaneous warfare against Artigas, and language welcoming his weakening.

**Evidence against the strongest version:** he objected to Portuguese movement beyond defined limits and had real constraints against opening another international war. No sale treaty bearing his authorization has been established.

**Effect:** Uruguayan and Argentine federalist memory frequently presents him as the archetype of Buenos Aires centralist betrayal.

### Liberal victim or authoritarian censor

Two rival myths developed:

- **Hagiographic version:** all opposition was sedition endangering independence.
- **Hostile version:** Pueyrredón invented conspiracies and “false news” solely to silence truthful critics.

The record supports wartime conspiracies, organized hostile propaganda, and genuine foreign threats, but also deportation and repression of political speech. Neither absolute version is adequate.

### Architect of Riego’s revolt

**Story:** Pueyrredón’s money and agents caused the 1820 Spanish revolution and thereby saved South American independence.

**Evidence:** agents Arguibel and Lezica were funded to disrupt the expedition; Pueyrredón later claimed credit.

**Against exclusive credit:** Spanish military discontent, unpaid troops, liberal organization, disease, and domestic politics independently explain the revolt.

**Effect:** The story expanded his reputation from regional administrator to covert transatlantic strategist.

### Escape through the window

**Story:** In July 1809 he escaped royal custody through an upper window while guards looked away, aided by his sister and patriot sympathizers.

**Evidence:** arrest, family intervention, escape, hiding, and flight to Brazil are supported. The precise staging may reflect later narrative embellishment.

**Effect:** It became a family legend emphasizing cleverness, loyalty, and the supposed inevitability of independence.

### Aristocratic title

**Story:** Pueyrredón was a French noble or marquis.

**Evidence:** the family claimed or traced connections to a noble Bearnais line. No evidence establishes that Juan Martín personally held or legally inherited a marquisate.

**Effect:** The claim reinforced both elite hagiography and hostile descriptions of him as aristocratic.

### Number and identity of his children

Later genealogies have attributed María de los Ángeles and Virginia to him in addition to Prilidiano.

- Virginia’s attribution is widely accepted but rests substantially on genealogical reconstruction.
- María de los Ángeles remains less certain.
- Claims that Prilidiano was his “only child” mean his only undisputed child by Calixta or only legitimate surviving son, not necessarily his sole biological child.

## Works by Juan Martín de Pueyrredón

Pueyrredón was not primarily a book author. His corpus consists of official papers, correspondence, proclamations, instructions, manifestos, and retrospective defenses.

### Contemporary and individually identifiable writings

1. **Letters and commercial correspondence, 1802–1806**  
   Genuine manuscript correspondence concerning family, commerce, Cádiz, Buenos Aires, and personal affairs. Modern edition:
   - *Juan Martín Pueyrredón, Correspondencia: 1802–1806*. Preface and study associated with César A. García Belsunce. Buenos Aires: Academia Nacional de la Historia, 2018.

2. **Escrito sobre la conquista de los ingleses y defensa de Buenos Aires** (c.1806–1807)  
   Genuine archival writing listed in the Museo Mitre’s Pueyrredón collection.

3. **Official correspondence of the Córdoba, Charcas, and Army of the North administrations** (1810–1812)  
   Genuine letters, orders, reports, and financial/military communications; dispersed in archives and later documentary editions.

4. **Correspondence with José de San Martín** (especially 1814–1819)  
   Genuine official and confidential letters concerning Cuyo, the Army of the Andes, Chile, Peru, supplies, politics, and civil war.

5. **Instrucciones reservadas de Pueyrredón a San Martín para la reconquista de Chile** (1816)  
   Official confidential instructions covering logistics, fortification, political warfare, treatment of royalist forces, and establishment of authority in Chile.

6. **Correspondence with José Gervasio Artigas and federal or littoral leaders** (1816–1819)  
   Genuine official documents, partially preserved in the *Archivo Artigas* and Argentine collections.

7. **Proclamations and messages as Supreme Director** (1816–1819)  
   Government publications issued under his authority. Individual authorship may include secretarial drafting; publication under his signature does not prove unaided composition.

8. **Memoria presentada al cesar en el cargo de Director Supremo** (1819)  
   Genuine retrospective governmental account. Reprinted in *Biblioteca de Mayo*, vol. III.

9. **Manifiesto de Juan Martín de Pueyrredón** (Montevideo, May 1820)  
   Political defense following his expulsion. Genuine authorship, though possible editorial assistance cannot be excluded.

10. **Refutación a una atroz calumnia** (1829)  
    Genuine polemical defense addressing charges against his former administration and asserting a role in disrupting Spain’s expedition through the Riego revolt.

11. **Private and political correspondence, later years**  
    Includes exchanges with San Martín, Bernardino Rivadavia, Francisco Castañeda, Manuel García, Portuguese officials, family members, and others.

### Major posthumous documentary compilations

1. **Museo Mitre, *Documentos del Archivo de Pueyrredón***. Buenos Aires: Coni Hermanos, 1912, 4 vols.  
   Archival compilation containing originals, contemporary copies, and printed documents. Edited posthumously; not a work authored as a unified book by Pueyrredón.

2. **Carlos A. Pueyrredon, ed., *La campaña de los Andes: cartas secretas e instrucciones reservadas de Pueyrredon a San Martín***. Buenos Aires: Peuser, 1942.  
   Posthumous documentary edition with facsimiles and map.

3. **Senado de la Nación, *Biblioteca de Mayo: Colección de obras y documentos para la Historia Argentina*, vol. III**. Buenos Aires, 1960.  
   Includes Pueyrredón’s *Memoria*.

4. **Published San Martín–Pueyrredón correspondence**  
   Appears in the documentary corpus of the Instituto Nacional Sanmartiniano, Museo Mitre, and specialist editions. These are editorial collections rather than newly authored works.

No patents, scientific treatises, novels, plays, artworks, or authenticated diaries by Pueyrredón were located.

- constitutional histories focused on centralism and monarchical projects;
- recent social and print-culture scholarship examining censorship, rumor, conspiracy, and exile.

A full critical biography must use all of these traditions without adopting any one as a complete account.

## Illness, Later Life, Death, and Burial

The consulted sources describe Pueyrredón as elderly and in declining health when he returned from Europe in October 1849, but they do not provide a reliable diagnosis.

No well-supported record was found of:

- chronic addiction;
- psychiatric illness;
- major disabling battlefield injury;
- suicide or violent death.

He died at the San Isidro quinta on **13 March 1850**, aged seventy-two if the 1777 birth year is correct. The Real Academia de la Historia gives 12 March. Most Argentine commemorative, cemetery, and biographical sources use 13 March.

His remains were interred in **Recoleta Cemetery**, Buenos Aires.

San Martín died at Boulogne-sur-Mer on 17 August 1850, five months later. Later biographers emphasized the near-contemporaneous deaths as a symbolic closure to the generation that organized the Andean campaign.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Nineteenth-century memory

Liberal-national history integrated Pueyrredón into a state-building sequence centered on Buenos Aires, the Revolution of May, independence, and San Martín. His papers, donated to Mitre, became part of the documentary foundation for this interpretation.

### San Martín’s indispensable administrator

The most durable favorable reputation presents him as the executive who accepted San Martín’s continental strategy and found resources when the state was nearly bankrupt. Schools, military histories, and commemorations repeatedly reproduce this image.

### Federalist and Uruguayan critique

Artiguist and federalist traditions remember him as:

- an enemy of provincial autonomy;
- a collaborator or passive accomplice of Portugal;
- the director who expended national troops against Santa Fe and Entre Ríos;
- an architect of failed centralism and the Constitution of 1819.

These charges rest on documented policies, though the most absolute “sale of Uruguay” story goes beyond what has been proved.

### Constitutional interpretation

Pueyrredón is often identified as the first effective head of state of independent Argentina because he governed after 9 July 1816. The description is retrospective: the state’s name, territorial reach, constitution, and provincial consent remained unsettled.

### Museum and heritage construction

The San Isidro quinta became a central memorial. Twentieth-century restoration altered its structure to evoke a purified colonial-patriotic past. Archaeology has since complicated that presentation by recovering evidence of work, agriculture, servants or laborers, sanitation, and changing occupation.

### Family cultural legacy

Prilidiano Pueyrredón became one of nineteenth-century Argentina’s central painters and engineers. This extended the family’s public significance from revolutionary politics into portraiture, landscape, architecture, and urban development.

### Place names

Pueyrredón’s name is attached to:

- Avenida Pueyrredón in Buenos Aires;
- General Pueyrredón Partido in Buenos Aires Province;
- schools, streets, railway stations, plazas, military units, and institutions.

The geographical spread of these names reflects state-sponsored commemoration rather than a neutral measure of historical consensus.

## Atlas Connections

Only relationships directly supported by the consulted sources are included. Shared nationality, chronology, or geography alone is excluded.

### José de San Martín — documented fact

Pueyrredón authorized San Martín’s Mounted Grenadiers in 1812, met him in San Luis and Mendoza in 1814, approved the trans-Andean strategy in Córdoba in 1816, and supplied the Army of the Andes from Buenos Aires. Their extensive correspondence documents military, financial, diplomatic, and personal cooperation. The relationship strained when San Martín resisted diversion of his army into civil war, but their correspondence and friendship continued.

**Dates and places:** Buenos Aires, 1812; San Luis and Mendoza, 1814; Córdoba, July 1816; correspondence among Buenos Aires, Mendoza, Chile, and Peru, 1816–1819 and later.

### Manuel Belgrano — documented fact

Belgrano replaced Pueyrredón as commander of the Army of the North at Yatasto on 26 March 1812. Belgrano’s victory at Tucumán after disobeying the First Triumvirate helped precipitate the government’s fall. Under Pueyrredón’s directorship, Belgrano again commanded the Army of the North, parts of which were ordered toward operations against Santa Fe.

**Dates and places:** Yatasto/Tucumán region, 1812; northern and central provinces, 1816–1819.

### Martín Miguel de Güemes — documented fact

Güemes assisted Pueyrredón during the retreat and removal of the Potosí treasury in 1811. Pueyrredón met him in the Salta region in 1816 and confirmed his role defending the northern frontier, an arrangement complementary to the Andean campaign.

**Dates and places:** Upper Peru, Tarija, Orán, Jujuy, and Salta, 1811; Salta/Fuerte de Cobos region, 1816.

### Carlos María de Alvear — documented fact

Alvear arrived from Europe in 1812 as part of the same revolutionary-military milieu that included San Martín. He later became Pueyrredón’s political rival. Alvearist exiles in Montevideo collaborated with Carrera-associated opposition and propaganda against Pueyrredón’s government.

**Dates and places:** Buenos Aires, from 1812; Montevideo and expatriate networks, approximately 1817–1820.

### Bernardo O’Higgins Riquelme — documented fact

O’Higgins and Pueyrredón were allied through San Martín’s liberation strategy. Their governments coordinated after Chacabuco in the military and diplomatic preparation for operations against Peru.

**Dates and places:** Buenos Aires and Chile, principally 1817–1819.

### Juan Manuel José Domingo Ortiz de Rozas y López de Osornio — documented fact

This roster entry is **Juan Manuel de Rosas**. Pueyrredón attempted to mediate between Rosas and Juan Lavalle during the civil conflict of 1829. Pueyrredón later left Argentina after Rosas returned to power in 1835.

**Dates and places:** Buenos Aires Province, 1829; political exile beginning in 1835.

### Domingo Faustino Sarmiento — reported fact, post-public-life association

Municipal museum tradition lists Sarmiento among later visitors to the San Isidro quinta. This does not establish a close personal or political relationship with Juan Martín; the site’s history also extends through Prilidiano and later owners. The precise visit date and host were not established.

### Simón Bolívar — no documented direct connection located

Pueyrredón’s support of the Chile–Peru campaign contributed to the wider independence war later completed in conjunction and tension with Bolívar’s forces. No direct meeting or correspondence between Pueyrredón and Bolívar was established from the sources consulted.

### Antonio José de Sucre — no documented direct connection located

Sucre belonged to the later northern-Andean phase of the continental war. No direct meeting, correspondence, or organizational relationship with Pueyrredón was established.

### Juan Domingo Perón, Eva Duarte de Perón, Domingo Faustino Sarmiento beyond the museum tradition, and other later Argentine roster figures — no direct lifetime connection established

They belong to later generations or appear only in the subsequent history of institutions and commemorative sites.

### Other roster names

No defensible direct connection was found with the remaining roster figures. Given the roster’s breadth, absence of a documented meeting, correspondence, family tie, collaboration, rivalry, shared institution, patronage relationship, or explicitly acknowledged influence is the appropriate finding.

| 8 Oct. 1812 | Triumvirate overthrown; Pueyrredón exiled. |
| 1812–1814 | Confined in San Luis; engages in agriculture and commerce. |
| 1813 | Approximate birth year of daughter Virginia to Juana Sánchez. |
| Sept. 1814 | San Martín visits him; Andean strategy discussed. |
| Early 1815 | Returns to Buenos Aires. |
| 27 May 1815 | Marries María Calixta Tellechea y Caviedes. |
| Early 1816 | Deputy for San Luis at Congress of Tucumán. |
| 3 May 1816 | Elected Supreme Director. |
| July 1816 | Meets San Martín at Córdoba and confirms continental strategy. |
| 9 July 1816 | Congress declares independence. |
| Jan.–Feb. 1817 | Army of the Andes crosses into Chile. |
| 12 Feb. 1817 | Victory at Chacabuco. |
| 1817 | Congress transfers to Buenos Aires; suppression or exile of several opponents. |
| 1816–1818 | Portuguese invasion of Banda Oriental; conflict with Artigas intensifies. |
| 5 April 1818 | Victory at Maipú. |
| April 1818 | Carrera brothers executed at Mendoza. |
| Late 1818–early 1819 | Directorial invasion of Santa Fe; retreat, devastation, and burning of Rosario. |
| 22 April 1819 | Centralist Constitution of 1819 sanctioned. |
| 9 June 1819 | Resignation takes effect; José Rondeau succeeds him. |
| 31 Jan. 1820 | Congress orders expulsion of Pueyrredón and Tagle. |
| Feb. 1820 | Collapse of Directory after Cepeda; Pueyrredón escapes to Montevideo. |
| May 1820 | Publishes political *Manifiesto*. |
| March 1821 | Returns to Buenos Aires. |
| Feb. 1822 onward | Participates in Bermejo/Famatina commercial projects. |
| 24 Jan. 1823 | Birth of Prilidiano Pueyrredón. |
| July 1826 | Official accusation of false property declaration and asset concealment; no conviction located. |
| 1829 | Publishes *Refutación a una atroz calumnia*; attempts mediation between Lavalle and Rosas. |
| 1835 | Leaves Argentina with Calixta and Prilidiano. |
| 1835–c.1840 | Lives principally at Bordeaux; maintains Iberian commercial contacts. |
| 1841–c.1844 | Family resides in Rio de Janeiro. |
| Later 1840s | Residence in Paris; Prilidiano studies engineering and art. |
| Oct. 1849 | Returns to San Isidro. |
| 13 March 1850 | Dies at the Quinta Pueyrredón; one reference gives 12 March. |
