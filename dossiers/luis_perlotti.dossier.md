# Luis Perlotti: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

Luis Perlotti left a large sculptural corpus but comparatively little published autobiographical writing. The principal documentary concentrations are:

- the Museo de Esculturas Luis Perlotti, occupying his former Buenos Aires house-workshop;
- its artwork, photographic, correspondence, teaching, and institutional archives;
- the Museo Benito Quinquela Martín;
- Salon and exhibition catalogues;
- municipal, provincial, and national heritage records;
- contemporary interviews and later art-historical studies.

The most detailed book-length studies—Carlos A. Foglia’s *Luis Perlotti, el escultor de Eurindia* (1963) and Sara M. Spinelli’s *Vida y obra de Luis Perlotti* (1979)—are not fully available online. Accordingly, some matters that may be recoverable through physical archival work, especially the identities of Perlotti’s parents, his complete travel itinerary, finances, private correspondence, and the circumstances of his fatal accident, remain incompletely established here.

Evidence labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by an institutional record, archival item, contemporary testimony, contract, official record, or surviving work.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a generally credible secondary source but not independently verified here from the underlying document.
- **Disputed or inconsistent account:** credible sources give incompatible versions.
- **Interpretation:** an art-historical or critical reading rather than a simple biographical fact.
- **Unverified:** encountered in weak or derivative sources without adequate corroboration.
- **No evidence located:** the searches conducted did not substantiate the proposition.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and immigrant background

**Documented/reported fact:** Perlotti was born into a poor working-class family of Italian immigrants. His father was a shoemaker and his mother a seamstress or dressmaker. The consulted public sources do not provide their given names, Italian places of origin, dates of migration, or marriage record.

The Museo Perlotti’s main history states that his mother died in **1899**, when Luis was nine, after which he worked to support the household. A 2025 museum accessibility guide says he was ten when she died. This is probably ordinary age-rounding rather than a substantive contradiction.

A more important inconsistency appears in a June 1955 interview. Perlotti recalled that he had to maintain his household because he was an **“huérfano de padre”**—an orphan of his father—whereas the museum narrative makes his mother’s death the decisive event. Several explanations remain possible:

1. both parents died during his youth;
2. the interview or its transcription confused which parent had died;
3. his father was absent or unable to support the family;
4. the later museum biography simplified a more complicated sequence.

No civil, parish, or probate record was found to resolve this.

### Siblings

**No evidence located:** None of the consulted sources gives the names or number of Perlotti’s siblings. Statements that he worked “to contribute to the family economy” establish a household obligation but not its exact composition.

### Marriage

**Documented fact:** Perlotti married **Filomena Bianco**, known affectionately as **Mima**, in **1949**. A recent museum guide says that Benito Quinquela Martín gave Luis and Filomena an artwork as a wedding present.

Filomena’s father was **Germán Bianco**, associated with a marble business. Oral-history testimony preserved by the city identifies Bianco as Perlotti’s father-in-law and records commercial connections between the Bianco marble firm and people in Perlotti’s teaching and professional network.

Filomena supported Perlotti’s intention to turn the Pujol house-workshop and its contents into a museum. She survived him and participated in preserving that project. One later cultural report credits her with initiating the museum’s annual sculptural nativity-scene distinction.

### Children and later family

The expression “family of the artist” appears in auction provenances for works sold decades after his death, and museum oral-history materials thank **Rosa Blanca Bianco** for assistance. These references do not establish that Perlotti had children. No reliable consulted source identifies a son or daughter, and no inference should be drawn from the existence of nieces, nephews, in-laws, heirs, or family-held artworks.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1890–1909

Luis Perlotti was born on 23 June 1890 in Buenos Aires, probably in Balvanera. His formative environment was that of Buenos Aires’s Italian immigrant working class.

After the early death of a parent—usually identified as his mother in 1899—he left or interrupted ordinary schooling and entered manual employment. The institutional biography records work as a laborer in several factories, including the **Rigolleau glassworks**, followed by employment in a cabinetmaking or woodworking shop. A museum educational text also mentions a workshop producing ornamental plasterwork for buildings.

These employments were central to his later practice:

- cabinetmaking taught him direct carving and respect for the material qualities of wood;
- plaster ornament provided experience in modelling, casting, repetitive forms, and architectural decoration;
- factory labor gave him technical familiarity absent from a purely academic education.

At the Italian mutual-aid association **Unione e Benevolenza**, he attended evening drawing classes. He subsequently studied in the workshops of the **Asociación Estímulo de Bellas Artes**, preparing for entrance to the national academy. The 2025 museum guide says his prior proficiency allowed him to enter directly into the third year, but the underlying enrolment record was not located.

## Education and Formation, 1909–1915

Perlotti said in 1955 that he entered the Academy of Fine Arts in **1909**, when he was almost twenty. An institutional study says he graduated in **1915** as a drawing professor.

Teachers attributed to him include:

- **Pío Collivadino** — painting and institutional artistic formation;
- **Carlos/Pablo Ripamonte** — sources vary in the spelling and identification; the better-supported form in histories of the academy is Carlos Ripamonte;
- **Enrique Fabbri** — painting;
- **Lucio Correa Morales** — sculpture and modelling;
- **Fontana** — mentioned by a museum educational guide, without a full name.

Correa Morales was particularly important. Perlotti and Ernesto Soto Avendaño are treated in later scholarship as pupils who retained academic modelling while redirecting it toward nativist and Indigenous subjects.

While studying, Perlotti made small commissioned works and portrait busts. Early commissions reportedly included:

- *El tambor de Tacuarí*;
- likenesses of Domingo Faustino Sarmiento for the Colegio Militar and Escuela Naval.

His earliest Salon chronology is inconsistent:

- Perlotti told an interviewer that his first submission was around **1912**.
- The Museo Perlotti’s history says his works were appearing in the Salón Nacional by **1914**.
- Both can be true if a 1912 submission preceded regular or accepted exhibition from 1914.

## Early Career and the Ministry of Agriculture, circa 1913–1921

While still a student, Perlotti entered the national **Ministry of Agriculture**, in or alongside the section responsible for forests and yerba-maté resources. The exact civil-service title varies among sources: draughtsman, carver, and even “guardabosques adscripto” to the drawing section.

He worked under or with **Eduardo L. Holmberg** and came into contact with ethnographer and archaeologist **Juan Bautista Ambrosetti**. A scholarly study based on earlier archival research reports that, for Argentina’s participation in the **1915 Panama–Pacific International Exposition in San Francisco**, Perlotti carved an allegory of the Republic with ornament, native dances, rural scenes, and plates illustrating national forest wealth.

Holmberg and Ambrosetti encouraged him to study Indigenous American designs, archaeology, textiles, ceramics, woodwork, and history. They supplied publications and brought him into contact with collections later identified as part of the Museo Etnográfico’s visual and material repertoire.

### Perito Moreno

The museum biography says Perlotti became a friend of **Francisco Pascasio “Perito” Moreno**, who supplied information about Indigenous life, customs, traditions, and cultures. The friendship is repeatedly reported but its dates and surviving correspondence were not found.

### Early travel claims

In 1955 Perlotti said that, while working with Holmberg and Ambrosetti, he had travelled throughout Argentina “from Chaco to Patagonia,” lived among Indigenous people, and learned to know them personally.

This is **first-person testimony**, but its breadth should not be accepted without qualification. Scholarship shows that he also derived motifs from museum collections, archaeological literature, staged performers, and established ethnographic imagery. His art therefore combined direct encounters with mediated academic and ethnographic representations.

### First indigenist work

Perlotti identified *El araucano* (**1918**) as his first indigenist sculpture. It marks his movement away from conventional commissioned portraiture toward the subject that would define his public reputation.

## Chronological Life History

### 1922: National recognition

Perlotti’s bronze portrait of **Benito Quinquela Martín** received the **Third Prize for sculpture at the XII Salón Nacional**.

A 1955 interview quotes Perlotti calling it his “first prize” and apparently saying “Primer Premio.” The contemporary institutional and later scholarly record consistently identifies the award as **Third Prize**. The best reconstruction is that Perlotti meant his first award, not the first-place award.

The portrait also documents a substantial friendship. Perlotti and Quinquela participated in Buenos Aires artistic circles, especially the **Peña del Café Tortoni**. Quinquela later collected his work, corresponded with him, and supplied two enamelled-iron murals for the Pujol house.

### 1923–1924: *Niña del Cuzco* and the Incaic theatre connection

Perlotti made *Niña del Cuzco*, carved in wood, using **María Ojeda** as model. Ojeda belonged to the Peruvian company **Arte Incaico**, directed or sponsored by Luis E. Valcárcel, which appeared in Buenos Aires in 1923–24.

This is important because the supposedly timeless “Cuzco girl” was not an invented racial abstraction: scholarship has recovered the model’s name and theatrical context.

Prize dating varies:

- Perlotti recalled a Municipal Salon award in **1923**.
- The Museo Perlotti assigns the **Premio Ciudad de Buenos Aires at the XIV Salón Nacional** to **1924**.

The difference may reflect production, submission, and award dates or confusion between municipal and national exhibitions.

### 1924: Ricardo Rojas and Eurindia

Ricardo Rojas’s *Eurindia* proposed a synthesis of European technique and Indigenous American cultural substance. Rojas explicitly placed Perlotti among artists following this path.

Perlotti’s work fitted the program:

- academically trained modelling;
- Indigenous, mestizo, criollo, and folkloric subjects;
- use of native woods and polychrome ceramic;
- incorporation of textile, ceramic, and architectural motifs;
- monumental art meant to create public historical memory.

Rojas later called him **“el escultor de Eurindia.”** A recent Museo Casa de Ricardo Rojas catalogue places the use of the precise title in a **1935 exhibition catalogue**, though the conceptual association existed earlier.

### 1925 onward: Andean journeys

In **1925** Perlotti made what the museum calls his first journey “through America,” particularly into the Andean world. He travelled through northern Argentina and, over successive journeys, Bolivia and Peru. Exact itineraries, dates, companions, border crossings, and travel diaries were not found online.

During the 1925 journey Quinquela Martín wrote urging him not to abandon the Americanist direction he had begun. This letter is cited through Sara Spinelli’s biography.

Works associated with this early phase include:

- *Danza de la flecha* (1925);
- *La tejedora*;
- *El acullico*;
- *La oración*;
- *Dolor indio*;
- *Niña aimará*;
- *Quechua*;
- *Ona en acecho*;
- *Yungueño*;
- *Promesante jujeña*;
- *Ñusta del Kero*.

Perlotti told a vivid story about *Danza de la flecha*: Salon jurors allegedly hid it in a corner because they wanted nothing to do with “gauchos or Indians”; journalist **Juan José de Soiza Reilly** protested in print; the controversy led Entre Ríos’s government to purchase the work for Parque Urquiza in Paraná. This is **Perlotti’s retrospective account**; the specific protest articles were not recovered.

### Humahuaca monument competition

Perlotti collaborated with architect/archaeologist **Héctor Greslebin** on a model for the Monument to Independence at Humahuaca. Perlotti said he received second prize in **1927**; Ernesto Soto Avendaño won the commission and ultimately executed the monument.

This was a significant professional defeat but also demonstrated Perlotti’s standing in national monumental competitions.

### 1927–1929: Municipal and Seville recognition

The chronology of the Seville exhibition is inconsistent:

- the museum history says Perlotti participated in an “Exposición Internacional de Sevilla” in **1927**;
- the major Ibero-American Exposition in Seville opened in **1929**;
- other summaries say he won a gold medal in Seville in **1928** or at the 1929 exposition.

The most defensible formulation is that Perlotti’s polychrome ceramics and works such as *La oración* were selected and received recognition connected with the **1929 Ibero-American Exposition**, but the exact award date requires the original exhibition catalogue or medal register.

### The Peña del Café Tortoni

Perlotti was a participant—and a recent museum guide calls him and Quinquela co-founders—of the Café Tortoni’s artistic-literary Peña. Regulars included Quinquela Martín, Alfonsina Storni, Juan de Dios Filiberto, Soiza Reilly, Enrique Muiño, and other artists, writers, musicians, and actors.

The Peña was both a friendship circle and a mechanism for:

- promoting national and popular art;
- organizing exhibitions and commemorations;
- circulating commissions;
- linking literature, theatre, tango, painting, and sculpture.

When the organization dissolved in **1943**, proceeds from selling its furnishings reportedly helped purchase the granite for Perlotti’s Alfonsina Storni monument.

### 1932–1935: Exhibitions and “Eurindia”

Perlotti received recognition at the **Salón de Otoño de La Plata** in 1932, though the exact prize category was not recovered.

In **1933**, the Asociación Ameghino of Luján organized an important exhibition of his work. Ricardo Rojas wrote a catalogue prologue. A later Museo Rojas catalogue dates Rojas’s designation of Perlotti as “sculptor of Eurindia” to a 1935 catalogue; there may have been closely related catalogues or reprints.

### 1935: Argentine-Irish monument

Perlotti created the bronze relief for the **Monumento-Mástil a la Confraternidad Argentino-Irlandesa** in Plaza Irlanda, Buenos Aires, inaugurated on 16 November 1935.

### 1938–1939: Civic monuments

The **Mástil de los Estudiantes** in San Isidro, inaugurated on 25 May 1938, contains Perlotti’s bronze reliefs *Cultura* and *Ofrenda*.

He made the **Monumento a Bartolomé Mitre** in Corrientes, generally dated 1938. Sources sometimes confuse this with the nearby or related Berón de Astrada memorial. In 1939 he executed a marble-and-bronze monument at Corrientes Cathedral containing the ashes of Governor **Genaro Berón de Astrada**.

### 1941: *Monumento a los Andes*

Perlotti’s bronze **Monumento a los Andes** was installed in Parque Los Andes, Buenos Aires. It represents Calchaquí, Tehuelche, and Ona/Selk’nam types.

The monument served as a focal point for an Association Folklórica Argentina observance of the **Día del Indio** in 1941. It therefore belonged not only to landscape decoration but to organized national debates about Indigenous inclusion, modernization, folklore, and citizenship.

Later critics have read works of this type ambivalently: they visibly inserted Indigenous subjects into national public space but often represented them as generalized racial archetypes or as inhabitants of a disappearing past.

### 1942: Monument to Alfonsina Storni

Perlotti created the monument to his friend **Alfonsina Storni** on the Mar del Plata coast near La Perla, associated with the place of her 1938 death. It presents Storni in profile, her clothing moving toward the sea, with her poem *Dolor* inscribed below.

The monument is both:

- a documented act of posthumous commemoration by a friend;
- an important component of the mythology connecting Storni permanently with the sea;
- a continuing site of homage, pilgrimage, photography, and graffiti.

### 1943 and the 1940s

The monument to irrigation engineer **César Cipolletti**, inaugurated in Río Negro in 1943, shows him with a theodolite and work papers.

Perlotti continued producing funerary monuments, civic portraits, historical reliefs, ceramic vessels, medals, and smaller marketable versions of monumental subjects.

A 1946 Recoleta Cemetery monument for **Francisca Olivera de Pignetto** employs dolomitic stone and a simplified female figure with raised arms. His funerary output also included commissions connected with Pablo Riccheri, Luis Ángel Firpo, Herminio J. Quirós, and other political, military, civic, and private patrons.

### Teaching career

Perlotti taught drawing, modelling, ornament, and model-making. Institutions associated with him include:

- Colegio Nacional Domingo Faustino Sarmiento;
- Escuela Industrial de la Nación N.º 1 “Otto Krause”;
- possibly other national technical or art-education institutions not clearly identified in online summaries.

At Otto Krause he taught from approximately **1924 until retirement**. Photographs show him with students in 1935 and 1952, while former pupils remembered his tactile, demonstrative method—showing forms “with his hands” and bringing promising students into workshop practice.

In **1948**, President **Juan Domingo Perón** confirmed him as titular professor for four weekly hours of modelling in the fifth-year Construction division. Perlotti thanked Secretary of Education Oscar Ivanissevich with a ceramic fragment of *Danza de la flecha*. The surviving decree and letter make this an administrative fact, not proof of personal political intimacy.

Students and workshop associates included:

- **Juan Carlos Ferraro**, who worked in Perlotti’s studio, collaborated on major monuments, and completed some unfinished works after 1969;
- **Rubén García Garriador**, an Otto Krause pupil;
- **Guillermo Ures**, an Otto Krause pupil;
- other students visible in the museum photographic archive.

### 1948–1949: The Pujol house-workshop

In **1948**, Perlotti acquired the property at Pujol 642/644 in Caballito and redesigned it as home, workshop, exhibition space, and future museum.

Features included:

- two large rooms for finished works and library holdings;
- patios and walls used to display Indigenous and gauchesque sculpture;
- ceramic and metal architectural decoration;
- two enamelled-iron murals presented by Quinquela Martín;
- workrooms suitable for assistants and monumental production.

Perlotti and Filomena married in 1949 and lived there together.

### *Retorno a la Patria*, Tunuyán

A national project was introduced in 1948, with mediation attributed to **Eva Duarte de Perón**, to finance and secure land for a monument at the Manzano Histórico in Tunuyán, Mendoza. Congress enacted Law 13,661 in 1949.

The commission was awarded to Perlotti. The monument, officially *Retorno a la Patria*, commemorates the meeting associated with José de San Martín’s return from the liberation campaigns. It combines bronze and travertine, measures approximately 15 by 28 metres, and was inaugurated on **31 December 1950**.

The work included large frontal figures, a four-metre allegory, and lateral bronze reliefs. Foundries or stone contractors identified with it include Sarubbi and Barili.

### 1951–1955: Competition successes and unrealized projects

Perlotti said that in 1951 he won competitions in Mendoza for:

- the **Monument to Italian-Argentine Brotherhood**, Plaza Italia;
- a monument to **Domingo F. Sarmiento**, then still unexecuted.

In 1955 he described a proposed monument to **José de San Martín in Mar del Plata** as potentially his definitive work. Available sources do not establish that this project was executed in the form he anticipated.

### Fragata *Libertad* figurehead

Perlotti designed an original figurehead for Argentina’s training ship *Libertad*. A later report says it was rejected after installation tests showed that its face pointed downward. The ship therefore undertook its first voyage without it. This was a technical/design failure rather than a scandal, and the account requires confirmation from naval design records.

### 1956–1960

A monument to **Artémides Zatti** outside the Viedma hospital was inaugurated on 19 May 1956.

Perlotti created numerous public and private commemorations during these years. A Galería Witcomb exhibition opened on **6 June 1960**; the associated speeches were subsequently printed.

### 1963: Major lifetime monograph

Carlos A. Foglia published *Luis Perlotti, el escultor de Eurindia* in Buenos Aires through Ediciones Áureas in **1963**. Auction catalogue descriptions record:

- approximately 206–208 pages;
- extensive photographic illustration;
- lists of works, museum and private holdings, awards, and bibliography;
- an edition of 3,000 copies;
- copies signed by Perlotti.

It remains the principal lifetime catalogue and biographical source, though its admiring commemorative purpose must be considered when using it critically.

### 1965: Monument to the Welsh settlers

Perlotti’s **Monumento a los colonos galeses** was inaugurated in Puerto Madryn on **28 July 1965**, the centenary of the first Welsh landing.

The contract called for:

- two reliefs approximately 4.5 metres long;
- one showing the immigrants’ landing;
- another showing agricultural success and fraternization with Indigenous residents;
- a three-metre allegorical woman above;
- a total height of approximately ten metres.

Perlotti assembled a team of assistants, prominently **Juan Carlos Ferraro**. The monument was declared a national work of artistic and historical interest by Decree 769/2019.

One museum page instead gives 31 December 1965 as the inauguration date. The national heritage listing’s precise **28 July** date, matching the centenary, is stronger.

### 1965: *Monumento a la Madre*

The **Monumento a la Madre** in Parque Rivadavia, near Perlotti’s Caballito home, was inaugurated on 15 October 1965.

### 1967–1968

The monument to **Cura Brochero** at La Tordilla was inaugurated on 8 September 1967. A 2025 report attributes the commission partly to Perlotti’s friendship with local landowner Constantino Carlos Vaudagna. The bronze monument incorporates reliefs representing estate owners, workers, and neighbours and records more than 130 local surnames.

Other late works include the 1967 ceramic *Escena pampeana*, documented in a later auction catalogue.

Some monuments or reliefs remained unfinished at Perlotti’s death. Ferraro completed or helped complete several, including work associated with Plaza Primera Junta in Caballito.

## Final Journey, Accident, and Death

Perlotti travelled to Punta del Este, Uruguay, in January 1969. On or shortly before **25 January 1969**, he was involved in a motor-vehicle accident, reportedly opposite or near the Punta del Este casino.

Two versions appear:

- **Institutional shorthand:** he died in Punta del Este on 25 January.
- **Derivative but more specific account:** he was taken to the British Hospital in Montevideo and died there from injuries caused by the accident.

No contemporaneous Uruguayan police report, hospital record, death certificate, passenger list, or newspaper account was located in the accessible search results. Consequently, the vehicle, driver, other occupants, exact time, immediate injuries, and officially registered place of death remain unresolved.

There is no evidence in the consulted record of suicide, intoxication, foul play, political violence, or deliberate killing.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Employment

- Factory labor, including Rigolleau glassworks.
- Cabinetmaking/woodworking workshop.
- Ornamental plaster workshop.
- Ministry of Agriculture, forests/yerba or drawing section.
- Colegio Nacional Domingo Faustino Sarmiento.
- Escuela Industrial Otto Krause.
- Independent studio and commissioned-monument practice.

### Workshop structure

Perlotti operated a professional workshop capable of producing:

- original clay and plaster models;
- carved wood and stone;
- polychrome ceramics;
- bronze monuments executed with foundries;
- architectural and funerary ensembles;
- serial or reduced versions for collectors.

Collaborators included assistants, former students, architects, marble contractors, ceramicists, and foundries. Important named collaborators were:

- Juan Carlos Ferraro;
- Héctor Greslebin;
- ceramicist **Valentín Cavalieri**;
- Germán Bianco’s marble business;
- Sarubbi and Barili;
- various unidentified workshop assistants.

### Property

The principal documented property was the Pujol 642/644 house-workshop, purchased in 1948. Perlotti altered it specifically so that it could function as an exhibition environment and eventual museum.

### Wealth and finances

No tax returns, estate valuation, bank records, business accounts, or detailed commission contracts were found, apart from references to individual public contracts. His economic history appears to have progressed from childhood poverty and wage labor to a stable professional income derived from:

- teaching salaries;
- public and private commissions;
- competition awards;
- portraits and funerary monuments;
- ceramics and smaller sculptural works.

He accumulated a substantial house, library, studio infrastructure, archaeological/decorative objects, and approximately 900 artworks. That accumulation indicates professional stability but does not establish exceptional liquid wealth.

### Patents, inventions, and technologies

No patents or registered inventions were found.

His technical practice included:

- direct carving in regional woods;
- bronze modelling and foundry production;
- Carrara and other marble;
- travertine and reconstructed stone;
- polychrome cement;
- glazed and polychrome ceramic;
- medals, reliefs, decorative vessels, iron and enamel elements.

## Artistic, Intellectual, and Political Development

### Academic foundation

Perlotti never abandoned academic figuration. His innovation lay less in formal abstraction than in applying academic composition and modelling to subjects excluded or marginalized by elite art institutions.

### Indigenism and nativism

His work belongs to early twentieth-century Latin American **indigenismo**, **americanismo**, and Argentine **nativismo**. The intellectual setting included:

- Ricardo Rojas’s cultural nationalism and *Eurindia*;
- Ambrosetti’s ethnography and archaeology;
- Holmberg’s natural-historical and state-survey environment;
- Perito Moreno’s Patagonian experience;
- Peruvian indigenism associated with Luis E. Valcárcel and José Sabogal;
- museum collections of textiles, ceramics, carvings, and archaeological forms.

Perlotti’s recurring categories included:

1. generalized portraits of Indigenous peoples;
2. named geographic or ethnic types;
3. myths and legends;
4. ritual, prayer, motherhood, grief, work, and weaving;
5. mestizo and colonial subjects;
6. gauchesque and criollo scenes.

### Eurindia

Rojas’s formula did not propose simply rejecting Europe. It imagined European technique being absorbed into an American cultural synthesis. Perlotti, an Argentine of Italian parentage using academy-derived modelling to represent Indigenous America, could be presented as the living embodiment of that formula.

The title “sculptor of Eurindia” is therefore simultaneously:

- a description of style;
- an intellectual affiliation;
- a promotional label;
- a myth of national synthesis;
- a way Rojas incorporated Perlotti into his own cultural project.

### Politics

No evidence establishes that Perlotti held elected office, belonged to a political party, participated in clandestine politics, or served an intelligence organization.

His public career necessarily intersected with multiple governments. He received state commissions and teaching appointments under conservative, radical, military, and Peronist administrations. Specific Peron-era links include:

- Perón’s 1948 confirmation of his teaching appointment;
- the project legislated for *Retorno a la Patria*;
- Eva Perón’s reported mediation for that project;
- a gift to Secretary Oscar Ivanissevich.

These establish state patronage and administrative interaction, not by themselves ideological Peronism.

### Religion

Perlotti produced Christs, pietàs, funerary figures, saints or devotional monuments, nativity-related works, and the Cura Brochero monument. No reliable source identifies his personal denomination, religious observance, conversion, anticlericalism, or theological beliefs.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Benito Quinquela Martín

**Documented fact:** close friend, artistic associate, portrait subject, correspondent, fellow participant in the Peña del Tortoni, collector/supporter, and contributor of murals to Perlotti’s house. Quinquela encouraged Perlotti’s Americanist course in a 1925 letter.

### Ricardo Rojas

**Documented fact:** intellectual patron and interpreter. Rojas promoted Perlotti as the “sculptor of Eurindia,” wrote catalogue material, collected several of his works, and incorporated him into a program of national-American aesthetics.

### Alfonsina Storni

**Reported/documented relationship:** friend through the Tortoni circle and subject of Perlotti’s most culturally persistent memorial. The personal correspondence record was not located, but multiple museum and literary institutions identify them as friends.

### Lucio Correa Morales

**Documented fact:** sculpture teacher and formative master.

### Pío Collivadino

**Documented fact:** academy teacher. Perlotti’s training under Collivadino linked him to the institutional reform and professionalization of Argentine arts education.

### Eduardo Holmberg and Juan B. Ambrosetti

**Documented/report-supported fact:** colleagues or supervisors in the Ministry of Agriculture environment and early mentors who directed him toward Indigenous subjects and documentary research.

### Francisco P. Moreno

**Reported fact:** friend and source on Patagonia and Indigenous cultures. The extent of their personal contact remains insufficiently documented online.

### Juan Carlos Ferraro

**Documented fact:** pupil/assistant, studio collaborator, major-project assistant, and posthumous completer of unfinished works.

### Héctor Greslebin

**Documented fact:** collaborator in architectural and sculptural monument competitions, notably the Humahuaca proposal.

### Valentín Cavalieri

**Documented fact:** ceramic collaborator on decorative works.

### José Sabogal and Marina Núñez del Prado

Scholarly exhibition literature places Perlotti in a trans-Andean indigenist network. Sabogal maintained ties with Argentine Americanist artists including Perlotti. Bolivian sculptor **Marina Núñez del Prado** befriended Perlotti during her Buenos Aires residence, 1936–38.

### Soiza Reilly

**Perlotti’s reported account:** journalist who publicly defended *Danza de la flecha* after its marginal placement in a Salon.

### Patrons

His patronage network consisted less of a single private magnate than of:

- municipal and provincial governments;
- national ministries and Congress;
- schools and military academies;
- commemorative committees;
- immigrant and mutual-aid communities;
- cemetery and family commissions;
- writers and artists providing intellectual and social sponsorship.

### Criminality, fraud, corruption, and litigation

**No evidence located:** No credible source connected Luis Perlotti personally with fraud, embezzlement, bribery, money laundering, criminal prosecution, political violence, sexual misconduct, abuse, intelligence activity, or a civil lawsuit.

Absence of searchable allegations is not proof that no dispute ever occurred, but nothing located met a threshold for inclusion as a personal accusation.

### Indigenous representation and appropriation

This is the principal substantive contested area.

**Documented fact:** Perlotti studied Indigenous designs through travel, personal encounters, archaeological literature, ethnographic collections, performers, and intellectual networks.

**Critical interpretation:** More recent scholarship places his work within a non-Indigenous, metropolitan, “winka” cultural production that selected, classified, transformed, and monumentalized Indigenous forms. Critics of historic indigenism note that such images often:

- turned living peoples into generalized racial “types”;
- associated Indigenous identity with an ancestral or vanishing past;
- treated textiles, ceramics, bodily features, and ritual signs as a national visual repertory;
- claimed documentary fidelity while relying on staged models and museum taxonomies;
- incorporated Indigenous culture into a mestizo national synthesis controlled by state and elite institutions.

**Countervailing documentary context:** Perlotti travelled, sought named models, studied regional materials, and repeatedly stated affection and respect for Indigenous people. His monuments brought Indigenous subjects into spaces that had generally excluded them.

These observations address different questions. Respectful intent does not determine whether a representation reproduced colonial categories; later critical analysis does not prove personal exploitation, theft, or fraudulent authorship.

### Museum-derived imagery

A scholarly article specifically examines Perlotti’s use of motifs from Museo Etnográfico collections. This qualifies later promotional language that presents his images solely as products of direct field testimony.

**Finding:** His work was neither pure ethnographic transcription nor entirely free invention. It was a synthesis of direct observation, institutional ethnography, archaeological illustration, stage performance, national ideology, and academic sculptural convention.

### Salon marginalization story

**Claim:** Jurors hid *Danza de la flecha* because they rejected gaucho and Indigenous subjects.

**Evidence:** Perlotti himself told this story in 1955 and named Soiza Reilly as the public defender.

**Unresolved:** The Salon installation plan and Soiza Reilly articles were not located. The story is plausible within the period’s hierarchy of subjects but remains only partly corroborated.

### Wrongly designed figurehead

**Reported fact:** Perlotti’s figurehead for the Fragata *Libertad* was rejected because its face pointed downward when fitted.

The episode concerns a failed technical design. No evidence suggests fraud or disciplinary action.

### Monument theft and deterioration after his death

Perlotti’s public works have repeatedly suffered theft, vandalism, environmental damage, and neglect.

A 2026 official municipal decree in General Paz referred to the December 2025 theft of Perlotti’s sculpture *La Llorona* from a municipal cemetery and opened an administrative investigation into possible employee negligence. This is a controversy concerning the custody of his legacy, not misconduct by Perlotti.

Other works, including *Monumento a los Andes*, the Corrientes monuments, and Recoleta memorials, have required restoration or lost bronze components.

### Museum chronology and donation language

Institutional accounts often say Perlotti “donated” the house in 1969 and died shortly afterward. Filomena Bianco’s role, the exact legal instrument, date of acceptance, estate conditions, and delay until public opening deserve further archival examination.

The museum project was initiated in **1987** under César Fioravanti and opened in **December 1990**, more than twenty years after Perlotti’s death. It later underwent major rebuilding and reopened on **22 December 2008**.

## Myths, Legends, Rumours, and Reputation-Making Narratives

### “The sculptor of Eurindia”

**Story:** Perlotti embodied a harmonious fusion of European technique and Indigenous American spirit.

**Origin and circulation:** Ricardo Rojas’s aesthetic program, articulated in *Eurindia* (1924) and attached explicitly to Perlotti in exhibition writing by the mid-1930s. Foglia’s 1963 monograph made the title central to the artist’s public identity.

**Evidence:** Perlotti was European-trained in technique, the child of Italian immigrants, and deeply committed to Americanist subject matter.

**Limit:** “Eurindia” is an ideological interpretation, not a neutral description. It can obscure the asymmetry between Indigenous source communities and the metropolitan institutions interpreting them.

**Effect:** The phrase remains the dominant museum and exhibition frame for Perlotti.

### The wholly self-made worker-artist

**Story:** A poor orphan discovered art through labor and rose solely through talent and effort.

**Evidence for:** Childhood wage work, factory and cabinetmaking experience, evening study, and delayed academy entrance are well supported.

**Qualification:** His success also depended on institutional education, ministry employment, state commissions, mentors, cultural patrons, and networks such as the Tortoni Peña. “Self-made” is accurate only if it does not erase those structures.

### Direct witness to “authentic” Indigenous life

**Story:** Perlotti travelled from Chaco to Patagonia and through the Andes, living with Indigenous people and directly recording their authentic character.

**Evidence for:** Perlotti’s own testimony, repeated travels, collected objects, named models, and a corpus tied to regional types.

**Evidence qualifying it:** Scholars document his use of museum holdings, archaeological publications, theatrical performers, and established visual conventions.

**Effect:** The fieldworker image strengthened claims that his sculptures were documentary rather than imaginative or ideological.

### The Alfonsina memorial as a portrait of her final walk

**Story:** The wind-driven profile shows Storni deliberately entering the sea and captures her final departure.

**Evidence:** Perlotti knew Storni and placed her image near the site of her death, incorporating *Dolor*.

**Mythic layer:** The visual narrative corresponds to the romantic story that Storni slowly walked into the sea. Her exact final movements cannot be reconstructed from the sculpture.

**Effect:** The monument helped fix the union of Alfonsina, poetry, and the sea in Argentine cultural memory.

### “Founder” of the Tortoni Peña

Recent museum material says Perlotti and Quinquela founded the Peña. Other histories emphasize a broader collective or different organizers.

**Finding:** Perlotti was unquestionably a central participant. Sole or joint founder status requires the organization’s original minutes or contemporary notices.

### “More than 900 works”

The museum’s founding collection is often described as approximately **900 pieces**. This number likely refers to objects transferred with the house, not necessarily the total number Perlotti created. His monuments, privately owned works, editions, studies, casts, and lost pieces make a complete lifetime total impossible from current public catalogues.

## Posthumous Museum History and Legacy

Perlotti intended his home and studio to become a museum. Filomena Bianco shared and sustained that intention.

The house and collection passed to the City of Buenos Aires under an arrangement commonly described as a donation. In **1987**, César Fioravanti promoted the institutional project. The Museo de Esculturas Luis Perlotti opened in **December 1990**.

Its mission expanded beyond memorializing one artist to include Argentine sculpture generally. Important later developments include:

- acquisition of works by other Argentine sculptors;
- a major donation of nearly 500 objects by Lidia Elsa Battisti, widow of Juan Carlos Ferraro;
- temporary exhibitions, sculpture prizes, educational programs, and nativity-scene competitions;
- architectural reconstruction and reopening on **22 December 2008**.

The museum is described as “autobiographical” because its core is Perlotti’s house, studio, collection, and self-fashioned legacy. This creates unusual richness but also requires critical attention: an artist’s preserved environment is simultaneously archive, memorial, and act of self-curation.

### Shifts in reputation

Perlotti’s standing has moved through several frames:

1. **1920s–30s:** working-class academy graduate and emerging nativist sculptor;
2. **1930s–50s:** official “sculptor of Eurindia,” successful portraitist and monument maker;
3. **1960s:** prolific senior public artist with national geographic reach;
4. **1970s–90s:** subject of commemorative biography and museum formation;
5. **Twenty-first century:** heritage figure reconsidered within histories of indigenism, nationalism, ethnography, appropriation, and public monuments.

His relative absence from international modernist canons reflects, in part, his commitment to figuration and civic monumentality at a time when critical prestige increasingly favored abstraction and avant-garde experimentation.

### Continuing influence

His legacy survives through:

- public monuments distributed across Argentina;
- the Caballito museum;
- the training and workshop lineage represented by Ferraro and other students;
- the preservation of Indigenous and popular subjects in Argentine civic imagery;
- contemporary debate over who may represent Indigenous culture and how;
- conservation controversies involving theft, damage, and environmental deterioration;
- the Alfonsina Storni memorial’s independent life as a literary landmark.
