# Ramon Llull: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

Ramon Llull’s surviving record is unusually rich for a thirteenth-century lay intellectual but uneven. The principal narrative source for his life is the *Vita coaetanea*—*Vida coetània* in Catalan—dictated by Llull to an anonymous Carthusian monk at Paris in 1311. It is near-contemporary and partly autobiographical, but it was also composed as a presentation of Llull and his program before the Council of Vienne. Its conversion scenes, visions, dangers, imprisonments, and missionary encounters therefore require the same source criticism as other medieval religious autobiography.

The *Vita* can be checked against:

- dated colophons in Llull’s works;
- royal and papal documents;
- records from Montpellier, Majorca, Paris, Genoa, Pisa, Sicily, and North Africa;
- the biographical miniatures in Thomas Le Myésier’s early-fourteenth-century *Breviculum*;
- Llull’s autobiographical poems and dialogues;
- later manuscript and cult traditions.

The chronology below follows the documentary synthesis maintained by the University of Barcelona’s Centre de Documentació Ramon Llull and modern studies by Anthony Bonner, Lola Badia, Albert Soler, Joan Santanach, Fernando Domínguez Reboiras, Charles Lohr, Jocelyn Hillgarth, Mark Johnston, Josep E. Rubio, and other specialists. Dates attached to early events are frequently approximate.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Primary name:** Ramon Llull.
- **Medieval and modern variants:** Ramon Lull; Ramón Llull; Ramón Lulio; Raimundo Lulio; Raimundus, Raymundus or Raimundus Lullus; Lullius; Raymond Lull; Raymond Lully; Raimondo Lullo; Raymond Lulle.
- **Self-description:** In Latin documents and works he identified himself in forms including *Raimundus Lullus* and *magister Raimundus Lul catalanus*.
- **Traditional titles:** *Doctor Illuminatus* (“Illuminated Doctor”), *Doctor Inspiratus*, *Arabicus Christianus* (“Christian Arab”), “Apostle of Africa,” “procurator of the infidels,” and “martyr of Majorca.” Some are later honorifics rather than offices held during his life.
- **Religious status:** Lay Christian missionary and penitential writer closely associated with Franciscan spirituality. Claims that he was formally a Franciscan friar are not securely documented. He became the object of an authorized local Catholic cult and is conventionally styled **Blessed Ramon Llull**, but he has not been universally canonized by the Roman Catholic Church.
- **Professions and roles:** Courtier, household official, troubadour or court poet, married landholder, religious author, philosopher, logician, theologian, missionary, polemicist, educational lobbyist, lecturer, and political-religious adviser.
- **Languages:** Catalan, Latin, and Arabic. He wrote or arranged versions in all three, although no Arabic autograph or complete Arabic manuscript of an authentic work is presently known to survive.
- **Birth:** Probably 1232, possibly 1233, in the city of Majorca—modern Palma, Mallorca.
- **Death:** After December 1315 and before 25 March 1316. Place and circumstances are uncertain: Tunis, at sea between North Africa and Mallorca, or Mallorca are the principal possibilities.
- **Burial:** Church of Sant Francesc, Palma, where his tomb became a focus of local veneration.
- **Documentary caution:** The familiar exact dates “1232–1316” are conventional endpoints, not securely recorded day-and-month dates.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and social origin

**Documented or strongly supported fact:** Llull was born into a Christian settler family originating in Barcelona. His father, normally identified as **Ramon Amat Llull**, participated in or followed the Catalan-Aragonese conquest of Muslim-ruled Mallorca by James I in 1229. The family received property in the new Christian kingdom and belonged to the prosperous urban or lesser-patrician stratum.

Llull’s mother is conventionally named **Isabel d’Erill**, although the documentary basis for her identity and ancestry is less secure than the general reconstruction of the Llull family’s Barcelona origin. Claims of especially elevated noble descent should be treated cautiously. The family had property and court access, but “nobleman” can overstate its demonstrated rank.

Mallorca during Llull’s childhood was a recently conquered frontier society. Muslims remained numerous, many under enslavement or coercive dependency; Jewish communities participated in trade, administration, medicine, and scholarship; Catalan Christian settlers occupied political dominance. This environment, rather than an abstract “meeting of three cultures,” supplied the unequal social setting in which Llull later learned Arabic and designed a program to convert Jews and Muslims.

No securely established list of his siblings has been reconstructed from the sources used for this dossier.

### Marriage and children

**Documented fact:** In about 1257 Llull married **Blanca Picany** or Blanca Picany/Picanys. They had at least two children:

- **Domènec Llull**, a son;
- **Magdalena Llull**, a daughter.

Llull’s later autobiographical narrative minimizes the domestic consequences of his conversion. He disposed of much property while retaining enough to provide for his wife and children. Blanca reportedly sought legal or administrative protection for the family’s property when his behavior became economically disruptive. This was not a modern divorce, and there is no firm evidence that the marriage was formally dissolved.

After approximately 1263, Llull ceased living as an ordinary courtier and husband. His writings present this as renunciation of a sinful former life. From the family’s perspective it also meant the effective withdrawal of a husband and father into study, asceticism, travel, and missionary work.

### Sexual and romantic life

**Self-reported fact, imprecisely documented:** Llull described his youth and early marriage as worldly, vain, and sexually disordered. The *Vita* says his conversion began while he was composing an amorous song for a woman whom he loved with “foolish love.” Later hagiography expanded this into stories of numerous adulteries and spectacular libertinism. No names, number of relationships, or independent evidence survive. The general admission of extramarital desire comes from Llull’s own penitential self-presentation; detailed tales of debauchery are later elaborations.

## Childhood and Early Life, approximately 1232–1257

Llull was born only a few years after James I’s conquest of Mallorca. He therefore belonged to the first generation raised under the island’s new Catalan Christian regime.

Little is known directly about his childhood. His later command of courtly culture suggests education appropriate to a prosperous household: vernacular literacy, familiarity with troubadour lyric and chivalric convention, household administration, and probably practical Latin. He did not undergo the normal university sequence in arts and theology followed by scholastic masters.

As a young man he entered the courtly world surrounding the future **James II of Majorca**, a younger son of James I. The *Vita* calls Llull a seneschal and *maior domus* or household official. The exact progression of offices is uncertain, but court service is well supported. He composed songs in the troubadour manner; none of the secular poems attributed to his pre-conversion career survives securely.

The court connected Mallorca to Montpellier, Roussillon, Catalonia, and Provence. These political-geographical ties later provided Llull with residences, patrons, and routes into papal and French circles.

## Conversion and Formation, approximately 1263–1274

### The visions of Christ

According to the *Vita coaetanea*, at about age thirty Llull was composing a love song when he saw Christ crucified appear at his right side. He resumed ordinary life, but the apparition recurred four or five times. He interpreted the repetitions as a command to abandon worldly pursuits.

**Evidentiary label:** Self-reported religious experience. The account was dictated nearly fifty years later and structured according to conversion and saintly-life conventions. The visions cannot be independently verified, but their role in Llull’s self-understanding and subsequent program is documented.

He formulated three lifelong objectives:

1. to accept death for Christ while working to convert non-Christians;
2. to write “the best book in the world” against what he regarded as the errors of unbelievers;
3. to persuade rulers and popes to establish schools where missionaries would learn Arabic and other necessary languages.

### Pilgrimage and advice from Ramon de Penyafort

Around 1265 Llull made pilgrimages to **Rocamadour** and **Santiago de Compostela**. He considered studying at Paris but consulted the Dominican canon lawyer and royal adviser **Ramon de Penyafort** in Barcelona. Penyafort reportedly advised him to remain in Mallorca rather than enter Parisian study unprepared.

**Reported fact:** The meeting is accepted in the standard chronology but known through Llull’s autobiographical tradition rather than a separate record by Penyafort.

### Property and withdrawal

Llull sold or redistributed property, reserving support for Blanca and the children. Later accounts depict him wearing coarse clothing, engaging in prayer, and separating himself from court life. His family apparently regarded aspects of his conduct as imprudent. There is no evidence that he embraced destitution in the strict Franciscan sense; throughout his career he traveled, employed scribes, commissioned manuscripts, petitioned courts, and drew upon patrons.

### Nine years of study

From approximately 1265 to 1274 he studied in Mallorca rather than matriculating at a university. His curriculum appears to have included:

- Latin grammar and Christian theology;
- logic, including a version of al-Ghazālī’s logic;
- Arabic language and religious vocabulary;
- Christian apologetics;
- elements of Aristotelian, Neoplatonic, Augustinian, and Arabic philosophical traditions.

He purchased an enslaved Muslim man to teach him Arabic. The teacher’s name is not preserved.

#### The enslaved teacher’s attack and death

The *Vita* reports that after years of instruction the enslaved man blasphemed Christ. Llull struck him in the mouth. The man retaliated with a sword and seriously wounded Llull. Llull prevented bystanders from immediately killing him but had him imprisoned. The prisoner subsequently hanged himself.

**Documented only through autobiographical narrative:** The episode is not independently corroborated. If substantially accurate, it records Llull as slaveholder, physical aggressor, victim of an attempted killing, and controller of the man’s imprisonment. The *Vita* interprets the suicide providentially, as a resolution of Llull’s anxiety over whether he should demand the man’s execution. The enslaved man’s own account is irretrievable.

Recent scholarship by José Bellver has questioned the routine assumption that the teacher was merely an uneducated, long-enslaved Mallorcan. His linguistic or intellectual background, place of origin, and possible contribution to Llull’s thought remain unresolved. The encounter is a major but asymmetrical intellectual relationship: an unnamed enslaved Muslim helped equip Llull to engage Arabic thought while leaving no independent textual voice.

### Randa and the conception of the Art

Around 1274 Llull withdrew to **Mount Randa** in Mallorca. He later said God illuminated his mind there, revealing the form of the universal method that became his *Art*.

**Evidentiary label:** Self-reported illumination. The existence of the resulting method and its early works is documented; its supernatural origin is a religious claim.

The early or “quaternary” Art used letters, divine attributes, principles, tables, and rotating or combinable figures. It was intended to organize every domain of knowledge and demonstrate Christian doctrines through necessary reasons acceptable, Llull believed, even to interlocutors who rejected biblical authority.

## Miramar and the First System, 1274–1287

### Early works

Between approximately 1271 and 1276 Llull produced:

- *Compendium logicae Algazelis* / *Lògica del Gatzell*, adapting material associated with al-Ghazālī;
- *Llibre de contemplació en Déu* (*Book of Contemplation in God*);
- *Ars compendiosa inveniendi veritatem* / *Art abreujada d’atrobar veritat*;
- *Llibre del gentil e dels tres savis*;
- *Llibre dels principis* series.

The *Book of the Gentile and the Three Wise Men* stages a discussion among a Jew, Christian, Muslim, and religiously unaffiliated gentile. Its apparent procedural openness is limited by Llull’s intention to prove Christianity, but its refusal to identify the gentile’s final choice gives the dialogue a notable literary ambiguity.

### Miramar language school

In 1275–1276 Llull obtained support from James II of Majorca for a school at **Miramar**, on Mallorca’s northwestern coast. Pope John XXI confirmed arrangements in 1276. About thirteen Franciscan friars were to study Arabic for missionary work.

**Documented achievement:** Miramar partially realized Llull’s plan for institutional language training.

**Uncertain outcome:** The school’s duration, effectiveness, and exact curriculum are poorly documented. It did not become the permanent international network Llull imagined.

### Literary expansion

During the late 1270s and 1280s he wrote major Catalan works that joined doctrine, narrative, poetry, social criticism, and encyclopedic instruction:

- *Romanç d’Evast e Blaquerna*, containing the *Llibre d’amic e amat*;
- *Llibre de l’orde de cavalleria*;
- *Doctrina pueril*;
- *Llibre de meravelles*, containing the politically charged animal fable *Llibre de les bèsties*.

*Blaquerna* imagines a protagonist who progresses through marriage, monasticism, ecclesiastical office, papacy, and eremitic contemplation. It includes a reforming pope who reorganizes Christian society. Calling it the “first Western utopia” is an interpretive modern claim, not an uncontested bibliographical classification.

---

## International Campaigns, 1287–1293

### Rome and Paris

Llull increasingly sought institutional validation. He petitioned popes, kings, cardinals, universities, Franciscans, Dominicans, and municipal authorities for:

- language colleges;
- trained disputants;
- recognition of the Art;
- missionary expeditions;
- reform of Christendom;
- eventually, military recovery of territories from Muslim rulers.

He traveled to **Rome** in or around 1287, but the death of Pope Honorius IV frustrated an anticipated hearing.

In **Paris**, probably 1287–1289, he lectured and presented his Art. University scholars found the early system excessively complicated or its claims unpersuasive. Llull revised it into the “ternary” phase, reducing and reorganizing its figures and principles.

His autobiographical poem *Desconhort* later expressed isolation, exhaustion, and frustration that rulers and clerics did not support his plans. This is evidence of a severe spiritual-emotional crisis, though not a basis for retrospective psychiatric diagnosis.

### Montpellier and Genoa

Montpellier, within the Majorcan royal sphere, became one of his most important recurring bases. He also built support among Genoese patrons and religious communities. Genoa was the principal embarkation point for his North African missions.

---

## First North African Mission: Tunis, 1293

Llull sailed to **Tunis** in 1293. The *Vita* says he publicly offered to debate Muslim scholars and claimed that he would accept Islam if they could rationally prove it true, while expecting to demonstrate Christianity instead.

Muslim authorities or scholars reportedly debated him, then imprisoned and expelled him. A Christian merchant community or Genoese contacts may have protected him.

**Reported fact with autobiographical shaping:** The journey, debate, detention, and expulsion are broadly accepted; exact speeches and dramatic confrontations come from Llull’s narratives.

He remained for a time near the harbor or aboard ship and composed material related to the disputation. There is no credible evidence that he converted any identifiable Muslim participant.

---

## Naples, Rome, Paris, Barcelona, and the Eastern Mediterranean, 1294–1305

### Naples and papal politics

In 1294 Llull lectured at **Naples**, then under Angevin rule, and sought support from the short-lived pope **Celestine V** and afterward **Boniface VIII**. He addressed petitions and works to the papacy but failed to obtain the comprehensive missionary institution he wanted.

He spent periods in Rome and perhaps other Italian centers. His political theology increasingly linked mission to ecclesiastical reform and geopolitical action.

### *Arbre de ciència* and systematic work

At Rome in 1295–1296 he composed the vast *Arbre de ciència* (*Tree of Science*), representing knowledge through a series of trees whose roots, branches, leaves, and fruits mapped divine, elemental, vegetal, animal, human, moral, ecclesiastical, and other orders.

### Crisis and *Desconhort*

*Desconhort*, probably written in 1295, dramatizes a conversation between Ramon and a hermit. Ramon laments that decades of work have produced little institutional response. It is simultaneously autobiography, argument, spiritual complaint, and public persuasion.

### Paris, 1297–1299

Llull returned to Paris, discussed his method, wrote against positions associated with Latin Averroism, and continued revising the Art. His relationship with university institutions was never a conventional professorship. Claims that he “taught at the University of Paris” should mean that he lectured and presented works in its intellectual environment, not that he held a securely documented salaried chair.

### Barcelona and royal authorization

In 1299 James II of Aragon authorized Llull to preach in synagogues and mosques within the king’s domains. The coercive setting matters: compulsory or royally enabled attendance at Christian preaching was not an exchange among equal religious communities.

### Mallorca and Cyprus, 1300–1302

After returning to Mallorca, Llull traveled east, probably in 1301–1302, to **Cyprus**, **Armenian Cilicia**, and possibly Jerusalem. He sought access to eastern Christian rulers and contact with Muslim intellectuals.

The *Vita* reports that in Cyprus he was poisoned by servants or members of his household and became gravely ill. The motive and perpetrators are unknown.

**Evidentiary label:** Reported in the autobiographical tradition; uncorroborated allegation. It should not be converted into an established assassination plot.

He met or sought audiences with **Henry II of Cyprus** and Christian religious leaders. His hopes for organized missionary debates again produced no lasting institution.

### Genoa and Montpellier, 1303–1305

He returned through Genoa and Montpellier. Important works of this period include:

- *Liber de ascensu et descensu intellectus*;
- *Liber de fine*;
- revisions leading toward the *Ars generalis ultima*.

The *Liber de fine* proposed a coordinated strategy for mission, language training, ecclesiastical unification, and military conquest.

---

## Bougie, Imprisonment, Shipwreck, and Pisa, 1307–1308

### Mission to Bougie

In 1307 Llull traveled to **Bougie**—Bijāya, in present-day Algeria. He publicly proclaimed Christian doctrine and attacked Islamic belief. Authorities imprisoned him, reportedly for about six months. He engaged in disputation with Muslim scholars while confined.

Some accounts say a local ruler or scholar protected him from execution. He was eventually expelled.

**Documented core:** Journey, preaching, imprisonment, disputation, and expulsion.

**Uncertain details:** Exact length of captivity, words spoken, identities of Muslim interlocutors, and whether a particular official personally saved him.

### Shipwreck

On the return voyage, the vessel was wrecked near **Pisa**. Llull survived but reportedly lost books and possessions.

**Reported fact:** The shipwreck is a stable part of the early narrative and fits the dated Pisa works; details remain dependent on Llullian sources.

### Pisa

Welcomed by Pisan citizens or religious supporters, he completed or reconstructed major works, including:

- *Ars generalis ultima*;
- *Ars brevis*;
- *Disputatio Raimundi christiani et Homeri saraceni*, a literary reconstruction of debate with a Muslim called “Homer,” probably a Latinization or corruption of ʿUmar.

Pisa considered but did not execute a proposed crusading expedition. Llull again experienced the difference between sympathetic reception and effective political implementation.

---

## Paris and the Anti-Averroist Campaign, 1309–1311

Llull returned to Paris for his most consequential academic campaign. He opposed propositions attributed to “Averroists,” especially claims concerning the unity of the intellect and tensions between philosophical demonstration and Christian doctrine.

He wrote numerous short works, presented his Art in a more disciplined form, and gained attestations from Parisian masters that his works had been examined or could be studied without the blanket rejection he had previously encountered.

This was a qualified success:

- he achieved greater academic visibility;
- some masters endorsed or tolerated study of his works;
- he did not displace Aristotelian scholastic methods;
- his claim that articles of faith could be demonstrated by “necessary reasons” remained controversial.

At Paris in 1311 he dictated the *Vita coaetanea*. The text functioned as an autobiographical dossier for his next political effort at Vienne.

---

## Council of Vienne, 1311–1312

Llull attended or lobbied around the **Council of Vienne**. His petitions included:

- permanent schools of Arabic, Hebrew, and “Chaldean” or Syriac;
- a unified military-religious order;
- campaigns for the Holy Land;
- reform of Christian life;
- suppression of philosophical doctrines he considered incompatible with faith.

The council ordered chairs in Hebrew, Arabic, and Chaldean at the papal court and at Paris, Oxford, Bologna, and Salamanca.

**Documented success:** This decree closely resembled a proposal Llull had promoted for decades.

**Qualification:** Implementation was irregular, and the decree cannot be credited to Llull alone. He was one advocate within a wider missionary and ecclesiastical movement.

Llull’s dialogue *Disputatio Petri clerici et Raimundi phantastici* presents a cleric seeking benefices for relatives and Ramon pursuing an apparently impossible religious mission. The title “Fantasticus” plays on whether Llull is inspired, impractical, or mad; he appropriates the accusation as self-defense.

---

## Final Years: Mallorca, Sicily, and Tunis, 1312–1315/16

### Mallorca, 1312–1313

Llull returned to Mallorca and composed sermons and works on preaching, contemplation, and the Art. He remained intensely productive in his eighties.

### Sicily, 1313–1314

He went to **Messina**, seeking support from **Frederick III of Sicily**. Sicily’s position between Latin Christendom, Byzantium, North Africa, and the eastern Mediterranean suited his proposals for Christian-Muslim disputation and eastern-Christian union.

He wrote *Liber de participatione christianorum et saracenorum*, proposing organized discussion between learned Christians and Muslims. The text combines comparative engagement with the declared aim of conversion.

### Final Tunis mission, 1314–1315

Llull returned to **Tunis**, apparently with diplomatic or royal protection and in a political climate more favorable than in 1293. He dedicated works to a ruler styled the king of Tunis or to Christian patrons connected with the mission.

His last dated works were completed at Tunis in December 1315. After that, he disappears from contemporary documentation.

## Death, Burial, and the Martyrdom Problem

### What can be established

- Llull was alive in Tunis in December 1315.
- He was dead by 25 March 1316, when documents or later administrative evidence presuppose his death.
- His body or remains were buried at Sant Francesc in Palma.
- The exact date, place, and cause of death are unknown.

### Traditional martyrdom narrative

The familiar story says that Llull preached at Bougie, was dragged outside the city, stoned by a Muslim crowd, rescued by Genoese merchants, and died either aboard ship within sight of Mallorca or shortly after landing.

**Evidentiary label:** Later hagiographical legend, not established contemporary fact.

Problems include:

- the earliest reliable account of his final journey places him in Tunis, not demonstrably at Bougie;
- his final surviving works do not mention a last Bougie mission;
- no contemporary record describes a stoning;
- versions disagree whether he died in Bougie, at sea, or in Mallorca;
- the story expanded as his local cult and campaign for recognition as a martyr developed.

A violent death remains possible, but the evidence does not justify presenting stoning as fact. Age, illness, travel-related disease, accident, or an undocumented death at sea or in Mallorca remain possible.

### Tomb and cult

Llull was entombed in the Franciscan church at Palma. His tomb became a civic and religious monument. Mallorcan devotion styled him a saint and martyr well before universal Roman recognition. His feast is generally observed locally on **27 November**.

## Education, Teachers, Mentors, and Intellectual Sources

### Directly reported teachers and advisers

- **Unnamed enslaved Muslim Arabic teacher:** Direct language instructor for several years; intellectual contribution cannot be measured because his identity and writings are unknown.
- **Ramon de Penyafort:** Adviser who reportedly directed Llull away from immediate Paris study.
- **Courtly and troubadour culture:** Formative milieu rather than an individual teacher.
- **Franciscan circles:** Important spiritual and institutional network; Llull’s exact juridical membership remains disputed.

### Intellectual influences

- **Augustine:** Divine attributes, illumination, ordered ascent, and the relation between belief and understanding.
- **Anselmian rational theology:** Confidence that faith can seek and attain rational understanding.
- **Aristotle and scholastic logic:** Categories, demonstration, and scientific ordering, though Llull constructed an alternative method.
- **Arabic philosophy and logic:** Especially material transmitted under al-Ghazālī’s name.
- **Islamic theology and devotional language:** Llull’s use of divine dignities and some contemplative forms has been compared with Islamic and Sufi traditions. Direct lines of borrowing remain debated.
- **Neoplatonic traditions:** Hierarchical emanation, participation, and correspondence between divine principles and creation.
- **Franciscan spirituality:** Poverty, mission, contemplation, Christ-centered affect, and the desire for martyrdom.
- **Troubadour lyric:** Transformed in the *Llibre d’amic e amat*, where the language of lover and beloved becomes mystical.

### Abraham Abulafia and Kabbalah

Llull and **Abraham ben Samuel Abulafia** were contemporaries in the western Mediterranean, and both used letters and combinations in religious-intellectual systems. Scholars have investigated possible Kabbalistic influence.

**Evidentiary label:** Theory, not documented personal connection. No surviving source establishes a meeting, correspondence, or direct borrowing. Similarity alone is insufficient, particularly because combinatory and letter-symbolic methods circulated through several traditions.

## Religious, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

### The Art

Llull’s *Art* was a general method designed to:

- establish basic principles shared across disciplines;
- combine divine attributes or “dignities”;
- generate and test propositions;
- move between levels of reality;
- answer questions through defined rules;
- demonstrate Christian doctrines;
- unify knowledge and guide action.

Letters such as B, C, D, and others stood for multiple concepts depending on the figure or table. Rotating wheels and combinatorial arrangements allowed systematic permutations. These were not mechanical computers in the modern sense, nor binary digital systems.

### Developmental phases

Modern scholarship generally distinguishes:

1. **Pre-Art works**, including the early logic and *Book of Contemplation*;
2. **Quaternary phase**, beginning around 1274, with more numerous principles and complex figures;
3. **Ternary phase**, from around 1290, simplifying the Art into groups of nine;
4. **Post-Art or late applications**, in which Llull applied the stabilized method to theology, philosophy, law, medicine, astronomy, rhetoric, preaching, and politics.

### Faith and proof

Llull argued that Christian doctrines could be demonstrated through reasons rooted in divine attributes rather than simply asserted from scripture. This allowed him to address Muslims and Jews who did not accept Christian textual authorities.

The project generated a persistent controversy:

- supporters saw a universal rational apologetic;
- scholastic critics doubted that mysteries such as the Trinity could be strictly demonstrated;
- later inquisitorial opponents treated some propositions as heretical or presumptuous;
- modern interpreters disagree whether “necessary reasons” meant formal proof, persuasive demonstration, or the disclosure of structural coherence.

### Dialogue and conversion

Llull preferred learned disputation and language competence to uninformed preaching. This distinguishes him from some contemporary polemicists. It does not make his project religiously neutral: his goal was the elimination of what he called error through conversion to Latin Christianity.

### Mission and crusade

Llull’s position was not static. He repeatedly praised rational persuasion and voluntary conversion, yet after the fall of Acre in 1291 he also produced detailed crusade proposals. These included:

- military seizure or blockade of Muslim territories;
- a unified military order;
- economic strategy;
- recovery of the Holy Land;
- preaching after conquest;
- pressure toward Christian unity.

The documentary record therefore supports neither a simple “apostle of peaceful dialogue” portrait nor a description of him as only a military ideologue. He advocated persuasion as the desired means of belief while also endorsing coercive geopolitical conditions and armed conquest.

## Political Activity and Institutional Networks

Llull did not hold a known mature governmental office comparable to his youthful court position. His politics took the form of persistent petitioning and policy design.

### Rulers and courts approached

- James I of Aragon;
- James II of Majorca;
- James II of Aragon;
- Philip IV of France;
- Charles II of Naples;
- Frederick III of Sicily;
- Henry II of Cyprus;
- rulers or officials in Tunis and Bougie;
- successive popes from the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries.

### Institutions

- royal court of Majorca;
- Miramar missionary school;
- Franciscan houses and networks;
- Carthusian house at Paris where the *Vita* was dictated;
- University of Paris milieu;
- papal court;
- Council of Vienne;
- Christian merchant communities in Mediterranean ports.

### Patrons and collaborators

- **James II of Majorca:** Early royal patron of Miramar.
- **James II of Aragon:** Granted preaching authorization in royal mosques and synagogues.
- **Frederick III of Sicily:** Target and temporary patron of late missionary plans.
- **Thomas Le Myésier:** Parisian disciple who preserved and systematized Llull’s work, assembling the *Electorium* and illustrated *Breviculum*.
- **Genoese and Pisan supporters:** Assisted travel, protection, copying, and reception, though individual identities are inconsistently recorded.
- **Franciscan communities:** Supplied institutional homes and audiences.

### Opponents

During Llull’s lifetime his primary intellectual targets were:

- Muslim and Jewish religious doctrines;
- Latin Averroists;
- clerical complacency and corruption;
- rulers unwilling to finance mission and reform.

His most consequential named enemy, **Nicolau Eymerich**, lived after Llull and constructed a major anti-Llullian inquisitorial campaign.

---

## Finances, Property, and Material Support

No complete estate inventory or balance sheet survives in the sources consulted.

What can be reconstructed:

- Llull was born into a property-owning settler family.
- Marriage linked him to another established Mallorcan family.
- He disposed of assets after conversion but reserved maintenance for his wife and children.
- He could purchase an enslaved teacher.
- His decades of travel, manuscript production, and lobbying required patronage, hospitality, scribal labor, and merchant transport.
- Miramar depended on royal and papal support.
- Genoese, Pisan, royal, Franciscan, and other religious networks provided intermittent resources.
- He repeatedly lacked the institutional funding necessary for his larger plans.
- No credible evidence identifies commercial companies, patents, investment partnerships, hidden wealth, fraud, or corrupt financial structures.

His diagrams and methods were textual technologies, not patented inventions. Statements calling him an inventor of “the first computer” are retrospective analogies.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Blanca Picany

Wife and mother of his children. The surviving record is centered on Ramon, leaving her experience largely invisible. Her reported effort to protect family assets indicates conflict between Llull’s renunciation and household obligations.

### The unnamed Muslim teacher

Teacher, enslaved dependent, victim of Llull’s blow, alleged attacker, prisoner, and suicide. This is one of the most consequential relationships in Llull’s formation and one of the least recoverable.

### Ramon de Penyafort

Reported mentor or adviser at the decisive moment after conversion.

### James II of Majorca

Court connection and patron of Miramar. Llull’s relationship to the Majorcan dynasty supplied political access but did not guarantee sustained implementation of his program.

### Thomas Le Myésier

The most important early disciple and compiler. His *Breviculum* preserved an illustrated version of Llull’s life, including conversion, illumination, missions, and presentations to rulers. It is invaluable iconography but also advocacy.

### Muslim interlocutors

The *Vita* and disputation texts describe learned Muslims in Tunis and Bougie. Their names and independent accounts generally do not survive. “Homer the Saracen” likely represents ʿUmar or a literary composite.

### Parisian masters

Llull sought certification from university theologians. Their reception ranged from skepticism to qualified approval. He was an outsider to the degree system but persistent within its institutional orbit.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Critical Questions

### Slaveholding and violence

**Documented through Llull’s own early biography:** Llull purchased and owned a Muslim man, struck him after alleged blasphemy, survived the man’s retaliatory sword attack, and had him imprisoned before the man’s suicide.

The *Vita* is the sole narrative witness. Its providential framing should not erase the underlying relations of enslavement, religious domination, violence, and confinement.

### Conversion under unequal power

Llull advocated rational disputation, but his mission operated within conquest societies. Royal permission to preach in mosques and synagogues did not create an equal forum. His aim was not pluralist coexistence but conversion and eventual worldwide Latin-Christian unity.

### Crusading policy

**Documented fact:** Llull wrote crusade treatises and proposed military strategies in *Tractatus de modo convertendi infideles*, *Quomodo Terra Sancta recuperari potest*, *Liber de fine*, and *Liber de acquisitione Terrae Sanctae*.

Interpretations differ:

- some scholars emphasize a transition from peaceful mission to crusade;
- others argue that persuasion and military planning coexisted within one program;
- apologetic portrayals sometimes omit the military texts;
- hostile portrayals sometimes omit his insistence that genuine belief required rational persuasion rather than forced verbal profession.

### Anti-Islamic and anti-Jewish polemic

Llull’s writings contain arguments intended to refute Islam and Judaism and demonstrate Christian superiority. Some works represent non-Christians as rational interlocutors; others reproduce polemical constructions and support political subordination or conversionary pressure.

His engagement with Islam was deeper than that of many Latin contemporaries because he learned Arabic and used concepts intelligible in an Arabic philosophical-theological setting. This competence does not cancel the anti-Islamic purpose.

### Gender and misogyny

Llull used conventional medieval gender hierarchies and female moral types. Scholarship on *Llibre de meravelles* identifies models of womanhood organized around sin, marriage, maternity, chastity, and sanctity.

He also addressed works to women, depicted women as capable of learning and transmitting the Art, and created forceful female characters. Neither fact erases the other. No modern egalitarian doctrine of gender can be attributed to him.

### Desire for martyrdom and psychological interpretation

Llull repeatedly expressed a desire to die while converting non-Christians, undertook dangerous missions in old age, and described despair when his plans failed.

**Documented fact:** The desire and behavior appear in his writings.

**Unresolved interpretation:** Historians variously describe ascetic vocation, conventional martyr spirituality, obsessive commitment, political performance, or psychological crisis. There is insufficient evidence for a clinical diagnosis.

### Heresy accusations and the Eymerich controversy

After Llull’s death, Dominican inquisitor **Nicolau Eymerich** assembled approximately 200 allegedly erroneous propositions from about twenty works. Papal letters in the 1370s ordered examination of Llullian writings.

A document known as *Conservationi puritatis*, dated 25 January 1376 and attributed to Gregory XI, was said to condemn works and propositions.

**Disputed documentary status:**

- Eymerich and anti-Llullists treated the condemnation as authentic.
- Llullists argued that propositions were distorted, absent from authentic texts, or extracted from apocrypha.
- A 1386 commission found that several examined propositions did not occur in the cited works.
- A 1419 judgment favored the Llullist defense.
- The supposed 1376 letter is absent from Gregory XI’s papal registers and has features inconsistent with normal curial form; significant modern scholarship considers it apocryphal or seriously compromised.
- Other papal orders to examine Llullian texts were genuine, so the entire controversy cannot be reduced to one forged document.

This was a posthumous doctrinal investigation, not a trial of Llull during his lifetime.

### Alchemy and false authorship

A large alchemical and magical corpus circulated under Llull’s name, including:

- *Testamentum*;
- *Codicillus*;
- *Liber de secretis naturae seu de quinta essentia*;
- *Lapidarium* and numerous recipes or compilations.

**Scholarly finding:** None of the traditional alchemical works is plausibly authentic. The earliest core postdates Llull, probably beginning around 1332. Authentic works contain skeptical or negative remarks about alchemy.

The attribution transformed Llull into an alchemist who could make gold, distill alcohol, produce medicines, or reveal occult secrets. The pseudo-Llullian corpus nevertheless had real historical influence in medicine, distillation, alchemy, and early modern encyclopedism.

### Fraud, lawsuits, criminality, and current allegations

The dedicated adversarial search combined Llull’s name with controversy, scandal, fraud, lawsuit, corruption, accusations, conspiracy, criticism, slavery, crusade, misogyny, and heresy, including 2024–2026 results.

No credible evidence was found that the historical Ramon Llull participated in financial fraud, corruption, money laundering, a commercial lawsuit, organized crime, or intelligence activity in the modern sense. Current litigation involving organizations bearing “Ramon Llull” in their names is unrelated to the medieval person and has therefore been excluded.

The major substantiated adverse or contested areas are slaveholding, interpersonal violence in the teacher episode, conversionary coercion, anti-Islamic and anti-Jewish polemic, crusade advocacy, medieval gender hierarchy, posthumous heresy accusations, and false alchemical attribution.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### The repeated crucifixion apparitions

- **Story:** Christ appeared five times while Llull composed an adulterous love song.
- **Source:** *Vita coaetanea*, dictated in 1311.
- **Label:** Self-reported vision framed by hagiographical convention.
- **Evidence:** Llull’s own retrospective account; no independent witness.
- **Effect:** Established the canonical division between libertine courtier and missionary penitent.

### Illumination at Randa

- **Story:** God instantaneously revealed the structure of the Art.
- **Source:** *Vita* and subsequent Llullian tradition.
- **Label:** Religious claim.
- **Evidence:** The system’s appearance in works after the reported retreat; supernatural causation is not historically testable.
- **Effect:** Produced the title *Doctor Illuminatus* and made Randa a symbolic Llullian site.

### The “licentious troubadour”

- **Story:** Llull was a notorious adulterer and libertine before conversion.
- **Source:** His own generalized penitential language, expanded by later biographies.
- **Label:** Partly self-reported fact, partly hagiographical amplification.
- **Evidence:** Court service, marriage, love-song episode, and confession of worldly sexuality; no independently documented catalogue of affairs.
- **Effect:** Sharpened the dramatic conversion narrative.

### Martyrdom by stoning

- **Story:** Muslims at Bougie stoned Llull; he died aboard a rescuing ship or upon reaching Mallorca.
- **Appearance:** Later medieval and early modern hagiography; not the earliest secure final-life documentation.
- **Label:** Legend; violent death unresolved.
- **Evidence against certainty:** Last dated works are from Tunis; no contemporary stoning record; contradictory death locations.
- **Effect:** Supported local sainthood, iconography with stones, and the designation “martyr.”

### Llull the alchemist

- **Story:** Llull learned transmutation, manufactured gold—sometimes for an English king—and discovered purified alcohol or the quintessence.
- **Appearance:** Pseudo-Llullian works from the fourteenth century onward.
- **Circulators:** Anonymous alchemists, manuscript compilers, printers, occultists, and later popular historians.
- **Label:** False attribution and legend.
- **Evidence against:** Texts postdate him; conflict with authentic works; linguistic and doctrinal discrepancies.
- **Effect:** Made “Raymond Lully” a major authority in European alchemical culture independently of the historical author.

### Llull as inventor of the computer or artificial intelligence

- **Story:** His rotating combinatorial wheels were the first computer, algorithm, programming language, or AI.
- **Label:** Modern analogy, sometimes promotional myth.
- **Evidence:** The Art did employ finite symbolic alphabets, combinations, diagrams, and procedural rules.
- **Limit:** It had no automated calculating mechanism, stored program, binary electronics, or empirical learning. Its symbols carried metaphysical and theological meanings.
- **Effect:** Llull is frequently incorporated into genealogies of computation and knowledge representation.

### Llull as inventor of modern voting systems

Lost and recovered works on elections contain procedures resembling later pairwise and preference-counting methods associated with Borda and Condorcet.

- **Label:** Documented mathematical-historical resemblance; priority claims require qualification.
- **Limit:** Llull’s procedures arose in medieval ecclesiastical election and were not directly transmitted into the eighteenth-century systems merely because they resemble them.

### Llull as a modern pluralist

- **Story:** Llull pioneered religious tolerance and equal interfaith dialogue.
- **Label:** Selective modern reinterpretation.
- **Evidence for:** Arabic study, reasoned dialogue, serious presentation of Jewish and Muslim positions, preference for intellectual persuasion.
- **Evidence against:** Declared conversionary objective, royal preaching authority over minority communities, polemics, and crusade proposals.
- **Effect:** Useful symbol of Mediterranean dialogue, but incomplete as literal biography.

### Llull as exclusively a fanatic or proto-colonialist

- **Story:** Llull’s entire project was simply ideological preparation for Christian conquest.
- **Label:** Reductionist interpretation.
- **Evidence for concern:** His crusade treatises, conversion program, and participation in a conquest society.
- **Evidence against completeness:** His engagement with Arabic thought, opposition to ignorant preaching, insistence on rational argument, and complex literary presentation of non-Christians.
- **Effect:** Corrects sanitized commemoration when carefully documented; distorts when it suppresses contradictory evidence.

## Works by Ramon Llull

### Corpus and authorship

Modern catalogues recognize approximately **260 authentic works**, depending on how versions, fragments, and related recensions are counted. Llull wrote in Catalan, Latin, and Arabic; many works circulated in more than one linguistic version, sometimes produced through collaborators. “Original language” can therefore be difficult to determine.

The authoritative working inventory is the **Ramon Llull Database**. The continuing Latin critical edition is *Raimundi Lulli Opera Latina* (ROL), projected at roughly fifty-five volumes in the *Corpus Christianorum, Continuatio Mediaevalis*. Catalan works appear principally in *Obres de Ramon Llull* (ORL) and *Nova Edició de les Obres de Ramon Llull* (NEORL).

- *Clavicula*;
- numerous *Lapidaria*, tincture recipes, and “natural magic” texts.

The *Breviculum* is also not authored by Llull: it was compiled by disciple Thomas Le Myésier from Llullian materials.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Fourteenth and fifteenth centuries

Llull’s disciples copied, abridged, diagrammed, and taught his works. Thomas Le Myésier constructed a curated visual and textual legacy. Schools of Lullism appeared in France, Catalonia, Valencia, Mallorca, and Italy.

The Eymerich controversy associated Llullism with suspected heresy, while royal and municipal defenders turned Llull into a symbol of regional learning and orthodoxy.

**Nicholas of Cusa** owned, copied, excerpted, and adapted Llullian material. His reception was selective and sometimes did not name Llull explicitly.

### Renaissance and early modern Lullism

Llullian techniques entered:

- logic and encyclopedism;
- rhetoric and universal method;
- memory arts;
- alchemy through false attributions;
- missionary theory;
- projects for a universal language or calculus.

**Giordano Bruno** wrote explicitly Llullian works and transformed the combinatory figures into instruments of memory, cosmology, and invention.

**Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa** engaged Llullian arts and pseudo-Llullian currents.

**Athanasius Kircher** produced *Ars magna sciendi* in a tradition consciously connected with Llull.

**Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz** cited Llullian and Llullist projects in developing his youthful *De arte combinatoria*. The relationship is documented intellectual reception, not a simple direct line from Llull’s wheels to modern computing.

### Modern Catalan and Mallorcan reception

Llull became:

- a foundational author of Catalan literary prose;
- a symbol of Catalan linguistic capacity in philosophy and science;
- a Mallorcan civic and religious patron;
- an object of Catholic apologetic biography;
- a figure in modern comparative religion;
- a claimed ancestor of formal logic, computer science, and artificial intelligence.

His use of Catalan for philosophy, theology, narrative, and technical subjects was genuinely innovative. “Founder of Catalan” is too broad—the language and literature predated him—but he decisively expanded its learned prose.

### Twentieth- and twenty-first-century reinterpretation

Modern philology separated authentic Llull from pseudo-Llullian alchemy. Critical editions reconstructed the chronology and linguistic versions. Current scholarship gives increased attention to:

- Arabic sources and Islamicate contexts;
- the unnamed enslaved teacher;
- coercion and conquest in Mallorca;
- the tension between dialogue and crusade;
- women’s participation in Llullian transmission;
- manuscript networks rather than heroic individual genius;
- the difference between combinatorial logic and modern computation.

### Canonical and symbolic afterlives

Institutions bearing his name include the Institut Ramon Llull, Universitat Ramon Llull, prizes, schools, streets, and cultural foundations. Their contemporary controversies do not constitute controversies involving the historical Llull.

## Chronology

- **1229:** James I conquers Mallorca; Llull’s Barcelona family acquires a place in the new Christian settler society.
- **1232 or 1233:** Ramon Llull born in Palma.
- **Approximately 1240s–1250s:** Receives courtly and practical education; enters the household surrounding the future James II of Majorca.
- **Approximately 1257:** Marries Blanca Picany; later father of Domènec and Magdalena.
- **Approximately 1263:** Experiences repeated visions of the crucified Christ while composing a love song; begins “conversion to penitence.”
- **Approximately 1265:** Pilgrimages to Rocamadour and Santiago; consults Ramon de Penyafort; begins years of private study in Mallorca.
- **Approximately 1265–1274:** Learns Arabic from an enslaved Muslim teacher; violent altercation, imprisonment, and teacher’s suicide reported.
- **1271–1274:** Composes early logic and *Llibre de contemplació*.
- **Approximately 1274:** Illumination at Mount Randa; formulates first Art.
- **1275–1276:** Miramar missionary-language school established with royal and papal support.
- **Late 1270s–1280s:** Writes *Blaquerna*, *Llibre d’amic e amat*, *Llibre de l’orde de cavalleria*, and related works.
- **Approximately 1287:** Travels to Rome; planned papal appeal frustrated by Honorius IV’s death.
- **1287–1289:** First major Paris stay; lectures on the Art; receives criticism and begins simplifying it.
- **1288–1289:** Composes *Llibre de meravelles* and *Llibre de les bèsties*.
- **1290:** Ternary phase of the Art begins.
- **1291:** Fall of Acre; crusade planning becomes increasingly prominent in Llull’s program.
- **1293:** First mission to Tunis; debates, imprisonment, and expulsion.
- **1294–1295:** Naples and papal court; petitions Celestine V and Boniface VIII.
- **1295–1296:** Writes *Arbre de ciència* and *Desconhort*.
- **1297–1299:** Paris; further teaching, writing, and anti-Averroist engagement.
- **1299:** In Barcelona; James II of Aragon authorizes preaching in synagogues and mosques.
- **1300–1301:** Mallorca; writes *Cant de Ramon*.
- **1301–1302:** Travels to Cyprus and Armenian Cilicia; alleged poisoning and grave illness.
- **1303–1305:** Genoa and Montpellier; writes major systematic and crusade works.
- **1305:** *Liber de fine* and *Liber de ascensu et descensu intellectus*.
- **1307:** Mission to Bougie; public preaching, arrest, imprisonment, and expulsion.
- **1307–1308:** Shipwreck near Pisa; survives and reconstructs lost work.
- **1308:** Completes *Ars generalis ultima*, *Ars brevis*, and the dispute with “Homer the Saracen.”
- **1309:** Writes *Liber de acquisitione Terrae Sanctae*.
- **1309–1311:** Paris; anti-Averroist campaign and improved academic reception.
- **1311:** Dictates *Vita coaetanea*; writes *Raimundus phantasticus*.
- **1311–1312:** Lobbies Council of Vienne.
- **1312:** Council orders chairs in Arabic, Hebrew, and Chaldean at five centers.
- **1312–1313:** Mallorca; sermon and preaching cycle.
- **1313–1314:** Messina; seeks Frederick III’s support.
- **1314–December 1315:** Final mission to Tunis; composes last dated works.
- **After December 1315, before 25 March 1316:** Dies at an unknown location and from an unknown cause.
- **Later fourteenth century:** Martyr cult and pseudo-Llullian alchemical corpus grow.
- **1370s:** Nicolau Eymerich promotes posthumous heresy accusations; papal examinations and disputed condemnation.
- **1386:** Commission finds selected condemned propositions absent from the cited Llullian works.
- **1419:** Pro-Llullian judgment challenges Eymerich’s dossier.
- **Fifteenth century:** Nicholas of Cusa reads and adapts Llull.
- **Sixteenth century:** Bruno, Agrippa, and other Renaissance authors transform Lullism.
- **1669:** Kircher publishes *Ars magna sciendi*.
- **1660s–1670s:** Leibniz develops an *ars combinatoria* partly in dialogue with the Llullian tradition.
- **Nineteenth–twentieth centuries:** Philology decisively separates authentic works from pseudo-Llullian alchemy.
- **1937:** Borges publishes “La máquina de pensar de Raimundo Lulio.”
- **1959 onward:** Critical Latin edition, *Raimundi Lulli Opera Latina*, develops.
- **1985:** Anthony Bonner publishes the two-volume English *Selected Works*.
- **2002 onward:** Ramon Llull Database becomes available online and continues expanding.
- **2015–2016:** Seven-hundredth-anniversary commemorations renew international interest.
- **2022:** José Bellver publishes a major reassessment of Llull’s enslaved Muslim teacher.
- **Present:** Llull remains studied as a Catalan writer, missionary, philosopher, logician, crusade theorist, interreligious polemicist, and subject of extensive posthumous myth.
