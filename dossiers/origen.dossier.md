# Origen: Research Dossier

## Scope, method, and evidentiary cautions

This dossier reconstructs the life, work, controversies, and reception of Origen of Alexandria from the surviving evidence. The evidentiary base is unusually uneven. Almost all connected biographical narrative comes from Eusebius of Caesarea, *Ecclesiastical History* book 6, written roughly sixty to eighty years after Origen's death by an author who admired him, inherited the Caesarean scholarly tradition associated with him, used Origen's correspondence, and knew people connected to his circle. Eusebius is indispensable but not neutral. Gregory Thaumaturgus's contemporary *Address of Thanksgiving to Origen* supplies an admiring pupil's account of Origen's Caesarean teaching. Porphyry supplies a hostile pagan witness, preserved by Eusebius. Later Christian witnesses—Pamphilus, Methodius, Eustathius, Epiphanius, Jerome, Rufinus, Theophilus of Alexandria, Justinian, and the conciliar traditions—write within battles over Origen's legacy. Their statements are evidence both for Origen and for the later construction of “Origenism.”

Dates before the securely datable events of 202, 231/232, 235–238, 248, and 250–251 are often approximate. The original Greek of many writings has disappeared. Some works survive only in Latin translations, especially those of Rufinus, who openly says that he corrected passages he believed had been interpolated or corrupted; Jerome's rival, more literal translation of *On First Principles* is lost. Consequently, a claim attributed to Origen may rest on: (1) extant Greek; (2) a Latin translation; (3) a hostile quotation; (4) a later summary; or (5) a doctrine of fourth- or sixth-century “Origenists.” These categories are kept distinct below.

## Family and Ancestry

### Father: Leonides

Origen's father is named **Leonides** (also Leonidas in modern forms). Eusebius describes him as a Christian who gave his son both scriptural training and the ordinary Greek liberal education. He required Origen to memorize and recite scripture daily before or alongside Greek studies. Leonides reportedly delighted privately in the boy's penetrating questions, even when he publicly told him not to look beyond the literal sense appropriate to his age.

Leonides was arrested during the persecution associated with Septimius Severus and executed by beheading in **202 CE**. His property was confiscated to the imperial treasury. Eusebius's chronology—Origen “not quite seventeen” in 202—is the principal basis for dating the birth to about 185. Leonides is therefore both a documented family member and the central figure in Origen's earliest martyr mythology.

### Mother and siblings

Origen's mother is not named in any surviving early source. Eusebius says that after Leonides' death she was left with Origen and **six younger sons**. None of the brothers is securely named; no later careers can be assigned to them. The household's confiscated property left the family without necessities.

The story that Origen's mother hid all his clothes to prevent the adolescent from leaving the house and presenting himself for martyrdom appears in Eusebius. It is not independently corroborated, but Eusebius says he used letters and testimony of persons who had known Origen. It should be classified as **early biographical tradition**, not demonstrable fact and not merely medieval legend.

### Ancestry and social standing

No grandparents, clan, legal status, or ancestral place is known. The combination of a substantial Greek education, a private library, and property large enough to be confiscated suggests a literate household of some means before 202, but its exact economic class cannot be recovered. The name “born of Horus” may point to Egyptian naming culture; it cannot bear the weight of a genealogical conclusion.

## Childhood and Early Life, c. 185–203

Origen was born in Alexandria, one of the Roman Empire's largest cities and a center of Greek literary scholarship, Jewish scriptural interpretation, Christian teaching, philosophical schools, book production, and religious competition. Eusebius presents him as Christian from childhood. Porphyry's later taunt that Origen was “a Greek educated in Greek doctrines” but lived according to Christian law confirms the depth of his Greek formation, while disputing its religious use.

Leonides trained him in scripture and Greek letters. Eusebius's story of Leonides uncovering and kissing the sleeping child's chest because the divine spirit dwelt there is **paternal hagiographic anecdote**, preserved only by the admiring biographer. It helped portray Origen as marked from childhood.

When Leonides was imprisoned, Origen wanted to join him. After his mother blocked his departure, he wrote his father an exhortation: “Take care not to change your mind on our account.” The sentence is the first fragment attributed to Origen. Leonides was executed and the family's property confiscated.

For a time Origen entered the household of an unnamed wealthy Alexandrian woman. Eusebius says she treated him like an adopted son and also patronized a celebrated Antiochene teacher named **Paul**, whom Eusebius calls a heretic. Origen accepted the woman's support but refused to join Paul's prayers. The episode establishes dependence on female patronage, exposure to rival Christian teaching, and the porous intellectual environment of Alexandria; it does not establish that Origen became a member of Paul's sect.

To support himself, he taught grammar and Greek literature. Eusebius says that he disposed of his collection of classical books in exchange for a daily allowance of four obols for several years. Whether this was a sale, an annuity, or a literary patronage arrangement is unclear. It was a small but regular income and fits his early program of radical austerity.

## Education and Formation

### Scriptural and Christian formation

Origen's first teacher was Leonides. Eusebius also names **Clement of Alexandria** as a teacher when Origen was a boy, although the character of their contact is uncertain and some modern historians doubt a formal master-pupil succession. **Pantaenus**, Clement's predecessor, influenced the Alexandrian model of Christian engagement with Greek learning; Origen later said that he followed Pantaenus's example. The famous “Catechetical School of Alexandria” should not be imagined automatically as a later university with continuous offices. The sources support teaching activity under episcopal oversight, but the institutional form and a neat Pantaenus–Clement–Origen succession may be Eusebian reconstruction.

### Greek paideia and philosophy

His education included grammar, literature, rhetoric, logic, arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, and philosophy. He later explained that philosophically trained heretics and pagans came to him, forcing him to study their doctrines. He wore the philosopher's cloak and constructed teaching as a way of life rather than merely a set of propositions.

Origen says that he attended an unnamed teacher of philosophy with whom Heraclas had already studied for five years. Eusebius calls this teacher **Ammonius**. Most modern discussions identify him with **Ammonius Saccas**, later teacher of Plotinus, but the identification is disputed. Ancient evidence also knows another learned Ammonius, and neither Eusebius nor Porphyry unambiguously says “Ammonius Saccas” in the relevant passage. A related dispute asks whether the Christian Origen was the same man as a Platonist Origen remembered by Porphyry and Longinus. Chronological difficulties—especially works assigned to the philosophical Origen after the Christian's death—have led many scholars to posit two men. Other scholars argue that the separation imposes a modern boundary between Christian and philosophical schools and that Porphyry's chronology is faulty. **Unresolved question:** Christian Origen's philosophical training is certain; identity with Plotinus's colleague is not.

His major philosophical resources included Plato and Middle Platonist traditions, Philo of Alexandria, Stoic logic and ethics, Aristotle and later Peripatetic interpretation, and probably Numenius. His attitude was appropriative and competitive: Greek learning could be “spoiled from the Egyptians” and used in biblical study, but Moses, the prophets, Jesus, and Paul remained superior authorities.

### Hebrew and Jewish learning

Eusebius says that Origen learned Hebrew, acquired Hebrew biblical manuscripts, and consulted versions by Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion, and otherwise unknown translators. Origen refers to a teacher called “the Hebrew” and repeatedly consults Jews about language and interpretation. At Caesarea he operated in contact and controversy with a strong Jewish scholarly community. These relationships coexisted with supersessionist claims and anti-Jewish polemic. He could respect Jewish philology, submit interpretations to learned Jewish interlocutors, and simultaneously argue that the Church had inherited Israel's scriptures and promises.

## Early Career in Alexandria, c. 203–216

Eusebius dates Origen's assumption of catechetical teaching to his eighteenth year. Persecution had dispersed established instructors; pagans came to the young grammar teacher seeking Christian teaching. Bishop **Demetrius of Alexandria** eventually entrusted or confirmed the work to him.

The school produced martyrs: **Plutarch**, **Serenus**, **Heraclides**, **Hero**, a second **Serenus**, and **Herais** are named by Eusebius. Origen visited prisoners, attended condemned Christians to execution, and publicly greeted martyrs, reportedly risking mob violence and official attention. These episodes established a life-long bond between teaching, ascetic discipline, and martyr witness.

He lived with deliberate severity: fasting, curtailed sleep, sleeping on the floor, going barefoot, avoiding wine and nonessential food, and refusing property offered by friends. Eusebius says this regimen endangered his health. It is probable that the ascetic pattern is factual in outline; its presentation also conforms to the ancient “philosophical life.”

As enrollment expanded, Origen delegated elementary teaching to **Heraclas**, keeping advanced students for himself. His advanced curriculum moved from preparatory sciences through the schools of philosophy to scripture. He did not require allegiance to one Greek school; Gregory later praised him for exposing pupils to all systems except atheistic ones and training them to test arguments.

## Chronological Life History

### c. 206–213: alleged self-castration

Eusebius places the deed while Origen was still a young catechist teaching both men and women. According to him, Origen read Matthew 19:12 literally and made himself a eunuch both to obey the saying and to prevent suspicions about teaching women. He kept it secret; Demetrius initially admired the zeal but later publicized it against him.

The evidence does **not** permit “Origen certainly castrated himself” or “the story was certainly invented.” Eusebius is the earliest explicit witness and was an admirer with access to the Caesarean archive; his embarrassed criticism can be read as evidence that he was reporting a notorious fact. Against that stands Origen's silence and his later *Commentary on Matthew*, which rejects literal genital mutilation as an irrational reading. Epiphanius could not verify the story. Scholars including Joseph Trigg have accepted it and interpreted the later commentary as mature repudiation; Henry Chadwick and John McGuckin have treated malicious gossip or Eusebian credulity as plausible. The correct classification is **ancient report, plausible but disputed**.

### c. 212–215: journey to Rome

During the episcopate of Zephyrinus (c. 199–217), Origen traveled to Rome because, in his own quoted words, he wished “to see the very ancient Church of the Romans.” He stayed briefly and returned to Alexandria. Hippolytus may have been active in Rome, but a personal meeting is not documented.

### c. 214–216: first Arabian summons

The governor of Arabia sent a soldier with letters to Demetrius and the prefect of Egypt requesting Origen for an interview. Origen went, completed the unidentified purpose, and returned. The official summons demonstrates that his reputation extended beyond ecclesiastical circles. The governor's identity and subject of the interview are unknown.

### 215/216–217: Caracalla's violence and refuge in Caesarea

Eusebius says that a serious conflict in Alexandria made Egypt unsafe and Origen went to Caesarea in Palestine. This is usually connected to Emperor Caracalla's punitive massacre or expulsion of Alexandrian youths in 215/216, although Eusebius does not name Caracalla in the passage. In Caesarea, bishops **Theoctistus of Caesarea** and **Alexander of Jerusalem** invited the layman to expound scripture publicly before bishops.

Demetrius protested that a layman preaching before bishops was unprecedented. Alexander and Theoctistus replied with counterexamples from Laranda, Iconium, and Synnada. Demetrius recalled Origen through deacons; Origen returned to Alexandria and resumed teaching. The quarrel reveals competing jurisdictions and developing clerical norms, not merely personal jealousy.

### c. 218–222: imperial interview at Antioch

**Julia Avita Mamaea**, mother of Emperor Severus Alexander, heard of Origen and summoned him while she was at Antioch. She sent a military escort. Origen stayed with her and explained Christian teaching, then returned to Alexandria. Eusebius calls her pious but does not say she converted. Claims that Origen served as imperial adviser, intelligence operative, or court theologian have no evidence.

### c. 218–230: Ambrose, the publishing establishment, and the Alexandrian corpus

**Ambrose**, originally associated according to different readings with Valentinianism or Marcionism, became Origen's student, friend, patron, and literary “taskmaster.” Ambrose financed more than seven rotating shorthand writers, at least as many copyists, and female calligraphers. This was a substantial private publication apparatus. It converted oral exposition and dictation into a vast corpus and underwrote the material costs of papyrus/parchment, labor, and copying.

In this period Origen began the *Commentary on John* against the Valentinian exegete Heracleon, commentaries on Genesis, Psalms, and Lamentations, two books *On the Resurrection*, ten books of *Stromateis* or *Miscellanies*, and the four books *On First Principles* (*Peri Archōn*). He also began or intensified the **Hexapla**, the enormous columnar comparison of Hebrew and Greek biblical texts.

### c. 230–232: ordination crisis and permanent move

Sent toward Greece on church business, Origen passed through Palestine and was ordained presbyter at Caesarea by Theoctistus and allied bishops, including Alexander. Demetrius regarded the foreign ordination as insubordination. Later accounts speak of two Alexandrian synods: one removed Origen from teaching and expelled him from Alexandria; another deposed him from the presbyterate. Surviving documentation is fragmentary, and Eusebius alludes to the controversy without preserving the decrees.

Reasons given or inferred include jurisdiction, the propriety of ordaining a self-mutilated man if the castration report was true, suspicion of speculative teaching, and rivalry between an increasingly powerful Alexandrian episcopate and a transregional celebrity. Eusebius attributes Demetrius's action to jealousy; that is a partisan interpretation. Palestine, Arabia, Phoenicia, and Achaea continued to recognize Origen; Rome reportedly accepted the Alexandrian action. Origen settled permanently at Caesarea around **231/232**. Demetrius died soon afterward; Heraclas succeeded him and did not restore Origen.

Origen defended his teaching in letters, including a lost letter to friends in Alexandria and letters to bishops such as Fabian of Rome. A hostile report that he taught the devil's salvation appears to have circulated already. Origen denied at least the crude proposition that the devil would attain blessedness, while his broader restoration theology remained capable of different readings.

### 232–235: school at Caesarea and mature teaching

At Caesarea he formed a new school integrated with the local church but intellectually wide in scope. Gregory Thaumaturgus and his brother **Athenodorus**, originally headed toward legal studies at Berytus, remained about five years. Gregory's farewell address describes Origen's method: personal moral diagnosis, dialectic, natural philosophy, ethics, theology, comparative reading of philosophical schools, and finally scripture. Gregory says Origen persuaded and emotionally bound pupils to the philosophical life; his rhetoric of being caught in a net registers both intense admiration and Origen's forceful pedagogical charisma.

Other pupils and associates included **Firmilian of Caesarea in Cappadocia**, who invited Origen to Cappadocia and also visited him; **Dionysius of Alexandria**, an earlier pupil; and numerous clergy from Palestine, Arabia, and Pontus. The Caesarean school and library became the institutional seed of the later Pamphilus–Eusebius scholarly establishment.

### 235–238: persecution of Maximinus Thrax

Maximinus targeted leaders associated with the household of Severus Alexander. Origen wrote *Exhortation to Martyrdom* for Ambrose and the presbyter **Protoctetus**, who had suffered in the persecution. Origen's own location during part of the crisis is uncertain; later reconstructions place him in concealment or Cappadocia, but direct evidence is limited. The work insists that idolatrous compromise cannot be reduced to inward reservation and treats martyrdom as public witness and sacrificial participation in Christ.

### c. 238–244: travels, textual work, and ecclesiastical arbitration

Origen traveled to Athens, where he completed the *Commentary on Ezekiel* and began the *Commentary on the Song of Songs*, later completed at Caesarea in ten books. A stay or preaching engagement at Tarsus is implied by Jerome's catalogue but otherwise unattested.

He corresponded with **Julius Africanus** over the story of Susanna. Africanus argued from Hebrew absence and Greek wordplay that it was not original to Daniel; Origen answered at length, defending ecclesiastical use while engaging the textual problem. This exchange is one of antiquity's clearest surviving Christian debates over textual canonicity.

At Bostra in Arabia, bishops summoned Origen to examine **Beryllus**, who taught that Christ did not possess a distinct pre-existent divinity before the incarnation. A recorded disputation or synodal transcript once survived. Eusebius says Origen clarified Beryllus's real position and persuaded him to recant; Beryllus later thanked him. The episode shows Origen functioning as doctrinal examiner without episcopal rank.

At another Arabian synod, he argued against people who held that the soul dies with the body and is re-created at resurrection. Eusebius says the assembly changed its view. In a public homily he also attacked the recently arrived **Elkesaites**, who rejected parts of scripture and permitted verbal denial under persecution.

The *Dialogue with Heraclides*, preserved in the Tura papyri and generally dated c. 244–249, records a synod-like conversation about the Father, Son, soul, and resurrection. It displays Origen questioning a bishop, defining terms, and seeking a formula the assembly could accept. Unlike later portraits of a solitary system-builder, it shows live, negotiated theology.

### c. 245–249: final literary period

At more than sixty, Origen permitted stenographers to record his public homilies. He wrote *On the Passover* and, at Ambrose's insistence, the eight books *Against Celsus* around **248**. Celsus's *True Logos* or *True Doctrine*, written around 175–180, attacked Christianity as irrational, socially disruptive, derivative from Judaism, and politically irresponsible. Origen answered sequentially, preserving extensive fragments of a work that would otherwise be lost.

His final known years included the large *Commentary on Matthew*, work on the Twelve Prophets, and numerous homilies. The twenty-nine Psalm homilies identified in Munich in 2012 are now regarded as among his last datable works and materially enlarge the Greek homiletic corpus.

Eusebius records letters to Emperor **Philip the Arab** and Philip's wife **Severa**. The contents are lost. They do not prove that Philip was a Christian. Origen's role was that of a famous religious intellectual corresponding upward, not a state officer.

### 250–251: arrest and torture under Decius

Decius's empire-wide sacrificial requirement exposed Christian leaders and communities to testing, imprisonment, and execution. Origen was arrested in Palestine. Eusebius, drawing on Origen's letters, lists chains, bodily torture, an iron collar, confinement, feet stretched four holes in the stocks for days, and threats of burning. He says the judge deliberately tried not to kill him, presumably seeking apostasy rather than martyrdom. Origen survived without recorded recantation and wrote consolatory letters afterward.

No court transcript, sentence, or libellus survives. “Imprisoned and tortured as a Christian confessor” is documented through early ecclesiastical testimony; exact procedures and duration are not independently verifiable.

### c. 253–254: death

Eusebius says Origen died under Gallus at age sixty-nine. Jerome locates his death and burial in **Tyre**. Later tradition commonly says that the Decian tortures broke his health and caused his death. This is plausible, and the interval was short, but neither Eusebius nor a medical witness states a demonstrable causal chain. The immediate cause of death is **unknown**. The traditional tomb at Tyre was shown in later centuries, but no securely identified grave survives.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

Ancient categories do not map cleanly onto companies or patents. No commercial company, landed estate, investment portfolio, patent, or personal fortune is documented.

### Alexandrian and Caesarean schools

Origen's core institution was the teacher's circle: first in Alexandria under the bishop's authority, later at Caesarea with Palestinian episcopal support. In both places he combined elementary instruction, advanced philosophy, scriptural exegesis, moral formation, and public preaching. The later Caesarean school trained bishops and supplied an intellectual network across Palestine, Cappadocia, Arabia, and Pontus.

### Ambrose's publication workshop

Ambrose financed an unusually large knowledge-production operation: rotating stenographers, copyists, and women trained in elegant script. Origen dictated rather than writing every copy by hand. This arrangement explains both the scale of production and variations caused by oral delivery, transcription, editorial revision, and translation. Ambrose's wealth—not Origen's—is the principal documented financial foundation of the corpus.

### The Hexapla as information technology

The **Hexapla** was a massive comparative biblical edition, conventionally arranged in six columns:

1. Hebrew text;
2. Hebrew transliterated into Greek letters;
3. Aquila's Greek translation;
4. Symmachus's Greek translation;
5. the Septuagint, annotated with critical signs;
6. Theodotion's Greek translation.

For some books Origen added fifth, sixth, or seventh Greek versions, producing Heptapla, Octapla, or Enneapla configurations. The **Tetrapla** contained the four Greek columns. Asterisks marked material supplied to the Septuagint; obeli marked material absent from the Hebrew or otherwise text-critically suspect. The work borrowed techniques of Alexandrian Homeric criticism and applied them to Jewish and Christian scripture. It may have occupied roughly forty or fifty codices and was not designed for ordinary ownership. It remained in the Caesarean library and survived in excerpts, marginal readings, Syriac witnesses, and fragments; an early fragment of its Greek transliteration column is preserved in the Cairo Genizah.

### Libraries and transmission

Origen assembled Hebrew and Greek texts at Alexandria and Caesarea. His books passed into the Caesarean collection cultivated by **Pamphilus** and **Eusebius**. The library enabled Eusebius's biblical scholarship and historical writing. Claims that the complete Hexapla was certainly destroyed in a specific Arab capture are conjectural; gradual loss, size, cost, selective copying, and later controversy all contributed.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Scripture and method

Origen treated scripture as a divine composition whose apparent impossibilities, moral scandals, repetitions, and discrepancies could signal deeper senses. In *On First Principles* 4 he analogized the text to the human being: **body** (literal/historical wording), **soul** (moral or ecclesial meaning), and **spirit** (Christological and heavenly mystery). He did not simply discard literal history; he argued that some passages were historical, some mixed, and some deliberately impossible at the literal level.

He produced three main exegetical forms: **scholia** (brief notes), **homilies** (church preaching), and **commentaries** (continuous scholarly analysis). His allegory developed from Paul and earlier Christian reading, Jewish exegesis, Philo, and Greek literary criticism. It became formative for patristic and medieval interpretation.

### Rule of faith and disciplined speculation

The preface to *On First Principles* distinguishes teachings handed down clearly in apostolic church proclamation from questions left open for disciplined investigation. Firm teachings include one creator God, Jesus Christ's incarnation, the Holy Spirit, rational souls, judgment, resurrection, and inspired scripture. Questions about the mode of creation, embodiment, celestial hierarchies, and final restoration could be explored hypothetically. Later readers often converted these exercises into dogmatic propositions, while defenders sometimes minimized how boldly he argued them.

### God and Trinity

Origen insisted that God is incorporeal, simple, invisible, and beyond spatial location. Anthropomorphic biblical language must be interpreted spiritually. He described Father, Son, and Holy Spirit as three **hypostases** and supplied important precursors to Nicene terminology, including an analogy of *homoousios*. He taught the Son's eternal generation: God was never without Wisdom or Logos.

At the same time, his order is hierarchical. The Father is *autotheos*, God in the primary sense; the Son derives eternally from the Father and is second; the Spirit's sphere is described more narrowly. Some passages say the Son differs in *ousia* and should not receive prayer in the same way as the Father. Later Arians and anti-Arians both appropriated Origen. The Cappadocians could use his three-hypostasis language; critics could cite subordination. Labeling him simply “Arian” is anachronistic because he died generations before Arius's controversy and also taught the Son's eternity in ways Arius's opponents could use.

### Creation, rational beings, and free will

Against Gnostic systems, Origen maintained one good creator of spiritual and material reality, creation from nothing, providence, and creaturely responsibility. Rational beings (*logika*) are free and mutable. Difference among angels, humans, and demons results not from different created substances or arbitrary predestination but from choices and divine pedagogy.

The disputed question is whether he taught a universal primordial existence of pure minds that cooled in love and fell into differently constituted bodies. Parts of *On First Principles* support pre-cosmic causes and a prior condition; hostile sources sharpen this into a complete fall-and-return system. The extant Greek does not yield a single unambiguous diagram. He rejected transmigration into animal bodies and the Stoic recurrence of identical worlds. “Pre-existence of souls” is therefore a reasonable description of one major line in his thought, but the precise temporality, universality, and bodily consequences remain disputed.

### Christ, incarnation, atonement, and resurrection

The eternal Logos truly assumed a human body and rational soul. Christ's soul alone adhered immovably to the Logos in love, becoming the instrument of incarnation. Origen opposed docetism. He used multiple atonement models: sacrifice, victory, pedagogy, healing, and ransom. The later “ransom to the devil” theory has an important Origenian formulation but does not exhaust his view.

He affirmed resurrection while refusing crude identity between resurrected body and corruptible flesh. The body's organizing form or *eidos* persists; the resurrected “spiritual body” is transformed. Methodius and later critics accused him of dissolving bodily resurrection into incorporeality. The surviving evidence supports real continuity and radical transformation, but scholars disagree whether a body remains at the final consummation.

### Sin, punishment, and apokatastasis

Punishment is medicinal and pedagogical, figured as fire that exposes and consumes sin. Freedom persists; God does not save rational creatures by abolishing agency. Origen repeatedly anticipates God becoming “all in all” and the Logos overcoming evil. This is the basis of the doctrine of **apokatastasis**, restoration.

What exactly he expected remains contested:

- **Documented:** he expects extensive postmortem purification; treats divine punishment as corrective; envisages a cosmic consummation; and refuses a metaphysical nature of evil.
- **Strong but disputed inference:** every human soul will ultimately be restored.
- **More disputed:** demons, Satan, or every rational being will attain salvation or beatitude. A surviving report says he denied the crude accusation of the devil's salvation. Other passages allow the devil's eventual submission or peace but distinguish submission from blessedness.
- **Later Origenism:** sixth-century systems of universal return, spherical resurrection bodies, abolition of material distinctions, and “equal-to-Christ” minds are not automatically Origen's own teaching.

### Prayer, sacraments, asceticism, and mysticism

*On Prayer* analyzes petition, providence, freedom, posture, direction, and the Lord's Prayer. Scripture is daily bread; Christ becomes present in the inspired word. Origen affirms baptism, Eucharist, church discipline, and ordained ministry while locating their spiritual efficacy beyond material signs. His Song of Songs exegesis presents the Church and the individual soul as bride of the Logos, creating a major vocabulary of Christian mystical desire.

His asceticism joined bodily discipline, celibacy, intellectual concentration, almsgiving, and readiness for martyrdom. He helped establish scriptural study as ascetic practice and strongly influenced later monasticism, even where monks ceased to name him.

### Politics, empire, violence, and public office

Origen held no state office and led no political party. In *Against Celsus* he rejects emperor worship, civic sacrifice, and Christian participation in killing. Christians serve the emperor through prayer as a priestly army and should not join military expeditions even under pressure. Yet he prays for a just emperor and for those who fight justly, and does not demand that the non-Christian empire instantly abolish force. His position combines Christian noncombatancy with spiritual support for public order. He interprets Israel's biblical wars spiritually for Christians and sees demons, passions, and false worship as the present battlefield.

### Judaism

Origen's relation to Jews is double and historically consequential. He sought Hebrew texts, learned from Jewish teachers, consulted Jewish readings, debated rabbis, and sometimes preserved Jewish interpretations otherwise lost. He could acknowledge Hebrew evidence against a convenient Christian reading. At the same time, he argued that Jews had failed to recognize Christ, that the Church inherited scriptural promises, and that Jerusalem's destruction signified judgment. These are supersessionist and at points anti-Jewish theological claims. The philological collaboration does not erase the polemic; the polemic does not make the collaboration fictitious.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Ecclesiastical Judgments

No evidence connects Origen to fraud, corruption, money laundering, commercial crime, intelligence services, organized political violence, or sexual abuse. The relevant controversies are bodily asceticism, clerical jurisdiction, doctrine, textual transmission, and posthumous heresy proceedings.

### Self-castration

**Status:** disputed ancient report. Eusebius states it; Origen does not; his late exegesis opposes literal mutilation; Epiphanius could not verify it. It became a biographical shorthand for excessive allegorist literalism—the paradox of the supreme allegorist committing his most famous literal reading.

### Irregular ordination and Alexandrian expulsion

**Status:** documented ecclesiastical conflict, poorly preserved procedurally. Palestinian bishops ordained him; Demetrius objected; Origen left Alexandria permanently; other churches divided over recognition. It was not a criminal trial. Claims that self-castration automatically made the ordination legally null depend on applying later or differently situated disciplinary rules to incomplete evidence.

### Alleged doctrinal errors during life

Origen defended himself against reports concerning the devil's salvation and other teachings. His debate with the Valentinian **Candidus** may have generated a distorted account: to defend free will against the assertion that the devil was evil by fixed nature, Origen argued that moral status follows choice; opponents could restate this as a promise of Satan's salvation.

### First waves of anti-Origenism

**Methodius of Olympus** attacked Origen's account of resurrection and other doctrines while also absorbing much from him. **Peter of Alexandria** and **Eustathius of Antioch** criticized aspects of Origenian exegesis and anthropology. These were posthumous theological controversies, not contemporary convictions.

### First Origenist controversy, c. 374–403

**Epiphanius of Salamis** made Origen a principal heresiological target in *Panarion* 64, charging him with errors about resurrection, creation, Trinity, souls, and allegory. In the 390s the dispute entangled **Jerome**, **Rufinus**, **John of Jerusalem**, and **Theophilus of Alexandria**. Jerome, formerly an avid user and translator of Origen, repositioned himself as a critic; Rufinus translated *On First Principles* while smoothing passages and alleging heretical interpolation. Their former friendship collapsed into public accusations of bad faith and falsification.

Theophilus initially opposed anthropomorphic conceptions of God, a position compatible with Origen. Under pressure from Egyptian monks and amid a separate conflict with the administrator Isidore and the **Tall Brothers**, he reversed course, convened an Alexandrian synod around 400, condemned Origenism, and expelled Origenist monks. The refugees appealed to **John Chrysostom** at Constantinople. Theophilus used John's reception of them in the campaign that deposed John at the Synod of the Oak in 403. Modern studies emphasize that property, episcopal authority, monastic faction, and patronage were inseparable from doctrine. The controversy does not supply a transparent list of Origen's own beliefs.

### Justinian's edict and the synod of 543

Emperor **Justinian I** sent Patriarch Mennas a theological edict against Origen, normally dated **543** (sometimes 542/544), with excerpts and nine or ten anathemas depending on enumeration. A Constantinopolitan home synod endorsed the condemnation. Targets included pre-existence, transformation of rational beings, cosmological cycles, and universal restoration. This is a **documented imperial and local-synodal condemnation**, nearly three centuries after Origen's death.

### The fifteen anathemas and Constantinople II, 553

The common statement “the Fifth Ecumenical Council issued fifteen anathemas against Origen” requires qualification.

- The **fifteen anti-Origenist anathemas** survive in Greek manuscript tradition and attack a developed sixth-century monastic Origenism, including pre-existent minds, a primordial henad, different embodiments, Christological speculations, spherical resurrection bodies, and a restoration that abolishes material distinctions.
- The surviving official Greek/Latin **acts of the council's eight sessions do not contain these fifteen anathemas** or a recorded debate on them. Modern critical editions often assign them to a pre-conciliar home synod in 553, or otherwise deny that they were formally issued in the extant council sessions.
- The council's **eleventh general anathema** against named heretics includes Origen in much of the manuscript tradition, though textual history has been debated. Origen is also treated as condemned by later sixth-century historians and councils.
- Thus two propositions can coexist: Origen was received in the imperial church as condemned in connection with Constantinople II; the famous fifteen are not securely part of the extant ecumenical acta.

The anathemas more directly fit doctrines of **Evagrius Ponticus** and sixth-century Palestinian factions, especially the **Isochristoi**, than every statement demonstrable in Origen's surviving works. They remain evidence for the history of Origen's reputation and of Origenism, not a verbatim inventory of his theology.

### Textual “falsification” dispute

Rufinus argued that heretics had interpolated Origen's books and translated him toward what Rufinus considered his consistent orthodoxy. Jerome accused Rufinus of laundering heresy and produced a literal rival version of *On First Principles*. Because the Greek is mostly lost, neither program can be fully checked. Modern editions compare Rufinus, Greek fragments, Jerome's quotations, Justinian's extracts, and the Philocalia. Any confident reconstruction must acknowledge translator intervention and hostile excerpting.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. The self-made eunuch

1. **Story:** the young Origen literally castrated himself after Matthew 19:12 so he could teach women without suspicion.
2. **Appearance:** explicit in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 6.8, early fourth century; later repeated and illustrated widely.
3. **Circulation:** Eusebius says Demetrius first praised and later publicized the deed; later polemicists, hagiographers, encyclopedists, and artists made it emblematic.
4. **Evidence:** one early connected report, no self-testimony, later inability to verify, and Origen's own condemnation of literal castration. Scholarly verdict divided.
5. **Effect:** it represented ascetic heroism to some, immature literalism to Eusebius, disqualification to opponents, and irony to modern interpreters.

### 2. The naked would-be martyr

1. **Story:** his mother stopped him from joining his condemned father only by hiding his clothes.
2. **Appearance:** Eusebius 6.2.
3. **Circulation:** Caesarean biographical tradition, then Christian histories.
4. **Evidence:** no independent witness; psychologically plausible but narratively polished.
5. **Effect:** established extreme youthful zeal and maternal preservation of a future teacher.

### 3. Leonides kissing the divine chest

1. **Story:** Leonides uncovered the sleeping boy's breast and kissed it as a shrine of the Holy Spirit.
2. **Appearance:** Eusebius 6.2.
3. **Circulation:** admiring biography and later hagiography.
4. **Evidence:** family anecdote with no corroboration.
5. **Effect:** supplied a childhood sign of election.

### 4. “Born of Horus” as proof of pagan or Coptic identity

1. **Story:** his name proves birth to pagan Egyptian parents, later conversion, or definitive Coptic ancestry.
2. **Appearance:** modern etymological inference; Epiphanius separately calls him Egyptian.
3. **Circulation:** reference works and identity-centered reception.
4. **Evidence:** etymology is credible; genealogical inference is not. Eusebius presents Christian parents.
5. **Effect:** enabled competing claims on Origen as Greek, Egyptian, or hybrid Alexandrian.

### 5. Origen promised salvation to Satan

1. **Story:** universal restoration necessarily included the devil in beatitude.
2. **Appearance:** accusations already answered in Origen's lost correspondence; repeated in later polemic.
3. **Circulation:** Alexandrian opponents, Jerome, anti-Origenist dossiers.
4. **Evidence:** Origen's restoration language is broad, but a reported letter ridicules the claim; surviving passages distinguish final subjection or cessation of enmity from beatitude. The question remains textually disputed.
5. **Effect:** became the most memorable charge against apokatastasis and a central reason admirers qualified their defense.

### 6. “The council condemned universal salvation in 553”

1. **Story:** Constantinople II straightforwardly issued the fifteen anathemas and thereby defined every universalist hope as heresy.
2. **Appearance:** later conciliar collections and confessional summaries.
3. **Circulation:** Byzantine, Catholic, Orthodox, and modern universalist controversies.
4. **Evidence:** the fifteen are absent from extant official session acts; a 543 condemnation is secure; a 553 pre-conciliar action is likely to many scholars; Origen's name occurs in the eleventh general anathema tradition. The condemned package links restoration to pre-existence and specific cosmology, not every possible doctrine of universal salvation.
5. **Effect:** fixed Origen as posthumous heresiarch while generating a continuing authenticity debate.

### 7. Origen died directly from torture

1. **Story:** Decian torture broke his body and caused death shortly afterward.
2. **Appearance:** later synthesis of Eusebius's torture narrative and death notice.
3. **Circulation:** ecclesiastical biographies and modern summaries.
4. **Evidence:** severe torture and later death are early reports; direct medical causation is not stated.
5. **Effect:** allowed defenders to call him effectively a martyr or, more precisely, a confessor who died from his wounds. Ancient formal status remained “confessor,” because he did not die during the persecution.

### 8. The grave at Tyre

1. **Story:** Origen's tomb could be seen at Tyre and marked his burial.
2. **Appearance:** Jerome locates burial there; medieval visitors knew a tomb tradition.
3. **Circulation:** local Christian memory and travel literature.
4. **Evidence:** ancient literary testimony but no securely identified archaeological remains.
5. **Effect:** gave geographical concreteness to a figure whose ecclesiastical memory was otherwise contested.

### 9. Two Origens

1. **Story:** Origen the Christian and Origen the Platonist were separate pupils of Ammonius.
2. **Appearance:** developed in early modern scholarship to reconcile Porphyry's notices and chronology.
3. **Circulation:** histories of Neoplatonism and patristics.
4. **Evidence:** post-253 works assigned to the philosophical Origen and silence about Christian writings favor two; shared teacher, intellectual network, and lack of incompatible doctrines favor one with confused chronology.
5. **Effect:** partitioned the history of philosophy from Christian intellectual history; recent scholarship has reopened the identification.

## Works by Origen

### Transmission categories

- **Extant substantially in Greek:** *Against Celsus*; *On Prayer*; *Exhortation to Martyrdom*; large portions of *Commentary on John* and *Commentary on Matthew*; *Dialogue with Heraclides*; *On the Passover*; Greek homilies on Jeremiah and the rediscovered Psalms; letters to Gregory and Julius Africanus; fragments and catenae.
- **Extant principally in Latin translation:** *On First Principles* (Rufinus); *Commentary on Romans* (Rufinus's abridged/adapted translation); many homilies on Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Joshua, Judges, Psalms, Song of Songs, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and Luke, translated by Rufinus or Jerome.
- **Fragmentary:** Hexapla; commentaries on many biblical books; *On the Resurrection*; scholia; letters; synodal dialogues.
- **Lost but anciently catalogued:** most of an output counted in hundreds of books and perhaps roughly two thousand individual treatises/homilies. Epiphanius's figure of six thousand is rhetorical and not a reliable item count.

### Text-critical and synthetic works

1. **Hexapla** — begun in Alexandria, continued for decades; multi-column Hebrew/Greek critical edition. Lost as a whole; fragments and derivative witnesses survive.
2. **Tetrapla** — four Greek versions in parallel; whether an earlier stage or derivative edition is disputed.
3. **Περὶ Ἀρχῶν / *Peri Archōn* / *De principiis* / *On First Principles*** — four books, c. 220–230. First surviving attempt at a comprehensive Christian account of God, rational creation, incarnation, freedom, judgment, and scriptural method. Mostly preserved in Rufinus's Latin; Greek fragments sometimes diverge sharply.
4. **Στρωματεῖς / *Stromateis* (“Miscellanies”)** — ten books, Alexandria; lost except fragments/notices.
5. **Περὶ ἀναστάσεως / *On the Resurrection*** — two books plus dialogues according to Jerome's catalogue; lost except hostile/secondary fragments, especially through Methodius.
6. **Μονόβιβλα / *Monobibla*** — obscure title in Jerome's catalogue; content unknown.

### Major independent treatises and dialogues

1. **Περὶ εὐχῆς / *On Prayer*** — c. 233–235; complete Greek. Prayer, freedom, providence, posture, direction, and Lord's Prayer.
2. **Εἰς μαρτύριον προτρεπτικός / *Exhortation to Martyrdom*** — 235–238; complete Greek; addressed to Ambrose and Protoctetus.
3. **Κατὰ Κέλσου / *Contra Celsum* / *Against Celsus*** — eight books, c. 248; complete Greek; preserves and answers Celsus.
4. **Περὶ πάσχα / *On the Passover*** — probably c. 245; Greek Tura papyrus; discovered in 1941.
5. **Διάλεκτος πρὸς Ἡρακλείδην / *Dialogue with Heraclides and His Fellow Bishops*** — c. 244–249; Greek stenographic record, Tura discovery.
6. **Dialogue against Candidus the Valentinian** — lost; important to the later devil-salvation accusation.
7. **On Martyrdom** — likely identical with *Exhortation to Martyrdom*.
8. **On Peace; On Fasting; On Monogamy and Trigamy; Exhortation to Pionia; homilies at Tarsus** — catalogued, lost or uncertain.

### Correspondence

Eusebius collected one hundred letters in books; Jerome catalogued nine books of Origen's letters plus synodal correspondence. Only a small fraction survives.

- **Letter to Gregory Thaumaturgus** — genuine, preserved in the Philocalia; recommends using Greek philosophy and sciences as instruments for Christianity, the “spoils of Egypt.” Date uncertain, commonly after Gregory's departure.
- **Letter to Julius Africanus** — genuine; long defense of Susanna and ecclesiastical textual tradition.
- **Letter to friends in Alexandria / defense of his writings** — fragmentary through hostile quotation; responds to falsification and doctrinal accusations.
- **Letters to Fabian of Rome and other bishops** — lost except notices; defended orthodoxy.
- **Letters to Emperor Philip and Empress Severa** — lost; Eusebius attests them.
- **Letter(s) to Beryllus** — lost; related synodal dialogue once extant.
- **Letter to Ambrose concerning publication** and many personal letters — fragmentary/lost.

### Old Testament commentaries and scholia catalogued in antiquity

Jerome's Letter 33, drawing on the Caesarean catalogue, lists: thirteen books on Genesis (Eusebius counted twelve); scholia on Exodus and Leviticus; thirty-six books and scholia on Isaiah; works on Hosea/Ephraim, Joel (2), Amos (6), Jonah (1), Micah (3), Nahum (2), Habakkuk (3), Zephaniah (2), Haggai (1), Zechariah (2 on the beginning), Malachi (2), and Ezekiel (29; Eusebius knew twenty-five); extensive books and scholia on Psalms; three books on Proverbs; scholia on Ecclesiastes; ten mature books and two youthful books on Song of Songs; five books on Lamentations; and problems in Proverbs. Most is lost or fragmentary.

### New Testament commentaries catalogued in antiquity

- **Commentary on Matthew** — twenty-five books; Greek books 10–17 survive substantially, with an anonymous Latin continuation covering later material.
- **Commentary on John** — originally at least thirty-two books; parts of books 1, 2, 6, 10, 13, 19, 20, 28, and 32 survive in Greek, with lacunae.
- **Commentary on Luke** — fifteen books, lost; homilies survive principally through Jerome.
- **Commentary on Romans** — fifteen books, known mainly through Rufinus's ten-book Latin adaptation and Greek fragments/Tura excerpts.
- Commentaries on **Galatians** (probably five books despite a corrupt catalogue figure of fifteen), **Ephesians** (three), **Philippians** (one), **Colossians** (three in manuscript evidence), **1 Thessalonians** (three), **2 Thessalonians** (one), **Titus** (one), and **Philemon** (one); fragmentary or lost.
- Commentaries or homilies on **Hebrews**, including the famous judgment that Paul's thoughts may underlie the work but “who wrote the epistle, God knows”; lost except fragments.

### Homilies

Jerome's catalogue records hundreds: Genesis (17 listed; 16 normally transmitted), Exodus (8 listed; 13 survive in Latin), Leviticus (a corrupt “II,” usually 16 surviving), Numbers (28), Deuteronomy (13), Joshua (26), Judges (9), Passover (8), 1 Kingdoms/Samuel (4), Job (22), Proverbs (7), Ecclesiastes (8), Song of Songs (2), Isaiah (32), Jeremiah (45 in the catalogue tradition, with Greek and Latin portions), Ezekiel (12), extensive Psalms, Matthew (25), Luke (39), Acts (17), Pauline epistles, and Hebrews (18).

The **twenty-nine Psalm homilies** in Munich Codex Graecus 314 were identified by Marina Molin Pradel in 2012 and authenticated through comparison with known Origenian material. They constitute the largest newly recovered Greek Origenian corpus in modern times.

### Philocalia

The **Philocalia of Origen** is not a book assembled by Origen. It is a fourth-century anthology of extracts from his works, traditionally associated with Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nazianzus. It preserves Greek passages on scripture, free will, fate, providence, and philosophical problems that would otherwise be lost. Attribution of every chapter is not equally secure; at least one dialogue on matter may belong to a different author named Maximus.

### Spurious, doubtful, or misassigned works

- Works **On Daemons** and **That the King Is the Only Maker** belong to the disputed “Platonist Origen” dossier; the latter was reportedly written under Gallienus, after Christian Origen's probable death, and should not be listed without qualification as Christian Origen's work.
- The **Philocalia** is a posthumous collection, not his own compilation.
- Catena fragments bearing Origen's name require individual authentication; chains of commentary frequently abridge, rewrite, or misattribute.
- Rufinus's Latin works are genuine translations/adaptations, not transparent reproductions of every Greek sentence.

## Death and Final Status

Origen survived Decian imprisonment and torture. Eusebius places his death in the reign of Gallus and gives an age of sixty-nine, while the birth calculation from the same historian produces some tension. The strongest range is **253–254**. Jerome says **Tyre** was both death place and burial place. No spouse or descendants were present in the record; his durable household was the school, patronage network, and library.

He was not executed, so ancient usage normally calls him a **confessor**, not a martyr. The claim that he died of torture injuries is plausible but unproved. No clinical illness, addiction, or psychological diagnosis is documented. His youthful desire for martyrdom, extreme austerity, and alleged mutilation should not be retrospectively converted into a modern psychiatric diagnosis.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Third and fourth centuries

Pamphilus and Eusebius canonized the image of the industrious scholar-confessor. The Caesarean library made his methods foundational for Eusebius. **Dionysius of Alexandria**, **Gregory Thaumaturgus**, **Firmilian**, **Theognostus**, **Pamphilus**, and **Didymus the Blind** transmitted parts of his theology. **Athanasius** could cite him positively on the Son's eternity. **Basil of Caesarea** and **Gregory of Nazianzus** helped preserve extracts in the Philocalia. **Gregory of Nyssa** developed themes of spiritual ascent, freedom, and restoration in ways often compared with Origen, though not reducible to him.

### Monastic and mystical traditions

Through Evagrius Ponticus, Cassian, Rufinus, translations, and anonymous appropriation, Origenian exegesis shaped Egyptian, Palestinian, Greek, Latin, Syriac, and later Byzantine monasticism. His distinction of literal and spiritual senses, ascent of the soul, spiritual senses, and Song of Songs bridal mysticism entered traditions that sometimes suppressed his name.

### Biblical scholarship

The Hexapla established a landmark in comparative textual criticism. His observations on the four Gospels, disputed Catholic epistles, and Hebrews preserve early canon history. His verse-by-verse commentaries created a durable Christian scholarly genre. Later exegetes frequently copied him while criticizing allegory.

### Trinitarian and Christological legacy

Origen supplied vocabulary and problems to both sides of fourth-century disputes: eternal generation and three hypostases on one side; hierarchical subordination on the other. Arius cannot simply be called his disciple, but Arian and Nicene theologians both worked in a field Origen helped create.

### Condemnation and survival

Repeated controversies made copying risky and selective. Much was lost, but destruction was not necessarily a single organized purge. Size, expensive reproduction, language change, doctrinal suspicion, excerpt culture, and the survival of safer Latin adaptations all mattered. *Against Celsus* survived intact in Greek partly because of its apologetic utility. The Philocalia and catenae preserved incriminated and admired passages together.

### Medieval and early modern reception

Latin medieval readers knew Origen chiefly through Rufinus and Jerome, often without clear attribution. He influenced monastic exegesis, Bernard of Clairvaux's Song tradition, and the multiple senses of scripture. Renaissance humanists printed and read *Against Celsus* and other Greek works; Erasmus admired his scriptural spirituality and edited Latin Origen. Confessional controversies made him alternately a witness to antiquity, a speculative heretic, and a victim of posthumous doctrinal policing.

### Modern reinterpretation

Twentieth-century scholarship—especially Daniélou, de Lubac, Crouzel, and later Origeniana studies—recovered Origen as a biblical theologian rather than a collection of condemned propositions. Other scholars warned that rehabilitation can be as selective as heresiology. Current disputes concern the identity of his philosophical teacher, one or two Origens, the extent of pre-existence, universal restoration, bodily resurrection, Jewish relationships, political theology, and the authenticity of the 553 anathemas.

The 1941 Tura discovery and 2012 Munich identification demonstrated that the corpus remains materially open. Origen is simultaneously: an ancient Christian presbyter deposed by his home church; a confessor tortured under Decius; the architect of the first surviving comprehensive Christian theological investigation; a foundational biblical critic; a source claimed by incompatible later parties; and a posthumously condemned name whose actual texts often resist the “Origenism” built from them.

## Atlas Connections

### Abraham

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Origen interpreted Abraham extensively in his *Commentary* and *Homilies on Genesis*, begun at Alexandria and continued during his Caesarean career (c. 220s–240s); he also defended Abraham’s ancestral and religious significance in *Against Celsus* 4.33–35 (c. 248, Caesarea). [Origen, *Against Celsus* 4](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/04164.htm)

### Aaron the Levite

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Origen repeatedly treated Aaron’s priesthood, vestments, sacrifices, and relationship to Moses in his Caesarean *Homilies on Exodus*, *Leviticus*, and *Numbers*, generally interpreting Aaronic institutions typologically in relation to Christ and the Church. The connection is textual, not historical contact.

### Noah

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Origen devoted his second *Homily on Genesis* to Noah’s ark and returned to Noah in his homilies on Ezekiel, where he interpreted Ezekiel’s triad of Noah, Daniel, and Job. These sermons belong to his third-century exegetical ministry, principally associated with Caesarea. [Origen, *Homilies on Genesis and Exodus*](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5d7fd6172ce70f2732bc7dd0/t/65e8a0d73614cf0867d8fac2/1709744343774/Origen%2B-%2BGenesis%2BHomily%2BII.pdf) [Origen, *Homilies on Ezekiel*](https://www.roger-pearse.com/weblog/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/Origen-Homilies_on_Ezekiel-ed_Hooker-2014.pdf)

### Moses

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Moses was a major subject throughout Origen’s commentaries and homilies on the Pentateuch and in *Against Celsus*. Origen defended Moses as legislator and theologian against Celsus while interpreting Mosaic law spiritually for Christians; this work spans Alexandria and Caesarea, c. 220s–248. [Origen, *Against Celsus* 1](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/04161.htm) [Origen, *Against Celsus* 3](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/04163.htm)

### King David

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Origen wrote commentaries, scholia, and numerous homilies on the Psalms attributed to David, from his Alexandrian period through his final Caesarean years. The twenty-nine Greek Psalm homilies recovered in Munich Cod. graec. 314 materially document this engagement. [Eusebius on Origen’s scriptural works](https://www.clarehall.cam.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/Eusebius_selected.pdf) [CUA Press, *Homilies on the Psalms*](https://www.cuapress.org/9780813233192/homilies-on-the-psalms/)

### Solomon

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Origen treated Solomon as the scriptural authorial persona behind Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and the Song of Songs. His early and mature commentaries and homilies on the Song—completed after work at Athens, probably in the 240s—made Solomon’s poetry a principal vehicle for ecclesial and mystical interpretation. [Eusebius on Origen’s scriptural works](https://www.clarehall.cam.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/Eusebius_selected.pdf)

### Ezekiel son of Buzi

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Origen composed a large *Commentary on Ezekiel* and preached surviving *Homilies on Ezekiel*. He worked on the commentary at Athens and Caesarea in the late 230s or 240s, interpreting Ezekiel historically, morally, and Christologically. [Origen, *Homilies on Ezekiel*](https://www.roger-pearse.com/weblog/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/Origen-Homilies_on_Ezekiel-ed_Hooker-2014.pdf)

### Jesus of Nazareth

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Jesus is the central subject of Origen’s *Commentaries on John* and *Matthew*, his homilies on Luke, *On First Principles*, and *Against Celsus*. Across Alexandria and Caesarea, c. 218–249, Origen wrote about Jesus’s historical ministry, eternal identity as Logos, incarnation, teaching, death, and resurrection. [Origen, *Commentary on Matthew* 12](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/101612.htm) [Origen, *Against Celsus*](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0416.htm)

### Paul the Apostle

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Origen wrote a fifteen-book *Commentary on Romans*, commentaries on several other Pauline epistles, and repeatedly interpreted Paul in *On First Principles* and *Against Celsus*. His engagement with Paul extended from the Alexandrian period into his Caesarean teaching and preaching. [Origen, *Against Celsus* 3](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/04163.htm)

### Peter the Apostle

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Origen wrote extensively about Peter in his Gospel commentaries, especially while interpreting Peter’s confession and the “rock” saying in *Commentary on Matthew* 12. He also discussed Peter’s occupation and apostolic role in *Against Celsus* 1.62. [Origen, *Commentary on Matthew* 12](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/101612.htm) [Origen, *Against Celsus* 1](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/04161.htm)

### Plato

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Origen repeatedly quoted, compared, and criticized Plato, most explicitly in *Against Celsus* (c. 248). He acknowledged points of agreement—including incorporeal theology—while arguing that biblical revelation surpassed Platonic teaching. [Origen, *Against Celsus* 7.42–43](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/04167.htm)

### Aristotle

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Origen explicitly discussed Aristotle as Plato’s dissident pupil and founder of a separate philosophical school in *Against Celsus* 3.12–13. Aristotle and Peripatetic reasoning also formed part of the philosophical environment Origen examined for Christian students. [Origen, *Against Celsus* 3](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/04163.htm)

### Homer

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Origen discussed Homer’s poetry and theology in *Against Celsus*, contrasting Homeric stories of divine violence and sexual misconduct with Mosaic teaching and invoking Plato’s exclusion of Homer from the ideal city. This was written at Caesarea around 248. [Origen, *Against Celsus* 4.36](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/04164.htm)

### Hesiod of Ascra

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** In *Against Celsus* 4.36, Origen named Hesiod alongside Homer and criticized the Greek theogonies for attributing generation and morally scandalous conduct to the gods. [Origen, *Against Celsus* 4](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/04164.htm)

### Zoroaster

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Origen addressed Zoroaster in *Against Celsus* 1.16 while reproducing and contesting Celsus’s catalogue of ancient sages. The notice documents written engagement but does not show access to authentic Zoroastrian writings. [Origen, *Against Celsus* 1](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/04161.htm)

### Flavius Josephus

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Origen cited Josephus by name and recommended his two books *On the Antiquity of the Jews*—now called *Against Apion*—as evidence for Jewish antiquity in *Against Celsus* 1.16. Elsewhere he cited Josephus concerning John the Baptist, James, and Jerusalem’s destruction, although the exact textual history of some passages is disputed. [Origen, *Against Celsus* 1](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/04161.htm)

### Augustine of Hippo

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Augustine wrote critically about doctrines attributed to Origen. In correspondence with Jerome around 397 he requested a clearer account of Origen’s condemned errors; in *On the Proceedings of Pelagius* (417) he associated Origen with the proposition that postmortem punishment might end. [Augustine, Letter 40](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1102040.htm) [Augustine, *On the Proceedings of Pelagius*](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1505.htm)

### Thomas Aquinas

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Aquinas incorporated numerous passages explicitly attributed to Origen into the *Catena Aurea*, his Gospel commentary compiled c. 1262–1268. The work places Origen’s interpretations in a continuous patristic chain, including substantial excerpts from the commentaries on Matthew and John. [Aquinas, *Catena Aurea* on Matthew](https://ccel.org/ccel/aquinas/catena1.ii.xvi.html) [Aquinas, *Catena Aurea* on John](https://www.fisheaters.com/srpdf/xaquinas-john.pdf)

### Justinian I

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Emperor Justinian wrote or promulgated a lengthy theological edict addressed to Patriarch Mennas in 543, summarizing and refuting propositions attributed to Origen and appending anti-Origenist anathemas. The document is a direct posthumous written engagement, although some condemned propositions more accurately represent later Origenism than securely recoverable teachings of Origen himself. [Justinian’s edict and anathemas](https://www.ldysinger.com/%40magist/0543-53_an-orig/03_anath_543-53.htm) [Richard Price, *Acts of Constantinople II*, anti-Origenist documents](https://afkimel.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/the-acts-of-the-council-of-constantinople-of-553-2-vol-edited-and-translated-by-richard-price-translated-texts-for-historians-volume-51-2009-9781846311789-da31e1d5d5690161fc03aafa9daef89.pdf)
