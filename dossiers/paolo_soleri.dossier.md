# Paolo Soleri: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

This dossier reconstructs Paolo Soleri’s life from published archival, institutional, scholarly, journalistic, autobiographical, and family sources consulted through September 3, 2026.

Evidentiary labels used for contested material:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporaneous records, institutional archives, or multiple independent sources.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a named witness or reputable secondary source but not independently verified here.
- **Allegation:** an accusation that was not adjudicated in court.
- **Disputed account:** materially different versions exist.
- **Rumor or myth:** a circulating story lacking adequate documentary confirmation.
- **Unresolved:** available evidence does not establish a conclusion.

Soleri died before the principal public sexual-abuse allegation against him appeared and therefore could not answer it. No criminal charge, civil judgment, or adjudicated finding concerning that allegation was located. The allegation is nonetheless unusually detailed, was made publicly by his daughter, had reportedly been disclosed privately decades earlier, received partial corroboration concerning prior disclosure from people in Soleri’s circle, and was formally acknowledged by the Cosanti Foundation.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Paolo Soleri.
- **Common form:** Paolo Soleri; no established pseudonym has been found.
- **Informal descriptions and titles:** architect, urban designer, planner, craftsman, ceramicist, sculptor, writer, philosopher, lecturer; “visionary architect”; “prophet in the desert,” a label particularly associated with architecture critic Ada Louise Huxtable and later publicity.
- **Nationality:** Italian-born; generally described by American museums as an American architect born in Italy. The date and legal basis of any United States naturalization were not established in the consulted sources.
- **Born:** June 21, 1919, Turin, Kingdom of Italy.
- **Died:** April 9, 2013, at his home at Cosanti in Paradise Valley, Arizona, aged 93.
- **Death-date discrepancy:** a notice from the Italian consulate gives April 8, but the Cosanti Foundation, major obituaries, and most institutional sources give April 9. April 9 is the stronger date.
- **Cause of death:** reported by his foundation and contemporary obituaries as natural causes. No more specific medical cause was publicly supplied.
- **Burial:** reported buried in the private cemetery at Arcosanti beside his wife.
- **Principal residences:** Turin; Grenoble, France; Taliesin West near Scottsdale, Arizona; Taliesin in Spring Green, Wisconsin; Italy, including Turin and Vietri sul Mare; from 1956, Cosanti in Paradise Valley, Arizona; regular work and residence at Arcosanti near Mayer/Cordes Junction, Arizona.
- **Spouse:** Corolyn “Colly” Woods Soleri, an American whom he met through the Dome House commission; died of colon cancer in 1982.
- **Children:** two daughters, Kristine and Daniela Soleri.
- **Known grandchildren:** contemporary obituaries report two grandchildren.
- **Principal organizations:** Frank Lloyd Wright’s Taliesin Fellowship; Cosanti Foundation; Cosanti Originals; Arcosanti.
- **Best-known concept:** **arcology**, Soleri’s term combining architecture and ecology.
- **Best-known built environments:** Cosanti and Arcosanti.
- **Best-known commercial objects:** ceramic and bronze windbells made through the Cosanti workshops.
- **Principal archive:** the original drawings, journals, sketchbooks, models, and personal papers are held by the Paolo Soleri Archives at Arcosanti. Arizona State University holds a related Paolo Soleri collection of audiovisual materials, slides, photographs, drawings, ephemera, and press files.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

The Italian *Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani* identifies Soleri’s parents as:

- **Emilio Soleri**, father, owner of a small factory producing ceramic electrical components.
- **Pia Soleri**, mother.

According to Antonietta Iolanda Lima’s biographical reconstruction, Paolo associated his mother with frugality and method and his father with long silences, contemplation, love of nature, and antifascism. These are interpretive family-character descriptions, not independently measurable facts.

Emilio’s occupation placed Paolo near ceramic manufacturing before his later work at the Solimene factory, although no documentary evidence establishes a direct technical apprenticeship in his father’s business.

### Siblings

A sister named **Maria Soleri** is documented. On June 14, 1933, Paolo and Maria reportedly applied together for admission to the art lyceum in Turin.

A complete sibling list and detailed genealogy were not located. Claims about deeper ancestry should therefore be treated as unestablished.

### Marriage and children

While constructing the Dome House in Cave Creek, Arizona, with Mark Mills in 1949–50, Soleri met **Corolyn Woods**, generally called **Colly**, the daughter of client Nora Woods. Paolo and Colly married and spent much of the following six years in Italy.

Their daughters were:

- **Kristine Soleri**, the elder daughter, born approximately 1951 according to the seven-year age difference reported in later journalism.
- **Daniela Soleri**, born in 1958.

Colly became central to the practical, social, and financial operation of Cosanti and Arcosanti. Later accounts portray the marriage not simply as domestic but as a working partnership organized substantially around Paolo’s career. Daniela described her mother as warm and socially adept and believed that Colly’s life was consumed by work for Paolo and his project. That assessment is Daniela’s retrospective interpretation.

Colly developed colon cancer. Daniela returned to Arizona and helped nurse her during the final months of her life. Colly died in 1982. The amphitheater at Arcosanti was subsequently named the **Colly Soleri Music Center** in her memory.

No evidence was found of a later marriage. Daniela later alleged that her father had sexual relationships or pursued sexual contact outside the marriage; individual relationships are discussed under controversies because the evidence and degree of consent vary.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1919–1940

Paolo Soleri was born in industrial Turin on the summer solstice, June 21, 1919. Later commemorative writing repeatedly emphasized the solstice birth, giving it symbolic resonance compatible with his later solar and cosmological vocabulary. There is no indication that the date itself was fabricated; the mythic element lies in its later presentation as an emblem of his solar orientation.

His childhood unfolded under Italian Fascism. Sources differ in the precision with which they describe the family’s movement to France:

- Italian biographical research states that he attended a school of industrial art in Grenoble and later returned to Turin.
- Some later summaries say the family went to Grenoble in 1933 to escape Fascism.
- The *Dizionario Biografico* specifically attributes antifascism to his father, but the exact circumstances, duration, and legal character of the family’s French residence remain incompletely documented in publicly accessible sources.

After Grenoble, Soleri returned to Turin. His June 14, 1933 application to an art lyceum with his sister Maria is one of the few precise records cited for his adolescence.

The conjunction of an industrial city, a family ceramics business, technical education, Alpine and Mediterranean landscapes, and Fascist-era austerity later supplied biographers with an explanatory framework for Soleri’s combination of craft, monumental engineering, frugality, and hostility to mass-consumer culture. The influence is plausible and sometimes autobiographically supported, but should not be mistaken for a complete causal explanation.

## Education and Formation, 1941–1946

Soleri entered the **Politecnico di Torino** during the Fascist and wartime period. Institutional and biographical sources variously date his formal study to 1941–46 or describe his degree as completed in 1946 or 1947.

The strongest detailed Italian account states:

- Soleri regarded himself as substantially self-taught until 1942.
- He completed his degree in **February 1946**.
- His thesis concerned the urban renewal or reconstruction of Turin.
- His drawings were already exceptionally elaborate and impressed his teachers.

English-language institutional biographies sometimes call the qualification a “Ph.D. in architecture” and date it to 1947. Italian usage and postwar degree equivalencies make that translation potentially misleading. The safest formulation is that he received his architecture degree from the Turin Polytechnic in 1946; 1947 may reflect the date of recognition, publication, or an English-language conversion of the credential.

His European formation exposed him to modernism, including Le Corbusier, Walter Gropius, Erich Mendelsohn, and Alvar Aalto. The decisive published encounter, according to later scholarship, was a small Italian book on **Frank Lloyd Wright**, particularly images of Taliesin West. Soleri wrote directly to Wright after graduation and asked to join the Taliesin Fellowship. Wright admitted him without the normal tuition charge, reportedly promising to put him “on the right track.”

## Taliesin and Frank Lloyd Wright, 1946/47–1949

### Arrival and apprenticeship

Soleri traveled to the United States in December 1946 or during 1947; sources differ by several months. He spent approximately eighteen months in the Taliesin Fellowship, working at:

- **Taliesin West**, Wright’s desert compound near Scottsdale, Arizona.
- **Taliesin**, at Spring Green, Wisconsin.

Because his English was initially weak, he reportedly worked as a waiter and gardener as well as participating in studio life. Later accounts say his early silence intensified his observation of desert light, the relationship between shelter and landscape, and the continuity between indoor and outdoor space.

Soleri later characterized his purpose succinctly: he was at Taliesin “to be a sponge.”

### Design development

At Taliesin, Soleri developed an ambitious prestressed-concrete bridge proposal remembered as **The Beast**. Elizabeth Mock, then connected with the Museum of Modern Art, recognized its structural and formal originality and included it in *The Architecture of Bridges* (1949). The publication gave Soleri early international notice.

Wright’s influence remained visible throughout Soleri’s career in:

- Organic, landscape-responsive form.
- Integration of craft and construction.
- Apprentices learning through communal manual work.
- Geometric vocabularies involving circles, domes, and triangular forms.
- A desert campus operating simultaneously as residence, school, workshop, and architectural demonstration.

Soleri nevertheless came to reject Wright’s **Broadacre City**, whose decentralized, automobile-dependent, low-density settlement pattern was nearly the inverse of arcology. Soleri’s later project proposed compression and three-dimensional density in place of Wright’s territorial dispersal.

### Departure: competing accounts

The end of Soleri’s fellowship is not documented by a single uncontested account.

- **Soleri’s later reported account:** Three other apprentices told Wright that they wished to leave with Soleri. Wright became angry and dismissed them.
- **Institutional shorthand:** Soleri left to pursue independent work.
- **A secondhand anecdote attributed to Lloyd Kiva New:** During a formal Taliesin performance, Wright asked Soleri to wear one of his neckties. Soleri allegedly replied with an obscenity—“F you, Dad”—and walked out.
- **Interpretation:** later writers have suggested a collision between two powerful egos and growing disagreement about urbanism.

The necktie episode is best labeled **reported anecdote**, transmitted from Soleri to Lloyd Kiva New and later retold by another participant. It is memorable but not contemporaneously documented. Soleri’s own account of dismissal is also retrospective. What is established is that the relationship became contentious and ended abruptly by 1949.

## Early Independent Career, 1949–1956

### Dome House, Cave Creek

Soleri and fellow former Taliesin apprentice **Mark Mills** designed and built the **Dome House** for Nora Woods in Cave Creek, Arizona, in 1949–50.

The partially earth-sheltered house used a glass dome that could move or retract to moderate exposure to the desert climate. It combined Wrightian organic architecture with technical experimentation and anticipated Soleri’s later interest in passive solar control, subterranean space, and architecture as a climatic instrument.

The Dome House brought Soleri into contact with the Woods family and with his future wife, Colly.

### Return to Italy

Around 1950 Paolo and Colly moved to Italy. Soleri worked in Turin and southern Italy and maintained a small private practice.

His most important realized Italian commission was the **Ceramica Artistica Solimene factory** at Vietri sul Mare, commissioned by ceramic manufacturer **Vincenzo Solimene** and generally dated 1954–55.

The factory:

- Occupied a steep site on the Amalfi Coast.
- Used conical or tree-like ceramic-clad volumes.
- Was largely top-lit.
- Arranged production along a descending spiral or ramp.
- Made the industrial process itself the generator of architectural form.
- Incorporated thousands of ceramic pieces into its skin.

The commission deepened Soleri’s technical knowledge of ceramic production. That knowledge became economically decisive after his return to Arizona, where ceramic and later bronze bells financed much of his architectural experimentation.

Soleri also designed ceramic objects and worked on bridge, dam, and urban concepts during the Italian period. Many remained drawings or models.

### Return to Arizona

Accounts date the permanent return variously to 1955 or 1956. The more precise property history places Paolo and Colly’s purchase of five acres in Paradise Valley in **1956**.

## Cosanti, 1956 onward

### Site, name, and construction

Paolo and Colly established their residence and studio on a five-acre desert parcel in what became Paradise Valley, near Scottsdale.

They called it **Cosanti**. The foundation now explains the word as a combination of the Italian *cosa* (“thing” or “things”) and *anti* (“against” or “before”), expressing an orientation “against things” or “before things”: a critique of material accumulation and consumer culture.

Soleri experimented with **earthcasting**:

1. Soil was mounded or excavated into a negative form.
2. Patterns, pigments, or reinforcement were added.
3. Concrete was poured or plastered over the earth form.
4. After curing, the soil was removed.
5. The resulting shell retained textures and relief from the earth mold.

This produced inexpensive, sculptural concrete apses, vaults, workshops, residential spaces, and outdoor rooms. Some Cosanti structures were partly below grade and used soil mass, shade, orientation, and seasonal solar exposure to moderate temperature.

Cosanti was never simply a private house. It became:

- The Soleri family residence.
- An architectural studio.
- A craft foundry.
- A school and workshop site.
- The headquarters of an educational foundation.
- A laboratory for construction techniques later used at Arcosanti.

Cosanti was eventually placed on the Arizona Register of Historic Places.

### Bells and the project economy

Soleri’s ceramic bells grew out of the knowledge acquired at Vietri. Bronze bells were added later through foundry casting.

The bells served several overlapping functions:

- Marketable craft objects bearing Soleri’s visual vocabulary.
- Training work for apprentices.
- Demonstrations of earth- and silt-casting.
- A continuing revenue stream for Cosanti and Arcosanti.
- Widely distributed public emblems of the project.

Bell sales, workshop tuition or fees, tours, donations, grants, publications, and volunteer or low-paid labor formed the economic base of the enterprise. Arcosanti was therefore not financed by a conventional real-estate-development model or by Soleri’s independent fortune.

The current institutional structure separates the nonprofit **Cosanti Foundation**, a tax-exempt 501(c)(3), from the for-profit **Cosanti Originals**, owned and operated by the foundation. The foundation’s federal tax identification number is 86-0208931. Contemporary descriptions often blurred the foundation, community, workshop, and commercial foundry; they were legally and operationally connected but not identical.

No reliable evidence was found that Soleri amassed major personal wealth. His daughter Daniela later explicitly rejected the image of him as a financial charlatan or secret profiteer. At the same time, the project depended heavily on workshop participants and devotees who supplied fees and labor, producing recurrent criticism about hierarchy and worker power.

## Arcology: Intellectual and Artistic Development

### Formation of the idea

During the late 1950s and 1960s, Soleri developed a series of immense bridge, dam, marine, space, mesa, and vertical-city proposals. Important conceptual projects included:

- **Mesa City**, developed from approximately 1959.
- **The Beast** and later multifunction bridge cities.
- **Omega Residential Bridge**.
- **Balance**, **Campanula**, **Flight**, and **Helium** bridge projects.
- **Babel** projects.
- **Novanoah**, a floating marine settlement.
- **Asteromo**, a proposed space city.
- Linear urban systems later developed as **Lean Linear City** or **Arterial Arcology**.

He coined or systematized the word **arcology**—architecture plus ecology—by 1969. Arcology was not simply a claim that a building should be “green.” It proposed that the city should operate as a compact, three-dimensional, metabolically organized structure.

Core propositions included:

- Extreme urban compactness rather than suburban dispersal.
- Mixed uses and intense social proximity.
- Reduction of private automobile travel.
- Concentrated infrastructure and shared services.
- Preservation of open landscape and agricultural land.
- Passive use of sun, shade, thermal mass, and seasonal variation.
- Minimization of material and energy consumption.
- Urban “miniaturization” paired with greater complexity.
- Architecture conceived as analogous to a living organism, but containing reflective human minds rather than functioning as an anthill.

Soleri called his alternative “lean.” He described suburban America as an engine of consumption and regarded conventional green consumerism as insufficient if it preserved continual material expansion.

### Teilhard de Chardin and cosmogenesis

Soleri was deeply influenced by Jesuit paleontologist and philosopher **Pierre Teilhard de Chardin**, especially *Le Phénomène humain*. Teilhard’s concepts of increasing complexity, consciousness, cosmogenesis, and the Omega Point entered Soleri’s own vocabulary.

Soleri developed related concepts including:

- **Aesthetogenesis:** reality becoming progressively more complex, conscious, compassionate, and beautiful.
- **Miniaturization–complexity–duration:** the proposition that evolution compacts matter while increasing complexity and extending meaningful processes.
- **The Omega Seed:** an eschatological hypothesis in which consciousness and cosmos participate in an unfinished evolutionary process.
- **Matter becoming spirit:** not a rejection of material reality but the idea that organized matter develops toward mind and spirit.

His position was spiritual and teleological, but no evidence was found that he adhered in adulthood to conventional Catholic doctrine or held clerical or sectarian office. He borrowed heavily from a Catholic thinker while constructing a personal cosmology.

### Politics

Soleri’s politics were expressed mainly as civilizational, environmental, and economic criticism rather than party organization.

Documented positions included:

- Opposition to Fascism in his family background.
- Hostility to consumerism and limitless material growth.
- Criticism of suburban sprawl and automobile dependency.
- Advocacy of urban density, shared services, and conservation.
- Skepticism about conventional “free enterprise” when separated from ecological and cultural purposes.
- Interest in peace, reflected in *Space for Peace* and related programming.
- Concern that technological growth without ethical or urban transformation would amplify consumption.

No evidence was found of elected office, formal party leadership, imprisonment, exile by legal order, intelligence service, clandestine political organization, political violence, or financial corruption.

His institutional practice was paradoxical: he spoke of democratic and social vitality, but critics and his daughter described Cosanti and Arcosanti as highly centralized around his authority.

## Recognition in the 1950s and 1960s

Soleri’s imaginative drawings and models gained attention more rapidly than his buildings.

Milestones included:

- Inclusion in Elizabeth Mock’s *The Architecture of Bridges* in 1949.
- Dome House inclusion in MoMA’s **Built in USA: Post-War Architecture** exhibition in 1953.
- Major coverage by Peter Blake in *Architectural Forum* in 1961.
- Exhibitions and publications featuring Mesa City and bridge proposals.
- A Graham Foundation fellowship or grant.
- A John Simon Guggenheim Memorial Foundation fellowship in architecture, planning, and design in 1964; some biographies also cite support in 1967.
- The American Institute of Architects’ **Gold Medal for Craftsmanship** in 1963.
- Lecturing at Arizona State University, including service as a distinguished visiting lecturer in 1962.

His work appealed to a 1960s audience interested in megastructures, alternative communities, ecology, systems theory, and resistance to suburban conformity.

## *Arcology* and the 1970 Retrospective

In 1969 MIT Press published Soleri’s oversized, heavily illustrated **_Arcology: The City in the Image of Man_**, with a foreword by Peter Blake.

The book combined:

- A theoretical critique of the modern city.
- Soleri’s cosmological and evolutionary vocabulary.
- Diagrams and data for approximately thirty arcologies.
- Proposed populations ranging from roughly 1,500 to several million.
- Oceanic, terrestrial, bridge, dam, and space settlements.

The Corcoran Gallery of Art in Washington, D.C., organized **“The Architectural Vision of Paolo Soleri”** or **“City in the Image of Man”** in 1970. Donald Wall prepared the associated retrospective publication *Visionary Cities: The Arcology of Paolo Soleri*. The exhibition traveled in the United States and Canada and reportedly drew unusually large attendance.

This moment established the public Soleri mythology: an expatriate Italian craftsman-philosopher in the Arizona desert, producing prophetic cities rejected by ordinary developers but embraced by students and the counterculture.

## Arcosanti, 1970–2013

### Land and foundation

In the late 1960s, the Cosanti Foundation acquired more than 800 acres near Cordes Junction, approximately seventy miles north of Phoenix. Construction of **Arcosanti** began in 1970.

The name joined **arcology** and **Cosanti**. Soleri described it as an **urban laboratory**, not a complete arcology. It was intended to demonstrate compact settlement for approximately 5,000 inhabitants, although changing master plans proposed different forms and densities.

The Cosanti Foundation’s precise founding date is inconsistently reported:

- Soleri-associated biographies sometimes say 1956, treating the activity begun at Cosanti as the foundation’s origin.
- Arizona State University and legal-institutional accounts give **1965** for establishment of the nonprofit organization.
- Construction at Arcosanti began in 1970.

These dates concern different stages and should not be collapsed.

### Workshop construction model

Arcosanti was built through short-term educational workshops, resident labor, staff supervision, and volunteer participation. Thousands of participants came from the United States and abroad.

Workshop participants generally:

- Paid tuition or fees.
- Received instruction in arcology and construction.
- Performed physical building work.
- Could sometimes remain as residents or staff.

This model allowed construction without conventional development capital but also generated a structural criticism: students paid to provide labor for a project governed by Soleri and the foundation. Defenders treated the arrangement as experiential education; critics described it as exploitative or undemocratic. No located court judgment established systematic wage theft, but labor-power criticism appeared from participants and intentional-community scholars.

### Built fabric

Major elements eventually included:

- Foundry and ceramic-production areas.
- South Vaults.
- Ceramics Apse.
- Foundry Apse.
- East Crescent residential and commercial units.
- Soleri Office and Drafting Unit.
- Colly Soleri Music Center/amphitheater.
- Community dining and gathering spaces.
- Guest rooms, residences, swimming pool, and agricultural facilities.

Earthcasting, silt-cast ornament, concrete vaults, south-facing apses, thermal mass, and seasonal solar orientation embodied aspects of the theory.

The apses admitted lower winter sunlight while providing summer shade. Activities that conventional cities distribute across wide distances—residence, fabrication, performance, dining, and administration—were compressed into pedestrian proximity.

### Population and incompletion

Arcosanti never approached its intended population.

Published estimates across different periods range from approximately fifty to 150 permanent residents. Near the end of Soleri’s life he estimated it was perhaps **5 percent complete**. Only a little more than a dozen principal structures had been realized after four decades.

Reasons cited in documentary and critical sources include:

- Chronic shortage of capital.
- Reluctance to accept financing or development arrangements that might compromise the concept.
- Dependence on incremental bell revenue and workshop fees.
- Remote location.
- Soleri’s centralized control and difficulty delegating design authority.
- Lack of detailed, conventional construction documents.
- Limited employment, schooling, and services on site.
- Long travel distances for residents’ children and outside work.
- Shifts in architectural fashion during the 1980s.
- The conceptual scale and technical ambition of the original plans.
- Soleri’s limited ability or willingness to build a broad institutional coalition.

The project’s empirical status is therefore mixed:

- **Success as an enduring experimental campus:** it survived, housed a community, trained thousands, and influenced ecological-urban discourse.
- **Failure relative to its declared urban objective:** it did not become a city of 5,000 or a demonstration of a fully operating arcology.
- **Unresolved prototype claim:** because the planned density and systems were never built, Arcosanti could neither fully validate nor decisively falsify arcology as proposed.

### Social organization

Soleri retained final authority over design and intellectual direction for most of the project’s history. Former participants reported a spectrum of experiences, from formative education and communal creativity to frustration with hierarchy, low wages, and inability to influence policy.

A 2001 intentional-community critique accused the organization of neglecting worker rights, democracy, social justice, and internal ethical questions while concentrating on Soleri’s remote cosmological hypotheses. It also alleged community problems involving alienation and substance abuse. These are **published participant/critical claims**, not judicial findings or comprehensive sociological measurements.

Daniela Soleri later argued that deference to her father produced intellectual isolation: without peers of equivalent status, disagreement was treated as disloyalty or compromise.

### Visitors and cultural network

Arcosanti and Cosanti attracted architects, artists, environmentalists, students, filmmakers, and countercultural visitors. A 2017 KJZZ report, based on the retrospective’s research, states that visitors included **Betty Friedan, Francis Ford Coppola, and George Lucas**. The character and duration of those contacts were not established in the material consulted; they should be understood as reported visits, not necessarily collaborations.

Musicians, performers, conferences, and festivals later made Arcosanti a cultural venue independent of its progress as a city.

## Other Buildings, Designs, and Public Projects

### Major realized works

- **Dome House**, Cave Creek, Arizona, 1949–50, with Mark Mills.
- **Ceramica Artistica Solimene**, Vietri sul Mare, Italy, approximately 1954–55.
- **Cosanti**, Paradise Valley, Arizona, from 1956.
- **Paolo Soleri Amphitheater**, Institute of American Indian Arts, Santa Fe, New Mexico, approximately 1966–70.
- **Arcosanti**, near Mayer/Cordes Junction, Arizona, from 1970.
- **DeConcini House**, Phoenix, 1982.
- **Arizona Cancer Center Chapel**, University of Arizona, Tucson, 1986.
- **Glendale Community College Amphitheater**, Glendale, Arizona, 1995.
- **Soleri Bridge and Plaza**, Scottsdale, Arizona, completed 2010 after a design history extending back to the 1980s.

### Santa Fe amphitheater disputes

The outdoor theater at the Institute of American Indian Arts was produced with the institution’s leadership, including Lloyd Kiva New, and with technical collaborators.

A later account by an architect involved in the project states:

- Soleri made extensive on-site design decisions.
- His conceptual drawings lacked normal construction details.
- Lighting was initially omitted or underdeveloped.
- The Bureau of Indian Affairs dismissed him during construction because he refused to wear required steel-toed shoes and a hard hat.
- Later workers had difficulty reproducing his hand-shaped surface effects.

This is a **reported firsthand/near-contemporaneous professional account**, but the specific dismissal reason was reportedly conveyed to the writer rather than personally witnessed. It illustrates the recurrent conflict between Soleri’s improvisational craft practice and institutional construction procedures.

The amphitheater was demolished by the State of New Mexico in 2010 despite preservation efforts. Soleri protested the loss. Its demolition contributed to the posthumous framing of his realized work as fragile and underprotected.

### Professional registration

A later reminiscence reports that Soleri applied for architectural registration in Arizona. After an interview by a retired mechanical engineer, the board asked him to take the licensing examination; Soleri abandoned the application. If accurate, he was not an Arizona-registered architect and relied on licensed collaborators for projects requiring stamped documents.

This is a **reported account**, not supported here by the actual licensing file.

### Patents and inventions

Soleri developed distinctive earthcasting and silt-casting methods and applied them to sculpture, bells, models, and construction. He and Scott M. Davis published a technical book on the process.

No verified United States patent issued personally to Paolo Soleri was located in the consulted materials. Earthcasting should therefore be described as a technique he developed and publicized, not as a confirmed patented invention.

## Later Career, 1980–2012

### After Colly’s death

Colly died from colon cancer in 1982. Her death removed a central administrator and social intermediary from the project. Arcosanti’s amphitheater was named for her.

Soleri continued writing and developing his cosmological and urban theory:

- *The Omega Seed*.
- *The Bridge Between Matter & Spirit Is Matter Becoming Spirit*.
- *Technology and Cosmogenesis*.
- *Space for Peace*.
- *Arcosanti: An Urban Laboratory?*
- “Minds for History” seminars.
- The “Paradox” conferences.
- “Hyper Building” and Lean Linear City studies.
- The collected *What If?* writings.

### Minds for History and Paradox

Beginning in the late 1980s, Soleri organized or inspired dialogues at Arcosanti under titles including **Minds for History** and later the **Paradox Project**. These brought together questions of technology, cyberspace, urbanism, evolution, and human purpose.

The proceedings extended Arcosanti’s function from construction workshop to intellectual forum, although critics argued that the increasingly abstract cosmology diverted attention from the settlement’s immediate governance and financial problems.

### International recognition

Important later distinctions included:

- Gold Medal at the World Biennale of Architecture, Sofia, 1981.
- Silver Medal of the Académie d’Architecture, Paris, 1984.
- Honorary fellowship in the Royal Institute of British Architects, 1996.
- Golden Lion for lifetime achievement at the Venice Architecture Biennale, 2000.
- Honorary degrees, including an honorary degree associated with Palermo in 2001.
- Cooper Hewitt National Design Award for Lifetime Achievement, 2006.

Some popular lists also call a 2010 distinction an “AIA Gold Medal.” This should not be confused with the American Institute of Architects’ national Gold Medal unless verified through AIA records. The securely documented early AIA honor is the **1963 Gold Medal for Craftsmanship**.

### Soleri Bridge and Plaza

The Soleri Bridge and Plaza in Scottsdale opened in 2010. The pedestrian bridge also functions as a solar calendar: its pylons and alignment mark seasonal solar events. It represented a rare late-life realization of Soleri’s bridge imagery at civic scale.

### Withdrawal from leadership

Daniela Soleri says she resigned from the Cosanti Foundation board in October 2010 and gave the board a letter explaining her father’s sexual abuse of her. Later reporting states the board learned of the allegations in 2010–11 and removed or induced Soleri to step down as president and director.

The precise sequence is not completely transparent in public records:

- Daniela dates her resignation letter to approximately 2010.
- The foundation and later reporting place board action in 2011.
- No public statement identifying the allegations was made at the time.
- Soleri’s age and succession planning may also have affected the leadership change.

It is therefore documented that he left formal leadership near the end of his life; the allegation was a reported and apparently substantial factor, but the board’s complete contemporaneous minutes were not available.

### Final years

Soleri continued drawing, writing, and appearing in interviews into advanced age. Documentary filmmaker Lisa Scafuro filmed him over several years for *The Vision of Paolo Soleri: Prophet in the Desert*. He saw a rough cut around his ninety-third birthday in June 2012 and reportedly approved it.

By this period Soleri acknowledged Arcosanti’s extreme incompletion. He attributed some of the failure to his lack of proselytizing ability; others attributed it to his controlling personality, impractical financing, and resistance to collaboration.

No documented dementia, addiction, major psychiatric diagnosis, or terminal illness was located.

---

## Death

Paolo Soleri died at his Cosanti home in Paradise Valley on April 9, 2013. His foundation reported natural causes.

He was survived by daughters Kristine and Daniela and two grandchildren. Contemporary obituaries emphasized that Arcosanti remained occupied but radically incomplete.

His burial at Arcosanti beside Colly symbolically joined the personal, familial, architectural, and institutional project to which most of his adult life had been devoted.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Cosanti Foundation

- Educational nonprofit established institutionally in approximately 1965, with roots in activity begun at Cosanti in 1956.
- Owner and steward of Cosanti and Arcosanti.
- Repository or custodian of the principal Soleri archive.
- Operator of educational workshops, tours, lectures, exhibitions, and cultural events.
- Federal tax-exempt organization, EIN 86-0208931.
- Governed by a board, although Soleri exercised dominant intellectual and design authority during his active lifetime.

### Cosanti Originals

- For-profit company owned and operated by the Cosanti Foundation.
- Manufactures and sells ceramic and bronze bells and other artisan objects.
- Provides income supporting the foundation and its sites.
- Continues operating after Soleri’s death.

### Financial model

Documented or consistently reported income sources included:

- Bell and craft sales.
- Workshop fees.
- Visitor tours.
- Grants and donations.
- Publication sales.
- Cultural programming and later festivals.
- Low-paid, resident, student, or volunteer labor.

No substantial stock portfolio, hidden company network, offshore entity, money-laundering scheme, or major private real-estate fortune was found.

The financial controversy concerns the project’s labor and governance model rather than documented embezzlement or fraud.

### Construction technologies

- Earthcasting.
- Silt-cast relief and decoration.
- Thin concrete shells and apses.
- Tilt-up or ground-cast concrete panels.
- Passive solar orientation.
- Thermal mass and partial earth sheltering.
- Dense mixed-use pedestrian design.
- Proposed greenhouse “energy aprons.”
- Urban systems integrating agriculture, circulation, housing, work, and cultural functions.

### Unrealized scale

Most of Soleri’s largest projects remained on paper. His reputation therefore rests on three different bodies of work that should be distinguished:

1. Speculative drawings and models.
2. Small-scale craft and construction experiments.
3. Partially realized institutional environments at Cosanti and Arcosanti.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### Sexual abuse of Daniela Soleri

**Status: detailed allegation; not judicially adjudicated; prior disclosure partially corroborated.**

On November 14, 2017, Daniela Soleri published “Sexual Abuse: It’s You, Him, and His Work.” She alleged:

- Her father began sexually molesting her in early adolescence.
- He eventually attempted to rape her when she was seventeen.
- His power within the family and the cultural prestige of his work contributed to her silence.
- She disclosed the abuse to members of his inner circle around 1993.
- She disclosed it again in her resignation letter from the Cosanti Foundation board around 2010.
- Institutional and personal loyalty to Soleri discouraged an adequate response.

Daniela said she had not spoken with or seen her father during the final two years of his life.

No criminal complaint, prosecution, civil action, or court finding was located. Soleri died in 2013, four years before the public essay, and no public response by him to the underlying private disclosure is available.

The allegation has contextual support beyond the mere fact of publication:

- Daniela provided a specific chronology.
- Former Arcosanti associate Sue Hoadley later recalled Daniela telling a group about abuse in 1993.
- The foundation acknowledged that Daniela’s disclosures affected its earlier governance decisions.
- The Cosanti Foundation publicly stated that it stood with Daniela and had to confront Soleri’s failings.

This does not convert the allegation into a legal finding. It does establish that it was not invented for the first time in 2017 without any earlier disclosure.

### Foundation response and institutional silence

**Status: documented institutional response; disputed adequacy.**

The Cosanti Foundation did not publicly disclose the allegations when Daniela resigned or when Soleri left leadership. After her 2017 essay, it issued a statement supporting her and distinguishing “the man Paolo Soleri” from the ideas of arcology.

Later institutional language said the foundation had experienced its own problems involving abuse and chose to address them through #MeToo policies and awareness.

Critics argue that the organization protected Soleri’s reputation for years. Participants quoted in later journalism expressed regret that they did not investigate or intervene when Daniela disclosed the abuse privately. The available evidence supports institutional delay and deference; it does not establish that every board member or resident knew the details.

### Conduct toward nude models

**Status: reported allegation concerning inappropriate sexual request; no charge or adjudication.**

Soleri regularly drew nude female models, reportedly advertising for women aged twenty-one to forty-one.

Writer **Margie Goldsmith** said that while modeling for the eighty-seven-year-old Soleri in 2006, he asked whether he could kiss her nipples. She refused, dressed, and left. Her account appeared before Daniela’s public 2017 essay.

Other models reportedly said Soleri behaved appropriately with them. Daniela said she heard numerous similar stories, but those additional accounts were not independently documented in the sources consulted.

The established conclusion is limited: one named model publicly reported an unwanted sexual request; broader claims of a pattern beyond Daniela’s abuse allegation remain incompletely substantiated.

### Sexual relationships and marital conduct

Daniela reported learning that her father had maintained a sexual relationship with a woman long connected to the project, who had also treated Colly cruelly. The identity and circumstances were not established in the consulted material.

This is a **family allegation/report**, not evidence of a crime. Whether the adult relationship was consensual has not been publicly clarified.

### Hierarchy, personality, and “cult” characterization

**Status: recurrent interpretation, not a formal cult finding.**

Soleri attracted devoted followers and occupied the symbolic and decision-making center of Arcosanti. Traits cited by family members and former associates include:

- Single-mindedness.
- Anger followed by rapid emotional recovery.
- Low tolerance for dissent.
- Refusal to compromise.
- Dependence on deferential younger followers rather than strong peers.
- Identification of the institution with his personal intellectual mission.

Some observers have described Arcosanti as cult-like or Soleri as a guru. No evidence was found of formal religious initiation, enforced seclusion, systematic confiscation of property, or a legal finding that the organization was a cult. “Cult” is therefore an **interpretive and sometimes hostile label**, meaningful as part of reputation but not an established institutional classification.

### Labor and worker-rights criticism

**Status: documented structural arrangement; evaluative claims disputed.**

Workshop participants paid to study and helped build Arcosanti. Residents and staff often received low compensation; one contemporary account stated that minimum wage was the upper limit even for Soleri.

Critics argued that:

- Paying participants furnished the project with subsidized labor.
- Workers had limited power over design and governance.
- The organization invoked idealism to normalize material sacrifice.
- Its hierarchy replicated social inequities that arcology purported to transcend.

Defenders replied that workshops were educational, voluntary, and comparable to architectural field schools or communal apprenticeships.

No located lawsuit or administrative judgment resolved whether the model violated labor law. The underlying fee-for-training-and-labor system is documented; “exploitation” remains an evaluative conclusion.

### Architectural practicality and professional conflict

Critics questioned:

- Lack of detailed construction drawings.
- Continuous on-site revisions.
- Unbuilt megastructures presented with limited engineering development.
- Dependence on a charismatic designer rather than reproducible processes.
- Incompatibility between Arcosanti’s remote location and its anti-automobile claims.
- Failure to provide sufficient jobs, schools, or services.
- Four decades of construction yielding only a small fraction of the plan.

Soleri acknowledged incompletion. He sometimes blamed his inability to recruit or persuade; Daniela placed more responsibility on his personality and refusal of sustained intellectual collaboration.

### Fraud, corruption, litigation, and crime

The requested adversarial searches combined Soleri’s name with controversy, scandal, lawsuit, fraud, accusations, conspiracy, criticism, and recent-year filters.

No credible evidence was located of:

- Financial fraud.
- Embezzlement.
- Money laundering.
- Bribery or corruption.
- Criminal conviction.
- Political violence.
- Intelligence-agency connections.
- Imprisonment.
- A major personal bankruptcy.
- Litigation establishing professional malpractice.
- A recent 2024–26 criminal or civil proceeding concerning Soleri personally.

The principal substantiated negative material is the daughter’s sexual-abuse allegation, the reported incident involving a model, institutional delay, and longstanding criticism of authoritarian governance, labor arrangements, and project failure.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Prophet in the desert”

**Story:** Soleri was a visionary ecological prophet ignored by conventional society but vindicated by later climate and urban crises.

**Appearance and circulation:** architectural criticism around the 1970 Corcoran exhibition, later museum publicity, obituaries, and Lisa Scafuro’s documentary title.

**Evidence for:** he advocated density, reduced automobile dependence, lower material consumption, passive solar design, and landscape preservation before these became standard sustainability themes.

**Evidence against or qualification:** he was not alone in ecological or megastructural urbanism; most of his proposals remained unbuilt; Arcosanti did not achieve its promised scale or self-sufficiency.

**Effect:** made Soleri an emblem of prescient environmental design while encouraging hagiographic treatment of his personality and institution.

### Arcosanti as a completed or self-sufficient “city”

**Story:** Arcosanti is a functioning arcology or completed city in the Arizona desert.

**Origin:** travel writing, tourism promotion, science-fiction comparison, and compressed media descriptions.

**Evidence:** it is a continuously occupied experimental settlement containing residential, productive, cultural, and educational functions.

**Against:** it never reached its planned population, remained dependent on external roads, supplies, employment, schools, and finance, and was perhaps 5 percent complete.

**Classification:** **promotional myth or misleading shorthand**. Arcosanti is a prototype and campus, not a completed arcology.

### Soleri as Wright’s chosen successor

**Story:** Soleri was the disciple who surpassed or was rejected by Wright because of superior vision.

**Evidence:** Wright waived tuition, Soleri produced important work at Taliesin, and several apprentices reportedly wished to leave with him.

**Qualification:** no evidence establishes Wright naming him as successor. Their relationship ended in conflict, and Soleri rejected Wright’s settlement model.

**Effect:** strengthened the dramatic father–son mythology surrounding their rupture.

### The necktie expulsion

**Story:** Wright offered Soleri a necktie for a formal event; Soleri insulted him and immediately left Taliesin.

**Source chain:** reportedly told by Soleri to Lloyd Kiva New, then retold by an architect associated with New.

**Evidence:** secondhand oral transmission; compatible with other evidence of conflict.

**Against:** no contemporaneous letter or diary was located; Soleri separately said Wright dismissed him after other apprentices wanted to follow him.

**Classification:** **reported anecdote/disputed departure story**.

### Soleri as penniless saint

**Story:** Soleri rejected possessions, paid himself almost nothing, and sacrificed all material comfort for humanity.

**Evidence:** austere environments, anti-consumerist writing, low-pay workshop culture, and reliance on bell sales rather than conventional development.

**Qualification:** Cosanti became valuable property and the project developed a recognizable commercial product line. Institutional assets should not be confused with personal wealth, but ascetic publicity also reinforced his authority and brand.

**Classification:** **partly supported hagiographic narrative**, financially underdocumented.

### Soleri as fraud or architectural charlatan

**Story:** Arcosanti was never intended to be completed and existed mainly to attract followers, fees, parties, or bell customers.

**Circulation:** hostile commentary, participant criticism, and online discussion.

**Evidence offered:** chronic incompletion, dependence on student labor, resistance to outside governance, and Soleri’s centrality.

**Evidence against:** decades of drawings, construction, publication, modest personal lifestyle, and Daniela’s statement that he was “no charlatan” and had no hidden financial agenda.

**Classification:** **unsupported or weakly supported hostile theory**. Project failure and problematic governance do not establish deliberate fraud.

### Solstice-born solar prophet

**Story:** his June 21 birth prefigured a life devoted to sunlight, desert construction, and cosmic evolution.

**Evidence:** the birth date is genuine; solar orientation was central to his work.

**Classification:** **symbolic retrospective association**, not causal history.

### Science-fiction “inventor of the arcology”

Soleri did coin or popularize the specific term and gave it a developed architectural program. Enclosed cities and megastructures preceded him, however. Later science fiction, games, and popular media detached “arcology” from his philosophical system and used it to mean almost any self-contained megastructure.

The mythology increased his cultural reach while often erasing his insistence on frugality, social intensity, and open connection to landscape.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Soleri’s death initially produced predominantly celebratory obituaries presenting him as a pioneering ecological architect whose ideas anticipated climate-conscious urbanism.

The 2017 sequence changed that reception:

1. SMoCA opened a major retrospective emphasizing his creative work.
2. Daniela Soleri published her sexual-abuse allegation one month later.
3. The Cosanti Foundation publicly supported Daniela and acknowledged institutional failings.
4. Subsequent interpretation increasingly separated arcology from Soleri’s personal authority.

His posthumous reputation now contains several concurrent strands:

- **Ecological precursor:** compact, walkable, low-consumption cities remain relevant to climate policy.
- **Visionary draftsman:** the large urban drawings retain independent artistic and architectural significance.
- **Failed master planner:** Arcosanti’s tiny population and incompletion remain central empirical objections.
- **Craft innovator:** earthcasting, bells, apses, and landscape-integrated construction remain influential regionally.
- **Authoritarian founder:** institutional dependence on him is treated as a warning about charismatic utopian projects.
- **Accused abuser:** Daniela’s allegations have permanently altered museum, foundation, and scholarly treatments.
- **Science-fiction influence:** “arcology” entered novels, games, film, futurism, and popular urban speculation, often detached from Soleri’s own philosophy.
- **Continuing institution:** Cosanti, Arcosanti, the foundation, bell production, archives, education, tours, and cultural events survived him.

Arcosanti remains neither an abandoned ruin nor the city Soleri promised. Its endurance permits continuing study, while its incompletion prevents a clean verdict on the full technical concept.

| **June 14, 1933** | Paolo and sister Maria apply to the Turin art lyceum. |
| **1941–46** | Studies architecture at the Politecnico di Torino during World War II. |
| **February 1946** | Completes architecture degree and Turin urban-renewal thesis; some English sources date the credential to 1947. |
| **December 1946/1947** | Travels to the United States after corresponding with Frank Lloyd Wright. |
| **1947–49** | Taliesin Fellowship at Taliesin West and Taliesin; produces *The Beast* bridge study. |
| **1949** | Bridge design published in Elizabeth Mock’s *The Architecture of Bridges*. |
| **1949–50** | Designs and builds Dome House with Mark Mills; meets Corolyn Woods. |
| **Circa 1950** | Marries Colly Woods and returns to Italy. |
| **1951** | Article with Mark Mills on Dome House appears in *Architectural Forum*. |
| **1954–55** | Designs Ceramica Artistica Solimene at Vietri sul Mare. |
| **1955–56** | Returns permanently to Arizona. |
| **1956** | Paolo and Colly acquire five-acre Paradise Valley site and begin Cosanti. |
| **Late 1950s** | Develops bridge cities, earthcasting, and early urban megastructures. |
| **1958** | Daughter Daniela born. |
| **1959 onward** | Develops Mesa City. |
| **1961** | Major Peter Blake coverage in *Architectural Forum*. |
| **1962** | Distinguished visiting lecturer at Arizona State University. |
| **1963** | Receives AIA Gold Medal for Craftsmanship. |
| **1964** | Receives Guggenheim fellowship. |
| **1965** | Cosanti Foundation legally/institutionally established according to ASU; publishes Cosanti proposal and Le Corbusier remembrance. |
| **Late 1960s** | Foundation acquires more than 800 acres north of Phoenix. |
| **1969** | Publishes *Arcology: The City in the Image of Man*; the term “arcology” becomes publicly established. |
| **1970** | Corcoran retrospective opens; construction begins at Arcosanti. |
| **1971** | *The Sketchbooks of Paolo Soleri* published; retrospective travels. |
| **1973** | Publishes *The Bridge Between Matter & Spirit Is Matter Becoming Spirit*. |
| **1970s** | Arcosanti vaults, apses, foundry, residences, and cultural spaces built through workshops. |
| **July 7, 1975** | Interviewed by Cynthia McCabe for Smithsonian/Hirshhorn archive. |
| **1981** | Receives Gold Medal at World Biennale of Architecture in Sofia. |
| **1982** | Colly Soleri dies of colon cancer; DeConcini House dated to this year. |
| **1983/87** | Editions of *Arcosanti: An Urban Laboratory?* appear. |
| **1984** | Publishes *Earth Casting* with Scott M. Davis and *Space for Peace*; receives Académie d’Architecture Silver Medal. |
| **1985** | Publishes *Technology and Cosmogenesis*. |
| **1986** | Designs Arizona Cancer Center Chapel. |
| **Late 1980s** | Develops Minds for History programming. |
| **Circa 1993** | Daniela says she privately discloses her father’s abuse to members of the Arcosanti circle. |
| **1995** | Glendale Community College Amphitheater. |
| **1996** | Made Honorary Fellow of the Royal Institute of British Architects. |
| **1997–99** | Paradox/technology conferences and Hyper Building work; Paradise Valley oral-history interview in 1999. |
| **2000** | Receives Golden Lion for lifetime achievement at Venice Architecture Biennale. |
| **2001** | Honorary-degree celebrations in Palermo. |
| **2002–03** | *What If? Collected Writings* and Italian anthology published. |
| **2006** | Cooper Hewitt Lifetime Achievement Award; National Building Museum interview; reported incident involving nude model Margie Goldsmith. |
| **2010** | Soleri Bridge and Plaza completed; Daniela resigns from foundation board and cites abuse in a private letter. |
| **2010** | Paolo Soleri Amphitheater in Santa Fe demolished. |
| **2011** | Soleri leaves foundation presidency/directorship; later accounts connect the action to Daniela’s disclosure. |
| **2012** | *Conversations with Paolo Soleri* published; Soleri views rough cut of Scafuro documentary. |
| **April 9, 2013** | Dies of natural causes at Cosanti, aged 93. |
| **2013** | *The Vision of Paolo Soleri: Prophet in the Desert* released. |
| **October 14, 2017** | Major SMoCA retrospective opens. |
| **November 14, 2017** | Daniela publicly alleges childhood molestation and attempted rape. |
| **December 2017 onward** | Cosanti Foundation publicly supports Daniela and begins reframing Soleri’s legacy. |
| **February 29, 2020** | Guardian publishes extended investigation and interviews concerning the allegation and institutional response. |
| **2020s** | Cosanti and Arcosanti continue under foundation governance; scholarship increasingly treats ecological innovation, project failure, labor hierarchy, and abuse allegations together. |
