# Sir Arthur Conan Doyle: Research Dossier

**Scope and method.** This dossier reconstructs Arthur Conan Doyle's life chronologically and separates documented events, Doyle's retrospective recollections, contemporary allegations, and later legend. Dates and titles have been checked against archival catalogues, the Arthur Conan Doyle Encyclopedia's bibliographical work, Doyle's autobiography and public-domain texts, institutional collections, and modern scholarship. Some family dates vary between reference works; disagreements are identified rather than silently resolved. The final source list contains only URLs consulted or relied upon.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal line

Doyle's father was **Charles Altamont Doyle** (1832–1893), an Edinburgh civil servant in the Office of Works and a gifted but commercially unsuccessful artist. Charles was a son of the political caricaturist **John Doyle** (1797–1868), who signed his work “H.B.,” and **Marianne Conan** (1795–1839). The artistic family included Charles's prominent brothers:

- **James William Edmund Doyle** (1822–1892), historical writer and illustrator;
- **Richard “Dicky” Doyle** (1824–1883), illustrator associated with *Punch* and designer of the magazine's masthead;
- **Henry Edward Doyle** (1827–1892), painter and director of the National Gallery of Ireland.

The contrast between the successful uncles and Charles's instability was marked. Charles drank heavily, experienced seizures and psychiatric deterioration, lost the capacity to support the household, and was institutionalised—first at Blairerno House, a home for men with alcoholism, then at Montrose Royal Lunatic Asylum and finally Crichton Royal Institution near Dumfries. He died there on 10 October 1893. A modern psychiatric-history article reconstructs his institutional years from records; posthumous diagnosis remains uncertain. “Alcoholism,” epilepsy or seizures, depression, and psychosis appear in the record, but a precise modern diagnosis cannot be established.

### Maternal line

Doyle's mother was **Mary Josephine Elizabeth Foley** (8 July 1837–30 December 1920), called **“the Ma'am.”** Her parents were William Foley and Catherine Pack. Mary was an accomplished storyteller and remained Arthur's central confidante. Hundreds of his letters to her survive. Her tales of ancestry, honour, history, and adventure fed his historical imagination; she also proposed or refined plots, including elements associated with “The Copper Beeches.”

Doyle and family tradition claimed descent through the Foley, Pack, Percy, Butler, and related lines from the Percys of Northumberland and ultimately Plantagenet royalty. Undershaw's stained glass displayed these heraldic claims. The family tradition is well documented as something Doyle believed and advertised; a complete modern genealogical proof of every link was not established in the sources consulted.

Doyle experienced the deaths of his son Kingsley, brother Innes, nephews, mother, Hornung, and sisters in close succession; these losses intensified—but did not originate—his Spiritualism.

### First marriage: Louisa Hawkins

Doyle met **Louisa Hawkins**, called **Touie**, through her brother Jack, a patient in his Southsea practice. They married on **6 August 1885**. Her birth year is inconsistently given: a specialist family page gives **10 April 1857**, while another family table gives **1854**. The 1857 date is more frequently reproduced, but the discrepancy should remain visible.

Children:

- **Mary Louise Conan Doyle** (28 January 1889–1976), who did not marry and later defended and curated aspects of her father's memory;
- **Arthur Alleyne Kingsley Conan Doyle** (“Kingsley,” 15 November 1892–28 October 1918), soldier and physician-in-training; wounded at the Somme in 1916, later died during the influenza pandemic, generally recorded as pneumonia following influenza.

Louisa developed tuberculosis in the early 1890s. Doyle sought healthful climates for her in Switzerland, Egypt, Surrey, and elsewhere and built Undershaw at Hindhead partly for its dry air. In 1906 she developed further complications described in specialist summaries as a tumour and partial paralysis. She died on **4 July 1906**.

### Jean Leckie and the long courtship

Doyle met **Jean Elizabeth Leckie** (14 March 1874–27 June 1940) on **15 March 1897** and fell in love with her while Louisa was alive. Doyle, Jean, his mother, and other relatives preserved extensive secrecy. Family and most biographical accounts say the relationship remained sexually platonic until Louisa's death, although Arthur and Jean met, corresponded, exchanged gifts, walked together, and maintained an emotionally intimate relationship for almost a decade. This “platonic” description is principally grounded in their own and family evidence; it cannot be independently demonstrated in the way a public event can. There is no documentary proof in the consulted record of a sexual affair before 1906. Louisa apparently was not told.

Arthur and Jean married on **18 September 1907**. Their children were:

- **Denis Percy Stewart Conan Doyle** (17 March 1909–1955), later involved in management of the literary estate; husband of Georgian-born Nina Mdivani;
- **Adrian Malcolm Conan Doyle** (19 November 1910–3 June 1970), writer, big-game hunter, collaborator on pastiche Holmes stories, and estate representative;
- **Lena Annette Jean Conan Doyle**, later Dame Jean Conan Doyle (21 December 1912–18 November 1997), an RAF officer during the Second World War and later a major custodian of the archive and copyrights.

Jean practised automatic writing and was an active partner in Doyle's Spiritualist mission, not merely a companion to it.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1859–1876

Doyle's first years were geographically unsettled. The residence record places the family at 11 Picardy Place (1859–1861), 3 Tower Bank in Portobello (1861–1864), a period of household dispersal as Charles's drinking worsened, Liberton Bank House with family friend Mary Burton (approximately 1866–1868), and 3 Sciennes Hill Place (1868–1875). The childhood household alternated between genteel cultural aspiration and acute financial insecurity.

At about nine, aided by wealthier relatives, Arthur was sent to **Hodder Place**, the preparatory school attached to the Jesuit **Stonyhurst College** in Lancashire (Hodder, 1868–1870; Stonyhurst, 1870–1875). He later described harsh discipline, intellectual rigidity, homesickness, and schoolboy violence, but also developed as a raconteur who traded stories for an audience among other boys. He read Walter Scott, Thomas Babington Macaulay, Jules Verne, Edgar Allan Poe, and adventure and historical literature. He contributed to or organised a school paper remembered as the *Stonyhurst Figaro*.

In 1875–1876 he spent a further year at the Jesuit **Stella Matutina** school in Feldkirch, Austria, improving his German and broadening his European experience. By his own later account, Jesuit schooling had driven him away from Catholic dogma. He moved from inherited Roman Catholicism to agnosticism or religious scepticism, later toward a non-creedal Spiritualism that he presented as experimentally established religion.

## Education and Formation, 1876–1885

### University of Edinburgh medical school

Doyle entered the **University of Edinburgh** medical school in 1876. Because the family needed income, he combined study with assistantships. Important teachers and models included:

- **Dr Joseph Bell**, surgeon at Edinburgh Royal Infirmary, for whom Doyle served as clerk or dresser. Bell inferred patients' occupations, origins, and histories from small physical and verbal details. Doyle explicitly told Bell in an 1892 letter that Holmes grew from Bell's teaching of observation, inference, and deduction.
- **Professor William Rutherford**, physiologist, whose physical presence and forceful manner contributed to Professor Challenger.
- Edinburgh's wider clinical culture, including the influence of antiseptic surgery and diagnostic observation. Claims that Doyle personally studied under every famous Edinburgh doctor sometimes overstate direct contact; Bell's influence is the best documented.
- **Dr Bryan Charles Waller**, a lodger, pathologist, and important financial and household presence in the Doyle family. Biographers differ sharply in their interpretation of Waller's role and relationship with Mary Doyle; no definitive evidence establishes the more sensational readings.

Student medical assistantships took him to Sheffield for roughly three weeks in spring 1878; Ruyton-XI-Towns, Shropshire, for several months in 1878; and Aston/Birmingham with Dr Reginald Ratcliff Hoare in 1879–1880. These furnished material for *The Stark Munro Letters* and medical tales.

His first known published fiction appeared while he was a student: **“The Mystery of Sasassa Valley”** in *Chambers's Journal* (6 September 1879) and **“The American's Tale”** in *London Society* (December 1879), both initially anonymous.

### Arctic whaling, 1880

From February to August or September 1880, Doyle served as an unqualified ship's surgeon aboard the Peterhead whaler **Hope**, under Captain John Gray, in Greenland waters. He treated injuries, joined seal and whale hunting, boxed, fell repeatedly into icy water, and kept an illustrated diary. The trip paid well enough to aid his family and supplied “The Captain of the Polestar,” “The Glamour of the Arctic,” and later memoir passages. The original diary was published posthumously as *Dangerous Work: Diary of an Arctic Adventure* (2012); it is genuine contemporary writing, unlike retrospective embellishment.

### Degrees and West Africa

He qualified **M.B., C.M.** in 1881 (the graduation date is commonly given as 22 October). Soon afterward he became medical officer aboard the African Steam Navigation Company's **SS Mayumba**, sailing to the West African coast. He experienced fire and storm aboard ship and a severe fever at Lagos, usually identified retrospectively as malaria, though the exact diagnosis cannot now be proved. He returned early in 1882.

His **M.D.** followed in 1885 for a thesis generally titled **“An Essay upon the Vasomotor Changes in Tabes Dorsalis.”** The thesis belongs to neurology and late-stage syphilitic disease; it did not lead to a research career.

## Early Medical and Literary Career, 1882–1891

In early 1882 Doyle briefly joined his former acquaintance **Dr George Turnavine Budd** in Plymouth. Budd was charismatic, financially erratic, and professionally combative. The partnership lasted about six weeks. Doyle then moved to **1 Bush Villas, Elm Grove, Southsea**, in June 1882 and established a general practice with very little capital. Later summaries sometimes call this an ophthalmology practice; that is inaccurate for the Southsea years. His specific ophthalmology training and proposed eye practice came later.

The slow practice gave him time to write, while medical fees provided intermittent support. He published fiction and semi-scientific pieces, including the Mary Celeste fantasy **“J. Habakuk Jephson's Statement”** (1884). Because readers confused it with fact, the story influenced later Mary Celeste mythology—including the erroneous ship name “Marie Celeste.” This is an early example of Doyle's fiction becoming public pseudo-history.

He married Louisa in 1885. In March–April 1886 he wrote *A Tangled Skein*, retitled **A Study in Scarlet**. Ward, Lock & Co. purchased all rights for **£25** after multiple rejections; it appeared in *Beeton's Christmas Annual* dated 1887 and in book form in 1888, illustrated in that edition by Charles Doyle. The flat-fee transaction later appeared exploitative in light of Holmes's value, but there is no evidence of fraud: Doyle knowingly accepted an all-rights offer in a weak bargaining position.

He joined **Phoenix Lodge No. 257**, Southsea, as a Freemason: initiated 26 January 1887, passed in February, raised a Master Mason 23 March. He resigned in 1889, rejoined in 1902, and remained until 1911. Claims that Sherlock Holmes is a coded Masonic corpus go beyond the membership record; Masonic language, fraternal networks, and motifs can be documented, but an encompassing secret allegory has not been demonstrated.

Historical fiction mattered more to Doyle's self-conception than detective fiction. *Micah Clarke* (1889), set amid the Monmouth Rebellion, secured serious attention. At J. M. Stoddart's London dinner in August 1889, Doyle and **Oscar Wilde** were separately commissioned for *Lippincott's Monthly Magazine*. The results were Doyle's **The Sign of the Four** (1890) and Wilde's **The Picture of Dorian Gray**. The familiar story of the dinner is supported by Doyle's recollection and publication history, although polished dialogue reported decades later should be treated as memoir, not transcript.

In 1890–1891 Doyle went to Vienna for ophthalmological study. His German proved inadequate for some instruction; he returned to London and opened an eye consulting room, usually located by sources at **2 Upper Wimpole Street** or the Devonshire Place area. He later joked that no patient ever crossed the threshold. The failure freed him to write full-time.

## Chronological Life History

### 1891–1894: the Strand, celebrity, and the death of Holmes

The decisive commercial event was publication of **“A Scandal in Bohemia”** in the July 1891 *Strand Magazine*, illustrated by **Sidney Paget**. Eleven further Holmes stories followed monthly. Doyle received 30 guineas apiece for the first group; rising circulation and demand soon enabled much larger fees. The visual Holmes—deerstalker in country scenes, sharp profile, capes—was partly Paget's construction, not Doyle's alone.

Doyle abandoned medicine in 1891. *The White Company* appeared the same year and remained one of his own favourites. By late 1891 he complained to his mother that Holmes blocked “better things.” After a second sequence, he sent Holmes and Professor Moriarty over the Reichenbach Falls in **“The Final Problem”** (December 1893).

Public protest is documented: cancelled subscriptions, angry letters, press complaints, and sustained publisher pressure. The often-repeated story that London clerks wore black mourning bands is not securely established by contemporary evidence and is best treated as later Holmes folklore.

Louisa's tuberculosis was diagnosed around this period. The family spent winter 1892–1893 in **Davos**, Switzerland. Doyle skied there using Norwegian equipment and publicised the sport in “An Alpine Pass on Ski.” He helped popularise skiing among English-speaking visitors in the Alps. The stronger claim that he single-handedly “introduced skiing to Switzerland” is a promotional simplification: forms of skiing and other practitioners were already present.

In 1893 he joined the **Society for Psychical Research (SPR)**. His interest in séances, telepathy, and survival after death had begun by the mid-1880s; this predates the First World War by approximately three decades.

In 1894 he toured the United States and Canada as a lecturer, met or visited literary figures including Rudyard Kipling in Vermont, and maintained correspondence with Robert Louis Stevenson. Henry Irving performed Doyle's one-act **A Story of Waterloo**. Doyle also collaborated unhappily with J. M. Barrie on the comic operetta **Jane Annie** (1893); it failed.

### 1895–1899: Egypt, Sudan, Undershaw, and Jean

Doyle and Louisa travelled to Egypt in 1895–1896. As conflict with Mahdist forces developed, he reported from the region for the *Westminster Gazette*. The journey contributed to **The Tragedy of the Korosko** (serial 1897; book 1898), an imperial adventure based on tourist vulnerability and frontier violence.

He produced the Brigadier Gerard stories, *Rodney Stone*, sea tales, medical tales, and plays. He met Jean Leckie in 1897. That same year the family moved into **Undershaw**, a house he commissioned at Hindhead, Surrey, designed for Louisa's health and funded by literary success.

The stage **Sherlock Holmes**, developed with American actor-playwright **William Gillette**, opened in 1899. Doyle's initial dramatic material was extensively reshaped; authorship is properly joint in its successful form. Gillette's performance, pipe, phrases, and romanticisation helped form the global Holmes icon. “Elementary, my dear Watson” does not appear as a complete phrase in Doyle's canonical Holmes stories; it emerged from adaptation and popular memory, often associated with the stage and early sound-film tradition.

### 1899–1902: South African War, politics, and knighthood

When the Second South African War began, Doyle tried to enlist in a military unit but was not accepted for combat service. He instead served from March to June 1900—some summaries extend the interval to August—as a volunteer senior physician at **John Langman's privately funded field hospital** in Bloemfontein. Enteric fever caused more work than wounds. Doyle observed British troops and wartime disease but was not a neutral observer of the broader war.

He rapidly published **The Great Boer War** (first edition 1900, repeatedly revised). His pamphlet **The War in South Africa: Its Cause and Conduct** (16 January 1902) defended British policy against foreign and domestic allegations. It acknowledged high camp mortality but characterised the concentration camps primarily as a humanitarian consequence of displaced civilians, defended British soldiers against allegations of systematic abuse, and presented the war as justified. The pamphlet circulated in numerous languages and large quantities.

Documented context: British scorched-earth policy, forced civilian removal, and catastrophic disease and mortality in camps—especially among Boer and Black African civilians—are established in the historical record. Doyle's pamphlet is therefore both a source for contemporary imperial apologetics and an evidence-based defence as he understood it; it should not be treated as a detached adjudication of British conduct.

Doyle stood as a **Liberal Unionist** for Edinburgh Central in October 1900, supporting the Union and the war. He lost to C. M. Brown, approximately 3,028 votes to 2,459. He stood again for the Border/Hawick Burghs in January 1906 and again lost. His party label moved in common description from Liberal Unionist toward Unionist, but he did not become a parliamentary officeholder.

He was notified of a knighthood in 1902. King Edward VII dubbed him a **Knight Bachelor at Buckingham Palace on 24 October 1902**. Doyle believed the honour recognised the South Africa pamphlet and war service, not Holmes. He was also appointed **Deputy Lieutenant of Surrey** and in 1903 Knight of Grace of the Order of St John.

### 1901–1907: Holmes returns, Louisa dies, Edalji campaign

**The Hound of the Baskervilles** ran in the *Strand* from 1901 to 1902 but was set before Holmes's supposed death. Large offers, especially from American publishers, brought Holmes fully back in **“The Empty House”** (1903). The resurrection was a commercial decision as well as a response to audience demand.

Louisa's health worsened. Doyle's second parliamentary defeat in early 1906 was followed by her death on 4 July. Contemporary and later family sources describe a period of prostration or near-depression.

He then took up **George Edalji's** case. Edalji, a Birmingham solicitor and son of an Anglican vicar of Parsi ancestry, had been convicted in 1903 of mutilating a pony during the “Great Wyrley outrages” and sentenced to seven years' penal servitude. Released in 1906 without exoneration, he asked Doyle for help.

Doyle interviewed him, inspected sites and documents, publicised his severe myopia, challenged supposed blood and hair evidence, attacked police reasoning, and published a series of articles. A Home Office committee in 1907 effectively cleared Edalji of the animal attack and restored his ability to practise law but denied compensation, asserting that he had written some anonymous letters and thus brought suspicion on himself.

**Finding status:** Edalji's conviction for maiming was formally pardoned as unsafe; Doyle's role was substantial. It is less accurate to say Doyle “proved every aspect of his innocence.” The committee retained an adverse, disputed conclusion about letters. Later discovery of a defence lawyer's suppressed handwriting material complicated the case further. The episode helped build pressure for the **Court of Criminal Appeal**, created in 1907, but it was one cause among several, not the sole cause.

Doyle married Jean in September 1907 and moved to **Windlesham**, Crowborough, where he remained until death.

### 1908–1913: reform campaigns, Congo, Slater, motoring, Challenger

Doyle reported on the dramatic finish of the 1908 Olympic marathon for the *Daily Mail*. Italian runner **Dorando Pietri** entered the stadium first, collapsed repeatedly, received assistance, and was disqualified. Doyle's account helped make Pietri an international hero. Doyle supported a public subscription for a silver cup. The claim that Doyle personally handed Pietri £5 is repeated but not firmly documented; Doyle himself denied being the race official who helped him across the line.

In **The Crime of the Congo** (1909), Doyle synthesised missionary testimony, the Casement Report, E. D. Morel's campaign, photographs, and other evidence against forced labour, hostage-taking, mutilation, killing, and concession-company rule in Leopold II's Congo. He wrote to political leaders and lent celebrity to the Congo Reform Association. His attack on Belgian imperial violence coexisted with support for the British Empire; there is no contradiction in the documentary sense—both positions were explicit parts of his politics.

He supported **divorce-law reform**, arguing that unequal and restrictive law produced concealed relationships, desertion, and injustice. On women's suffrage, he accepted aspects of women's civic claims but opposed militant suffragette tactics and warned of violent reaction. His precise position shifted with context; he cannot be reduced either to a suffrage advocate or an across-the-board opponent.

In 1911 he participated in the **Prince Henry Tour**, driving a 16-horsepower Dietrich-Lorraine from Germany toward Britain in an international reliability event. He was interested in motor vehicles, aviation, submarines, naval defence, and technical military questions but held no major patent found in the consulted sources.

The **Oscar Slater** case began with the 1908 murder of Marion Gilchrist in Glasgow. Slater, a German-born Jewish gambler with a disreputable reputation, was convicted in 1909 on weak identification and circumstantial evidence, sentenced to death, and reprieved to life imprisonment. Doyle studied the record and published **The Case of Oscar Slater** (1912), identifying suggestive identification procedures, withheld or mishandled evidence, and an implausible prosecution theory. He did not cause the original reprieve, contrary to some compressed biographies; his campaign helped sustain the case.

**The Lost World** (1912) introduced Professor Challenger and established a durable “lost world” template, though it belonged to an existing tradition of imperial exploration romance. Doyle theatrically posed with companions as the expedition cast for publicity. *The Poison Belt* followed in 1913.

### 1914–1918: First World War

At 55 Doyle was too old for normal front-line enlistment. He helped organise the Crowborough volunteer company, later associated with the 6th Royal Sussex Volunteer Regiment. He attended the secret September 1914 writers' meeting convened by **Charles Masterman at Wellington House**, the British War Propaganda Bureau, signed the Authors' Declaration defending Britain's entry, and published **To Arms!**, *The German War*, and other pro-war writing. This was an overt and, initially, covertly coordinated connection to state propaganda—not an intelligence-service career. No evidence in the consulted record shows Doyle as an operational spy.

He visited the Western, Italian, and French fronts in 1916, met military and political figures, and wrote **The British Campaign in France and Flanders**, six volumes published 1916–1920. Access to commanders gave him information but also embedded him within official networks. The work is useful contemporary military history and a patriotic narrative with limited independence from British assumptions.

Doyle publicly proposed or pressed for:

- steel helmets and body armour to reduce wounds;
- large shields and protective equipment;
- improved naval life-saving provision;
- channel-tunnel and national-defence preparations;
- tactical changes in response to machine guns and submarines.

Some proposals anticipated or coincided with adopted measures; claims that he personally invented the British steel helmet or single-handedly caused adoption are not supported. He worked through letters, prototypes, lobbying, and press pressure rather than a patented industrial enterprise.

Kingsley was badly wounded at the Somme in 1916 but recovered sufficiently to return to service and later died in 1918 of influenza/pneumonia. Doyle's brother Innes died in February 1919. Doyle also lost nephews and close associates.

He sought clemency for **Roger Casement**, convicted of treason after seeking German aid for Irish independence. Doyle argued that Casement's prior humanitarian service and possible mental disturbance merited mercy. Casement was hanged on 3 August 1916. Doyle's intervention failed. The later controversy over Casement's “Black Diaries” is separate: Doyle's clemency position did not depend on proving every diary question.

### Religious transformation during the war

Doyle publicly announced his commitment to Spiritualism in *Light* in October 1916 and gave his first major public lecture in 1917. **The New Revelation** (1918) and **The Vital Message** (1919) framed spirit communication as empirical confirmation of survival, a new revelation compatible with selected Christian ethics but opposed to church dogma.

The widespread story that Kingsley's death converted Doyle is chronologically false. Doyle had read Spiritualist literature, attended sittings, experimented with telepathy, and joined the SPR long before Kingsley was born. Wartime bereavement changed the scale, urgency, and public centrality of the commitment.

### 1919–1924: international Spiritualist missionary and Houdini

Doyle devoted increasing proportions of his fortune and time to lectures, sittings, publications, and organisations. He toured **Australia and New Zealand in 1920–1921**, then the **United States and Canada in 1922 and 1923**. His wife and three younger children often travelled with him. His travel books mix itinerary, colonial observation, polemic, and reports of mediums.

He met **Harry Houdini** in person around 1920 after correspondence and introductions. Their friendship rested on celebrity, athletic self-fashioning, family sociability, and shared interest in survival after death, but not agreement about mediums. Houdini hoped for evidence of an afterlife yet recognised conjuring methods and exposed fraudulent practitioners. Doyle accepted much physical and mental mediumship as genuine and, remarkably, came to believe Houdini's escapes themselves involved dematerialisation or psychic power despite Houdini's insistence that they were tricks.

Houdini initially muted his scepticism, accepted Doyle's introductions to British mediums, and treated the older writer respectfully. Doyle interpreted Houdini's continued investigation as openness to conversion. Their correspondence shows genuine affection alongside growing mutual frustration.

#### The Atlantic City séance, 18 June 1922

At the Ambassador Hotel in Atlantic City, **Lady Jean Conan Doyle** offered to contact Houdini's deceased mother, Cecilia Steiner Weiss, through automatic writing. She produced approximately fifteen pages, opening with a Christian cross and in fluent English. Houdini later objected that his Jewish mother did not use English and that the message failed to acknowledge her birthday, which fell that day. Some details of what Houdini said immediately afterward vary: the Doyles believed him moved and receptive; Houdini later said he had concealed his disbelief out of courtesy.

The séance did not by itself end the friendship. It created a private injury and became public evidence in later conflict. In 1923 the men disputed mediumistic cases in letters and newspapers. Their overlapping American lecture tours sharpened the divide. Houdini's **A Magician Among the Spirits** (1924) attacked fraudulent mediumship and criticised Doyle's standards; Doyle defended mediums and accused Houdini of prejudice and publicity-seeking. Both still sometimes expressed admiration for the other.

#### Margery, Pecoraro, and contested tests

Doyle supported or credited mediums Houdini challenged, including the Boston medium **Mina “Margery” Crandon** and the tied medium **Nino Pecoraro**. Scientific American committee tests and later observers documented methods and episodes consistent with fraud; Pecoraro eventually confessed trickery. Margery's case included adverse observations, an unfavourable Harvard group, and later identification of supposed spirit evidence with ordinary biological or human sources. Doyle attacked some investigators as biased and continued to defend her.

The friendship therefore became a **feud-friendship**: affection survived doctrinal breakdown, while each man's public identity benefited from and was damaged by the other. Doyle later called Houdini extraordinarily brave while maintaining he was psychically gifted and spiritually blind. After Houdini's death in 1926, Doyle interpreted reports of posthumous communication as confirmation; Houdini's widow Bess later participated in annual séances but ultimately announced that the agreed code had not been received.

### 1920–1922: Cottingley fairies

The first two Cottingley photographs were made in 1917 by cousins **Elsie Wright**, then sixteen, and **Frances Griffiths**, then nine. They showed Frances or Elsie with small winged figures. The pictures moved through family and Theosophical networks to **Edward L. Gardner**, then to Doyle in 1920 while the *Strand* had commissioned him to write on fairies.

Doyle did not simply accept the first prints without inquiry. He and Gardner sought photographic opinions:

- technician **Harold Snelling** said the negatives were straightforward photographs of whatever had been before the camera and showed no studio manipulation; this was not proof the objects were living fairies;
- Kodak staff reportedly found no conclusive technical alteration but refused an authenticity certificate;
- Ilford personnel reportedly saw evidence of fabrication;
- physicist and psychical researcher **Sir Oliver Lodge**, himself sympathetic to psychic research, suspected a fake and thought the fairy figures resembled dancers.

Gardner supplied a camera and plates; the girls made three further images in 1920. Doyle published **“Fairies Photographed”** in the December 1920 *Strand* and another article in 1921, then **The Coming of the Fairies** (1922). He treated the photographs as strong evidence for nature spirits and as a possible bridge toward broader acceptance of psychic realities. Contemporary critics immediately noted flatness, fashionable hair, storybook poses, and the ease of using cut-outs. Major John Hall-Edwards and writers such as Maurice Hewlett mocked or challenged the claims.

**Later confession and evidence:** In 1983 Elsie and Frances acknowledged that the principal photographs used paper/card figures copied from Claude Shepperson illustrations in *Princess Mary's Gift Book*, fitted with drawn wings and supported with hatpins. Elsie said all five were false; Frances continued to claim that the fifth, a “fairy bower,” was genuine. Geoffrey Crawley proposed a double exposure for the fifth. Their confession, source illustration, technical appearance, and physical method establish fabrication of the famous figure photographs. Their continued statement that they had genuinely seen fairies is a belief claim, not independently verifiable evidence.

### 1922: William Hope and spirit photography

Psychical investigator **Harry Price**, working with the Society for Psychical Research, tested spirit photographer **William Hope** by secretly marking photographic plates. The resulting “spirit” image appeared on substituted plates, evidence of switching. Doyle rejected Price's conclusion, defended Hope and the Crewe Circle in **The Case for Spirit Photography** (1922), and attacked investigators he regarded as hostile.

This was not merely a difference over an unexplained photograph. The marked-plate experiment supplied positive evidence of substitution. Later historical and museum scholarship describes Hope as almost certainly fraudulent. Doyle's defence demonstrates a recurring evidentiary asymmetry: testimony and apparently successful sittings counted heavily for genuineness, while exposure could be attributed to investigator misconduct, one bad sitting, or malicious scepticism.

### 1923–1929: organisations, bookshop, tours, Slater vindication

From 1923 Doyle maintained a London flat at Buckingham Palace Mansions. In 1925 he opened the **Psychic Bookshop and Library** in Victoria Street, London, as a distribution and organising centre. It was a mission-funded enterprise, not a successful investment: specialist records give annual rent of **£750**, personally supported by Doyle, and report that it did not earn a real profit. He also financed the Psychic Press and Spiritualist activities. Claims that Spiritualism consumed his entire fortune are exaggerated; probate shows a substantial estate, but the expenditures were material.

He was honorary president of the 1925 International Spiritualist Congress in Paris and chaired or played a leading role in later congresses. He published the two-volume **History of Spiritualism** (1926), the Spiritualist Challenger novel **The Land of Mist** (1926), **Pheneas Speaks** (1927), and extensive pamphlets and press letters.

Oscar Slater's case revived after William Park's **The Truth About Oscar Slater** (1927), with a foreword by Doyle. Scottish legislation enabled an appeal; Slater's conviction was quashed in July 1928, after nearly nineteen years' imprisonment, and he received **£6,000** compensation.

A bitter financial dispute followed. Doyle and supporters had funded legal work; Doyle asked Slater to reimburse contributors, including approximately £300 connected with Doyle. Slater accused Doyle of profiting from writing about him; Doyle denied making money from the Scottish press campaign. Their exchange became public and ended the association. **Documented result:** Doyle materially aided the campaign; Slater was freed and compensated; the two then quarrelled over expenses. **Unresolved character claims:** descriptions of Slater as ungrateful or Doyle as grasping come from partisan accounts and do not settle every accounting detail.

Spiritualist tours continued through South Africa, Rhodesia, Kenya, continental Europe, Holland, and Scandinavia. **Our African Winter** records the 1928–1929 journey. His observations use the racial and imperial vocabulary of his period and sometimes treat colonised societies hierarchically, even as he condemned particular abuses.

### 1929–1930: cardiac illness and final campaign

By 1929 Doyle had angina and recurrent cardiac attacks. Doctors advised rest, but he continued lecturing and campaigning. A fire badly damaged **Bignell Wood**, the family's New Forest property, on 15 August 1929; photographs show the family salvaging furniture.

His last major public cause was legal treatment of mediums under old witchcraft and vagrancy provisions. On **1 July 1930**, visibly ill, he met Home Secretary J. R. Clynes and argued for reform. He died six days later.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Literary and publishing business

Doyle operated principally as an individual author contracting with publishers and periodicals rather than through a large personal company. Major relationships included Ward, Lock & Co.; George Newnes and the *Strand*; Smith, Elder & Co.; Hodder & Stoughton; John Murray; American magazines and lecture agents; and actor William Gillette.

Key financial markers:

- **£25** for all rights to *A Study in Scarlet*;
- **30 guineas each** for the first twelve *Strand* Holmes stories;
- escalating, unusually high fees for later Holmes series and American rights, which enabled houses, travel, household support, and philanthropy;
- gross estate at probate: **£83,491**, net personalty **£55,708**, under a will dated 6 May 1930;
- annuity of **£250 per year** to long-serving secretary **Alfred Wood**;
- Psychic Bookshop rent reported at **£750 per year**, paid personally.

Doyle was listed at death as a director of **Raphael Tuck & Sons Ltd**, the art-card and publishing company. The exact scale of his shareholding or remuneration was not established in the consulted sources.

### Property

Principal substantial property included Undershaw at Hindhead; Windlesham at Crowborough; and Bignell Wood near Minstead, purchased in 1925. He also kept London premises. Undershaw's design, stained glass, sporting grounds, and sickroom arrangements represented the conversion of literary income into family property.

### Psychic Bookshop and Psychic Press

The Psychic Bookshop was a physical shop, library, lecture and distribution hub. It carried Spiritualist literature and issued Doyle-associated material through Psychic Press. It remained subsidised. In May 1930 Doyle considered converting it into a company but died before completion.

### Technical and military projects

Doyle did not establish a durable technology company and no central patent portfolio was found. His technical interventions were public proposals and trials: body armour, shields, helmets, naval safety, military tactics, motor reliability, and defence preparation. He sometimes tested materials and corresponded with manufacturers. Attribution should therefore be “campaigner, proposer, or prototype sponsor,” not inventor of every device later adopted.

### Literary estate and later litigation

After his death, children and descendants managed copyrights through changing estate arrangements. In **Klinger v. Conan Doyle Estate Ltd.** (2013–2014), U.S. courts rejected the estate's effort to treat the characters as wholly protected until the last stories expired; Judge Richard Posner criticised a licensing strategy that sought fees for public-domain elements. The final U.S. Holmes copyrights expired at the end of 2022, placing the complete canon in the U.S. public domain in 2023. These were posthumous estate controversies, not misconduct adjudicated against Doyle personally.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Political structure

Doyle's politics combined Unionism, patriotism, social reform, humanitarian campaigns, and imperial hierarchy:

- Liberal Unionist/Unionist parliamentary candidate, 1900 and 1906;
- opponent of immediate Irish Home Rule for much of his career, later more receptive to qualified Home Rule as conditions changed;
- defender of the British cause in South Africa and the First World War;
- critic of Leopoldian and Belgian rule in Congo;
- advocate of criminal-appeal reform, divorce reform, military preparedness, and clemency in selected cases;
- critic of militant suffrage tactics;
- supporter of compulsory vaccination in contemporary public-health controversies;
- public nationalist and imperialist, without evidence of party office or elected power.

### Empire, race, religion, and representation

Doyle's fiction and non-fiction contain assumptions and vocabulary common to late Victorian and Edwardian imperial writing: racial typologies, civilisational ranking, exoticised peoples, and confidence in British authority. Specific texts generated later controversy:

- the Mormon section of *A Study in Scarlet* depicts coercion and murder through a sensational secret-police image; the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints disputed it, and Doyle later acknowledged he had written from hostile sources while not fully retracting the dramatic picture;
- stories including “The Adventure of the Three Gables” use racial caricature and slurs about Black characters;
- Jewish, Romani, colonial, Irish, American, and other figures are at times constructed through period stereotypes, though individual works vary;
- *The Crime of the Congo* uses humanitarian outrage but often speaks about Africans rather than from Congolese testimony directly;
- South Africa writing defended empire and under-described Black African experience.

These are textual facts and contexts; they do not establish a single uniform private doctrine about every group.

### From Catholicism to agnosticism to Spiritualism

1. **Childhood:** Roman Catholic family and Jesuit schooling.
2. **Young adulthood:** rejection of Catholic dogma; agnosticism and confidence in science, observation, evolution, and moral conduct.
3. **1880s–1890s:** reading in psychical research and Spiritualism; experimental séances; interest in telepathy; SPR membership.
4. **1916 onward:** public declaration that spirit communication and survival were proved facts; Christ treated as a supreme psychic and ethical teacher rather than in orthodox Trinitarian terms.
5. **1920s:** missionary Spiritualism, institutional leadership, defence of physical mediums, fairies, spirit photography, automatic writing, and prophecy.

Doyle framed this not as surrender of reason but as expansion of science to neglected evidence. Critics, including Houdini and members of psychical-research bodies, argued that his standards failed to control for conjuring, sensory leakage, suggestion, and fraud.

### Literary aesthetics

Doyle ranked historical romance above detective fiction. Scott, Macaulay, Froissart, Poe, Stevenson, and adventure narrative were formative. Holmes joined Poe's analytic detective tradition with Bell's clinical method and serial magazine economics. Professor Challenger combined scientific authority, comic aggression, imperial expedition, and eventually Spiritualist conversion. Brigadier Gerard allowed affectionate satire of Napoleonic courage and vanity.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, and Financial Questions

### No personal criminal conviction or corruption case

No evidence was found that Doyle was arrested, indicted, convicted, or formally found liable for fraud, corruption, money laundering, political violence, or intelligence crimes. His controversies concern political advocacy, evidentiary judgment, private relationships, public accusations against others, and posthumous copyright management.

### Relationship with Jean during Louisa's life

- **Documented:** love beginning in 1897; meetings and concealed emotional intimacy; family knowledge; Louisa apparently uninformed.
- **Family/biographical claim:** no sexual consummation before Louisa's death.
- **Unresolved:** private sexual facts cannot be proved from the surviving public record. No affirmative evidence of adultery was found.

### Boer War apologetics

- **Documented:** Doyle defended British causes and conduct and minimised or reframed camp policy.
- **Established historical counter-record:** forced removals, scorched-earth operations, and severe civilian mortality occurred.
- **Status:** political polemic based on selected evidence, not a criminal allegation against Doyle.

### Wartime propaganda and “intelligence connections”

- **Documented:** secret Wellington House meeting, coordinated publications, access to commanders, patriotic war histories.
- **Not established:** operational intelligence service, espionage, covert violence, or secret-agent status.

### Mediums and fraud

Doyle repeatedly endorsed people later exposed or strongly evidenced as fraudulent, including William Hope and Nino Pecoraro, and defended Mina Crandon against adverse tests. In each instance, distinguish:

- Doyle's sincerity: no evidence shows he knowingly participated in fabrication;
- the medium's conduct: plate switching, loosened restraints, concealed objects, ordinary-source fingerprints or materials, and later confession vary by case;
- Doyle's public responsibility: his celebrity supplied credibility and audiences to disputed practitioners.

### Cottingley

Doyle did not create the fake cut-outs or take the photographs. He investigated imperfectly, interpreted inconclusive technical findings as confirmation, published and amplified the claim, and never lived to see the 1983 confession.

### Edalji

Doyle's investigative work corrected a miscarriage of justice, but his assertion of complete innocence extended beyond the Home Office's finding about anonymous letters. Modern legal-historical work remains attentive to withheld handwriting evidence. The animal-maiming conviction itself was unsafe and pardoned.

### Oscar Slater expenses

After vindication, Doyle and Slater publicly disputed reimbursement and literary profit. Surviving letters and auction descriptions document the conflict. No court finding of misappropriation or fraud against either man resulted.

### Estate copyright strategy

U.S. appellate criticism of the estate's licensing demands occurred many decades after Doyle's death. It belongs to legacy management, not his personal acts.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. “The rational creator of Holmes suddenly went mad after his son died”

- **Story:** grief transformed a pure rationalist into a credulous occultist.
- **Appearance/circulation:** obituaries, popular biographies, sceptical retellings, film and journalism.
- **Evidence against:** interest in Spiritualism by 1886, sittings in the 1880s, SPR membership in 1893, public conversion before Kingsley's 1918 death.
- **Historical effect:** supplies a neat irony—Holmes versus fairies—but erases Doyle's long attempt to unite science and survival research.

### 2. Cottingley as “photographs that fooled Sherlock Holmes”

- **Story:** two children effortlessly fooled the world's greatest detective.
- **Origin:** contemporary mockery from 1920 onward; later crystallised after the confession.
- **Evidence:** Doyle was not Holmes; he sought expert opinions, some misleadingly favourable, but discounted adverse evidence and ordinary explanations. The figures were cut-outs.
- **Effect:** became the defining hostile myth of Doyle's later life and an icon of motivated belief.

### 3. Houdini and Doyle as instant enemies

- **Story:** believer and magician met as opposites and immediately feuded.
- **Evidence against:** affectionate correspondence, family visits, introductions, and mutual admiration preceded a gradual split. The 1922 séance wounded trust; public rupture developed in 1923–1924.
- **Effect:** supports dramatic adaptations such as *Houdini & Doyle*, which invent joint criminal investigations without historical basis.

### 4. Houdini possessed supernatural powers

- **Story:** Doyle believed Houdini dematerialised, used psychic gifts, and concealed them.
- **Source:** Doyle's own writings, notably later Spiritualist work.
- **Evidence against:** Houdini explained that he was an illusionist and escape technician; controlled performance does not establish paranormal ability.
- **Effect:** intensified Houdini's frustration and became an emblem of Doyle's resistance to conjuring explanations.

### 5. “Elementary, my dear Watson”

- **Story:** Holmes habitually speaks this line in Doyle.
- **Evidence:** the exact phrase is absent from the canonical four novels and fifty-six stories, though “elementary” and “my dear Watson” occur separately. Stage, film, parody, and memory fused them.
- **Effect:** possibly the most famous non-canonical quotation in detective fiction.

### 6. Black armbands after Holmes's death

- **Story:** City of London workers wore mourning bands in 1893.
- **Evidence:** public anger and subscription cancellations are documented; secure contemporary evidence for a broad armband phenomenon is elusive.
- **Status:** plausible-sounding later legend, not established mass event.

### 7. Doyle invented skiing in Switzerland

- **Story:** he introduced skis to Switzerland or the Alps.
- **Evidence:** he was an early British promoter and made notable Davos excursions; skis and other practitioners existed already.
- **Effect:** sustains the “man of action” mythology.

### 8. Doyle helped Pietri across the 1908 marathon finish and gave him £5

- **Story:** the author physically helped Pietri, causing disqualification, then paid him.
- **Evidence:** photographs identify race officials as helpers; Doyle was reporting from the stands. He advocated recognition and a subscription cup. The personal £5 story is weakly sourced.
- **Effect:** binds Doyle to one of Olympic history's foundational legends.

### 9. Holmes as a portrait of Doyle himself

- **Story:** Doyle was a real-life Holmes.
- **Evidence:** Edalji and Slater campaigns encouraged the image, but Doyle consistently named Joseph Bell as the main model; Poe's Dupin and other literary detectives mattered. Doyle's temperament was generally more romantic, athletic, and publicly crusading than Holmes's.
- **Effect:** gave exceptional authority to his crime investigations and exceptional comic force to his errors over mediums.

### 10. Royal/Plantagenet ancestry

- **Story:** Doyle descended from the Percys and Plantagenets.
- **Source:** maternal family tradition, heraldic display at Undershaw, biographical repetition.
- **Evidence:** parts of the genealogy may be traceable, but a full proof was not located in the consulted material.
- **Effect:** reinforced Doyle's romance of chivalry, honour, lineage, and historical mission.

### 11. Last words: “You are wonderful”

- **Story:** dying Doyle spoke those words to Jean.
- **Source:** family and early biographical tradition.
- **Evidence:** consistent but intimate testimony; no independent transcript.
- **Effect:** became the accepted closing scene of biographies.

Hundreds of Holmes adaptations are works about Doyle's character, not biographical works about Doyle. Important early adaptation history includes William Gillette's stage play; the 1916 silent *Sherlock Holmes* with Gillette; Eille Norwood's 1921–1923 Stoll film series, admired by Doyle; and *The Lost World* (1925), whose stop-motion footage Doyle used in a famous illusion demonstration.

### Principal archives

- **British Library, Arthur Conan Doyle Papers:** more than 2,100 items, including family papers from Dame Jean and acquired manuscripts; includes *The Narrative of John Smith*.
- **Portsmouth History Centre, Richard Lancelyn Green/Arthur Conan Doyle Collection:** approximately 40,000 archival items, 16,000 books, and 3,000 objects; exceptionally broad Holmes, Doyle, and Spiritualism holdings.
- **Harry Ransom Center, University of Texas at Austin:** Doyle manuscripts, correspondence, family and financial records, Spiritualist photographs and transcripts, and Houdini-related correspondence; approximately nineteen document boxes in the principal collection.
- **New York Public Library, Berg Collection:** synthetic Doyle collection of manuscripts, typescripts, correspondence, financial/legal documents, and photographs, dated 1881–1944.
- **Newberry Library, C. Frederick Kittle Collection of Doyleana:** letters, notes, family material, and printed Doyleana.
- **University and specialist collections:** Joseph Bell material at Kent State; Royal College of Surgeons of Edinburgh Bell records; Sherlock Holmes Society of London; private Conan Doyle Estate archive.

The family archive and estate are interested parties in reputation and licensing but also hold unique primary material. The Arthur Conan Doyle Encyclopedia is unusually extensive and useful, yet individual entries vary in editorial polish and must be checked against primary or institutional evidence for disputed claims.

## Death, Burial, and Estate

On 7 July 1930 Doyle suffered his final cardiac collapse at Windlesham. Accounts say he was found or supported in the hall clutching his chest and told Jean, “You are wonderful.” He died at dawn or early morning.

Because he rejected orthodox Christianity and identified as a Spiritualist, burial presented a religious problem. A large open-air funeral was held at Windlesham on 11 July, and he was buried in the rose garden. Spiritualists held a memorial gathering at Royal Albert Hall, where an empty chair symbolised his continuing presence; reports of mediums perceiving him became part of Spiritualist hagiography.

Jean died in 1940. In **1955**, their remains were transferred to the churchyard of **All Saints, Minstead**, Hampshire, near Bignell Wood. The grave bears the words:

> Steel true  
> Blade straight  
> Arthur Conan Doyle  
> Knight  
> Patriot, physician & man of letters

His will, dated 6 May 1930, was proved with gross value £83,491 and net personalty £55,708. Jean and Denis were executors. Literary rights became the object of complicated family succession, corporate licensing, and later litigation.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Literature and media

Sherlock Holmes eclipsed every reputation Doyle preferred for himself. The Holmes canon helped standardise the serial consulting detective, forensic clue, sidekick narrator, metropolitan case file, and adversarial super-criminal. Holmes became an autonomous modern myth: adapted in theatre, silent film, radio, cinema, television, comics, games, pastiche, advertising, and fan scholarship.

Professor Challenger shaped lost-world and scientific-adventure fiction. *The Lost World* became a generic label and influenced dinosaur cinema. Doyle's historical novels, once central to his prestige, receive less general readership but remain significant evidence of late Victorian medievalism, chivalric nationalism, and popular history.

### Crime and law

Edalji and Slater established Doyle as a celebrity investigator. His work helped expose unreliable identification, prejudicial policing, weak physical evidence, and the absence or insufficiency of appeal mechanisms. Later literary retellings sometimes make him infallible; the actual record is stronger when treated as persistent advocacy with both acute observations and overstatement.

### Spiritualism

Within Spiritualist memory Doyle became a missionary figure, sometimes called the **“St Paul of Spiritualism.”** In sceptical history he became a case study in motivated reasoning and the inability of intelligence alone to prevent deception. Both reputations select real evidence. He invested money, health, organisational labour, and literary standing in the movement; he also defended demonstrable frauds and the Cottingley fabrication.

### Heritage, archives, and fan institutions

The **Sherlock Holmes Society of London**, Baker Street Irregulars, museums, statues in Edinburgh and Crowborough, the Portsmouth collection, and international societies preserve and ritualise the canon. The fictive address **221B Baker Street** became a real-world destination. Fan chronology and “the Grand Game”—treating Holmes and Watson as historical and Doyle as literary agent—form a deliberate learned mythology.

### Reinterpretation

Recent scholarship increasingly integrates rather than partitions Doyle's medicine, empire, photography, law reform, war propaganda, family secrecy, masculinity, and Spiritualism. His belief in fairies is no longer merely an eccentric appendix; it is studied through media technology, standards of evidence, postwar grief, Theosophy, childhood authority, and photographic culture. Colonial and racial readings examine both his Congo activism and imperial assumptions. Copyright litigation shifted the legacy from family-controlled property toward global public-domain reuse.

## Atlas Connections

### Arthur James Balfour

- **[T5]** **Reported fact.** Conan Doyle personally met former prime minister Arthur Balfour, probably through the political and literary circles in which Doyle moved during the early twentieth century. In *Memories and Adventures* (1924), Doyle included Balfour among the notable people he had met and described his appearance and character. The meeting is supported by Doyle’s firsthand retrospective testimony, although the available account does not specify its date or venue. [*Memories and Adventures*](https://www.arthur-conan-doyle.com/index.php/Memories_and_Adventures)

### Edgar Allan Poe

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact.** Doyle repeatedly wrote and lectured about Poe as the foundational literary predecessor of modern detective fiction. He praised Poe in “Facts About Fiction” (1893), discussed him during his 1894 American lecture tour, devoted much of chapter VI of *Through the Magic Door* (1906–1907) to him, and presided over a Poe memorial lecture in 1909. Doyle specifically credited Poe’s Dupin stories with demonstrating that detective fiction could possess literary value. [Arthur Conan Doyle Encyclopedia: Edgar Allan Poe](https://www.arthur-conan-doyle.com/wiki/Edgar_Allan_Poe)

### Ernest Shackleton

- **[T5]** **Reported fact.** Contemporary and later accounts describe Conan Doyle and Antarctic explorer Ernest Shackleton as friends. Following Shackleton’s death in 1922, Doyle wrote to Emily, Lady Shackleton, concerning messages that purported to come from her husband through mediumship; two such letters are catalogued in 1924. The correspondence proves Doyle’s connection to the Shackleton family, while the characterization of the two men as personal friends rests on reported biographical evidence. [The Irish Times](https://www.irishtimes.com/news/conan-doyle-letters-to-shackleton-s-wife-1.507329), [Christie’s catalogue](https://www.christies.com/en/lot/lot-2777935)

### H. G. Wells

- **[T3]** **Documented fact.** Wells corresponded with Conan Doyle. At least seven autograph letters from Wells to Doyle survive, including letters dated 30 August 1900 and 3 July 1902; the former welcomed Doyle back from his South African War service and asked about his impressions. This establishes direct literary and personal discourse, although the cited archive description alone does not establish a close friendship. [Christie’s catalogue](https://www.christies.com/en/lot/lot-4290357)

### Harry Houdini

- **[T5]** **Documented fact.** Conan Doyle and Houdini met personally, corresponded, exchanged family hospitality, and initially became friends beginning around 1920. Their relationship deteriorated during 1922–1924 over Spiritualism, particularly after Lady Conan Doyle’s automatic-writing séance for Houdini’s deceased mother at the Ambassador Hotel in Atlantic City on 18 June 1922 and Houdini’s subsequent public exposure of fraudulent mediums. The connection was therefore both a genuine friendship and a later public antagonism, not an invented meeting or instantaneous feud. [PBS *American Experience*](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/americanexperience/features/houdini-conan-doyle/), [Harry Ransom Center](https://sites.utexas.edu/ransomcentermagazine/2016/09/28/houdini-speaks-to-the-living/)

- **[T3]** **Documented fact.** Their surviving correspondence and reciprocal publications constitute extensive written engagement. Houdini’s *A Magician Among the Spirits* (1924) disputed Doyle’s evidentiary standards, while Doyle defended mediums challenged by Houdini and continued to argue that Houdini possessed unacknowledged psychic powers.

### James Matthew Barrie

- **[T5]** **Documented fact.** Doyle and Barrie were friends and frequent associates from the early 1890s. They played cricket together—Doyle appeared for Barrie’s Allahakbarries—and moved in overlapping British literary circles. A surviving 22 May 1903 team photograph places both men with the Authors’ side against the Artists. [Authors–Artists cricket photograph and discussion](https://emsworthmuseum.org.uk/emcms/files/original/b3ad0415d9eab00e6fd7419f00ab4f1b.pdf), [Conan Doyle–Barrie account](https://www.conandoyleinfo.com/life-conan-doyle/family/conan-doyle-and-james-m-barrie/)

- **[T3]** **Documented fact.** Barrie and Doyle collaborated on the comic operetta *Jane Annie; or, The Good Conduct Prize* (1893), with music by Ernest Ford. The production failed commercially, but it is a firmly documented instance of sustained joint authorship and theatrical work.

### Mark Twain

- **[T3]** **Documented fact.** Doyle engaged with Twain in print, although no personal meeting is securely established here. In *The Wanderings of a Spiritualist* (1921), Doyle attributed to Twain a humorous saying about “turning the other cheek”; in “A Glimpse of Harry Lauder” he grouped Twain with Jerome K. Jerome and Lauder as humorists capable of pathos. Scholarship also identifies probable engagement with *Roughing It* behind aspects of the Mormon episodes in *A Study in Scarlet*, but that particular influence remains an informed literary inference rather than a documented admission by Doyle. [*The Wanderings of a Spiritualist*](https://www.arthur-conan-doyle.com/wiki/The_Wanderings_of_a_Spiritualist), [“The Absence of Holmes”](https://www.arthur-conan-doyle.com/wiki/The_Absence_of_Holmes)

### Napoleon Bonaparte

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact.** Doyle wrote extensively about Napoleon and the Napoleonic era. Napoleon appears as a major historical character in *Uncle Bernac* (1897), while the Brigadier Gerard stories repeatedly situate their fictional narrator within Napoleon’s campaigns. Doyle also assessed Napoleon in *Through the Magic Door*. Contemporary reviewers recognized *Uncle Bernac* as substantially a literary portrait of the emperor. [*Uncle Bernac*](https://www.arthur-conan-doyle.com/index.php?title=Uncle_Bernac), [“A Portrait of Napoleon”](https://www.arthur-conan-doyle.com/wiki/A_Portrait_of_Napoleon)

### Oscar Wilde

- **[T5]** **Documented fact.** Doyle and Wilde met at the Langham Hotel in London on 30 August 1889 at a dinner arranged by *Lippincott’s Monthly Magazine* editor J. M. Stoddart. Both accepted commissions there: Doyle produced *The Sign of the Four* and Wilde produced *The Picture of Dorian Gray*. Doyle’s pocket diary records the date and commission, and he later recalled the occasion warmly as a “golden evening.” The evidence supports a cordial literary acquaintance, not a close lifelong friendship. [Conan Doyle’s 1889 pocket diary](https://www.arthur-conan-doyle.com/wiki/Conan_Doyle%27s_Pocket_Diary_for_1889), [Sherlock Holmes Society of London](https://sherlock-holmes.org.uk/a-golden-day/)
