# Guido Keller: Research Dossier

## Scope and Identification Note

This dossier concerns **Guido Keller von Kellerer** (1892–1929), the Milan-born Italian military aviator, First World War combat pilot, associate of Gabriele d’Annunzio, participant in the occupation of Fiume, political adventurer, naturist, publicist, and co-founder of the Fiuman movement and journal *Yoga*.

The documentary record is uneven. Keller left scattered articles, manifestos, letters, and project notes, but no systematic autobiography. Much of his biography comes from military records; papers in the Vittoriale archives; his own writings; contemporary witnesses such as Giovanni Comisso and Léon Kochnitzky; and early memorial biographies by Atlantico Ferrari, Sandro Pozzi, and Igino Mencarelli. Those writers helped preserve evidence but also contributed to Keller’s legend. Episodes reported only through memoir or later biography are identified accordingly.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Guido Keller; several sources give the fuller aristocratic form **Guido Keller von Kellerer**. German-language bibliographical sources additionally record **Guido Baron Keller von Kellerer und Wolkenkeller**.
- **Common name:** Guido Keller.
- **Titles and designations:**
  - Baron or *barone*, reflecting the family’s claimed or recorded nobiliary status.
  - Lieutenant in the Italian Army’s air service during the First World War.
  - “Officiale di Ronchi,” a designation used by officers who entered Fiume with d’Annunzio.
  - “Segretario d’azione” to Gabriele d’Annunzio at Fiume.
  - Head of the *Ufficio Colpi di Mano*.
- **Self-designations and signatures:** Reportedly signed some communications **“Cavaliere dell’Ideale”** and **“Zarathustra.”**
- **Military sobriquet:** **“Asso di cuori”**—Ace of Hearts—used as his personal aircraft emblem and later as the title of Atlantico Ferrari’s biography.
- **Other descriptions:** “Uscocco,” “argonauta,” “corsair,” “futurist,” “yogi,” and “the most eccentric legionary” are retrospective or movement-specific descriptions rather than formal titles.
- **Born:** **6 February 1892**, Milan, Kingdom of Italy.
- **Died:** **9 November 1929**, following a motor-vehicle accident near **Magliano Sabina**, then in the province of Rieti; some accounts specify the road near Otricoli.
- **Age at death:** 37.
- **Burial:** Mausoleum of the Vittoriale degli Italiani, Gardone Riviera. His remains occupy one of the outer stone arks surrounding the later tomb of Gabriele d’Annunzio.
- **Nationality:** Italian; descended from a family of Swiss Grisons origin.
- **Known occupations:** Military and civilian pilot, air-service officer, political activist, irregular military organizer, publicist, journal director, commercial representative, would-be aviation entrepreneur, and project designer.
- **Marital status:** No documented marriage has been established.
- **Children:** None securely documented.
- **Sexual relationships:** Contemporary and later accounts describe relationships with women, prostitutes, and men. No durable named partnership has been securely reconstructed from the consulted sources.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal and ancestral background

The Keller family reportedly originated in the Swiss canton of **Graubünden/Grisons** and moved into Lombardy during the eighteenth century in connection with textile manufacture and commerce. Sources describe the family as patrician or aristocratic and identify its earlier members as barons. The exact legal status of Keller’s nobiliary titles requires confirmation in contemporary heraldic and civil records.

The family became associated with textile manufacturing in Brianza. A family spinning mill was destroyed by fire, an event said to have caused serious financial reverses. The date and ownership structure of the mill are not specified in the accessible sources.

Keller’s grandfather **Alberto Keller** was a Milanese industrialist and philanthropist. In 1874 he helped finance Milan’s secular institute for blind people. He was also associated with the creation of the cremation temple at Milan’s Monumental Cemetery. These activities place the elder Keller in Milan’s philanthropic, secular, and cremationist bourgeois milieu.

### Parents and siblings

Keller’s mother is identified as **Luisa Osnago**. The Enciclopedia Dannunziana calls Keller’s father Alberto in connection with the aviator’s later donation of medal payments to establish a nursery bearing his father’s name. This creates a possible duplication of names between father and grandfather that should be verified against civil registers.

A brother, **Alberto Keller**, was active in the Florentine fascist university environment and is described as one of its leaders. Alberto later went to Brazil and died there suddenly in **July 1929**. Guido wanted to join or console their mother after Alberto’s death but reportedly could not travel because his passport had been withdrawn.

A cousin, **Margherita Incisa di Camerana**, later **Margherita Rossi Passavanti**, participated in the Fiume enterprise, was connected with the Arditi, and acted as an intermediary between Keller and d’Annunzio. Some journalistic sources call her Margherita Keller and give her the pseudonym **“Fiammetta.”** The precise genealogical link has not been independently established here.

### Family finances

The family had once occupied a prominent place in Milanese society but suffered economic decline after the destruction of its textile property. Keller retained the manner and cultural formation associated with privilege but repeatedly lacked money. By the late 1920s he was living in reduced circumstances and depended upon friends.

No evidence was found of substantial personal property, investments, shareholdings, patents, or an enduring business organization owned by Keller.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1892–1914

Keller was born in Milan on 6 February 1892. The family later moved to **Turin**, where he continued his studies. He reportedly encountered or attended the teaching of poet, literary historian, and professor **Arturo Graf**. Later biographers attribute part of Keller’s interest in esotericism and mystical symbolism to Graf, but the exact institutional setting and duration of their contact remain unclear.

Keller’s parents attempted to control his resistance to ordinary schooling by sending him to a strict Swiss boarding school, usually rendered in the sources as **Trozen** or **Trotzen**. He was expelled for indiscipline. The school’s exact official name and location have not been securely identified from the consulted records.

After his return, the family settled in **Fiesole**, near Florence. Fiesole remained an important base for Keller after the First World War and after Fiume.

Accounts of his youth emphasize several persistent traits:

- hostility to institutional discipline;
- a preference for outdoor and solitary life;
- interest in philosophy, literature, art, and mystical thought;
- rejection of middle-class domestic routine;
- enthusiasm for speed, automobiles, and aircraft;
- physical culture, sunbathing, nudism, and “hygienist” or naturist practices.

These descriptions derive largely from friends and memorial biographies and should be understood both as observations and as components of the Keller persona later cultivated at the front and in Fiume.

## Education and Formation

Keller does not appear to have completed a conventional university program. His formation was literary, technical, military, and self-directed.

Important influences included:

- **Arturo Graf:** Reported teacher or intellectual influence in Turin.
- **Friedrich Nietzsche:** Keller’s individualism, anti-bourgeois rhetoric, heroic vocabulary, and occasional “Zarathustra” signature were explicitly associated by contemporaries and later scholars with Nietzschean superman ideas.
- **Italian Renaissance condottieri:** He idealized the mobile, charismatic company of warriors bound personally to a leader rather than to bureaucracy.
- **Benvenuto Cellini:** Keller reportedly carried Cellini’s *Vita* when flying.
- **Ludovico Ariosto:** He also reportedly read *Orlando furioso* in the cockpit.
- **Francis of Assisi:** Keller’s mystical cult of nature and renunciation incorporated Franciscan imagery, although it coexisted with radically anti-clerical gestures.
- **Gabriele d’Annunzio:** From their wartime meeting onward, d’Annunzio became Keller’s most consequential personal, literary, and political point of reference.
- **Eastern and esoteric thought:** Keller used the names Krishna, Yoga, and related symbols in an eclectic manner. His “Yoga” was not a disciplined transmission of an Indian school; it was a Fiuman synthesis of spiritualism, naturism, anti-rationalism, political revolt, and avant-garde performance.
- **Futurism:** He adopted futurist disruption, flight imagery, manifestos, theatrical politics, and technological spectacle while simultaneously opposing industrial materialism and mechanized bourgeois civilization.

## Aviation Training, 1914–1915

Keller obtained a civilian pilot’s qualification in **December 1914**, reportedly at **Cameri**, near Novara. Sources also associate his training with the civilian aviation battalion and flying establishment at **Turin-Mirafiori**.

One frequently repeated training episode states that Keller landed a Blériot XI with its engine stopped, attempted to restart it himself, and was struck or grazed by its propeller, suffering a scalp wound. Fellow aviators supposedly joked that his head was hard enough to break a propeller. He replied by creating a “Society of Friends of Hair,” collecting locks of members’ hair and scattering them over Turin from an aircraft. This is a **reported anecdote**, preserved through later biographical literature; its precise date and contemporary documentation remain unverified.

On **1 December 1915**, Keller entered operational military aviation as a second lieutenant. He was assigned to the airfield at **Verona-Tombetta**, initially with the 4th Fighter Squadron, equipped with SAML/Aviatik machines. In the service-wide renumbering of 15 April 1916, this formation became the **73rd Squadron**.

## First World War, 1915–1918

### Early operational service

At Verona and in the mountain theater Keller flew Aviatik aircraft. In May 1916, according to squadron histories, he proposed moving part of the formation to the high plateau at **Vezzarde**, near Sant’Anna d’Alfaedo, to escape the fog affecting operations around Verona.

Keller developed a reputation for exceptional flying skill coupled with habitual disregard for convention. He sometimes flew lightly dressed, used a bersagliere fez in place of standard headgear, and kept books, tea things, flowers, and biscuits in or near his cockpit. During good weather he slept outdoors, exercised naked, took sun baths, and reportedly made his quarters in a hollow or shelter under a tree.

These practices were tolerated because, in the memoir tradition, Keller was willing to fly dangerous missions and substitute for married pilots. Ferrari quotes him as offering to take another man’s duty because Keller was unmarried.

### Aircraft and squadron assignments

- Initial service with the 4th Fighter Squadron at Verona-Tombetta, redesignated 73rd Squadron in April 1916.
- Transfer from Aviatik aircraft to the more modern **Nieuport** on **22 December 1916**.
- Assignment to the **80th Squadron** on **28 February 1917**, flying the Nieuport “bébé.”
- Transfer during the Caporetto retreat, on or about **1 November 1917**, to the celebrated **91st Fighter Squadron**, commanded by Francesco Baracca.
- Later operations from Nove di Bassano and **Quinto di Treviso**.

### Combat record

The official Vittoriale encyclopedia entry, relying on Mencarelli’s air-force study, credits Keller with:

- 116 escort and fighter flights;
- 137 cruising flights;
- numerous reconnaissance missions;
- seven claimed Austrian aircraft destroyed;
- three officially confirmed aerial victories;
- participation in other successful combats;
- three Silver Medals for Military Valor;
- a proposal for a Gold Medal that was not ultimately awarded.

Other squadron summaries assign Keller only one individually credited victory in particular enumerations. The difference reflects the difficult distinction between personal claims, shared victories, wartime confirmations, and later compilations. The safest formulation is that **three victories were officially confirmed according to Mencarelli**, while Keller and his associates claimed a larger total.

### The “chivalric duel”

A celebrated story has Keller dropping a written challenge over an Austrian airfield in May 1917, proposing a weaponless aerial contest in which victory would go to the pilot who maneuvered behind the other. The Austrian side supposedly accepted; Keller prevailed; and enemy pilots escorted him toward Trieste before exchanging salutes.

This is a **reported episode**, found in Mencarelli and the Keller memorial tradition. The accessible sources do not identify the Austrian pilot or furnish matching Austro-Hungarian records. It therefore cannot be treated as independently verified fact.

### Francesco Baracca and the “Squadron of Aces”

Keller joined the 91st Squadron during the crisis following Caporetto. Its pilots included Francesco Baracca, Pier Ruggero Piccio, Fulco Ruffo di Calabria, Ferruccio Ranza, Bartolomeo Costantini, Mario de Bernardi, Adriano Bacula, Gastone Novelli, and others.

Keller’s personal emblem was the Ace of Hearts. His later legend often calls him an “ace” in the broader sense of an elite pilot, although the conventional threshold for formal ace status is five confirmed victories. His verified victory count, as presented by Mencarelli, was three.

Keller and d’Annunzio met during wartime aviation operations at **Quinto di Treviso**, probably in mid-1918. Keller later mythologized the meeting as an encounter between divine or epic figures, calling d’Annunzio “the Ardent” and himself Krishna. The two developed an unusually intimate political and emotional bond.

### Personal habits and wartime mythology

Keller reportedly:

- flew wearing a fez;
- kept tea, biscuits, flowers, and books aboard;
- camped outdoors instead of using officers’ quarters;
- practiced nudism and sun worship;
- carried a skull as a talisman during the Battle of the Piave;
- kept and trained an eagle from the Dinaric Alps, also named Guido;
- embraced an aristocratic conception of aerial combat as personal duel.

Some of these claims are supported by multiple memoirs and photographs; others survive principally through the admiring recollections of friends.

### Decorations and philanthropy

Keller received three Silver Medals for Military Valor. After the war, he reportedly donated the three financial allowances attached to the decorations to establish a nursery named for his father Alberto. The foundation or continued operation of this nursery has not been independently verified in the consulted material.

## First Postwar Period and Libya, 1918–1919

After the Armistice, Keller briefly took part in an aviation competition honoring Francesco Baracca. One account says that, when near victory, he landed deliberately in an Umbrian meadow as a tribute to Saint Francis, abandoning the race. This is a **reported symbolic episode**, not independently confirmed.

Unable to adapt to peacetime, Keller went with the **90th Squadron to Libya**, where Italy was extending its colonial control in Cyrenaica. Details of this first Libyan posting are sparse.

By spring 1919, he had returned to Italy and rejoined d’Annunzio’s circle. On **6 May 1919**, he was among aviators whom d’Annunzio publicly presented during the nationalist speech from the Capitoline Hill in Rome. On **9 July**, at an aviation assembly at **Centocelle**, Keller heard d’Annunzio’s *Primavere sacre dell’Italia alata*. D’Annunzio called him a “master of eccentricities and feats.”

After demobilization in August 1919, Keller moved to d’Annunzio’s **Casetta Rossa** in Venice. He contacted officers including Sante Ceccherini, Ottaviano Ricaldoni, and Gustavo Fara while plans were being made for action at Fiume.

## Fiume: Planning and Entry, September 1919

Keller played a logistical role in the expedition that occupied Fiume.

The Enciclopedia Dannunziana reports that he and Captain Miani seized approximately thirty motor trucks at **Palmanova**, arranged a motorboat to carry the organizers to the San Giuliano pier, and prepared the red Fiat Type 4 used by d’Annunzio. Other accounts give slightly different vehicle totals, including twenty-five trucks and several armored cars.

Keller reportedly appeared before the fatigued d’Annunzio with red roses and a skull wearing a black fez, a theatrical appeal to resume action. This episode belongs to the well-established Fiuman memory tradition but is difficult to date precisely.

On **11–12 September 1919**, Keller accompanied d’Annunzio and the column from Ronchi into Fiume. He was among the original officers entitled to call themselves “Officers of Ronchi.”

## Fiume: Offices, Raiding, and Aviation

D’Annunzio initially considered making Keller his private secretary. Keller refused what he regarded as an office-bound position. A special office was created for him:

- **Secretary of Action**
- Head of the **Ufficio Colpi di Mano**, or Office of Raids/Commando Actions
- Participant in the direction of Fiuman aviation

Fiume was subjected to an Italian blockade. Keller’s office organized irregular seizures and diversions of ships, vehicles, food, arms, and supplies. D’Annunzio called these raiders **uscocchi**, after Adriatic corsairs.

Ships seized or redirected by the broader Uscocchi system included the *Persia*, captured on 19 October 1919, and later the *Venezia*, *Presidente*, *Cogne*, *Trapani*, and *Cicladi*. Keller’s personal role varied from operation to operation and should not be assumed for every seizure.

The associated **Ufficio Falsi** reproduced identity papers, permissions, and passports. Keller’s position connected him to this logistical and clandestine system, although the detailed division of responsibility between the two offices remains uncertain.

Fiuman aviation reportedly possessed nine landplanes and naval seaplanes operating from **Grobnico**, north of the city. Keller shared direction with Giovanni Bonmartini, Bruno Cattoi, Ludovico Censi, and Giordano Bruno Granzarolo.

### The pig flight

A famous story says Keller stole or requisitioned a pig for hungry Fiume and placed it aboard his aircraft. The pig’s weight broke through the fuselage floor, leaving it suspended beneath the machine like an improvised landing carriage.

This is a **widely circulated anecdote**, repeated in Keller biographies. It expresses both the provisioning crisis and his comic legend. The absence of an identified date, aircraft log, or contemporaneous report makes the precise details unverified.

## La Disperata and Military Reform

Keller helped organize **La Disperata**, a body of irregular volunteers who had initially been left outside regular command structures and camped around the shipyards. The group became d’Annunzio’s ceremonial and personal guard.

Its members combined military drill with public spectacle, collective singing, partial nudity, and a cultivated reputation for recklessness. La Disperata later influenced the names and styles adopted by fascist squadrist formations, but the original Fiuman unit was not identical to the later regime organizations bearing the name.

Keller wanted to reconstruct armed organization around small voluntary companies and personal loyalty. He opposed bureaucratic hierarchy and favored abolishing ranks above captain. With Captain **Giuseppe Piffer**, he influenced the *Disegno di un nuovo ordinamento dell’esercito liberatore*, drafted with d’Annunzio. It proposed:

- personal confidence between commander and volunteers;
- a military council;
- suspension of rank authority while that council deliberated;
- equal voting rights among participating ranks;
- partial self-government in peacetime.

The project was never implemented as an enduring military system.

## Political and Diplomatic Activity at Fiume

Keller met **Antonio Grossich**, president of the Fiuman National Council, and other council members while power was being concentrated around d’Annunzio.

In **May 1920**, using the identity **“Engineer August Römer,”** Keller traveled through **Zara, Split, Korčula, Kotor, and Kosovo**. He met secret agents, communist representatives, and Montenegrin militia figures to investigate a possible anti-Serbian revolt. The operation was clandestine and its exact institutional authorization is not fully established. It illustrates the Fiuman regime’s attempts to connect with Balkan separatists and revolutionary movements.

Keller and Belgian writer **Léon Kochnitzky** promoted the **League of Fiume**, conceived as an alliance of oppressed nations and anti-colonial or separatist movements opposed to the postwar great-power order and the League of Nations. Keller imagined Fiume as the center of a global coalition against wealthy imperial states. D’Annunzio’s conservative advisers resisted the project, and Kochnitzky was marginalized.

Keller also went to Rome in an unsuccessful effort to persuade War Minister **Ivanoe Bonomi** and Admiral **Paolo Thaon di Revel** to support Fiume.

## Cosala Withdrawal and the “Wild” Life

When conservative figures gained influence around d’Annunzio, Keller withdrew in protest to **Cosala/Kozala**, above Fiume. Giovanni Comisso and American writer **Henry Furst** joined him.

Accounts describe them living on fruit, milk, goat cheese, and rose petals dipped in honey; sunbathing and swimming naked; and rejecting routine political life. Keller was photographed or described as Neptune, with flowing beard and trident.

This episode is well represented in the memoir literature, but some details were consciously theatrical. It should be read as both a residence and an intentional performance of anti-bourgeois life.

## Yoga: Movement and Journal, 1920

In November 1920 Keller and **Giovanni Comisso**, with participation by **Mino Somenzi**, Guido Comessatti, and others, formed the movement **Yoga**, described as a “union of free spirits tending toward perfection.”

Its weekly paper, *Yoga*, was printed at the Miriam press in Fiume. Four issues are known, appearing from **13 November through 4 December 1920**. No public institution is known to possess a single complete original run; surviving issues have been reconstructed from holdings in Rome and the Comisso archive at Treviso.

The journal was formally directed by Keller, but scholarship credits Comisso with much of its literary substance. Articles were often unsigned or signed through symbols, which makes individual authorship uncertain.

Yoga combined:

- political revolt against the Treaty of Rapallo;
- hostility to bourgeois respectability;
- naturism;
- spiritual elitism;
- anti-rationalism;
- Nietzschean self-creation;
- criticism of capitalism and positivist materialism;
- anti-imperial rhetoric;
- agricultural and pastoral ideals;
- avant-garde performance;
- the aspiration to unite political and personal liberation.

Its symbols included a **five-petaled rose** and the **swastika**, described by the group as a solar sign or “chariot of the sun.” This occurred before the swastika’s definitive monopolization by German National Socialism. Its use is documented, but equating Yoga’s 1920 symbol automatically with later Nazi ideology would be anachronistic.

Yoga nevertheless contained authoritarian and aristocratic elements alongside libertarian ones. Its rhetoric of superior “free spirits,” heroic minorities, race, perfection, and anti-rationalism did not form a coherent democratic program.

The group’s relations with Italian Futurism were similarly mixed. Keller admired futurist destructive energy, typography, flight, and public gesture while rejecting industrial modernism and worship of the machine.

## Treaty of Rapallo and Flight over Rome, November 1920

The Treaty of Rapallo, signed on **12 November 1920**, made Fiume an independent state rather than annexing it to Italy.

Keller contemplated kidnapping Prime Minister **Giovanni Giolitti** while Giolitti traveled to Liguria for the negotiations. According to the standard account, Keller’s associates withdrew and the plan was abandoned. No evidence indicates that an operational abduction attempt occurred.

On **14 November 1920**, Keller flew an Ansaldo SVA from Fiume to Rome. His own account states that he:

- dropped red roses over the Vatican in honor of “Brother Francis”;
- dropped roses over the Quirinal for the queen and the Italian people;
- dropped an enamel chamber pot, decorated with carrots and turnips and attached to a hostile message, over **Montecitorio**, seat of the Chamber of Deputies.

The message represented the vessel as the tangible allegory of the value of a government sustained by lies and fear. The wording varies slightly in later reproductions.

The flight is a **documented political action**. Its later presentation as a wholly spontaneous prank understates the carefully constructed symbolic sequence: church, monarchy, people, and parliament each received a different offering.

## The Christmas of Blood, December 1920–January 1921

After the Regency rejected the Treaty of Rapallo, the Italian government moved militarily against Fiume. Fighting began on 24 December 1920.

One account says Keller refused to shoot at Italian soldiers and charged on horseback using a bamboo cane. He nevertheless captured troops, a gun, and an armored car. This is a **reported heroic episode** from the Keller tradition; the exact operational record has not been independently confirmed.

The fighting killed both legionaries and soldiers of the regular Italian army and caused civilian casualties. D’Annunzio resigned to stop the bombardment.

At the final handover, Keller reportedly sent government officers a child-sized coffin. Believing it might contain a bomb, they had it thrown into the sea without opening it. Giovanni Comisso recorded the story.

On **18 January 1921**, Keller left Fiume with d’Annunzio, traveling toward Venice. Their political project was defeated, although Keller continued attempting to revive its revolutionary potential.

## Post-Fiume Political Activity, 1921–1923

Keller maintained contacts among former legionaries, Arditi, left-wing or dissident fascists, futurists, and d’Annunzio’s entourage. A surviving message dated **13 January 1922**, written from Fiesole and entrusted to Margherita Rossi Passavanti, asked d’Annunzio:

1. how he should deal with the Uruguayan diplomat and journalist Enrique Arraga-Vidal;
2. whether Keller could see and speak with d’Annunzio;
3. for a definitive decision.

In the accompanying letter, Keller said he was trying to re-enter military aviation and might go to the colonies if “the Numen” no longer wanted to see him. Whether the message reached d’Annunzio is uncertain.

Keller participated in the **March on Rome in October 1922** and formally enrolled in the Florence Fascio in November. This is a **documented affiliation**.

His relationship with fascism was unstable:

- He shared its combatant nationalism, cult of action, anti-parliamentarianism, and hostility to liberal institutions.
- He participated in the movement that brought Mussolini to power.
- He disliked discipline, official bureaucracy, political routine, and the transformation of a movement into a state regime.
- In a letter of **8 July 1922**, preserved in the Vittoriale archive, he called Mussolini **“a dead calf’s head”** (*una testa di vitello morto*).
- Fascist officials consequently regarded him as politically unreliable or unmanageable.

Keller was therefore neither a simple outsider to fascism nor a stable regime functionary. He participated in early fascism but maintained an idiosyncratic Fiuman revolutionary program and criticized Mussolini.

## Return to Aviation, Turkey, Germany, and Libya, 1923–1925

### Garda assignment

By 1923 Keller was in severe financial difficulty. Through d’Annunzio’s intervention, he obtained an April appointment connected with organizing a seaplane squadron on **Lake Garda**. He did not remain satisfied with the position.

### Turkey

In 1923 Keller went to **Turkey**, intending to offer his aviation services to **Mustafa Kemal Atatürk** or to establish an air service. The projected airline or aviation organization failed.

Accounts say Keller became fascinated with Islam and entered a Quranic or Sufi confraternity. This is a **reported claim** lacking identification of the order, sponsors, initiation record, or exact location. It may reflect a real encounter subsequently magnified into the legend of Keller’s Eastern initiation.

### Berlin

In **November 1924**, Keller was attached to the Italian embassy in **Berlin**, commonly described as an aviation or aeronautical attaché. The Enciclopedia Dannunziana more cautiously calls him an embassy employee.

He devoted attention to gliding. This activity reportedly exceeded or displaced his official duties, and he was recalled to Italy. The precise date and administrative grounds for recall require confirmation from Foreign Ministry or air-force personnel records.

### Second Libyan service

In **1925**, Keller served at **Benghazi** with a formation commanded by Major **Ferruccio Capuzzo**, during Italy’s colonial war against Libyan resistance.

After Capuzzo and his crew were killed, Keller volunteered for retaliatory operations. A later account states that he killed some insurgents, made a forced landing after engine trouble, and was then mistaken by local Arabs for one of their own or accepted as a ruler for several months.

The forced landing and retaliatory context may be grounded in service events. The claim that a local community made him “king and lord” is a **legendary or weakly documented narrative**. No local names, dates, Arabic sources, or colonial reports supporting the kingship story were identified. It should not be stated as fact.

## South American Journey, c. 1926–1928

Keller later traveled through parts of Latin America for commercial purposes. Sources name:

- Peru;
- Chile;
- Venezuela;
- the Orinoco basin;
- sometimes Brazil and the Amazon, although accounts disagree about whether he ascended the Amazon or the Orinoco.

He spent at least one winter in Venezuela. Some biographies call him a gold seeker. Others describe him as a commercial representative investigating markets.

Keller imagined creating new South American republics or revolutionary communities capable of resisting United States influence. There is no evidence that he established such a state, movement, company, or durable political network.

The itinerary, dates, employers, business transactions, and finances of the South American period remain among the least documented parts of his life.

## Final Years, 1928–1929

Keller returned to Italy in **1928**. He planned seaplane journeys along South American rivers and coasts.

His former 91st Squadron colleague **Mario de Bernardi** helped obtain work for him as a commercial seaplane pilot on a route linking **Orbetello and Sanremo**, with stops or passage along the Versilian coast.

Keller reportedly suffered another aircraft accident while transferring a seaplane to Sanremo. The exact aircraft, date, operator, and injury record were not established.

He lived principally at **Ostia**, at the Pensione Regina, in poverty or near-poverty. Friends provided financial assistance. Despite his former family position and military reputation, he did not accumulate wealth from aviation, politics, or business.

### Città di Vita

Keller continued planning a **“City of Life”**, intended to reproduce Fiume’s union of art, nature, free social relations, and political experimentation. He considered **Isola Maggiore in Lake Trasimeno** as a possible site.

A letter of 13 January 1922 already presents the City of Life as a post-Fiuman objective. No land purchase, corporate body, financial subscription, or construction resulted.

### La conquista del sole

With futurist artist and aviator **Fedele Azari**, Keller planned an aerial theatrical spectacle called **La conquista del sole**. The noise of approximately one hundred aircraft engines would constitute the music or acoustic foundation of the performance.

The project remained unrealized. It is important as a bridge between Keller’s military aviation, futurist spectacle, spiritual solar symbolism, and his desire to transform machinery into collective ritual.

### Contacts with d’Annunzio and surveillance

Keller repeatedly visited the Vittoriale. A Carabinieri report dated **12 May 1927** documented his presence at Gardone Riviera with aviator **Adriano Bacula** and the podestà of Zara. The report alleged that police officials were restricting information reaching the Carabinieri about visitors to d’Annunzio.

In 1929 Keller reportedly considered asking d’Annunzio to lead another march toward Dalmatia. According to Sandro Pozzi, the plan was abandoned because it conflicted with Italian foreign policy and because d’Annunzio was no longer willing or able to command such an expedition. This is a **reported plan**, not evidence of an operational conspiracy.

## Death

In July 1929 Keller’s brother Alberto died in Brazil. Guido reportedly wanted to travel to his mother Luisa Osnago but had no valid passport.

On **9 November 1929**, Keller traveled in a **Fiat 525** with:

- **Atlantico Ferrari**, driving;
- Chilean-Italian war volunteer and Gold Medal recipient **Vittorio Montiglio**;
- Captain **Giovanni Battista Salina**.

They were reportedly heading toward **Vallombrosa**. Rain, poor visibility, and a slippery road contributed to a crash near **Magliano Sabina**. Keller and Montiglio died; Salina’s fate is reported inconsistently in short secondary accounts, while Ferrari survived.

The death was accidental. No credible evidence of assassination or political sabotage was found in the consulted sources.

D’Annunzio described Keller after his death as one of the very few who had known how to love him in the manner he desired: “He loved me harshly.”

Keller was eventually placed in one of the stone arks around d’Annunzio’s mausoleum at the Vittoriale. His inclusion among the commander’s permanent symbolic guard formalized his posthumous status within the d’Annunzian cult.

### Military and governmental institutions

- Italian Army, Corps of Engineers and Military Aviation.
- 4th Fighter Squadron/73rd Squadron.
- 80th Squadron.
- 91st Fighter Squadron.
- 90th Squadron in Libya.
- Fiuman aviation command.
- Ufficio Colpi di Mano.
- Associated Ufficio Falsi.

- Italian embassy in Berlin.
- Later Italian colonial aviation service in Benghazi.

### Political and cultural organizations

- D’Annunzio’s Fiuman command.
- League of Fiume project.
- Yoga movement.
- *Yoga* journal.
- Collaboration with or contributions to Mario Carli’s *La Testa di Ferro*.
- Florence Fascio, joined November 1922.
- Networks of former Fiuman legionaries and the later Unione Spirituale Dannunziana.

### Aviation and technical projects

- Proposed Turkish air service or airline: unsuccessful and poorly documented.
- Study of gliding in Germany.
- Commercial seaplane service between Orbetello and Sanremo.
- Proposed South American seaplane expeditions.
- *La conquista del sole*, an unrealized aerial theater using massed aircraft engines.
- Proposed *Città di Vita* on Lake Trasimeno.

### Patents and inventions

No patent registered to Keller was identified in the consulted sources. His projects were conceptual, organizational, theatrical, or operational rather than patented technical inventions.

### Personal finances and property

No substantial personal business structure or investment portfolio has been documented. Family wealth had declined before his maturity. Keller’s medal allowances were reportedly donated. He repeatedly relied on d’Annunzio’s influence, military employment, commercial assignments, and assistance from friends. He was living in straitened circumstances at Ostia before his death.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Nationalism and irredentism

Keller embraced interventionist and territorial nationalism emerging from the First World War. He supported Fiume’s union with Italy and opposed the postwar diplomatic settlement.

### Anti-parliamentarianism

His hostility to parliament was literal and performative. The chamber-pot flight represented government and Parliament as cowardly, mendacious, and corrupt. He contemplated replacing parliamentary politics with direct action led by heroic minorities.

### Revolutionary ambiguity

Keller associated with nationalists, Arditi, futurists, syndicalists, communists, separatists, and anti-colonial representatives. His project did not follow a conventional left-right division. It joined:

- Italian nationalism;
- anti-capitalism;
- anti-imperial rhetoric;
- anti-Americanism;
- social and personal liberation;
- military elitism;
- charismatic leadership;
- hostility to bureaucracy;
- utopian communalism.

### Fascism

Keller participated in the March on Rome and enrolled in the Fascio. These are not merely associations imposed retrospectively. He was a participant in early fascism.

At the same time, he criticized Mussolini, resisted centralized discipline, and retained ideas inconsistent with a stable totalitarian state. Later descriptions of him as simply “anti-fascist” erase his participation; descriptions of him as a conventional regime fascist erase his dissent and marginality.

### Naturism and sexuality

Naturism was central to Keller’s daily practice and public identity. Nudity, sun exposure, outdoor sleeping, physical exercise, and sparse eating formed a personal counter-culture. At Fiume this extended into opposition to bourgeois sexual norms.

Sources report relations with women and men and use modern classifications such as homosexual or bisexual. Keller does not appear to have left a stable self-definition in those terms. The documentary conclusion is that contemporaries represented his sexuality as promiscuous and not exclusively heterosexual.

### Drugs

The Enciclopedia Dannunziana states that Keller used opium and became dependent on cocaine in Fiume’s port and brothel environment. This is presented by that specialist source as biographical fact, apparently derived from the Comisso/Fiume literature. Medical records, treatment records, or a quantified history of use were not identified. Claims that he was a “cocainomane” therefore have substantial secondary support but incomplete clinical documentation.

### Religion and esotericism

Keller drew eclectically upon:

- Franciscan nature mysticism;
- Krishna imagery;
- Yoga;
- Nietzschean Zarathustra;
- solar symbolism;
- Islam during his Turkish period;
- heroic paganism;
- occult or esoteric currents associated with late nineteenth-century European culture.

There is no evidence that he consistently adhered to a formal Catholic, Hindu, Buddhist, Muslim, or occult order throughout his life. His religious language was syncretic and performative.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Gabriele d’Annunzio

Keller’s central relationship. They met through wartime aviation, organized the Fiume expedition, and maintained a bond combining devotion, friendship, political advice, symbolic identification, and intermittent frustration. Keller was reportedly the only young legionary permitted to address d’Annunzio with the familiar *tu*. Their correspondence is preserved in the Vittoriale archives.

### Giovanni Comisso

Close friend, collaborator, and literary witness. Keller profoundly influenced Comisso’s postwar rejection of bourgeois life. Together they founded Yoga. Comisso later transformed their shared experiences into literature and memoir, particularly *Il porto dell’amore* and *Le mie stagioni*.

### Léon Kochnitzky

Collaborator in the League of Fiume and international revolutionary diplomacy. Their proposed coalition of oppressed peoples was marginalized by conservative elements at Fiume.

### Henry Furst

American writer and companion in Keller’s withdrawal to Cosala. Associated with Comisso and the radical Fiuman group.

### Mino Somenzi

Futurist and participant in Yoga. Later recollections credit him with publishing the *Quaderni della Yoga*.

### Mario Carli

Futurist writer and editor of *La Testa di Ferro*. Keller contributed to or worked within the journal’s political-cultural milieu. Carli later portrayed him in *Trillirì* as subtle, witty, contemplative, destructive, and mocking.

### Francesco Baracca

Commander of the 91st Squadron and Keller’s wartime superior. Keller’s later identity remained linked to the “Squadron of Aces.”

### Mario de Bernardi

Fellow 91st Squadron pilot and later friend. De Bernardi helped Keller obtain commercial seaplane work during his final years.

### Adriano Bacula

Wartime aviator and later associate. A 1927 Carabinieri report recorded Bacula visiting the Vittoriale with Keller.

### Fedele Azari

Futurist aeropainter and collaborator on the unrealized *Conquista del sole* aerial spectacle.

### Cristoforo Mercati, “Krimer”

Writer and artist who met Keller in 1928. Keller introduced him to aviation. Mercati later wrote *Incontro con Guido Keller*.

### Margherita Rossi Passavanti d’Incisa

Cousin, Fiuman legionary figure, and intermediary in Keller’s correspondence with d’Annunzio.

### Atlantico Ferrari

Friend, early biographer, and survivor of the 1929 automobile accident in which Keller died.

### Vittorio Montiglio

Companion on Keller’s final journey and fellow fatality in the crash.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, and Financial Questions

### Wartime killing

**Documented context:** Keller served as an armed combat pilot and participated in aerial warfare. His confirmed victories involved the destruction of enemy aircraft and likely deaths or injuries, though individual casualties were not reconstructed.

### Fiuman seizure of property and vessels

**Documented fact:** As head of the Ufficio Colpi di Mano, Keller helped organize the coercive diversion or seizure of vehicles, supplies, and shipping to sustain an unrecognized occupying regime. These actions would ordinarily constitute hijacking, piracy, theft, or military requisition depending on the legal authority accepted. The Fiuman command presented them as patriotic *uscocco* actions.

### False documents

**Documented institutional practice:** The Fiuman Ufficio Falsi produced documents and passports. Keller’s Ufficio Colpi di Mano worked within the same clandestine logistics system. Direct authorship of particular forged documents has not been established.

### Anti-Serbian clandestine mission

**Reported and substantially documented activity:** Keller traveled under the alias August Römer and met secret agents, communists, and Montenegrin representatives to assess an anti-Serbian revolt. No formal prosecution is known.

### Proposed kidnapping of Giolitti

**Reported plan:** Keller planned to kidnap Prime Minister Giovanni Giolitti around the Rapallo negotiations. The project collapsed before execution. No kidnapping occurred.

### Chamber-pot attack on Parliament

**Documented fact:** Keller dropped an enamel chamber pot and political message over Montecitorio on 14 November 1920. The episode was a symbolic attack and aviation violation as well as political theater. No resulting conviction was identified.

### Colonial violence in Libya

**Reported fact with incomplete documentation:** Keller volunteered for operations intended to avenge the death of Ferruccio Capuzzo and reportedly killed Libyan resistance fighters. Numbers, identities, circumstances, and military records were not recovered. The later “king among the Arabs” narrative is unverified.

### Drug use and dependency

**Reported fact:** Multiple later accounts describe cocaine and opium use at Fiume; the Vittoriale encyclopedia describes cocaine dependence. No medical diagnosis or criminal narcotics case was identified.

### Sexuality and scandal

**Reported fact:** Contemporary memory and later scholarship describe both heterosexual and homosexual encounters. These became part of Keller’s hostile and admiring mythology. No relevant criminal prosecution was identified.

### Police surveillance and passport restriction

**Documented or reported official action:** Keller and visitors to d’Annunzio were monitored. A Carabinieri report of 12 May 1927 concerns Keller’s visits. By 1929 his passport had reportedly been withdrawn, preventing travel after his brother’s death. The order and its stated legal grounds were not located.

### Financial misconduct

No documented evidence of embezzlement, corruption for personal enrichment, money laundering, fraudulent investment schemes, or secret accumulation of wealth was found. Keller participated in coercive Fiuman supply operations but appears to have died poor.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The man whose head broke an airplane propeller”

- **Story:** Keller tried to stop a moving aircraft by grabbing its wing, struck the propeller, and emerged with only a scalp wound; his head was said to have broken the propeller.
- **Appearance and circulation:** Early aviation and memorial biographies.
- **Evidence:** Plausible training accident, but precise records were not located.
- **Assessment:** Reported anecdote with legendary embellishment.
- **Effect:** Established Keller as physically indestructible and absurdly fearless.

### The weaponless duel with an Austrian pilot

- **Story:** Keller challenged enemy pilots to an unarmed contest of maneuver, won, and was honorably escorted home.
- **Circulation:** Mencarelli and later biographies.
- **Evidence:** No matching opponent or Austrian record identified.
- **Assessment:** Unverified military legend.
- **Effect:** Supported the image of Keller as a Renaissance knight of the air.

### The airborne pig

- **Story:** Keller flew a stolen pig to hungry Fiume; it broke through the aircraft floor and hung below the fuselage.
- **Circulation:** Fiuman memoirs, biographies, popular retellings.
- **Evidence:** No flight log or dated contemporary report identified.
- **Assessment:** Widely reported anecdote; exact mechanics uncertain.
- **Effect:** Became one of the principal comic stories attached to Keller.

### Nudist in a tree

- **Story:** Keller lived naked in or beneath a tree at military airfields and at Fiume.
- **Evidence:** Multiple memoir descriptions and the consistency of his documented naturism make the general practice credible.
- **Disputed extension:** A modern article suggests Keller may have inspired Italo Calvino’s *Il barone rampante*.
- **Assessment:** The naturist tree-dwelling is reported fact; the Calvino influence is an unsupported theory.
- **Effect:** Keller became a precursor figure for Italian counterculture and ecological anti-modernism.

### “King of the Arabs”

- **Story:** After a forced landing in Libya, Arabs mistook Keller for one of their own and accepted him for months as king and lord.
- **Evidence:** Later Italian biography; no identified Arabic, colonial, or military corroboration.
- **Assessment:** Legend or highly embellished incident.
- **Effect:** Extended the Keller mythology from European adventurer to charismatic desert ruler.

### Eastern initiate

- **Story:** Keller entered an Islamic or Quranic brotherhood in Turkey.
- **Evidence:** Repeated in biographies but without naming the order or producing contemporary documentation.
- **Assessment:** Possible reported fact, presently unverified.
- **Effect:** Reinforced his image as a traveler moving beyond European political categories.

### Fascist or anti-fascist?

- **Pro-fascist narrative:** Keller’s nationalism, March on Rome participation, Fascio membership, military elitism, and anti-parliamentarianism make him an early fascist.
- **Anti-fascist or libertarian narrative:** His criticism of Mussolini, anti-capitalism, sexual libertarianism, hostility to state discipline, and marginalization by the regime place him outside orthodox fascism.
- **Evidence:** Both bodies of evidence are genuine.
- **Assessment:** Keller participated in early fascism but did not become a disciplined or reliable regime fascist. Retrospective attempts to make him wholly one or wholly the other suppress contradictory documentation.
- **Effect:** His politics remain a field of competing appropriation.

### Proto-hippie or countercultural saint

- **Story:** Keller anticipated the 1960s through nudism, communal living, sexual freedom, drugs, ecology, Eastern spirituality, and anti-bourgeois revolt.
- **Origin:** Postwar cultural histories and modern journalism.
- **Evidence:** The underlying practices are substantially documented.
- **Assessment:** “Proto-hippie” is an anachronistic interpretive label, not Keller’s identity.
- **Effect:** Revived interest in Keller beyond military and fascist history.

### Assassination

No substantial source found in this research claimed that Keller’s death was an assassination. The documented circumstances support an accident in bad weather. Any sabotage theory should presently be classified as unsupported rumor.

## Works by Guido Keller

Keller’s surviving corpus is dispersed. The most comprehensive modern collection is *Ala = Pensiero e Azione*, edited by Alessandro Gnocchi. Attribution remains difficult because Fiuman journals often printed anonymous or symbolically signed material.

### Lifetime writings and publications

- **Articles in *La Testa di Ferro***, Fiume, 1920.
  - Individual titles and signatures require collation against the original run.
  - Genuine Keller authorship is established for some pieces; others are attributed editorially.

- **“Il Fior di Loto — Il Movimento della Yoga”**, *Yoga*, Year I, no. 1, Fiume, 13 November 1920.
  - Programmatic manifesto associated with Keller and Giovanni Comisso.
  - Later reprinted as **“Movimento Yoga”** in *Yoga*, no. 4, 4 December 1920.
  - Bibliographies sometimes confuse it with the separate broadside *Fondazione a Fiume della Yoga*.

- **Articles and declarations in *Yoga: Unione di spiriti liberi tendenti alla perfezione***, four issues, 13 November–4 December 1920.
  - Keller was formal director.
  - Much content is anonymous or marked by symbols.
  - Authorship must be separated from editorship and collective declaration.

- **“Fondazione a Fiume della Yoga”**, broadside or manifesto, Fiume, c. November 1920.
  - Collective movement document; exact division of authorship is uncertain.

- **Commentary on the Carta del Carnaro**, c. 1920.
  - Preserved and republished in modern collections.
  - Genuine Keller text according to the modern editor.

- **Account/message concerning the flight over Rome**, 14 November 1920.
  - Keller’s own description of the roses and chamber pot.
  - Preserved through quotation in Mencarelli and later works.

- **Letters to Gabriele d’Annunzio**, various dates.
  - Manuscripts and copies are held in the Archivi del Vittoriale.

- **Letter and message to Margherita Rossi Passavanti and d’Annunzio**, Fiesole, 13 January 1922.
  - Autograph documentary material.
  - Later reproduced and discussed by L’Arengario.

- **Nel pensiero e nelle gesta**, autobiographical lecture.
  - Keller delivered or prepared this self-presentation; its publication history is unclear.

- **Plans and texts for *La conquista del sole***, late 1920s.

- **Plans and correspondence for the *Città di Vita***, 1920s.

## Later Reputation and Legacy

Keller’s reputation developed in several distinct phases.

### 1929–1945: heroic memorialization

Friends and former Fiuman associates constructed Keller as:

- the Ace of Hearts;
- the perfect loyal companion of d’Annunzio;
- a chivalric aviator;
- a modern condottiere;
- a national revolutionary;
- an incorruptible adventurer destroyed by ordinary life.

Ferrari, Pozzi, and Mercati were central to this phase.

### Postwar marginalization

Keller became peripheral in general histories because:

- he died before institutional fascism reached its mature phase;
- he held no major state office;
- his ideas were inconsistent and difficult to classify;
- the Fiuman adventure was usually narrated through d’Annunzio;
- his principal writings remained scattered and anonymous.

### Countercultural recovery

From the later twentieth century, historians of sexuality, Futurism, anarchism, naturism, and alternative culture emphasized:

- nudism;
- communal living;
- homosexuality or bisexuality;
- drug use;
- ecological and anti-industrial themes;
- Yoga and Eastern spirituality;
- attacks on parliamentary respectability;
- the festive and theatrical politics of Fiume.

This produced the “proto-hippie” Keller, a useful but anachronistic construction.

### Political appropriation

Sections of the radical right celebrate Keller’s courage, nationalism, Fiuman loyalty, and participation in the March on Rome. Libertarian or countercultural writers stress his anti-capitalism, sexual rebellion, anti-authoritarianism, and contempt for Mussolini. Both traditions select authentic evidence but can become misleading when they suppress the opposing evidence.

### Military memory

Keller remains part of the mythology and history of Italy’s early combat aviation. His confirmed record was distinguished, although popular descriptions sometimes inflate it by treating all claimed or shared victories as confirmed and by calling him an “ace” without qualification.

### Material memorial

His burial at the Vittoriale permanently incorporated him into d’Annunzio’s posthumous symbolic order. The mausoleum gives him a prominence that he did not possess in the Italian state at his death.

## Chronology

- **6 February 1892:** Born in Milan.
- **Childhood:** Family moves to Turin; Keller studies there and encounters Arturo Graf.
- **Adolescence:** Sent to a strict Swiss college at Trozen/Trotzen; expelled for indiscipline.
- **Before 1914:** Family settles in Fiesole.
- **December 1914:** Obtains civilian pilot’s qualification, reportedly at Cameri.
- **1915:** Trains or flies at Mirafiori.
- **1 December 1915:** Assigned as a second lieutenant to military aviation at Verona-Tombetta.
- **15 April 1916:** 4th Fighter Squadron redesignated 73rd Squadron.
- **May 1916:** Proposes high-altitude deployment near Vezzarde/Sant’Anna d’Alfaedo.
- **22 December 1916:** Transfers from Aviatik to Nieuport aircraft.
- **28 February 1917:** Assigned to the 80th Squadron.
- **May 1917:** Traditional date of the alleged weaponless duel with an Austrian pilot.
- **1 November 1917:** Joins Francesco Baracca’s 91st Squadron during the Caporetto retreat.
- **1918:** Operates from Nove di Bassano and Quinto di Treviso; meets d’Annunzio during wartime aviation operations.
- **War’s end:** Completes a record later summarized as 116 escort/fighter sorties, 137 cruising flights, numerous reconnaissance flights, three confirmed victories, and three Silver Medals.
- **Late 1918–early 1919:** Serves in Libya with the 90th Squadron.
- **6 May 1919:** Appears among d’Annunzio’s aviator companions at the Capitoline speech.
- **9 July 1919:** Attends aviation gathering at Centocelle.
- **August 1919:** Demobilized; joins d’Annunzio’s circle in Venice and helps plan action at Fiume.
- **11–12 September 1919:** Helps procure transport and enters Fiume with d’Annunzio.
- **September 1919:** Becomes Secretary of Action and head of the Ufficio Colpi di Mano.
- **19 October 1919:** *Persia* seized by Fiuman Uscocchi.
- **1919–1920:** Helps organize La Disperata and Fiuman aviation; participates in supply raids.
- **May 1920:** Travels under the alias August Römer through Dalmatia, Montenegro, and Kosovo.
- **1920:** Promotes the League of Fiume with Léon Kochnitzky.
- **1920:** Withdraws temporarily to Cosala with Comisso and Henry Furst.
- **November 1920:** Co-founds Yoga movement and journal.
- **12 November 1920:** Treaty of Rapallo signed.
- **13 November 1920:** First issue of *Yoga* published.
- **14 November 1920:** Flies over Rome; drops roses over Vatican and Quirinal and a chamber pot over Montecitorio.
- **4 December 1920:** Fourth and final known issue of *Yoga*.
- **24–30 December 1920:** Christmas of Blood; Italian forces attack Fiume.
- **18 January 1921:** Leaves Fiume with d’Annunzio.
- **13 January 1922:** Writes from Fiesole seeking renewed access to d’Annunzio and guidance concerning Enrique Arraga-Vidal.
- **8 July 1922:** Letter records his contemptuous description of Mussolini.
- **October 1922:** Participates in the March on Rome.
- **November 1922:** Joins the Florence Fascio.
- **April 1923:** Through d’Annunzio, receives assignment to organize seaplane activity on Lake Garda.
- **1923:** Travels to Turkey and attempts to enter Kemalist aviation service or create an airline.
- **November 1924:** Posted to or employed at the Italian embassy in Berlin; studies gliding.
- **1925:** Serves in Benghazi during Italian colonial operations.
- **c. 1926–1928:** Travels in Peru, Chile, Venezuela, and the Orinoco region on commercial and exploratory projects.
- **12 May 1927:** Carabinieri report records visits to the Vittoriale with Adriano Bacula.
- **1928:** Returns to Italy; plans South American seaplane expeditions; obtains commercial flying work through Mario de Bernardi.
- **1928–1929:** Lives in reduced circumstances at Ostia; develops *Città di Vita* and *La conquista del sole* projects.
- **July 1929:** Brother Alberto dies in Brazil.
- **9 November 1929:** Keller and Vittorio Montiglio die in a Fiat 525 crash near Magliano Sabina.
- **Posthumously:** Buried in an ark surrounding d’Annunzio’s mausoleum at the Vittoriale.

## Atlas Connections

The roster contains Guido Keller himself. The following additional connections are supported by the consulted sources. Mere contemporaneity at Fiume has not been counted.

### Benito Mussolini — documented fact

Keller participated in the March on Rome in October 1922 and joined the Florence Fascio the following month. His relationship to Mussolini’s movement was nevertheless adversarial and unstable. In an archival letter dated 8 July 1922, Keller called Mussolini “a dead calf’s head.” Keller later remained under suspicion as a politically unreliable former Fiuman. No securely documented private meeting between Keller and Mussolini was established.

### Filippo Tommaso Emilio Marinetti — documented fact

Marinetti stayed in occupied Fiume in 1919 and was photographed with Keller and Ferruccio Vecchi. Keller worked within the Fiuman futurist scene, contributed to the milieu of *La Testa di Ferro*, and adapted futurist political performance and aviation aesthetics. Modern scholarship explicitly treats Keller’s position “between d’Annunzio and Marinetti.” Their approaches overlapped in destructive theater and anti-parliamentary action but diverged over industrial modernism and Keller’s naturist-spiritualist orientation.

### Friedrich Nietzsche — documented influence

Scholarship on Yoga and the Enciclopedia Dannunziana identify Nietzschean superman thought as an influence upon Keller’s individualism and the Yoga movement’s ideal of self-perfecting free spirits. Keller reportedly used “Zarathustra” as a signature. This is an intellectual influence, not a personal meeting.

### Francis of Assisi — documented symbolic influence

Keller invoked “Brother Francis” during the 14 November 1920 flight over Rome, dropping roses over the Vatican in his honor. A postwar aviation story also says Keller abandoned a race by landing in an Umbrian meadow as a Franciscan tribute. The Roman invocation is documented through Keller’s account; the race episode is a reported anecdote.

### San Vito — documented symbolic association

A Keller text quoted by the Enciclopedia Dannunziana refers to the “night of the frenzied dance of Sanvito.” This concerns the Fiuman cultural and ritual setting, not a historical personal relationship with Saint Vitus.

### Zoroaster/Zarathustra — reported self-created association

Keller reportedly signed some communications “Zarathustra,” drawing on Nietzsche’s literary figure and, at a further remove, the ancient Iranian religious name. Evidence supports the Nietzschean self-styling; it does not establish a substantive study or adherence to Zoroastrian religion.

### Gabriele d’Annunzio

D’Annunzio is not included in the supplied atlas roster under his name, so he is not formally counted as an atlas connection. He was nevertheless Keller’s central documented associate, commander, correspondent, patron, political collaborator, and posthumous memorializer.

### No supported connections found

No documentary connection was found between Keller and the overwhelming majority of the atlas roster. In particular, shared themes such as aviation, occultism, anti-modernism, fascism, anarchism, or residence in Italy were not treated as evidence of personal contact or acknowledged influence.
