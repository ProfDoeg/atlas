## Works by Doris Batcheller Humphrey

### Concert choreographies

- **1920:** *Soaring*. A scarf dance to music by Schumann, created at Denishawn; some accounts treat it as a collaboration with Ruth St. Denis rather than Humphrey’s wholly independent choreography.
- **1920:** *Hoop Dance*. A Denishawn solo to music by Ingenfritz.
- **1926:** *In Spring*. A concert dance to music by Liszt.
- **1926:** *Valse Caprice*. A concert dance to music by Chaminade; later reconstructions have helped keep this early Denishawn-period work in circulation.
- **1927:** *Sonata Tragica*. A concert choreography to music by Chaminade.
- **1927:** *The White Peacock*. A concert dance to music by Charles Tomlinson Griffes.
- **1928:** *Air for the G String*. A concert choreography to Bach.
- **1928:** *Bagatelle*. A concert dance to Beethoven.
- **1928:** *Gigue*. A concert dance to Bach.
- **1928:** *Pavane for a Sleeping Beauty*. A concert choreography to Ravel.
- **1928:** *The Fairy Garden*. A concert dance to Ravel.
- **1928:** *Papillon*. A concert dance to music by Rosenthal.
- **1928:** *Waltz: La plus que lente*. A concert choreography to Debussy.
- **1928:** *The Fairy Who Announces Death*. A concert dance to music by Henry Cowell.
- **1928:** *Color Harmony*. An abstract ensemble choreography organized through color, spatial design, and movement rather than narrative.
- **1928:** *Water Study*. An ensemble dance performed without conventional musical accompaniment, its rhythm arising from breath and bodily movement; its continuing life through notation and reconstruction makes the “work” inseparable from changing manifestations.
- **1928:** *Sarabande*. A concert dance to Rameau in a Godowsky arrangement.
- **1929:** *Air (Basso Ostinato)*. A concert choreography to Purcell.
- **1929:** *Piano Concerto in A Minor*. A concert choreography to Grieg.
- **1929:** *Life of the Bee*. An ensemble dance, also identified with its “Humming Chorus,” which turns collective labor and social organization into bodily design.
- **1929:** *Speed*. A concert dance whose object is choreography rather than a surviving autonomous text or score.
- **1929:** *Air Study*. An experimental movement study.
- **1929:** *Jesu, Joy of Man’s Desiring*. A concert choreography to Bach.
- **1929:** *The Call*. A concert dance to music by Dane Rudhyar.
- **1929:** *Mazurka with Imaginary Music*. A choreography whose title explicitly resists treating musical accompaniment as the dance’s defining object.
- **1929:** *Andante, Op. 13*. A concert dance to Schumann.
- **1929:** *Study No. 2*. A concert dance to Schumann.
- **1929:** *Prelude No. 2*. A concert choreography to Bach.
- **1929:** *From the Depths*. A concert dance to MacDowell.
- **1929:** *Andante Doloroso*. A concert choreography to Bach.
- **1929:** *Dance No. 7*. A concert dance to Schubert.
- **1930:** *Breath of Fire*. A concert choreography to music by Dane Rudhyar.
- **1930:** *Choreographic Waltz*. A choreography to Ravel.
- **1930:** *Descent*. A concert dance to music by Weiss.
- **1930:** *Gargoyle*. A concert dance to music by Weiss.
- **1930:** *Suite*. A concert choreography to Scriabin.
- **1930:** *Drama of Motion*. An experimental dance without music, constructed from design, rhythm, and dynamics; it is an obra of bodies and duration rather than a literary “text.”
- **1930:** *Salutation to the Depths*. A concert choreography to music by Dane Rudhyar; sources also give the title as *Salutations to the Depth* or *Salutations to the Depths*.
- **1930:** *Salutation*. A separate concert choreography in the standard checklist, although its proximity in title to *Salutation to the Depths* can make cataloguing ambiguous.
- **1930:** *Bourrée*. A concert choreography to Bach.
- **1930:** *Dance for Women*. An ensemble choreography to music by Dane Rudhyar, also encountered as *Dances for Women*.
- **1930–1931:** *The Shakers: Dance of the Chosen*. An ensemble dance based on Shaker worship, using traditional music, drum, accordion, and voice; sources disagree between 1930 and 1931 because they variously date its first version and later established form.
- **1931:** *Introduction and Allegro*. A concert choreography to Ravel.
- **1931:** *La Valse*. A concert choreography to Ravel.
- **1931:** *Tambourin and Burlesca*. A concert dance to music associated with Rameau and Bossi.
- **1931:** *Three Mazurkas*. A concert choreography to Tansman.
- **1931:** *Two Ecstatic Themes*. A two-part choreography comprising *Circular Descent* and *Pointed Ascent*, set respectively to music by Medtner and Malipiero; the parts can appear separately, but the obra is the diptych.
- **1931:** *Variations on a Theme of Handel*. A concert choreography to Brahms.
- **1931:** *Ecossaises*. A concert choreography to Beethoven.
- **1932:** *Dionysiacs*. A concert dance to music by Schmidt.
- **1932:** *Orestes*. A dance-drama to music by Milhaud.
- **1933:** *Suite in F*. A concert choreography to Roussel.
- **1934:** *Exhibition Piece*. A concert choreography to music by Nicolas Slonimsky.
- **1934:** *Pleasures of Counterpoint*. A concert choreography using music by Joseph Achron and others.
- **1934:** *Rustic Poem*. A concert choreography to Villa-Lobos.
- **1935:** *Credo*. A concert choreography associated in the checklist with music by Carlos Chávez and Roy Harris.
- **1935:** *Duo-Drama*. A duet created for Humphrey and Charles Weidman; the danced relationship, rather than a separately reproducible verbal text, is the work.
- **1935:** *New Dance*. The culminating section and commonly used title of the larger *New Dance Trilogy*, to music by Wallingford Riegger; *Theatre Piece* and *With My Red Fires* were subsequently understood as its companion works.
- **1935 or 1936:** *Theatre Piece*. An ensemble choreography to Wallingford Riegger; authoritative secondary chronologies differ by a year, apparently reflecting composition and premiere histories.
- **1936:** *Parade*. A concert choreography to music by Tcherepnin.
- **1936:** *Aida*. A concert choreography using Verdi.
- **1936:** *With My Red Fires*. A dance-drama to Wallingford Riegger, usually treated as part of the *New Dance Trilogy*; its theatrical roles and group architecture make it more than an illustration of the musical score.
- **1936:** *To the Depths*. A concert choreography to music by Dane Rudhyar, distinct in the standard checklist from the earlier *Salutation to the Depths*.
- **1937:** *To the Dance (Preludes to the Dance)*. A concert work to music by Norman Lloyd and Leonard; both titles occur in the record.
- **1938:** *American Holiday*. An ensemble choreography to music by Mamorsky.
- **1938:** *Race of Life*. A dance-drama to music by Irving Fine.
- **1938:** *Passacaglia in C Minor*. A large ensemble choreography to Bach, often called *Passacaglia and Fugue in C Minor*; its continuing reconstructed forms are manifestations of choreography that was never reducible to a single fixed object.
- **1939:** *Square Dances*. A choreography to music arranged or composed by Lionel Nowak.
- **1940:** *Variations*. A concert choreography listed in modern scholarly chronologies but not consistently distinguished from the first portion of *Decade*.
- **1940–1941:** *Decade*. A two-part choreography using music by Copland and others; the checklist dates Part I to 1940 and Part II to 1941, while selected-work lists often assign the whole to 1941.
- **1941:** *Dance “Isms”*. A choreography to music by Lionel Nowak; the quotation marks belong to the historically transmitted title.
- **1940–1942:** *Song of the West*. A multipart dance work to Roy Harris; sources variously date its development to 1940 or its completed/premiered form to 1942, and excerpts such as *Desert Gods* have circulated independently.
- **1942:** *Four Chorale Preludes*. A suite of choreographies to Bach.
- **1942 or 1943:** *Partita in G*. A concert choreography to Bach; sources disagree over both the year and whether the key should be styled G major or G minor.
- **1943:** *El Salón México*. A concert choreography to Copland.
- **1944:** *Canon*. A concert choreography to Paul Nordoff.
- **1944:** *Inquest*. A concert work to music by Norman Lloyd.
- **1946:** *The Story of Mankind*. A concert choreography to music by Lionel Nowak.
- **1946:** *Lament for Ignacio Sánchez Mejías*. A dance-drama for the José Limón company, based on Federico García Lorca’s elegy and set to music by Norman Lloyd; Humphrey made a new bodily and theatrical obra rather than reproducing the poem.
- **1947:** *Day on Earth*. A dance-drama to Copland made for José Limón and his company.
- **1948:** *Corybantic*. A concert choreography to Bartók.
- **1949:** *Invention*. A concert choreography to music by Norman Lloyd.
- **1951–1952:** *Quartet No. 1 (Night Spell)*. A dance work to music by Priaulx Rainier; sources use both titles and date it either to its 1951 creation or 1952 premiere.
- **1952:** *Fantasy and Fugue*. A choreography using Mozart; some records expand the title to *Fantasy and Fugue in C Major and Fugue in C Minor*.
- **1953:** *Ritmo Jondo*. A dance work to music by Carlos Surinach, built from Spanish-derived rhythms without being a book-like or purely musical object.
- **1953:** *Ruins and Visions*. A dance-drama to music by Benjamin Britten.
- **1954:** *Felipe el Loco*. A choreography using traditional Spanish music.
- **1955:** *The Rock and the Spring*. A dance work to music by Martin.
- **1955:** *Airs and Graces*. A concert choreography to Locatelli.
- **1955 or 1956:** *Theatre Piece No. 2*. A choreography to Otto Luening; scholarly lists disagree by one year.
- **1956:** *Dawn in New York*. An ensemble choreography to music by Hall Johnson.
- **1957:** *Descent into the Dream*. A choreography to music by Vivian Fine or Robert Abramson Petrash according to differing records; the music attribution requires further archival resolution.
- **1957:** *Dance Overture*. A concert choreography to Paul Creston.
- **1958–1959:** *Brandenburg Concerto No. 4*. Humphrey’s final choreographic project, completed or staged with Ruth Currier and premiered after Humphrey’s death; it therefore resists attribution as a wholly completed solo-authored work.

### Choreography for plays, musicals, and composite theatrical productions

- **1930:** *Lysistrata*. Choreography for the Broadway revival, credited jointly to Humphrey and Charles Weidman.
- **1930:** *Les Romanesques*. Dances for a production of Edmond Rostand’s play.
- **1932:** *Americana*. Choreography for a musical revue, incorporating *Water Study*, *The Shakers*, and dances to Bizet’s *Carmen*; this is a composite theatrical manifestation rather than one self-contained dance.
- **1933:** *Run, Little Chillun*. Dances arranged by Humphrey for Hall Johnson’s Broadway production.
- **1933:** *The School for Husbands*. Choreography for the Broadway musical and its *Dream of Sganarelle*, undertaken with Charles Weidman; some material adapted choreography attributed to Jean-Baptiste Lully.
- **1934:** *Christmas Oratorio*. Staged dances to Bach for a theatrical or concert production.
- **1939:** *To the Dance*. A Broadway presentation directed and written with Charles Weidman that incorporated Humphrey-Weidman repertory; it is a composite stage event, not simply the similarly titled 1937 choreography.
- **1944–1945:** *Sing Out, Sweet Land*. Choreography for Walter Kerr’s Broadway musical pageant, credited jointly to Humphrey and Charles Weidman.
- **1950:** *The Barrier*. A short-lived Broadway musical staged by Humphrey, with choreography credited jointly to Humphrey and Charles Weidman.
- **1957:** *The Child and the Enchantments*. Choreography for a production of Ravel’s *L’Enfant et les sortilèges*; translated and shortened titles vary in the documentary record.

### Choreographic method and pedagogical project

- **Developed principally in the 1920s–1950s:** *Fall and recovery*. A movement principle and teaching practice organized around yielding to and resisting gravity. It is not a single fixed work: it is a protocol embodied in classes, exercises, performers, and successive pedagogical lineages, with Charles Weidman sharing in the development of what is often called Humphrey-Weidman technique.
- **1928–1940s:** *Humphrey-Weidman repertory and company practice*. A collaborative project comprising a company, school, technique, and changing body of performance works; it resists classification as Humphrey’s individual obra because Pauline Lawrence, Charles Weidman, dancers, composers, and designers materially participated in making it.
- **1946–1958:** *Artistic direction of the José Limón company*. An extended creative and curatorial project in which Humphrey made dances, coached performers, shaped repertory, and advised Limón. Artistic direction is consequential making, but not every Limón choreography produced under it is therefore a Humphrey work.
- **1952 onward:** *The Merry-Go-Rounders*. A dance-theatre project for children directed by Humphrey. The company and its performances constitute a collaborative body of manifestations rather than a single securely bounded work.

### Books and assembled writings

- **Written before 1958; published 1959:** *The Art of Making Dances*. A posthumously published treatise and protocol for choreographic composition, edited by Barbara Pollack; the editorially produced book is a manifestation of Humphrey’s unfinished manuscript and teaching rather than an object she personally brought to publication.
- **Begun before 1958; published 1972:** *Doris Humphrey: An Artist First—An Autobiography*. Humphrey wrote the autobiographical first part; Selma Jeanne Cohen completed the later biographical narrative and edited the volume, so the published book is explicitly composite and should not be silently assigned to Humphrey alone.
- **Date not fixed:** *Greek Sacrificial Dance*. A dance writing preserved in the collected autobiographical volume; it is a verbal record concerning choreography, not the dance itself.
- **Date not fixed:** *Circular Descent*. A dance writing preserved alongside the choreographic work of that name; text and performed dance are related manifestations, not synonyms.
- **Date not fixed:** *New Dance*. Humphrey’s writing on the choreography, collected posthumously; it describes and theorizes a work but is itself a distinct text.
- **Date not fixed:** *Theatre Piece*. A choreographic writing preserved in the collected volume, distinct from any particular performance of the dance.
- **Date not fixed:** *Lament*. A writing on *Lament for Ignacio Sánchez Mejías*, distinct from the choreography and Lorca’s poem.
- **Date not fixed:** *Invention*. A choreographic writing preserved posthumously, not identical with the danced obra.
- **Date not fixed:** *Interpreter or Creator?*. An essay on the dancer’s creative status.
- **Date not fixed:** *What Shall We Dance About?*. An essay that operates partly as a manifesto for choreographic subject matter.
- **Date not fixed:** *On Choreographing Bach*. An essay on the practical and aesthetic relation between choreography and Bach’s music.
- **Date not fixed:** *Advice to Young Choreographers*. A pedagogical protocol for making dances.
- **Date not fixed:** *Doris Humphrey Answers the Critics*. A collected critical statement whose original occasion is not fixed by the consulted contents record.
- **Date not fixed:** *Doris Humphrey Speaks …*. A collected statement or address; the posthumous heading does not establish that Humphrey herself gave the text this title.
