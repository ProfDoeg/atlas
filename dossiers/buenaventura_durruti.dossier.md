# José Buenaventura Durruti Dumange: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

This dossier reconstructs the life of José Buenaventura Durruti Dumange from archival authority records, contemporary and near-contemporary testimony, historical scholarship, bibliographic catalogues, documentary texts, and later investigations.

Evidence labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by archival records, contemporaneous documents, or convergent scholarly evidence.
- **Reported fact:** found in credible testimony or historical literature but not independently established here from a primary record.
- **Allegation:** attributed by police, prosecutors, newspapers, political organizations, or witnesses without a demonstrated judicial finding.
- **Disputed claim:** substantial competing versions exist.
- **Rumor or myth:** unsupported or demonstrably embellished narrative that nevertheless influenced Durruti’s reputation.
- **Unresolved:** surviving evidence does not establish a secure conclusion.

Names and dates are unusually unstable in the Durruti literature. Spanish archival authorities generally authorize **Buenaventura Durruti**, 14 July 1896–20 November 1936, while his second surname appears as **Dumange**, **Domange**, **Domingo**, and, less plausibly, **Domínguez**. “Dumange” is used here because it is the form in the requested identification and is widely associated with his maternal family; variants are retained when describing catalogues or publications.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and household

**Documented or consistently reported facts**

Durruti was born in León’s working-class Santa Ana district, the second of eight children of:

- **Santiago Durruti**, a railway worker, described in biographical literature as a socialist or socialist sympathizer.
- **Anastasia Dumange**—also printed as Domingo or Domange.

The family was working-class and closely connected to the railway industry. Several accounts describe Durruti’s father and brothers as railway workers. The family’s precise ancestry beyond the parents is poorly documented in the accessible literature; no reliable genealogy establishing earlier generations was located.

A prolonged tanners’ strike in León in 1903 reportedly caused economic hardship throughout the neighborhood and affected the Durruti household. In a 1927 letter to his sister Rosa, Durruti retrospectively associated his childhood rebellion with the poverty and suffering he had observed around him. The statement survives through later edited biographical transmission rather than a publicly accessible autograph examined here.

### Siblings

The literature identifies several siblings, but lists and spellings are inconsistent. The most historically visible were:

- **Rosa Durruti**, a correspondent of Buenaventura and an important recipient of surviving family letters.
- **Pedro or Marciano Pedro Durruti**, the so-called “other Durruti.”

Marciano Pedro’s political history became a posthumous controversy. He had reportedly passed through anarchist circles before associating with Falangism or national syndicalism. Republican authorities prosecuted and executed him in 1937. Later narratives have variously called him a Falangist, an “anarcho-Falangist,” a provocateur, or merely an unstable political adventurer. Surviving reporting indicates that Buenaventura repudiated his brother’s exploitation of the Durruti name. There is no evidence that Buenaventura shared Marciano’s Falangist affiliations. A later examination of the proceedings argues that the evidence used to impose the death penalty on Marciano consisted largely of declarations and intentions rather than completed acts.

### Partner and child

Durruti met **Émilienne Léontine Morin**—“Mimi” or “Mimi-FAI”—at the International Anarchist Bookshop on rue des Prairies in Paris on **14 July 1927**, his thirty-first birthday. Morin was a French anarcho-syndicalist, stenographer, journalist, and activist from an anarchist family. They became companions but apparently did not enter a conventional civil or religious marriage.

They lived together in Belgium and later Barcelona. Their household was repeatedly disrupted by Durruti’s imprisonment, deportation, clandestinity, and political travel. Morin worked outside the home; testimony also depicts Durruti cooking, cleaning, and caring for their child when he was free.

Their only known child, **Diana Colette Durruti Morin**, was born in Barcelona on **4 December 1931**. Later known by her married surname, Marlot, she preserved family letters and provided them to Durruti’s biographer Abel Paz. She died in France on 19 April 2025.

The family’s material circumstances were modest. Accounts describe unpaid rent, frequent moves, secondhand or missing furniture, and Morin’s employment as a cinema usher. Durruti’s prolonged absences meant that Morin and the CNT activist **Teresa Margalef/Margaleff** bore most of the childcare.

No reliable evidence was found of other spouses, partners, or children.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1896–1910

Durruti grew up in León, a provincial and episcopal city with railway workshops and an expanding wage-working population. His neighborhood, Santa Ana, consisted largely of modest workers’ housing.

He began primary school at approximately five years old. Accounts identify an early school on the Calle de la Misericordia and later the school of **Ricardo Fanjul**. One teacher reportedly remembered him as mischievous but good-natured. Claims that he attended a “liceo” or received extended secondary education are not securely corroborated. The strongest reconstruction is that he received elementary schooling and left formal education at fourteen.

The tanners’ strike of 1903 and its economic effects became part of his retrospective formative narrative. His father’s socialist commitments, the railway workplace, neighborhood poverty, and repeated labor conflicts supplied his earliest political environment.

No evidence was found of university education, theological education, inherited wealth, or formal military schooling.

## Education and Formation

### Mechanical apprenticeship

In **1910**, aged fourteen, Durruti became an apprentice mechanic in the workshop of **Melchor Martínez**, described as a socialist with a local reputation for militancy. Martínez was an employer and formative political influence rather than a classroom teacher.

Durruti subsequently worked as a fitter or mechanic and developed skills that remained economically useful throughout his exile. Mechanical work was not incidental to his identity: he repeatedly supported himself in industrial employment and organized other metalworkers.

### Socialist and syndical formation

Durruti’s first organized labor affiliation was socialist, reportedly through the metalworkers’ union and then the railway workers’ organization associated with the **UGT**. His break with socialist union leadership followed the failed general strike of August 1917. He believed the leadership had abandoned or insufficiently supported militant railway workers; the union in turn expelled participants accused of sabotage.

His anarchism developed through:

- the experience of the 1917 strike and its repression;
- Spanish anarchist exiles in France;
- the CNT’s model of revolutionary syndicalism;
- the violence of Barcelona’s *pistolerismo*;
- friendships with Manuel Buenacasa, Francisco Ascaso, Gregorio Jover, Joan García Oliver, Ricardo Sanz, and other action-group militants;
- international anarchist contacts during exile.

Later meetings with **Nestor Makhno** and **Sébastien Faure**, and his relationship with Émilienne Morin, broadened his exposure to transnational anarchism. He was neither a systematic theorist nor a prolific author. His political thought was expressed primarily through organizing, speeches, interviews, letters, and exemplary action—what later admirers called “the language of deeds.”

## Early Career and the 1917 Strike

### Railway employment

By 1916 Durruti was employed by the **Compañía de los Caminos de Hierro del Norte de España**, usually called the Northern Railway Company. He worked in railway mechanical operations after earlier workshop employment.

### August 1917 general strike

**Documented core:** Durruti participated in the railway strike within the nationwide revolutionary general strike of August 1917. The government deployed the army; the movement was defeated, and participants were dismissed, arrested, or forced underground.

**Reported detail:** Durruti and other young railway workers sabotaged rolling stock, obstructed railway movement, or damaged locomotives and depots. The exact acts personally performed by Durruti cannot be reconstructed from the accessible evidence.

He was dismissed and expelled from the socialist railway union. Facing prosecution for strike activity, sabotage, and possibly military desertion, he escaped to France late in 1917.

## Chronological Life History

## First French exile and return, 1917–1920

Durruti’s first French exile lasted approximately from December 1917 to early 1919. He worked as a mechanic and mixed with Spanish anarchist exiles.

He returned to Spain in 1919. Biographical accounts say that his military status was discovered, that he was detained and hospitalized in Burgos, and that he escaped or was helped to escape. He returned to France in July 1919 and worked at, or in industrial employment associated with, **Renault** in Paris.

By 1920 he had returned to northern Spain and then moved into the militant CNT networks of San Sebastián, Zaragoza, and Barcelona.

## Los Justicieros, 1920–1922

In San Sebastián and Zaragoza he developed a close relationship with CNT organizer **Manuel Buenacasa** and participated in forming **Los Justicieros**.

The group emerged amid state repression, employer-sponsored gunmen, police violence, and anarchist reprisals. Its activities included acquiring weapons and planning attacks against officials regarded as responsible for anti-union terror.

### Alfonso XIII tunnel plot

**Documented plot; incomplete execution:** Los Justicieros planned an attack on King **Alfonso XIII** during an anticipated visit. Accounts describe the construction or intended construction of a tunnel beneath a route or venue and assign Durruti the task of obtaining explosives.

Police discovered the conspiracy before an attack occurred. Contemporary newspapers identified Durruti, Marcelino del Campo, and Gregorio Suberviola as plotters. This was a planned political assassination, not a completed one.

## Barcelona and Los Solidarios, 1922–1923

Durruti moved into Barcelona during the climactic phase of *pistolerismo*. Employers, the Sindicato Libre, police officials, and state authorities used gunmen and extrajudicial tactics against CNT organizers; anarchist action groups responded with armed robbery and assassination.

In 1922 Durruti, **Francisco Ascaso**, **Joan García Oliver**, **Gregorio Jover**, **Ricardo Sanz**, and others formed **Los Solidarios**. Membership lists vary, and the group had no conventional corporate hierarchy. Durruti later became its best-known member, which led police and press accounts to attribute collective activities to “Durruti’s gang” regardless of his presence.

### Assassination of Cardinal Juan Soldevila, 4 June 1923

Cardinal-Archbishop **Juan Soldevila y Romero** was shot and killed in Zaragoza.

- **Documented fact:** members or associates of Los Solidarios were implicated; Francisco Ascaso and Rafael Torres Escartín were the names most directly associated with the shooting.
- **Contemporary allegation:** police and press blamed Durruti or “the gang led by Durruti.”
- **Evidentiary qualification:** no reliable evidence located for this dossier establishes that Durruti fired on Soldevila or was present. Membership in the group is not proof of personal participation.
- **Historical context, not exculpation:** anarchists accused Soldevila of supporting the Sindicato Libre and anti-union repression.

### González Regueral

Former Bilbao civil governor **Fernando González Regueral** was assassinated in León in May 1923.

- **Allegation:** Durruti was repeatedly accused of participation.
- **Counterevidence:** some reconstructions state that Durruti was imprisoned when the assassination occurred.
- **Unresolved attribution:** the action has been assigned to Los Solidarios collectively, but personal responsibility remains inadequately demonstrated.

The accusation later affected Durruti’s 1932 deportation: the governor of Villa Cisneros, identified as Regueral’s son, reportedly refused to receive him.

### Arrest in May 1923

Durruti was arrested at or near the Arcos railway station while traveling between Barcelona and Madrid. Allegations included:

- armed robbery of a contractor named Mendizábal;
- participation in an intended attack on Alfonso XIII;
- desertion and escape from military custody.

He was transferred to San Sebastián. Sources differ over the precise disposition of these accusations.

### Banco de España robbery, Gijón, 1 September 1923

**Strongly reported collective responsibility:** Los Solidarios robbed the Gijón branch of the Bank of Spain, taking approximately **500,000–650,000 pesetas**, depending on the source.

The operation involved gunfire and the death of an employee or bank director in some accounts. Durruti was widely identified as a participant or organizer.

The money was reportedly intended for:

- legal defense and bail for imprisoned comrades, especially those accused in the Soldevila case;
- underground CNT activity;
- support for an exile propaganda center.

No surviving financial ledger permits a complete accounting. The robbery created the enduring image of Durruti as Spain’s prototypical anarchist “expropriator.”

## Primo de Rivera dictatorship and European exile, 1923–1924

After General Miguel Primo de Rivera’s coup in September 1923, Durruti and Ascaso escaped to France. They worked as mechanics, maintained CNT networks, gathered weapons, and discussed revolutionary action against the dictatorship.

French and Spanish police treated them as dangerous international militants. They had no stable capital or conventional business holdings; industrial wages, movement solidarity, and proceeds from armed expropriations financed their lives and political operations.

## Latin American journey, 1924–1926

At the end of 1924, Durruti and Ascaso left Europe. Their shifting group—usually including **Gregorio Jover** and, at various stages, Alejandro Ascaso and other militants—was called **Los Errantes** and, by sections of the Latin American press, **Los Apaches**.

The best broad reconstruction places them in:

- Cuba
- Mexico
- Chile
- Argentina
- Uruguay
- possibly Peru and Colombia during transit

Documentation is uneven; memoir, police, and press accounts often contradict one another.

### Cuba

They reportedly worked in the sugar industry and organized labor while making contact with Cuban anarchists. Accounts attribute robberies or attempted expropriations to them, but the exact incidents and participants remain incompletely documented.

### Mexico

They joined Spanish and Mexican anarchist networks. The group reportedly robbed a textile payroll or bank at **La Carolina**, where a victim was killed.

- **Documented/strongly reported:** the group conducted an armed expropriation.
- **Unresolved individual responsibility:** sources do not securely establish who fired the fatal shot.
- Money was said to have been distributed to anarchist organizations and prisoners’ campaigns, but no complete audit exists.

### Chile

The group reached Chile in June 1925. Durruti was later celebrated or condemned as a participant in what some accounts call Chile’s first modern bank robbery, involving the Banco de Chile or a branch in Santiago.

“First bank robber in Chile” is a journalistic superlative, not a secure legal classification. Earlier armed thefts existed; the claim generally means a conspicuous automobile-era bank raid attributed to foreign anarchists.

### Argentina

Durruti used aliases; Argentine police material identifies **Ramón Carcaño Caballero** as one of his identities. The group was linked to the robbery of the Banco de la Provincia branch at San Martín and other operations around Buenos Aires.

**Allegations and reported facts:**

- Argentine police attributed several armed robberies and deaths to the group.
- A death sentence or capital prosecution is mentioned in later literature, but accounts disagree about whether there was a final judgment against Durruti personally, an indictment in absentia, or a police claim.
- Osvaldo Bayer’s research places Durruti within the wider Argentine tradition of anarchist expropriation but warns, implicitly through its documentation, against treating every police attribution as proven.

### Uruguay and departure

The fugitives crossed the Río de la Plata and operated or found refuge in Uruguay. They eventually obtained passage back to Europe.

### Character of the “expropriations”

The participants described bank robberies as **expropriations**: seizure of capitalist funds for revolutionary defense, publications, prisoners, and organization.

The legal and material facts remain that the operations were armed robberies and sometimes resulted in death. Political purpose does not establish who committed each killing, and later romantic accounts do not supply a verified accounting of the money.

## France: Alfonso XIII plot, arrest, and extradition crisis, 1926–1927

Durruti, Ascaso, and Jover returned to France. In June 1926 they prepared another attempted attack on Alfonso XIII during the king’s Paris visit.

A driver or intermediary informed the police. French authorities arrested the men on **25 June 1926**. Weapons and plans supplied the basis for prosecution.

Spain and Argentina requested extradition. A broad asylum campaign argued that extradition would expose them to torture or execution. Participants included **Louis Lecoin**, **Nicolas Faucier**, Sébastien Faure, lawyers, unions, and international anarchists.

The French proceedings produced limited prison sentences rather than extradition. Durruti and his comrades had effectively served those sentences in pretrial detention. Public pressure helped secure their release in July 1927.

## Émilienne Morin, Belgium, Germany, and transnational exile, 1927–1931

Durruti met Morin in Paris on 14 July 1927. French authorities soon expelled him. He moved among Belgium, Germany, and other European refuge networks while states disputed where he could legally reside.

In Belgium, Durruti and Morin lived in severe financial insecurity. He worked intermittently as a mechanic; she used her stenographic and office skills. Their acquaintances included Spanish CNT exiles and European anarchists.

Durruti met the exiled Ukrainian anarchist **Nestor Makhno** in Paris or its exile milieu. Later literature dramatized this as a transmission of revolutionary military wisdom, but the documented evidence establishes acquaintance more securely than formal mentorship.

## Return under the Second Republic, 1931

The proclamation of the Spanish Second Republic in April 1931 permitted Durruti’s return. He settled in Barcelona with Morin, joined CNT activity, and became a nationally visible speaker and organizer.

He entered **Nosotros**, a successor affinity group associated with Durruti, Ascaso, García Oliver, Jover, and others. This current favored revolutionary initiative and opposed subordinating the CNT to republican parliamentary politics.

Durruti continued to work as a mechanic when possible. There is no evidence that he accumulated property, investments, business equity, or substantial personal wealth.

His daughter Colette was born on 4 December 1931. The family shortly afterward traveled to León for Santiago Durruti’s funeral.

## Deportation following the Alto Llobregat uprising, 1932

In January 1932, miners and textile workers in the Alto Llobregat proclaimed libertarian communism and seized local institutions. The government suppressed the uprising.

Durruti was arrested although the degree of his operational involvement is disputed. The government deported more than one hundred militants without ordinary completed criminal trials aboard the steamship **Buenos Aires**, which left Barcelona on 10 February.

The deportees were transported toward Spanish African possessions. Durruti and several others were moved through:

- the voyage aboard *Buenos Aires*;
- Las Palmas;
- attempted or disputed placement at **Villa Cisneros** in Spanish Sahara;
- **Puerto de Cabras, Fuerteventura**, where Durruti arrived in April 1932.

He remained on Fuerteventura approximately four and a half months. His letters describe hunger-strike agitation, political discussion, separation from Morin, and anxiety about his sick infant daughter.

The deportations became a major grievance against the Republic. They were administrative-political repression, not punishment following individual convictions for each deportee.

## Insurrections and imprisonment, 1933–1934

Durruti participated in or supported CNT insurrectionary strategy during 1933. His exact role in the January 1933 uprising is less clearly documented than his role in December.

After the right’s electoral victory in November, a CNT plenary created a revolutionary committee for an uprising centered on Aragón. Durruti joined that committee with **Cipriano Mera**, Antonio Ejarque, Joaquín Ascaso, and others.

The **December 1933 insurrection** involved armed confrontations, seizures, sabotage, deaths, and declarations of libertarian communism. It failed. Durruti was arrested in Barcelona on 13 December.

An archival catalogue from Aragón records criminal proceedings for offenses against the form of government involving Durruti, Teodoro Goñi, Miguel Abós, Santiago Baranda, Joaquín Ascaso, Augusto Alcrudo, and others.

Durruti was transferred to Burgos prison in February 1934 and held under close surveillance. From prison he argued for a workers’ alliance between CNT and UGT constructed from below through workers’ councils, without political-party control.

Militants reportedly stole the prosecution dossier from the Zaragoza commercial court at gunpoint. Members of the revolutionary committee then claimed primary responsibility in an effort to relieve rank-and-file prisoners whose confessions were allegedly coerced.

A general amnesty in April 1934 freed Durruti along with both left-wing insurrectionaries and participants in the right-wing Sanjurjo coup.

## 1934–February 1936

Durruti resumed organizing amid intense repression. He criticized police chief **Miquel Badia**, particularly after the killing of the Italian anarchist Bruno Alpini under circumstances anarchists described as the *ley de fugas*. Later accounts attribute private threats or wishes for Badia’s death to Durruti, but no evidence establishes his participation in Badia’s 1936 assassination.

During the October 1934 crisis, Durruti was again detained. Sources disagree about the exact duration and legal basis. His name appears in connection with imprisonment in Burgos, but some summaries merge this detention with his earlier 1934 imprisonment.

The CNT debated electoral abstention before the February 1936 election. Durruti did not become a parliamentarian or endorse ordinary electoral politics, but he reportedly advised militants in language that permitted or implicitly encouraged voting for the Popular Front, principally to obtain amnesty for prisoners. This tactical flexibility later complicated portrayals of him as an invariant abstentionist.

## July 1936: military uprising and revolution in Barcelona

The military revolt reached Barcelona on 19 July 1936. CNT defense committees, armed workers, loyal security forces, and republican personnel defeated the insurgent garrison.

Durruti and Francisco Ascaso participated directly in the fighting, including the assault around the **Atarazanas/Drassanes barracks**. Ascaso was killed.

Durruti emerged as one of the most visible CNT representatives in the revolutionary situation. He participated in the **Central Committee of Antifascist Militias of Catalonia**, although he held no conventional ministerial office.

The CNT faced a choice between taking exclusive power, joining the Generalitat, or maintaining a dual structure of revolutionary committees and formal republican government. Durruti accepted the compromise structure. Later anarchist critics regarded this as the beginning of a retreat from revolution; others saw it as a practical response to war, divided authority, and the danger of foreign intervention.

## The Durruti Column and the Aragón front, July–November 1936

On approximately **24 July 1936**, Durruti led a militia column from Barcelona toward Zaragoza. Estimates at departure range from roughly 2,000 to more than 3,000; strength later fluctuated and may have grown considerably.

The force included:

- CNT and FAI militia groups;
- some regular soldiers;
- international volunteers;
- women combatants and support personnel;
- military advisers, prominently **Major Enric Pérez i Farràs** and later **Sergeant José Manzana**.

The column advanced through Aragón, taking or occupying towns and establishing headquarters at **Bujaraloz**. Its ultimate objective, Zaragoza, was not captured.

### Military record

**Successes**

- helped stabilize the republican front in eastern Aragón;
- occupied territory and secured communications;
- became an international symbol of worker militias;
- encouraged supply, medical, cultural, and propaganda services;
- supported village-level revolutionary restructuring.

**Failures and limitations**

- failed to capture Zaragoza;
- suffered from shortages of rifles, machine guns, artillery, transport, and ammunition;
- displayed uneven training and discipline;
- operated through decentralized units that complicated coordinated offensives;
- became immobilized in positional warfare.

Claims that government or Communist authorities deliberately starved the column of arms are common in anarchist testimony. Unequal weapons distribution is documented, but a centrally ordered plan specifically intended to destroy Durruti has not been established.

### Collectivization and violence behind the front

The Durruti Column’s advance coincided with agrarian collectivization. Village committees abolished or reorganized private property, collectivized land, issued local vouchers, and redistributed goods.

Participation varied:

- in some towns collectivization was voluntary or enjoyed broad poor-peasant support;
- in others armed militia presence created coercive conditions;
- property owners, clergy, political opponents, and alleged fascist sympathizers were arrested, expelled, or killed.

Durruti advocated revolutionary transformation and defended collectivization. The available evidence does not permit every execution or seizure in the column’s zone to be personally attributed to him. Conversely, command prominence makes it inadequate to treat all rear-area coercion as wholly unrelated to his force.

Historians of Republican-zone violence differ on the column’s direct responsibility. Some hostile accounts attribute large numbers of killings to “Durruti” personally or to a “Durruti gang” without establishing chains of command. Sympathetic accounts sometimes minimize coercion by describing all seizures as spontaneous peasant action. Neither formulation resolves incident-level responsibility.

### Discipline and militarization

Durruti rejected conventional barracks discipline while demanding operational obedience. His reported position distinguished voluntary revolutionary discipline from externally imposed military hierarchy.

By autumn 1936 he showed increasing concern about desertion, ammunition use, and disorder. He accepted military specialists and centralized tactical direction while resisting full bureaucratic militarization.

The famous slogan **“Renunciamos a todo menos a la victoria”** (“We renounce everything except victory”) became central to later CNT propaganda. Recent scholarship considers its attribution doubtful or likely fabricated as part of wartime militarizing discourse.

## Political and intellectual development in 1936

Durruti’s wartime position combined:

- defeat of the military rebellion;
- immediate social revolution;
- worker and peasant control;
- hostility to restoration of bourgeois property;
- suspicion of state power and political parties;
- pragmatic coordination with republican institutions;
- growing insistence on war discipline.

He criticized the separation of “war first” from “revolution later.” His rhetoric nevertheless moved toward sacrifice, supply mobilization, and unified military effort as the war worsened.

On 4 November 1936 he delivered a major radio speech from Barcelona. Addressing CNT members and militia fighters, he demanded work, discipline, mobilization, and an end to the gap between front-line sacrifice and rear-guard disorder. Transcripts exist in censored and uncensored forms; editions should not be assumed textually identical.

## Emma Goldman meeting

In October 1936 **Emma Goldman** visited Durruti at the Aragón front, identified in sources as Pina de Ebro or the surrounding front sector. She described him in a private letter as the most impressive anarchist personality she had met in Spain.

Goldman later wrote the memorial text **“Durruti Is Dead, Yet Living.”** Her portrayal contributed strongly to the international heroic image of Durruti but was founded on an actual meeting rather than imaginary association.

## Transfer to Madrid, November 1936

Madrid faced a major Nationalist assault. Republican and CNT leaders requested reinforcements from Aragón. Durruti initially resisted weakening the front but agreed to transfer a selected force.

He arrived in Madrid around **14 November 1936** with approximately 1,400–1,800 men according to many military reconstructions; the frequently repeated figure of 3,000–3,500 may refer to intended, nominal, or broader strength.

The unit fought around:

- Casa de Campo;
- Ciudad Universitaria;
- Hospital Clínico;
- Parque del Oeste;
- the Moncloa sector.

It suffered heavy casualties and disorganization. Opponents accused Durruti’s militiamen of breaking and fleeing; anarchist accounts emphasized inadequate preparation, misleading orders, exposed positions, and severe losses. Both retreat and subsequent continued fighting are documented. The claim that the column alone “saved Madrid” is hagiographic; the defense involved numerous Spanish and International Brigade formations.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Employment and institutions

Durruti’s known employers and work settings included:

- Melchor Martínez’s mechanical workshop in León;
- railway workshops or operations of the Northern Railway Company;
- French industrial employment, commonly associated with Renault;
- later mechanical employment in Barcelona.

### Organizations

- UGT-associated metal and railway unions
- CNT
- FAI
- Los Justicieros
- Los Solidarios
- Los Errantes
- Nosotros
- Central Committee of Antifascist Militias of Catalonia
- Durruti Column

### Businesses, property, and investments

No evidence was found that Durruti:

- owned a substantial business;
- held corporate shares or investment portfolios;
- possessed significant real estate;
- registered patents;
- developed a commercially exploited invention;
- maintained a personal financial structure separate from movement funds.

His relevant financial activity consisted of wages, household support, solidarity collections, organizational funds, and armed expropriations. Claims that he enriched himself from robberies are not supported by identifiable assets or a documented estate. Claims that every peseta went to the movement are likewise not independently auditable.

### Technologies

Durruti was a trained mechanic rather than an inventor. Technologies central to his activity included railway machinery, automobiles, printing presses, telephones and radio, and small arms. No patents are known.

## Political, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

Durruti moved from a socialist labor environment to revolutionary syndicalism and anarchism.

His identifiable commitments were:

- abolition of capitalism and wage domination;
- abolition or displacement of the state by federated workers’ and communal bodies;
- direct action and revolutionary syndicalism;
- armed self-defense and insurrection;
- anti-clericalism;
- internationalism;
- worker control and agrarian collectivization;
- opposition to professional political leadership.

He was not an individualist anarchist in the narrow sense. His politics centered on mass unions and collective social transformation, although he accepted small armed affinity groups as instruments of defense, finance, and revolutionary preparation.

His record also shows tactical change:

- socialist trade unionism before 1917;
- clandestine armed action in the 1920s;
- mass CNT insurrectionism in the early Republic;
- partial electoral flexibility in 1936;
- wartime cooperation with government-linked structures;
- increasingly stringent military discipline.

There is no evidence of conventional religious observance in adulthood. He was associated with the anti-clerical culture of Spanish anarchism, but specific personal theological writings are absent.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Francisco Ascaso

Durruti’s closest political companion during the 1920s. They shared exile, armed actions, Latin American travel, arrests, and extradition proceedings. Ascaso died during the fighting in Barcelona on 20 July 1936.

### Gregorio Jover

Member of Los Solidarios and Los Errantes; companion during the Latin American journey and French proceedings.

### Joan García Oliver

Co-founder or central participant in Los Solidarios and Nosotros. Their relationship combined friendship and strategic disagreement. García Oliver later entered the Republican government as justice minister, an accommodation that exposed divisions within anarchism.

### Ricardo Sanz

Action-group comrade and later successor connected to the Durruti Column after Durruti’s death.

### Manuel Buenacasa

CNT organizer who helped introduce Durruti to anarchist militant networks around Zaragoza and San Sebastián.

### Émilienne Morin

Companion, political collaborator, wage earner, wartime press worker, and later guardian of Durruti’s memory. Their partnership survived repeated separations but placed a disproportionate domestic burden on Morin.

### Colette Durruti

Daughter and later custodian of family correspondence. Because Durruti was repeatedly imprisoned or absent, her direct childhood relationship with him was limited.

### Teresa Margalef/Margaleff

CNT activist who cared for Colette while Morin worked and Durruti was imprisoned or deported.

### Cipriano Mera

CNT militant and later militia commander. Collaborated in revolutionary activity and the defense of Madrid. His memoir transmitted José Manzana’s accidental-shooting account.

### José Manzana

Military adviser to Durruti in Madrid and one of the small number of men present when he was shot. Later investigations have considered the possibility that the fatal round came from Manzana’s weapon.

### Louis Lecoin and Nicolas Faucier

French anarchists central to the campaign against extradition of Durruti, Ascaso, and Jover.

### Nestor Makhno

Exiled Ukrainian anarchist whom Durruti met in France. Claims of a formal teacher-pupil relationship are exaggerated; their documented connection was political acquaintance and discussion.

### Federica Montseny and Diego Abad de Santillán

Senior CNT-FAI figures in 1936. They interacted with Durruti in debates over war, revolution, government collaboration, and his deployment to Madrid.

### Enemies and institutional opponents

These changed over time:

- railway management and military authorities after 1917;
- Spanish monarchy and police;
- Primo de Rivera’s dictatorship;
- French, Argentine, Chilean, and other police services;
- Republican governments during the 1932–34 insurrectionary period;
- Nationalist military forces in 1936;
- political rivals inside the antifascist coalition, especially Communist and state-centralizing currents.

Opposition did not imply a single continuous conspiracy: the institutions and conflicts differed substantially by period.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, and Political Violence

## Armed robbery and homicide

Durruti knowingly belonged to groups that conducted armed robberies. Participation in the Gijón operation and Latin American expropriations is strongly supported, although incident-level roles vary.

Deaths occurred during several attributed operations. The evidence is insufficient to identify Durruti as the shooter in every case. Neither wholesale denial nor automatic attribution is warranted.

## Political assassination

Durruti participated in at least two conspiratorial milieus planning the assassination of Alfonso XIII. The planned attacks were not completed.

He was accused of involvement in the killings of Cardinal Soldevila and González Regueral. The evidence more directly implicates other Los Solidarios members in the Soldevila shooting and conflicts with Durruti’s alleged presence in the Regueral case.

## “Band of Durruti”

Contemporary police and press frequently called Los Solidarios or Los Errantes “Durruti’s band.” This personalized a decentralized group and facilitated attributing unrelated or poorly solved crimes to its most recognizable member.

That does not mean all accusations were invented. It means each incident requires separate proof.

## Insurrection against the Second Republic

Durruti did not consistently defend republican legality. He participated in efforts to overthrow or bypass the Republic through libertarian revolution in 1932 and 1933. Arrest, deportation, and imprisonment followed.

His later military defense of the Republic’s territory against Franco did not erase his earlier opposition to the republican state. Conversely, opposition to the state does not establish responsibility for every violent act committed during CNT uprisings.

## Coercion and killings in Aragón

Militia-controlled territory experienced killings of clergy, landowners, rightists, and alleged coup supporters, as well as confiscations and compulsory collectivization.

Durruti bore political and command responsibility for a major armed column operating in this environment. The surviving material does not establish that he ordered a general extermination policy, nor does it justify removing the column entirely from responsibility for violence committed under its occupation.

## Military competence

Critics portray Durruti as an amateur whose romantic rejection of military organization caused unnecessary deaths. Admirers depict him as an intuitive strategist obstructed by withheld weapons.

The record supports a mixed assessment without assigning merit:

- he had no formal officer training;
- he successfully mobilized and retained a large volunteer force;
- the column failed to take Zaragoza;
- logistics and arms shortages were serious;
- decentralized organization impeded some operations;
- by late 1936 Durruti demanded stronger discipline and accepted professional military advice.

## Relationship with Communist forces

Communists and anarchists disputed command, arms allocation, revolution, and state authority. Later anarchist narratives accused Stalinists of arranging Durruti’s death. Communist narratives sometimes appropriated him as a late convert to discipline or even claimed that he wanted Stalin’s portrait on his desk.

No reliable evidence establishes that Durruti was becoming a Communist. The Stalin-portrait claim has been described by historians as fabricated propaganda.

## No discovered evidence of conventional fraud or corruption

The adversarial search located accusations of robbery, assassination, insurrection, coercion, and military irresponsibility. It did not locate credible evidence of:

- commercial accounting fraud;
- embezzlement for personal enrichment;
- money laundering in the modern financial sense;
- corporate corruption;
- patent fraud;
- civil lawsuits over property or business dealings.

The absence of such allegations reflects the nature of his life, not proof that all movement finance was transparent.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## The “pile of ruins” quotation

### The story

Durruti allegedly told Canadian journalist **Pierre van Paassen** that workers were not afraid of ruins because they carried a new world in their hearts. The quotation became perhaps the most famous statement attributed to Spanish anarchism.

### Appearance and circulation

Van Paassen’s report appeared in the **Toronto Daily Star on 18 August 1936**. It was repeatedly reprinted, paraphrased, placed on posters, and incorporated into biographies and films.

### Evidence problem

Historian Danny Evans has shown serious problems with the purported interview:

- Van Paassen may not have been in Spain at the necessary time.
- His later autobiographical accounts contain demonstrable embellishments.
- Details in the article do not align securely with Durruti’s movements.
- No independent witness or recording confirms the exchange.

### Status

**Probably fabricated or heavily reconstructed attribution.**

The words remain historically important because they condensed an international image of revolutionary confidence and indifference to bourgeois property.

## “We renounce everything except victory”

### Story and use

The slogan presented Durruti as accepting the suspension of revolutionary aims for military victory.

### Evidence

Recent scholarship on anarchist militarization regards the dictum as likely fabricated or at least textually insecure. It appeared in CNT propaganda useful to leaders defending militarization and institutional compromise.

### Status

**Disputed attribution; likely propaganda formulation.**

## Robin Hood of the working class

Latin American and anarchist memory portrayed Durruti and Los Errantes as working-class Robin Hoods who robbed banks solely to free prisoners and finance revolution.

Documented movement spending supports part of the story. No complete accounting proves that all proceeds were distributed, while the group’s poverty weighs against a simple personal-enrichment theory.

**Status:** symbolic interpretation built on genuine expropriations but financially unauditable.

## The flawless proletarian household

Accounts of Durruti in an apron washing dishes and caring for Colette became evidence of anarchist gender equality.

There is credible testimony that he performed domestic work. Morin’s life nevertheless shows that she carried most childcare and financial responsibility during his absences.

**Status:** documented domestic participation expanded into a simplified exemplary legend.

## Durruti as supreme commander

Popular memory calls him “General Durruti.” He did exercise effective command and enormous personal authority, but anarchist units used terms such as delegate and rejected formal rank structures.

**Status:** retrospective title, not a regular military appointment.

## Durruti as secret Communist convert

Communist memorial narratives claimed that he had embraced centralized discipline, supported the Communist line, or wanted Stalin’s portrait.

His late emphasis on discipline is documented. Conversion to Stalinism is not.

**Status:** political appropriation unsupported by reliable evidence.

## The untrained genius who could not lose

Hagiography credits Durruti with nearly single-handedly defeating the Barcelona coup, liberating Aragón, and saving Madrid.

He was important in all three theaters, but:

- many organizations defeated the Barcelona garrison;
- Zaragoza remained under Nationalist control;
- the Madrid defense involved Republican units, other militias, Soviet-supported formations, and International Brigades.

**Status:** heroic compression of collective events.

## Death: Documentary Record and Competing Explanations

## Wounding

On **19 November 1936**, Durruti was shot in Madrid near the Ciudad Universitaria/Moncloa front. The most frequently specified location is Calle de Isaac Peral or its vicinity.

He was traveling with a small party that included José Manzana and driver **Julio Graves** or a similarly rendered name. The group stopped after seeing militiamen retreating or moving away from the front. Durruti got out or prepared to get out of the vehicle; moments later he was struck in the chest.

He was taken to the former Hotel Ritz, then functioning as a militia blood hospital.

## Medical evidence

The bullet penetrated the thorax and caused severe internal damage. Later summaries describe a **9 mm Largo** projectile. Durruti underwent treatment or surgery but died at approximately 4:00 a.m. on **20 November 1936**.

Some contemporary announcements obscured the timing and circumstances to avoid panic.

## Principal theories

### 1. Nationalist sniper

The first public version attributed the shot to enemy fire from the Hospital Clínico area.

**Evidence for:** the party was within an active battle zone exposed to Nationalist positions.

**Evidence against:** medical descriptions of powder burning or a close-range wound, if accurately transmitted, are incompatible with a distant sniper. Witness accounts also place companions and weapons close to Durruti.

**Status:** possible contemporary explanation, weakened by later medical and eyewitness evidence.

### 2. Accidental discharge from Durruti’s own weapon

The best-known accidental version says Durruti’s **Naranjero** submachine gun discharged when its butt struck the automobile’s running board or door.

**Evidence for:** the weapon type could discharge accidentally; reported close-range characteristics fit an adjacent weapon; companions may have concealed an embarrassing accident.

**Evidence against:** descriptions of who was carrying which weapon conflict. Some witnesses said Durruti habitually carried a pistol rather than the Naranjero at that moment.

**Status:** plausible but unproven.

### 3. Accidental discharge from Manzana’s weapon

Recent investigations, including a 2025 historical study, argue that the shot may have come from the Naranjero carried by José Manzana, either accidentally or intentionally.

**Evidence for:** witness and medical reconstructions place Manzana and his weapon near Durruti; some testimony says Manzana, not Durruti, carried the Naranjero.

**Evidence against:** the surviving testimony is late, contradictory, and affected by decades of political controversy.

**Status:** plausible recent reconstruction; intentionality unresolved.

### 4. Deliberate killing by Communist or Soviet agents

Anarchist rumors soon alleged a Stalinist assassination intended to remove an independent revolutionary leader.

**Evidence for:** real political conflict existed over militarization, governmental authority, and Soviet influence.

**Evidence against:** no operational order, identified assassin, forensic proof, or contemporaneous documentary chain has emerged. Political motive alone is insufficient.

**Status:** allegation/conspiracy theory, unproven.

### 5. Deliberate killing by one of his own militiamen

Some Communist and hostile accounts said a retreating or resentful member of Durruti’s column shot him after being reprimanded.

**Evidence for:** retreat, disciplinary confrontation, and anger within the unit are documented possibilities.

**Evidence against:** accounts often differ on the date, location, direction of fire, and identity of the alleged shooter; some are plainly propagandistic.

**Status:** unresolved allegation.

### 6. Anarchist internal assassination

A variant says opponents of CNT compromise, militarization, or Durruti’s leadership killed him.

No identified perpetrator or reliable documentary trail supports it.

**Status:** rumor.

## Why the truth was obscured

Abel Paz called the aftermath a “conspiracy of fear.” An accident could demoralize the CNT; murder allegations could provoke armed conflict within the Republican camp; a sniper version supplied an immediately usable martyr narrative.

The surviving evidence most strongly favors a **close-range discharge from a weapon inside or beside Durruti’s own party**, probably accidental, but it does not establish whose weapon fired or exclude intentional action beyond reasonable historical doubt.

## Funeral and Immediate Aftermath

Durruti’s body was transported to Barcelona. His funeral on **23 November 1936** became one of the largest mass demonstrations in the city’s history. Estimates range widely, frequently reaching several hundred thousand.

The procession included anarchists, socialists, communists, republican officials, militia units, foreign observers, and ordinary residents. Organizational disorder at the cemetery complicated burial, and the grave had to be completed later.

His death produced:

- immediate CNT-FAI martyr imagery;
- poems, ballads, posters, funeral films, and commemorative brochures;
- the renaming of units and institutions;
- competing attempts by anarchist and Communist organizations to define his final political meaning;
- rumors of revenge killings.

A claim that CNT-FAI personnel killed fifty-two imprisoned policemen in immediate retaliation circulates in later secondary summaries. The accessible search did not locate adequate incident-level primary documentation tying such an action to a direct order by Durruti—who was already dead—or securely establishing the claimed motive. It should therefore not be repeated as a settled personal action.

Durruti was buried in Montjuïc Cemetery near Francisco Ascaso and Francesc Ferrer i Guàrdia.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Durruti’s death fixed his public image before the compromises, defeats, and exile that transformed surviving CNT leaders.

Different traditions constructed different Durrutis:

- **CNT-FAI memorial culture:** incorruptible worker, man of action, revolutionary commander, and martyr.
- **Republican unity propaganda:** heroic antifascist whose example justified discipline and centralized war mobilization.
- **Communist propaganda:** a militant supposedly moving toward Communist military positions.
- **Francoist historiography:** criminal, bandit, terrorist, assassin, and symbol of “red” disorder.
- **Later libertarian criticism:** revolutionary figure whose image was used by CNT committees to conceal retreat, government collaboration, or militarization.
- **Popular culture:** romantic outlaw, worker-warrior, and “Spanish Robin Hood.”
- **Modern scholarship:** a historically important labor militant whose actual record must be separated from both hagiography and accumulated police attribution.

### Friends of Durruti

The **Friends of Durruti Group** formed in 1937, after his death, among anarchists critical of CNT participation in government and the abandonment of revolutionary power. Durruti did not found, direct, or write the group’s program. His name supplied symbolic legitimacy.

### Material commemorations

His name has been used for:

- streets and public spaces;
- anarchist groups and publishing projects;
- militia reenactment and historical-memory organizations;
- songs, posters, murals, theater, comics, and political banners;
- the post-punk band **The Durutti Column**, whose altered spelling referenced a Situationist poster and Durruti’s column.

His grave in Montjuïc remains a site of anarchist commemoration.

### Historical reinterpretation

Recent research has especially revised:

- the authenticity of celebrated quotations;
- the scale and exact membership of the Durruti Column;
- the coercive dimensions of Aragón collectivization;
- the political construction of his funeral;
- the fatal shooting and possibility that Manzana’s weapon fired it;
- the distinction between crimes demonstrably committed by Durruti and those attributed to “his band.”

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by searched sources are included. Mere ideological resemblance or presence in the same era is excluded.

### Emma Goldman

**Documented fact.** Goldman traveled to CNT-FAI Barcelona in 1936 and visited Durruti on the Aragón front in October, identified as Pina de Ebro or its vicinity. She described him in a letter of 17 October and later wrote *Durruti Is Dead, Yet Living*. The meeting materially influenced Durruti’s international memorial image.

### Lucía Sánchez Saornil

**Reported institutional connection, not a securely documented personal relationship.** Sánchez Saornil was a prominent CNT-affiliated anarchist and Mujeres Libres organizer. Her name appears among contributors to or participants in later radio and printed Durruti memorial material, including *Hora Durruti*. The present research did not establish a specific documented meeting, correspondence, or collaboration between the two.

### George Orwell

**No lifetime crossing found.** Orwell reached Barcelona in late December 1936, after Durruti’s death. He encountered the revolutionary milieu and militia system with which Durruti was associated but did not meet him. Their later joint appearance in cultural retrospectives is commemorative, not biographical.

### Albert Camus

**No documented personal crossing found.** Camus’s later libertarian sympathies and engagement with the Spanish Republican exile do not demonstrate meeting, correspondence, or acknowledged direct influence from Durruti.

### Erich Mühsam, Gustav Landauer, Rudolf Rocker, Mikhail Bakunin, Karl Marx, Vladimir Ilyich Ulyanov, Rosa Luxemburg, Walter Benjamin, Federico García Lorca, and Pablo Picasso

**No specific personal crossing established in the sources consulted.** Some were ideological predecessors, contemporaries, or later interpreters of related revolutionary movements, but no meeting, correspondence, collaboration, or explicit individually acknowledged influence satisfying the atlas standard was located.

Absence is therefore the principal atlas finding apart from Emma Goldman and the limited memorial-institutional connection with Lucía Sánchez Saornil.
