## Works by Simonides of Ceos

### Commemorative lyric poems

- **490 BCE:** *Elegy for the Athenians who fell at Marathon*. A public memorial poem with which Simonides reportedly defeated Aeschylus in an Athenian competition; no securely identifiable text survives, and both its form and occasion depend on later testimony. [Smith, “Simonides”](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.04.0104%3Aentry%3Dsimonides-bio-2)

- **480 BCE:** *Song on the dead at Thermopylae* (PMG 531; Poltera fr. 4). A surviving fragment of a public choral commemoration of Leonidas and the fallen Greeks. Diodorus calls it an encomium, while scholars have also understood it as a hymn or threnodic song; consequently its genre should not be silently fixed. [Diodorus, *Library* 11.11](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Diod.+11.11&fromdoc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0084), [Boedeker](https://www.uni-koeln.de/phil-fak/ifa/zpe/downloads/1995/107pdf/107217.pdf)

- **480 BCE:** *Poem on the battle of Artemisium* (PMG 533; West/Sider elegies 1–4). A fragmentary Persian-War composition whose medium resists firm classification: Priscian’s evidence points to lyric metre, whereas the confused notice in the *Suda* has encouraged classification as an elegy. It may also be wrong to assume that all surviving Artemisium material belongs to one poem. [Sider review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2022/2022.04.08/), [Boedeker](https://www.uni-koeln.de/phil-fak/ifa/zpe/downloads/1995/107pdf/107217.pdf)

- **480 BCE:** *Poem on the battle of Salamis* (West/Sider elegies 5–9). A nearly lost narrative or commemorative poem named by the *Suda*; a few fragments have been tentatively assigned to it. Whether it was elegiac, lyric, or even the poem to which those fragments belonged remains disputed. [West review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/1993/1993.02.14/), [Sider review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2022/2022.04.08/)

- **479 BCE or later:** *Plataea Elegy* (West/Sider elegies 10–18, with related papyrus fragments). An extended elegiac narrative commemorating the campaign and battle of Plataea, invoking the Trojan War as a model for the recent conflict. Its surviving papyrus leaves are substantial but incomplete, and the precise boundaries and arrangement of the poem remain conjectural. [Oxyrhynchus Papyri](https://oxyrhynchus.web.ox.ac.uk/node/3486126), [West review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/1993/1993.02.14/), [Sider review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2022/2022.04.08/)

### Epinician songs

- **Date unknown:** *Victory ode for Crius of Aegina* (Poltera fr. 16). An epinician—a commissioned performance celebrating an athletic victor—known only in fragments and ancient headings; its conventional modern title identifies the recipient rather than an authorial title. [Rawles, “Poem-titles in Simonides, Pindar, and Bacchylides”](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:64598c0b-64a9-476e-a805-9bf51d5d9026)

- **Dates unknown:** *Epinician odes*. Simonides made a large body of victory songs, apparently collected in antiquity in several books and arranged by athletic event rather than festival. Only small quotations and papyrus scraps survive, generally too disconnected to determine how many individual songs they represent; the surviving “epinician fragments” are therefore remains of works, not themselves a defensible list of complete works. [Carey, in *A Companion to Greek Lyric*](https://erenow.org/ancient/a-companion-to-greek-lyric/23.php), [Poltera review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2011/2011.06.32/)

### Encomia and sympotic songs

- **Late sixth or early fifth century BCE:** *Encomium for Scopas* (PMG 542; Poltera fr. 260). A moralizing song for the Thessalian aristocrat Scopas, partly preserved through Plato’s *Protagoras*. It is an encomium and performance text rather than a “book”; the anecdote that Scopas withheld half Simonides’ fee is later biographical tradition, not secure evidence for the poem’s production. [Plato, *Protagoras* 339a–347a](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Plat.+Prot.+339a), [Poltera review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2011/2011.06.32/)

- **Dates unknown:** *Sympotic elegies and drinking songs* (West fragments 19–33; Sider’s enlarged elegiac and sympotic corpus). These are fragmentary songs for convivial performance, including ethical reflection, praise, and personal address. The papyrus and quotation fragments cannot securely be divided into individual original poems, and some pieces have alternative authorial attributions. [West review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/1993/1993.02.14/), [Sider review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2022/2022.04.08/)

- **Date unknown:** *Skolion of disputed attribution* (PMG 890; Sider elegy 111). A lyric drinking song attributed to Simonides by several ancient sources but also assigned elsewhere; Poltera omitted it, while Sider includes it with explicit hesitation. It resists entry as a secure Simonidean work. [Sider review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2022/2022.04.08/)

### Mythic narrative songs and dithyrambs

- **Date unknown:** *Danaë* or *Lament of Danaë* (PMG 543; Poltera fr. 271). A substantial lyric fragment representing Danaë at sea with the infant Perseus. The customary title is modern and descriptive: the fragment may have belonged to a dithyramb about Perseus, but neither its original title nor its complete performative form is known. [Poltera review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2011/2011.06.32/), [Smith, “Simonides”](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.04.0104%3Aentry%3Dsimonides-bio-2)

- **Date unknown:** *Memnon*. A lost dithyramb known from Strabo’s citation of Simonides concerning Memnon’s tomb in Syria. Its ancient title appears to identify a mythic narrative song, but no continuous text survives. [Strabo, *Geography* 15.3.2](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Strab.+15.3.2)

- **Dates unknown:** *Dithyrambs* (also called “tragedies” in some ancient testimony). Simonides reportedly won fifty-six dithyrambic competitions, implying a large corpus of choral manifestations combining words, music, dance, and civic performance. The lost performances were closer to a temporary Gesamtkunstwerk than to books, and neither fifty-six victories nor the fragment numbers supply a reliable count of distinct compositions. [Poltera review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2011/2011.06.32/), [Smith, “Simonides”](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.04.0104%3Aentry%3Dsimonides-bio-2)

### Hymns, paeans, laments, and other performance genres

- **Dates unknown:** *Hymns and prayers or curses*. Ancient cataloguing testimony assigns hymns and *kateuchai* to Simonides, but no surviving fragment can be organized into a securely titled, complete work. These are attested fields of practice rather than recoverable individual obras. [Smith, “Simonides”](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.04.0104%3Aentry%3Dsimonides-bio-2), [Poltera review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2011/2011.06.32/)

- **Dates unknown:** *Paeans*. Ancient sources credit Simonides with choral songs of healing, supplication, or divine celebration. Their number, occasions, and titles are lost, so “paeans” names a corpus rather than one work. [Smith, “Simonides”](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.04.0104%3Aentry%3Dsimonides-bio-2)

- **Dates unknown:** *Threnoi* (laments). Simonides was renowned for composed laments, but the surviving quotations cannot generally be assigned to separately identifiable poems. Because a threnos was made for performance in mourning, reducing the corpus to written fragments suppresses part of the original object. [Smith, “Simonides”](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.04.0104%3Aentry%3Dsimonides-bio-2), [Poltera review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2011/2011.06.32/)

- **Dates unknown:** *Partheneia*. Ancient testimony attributes songs for choruses of young women to Simonides, but no individually titled parthenion survives securely enough to list as a separate poem. [Smith, “Simonides”](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.04.0104%3Aentry%3Dsimonides-bio-2)

- **Dates unknown:** *Hyporchemes*. These were choral works joining poetry, music, and mimetic dance; ancient testimony assigns the genre to Simonides, but no recoverable catalogue of individual compositions exists. As performed objects they resist treatment as texts alone. [Smith, “Simonides”](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.04.0104%3Aentry%3Dsimonides-bio-2)

### Epigrams and inscribed memorials

- **480 BCE or shortly afterward:** *Epitaph for Megistias* (Herodotus 7.228; Page FGE 22a; Sider epigram 3). An epitaph for the seer killed at Thermopylae, preserved by Herodotus with an explicit statement that Simonides composed it. It is the only epigram attributed to him that modern scholarship commonly regards as securely authentic. [Herodotus, *Histories* 7.228](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Hdt.+7.228), [Oxford Classical Dictionary, “epigram, Greek”](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548935816)

- **480 BCE or shortly afterward:** *Epitaph for the Spartans at Thermopylae* (Herodotus 7.228; Page FGE 22b; Sider epigram 2). An inscribed memorial voice for the Spartan dead, quoted by Herodotus but not there attributed to Simonides. Its celebrated Simonidean authorship is a later and disputed attachment; it should be catalogued as “attributed to Simonides,” not simply as his work. [Herodotus, *Histories* 7.228](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Hdt.+7.228), [Oxford Classical Dictionary, “epigram, Greek”](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548935816)

- **After 479 BCE:** *Dedication for Pausanias*. A dedicatory epigram associated with Pausanias and the Persian-War victory. Its attribution to Simonides belongs to an unstable epigrammatic tradition and is not comparable in security to the Megistias epitaph. [Sider review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2022/2022.04.08/)

- **Fifth century BCE, if authentic:** *Persian-War epitaphs and dedications for Athenians, Corinthians, Spartans, and other Greeks*. These short public memorial objects survive through inscriptions, historical quotation, or the later Greek Anthology. Individual authorship is usually disputed: Simonides became a magnetic name to which anonymous Persian-War memorial verse was attached. [Page tradition discussed in the *Oxford Classical Dictionary*](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548935816), [Sider review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2022/2022.04.08/)

- **Dates various or unknown:** *Seventy-four epigrams attributed to Simonides* (Sider epigrams 1–74). This is the fullest modern critical collection of the epigrammatic attribution, not seventy-four securely authenticated Simonidean works: Sider intentionally includes poems assigned to him in antiquity regardless of plausibility, while excluding seven pieces printed by Page that ancient sources never actually attributed to him. Several are demonstrably later, and many survive only as literary epigrams rather than inscriptions. [Sider review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2022/2022.04.08/)

### Elegiac and lyric remains that cannot be separated into works

- **Dates unknown:** *Untitled elegiac fragments* (West fragments 34–92; Sider elegies and sympotica through 119). These include ethical, personal, martial, dedicatory, and convivial material preserved on papyrus or through quotation. The standard editions number textual witnesses, not necessarily poems: separate fragments may come from one work, while a single numbered item may preserve only testimony about one, so treating every number as an obra would create fictitious works. [West review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/1993/1993.02.14/), [Sider review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2022/2022.04.08/)

- **Dates unknown:** *Untitled lyric fragments* (Poltera’s Simonidean lyric corpus). These are quotations, papyrus scraps, grammatical examples, and testimonia assigned with varying confidence to hymns, encomia, epinicians, laments, dithyrambs, and uncertain genres. They are manifestations of lost compositions, not a catalogue in which every fragment can responsibly be named as a separate work. [Poltera review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2011/2011.06.32/)

- **Date unknown:** *Metapoetic papyrus fragment* (Sider elegy 91). A minute dactylo-epitrite fragment apparently concerning the shaping of metre and possibly bearing Simonides’ name. Its attribution and even its status as part of a Simonidean poem remain uncertain; Sider includes it, whereas Poltera did not. [Sider review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2022/2022.04.08/)

- **Date unknown:** *Lyric passage transmitted by Aristides* (Sider elegy 113; compare PMG 947). Sider treats Aristides’ introductory wording as Simonidean verse, against editors who classified the associated material as anonymous. It is therefore a disputed manifestation, not a securely established work. [Sider review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2022/2022.04.08/)

### Works known only from ancient cataloguing

- **Date unknown:** *The Reign of Cambyses and Darius*. Ancient bibliographical testimony names a poem on Persian imperial history, but its metre, extent, and even exact status as a discrete title are uncertain; no text securely survives. [Smith, “Simonides”](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.04.0104%3Aentry%3Dsimonides-bio-2)

- **Dates unknown:** *Encomia*. Ancient catalogues and citations attest a broader body of praise poetry beyond the identifiable Scopas and Thermopylae compositions. Neither its original collection structure nor the number of individual works can now be reconstructed. [Smith, “Simonides”](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.04.0104%3Aentry%3Dsimonides-bio-2)

### Sources

- Orlando Poltera, ed., *Simonides lyricus: Testimonia und Fragmente* (Basel: Schwabe, 2008), the principal modern critical edition of the lyric testimonia and fragments; overview in [Bryn Mawr Classical Review 2011.06.32](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2011/2011.06.32/).

- David Sider, ed., *Simonides: Epigrams and Elegies* (Oxford University Press, 2020/2021), the comprehensive modern edition of 74 attributed epigrams and 119 elegiac and sympotic fragments; detailed contents and editorial principles in [Bryn Mawr Classical Review 2022.04.08](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2022/2022.04.08/).

- M. L. West, ed., *Iambi et Elegi Graeci ante Alexandrum cantati*, 2nd ed., vol. 2 (Oxford University Press, 1992), the standard numeration underlying much Simonidean elegiac scholarship; contents summarized in [Bryn Mawr Classical Review 1993.02.14](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/1993/1993.02.14/).

- D. L. Page, ed., *Poetae Melici Graeci* (Oxford, 1962), the conventional PMG numbering for lyric fragments, and *Further Greek Epigrams* (Cambridge University Press, 1981), the earlier standard collection of attributed epigrams; their relation to the newer editions is documented in the [Sider review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2022/2022.04.08/).

- Herodotus, *Histories* 7.228, the principal early witness for the Thermopylae inscriptions and Simonides’ authorship of the Megistias epitaph: [Perseus Digital Library](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Hdt.+7.228).

- Diodorus Siculus, *Library of History* 11, an ancient witness for Simonides’ Thermopylae commemoration: [Perseus Digital Library](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0084%3Abook%3D11).

- Deborah Boedeker, “Simonides on Plataea: Narrative Elegy, Mythodic History,” *Zeitschrift für Papyrologie und Epigraphik* 107 (1995): 217–229: [University of Cologne PDF](https://www.uni-koeln.de/phil-fak/ifa/zpe/downloads/1995/107pdf/107217.pdf).

- The Oxyrhynchus Papyri, “Simonides, Elegies,” description of the papyrus preserving the Plataea poem: [University of Oxford](https://oxyrhynchus.web.ox.ac.uk/node/3486126).

- Richard Rawles, “Poem-titles in Simonides, Pindar, and Bacchylides,” for the status and transmission of ancient and editorial titles: [Oxford University Research Archive](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:64598c0b-64a9-476e-a805-9bf51d5d9026).

- “Epigram, Greek,” *Oxford Classical Dictionary*, for the modern assessment of Simonidean epigram authorship: [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548935816).
