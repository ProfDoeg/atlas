# Simón Rodríguez: Research Dossier

**Subject:** Simón Rodríguez (also Simón Narciso de Jesús; Simón Carreño; Samuel Robinson)  
**Life:** Caracas, 28 October 1769 (traditional and currently best-supported date; 1771 appears in older literature) — Amotape, Peru, 28 February 1854  
**Prepared:** 22 August 2026  
**Methodological note:** This dossier distinguishes contemporary documentation, later testimony, reasonable inference, and legend. Orthography in titles and short quotations is preserved where it is significant to Rodríguez's experimental typography. Assertions about the poorly documented European years, parentage, Rousseau, and the Monte Sacro oath are expressly qualified.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Common name:** Simón Rodríguez.
- **Baptismal/given names:** Usually reconstructed as **Simón Narciso de Jesús**. The expanded modern form **Simón Narciso de Jesús Carreño Rodríguez** combines names and surnames not consistently used by him and should not be mistaken for an unproblematic contemporary civil registration.
- **Other names:** **Simón Carreño Rodríguez** and **Simón Carreño** occur in biographical traditions. He called himself **Samuel Robinson** during much of his exile, beginning in Jamaica in 1797; Bolívar still addressed him affectionately as “Robinson” in 1824.
- **Honorifics and epithets:** Don Simón; “Maestro del Libertador”; “Maestro de América”; “Sócrates de Caracas” or “Sócrates de Colombia”; “el Loco” in hostile, familiar, and later romanticized traditions. These are reputational labels, not offices.
- **Occupations:** Primary-school teacher, private tutor, educational administrator, political and social theorist, author, translator, printer/typesetter, workshop-school organizer; by his own later account, also a worker in industrial chemistry and a schoolmaster in Russia. He undertook technical writing on waterworks, terrain, and earthquake damage.
- **Birth:** Caracas, Captaincy General of Venezuela, **28 October 1769**. Some influential twentieth-century editions and biographies give **1771**. The 1769 date is now conventional and is used by the Fundación Empresas Polar and modern authority records. His age was guessed as about ninety in the 1854 burial entry, not derived from a birth certificate.
- **Death:** **28 February 1854**, Amotape, Piura region, Peru. Buried 1 March in the parish church of San Nicolás de Amotape.
- **Nationality/status:** Born a subject of the Spanish monarchy; later identified with the new Spanish-American republics and Venezuela. “Venezuelan” is the normal retrospective designation.
- **Known portraiture:** Very little was made from life. The familiar mature portrait with spectacles raised on the forehead seems derived from a lost or unlocated miniature; an Ecuadorian image shows him holding *Sociedades americanas en 1828*. Agustín Guerrero drew him late in life in profile with hat and cane. Tito Salas's Monte Sacro painting is a later historical visualization, not eyewitness iconography.

## Family and Ancestry

### Documentary minimum

Rodríguez explicitly described himself as **“expósito de esta feligresía”** when he married María de los Santos Ronco in Caracas in June 1793. “Expósito” denotes a foundling or child whose legal parentage was not acknowledged. This is the strongest contemporary fact about his origins. Later attempts to assign parents must be read against that status.

### Competing parentage traditions

1. **Rosalía Rodríguez and a Carreño father:** A widespread account makes his mother Rosalía Rodríguez, sometimes described as the daughter of a Canary Island landholder, and his father an unidentified man surnamed Carreño.
2. **The cleric Alejandro Carreño:** Another tradition, already reported by the Chilean biographer Miguel Luis Amunátegui soon after Rodríguez's death, says his father was a cleric named Carreño. Later writers identify him as Alejandro Carreño. This is plausible as oral history but is not equivalent to a surviving acknowledgment of paternity.
3. **Carreño household/fosterage:** Sources connect Rodríguez with the priest Alejandro Carreño and the musician Cayetano Carreño. The frequent claim that the celebrated musician **José Cayetano Carreño** was Rodríguez's brother is traditional, not conclusively demonstrated by a shared legal parentage record.

The safest formulation is that Rodríguez entered the record as an expósito; he was associated with the Rodríguez and Carreño families; exact biological parentage remains unresolved.

### Marriages, partners, and children

- **María de los Santos Ronco:** Married in Caracas in June 1793. Little is securely documented about the marriage after Rodríguez fled in 1797. He never returned to Venezuela. Whether they formally separated, whether she died before his second union, and the complete legal history are not firmly established in the sources consulted.
- **Manuela Gómez:** The Amotape death/burial entry records that Rodríguez said he had married twice, that his last wife, **Manuela Gómez**, was Bolivian and already dead, and that he left one son, **José Rodríguez**. The marriage is usually dated approximately to 1831 in Peru, but the date is uncertain.
- **José Rodríguez:** The only child directly supported by the death entry. He accompanied his father during the final journey toward Peru in 1853, with Camilo Gómez, although accounts differ about who remained at the bedside.
- **“Choclo, Zapallo/Sapallo, and Zanahoria”:** A much-repeated anecdote says Rodríguez named two boys and a girl after vegetables, either to defy saints' names and clerical convention or to keep them from being confused with other children. It conflicts with the death entry's statement that he left only one son but is not logically impossible (the others could have died). No baptismal or civil records for three such children were established in the sources consulted. It should be treated as **later anecdote/legend**, not a verified family register.

No reliable evidence of inherited wealth, a durable estate, investments, or property holdings emerged. His life after 1826 was economically precarious and mobile.

## Childhood and Early Life

The childhood record is exceptionally sparse. Born in late-colonial Caracas and legally marked as an expósito, Rodríguez grew up near the margins of the caste-conscious colonial order while nonetheless acquiring literacy, languages, and enough professional competence to become a municipal teacher. Later biographies sometimes fill this absence with confident stories of adoption and household formation; the surviving documentary base does not sustain a full childhood narrative.

His proximity to the Carreño musical family and to Caracas's artisan, clerical, and educational worlds is plausible. There is no secure enrollment record proving a university education. His formation appears substantially self-directed and practical.

## Education and Formation

Rodríguez's intellectual formation joined several streams:

- **Colonial first-letters teaching:** Reading, writing, arithmetic, catechism, penmanship, and the organization of municipal schooling gave him the system he would criticize from inside.
- **The European Enlightenment:** His 1794 reform memorandum displays arguments for rational organization, teacher competence, public responsibility, broader access, and education beyond mechanical literacy.
- **Republicanism and social equality:** By the 1820s he treated education not as charity or ornament but as the mechanism that created republican citizens. Legal independence without social and economic transformation was inadequate.
- **Craft and technology:** Printing, typography, workshop labor, agriculture, chemistry, roads, mines, and water management were integral to his pedagogy. Manual and intellectual work belonged together.
- **Language and visual form:** His unconventional spelling, capitalization, spacing, braces, tables, and typographic arrangements were part of the argument. “La Página” was meant to stage relations among ideas, not merely carry prose.

### Rousseau and the “Émile thesis”

The conventional narrative says Rodríguez directly modeled Bolívar's education on Jean-Jacques Rousseau's *Émile, ou De l'éducation* (1762), using nature, exercise, experience, and freedom in place of rote learning. There are genuine affinities: suspicion of verbalism, education through experience, bodily activity, the formation of autonomous judgment, and interest in *Robinson Crusoe*. Rodríguez's exile name “Samuel Robinson” has encouraged the connection.

But **direct dependence is disputed**. Maximiliano Durán's study, “La supuesta influencia de Rousseau en el pensamiento de Simón Rodríguez: la ‘tesis del Emilio’,” re-examines the evidence and warns that later biographies often project Rousseau onto the teacher-pupil relationship without contemporary documentation showing that Rodríguez systematically educated Bolívar from *Émile*. Rodríguez's mature program also differed sharply from Rousseau: it was public, collective, coeducational in aspiration, vocational, and designed to constitute a republic, rather than centered on the insulated education of one fictional male pupil. Rousseau is best treated as an important intellectual analogue and likely influence, not a demonstrated lesson plan governing Bolívar's childhood.

Other formative figures and contexts include Socratic questioning; Enlightenment republicanism; the French Revolutionary and Napoleonic world; industrial and printing practice; and, after his return, the multilingual, Indigenous, mestizo, and African-descended societies of the Andes.

## Early Career: Caracas, 1791–1797

### Municipal school and Bolívar

In **May 1791**, the Caracas Cabildo admitted Rodríguez as teacher in the public school of first letters for boys, associated with Guillermo Pelgrón. In 1792 the nine-year-old Simón Bolívar entered the school. By May 1793 Rodríguez reportedly had **114 pupils**, indicating both overcrowding and the institutional scale behind his reform demands.

In 1794 Rodríguez submitted *Reflexiones sobre los defectos que vician la escuela de primeras letras de Caracas y medio de lograr su reforma por un nuevo establecimiento*. It criticized the low status and poor organization of elementary schooling, deficient methods, inadequate compensation, and restricted access, and proposed a reorganized establishment. The plan was not durably implemented. This was a significant early failure institutionally and a foundational success intellectually: it contains the premises of his lifelong educational politics.

In 1795, amid litigation over the orphaned Bolívar's guardianship, the boy fled the house of his uncle/tutor Carlos Palacios. Authorities placed him for several months in Rodríguez's household. This episode was real, but later retellings inflate a limited period of instruction and residence into exclusive lifelong tutelage. Bolívar had multiple teachers, including Andrés Bello; Rodríguez's influence was unusually durable, but not solitary.

### Political conspiracy and flight

Rodríguez became connected in **1797** with the revolutionary network surrounding **Manuel Gual, José María España, and Juan Bautista Picornell**. The conspiracy sought to overturn colonial rule and drew on radical republican currents. Precise proof of Rodríguez's operational role is thinner than the fact of his association and flight. When authorities broke the movement, he left Caracas permanently. He was not tried or convicted in a completed proceeding established here; “exile” describes practical flight from danger rather than a documented judicial sentence of banishment.

## Chronological Life History

### 1797–1800: Jamaica and the United States

Rodríguez went to **Kingston, Jamaica**, in 1797 and adopted **Samuel Robinson**, probably for concealment and reinvention. He then lived in the United States, including **Baltimore**, until late 1800. Later accounts say he worked as a printer/typesetter for roughly three years. His mature control of type and page design supports real printing experience, even when day-to-day employment records remain elusive.

### 1801–1806: France, translation, and reunion with Bolívar

He was in **Bayonne** by April 1801 and moved to **Paris**. The first Spanish translation of Chateaubriand's *Atala* (Paris, 1801) is associated with Samuel Robinson and **Fray Servando Teresa de Mier**. Scholarship debates the division of labor and attribution; it should be described as a collaborative or contested translation, not uncritically as Rodríguez's sole work.

Rodríguez encountered Bolívar again in Paris in 1804. The young widower was grieving María Teresa Rodríguez del Toro and immersed in European society. Rodríguez became companion, interlocutor, and political mentor. In April 1805 they traveled via Lyon and Chambéry across the Alps into Italy: Milan, Venice, Ferrara, Bologna, Florence, and Rome. They witnessed Napoleon's coronation as king of Italy in Milan.

On **15 August 1805**, according to the later canonical account, Bolívar made his oath to liberate Spanish America before Rodríguez and Fernando Rodríguez del Toro on Rome's **Monte Sacro**. Bolívar's 19 January 1824 letter independently confirms that he and Rodríguez remembered going together to Monte Sacro “a jurar” the freedom of the homeland. The polished speech universally quoted was preserved/reconstructed later through Rodríguez and is not a contemporary transcript. Earlier historians proposed the Aventine or Palatine; documentary discussion now generally favors Monte Sacro because Bolívar named it in 1824. The event is historically grounded; the verbatim text and scenic detail are retrospective.

After a possible trip by Bolívar to Naples, the party returned toward Paris. Rodríguez and Bolívar separated in 1806.

### 1806–1823: The obscure European decades

This is the largest gap in the biography. Later itineraries place Rodríguez in Italy, German lands, Prussia, Poland, and Russia. Rodríguez himself later summarized that he had remained in Europe more than twenty years, worked in an industrial chemistry laboratory, attended secret meetings “of a socialist character,” studied literature, learned languages, and ran a primary school in a small Russian town.

These first-person recollections are valuable but broad and retrospective. They do not justify precise modern claims about membership in a named socialist organization, intelligence activity, or continuous residence in every country. “Socialist” may reflect the vocabulary in which Rodríguez later described European associations. No evidence located establishes espionage, imprisonment, criminal conviction, or formal political office in Europe.

He was in **London in 1823**, where he met or renewed contact with **Andrés Bello**, and decided to return to the newly independent Americas.

### 1823–1825: Return to America and reunion with Bolívar

Rodríguez entered through **Cartagena de Indias**, resumed the name Simón Rodríguez, and went to Bogotá. In 1824 he organized a **school-workshop**, seeking to combine elementary teaching with productive crafts.

Bolívar, learning of his return, wrote from **Pativilca on 19 January 1824**: “Ud. formó mi corazón para la libertad, para la justicia, para lo grande, para lo hermoso.” This contemporary letter is the strongest evidence for Bolívar's own later assessment of Rodríguez's influence.

With assistance arranged through Vice President **Francisco de Paula Santander**, Rodríguez traveled by Cartagena, Panama, and Guayaquil toward Peru. Accounts place him teaching agriculture and botany at Latacunga, proposing an eastern Ecuador colonization plan in Quito, and forming a mutual-aid society in Ibarra. Exact dates are sometimes late 1824 or early 1825.

Bolívar received him in Lima in 1825 and added him to his collaborators. From April they traveled through Peru—Arequipa, Cuzco, Tinta, Lampa, Puno, Zepita—and into Upper Peru/Bolivia—La Paz, Oruro, Potosí, and Chuquisaca.

### 1825–1826: Bolivia—highest office and decisive institutional failure

In November 1825 Bolívar appointed Rodríguez **Director of Public Instruction, Physical and Mathematical Sciences and Arts** and **Director General of Mines, Agriculture, and Public Roads** in the new Bolivian republic.

At Chuquisaca he attempted his most ambitious institutional experiment. The school was to take in poor, orphaned, Indigenous, and socially excluded children; teach literacy, republican social conduct, crafts, and productive work; and connect education to economic self-support. The project challenged elite expectations that schools reproduce classical, clerical, and status hierarchies.

Bolívar left for Lima on 7 January 1826; he and Rodríguez never met again. President **Antonio José de Sucre**, local officials, clergy, parents, and administrators resisted aspects of the plan. Complaints concerned expense, coercive recruitment or removal of children, workshop organization, discipline, alleged disorder, and Rodríguez's temperament. Rodríguez believed opponents sabotaged the plan and misrepresented it. Sucre found him impractical and difficult. The school collapsed or was transformed; Rodríguez resigned in 1826.

This conflict generated the central hostile image of the “mad” visionary wasting funds on an unworkable scheme. The established facts are that the experiment was costly, controversial, short-lived, and institutionally defeated. Claims of corruption, embezzlement, or criminal fraud were **not established** in the consulted evidence. The dispute was administrative and political, with hostile descriptions functioning partly as factional rhetoric.

### 1827–1833: Arequipa and Peru

Rodríguez settled in **Arequipa**, supported himself through teaching and practical enterprises, and began printing his mature works.

- **1828:** Published the Arequipa *Pródromo* of *Sociedades americanas en 1828: cómo serán y cómo podrían ser en los siglos venideros*. Its famous formula states that Spanish America is original and therefore needs original institutions: “O Inventamos o Erramos.”
- **1830:** Published *El Libertador del Mediodía de América y sus compañeros de armas, defendidos por un amigo de la causa social*, a defense of Bolívar and the social meaning of independence amid Bolívar's political collapse.
- **1830:** Published *Observaciones sobre el terreno de Vincocaya [variants: Vinco-caya/Vincocoya]* concerning a proposal to divert waters toward Arequipa. It combines terrain, hydrology, economy, and the social consequences of engineering.
- **c. 1831:** Probable marriage or union with Manuela Gómez; documentary details remain uncertain.

Bolívar's death in December 1830 removed Rodríguez's most powerful patron. He never obtained stable state sponsorship comparable to his brief Bolivian appointment.

### 1834–1842: Chile and renewed publication

Rodríguez moved from Lima to **Concepción, Chile**, in 1834, directed a school, and printed *Luces y virtudes sociales*, a developed installment of the *Sociedades americanas* project. He distinguished **instruction** (transmission of knowledge) from **education** (formation for social life and republican conduct).

After the destructive **Concepción earthquake of 20 February 1835**, he wrote an *Informe* on the city and disaster. His technical-social eye linked rebuilding, materials, settlement, and public welfare.

He traveled through **Trilaleubu and Monteblanco (1836)** and **Tucapel (1837)**. In Santiago he encountered Andrés Bello again. Their shared Caracas origins and later intellectual stature invite claims of close collaboration, but the extent of their relationship should not be exaggerated.

In **Valparaíso**, he issued another version of *Luces y virtudes sociales* (usually dated 1838; some bibliographies point to 1840 printing/publication details) and contributed to *El Mercurio*. His successive editions were not ordinary reprints: they were fragments, revisions, and typographic reconfigurations of a larger, never fully realized project.

By **1842** he was in Lima, where another installment/edition of *Sociedades americanas en 1828* appeared, followed by related Lima printing in 1843.

### 1843–1851: Paita, Ecuador, New Granada, and Latacunga

Traveling north in 1843, Rodríguez met **Manuela Sáenz** at Paita. Both were survivors of Bolívar's circle living far from the center of the successor states. Later literature makes the meeting richly symbolic; detailed dialogue should be treated cautiously unless anchored in contemporary correspondence.

He reached Ecuador late in 1843, visiting Guayaquil and Quito and teaching at the **Colegio de San Vicente in Latacunga**. He passed through Quito in 1846 and was at **Túquerres** in New Granada in 1847.

In 1849 Bogotá's *El Neo-Granadino* printed his *Extracto sucinto de mi obra sobre la educación republicana*. In 1850 he returned to Latacunga. His **1851** *Consejos de amigo dados al Colejio de Latacunga* proposed curricular replacement of Latin-centered scholasticism with Spanish, Quechua, physics, chemistry, natural history, crafts, pottery/glass manufacture, masonry, carpentry, and smithing; he also proposed reliable public financing. Economic revolution, he argued, should begin in the countryside and pass to workshops.

### 1852–1854: Guayaquil, loss, final journey, and death

Accounts place him in Guayaquil late in life, where a fire destroyed papers and perhaps unpublished portions of his work. The scale and exact contents of the loss cannot be reconstructed.

In 1853 he set out again for Peru, accompanied at stages by his son José and **Camilo Gómez**. Ill and impoverished, he reached Amotape. Camilo Gómez's later recollection and the parish burial entry are the principal evidence for the end.

Rodríguez died on **28 February 1854**. The parish priest Santiago Sánchez recorded on 1 March that the deceased, judged about ninety, had confessed while mentally lucid, received the sacraments, said he was from Caracas, had married twice, named his late second wife Manuela Gómez of Bolivia, and left one son José Rodríguez. Later accounts variously name an illness, “fever,” or general decline; no modern medical diagnosis or secure specific cause should be asserted.

Rodríguez did not create a durable company or investment empire. His “enterprises” were pedagogical, productive, and often temporary.

### Finances

He earned money through salaries, teaching, printing, and practical work. Bolívar and republican governments facilitated travel and appointments. After the Bolivian rupture and Bolívar's death, he repeatedly sought institutional support but remained precarious. His self-financed or author-supervised printing likely imposed costs. There is no documented large property portfolio, patent income, corporate holding, or hidden fortune. His final poverty is well attested in broad outline, though romantic biographies sometimes heighten it into absolute destitution.

### Technologies and inventions

No patent is known. His innovations were systems rather than patentable devices:

- integrated school-workshops;
- productive education joining literacy, science, agriculture, and trades;
- distinctive “ideographic” typography and spatial page composition;
- technical analysis of water diversion and earthquake reconstruction;
- mutual aid, smallholder colonization, and publicly financed popular schooling.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Republicanism

Rodríguez distinguished emancipation from mere change of rulers. Republics required republican people, produced through social education. “Colonize the country with its own inhabitants” opposed dependence on European settlers and elite mimicry.

### Equality and popular education

His schools were meant for those excluded from elite education—poor children, orphans, Indigenous people, mestizos, and others classified by colonial hierarchy. Equality was not only a future reward but a premise of common schooling. He favored education of girls as well as boys in passages and projects, although implementation evidence is incomplete.

### Labor and political economy

He rejected education detached from subsistence and production. Agriculture and workshops would make citizens useful and less dependent, while association would overcome fragmented economies. He favored access to land in workable parcels backed by education, not simply redistribution without institutional formation.

### Language, Indigenous America, and originality

His mature writings insisted that American societies could use European knowledge without copying European institutions. He advocated Spanish and Quechua in relevant educational settings and treated local demography and geography as political facts.

### Religion

Rodríguez criticized clerical domination and scholastic curricula and is often described as anticlerical. Stories about vegetable names dramatize this reputation. Yet his burial record says he confessed and received the Catholic sacraments while lucid. This does not settle private belief, but it complicates portraits of categorical atheism. No evidence located supports formal excommunication or prosecution for heresy.

### Socialism

He later said he attended secret meetings of a “socialist” character in Europe. His advocacy of association, public education, productive workshops, and social property questions led later socialist and revolutionary traditions to claim him. He did not leave a systematic Marxist doctrine; Marxism postdated much of his formation. “Utopian socialist,” “social republican,” and “radical democrat” are interpretive classifications, not party memberships.

## Important Relationships and Networks

- **Simón Bolívar:** Pupil, later friend and patron. Childhood teaching, the 1804–1806 European companionship, the 1824 Pativilca letter, 1825 reunion, and appointments are documented. Bolívar's praise is primary evidence; later nation-building amplified the relationship into a two-person creation myth.
- **Andrés Bello:** Fellow Caracas educator/intellectual; met in London in 1823 and again in Chile. Claims that both taught Bolívar are sound in general; details of collaboration are limited.
- **Manuel Gual, José María España, Juan Bautista Picornell:** Revolutionary network associated with Rodríguez's 1797 flight. His exact clandestine tasks remain uncertain.
- **Fray Servando Teresa de Mier:** Associated collaborator in the 1801 Spanish *Atala*. Attribution and division of translation work are debated.
- **Fernando Rodríguez del Toro:** Companion on the Italian journey and traditional witness at Monte Sacro.
- **Francisco de Paula Santander:** Assisted Rodríguez's onward journey under Bolívar's direction; not evidence of enduring political intimacy.
- **Antonio José de Sucre:** President of Bolivia and decisive institutional adversary. Their conflict concerned administration, cost, authority, and feasibility, not a proven personal conspiracy.
- **Manuela Sáenz:** Met at Paita in 1843; later memory makes them paired custodians of Bolívar's legacy.
- **Camilo Gómez:** Companion of José Rodríguez and witness/caregiver near Rodríguez's death; important to later death narratives.
- **Agustín Guerrero:** Ecuadorian disciple/artist who produced a late likeness.

No well-documented “enemy list,” intelligence service connection, patronage corporation, or criminal network appears in the record.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

### Gual and España conspiracy

**Documented:** association with the 1797 revolutionary movement and permanent flight after discovery.  
**Not established here:** a surviving conviction, imprisonment, or detailed operational file proving a particular act of violence.  
**Historical classification:** clandestine anti-colonial political activity; criminal under Spanish colonial law, later commemorated as independence precursor activity.

### Chuquisaca school conflict

**Documented:** public appointment, radical inclusive school, expenditure and administrative conflict, resistance by Sucre and local elites, resignation/failure in 1826.  
**Allegations/hostile descriptions:** impracticality, disorder, mistreatment or coercive collection of children, extravagance, and madness.  
**Finding:** no court judgment or conviction for fraud/corruption located. Complaints should be retained as evidence of the conflict, not converted into proven embezzlement.

### “Madness”

Contemporaries and later biographers called him eccentric or mad, citing dress, restless travel, naming anecdotes, typography, blunt speech, and failed schemes. Arturo Guevara even published a posthumous “psychiatric sketch.” No competent clinical diagnosis made during Rodríguez's life is known. Retrospective psychiatric labeling is interpretive and often served to depoliticize or romanticize radical proposals.

### Family and sexual life

His first marriage, decades of separation, second marriage/union, and children generated speculation. The documentary floor is the 1793 marriage and 1854 death entry. Claims of bigamy cannot be established without the date/status of María de los Santos Ronco's death or a formal dissolution record. The vegetable-child story is anecdotal.

### Religion

Anticlerical positions and irreverent stories are documented as reputation; sacramental death is documented by the priest. Neither side alone proves a settled lifelong creed.

### Other requested categories

No reliable evidence was found of money laundering, patent theft, corporate fraud, criminal conviction, imprisonment, addiction, an intelligence-service role, or personal participation in political killing. Absence from the consulted record is not proof of impossibility, but such claims should not be introduced without primary evidence.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. “Rodríguez created Bolívar”

1. **Story:** One genius teacher took the child Bolívar into nature, educated him from Rousseau, and made the future Liberator.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** Bolívar's own 1824 praise supplied an authentic core; nineteenth- and twentieth-century biographies, school texts, murals, and state commemorations expanded it.
3. **Circulators:** Bolivarian historians, educators, Venezuelan institutions, later revolutionary governments.
4. **Evidence:** Rodríguez taught Bolívar and housed him briefly; they traveled together as adults; Bolívar credited him profoundly. Against exclusivity: Bolívar had several teachers, and no daily Rousseau-derived curriculum survives.
5. **Effect:** Made Rodríguez famous but often reduced his independent social theory to a supporting role in Bolívar's biography.

### 2. The verbatim Monte Sacro oath

1. **Story:** On 15 August 1805 Bolívar delivered the canonical polished oath before Rodríguez and Fernando Toro.
2. **Appearance:** The event is corroborated retrospectively by Bolívar's 1824 letter; the full wording came through later recollection/transmission associated with Rodríguez.
3. **Circulators:** Bolívar, Rodríguez, biographers, academies, state art and ritual.
4. **Evidence:** Strong for a Roman/Monte Sacro oath; weaker for a stenographically exact speech. Place disputes (Aventine/Palatine) existed, though Bolívar's own “Monte Sacro” wording is weighty.
5. **Effect:** Became a foundational scene of continental independence and fixed Rodríguez as witness-prophet.

### 3. Rousseau's *Émile* as Bolívar's lesson plan

1. **Story:** Rodríguez directly applied Rousseau's book to Bolívar.
2. **Appearance:** Later biographical synthesis based on philosophical resemblances and romantic educational anecdotes.
3. **Circulators:** Biographers, textbooks, popular culture.
4. **Evidence:** Strong thematic affinities and likely familiarity; no direct contemporary program proves systematic application. Durán explicitly disputes the standard thesis.
5. **Effect:** Linked Rodríguez to the Enlightenment but obscured his collective, American, vocational, and republican departures from Rousseau.

### 4. Choclo, Zapallo, and Zanahoria

1. **Story:** He named three children after vegetables to oppose saints' names or prevent confusion.
2. **Appearance:** Later anecdotal and biographical literature; no primary registration located.
3. **Circulators:** Admirers and detractors alike, because the tale signifies wit, anticlericalism, eccentricity, or madness.
4. **Evidence:** His death entry names only one surviving son, José. The anecdote is possible but unverified.
5. **Effect:** One of the chief props of the “loco genial” image.

### 5. The wholly destitute wandering sage

1. **Story:** Rodríguez rejected all comfort, wandered friendless, and died abandoned.
2. **Appearance:** Romantic/hagiographic biographies and commemorative narratives.
3. **Circulators:** Admirers emphasizing sacrifice; critics emphasizing impracticality.
4. **Evidence:** Recurrent precarious employment and a poor final illness are credible; he also held offices, directed schools, published, formed a family, and traveled with companions.
5. **Effect:** Converted structural institutional failure into a morality tale of genius unrecognized.

### 6. The prophetic author of every modern progressive reform

1. **Story:** Rodríguez anticipated socialism, decolonial theory, popular education, environmentalism, vocational education, bilingualism, feminism, and every later Bolivarian policy.
2. **Appearance:** Twentieth- and twenty-first-century academic and political appropriation.
3. **Circulators:** Socialists, educators, cultural theorists, and Venezuelan state projects such as **Misión Robinson** and the renamed **Universidad Nacional Experimental Simón Rodríguez**.
4. **Evidence:** His texts genuinely support many elements—association, originality, Indigenous languages, productive schooling, women and excluded children—but modern labels can exceed his vocabulary and institutional context.
5. **Effect:** Revived his work while sometimes turning fragments into a seamless modern platform.

## Works by Simón Rodríguez

Rodríguez's corpus is bibliographically difficult. Some texts survive only in later editions; *Sociedades americanas* was a multi-installment project printed in changing forms; editors normalize or disrupt his meaningful typography. Dates below distinguish original issue from later compilation where possible.

### Lifetime publications and documents

1. **Representación al Ayuntamiento** (Caracas, 1793). Administrative representation associated with his school position; survives through archival/edited transmission.
2. **Reflexiones sobre los defectos que vician la escuela de primeras letras de Caracas y medio de lograr su reforma por un nuevo establecimiento** (Caracas, 1794). Reform memorandum to the Ayuntamiento; manuscript/administrative document, published later in collections.
3. **Atala, o los amores de dos salvajes en el desierto** (Paris, 1801), translation of Chateaubriand. Published under/associated with Samuel Robinson and Fray Servando Teresa de Mier. **Collaborative/disputed attribution**; Rodríguez should not automatically receive sole credit.
4. **Plan de colonización para el Oriente del Ecuador** (Quito, c. 1824–1825). Project/document; survival and title vary in editions.
5. **Sociedades americanas en 1828. Cómo serán y cómo podrían ser en los siglos venideros** (Arequipa, 1828). The *Pródromo*, an authorial installment of a projected larger work.
6. **El Libertador del Mediodía de América y sus compañeros de armas, defendidos por un amigo de la causa social** (Arequipa, 1830).
7. **Observaciones sobre el terreno de Vincocaya con respecto a la empresa de desviar el curso natural de sus aguas y conducirlas por el río Zumbai al de Arequipa** (Arequipa, Imprenta del Gobierno, 1830). Spelling of Vincocaya and Zumbai varies bibliographically.
8. **Sociedades americanas en 1828… [Luces y virtudes sociales]** (Concepción, Imprenta del Instituto, 1834). Revised/developed installment, not simply a conventional reprint.
9. **Informe sobre Concepción después del terremoto de febrero de 1835** (Concepción, 1835).
10. **Partidos** (Concepción/Chile, 1830s; bibliographical details vary). Political fragment included in collected works.
11. **Sociedades americanas en 1828… Primera parte: Luces y virtudes sociales** (Valparaíso, Imprenta del Mercurio, 1838; some catalogs/secondary sources use 1840 for related issue).
12. **“Extractos de la obra Sociedades americanas en 1828”** and related articles in *El Mercurio de Valparaíso* (c. 1840). Periodical fragments; identify by issue in specialized editions.
13. **Sociedades americanas en 1828…** (Lima, Imprenta del Comercio, 1842), with related continuation/material dated 1843. The 2018 UAM facsimile project treats the five historic imprints as a connected editorial project.
14. **Crítica de las providencias del Gobierno** / governmental and educational fragments (1840s; titles and dates vary in posthumous compilations).
15. **Extracto sucinto de mi obra sobre la educación republicana** (*El Neo-Granadino*, Bogotá, 1849).
16. **Consejos de amigo dados al Colejio de Latacunga** (Latacunga, 1851). Manuscript/advice later published; original spelling “Colejio” is authorial.
17. **Letters** (various dates, especially 1820s–1850s). All extant letters were collected posthumously; the 2001 UNESR *Cartas* emphasizes their dispersed publication history.

### Posthumous collections and selections

- *Escritos de Simón Rodríguez*, 3 vols. (Sociedad Bolivariana de Venezuela, 1954), edited/assembled posthumously.
- *Obras completas*, 2 vols. (Universidad Simón Rodríguez, 1975; later editions 1988, 1999, 2016). **Posthumous editorial corpus**, not an author-supervised “complete works.”
- *Sociedades americanas* (Biblioteca Ayacucho, 1990), critical/selected modern edition.
- *Inventamos o erramos* (Monte Ávila, 1988 and later editions), **posthumous thematic selection**; the title uses his famous phrase but is not a single lifetime book bearing that title.
- *Cartas* (UNESR, 2001), posthumous epistolary compilation.
- *Sociedades americanas en 1828: edición facsimilar documentada y anotada de los cinco impresos que conforman el proyecto editorial*, 6 vols., coordinated by María del Rayo Ramírez Fierro, Rafael Mondragón Velázquez, and Freja Ininna Cervantes Becerril (UAM-Iztapalapa, 2018). Modern scholarly facsimile edition.

### Lost, projected, and uncertain material

Rodríguez repeatedly presented *Sociedades americanas* as part of a larger architecture that he never completed or could not fully print. Papers reportedly burned in Guayaquil. Later collections mix published sheets, manuscripts, letters, and editorially supplied titles; consequently “complete bibliography” means the fullest surviving reconstruction, not the full production of his lifetime.

## Death and Remains

Rodríguez died at Amotape on 28 February 1854 and received ecclesiastical burial on 1 March. The priest described him as “casta de español,” approximately ninety, lucid at confession, twice married, widower of Manuela Gómez, and father of José. The age and caste label reflect the recorder's perception and inherited vocabulary, not exact biological proof.

His remains stayed at Amotape for about seventy years. In **1924** they were transferred to the **Panteón de los Próceres in Lima**. On the centenary of his death they were repatriated to Caracas and placed in Venezuela's **Panteón Nacional on 28 February 1954**. The transfers transformed a mobile, often marginal teacher into a state ancestor in both Peru and Venezuela.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Rodríguez's reputation moved through several overlapping phases:

1. **Bolívar's extraordinary teacher:** Bolívar's 1824 letter guaranteed memory but subordinated Rodríguez's career to his pupil.
2. **Eccentric failure:** Nineteenth-century and early twentieth-century accounts emphasized the Chuquisaca collapse, itinerancy, typography, anticlericalism, and supposed madness.
3. **Recovered author:** Pedro Grases and mid-century Venezuelan scholars reconstructed the scattered corpus, first editions, and letters.
4. **Continental pedagogue:** Educators reframed him as a founder of popular, vocational, republican, and emancipatory education.
5. **Political symbol:** Governments and movements across ideological periods claimed him. Venezuela established the **Universidad Nacional Experimental Simón Rodríguez** and, in the twenty-first century, used **Misión Robinson** for literacy campaigns. “Inventamos o erramos” became an institutional and political slogan.
6. **Philosopher of form and decolonial originality:** Later scholarship studies his typography, irony, political economy, Indigenous-language proposals, concept of citizenship, and refusal of institutional copying.

Commemoration has preserved and distorted him simultaneously. Hagiography turns every failure into persecution of genius; hostile mythology turns institutional opposition into proof of insanity; state appropriation makes him endorse programs he never saw. The primary writings reveal a less seamless figure: radical yet practical in his attention to trades and funding, European-formed yet anti-imitative, anticlerical in institutional critique yet sacramentally buried, and celebrated chiefly through a pupil despite an independent continental project.

## Atlas Connections

### Christopher Columbus

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Rodríguez discussed Columbus in his mature writings, calling him a virtuous Italian who did not intend slaughter but “opened the doors” to conquistadors whom he mistakenly trusted as Christians. This is direct written engagement with the earlier figure, preserved in Rodríguez’s *Obras completas*. [Text of Rodríguez’s works](https://biblioteca.clacso.edu.ar/clacso/se/20190926042843/Simon_Rodriguez_Obras_Completas.pdf)

### Jean-Jacques Rousseau

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Rodríguez explicitly assessed Rousseau’s political and educational arguments—for example, Rousseau’s reservations about general instruction—rather than merely displaying an inferred philosophical resemblance. The evidence supports Rodríguez writing about Rousseau; it does not prove the later legend that he systematically educated Bolívar according to *Émile*. [Rodríguez’s *Obras completas*](https://biblioteca.clacso.edu.ar/clacso/se/20190926042843/Simon_Rodriguez_Obras_Completas.pdf)

### Simón Bolívar

- **[T5]** **Documented fact:** Rodríguez taught Bolívar in Caracas during the 1790s, housed him for several months in 1795, reunited with him in Paris in 1804, traveled with him through France and Italy in 1805, witnessed the Monte Sacro oath, and rejoined him in Peru and Upper Peru in 1825. Their final personal separation came when Bolívar left Bolivia on 7 January 1826. [Fundación Empresas Polar biography](https://bibliofep.fundacionempresaspolar.org/dhv/entradas/r/rodriguez-simon/)

- **[T3]** **Documented fact:** The two maintained written engagement. Bolívar’s Pativilca letter of 19 January 1824 addressed Rodríguez as teacher and friend, credited him with forming his heart for liberty and justice, and recalled their joint visit to Monte Sacro; Rodríguez’s surviving correspondence includes a letter to Bolívar from Guayaquil dated 30 November 1824. [Bolívar’s Pativilca letter](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/obra-visor/carta-que-envia-simon-bolivar-a-su-maestro-don-simon-rodriguez--0/html/ff6c3814-82b1-11df-acc7-002185ce6064_2.html), [Rodríguez correspondence collection](https://biblioteca-repositorio.clacso.edu.ar/bitstream/CLACSO/15386/1/cartas.pdf)

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Rodríguez wrote extensively about Bolívar in *El Libertador del Mediodía de América y sus compañeros de armas, defendidos por un amigo de la causa social* (Arequipa, 1830), defending Bolívar’s conduct and interpreting independence as a social as well as military project. [Rodríguez’s *Obras completas*](https://biblioteca.clacso.edu.ar/clacso/se/20190926042843/Simon_Rodriguez_Obras_Completas.pdf)

### Napoleon Bonaparte

- **[T3]** **Reported fact:** Biographical accounts place Rodríguez and Bolívar as eyewitnesses to Napoleon’s coronation as king of Italy in Milan on 26 May 1805. The coronation itself is contemporaneously documented; Rodríguez’s attendance rests on retrospective itinerary and biographical evidence rather than a located admission register or eyewitness note written by him that day. [Fundación Empresas Polar biography](https://bibliofep.fundacionempresaspolar.org/dhv/entradas/r/rodriguez-simon/), [Fondation Napoléon chronology of the coronation](https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/timelines/napoleons-consecration-and-coronation-in-milan-26-may-1805/)

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Rodríguez later discussed Napoleon’s government, military achievement, monarchy, and coronation in his political writings. This textual connection is independent of the reported Milan sighting. [Rodríguez’s *Obras completas*](https://biblioteca.clacso.edu.ar/clacso/se/20190926042843/Simon_Rodriguez_Obras_Completas.pdf)

### Antonio José de Sucre

- **[T5]** **Documented fact:** Rodríguez worked personally under President Sucre in Chuquisaca during the 1825–1826 educational experiment. Their relationship deteriorated over the institution’s expenses, administration, treatment and recruitment of children, and Rodríguez’s authority, culminating in the project’s collapse and Rodríguez’s resignation.

- **[T3]** **Documented fact:** Their conflict generated written communications: Rodríguez addressed letters or representations to Sucre, while Sucre reported critically about Rodríguez and the school to Bolívar in July 1826. The surviving record supports written engagement even where Sucre apparently left particular appeals unanswered. [Study incorporating the July 1826 correspondence](https://repositorio.ufmg.br/bitstream/1843/BUOS-AWKMEA/1/a_pol_tica_p_blica_educacional_de_sim_n_rodr_guez_para_peru_e_bol_via__1824_1854_ageu_mazil_o.pdf)

### Manuela Sáenz

- **[T5]** **Reported fact:** Rodríguez visited Sáenz at Paita, Peru, in early 1843, where the two surviving members of Bolívar’s circle reportedly discussed Bolívar, Santander, and their shared political past. The meeting is accepted in biographical tradition, but the elaborate dialogue attributed to it derives from the contested modern publication of Sáenz’s purported “lost diaries” and should not be treated as securely authenticated verbatim testimony. [Published *Diario de Paita* text](https://recursos.educacion.gob.ec/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Los-diarios-perdidos-de-Manuela-Saenz-y-otros-papeles.pdf)

### Hugo Chávez

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Chávez wrote about Rodríguez in *El Libro Azul* (composed around 1991 and subsequently published), making the “Robinsonian root” one component of his “Tree of Three Roots” alongside Bolívar and Zamora. He quoted *Sociedades americanas*, described Rodríguez’s thought as a Venezuelan political model, and connected “inventamos o erramos” to his proposed national project. [Hugo Chávez, *El Libro Azul*](https://hugochavez.ve/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/LIBRO-AZUL-DESCARGA.pdf)

### Nicolás Maduro

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** In his November 2013 preface to an edition of Chávez’s *El Libro Azul*, Maduro wrote about Rodríguez’s originality, political thought, “Pueblo Legislador,” and influence on Chávez, describing Rodríguez as a foundational source for the later Bolivarian project. This establishes a textual connection, not a personal one. [Maduro’s preface in *El Libro Azul*](https://hugochavez.ve/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/LIBRO-AZUL-DESCARGA.pdf)

No sufficiently documented personal or textual crossing was established with other tempting roster candidates such as Alexander von Humboldt, Domingo Faustino Sarmiento, José Martí, or Paulo Freire. Later biographies and comparative scholarship sometimes place their names beside Rodríguez’s, but parallel ideas, possible undocumented encounters, and posthumous comparison alone do not satisfy the specified connection types.
