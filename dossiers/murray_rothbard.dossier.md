# Murray Newton Rothbard: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier concerns the American economist, historian, political theorist, editor, and activist Murray Newton Rothbard (1926–1995).

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by Rothbard’s own dated writings, institutional records, published correspondence, government documents, or multiple independent sources.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a biographer, colleague, relative, or contemporary, but not independently established here from a primary record.
- **Allegation:** an identified person or publication attributed conduct to Rothbard without a judicial or otherwise conclusive finding.
- **Interpretation:** a scholar’s characterization of Rothbard’s ideas, influence, or motives.
- **Rumor or myth:** a circulating story for which the available evidence is weak, derivative, or contradictory.

Rothbard’s admirers and institutional successors produced much of the detailed biographical record. Their accounts preserve indispensable correspondence and eyewitness testimony but are not disinterested. Critical scholarship and journalism are used alongside them. No evidence was found that Rothbard was indicted or convicted of a crime.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Murray Newton Rothbard.
- **Usual publication name:** Murray N. Rothbard.
- **Nickname:** “Murray,” universally among associates.
- **Movement honorifics:** “Mr. Libertarian” and “Dean of the Austrian School” appear in sympathetic literature; these were reputational titles, not offices.
- **Birth:** March 2, 1926, the Bronx, New York City.
- **Death:** January 7, 1995, New York City, aged sixty-eight.
- **Cause of death:** heart attack.
- **Nationality:** American.
- **Ancestry:** Jewish, through immigrant parents from the Russian Empire/Poland.
- **Religious position:** born into a Jewish family but personally secular or atheist. His mature politics was not derived from religious observance.
- **Principal occupations:** economist, economic historian, political theorist, polemicist, editor, teacher, movement organizer, and consultant.
- **Academic fields:** Austrian economics, monetary theory, business-cycle theory, history of economic thought, American political and economic history, natural-law theory, and libertarian political philosophy.
- **Principal appointments:** Brooklyn Polytechnic Institute, later Polytechnic Institute of New York; S.J. Hall Distinguished Professor of Economics at the University of Nevada, Las Vegas.
- **Spouse:** JoAnn Beatrice Schumacher Rothbard.
- **Children:** none.
- **Principal residences:** New York City for most of his life; Las Vegas during his UNLV appointment, while maintaining close personal and organizational ties to New York.
- **Political affiliations and alliances:** Young Republican and Old Right circles; briefly the Strom Thurmond States’ Rights campaign; conservative and antiwar movements; New Left alliances; Peace and Freedom Party activity; Libertarian Party; Libertarian Party Radical Caucus; and, late in life, paleolibertarian and paleoconservative coalitions.
- **Major organizations:** Foundation for Economic Education orbit; William Volker Fund; Circle Bastiat; Left and Right; Center for Libertarian Studies; Cato Institute; Libertarian Party; Ludwig von Mises Institute; John Randolph Club; Rothbard-Rockwell Report.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

Rothbard was the only child of **David Rothbard** and **Rae Babushkin Rothbard**.

**David Rothbard:** Rothbard’s autobiographical account says his father was born near Warsaw into an Orthodox Jewish milieu, came to the United States in 1910, and rejected both religious orthodoxy and communism. He became a chemist and later worked in petroleum refining and management. Murray’s middle name, “Newton,” reportedly reflected David’s admiration for science and rationalism. David’s anti-socialism and confidence in science were important early influences.

A frequently repeated story holds that David was trapped inside the Tide Water Oil refinery in New Jersey during a 1952 strike. The episode appears in Rothbard biographies as an event reinforcing the family’s hostility to organized labor. Its broad outline is **reported fact**, but this investigation did not locate a contemporaneous company or newspaper record establishing every detail.

**Rae Babushkin Rothbard:** Sources identify Rae as a Russian- or Polish-Jewish immigrant. A book-length intellectual biography reports that she immigrated to the United States as a child. Murray remembered both parents as intellectually alert, interested in literature, and unusually supportive of his reading.

The exact birth dates, birth villages, immigration dates, and naturalization records of David and Rae were not established from primary civil records in the consulted sources. Descriptions fluctuate between “Russian Jewish,” “Polish Jewish,” and immigrants from territory near Warsaw; these descriptions are compatible with changing borders in the former Russian Empire.

### Household culture

In Rothbard’s surviving youthful autobiography, he described a liberal, nonreligious home in which questioning was encouraged. He also recalled the surrounding New York intellectual environment as heavily pro-New Deal and communist, exaggerating for polemical effect that he had grown up in a “Communist culture.” This meant the broader social and school environment, not that his parents were communists.

He claimed to have learned the alphabet at seventeen months and to have begun composing verse exceptionally early. That is an **autobiographical childhood recollection**, preserved because it became part of the prodigy narrative around him; it cannot be independently verified.

### Marriage

Rothbard married **JoAnn Beatrice Schumacher**, generally known as Joey or JoAnn Rothbard. The date is inconsistently reported:

- The standard biography and chronology give **January 16, 1953**.
- Some derivative records give January 16, 1963, almost certainly a transcription error.

The 1953 date is the stronger version. They had no children.

JoAnn was a close intellectual and organizational partner, reader, typist, social organizer, and guardian of his reputation and papers. Rothbard dedicated important work to her, calling her the “indispensable framework” of his life. After his death she contributed reminiscences and prefaces to memorial and posthumous editions. Accounts by friends depict their Manhattan apartment as a social center for Rothbard’s students and political allies.

No evidence was found of another spouse, extramarital partner, or acknowledged child.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1926–1942

Rothbard was born in the Bronx and raised in New York City. His family subsequently lived in Manhattan. He attended public school initially. Sympathetic oral histories preserve a story that an elementary-school principal told his parents that he was too intellectually advanced for ordinary public schooling. He thereafter attended private schools on scholarships, including the Birch Wathen School.

Rothbard’s own recollections emphasize:

- constant reading;
- early fascination with history, geography, literature, and classical music;
- argumentative independence;
- alienation from classmates and teachers who supported the New Deal;
- parental encouragement rather than religious training.

These recollections were written retrospectively and contributed to a later hagiographic picture of a fully formed libertarian prodigy. His earliest preserved writings do show precocious literary and political interests, but his mature Austrian economics and anarchism developed much later.

New York remained decisive to Rothbard’s identity. The density of publishers, émigré intellectuals, political clubs, salons, and university seminars permitted him to participate simultaneously in academic economics and marginal political movements.

## Education and Formation

### Columbia University

Rothbard entered Columbia University in 1942 at sixteen.

His degrees were:

- **B.A., mathematics, 1945**;
- **M.A., economics, 1946**;
- **Ph.D., economics, 1956**.

Some secondary chronologies mistakenly call the master’s degree a mathematics degree. Columbia-related accounts and intellectual biographies more commonly identify his graduate work as economics.

Important Columbia teachers and figures included:

- **Joseph Dorfman**, economic historian and eventual dissertation supervisor;
- **Arthur F. Burns**, economist, family acquaintance, Columbia professor, and later chairman of the Federal Reserve;
- **George Stigler**, associated with the price-theory and free-market milieu, although not Rothbard’s principal mentor;
- the broader Columbia tradition of institutional and American economic history.

### Dissertation dispute

Rothbard’s dissertation became *The Panic of 1819: Reactions and Policies*. According to Rothbard and his biographers, Arthur Burns rejected or obstructed an earlier version. Joseph Dorfman eventually supervised the successful dissertation, awarded in 1956. The ten-year interval between the M.A. and Ph.D. reflected this conflict, extensive rewriting, and Rothbard’s growing outside work.

That Burns blocked the dissertation is consistently reported. Claims that he acted from personal malice or because Rothbard refused Keynesian orthodoxy are principally Rothbardian interpretations. The accepted dissertation was published by Columbia University Press in 1962 and received respectful academic reviews, which supporters later cited as evidence that the obstruction had lacked scholarly justification.

### Ludwig von Mises’s seminar

In 1949 Rothbard began attending **Ludwig von Mises’s private seminar at New York University**, held near Washington Square. Mises, an Austrian-Jewish émigré, had published *Human Action* that year.

The seminar was Rothbard’s central intellectual conversion. From Mises he absorbed:

- praxeology, the deductive study of human action;
- methodological individualism;
- subjective value;
- Austrian capital and business-cycle theory;
- opposition to socialism, central planning, and monetary inflation.

Rothbard’s 1950 review of *Human Action* and his 1951 exchanges in the *American Economic Review* were among his first scholarly interventions. He defended Mises’s method more uncompromisingly than many other American economists.

Mises was a mentor but not an anarchist. Rothbard extended Misesian economics into a theory that regarded even limited government as economically unnecessary and morally illegitimate. Mises reportedly considered Rothbard’s anarchism extreme but continued to treat him cordially.

### Other formative influences

Rothbard combined sources rarely joined in one system:

- **Carl Menger, Eugen Böhm-Bawerk, and Mises:** Austrian economics.
- **John Locke and natural-rights theory:** self-ownership and property.
- **Thomas Aquinas and scholastic natural law:** objective moral order, despite Rothbard’s atheism.
- **Frédéric Bastiat and Gustave de Molinari:** free markets and competitive provision of security.
- **Herbert Spencer, Lysander Spooner, Benjamin Tucker, and American individualist anarchism:** anti-state theory.
- **Albert Jay Nock, H. L. Mencken, Frank Chodorov, Garet Garrett, and John T. Flynn:** the anti-New Deal, antiwar Old Right.
- **Harry Elmer Barnes:** revisionist diplomatic history.
- **Charles Beard:** economic and revisionist approaches to American history.
- **Ayn Rand:** natural rights, moral defense of capitalism, and movement-building, followed by a hostile break.

## Early Political and Professional Formation, 1945–1959

### Young Republicans and the Old Right

After World War II, Rothbard entered New York Young Republican politics and associated with the anti-New Deal, noninterventionist Old Right. He opposed the emerging Cold War consensus and military confrontation with the Soviet Union.

In 1948 he organized Columbia support for **Strom Thurmond’s States’ Rights Democratic presidential candidacy**. This is a documented and politically important episode. Rothbard later explained the choice in terms of states’ rights, opposition to Harry Truman, and hostility to centralized power. Thurmond’s campaign was explicitly organized in defense of racial segregation. No evidence shows that Rothbard joined a segregationist organization beyond the campaign, but treating the campaign as merely a neutral decentralist vehicle would omit its central racial program.

### Frank Chodorov and *Analysis*

**Frank Chodorov**, editor of *Analysis*, became an early mentor. Chodorov’s anti-statism, opposition to taxation, and noninterventionism strongly shaped Rothbard. Rothbard’s first known published pieces were book reviews in *Analysis* in 1949, including reviews of H. L. Mencken, George Orwell, and John T. Flynn.

He also contributed to *Faith and Freedom* and later *The Freeman*, associated with the Foundation for Economic Education.

### William Volker Fund

Beginning in the early 1950s, Rothbard worked under grants or contracts for the **William Volker Fund**, a private foundation that financed classical-liberal scholarship. His duties included reviewing books, assessing manuscripts, preparing memoranda, and developing a systematic economics text.

The arrangement was his principal intellectual patronage in the 1950s. It allowed him to write outside a conventional university career. The surviving Volker memoranda show him evaluating economists, historians, philosophers, conservatives, and libertarians with extraordinary breadth and often severe judgments. David Gordon later edited selections as *Confidential: The Private Volker Fund Memos of Murray N. Rothbard*.

### *Man, Economy, and State*

The Volker Fund asked Rothbard to prepare a guide to Mises’s *Human Action*. The project expanded into an independent treatise. Rothbard wrote much of it during the 1950s. The resulting *Man, Economy, and State* presented production, exchange, money, capital, interest, monopoly, and intervention through a Misesian framework.

A concluding section applied the theory to government intervention. The publisher separated this material; it later appeared as *Power and Market*. This publication history generated a durable misconception that *Power and Market* was conceived as an unrelated later book.

### Circle Bastiat

Around the Mises seminar, Rothbard formed an informal group nicknamed the **Circle Bastiat**. Participants included Ralph Raico, Leonard Liggio, George Reisman, Robert Hessen, and others at different times. The circle combined political discussion, scholarship, satire, films, games, and social gatherings.

The group’s name invoked Frédéric Bastiat and distinguished it from both mainstream conservatives and Ayn Rand’s inner circle.

### Ayn Rand relationship and break

Rothbard met **Ayn Rand** in the 1950s and admired *Atlas Shrugged* intensely. A surviving letter praised the novel in extraordinary terms. Rand introduced or reinforced his engagement with natural rights, though he rejected her claim to have created an unprecedented philosophical system.

Relations deteriorated over:

- Rothbard’s anarchism;
- Rand’s insistence that government was necessary;
- competing claims to intellectual authority;
- the personal and cultural conformity expected within Rand’s circle;
- hostility toward JoAnn’s religious background and refusal to conform, according to Rothbard’s later account.

Rothbard left, briefly returned, and broke decisively. He later wrote “The Sociology of the Ayn Rand Cult” and the one-act satire *Mozart Was a Red*. His version is detailed but adversarial. Rand and Nathaniel Branden’s circles supplied competing accounts of the broader movement, though neither produced an equally detailed public rebuttal specifically to Rothbard’s narrative.

## Chronological Life History

## 1960–1964: Major books and separation from conservatism

Rothbard’s first three major books appeared in quick succession:

- *Man, Economy, and State* (1962);
- *The Panic of 1819* (1962);
- *America’s Great Depression* (1963).

*America’s Great Depression* attributed the downturn principally to monetary expansion during the 1920s and criticized Herbert Hoover as an interventionist rather than a passive liquidationist. Its causal argument became canonical within Rothbardian Austrian economics but remains contested by mainstream economic historians.

Rothbard briefly contributed to William F. Buckley Jr.’s *National Review*. He broke with the postwar conservative movement over Cold War militarism, nuclear strategy, and intervention abroad. Buckley’s conservatism accepted a powerful national-security state as necessary against communism; Rothbard regarded that acceptance as a surrender of anti-statism.

His essay “The Mantle of Science” attacked positivism and scientism in the social sciences. “War, Peace, and the State” formulated an antiwar libertarian position that condemned state attacks on civilians and collective punishment.

## 1965–1968: New Left alliance and *Left and Right*

Rothbard, Leonard Liggio, and others founded *Left and Right: A Journal of Libertarian Thought* in 1965. Its thesis was that libertarianism inherited radical strands from the classical left and should ally with the New Left against war, conscription, imperialism, and centralized corporate government.

He engaged students and activists associated with:

- the antiwar movement;
- Students for a Democratic Society;
- revisionist historians;
- black-power and community-control currents;
- the Peace and Freedom Party.

Rothbard supported black resistance to police abuse and praised aspects of nonviolent civil-rights action. He also opposed federal civil-rights mandates on private property, school integration imposed by government, and affirmative action. Thus his record combines opposition to state-enforced segregation and police violence with opposition to federal remedies for private discrimination.

He wrote syndicated columns for Pine Tree Features and newspapers in the Freedom Newspapers chain. The columns addressed Vietnam, urban uprisings, Israel and the Six-Day War, political assassinations, and the 1968 election.

His anti-Zionist analysis treated Israel as a state created through displacement and sustained by Western power. Admirers describe the position as consistently anti-state and anti-imperialist. Critics have identified generalizations about organized Jewish or Zionist influence as conspiratorial or antisemitic. Rothbard, himself of Jewish ancestry, sharply distinguished Judaism from Zionism and rejected the proposition that criticism of Israel was intrinsically antisemitic. Jewish ancestry does not by itself settle the content or effect of the disputed language.

## 1969–1974: Independent libertarian movement

Rothbard founded or co-founded *The Libertarian Forum*, published from 1969 to 1984. It became his main vehicle for strategy, factional argument, commentary, and movement news.

In 1969–1970, *Power and Market: Government and the Economy* appeared. It classified state intervention as autistic, binary, or triangular and argued that taxation, regulation, monopoly privilege, and government services distorted voluntary exchange.

*For a New Liberty: The Libertarian Manifesto* appeared in 1973. It translated his economics and natural-rights theory into a program calling for the abolition of compulsory state institutions, including taxation, public education, conscription, central banking, and state monopolies in courts and policing.

Rothbard joined the newly established **Libertarian Party** in the 1970s. He brought theoretical authority, polemical energy, and factional habits to a party containing minarchists, anarchists, electoral pragmatists, countercultural libertarians, and business-backed professionals.

In 1974 he helped establish the **Center for Libertarian Studies** in New York. The center sponsored conferences, publications, and scholarly networks and later published the *Journal of Libertarian Studies*.

## 1975–1979: Historical work, Koch patronage, and Cato

Rothbard published the four-volume *Conceived in Liberty* between 1975 and 1979. It reconstructed colonial and revolutionary America as a struggle among decentralized communities, commercial interests, imperial power, and radical republican ideas. A projected fifth volume was not then published; a recovered manuscript later appeared posthumously as *The Lost Papers of Murray Rothbard* or as the fifth volume in later editions.

He became associated with businessman **Charles G. Koch**, who financed libertarian organizations. Rothbard, Koch, and political organizer **Edward H. Crane III** collaborated in building a national institutional network.

The **Cato Institute** opened in San Francisco in 1977, emerging from the Charles Koch Foundation. Cato’s institutional history lists Crane and Koch as founders and Rothbard among its early directors; other histories treat all three as co-founders. Rothbard proposed the name “Cato,” invoking the eighteenth-century libertarian essays *Cato’s Letters*.

Rothbard also edited or influenced *Libertarian Review* and was associated with *Inquiry*. His relationship to this network included intellectual leadership, paid writing, grants, and organizational influence, but available sources do not provide a complete accounting of his income, shares, or consulting agreements.

During this period he joined the **Libertarian Party Radical Caucus**, associated with Justin Raimondo, Eric Garris, Bill Evers, and other activists. The caucus opposed moderate electoral positioning and called for abolitionist rather than gradualist programs.

## 1980–1983: Clark campaign, Cato rupture, and Mises Institute

The 1980 Libertarian presidential campaign nominated **Ed Clark**, financed heavily by David Koch, the vice-presidential nominee. The campaign emphasized low taxes and limited government. Rothbard condemned its “low-tax liberalism” as a dilution of libertarian principle.

The strategic conflict merged with personal and organizational disputes involving Crane and Charles Koch. Rothbard objected to Cato hiring non-Austrian economists, moving toward Washington respectability, and subordinating theory to professional policy advocacy.

On March 27, 1981, Cato’s board removed Rothbard. Rothbard later called the episode “Black Friday” and accused Koch and Crane of wrongfully appropriating or canceling a Cato share he had left in Koch’s Wichita office. This is an **allegation by Rothbard**. The institutional action—his removal—is documented; the claim that the handling of his share was illegal was not adjudicated in any located court decision. Crane and Cato disputed Rothbard’s portrayal and largely declined detailed public discussion.

The break divided organized American libertarianism between a Koch/Cato policy network and a more radical Rothbardian network.

In 1982 **Llewellyn H. Rockwell Jr.** founded the Ludwig von Mises Institute. Rothbard became its academic leader and a principal contributor. The institute gave him an institutional home for Austrian economics after the Cato rupture. He supported its seminars, conferences, journals, and graduate-student network.

*The Ethics of Liberty* appeared in 1982. It grounded self-ownership, original appropriation, contract, punishment, children’s rights, and anarchism in natural law. The book’s discussions of parental obligations, abortion, children leaving home, and the possible market transfer of guardianship generated lasting controversy.

*The Mystery of Banking* followed in 1983, presenting fractional-reserve banking and central banking as interlocking systems of monetary expansion and privilege.

## 1984–1988: Academic consolidation and Ron Paul

Rothbard’s *The Libertarian Forum* ended in 1984. He continued to publish in the *Journal of Libertarian Studies*, the *Austrian Economics Newsletter*, and popular outlets.

He had taught economics at **Brooklyn Polytechnic Institute** from the 1960s into the mid-1980s. Accounts describe a modest position, limited institutional prestige, and an office environment dominated by economists unsympathetic to Austrian theory. He nevertheless attracted students through lectures and private seminars.

In 1986 he became **S.J. Hall Distinguished Professor of Economics at the University of Nevada, Las Vegas**. The endowed chair supplied the academic title and stability he had not obtained in New York. At UNLV he worked closely with **Hans-Hermann Hoppe**, who became a major disciple and extended Rothbardian property theory into arguments for covenant communities, cultural conservatism, and monarchy over democracy.

Rothbard supported former congressman **Ron Paul**, whom he knew through Rockwell and the hard-money movement. Paul credited Rothbard, Rockwell, and coin dealer Burt Blumert with influencing his monetary and political ideas. Rothbard supported Paul’s 1988 Libertarian presidential candidacy despite earlier skepticism about electoral compromise.

A 1987 *Wall Street Journal* profile, reprinted in the *Congressional Record*, described Rothbard’s “legendary” feuds but also his influence on the party. It noted that he had no children and treated younger activists as intellectual protégés.

## 1989–1991: Libertarian Party exit and paleolibertarian turn

Rothbard left the Libertarian Party in 1989 after sustained conflict with its pragmatic leadership. He and Rockwell developed **paleolibertarianism**, an alliance between radical free-market economics, antiwar politics, decentralization, and culturally conservative or populist constituencies.

They joined paleoconservatives associated with the Rockford Institute and founded the **John Randolph Club**. Rothbard served as its founding president. Associates included Thomas Fleming, Samuel Francis, Paul Gottfried, and Pat Buchanan, although they disagreed on economics, nationalism, immigration, and the state.

The alliance marked both continuity and change:

- Continuity: hostility to war, Washington institutions, central banking, and elite-managed politics.
- Change: abandonment of the 1960s New Left alliance; sharper cultural conservatism; opposition to immigration under state-welfare conditions; and explicit outreach to constituencies mobilized by racial and social resentment.

## 1991–1994: Buchanan, Duke, race, and final works

Rothbard supported **Patrick Buchanan’s 1992 Republican presidential-primary campaign**, emphasizing Buchanan’s opposition to the Gulf War, trade and foreign-policy nationalism, and challenge to the Republican establishment. Rothbard did not accept every Buchanan policy, notably protectionism, but treated the campaign as a vehicle for an anti-establishment coalition.

### David Duke and “right-wing populism”

After former Ku Klux Klan leader **David Duke** won a majority of the white vote but lost Louisiana’s 1991 gubernatorial election, Rothbard published “Right-Wing Populism: A Strategy for the Paleo Movement” in January 1992.

He wrote that Duke’s current platform contained demands paleoconservatives or paleolibertarians could embrace, including lower taxes, welfare cuts, attacks on affirmative action, and “equal rights for whites.” He proposed a program directed against elites, street crime, welfare recipients, and the civil-rights establishment, and identified Duke and Senator Joseph McCarthy as strategic examples.

**Documented fact:** Rothbard praised the electoral packaging of parts of Duke’s platform and treated his campaign as a model of populist outreach.

**Necessary qualification:** Rothbard did not endorse the Ku Klux Klan as an organization and did not adopt a biological white-supremacist doctrine in the essay. However, Duke’s well-documented white-supremacist history was public, and Rothbard’s presentation minimized it while adopting racialized campaign themes. Consequently, describing the article simply as color-blind libertarian outreach is incomplete; describing Rothbard as formally joining the Klan is unsupported.

Rothbard also favorably reviewed aspects of Richard Herrnstein and Charles Murray’s *The Bell Curve*, accepting that group differences in measured intelligence merited discussion. His treatment of race and intelligence remains a major source of criticism.

### Los Angeles uprising

Following the 1992 Los Angeles unrest, Rothbard advocated severe action against rioters and defended private and community self-defense. Critics contrast this punitive rhetoric with his earlier analysis of police brutality and black resistance. Supporters distinguish violent attack on persons and property from protest against state power. The writings document a substantial shift in emphasis even where the nonaggression framework remained formally constant.

### Malcolm X

Rothbard repeatedly praised Malcolm X’s evolution toward black nationalism, self-defense, and skepticism of integrationist leadership. This has been cited against a simple account of Rothbard as uniformly hostile to black political autonomy. It does not cancel his later racialized populist strategy; both belong to the record.

### Final scholarly projects

Rothbard completed the two-volume *An Austrian Perspective on the History of Economic Thought*:

1. *Economic Thought Before Adam Smith*;
2. *Classical Economics*.

Published in 1995, the books argued that economic thought did not progress linearly from Adam Smith. Rothbard praised scholastic, French, and continental traditions and criticized Smith for displacing more developed subjective-value ideas.

He also published *The Case Against the Fed* and collections of contemporary economic commentary.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### William Volker Fund

- **Relationship:** recipient of research support, reviewer, and consultant.
- **Period:** principally 1951–1962.
- **Function:** financed research that became *Man, Economy, and State* and other writings.
- **Financial detail:** no comprehensive salary or grant total was established.

### Brooklyn Polytechnic Institute

- **Relationship:** economics instructor/professor.
- **Period:** approximately the mid-1960s to 1986.
- **Reported conditions:** limited prestige and resources; teaching concentrated into a short weekly schedule, leaving time for writing and activism.
- **Dismissal:** no evidence of dismissal for misconduct. He left for the endowed UNLV appointment.

### Center for Libertarian Studies

- **Founded:** 1974.
- **Role:** co-founder and central intellectual figure.
- **Activities:** conferences, publications, *Journal of Libertarian Studies*, and preservation of libertarian scholarship.
- **Patrons and collaborators:** Burton “Burt” Blumert, Leonard Liggio, George Resch, and donors in the Koch network during the early period.

### Cato Institute

- **Founded/opened:** 1977 in San Francisco.
- **Role:** founding director and, in many histories, co-founder.
- **Naming:** Rothbard suggested “Cato.”
- **Financial structure:** Cato unusually had individual shares. Rothbard possessed or was allocated a share.
- **Termination:** removed by board action in March 1981.
- **Disputed financial question:** Rothbard accused Charles Koch and Ed Crane of confiscating or canceling his share. No located judicial decision resolved Rothbard’s accusation.

### Ludwig von Mises Institute

- **Founded:** 1982 by Lew Rockwell.
- **Rothbard’s role:** academic leader, teacher, author, fundraiser-attraction, and symbolic co-founder, although corporate-founder terminology varies.
- **Funding:** private donations; the consulted records do not support a claim that Rothbard personally owned the institute.
- **Legacy:** became the principal institutional publisher of his works and custodian of his intellectual estate.

### University of Nevada, Las Vegas

- **Position:** S.J. Hall Distinguished Professor of Economics, 1986–1995.
- **Importance:** his highest-ranking and most secure academic appointment.
- **Project:** an Austrian-oriented teaching and research group with Hoppe and students.

### Personal wealth and property

Rothbard did not become a major business owner or investor. He earned income from teaching, grants, consulting, writing, lectures, and institutional appointments. Accounts of his New York years emphasize modest academic circumstances rather than wealth.

No verified evidence was found of:

- substantial corporate holdings other than the disputed Cato share;
- patents or inventions;
- offshore structures;
- criminal financial activity;
- bankruptcy;
- securities prosecution;
- tax litigation;
- inherited industrial wealth.

## Political, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

### From limited government to anarcho-capitalism

Rothbard began as an Old Right Republican and initially considered limited constitutional government. By the early 1950s he concluded that the state’s compulsory territorial monopoly contradicted self-ownership and free exchange.

He synthesized:

1. Austrian economics;
2. Lockean and scholastic natural rights;
3. individualist anarchism;
4. competitive markets in police, defense, courts, and money.

He sometimes called his position “nonarchist” or “anarchist,” while “anarcho-capitalism” became the standard label. He distinguished it from communist and collectivist anarchism.

### Nonaggression axiom

Rothbard defined aggression as the initiation of physical force against persons or legitimately owned property. From this he derived opposition to taxation, conscription, regulation, and state monopoly.

Critics dispute:

- whether property rights can be derived without circularity;
- whether initial appropriation establishes morally binding title;
- whether all enforceable obligations reduce to property;
- how competing private courts would avoid warfare or cartelization;
- whether children’s and dependent persons’ interests fit the system.

### Economics

His economics defended:

- subjective value and marginal utility;
- time preference as the basis of interest;
- Austrian capital theory;
- monetary regression and commodity money;
- 100-percent-reserve or nonfraudulent banking;
- the Austrian business-cycle theory;
- rejection of econometric prediction as a foundation for economic law;
- rejection of monopoly definitions based merely on market share;
- opposition to state antitrust, central banking, and price controls.

Mainstream economics largely rejected his strict apriorism and his refusal to use empirical testing in the ordinary manner. Within Austrian economics, he became one pole of a methodological divide involving Hayekian, Kirznerian, and Lachmannian approaches.

### History

Rothbard’s historical method combined archival detail with a strong interest in:

- who benefited financially from state policy;
- banking and bondholding networks;
- war finance;
- intellectuals as legitimators of power;
- factional coalitions behind ostensibly ideological programs.

This yielded influential institutional histories but also criticism that he reduced complex events to networks of bankers, state officials, and favored corporations.

### War and foreign policy

Rothbard opposed:

- American entry into World Wars I and II;
- the Cold War;
- nuclear brinkmanship;
- Korea and Vietnam;
- military alliances and foreign bases;
- aid to Israel and other client states;
- the Gulf War.

He regarded states, not private ideological enemies, as the principal agents of organized mass violence. His radical noninterventionism was one of the most consistent features across his shifting alliances.

### Religion and culture

Rothbard was personally secular but increasingly argued that libertarian political institutions could coexist with traditional religious and familial authority. Late paleolibertarianism distinguished coercive state power from nonstate social authority.

He admired classical music and popular film, disliked modernist and avant-garde cultural trends, and wrote cultural criticism with a comic and combative style.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Ludwig von Mises

**Documented fact:** teacher, mentor, and primary economic influence from 1949 until Mises’s death in 1973. Rothbard extended Misesian economics beyond Mises’s limited-government conclusions.

### JoAnn Rothbard

**Documented fact:** spouse, editorial assistant, intellectual confidante, hostess, and posthumous guardian of his work.

### Joseph Dorfman

**Documented fact:** Columbia dissertation adviser who enabled completion of the Ph.D.

### Arthur Burns

**Reported and substantially documented:** family acquaintance and Columbia economist who opposed Rothbard’s dissertation. Later portrayed in Rothbardian literature as an establishment adversary.

### Frank Chodorov

**Documented fact:** editor, mentor, and conduit to the individualist Old Right.

### Henry Hazlitt

**Documented fact:** economic journalist and associate in the New York free-market network; helped connect figures around Mises and Rand.

### Ayn Rand and Nathaniel Branden

**Documented fact:** temporary alliance and intellectual exchange followed by a bitter break. Rothbard’s later satire helped create the enduring image of Rand’s circle as a “cult.”

### Leonard Liggio

**Documented fact:** friend, co-editor of *Left and Right*, historical collaborator, and bridge to New Left and European classical-liberal scholarship.

### Ralph Raico

**Documented fact:** Circle Bastiat friend, historian, Mises seminar participant, and later Mises Institute associate.

### George Reisman and Robert Hessen

**Documented fact:** participants in the New York libertarian/Randian milieu. Their paths diverged after disputes involving Rand and anarchism.

### Harry Elmer Barnes

**Documented fact:** revisionist historian and influence on Rothbard’s interpretation of American entry into war. Barnes’s late involvement with Holocaust-revisionist and denial literature created a continuing controversy around Rothbard’s willingness to publish and praise him.

### Charles G. Koch

**Documented fact:** patron, organizational collaborator, and Cato associate, followed by an acrimonious rupture.

### Edward H. Crane III

**Documented fact:** Libertarian Party strategist and Cato executive; first an ally, later a principal rival over pragmatism, control, and institutional direction.

### Burton “Burt” Blumert

**Documented fact:** coin dealer, fundraiser, Center for Libertarian Studies officer, friend, and link to Ron Paul.

### Lew Rockwell

**Documented fact:** protégé, collaborator, founder of the Mises Institute, and co-editor of the *Rothbard-Rockwell Report*. Rockwell became executor of Rothbard’s estate and a principal architect of his posthumous reputation.

### Ron Paul

**Documented fact:** political ally and student of Rothbard’s monetary and antiwar thought. Rothbard supported Paul’s 1988 campaign and influenced the intellectual network around him.

### Hans-Hermann Hoppe

**Documented fact:** UNLV colleague and disciple. Hoppe developed elements of Rothbard’s property theory in more explicitly culturally exclusionary directions.

### Justin Raimondo

**Documented fact:** Libertarian Party Radical Caucus ally, later John Randolph Club associate, memorialist, and author of the first full biography, *An Enemy of the State*.

### Pat Buchanan

**Documented fact:** political ally during the 1992 campaign and paleoconservative coalition, despite disagreements about trade and nationalism.

### David Duke

**Documented fact:** not a known personal friend or collaborator. Rothbard analyzed Duke’s campaigns favorably as a strategic model. Claims of a formal Rothbard–Duke organizational partnership were not substantiated.

## Controversies, Allegations, and Disputed Questions

## Race and civil rights

### Established positions

Rothbard opposed:

- Jim Crow laws and compulsory segregation;
- police brutality;
- state-enforced racial discrimination;
- federal laws compelling private owners to serve or employ without discrimination;
- affirmative action and racial preferences;
- compulsory school integration.

The combination followed his property-rights doctrine but meant that he opposed central instruments used to dismantle private as well as governmental discrimination.

### Was Rothbard a racist?

This is an interpretive dispute rather than a judicially decidable fact.

Evidence cited by critics includes:

- participation in Thurmond’s 1948 campaign;
- opposition to portions of federal civil-rights law;
- the 1992 Duke essay;
- rhetoric about crime, welfare, and “racialist science”;
- paleolibertarian outreach to racially resentful voters;
- association with revisionists and paleoconservatives who published racial-nationalist work.

Evidence cited against a unitary characterization includes:

- opposition to legal segregation and imperialism;
- defense of black resistance to police violence;
- praise for Malcolm X;
- support for community control and secession;
- insistence that individual rights, not racial entitlement, were primary;
- absence of a developed doctrine of innate white political supremacy in his major theoretical works.

The documented record supports describing parts of his late political strategy as racialized and as accommodating white backlash. It does not establish membership in a white-supremacist organization.

## David Duke

The claim that Rothbard “endorsed David Duke” requires precision.

- He defended major elements of Duke’s 1991 platform.
- He deplored what he considered establishment hysteria against Duke.
- He treated the campaign as a political model.
- He did not endorse Duke’s former Klan activity or openly formulate a white-supremacist program.

Thus “strategic and issue-based praise” is documented; a formal personal endorsement or organizational alliance is not established from the consulted documents.

## Holocaust revisionism and denial associations

Rothbard promoted revisionist examination of the origins of World War II and admired Harry Elmer Barnes. Barnes in his final decades defended or enabled authors who denied or minimized the Holocaust.

Rothbard’s defenders distinguish:

- revisionism about war responsibility;
- procedural opposition to suppressing controversial historical claims;
- denial of the Nazi extermination program.

Critics argue that Rothbard failed to draw adequate boundaries and conferred respectability on denial-adjacent networks.

No primary text located in this research shows Rothbard denying that Nazi Germany systematically murdered European Jews. The strongest documented criticism concerns his associations, publishing decisions, and treatment of Barnes—not a demonstrated authored denial thesis.

## Antisemitism and Zionism

Rothbard attacked Zionism, Israel, American aid to Israel, and pro-Israel political organizations. Some critics called his rhetoric antisemitic; Rothbard denied the equation of Jewish identity with Zionism.

Evidence establishes intense anti-Zionism. Whether particular generalizations about “Zionist control” crossed into antisemitic conspiracy language must be judged text by text. His Jewish ancestry and atheism are relevant biography, not dispositive evidence either way.

## Children, parental obligation, and “baby markets”

In *The Ethics of Liberty*, Rothbard argued that:

- a parent may not aggress against a child;
- a parent has no enforceable positive obligation to provide care;
- a child should be free to leave when capable;
- guardianship rights could be transferred through a market.

Critics summarize this as permitting child neglect or the sale of children. Rothbard distinguished alienable guardianship services from ownership of a child’s person and argued that a market would move children toward willing caregivers. The text nevertheless permits consequences that many legal and moral theories classify as neglect, and it remains among his most controversial arguments.

## Abortion

Rothbard treated the pregnant woman as the owner of her body and the fetus as lacking a right to remain there without consent; he therefore defended abortion as removal rather than permissible aggression. His later alliance with culturally conservative anti-abortion activists did not erase this theoretical position.

## Cato share and institutional funds

Rothbard charged that Koch and Crane took or voided his Cato share and transformed organizations built around his ideas into donor-controlled institutions. The board removal is established. The alleged illegality, theft, or breach of agreement remains unresolved in the located public record.

No evidence was found of a criminal complaint, indictment, civil judgment, or damages award in Rothbard’s favor.

## Ron Paul newsletters

Racist, homophobic, and conspiratorial passages appeared in newsletters published under Ron Paul’s name from the late 1980s through the 1990s. Journalists and former associates attributed much of the writing to Lew Rockwell or other staff.

Rothbard’s status requires careful labeling:

- **Documented fact:** his and Rockwell’s contemporaneous paleolibertarian strategy supplied part of the political context.
- **Documented fact:** Paul’s newsletter promoted the separate *Rothbard-Rockwell Report*.
- **Reported allegation:** former Paul aide Eric Dondero alleged that Rothbard provided “major input.”
- **Unresolved authorship:** no bylined document or conclusive manuscript evidence located here proves that Rothbard wrote the notorious unsigned passages.
- **Unsupported overstatement:** treating Rothbard as conclusively identified as their author.

## Factionalism and personal attacks

Contemporaries described Rothbard as generous and convivial in person but ferocious in print. He repeatedly constructed coalitions and then broke with them: Buckley conservatives, Randians, the New Left, Koch and Cato, and Libertarian Party pragmatists.

Critics interpret this as sectarianism, personal vindictiveness, and a tendency to treat strategic disagreement as betrayal. Supporters interpret it as refusal to compromise antiwar and anti-state principles. The frequency and intensity of the breaks are documented; motive remains interpretive.

## Conspiracy-style history

Rothbard wrote extensively about banking interests, Morgan and Rockefeller networks, the Federal Reserve, war finance, and intellectual elites. Some relationships he traced are supported by corporate, family, and government records. Critics faulted him for selecting evidence to fit a ruling-class model and turning overlapping interests into coordinated designs.

There is no evidence that he belonged to an intelligence service or clandestine political organization. Assertions that he was controlled by the Koch family, the Soviet Union, Israel, or an intelligence agency belong to polemic or internet rumor unless tied to specific documentary evidence.

## Criminal conduct, lawsuits, and investigations

The searches conducted for this dossier found no reliable evidence that Rothbard was:

- arrested or imprisoned;
- indicted or convicted;
- formally investigated for fraud, corruption, money laundering, or espionage;
- sued to judgment for his political or financial activities;
- involved in political violence.

His controversies were principally intellectual, factional, financial-organizational, and reputational.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Propaganda

### “Founder of libertarianism”

**Story:** Rothbard single-handedly created modern libertarianism.

**Circulation:** sympathetic biographies, memorials, and institutional publicity.

**Evidence:** he was central to anarcho-capitalist theory and to building an independent American libertarian movement.

**Against:** classical liberalism, individualist anarchism, the Foundation for Economic Education, Mises, Rand, Chodorov, Nock, and others preceded him. The Libertarian Party and Cato had multiple founders.

**Reputational effect:** elevates Rothbard from major synthesizer and organizer to sole originator.

### “Inventor of anarcho-capitalism”

**Story:** Rothbard invented both the term and doctrine.

**Evidence:** he produced its most systematic twentieth-century synthesis.

**Against:** comparable ideas appeared in Molinari, individualist anarchists, and other market-anarchist writing. Usage of the term cannot securely be attributed to him alone.

### The fully formed child prodigy

**Story:** Rothbard read almost from infancy and was already recognizably himself in elementary school.

**Origin:** his youthful autobiography and friends’ recollections.

**Evidence:** early writings establish precocity.

**Limitation:** exact claims such as mastering the alphabet at seventeen months rest on family memory.

### “Mises’s greatest student”

**Story:** Rothbard was Mises’s uniquely faithful or greatest pupil.

**Origin:** Mises Institute memorial culture.

**Evidence:** Rothbard constructed the most extensive American Misesian system of his generation.

**Against:** “greatest” is evaluative; Israel Kirzner, F. A. Hayek, Fritz Machlup, and others made different contributions and did not accept Rothbard’s anarchism or methodological rigidity.

### “Expelled by Ayn Rand because his wife was Christian”

**Story:** Rand demanded that Rothbard divorce or repudiate JoAnn solely because she was religious.

**Evidence:** Rothbard’s retrospective account describes pressure surrounding JoAnn and religious conformity.

**Against:** the dispute also concerned anarchism, philosophical credit, group discipline, and personal authority. Rand’s own detailed account of this particular confrontation is unavailable.

**Status:** reported by Rothbard, not independently resolved.

### “The Kochs stole Cato”

**Story:** Charles Koch and Ed Crane stole Rothbard’s share and hijacked his institute.

**Evidence:** Rothbard was removed; Cato possessed an unusual shareholder structure; the ideological transformation and personal rupture are documented.

**Against:** “stole” is Rothbard’s legal and moral characterization. No located judgment establishes theft.

### “Rothbard endorsed the Klan”

**Story:** his praise for Duke amounted to endorsement of the Ku Klux Klan.

**Evidence:** he knowingly promoted Duke’s post-Klan electoral strategy.

**Against:** no evidence shows that he endorsed or joined the Klan itself.

**Effect:** condensed a nuanced but damaging documented episode into a simpler hostile label.

### “Secret author of the Ron Paul newsletters”

**Story:** Rothbard wrote the most notorious racist newsletter passages.

**Origin:** later journalistic reconstruction and allegations by former associates.

**Evidence:** ideological overlap, close connections, and an allegation of input.

**Against:** absence of bylines, drafts, or conclusive attribution.

**Status:** unresolved allegation.

### “Ignored only because he was too radical”

**Story:** academic neglect reflected establishment fear rather than scholarly judgment.

**Evidence:** his politics and methodology lay far outside postwar economics, and donor-supported institutions sometimes substituted for ordinary academic advancement.

**Against:** economists also raised substantive objections to his methodology, banking theory, welfare economics, and empirical practice.

### “Blueprint for Trump and the alt-right”

**Story:** Rothbard directly designed twenty-first-century Trumpism or the alt-right.

**Evidence:** recent scholars identify striking continuities between his 1992 populist program and later anti-elite, racialized right-wing politics; some later far-right intellectuals cite him.

**Against:** Rothbard died in 1995, opposed protectionism and presidential state power, and cannot be treated as author of later movements. Influence is indirect and mediated through Rockwell, paleoconservatism, the Mises Institute, Ron Paul networks, and later online subcultures.

## Death and Final Disposition

Rothbard died of a heart attack on January 7, 1995, in New York City. Some notices specify St. Luke’s–Roosevelt Hospital in Manhattan. He was sixty-eight.

He had remained active through late 1994, writing for the *Rothbard-Rockwell Report*, teaching at UNLV, and completing his history of economic thought. No well-sourced evidence was found of a prolonged terminal illness, addiction, suicide attempt, psychiatric hospitalization, disabling accident, or suspicious circumstances.

He was survived by JoAnn. Contemporary memorials record the shock of an apparently sudden death. Details of burial or cremation were not reliably established from the sources consulted.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Austrian economics

Rothbard became one of the principal reference points for the post-1970s Austrian revival. The Mises Institute made his books, lectures, and articles widely available, often without charge. “Rothbardian” denotes a distinct current characterized by:

- strict Misesian praxeology;
- natural-rights anarchism;
- hostility to fractional-reserve banking;
- revisionist history;
- abolitionist political rhetoric.

Other Austrian economists accept portions of his price, capital, and monetary theory while rejecting his ethics, anarchism, or methodological exclusivity.

### Libertarian movement

His organizational influence survives through:

- the Mises Institute;
- Center for Libertarian Studies archives;
- Ron Paul and associated antiwar/hard-money movements;
- Libertarian Party radical and Mises Caucus currents;
- market-anarchist publishing;
- cryptocurrency and private-money discussions;
- secessionist and decentralist political theory.

He did not invent every constituent idea, but his synthesis remains foundational to anarcho-capitalism.

### Scholarship

His academic standing is uneven:

- *The Panic of 1819* remains cited as a serious early monograph.
- *Man, Economy, and State* is canonical within Rothbardian Austrian economics.
- *America’s Great Depression* remains influential but heavily disputed.
- His history of economic thought prompted renewed interest in scholastic and French traditions while attracting criticism for polemical treatment of Adam Smith and other figures.
- His political philosophy is widely discussed in libertarian and anarchist literature but less accepted in mainstream philosophy.

### Political afterlives

Late Rothbardian strategy is increasingly studied as a predecessor to right-populist coalitions. The historical chain is not linear:

Rothbardian paleolibertarianism → Rockwell/Mises Institute and Ron Paul networks → later libertarian-populist and online currents.

Some white nationalists and alt-right figures appropriated Rothbardian arguments for secession, private exclusion, and anti-democratic property orders. Other Rothbardians rejected nationalism, racism, border controls, and state authority. His legacy therefore supports rival libertarian tendencies rather than one unified successor ideology.

### Posthumous institutional mythology

The Mises Institute presents Rothbard as an overlooked polymath of stature comparable to great system-builders. Cato’s official histories recognize his early involvement but generally place less emphasis on him than Rothbardian histories. The asymmetry reflects the unresolved institutional rupture of 1981.

### Current reinterpretation

Recent research has shifted attention from abstract anarcho-capitalist theory to:

- his political coalition-making;
- race and civil-rights backlash;
- private philanthropic power;
- the history of Cato and the Koch network;
- the John Randolph Club;
- the 1992 populist essay;
- connections between market radicalism and cultural traditionalism.

These interpretations correct earlier accounts that separated Rothbard the economist from Rothbard the activist. They can overreach when they treat every later right-populist development as his intended creation.

## Atlas Connections

Only documented or explicitly reported connections with persons on the supplied atlas roster are listed. Mere shared subject matter is excluded.

### Adam Smith

- **Nature:** major historical and intellectual target.
- **Dates:** especially Rothbard’s final history-of-thought project, published in 1995.
- **Evidence label:** documented intellectual connection.
- **Details:** Rothbard argued that Smith was not the uncomplicated founder of economics and that he displaced superior subjective-value insights developed by scholastics, Cantillon, Turgot, and others. This was influence-through-criticism, not a personal connection.

### Carl Menger

- **Nature:** foundational theoretical influence through the Austrian School.
- **Evidence label:** documented intellectual influence.
- **Details:** Rothbard adopted subjective value, marginal analysis, and causal-realist economics derived from Menger’s tradition.

### Eugen Böhm Ritter von Bawerk

- **Nature:** influence on capital and interest theory.
- **Evidence label:** documented intellectual influence.
- **Details:** Rothbard built on Böhm-Bawerk while revising aspects of the theory through Misesian time preference.

### Friedrich August von Hayek

- **Nature:** fellow Austrian economist, acquaintance in the transatlantic classical-liberal network, and intellectual contrast.
- **Evidence label:** documented professional and intellectual connection.
- **Details:** Rothbard respected Hayek’s scholarship but criticized his evolutionary, rule-based, and less strictly rationalist liberalism. Unpublished Volker memoranda and later essays make the disagreement explicit.

### Ludwig Heinrich Edler von Mises

- **Nature:** teacher, mentor, seminar leader, and dominant economic influence.
- **Dates and places:** from 1949, Mises’s NYU seminar, New York City, until Mises’s death in 1973.
- **Evidence label:** documented fact.
- **Details:** Rothbard defended and extended Misesian praxeology and business-cycle theory, while departing from Mises by embracing anarchism.

### Ayn Rand

Ayn Rand does not appear under that familiar name in the supplied roster. No connection is entered under a different roster identity.

### Milton Friedman

- **Nature:** contemporary economist, personal acquaintance within free-market networks, and frequent theoretical opponent.
- **Evidence label:** documented intellectual and professional connection.
- **Details:** Rothbard attacked Friedmanite monetarism, positive economics, flexible exchange-rate arguments, and proposals such as education vouchers where he regarded them as reforms preserving state power. Both participated in broader postwar free-market circles, but they represented rival schools.

### John Locke

- **Nature:** central philosophical influence.
- **Evidence label:** documented intellectual influence.
- **Details:** Rothbard drew on Lockean self-ownership, original appropriation, and resistance to political authority, modifying Locke toward stateless natural-rights theory.

### Thomas Aquinas

- **Nature:** natural-law influence mediated through scholastic philosophy.
- **Evidence label:** documented intellectual influence.
- **Details:** Rothbard, although atheist, treated the Thomistic natural-law tradition as compatible with reason-based objective ethics.

### Aristotle

- **Nature:** philosophical influence mediated through Rand, scholasticism, and natural-law theory.
- **Evidence label:** documented intellectual influence.
- **Details:** Rothbard regarded human nature and teleological reasoning as foundations for ethics, though he was not an Aristotelian specialist.

### Karl Marx

- **Nature:** major intellectual opponent, with limited methodological appreciation.
- **Evidence label:** documented intellectual connection.
- **Details:** Rothbard rejected socialism, labor-value theory, and Marxian class analysis but sometimes adopted a conflict model in which state-connected elites exploit taxpayers and consumers.

### Friedrich Nietzsche

- **Nature:** discussed and criticized in philosophical and cultural writings.
- **Evidence label:** documented intellectual connection.
- **Details:** no evidence of a meeting or direct historical relationship; the connection is textual only.

### Immanuel Kant

- **Nature:** philosophical opponent in Rothbard’s writings on natural law and epistemology.
- **Evidence label:** documented intellectual connection.
- **Details:** Rothbard rejected Kantian ethical formalism and epistemological dualism.

### John Maynard Keynes

- **Nature:** principal economic and historical opponent.
- **Evidence label:** documented intellectual connection.
- **Details:** Rothbard attacked Keynesian fiscal policy, monetary theory, and the intellectual politics surrounding Keynes. No personal meeting is established.

### George Orwell

- **Nature:** subject of an early book review.
- **Date:** 1949.
- **Evidence label:** documented intellectual connection.
- **Details:** Rothbard reviewed *Nineteen Eighty-Four* in *Analysis* under the title “Our Future.”

### Abraham Lincoln

- **Nature:** historical subject and political antagonist in Rothbard’s interpretation.
- **Evidence label:** documented intellectual connection.
- **Details:** Rothbard treated Lincoln and the Civil War as major accelerants of centralized state power. No personal connection was possible.

### Thomas Jefferson

- **Nature:** political and intellectual model.
- **Evidence label:** documented intellectual influence.
- **Details:** Rothbard praised Jeffersonian decentralism, agrarian republicanism, and suspicion of banks and centralized power while criticizing inconsistencies.

### Alexander Hamilton

- **Nature:** historical antagonist.
- **Evidence label:** documented intellectual connection.
- **Details:** Rothbard associated Hamilton with central banking, public debt, mercantilism, and national consolidation.

### George Washington

- **Nature:** subject in Rothbard’s histories of colonial America and the Revolution.
- **Evidence label:** documented intellectual connection.
- **Details:** Rothbard’s treatment was more critical than conventional patriotic biography, emphasizing land, military, and political interests.

### Benjamin Franklin

- **Nature:** subject in *Conceived in Liberty* and related historical writings.
- **Evidence label:** documented intellectual connection.

### Alexis de Tocqueville

- **Nature:** discussed within traditions of decentralization and American political analysis.
- **Evidence label:** reported textual connection; no direct personal connection.

### Charles de Ganahl Koch

- **Nature:** patron, collaborator, Cato co-founder, and later adversary.
- **Dates and places:** principally the 1970s to March 1981; Wichita, San Francisco, New York, and libertarian conferences.
- **Evidence label:** documented fact.
- **Details:** Koch financed institutions through which Rothbard worked. Their rupture concerned strategy, personnel, power, and the disputed Cato share.

### Javier Gerardo Milei

- **Nature:** posthumous intellectual influence.
- **Evidence label:** reported and publicly acknowledged influence.
- **Details:** Milei has identified Austrian economists, including Rothbard, among important influences. There was no meeting: Milei was born in 1970, and the influence occurred through writings.

### Peter Andreas Thiel

- **Nature:** sometimes placed by recent commentators in a broad political tradition influenced by paleolibertarian and anti-democratic market thought.
- **Evidence label:** interpretive association, not a documented personal connection.
- **Details:** no source consulted established correspondence, meeting, or explicit Rothbard acknowledgment by Thiel sufficient for a stronger label.

### Elon Reeve Musk

- **Nature:** recent commentators sometimes group Musk with political currents indirectly influenced by paleolibertarianism.
- **Evidence label:** theory or interpretation only.
- **Details:** no documented personal or explicit intellectual connection to Rothbard was established. It should not be represented as a biographical crossing.

### Avram Noam Chomsky

- **Nature:** contemporary antiwar intellectual and theoretical opponent concerning property and anarchism.
- **Evidence label:** documented intellectual opposition; no verified meeting.
- **Details:** both opposed American intervention abroad, but Chomsky rejects anarcho-capitalism as incompatible with historical anarchism.

### Mikhail Aleksandrovich Bakunin

- **Nature:** historical anarchist discussed as part of the collectivist tradition Rothbard rejected.
- **Evidence label:** documented intellectual connection only.

### Murray Newton Rothbard

- **Nature:** the subject himself appears on the supplied roster.
- **Evidence label:** documented identity.

No reliable biographical crossings were found with the great majority of the atlas roster. Absence of an entry means the research did not establish a meeting, correspondence, collaboration, rivalry, family relationship, common institution, or explicitly acknowledged influence.

## Chronology

- **1926, March 2:** Born in the Bronx to David and Rae Rothbard.
- **1930s:** Educated in New York public and private schools; develops interests in reading, history, mathematics, music, and politics.
- **1942:** Enters Columbia University at sixteen.
- **1945:** Receives B.A. in mathematics; enters New York Young Republican and Old Right circles.
- **1946:** Receives M.A. in economics.
- **1948:** Organizes Columbia activity for Strom Thurmond’s States’ Rights presidential campaign.
- **1949:** Begins attending Ludwig von Mises’s NYU seminar; publishes early reviews in *Analysis*.
- **1950–1951:** Publishes reviews of *Human Action* and methodological articles in the *American Economic Review*.
- **1951:** Begins major work for the William Volker Fund.
- **1953, January 16:** Marries JoAnn Schumacher.
- **Mid-1950s:** Organizes Circle Bastiat; associates with Ayn Rand’s circle.
- **1956:** Receives Ph.D. from Columbia under Joseph Dorfman.
- **1957–1958:** Praises *Atlas Shrugged*; relationship with Rand’s circle deteriorates.
- **Late 1950s/early 1960s:** Final break with Rand; writes *Mozart Was a Red*.
- **1960:** Break with *National Review* conservatism becomes pronounced over Cold War interventionism.
- **1962:** Publishes *Man, Economy, and State* and *The Panic of 1819*.
- **1963:** Publishes *America’s Great Depression* and the first version of *What Has Government Done to Our Money?*
- **1965:** Co-founds *Left and Right*; begins organized outreach to the New Left.
- **1967–1968:** Writes Pine Tree Features columns on war and domestic politics.
- **1969:** Launches *The Libertarian Forum*.
- **1970:** Publishes *Power and Market*.
- **1973:** Publishes *For a New Liberty*; Mises dies.
- **1974:** Helps establish the Center for Libertarian Studies; publishes *Egalitarianism as a Revolt Against Nature*.
- **1975–1979:** Publishes four volumes of *Conceived in Liberty*.
- **1977:** Helps establish or becomes a founding director of the Cato Institute; launches the *Journal of Libertarian Studies*.
- **1978–1983:** Associated with the Libertarian Party Radical Caucus.
- **1980:** Opposes the strategic moderation of the Ed Clark–David Koch Libertarian campaign.
- **1981, March 27:** Removed from Cato’s board in the “Black Friday” rupture.
- **1982:** Becomes the principal academic figure at the newly founded Mises Institute; publishes *The Ethics of Liberty*.
- **1983:** Publishes *The Mystery of Banking*.
- **1984:** *The Libertarian Forum* ceases publication.
- **1986:** Leaves Brooklyn Polytechnic for the S.J. Hall Distinguished Professorship at UNLV.
- **1988:** Supports Ron Paul’s Libertarian presidential campaign.
- **1989:** Leaves the Libertarian Party; helps form the paleolibertarian/paleoconservative alliance and John Randolph Club.
- **1990:** Begins the *Rothbard-Rockwell Report*.
- **1991:** David Duke’s Louisiana campaign becomes a model in Rothbard’s emerging populist strategy.
- **1992:** Supports Pat Buchanan; publishes “Right-Wing Populism.”
- **1994:** Publishes *The Case Against the Fed* and “Nations by Consent.”
- **1995, January 7:** Dies of a heart attack in New York City.
- **1995:** Two-volume *An Austrian Perspective on the History of Economic Thought* appears.
- **1997 onward:** Essays, unfinished manuscripts, lectures, memoranda, correspondence, and serials are issued in posthumous collections.
- **2000:** Justin Raimondo publishes *An Enemy of the State*.
- **2007:** *The Betrayal of the American Right* appears posthumously.
- **2010s–2020s:** Renewed scholarship examines his role in donor institutions, race politics, paleolibertarianism, populism, and the intellectual ancestry of parts of the contemporary right.
