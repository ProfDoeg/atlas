# Alfred Jarry: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier concerns Alfred Henri Jarry (1873–1907), the French writer, dramatist, poet, graphic artist, editor, journalist, cyclist, and originator or principal codifier of ’pataphysics.

Evidence is classified as follows:

- **Documented fact:** supported by civil, military, notarial, manuscript, publication, contractual, or comparably direct records.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a contemporary witness or later specialist but not independently established here from an underlying record.
- **Disputed claim:** supported by conflicting testimony or interpretations.
- **Rumor or myth:** a circulating story for which reliable confirmation is absent.
- **Interpretation:** a scholarly conclusion rather than an independently verifiable biographical event.

Jarry deliberately blurred art and life, spoke in the manner of Père Ubu, cultivated false genealogies, and encouraged stories about his weapons, alcohol, physical powers, and eccentricities. Anecdotes about him therefore require unusual caution. The chronology maintained by the Société des Amis d’Alfred Jarry, archival descriptions from FranceArchives and the Bibliothèque nationale de France, surviving manuscripts, contemporaneous publications, and modern critical biographies have been given greater weight than unsourced retellings.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full registered name:** Alfred Henri Jarry. “Henry” occasionally appears in period typography or secondary references.
- **Usual name:** Alfred Jarry.
- **Birth:** 8 September 1873, at approximately 5:00 a.m., 8 quai de la Mayenne—then also identified as quai de l’Impératrice and now quai Jehan-Fouquet—Laval, Mayenne, France.
- **Emergency baptism or ondoyement:** 8 September 1873, administered by Félix Hélie, vicar of the parish of La Trinité.
- **Formal baptism:** 8 June 1874, Laval cathedral. The specialist chronology says his sister was baptized at the same ceremony.
- **Death:** 1 November 1907, 4:15 p.m., Hôpital de la Charité, Paris.
- **Recorded cause:** tuberculous meningitis.
- **Burial:** 3 November 1907, after a short service at Saint-Sulpice, in the Cimetière parisien de Bagneux. The original grave concession was not permanent and the precise disposition of his remains later became uncertain.
- **Nationality:** French.
- **Principal occupations:** writer, dramatist, poet, novelist, essayist, art critic, journalist, editor, translator, theatrical secretary, draughtsman, and wood engraver.
- **Literary-artistic context:** Symbolism; an important precursor for Dada, Surrealism, Futurism, the Theatre of the Absurd, Oulipo, conceptual art, and several strains of experimental theatre.
- **Religious identification:** baptized Catholic; his adult position moved between Symbolist religious imagery, heterodox metaphysics, irony, and an apparently sincere but idiosyncratic Catholic return during his last illness.
- **Political position:** never a stable party activist. His writings pass through individualist, anti-bourgeois, anarchic, anti-militarist, anti-colonial, and later Catholic-traditionalist tendencies. Late manuscripts and testimony contain antisemitic material.
- **Marital status:** never married.
- **Known children:** none.
- **Physical description:** short and slight; later portraits show a conspicuous moustache and long hair. Precise height claims vary and should not be treated as securely documented.
- **Languages:** French; classical education in Latin and Greek; sufficient Greek and English for collaborative translation, though the extent of his independent mastery is debated.

### Names, signatures, titles, and personae

- **Alain Jans:** documented pseudonym used for some writing.
- **Alfred-Henry Jarry:** early expanded signature, including work for *L’Art littéraire*.
- **Père Ubu / Ubu:** originally a fictional figure, increasingly used by Jarry as a public persona. Friends reported that he spoke of “we” and used Ubu’s vocabulary in ordinary life.
- **Dr Faustroll:** fictional pataphysician, not a conventional pseudonym, but sometimes made to function as an authorial or theoretical mask.
- **“Celui qui revolver”:** posthumous or commemorative epithet derived from Jarry’s weapon-centered public mythology.
- **“Poète breton” and elaborate aristocratic ancestry:** partly self-created identity. Jarry’s genuine maternal Breton associations are documented; his grander genealogical claims are not.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Anselme Jarry** was born in 1837. He was a cloth merchant and later commercial representative. On 4 July 1863 he signed a marriage contract with Caroline-Marie Quernest; they married at Hédé on 16 July. Their household and business were initially established at 13 rue de Bootz, Laval, headquarters of the firm Société Jarry-Marie.

The business failed in or around 1879. Later accounts describe Anselme as alcoholic, but the degree and chronology are chiefly biographical testimony rather than a diagnosis. Even after the effective marital separation, recent archival research indicates that he continued providing some financial support.

**Caroline-Marie Quernest**, born in 1842, was the daughter of Charles Jean-Baptiste Quernest, a retired justice of the peace associated with Hédé, Brittany, and Octavie-Sophronie Coutouly. She was musically and literarily inclined. Some biographies describe mental illness in her family and the institutionalization of her mother and a brother. These claims recur in modern scholarship, but particular diagnoses are not consistently documented in publicly accessible records.

After Anselme’s commercial failure, Caroline took the children to Saint-Brieuc. She later moved them to Rennes and then accompanied Alfred to Paris. She died in Paris on 10 May 1893. Her death was a major personal and financial turning point for him.

### Siblings

- **Caroline-Marie Jarry, called Charlotte:** born 8 February 1865 at Laval; died in 1925. She was Alfred’s elder sister, eventual housemate, nurse, correspondent, and custodian of family papers. She assisted him during the cerebral crisis of 1906, recorded his last wishes, and later helped preserve or reconstruct unfinished work.
- **Gustave Anselme Jarry:** born 31 August 1870 and died approximately fifteen days later.

No other surviving siblings are securely documented.

### Ancestry and invented genealogy

Jarry’s paternal background was commercial and provincial rather than aristocratic. His maternal family supplied a genuine Breton connection through the Quernest and Coutouly lines. Late in life, however, Jarry promoted elaborate names and pedigrees, including “Trernec’h de Coutouly de Dorset,” Norman companions of William the Conqueror, and other aristocratic or legendary ancestors.

**Evidentiary status: self-created mythology.** A mock or anticipatory death notice dictated during his 1906 illness called his mother “Caroline Jarry, née Trernec’h de Coutouly de Dorset” and surrounded him with an imposing network of titled or grandly styled relatives. The document reveals how he wanted lineage to function in his personal mythology; it is not a reliable civil genealogy.

### Marriage, partners, and children

Jarry never married and left no known children.

His surviving correspondence and literary transpositions indicate important emotional and erotic attachments, but the boundary between lived relationship and fiction is difficult to fix:

- **Léon-Paul Fargue:** Jarry’s close school and early Paris friend. Modern biographers interpret *Haldernablou* and related evidence as reflecting an intense romantic or sexual relationship. The description “brief passionate relationship” is common in scholarship, but direct documentation is incomplete. **Evidentiary status: strongly supported interpretation, not a formally documented partnership.**
- **Lord Alfred Douglas:** stories of a sexual encounter circulate in later commentary. The available evidence is indirect and insufficient. **Evidentiary status: unverified claim.**
- **Fanny Zaessinger:** a female friend sometimes proposed as a lover. No conclusive evidence establishes a sexual relationship.
- **Berthe de Courrière:** model, literary hostess, and partner of Remy de Gourmont. Accounts describe her fascination with Jarry, but her precise relationship with him remains uncertain.
- **Rachilde:** his most durable close friendship with a woman; no reliable evidence makes it a sexual partnership.

Jarry’s late-adolescent writings and associations support the conclusion that he understood himself for a time as “Uranian,” the period term for male homosexual orientation. His later sexual life is too poorly documented to impose a definitive modern identity label.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1873–1888

The Jarry family moved among several Laval addresses: quai de la Mayenne, quai d’Avenières, quai du Pont-Neuf, and rue de Bootz. In 1878 Anselme built a house at 21 rue de Bootz.

Alfred entered the “minimes” division of the Petit Lycée de Laval on 14 May 1878 and remained there through July 1879.

In spring 1879, following the collapse of Anselme’s business, Caroline separated in practice from her husband and moved with Charlotte and Alfred to Saint-Brieuc. They initially stayed with her parents and later occupied 12 boulevard Charner.

From October 1879 through July 1888, Alfred attended the Lycée de Saint-Brieuc. School records and later recollections characterize him as a brilliant pupil. By about 1885 he was composing verse and prose plays. As an adult he organized these juvenilia under the retrospective title *Ontogénie*.

Early pieces include:

- *Les Brigands de la Calabre*;
- *Le Parapluie-Seringue du docteur Thanaton*;
- *Le Procès*;
- *Les Antliaclastes*;
- other school plays, poems, dramatic fragments, and parodies later collected in *Ontogénie*.

These writings already combine mock science, verbal deformation, violence, parody, schoolboy performance, and challenges to literary hierarchy.

## Education and Formation

### Rennes, 1888–1891

In October 1888 Caroline moved with her children to Rennes, partly to advance their education. Jarry entered the Lycée de Rennes, now Lycée Émile-Zola.

His physics teacher **Félix-Frédéric Hébert** had been the object of a collective school mythology transmitted through generations of pupils. Hébert was variously called P. H., Père Heb, Père Ebé, Ébon, Ébouille, and related forms.

The brothers **Charles and Henri Morin** participated in this collective cycle. Henri gave Jarry an episode written by Charles in which the caricatured teacher became king of Poland: *Les Polonais*. Jarry adapted, reorganized, staged, and progressively transformed this material.

Performances occurred:

- in December 1888 and January 1889 in the Morin family attic;
- later in the Jarry apartment;
- through marionettes and a domestic “Théâtre des Phynances.”

The school play is the earliest recoverable form of what became *Ubu roi*. Jarry also developed material that became *Ubu cocu*, through titles such as *Onésime ou les Tribulations de Priou*, *Les Cornes du P. H.*, and *Les Polyèdres*.

### The authorship problem of *Ubu roi*

- **Documented:** the Ubu figure and *Les Polonais* emerged from collective Rennes school culture before the mature published play.
- **Documented:** Charles and Henri Morin contributed early material.
- **Documented:** Jarry substantially rewrote, staged, published, theorized, and integrated the material into a larger Ubu cycle.
- **Disputed formulation:** calling Jarry the sole “inventor” of Ubu erases collective origins; calling him merely an appropriator erases the transformation through which the school caricature became a major literary and theatrical construction.

Jarry passed the second part of the baccalauréat in 1890 with the grade *bien*.

### Paris and Lycée Henri-IV, 1891–1893

In October 1891 Jarry and his mother moved to Paris. He entered the rhetoric-superior or preparatory curriculum at Lycée Henri-IV, seeking admission to the École normale supérieure and pursuing advanced literary studies.

His philosophy teacher was **Henri Bergson**. Jarry took extensive notes on Bergson’s lectures. Bergsonian problems—duration, motion, perception, memory, and the limits of analytic thought—later intersected with Jarry’s treatments of time machines, bicycles, imaginary science, and the “ethics of living.”

Other formative currents included:

- François Rabelais;
- Shakespeare;
- Edgar Allan Poe;
- Stéphane Mallarmé;
- Arthur Rimbaud;
- Paul Verlaine;
- Lautréamont;
- Catholic mysticism and Apocalypse imagery;
- Greek and Latin literature;
- Symbolist theories of suggestion;
- popular puppetry and Guignol;
- science writing, mechanics, mathematics, and speculative technology;
- H. G. Wells, particularly *The Time Machine*;
- visual sources ranging from medieval and popular prints to Gauguin and the Nabis.

Jarry failed the École normale entrance examination and later failed attempts at the licence ès lettres. He did not obtain a university degree.

## Early Career and Entry into the Paris Literary World, 1892–1895

Jarry’s early Paris address at 78 boulevard de Port-Royal was mockingly named the “Calvaire du Trucidė.” He and friends, including Léon-Paul Fargue, staged versions of Ubu there.

On 28 April 1893, *L’Écho de Paris littéraire illustré*, edited by Catulle Mendès and Marcel Schwob, published Jarry’s “Guignol,” bringing Père Ubu into print. This led to a friendship with **Marcel Schwob**, later the dedicatee of *Ubu roi*.

After his mother’s death in May 1893, Jarry increasingly entered Symbolist circles:

- Rachilde’s Tuesday salon at the *Mercure de France*;
- Alfred Vallette’s publishing network;
- Stéphane Mallarmé’s Tuesday gatherings;
- friendships with Remy de Gourmont, Paul Fort, Marcel Schwob, Franc-Nohain, Léon-Paul Fargue, and others.

### Tailor dispute, 1894

On 5 January 1894 Jarry returned from Laval to Paris without answering a justice-of-the-peace summons concerning a coat made by the Laval tailor Quinchon. Jarry had refused delivery because he considered it badly cut. His father was ordered to pay 200 francs, including much of the coat’s price, plus 14 francs in bailiff’s costs.

**Evidentiary status: documented civil debt dispute.** It was not a criminal prosecution.

### Art criticism and Pont-Aven

From February through July 1894 Jarry wrote art criticism. He praised the then-ridiculed painter **Henri Rousseau**, called Le Douanier, and contributed to his early literary-artistic recognition.

In June 1894 Jarry visited Pont-Aven, where he encountered the milieu of **Paul Gauguin** and **Charles Filiger**. Filiger’s mystical, geometrical imagery particularly affected him.

In July *Mercure de France* published *Haldernablou*. In October the same house issued Jarry’s first book, *Les Minutes de sable mémorial*.

With **Remy de Gourmont**, Jarry founded and edited *L’Ymagier*, a luxurious journal devoted to old and new prints, religious imagery, popular iconography, and experimental graphic art. Five issues appeared under their joint direction. Jarry made woodcuts and contributed criticism and editorial work.

### Military service, 1894–1895

Jarry entered the Corbineau barracks at Laval on 13 November 1894. His experiences informed *Les Jours et les nuits, roman d’un déserteur*.

On 14 December 1895 he was permanently discharged for chronic biliary lithiasis and received a certificate of good conduct. This is inconsistent with later legends that he formally deserted: his novel concerns desertion, but his own release was administrative and medical.

His father died at Laval on 18 August 1895. The division of the estate brought Jarry more than 15,000 gold francs, a substantial sum that he spent rapidly on publishing, art, housing, theatrical work, equipment, hospitality, and personal consumption.

## Chronological Life History

## 1896: *Perhinderion*, the Théâtre de l’Œuvre, and *Ubu roi*

A quarrel with Remy de Gourmont ended their collaboration. The causes involved editorial authority, images, ownership, and temperament; surviving accounts do not justify reducing the break to a single incident.

Jarry founded *Perhinderion*, another high-quality print review. Only two issues appeared, illustrating both his graphic ambitions and his inability to sustain expensive publishing ventures.

From June he served as secretary to **Aurélien Lugné-Poe** at the Théâtre de l’Œuvre. He proposed *Les Polyèdres*, persuaded the theatre to mount the transformed Ubu play, helped plan its radically simplified staging, and promoted depersonalized acting, placards, masks, symbolic scenery, and a visible rejection of naturalism.

*Ubu roi* appeared in Paul Fort’s *Le Livre d’art* in April–May and as a Mercure de France volume on 11 June.

On 30 November Jarry bought a Clément Luxe 96 racing bicycle from Jules Trochon of Laval for 525 francs. He never completed payment.

The dress rehearsal on 9 December and public premiere on 10 December 1896 became famous. Firmin Gémier played Ubu. Jarry delivered an introductory speech. The opening “Merdre!”—a distorted form of *merde*—provoked shouting, laughter, objections, and counter-demonstrations. Accounts often describe a riot, but the event was a turbulent theatrical confrontation rather than a large-scale public riot.

Only a small number of performances followed. The production was a short-run commercial failure but a lasting notoriety success. Mallarmé, W. B. Yeats, and other writers recognized its significance, while hostile critics treated it as childish obscenity or proof of Symbolist degeneracy.

### Theatrical significance

Jarry’s published theory, particularly “De l’inutilité du théâtre au théâtre,” rejected illusionistic scenery and psychologically natural acting. He favored conventional signs, masks, puppetry, abstract space, and a theatre whose artificiality was explicit.

---

## 1897: Debt, violence, fiction, and reduced circumstances

In January *La Revue blanche* published “Questions de théâtre,” Jarry’s answer to critics of *Ubu roi*.

His inherited money was disappearing. He left the costly 162 boulevard Saint-Germain apartment and returned to boulevard de Port-Royal.

On 2 March, at a literary dinner and while intoxicated, Jarry fired a revolver at or near the young Belgian poet **Christian Beck**. Accounts differ over whether the shots were intended to hit Beck, intimidate him, or formed part of drunken theatricality. No known homicide or serious injury resulted.

**Evidentiary status: reported incident with multiple contemporary witnesses; intent and exact trajectory disputed.**

*Les Jours et les nuits, roman d’un déserteur* appeared on 18 May. Its antimilitary material combines autobiographical barracks experience, hallucination, eroticism, and formal experimentation.

On 9 July Jarry bought a mahogany skiff or *périssoire*, called *L’As*, for fishing and river journeys.

Evicted from boulevard de Port-Royal, he was temporarily housed by Henri Rousseau at 14 avenue du Maine. In November he rented a tiny, very low-ceilinged room at 7 rue Cassette. Because the building’s floor arrangement placed it between conventional levels, he called it his “deuxième et demi” and “Grande Chasublerie.” It remained his principal Paris address.

---

## 1898: The Phalanstère and *Faustroll*

On 20 January, *Ubu roi* was performed with puppets designed by **Pierre Bonnard** at the Théâtre des Pantins.

In spring Jarry joined friends from the *Mercure de France* in renting a villa at 19 quai de l’Apport, Corbeil. Known as the **Phalanstère**, it was associated with Rachilde, Alfred Vallette, Franc-Nohain, and other members of their circle. There Jarry wrote much of *Gestes et opinions du docteur Faustroll, pataphysicien*.

On 19 May he met **Oscar Wilde**, then living in France after imprisonment. The fact of acquaintance is accepted in specialist chronology; expansive claims about intimacy are not securely documented.

*L’Amour en visites* appeared in June through Pierre Fort. Jarry attended Mallarmé’s funeral at Valvins on 11 September.

By December he had completed the operetta *Par la taille*, not published until 1906.

The first *Almanach du Père Ubu*, illustrated by Pierre Bonnard, appeared late in the year.

---

## 1899–1900: River settlements, *L’Amour absolu*, and *Ubu enchaîné*

The Corbeil Phalanstère dissolved in January 1899 when the owner reclaimed the property. In May Jarry and associates rented another villa at La Frette; that arrangement ended in November.

Jarry wrote *Ubu enchaîné*, reversing the first play’s dictatorship through a world in which Ubu insists upon enslavement and thereby masters those around him.

In May he produced *L’Amour absolu* at his own expense as a facsimile of his handwriting. Its extremely small circulation compounded later bibliographical confusion.

In 1900 the Vallettes rented a villa at Les Bas-Vignons beside the Seine. Jarry occupied a hut near the Coudray barrage, close enough to visit them while maintaining an independent river existence.

He collaborated with **Eugène Demolder** and composer **Claude Terrasse** on operettas and theatrical projects. These included *Pantagruel*, *Le Moutardier du pape*, *Le Manoir enchanté*, and other completed or unfinished works whose authorship was sometimes divided among librettists and composer.

The Revue blanche published *Ubu enchaîné*, preceded by *Ubu roi*, in one volume.

On 5 June a bailiff demanded 553.75 francs still owed to the cycle dealer Jules Trochon. The notice went to an obsolete address. The famous bicycle thus became both a genuine financial default and a literary relic.

From July to September *Messaline* appeared serially in *La Revue blanche*. A second illustrated Ubu almanac, with Bonnard’s drawings and music by Terrasse, appeared near the end of 1900.

---

## 1901–1902: Journalism, *Messaline*, *Ubu sur la Butte*, and *Le Surmâle*

Jarry wrote “Spéculations” and later “Gestes” for *La Revue blanche*. These short pieces recast current events, science, sport, technology, colonial politics, accidents, and newspaper curiosities through pataphysical logic. They were later collected posthumously as *La Chandelle verte* and *Spéculations*.

The book version of *Messaline, roman de l’ancienne Rome* appeared in 1901. Its historical research is transformed through decadent aesthetics, sexuality, cruelty, and anachronistic psychological speculation.

Late in November 1901, a reduced two-act adaptation called *Ubu sur la Butte* was performed by marionettes at the Guignol des Gueules de Bois in the Cabaret des Quat’z-Arts.

On 22 March 1902 Jarry spoke about marionettes at the Libre Esthétique salon in Brussels, one of his best-documented journeys outside France.

In May *Le Surmâle, roman moderne* appeared. It brings together bicycle racing, sexual endurance, mechanization, athletic science, advertising, nutrition, and a contest between body and machine. The fictional five-man bicycle and “perpetual-motion food” belong to literature, not to patented inventions.

---

## 1903–1904: Periodical work, Apollinaire, and the unfinished operas

In 1903 Jarry contributed regularly to *La Plume* and to Franc-Nohain’s weekly *Le Canard sauvage*. The latter closed in October.

“La Bataille de Morsang,” later incorporated into *La Dragonne*, appeared in *La Revue blanche* on 1 April.

Jarry met and befriended **Guillaume Apollinaire**, probably through the *La Plume* evenings at the Caveau du Soleil d’Or. Apollinaire published part of Jarry’s *L’Objet aimé* in the second issue of *Le Festin d’Ésope* in December 1903. Apollinaire later became one of the principal transmitters of Jarry’s reputation and anecdotes.

In November Jarry went to stay with Claude Terrasse at Le Grand-Lemps near Grenoble to work on *Pantagruel*, a project begun as early as 1897.

*La Plume* ceased regular publication in January 1904, removing an important source of income. Jarry returned to Paris in May. **Félix Fénéon** introduced him at *Le Figaro*, but only two contributions appeared. He went back to Le Grand-Lemps in November in another effort to complete *Pantagruel*.

---

## 1905: The Tripode, translation, and another revolver incident

At an April dinner in Maurice Raynal’s apartment on rue de Rennes, Jarry, intoxicated, fired blank cartridges at the Spanish sculptor **Manolo Hugué**. Apollinaire reportedly disarmed him.

**Evidentiary status: reported fact.** The dinner and weapon episode are supported by recollections; the exact guest list, the presence of Picasso, and subsequent history of the weapon vary by source.

Late in April Jarry and Claude Terrasse signed an agreement concerning rights in *Pantagruel*. The project remained incomplete at Jarry’s death and was staged and published posthumously.

During the summer Jarry purchased a small parcel at Les Bas-Vignons. A local wheelwright named Dubois built a simple plank shelter on a cement base, poorly or incompletely paid. Jarry named it the **Tripode**. This was his most tangible property project, though little more than a cabin.

In October Jarry and the Greek-born physician **Jean Saltas** began translating Emmanuel Rhoïdes’s *La Papesse Jeanne*. Jarry’s command of Greek was probably supplemented substantially by Saltas; its presentation as a simple solo translation would be misleading.

---

## 1906: Destitution, cerebral crisis, testament, and religious return

By 1906 Jarry was in extreme poverty and poor health. Friends bought railway tickets, provided meals, arranged subscriptions, advanced money, and supplied medical assistance.

The first chapter of *La Dragonne* appeared in *Vers et prose* in April.

On 11 May Alfred Vallette and friends at the *Mercure* paid for Jarry’s train journey to Laval, where Charlotte cared for him at 13 rue Charles-Landelle. A grave cerebral illness followed. Symptoms were described as “brain fever” or a cerebral crisis; later biographers have proposed interactions among tuberculosis, malnutrition, alcoholism, and neurological infection. Retrospective precision is impossible.

Believing himself near death, Jarry:

- dictated testamentary instructions;
- supplied a plan for *La Dragonne*;
- created an anticipatory death announcement;
- wrote intensely to Rachilde;
- received Catholic sacraments;
- was attended by Dr Henri Bucquet.

Charlotte reported that his piety moved the priest. Jarry’s own subsequent language remained idiosyncratic: he imagined decomposition, postmortem cerebral activity, dreams as paradise, Christ, Satan, Ubu, and pataphysical reversal within the same conceptual field.

He recovered enough to return to Paris in July.

Sansot published *Ubu sur la Butte* and then *Par la taille* as the two volumes of Jarry’s proposed “Théâtre Mirlitonesque.”

---

## 1907: Final year, creditors, hospitalization, and death

Jarry’s health and finances continued deteriorating. Vallette and other friends financed an edition of *Le Moutardier du pape* by subscription.

Victor Lemasle published *Albert Samain: souvenirs* in June. Jarry himself called it hurried or botched.

He spent much of the year with Charlotte in Laval. Nonpayment of rent forced them to leave rue Charles-Landelle on 5 May and return to the family property at 13–15 rue de Bootz. Pharmacist **Émile Labbé** supplied medicines.

Because creditors were pursuing him, Vallette advised him not to return to Paris. Jarry nevertheless left for Paris on 7 October and resumed residence at 7 rue Cassette.

On 29 October, after he had not been seen for several days, Vallette and Saltas went to the room. Jarry was too weak to open the door; a locksmith was called. He was taken to Hôpital de la Charité.

He died at 4:15 p.m. on 1 November 1907. The cause recorded in specialist chronology is tuberculous meningitis.

The frequently repeated story that his last request was for a toothpick comes through Saltas or later recollection. A toothpick was reportedly brought to him, but whether these were literally his final words cannot be established.

## Residences, Travels, and Geographical Movements

### Principal residences

- **1873–1879:** several addresses in Laval, principally around quai de la Mayenne and rue de Bootz.
- **1879–1888:** Saint-Brieuc, including 12 boulevard Charner.
- **1888–1891:** Rennes.
- **1891 onward:** Paris, initially boulevard de Port-Royal.
- **1894–1897:** 162 boulevard Saint-Germain for part of the period, alternating with boulevard de Port-Royal.
- **1897:** temporary lodging with Henri Rousseau, 14 avenue du Maine.
- **November 1897–1907:** 7 rue Cassette, the “deuxième et demi,” though he spent long periods elsewhere.
- **1898:** 19 quai de l’Apport, Corbeil, the Phalanstère.
- **1899:** La Frette.
- **From 1900:** hut by the Coudray barrage.
- **From 1905:** Tripode at Les Bas-Vignons.
- **1906–1907:** extended stays with Charlotte at rue Charles-Landelle and rue de Bootz, Laval.

### Significant travel

- Rennes and Laval family journeys throughout adolescence.
- Pont-Aven, June 1894.
- Valvins for Mallarmé’s funeral, September 1898.
- River travel along the Seine by bicycle and skiff.
- Brussels, March 1902.
- Le Grand-Lemps near Grenoble, 1903–1904.
- Repeated Paris–Laval journeys, especially during his terminal illness.

No exile or imprisonment is documented. The apparent desertion associated with his novel is fictionalized from military service; he was legally discharged.

## Employment, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Institutions and employers

- Lycées of Laval, Saint-Brieuc, Rennes, and Henri-IV.
- *L’Écho de Paris littéraire illustré*.
- *Mercure de France* as publisher and social-professional center.
- Co-editor of *L’Ymagier*.
- Founder-editor of *Perhinderion*.
- Secretary at Lugné-Poe’s Théâtre de l’Œuvre.
- Contributor to *La Revue blanche*, *La Plume*, *Le Canard sauvage*, *Le Figaro*, *Vers et prose*, *Le Festin d’Ésope*, and other periodicals.
- Collaborator with Claude Terrasse, Eugène Demolder, Franc-Nohain, Jean Saltas, Pierre Bonnard, and others.

### Publishing enterprises

**L’Ymagier** was an editorial collaboration with Remy de Gourmont, combining reproductions of popular and religious prints with contemporary work. It was culturally important but expensive and short-lived.

**Perhinderion** was Jarry’s independent successor project. Only two issues appeared. Financial impracticality and limited distribution were central failures.

### Technologies and inventions

Jarry obtained no known patents and founded no durable manufacturing business. His engagement with technology was literary, theoretical, and performative:

- bicycle mechanics;
- boats and river movement;
- chronophotography and speed;
- time machines;
- perpetual motion;
- exercise physiology;
- electrical and mechanical sex machines;
- scientific journalism;
- imagined geometries and dimensions.

“Commentaire pour servir à la construction pratique de la machine à explorer le temps” treats Wells’s time machine through deliberately elaborate quasi-scientific reasoning. It is a speculative literary text, not evidence that Jarry constructed a working device.

### Finances and property

Jarry’s principal capital came from inheritance after his parents’ deaths. More than 15,000 gold francs reportedly came from his father’s estate. It was expended quickly.

Recurring financial relationships included:

- advances and payments from Alfred Vallette and publishers;
- support from Rachilde and the *Mercure* circle;
- unpaid debt to Jules Trochon for the Clément bicycle;
- unpaid or underpaid rent;
- unpaid work on the Tripode;
- creditor pursuit;
- benefit or subscription editions organized by friends;
- ownership of a small Les Bas-Vignons plot;
- the family house in rue de Bootz, shared with Charlotte.

No evidence was found of complex corporate structures, investments, securities, secret wealth, money laundering, or fraud. His financial history is one of inheritance depletion, small-scale publishing risk, debt, irregular income, and dependence upon friends.

## Artistic, Intellectual, Religious, and Political Development

## Symbolism

Jarry’s early books use dense Symbolist methods: hermetic association, archaic and specialized vocabulary, typography, emblematic images, synesthesia, and the replacement of ordinary narrative by networks of signs.

He nevertheless destabilized Symbolism from within. *Ubu roi* converted high Symbolist theatrical abstraction into schoolboy insult, puppet mechanics, scatology, and political brutality.

## ’Pataphysics

In *Gestes et opinions du docteur Faustroll*, ’pataphysics is called the science of imaginary solutions and a science of exceptions. It studies laws governing exceptions and describes a universe supplementary to the conventional one.

The word’s origin is not exclusively Jarry’s:

- related forms occurred in Rennes school culture;
- Henri Morin’s *La Chasse aux polyèdres* reportedly contains an early use;
- Jarry gave the term its mature literary, philosophical, and institutional afterlife.

The apostrophe in **’pataphysique** was intended to prevent an obvious pun or to mark the term’s exceptional status; practice varies.

Pataphysics is not simply “absurdism.” It treats every explanatory system as a constructed solution, elevates exceptions over generalized laws, and exploits the equal availability of scientific, metaphysical, theological, linguistic, and comic models.

## Individualism, anarchism, and antimilitarism

Jarry moved in circles containing anarchists and radical individualists. His early writing attacks bourgeois propriety, military discipline, legal bureaucracy, colonial domination, and fixed authority. *Les Jours et les nuits* is explicitly antimilitary.

No evidence establishes sustained membership in an anarchist organization, participation in bombings, intelligence activity, or organized political violence. His anarchism was principally aesthetic, behavioral, and individualist.

## Dreyfus Affair and antisemitism

Jarry’s relation to the Dreyfus Affair is complicated.

Earlier works and the Ubu almanacs frequently parody the political press without consistently identifying with either camp. Scholarship on “Celui qui Dreyfuse” finds satire of the Affair but little evidence that Jarry engaged in organized Dreyfusard or anti-Dreyfusard activism.

Late *Dragonne* drafts contain severe antisemitic imagery. A Jewish officer—at stages given names recalling Dreyfus—is represented as a seducer, and one projected episode subjects him and his family to grotesque collective destruction associated with inherited guilt for Christ’s death.

Henry de Bruchard later claimed that Jarry said he had returned to the Church, wanted to write antisemitic campaigns for *La Libre Parole*, and wished to “perforate” a Jew.

Evidence should be separated:

- **Documented fact:** antisemitic names, imagery, and collective-guilt motifs exist in surviving *Dragonne* material.
- **Reported fact:** Bruchard attributed explicit antisemitic statements to Jarry.
- **Qualification:** Bruchard was himself intensely antisemitic and published the recollection after Jarry’s death.
- **Unresolved question:** whether Jarry was speaking literally, mimicking Bruchard, performing an outrageous persona, or expressing a sustained late political commitment.
- **Strongest supportable conclusion:** late Jarry adopted or experimented with Catholic-traditionalist and antisemitic positions, but the duration and practical seriousness of the change cannot be fixed.

## Catholic return

Jarry was baptized and never entirely abandoned Catholic imagery. During the 1906 crisis he received sacraments and explicitly described himself as Catholic. Rachilde also treated his belief as real enough to mock in correspondence.

His Catholicism remained heterodox. In late notes, doctrinal terms coexist with Ubu, Faustroll, postmortem brain activity, and the redemption of Satan. Calling it either a mere joke or a conventional orthodox conversion exceeds the evidence.

## Gender and sexuality

Jarry’s works explore male-male desire, bisexuality, misogyny, erotic domination, impotence, mechanical sexuality, and unstable gender symbolism. Rachilde preserved remarks by him depicting women as soulless or treating female honor as negligible.

- **Documented literary fact:** misogynistic statements and constructions occur in the works and reported conversation.
- **Contextual fact:** several of his closest and most durable supporters were women, especially Rachilde and Charlotte.
- **Interpretation:** personal dependence on women did not erase misogyny; neither does textual misogyny alone establish every private attitude.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Negative Material

## Collective authorship of Ubu

The best evidence rejects a simple sole-authorship legend. Rennes pupils created the Hébert caricature and *Les Polonais* before Jarry’s mature text. Jarry’s contribution was nevertheless decisive: adaptation, rewriting, publication, staging, theory, sequels, images, and lifelong development.

**Label:** documented collaborative origin; exact division of words and scenes unresolved.

## Revolver incidents

At least two serious episodes are reported:

1. Christian Beck, 2 March 1897.
2. Manolo Hugué, April 1905, reportedly with blank cartridges.

Jarry also habitually carried and displayed firearms. Stories of his firing at mirrors, birds, neighbors, dinner guests, or street targets multiplied after his death.

**Label:** firearm possession and two confrontations are well supported; much of the larger shooting cycle is anecdotal.

## Debt and nonpayment

Jarry did not pay the full price of his Clément bicycle; the debt and bailiff’s demand are documented. He also accumulated rent and artisan debts.

**Label:** documented civil indebtedness, not fraud unless deceptive intent could be proved; no such proof was found.

## Alcohol and drugs

Absinthe and other alcohol were central to his public image and late decline. Friends repeatedly described intoxication. Some sources also use the vague term “drugs,” but evidence for a defined non-alcohol drug addiction is weak.

**Label:** chronic heavy drinking is documented by testimony; exact daily quantities and many spectacular drinking stories are legendary.

## Violence, illegality, and criminal record

The firearm episodes could have produced criminal liability, but no substantial prosecution or conviction was located. His military “desertion” was fictional; he was medically discharged. No evidence was found of imprisonment, organized crime, corruption, fraud schemes, intelligence connections, terrorism, or money laundering.

## Misogyny

Misogynistic remarks are attributed to Jarry by Rachilde and appear in his literary constructions.

**Label:** documented textual pattern and reported speech; incompatible with any sanitized presentation of him, though it should not be expanded into undocumented conduct.

## Antisemitism

As detailed above, late manuscript evidence is explicit and serious. The Bruchard recollection supplies additional but interested testimony.

**Label:** documented in late projected fiction; reported in speech; extent of settled political commitment disputed.

## Colonial and racial representation

The almanacs parody colonial administration and imperial pretensions, sometimes in ways scholars read as anti-colonial. They also use period racial caricature and “primitive” stereotypes.

**Label:** textual fact; political function contested. Both subversion of colonial ideology and participation in fin-de-siècle stereotype can operate in the same material.

## Appropriation versus transformation

A recurring accusation is that Jarry appropriated classmates’ Ubu material and received sole fame. The documentary answer is mixed: he did appropriate collective school material without modern co-authorship credits, but he also transformed it extensively and never entirely concealed the school origin from intimates.

## Adversarial search findings, including 2024–2026 material

A dedicated search combined Jarry’s name with scandal, controversy, allegation, fraud, lawsuit, accusation, conspiracy, crime, antisemitism, misogyny, colonialism, and criticism.

The major substantiated issues remain:

- collective authorship of Ubu;
- civilian debt and nonpayment;
- firearm intimidation while intoxicated;
- heavy alcohol use;
- misogynistic speech and writing;
- late antisemitic material;
- uncertainty around sexual biography;
- self-mythologizing and unreliable anecdotes.

Recent coverage connected with the 2025–2026 Museu Picasso exhibition revisits the uncertain Picasso meeting and revolver-relic story. It does not supply evidence of newly discovered crimes, litigation, fraud, or political conspiracies. No credible recent investigation, indictment, or court finding concerning Jarry was located.

## Illness, Addiction, Psychological Crises, and Cause of Death

Documented or strongly reported medical episodes include:

- chronic biliary lithiasis, the basis of his permanent military discharge;
- sustained heavy alcohol use;
- malnutrition and extreme poverty;
- a grave cerebral crisis in Laval in May 1906;
- tuberculosis culminating in tuberculous meningitis;
- progressive weakness in 1907.

Family history has led some biographers to speculate about hereditary mental illness. Jarry’s behavior also prompted retrospective psychiatric labels. Such diagnoses cannot be reliably imposed from eccentric conduct, literary experimentation, alcoholism, and partisan testimony.

The 1906 crisis included an expectation of imminent death and intense religious-metaphysical statements. Whether delirium, infection, withdrawal, psychological crisis, conversion, or several factors were present cannot now be separated conclusively.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

## “Jarry became Père Ubu”

**Story:** Jarry ceased behaving as an ordinary individual, spoke in Ubu’s royal plural, called his bicycle his “external skeleton,” used “phynance” for money, and converted his life into a continuous Ubu performance.

**Origin and circulation:** friends such as Rachilde, Apollinaire, Saltas, and later pataphysical writers; amplified by early biographies.

**Evidence:** he did adopt Ubu vocabulary and theatrical self-presentation. Total psychological identification is interpretive.

**Reputational effect:** made Jarry’s life easier to remember than most of his non-Ubu writing, while sometimes reducing a complex author to an eccentric drunk.

## The bicycle as “external skeleton”

**Story:** the Clément Luxe 96 was inseparable from Jarry and functioned as an extension of his body.

**Evidence:** the purchase and unpaid debt are documented; his devotion to cycling is abundantly reported. The exact phrase is associated with Jarry but survives through literary recollection.

**Effect:** transformed a creditor’s object into a central emblem of avant-garde modernity, speed, and body-machine fusion.

## The revolver passed to Picasso

**Story:** Apollinaire took Jarry’s pistol and gave it to Picasso, who carried it as a talisman and sometimes used it to frighten admirers of conventional art.

**Evidence:** Apollinaire reportedly disarmed Jarry; Picasso’s possession of a Jarry-associated weapon is widely reported. The transfer chain, identity of the weapon, and individual episodes remain uncertain.

**Status:** reported tradition with legendary embellishment.

**Effect:** linked the Ubu legend to Picasso’s avant-garde persona.

## Did Jarry meet Picasso?

Some accounts place both at Maurice Raynal’s 1905 dinner and in the same Montmartre network. Specialist research notes that no conclusive direct testimony from either man confirms a meeting and that arguments against certainty remain substantial.

**Status:** disputed claim. Mutual awareness and Picasso’s fascination with Jarry are documented; personal meeting is possible but unproved.

## Superhuman drinking and physical strength

Stories portray Jarry consuming impossible quantities of absinthe, cycling immense distances, overpowering larger men, or remaining lucid after extraordinary intoxication.

**Evidence:** heavy drinking, athletic cycling, fishing, and physical eccentricity are real. Precise feats generally rest on anecdote.

**Status:** legend built around documented habits.

## The last toothpick

**Story:** Jarry’s last request was for a toothpick.

**Evidence:** Saltas reportedly brought one at the hospital. It is unclear whether the request was literally his final utterance.

**Status:** reported deathbed anecdote, conventionally but not conclusively described as last words.

## Death from absinthe or starvation

**Story:** Jarry drank himself to death, died of absinthe, or died solely from starvation.

**Evidence:** the recorded terminal disease was tuberculous meningitis. Alcoholism, malnutrition, poverty, and neglect probably reduced his resistance.

**Status:** oversimplified legend resting on contributing conditions rather than the recorded cause.

## Aristocratic Breton descent

**Story:** Jarry descended from ancient Breton or Norman nobility, sometimes through companions of William the Conqueror.

**Evidence:** genuine Breton maternal connections; no adequate evidence for the grander pedigree.

**Status:** self-created mythology.

## Inventor of Ubu and ’pataphysics

**Story:** Jarry alone invented both Ubu and the word ’pataphysics.

**Evidence:** both had collective Rennes antecedents. Jarry decisively transformed, codified, published, and internationalized them.

**Status:** simplified authorship myth.

## Ubu as a prophecy of twentieth-century dictators

Ubu has been retrospectively read as anticipating Hitler, Stalin, Franco, totalitarianism, bureaucratic kleptocracy, and postcolonial dictatorships.

**Evidence:** the character is a murderous, cowardly usurper who taxes, confiscates, and destroys. These later applications are historically powerful but were not specific predictions.

**Status:** posthumous interpretive mythology.

## Death, Funeral, and Disposition of the Estate

Jarry died without spouse or children. Charlotte was his closest surviving family custodian. His estate contained manuscripts and literary rights but was complicated by debts, unfinished contracts, dispersed papers, and objects already held by friends.

The funeral occurred on 3 November 1907 after a short Catholic service at Saint-Sulpice. He was buried at Bagneux. Later memorial culture sometimes treats the grave as lost or emptied because the concession was temporary and the remains were apparently transferred to an ossuary; publicly accessible evidence does not provide a secure present individual grave location.

Several major works were unpublished or incomplete:

- *Faustroll* appeared in 1911;
- *Pantagruel* was completed and produced posthumously;
- *La Papesse Jeanne* appeared in 1908;
- *La Dragonne* was reconstructed and published in 1943;
- journalism became *La Chandelle verte*;
- many plays and fragments entered collected editions only decades later.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Immediate preservation

Rachilde, Vallette, Saltas, Apollinaire, Charlotte, and other associates preserved manuscripts and anecdotes. This rescue was inseparable from myth-making: witnesses turned Jarry into an exemplary bohemian, saint of absurdity, armed humorist, or self-created Ubu.

### Surrealism

André Breton lectured on Jarry in March 1918 and later included him in the *Anthologie de l’humour noir* (1940). Surrealists treated him as a predecessor in black humor, objective chance, linguistic revolt, and refusal of ordinary reality.

### Dada and Futurism

Jarry’s attack on theatrical convention, typography, nonsense, simultaneity, speed, and mechanical bodies provided models that later commentators connected with Dada and Futurism. His direct correspondence with Marinetti strengthens the Futurist connection, although Jarry died before the 1909 Futurist manifesto.

### Antonin Artaud

Artaud and Roger Vitrac founded the Théâtre Alfred Jarry in 1926–1927, using his name to designate an anti-naturalist and confrontational theatre. This was a major institutional stage in his canonization.

### Theatre of the Absurd

Ionesco, Beckett, and later critics recognized *Ubu roi* and *Faustroll* as antecedents of dramatic worlds governed by circular language, empty authority, repetitive violence, and failed logic. “Precursor” should not be mistaken for membership in a movement established decades after his death.

### Collège de ’Pataphysique

The Collège de ’Pataphysique was created in 1948, not by Jarry. It installed the fictional Dr Faustroll as its “Inamovible Curator,” created offices, orders, a calendar, and an extensive publication system.

Members and associates have included writers, artists, scientists, and filmmakers such as Boris Vian, Eugène Ionesco, Raymond Queneau, Marcel Duchamp, Max Ernst, Joan Miró, and others. The Collège helped recover Jarry’s manuscripts, publish neglected works, and stimulate Oulipo and related “potential” workshops.

### Language

“Ubuesque” entered French and other languages as a description of grotesque, absurd, cruel, incompetent, or extravagantly bureaucratic power. Common usage often strips the word of its precise literary origin.

### Visual art

Ubu became a durable modern-art figure:

- Pierre Bonnard illustrated and designed early Ubu works;
- Picasso collected Jarry material and repeatedly used Ubu-like forms;
- Max Ernst painted *Ubu Imperator*;
- Joan Miró developed *Mori el Merma*;
- later artists including Roberto Matta, Enrico Baj, William Kentridge, and others reworked Ubu imagery.

### Experimental literature and science culture

*Faustroll* influenced Raymond Roussel’s reception, Queneau, Boris Vian, Oulipo, conceptual writing, artist books, speculative design, and humor about mathematical or scientific systems.

Jarry’s contemporary standing is double:

- *Ubu roi* remains the public emblem;
- specialists increasingly emphasize the novels, journalism, visual art, sexuality, politics, translations, religious development, and sophisticated scientific-literary methods that the Ubu legend once obscured.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are included. Mere contemporaneity has been excluded.

### Henri-Louis Bergson

- **Nature:** teacher–student relationship.
- **Date and place:** approximately 1891–1893, Lycée Henri-IV, Paris.
- **Evidence:** Jarry attended Bergson’s philosophy course and took extensive notes. Problems of time, motion, and perception later intersect with his writing.
- **Label:** **documented fact**.

### Oscar Fingal O’Flahertie Wills Wilde

- **Nature:** personal acquaintance.
- **Date and place:** 19 May 1898, Paris.
- **Evidence:** Jarry’s specialist chronology records the meeting. Claims of a deeper sexual or sustained relationship are not established.
- **Label:** **documented or strongly reported fact** for the meeting; **unverified claim** for sexual intimacy.

### Filippo Tommaso Emilio Marinetti

- **Nature:** correspondence and literary contact.
- **Date:** late in Jarry’s life, before 1907.
- **Evidence:** surviving correspondence includes a letter in which Jarry discussed or presented his sister Charlotte to Marinetti; critical scholarship places Jarry among Marinetti’s proto-Futurist precursors.
- **Label:** **documented fact** for correspondence; **scholarly interpretation** for influence on Futurism.

### Pablo Ruiz Picasso

- **Nature:** powerful posthumous artistic influence; possible personal meeting.
- **Date and place:** possible meeting around 1905 in the Paris/Montmartre circle, including Maurice Raynal’s dinner.
- **Evidence:** Picasso knew Jarry’s work, collected Jarry material, used Ubu imagery, and was traditionally said to possess Jarry’s revolver. Specialist research has not found conclusive proof that the men met.
- **Label:** **documented fact** for artistic reception; **disputed claim** for a personal meeting; **reported tradition** for the revolver’s transfer.

### André Breton

- **Nature:** posthumous advocate and canonizer.
- **Dates:** conference on Jarry in March 1918; inclusion in *Anthologie de l’humour noir*, 1940.
- **Evidence:** documented publications and reception history.
- **Label:** **documented fact**.

### Antonin Artaud

- **Nature:** posthumous institutional and theatrical influence.
- **Dates and place:** Théâtre Alfred Jarry, Paris, founded in 1926–1927 with Roger Vitrac.
- **Evidence:** the theatre deliberately adopted Jarry’s name and anti-naturalist legacy.
- **Label:** **documented fact**.

### William Butler Yeats

- **Nature:** witness to and commentator on the *Ubu roi* premiere.
- **Date and place:** Théâtre de l’Œuvre, Paris, December 1896.
- **Evidence:** Yeats’s famous retrospective diary account treated the production as a sign of cultural rupture. His language helped build the “savage god” reception myth.
- **Label:** **documented fact** for attendance/recollection; **interpretation** for its prophetic significance.

### Edgar Allan Poe

- **Nature:** acknowledged literary influence and one of the “equivalent books” or *livres pairs* in the conceptual library of Dr Faustroll.
- **Evidence:** textual and bibliographical.
- **Label:** **documented intellectual influence**, not personal contact.

### Friedrich Nietzsche

- **Nature:** intellectual influence mediated partly by Jarry’s friendship with Henri Albert, an early French translator of Nietzsche.
- **Evidence:** modern scholarship identifies Nietzschean problems of force, repetition, morality, and the superman in Jarry, especially around *Le Surmâle*.
- **Label:** **documented reading context; scholarly interpretation** regarding the degree of influence.

### Herbert George Wells

- **Nature:** direct literary and technological influence.
- **Evidence:** Jarry’s “Commentaire pour servir à la construction pratique de la machine à explorer le temps” explicitly responds to the machine in Wells’s *The Time Machine*.
- **Label:** **documented intellectual influence**.

### Gustave Flaubert

- **Nature:** literary influence and presence in Jarry’s reading culture.
- **Evidence:** Flaubert’s work belongs to the literary field against which Jarry developed encyclopedic parody, received ideas, and stylistic impersonality; specialist studies discuss the relationship.
- **Label:** **reported/documented reading influence**, not personal contact.

### William Shakespeare

- **Nature:** primary dramatic source.
- **Evidence:** *Ubu roi* parodies and compresses structures from *Macbeth*, *Hamlet*, and royal tragedy.
- **Label:** **documented textual influence**.

### Miguel de Cervantes Saavedra

- **Nature:** literary precedent relevant to comic pseudo-history and the unstable relation between book and reality.
- **Evidence:** present in critical accounts of Jarry’s reading, but no personal or direct documentary connection is possible.
- **Label:** **reported literary influence**.

### François Rabelais

The atlas roster does not list Rabelais, although he is among Jarry’s most consequential influences and the basis of *Pantagruel*. He is noted here only to clarify that a major connection falls outside the supplied roster.

### Achille-Claude Debussy and Erik Satie

Jarry moved in artistic networks overlapping those of both composers, and later scholarship often groups him with Satie. Claude Terrasse—not Debussy or Satie—was his principal documented musical collaborator. No sufficiently specific direct meeting or collaboration with Debussy or Satie was established from the sources consulted.

- **Label:** **absence of a securely documented direct connection**.

### Marcel Proust

Both belonged to fin-de-siècle Paris literary society, but no adequately documented meeting, correspondence, or collaboration was found in the consulted sources.

- **Label:** **absence of a supported connection**.

### Remaining atlas roster

No reliable direct connection was identified with the great majority of the 896 names. Shared nationality, artistic movement, later comparison, or presence in Paris was not counted without evidence of meeting, correspondence, collaboration, explicit influence, or institutional succession.

## Compact Chronology

- **1863, 16 July:** Anselme Jarry and Caroline-Marie Quernest marry at Hédé.
- **1865, 8 February:** Caroline-Marie “Charlotte” Jarry is born.
- **1870, 31 August:** Gustave Anselme Jarry is born; dies approximately fifteen days later.
- **1873, 8 September:** Alfred Henri Jarry is born at Laval and receives emergency baptism.
- **1874, 8 June:** formally baptized in Laval cathedral.
- **1878, 14 May:** enters the Petit Lycée de Laval.
- **1879:** Anselme’s business fails; Caroline takes Charlotte and Alfred to Saint-Brieuc.
- **1879–1888:** studies at Lycée de Saint-Brieuc.
- **Circa 1885:** begins composing surviving plays and poems.
- **1888, October:** family moves to Rennes; Jarry enters Lycée de Rennes.
- **1888–1890:** adapts the schoolboy Hébert cycle and *Les Polonais*; marionette performances establish the Ubu material.
- **1890:** passes the baccalauréat with *bien*.
- **1891, October:** moves to Paris and enters Lycée Henri-IV; studies under Bergson.
- **1893, 28 April:** “Guignol” appears in *L’Écho de Paris*.
- **1893, 10 May:** Caroline Jarry dies.
- **1894, January:** coat dispute with tailor Quinchon.
- **1894, March:** after academic failure, intensifies participation in Symbolist salons.
- **1894, June:** visits Pont-Aven.
- **1894, July:** publishes *Haldernablou*.
- **1894, October:** publishes *Les Minutes de sable mémorial*; begins *L’Ymagier* with Gourmont.
- **1894, 13 November:** begins military service at Laval.
- **1895, 18 August:** Anselme Jarry dies.
- **1895, 14 December:** medically discharged for chronic biliary lithiasis.
- **1896, March:** breaks with Gourmont and founds *Perhinderion*.
- **1896, June:** becomes secretary at Théâtre de l’Œuvre; book edition of *Ubu roi*.
- **1896, 30 November:** buys the Clément bicycle on credit.
- **1896, 10 December:** premiere of *Ubu roi*.
- **1897, 2 March:** reported revolver attack on Christian Beck.
- **1897, 18 May:** *Les Jours et les nuits* published.
- **1897, November:** rents 7 rue Cassette.
- **1898, 20 January:** Bonnard-puppet production of *Ubu roi*.
- **1898, spring:** joins Corbeil Phalanstère; writes much of *Faustroll*.
- **1898, 19 May:** meets Oscar Wilde.
- **1898, September:** attends Mallarmé’s funeral.
- **1899, May:** self-publishes *L’Amour absolu*.
- **1899:** writes *Ubu enchaîné*.
- **1900:** settles periodically near the Coudray barrage; *Ubu enchaîné* published.
- **1900, 5 June:** bailiff demands unpaid bicycle balance.
- **1900:** *Messaline* serialized; second Ubu almanac issued.
- **1901:** *Messaline* published in book form.
- **1901, November:** *Ubu sur la Butte* performed.
- **1902, 22 March:** Brussels lecture on marionettes.
- **1902, May:** *Le Surmâle* published.
- **1903:** regular work for *La Plume* and *Le Canard sauvage*; friendship with Apollinaire.
- **1903, November:** goes to Le Grand-Lemps to work with Terrasse.
- **1904:** brief *Figaro* connection through Fénéon.
- **1905, April:** reported blank-cartridge attack on Manolo Hugué; Apollinaire disarms Jarry.
- **1905, summer:** Tripode built at Les Bas-Vignons.
- **1905, October:** begins translating *La Papesse Jeanne* with Saltas.
- **1906, 11 May:** returns gravely ill to Laval.
- **1906, May–June:** cerebral crisis, last-will instructions, sacraments, and work on *La Dragonne*.
- **1906, July:** returns to Paris.
- **1906:** *Ubu sur la Butte* and *Par la taille* published.
- **1907, May:** Charlotte and Alfred leave rue Charles-Landelle for rue de Bootz because of unpaid rent.
- **1907, June:** *Albert Samain: souvenirs* published.
- **1907, 7 October:** returns to Paris despite creditors and poor health.
- **1907, 29 October:** found incapacitated at rue Cassette; taken to Hôpital de la Charité.
- **1907, 1 November:** dies at 4:15 p.m. of tuberculous meningitis.
- **1907, 3 November:** Catholic funeral and burial at Bagneux.
- **1908:** *La Papesse Jeanne* published posthumously.
- **1911:** *Faustroll* and *Pantagruel* published or produced posthumously.
- **1918:** André Breton lectures on Jarry.
- **1926–1927:** Artaud and Vitrac establish Théâtre Alfred Jarry.
- **1940:** Breton includes Jarry in *Anthologie de l’humour noir*.
- **1943:** reconstructed *La Dragonne* published.
- **1948:** Collège de ’Pataphysique founded.
- **1965:** Jean-Christophe Averty’s television *Ubu roi*.
- **1969:** *La Chandelle verte* issued as an edited posthumous collection.
- **1972–1988:** three-volume Pléiade *Œuvres complètes* published.
- **1979:** Société des Amis d’Alfred Jarry founded.
- **2012–2022:** six-volume Classiques Garnier *Œuvres complètes* published.
- **2025–2026:** Museu Picasso Barcelona presents *Ubú pintor: Alfred Jarry et les arts*, renewing research into Jarry, Picasso, and the visual arts.
