# Benjamin Franklin: Research Dossier

## Scope and Evidentiary Method

Benjamin Franklin’s surviving documentary record is exceptionally large: roughly 30,000 extant papers are projected for the scholarly edition, while the Library of Congress alone holds about 8,000 items. This dossier prioritizes Franklin’s writings, correspondence, public records, contemporary publications, archival descriptions, and modern scholarship based on those materials.

Evidentiary labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by primary records or strong archival documentation.
- **Reported fact:** supported by reputable secondary scholarship but not independently established here from a primary record.
- **Disputed claim:** competing interpretations exist.
- **Allegation or theory:** asserted without conclusive evidence.
- **Rumor or myth:** folkloric, propagandistic, sensationalized, or unsupported.
- **Unresolved question:** surviving evidence is insufficient.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Benjamin Franklin.
- **Common forms:** Ben Franklin; Dr. Franklin; Doctor Franklin; “the American Sage”; “the Sage of Philadelphia.”
- **Born:** January 17, 1706, New Style calendar—January 6, 1705, under the Old Style calendar then used in British America.
- **Birthplace:** Milk Street, Boston, Province of Massachusetts Bay.
- **Died:** April 17, 1790, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, United States, aged 84.
- **Burial:** Christ Church Burial Ground, Philadelphia, beside Deborah Franklin.
- **Nationality and political status:** British colonial subject until American independence; thereafter a citizen and diplomat of the United States.
- **Principal professions:** printer, publisher, bookseller, writer, postmaster, public administrator, scientist, inventor, political agent, legislator, revolutionary, diplomat, constitution-maker.
- **Religious identity:** raised in Boston Congregationalism; as an adult professed a providential, ethically oriented and anti-dogmatic Christianity often described as deistic, though his beliefs did not fit a single stable denominational category.
- **Masonic titles:** Freemason; grand master of the Provincial Grand Lodge of Pennsylvania in 1734; later member and presiding officer of the Parisian Lodge of the Nine Sisters.
- **Major offices:**
  - Clerk of the Pennsylvania Assembly, 1736–1751.
  - Postmaster of Philadelphia, 1737–1753.
  - Member of the Pennsylvania Assembly, 1751–1764.
  - Joint deputy postmaster general of British North America, 1753–1774.
  - Colonial agent in London, principally 1757–1762 and 1764–1775.
  - Delegate to the Second Continental Congress, 1775–1776.
  - First United States postmaster general, 1775–1776.
  - Commissioner and minister to France, 1776–1785.
  - Peace commissioner and signer of the Treaty of Paris, 1783.
  - President of Pennsylvania’s Supreme Executive Council, 1785–1788.
  - Delegate to the Constitutional Convention, 1787.
  - President of the Pennsylvania Society for Promoting the Abolition of Slavery, 1787–1790.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and ancestry

Franklin’s father, **Josiah Franklin** (1657–1745), was born in Ecton, Northamptonshire, England. He migrated to New England in 1683 and worked in Boston as a tallow chandler, soap boiler, and candle maker. Josiah first married Anne Child, then **Abiah Folger** (1667–1752), Benjamin’s mother.

Abiah was a daughter of **Peter Folger** and Mary Morrill Folger of Nantucket. Peter Folger was a surveyor, interpreter, schoolteacher, and polemical writer. Franklin later took pride in Folger’s dissenting religious and political independence.

Benjamin was Josiah’s fifteenth child and youngest son, and one of seventeen children in the combined household. Important siblings included:

- **James Franklin** (1697–1735), printer and founder of the *New-England Courant*, Benjamin’s master and later adversary.
- **John Franklin** (1690–1756), for whom Benjamin designed a flexible urinary catheter.
- **Jane Franklin Mecom** (1712–1794), his closest sibling and a lifelong correspondent.
- **Samuel, Hannah, Josiah Jr., Anne, Joseph, Joseph II, Ebenezer, Thomas, Lydia, Sarah, Peter, Mary, and Elizabeth**, among the children of Josiah’s two marriages.

### Marriage and household

Franklin met **Deborah Read** (c. 1708–1774) soon after reaching Philadelphia in 1723. While Franklin was in London, Deborah married potter John Rogers, who deserted her. Rogers’s legal status and possible prior marriage made a conventional remarriage risky. On September 1, 1730, Deborah and Franklin entered what is generally described as a common-law marriage.

Deborah helped operate the stationery, book, postal, and household sides of Franklin’s commercial establishment. During Franklin’s long absences she managed property, accounts, correspondence, construction, tenants, family obligations, and social contacts. Older biographies often portrayed her as merely provincial or intellectually inadequate; surviving correspondence and recent scholarship present her as a capable business and household manager.

Franklin spent about fifteen of the final seventeen years of Deborah’s life overseas. She feared ocean travel and refused to move to London. Their marriage therefore became largely epistolary. She suffered strokes and died in Philadelphia on December 19, 1774, before Franklin returned.

### Children

1. **William Franklin** (probably born c. 1730 or 1731; died 1813): Benjamin’s acknowledged illegitimate son.  
   - **Unresolved question:** William’s mother is not securely identified. Eighteenth-century political gossip described her variously as a servant, “low woman,” or impoverished woman. A later theory proposed Deborah herself, but most scholarship does not accept that as established.
   - Deborah raised William in the Franklin household.
   - William served in the colonial military, studied law in London, and became royal governor of New Jersey in 1763.
   - During the Revolution he remained a Loyalist, was arrested and imprisoned by American authorities, and later helped organize Loyalist activity from British-held New York.
   - Father and son became permanently estranged. Franklin’s will left William little beyond debt forgiveness and lands whose value had been impaired, adding that had Britain prevailed William would have had no property to leave him.

2. **Francis Folger Franklin** (October 1732–November 21, 1736): legitimate son of Benjamin and Deborah. He died of smallpox at age four. Franklin’s later recollection that he had delayed inoculation generated persistent guilt and misunderstanding; Franklin stated that the child died before an intended inoculation could occur and urged other parents to inoculate.

3. **Sarah “Sally” Franklin Bache** (September 11, 1743–October 5, 1808): married Richard Bache in 1767. She ran substantial wartime relief work for Continental soldiers and cared for Franklin in his final illness. Their children included printer Benjamin Franklin Bache.

### Grandchildren

- **William Temple Franklin** (1760–1823), William Franklin’s illegitimate son, served as Benjamin’s secretary in France and later edited and selectively published his papers.
- **Benjamin Franklin Bache** (1769–1798), Sally’s son, was educated partly under Franklin’s supervision and became the combative Jeffersonian publisher of the *Philadelphia Aurora*.
- Franklin lived with or supported several other Bache grandchildren.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1706–1723

Franklin grew up in a crowded Boston artisan household. His father initially considered him for the ministry, but the family could afford only about two years of formal schooling: Boston Latin School and George Brownell’s writing and arithmetic school. He performed well in reading and writing but poorly in arithmetic.

At about ten he entered Josiah’s shop. He wanted to go to sea, which his father opposed. At twelve, in 1718, he was indentured to James Franklin as a printer.

James’s *New-England Courant*, founded in 1721, opposed clerical and governmental authority and became involved in repeated confrontations with Massachusetts officials. Benjamin learned typesetting, presswork, newspaper production, polemic, and distribution. He trained himself through intensive reading, imitation of *The Spectator*, vegetarian economizing, and exercises in prose construction.

In 1722 the sixteen-year-old secretly submitted the **Silence Dogood** letters, adopting the voice of an opinionated middle-aged widow. Fourteen essays satirized education, clergy, courtship, pretension, and censorship. Their reception established his literary confidence.

Relations with James deteriorated. James beat him, and Benjamin exploited a technical rearrangement of the apprenticeship after authorities temporarily prohibited James from publishing. Benjamin fled Boston in 1723, violating his indenture. He traveled through New York to Philadelphia.

## Education and Formation

Franklin had little institutional education but constructed an extensive program of self-instruction:

- He read Bunyan, Plutarch, Defoe, Cotton Mather, Locke, Shaftesbury, Collins, and *The Spectator*.
- Xenophon’s “Socratic method” influenced his conversational technique.
- He practiced writing by reconstructing essays from notes and converting prose into verse and back.
- Early vegetarianism saved money for books.
- His reading of anti-religious polemics temporarily pushed him toward determinism and deism.
- Practical experience in printing exposed him to commercial, scientific, theological, and political networks.

Important formative relationships included:

- **Josiah Franklin**, who encouraged careful judgment and steered him toward printing.
- **James Franklin**, whose shop supplied professional training and whose harshness helped provoke Benjamin’s independence.
- **Samuel Keimer**, his first important Philadelphia employer.
- **William Bradford** and Andrew Bradford, printers in New York and Philadelphia.
- **Sir William Keith**, governor of Pennsylvania, whose unfulfilled promises sent Franklin to London without the expected credit.
- **James Ralph**, poet and companion in London; their friendship later collapsed partly over money and Franklin’s sexual advance toward Ralph’s partner.
- **Peter Collinson**, London merchant and scientific intermediary who communicated Franklin’s electrical work to the Royal Society.
- **Cadwallader Colden**, colonial scientist and correspondent.
- **Joseph Priestley**, later friend, scientific historian, and source for the kite experiment account.

## Early Career and Commercial Rise, 1723–1748

Franklin reached Philadelphia in October 1723, nearly penniless. His later image of walking through Market Street carrying three large rolls became a foundational episode in the *Autobiography*.

He worked for Samuel Keimer and lodged with the Read family. Governor Keith encouraged him to establish a press and sent him to London in 1724, supposedly with letters of credit. Keith supplied none. Franklin worked as a journeyman at Samuel Palmer’s and John Watts’s printing houses from 1725 to 1726.

In London he printed his deistic **A Dissertation on Liberty and Necessity, Pleasure and Pain** (1725). He later called its publication an “erratum.” He developed friendships, swam in the Thames, considered teaching swimming, and acquired technical printing experience.

He returned to Philadelphia in 1726 as clerk and bookkeeper for merchant Thomas Denham. After Denham’s death he returned to Keimer. In 1728 he formed a printing partnership with Hugh Meredith and opened a shop.

### Junto and civic enterprise

In 1727 Franklin founded the **Junto**, or Leather Apron Club, a mutual-improvement and discussion society of artisans and tradesmen. It became the organizational seedbed for several institutions:

- The Library Company of Philadelphia, founded in 1731.
- Union Fire Company, 1736.
- Philadelphia Contributionship for the Insurance of Houses from Loss by Fire, 1752.
- Proposals leading to the Academy and College of Philadelphia.
- Civic improvements involving paving, street lighting, sanitation, firefighting, and night watch organization.

### Printing empire

Franklin and Meredith acquired the failing *Pennsylvania Gazette* in 1729. Franklin soon became sole proprietor. He expanded its news, essays, advertisements, and political influence.

His business included:

- Newspaper and almanac publication.
- Books, pamphlets, ballots, forms, paper currency, legal and government printing.
- A book and stationery shop.
- Postal privileges and distribution.
- Partnerships or franchises with printers in Charleston, New York, Newport, Antigua, Lancaster, New Haven, and elsewhere.
- Government printing contracts.
- Income from enslaved-person sale and runaway advertisements, alongside ordinary commercial advertising.

Beginning in 1732 he issued **Poor Richard’s Almanack**, under “Richard Saunders.” It combined calendars and practical information with borrowed, adapted, and original maxims. It became highly profitable and widely circulated. The prefatory address for the 1758 issue was separately reprinted as **The Way to Wealth**.

Franklin retired from daily printing management in 1748 under a profit-sharing arrangement with David Hall, but retained continuing income. “Retirement” freed him for scientific and public work rather than ending his commercial interests.

## Science, Technologies, Inventions, and Projects

### Electricity

Franklin began systematic electrical experimentation around 1746. Working with Ebenezer Kinnersley, Philip Syng, Thomas Hopkinson, and others, he developed a coherent experimental vocabulary and theory:

- Positive and negative electrical charge.
- Conservation of charge.
- The “single-fluid” theory.
- Battery, conductor, charge, discharge, plus, minus, and related terminology.
- The distinction between conductors and insulators.
- The action of pointed conductors.
- The identification of lightning with laboratory electricity.

His letters to Peter Collinson were published in London as **Experiments and Observations on Electricity** beginning in 1751. European translations and experiments made him internationally famous.

### Kite and sentry-box experiments

Franklin proposed that a pointed rod could draw electrical charge from a storm cloud. French experimenters, particularly Thomas-François Dalibard, successfully performed the proposed insulated-rod experiment at Marly-la-Ville in May 1752 before news of Franklin’s kite experiment appeared.

**Documented core:** Franklin published instructions for a kite apparatus, and Joseph Priestley later reported that Franklin and his son performed the experiment in Philadelphia in June 1752.

**Important correction:** The kite was not struck by a lightning bolt. Franklin reportedly obtained charge from an electrified cloud through a damp string while remaining insulated under shelter. A direct strike would probably have killed him.

### Lightning rod

Franklin devised and promoted pointed grounded rods to protect buildings and ships. His preference for pointed rods later produced a transatlantic scientific-political dispute when British authorities favored blunt conductors during the Revolution.

### Other inventions and improvements

- **Pennsylvania fireplace or Franklin stove** (1742): designed to provide more heat with less fuel and smoke. Early versions had draft problems; later improvements by David Rittenhouse made the general form more successful.
- **Glass armonica** (1761): nested glass bowls rotated by a foot treadle. It inspired music by Mozart, Beethoven, and other composers.
- **Bifocal or “double” spectacles:** documented in Franklin’s 1784 correspondence; he combined near- and distance-vision lenses.
- **Flexible urinary catheter** (1752): made for his brother John from articulated silver segments.
- **Swim paddles:** hand-held wooden devices recalled from childhood.
- **Odometer:** an improved carriage-distance mechanism associated with postal surveying.
- **Long-reach device, library chair, and practical household adaptations.**
- **Gulf Stream mapping:** Franklin and Nantucket sea captain Timothy Folger charted the current for postal and maritime purposes.
- Studies of refrigeration by evaporation, oceanography, meteorology, population growth, lead poisoning, ventilation, ship design, daylight use, and the behavior of oil on water.

Franklin did **not** discover electricity and did **not** invent daylight-saving time. His 1784 candle-saving proposal to Parisians was satirical and concerned waking earlier, not changing clocks.

### Patents

Franklin obtained no patents. He wrote that inventions should be shared because inventors benefit from the inventions of others. This position also served his public identity as a disinterested natural philosopher.

## Public Career before Independence, 1736–1775

Franklin became clerk of the Pennsylvania Assembly in 1736 and Philadelphia postmaster in 1737. In 1751 he entered the Assembly.

He helped Thomas Bond secure public support for **Pennsylvania Hospital** and promoted the **Academy and Charitable School of Philadelphia**, ancestor of the University of Pennsylvania. His educational plan emphasized English, modern languages, history, practical science, and civic usefulness rather than exclusively classical or sectarian learning.

### Defense and imperial war

A pacifist-influenced Quaker political establishment complicated military preparations. Franklin organized the Association for voluntary defense in 1747–1748 and helped arrange artillery. During the French and Indian War he:

- Supported frontier defense and supply measures.
- Helped obtain wagons for General Edward Braddock’s expedition.
- suffered financial exposure when wagon owners claimed compensation after Braddock’s defeat.
- supervised construction of frontier forts in 1756.
- served briefly as a militia colonel, though he did not build a professional military career.

### Albany Congress

In 1754 Franklin represented Pennsylvania at the Albany Congress. His **Albany Plan of Union** proposed a president-general appointed by the Crown and a colonial grand council. Colonial assemblies and imperial officials both rejected it, for opposite reasons.

The accompanying **“Join, or Die”** segmented-snake image, published in the *Pennsylvania Gazette* on May 9, 1754, became the best-known early American political cartoon. Its original purpose was colonial union during imperial war, not yet independence.

### Proprietary struggle and London agency

Pennsylvania politics revolved around the elected Assembly’s conflict with the Penn proprietors, especially taxation of proprietary lands. Franklin served as Assembly agent in London from 1757 to 1762. He lived principally at 36 Craven Street in Margaret Stevenson’s household.

He returned to Pennsylvania in 1762 but lost his Assembly seat in the bitter election of 1764. Opponents attacked his ambition, religion, sexuality, cosmopolitanism, and alleged hostility to Pennsylvania liberties. The Assembly nevertheless sent him back to London to seek royal government in place of proprietary rule.

Franklin also represented Massachusetts, New Jersey, and Georgia at different times.

### Stamp Act

Franklin initially underestimated colonial resistance and helped secure a stamp-distributor appointment for his associate John Hughes. This damaged his reputation in Pennsylvania. His 1766 testimony before the House of Commons cogently described American resistance and helped support repeal of the Stamp Act.

### Hutchinson letters affair

In 1772 Franklin obtained private letters written by Massachusetts governor Thomas Hutchinson and lieutenant governor Andrew Oliver. The letters favored curtailing certain colonial liberties. Franklin sent them to Thomas Cushing in Massachusetts with restrictions against publication. They were nevertheless copied and published in 1773.

When suspicion led to a duel and threatened another, Franklin publicly acknowledged transmitting them. He never revealed his source.

On January 29, 1774, solicitor general Alexander Wedderburn denounced Franklin before the Privy Council as a thief and dishonorable intriguer. The Council rejected Massachusetts’s petition to remove Hutchinson, and Franklin was dismissed from the deputy postmaster-generalship.

- **Documented fact:** Franklin transmitted private letters he had not authored, imposed restrictions, and later admitted his role.
- **Unresolved question:** how he obtained them.
- **Disputed issue:** whether transmission was responsible political disclosure or misuse of confidential correspondence.
- **Political result:** the public humiliation helped complete Franklin’s movement from imperial reformer to American revolutionary.

## Indigenous Relations and the Paxton Crisis

Franklin’s writings about Indigenous peoples were inconsistent across time and context.

His 1751 **Observations Concerning the Increase of Mankind** used racial and demographic categories, expressed a preference for an Anglo-Saxon settler population, and described much of the world as non-“white.” The essay has become central to criticism of his racial thought.

Elsewhere Franklin criticized European claims of “civility,” described Indigenous diplomacy and hospitality favorably, and noted that European captives sometimes preferred Indigenous society.

In December 1763 the Paxton Boys murdered peaceful Conestoga people at Conestoga Manor and Lancaster. Franklin’s **A Narrative of the Late Massacres** named victims, rejected collective racial guilt, and called the killers “Christian White Savages.” When armed frontiersmen marched toward Philadelphia in early 1764, Franklin joined negotiators at Germantown and helped persuade them to disperse.

This defense of the surviving Indigenous refugees was documented, but it did not erase Franklin’s participation in settler expansion, wartime frontier administration, or racialized demographic thinking.

## Revolution, 1775–1776

Franklin returned to Philadelphia in May 1775 and was elected to the Second Continental Congress. He served on committees dealing with defense, finance, diplomacy, and post.

Congress appointed him first postmaster general in July 1775. His reorganization of postal routes helped create an independent communications structure.

He proposed articles of confederation and, with Benjamin Harrison and Thomas Lynch, traveled to Cambridge to consult George Washington.

From March to May 1776 Franklin joined Samuel Chase and Charles Carroll of Carrollton on an unsuccessful mission to persuade Canada to join the American cause. Poor weather and Franklin’s age and health made the journey dangerous.

On June 11, 1776, Congress appointed Franklin, Thomas Jefferson, John Adams, Roger Sherman, and Robert R. Livingston to draft a declaration of independence. Jefferson produced the principal draft; Franklin and Adams reviewed it and suggested changes. Franklin signed the adopted Declaration.

## Mission to France and Revolutionary Diplomacy, 1776–1785

Franklin sailed for France in October 1776 with grandsons William Temple Franklin and Benjamin Franklin Bache. He reached France in December and settled at Passy, in a house provided within Jacques-Donatien Le Ray de Chaumont’s estate.

His scientific reputation, plain dress, spectacles, fur cap, and carefully managed image made him a celebrity. French portraits, medallions, prints, porcelain, clothing, and souvenirs turned him into a symbol of republican simplicity—although the simplicity was partly theatrical and operated within elite court and salon culture.

### Alliance and finance

Franklin worked with Silas Deane and Arthur Lee as commissioners. Relations among the Americans were often rancorous:

- Arthur Lee suspected colleagues of corruption or disloyalty.
- Deane faced accusations over accounts and commissions.
- Franklin’s informal administrative habits and use of family members attracted criticism.
- Congress investigated the mission’s finances but did not establish that Franklin personally embezzled French aid.

Franklin cultivated foreign minister **Charles Gravier, comte de Vergennes**, financiers, court figures, scientists, printers, salon hosts, and agents. After the American victory at Saratoga, he negotiated and signed the treaties of amity and commerce and alliance with France on February 6, 1778.

He became minister plenipotentiary in September 1778. French loans, subsidies, arms, troops, and naval power were indispensable to American victory.

### Propaganda, intelligence, and private war

Franklin’s Passy mission was a diplomatic and intelligence center:

- It issued passports, handled prisoners and exchanges, purchased supplies, and distributed funds.
- Franklin supported privateering and worked with John Paul Jones.
- His circle produced propaganda and covert publications, including *Affaires de l’Angleterre et de l’Amérique*.
- British intelligence penetrated the mission. Franklin’s secretary **Edward Bancroft** secretly reported to Britain.
- Franklin knew Passy was watched and sometimes exploited its openness, but there is no reliable evidence that he knew Bancroft was a paid British agent.

### Peace negotiations

Congress appointed Franklin, John Adams, John Jay, Henry Laurens, and Thomas Jefferson peace commissioners; Jefferson did not serve in the negotiations. Franklin, Adams, and Jay negotiated separately with British representatives despite instructions emphasizing consultation with France.

They signed preliminary articles on November 30, 1782, and the definitive **Treaty of Paris** on September 3, 1783. Britain recognized American independence, and the United States obtained boundaries extending to the Mississippi.

### Relationships in France

Documented intimate friendships included:

- **Anne-Louise Brillon de Jouy**, musician and salonnière.
- **Anne-Catherine de Ligniville Helvétius**, widow of philosopher Claude-Adrien Helvétius, to whom Franklin made a playful marriage proposal.
- **Madame Campan** and other court or salon figures.
- **Madame Foucault**, Madame La Rochefoucauld, and members of the Passy social circle.

Their correspondence contains flirtation and affectionate language.

- **Documented fact:** Franklin enjoyed flirtatious friendships and had admitted sexual “intrigues” in youth.
- **Unresolved question:** whether any French friendships were consummated.
- **Scholarly assessment:** no surviving evidence proves the popular image of Franklin as a prolific sexual libertine in France. Scholars have interpreted much of the language as stylized *amitié amoureuse* and salon play.

## Return, Constitutional Convention, and Final Public Work, 1785–1790

Franklin left France in July 1785 and returned to Philadelphia in September. He was elected president of Pennsylvania’s Supreme Executive Council for three one-year terms, serving from 1785 to 1788.

At the 1787 Constitutional Convention he was the oldest delegate and suffered from painful illness. He generally favored a stronger union, pragmatic compromise, and a plural executive less than some colleagues desired. He backed the Connecticut compromise and moved that sessions begin with prayer, though the convention did not adopt the proposal.

On September 17 James Wilson read Franklin’s final speech because Franklin’s voice was too weak. The speech acknowledged defects in the Constitution while urging delegates to suppress private objections and sign unanimously.

After the Convention, Elizabeth Willing Powel reportedly asked what form of government had been created. Franklin’s celebrated answer was “A republic, if you can keep it.”

- **Reported fact with strong contemporary provenance:** The exchange derives from Powel’s later written recollection.
- **Caution:** exact punctuation and wording vary in retellings, and the aphorism became more famous long afterward.

### Slavery and abolition

Franklin’s relationship to slavery changed substantially:

- The Franklin household kept enslaved workers, including people identified in records as **King**, **George**, and possibly others.
- The *Pennsylvania Gazette* printed notices for slave sales and rewards for capturing freedom-seekers.
- His business and social world therefore profited directly and indirectly from slavery.
- His 1751 population essay contained racial hierarchy and anxiety over the growth of Black and non-English populations.
- In the 1750s and 1760s he supported schools for Black children associated with the Bray organization. His observations of Black students contributed to his rejection of claims of innate intellectual incapacity.
- By the 1780s he supported gradual abolition, education, and economic assistance for formerly enslaved people.
- In 1787 he became president of the reorganized Pennsylvania abolition society.
- On February 3, 1790, he signed its petition asking Congress to use its powers to end the slave trade and slavery.
- Representative James Jackson of Georgia defended slavery. Franklin replied through the satirical **“Sidi Mehemet Ibrahim on the Slave Trade,”** published shortly before his death, paralleling American proslavery arguments with a fictional Algerian defense of enslaving Christians.

The development is documented; claims that Franklin was always an abolitionist or never meaningfully changed are both inaccurate.

## Religious, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

Franklin was raised in a Puritan Congregationalist environment. As a youth he rejected parts of orthodox Calvinism and published the deterministic *Dissertation on Liberty and Necessity*. He later repudiated its implications.

His 1728 **Articles of Belief and Acts of Religion** proposed a private liturgy centered on a supreme deity, subordinate spiritual powers, gratitude, virtue, and service.

In the *Autobiography*, Franklin described belief in:

- One God.
- Providence.
- Moral obligation.
- The soul’s immortality.
- Reward and punishment.
- The value of religion’s ethical core.

He contributed money to different congregations and praised the preaching of George Whitefield while declining to share Whitefield’s theology. Franklin and Whitefield maintained an important friendship and publishing relationship.

His famous thirteen-virtue program—temperance, silence, order, resolution, frugality, industry, sincerity, justice, moderation, cleanliness, tranquility, chastity, and humility—was a system of behavioral self-monitoring. Franklin conceded that pride survived even attempts at humility.

He favored freedom of conscience, religious pluralism, empirical inquiry, useful knowledge, sociability, civic association, and compromise. His thought combined Enlightenment natural philosophy, Protestant moral habits, artisan practicality, and imperial then republican politics.

### Freemasonry

Franklin was initiated into Philadelphia Freemasonry by 1731. He printed the first Masonic book in America, an edition of James Anderson’s *Constitutions of the Free-Masons* (1734), and served as provincial grand master.

In France he joined the **Lodge of the Nine Sisters**, an elite intellectual lodge, and served as venerable master. This membership is documented and formed part of his diplomatic and cultural network.

## Wealth, Business Structures, Property, and Finance

Franklin’s “rags-to-riches” image was substantially grounded in reality but simplified in his self-presentation.

Sources of wealth included:

- The printing shop and *Pennsylvania Gazette*.
- *Poor Richard’s Almanack*.
- Government printing and paper-money contracts.
- Book and stationery retail.
- Postal privileges and distribution advantages.
- Printing partnerships in other colonies.
- Profit-sharing with David Hall after retirement.
- Urban real estate, rents, and loans.
- Public salaries and diplomatic compensation.

He built a large courtyard residence behind Market Street and rental houses at 316 and 318 Market Street. The rental properties supplemented his income.

His business system often supplied presses or capital to partners in exchange for a share of profits. Results varied: some partners succeeded; others produced disputes, bad accounts, or losses.

His diplomatic accounts became contentious because Congress had weak accounting systems and Franklin used informal procedures. Critics, particularly Arthur Lee and later political opponents, insinuated misuse. Congress eventually accepted the major accounts; no court or legislative finding established personal fraud.

### Will and trusts

Franklin’s will distributed property among Sally Bache, grandchildren, relatives, friends, institutions, and civic causes. He left £1,000 each to Boston and Philadelphia as long-term loan funds for young artisans. The trusts accumulated for two centuries, though their later administration produced mixed results and changing interpretations of his instructions.

## Health, Illness, and Death

Documented or strongly reported conditions included:

- Recurrent pleurisy, including serious attacks in 1727 and later life.
- Gout, increasingly disabling in old age.
- Bladder or kidney stones and urinary obstruction.
- Obesity or stoutness in later life.
- Deteriorating eyesight, prompting bifocals.
- Weakness and reduced mobility during the Constitutional Convention.
- A severe final respiratory illness, traditionally described as pleurisy complicated by an abscess or empyema of the lung.

Franklin remained mentally active during his final illness. He died at his daughter’s Philadelphia home on April 17, 1790.

Approximately 20,000 mourners reportedly participated in or witnessed the funeral observances. Congress wore mourning; revolutionary France commemorated him. He was buried beneath a plain stone bearing the names Benjamin and Deborah Franklin.

His youthful proposed epitaph compared his body to the worn cover of a book awaiting a corrected new edition, but it was not used on the grave.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Contested Conduct

### Slaveholding and commercial participation in slavery

**Documented fact.** Franklin owned or controlled enslaved household labor, printed slave-sale and runaway advertisements, and profited from a commercial network embedded in slavery. His later abolitionism was genuine but followed decades of participation.

### Racial writings

**Documented fact.** The 1751 population essay used racialized categories and expressed anxiety that Germans and nonwhite peoples would displace English settlers. Franklin later revised some beliefs after observing Black students, but the earlier text remains part of the record.

### William Franklin’s mother

**Unresolved question.** Franklin acknowledged William but never securely identified the mother. Claims naming Deborah, a servant named Barbara, an oyster seller, or another “low woman” remain speculative or derive from hostile gossip.

### Sexual conduct

**Documented fact:** Franklin admitted youthful encounters with “low women” and attempted to seduce James Ralph’s partner. His 1745 private letter **Advice to a Young Man on the Choice of a Mistress** offered explicit, partly comic advice favoring older women if marriage was refused.

**Disputed claim:** that Franklin conducted numerous affairs in France. His letters demonstrate flirtation and erotic humor, not conclusively sexual relationships. No reliable documentation supports precise numbers sometimes claimed online.

### Neglect of Deborah

**Documented circumstances; interpretive judgment disputed.** Franklin remained abroad for many years despite Deborah’s reluctance and declining health. She managed his American affairs and repeatedly hoped for his return. Whether “abandonment” is the proper characterization is interpretive; prolonged separation is indisputable.

### Smallpox and Francis’s death

**Myth:** Franklin opposed inoculation and thereby caused Francis’s death.

**Evidence:** Franklin publicly stated that Francis died of smallpox before planned inoculation and that parents should inoculate. His grief was intensified because observers wrongly assumed the child had died from inoculation.

### Political patronage and officeholding

**Documented fact.** Franklin used family, printing, postal, scientific, and political networks to obtain offices and place associates. William’s governorship, John Hughes’s stamp appointment, and printing partnerships exposed him to accusations of building a family interest. This was patronage, but evidence does not establish a modern criminal bribery scheme.

### Stamp Act miscalculation

**Documented fact.** Franklin initially misread colonial sentiment and facilitated Hughes’s appointment. Pennsylvania protesters treated him as complicit. His later parliamentary testimony and support for repeal repaired much of the damage.

### Hutchinson letters

**Documented fact and disputed ethics.** Franklin transmitted private correspondence and concealed his source. Publication escaped the limits he imposed. No criminal conviction resulted, but the Privy Council humiliation and postal dismissal were official consequences.

### Diplomatic accounts and corruption accusations

**Allegation, not established finding.** Arthur Lee and others accused Franklin’s circle of favoritism, lax accounting, conflicts of interest, or corruption. Records confirm administrative disorder, reliance on relatives, overlapping public-private networks, and difficulty accounting for supplies and commissions. They do not demonstrate that Franklin stole French funds.

### Treatment of William Franklin

**Documented estrangement; motives disputed.** Benjamin attempted to win William to the revolutionary side. William remained Loyalist and participated in British-associated irregular warfare. Franklin refused reconciliation and penalized him in the will. Interpretations range from political principle to vindictive family punishment.

### Indigenous policy

**Documented complexity.** Franklin forcefully defended the Conestoga victims and condemned racial collective punishment. He also participated in colonial expansion, defense, and demographic thought that privileged English settlement.

### Constitutional compromises over slavery

**Documented fact.** Franklin signed a Constitution containing compromises that protected slavery. He then led an abolition society and petitioned Congress. Whether this represents pragmatic compromise, failure of principle, or strategic gradualism is interpretation.

### Freemasonry and secrecy

**Documented fact:** Franklin was a prominent Freemason.

**Unsupported extensions:** membership does not establish Illuminati control, occult criminality, satanism, or a secret plan governing the Revolution.

### “British spy” or double agent

**Allegation or theory.** Franklin remained an imperial conciliator until 1775, maintained British friendships, conducted secret negotiations, and operated within intelligence-heavy environments. British intelligence penetrated his Paris mission through Edward Bancroft.

No persuasive primary evidence establishes that Franklin knowingly served Britain after joining the American revolutionary cause. Claims that he was a British double agent often depend on circumstantial interpretation, confusion with Bancroft, or sensational secondary literature.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Franklin discovered electricity”

- **Story:** Franklin discovered electricity with a kite.
- **Origin and circulation:** simplified nineteenth- and twentieth-century school narratives.
- **Evidence:** electricity was studied long before Franklin. His achievement was a major theory of charge, experimental terminology, identification of lightning’s electrical nature, and practical protection.
- **Status:** myth based on real experiments.

### Kite struck by lightning

- **Story:** lightning struck Franklin’s kite and key.
- **Evidence against:** a direct strike would likely have killed him. The reported experiment collected ambient charge.
- **Status:** false embellishment.
- **Reputational effect:** produced the iconic solitary genius image and obscured European collaborators and prior demonstrations.

### “A republic, if you can keep it”

- **Story:** Franklin gave this answer upon leaving the Constitutional Convention.
- **Evidence:** Elizabeth Willing Powel recorded the exchange later; it has credible near-contemporary provenance.
- **Status:** probably authentic in substance, exact wording not absolutely certain.
- **Effect:** became a recurring warning about republican fragility.

### The turkey as national bird

- **Story:** Franklin proposed the turkey instead of the bald eagle as the official national emblem.
- **Evidence:** in a 1784 private letter to Sally Bache he criticized the eagle’s character and praised the turkey as courageous. He did not formally propose replacing the Great Seal’s eagle.
- **Status:** myth derived from a real joke and opinion.

### Daylight-saving time

- **Story:** Franklin invented daylight-saving time.
- **Evidence:** his anonymous 1784 *Journal de Paris* satire recommended waking Parisians earlier and calculated candle savings; it proposed no seasonal clock change.
- **Status:** myth.

### Serial killer and Craven Street bones

- **Story:** human bones discovered beneath Franklin’s London house prove he murdered people.
- **Appearance:** sensational press, television, websites, and later social-media conspiracy posts following the 1998 discovery.
- **Evidence:** remains of multiple people and animals bore dissection marks and dated from Franklin’s residence. Anatomist **William Hewson**, who married Polly Stevenson and ran an anatomy school there, is the strong contextual explanation. Turtle remains and mercury support anatomical instruction.
- **Against:** no evidence connects Franklin to murder; no murder victims have been identified; the site was associated with medical dissection.
- **Unresolved element:** Franklin may have known of the anatomy work, but direct proof of his knowledge or involvement is lacking.
- **Status:** murder claim is rumor/conspiracy narrative.

### Hellfire Club membership

- **Story:** Franklin belonged to Sir Francis Dashwood’s sexually libertine or satanic Hellfire Club.
- **Evidence offered:** Franklin knew Dashwood and collaborated with him on an abridged prayer book; he moved in overlapping elite circles.
- **Evidence against:** no surviving membership roll or primary document securely establishes Franklin’s attendance at Medmenham meetings. The club’s alleged satanism was itself amplified by political enemies and sensational literature.
- **Status:** unverified association, frequently presented as fact without adequate documentation.

### Prolific French womanizer

- **Story:** Franklin secured French aid principally by sleeping with numerous aristocratic women.
- **Evidence:** affectionate and flirtatious correspondence, playful proposals, salon celebrity, and his own candid youthful sexual recollections.
- **Against:** editors familiar with the complete papers find no proof of sexual affairs with the prominent French women concerned.
- **Status:** enduring legend resting on documented charm but unproved sexual claims.
- **Effect:** alternately humanized, scandalized, and trivialized his diplomatic work.

### Model of perfect thrift and sobriety

- **Story:** Franklin consistently embodied every Poor Richard maxim.
- **Evidence against simplification:** Franklin enjoyed wine, dining, sociability, travel, fine company, and extensive household support; many maxims were conventional or borrowed. His virtue chart recorded failures rather than perfection.
- **Status:** hagiographic simplification encouraged partly by his *Autobiography*.

### Self-made man without assistance

- **Story:** Franklin rose solely through thrift and individual effort.
- **Evidence:** his labor, skill, and strategy were extraordinary, but advancement also depended on family training, partners, credit, government printing, postal office, patronage, Deborah’s management, enslaved and free household labor, transatlantic intermediaries, and imperial institutions.
- **Status:** influential self-created mythology containing truth but omitting enabling networks.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Franklin’s reputation has repeatedly changed:

### 1790–early nineteenth century

He was commemorated as an international philosopher, revolutionary diplomat, and exemplar of useful knowledge. French revolutionary bodies mourned him. Early editions emphasized moral essays and suppressed or softened sexual, irreligious, and politically awkward material.

### Nineteenth century

Franklin became an emblem of thrift, discipline, self-education, and bourgeois upward mobility. The *Autobiography* became a school text. Critics saw the same image as materialistic, calculating, or philistine. Romantic and transcendentalist writers sometimes resisted “Franklinesque” utility.

### Twentieth century

Professional scholarship restored the scientist, imperial politician, transatlantic networker, and complicated diplomat. Carl Van Doren’s biography and the Yale papers transformed the evidence base.

Franklin’s image appeared on the United States $100 bill, postage stamps, municipal symbols, institutions, ships, counties, towns, schools, scientific medals, and businesses. The Franklin Institute, American Philosophical Society, University of Pennsylvania, Library Company, Pennsylvania Hospital, and Philadelphia civic landscape became central memorial sites.

### Twenty-first century reinterpretation

Recent scholarship emphasizes:

- Deborah Franklin and other women’s labor.
- Jane Mecom’s contrasting life and poverty.
- William Franklin’s coherent Loyalist politics rather than treating him only as a failed son.
- Franklin’s slaveholding, slave advertising, racial thought, and eventual abolitionism.
- Indigenous diplomacy and the Paxton massacre.
- The collective and international nature of his science.
- Propaganda, celebrity construction, espionage vulnerabilities, and family patronage.
- The distinction between Franklin’s crafted *Autobiography* and the less orderly archival life.

His legacy consequently contains several simultaneous Franklins: printer-capitalist, artisan intellectual, slaveholder turned abolitionist, imperial reformer turned revolutionary, family patriarch and absentee husband, experimental scientist, civic institution-builder, propagandist, diplomat, humorist, and consciously manufactured public symbol.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the reviewed record are included. Mere contemporaneity has been excluded.

### Adam Smith

**Evidentiary label: reported fact / disputed extent.** Franklin and Smith moved in overlapping British Enlightenment circles, particularly through David Hume, and later accounts place them together in London during the period when Smith was revising *The Wealth of Nations*. Claims that Franklin substantially shaped specific passages remain debated. No extant personal correspondence between Franklin and Smith has been identified, so the intimacy and intellectual effect of the relationship should not be overstated.

### Alexander Hamilton

**Evidentiary label: documented fact.** Franklin and Hamilton were delegates to the Constitutional Convention in Philadelphia in 1787. They participated in the same institutional proceedings, though no major personal partnership comparable to Franklin’s relationships with Washington or Jefferson is evident.

### Antoine Court de Gébelin

**Evidentiary label: documented fact.** Court de Gébelin and Franklin belonged to the Lodge of the Nine Sisters in Paris. Court served as lodge secretary, corresponded with Franklin, and collaborated in the publication network around *Affaires de l’Angleterre et de l’Amérique*, c. 1776–1778.

### Franz Anton Mesmer

**Evidentiary label: documented fact.** In 1784 Louis XVI appointed Franklin to a royal commission investigating Mesmer’s claims concerning “animal magnetism.” Franklin, Antoine Lavoisier, Jean-Sylvain Bailly, Joseph-Ignace Guillotin, and other commissioners concluded that the observed effects did not require a magnetic fluid and were attributable substantially to imagination and imitation. Franklin’s frailty limited some participation, and experiments were conducted at Passy.

### George Washington

**Evidentiary label: documented fact.** Franklin corresponded and collaborated with Washington during the Revolution. Franklin visited the Continental Army’s Cambridge headquarters in 1775, supported Washington diplomatically from France, and exchanged letters with him afterward. Washington wrote Franklin during Franklin’s final illness.

### Marie-Joseph Paul Yves Roch Gilbert du Motier, Marquis de Lafayette

**Evidentiary label: documented fact.** Franklin met and worked with Lafayette within the Franco-American revolutionary network. Franklin’s Paris mission facilitated French support and dealt with Lafayette’s American service, correspondence, and political role. Both later participated in transatlantic antislavery discussions, though their methods differed.

### Louis XVI

**Evidentiary label: documented fact.** Franklin was formally received at the court of Louis XVI in 1778 after the Franco-American treaties. The king’s government recognized the United States, supplied loans, military aid, troops, and naval support, and appointed Franklin to the 1784 commission investigating Mesmerism. Their relationship was institutional and diplomatic rather than personally intimate.

### Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart

**Evidentiary label: documented technological influence, not documented personal relationship.** Mozart composed music for glass armonica, including the *Adagio and Rondo in C minor*, K. 617, and *Adagio in C*, K. 356/617a. The instrument was Franklin’s invention. No evidence reviewed here establishes that Franklin and Mozart met.

### Thomas Jefferson

**Evidentiary label: documented fact.** Franklin and Jefferson served together in the Second Continental Congress and on the five-member Declaration drafting committee in Philadelphia in 1776. Franklin reviewed Jefferson’s draft and proposed changes. Jefferson was later appointed a peace commissioner but did not travel for the negotiations; in 1785 he succeeded Franklin as United States minister in France.

### George Washington, Thomas Jefferson, and Alexander Hamilton as a shared institutional network

**Evidentiary label: documented fact.** All intersected Franklin through the creation of the United States, but in distinct settings: Washington through military and executive correspondence, Jefferson through Congress and diplomacy, Hamilton chiefly through the 1787 Convention. Grouping them should not imply equal closeness.

### Sir Isaac Newton

**Evidentiary label: documented intellectual influence, no personal meeting.** Newton died in 1727, before Franklin’s scientific fame. Newtonian experimental philosophy formed part of the intellectual environment in which Franklin worked. Franklin’s European reputation was often compared to Newton’s, but they were not collaborators.

### John Locke

**Evidentiary label: documented intellectual influence, no personal meeting.** Franklin read Locke during his self-education. Lockean empiricism and arguments concerning knowledge, toleration, and government formed part of his intellectual background. Locke died when Franklin was a child.

### Francis Bacon

**Evidentiary label: documented intellectual influence, no personal meeting.** Franklin’s program of organized useful knowledge and experimental inquiry belonged to a Baconian tradition. The relationship is intellectual and posthumous, not personal.

### Immanuel Kant

**Evidentiary label: reported intellectual reception, no documented meeting.** Kant knew Franklin’s electrical reputation within the European Enlightenment. Franklin’s lightning experiments contributed to the scientific culture Kant followed, but no direct correspondence or meeting was established in the reviewed sources.

### Michael Faraday, Thomas Edison, Nikola Tesla, Alexander Graham Bell, and Albert Einstein

**Evidentiary label: documented posthumous scientific lineage, not personal connections.** These later scientists and inventors operated in fields transformed by electrical science. Franklin was a predecessor and cultural model, but none could have met him. No specific personal connection should be inferred.

### Benjamin Franklin

The roster includes the dossier’s subject himself; this is not treated as a separate connection.

### Roster names for which no supported personal or explicit influence connection was established

The research located no defensible reason to connect Franklin to most roster members. Shared nationality, Freemasonry in different periods, residence in the same city, engagement with electricity, or later quotation of Franklin is insufficient without documentary support. In particular, no supported direct connection was established here with **Prince Hall**, **Francisco de Miranda**, **Ann Lee**, **Abraham Lincoln**, **Napoleon Bonaparte**, **Alexander von Humboldt**, or the numerous later political, artistic, occult, technological, and financial figures on the roster.

- **1789:** Issues abolition-society address; continues autobiography and scientific correspondence.
- **1790, February:** Signs abolition petition to Congress.
- **1790, March:** “Sidi Mehemet Ibrahim” satire published.
- **1790, April 17:** Dies in Philadelphia.
- **1791:** First partial publication of autobiography, in French.
- **1817–1818:** William Temple Franklin publishes edited *Memoirs and Writings*.
- **1959–present:** Yale/APS scholarly *Papers of Benjamin Franklin* published.
- **1998:** Human and animal remains discovered at Craven Street, later linked contextually to William Hewson’s anatomy school.
- **2022:** Ken Burns documentary renews broad public examination.
- **2024:** *Franklin* dramatizes the French mission; new scientific biography appears.
- **2025–2026:** Revolutionary-era reassessments continue to foreground William Franklin, slavery, Indigenous nations, women’s labor, and the distance between archive and patriotic mythology.
