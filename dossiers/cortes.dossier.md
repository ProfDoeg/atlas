# Hernán Cortés: Research Dossier

## Scope, method, and evidentiary labels

This dossier reconstructs Hernán Cortés’s life from surviving correspondence, legal records, testamentary material, sixteenth-century chronicles, modern archival scholarship, and recent historiography. Cortés’s own writings are indispensable but interested documents: they were reports and petitions designed to justify his conduct and secure royal favor. Later Spanish, Nahua, Tlaxcalan, and colonial accounts likewise reflect particular political settings and purposes.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary documents, records, or convergent independent testimony.
- **Reported fact:** found in a credible narrative source but not independently established.
- **Allegation:** asserted in testimony, litigation, accusation, or polemic without a dispositive finding.
- **Disputed claim:** supported and challenged by identifiable evidence or interpretations.
- **Rumor or myth:** a culturally consequential story not established as fact.
- **Unresolved question:** evidence is insufficient to determine what occurred.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Preferred modern name:** Hernán Cortés.
- **Name used in his own documents:** Hernando Cortés; modern scholars also print Fernando Cortés.
- **Full genealogical form sometimes supplied:** Hernando Cortés de Monroy y Pizarro Altamirano. He did not ordinarily use this extended form in his surviving writings.
- **Titles and offices:**
  - Captain and expedition commander under Diego Velázquez de Cuéllar, 1518–1519.
  - Justicia mayor and captain-general under the municipal authority created at Villa Rica de la Vera Cruz, 1519; the legality of this maneuver was contested.
  - Royal governor and captain-general of New Spain, formally confirmed in 1522.
  - **Marqués del Valle de Oaxaca**, created by Charles V on 20 July 1529.
  - Captain-general of New Spain and the South Sea under the 1529 grants, but without restoration of the civil governorship.
  - Patron of the Hospital de la Concepción de Nuestra Señora, later called the Hospital de Jesús Nazareno.
- **Common descriptions:** conquistador, conqueror of Mexico, colonial administrator, encomendero, marquis, shipowner, mine owner, agricultural entrepreneur.
- **Nickname in some popular traditions:** *el Conquistador*; this was a descriptive epithet rather than a personal nickname consistently used during his life.
- **Birth:** usually placed in **1485**, Medellín, Extremadura, Crown of Castile. Some sources give 1484 or 1486. No baptismal record conclusively fixes the year.
- **Death:** **2 December 1547**, Castilleja de la Cuesta, near Seville, Castile.
- **Cause of death:** not securely diagnosed. Contemporary and later accounts mention illness involving severe intestinal or respiratory symptoms; “dysentery” and pleurisy have both been proposed. A modern medical diagnosis is impossible.
- **Religion:** Roman Catholic. His professions of faith, patronage, foundations, testament, and conquest rhetoric were explicitly Catholic, although the relationship between missionary language and material or political objectives is a central historical dispute.
- **Principal residences:** Medellín; Salamanca; Valladolid; Santo Domingo, Hispaniola; Azua; Baracoa and Santiago de Cuba; Veracruz and the central Mexican campaign route; Coyoacán; Mexico-Tenochtitlan/Mexico City; Cuernavaca; Oaxaca and Tehuantepec properties; Honduras during the Hibueras expedition; various residences in Castile during his final years.

## Physical Description and Personal Characteristics

Francisco López de Gómara, who knew Cortés in Spain but did not witness the conquest, described him as of medium stature, with a pale complexion, sparse beard, broad chest, and a serious manner. Bernal Díaz del Castillo remembered a capable horseman and swordsman, temperate in food and drink, watchful, energetic, and persuasive. Both authors wrote with personal or literary purposes, and neither description is a neutral medical portrait.

Cortés’s documents reveal:

- familiarity with notarial and legal forms;
- an ability to dictate or compose extended political narratives;
- close attention to accounts, contracts, patronage, logistics, and the division of spoils;
- persistent pursuit of royal recognition through petitions and litigation;
- a recurrent combination of negotiation, hostage-taking, exemplary violence, political alliance, and religious justification.

Claims that he suffered syphilis in Hispaniola occur in later biographies but lack a securely identifiable contemporary diagnosis. Illness did delay or alter some early plans, but the precise condition is unresolved.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

- **Father:** Martín Cortés de Monroy, an Extremaduran hidalgo of modest means.
- **Mother:** Catalina Pizarro Altamirano, also commonly called Leonor Pizarro in biographical traditions. The variation in her given name and the reconstruction of Cortés’s maternal descent have generated genealogical disagreement.
- Cortés was apparently their only child.

The family belonged to the lower provincial nobility rather than the highest aristocracy. Hidalgo status offered honor and social aspirations but did not guarantee substantial wealth. Cortés’s later career cannot be explained by inherited fortune.

His maternal Altamirano relations included local notarial and administrative figures. A recent genealogical study has revisited the paternal line and cautions against treating every elaborate pedigree found in later accounts as established.

### Claimed Pizarro kinship

Cortés has often been described as a distant cousin of Francisco Pizarro and of the Pizarro brothers. **Disputed genealogical claim:** both families came from Extremadura and shared Pizarro ancestry in some reconstructions, but the precise degree of relationship is not consistently documented. Popular claims that Cortés and Francisco Pizarro were close second cousins, met only once, or consciously competed as relatives exceed the strongest available evidence.

### Marriages and partners

#### Catalina Suárez Marcaida

Cortés married Catalina Suárez—also written Xuárez or Juárez and called Marcaida—in Cuba, probably in 1514 or 1515. Her family had come from Spain through Hispaniola. Diego Velázquez was associated with the family and reportedly promoted or pressured the match. Cortés and Catalina lived in Baracoa and Santiago de Cuba.

She joined him in New Spain in 1522 and died suddenly at Coyoacán around late October or the night traditionally given as 1–2 November 1522.

Her death became the subject of a major unresolved allegation discussed below.

#### Juana Ramírez de Arellano y Zúñiga

Cortés married Juana de Zúñiga in 1529. She was the daughter of Carlos Ramírez de Arellano, second count of Aguilar, and Juana de Zúñiga. The marriage connected Cortés to an established noble house and helped translate American wealth into aristocratic status.

Juana traveled to New Spain in 1530 and established the family household principally at Cuernavaca. She remained there when Cortés returned to Castile in 1540; they did not meet again before his death.

### Natural children and their mothers

Surviving records identify at least five natural children:

1. **Catalina Pizarro**, daughter of Leonor Pizarro, a Spanish woman. Cortés later litigated or petitioned over property intended for Catalina.
2. **Martín Cortés**, commonly called Martín Cortés *el Mestizo*, son of Malintzin/Marina. Born probably in 1522 or 1523. He was legitimized by papal bull, educated at court, received the habit of Santiago, and pursued a military career.
3. **Luis Cortés**, son of Elvira or Antonia de Hermosillo. The mother’s given name varies in the records and scholarship.
4. **Leonor Cortés Moctezuma**, daughter of Tecuichpo Ixcaxochitzin, baptized Isabel Moctezuma, daughter of Motecuhzoma Xocoyotzin.
5. **María Cortés**, daughter of an unidentified Indigenous noblewoman. A physical impairment of one arm is recorded.

A 1529 papal bull legitimized Martín, Luis, and Catalina. Leonor and María were not included, for reasons not established, but Cortés recognized them materially and included them in succession or marriage provisions.

### Legitimate children with Juana de Zúñiga

The marriage produced six recorded children:

1. **Luis**, born during the family’s wait at Texcoco; died in infancy.
2. **Catalina**, died young.
3. **Martín Cortés**, born at Cuernavaca in September 1532; heir and second marquis of the Valley of Oaxaca.
4. **María Cortés**, later countess of Luna through marriage to Luis Fernández de Quiñones.
5. **Catalina Cortés**, the second daughter given that name.
6. **Juana Cortés**, later duchess of Alcalá through marriage.

Cortés’s 1535 entail designated the legitimate Martín as heir but allowed eventual succession through his natural children if legitimate lines failed.

## Childhood and Early Life, c. 1485–1504

Cortés grew up in Medellín, a fortified Extremaduran town marked by conflicts among noble factions, military traditions, and proximity to the Portuguese frontier. His parents reportedly intended him for a legal or ecclesiastical career.

Later biographies portray him as a sickly child who became an energetic and unruly adolescent. These details depend heavily on Gómara and later retellings and should be treated as **reported facts**, not independently documented childhood evidence.

Around age fourteen, he was sent to Salamanca. The traditional claim that he formally studied law for two years at the University of Salamanca is not confirmed by a matriculation record. María del Carmen Martínez Martínez and other scholars emphasize that he lived with or near the household of the notary Francisco Núñez de Valera and acquired practical familiarity with Latin, legal formulas, documentary procedure, and account-keeping. His later writings show substantial legal-notarial competence, but he did not obtain a university degree.

He returned to Medellín after roughly two years. According to Gómara, he considered campaigns in Italy under Gonzalo Fernández de Córdoba and a voyage to the Indies with Nicolás de Ovando. Illness or injury allegedly prevented an earlier departure. The stories differ in their details.

## Hispaniola, 1504–1511

Cortés sailed for Hispaniola in 1504, probably aboard a merchant vessel commanded by Alonso Quintero. He arrived in Santo Domingo during the governorship of Nicolás de Ovando.

He registered as a settler and received land and Indigenous labor at Azua. He served as a notary or municipal functionary and participated in military operations associated with the completion of Spanish domination of Hispaniola. These operations occurred amid forced labor, displacement, warfare, and catastrophic Indigenous mortality.

The frequently repeated claim that he contracted syphilis during these years is **unverified**. His absence from Diego de Nicuesa’s 1509 expedition is sometimes attributed to illness, but the diagnosis is retrospective.

## Cuba, 1511–1518

In 1511 Cortés joined Diego Velázquez de Cuéllar’s conquest of Cuba, serving under Pánfilo de Narváez or another subordinate command depending on the account. He received an encomienda and became a notary at Baracoa. He later held municipal office, including alcalde of Santiago de Cuba.

### Relationship with Diego Velázquez

Velázquez was successively Cortés’s commander, patron, political superior, marriage connection, rival, and enemy.

Cortés accumulated land, livestock, Indigenous labor, and mining interests. His household and business position gave him more capital than the image of a penniless adventurer suggests, though he was not yet extraordinarily wealthy.

Relations with Velázquez deteriorated more than once. Cortés was reportedly imprisoned for involvement in settler complaints against the governor, escaped, reconciled, and married Catalina Suárez. The most colorful escape stories come from later chronicles and may be embellished.

### Appointment to the Mexican expedition

After the voyages of Francisco Hernández de Córdoba in 1517 and Juan de Grijalva in 1518 revealed wealthy mainland societies, Velázquez commissioned Cortés to lead a new expedition. Cortés borrowed heavily, mortgaged property, recruited men, obtained arms, horses, ships, and provisions, and invested much of his available fortune.

Velázquez soon feared that Cortés would act independently. Orders were sent to remove him, but Cortés sailed from Santiago in November 1518 and completed preparations elsewhere in Cuba. Whether he was already legally dismissed when he departed is disputed; what is clear is that his authority was contested before he reached Mexico.

## The Expedition and Conquest, 1519–1521

### Cozumel and Tabasco

Cortés left Cuba with approximately eleven ships, more than 500 soldiers, sailors, Indigenous and African servants, sixteen horses, artillery, and a small number of women. Exact totals differ by source.

At Cozumel, he recovered **Jerónimo de Aguilar**, a Spaniard shipwrecked years earlier who spoke a Maya language. In March 1519 Cortés’s force fought Chontal Maya forces at or near Centla in Tabasco. After Spanish victory, local leaders supplied food, gifts, and twenty women.

One was **Malintzin**, baptized Marina. She spoke Nahuatl and Chontal Maya. Initially Cortés could communicate through a chain—Spanish to Aguilar’s Maya, then Marina’s Maya to Nahuatl. Marina rapidly learned Spanish and became interpreter, diplomatic broker, political intelligence source, and visible participant in negotiations. Sources often represent her standing beside Cortés and speaking publicly. Describing her merely as his “translator” understates her role; describing the relationship as a verified romance imposes a later sentimental category not established by the evidence.

### Veracruz and the break with Velázquez

Cortés reached the Gulf coast and received embassies and gifts from Motecuhzoma Xocoyotzin. He founded or caused his supporters to found the **Villa Rica de la Vera Cruz**. Its cabildo resigned the authority derived from Velázquez and appointed Cortés captain-general and justicia mayor in the king’s name.

This was a calculated legal-political maneuver. Cortés presented it as the free action of settlers acting for the Crown; opponents saw it as rebellion and usurpation.

The cabildo sent Charles V a letter dated 10 July 1519, accompanied by treasure and representatives. The original “First Relation” by Cortés, if it existed as a separate document, is lost. The cabildo’s letter has often been printed in its place but is not straightforwardly a personal work by Cortés.

### The ships

Cortés ordered most of the expedition’s ships rendered unusable. Contemporary language indicates beaching, boring, dismantling, or “giving them across,” with usable material recovered. **Myth:** he did not literally stage a dramatic burning of all his ships. The burning version emerged in later sixteenth-century literature and became a symbol of irreversible commitment.

### Indigenous alliances

Cortés’s success depended on Indigenous political divisions and alliances. Totonac communities at Cempoala sought leverage against Mexica tribute power. Cortés encouraged or accepted their seizure of Mexica tribute collectors, then maneuvered as mediator.

The expedition fought several battles against Tlaxcalan forces before negotiating an alliance with Tlaxcala’s governing factions. Tlaxcalans supplied thousands—eventually tens of thousands—of warriors, porters, food suppliers, and intelligence. Other Indigenous polities later joined, including Huejotzingo and Texcoco factions.

The conquest was therefore not a campaign of a few hundred Europeans acting alone. It was a shifting, multi-polity war in which Cortés led one component of a much larger Indigenous-Spanish coalition.

### Cholula, October 1519

At Cholula, Cortés ordered or permitted a coordinated attack in which Spaniards and Tlaxcalans killed a large number of Cholulan nobles, warriors, and inhabitants. Numbers in the sources vary widely.

- **Documented fact:** a major massacre occurred.
- **Cortés’s justification:** Marina and allied informants uncovered a planned ambush authorized by Motecuhzoma.
- **Disputed claim:** whether an imminent ambush existed. Tlaxcalan hostility toward Cholula, coercive interrogation, and Cortés’s need to intimidate prospective opponents complicate the Spanish version.
- **Responsibility:** Cortés directed the operation and later presented it as punitive and preventive action.

### Meeting with Motecuhzoma

Cortés and the allied army entered Tenochtitlan on **8 November 1519**. He met Motecuhzoma on the causeway. The parties exchanged speeches and gifts, but the precise words survive only through mediated, later, or politically interested texts.

Cortés soon placed Motecuhzoma under Spanish custody, using the killing of Spaniards at Nautla as justification. The Spaniards governed through the captive ruler and seized royal treasure. Cortés’s later claim that Motecuhzoma voluntarily transferred sovereignty to Charles V is widely questioned as a legal-literary construction.

### Narváez and the Toxcatl crisis

Velázquez sent Pánfilo de Narváez with a substantially larger force to arrest Cortés. Cortés left Pedro de Alvarado in charge at Tenochtitlan, marched to the coast, surprised Narváez at Cempoala in May 1520, captured him, and persuaded most of his men to join the conquest.

While Cortés was absent, Alvarado’s force massacred Mexica nobles and participants during the Toxcatl festival. Cortés did not personally command the immediate massacre, but he had left Alvarado in authority and resumed command during its consequences.

Returning to Tenochtitlan, Cortés found the city in revolt. Motecuhzoma died during the crisis. Spanish accounts claimed he was struck by stones thrown by his own people; Nahua traditions and later narratives accused the Spaniards of killing him. **Unresolved question:** the evidence does not establish the manner of death.

### Flight, 30 June–1 July 1520

The Spaniards and allies attempted a night escape carrying treasure. Mexica forces attacked them on the causeways. Hundreds of Spaniards and many more Indigenous allies died; treasure, artillery, and horses were lost. The episode became known to Spaniards as the **Noche Triste**, now often called the Noche Victoriosa from the Mexica perspective.

The story that Cortés sat beneath a particular ahuehuete and wept is **legend**, not a securely documented eyewitness fact.

The survivors fought their way toward Tlaxcala, including the battle of Otumba. Tlaxcala did not abandon the alliance despite Spanish weakness.

### Smallpox

Smallpox, probably introduced by a person connected to Narváez’s expedition, spread through central Mexico in 1520. It killed Cuitláhuac, Motecuhzoma’s successor, and an enormous but unquantifiable number of people. Cortés did not intentionally introduce the epidemic according to the surviving evidence. Claims of deliberate biological warfare in 1520 are **unsupported**; nevertheless, the epidemic materially assisted the coalition’s military recovery.

### Siege and fall of Tenochtitlan

At Tlaxcala and Texcoco, Cortés rebuilt his forces and constructed thirteen brigantines for Lake Texcoco. The campaign systematically cut roads, aqueducts, food supplies, and allied towns.

Modern research documents massacres, enslavement, terror tactics, destruction of settlements, and the tolerated participation of Indigenous allies in customary war practices, including reported consumption of captives. Cortés sometimes objected to allied practices in his narratives but also relied on the coercive effect of his coalition.

The siege began in May 1521. Fighting, starvation, disease, and destruction devastated Tenochtitlan and Tlatelolco. Cuauhtémoc was captured on **13 August 1521**. The demographic loss cannot be separated into precise shares caused by combat, famine, epidemic disease, displacement, and later colonial conditions.

## Government of New Spain, 1521–1524

Cortés initially governed from Coyoacán while Mexico City was reconstructed over Tenochtitlan. He distributed urban lots and encomiendas, appointed municipal authorities, sponsored churches, and rewarded followers. He also concentrated property, tribute, labor, and political influence in his own hands.

Charles V confirmed him as governor and captain-general in 1522, resolving the immediate conflict with Velázquez in Cortés’s favor without validating every action he had taken.

### Encomienda and forced labor

Cortés initially professed reservations about extending encomienda to the mainland. He then distributed most of central Mexico’s subject population among himself and his followers despite contrary royal policy.

Encomienda did not formally transfer ownership of Indigenous people, but it assigned tribute and labor obligations to colonists and was frequently coercive, violent, and locally difficult to distinguish from forced servitude. Cortés defended his arrangements as protective and Christianizing. Scholarship on Ixmiquilpan and the later Marquesado demonstrates that preconquest Indigenous political and economic units were deliberately identified and redirected toward Spanish grants.

### Slavery and branding

Cortés’s campaigns enslaved captives classified as rebels or taken in what Spaniards called just war. Captives were branded. A 1539 donation recorded **one hundred enslaved Indigenous men and women**, bearing the royal brand and working with tools and furnaces in Cortés’s Taxco mines, divided as a benefit for three sons.

His testament also dealt with enslaved people, debts, and possible manumissions. Cortés’s documented ownership and transfer of enslaved people makes blanket portrayals of him as an opponent of Indigenous slavery untenable. At the same time, the legal categories of chattel slavery, war captivity, encomienda tribute, and compulsory service were distinct even when all could produce extreme coercion.

### Conversion and the Church

Cortés destroyed or appropriated Indigenous religious images and temples, installed crosses and Marian images, requested mendicant missionaries, and greeted the first twelve Franciscans in 1524. His correspondence represented conversion as a principal royal and providential purpose.

He also protected clerics useful to his political program, endowed religious institutions, and supported the hospital later called the Hospital de Jesús. Critics, including some churchmen, identified contradictions between Christian claims and conquest, forced labor, sexual appropriation, enslavement, and killing.

### Catalina Suárez’s death

Catalina joined Cortés at Coyoacán and died suddenly after a banquet in late 1522.

**Documented facts:**

- She had appeared well enough to attend church, entertain guests, dine, and dance.
- She died in the marital bedroom.
- Household witnesses described inconsistent signs, including an alleged mark on her neck, a wet bed, and broken beads.
- Cortés summoned attendants, publicly mourned, arranged burial, and later endowed observances for her.
- Years later Catalina’s mother and brother accused him during the residencia of strangling her with cloth.
- Cortés denied homicide and said Catalina suffered episodes described as *mal de corazón*, fainting, or catalepsy.
- No surviving criminal conviction established that he murdered her.

**Allegation:** Cortés strangled Catalina because their relationship was troubled or because he wished to make an aristocratic marriage.

**Evidence supporting suspicion:** sudden death in his presence; some testimony about neck marks and disorder in the bed; household rumors; later sworn accusations.

**Evidence against certainty:** delayed accusation; contradictory descriptions; witnesses who did not observe marks; evidence of earlier illness; inheritance litigation that may have affected testimony; no modern autopsy and no dispositive judgment.

Alfonso Toro argued for guilt in a historical-medico-legal study. Francisco Fernández del Castillo defended Cortés. Catalina’s nephew Juan Suárez de Peralta attributed her death to illness; Bernal Díaz referred to asthma. The case remains an **unresolved question**, not an established murder.

## The Hibueras Expedition, 1524–1526

Fearing that Cristóbal de Olid had defected while attempting to conquer Honduras, Cortés personally led an overland expedition from Mexico toward Las Hibueras in October 1524. The journey crossed Tabasco, Acalan, the Maya lowlands, and difficult territory in present-day Guatemala and Honduras. Malintzin traveled with the expedition and married Juan Jaramillo during it.

Cortés brought Cuauhtémoc, the former ruler of Tenochtitlan, and other Indigenous lords, apparently to prevent rebellion during his absence.

### Execution of Cuauhtémoc

At Acalan or Itzamkanac in 1525, Cortés ordered Cuauhtémoc and the ruler of Tlacopan hanged after allegations of a conspiracy.

- **Documented fact:** Cortés ordered the executions.
- **Cortés’s account:** a participant disclosed a plot to kill the Spaniards.
- **Disputed claim:** the conspiracy’s reality. Testimony was limited, proceedings were summary, and even Spanish companions reportedly considered the execution unjust.
- The act damaged Cortés’s reputation at court and in later chronicles.

The expedition experienced hunger, disease, dangerous crossings, desertion, and severe logistical failures. Cortés reached Honduras to discover that Olid had already been killed by opponents. His absence destabilized Mexico, where rival officials spread reports that he was dead and fought over government and property.

He returned to Mexico in 1526. The expedition was strategically costly and one of his clearest failures.

## Royal Investigation and Loss of Civil Power, 1526–1528

Charles V sent **Luis Ponce de León** to conduct a *juicio de residencia*, the standard royal audit of an official’s tenure. Ponce arrived in July 1526 and died shortly thereafter, as did his successor Marcos de Aguilar. Rumors that Cortés had poisoned royal officials circulated, but no reliable evidence proves poisoning.

The investigation accumulated extensive accusations:

- illegal rebellion against Velázquez;
- manipulation of the Veracruz cabildo;
- misappropriation of royal treasure and the Crown’s fifth;
- unequal or fraudulent distribution of booty;
- favoritism in encomiendas and offices;
- coercion and violence against Spaniards and Indigenous communities;
- unauthorized warfare and enslavement;
- the deaths or mistreatment of rivals and royal officials;
- the murder of Catalina Suárez;
- misconduct during the Honduras expedition.

These were accusations in an administrative-judicial inquiry, not a single criminal indictment ending in one general verdict. Cortés submitted a lengthy defense beginning in 1534. The proceedings continued in fragments for years and were never reduced to a simple acquittal or conviction.

The first Audiencia under Nuño Beltrán de Guzmán became a hostile center of power. Guzmán and Cortés were political enemies; property and jurisdiction were reassigned amid reciprocal accusations. Cortés traveled to Castile in 1528 to defend himself personally.

## First Return to Castile, 1528–1530

Cortés arrived with treasure, exotic animals, Indigenous nobles and attendants, and his son Martín by Marina. He was received by Charles V and participated in court ceremonies. His public display converted American conquest into political capital, but the Crown would not restore him as unrestricted governor.

In 1529 Charles V granted:

- the hereditary title **Marqués del Valle de Oaxaca**;
- a lordship nominally encompassing twenty-three towns and approximately 23,000 vassals, though boundaries and population counts produced lasting disputes;
- renewed military authority as captain-general;
- rights connected with exploration of the Pacific or South Sea.

He married Juana de Zúñiga. A papal bull legitimized three natural children. Cortés sought the habit of Santiago for Martín, his son by Marina, and arranged his upbringing at court.

## The Marquesado and Return to New Spain, 1530–1540

Cortés and Juana returned to New Spain in 1530. Civil government was increasingly controlled by the second Audiencia and, from 1535, Viceroy Antonio de Mendoza.

### Territorial and business structure

The Marquesado was geographically discontinuous. Its principal centers included:

- Cuernavaca and surrounding sugar lands;
- the Valley of Oaxaca;
- Coyoacán and Toluca-related holdings;
- Tehuantepec, valued for Pacific access;
- houses and shops in Mexico City;
- mills, agricultural estates, mines, workshops, and ports.

Indigenous communities remained corporate and politically active, contesting tribute, land, labor, and jurisdiction through lawsuits and petitions. The estate was not simply private land in the modern sense; it combined seigneurial jurisdiction, tribute rights, grants, leases, commercial ventures, and contested royal privileges.

### Agriculture and sugar

Cortés developed sugar production near Cuernavaca, importing expensive machinery and employing enslaved Africans, enslaved Indigenous people, and compelled or wage labor. He invested in livestock, grain, orchards, and processing facilities.

### Mining

He held or financed mining interests, especially in areas including Taxco. Mines depended on capital equipment, Indigenous labor, enslaved workers, transport networks, and royal regulation. Litigation concerned both ownership and the obligations of Indigenous communities.

### Shipbuilding and Pacific exploration

Tehuantepec became a center for shipbuilding and Cortés’s Pacific ambitions. He contracted carpenters, blacksmiths, overseers, sailors, and suppliers; requisitioned Indigenous labor and timber; and financed expeditions seeking a strait, islands, pearls, trade routes, and access toward the Spice Islands.

Projects associated with his organization included:

- **Álvaro de Saavedra Cerón’s expedition**, 1527–1529, sent toward the Moluccas. Saavedra crossed the Pacific but failed to find a viable eastward return route and died at sea.
- **Diego Hurtado de Mendoza’s expedition**, 1532, which ended disastrously.
- **Diego de Becerra and Hernando de Grijalva’s expedition**, 1533; mutiny killed Becerra, while Fortún Jiménez reached the Baja California region and was killed.
- **Cortés’s own expedition**, 1535–1536, to the bay he named Santa Cruz, near present-day La Paz. Supply failures and harsh conditions forced abandonment.
- **Francisco de Ulloa’s expedition**, 1539–1540, which explored the Gulf of California and Pacific coast of Baja California and helped establish that the peninsula was not readily connected to the imagined wealthy islands sought by investors.

The frequent description of Cortés as “discoverer of California” requires qualification. Fortún Jiménez was the first European in Cortés’s enterprise known to have landed on the peninsula; Cortés followed with a colonizing expedition. Indigenous peoples had inhabited the region for millennia.

### Finances

Cortés became one of the wealthiest private persons in New Spain, but his assets were encumbered by:

- expedition debts;
- shipping losses;
- litigation;
- dowries and family settlements;
- court expenses;
- salaries and retainers;
- contested tribute and land rights;
- delayed or disputed royal payments.

His estate was extensive but illiquid and administratively complex. The image of limitless treasure ignores recurrent cash-flow problems and the high failure rate of maritime projects.

### Majorat

On 9 January 1535 at Colima, Cortés established a **mayorazgo**, binding core lands and privileges to hereditary succession. Included were royal-grant towns, residences, shops at Mexico City’s Plaza Mayor, mills, lake properties, South Sea acquisitions, and the patronage of his hospital.

### Relationship with Viceroy Mendoza

Cortés and Antonio de Mendoza cooperated intermittently but competed over jurisdiction, exploration, patronage, and resources. The Crown’s appointment of a viceroy confirmed that New Spain would not become Cortés’s personal government.

## Second Return to Castile and Algiers, 1540–1547

Cortés sailed for Spain in 1540 accompanied by his legitimate heir Martín and natural son Luis. Juana and their daughters remained in New Spain.

He pursued claims for reimbursement, jurisdiction, unpaid income, and recognition. His final years were not destitute, but they were dominated by litigation and frustration.

### Algiers, 1541

Cortés joined Charles V’s failed expedition against Algiers as a volunteer. A storm wrecked much of the imperial fleet. Later tradition says Cortés advised an aggressive assault and was ignored; he lost horses and valuables and escaped with difficulty. Claims that the emperor deliberately humiliated him are partly products of later heroic biography.

### “Who are you?” anecdote

A famous story says Cortés climbed onto Charles V’s carriage and, when challenged, replied: “I am the man who gave you more kingdoms than your ancestors left you cities.”

**Rumor or legend:** no secure contemporary source verifies the exchange. It dramatizes the real tension between Cortés’s sense of service and the monarchy’s refusal to grant him sovereign or gubernatorial authority.

### Final family arrangements

Cortés negotiated elite marriages for his children, maintained correspondence with Juana, supported his sons, and attempted to settle claims among legitimate and natural heirs. In 1539 he had divided one hundred enslaved Indigenous mine workers among his sons Martín the heir, Martín the Mestizo, and Luis.

His will provided:

- succession of the mayorazgo to the legitimate Martín;
- dowries or religious portions for daughters;
- annual incomes for natural sons;
- payment of debts and investigation of improperly held property or tribute;
- provisions affecting enslaved people;
- continuation of the hospital;
- eventual burial in New Spain.

The presence of restitution clauses has been interpreted variously—as conventional Christian preparation for death, an admission of uncertain titles, or genuine concern about unresolved obligations. It is not, by itself, proof of a comprehensive repudiation of conquest.

## Death and Burial History

Cortés made or confirmed his final testament on 12 October 1547. Ill and intending to sail again for New Spain, he moved from Seville to Castilleja de la Cuesta, where he died on 2 December.

His remains underwent several transfers:

1. First buried in the monastery of San Isidoro del Campo near Seville.
2. Transferred within the monastery according to family arrangements.
3. Sent to New Spain in 1566 and placed at San Francisco in Texcoco with his mother.
4. Moved in 1629 to the Franciscan church in Mexico City.
5. Transferred with ceremony in 1794 to the church adjoining the Hospital de Jesús.
6. After Mexican independence, fears that the tomb would be desecrated led Lucas Alamán and others to conceal the remains in 1823. A false story was circulated that they had been sent to Italy.
7. The hidden location was revealed and the remains authenticated by a Mexican scholarly commission in 1946. They were returned to a wall niche at the Hospital de Jesús church beneath a discreet plaque.

Relic fragments said to have been removed in 1946 were later held by the Gutiérrez-Colomer family and transferred to the Historical Archive of Cáceres. As reported in 2024, a proposed public display at Medellín remained stalled. These fragments should not be confused with the principal remains in Mexico City.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Malintzin / Marina

**Documented fact:** interpreter, diplomatic intermediary, mother of Cortés’s son Martín, and a visible participant in the campaigns of 1519–1524. She enabled communications central to alliances, threats, intelligence, and negotiations.

**Unresolved personal dimension:** sexual relations occurred, demonstrated by their child; whether there was affection, coercion, partnership, or a “romance” in the modern sense cannot be reconstructed. Marina had been transferred as an enslaved or dependent woman after warfare, creating an extreme imbalance of power.

### Motecuhzoma Xocoyotzin

Host, political counterpart, captive ruler, and central figure in Cortés’s legitimating narrative. Their meeting on 8 November 1519 is documented; the supposed voluntary surrender of sovereignty and recognition of Cortés as a returning god are disputed.

### Cuauhtémoc

Leader of Mexica resistance during the siege, captive after 13 August 1521, tortured for treasure, taken on the Honduras expedition, and executed on Cortés’s order in 1525.

### Diego Velázquez de Cuéllar

Early patron, governor, sponsor of the expedition, family connection through Catalina Suárez, and long-term legal rival. Their conflict shaped Cortés’s constitutional fiction at Veracruz and years of litigation.

### Indigenous allies

- **Tlaxcalan leaders:** Xicoténcatl the Elder, Maxixcatzin, and other factional rulers entered a negotiated alliance after warfare.
- **Xicoténcatl the Younger:** fought the Spaniards, later joined the coalition, and was executed in 1521 on Cortés’s order for alleged desertion. The charges and political context remain disputed.
- **Totonac leaders at Cempoala:** supplied the first major anti-Mexica alliance.
- **Ixtlilxochitl and Texcocan factions:** provided crucial military and political support in 1521.
- These were allies pursuing their own objectives, not merely Cortés’s auxiliaries.

### Spanish captains and collaborators

- **Pedro de Alvarado:** major captain; commanded at Tenochtitlan during the Toxcatl massacre.
- **Gonzalo de Sandoval:** trusted commander and companion.
- **Cristóbal de Olid:** captain who defected in Honduras.
- **Francisco de Montejo and Alonso Hernández Portocarrero:** representatives sent to court in 1519.
- **Jerónimo de Aguilar:** interpreter.
- **Bernal Díaz del Castillo:** expedition member and later chronicler who both admired and criticized Cortés.
- **Francisco López de Gómara:** Cortés’s chaplain or household associate in Spain and author of a strongly Cortés-centered history, published after Cortés’s death.
- **Andrés de Tapia:** captain and author of a conquest account.

### Royal and administrative network

- **Charles V:** sovereign, recipient of the *Cartas de relación*, source of Cortés’s governorship and noble title, and ultimate limiter of his authority.
- **Luis Ponce de León:** royal judge sent to conduct the residencia.
- **Nuño Beltrán de Guzmán:** president of the first Audiencia and bitter political enemy.
- **Antonio de Mendoza:** first viceroy of New Spain and institutional counterweight.
- **Pope Clement VII:** granted legitimations and ecclesiastical privileges.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

Cortés’s surviving intellectual record is practical rather than systematic. His worldview joined:

- Castilian monarchy and royal service;
- late-medieval theories of just war, vassalage, and jurisdiction;
- Catholic providentialism and conversion;
- humanist-inflected admiration for ordered cities and political hierarchy;
- legalistic self-representation;
- aristocratic lineage and hereditary lordship;
- mercantile and technological experimentation in ships, mines, mills, and sugar.

His letters describe Indigenous cities with admiration while subordinating them to a program of imperial possession. He praised Tenochtitlan’s markets, engineering, and political order but destroyed temples, seized rulers, and claimed sovereignty for Charles V.

His religious language cannot be dismissed as automatically insincere, nor can it be treated as disproving material motives. Conversion, royal expansion, personal honor, enrichment, and lordship operated together.

## Technologies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Technologies and logistics

Cortés was not an inventor in the patent sense. No patents are known. His importance lay in organizing available technologies:

- transatlantic and coastal sailing ships;
- lake brigantines assembled from transported components;
- horses, artillery, crossbows, firearms, steel weapons, and siege techniques;
- interpreters and multilingual diplomatic chains;
- notarial instruments and municipal corporations;
- sugar mills, mining furnaces, bellows, waterworks, roads, and ports.

### Institutions founded or reshaped

- Villa Rica de la Vera Cruz.
- Spanish municipal government at Mexico City.
- Hospital de la Concepción/Hospital de Jesús.
- Marquesado del Valle de Oaxaca.
- Shipyards and ports in Tehuantepec.
- Agricultural and industrial estates around Cuernavaca.

### Commercial structure

Cortés financed ventures through personal capital, loans, partnerships, royal privileges, tribute, compulsory labor, enslaved labor, rents, and reinvestment. His household included administrators, lawyers, factors, mayordomos, accountants, artisans, sailors, enslaved workers, servants, and Indigenous intermediaries.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Violence, and Financial Questions

### Unauthorized departure from Cuba

- **Documented fact:** Velázquez attempted to revoke or restrict Cortés’s command.
- **Cortés’s legal position:** Veracruz’s cabildo appointed him directly in the king’s name.
- **Opponents’ position:** the cabildo was engineered to conceal rebellion.
- **Outcome:** royal favor eventually superseded Velázquez, but the initial legality remained contested.

### Massacre at Cholula

- **Documented fact:** Cortés’s coalition killed many people at Cholula.
- **Claimed justification:** prevention of an ambush.
- **Disputed:** whether the plot was real, exaggerated, elicited under pressure, or constructed retrospectively.
- **No judicial acquittal** establishes the massacre as necessary.

### Captivity of Motecuhzoma

- **Documented fact:** Motecuhzoma was held under armed control.
- **Cortés’s representation:** the ruler accepted Spanish sovereignty.
- **Modern assessment:** the voluntary-transfer narrative is widely treated as a juridical fiction or heavily manipulated account.

### Torture of Cuauhtémoc and Tetlepanquetzal

After the fall of Tenochtitlan, Spaniards tortured Cuauhtémoc and the ruler of Tlacopan by burning or oiling their feet to force disclosure of hidden treasure.

- **Documented fact:** torture occurred under Cortés’s authority.
- **Cortés’s later presentation:** pressure came from dissatisfied royal officials and soldiers.
- Command responsibility remains his regardless of who demanded it.

### Execution of Cuauhtémoc

Documented execution following a summary conspiracy accusation; the evidence for the plot is disputed and no independent judicial proceeding established guilt.

### Xicoténcatl the Younger

Cortés ordered him hanged in 1521 for alleged desertion. Later nationalist and Tlaxcalan narratives alternately treat him as traitor, patriot, factional rival, or victim of coalition politics. The documentary record is insufficient to reduce him to one category.

### Enslavement, branding, and forced labor

Directly documented through campaign descriptions, contracts, grants, accounts, and Cortés’s family donation. Encomienda was legally distinct from slavery but facilitated coercive extraction. Cortés’s defenders sometimes emphasize his regulations against excessive abuse; those regulations do not erase his ownership of enslaved people or reliance on compulsory labor.

### Sexual coercion and distribution of women

Spanish forces received and redistributed women after battles and alliance negotiations. Marina herself entered Cortés’s company through such a transfer. Individual consent is usually undocumented. Modern romantic language should not obscure slavery, captive status, and power asymmetry, but neither should undocumented specific acts be labeled rape as judicially proven.

### Destruction and demographic catastrophe

Cortés’s coalition conducted siege warfare, massacres, enslavement, food destruction, and urban devastation. Epidemic disease caused immense additional mortality.

Calling Cortés personally the sole “destroyer of the Aztecs” obscures Indigenous alliances, autonomous Indigenous strategy, disease, factional politics, and later colonial institutions. Conversely, presenting the conquest solely as an Indigenous civil war obscures Spanish leadership, imperial objectives, weapons, legal claims, and the colonial order that followed.

### “Genocide”

**Contested retrospective classification:** no sixteenth-century court considered a charge called genocide, a twentieth-century legal concept. Some writers use the term broadly for conquest and colonial demographic destruction; others reject it because there is no demonstrated Cortés policy to exterminate all Nahua or Indigenous peoples as such. Documented massacres, enslavement, forced labor, dispossession, cultural destruction, and mass mortality should be stated directly rather than made dependent on a single retrospective label.

### The royal fifth and treasure

Cortés was repeatedly accused of concealing treasure, manipulating appraisal, diverting the king’s fifth, and favoring himself and his associates in distributions. Soldiers also accused him of taking an excessive share.

Some accounting disputes were inherent in lost treasure, informal wartime seizure, gifts, melting, transport, and competing claims. The residencia preserved allegations and defenses but produced no simple comprehensive finding that all accounting was honest or that all alleged fraud occurred.

### Deaths of royal officials

Rumors accused Cortés of poisoning Luis Ponce de León, Marcos de Aguilar, and others who died after arriving in Mexico.

- **Rumor or allegation:** poisoning.
- **Evidence:** political motive and clustered deaths.
- **Against:** infectious disease and poor health were common; no reliable forensic evidence or conviction exists.
- **Status:** unverified.

### Murder of Catalina Suárez

Unresolved, as detailed above. It must not be presented either as proven homicide or as definitively disproven.

### Conflict with the Audiencia

Nuño de Guzmán’s administration confiscated or redistributed some Cortés interests and pursued accusations against him. Guzmán himself was later prosecuted for abuses elsewhere. His misconduct does not automatically invalidate every allegation against Cortés; nor do accusations generated by his hostile administration automatically establish guilt.

### Recent two-year adversarial pass, 2024–2026

Searches combining Cortés’s name with scandal, accusations, fraud, conspiracy, lawsuit, criticism, and controversy produced no new evidence of a previously unknown sixteenth-century criminal proceeding. Recent disputes concern:

- public commemoration and whether monuments honor conquest;
- the stalled display of relic fragments in Extremadura;
- political demands for acknowledgment or apology concerning colonial violence;
- repatriation of a stolen 1527 Cortés manuscript;
- competing “liberator,” “founder,” “invader,” and “genocidal conqueror” narratives.

These are controversies over evidence, memory, public honor, and cultural property—not new criminal findings against Cortés.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Cortés burned his ships”

- **Story:** Cortés burned every ship so his men had no possibility of retreat.
- **Appearance and circulation:** later sixteenth-century histories, heroic literature, paintings, schoolbooks, and management rhetoric.
- **Evidence:** early accounts describe ships beached, disabled, dismantled, or sunk after they were judged unseaworthy; fittings were salvaged.
- **Assessment:** literal burning is a myth built around a real decision to eliminate independent retreat.
- **Effect:** made Cortés an emblem of audacity and irreversible commitment.

### “Motecuhzoma thought Cortés was Quetzalcoatl”

- **Story:** Mexica prophecy fixed 1519 as the year of Quetzalcoatl’s eastern return, so Motecuhzoma mistook Cortés for the god.
- **Sources:** Cortés’s constructed surrender speech; later Spanish chronicles; Sahagún’s postconquest compilation; subsequent nationalist and popular histories.
- **Against:** Camilla Townsend and other scholars find no secure preconquest evidence for a specific returning-god prophecy or Indigenous belief that the Spaniards were literally gods.
- **Competing view:** some scholars argue Nahuatl-language and early colonial traditions preserve genuine religious interpretation, though their texts were produced after conquest.
- **Status:** disputed postconquest narrative, not established explanation of Mexica policy.
- **Effect:** historically diminished Indigenous political rationality and magnified European inevitability.

### “A few hundred Spaniards conquered an empire”

- **Story:** Cortés’s tactical genius and European weapons allowed several hundred men to defeat millions.
- **Evidence against:** the coalition included very large Tlaxcalan, Texcocan, Totonac, and other Indigenous armies, plus logistical support and epidemic disruption.
- **Status:** misleading heroic compression.
- **Effect:** centered Cortés and European technology while erasing Indigenous decision-making.

### “Cortés liberated all Indigenous peoples from Aztec tyranny”

- **Story:** the campaign was primarily a liberation of oppressed tributaries.
- **Evidence:** some polities had strong grievances against Mexica tribute, warfare, and political domination and deliberately allied with Cortés.
- **Against universalization:** allies pursued local sovereignty and advantage; the resulting colonial system imposed encomienda, tribute, forced labor, religious transformation, and Spanish supremacy.
- **Status:** partly grounded in documented alliances but propagandistic when treated as universal liberation.

### “All Indigenous allies were traitors”

- **Story:** Tlaxcalans and Marina betrayed an already unified Mexican nation.
- **Evidence against:** no Mexican nation-state encompassing these groups existed in 1519; polities had distinct identities, interests, and conflicts.
- **Status:** later nationalist mythology.
- **Effect:** stigmatized Tlaxcalans and generated the Spanish word *malinchismo* from Marina’s name.

### “Malinche was Cortés’s lover and the mother of Mexico”

- **Story:** an intense romance united conqueror and interpreter, producing the archetypal first mestizo.
- **Evidence:** they had a son, Martín; she performed indispensable political and linguistic work.
- **Against:** no surviving source documents romantic love, and neither Martín nor their union was biologically the first mixed Indigenous-European family.
- **Status:** later romantic and national origin myth.
- **Effect:** alternately made Marina betrayer, violated mother, cultural translator, or co-founder.

### The “first mestizo”

Martín Cortés is often called the first mestizo of New Spain. Mixed-ancestry children existed earlier in the Caribbean and likely on the mainland. The claim is symbolic, not literal.

### Cortés weeping beneath the Tree of the Sad Night

No contemporary evidence securely identifies the tree or confirms the scene. The story localized Spanish defeat and became part of Mexico City’s commemorative landscape.

### Cortés as sole founder of modern Mexico

This is an ideological claim rather than a documentary fact. Spanish colonial institutions, Indigenous polities, African and Asian migrations, the viceroyal state, independence, nineteenth-century wars, and modern nation-building all contributed to Mexico. Cortés was a central agent in the violent creation of New Spain, not the sole author of Mexico.

### Cortés as uniquely monstrous or uniquely humane

Both poles flatten the record. Documentation supports negotiation, admiration, institutional construction, family concern, missionary patronage, massacre, torture, enslavement, coercive labor, self-enrichment, and political calculation. None cancels the others.

## Works by or Under the Name of Hernán Cortés

Cortés’s corpus is documentary rather than a conventional literary bibliography. Many texts were dictated to scribes, prepared through secretaries, or issued in his official name. “Authorship” therefore ranges from personal composition to authorized chancery production.

### The five *Cartas de relación*

1. **Primera relación**, 1519 — **lost or unlocated**. Its existence is mentioned in contemporary or near-contemporary sources. It must not be automatically identified with the Veracruz cabildo letter.
2. **Carta del cabildo de la Villa Rica de la Vera Cruz**, 10 July 1519 — corporate letter signed for the municipality and often printed as the First Relation. **Not solely authored by Cortés; Cortés’s influence is probable but the degree is disputed.**
3. **Segunda carta de relación**, Segura de la Frontera, 30 October 1520 — first printed at Seville in 1522. Describes entry into Tenochtitlan, Motecuhzoma’s captivity, Narváez, the uprising, and flight.
4. **Tercera carta de relación**, Coyoacán, 15 May 1522 — first printed at Seville in 1523. Describes recovery, siege, and fall of Tenochtitlan.
5. **Cuarta carta de relación**, Tenochtitlan, 15 October 1524 — first printed at Toledo in 1525. Reports government, expeditions, conversion, and resources.
6. **Quinta carta de relación**, Tenochtitlan, 3 September 1526 — circulated in manuscript and published much later. Narrates the Honduras expedition and Cortés’s return.

The numbering convention treats the lost 1519 letter or substituted cabildo letter as the first, leaving four extant personal relations numbered second through fifth.

### Other genuine, dictated, or officially issued documents

Hundreds survive, including:

- letters to Charles V and royal councils;
- letters to Cortés’s father, mother, wife, relatives, agents, and lawyers;
- private correspondence with the licentiate Francisco Núñez;
- instructions to captains, factors, shipmasters, and administrators;
- contracts for ships, mills, furnaces, craftsmen, and supplies;
- petitions for grants, offices, encomiendas, jurisdiction, and reimbursement;
- memorials on the government and conversion of New Spain;
- ordinances for settlers and encomenderos;
- letters to rulers and officials in the Pacific sphere, including the 1527 letter addressed to the ruler of Cebu;
- powers of attorney, receipts, accounts, donations, marriage settlements, and estate documents;
- responses and interrogatories in the *juicio de residencia*;
- the 1535 instrument founding the mayorazgo;
- the testament dated 12 October 1547 and related codicillary or probate records.

### Major editions

- *Cartas y relaciones de Hernán Cortés al emperador Carlos V*, edited by Pascual de Gayangos, 1866.
- *Letters of Cortés: The Five Letters of Relation to the Emperor Charles V*, translated and edited by Francis Augustus MacNutt, 1908.
- *Cartas de relación*, multiple modern editions; Ángel Delgado Gómez’s Castalia edition, 1993, is a major critical literary edition.
- José Luis Martínez, editor, *Documentos cortesianos*, four volumes, UNAM/Fondo de Cultura Económica, 1990–1992/1990–1999 depending bibliographic treatment.
- María del Carmen Martínez Martínez’s work on Cortés’s correspondence and epistolary networks substantially expands contextual understanding of the documents.

### Disputed or falsely enlarged authorship

- The Veracruz cabildo letter is often published under Cortés’s name but is formally a municipal corporate document.
- Christian Duverger has argued that Cortés secretly authored Bernal Díaz del Castillo’s *Historia verdadera de la conquista de la Nueva España*. This theory has not won general acceptance and conflicts with manuscript, biographical, and textual arguments supporting Bernal Díaz’s authorship.
- Francisco López de Gómara’s *Conquista de México* drew on Cortés and his circle but was written by Gómara after Cortés’s death; it is not a ghostwritten Cortés memoir established by evidence.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Sixteenth and seventeenth centuries

Gómara constructed Cortés as the central Christian and imperial hero of the conquest. Bernal Díaz admired his leadership but objected to Gómara’s erasure of ordinary soldiers and sometimes criticized Cortés’s distribution of wealth and decisions.

Cortés’s descendants inherited the Marquesado. In 1566, his legitimate son Martín, second marquis, and Martín the Mestizo became entangled in the so-called conspiracy of the marquis of the Valley. This occurred after Cortés’s death and must not be projected backward as his conspiracy.

### Colonial veneration and criticism

Imperial histories celebrated Cortés as a new Caesar, Alexander, or Moses. Critics of conquest used episodes of massacre, torture, forced conversion, and enslavement to expose colonial violence. Indigenous histories preserved more complex images: destroyer, military partner, foreign lord, or figure subordinated to the actions of Indigenous allies.

### Independence-era Mexico

Cortés became politically dangerous as a symbol of Spanish dominion. His tomb was concealed in 1823 partly because of fears that his remains might be destroyed. Nationalist narratives elevated Cuauhtémoc and stigmatized Cortés and Marina.

### Nineteenth-century Romantic history

William Prescott’s history made Cortés a globally recognized figure of courage, ambition, political genius, and ruthless conquest. Painting and opera amplified encounters with Motecuhzoma and Marina.

### Twentieth-century reinterpretation

Mexican thought frequently treated Cortés and Marina as symbolic parents of a conflicted mestizo nation. Octavio Paz’s discussion of *la Malinche* profoundly shaped popular language, though it was literary-cultural analysis rather than archival biography.

Professional historians increasingly emphasized:

- Indigenous allies and Indigenous political agency;
- epidemic disease;
- the conquest as a prolonged process rather than a single Spanish victory;
- Cortés’s legal and rhetorical self-fashioning;
- colonial labor systems and slavery;
- women’s roles and coercive conditions;
- the unreliability of Quetzalcoatl and “burned ships” legends.

### Twenty-first-century controversy

Cortés remains a symbol in disputes between Mexican and Spanish political actors over apology, colonial violence, national identity, and monuments. Statues and commemorations have drawn both defense and protest.

Recent scholarship generally rejects both the lone-genius model and a narrative in which Spaniards were marginal spectators to an Indigenous civil war. The reconstruction instead centers a coalition organized through Cortés’s expedition but made militarily possible by Indigenous forces, amid epidemic disease and competing regional states.

## Atlas Connections

Only direct, source-supported intersections with the supplied atlas roster are included. Mere shared period, geographical proximity, or later comparison is excluded.

### Motecuhzoma Xocoyotzin

- **Evidentiary label:** Documented fact.
- **Connection:** political counterpart, host, and captive ruler.
- **Dates and places:** embassies exchanged on the Gulf coast and campaign route in 1519; personal meeting on 8 November 1519 at Tenochtitlan; Motecuhzoma held under Spanish control until his death in June 1520.
- **Qualification:** the exact speeches and alleged voluntary transfer of sovereignty survive through interested accounts and are disputed.

### Margarita / Marina / Pelagia

The atlas entry appears to encompass the historical woman best known as **Malintzin, Marina, or La Malinche**.

- **Evidentiary label:** Documented fact.
- **Connection:** interpreter, negotiator, diplomatic intermediary, travel companion, sexual partner, and mother of Cortés’s son Martín.
- **Dates and places:** entered the expedition’s possession in Tabasco in March 1519; active from the Gulf coast through Tlaxcala, Cholula, Tenochtitlan, and later on the Honduras journey, 1519–1524/25.
- **Qualification:** descriptions of romantic love are later speculation; the structural coercion inherent in her transferred and dependent status is documented, but her private intentions are not.

### Francisco Pizarro González

- **Evidentiary label:** Disputed claim.
- **Connection:** both were Extremaduran conquistadors; later genealogies describe distant family ties through Pizarro lines.
- **Dates and places:** both were in the orbit of Charles V’s court around the late 1520s, but a securely documented personal meeting was not established in the consulted sources.
- **Qualification:** popular claims that they were close second cousins, met exactly once, or maintained a personal rivalry should be treated as unverified unless supported by a specific genealogical or correspondence record.

### Christopher Columbus

- **Evidentiary label:** Documented institutional influence; no personal meeting.
- **Connection:** Columbus’s voyages and the colonial institutions subsequently created in Hispaniola formed the Atlantic system into which Cortés migrated.
- **Dates and places:** Cortés reached Hispaniola in 1504, two years before Columbus’s death. No meeting is documented.
- **Qualification:** this is a documented historical influence of institutions and precedent, not a personal relationship.

### Alexander the Great and Gaius Julius Caesar

- **Evidentiary label:** Documented posthumous symbolic association; not a life connection.
- **Connection:** early modern and later authors compared Cortés’s conquests and ambition to Alexander and Caesar.
- **Qualification:** no direct influence explicitly acknowledged by Cortés was established in the sources consulted. These are reception-history comparisons, not personal crossings.

### Atahualpa

- **Evidentiary label:** No documented personal connection.
- **Finding:** Cortés did not participate in Atahualpa’s capture or execution, and no correspondence or meeting between them was found. Their names were joined later as parallel symbols of Spanish conquests in Mexico and Peru.

### El Inca Garcilaso de la Vega

- **Evidentiary label:** No documented personal connection.
- **Finding:** Garcilaso’s historical writing belongs to the broader corpus of conquest historiography but postdates Cortés’s death; no meeting or correspondence was found.

### Felipe Guamán Poma de Ayala and Blas Valera

- **Evidentiary label:** No documented personal connection.
- **Finding:** both belong to later Andean colonial intellectual history. No direct crossing with Cortés was identified.

### Hernán Cortés

- The subject appears in the atlas roster itself; this is not counted as a connection.

### Absence finding

No documentary crossing was found with the remaining roster members. Most belong to other centuries or regions. Similarity of occupation, later artistic representation, or shared inclusion in histories of empire does not by itself constitute an Atlas connection.
