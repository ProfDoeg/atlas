# Jean-Paul Charles Aymard Sartre: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

This dossier concerns the French philosopher, novelist, playwright, critic, editor, journalist, and political activist **Jean-Paul Charles Aymard Sartre (1905–1980)**.

Labels used for contested material:

- **Documented fact**: supported by primary records, Sartre’s published writings or correspondence, institutional archives, or convergent scholarship.
- **Reported fact**: supported by credible testimony or scholarship but not independently established here from a primary record.
- **Allegation**: a claim made by an identified person that remains partly contested or cannot be independently proved.
- **Interpretation**: a scholarly or political reading of documented events.
- **Rumor or myth**: a circulating story inadequately supported by evidence.

The dossier draws especially on the Bibliothèque nationale de France’s Sartre exhibition and catalogues, the Institut des textes et manuscrits modernes manuscript catalogue, Nobel records, contemporary press archives, Sartre’s own publications and correspondence, and major biographical and philosophical scholarship.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full registered name:** Jean-Paul Charles Aymard Sartre.
- **Common name:** Jean-Paul Sartre.
- **Family nickname:** **Poulou**, used during childhood.
- **ENS friendship nickname:** Sartre and Paul Nizan were collectively called **“Nitre et Sarzan,”** a playful fusion of their surnames.
- **Pseudonyms in wartime political activity:** Sartre’s clandestine resistance group used organizational anonymity, but no durable literary pseudonym became attached to him.
- **Titles and occupations:** philosopher; novelist; short-story writer; playwright; screenwriter; literary and art critic; biographer; autobiographer; editor; journalist; lecturer; secondary-school philosophy teacher; political activist.
- **Nationality:** French.
- **Birth:** **21 June 1905**, at his maternal grandparents’ home, rue Mignard, Paris’s 16th arrondissement.
- **Death:** **15 April 1980**, Hôpital Broussais, Paris’s 14th arrondissement.
- **Cause of death:** pulmonary edema in the setting of uremia and prolonged declining health.
- **Burial:** cremated at Père-Lachaise; ashes interred in Montparnasse Cemetery. Simone de Beauvoir was buried beside him in 1986.
- **Marital status:** never married.
- **Principal lifelong partner:** Simone de Beauvoir, from 1929 until Sartre’s death.
- **Children:** no biological children are documented.
- **Adopted daughter and literary heir:** Arlette Elkaïm-Sartre, legally adopted as an adult in 1964.
- **Religion:** raised between Catholic paternal and Protestant maternal family traditions; became an atheist.
- **Principal intellectual identifications:** phenomenology, existentialism, existential psychoanalysis, philosophical anthropology, Marxism understood as an unfinished historical framework, anticolonialism, and theories of politically “committed” literature.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal family

Sartre’s father, **Jean-Baptiste Eymard Sartre (1874–1906)**, came from a Catholic medical family associated with Thiviers in the Dordogne. He entered the École Polytechnique in 1895, became a French naval officer, and served in East Asia. He married **Anne-Marie Schweitzer** in Paris in May 1904.

Jean-Baptiste died in September 1906, when Jean-Paul was approximately fifteen months old. The standard account attributes the death to an infectious tropical illness—commonly identified as yellow fever—contracted in connection with service in Indochina. Sartre therefore retained no personal memory of his father.

In *Les Mots*, Sartre provocatively represented his father’s early death as a liberation from paternal authority. That formulation belongs to a deliberately constructed autobiography written decades later and should not be treated as an unmediated childhood recollection.

### Maternal family

His mother, **Anne-Marie Madeleine Schweitzer (1882–1969)**, belonged to an Alsatian Protestant family of teachers and intellectuals. She was related to theologian, physician, musician, and humanitarian **Albert Schweitzer**; the exact relationship is usually described as first cousins once removed or, more loosely, cousins.

After being widowed, Anne-Marie returned with her child to her parents:

- **Charles Schweitzer (1844–1935):** German-language teacher, author of educational textbooks, and the dominant intellectual figure of Sartre’s childhood.
- **Louise Guillemin Schweitzer:** Sartre’s maternal grandmother.

Charles introduced the child to French and German literature and gave him access to a substantial household library. Sartre later treated this library as the central theater of his childhood: books preceded direct experience, and he initially mistook literary classification for knowledge of the world.

Anne-Marie married **Joseph Mancy**, an engineer and École Polytechnique graduate, in 1917. Sartre resented the remarriage, later describing it as a betrayal that displaced him from an unusually intimate mother-son household. The family moved to La Rochelle, where the adolescent Sartre had a difficult school and social experience.

Sartre was an only child. No full or half-siblings are documented.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1905–1921

Following his father’s death, Sartre grew up principally in the Schweitzer household in Paris and Meudon. He was privately instructed for part of childhood and learned to read early. His grandfather encouraged literary performance, intellectual precocity, and a self-image centered on authorship.

Sartre had a serious disorder of the right eye, variously described as a childhood corneal or lenticular condition followed by amblyopia, sensory exotropia, and progressive loss of sight. The visibly divergent eye later contributed to his distinctive appearance. Retrospective accounts connect his appearance with adolescent bullying, although the extent and precise motives cannot be quantified.

At approximately ten, he entered the Lycée Henri-IV. His early childhood had involved limited ordinary peer contact; school confronted him with a social world not organized around the family’s admiration.

The 1917 move to **La Rochelle**, following Anne-Marie’s remarriage, was formative. Sartre later depicted local schoolboys as violent and himself as isolated, physically vulnerable, and compelled to invent protective identities. Because these descriptions come partly from later autobiographical reconstruction, their psychological interpretation remains his own.

He also read adventure literature, popular serials, classical authors, Victor Hugo, and French school canon. Cinema became another early enthusiasm. His later autobiographical writing presented childhood authorship as theatrical: he wrote partly to occupy the heroic cultural role the adults around him had prepared for him.

In 1920–1921 he returned to Paris and Henri-IV. He renewed or intensified his friendship with **Paul Nizan**, who became his closest youthful intellectual companion.

## Education and Formation, 1921–1931

### Lycées and École normale supérieure

After the baccalauréat, Sartre and Nizan entered the preparatory class at **Lycée Louis-le-Grand**. In 1924 both won admission to the **École normale supérieure**, rue d’Ulm. Sartre’s cohort included **Raymond Aron**, Georges Canguilhem, Daniel Lagache, and others who later became major French intellectuals.

The ENS years combined formal study with pranks, amateur performance, philosophical argument, and sustained writing. Sartre participated in an antimilitarist revue in 1927 whose notoriety became associated with director Gustave Lanson’s resignation. The precise causal claim that Sartre’s prank alone forced Lanson out is better treated as campus legend attached to a documented scandal.

His principal curriculum included philosophy, psychology, logic, ethics, sociology, and the history of philosophy. Important intellectual materials included:

- René Descartes and French rationalism;
- Immanuel Kant and French neo-Kantianism;
- Henri Bergson, especially the analysis of time and immediate consciousness;
- Edmund Husserl’s phenomenology;
- Martin Heidegger’s ontology;
- Hegel, increasingly important for intersubjectivity and history;
- Marx, initially less central than later;
- Freud, whom Sartre criticized while adapting the idea that behavior expresses a total psychic project.

Sartre failed the philosophy **agrégation** in 1928. Later narratives blamed excessive originality, but this is retrospective explanation rather than a demonstrable jury finding. He prepared again in 1929 and placed first. Simone de Beauvoir placed second; Jean Hyppolite and Paul Nizan also passed.

### Simone de Beauvoir

Sartre met **Simone Lucie Ernestine Marie Bertrand de Beauvoir** during the 1929 agrégation period. They became a couple that autumn. Their relationship was neither marriage nor a conventional shared household. They distinguished their “necessary” bond from “contingent” affairs and promised extensive disclosure to one another.

This pact remained central for fifty-one years, but surviving letters show disparities between its idealized public presentation and its operation. Both concealed or manipulated information at times; younger lovers were sometimes incorporated into a social network they called “the family.” Beauvoir and Sartre nevertheless remained one another’s most durable intellectual readers, correspondents, financial allies, and emotional reference points.

### Military service

Sartre completed obligatory military service in meteorology. He developed an enduring friendship and intellectual rivalry with Raymond Aron, whose report of phenomenology as a method capable of analyzing ordinary objects helped prompt Sartre’s German studies.

## Early Teaching Career and Philosophical Formation, 1931–1939

### Le Havre

In 1931 Sartre received a philosophy teaching appointment at the **Lycée du Havre**. His provincial teaching years furnished material for *La Nausée*, whose Bouville is a fictional transformation of Le Havre.

Students remembered an unconventional teacher who challenged academic solemnity. He wrote fiction and philosophy intensively but struggled initially to publish. A manuscript known at different stages as *Factum sur la contingence* and *Melancholia* became *La Nausée* after extensive revision and editorial intervention.

### Berlin, 1933–1934

Sartre succeeded Raymond Aron at the **Institut français in Berlin** for the 1933–1934 academic year. He studied Husserl, Heidegger, and Max Scheler. This was not a formal discipleship under Heidegger: Sartre read him and later transformed his concepts, but no teacher-student relationship existed.

Germany was already under Nazi government. Sartre later acknowledged that his political awareness remained limited; he concentrated on phenomenology rather than making the regime the center of his Berlin experience.

### Mescaline episode

In 1935 Sartre participated in a medically supervised mescaline experiment conducted by psychiatrist **Daniel Lagache**. He experienced disturbing hallucinations, including persistent imagery of crabs or sea creatures. Sartre and Beauvoir later incorporated the “crabs” into their private mythology. Claims that he was permanently psychotic are unsupported; he described lingering anxiety and visual aftereffects but continued teaching and writing.

### Laon and Paris

Sartre taught briefly at Laon and then at the Lycée Pasteur in Neuilly. His prewar publications established him in distinct fields:

- *La Transcendance de l’Ego* argued that the ego is an object for consciousness, not a resident substance within it.
- *L’Imagination* and *L’Imaginaire* developed a phenomenology of image-consciousness.
- *La Nausée* dramatized contingency through Antoine Roquentin.
- *Le Mur* explored death, bad faith, sexuality, violence, and absurdity.

*La Nausée* appeared in 1938 after Gallimard had rejected earlier versions. It was a critical success rather than an immediate mass-market phenomenon.

## War, Captivity, Occupation, and Resistance, 1939–1944

### Mobilization and war diaries

Mobilized in September 1939, Sartre served as a meteorological soldier. He wrote voluminous notebooks and letters, later published as *Carnets de la drôle de guerre* and within the correspondence with Beauvoir.

His wartime notebooks record rapid ethical and political development, alongside affairs and tensions within the Beauvoir circle.

### Capture and imprisonment

German forces captured Sartre at **Padoux in Lorraine on 21 June 1940**. After transfers through Nancy and Baccarat, he was sent to **Stalag XII-D near Trier**.

At the camp he:

- read Heidegger;
- lectured fellow prisoners;
- associated with Catholic priests, including Marius Perrin;
- wrote and staged the Christmas play *Bariona, ou le Fils du tonnerre* in December 1940.

Sartre was released in early 1941. Accounts differ over whether the release resulted strictly from his eye disability, medical certification, or assistance and administrative maneuvering. An escape story sometimes appears in popular retellings, but the strongest documentary account is medical release, not a dramatic escape.

### Paris under occupation

Back in Paris, Sartre helped form **Socialisme et Liberté**, a small, short-lived resistance discussion group. Participants included Beauvoir and Maurice Merleau-Ponty. Sartre traveled in the unoccupied zone seeking support from André Gide and André Malraux, without creating an effective national organization. The group dissolved.

He resumed teaching and accepted a post at the **Lycée Condorcet**. The predecessor associated with that position, Henri Dreyfus-Le Foyer, had been removed under anti-Jewish legislation.

- **Documented fact:** Sartre occupied a teaching position made available in a system that had expelled Jewish teachers.
- **Unresolved question:** evidence does not establish conclusively what Sartre knew at the moment of appointment or whether he personally displaced Dreyfus-Le Foyer.
- **Interpretation:** critics call the appointment opportunistic or “shadow collaboration”; defenders note that Sartre neither authored the racial law nor controlled the appointment. The event complicates later heroic accounts of uninterrupted resistance.

Sartre published legally under occupation. *L’Être et le Néant* appeared in 1943. *Les Mouches* was staged that June under Charles Dullin, and *Huis clos* in 1944.

The plays passed German and Vichy-era censorship. Contemporary collaborationist reviewers often disliked *Les Mouches* without identifying its anti-occupation allegory; clandestine or resistance-oriented readers perceived themes of freedom against imposed guilt.

- **Documented fact:** the works were legally performed and reviewed in occupied Paris.
- **Interpretation:** they can be read as resistance drama.
- **Disputed mythology:** the later image of *Les Mouches* as an obvious public call to resistance exaggerates how clearly its politics were recognized in 1943.
- **Counter-myth:** the claim that legal publication alone made Sartre a collaborator is not supported by evidence of political service to Germany.

Sartre contributed to *Combat* during the liberation of Paris, writing reports collected as “Un promeneur dans Paris insurgé.” His resistance record was real but limited compared with clandestine organizers exposed to prolonged risks of arrest, torture, or execution.

## Postwar Ascendancy, 1944–1951

The immediate postwar period made Sartre an international celebrity.

### Les Temps modernes

In 1945 Sartre, Beauvoir, Merleau-Ponty, Raymond Aron, Michel Leiris, Jean Paulhan, and others established **Les Temps modernes**. Sartre served as founding director and principal symbolic authority. The journal linked literature to political responsibility and published philosophy, fiction, reportage, anticolonial analysis, and polemic.

Sartre did not principally operate it as a profit-seeking business. Publishing income, theatrical royalties, translations, and speaking engagements eventually made him financially independent. He used significant sums to support lovers, friends, activists, publications, and political causes.

### American journeys

Sartre traveled to the United States in 1945 and 1946 as a journalist and lecturer. He visited New York and other cities, reported on American society, and developed a relationship with American writer **Dolores Vanetti Ehrenreich**, known in correspondence as “Dolores.”

His American reports combined fascination with technology and urban scale with criticism of racism, segregation, conformism, and capitalist social relations.

### Existentialism as a public phenomenon

The lecture “L’existentialisme est un humanisme,” delivered in Paris in October 1945 and published in 1946, popularized the formula that existence precedes essence. Sartre maintained that human beings first exist and subsequently define themselves through acts for which they bear responsibility.

The Saint-Germain-des-Prés nightclub image—black clothes, jazz, cafés, sexual unconventionality—became attached to existentialism. Sartre and Beauvoir participated in the milieu but did not create every fashion marketed under their names.

### Literature and drama

Major postwar works included:

- *Les Chemins de la liberté*, an unfinished novel cycle;
- *Morts sans sépulture*;
- *La Putain respectueuse*, addressing American racism;
- *Les Mains sales*, on revolutionary politics and political murder;
- *Baudelaire*;
- the essays later collected as *Situations*.

### Rassemblement démocratique révolutionnaire

In 1948 Sartre helped found the **Rassemblement démocratique révolutionnaire (RDR)** with David Rousset and Georges Altman. It sought a democratic socialist alternative independent of both American capitalism and Stalinist communism. The movement failed to develop a mass constituency and dissolved by 1949.

This failure and the intensification of the Cold War contributed to Sartre’s later tactical alignment with the French Communist Party, although he never joined it.

## Communism, Anticolonialism, and the Camus Break, 1952–1961

### Camus rupture

Sartre and **Albert Camus** had met during the occupation and became friends, collaborators, and public representatives of postwar literary revolt. Camus worked at *Combat*; Sartre wrote for it.

The rupture followed Camus’s *L’Homme révolté* (1951). Francis Jeanson published a severe review in *Les Temps modernes*. Camus replied directly to Sartre; Sartre answered with a letter attacking Camus’s political reasoning and intellectual competence. Their friendship ended.

The dispute concerned revolutionary violence, Soviet communism, history, and the intellectual’s role. Later retellings often reduce it to “Camus against totalitarianism, Sartre for Stalinism,” but their positions and evolution were more complicated. Sartre nevertheless made political claims about the Soviet system that were demonstrably false or gravely misleading.

### Relations with the Communist movement

From approximately 1952 Sartre became a **compagnon de route**, or fellow traveler, of the French Communist Party. He argued for cooperation with the Communist working-class movement while retaining formal independence.

After visiting the Soviet Union in 1954, he publicly described a high degree of freedom of criticism. This statement was contradicted by the Soviet censorship and repression then in force. Sartre later acknowledged that he had lied or suppressed criticism because he believed public truth-telling at that moment would harm political objectives.

- **Documented fact:** Sartre made the favorable statement.
- **Documented fact:** the Soviet Union did not possess the freedom of criticism he described.
- **Interpretation:** this is widely treated as one of his clearest cases of politically motivated dishonesty.

He condemned the Soviet invasion of Hungary in 1956 and broke sharply with the French Communist Party over its response. He never returned to uncomplicated Soviet alignment.

### Saint Genet

*Saint Genet, comédien et martyr* (1952) interpreted Jean Genet’s life as the conversion of social stigmatization into freely assumed identity and literary practice. Genet reportedly found the totalizing analysis psychologically devastating and wrote little for several years. Sartre’s method has been praised as existential biography and criticized as appropriating its subject into an intellectual system.

### Anticolonial activity

Sartre supported anticolonial struggles in Indochina, Algeria, and sub-Saharan Africa. He defended Algerian independence, condemned torture by French forces, supported the right of soldiers to refuse participation in the Algerian War, and signed the **Manifesto of the 121** in 1960.

The right-wing **Organisation armée secrète (OAS)** bombed his residence on rue Bonaparte twice, in 1961 and 1962. Sartre was not killed. Crowds later reportedly chanted “OAS, assassins; Sartre is our friend.”

His preface to Frantz Fanon’s *Les Damnés de la terre* used deliberately violent rhetoric about colonizer and colonized. It became one of his most controversial texts, read alternatively as diagnosis, endorsement of revolutionary violence, or rhetorical appropriation of Fanon.

### Cuba and revolutionary travel

Sartre and Beauvoir visited revolutionary Cuba in 1960, meeting **Fidel Castro** and **Ernesto “Che” Guevara**. Sartre initially praised the revolution’s improvisational energy. He later criticized the Castro government, particularly after the 1971 arrest and coerced self-criticism of poet Heberto Padilla.

## Mature Philosophy and Public Intervention, 1960–1967

### Critique of Dialectical Reason

*Critique de la raison dialectique*, volume I, appeared in 1960. Sartre attempted to reconcile existential freedom with Marxist historical explanation. Central concepts included scarcity, seriality, the group-in-fusion, institutionalization, and the practico-inert.

The projected second volume remained unfinished. Sartre’s lifelong pattern of enormous but incomplete projects also affected *Les Chemins de la liberté*, his ethics, the second volume of the *Critique*, and the Flaubert biography.

### Nobel Prize, 1964

The Swedish Academy awarded Sartre the **1964 Nobel Prize in Literature**. He tried to notify the Academy in advance that he would refuse it, but the decision had already been made.

He explained that he consistently declined official honors, did not wish to be transformed into an institution, and believed a writer should retain independence from both Eastern and Western establishments. He distinguished his refusal from hostility toward the Swedish Academy.

A persistent story says Sartre later asked for the prize money. Evidence for a formal attempt to reverse his refusal is weak and inconsistent; it should be treated as rumor unless supported by a specific archival document. He remained officially the laureate despite declining the medal and money.

### *Les Mots*

Published in 1964, *Les Mots* reconstructed childhood through the paired categories “Lire” and “Écrire.” It is a literary autobiography, not a neutral record. Sartre dismantled his own childhood mythology of the predestined writer even as he produced a highly controlled new mythology.

### Vietnam and the Russell Tribunal

Sartre served as executive president of the **International War Crimes Tribunal**, organized with Bertrand Russell to investigate American conduct in Vietnam. Sessions took place in Stockholm and Roskilde in 1967.

- **Documented fact:** the tribunal gathered testimony and rendered findings condemning American actions.
- **Documented limitation:** it possessed no governmental jurisdiction, subpoena power, or neutral judicial mandate.
- **Interpretation:** supporters regarded it as a tribunal of conscience responding to institutional impunity; critics regarded its composition and procedure as politically predetermined.

## Radical Politics, Maoism, and May 1968, 1968–1974

### May 1968

During the May 1968 revolt Sartre publicly supported students and striking workers. He visited the occupied Sorbonne, spoke with activists, and interviewed Daniel Cohn-Bendit. Students treated him with a mixture of respect and impatience; a famous slogan, “Sartre, be brief,” symbolized generational displacement.

President Charles de Gaulle reportedly rejected a proposal to arrest Sartre with the words, “One does not arrest Voltaire.” The remark is widely repeated but inadequately documented in a contemporaneous primary record. It should be labeled **reported anecdote**, not verbatim established fact.

### Maoist associations

After 1968 Sartre aligned with young Maoist militants, especially the **Gauche prolétarienne**. When the government suppressed *La Cause du peuple*, Sartre lent his name as director and sold the newspaper in the street, making arrest politically difficult.

His collaboration with **Pierre Victor**, later known as **Benny Lévy**, became increasingly important. Lévy served as his secretary in Sartre’s last years and participated in extended philosophical conversations.

Sartre’s Maoism was not sustained adherence to the Chinese state’s entire doctrine. It was a shifting alliance with French militants whom he believed were developing direct popular action outside established parties. Nevertheless, he sometimes romanticized revolutionary violence and subordinated evidentiary caution to militancy.

### Munich massacre article

After Black September killed Israeli athletes during the 1972 Munich Olympics, Sartre published “À propos de Munich” in *La Cause du peuple/J’accuse*.

- **Documented fact:** Sartre contextualized Palestinian terrorism as the weapon available to an oppressed people in an unequal war.
- **Documented limitation:** contextualization included language widely read as justification and did not center the murdered hostages as individual victims.
- **Later controversy:** contemporary and later critics accused him of defending the massacre.
- **Nuance:** Sartre simultaneously affirmed Israel’s legitimacy at other points and sought Israeli-Palestinian dialogue. His position combined support for Israel’s existence, sympathy with Jewish historical vulnerability, and support for Palestinian national claims.

### Libération

In 1973 Sartre joined Serge July, Philippe Gavi, Maurice Clavel, and others in establishing the daily newspaper **Libération**. He served as its first nominal director. The paper initially sought collective organization, refusal of conventional advertising and hierarchy, and a direct link to radical social movements.

Sartre’s deteriorating eyesight limited operational control. The modern corporate history of *Libération* should not be confused with Sartre’s personal ownership; he was a political sponsor and founding director, not a conventional media proprietor accumulating equity wealth.

## Later Life, Blindness, and Final Intellectual Dispute, 1973–1980

### Health decline

Sartre worked at extraordinary intensity and was a heavy smoker. Biographical testimony records extensive use of stimulants—especially **Corydrane**, an aspirin-and-amphetamine preparation—to maintain long writing schedules. He also consumed alcohol and sedatives.

By the early 1970s he had hypertension, vascular and renal problems, and severe visual impairment. In 1973 he became almost completely blind. He could no longer read or write normally and relied on assistants, recorded conversation, and dictation.

These practices affected but did not wholly end his activity. He continued interviews, political appearances, and discussions with Benny Lévy.

### Baader visit

In December 1974 Sartre visited imprisoned West German militant **Andreas Baader** at Stuttgart-Stammheim prison, intending to protest conditions of incarceration. Afterward Sartre criticized the prison regime but privately or through his interpreter reportedly found Baader politically inadequate or “an idiot.” The visit demonstrated Sartre’s concern with prisoner treatment, not proven endorsement of every act committed by the Red Army Faction.

### Jerusalem doctorate

In 1976 Sartre accepted an honorary doctorate connected with the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, reportedly because he considered the occasion politically useful for dialogue. This was unusual given his refusal of most official honors.

### “Hope Now” controversy

Benny Lévy’s conversations with Sartre were published shortly before Sartre’s death as **“L’Espoir maintenant”** in *Le Nouvel Observateur*. They appeared to revise earlier positions concerning revolution, ethics, Jewish history, and hope.

- **Documented fact:** Sartre participated in and authorized interviews.
- **Dispute:** Beauvoir and members of the older Sartrean circle alleged that Lévy had manipulated a blind, physically weakened man.
- **Counter-position:** Lévy and defenders maintained that Sartre remained intellectually responsible and was continuing a genuine philosophical development.
- **Unresolved question:** Sartre’s exact degree of authorship over particular formulations cannot be reconstructed solely from the published text.
- **Historical effect:** the dispute created competing images of a final “conversion,” an imposed repudiation of Sartre, or a late ethical reorientation.

Sartre did not formally convert to Judaism or embrace religious orthodoxy. Claims of a deathbed religious conversion are unsupported.

## Death and Funeral

Sartre entered Hôpital Broussais suffering from severe systemic illness. He died on **15 April 1980**, aged seventy-four, of pulmonary edema associated with uremia.

His funeral procession on **19 April 1980** drew an estimated **tens of thousands**, frequently stated as approximately 50,000 people. The unorganized crowd accompanied the cortege through Montparnasse.

The funeral was subsequently mythologized as “the last demonstration of May 1968” and the burial of the French universal public intellectual. Those descriptions are interpretations of the crowd’s symbolism, not literal organizational facts.

## Residences, Travels, and Geographical Movements

Principal documented locations included:

- **1905–1917:** Paris and Meudon, in the Schweitzer household.
- **1917–1920:** La Rochelle following his mother’s remarriage.
- **1920s:** Paris; Henri-IV, Louis-le-Grand, ENS rue d’Ulm.
- **1931–1936:** teaching posts centered on Le Havre, with a Berlin study year in 1933–1934 and later Laon.
- **Late 1930s:** Neuilly and Paris.
- **1939–1940:** military postings in northeastern France.
- **1940–1941:** captivity near Trier.
- **Occupation:** Paris; rooms and hotels associated with Saint-Germain-des-Prés, including Hôtel La Louisiane.
- **Postwar:** frequent use of cafés and hotels rather than a conventional shared domestic household with Beauvoir.
- **Rue Bonaparte:** a major postwar residence; twice bombed by the OAS.
- **After 1962:** Boulevard Raspail/Montparnasse area.
- **International travel:** Germany, United States, Soviet Union, China, Cuba, Brazil, Scandinavia, Israel, Egypt, and other European and political destinations.

Important journeys included:

- Berlin for phenomenological study;
- United States reporting trips;
- Soviet and Eastern European cultural delegations;
- 1955 visit to China;
- 1960 Cuba and Brazil journey with Beauvoir;
- Stockholm and Roskilde for the Vietnam tribunal;
- Middle Eastern travel and Israeli-Palestinian dialogue;
- Stuttgart to visit Baader.

Exact itineraries of every lecture and political journey exceed the surviving consolidated chronology and should be reconstructed from correspondence, passports, press files, and *Les Temps modernes* archives.

## Employment, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Teaching appointments

Sartre taught philosophy at institutions including:

- Lycée du Havre;
- Lycée de Laon;
- Lycée Pasteur, Neuilly;
- Lycée Condorcet, Paris.

Following postwar literary success, he left full-time state teaching.

### Publishing and editorial structures

His primary publisher was **Éditions Gallimard**. Royalties from novels, plays, essays, translations, theatrical productions, and foreign editions became his principal financial base.

Major institutional projects:

- **Les Temps modernes:** founder, director, editor, and contributor.
- **Rassemblement démocratique révolutionnaire:** co-founder.
- **Russell Tribunal:** executive president.
- **Secours rouge and prisoner/militant campaigns:** public sponsor or participant.
- **La Cause du peuple/J’accuse:** nominal director and contributor during repression.
- **Libération:** founding political sponsor and first director.

### Wealth and money

Sartre became financially successful but did not build a conventional business empire. He lived in hotels and relatively modest apartments, while distributing money across an extensive personal and political network. Correspondence and biographies record financial support for Beauvoir, lovers, adopted family, publications, and activists.

No significant evidence has emerged of patents, industrial technologies, private corporations, money laundering, investment fraud, offshore structures, or major real-estate accumulation.

Adult adoption of Arlette Elkaïm had personal and inheritance consequences. After Sartre’s death, rivalry developed between Arlette Elkaïm-Sartre, Sartre’s executor, and Sylvie Le Bon de Beauvoir, Beauvoir’s adopted daughter and executor, over correspondence, permissions, privacy, and interpretation.

## Philosophical, Literary, and Ideological Development

### Freedom and consciousness

Sartre’s early phenomenology described consciousness as intentional: consciousness is always consciousness *of* something. It is not a thing or container. Because consciousness separates itself from what it is, the human “for-itself” is characterized by negation and freedom.

### Bad faith

“Bad faith” describes attempts to deny freedom by treating oneself exclusively as a fixed object, social role, or determined essence—or, conversely, denying factual constraints by imagining oneself pure freedom. Sartre’s famous examples include the café waiter and the woman postponing acknowledgment of an erotic situation.

Critics have objected that these examples encode class and gender assumptions. Later scholarship also disputes interpretations that equate bad faith simply with lying.

### The Other

Sartre analyzed shame, conflict, objectification, and “the look.” The popular line “Hell is other people,” from *Huis clos*, is often misread as the claim that all social relations are inherently hellish. In context, the play portrays people who surrender their self-understanding to mutually imprisoning judgments.

### Existential psychoanalysis

Sartre rejected a mechanistic unconscious conceived as a second mind concealing information from consciousness. He proposed reconstructing an individual’s fundamental project through behavior, writing, and situation. His studies of Baudelaire, Genet, and Flaubert applied this approach.

### Marxism and history

After the war Sartre increasingly treated individual freedom as socially and historically conditioned. The *Critique de la raison dialectique* tried to explain collective action without dissolving persons into structural forces.

Sartre called Marxism the unsurpassable philosophy of its era while arguing that orthodox Marxism had become intellectually frozen. His position differed from party doctrine but often left him vulnerable to political rationalization on behalf of regimes or movements he considered historically progressive.

### Ethics

Sartre repeatedly planned but never completed the systematic ethics promised at the end of *L’Être et le Néant*. Posthumous notebooks reveal attempts to develop responsibility, generosity, conversion from bad faith, and reciprocal freedom.

### Literature

Sartre’s theory of committed literature held that prose discloses the world and implicates reader and writer in freedom. He generally distinguished prose from poetry, a division criticized as restrictive. His theater placed agents in extreme situations where action exposes freedom, self-deception, and political responsibility.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Simone de Beauvoir

Lifelong partner, primary interlocutor, editor, correspondent, critic, financial associate, and collaborator. They did not marry or permanently share a household. Their correspondence documents both intellectual cooperation and the management of other lovers.

### Paul Nizan

Closest school and ENS friend. Nizan joined the French Communist Party and died in 1940. After the party attacked Nizan posthumously as a traitor, Sartre helped rehabilitate him, notably through the 1960 preface to *Aden Arabie*.

### Raymond Aron

ENS friend, philosophical stimulus, political adversary, and long-term point of comparison. Aron introduced Sartre to the promise of phenomenology. Their Cold War politics diverged sharply; Aron became a liberal anticommunist critic of Sartrean engagement.

### Maurice Merleau-Ponty

Co-founder of *Les Temps modernes* and major philosophical colleague. Differences over communism, historical violence, and editorial responsibility led to rupture in the early 1950s. Sartre answered him in “Merleau-Ponty vivant.”

### Albert Camus

Friend during and immediately after the occupation, collaborator in the *Combat* milieu, then public rival after 1952.

### Jean Genet

Friend and subject of *Saint Genet*. The relationship mixed advocacy, philosophical appropriation, and personal strain.

### Arlette Elkaïm-Sartre

Born in Constantine in 1935, met Sartre in the 1950s, was briefly his lover according to biographical accounts, and was legally adopted in 1964. She edited posthumous works and controlled the literary estate.

### Benny Lévy/Pierre Victor

Maoist leader, later Sartre’s secretary and final philosophical interlocutor. Their relationship became the center of the “Hope Now” authorship dispute.

### Other personal relationships

Documented or strongly reported romantic or sexual relationships included:

- **Olga Kosakiewicz;**
- **Wanda Kosakiewicz;**
- **Bianca Bienenfeld/Lamblin;**
- **Dolores Vanetti Ehrenreich;**
- **Michelle Vian;**
- **Arlette Elkaïm;**
- **Lena Zonina** and other correspondents or lovers identified in letters.

The exact sexual character, duration, and overlap of every relationship cannot always be established. Sartre’s published letters used pseudonyms for several living women, complicating identification.

## Controversies, Allegations, and Adversarial Findings

### Relationships with students and younger women

Sartre and Beauvoir’s “family” included young women, some first encountered as Beauvoir’s students.

The most detailed adverse testimony came from **Bianca Lamblin, née Bienenfeld**, born in 1921. She had been Beauvoir’s pupil in 1937–1938 and subsequently had relationships with both Beauvoir and Sartre.

- **Documented fact:** the relationships occurred; Sartre and Beauvoir’s correspondence discusses Bianca under the pseudonym “Louise Védrine.”
- **Allegation/testimony:** Lamblin described the couple’s conduct as seduction, manipulation, sexual exploitation, and abandonment.
- **Documented context:** she was approximately seventeen when her relationship with Beauvoir began and older when involved with Sartre; France’s legal and institutional context differed from present standards, but the teacher-pupil power imbalance existed.
- **Supporting evidence:** the couple’s private letters reveal strategic discussion of lovers, disparaging descriptions, concealment, and coordination.
- **Unresolved question:** private encounters cannot be independently reconstructed in every detail.
- **Reputational effect:** publication of *Mémoires d’une jeune fille dérangée* in 1993 significantly altered the public legend of the transparent, egalitarian Sartre-Beauvoir pact.

No court conviction or criminal charge against Sartre arising from these relationships is documented.

### 1977 petitions concerning minors and sexual law

Sartre signed petitions during the 1977 French debate over sexual offenses involving minors.

- **Documented fact:** his name appeared among signatories protesting prolonged pretrial detention in the Versailles case and supporting revision of laws governing adult-minor sexual relations.
- **Documented content:** texts asserted that minors in certain cases had described themselves as consenting, criticized legal inequalities—particularly those affecting homosexual relations—and questioned fixed legal presumptions.
- **Historical context:** some advocates sought equality in ages of consent; others advanced broader theories of children’s sexual autonomy. These positions overlapped without being identical.
- **Modern controversy:** the petitions are frequently described as demands to abolish the age of consent. That shorthand captures part of the broader campaign but can obscure differences among the petitions and signatories.
- **What cannot be inferred:** Sartre’s signature does not by itself prove he personally engaged in sexual acts with prepubescent children.
- **What is established:** he publicly supported texts that treated supposed minor consent as legally significant and minimized conduct now recognized as structurally incapable of equal consent.

### Soviet misinformation and political dishonesty

Sartre’s 1954 presentation of Soviet freedom of criticism was false.

- **Documented fact:** he made the claim.
- **Documented fact:** Stalinist censorship, political imprisonment, and suppression remained.
- **Reported admission:** Sartre later acknowledged withholding truth for political reasons.
- **Finding:** this is not merely an ideological disagreement; it is a documented instance in which strategic political loyalty overrode accurate reporting.

### Revolutionary violence

Sartre defended or contextualized violence by colonized and revolutionary groups. Major examples include the Fanon preface, Maoist activism, and the Munich article.

No evidence shows Sartre personally organized a bombing, killing, or armed cell. His role was rhetorical, editorial, fundraising, testimonial, or protective. Critics argue that his prestige normalized violence whose risks were borne by others; supporters distinguish analysis of structural violence from operational incitement.

### Occupation record

Two opposing myths developed:

1. **Heroic myth:** Sartre was a central armed or clandestine Resistance leader.
2. **Hostile myth:** Sartre was a Nazi collaborator who fabricated his resistance after liberation.

The record supports neither extreme. He was a prisoner of war, organized a small resistance circle, wrote politically interpretable drama, and contributed to liberation journalism. He also published and worked legally, occupied a post arising within the anti-Jewish dismissal system, and was never a major clandestine operative.

### Antisemitic language in private correspondence

Lamblin and later critics identified disparaging language about her Jewish background in Beauvoir-Sartre correspondence.

- **Documented fact:** private correspondence contains stereotypes and offensive descriptions.
- **Context:** Sartre later wrote *Réflexions sur la question juive*, a major attack on antisemitism, and supported Israel’s existence.
- **Interpretation:** later antiracist commitments do not erase the private language; nor does the private language establish consistent political antisemitism across his career.

### Accuracy and totalizing biography

Sartre’s biographies of Baudelaire, Genet, and Flaubert frequently infer deep psychological projects from selected evidence. Critics describe this as reductionist, speculative, or colonizing the subject. The incompleteness of *L’Idiot de la famille* illustrates both the ambition and practical limits of “totalization.”

### No substantiated financial-criminal dossier

The adversarial search found no credible evidence that Sartre committed financial fraud, corruption, money laundering, patent theft, corporate malfeasance, or intelligence-directed criminal activity.

French, American, Soviet, and other security services monitored intellectual and radical networks in which he participated. Appearance in surveillance files establishes state interest, not recruitment as an intelligence agent.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Propaganda

### “Existentialism means despair or nihilism”

- **Story:** Sartre taught that nothing matters and life is hopeless.
- **Origin:** hostile postwar religious, conservative, and popular criticism; reinforced by *La Nausée* and the bleak setting of *Huis clos*.
- **Evidence against:** Sartre emphasized choice, responsibility, projects, solidarity, and political action.
- **Effect:** helped turn existentialism into both a scandal and a youth-market identity.

### “Hell is other people”

- **Story:** Sartre believed every relationship is intrinsically hell.
- **Evidence:** the line occurs in *Huis clos*.
- **Qualification:** Sartre later explained that relations become hellish when people depend entirely on others’ judgments and refuse change.
- **Effect:** became his most famous and most decontextualized quotation.

### The permanent crabs

- **Story:** mescaline caused Sartre to see lobsters or crabs following him for years.
- **Evidence:** based on Sartre’s later accounts of lingering imagery and anxiety.
- **Qualification:** humorous retellings often convert intermittent aftereffects and metaphor into continuous literal hallucination.
- **Effect:** supports the popular image of the chemically driven eccentric philosopher.

### “De Gaulle said one does not arrest Voltaire”

- **Story:** de Gaulle personally prevented Sartre’s arrest with this declaration.
- **Evidence:** widely reported retrospective anecdote.
- **Problem:** a definitive contemporaneous record is elusive.
- **Effect:** symbolized Sartre’s status as an intellectual institution despite his refusal to become one.

### Nobel money rumor

- **Story:** after refusing the Nobel Prize, Sartre later asked for its money.
- **Evidence:** repeated in hostile anecdotes; no strong primary documentation located in this research.
- **Label:** rumor or unresolved claim.
- **Effect:** used to portray his refusal as vanity or hypocrisy.

### Sartre as the “Pope of existentialism”

- **Story:** Sartre singularly invented and ruled existentialism.
- **Evidence against:** the movement had important predecessors and contemporaries—Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, Husserl, Heidegger, Jaspers, Marcel, Beauvoir, Camus, and Merleau-Ponty.
- **Effect:** amplified Sartre’s fame while eclipsing collaborators, particularly Beauvoir’s independent philosophical contributions.

### Sartre-Beauvoir perfect free-love partnership

- **Story:** their pact eliminated jealousy, deception, hierarchy, and possession.
- **Evidence against:** correspondence and testimony document jealousy, concealment, coordinated management of lovers, and unequal power.
- **Historical importance:** the public model influenced debates about nonmarriage and sexual freedom even though private practice diverged from its simplified legend.

### Deathbed conversion

- **Story:** Sartre abandoned atheism and converted under Benny Lévy’s influence.
- **Evidence:** the final dialogues show increased attention to Jewish messianic thought, ethics, and hope.
- **Evidence against:** no formal conversion or unambiguous profession of religious faith is documented.
- **Label:** disputed interpretation, often circulated as polemical myth.

## Awards, Honors, and Formal Recognition

- **1929:** first place in the philosophy agrégation.
- **1940s onward:** extensive literary and theatrical honors were proposed; Sartre generally resisted institutional decoration.
- **1945:** refused the Légion d’honneur when offered, according to standard biographical accounts.
- **1964:** awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature; declined the medal and monetary award.
- **1976:** accepted an honorary doctorate associated with the Hebrew University of Jerusalem for stated political reasons.

He never held elected office, cabinet office, or a permanent university chair.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Sartre’s prestige declined during the 1980s and 1990s as structuralism, post-structuralism, analytic philosophy, liberal antitotalitarianism, and reassessment of communist regimes displaced his intellectual centrality.

Posthumous publications also altered his image:

- wartime notebooks complicated the polished public autobiography;
- correspondence exposed manipulation and asymmetry in the “family”;
- unfinished ethics showed a broader moral project;
- the second *Critique* revealed the scale and incompleteness of his philosophy of history;
- the Benny Lévy conversations generated a dispute over his final direction.

Since the centenary of his birth in 2005, scholarship has renewed attention to:

- the technical phenomenology of consciousness;
- emotion and imagination;
- social ontology;
- race and colonialism;
- collective action and seriality;
- disability and embodiment;
- connections and differences between Sartre and Beauvoir;
- the unfinished ethics;
- global receptions of existentialism.

His vocabulary remains culturally pervasive: bad faith, existence preceding essence, the look, commitment, situation, and the burden of freedom. His plays remain widely performed; *La Nausée*, *Huis clos*, *Les Mots*, and *L’Être et le Néant* remain major points of entry.

Political reinterpretation is divided. Sartre is variously treated as:

- a model of the writer accepting public responsibility;
- an example of intellectual courage in Algeria and Vietnam;
- an apologist who distorted facts for revolutionary alliances;
- an anticolonial theorist whose rhetoric sometimes romanticized violence;
- a precursor of theories of oppression and social construction;
- an overly voluntarist thinker insufficiently attentive to institutions;
- a figure whose private practices contradicted claims about reciprocity and freedom.

No single posthumous verdict has displaced these competing receptions.

## Atlas Connections

Only documented or explicitly sourced connections from the supplied atlas roster are included.

- **Simone Lucie Ernestine Marie Bertrand de Beauvoir — documented fact.** Met during preparation for the 1929 philosophy agrégation in Paris; lifelong partner, correspondent, editor, political collaborator, and principal intellectual interlocutor until 1980.

- **Albert Camus — documented fact.** Met in occupied Paris; friendship and collaboration in the *Combat* milieu; Camus’s plays and writing intersected with Sartre’s circle. Public rupture in 1952 following the *Les Temps modernes* review of *L’Homme révolté*.

- **Martin Heidegger — documented intellectual influence; reported meeting/contact.** Sartre studied Heidegger intensively in Berlin and captivity and later corresponded or sought dialogue after the war. Heidegger’s ontology decisively influenced *L’Être et le Néant*, though Sartre rejected central aspects.

- **Karl Marx — documented intellectual influence.** Sartre increasingly treated Marxism as the essential philosophy of the historical era while criticizing orthodox and Stalinist forms. No personal connection was possible.

- **Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel — documented intellectual influence.** Hegelian recognition, negation, history, and dialectic entered Sartre partly through the French Hegel renaissance, although Sartre’s relation to Alexandre Kojève’s lectures was indirect rather than a documented regular attendance.

- **Friedrich Nietzsche — documented intellectual influence.** Present in Sartre’s formation and accounts of freedom, value creation, literature, and atheism; not a personal connection.

- **Søren Aabye Kierkegaard — documented intellectual predecessor.** Sartre engaged existential themes associated with Kierkegaard while rejecting Christian resolution.

- **Sigmund Freud — documented intellectual rivalry/influence.** Sartre criticized Freudian metapsychology, proposed existential psychoanalysis, and wrote a major screenplay about Freud for John Huston.

- **Henri-Louis Bergson — documented intellectual influence.** Reading Bergson helped turn the adolescent Sartre toward philosophy; Sartre later departed sharply from Bergsonian metaphysics.

- **René Descartes — documented intellectual influence.** Sartre inherited and transformed Cartesian themes of consciousness and freedom.

- **Immanuel Kant — documented intellectual influence.** Central to his formal philosophical education and later ethical problems.

- **Plato and Aristotle — documented intellectual background.** Persistent reference points in Sartre’s classical philosophical education; no special biographical crossing beyond intellectual inheritance.

- **Pablo Ruiz Picasso — documented social and artistic connection.** Both moved in postwar Parisian intellectual and artistic networks; Sartre wrote on visual art and encountered Picasso socially.

- **André Breton — documented political/literary network connection.** Breton joined Sartre and others in protesting Communist attacks on Paul Nizan’s memory; the two represented distinct and sometimes competing avant-garde traditions.

- **Paul-Michel Foucault — documented political association.** Participated with Sartre in post-1968 campaigns, including prison activism and *Libération*-related projects. Foucault also became emblematic of an intellectual generation often said to have displaced Sartre.

- **Gilles Louis René Deleuze — documented political and petition-network association.** Shared post-1968 campaigns and appeared with Sartre among signatories of the 1977 sexual-law petition; philosophical positions remained distinct.

- **Jacques Derrida — documented institutional/petition connection.** Later French philosopher whose name appeared alongside Sartre’s on the May 1977 petition; no close personal or teacher-student relationship established here.

- **Jacques Marie Émile Lacan — documented shared intellectual scene.** Both were central figures of postwar Paris and engaged psychoanalysis from sharply different positions. Sartre was not Lacan’s disciple.

- **Roland Gérard Barthes — documented journal/intellectual-network connection.** Published in and interacted with the broader *Les Temps modernes* intellectual world; later associated with structuralism’s challenge to Sartrean humanism. Both signed the 1977 petition.

- **Pierre-Félix Guattari — documented political-network connection.** Shared post-1968 causes and the 1977 petition milieu.

- **Guy-Ernest Debord — documented shared radical field, but no close relationship established.** Situationist theory opposed the celebrity intellectual model represented by Sartre. Mere presence in the 1968 field should not be inflated into collaboration.

- **Antonio Francesco Gramsci — documented intellectual influence.** Sartre’s theories of engagement and intellectual responsibility have been compared with Gramsci; direct influence is less central than Marx, Hegel, Husserl, or Heidegger.

- **Mao Zedong — documented political-symbolic connection, no personal meeting established.** Sartre associated with French Maoists and traveled to China in 1955, but his later French Maoism did not amount to a stable personal relationship with Mao.

- **Fidel Alejandro Castro Ruz — documented fact.** Sartre and Beauvoir met Castro during their 1960 Cuban journey; Sartre initially supported the revolution and later criticized repression in the Padilla affair.

- **Ernesto “Che” Guevara — documented fact.** Met in Cuba in 1960; Sartre published an admiring portrait of Guevara as a revolutionary intellectual.

- **Patrice Émery Lumumba — documented political subject/influence.** Sartre wrote on Lumumba and African decolonization after his assassination; no personal meeting established here.

- **Frantz Fanon is not on the supplied roster**, but his absence should be noted because Sartre’s preface to *Les Damnés de la terre* was one of Sartre’s most important anticolonial connections.

- **Avram Noam Chomsky — documented political-intellectual intersection, not close association.** Both opposed the Vietnam War and were prominent critics of American policy; no substantial direct collaboration established in the consulted sources.

- **Herbert Marcuse — documented shared New Left environment.** Both influenced 1960s radical movements and opposed the Vietnam War; no close personal relationship established here.

- **Jürgen Habermas — documented intellectual opposition/reception rather than personal relationship.** Habermas’s critical theory developed partly against existentialist and decisionist traditions; no important meeting established here.

- **Slavoj Žižek — posthumous interpreter/critic only.** Sartre died before Žižek’s international prominence; there was no personal connection.

- **Jean Baudrillard — postwar intellectual succession, not established personal connection.** Baudrillard belonged to the later theoretical scene that displaced Sartrean dominance.

- **Yanis Varoufakis — no documented biographical crossing.** Born in 1961; no sourced meeting found.

- **George Orwell — documented thematic and political comparison, no confirmed meeting found.** Both addressed political responsibility and totalitarianism, but the consulted sources do not establish a personal relationship.

- **Walter Benjamin — intellectual comparison only.** No documented personal meeting found.

- **Theodor W. Adorno and Max Horkheimer — intellectual contemporaries, but no meaningful documented personal connection located.**

- **Claude Lévi-Strauss — documented intellectual rivalry.** Structural anthropology became a principal challenge to Sartrean humanism and philosophy of history; Sartre criticized structuralism, and Lévi-Strauss answered the historical framework of the *Critique* in *La Pensée sauvage*.

- **Gabriel José García Márquez and Julio Cortázar — shared international left-intellectual milieu, but no sufficiently specific life connection established in the consulted sources.**

- **Jorge Luis Borges — no documented personal connection found.**

- **Nelson Rolihlahla Mandela — no documented personal crossing found.**

- **Salvador Guillermo Allende Gossens — Sartre opposed the Chilean coup environment, but no documented personal meeting found in the consulted sources.**

- **Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi — intellectual and political comparison only; no personal connection. Sartre’s defense of some revolutionary violence diverged from Gandhian nonviolence.**

- **Absence finding:** For the great majority of the 896-person roster—especially ancient, medieval, early-modern, scientific, financial, architectural, dance, occult, and contemporary technology figures—no documentary meeting, correspondence, collaboration, institutional tie, or explicit Sartrean acknowledgment was found. Mere contemporaneity, common residence in Paris, or broad philosophical comparability was not counted.

## Compact Chronology

- **1905, 21 June:** Born in Paris.
- **1906:** Father Jean-Baptiste Sartre dies; Jean-Paul and Anne-Marie return to the Schweitzer household.
- **1915–1917:** Schooling at Lycée Henri-IV.
- **1917:** Mother marries Joseph Mancy; move to La Rochelle.
- **1920–1921:** Returns to Paris and Henri-IV; close friendship with Paul Nizan.
- **1922–1924:** Preparatory study at Louis-le-Grand.
- **1924:** Enters École normale supérieure with Nizan, Aron, and others.
- **1927:** Participates in ENS antimilitarist revue scandal.
- **1928:** Fails philosophy agrégation.
- **1929:** Places first in agrégation; meets and begins lifelong partnership with Beauvoir.
- **1931:** Begins teaching at Le Havre.
- **1933–1934:** Studies phenomenology in Berlin.
- **1935:** Mescaline experiment and subsequent disturbing imagery.
- **1936:** Publishes *La Transcendance de l’Ego* and *L’Imagination*.
- **1938:** Publishes *La Nausée*.
- **1939:** Publishes *Le Mur*; mobilized for war.
- **1940, 21 June:** Captured by German forces.
- **1940, December:** *Bariona* performed in Stalag XII-D.
- **1941:** Released; returns to Paris; helps form Socialisme et Liberté; resumes teaching.
- **1943:** Publishes *L’Être et le Néant*; premiere of *Les Mouches*.
- **1944:** Premiere of *Huis clos*; writes liberation reportage for *Combat*.
- **1945:** Founds *Les Temps modernes*; visits United States; publishes first two *Chemins de la liberté* novels; existentialism lecture.
- **1946:** Publishes *L’existentialisme est un humanisme* and *Réflexions sur la question juive*.
- **1948:** RDR founded; *Les Mains sales* staged; *Qu’est-ce que la littérature?* published.
- **1949:** RDR collapses; third *Chemins de la liberté* volume appears.
- **1952:** *Saint Genet*; public rupture with Camus; closer tactical alignment with Communists.
- **1954:** Soviet visit and misleading public claims concerning freedom of criticism.
- **1955:** Visits China.
- **1956:** Condemns Soviet invasion of Hungary; breaks with PCF alignment.
- **1958–1962:** Major activism against Algerian War and torture.
- **1960:** Publishes *Critique de la raison dialectique*; visits Cuba and Brazil; signs Manifesto of the 121.
- **1961:** Preface to Fanon; first OAS bombing.
- **1962:** Second OAS bombing; Algeria becomes independent.
- **1964:** Publishes *Les Mots*; awarded and refuses Nobel Prize; adopts Arlette Elkaïm.
- **1966–1967:** Leads Russell Tribunal proceedings on Vietnam.
- **1968:** Supports student and worker revolt; visits occupied Sorbonne.
- **1970–1973:** Associates with French Maoists; directs suppressed radical newspapers.
- **1972:** Publishes controversial Munich article.
- **1973:** Helps found *Libération*; nearly complete blindness.
- **1974:** Visits Andreas Baader in prison.
- **1976:** Accepts honorary doctorate linked to Hebrew University of Jerusalem.
- **1977:** Signs petitions seeking revision of sexual laws involving minors.
- **1979–1980:** Final conversations with Benny Lévy.
- **1980, 15 April:** Dies at Hôpital Broussais.
- **1980, 19 April:** Funeral procession through Montparnasse; tens of thousands attend.
- **1983–1995:** Major notebooks, letters, ethics manuscripts, second *Critique*, and final conversations published posthumously.
- **2005 onward:** Centenary-era revival in Sartrean scholarship.
- **2024–2026:** Renewed reassessment of his politics, private relationships, final dialogues, and rivalry with Raymond Aron and Albert Camus.
