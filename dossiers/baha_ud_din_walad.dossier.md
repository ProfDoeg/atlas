# Baha ud-Din Walad: Research Dossier

## Scope, Identification, and Evidentiary Method

This dossier concerns **Bahāʾ al-Dīn Muḥammad Walad**, the Persian-speaking jurist, preacher, and mystical writer best known as the father of Jalāl al-Dīn Rūmī.

The surviving record is uneven. The principal evidence falls into four classes:

1. **Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s own words**, preserved in the *Maʿārif*. These provide the strongest evidence for his age, residences, social environment, disputes, preaching, religious psychology, and some family circumstances, but they are not an orderly autobiography.
2. **Early family tradition**, especially the *Ibtidā-nāma* or *Walad-nāma* of his great-grandson Sultan Walad. This is indispensable but already hagiographical and was written decades after Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s death.
3. **Mevlevi hagiographies**, principally Farīdūn Sipehsālār’s *Risāla* and Shams al-Dīn Aḥmad Aflākī’s *Manāqib al-ʿārifīn*. They preserve early institutional memory but also miracles, retrospective genealogies, political theater, contradictory chronology, and stories designed to demonstrate sanctity.
4. **Modern critical scholarship**, especially Badīʿ al-Zamān Furūzānfar, Fritz Meier, Franklin D. Lewis, Hamid Algar, Nazif Şahinoğlu, Abdülbaki Gölpınarlı, Hermann Landolt, and Annemarie Schimmel.

Evidentiary labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported directly by Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s own work, reliable near-contemporary evidence, or convergent independent evidence.
- **Reported fact:** plausible information from an early narrative source but not independently established.
- **Disputed reconstruction:** a modern reconstruction for which significant chronological or interpretive disagreement remains.
- **Hagiographic tradition:** a saint’s-life account intended partly to establish spiritual rank.
- **Legend or myth:** a story unsupported by sufficiently early evidence, contradicted by chronology, or overtly miraculous.
- **Unresolved:** the surviving evidence does not permit a responsible conclusion.

### Birth date

**Documented basis:** In the *Maʿārif*, Bahāʾ al-Dīn says that on 1 Ramadan 600 AH—converted by Hamid Algar as 3 March 1203—he was approaching fifty-five. This places his birth in approximately 546 AH/1151–52.

Some publications give 545/1150 or 1150–51. These differences arise from the interpretation of “approaching fifty-five,” lunar-to-solar conversion, and whether age is counted inclusively. No surviving birth record fixes an exact day.

### Death date discrepancy

The strongest specialist reference works give 18 Rabīʿ II 628/23 February 1231. Some museum and popular sources give 12 January 1231 or even call the year 1230. These should be treated as conversion or transmission errors unless supported by a specific calendar calculation. The Hijri date, 18 Rabīʿ II 628, is the more stable datum.

## Names, Titles, and Self-Presentation

“Walad,” literally “son” or “child,” became part of the name by which he was remembered. In his lifetime he was commonly called **Bahāʾ-i Walad**.

### Sultan al-ʿUlamāʾ

Bahāʾ al-Dīn himself reports in the *Maʿārif* that a radiant old man appeared to him in a dream and conferred the title **Sultan al-ʿUlamāʾ**. He thereafter insisted on using it when signing legal opinions.

Later Mevlevi tradition expanded the episode: all the muftis of Balkh supposedly dreamed on the same night that the Prophet Muhammad had designated Bahāʾ al-Dīn “Sultan of the Scholars,” after which they came collectively to acknowledge him.

Classification:

- The claim that Bahāʾ al-Dīn used the title: **documented fact**.
- His account that it originated in his own dream: **documented self-report of a dream**, not an independently verifiable supernatural event.
- The simultaneous dream of all Balkh’s muftis: **hagiographic legend**.
- The title’s later centrality to Mevlevi memory: **documented reception history**.

His attachment to the title is important evidence about his self-conception. His mystical experiences did not erase his identity as a credentialed scholar, preacher, and legal authority.

## Family and Ancestry

## Paternal Family

His father was **Ḥusayn ibn Aḥmad Khaṭībī**, represented as a respected scholar and ascetic. His grandfather was **Aḥmad Khaṭīb**. The family had reportedly lived in Khurasan for several generations and followed the profession of *khaṭīb*—preacher—from which its name Khaṭībī derived.

Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s family belonged to the Hanafi learned class. This background helps explain his:

- training in jurisprudence and scriptural interpretation;
- employment as preacher and mufti;
- insistence on wearing scholars’ clothing;
- preference for lodging in madrasas rather than Sufi convents;
- conviction that his mystical teaching remained subordinate to the revealed law.

### Ferdows Khatun and al-Sarakhsi

A family report identifies Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s paternal grandmother or great-grandmother **Ferdows Khatun** as a daughter of **Shams al-Aʾimma Abū Bakr al-Sarakhsī**, the prominent eleventh-century Hanafi jurist. More precisely, the tradition says that Ferdows Khatun was the mother of Aḥmad Khaṭīb, Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s grandfather.

This connection is chronologically possible and consistent with the family’s Hanafi identity, but it depends on genealogical tradition rather than contemporary civil documentation. It is best classified as a **plausible reported family connection**.

## Mother

Bahāʾ al-Dīn refers to his mother familiarly as **Māmī** or “Mama” in the *Maʿārif*. Her personal name is not securely known. References in his text suggest that she survived into the early thirteenth century.

Later tradition made her a daughter of a Khwarazmshah. The particular version identifying her father as ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn Muḥammad, who died in 1200, is chronologically implausible: Bahāʾ al-Dīn had already been born around 1151–52. Modern specialists consequently reject this royal genealogy.

Classification:

- Existence of a mother called Māmī by her son: **documented fact**.
- Her personal identity and ancestry: **unresolved**.
- Khwarazmshah princess claim: **hagiographic dynastic genealogy, chronologically untenable in its usual form**.

## Alleged Descent from Abu Bakr

Sipehsālār, Aflākī, Jāmī, and later Mevlevi tradition traced the family to **Abu Bakr**, the first caliph.

Evidence against treating this as established genealogy:

- Bahāʾ al-Dīn does not assert it in his surviving work.
- Rumi does not assert it in his surviving work.
- It was not placed on their sarcophagi.
- The genealogies supplied by later sources contain only six to eight generations for an interval of approximately six centuries.
- Lines asserting the descent in some manuscripts of Sultan Walad’s work appear to be later copyist interpolations.
- Confusion may have arisen between Caliph Abu Bakr and Abū Bakr al-Sarakhsī, the Hanafi jurist linked to the family through Ferdows Khatun.

Classification: **later genealogical claim, unproved and probably secondary**.

## Spouse or Spouses

The sources do not provide a fully reliable marital history.

One wife is named **Amīna Khatun** in specialist summaries. She died at Laranda, present-day Karaman, and was buried there. Some popular traditions identify Rumi’s mother as **Muʾmina Khatun**. The similarity of the names, variant transmission, and possibility of more than one wife have produced ambiguity.

Responsible reconstruction:

- Bahāʾ al-Dīn was married and had several children: **documented or strongly reported fact**.
- A wife named Amīna or Muʾmina Khatun traveled west with the family and died at Laranda: **reported fact**.
- Whether Amīna and Muʾmina are variants for one woman or two different wives: **unresolved**.
- Claims that she was a Khwarazmian princess reproduce the chronologically defective royal-lineage tradition.

## Children

The children attributed to him include:

- **Jalāl al-Dīn Muḥammad**, later known as Rumi, born in 1207.
- **ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn Muḥammad**, a son who reportedly died and was buried at Laranda.
- **Fāṭima Khatun**, a married daughter who, according to Aflākī, died young shortly before the family’s westward migration.
- Some later accounts imply additional children, but no secure complete register survives.

Rumi’s marriage to **Gawhar Khatun**, daughter of Sharaf al-Dīn Lālā of Samarqand, took place during the family’s Laranda period, conventionally in 1224 or 1225. Bahāʾ al-Dīn thereby became grandfather to:

- Bahāʾ al-Dīn Muḥammad, later known as **Sultan Walad**;
- ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn Muḥammad.

Sultan Walad was named for his grandfather and became a major organizer of the post-Rumi Mevlevi lineage.

## Childhood and Early Life

Little securely dated information survives about Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s childhood.

He grew up in the eastern Iranian and Central Asian Islamic cultural zone, probably in or around Balkh. The family’s hereditary occupation placed him inside an established network of Hanafi jurists and preachers. His father is described as both learned and ascetic, suggesting that legal education and disciplined devotional practice were combined in his upbringing.

No reliable source supplies:

- an exact birthplace;
- the names of childhood schools;
- a complete list of siblings;
- the identity of all teachers;
- dates of formal scholarly authorization;
- a traceable youthful journey for study.

Later hagiographies fill these gaps with prestigious teachers and spiritual lineages. These require separate evaluation.

## Education, Teachers, and Formation

## Formal Education

His later career indicates advanced training in:

- Qurʾanic exegesis;
- hadith;
- Hanafi jurisprudence;
- preaching;
- legal opinion;
- Arabic religious terminology;
- the traditions of Persian ethical and mystical instruction.

His own discourses cite or interpret Qurʾanic verses and prophetic traditions and address legal and theological problems. He was recognized as a mufti and signed fatwas.

## Alleged Lineage through Ahmad al-Ghazali

Sipehsālār and Aflākī connect Bahāʾ al-Dīn through his grandfather to **Aḥmad al-Ghazālī**, the mystic and brother of Abū Ḥāmid al-Ghazālī.

This is a **reported spiritual or educational genealogy**, not a firmly documented teacher-student encounter. Ahmad al-Ghazali died around 1126, approximately twenty-five years before Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s probable birth, so any connection had to be indirect.

## Alleged Discipleship under Najm al-Din Kubra

Jāmī and other later writers associate him with **Najm al-Dīn Kubrā**, founder of the Kubrawiyya.

Reasons for caution:

- Bahāʾ al-Dīn never mentions Kubrā in the surviving *Maʿārif*.
- His discourses do not display the distinctive visionary vocabulary normally associated with Kubrawi teaching.
- Jāmī reports the affiliation with reservation.
- Bahāʾ al-Dīn did not describe himself as a Sufi or operate unambiguously as the head of a technical Sufi order.

Classification: **later reported affiliation, doubtful**.

## Was He a Sufi?

This depends on definition.

- He recorded intense experiences of divine presence, visionary dreams, contemplative states, and an intimate, affective perception of God: **documented fact**.
- He trained a principal disciple in what later sources interpreted as a Sufi relationship: **strongly reported fact**.
- He consistently emphasized the sharia and his status as a scholar.
- He preferred madrasas to *khānaqāhs* and apparently did not call himself a Sufi.

Fritz Meier and Hermann Landolt therefore distinguish between Bahāʾ al-Dīn as a genuine **mystic** and Bahāʾ al-Dīn as a formally affiliated “Sufi” in the institutional sense. The former is well supported; the latter remains uncertain.

---

## Early Career in Balkh and Wakhsh

Bahāʾ al-Dīn worked as a **preacher, teacher, and mufti**. Balkh is the city most strongly associated with his public reputation, but the *Maʿārif* shows that his actual eastern geography was more complicated.

Between approximately 1203 and 1210 he lived for substantial periods in **Wakhsh**, in the upper Oxus region, or traveled between Wakhsh and Balkh. He possessed or occupied a house there and had relations with the local ruler or emir. This evidence has led modern scholars to reject the simple popular picture of Rumi’s family continuously residing in Balkh until a single dramatic departure.

The most cautious reconstruction is:

- Bahāʾ al-Dīn belonged socially and genealogically to the Balkh learned world.
- He resided in Wakhsh during at least part of the decade in which Rumi was born.
- He may have moved repeatedly among Balkh, Wakhsh, and Samarqand.
- “Balkhī” need not mean that every major family event occurred inside Balkh itself.

## Daily and Professional Life

Later sources describe a schedule in which he:

- taught students from morning until midday;
- instructed disciples afterward;
- preached publicly on Fridays and Mondays;
- issued legal opinions;
- continued arranging or dictating material that became the *Maʿārif*.

This schedule is plausible and consistent with the contents of his work, although its precise regularity comes from later biographical tradition.

## Income and Finances

Sipehsālār says that Bahāʾ al-Dīn lived from a stipend paid by the public treasury and refused to consume income belonging to religious endowments.

Classification: **reported fact from a partisan but relatively early source**.

No reliable evidence survives of:

- commercial businesses;
- private investments;
- large landed estates;
- banking or lending operations;
- patents or inventions;
- inherited royal wealth;
- a formally incorporated religious organization;
- systematic personal enrichment.

His family was socially prominent and possessed sufficient means to migrate with dependents, books, and followers. That does not establish great private wealth.

## Intellectual and Religious Development

## Hanafi and Scriptural Orientation

Bahāʾ al-Dīn repeatedly affirmed:

- the binding authority of the sharia;
- observance of the Prophet’s custom;
- prayer, ritual discipline, and lawful conduct;
- the duty of qualified persons to command right and oppose wrong;
- the superiority of revealed religious knowledge over speculative philosophical systems.

His surviving voice is not that of a detached philosopher. It is often immediate, personal, exhortatory, emotionally intense, and sometimes combative.

## Opposition to Rationalist Theology and Philosophy

He criticized speculative theologians and philosophers, especially **Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī**, whom he called an innovator. In one passage he attacks Rāzī and his royal patron for abandoning the numerous miracles and proofs of revealed religion in favor of a few “dark corners” of speculation.

The disagreement involved overlapping issues:

- competing forms of religious authority;
- scriptural and experiential knowledge versus dialectical theology;
- patronage at the Khwarazmshah court;
- rivalry among learned elites;
- Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s own irascible response to critics;
- the political risks of publicly denouncing the ruler’s intellectual associates.

## Mysticism of Divine “Being-With”

Fritz Meier identified **maʿiyyat**, God’s “being with” creation, as a central feature of Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s thought.

His *Maʿārif* repeatedly explores:

- God perceived in and behind created things;
- devotional pleasure or savor, *maza*;
- the sanctification of bodily and sensory experience;
- imagination as an instrument of religious apprehension;
- emotional states as sources of knowledge;
- divine action underlying human action;
- attraction between corresponding beings;
- movement from outward forms to inward meanings;
- the intimacy of God without a systematic claim that creation is identical with God.

Meier and Landolt caution against labeling him simply a monist or pantheist. Hyperbolic expressions such as “God—all is He” occur, but his conceptual center is relational presence, not a philosophical doctrine that God and the cosmos are numerically identical.

## Good and Evil

According to the Turkish scholarly summary of the *Maʿārif*, Bahāʾ al-Dīn treats good and evil as relational:

- unbelief may be wisdom with respect to God’s total ordering;
- the same unbelief is calamity from the human standpoint;
- divine causality encompasses acts, while human religious obligations remain operative.

This places his experiential mysticism beside strong doctrines of divine agency. The surviving text does not organize these insights into a formal scholastic system.

## Women, Sexuality, and Embodied Imagery

The *Maʿārif* is notable for candid bodily, domestic, affective, and sometimes erotic imagery. Modern interpreters have differed over whether this represents spiritualized sensuality, unusually frank mystical psychology, or literary metaphor.

The text’s presence of embodied imagery is **documented**. Claims that it amounts to a coherent modern doctrine of gender equality, sexuality, or “divine marriage” are later interpretive constructions and should not be projected backward without qualification.

---

## Principal Disciple: Burhan al-Din Muhaqqiq Tirmidhi

All major sources agree that **Sayyid Burhān al-Dīn Muḥaqqiq Tirmidhī** was Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s principal disciple in the spiritual sense.

The precise chronology of their association is incomplete. Burhan al-Din apparently separated from the family before or during its migration. After Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s death, he reached Anatolia and became an important teacher of Rumi. He required Rumi to study and internalize his father’s *Maʿārif*, thereby serving as a bridge between father and son.

This succession is central to Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s historical influence:

Bahāʾ al-Dīn → Burhan al-Din Tirmidhi → Rumi → later Mevlevi teaching.

It does not establish that Bahāʾ al-Dīn founded the Mevlevi order. That institution developed after his death around Rumi’s family and successors.

## Political Environment and Conflict

## The Khwarazmshah Realm

Bahāʾ al-Dīn lived during the expansion of the Khwarazmshah state under ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn Muḥammad. The regime’s conflicts with regional rulers, Samarqand, the Abbasid caliphate, and eventually the Mongols destabilized the entire region.

His political activity was primarily religious and rhetorical, not governmental. No evidence shows that he held civil office, commanded troops, collected taxes, or organized an insurrection.

## Conflict with Fakhr al-Din al-Razi

**Documented core:** Bahāʾ al-Dīn attacked Rāzī’s ideas and court influence in the *Maʿārif*.

**Later narrative:** Rāzī became jealous of Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s popularity and incited the Khwarazmshah to believe that the preacher might seize political power. Bahāʾ al-Dīn then swore to leave the ruler’s territory.

Problems:

- Rāzī died around 1209 or 1210.
- Evidence from the *Maʿārif* suggests Bahāʾ al-Dīn remained in Wakhsh until approximately 1210–11.
- Competing departure dates range from around 1212 to 1221.
- A departure close to 1219–20 cannot be directly caused by a living Rāzī.
- The narrative turns intellectual rivalry into a dramatic contest between saint, philosopher, and king.

Conclusion: The antagonism was real, but Rāzī’s personal conspiracy as the single cause of migration is a **hagiographic and chronologically problematic explanation**.

## Other Adversaries

Aflākī names:

- Qāḍī Zayn al-Dīn Farāzī;
- ʿAmīd Marvazī;
- Rashīd al-Dīn Khānī;
- judges or scholars of Balkh and Wakhsh.

They allegedly opposed Bahāʾ al-Dīn out of jealousy, excessive reliance on formal learning, or allegiance to the court. The existence of learned opposition is plausible; Aflākī’s explanation of every opponent as jealous is a standard hagiographic device.

## Abbasid-Khwarazmshah Conflict

A supplementary political motive may have been Khwarazmshah Muḥammad’s confrontation with the Abbasid caliph and his attempt to replace the caliph with ʿAlāʾ al-Mulk, a sayyid from Tirmidh. A Hanafi preacher invested in Sunni institutional legitimacy could have found this disturbing.

This is a **historical contextual inference**, not a motive explicitly established in Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s surviving statements.

## Chronological Life History

## c. 1151–52: Birth

Born into the Khaṭībī learned family, probably in Balkh or its cultural region. His exact birthplace and day of birth are unknown.

## Later Twelfth Century: Education and Entry into the Learned Profession

He received advanced religious training and entered the family professions of preaching and legal scholarship. He acquired recognition as a mufti and public preacher.

No dependable chronology survives for these decades.

## Before 1200: Marriage and Household Formation

He married and began a family. Exact dates are unavailable. The household eventually included Jalāl al-Dīn Rumi, ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn Muḥammad, Fāṭima Khatun, one or more wives, dependents, students, and disciples.

## 1203: Self-Dated Age Statement

On 1 Ramadan 600, Bahāʾ al-Dīn described himself as approaching fifty-five. This is the principal basis for dating his birth.

## 1204–10: Wakhsh

Internal evidence places him in a house at Wakhsh for substantial portions of this period. Rumi was born in 1207, making Wakhsh a serious candidate for Rumi’s actual birthplace despite the family’s conventional identification with Balkh.

Bahāʾ al-Dīn dealt with the local emir and local judges. His references indicate a socially active life rather than monastic withdrawal.

## 1207–12: Samarqand Episode

Rumi later recalled being with his father during a siege or conquest of Samarqand by the Khwarazmshah.

Chronology varies:

- Rumi’s recollection, recorded in *Fīhi mā fīh*, places father and son at Samarqand during its conquest.
- Ibn al-Athir and Juvayni date relevant campaigns differently, around 1210–12.
- Encyclopaedia Iranica summarizes the event under 604/1207–08.
- TDV argues that Bahāʾ al-Dīn probably moved to Samarqand before 1212 and later returned toward Balkh.

The family’s presence in Samarqand is a **strongly reported fact**, but the precise year and military episode remain disputed.

## c. 1210–19: Return to Balkh or Continued Regional Movement

A plausible reconstruction has Bahāʾ al-Dīn returning from Samarqand to Balkh and remaining in the region for seven or eight years. The evidence does not permit a continuous itinerary.

## Date of Departure West

Competing dates include:

- **c. 1212–14:** Furūzānfar preferred approximately 610/1214 after reviewing the traditions.
- **late 1219:** TDV’s reconstruction, linking departure to the imminent Mongol danger.
- **618/1221:** Hamd Allah Mustawfi’s date.
- Popular accounts often make Rumi five or six when the family departed, producing dates around 1212–13; this conflicts with other elements of the itinerary and with the Mongol-destruction narrative.

No single departure date is established. Fritz Meier’s critical study concluded that neither the precise date nor the true cause of departure can be recovered with confidence.

## Motives for Migration

Potential causes were cumulative:

- conflict with the Khwarazmshah’s scholarly and political circle;
- instability created by the conquest of Samarqand;
- resentment toward judges or rivals in Balkh and Wakhsh;
- the Khwarazmshah’s conflict with the Abbasid caliph;
- search for a more favorable center of patronage;
- fear of Mongol expansion.

The familiar explanation “he foresaw the Mongol invasion and left Balkh immediately before it” is partly hagiographic. Mongol danger was real, but prophetic foresight is not needed to explain alarm during a period of widely reported conquest.

## Nishapur

Dawlatshah and Jāmī report that the migrating party passed through Nishapur and met **Farīd al-Dīn ʿAṭṭār**. Attar supposedly:

- recognized Rumi’s future spiritual greatness;
- gave the boy his *Asrār-nāma*;
- told Bahāʾ al-Dīn that the child would set the world’s combustible souls on fire;
- or declared, in another form, that a sea was following an ocean.

The meeting is chronologically possible if the family passed Nishapur before Attar’s death. However:

- it is absent from the earliest family sources, including Sultan Walad and Aflākī;
- it first emerges in later literary biography;
- its prophecy is tailored to Rumi’s subsequent fame.

Classification: **late hagiographic tradition; possible encounter, unproved dialogue and prophecy**.

## Baghdad

The party reportedly reached Baghdad, where later tradition says:

- the celebrated Sufi ʿUmar al-Suhrawardī greeted Bahāʾ al-Dīn;
- the Abbasid caliph sent gifts, which Bahāʾ al-Dīn rejected;
- Bahāʾ al-Dīn criticized the caliph in a Friday sermon;
- he predicted the Mongol destruction of Baghdad and the end of the Abbasid caliphate;
- the party lodged in the Mustansiriyya madrasa.

Evaluation:

- A visit to Baghdad is plausible and fits the pilgrimage route.
- A meeting with al-Suhrawardī is possible but not independently documented.
- Rejection of caliphal gifts and bold criticism are saintly-independence motifs.
- Predicting disaster after news of Mongol conquests had reached Baghdad would not require supernatural foresight.
- Residence in the **Mustansiriyya is impossible**: its construction belongs to 1228–34 and it was completed after Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s death.

## Kufa, Mecca, and the Hajj

Sources state that the family went from Baghdad through Kufa to Mecca and performed the hajj. The journey then continued through Syria.

This is a **strongly reported itinerary**, although the exact pilgrimage year is uncertain.

## Syria

The party traveled through Syria, conventionally including Damascus. No secure evidence establishes a personal meeting between Bahāʾ al-Dīn and Ibn ʿArabī, despite their overlapping presence in the wider Syrian-Anatolian intellectual world.

## Anatolia: Malatya, Erzincan, Akşehir, and Laranda

The sources disagree substantially.

### Sipehsālār’s version

From Syria, Bahāʾ al-Dīn went to Erzincan and then Akşehir near Erzincan. He stayed approximately one year in a *khānaqāh* founded by **ʿIṣmat Khatun**, wife of the Mengüjekid ruler Fakhr al-Dīn Bahrāmshāh, and then went toward Konya.

This clashes with Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s reported preference for madrasas over Sufi convents.

### Aflākī’s version

He traveled:

- Damascus;
- Malatya;
- Erzincan;
- Akşehir;
- Laranda.

Aflākī gives four years at Akşehir and nearly seven at Laranda. Elsewhere, however, Aflākī says Bahāʾ al-Dīn refused to enter Erzincan because of its inhabitants’ wickedness.

### Jāmī’s version

The family spent four years at Erzincan.

### Critical conclusion

The likely general direction—Syria to eastern or central Anatolia, then Laranda and Konya—is credible. The stated durations cannot all be reconciled and, when added together, often exceed the available chronological interval.

## Laranda, Present-Day Karaman

Laranda is the best-supported prolonged Anatolian residence before Konya.

Events associated with it include:

- death and burial of Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s wife Amīna/Muʾmina Khatun;
- death and burial of his son ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn;
- Rumi’s marriage to Gawhar Khatun in 1224 or 1225;
- births of Rumi’s sons Sultan Walad and ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn;
- teaching and preaching by Bahāʾ al-Dīn.

The deaths and family formations make a stay of several years plausible, even if “seven years” cannot be independently verified.

## c. 1229: Arrival in Konya

Bahāʾ al-Dīn reached Konya around 626/1229.

The relatively sober *Walad-nāma* says he lived in Konya for two years before his death. This aligns with arrival around 1229.

The Seljuk ruler **ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn Kayqubād I** and leading residents became patrons or admirers. Bahāʾ al-Dīn reportedly declined lodging in or near the palace and chose the **Altunapa/Altuniya Madrasa**, declaring that palaces belonged to rulers, madrasas to scholars, convents to shaykhs, inns to merchants, hospices to wanderers, and caravanserais to travelers.

The saying expresses his scholarly self-definition. Its exact wording comes through later tradition.

### Royal Escort Legend

Sipehsālār and Aflākī say Kayqubād personally went out to escort Bahāʾ al-Dīn ceremonially into the city. Sultan Walad’s earlier account is more restrained. Modern specialists reject or doubt the royal-procession story.

The king’s subsequent patronage is plausible; the theatrical escort is **hagiographic embellishment**.

## 1231: Illness and Death

Bahāʾ al-Dīn died after a short illness on Friday, 18 Rabīʿ II 628/23 February 1231.

Sultan Walad describes him as physically powerful, large, and strongly built. No specific disease is identified. There is no reliable evidence of poisoning, violence, suicide, addiction, or chronic psychiatric illness.

Kayqubād reportedly proclaimed seven days of mourning. An enclosure was constructed around the grave; a dome was later built by the emir Badr al-Dīn Gawhartāsh.

Rumi was approximately twenty-three years old.

## Employment, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

## Offices and Professions

Bahāʾ al-Dīn functioned as:

- *khaṭīb*, public preacher;
- *mufti*, issuer of legal opinions;
- teacher in the religious sciences;
- interpreter of the Qurʾan and hadith;
- spiritual instructor;
- head of a traveling scholarly household.

No securely documented formal government title survives beyond religious functions.

## Institutions

Associated institutions include:

- scholarly and preaching circles in Balkh and Wakhsh;
- madrasas used during travel;
- the disputed Baghdad Mustansiriyya claim;
- the Altunapa/Altuniya Madrasa in Konya;
- patronage networks of the Seljuk court;
- the burial site that became the Mevlevi mausoleum.

## Books and Manuscripts as a Migrating Archive

The household likely carried manuscripts westward. Later research on early Mevlevi libraries indicates that the migration transmitted an eastern Iranian Hanafi-mystical intellectual inheritance into Anatolia. Exact inventories of Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s personal books do not survive.

## Property

No reliable valuation or property register has been found. Traditions about the Seljuk ruler granting the palace rose garden as a burial place describe patronage of the family, not necessarily Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s privately alienable estate.

## Technology, Inventions, and Patents

None are documented or relevant in the modern sense.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Ḥusayn ibn Aḥmad Khaṭībī

Father and formative model. Scholar, ascetic, and member of the family preaching tradition.

### His Mother, “Māmī”

Mentioned affectionately in the *Maʿārif*. Her presence provides rare evidence of Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s domestic voice. Her name and ancestry remain unknown.

### Fakhr al-Din al-Razi

Intellectual opponent. Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s attacks on him are documented; the elaborate story that Rāzī engineered his exile is not.

### ʿAlāʾ al-Din Muhammad Khwarazmshah

Ruler criticized with Rāzī as a patron of innovation. The extent of direct personal contact is unknown.

### Burhan al-Din Muhaqqiq Tirmidhi

Principal spiritual disciple and later transmitter of Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s teaching to Rumi.

### Jalal al-Din Rumi

Son and student. Bahāʾ al-Dīn provided Rumi’s first learned and devotional environment. After his father’s death, Rumi repeatedly read and taught the *Maʿārif*. Verbal and conceptual echoes passed into the *Mathnawī* and other works.

### Sharaf al-Din Lala of Samarqand

Associate of the migrating family and father of Rumi’s first wife, Gawhar Khatun.

### ʿIsmat Khatun and Fakhr al-Din Bahramshah

Regional patrons connected with the family’s stay around Akşehir/Erzincan. Details depend on later narratives.

### ʿUmar al-Suhrawardi

Possible host or greeter in Baghdad. The encounter is chronologically possible but preserved in hagiographical sources.

### ʿAlaʾ al-Din Kayqubad I

Seljuk ruler and principal Konya patron. Royal admiration and material support are well reported; the ceremonial details are embellished.

### Badr al-Din Gawhartash

Seljuk emir credited with later placing a dome over Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s tomb.

## Controversies, Allegations, Political Conflicts, and Financial Questions

A dedicated adversarial search was conducted in English, Turkish, Persian, and Arabic using combinations of the name with terms corresponding to controversy, accusations, criticism, scandal, lawsuit, fraud, conspiracy, and allegations, including results from 2024–26.

No credible evidence was located for:

- criminal charges or convictions;
- fraud or embezzlement;
- money laundering;
- sexual crime;
- commercial misconduct;
- imprisonment;
- litigation;
- intelligence activity;
- organized political violence;
- military command;
- homicide;
- corruption proceedings;
- addiction;
- secret societies.

For a thirteenth-century preacher, the absence of modern judicial categories is expected. His major controversies were theological, political, and historiographical.

## Public Denunciation of Opponents

**Documented fact:** Bahāʾ al-Dīn used the pulpit and his discourses to condemn rival scholars, judges, philosophers, and their patrons as innovators.

His rhetoric could be harsh. Hamid Algar describes the authorial personality emerging from the *Maʿārif* as powerful, perceptive, and often irascible.

## Supposed Political Ambition

Later narratives say enemies convinced the Khwarazmshah that Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s popularity threatened the throne.

Classification: **allegation embedded in hagiography**. No administrative record, indictment, or independent chronicle establishes that he planned rebellion.

## Court Rivalry

Modern historians have suggested that Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī’s success and prestige at court may have overshadowed Bahāʾ al-Dīn. This may have produced anxiety or rivalry. That is a **scholarly psychological reconstruction**, not a documented confession.

## Opposition to Philosophy

Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s hostility to speculative philosophy and dialectical theology is well attested. The later portrayal of all “external scholars” as spiritually blind enemies is a polemical construction of Mevlevi hagiography.

## Wealth and Public Funds

The only specific financial report says he drew a treasury stipend but would not use endowment income. This was written by an admirer and lacks independent fiscal documentation.

No evidence establishes either corruption or poverty.

## Historical Visibility

A modern Turkish discussion notes that independent works by contemporary scholars do not prominently mention Bahāʾ al-Dīn and that he left no known formal jurisprudential treatise or major school of legal pupils. This complicates the later image of universal scholarly supremacy.

Possible explanations include:

- local rather than empire-wide fame;
- loss of records during war and migration;
- posthumous inflation of status by Mevlevi writers;
- his primary strength as preacher and religious personality rather than systematic jurist;
- survival bias favoring the later fame of Rumi.

This is a significant historiographical issue, not proof that he was an invented person or complete impostor.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

## 1. Simultaneous Dream of the Muftis

**Story:** Every mufti in Balkh dreamed that Muhammad named Bahāʾ al-Dīn “Sultan of the Scholars.”

**Appearance:** Sultan Walad, Sipehsālār, and Aflākī; Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s own version describes only his personal dream of a radiant elder.

**Assessment:** The collective version is a hagiographic amplification.

**Effect:** It legitimated Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s authority as bestowed by prophetic rather than merely institutional recognition.

## 2. Descent from Caliph Abu Bakr

**Story:** The Khaṭībī family descended from Abu Bakr.

**Circulation:** Medieval Mevlevi biographies and later genealogies.

**Evidence against:** Insufficient generations, silence of father and son, absent tomb inscriptions, possible manuscript interpolation, and confusion with Abū Bakr al-Sarakhsī.

**Effect:** It attached caliphal and Sunni prestige to the Mevlevi lineage.

## 3. Maternal Descent from the Khwarazmshahs

**Story:** Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s mother was a Khwarazmian princess.

**Evidence against:** The standard genealogy is chronologically impossible.

**Effect:** It gave Rumi’s family royal ancestry while simultaneously heightening the drama of conflict with the reigning dynasty.

## 4. Fakhr al-Din al-Razi Caused the Exile

**Story:** Rāzī became jealous, persuaded the king that Bahāʾ al-Dīn planned to take power, and drove him out.

**Evidence:** Real doctrinal hostility appears in the *Maʿārif*.

**Evidence against the full story:** Contradictory dates; Rāzī died well before some proposed departure dates; the migration had multiple geopolitical causes.

**Effect:** It staged a moral conflict between saintly inner knowledge and jealous rationalist learning.

## 5. The Mongol Invasion as Punishment for Balkh’s Rejection

**Story:** The Mongols destroyed Balkh because its people or ruler injured Bahāʾ al-Dīn and caused him to leave.

**Appearance:** Aflākī and later devotional retellings.

**Assessment:** Providential hagiography, not a historical causal explanation.

**Effect:** It transformed the migration into a sacred exodus and the destruction of Balkh into vindication of the saint.

## 6. Meeting with Attar

**Story:** Attar recognized young Rumi’s future greatness, presented the *Asrār-nāma*, and uttered a prophecy.

**Evidence for:** Chronological possibility and later literary tradition.

**Evidence against:** Absence from the earliest narratives; prophecy shaped by hindsight.

**Assessment:** Possible meeting, legendary dialogue.

## 7. Baghdad Triumph and Prophecy

**Story:** Al-Suhrawardi and the city welcomed Bahāʾ al-Dīn; he rejected the caliph’s gifts, reproached him publicly, predicted Baghdad’s destruction, and stayed at the Mustansiriyya.

**Evidence against:** The Mustansiriyya was not available as claimed. The prophecy appears retrospectively tailored to the Mongol sack of 1258.

**Assessment:** Composite hagiographic narrative containing a plausible Baghdad visit and impossible or unverified elaborations.

## 8. Refusal to Enter Erzincan

Aflākī says he would not enter Erzincan because its people were wicked. Jāmī gives a four-year Erzincan residence.

**Assessment:** Mutually contradictory narrative traditions. Neither duration nor refusal can be accepted without qualification.

## 9. Royal Escort into Konya

**Story:** Sultan Kayqubād personally led the reception.

**Evidence against:** The earlier *Walad-nāma* gives a less theatrical account.

**Assessment:** Likely ceremonial embellishment of genuine royal patronage.

## 10. Founder of the Mevlevi Order

Popular accounts sometimes call Bahāʾ al-Dīn a founder or master of the Mevlevi order.

**Assessment:** Anachronistic. He supplied family authority, texts, and a spiritual genealogy, but the Mevlevi order formed after his death around Rumi, Sultan Walad, and their successors.

## 11. Modern Ethnic Claims

Modern Afghan, Tajik, Iranian, and Turkish cultural narratives sometimes classify him—and by extension Rumi—as exclusively Afghan, Tajik, Persian, Iranian, or Turkish.

Documentary distinctions:

- His language and literary culture were Persian.
- His family belonged to greater Khurasan and the Balkh-Wakhsh region.
- Modern nation-states did not map onto his political world.
- He ended his life under Seljuk rule in Anatolia.
- “Balkhī,” Persian-speaking, and resident of Anatolia are compatible facts, not mutually exclusive modern national verdicts.

## Works by Baha ud-Din Walad

## Maʿārif

**Original title:** معارف (*Maʿārif*)

**Genre:** Persian discourses, scriptural interpretations, responses to religious and legal questions, visionary reports, spiritual diary-like reflections, sermon materials.

**Authorship:** The contents substantially preserve Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s speech and reflections, but the work was recorded, collected, and arranged by disciples. It should not automatically be treated as a continuous autograph manuscript.

**Structure:** Four books or parts. The fourth may partly preserve drafts or alternative forms of material included in the first three.

**Content includes:**

- Qurʾanic exegesis;
- hadith interpretation;
- legal and ethical questions;
- dreams and interior experiences;
- divine presence;
- sensory and affective mystical imagery;
- family and domestic observations;
- polemics against rivals;
- autobiographical chronological clues.

### Principal Persian Edition

Badīʿ al-Zamān Furūzānfar, ed., *Maʿārif-i Bahāʾ al-Dīn Muḥammad ibn Ḥusayn Khaṭībī Balkhī*:

- Volume I, containing books 1–3, Tehran, 1333 SH/1954.
- Volume II, containing book 4, Tehran, 1338 SH/1959.
- Reprinted in 1352 SH/1973.

### English Translations and Adaptations

**A. J. Arberry**, selections published in 1964. Only the first nineteen sections were translated, not the entire text.

**Coleman Barks and John Moyne, *The Drowned Book: Ecstatic and Earthy Reflections of Bahauddin, the Father of Rumi***, 2004.

This volume requires a clear attribution warning:

- Moyne supplied Persian-based translations.
- Barks produced interpretive English versions.
- The resulting poems are not literal translations and sometimes omit or transform the original wording.
- They should not be quoted as exact evidence of Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s language.

Franklin D. Lewis and Hassan Lahouti have reportedly prepared or revised a complete English translation. The sources consulted for this dossier did not establish that a full scholarly English edition has been formally published.

## Fatwas

Bahāʾ al-Dīn says he signed fatwas using “Sultan al-ʿUlamāʾ.” No independently catalogued corpus of his legal opinions is known to survive.

Classification: **documented activity; lost or unidentified writings**.

## Sermons and Oral Discourses

The *Maʿārif* incorporates sermon and teaching material. No separate securely authenticated sermon collection is known.

## Works Incorrectly Confused with His

He must not be confused with:

- Sultan Walad, Rumi’s son, who also bore the name Bahāʾ al-Dīn Muḥammad;
- Bahāʾ al-Dīn Naqshband;
- Bahāʾ al-Dīn al-ʿĀmilī;
- Aḥmad al-Būnī’s occult *Shams al-Maʿārif*.

---

## Major Primary and Near-Primary Sources

### Bahāʾ al-Dīn Walad, *Maʿārif*

The central first-person source. Strongest for interior life, polemic, residences, age, vocation, and theology; weaker as a continuous chronology because of its compilation history and nonlinear organization.

### Jalāl al-Dīn Rumi, *Fīhi mā fīh*

Preserves Rumi’s recollection of being with his father at Samarqand and evidence of the father’s continuing place in his thought.

### Sultan Walad, *Ibtidā-nāma* / *Walad-nāma*

Written by Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s great-grandson and Rumi’s son. An early source for the family and Mevlevi succession, but also hagiographic.

### Farīdūn Sipehsālār, *Risāla-yi Sipehsālār*

An early Mevlevi biography preserving family and institutional traditions. Contains legendary and inconsistent material.

### Shams al-Dīn Aḥmad Aflākī, *Manāqib al-ʿārifīn*

Written in the fourteenth century. Extremely rich in names, places, anecdotes, and institutional memory but deliberately hagiographical. It cannot be used as a transparent factual chronicle.

### Hamd Allah Mustawfi, *Tārīkh-i guzīda*

Provides a departure date of 618/1221 and later historical tradition.

### Juvayni, *Tārīkh-i Jahāngushā*

Important for the Khwarazmshah and Mongol political context.

### Ibn al-Athir, *al-Kāmil fī al-tārīkh*

Important for dating the Samarqand campaigns and regional conflict.

### Jāmī, *Nafaḥāt al-uns*

Later Sufi biographical tradition, including the tentative Kubrawi affiliation and travel accounts.

### Dawlatshah Samarqandi, *Tadhkirat al-shuʿarāʾ*

An important source for the late Attar-meeting tradition.

## Books and Major Studies About Baha ud-Din Walad

### Dedicated Study

Fritz Meier, *Bahāʾ-i Walad: Grundzüge seines Lebens und seiner Mystik*. Leiden: Brill, 1989.

The most substantial dedicated critical study. Meier reconstructs the life through the *Maʿārif* and hagiographical traditions, distinguishes mysticism from formal Sufi affiliation, and analyzes divine presence, pleasure, imagination, embodiment, and religious psychology.

Persian translations appeared in 2003, including translations associated with Āfāq Bāybordī and Maryam Musharraf.

### Review and Methodological Criticism

Hermann Landolt, review of Meier, *Bulletin critique des Annales islamologiques* 8 (1992), pp. 50–51.

Landolt emphasizes Meier’s “demythologization” and agrees that the real Bahāʾ revealed in the authentic portions of the *Maʿārif* differs considerably from the Mevlevi hagiographic figure. He questions whether some of Meier’s analytical categories are imposed too abstractly on the text.

### Major Rumi Biographies with Substantial Treatment

Franklin D. Lewis, *Rumi: Past and Present, East and West: The Life, Teachings, and Poetry of Jalāl al-Din Rumi*. Oxford: Oneworld, 2000; revised editions.

Lewis provides the most useful English-language synthesis of Meier, Furūzānfar, early Persian sources, migration chronology, family traditions, and the distinction between historical and mythical biographies.

Badīʿ al-Zamān Furūzānfar, *Risāla dar taḥqīq-i aḥwāl va zindagānī-yi Mawlānā Jalāl al-Dīn Muḥammad*. Tehran, first published 1936; later editions.

A foundational Persian critical study of Rumi’s life and family, including detailed chronological criticism of Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s migration.

Abdülbaki Gölpınarlı, *Mevlânâ Celâleddin*. Istanbul, 1952.

Major Turkish study drawing on Mevlevi sources while critically examining their traditions.

Annemarie Schimmel, *The Triumphal Sun: A Study of the Works of Jalaloddin Rumi*. London, 1978.

Important for the father’s influence on Rumi, although Meier’s later research caused revisions in the field.

### Reference Studies

Hamid Algar, “Bahāʾ-al-Dīn Moḥammad Walad,” *Encyclopaedia Iranica*, vol. III, fasc. 4, pp. 431–33.

Nazif Şahinoğlu, “Bahâeddin Veled,” *Türkiye Diyanet Vakfı İslâm Ansiklopedisi*, vol. 4, pp. 460–62.

Both are compact but highly useful critical syntheses, particularly for contradictory chronology and lineage claims.

### Thematic Scholarship

Studies of Bahāʾ al-Dīn and Rumi’s epistemology have examined their treatment of emotion as a source of knowledge. Other scholarship considers his rhetoric, embodied mystical language, conceptions of women, scriptural interpretation, and influence on Rumi’s *Mathnawī*. These are interpretive studies rather than independent biographical evidence.

## Films, Documentaries, Interviews, and Archival Material

No major documentary devoted exclusively to Bahāʾ al-Dīn was identified in the consulted sources. He appears mainly as a supporting figure in productions about Rumi and the Mevlevi tradition.

Relevant visual and archival materials include:

- manuscripts of the *Maʿārif* and early Mevlevi chronicles;
- manuscript miniatures representing Bahāʾ al-Dīn preaching;
- the *Jāmiʿ al-siyar* miniature in the Topkapı Palace Museum Library, Hazine 1230, fol. 112a;
- the Mevlana Museum complex in Konya;
- tombs attributed to members of the family in Karaman;
- modern Turkish museum and cultural presentations of the migration.

Film and television portrayals should generally be treated as dramatizations based on Aflākī and later devotional tradition unless their sourcing demonstrates otherwise.

## Death, Burial, and Mausoleum

Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s short final illness is not medically described. He died in Konya in 1231 and was buried in a garden associated with Seljuk royal patronage.

His grave became the nucleus of a larger sacred complex after Rumi was buried beside him in 1273. Later construction, renovation, Ottoman patronage, the institutional development of the Mevlevi lodge, and conversion into the modern Mevlana Museum substantially altered the setting.

The arrangement reverses the modern hierarchy of fame: the father originally gave sanctity and prestige to the burial place; after Rumi’s death, the son’s worldwide reputation made Bahāʾ al-Dīn appear primarily as “Rumi’s father.”

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

## Influence on Rumi

Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s most demonstrable legacy lies in Rumi’s continued engagement with the *Maʿārif*. Rumi:

- read it repeatedly;
- lectured on it to disciples;
- may have written glosses on its fourth portion;
- absorbed its language and theological motifs;
- continued its movement between scriptural exposition, bodily imagery, emotion, and divine presence.

The resemblance is not total. Rumi’s later encounter with Shams al-Dīn Tabrizi, poetic genius, use of music and dance, and social setting in Konya carried him beyond his father’s scholarly-preacher model.

## Influence through Burhan al-Din

Burhan al-Din required Rumi to assimilate his father’s teachings and provided disciplined spiritual training. This gave the *Maʿārif* an institutional afterlife.

## Mevlevi Hagiography

The early Mevlevi community transformed Bahāʾ al-Dīn into:

- universally acknowledged Sultan of the Scholars;
- heir to caliphal and royal blood;
- opponent of jealous rationalists;
- prophet of Mongol catastrophe;
- sacred emigrant from Balkh;
- recipient of royal submission in Konya;
- founding ancestor of the Mevlevi spiritual dynasty.

These stories established legitimacy for Rumi’s descendants and successors.

## Literary Neglect and Modern Recovery

For centuries, Bahāʾ al-Dīn remained overshadowed by Rumi. Modern philology recovered him as an independent mystical author through:

- Furūzānfar’s edition of the *Maʿārif*;
- Meier’s monograph;
- Arberry’s translations;
- Lewis’s biographical synthesis;
- later Persian, Turkish, and English studies.

## National and Cultural Appropriation

His migrations make him a symbolic figure in several cultural histories:

- Afghan and Balkh heritage;
- Tajik and wider Persian-speaking Central Asian history;
- Iranian/Persian literary history;
- Turkish and Anatolian religious history;
- the transregional history of Sufism.

These receptions reflect modern national institutions. Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s own world was organized through dynasties, cities, scholarly families, religious schools, and Persianate networks rather than present-day nationality.

## Assessment of Historical Importance

Without his son, Bahāʾ al-Dīn would still merit study as the authorial personality behind an unusually candid Persian mystical document. Through Rumi, his significance expanded considerably:

- he transmitted Hanafi scholarly culture from Khurasan to Anatolia;
- he supplied a major source of Rumi’s religious vocabulary;
- his household became the genealogical center of the Mevlevi tradition;
- his grave became part of one of the world’s most visited Sufi memorial complexes;
- his biography became a major testing ground for distinguishing Persian hagiography from recoverable social history.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the reviewed sources are included. Mere contemporaneity or shared geography has not been treated as a connection.

### Jalal al-Din Rumi — Documented fact

**Nature:** Son, pupil, intellectual heir, and principal source of Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s posthumous fame.

**Dates and places:** Rumi was born in 1207, probably while the family was based in Wakhsh or the Balkh-Wakhsh region. He accompanied his father through Samarqand and the westward migration, lived with him in Laranda and Konya, and was present in the family circle until Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s death in Konya in 1231.

**Evidence:** Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s *Maʿārif*, Rumi’s *Fīhi mā fīh*, Sultan Walad’s *Walad-nāma*, and later Mevlevi sources. Rumi’s repeated study of the *Maʿārif* and its echoes in his work are documented literary influence.

### Farid al-Din Attar — Hagiographic tradition; possible encounter

**Nature:** Alleged meeting during the family’s passage through Nishapur. Attar supposedly gave young Rumi the *Asrār-nāma* and predicted his greatness.

**Date and place:** Conventionally Nishapur, between the family’s eastern departure and Attar’s death around 1220 or 1221.

**Evidence:** The story appears in Dawlatshah and Jāmī but not in the earliest family and Mevlevi accounts. Chronologically possible, not established.

### Genghis Khan — Reported historical impact, not a personal connection

**Nature:** No meeting, correspondence, or personal relationship is documented. Mongol expansion under Genghis Khan created the geopolitical danger commonly treated as one cause of the family’s migration.

**Dates and places:** Central Asia and Khurasan, especially 1219–21.

**Evidence:** Contemporary histories establish Mongol conquest. Whether fear of it was the decisive reason for Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s departure remains disputed. This is an indirect historical impact rather than a biographical crossing in the strict sense.

### Shams al-Din Muhammad of Tabriz — No documented lifetime connection

Shams became transformative in Rumi’s life more than a decade after Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s death. No reliable source establishes a meeting or correspondence between Shams and Bahāʾ al-Dīn.

### Ibn Arabi — No documented connection

Both belonged to the early thirteenth-century Islamic mystical world, and their geographic trajectories touched Syria and Anatolia. No source consulted establishes a meeting, correspondence, teacher-student relationship, or acknowledged personal influence.

### Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani — No documented connection

ʿAbd al-Qādir died in Baghdad in 1166, when Bahāʾ al-Dīn was young. No reliable teacher-student or family connection was found.

### Khwaja Mu'in al-Din Hasan Chishti — No documented connection

Despite approximate contemporaneity and eastern Islamic origins, no supported meeting, correspondence, or institutional relationship was found.

### Nizam ad-Din Auliya and Amir Khusrow Dehlavi — No connection

They belonged to a later Indian Sufi milieu. No direct connection to Bahāʾ al-Dīn is documented.

### Muhammad Baha al-Din Naqshband Bukhari — No connection

The shared name “Bahāʾ al-Dīn” has occasionally caused search confusion. Naqshband was born in the fourteenth century, long after Bahāʾ al-Dīn Walad’s death.

### Mansur al-Hallaj, Abu Yazid al-Bistami, al-Junayd, al-Qushayri, Rabia al-Adawiyya, and Ibrahim ibn Adham — No documented personal connection

These earlier mystical figures formed part of the broad inherited Sufi tradition, but the sources consulted did not establish the direct, explicitly acknowledged influence required by the atlas criteria.

**Atlas finding:** The only certain roster connection is the father-son and teacher-heir relationship with **Jalāl al-Dīn Rumi**. The Attar meeting is an important but late hagiographic tradition. Genghis Khan represents indirect geopolitical impact rather than personal contact. No additional roster connection should be inferred.
