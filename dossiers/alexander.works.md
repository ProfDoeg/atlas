## Works by Alexander the Great

### State texts and inscriptions

- **334 BC:** *Letter to the Chians*. A royal directive preserved in an inscribed copy, regulating the return of exiles, the constitution, trials for treason, and the stationing of a Macedonian garrison; the document acts upon Chian government and is therefore both letter and manifesto of royal authority. Its wording is generally accepted as substantially authentic, although the surviving stone is the city’s published copy rather than Alexander’s original dispatch. ([Heisserer, *Alexander the Great and the Greeks*](https://books.google.com/books?id=QVI8AAAAIAAJ); [Livius](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/alexander-s-letter-to-the-chians/))

- **probably 334 BC; inscribed or reinscribed later:** *Edict concerning Priene and Naulochon*. A fragmentary royal ruling on land, taxation, civic status, and dependent communities, preserved across marble blocks at Priene. Scholars dispute its precise date, reconstruction, archival history, and whether “edict” or “letter” best describes this performative state text. ([PHI, *Priene* 132](https://inscriptions.packhum.org/text/253012); [Chaniotis, “Ancient archives: the edict of Alexander to Priene”](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0075426912000071))

- **334 BC:** *Dedication of the Temple of Athena Polias at Priene*. A short monumental inscription naming Alexander as dedicant after he supplied funds toward the temple; it is simultaneously a text, a political-religious act, and a claim upon a building designed by Pytheos rather than personally authored by Alexander. ([British Museum, object 1870,0320.88](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/G_1870-0320-88); [Axon](https://edizionicafoscari.unive.it/en/edizioni4/riviste/axon/2018/2/dedica-del-tempio-di-atena-polias-a-priene-da-part/))

- **334–323 BC:** *Royal correspondence attributed to Alexander*. Ancient historians transmit numerous letters or summaries of letters—to Darius III, Greek cities, officials, commanders, Olympias, Antipater, and others—but no authorial archive survives, and the authenticity and verbal fidelity of almost every literary example are disputed individually. This is best treated as a fragmentary administrative body of work rather than a securely recoverable collection; letters in the later *Alexander Romance*, including exchanges with Amazons and fabulous rulers, are pseudepigraphic manifestations of the Alexander tradition, not works by Alexander. ([Monti, *Alexander the Great: Letters—A Selection*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv33b9w5q); [Monti, “Le lettere di Alessandro: storia degli studi”](https://histos.org/index.php/histos/article/view/596))

### Coinage and royal image

- **c. 336–323 BC:** *Coinage issued in the name of Alexander III*. A vast state-produced system of gold staters, silver tetradrachms and drachms, and bronze denominations, principally using Athena/Nike and Herakles/Zeus types. It resists treatment as Alexander’s personal artwork: dies were made by anonymous artisans and issues were administered by many mints, while enormous numbers bearing Alexander’s name were struck after his death; even the boundary between lifetime and posthumous issues remains disputed for some catalogue groups. ([Price, *The Coinage in the Name of Alexander the Great and Philip Arrhidaeus*](https://books.google.com/books?id=S-T2GAAACAAJ); [American Numismatic Society, *Studies in the Macedonian Coinage of Alexander the Great*](https://numismatics.org/digitallibrary/ark:/53695/nnan174624); [ALTAR](https://artemis-collection.com/altar/about/))

### Cities and settlements

- **331 BC:** *Alexandria in Egypt*. A planned city founded near the Canopic mouth of the Nile, traditionally laid out under the architect Deinocrates. Alexander selected and ceremonially marked the site, but its buildings and mature urban form were overwhelmingly the work of Ptolemaic successors, so the obra is most accurately the foundation project rather than the later metropolis. ([Fraser, *Cities of Alexander the Great*](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198150060.001.0001); [Cohen, *The Hellenistic Settlements in Syria, the Red Sea Basin, and North Africa*](https://academic.oup.com/california-scholarship-online/book/32975))

- **330 BC:** *Alexandria in Aria*. A strategic settlement generally identified with or placed near modern Herat; its foundation by Alexander belongs to the small core Fraser accepts, although its exact site and relationship to an earlier Achaemenid center remain uncertain. ([Fraser, *Cities of Alexander the Great*](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198150060.001.0001); [Bryn Mawr Classical Review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/1997/1997.04.25/))

- **330–329 BC:** *Alexandria in Arachosia*. A military and administrative foundation normally identified with Old Kandahar. Archaeology supports an early Hellenistic settlement, but the amount of new construction attributable specifically to Alexander cannot be isolated from the pre-existing Achaemenid site. ([Fraser, *Cities of Alexander the Great*](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198150060.001.0001); [Bryn Mawr Classical Review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/1997/1997.04.25/))

- **329 BC:** *Alexandria in the Paropamisadai*, also called *Alexandria of the Caucasus* or *Alexandria in Opiane*. A garrison-colony near the Hindu Kush, commonly located around Begram but not securely identified. Its several ancient names may designate one settlement rather than multiple works. ([Fraser, *Cities of Alexander the Great*](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198150060.001.0001); [Bryn Mawr Classical Review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/1997/1997.04.25/))

- **329 BC:** *Alexandria Eschate* (“Furthest Alexandria”). A fortified settlement on the Jaxartes, usually identified with modern Khujand, created by rebuilding or enlarging an existing settlement associated with Cyrus. It is consequently both a refoundation and a Macedonian military colony, not an entirely new city made from nothing. ([Fraser, *Cities of Alexander the Great*](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198150060.001.0001); [Bryn Mawr Classical Review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/1997/1997.04.25/))

- **326 BC:** *Bucephala*. A settlement founded beside the Hydaspes and named for Alexander’s dead horse Bucephalas. Its precise location, later history, and even its relationship to nearby Nicaea remain unresolved. ([Fraser, *Cities of Alexander the Great*](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198150060.001.0001); [Bryn Mawr Classical Review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/1997/1997.04.25/))

- **326 BC:** *Nicaea*. Bucephala’s paired foundation on or near the Hydaspes battlefield, its name commemorating victory. It was probably a military settlement rather than a fully realized polis, and no secure archaeological identification has been established. ([Fraser, *Cities of Alexander the Great*](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198150060.001.0001); [Bryn Mawr Classical Review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/1997/1997.04.25/))

- **325 BC:** *Alexandria at the confluence of the Acesines and Indus*. A fortified settlement established during the southward voyage through India, probably upon or beside an older Achaemenid installation. Its location and subsequent name are uncertain, making the foundation act more securely attested than the city’s identity. ([Fraser, *Cities of Alexander the Great*](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198150060.001.0001); [Livius, “Alexander’s City Foundations”](https://www.livius.org/articles/misc/alexander-s-city-foundations/))

- **325 BC:** *Alexandria among the Oreitae*, also called *Alexandria-Rambacia*. A colonial and logistical foundation in the territory of the Oreitae, near the earlier settlement Rambacia on the Gedrosian coast. The ancient evidence does not establish whether Alexander created a separate city or reorganized and renamed an existing place. ([Fraser, *Cities of Alexander the Great*](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198150060.001.0001); [Bryn Mawr Classical Review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/1997/1997.04.25/))

- **324 BC:** *Alexandria on the Tigris*, later *Charax Spasinou*. A settlement and harbor near the head of the Persian Gulf, traditionally founded or refounded by Alexander and later rebuilt by Antiochus IV and Hyspaosines. Its succession of rebuildings makes Alexander’s particular material contribution difficult to distinguish. ([Fraser, *Cities of Alexander the Great*](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198150060.001.0001); [Bryn Mawr Classical Review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/1997/1997.04.25/))

- **334–323 BC, various:** *Other alleged Alexandrias and foundations*. Ancient and medieval lists credit Alexander with dozens—sometimes seventy—of cities, including proposed foundations in Margiana, Bactria, Sogdiana, India, Asia Minor, and the Levant. Fraser’s source-critical catalogue reduces the credible group to fewer than a dozen: many names are duplicates, later renamings, local foundation legends, or products of the *Alexander Romance*, so these resist being catalogued as individual works by Alexander. ([Fraser, *Cities of Alexander the Great*](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198150060.001.0001); [Bryn Mawr Classical Review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/1997/1997.04.25/))

### Engineering, monuments, and ritual constructions

- **332 BC:** *Mole at Tyre*. A causeway constructed from the mainland to the island city during Alexander’s siege, using stone, timber, rubble, and material from old Tyre. It was collective military engineering rather than personal handiwork, but Alexander initiated and directed the project; accumulated silting around it permanently altered the coastline. ([Arrian, *Anabasis* 2.18–24](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Arr.+An.+2.18); [Britannica, “Siege of Tyre”](https://www.britannica.com/event/Siege-of-Tyre-332-BCE))

- **326 BC:** *Twelve altars beside the Hyphasis*. Arrian reports that Alexander had twelve exceptionally large stone altars erected after his army refused to advance farther east, presenting the endpoint of the expedition as a ritual monument and boundary. No securely identified remains survive, and their form is known only through literary accounts. ([Arrian, *Anabasis* 5.29](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Arr.+An.+5.29))

- **325–324 BC:** *Indian Ocean fleet*. Hundreds of ships were assembled or built on Alexander’s orders for the descent of the Hydaspes and Indus and Nearchus’s voyage to the Persian Gulf. This is a distributed logistical obra made by shipwrights and crews; ancient totals vary and individual vessels are unnamed. ([Arrian, *Indica* 18–21](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Arr.+Ind.+18))

- **324–323 BC:** *Hephaestion’s funeral pyre or monument at Babylon*. Alexander commissioned an enormous, elaborately decorated temporary structure after Hephaestion’s death. Diodorus describes it as constructed, whereas other evidence and modern scholarship treat the colossal design as planned but perhaps never executed; it therefore resists classification between ephemeral Gesamtkunstwerk and unrealized memorial project. ([Diodorus, *Library* 17.115](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Diod.+17.115); [Palagia, “Hephaestion’s Pyre and the Royal Hunt of Alexander”](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780198152873.003.0006); [Oxford Classical Dictionary](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548933024))

### Unfinished and disputed projects

- **323 BC:** *Arabian expedition*. Ships, crews, reconnaissance voyages, and harbor works were being prepared at Babylon for an expedition around or against Arabia when Alexander died. This was a real project in an advanced preparatory state, but its intended scale and objectives are reported differently by the surviving historians. ([Arrian, *Anabasis* 7.19–21](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Arr.+An.+7.19))

- **reported after 323 BC:** *Completion of Hephaestion’s monument*. This appears among the written memoranda found after Alexander’s death and was cancelled by the Macedonian assembly. Whether it was a distinct permanent monument beyond the disputed pyre, and how much work had begun, are unclear. ([Diodorus, *Library* 18.4](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/diodorus/alexanders-last-plans/))

- **reported after 323 BC:** *Fleet of one thousand large warships*. Diodorus says Alexander’s memoranda proposed construction in Phoenicia, Syria, Cilicia, and Cyprus for campaigns along North Africa and toward Iberia. The project was never executed, and scholars dispute whether the “last plans” preserve Alexander’s intentions, Perdiccas’s politically exaggerated presentation, or later invention. ([Diodorus, *Library* 18.4](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/diodorus/alexanders-last-plans/); [Tarn, *Alexander the Great*, vol. II, appendix 24](https://pahar.in/pahar/Books%20and%20Articles/Central%20Asia/1948%20Alexander%20the%20Great%20Vol%202--Sources%20and%20Studies%20by%20Tarn%20s.pdf))

- **reported after 323 BC:** *North African coastal road, harbors, and shipyards*. A connected infrastructure project in the posthumously reported memoranda, intended to support the western naval expedition as far as the Pillars of Heracles. It was cancelled without construction, and its authenticity shares the dispute surrounding the entire program. ([Diodorus, *Library* 18.4](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/diodorus/alexanders-last-plans/))

- **reported after 323 BC:** *Temple at Delos*. One of six extraordinarily expensive temple projects attributed to the last memoranda and rejected after Alexander’s death. Nothing was built under this program, and Tarn noted that the account problematically speaks of building sanctuaries where temples already existed. ([Diodorus, *Library* 18.4](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/diodorus/alexanders-last-plans/); [Tarn, *Alexander the Great*, vol. II, appendix 24](https://pahar.in/pahar/Books%20and%20Articles/Central%20Asia/1948%20Alexander%20the%20Great%20Vol%202--Sources%20and%20Studies%20by%20Tarn%20s.pdf))

- **reported after 323 BC:** *Temple at Delphi*. Another unexecuted temple in the disputed last plans. Since Apollo’s temple was already being reconstructed and Alexander had apparently contributed to it earlier, both the nature and authenticity of this proposed “building” are doubtful. ([Diodorus, *Library* 18.4](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/diodorus/alexanders-last-plans/); [Tarn, *Alexander the Great*, vol. II, appendix 24](https://pahar.in/pahar/Books%20and%20Articles/Central%20Asia/1948%20Alexander%20the%20Great%20Vol%202--Sources%20and%20Studies%20by%20Tarn%20s.pdf))

- **reported after 323 BC:** *Temple at Dodona*. An unexecuted sanctuary project named in the disputed memoranda. No independent evidence connects Alexander with its realization. ([Diodorus, *Library* 18.4](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/diodorus/alexanders-last-plans/); [Tarn, *Alexander the Great*, vol. II, appendix 24](https://pahar.in/pahar/Books%20and%20Articles/Central%20Asia/1948%20Alexander%20the%20Great%20Vol%202--Sources%20and%20Studies%20by%20Tarn%20s.pdf))

- **reported after 323 BC:** *Temple of Zeus at Dion*. A proposed monumental temple in Macedonia, never realized under Alexander and known only from the contested last plans. ([Diodorus, *Library* 18.4](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/diodorus/alexanders-last-plans/))

- **reported after 323 BC:** *Temple of Artemis Tauropolos at Amphipolis*. A planned sanctuary cancelled after Alexander’s death; its enormous reported budget and the authenticity of the encompassing memorandum remain disputed. ([Diodorus, *Library* 18.4](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/diodorus/alexanders-last-plans/))

- **reported after 323 BC:** *Temple of Athena at “Cyrnus”*. The sixth temple project in Diodorus’s list. The place-name or text is uncertain, the project was never executed, and emendations proposed to identify its location remain conjectural. ([Diodorus, *Library* 18.4](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/diodorus/alexanders-last-plans/); [Tarn, *Alexander the Great*, vol. II, appendix 24](https://pahar.in/pahar/Books%20and%20Articles/Central%20Asia/1948%20Alexander%20the%20Great%20Vol%202--Sources%20and%20Studies%20by%20Tarn%20s.pdf))

- **reported after 323 BC:** *Monumental temple of Athena at Troy*. Diodorus’s summary says the memoranda included lavish offerings or construction at Ilium, but the exact relationship between this proposal, the existing sanctuary, and Alexander’s earlier dedications is unclear. It remained a project rather than a completed Alexander monument. ([Diodorus, *Library* 18.4](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/diodorus/alexanders-last-plans/))

- **reported after 323 BC:** *Monumental tomb for Philip II*. A proposed tomb intended to rival the greatest Egyptian pyramids, known solely from the disputed posthumous memorandum and never built. It should not be confused with the surviving royal tomb traditionally associated with Philip at Aegae. ([Diodorus, *Library* 18.4](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/diodorus/alexanders-last-plans/))

- **reported after 323 BC:** *New cities and reciprocal transplantation of European and Asian populations*. Diodorus attributes to Alexander a program intended to join the continents through settlement, intermarriage, and kinship. It is an unrealized imperial-social project rather than a discrete object, and its idealizing language is among the reasons scholars question the last plans’ authenticity. ([Diodorus, *Library* 18.4](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/diodorus/alexanders-last-plans/))
