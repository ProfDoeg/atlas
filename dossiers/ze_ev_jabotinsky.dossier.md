# Ze'ev Jabotinsky: Research Dossier

**Subject:** Vladimir (Ze'ev) Jabotinsky (1880–1940)  
**Scope:** Life, family, journalism, literature, Zionist politics, military activity, institutions, controversies, myths, works, reception, and the line from Revisionist Zionism through Menachem Begin and the Netanyahu family  
**Prepared:** 25 August 2026  

## Source and terminology note

Jabotinsky wrote principally in Russian, Hebrew, Yiddish, and English, sometimes first publishing one text in Russian and later authorizing or receiving translations with different titles. His surname appears as **Jabotinsky, Zhabotinsky, Zhabotinski, Z'aboṭinsḳi, Žabotinskij**, and **Zhabotynsky**. The archive of the Jabotinsky Institute is indispensable but is also an institution created to preserve his movement's heritage; its factual material has therefore been checked, where possible, against contemporaneous records and independent scholarship.

Several institutional descriptions require precision. Jabotinsky was the founder of the political doctrine and party called Revisionist Zionism. Betar was organized at Riga in 1923 by young Latvian activists, especially Aharon Propes, after Jabotinsky's visit; Jabotinsky became its ideological head and, formally, *Rosh Betar* in 1929. The Irgun originated in a 1931 split from the Haganah under Avraham Tehomi; Jabotinsky did not create the original underground but assumed supreme political command of its Revisionist remnant in 1937. Calling him “founder of Betar” or “founder of the Irgun” is therefore movement shorthand rather than exact institutional history.

Dates before 1918 may appear in Russian Old Style in contemporary sources. This dossier uses Gregorian dates unless otherwise stated. Jabotinsky's death is reported as **3 August 1940** in the contemporaneous Jewish Telegraphic Agency report (“shortly before midnight”) and as **4 August 1940** in many institutional and biographical sources. The discrepancy is preserved rather than silently resolved.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full birth name:** Vladimir Yevgenyevich Zhabotinsky; patronymic also rendered Yevnovich.
- **Hebrew/public name:** Ze'ev Jabotinsky; **Ze'ev** means “wolf” and is the Hebrew counterpart he adopted for Vladimir.
- **Other renderings:** Vladimir Ze'ev Jabotinsky; Zeev Jabotinsky; Volodymyr Zhabotynsky in modern Ukrainian usage.
- **Principal pseudonym:** **Altalena**, Italian for “swing.” In his retrospective story, he had thought the word meant “elevator”; when corrected, he accepted “swing” as appropriate to his changeable or balancing temperament.
- **Other early signatures:** V. Egal, Vl. Egal, V.E.; his journalistic corpus also contains unsigned and translated texts whose attribution depends on editorial and archival work.
- **Born:** 17 October 1880, Odessa, Kherson Governorate, Russian Empire; now Odesa, Ukraine.
- **Died:** shortly before midnight on 3 August 1940 according to the contemporaneous JTA report; conventionally recorded as 4 August 1940, Camp Betar, Hunter, Greene County, New York, United States.
- **Cause:** coronary thrombosis/heart attack; no documentary basis was found for poisoning, assassination, or another violent cause.
- **Original burial:** New Montefiore Cemetery, Farmingdale, Long Island, New York.
- **Reburial:** Jabotinsky and his wife Johanna were reinterred on Mount Herzl, Jerusalem, on 9 July 1964, after an Israeli government order under Prime Minister Levi Eshkol.
- **Occupations and roles:** journalist, foreign and war correspondent, feuilletonist, novelist, poet, playwright, translator, editor, orator, political organizer, British Army soldier and lieutenant, Zionist executive member, founder and president of Hatzohar and the New Zionist Organization, head of Betar, and supreme political commander of the Irgun after 1937.
- **Military service:** private in the 20th Battalion, London Regiment; later lieutenant in the 38th Battalion, Royal Fusiliers, one of the Jewish Legion battalions; Palestine campaign, 1918.
- **Honor:** appointed **Member of the Order of the British Empire (MBE)** in December 1919 for wartime service.
- **Languages:** Russian was his primary literary language; he acquired Italian fluency, wrote and spoke Hebrew, used Yiddish politically and journalistically, and worked in English and other European languages. Claims that he was equally fluent in eight or more languages are plausible as descriptions of wide competence but rarely define fluency consistently.
- **Health and personal habits:** The consulted medical record is thin. No documented addiction, psychiatric hospitalization, sustained psychological collapse, or disabling chronic illness was found. Contemporaries described exhaustion under constant travel and political work. His final collapse was diagnosed as coronary thrombosis/heart attack.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and siblings

Jabotinsky was born into an assimilated, Russian-speaking, middle-class Jewish household. His father, **Yevno/Yevgeny Grigoryevich Zhabotinsky**, came from Nikopol and worked in grain or wheat commerce; accounts connect him with the Russian Society of Shipping and Trade. His mother, **Chava/Eva Markovna Zach** (usually dated 1835–1926), came from Berdychiv. Vladimir's elder brother **Myron** died when Vladimir was an infant. His sister **Tereza**, also called **Tamara Yevgenyevna Zhabotinskaya-Kopp**, later founded or directed a private girls' school in Odessa.

In 1885 the family went to Germany seeking treatment for the father's illness. Yevno died in 1886, when Vladimir was six. The family returned to Odessa with reduced finances. Chava supported it in part by operating a stationery or writing-supplies shop. Later heroic portraits sometimes describe an affluent childhood; Jabotinsky himself remembered his father's death as leaving the family poor. “Previously comfortable, then financially constrained” best reconciles the evidence.

The household was Jewish by family identity but not strongly observant. He received some childhood Hebrew instruction—Yehoshua Ravnitzky is named as a teacher before his bar mitzvah—but later wrote that he had grown up without an inner attachment to Jewish religion or traditional culture. His subsequent nationalism was a political and cultural acquisition, not the uninterrupted continuation of a religious upbringing.

### Marriage and child

In October 1907 he married **Johanna/Joanna/Jeanne/Ania Galperina** (1884–1949), an Odessa acquaintance. Their only child, **Eri (Ari) Theodore Jabotinsky**, was born 16 December 1910. Eri became an engineer or mathematician, Revisionist activist, participant in the Bergson Group in the United States, and member of Israel's First Knesset. He resigned from Herut's parliamentary faction in 1951 amid disagreements about party discipline and died in 1969.

Jabotinsky's itinerant career repeatedly separated him from Johanna and Eri. Wartime and political correspondence shows affection, but associates and Eri remembered long absences. The family lived at different times in Odessa, Constantinople, Palestine, Paris, and London or maintained households while he traveled. No reliable evidence of a second family, acknowledged extramarital partnership, or other children was found in the consulted record. Biographers discuss flirtations, emotional attachments, and his cosmopolitan social world, but the documentary basis is too uneven to turn these into a catalogue of affairs.

### Descendants and political dynasty claims

Jabotinsky was not biologically related to the Netanyahu family. The connection is ideological and organizational. **Benzion Netanyahu** joined the Revisionist apparatus, worked in 1940 as an assistant in Jabotinsky's American circle—more precisely, under or alongside Jabotinsky's secretary Benjamin Akzin—and after Jabotinsky's death became executive director of the New Zionist Organization of America. Benjamin Netanyahu is consequently often called Jabotinsky's “ideological grandson.” This is a political genealogy, not kinship.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1880–1898

Odessa was a Black Sea imperial port with Russian, Ukrainian, Jewish, Greek, Italian, and other communities, a strong commercial press, and a Jewish intelligentsia less enclosed by traditional communal life than in many inland towns. Jabotinsky attended Russian schools and one of Odessa's gymnasia. He disliked the routine and did not complete a conventional secondary credential. He wrote poems, skits, and journalism as a teenager and moved in a literary circle that included **Korney Chukovsky**, later a major Russian critic and children's writer.

His retrospective accounts made certain childhood moments foreshadow Zionism. Modern scholars, especially Michael Stanislawski and reviewers of Brian Horowitz's archival work, warn that *Story of My Life* sometimes backdated a mature Zionist identity into adolescence. The distinction matters: the young Jabotinsky was first a Russian-language cosmopolitan, aesthete, and radical journalist. The Kishinev pogrom and Russian political crises turned Jewish nationalism into the central organizing fact of his life.

## Education and Formation, 1898–1903

### Bern and Rome

At seventeen or eighteen, with an Odessa newspaper assignment, Jabotinsky left for **Bern** and then **Rome**. From autumn 1898 he registered as a law student at the University of Rome (Sapienza) for several years but attended irregularly and did not graduate. Some older reference works say he “studied law in Bern and Rome”; his actual life was closer to that of a foreign correspondent and bohemian man of letters than a professional law student.

Italy supplied enduring models: the language, feuilleton style, civic nationalism, **Giuseppe Mazzini**, **Giuseppe Garibaldi**, the Risorgimento, and an ideal of public dignity and theatrical politics. These influences predated Fascism. Later Revisionist borrowing from Italian martial style cannot be reduced to his youthful love of Italy, but neither can the continuity be ignored.

He sent dispatches to *Odesskii Listok* and later *Odesskie Novosti*. His light, ironic columns under “Altalena” made him known before his Zionist career. He returned to Odessa in 1901. In April 1902 tsarist authorities arrested him for anti-establishment feuilletons and/or association with a radical Italian publication. He spent roughly two months in detention. This was a political press arrest, not a conviction for violence or financial crime.

### Intellectual formation

His thought combined elements not easily placed on a single left-right line:

- nineteenth-century liberal individualism and suspicion of state coercion;
- national self-determination and minority-rights theory developed in the multinational empires;
- Italian civic nationalism and heroic literature;
- positivist and Social-Darwinian language common to the fin de siècle;
- admiration for discipline, ceremony, physical training, and military preparedness;
- anti-socialism and hostility to class struggle, balanced by a state-guaranteed social minimum;
- secular Jewish nationalism, later supplemented by greater respect for biblical tradition without orthodox observance.

## Early Zionist and Russian Career, 1903–1914

### Kishinev, self-defense, and the Sixth Zionist Congress

The April 1903 Kishinev pogrom became the decisive public turning point. Jabotinsky helped organize Jewish self-defense in Odessa and traveled or spoke to encourage armed preparedness elsewhere. He translated **Hayim Nahman Bialik's** pogrom poem *Be-'Ir ha-Haregah* (*In the City of Slaughter*) into Russian; the translation carried the poem's condemnation of Jewish powerlessness to a broad Russian-speaking audience.

He was elected a Russian delegate to the **Sixth Zionist Congress** at Basel in August 1903, the last attended by Theodor Herzl. Jabotinsky opposed the Uganda proposal and increasingly associated Zionism with Hebrew culture, a territorial center in Palestine, and political sovereignty. He did not become Herzl's intimate disciple; later movement genealogy magnified a short chronological overlap into a direct apostolic succession.

### Russian Revolution, minority rights, and Helsingfors

During the 1905 revolution and its pogroms, he combined Zionism with a campaign for Jewish civil and national rights in Russia. He helped organize the **League for the Attainment of Full Rights for the Jewish People in Russia** and participated in electoral politics but failed to win a seat in the Second Duma.

At the 1906 **Helsingfors Conference** of Russian Zionists, he helped secure a program of *Gegenwartsarbeit*—work in the present: Zionists would pursue a future center in Palestine while demanding equal civil rights, national-cultural autonomy, education, language rights, and representative institutions for Jews where they lived. This minority-rights program later informed his proposed guarantees for Palestinian Arabs, although under his Palestinian plan Jews would first obtain demographic and sovereign predominance.

### Language, schools, press, and Ottoman politics

He promoted Hebrew-language schooling and the future Hebrew University. He also proposed rendering Hebrew in Latin characters to accelerate mass acquisition; *Taryag Milim* (“613 Words”), later published posthumously, exemplified this program. His Hebrew translations of Edgar Allan Poe's “The Raven” and “Annabel Lee” became canonical demonstrations of his linguistic craft.

Following the 1908 Young Turk Revolution, he worked in Constantinople/Istanbul and participated in Zionist press and information projects aimed at Ottoman public opinion. He edited or wrote for papers including *Rassvet* (“Dawn”) and contributed across the Russian Jewish press. He traveled widely through Russia and Europe as lecturer and organizer. A January 1912 column, “Horoscope,” foresaw a technologically destructive war among major European powers; later hagiography treats it as prophecy, though fear of a general European war was not unique to him.

His 1905 image of the “new Hebrew” was constructed as the physical and psychological opposite of the stereotyped Diaspora “Yid”: proud, beautiful, commanding, vigorous, and unafraid. The passage is documented and uses internalized antisemitic physiognomic stereotypes. Admirers read it as regenerative revolt against persecution; critics read it as contempt for Diaspora Jews and a racialized masculinity. Both receptions belong to the history of the text.

## First World War and the Jewish Legion, 1914–1919

### War correspondence and Alexandria

When war began, the World Zionist Organization declared neutrality. Jabotinsky worked as a correspondent for the liberal Moscow newspaper *Russkie Vedomosti*, traveling on European and North African fronts. In early 1915 he visited Jewish refugees in Alexandria—Russian subjects and Palestinian Jews deported by Ottoman authorities—and met **Joseph Trumpeldor**, a decorated, one-armed veteran of the Russo-Japanese War.

### Zion Mule Corps: collaboration and disagreement

Jabotinsky and Trumpeldor petitioned British commander General John Maxwell for a Jewish unit that would fight for Palestine. Maxwell would authorize only a transport unit for another theater. Trumpeldor accepted; Jabotinsky regarded mule transport and a name lacking explicit Jewish national status as inadequate and did not serve in the resulting **Zion Mule Corps**. Under Lieutenant-Colonel **John Henry Patterson** and Trumpeldor, approximately 650 men served at Gallipoli with about 750 mules; eight were killed and fifty-five wounded, and three received decorations.

It is therefore accurate to call Jabotinsky a political co-initiator of the Zion Mule Corps but inaccurate to imply that he fought with it at Gallipoli.

### Creation of Jewish battalions

After the Mule Corps was disbanded in 1916, Jabotinsky campaigned in London for a combat formation. Established Anglo-Jewish leaders feared that a visibly Jewish unit would expose Jews to charges of dual loyalty or endanger Ottoman Jews. Jabotinsky enlisted as a private in January 1917 in the 20th Battalion, London Regiment, alongside former Mule Corps volunteers. With Patterson, Trumpeldor, Leo Amery, and other supporters, he pressed the War Cabinet. Authorization followed for the 38th, 39th, and 40th battalions of the Royal Fusiliers, collectively remembered as the **Jewish Legion**.

Jabotinsky was commissioned and served in the 38th. He took part in the 1918 Palestine campaign, including operations at the Jordan crossings and in Transjordan. He was not the Legion's battlefield commander; Patterson commanded the 38th, and British authority remained decisive. The claim that the Legion “conquered Palestine” is movement mythology. It was one component of a much larger imperial force.

He argued after the armistice that a permanent Jewish garrison should protect the developing national home. The British demobilized most of the force. After criticizing General Allenby's policy and the reduction of the Legion, Jabotinsky was demobilized in September 1919. His MBE followed in December.

## Palestine, Self-Defense, Trial, and Zionist Executive, 1919–1923

### Jerusalem defense and imprisonment

Remaining in Palestine after demobilization, Jabotinsky organized and openly drilled a Jewish self-defense network in Jerusalem. During the Nabi Musa disturbances of April 1920, Arab crowds attacked Jews in and near the Old City. British police searched Zionist premises and found weapons associated with Jabotinsky's group—contemporary summaries give three rifles, two pistols, and 250 rounds.

When nineteen associates were arrested, Jabotinsky declared himself their commander. A British military court sentenced him to **fifteen years' hard labor** for illegal possession and organization of armed force; the other men received shorter terms. He was confined at Acre. The sentence was reduced, and in July 1920 High Commissioner Herbert Samuel's general amnesty released Jews and Arabs convicted in connection with the disturbances. His conviction was not a finding that he had initiated the anti-Jewish attacks; it punished illegal armament under British rule.

Silent film made by Yaakov Ben-Dov records Jabotinsky and his fellow prisoners at Acre. The imprisonment became a founding martyr-story of organized Jewish defense and later Haganah and Irgun memory.

### Executive office and break with Weizmann

In 1920 Jabotinsky entered the first Assembly of Representatives of the Yishuv. Chaim Weizmann brought him into the Zionist Executive in 1921. He helped found **Keren Hayesod** and directed propaganda or information work, lecturing in North America.

His differences with Weizmann concerned pace, declared objectives, military protection, immigration, and Britain's 1922 removal of Transjordan from provisions for the Jewish national home. Jabotinsky wanted the Zionist movement to state openly that it sought a Jewish majority and eventual state on both banks of the Jordan, backed by a Jewish force within the British imperial system. Weizmann pursued incremental diplomacy and avoided a declaration likely to provoke British or Arab resistance.

### Petliura/Slavynsky agreement

In 1921 Jabotinsky negotiated through **Maksym Slavynsky**, representative of Symon Petliura's Ukrainian government-in-exile, for a Jewish gendarmerie to accompany a projected Ukrainian army and prevent pogroms. Forces nominally under Petliura had committed extensive anti-Jewish massacres during the Ukrainian civil wars, although the extent of his personal orders and control remains debated. Jabotinsky argued that an armed Jewish detachment could save lives and that Slavynsky accepted safeguards; opponents regarded any compact with Petliura as rehabilitation of a pogrom regime. The projected force was never created.

The agreement was a major cause of censure within the Zionist Executive. It is a documented political negotiation, not evidence that Jabotinsky organized pogroms; it is also not adequately described as an innocent friendship detached from the record of Petliura's armies.

On 18 January 1923 Jabotinsky resigned from the Executive and for a time from the Zionist Organization.

## Revisionist Zionism and Its Institutions, 1923–1930

### “The Iron Wall”

Jabotinsky first used “iron wall” publicly in the early 1920s and gave it canonical form in two Russian essays published in *Rassvet* in November 1923: **“O zheleznoi stene (My i araby)”** (“On the Iron Wall [We and the Arabs]”) and its ethical sequel.

The argument was explicit:

1. Palestinian Arabs were a native people with ordinary national attachment, not fools who could be bribed by economic benefit.
2. No native population voluntarily accepts colonization or loss of majority status.
3. Zionist settlement aimed at a Jewish majority and would therefore meet rational Arab resistance.
4. Settlement could proceed only behind an “iron wall”—a power independent of Arab consent, at that stage principally British military protection.
5. Once Arabs lost hope of defeating Zionism, representative Arab leaders would negotiate practical guarantees and mutual concessions.

The essay is simultaneously more candid about colonial conflict and more coercive than gradualist Zionist rhetoric. It does not promise immediate reconciliation; it makes military superiority the precondition of negotiation. It also does not call Palestinians subhuman or deny that their resistance is patriotic. Both selective versions circulate widely.

### Hatzohar

Jabotinsky moved through Berlin to Paris and united opposition groups. In April 1925 they founded the **Union of Zionists-Revisionists**, Hebrew acronym **Hatzohar**. “Revision” meant revision of Weizmann's strategy: an explicit Jewish-state goal, mass immigration, a Jewish Legion, resistance to restrictions on Transjordan, private capital, and an alternative to Labor Zionist control of institutions.

At the 1925 Zionist Congress, Jabotinsky was initially the only Revisionist delegate. The movement grew rapidly, especially in Poland and the Baltic states, but never captured the World Zionist Organization during his life.

### Betar

After Jabotinsky spoke in Riga in 1923, **Aharon Propes** and other young activists created **Brit Yosef Trumpeldor**, abbreviated Betar and evoking the ancient fortress of Betar. It emphasized Hebrew, immigration, physical training, uniforms, ceremony, obedience, *hadar* (dignified bearing), and military preparedness. Jabotinsky supplied texts, anthem, symbols, and doctrine and became *Rosh Betar* in 1929.

Betar's discipline produced self-defense cadres and later underground fighters; its pageantry also generated accusations of fascism. Activists within the movement, including **Abba Ahimeir**, openly admired Mussolini or authoritarian “national revolution.” Jabotinsky sometimes tolerated them, sometimes rebuked them, and rejected proposals to make him an unlimited dictator. The organization cannot be described accurately either as a liberal debating club or as a uniform fascist party.

### Doar Hayom and exclusion from Palestine

In 1928–1929 Jabotinsky lived in Palestine and edited the Jerusalem Hebrew daily **Doar Hayom**. He left on a foreign lecture tour in late 1929. British authorities then denied him permission to return; formal notification is often dated 1930. He never again entered Palestine. “Exile” is politically apt but legally imprecise: this was an administrative exclusion under the Mandate, not deportation after a new conviction.

## Ideology: State, Society, Religion, Race, and Economy

### National state and territory

Jabotinsky demanded a Jewish majority and state throughout Mandate Palestine, including Transjordan. He usually imagined it connected to Britain as a dominion or strategic ally, not initially born through anti-British war. He rejected the Peel Commission's 1937 small Jewish state because it lacked territory for millions of refugees and conceded land east and west of the Jordan.

### Individual liberty and minority rights

His maxim was “every individual is a king.” He defended elections, opposition, press freedom, legal equality, and national-cultural autonomy. A 1934 Revisionist constitutional draft, printed in *The Jewish War Front* (1940), envisaged:

- equal citizenship and public service;
- Hebrew and Arabic with equal legal status;
- state offices answering citizens in either language;
- autonomous Jewish and Arab cultural communities;
- nondiscriminatory land allocation;
- an Arab deputy premier when the premier was Jewish and the reverse arrangement when the premier was Arab.

These provisions were designed for a state whose demographic and sovereign character would first be Jewish. Equality was not a substitute for the Jewish majority; it was the promised constitutional order after the majority had been secured.

### Arabs, expulsion, and transfer

For most of his career Jabotinsky publicly rejected expulsion. In a 1927 correction to *Ha-Olam*, he called removal of all or part of the Arab population immoral and impossible. The “Iron Wall” sequel pledged that two peoples would remain and that his movement would not eject anyone. These are documented positions, not inventions of later liberal admirers.

Gil Rubin's archival research identifies a late change after the outbreak of the Second World War. Notes dated 11 November 1939 on “population exchanges” and related 1940 material show Jabotinsky contemplating removal or transfer of Palestinian Arabs to make room for millions of displaced European Jews in a postwar order of ethnically homogenized states. Rubin describes this as initial evidence of support for transfer, contrary to Jabotinsky's previous program. The record supports neither “he always planned expulsion” nor “he never considered it.” The documented sequence is decades of anti-expulsion constitutionalism followed by serious, incompletely developed transfer thinking in 1939–1940.

### Economy and welfare

Jabotinsky opposed Marxism, class war, the Histadrut's institutional monopoly, and compulsory socialist organization. He sought private investment, entrepreneurship, arbitration in labor disputes, and a middle-class economy. Yet he was not a pure laissez-faire theorist. His “five M's”—food, dwelling, clothing, education, and medicine, named from Hebrew terms beginning with *mem*—were necessities the state must guarantee. He defended a social floor funded through taxation or compulsory service while resisting collective ownership as a governing ideal.

### Religion

He was personally secular and initially treated Zionism as national rather than theological. Biblical literature supplied symbols—Samson, the Jubilee, kingship, sacrifice—and his later writings showed increased respect for Jewish tradition. He worked with religious Zionists and demanded freedom of conscience. No evidence makes him an orthodox believer, atheist doctrinaire, or advocate of clerical government.

### Race and the “new Jew”

Jabotinsky used the vocabulary of race, stock, physical type, and national character common in early twentieth-century Europe. His idealized muscular “Hebrew” repudiated the allegedly weak and unattractive Diaspora Jew. Scholars disagree on whether his nationalism is best called cultural, racial, environmental, or a shifting combination. The language is documented; it should not be retroactively converted either into Nazi biological doctrine, which he opposed, or into contemporary race-neutral civic nationalism.

## Radicalization, Fascist Italy, and Conflict with Labor Zionism, 1930–1935

### Ben-Gurion and the labor conflict

The principal internal rival was **David Ben-Gurion**, leader of Mapai and the Histadrut. Their camps fought over organized labor, immigration certificates, picketing, strikebreaking, institutional control, and political authority. Revisionists established the National Labour Federation as an alternative to the Histadrut. Street violence and inflammatory newspapers deepened the conflict.

Ben-Gurion called Jabotinsky “Vladimir Hitler” and published a polemic headed “Jabotinsky in the footsteps of Hitler.” Jabotinsky answered with mockery, including “Ben Bouillon.” The Nazi analogy was political invective before the Holocaust, not a court or scholarly finding.

Through industrialist **Pinhas Rutenberg**, the two leaders met repeatedly in London in 1934 and signed agreements to suppress intra-Zionist violence, regulate labor conflicts, and normalize party relations. Jabotinsky secured Revisionist approval; a Histadrut referendum rejected the accords in 1935. The negotiations show personal capacity for compromise despite public demonization.

### Arlosoroff murder

Labor Zionist official **Haim Arlosoroff** was shot on a Tel Aviv beach on 16 June 1933. Revisionists **Abba Ahimeir, Avraham Stavsky, and Zvi Rosenblatt** were arrested. Ahimeir was discharged from the murder case for insufficient evidence; Rosenblatt was acquitted at trial; Stavsky was convicted and sentenced to death on Sima Arlosoroff's identification, then acquitted on appeal for lack of legally required corroboration. The murder remains unsolved.

Jabotinsky made their defense a movement cause and described the prosecution as a blood libel. There is no documentary proof that he ordered or knew of a murder plan. The accused's Revisionist affiliations, violent political atmosphere, contradictory witness evidence, original conviction, appellate acquittal, and later alternative theories all belong in the record. An Israeli state commission in the 1980s did not establish the identity of the killers.

### Fascism: documented contacts and contested classification

The following are documented:

- Jabotinsky admired the Italian Risorgimento and sought Italian diplomatic support.
- He wrote to Mussolini in 1922 and pursued contacts with Fascist officials in the 1930s as British support weakened.
- Fascist Italy permitted the Revisionist **Betar Naval Academy** at Civitavecchia, opened in 1934 and run operationally by **Jeremiah Halpern** under nominal Italian director Nicola Fusco, a local Fascist official.
- Cadets trained in an Italian institutional environment, participated in Fascist public ceremony, expressed support for Mussolini, marched with Italian forces, and collected scrap during the Ethiopian war.
- Some Revisionist Maximalists explicitly praised fascism, dictatorship, and the Nazi assault on Marxism before Nazi anti-Jewish policy made such rhetoric untenable.
- Jabotinsky tolerated some authoritarian followers and valued uniforms, hierarchy, sacrifice, national unity, and militarization.

Also documented:

- His 1925/1926 essay commonly translated “Zionist Fascism” attacked blind leadership and compared Weizmann's following—not his own desired constitution—to fascist obedience.
- He rejected Mussolini's “Doctrine of Fascism” when Leone Carpi sent it to him.
- He defended parliamentary liberty, press freedom, individual rights, and minority autonomy, all incompatible with the Fascist one-party state.
- He rejected demands that he accept unlimited authority.
- In “Jews and Fascism—Some Remarks and a Warning” (1935), he warned Jewish admirers of the regime; after Italy aligned with Nazi Germany and enacted racial laws, the political relationship collapsed.

Scholarly classifications therefore divide. Michael Tress and critics identify fascist components in his myth of rebirth, militarized youth, leader principle, and political style. Jacob Shavit, Colin Shindler, and liberal interpreters distinguish Jabotinsky from fascist doctrine while documenting fascist currents within Revisionism. The evidence supports **an ideologically hybrid liberal nationalist whose movement contained organized fascists and whose realpolitik produced collaboration with Fascist Italy**, rather than a simple acquittal or identity equation.

### New Zionist Organization

After the World Zionist Congress refused to make immediate statehood its formal objective, Jabotinsky left the WZO and in September 1935 founded the **New Zionist Organization (NZO or Hatzach)**. It became his diplomatic-political arm; Betar was the youth and training arm; the surviving Irgun became the underground military arm.

## Evacuation, Poland, Irgun, and the Approach of War, 1936–1939

### The Evacuation Plan

In 1936 Jabotinsky proposed transferring roughly **1.5 million European Jews** to Palestine over ten years, often itemized as 750,000 from Poland plus Jews from Germany, Hungary, Romania, and the Baltic states. He negotiated with Polish foreign minister Józef Beck and officials in Hungary and Romania. Poland wanted large-scale Jewish emigration and later supplied arms and training to Irgun/Betar cadres.

The alliance had contrary meanings. Jabotinsky sought a state and refuge; Polish officials sought reduction of a Jewish minority subjected to state and popular antisemitism. He demanded that Poland guarantee equal rights even while assisting emigration, but failed to secure the declaration he wanted. The Bund, mainstream Zionists, and other Polish Jewish leaders accused him of legitimizing the antisemites' program. The Bund's hostile manifesto charged that “decent” Jews had endorsed Endecja's demand to drive Jews out. The British immigration regime made the numerical plan impossible; the WZO did not adopt it.

Later hagiography calls the plan a feasible rescue rejected by blind Jewish leaders. That overstates available ships, British certificates, Palestinian absorptive capacity, financing, and popular willingness to leave. Hostile accounts that call it only collaboration omit Jabotinsky's genuine attempt to create an escape route from escalating danger.

### “Liquidation of the Diaspora” and alleged Holocaust prophecy

The authenticated slogan **“If you do not eliminate/liquidate the Diaspora, the Diaspora will eliminate you”** expressed his demand for mass migration; “liquidate” meant dismantle Jewish exile through emigration, though its later sound after the Holocaust gives it a grimmer resonance.

A more elaborate speech said to have been delivered in Warsaw on Tisha B'Av 1938 describes Jabotinsky as ceaselessly warning Polish Jews of a volcano about to emit “all-consuming lava” or “fires of destruction.” It has been repeated by Israeli officials, museums, and social media as a precise Holocaust prophecy. Recent archival researchers argue that no contemporaneous report records this speech and trace the familiar wording to posthumous publication, suggesting fabrication or conflation. Jabotinsky unquestionably warned of European Jewish danger, demanded evacuation, and spoke of millions needing rescue. The theatrical “volcano” text should be labeled **disputed/apocryphal**, not used as verbatim proof without qualification.

His foresight also had limits. Scholarship cited by Gil Rubin notes that Jabotinsky at times doubted a general European war would occur. The later “Cassandra who foresaw the Holocaust exactly” is a cultic synthesis of genuine alarm, failed plans, selective quotation, and postwar knowledge.

### Irgun and political violence

The Irgun began in 1931 as Haganah Bet under **Avraham Tehomi**. After a 1937 reconciliation returned roughly half its membership and many officers to the Haganah, Jabotinsky assumed authority over the smaller Revisionist remnant. Operational commanders included Robert Bitker, Moshe Rosenberg, and **David Raziel**.

Jabotinsky authorized ending *havlagah* (restraint) during the Arab Revolt. Irgun reprisals in 1937–1939 included shootings and bombs in Arab markets, buses, and neighborhoods, killing civilians. The organization called these “active defense” or retaliation for Arab killings; British, Arab, Jewish Agency, and later historical sources describe many as terrorism or indiscriminate reprisals. Jabotinsky was outside Palestine but was political supreme commander and approved the strategic shift. Exact authorization for each attack is not established in the consulted record.

After the 1939 White Paper, Jabotinsky sent coded plans for a dramatic revolt: he and illegal immigrants would land near Tel Aviv, the Irgun would seize Government House and other centers, raise a flag, hold for at least twenty-four hours, and an external provisional government would proclaim a Jewish state. Avraham Stern proposed tens of thousands of armed immigrants; Poland trained and armed cadres. The outbreak of the European war ended the project. It was a concrete conspiracy to launch insurrection against British rule, not merely rhetoric, but it never reached attempted execution.

### Illegal immigration

Hatzohar, Betar, and Irgun organized **Af-Al-Pi** (“Nevertheless”) immigration in defiance of British quotas. Ships carried thousands—movement totals vary, and official Jabotinsky sources offer figures from about 18,000 to “tens of thousands.” Conditions were crowded and dangerous. The activity rescued migrants and developed maritime and clandestine capacity; it also violated Mandate law and sometimes exposed passengers to interception or shipboard danger.

## Final Year, Jewish Army Campaign, and Death, 1939–1940

After Britain and France declared war on Germany, Jabotinsky suspended the proposed anti-British revolt and sought a Jewish army to fight Nazi Germany. In May 1940 he offered Winston Churchill support for a large Jewish volunteer corps, often stated as 130,000 men. He also approached Weizmann and Ben-Gurion about a united front; no common command emerged.

He went to the United States to mobilize political and financial support for the NZO and Jewish army. His American circle included **Benjamin Akzin, Benzion Netanyahu, Joseph Schechtman, Hillel Kook (Peter Bergson), Ari Jabotinsky**, and other young Revisionists. After his death, parts of this network formed the Committee for a Jewish Army and Emergency Committee to Save the Jewish People of Europe—the “Bergson Group.” Their later record should not be projected wholly onto Jabotinsky, who died before the extermination program was publicly understood in its full scope.

On 3 August 1940 he visited Camp Betar at Hunter, New York, inspected a formation or parade, became ill, and died from coronary thrombosis shortly before midnight. Institutional calendars often date the death 4 August, perhaps from the Hebrew date or post-midnight certification. He was fifty-nine.

His 3 November 1935 will directed that he be buried or cremated where he died and that his remains be moved to Palestine only by order of a future Jewish government. He was buried at New Montefiore Cemetery. Johanna died in New York in 1949 and was buried beside him.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Institutions created or led

- Jewish self-defense organizations in Odessa/Russia, beginning 1903.
- League for full Jewish rights in Russia and the Helsingfors minority-rights program.
- Political campaign for the Zion Mule Corps and Jewish Legion, 1915–1917.
- Keren Hayesod founding and propaganda work, 1920–1923.
- Union of Zionists-Revisionists/Hatzohar, founded 1925.
- Betar: inspired and headed, but locally founded in Riga in 1923.
- National Labour Federation in Palestine: Revisionist alternative to the Histadrut.
- New Zionist Organization, founded 1935.
- Irgun: supreme political command from 1937, not original founder.
- Betar Naval Academy, Civitavecchia: sponsored by the Revisionist movement; Jeremiah Halpern was the practical organizer.
- Af-Al-Pi illegal immigration: coordinated political, youth, maritime, and underground project.

### Journalism and publishing

Jabotinsky earned much of his living through newspaper correspondence, editing, speaking, and books. Important outlets included *Odesskii Listok*, *Odesskie Novosti*, *Russkie Vedomosti*, *Rassvet*, *Doar Hayom*, *Haynt*, and numerous Revisionist weeklies. Political parties and supporters subsidized movement newspapers and travel, but consulted sources do not disclose a stable personal fortune or a transparent modern party-finance structure.

### Wealth, property, and business

No significant patents, industrial inventions, investment portfolio, corporate empire, or large personal property holdings were found. His father had been a grain merchant; this was family background, not Jabotinsky's later business. His adult finances were often insecure and dependent on journalism, lecture tours, publication, and movement support. The New Zionist Organization proposed development banks and national loans, but these were political-economic schemes, not proof of personal ownership.

No reliable evidence was found of personal fraud, embezzlement, corruption, money laundering, tax crime, or private enrichment. His documented illegal activity was political and military: unauthorized arms training, possession, clandestine immigration, command responsibility for an underground, and planning insurrection.

## Jabotinsky, Menachem Begin, Ben-Gurion, and Netanyahu

### Ben-Gurion

Jabotinsky and Ben-Gurion represented rival state-building systems. Ben-Gurion's Mapai built farms, unions, the Histadrut, Haganah, Jewish Agency bureaucracy, and a coalition capable of accepting partition and declaring a state in 1948. Jabotinsky built a transnational party, youth movement, rival union, illegal immigration apparatus, and armed underground centered on explicit sovereignty and military deterrence. Ben-Gurion won institutional power during both men's lifetimes; Jabotinsky won a large share of post-1977 ideological symbolism.

Their later images conceal intersections. Both sought a Jewish majority and state, organized force, believed large-scale immigration essential, and could entertain territorial tactics. They differed in class organization, British policy, party sovereignty, public candor about Arab resistance, and willingness to accept partition. Ben-Gurion's “Vladimir Hitler” label and refusal to order reburial became elements of Revisionist martyrology.

### Begin and Likud

Jabotinsky appointed **Menachem Begin** head of Betar Poland in 1939. Begin later commanded the Irgun from 1943, founded **Herut** in 1948, joined the Liberal Party in **Gahal** in 1965, assembled **Likud** in 1973, and became prime minister in 1977. This is the clearest organizational descent:

**Jabotinsky's Revisionism → Betar/Irgun → Begin's Herut → Gahal → Likud.**

It is not identity across time. Begin accepted the partition-created State of Israel, returned Sinai to Egypt, and abandoned practical claims to the East Bank of the Jordan. Later Likud territorial focus shifted to the West Bank and Gaza. Jabotinsky's proposed Arab cultural autonomy and bilingual constitutional equality do not map neatly onto later Likud legislation.

### The Netanyahu line

**Benzion Netanyahu** was an adherent rather than a principal lieutenant during most of Jabotinsky's life. In 1940 he joined the American NZO work, associated with Benjamin Akzin's secretariat, and after Jabotinsky died became executive director of the American organization through 1948. He opposed partition and transmitted a catastrophic view of Jewish history, distrust of Arab compromise, territorial maximalism, and the need to identify threats early.

Benjamin Netanyahu repeatedly invokes Jabotinsky's “iron wall,” Jewish military power, free-market economics, and warning against appeasement. Yet he has also accepted, rhetorically or temporarily, positions outside classic Revisionism: the existing Jordanian state, Oslo-derived arrangements, withdrawal from Hebron, and in 2009 a demilitarized Palestinian state. His governments' religious coalitions and the 2018 Nation-State Law also sit uneasily with Jabotinsky's secularism, equal status for Arabic, and formal collective rights.

“Jabotinsky → Benzion → Benjamin Netanyahu” is therefore a documented transmission of political vocabulary, historical sensibility, and movement affiliation, not proof that every Netanyahu policy executes a Jabotinsky blueprint.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. “The prophet who precisely foresaw the Holocaust”

- **Story:** In Warsaw in 1938 he warned Jews that a volcano would soon emit all-consuming fire and begged them to flee.
- **Appearance/circulation:** The text became prominent posthumously in Revisionist memorial culture and is now repeated by Israeli officials, heritage institutions, and social media.
- **Evidence:** His evacuation campaign and warnings about millions are contemporary facts. Recent research reports no contemporary trace of the familiar speech and argues that the wording was invented after his death.
- **Reputation effect:** Established a Cassandra cult: Jabotinsky saw annihilation while Weizmann, Ben-Gurion, the Bund, and Polish Jews refused salvation.
- **Classification:** The general warning is documented; the famous verbatim 1938 speech is disputed/apocryphal.

### 2. “Founder of the Israel Defense Forces”

- **Story:** The Jewish Legion and 1920 self-defense made Jabotinsky the IDF's father.
- **Evidence:** He was a crucial advocate of Jewish military formations and Jerusalem self-defense. The IDF arose in 1948 principally through the Haganah's institutions, while incorporating Irgun and Lehi personnel after severe conflict.
- **Effect:** Supplies Revisionism with a claim to state legitimacy despite Labor's pre-state institutional dominance.
- **Classification:** Symbolic lineage, not institutional identity.

### 3. “Founder of Betar and Irgun”

- **Story:** He personally founded every arm of Revisionism.
- **Evidence:** Propes and Riga activists organized Betar; Tehomi and dissident Haganah officers organized Haganah Bet. Jabotinsky became Betar's supreme head and later Irgun's commander.
- **Effect:** Concentrates a distributed movement into a single founder cult.
- **Classification:** Hagiographic shorthand.

### 4. “Vladimir Hitler”

- **Story:** Jabotinsky was a Jewish Hitler or direct fascist equivalent.
- **Origin:** Ben-Gurion and Labor polemic amid violent labor and Arlosoroff conflicts.
- **Evidence for:** militarized youth, authoritarian followers, Mussolini contacts, academy, civilian reprisals, and maximalist nationalism.
- **Evidence against identity claim:** multiparty liberalism, individual rights, free press, Arab civic equality, rejection of one-party dictatorship, Jewish target status under Nazism, and campaign for a Jewish army against Hitler.
- **Effect:** Fixed his image on the Zionist left and helped justify his exclusion from national honor under Ben-Gurion.
- **Classification:** Propaganda epithet attached to a real fascism controversy, not a literal equivalence.

### 5. “Flawless liberal democrat”

- **Story:** Jabotinsky's equality clauses prove that contemporary liberal democracy was his complete and consistent position.
- **Evidence against simplification:** he required a Jewish majority imposed despite native opposition, used racial and colonial vocabulary, approved coercive reprisals, and contemplated transfer late in life; Betar institutional culture privileged command and sacrifice.
- **Effect:** Allows contemporary Israeli liberals and Likud politicians to claim him selectively against illiberal legislation.
- **Classification:** Selective hagiography based on genuine liberal texts.

### 6. “Permanent expulsion plan from 1923 onward”

- **Story:** The Iron Wall was always a coded plan to remove Palestinians.
- **Evidence:** The essay openly sought colonization behind force, but its sequel, 1927 correction, and constitutional schemes explicitly retained an Arab population with equal rights. Transfer appears in the known record only in notes after the 1939 outbreak of war.
- **Effect:** Makes the later Nakba the straightforward execution of a single founding blueprint.
- **Classification:** Anachronistic compression; the coercive majority project is documented, the continuous expulsion plan is not.

### 7. “Jabotinsky as Netanyahu's personal mentor”

- **Story:** Benjamin Netanyahu inherits direct instruction from Jabotinsky.
- **Evidence:** Benjamin was born in 1949. His father Benzion briefly worked in Jabotinsky's final American apparatus and then led its American organization.
- **Effect:** Creates charismatic succession and legitimates Netanyahu as heir to a founder.
- **Classification:** Ideological genealogy, not personal relationship.

### 8. Altalena

Jabotinsky's pseudonym became the name of the Irgun arms ship shelled by the Israeli government in June 1948. He had been dead eight years and had no role in the incident. The name symbolically joined his literary persona to the near civil war between Begin's Irgun and Ben-Gurion's state, deepening the founder myth.

## Works by Jabotinsky

### Authorship cautions

Jabotinsky's oeuvre is dispersed across newspapers and languages. Posthumous books often translate, excerpt, retitle, or combine earlier writings. Publication under his name after 1940 does not mean composition after death. The Hebrew *Ketavim* was edited and published by Eri Jabotinsky and others in eighteen volumes from 1947 to 1959; the eleven-volume *Igrot* is a scholarly edition of letters, not a book he prepared.

### Major books, fiction, drama, memoir, and political works

- **1902–1920s — early plays, poems, stories, and feuilletons**, mainly Russian; dispersed and later collected. The play *Chuzhbina* is rendered *In a Foreign Land*.
- **1917 — *Turkey and the War*** (London: T. Fisher Unwin). Wartime geopolitical study.
- **1922/1928 — *Slovo o polku* / *Megilat ha-Gedud***, memoir-history of the Jewish battalions; English posthumous editions include *The Story of the Jewish Legion* (1945). Titles and first-edition dating vary by language.
- **1925 — *A Pocket Edition of Several Stories Mostly Reactionary***, Paris; story collection.
- **1926–1927 — *Samson Nazorei* (*Samson the Nazarite*)**, Russian serialization/book publication; English edition, 1930. Historical-biblical novel, later also excerpted as *Prelude to Delilah* (1945).
- **1932 — *Memories of a Contemporary***, autobiographical sketches; later collected.
- **1936 — *Piatero* (*The Five*)**, Paris, Russian. Novel of an assimilated Jewish family in pre-revolutionary Odessa; English translation by Michael R. Katz, Cornell University Press, 2005.
- **1936 — *Sippur Yamai* (*Story of My Life*)**, Hebrew autobiography. English scholarly edition by Brian Horowitz and Leonid Katsis, Wayne State University Press, 2015. It ends well before 1940 and is self-fashioning as well as testimony.
- **1940 — *The Jewish War Front*** (London: George Allen & Unwin). Wartime political program; includes constitutional material devised within the Revisionist leadership.
- **1942 — *The War and the Jew*** (New York: Dial Press), posthumous publication of wartime material.
- **1949 — *Taryag Milim: 613 Hebrew Words***, posthumous Latin-character Hebrew primer.
- **“Galilean Romance”**, film scenario preserved in the Jabotinsky Institute archive; unpublished in his lifetime.

### Foundational essays, manifestos, and speeches

- **1905 — “Dr. Herzl” / the “new Hebrew” passage.** Cultural-physiognomic national regeneration.
- **1906 — Helsingfors Program interventions.** Jewish minority rights and national-cultural autonomy; programmatic authorship was collective.
- **1912 — “Horoscope.”** Prediction of a technologically catastrophic European war.
- **1923 — “O zheleznoi stene (My i araby)” / “The Iron Wall (We and the Arabs).”** First published in Russian, *Rassvet*, 4 November 1923 according to the Institute catalogue.
- **1923 — “The Ethics/Morality of the Iron Wall.”** Sequel specifying negotiations and rights after resistance is defeated.
- **1925/1926 — “Zionist Fascism.”** Polemic against blind leader-following; frequently misread from title alone.
- **1926 — “What the Revisionist-Zionists Want.”** State, majority, immigration, and Arab-welfare program.
- **1926 — “The Aims of Zionism”; “American Zionism”; “Land Reform for Palestine.”**
- **1927 — Letter to the editor of *Ha-Olam*.** Denial that he advocated Arab expulsion.
- **1928 — “The Minorities Dream.”** National autonomy.
- **1929 — “On Militarism”; “Brit Trumpeldor.”** Defense of preparation and Betar.
- **1930 — “Bi-national Palestine.”** Relevant to federal/autonomist analysis despite his rejection of equal binational sovereignty.
- **1931 — “The Jewish State”; “What England Promised”; “Jewish Types in Russian Fiction.”**
- **1932 — “The Meaning of Preparedness”; “The Rule of the Fist in the Yishuv”; “Zion and Communism.”**
- **1934 — “The Idea/Ideology of Betar”; “Leader”; “Social Redemption.”** The last contains the five-M welfare program.
- **1935 — “Jews and Fascism—Some Remarks and a Warning”; “National Labour Federation.”**
- **1936–1938 — Evacuation speeches and evidence to the Palestine Royal Commission**, including testimony of 11 February 1937 on behalf of the NZO.
- **1937 — letters and articles opposing Peel partition; English republication of “The Iron Wall.”**
- **1939 — “National Sport”; “A White Paper against Diaspora Jewry”; “Population exchanges” manuscript notes.**
- **1940 — “ABC of the Jewish Army”; “Shall We Try?”; “Jews after the War” outline; Manhattan Center lecture; Jewish army proposals.**

## Death, Burial, and Reburial

The contemporaneous JTA obituary says Jabotinsky died of coronary thrombosis shortly before midnight at Camp Betar, establishing 3 August as the likely civil date. The Jabotinsky Institute, many biographies, and Israeli commemorations use 4 August/29 Tammuz 5700. Both dates should be searchable.

Ben-Gurion declined requests to order transfer of the remains after the State of Israel was created, reportedly arguing that Israel needed living Jews rather than bones. His refusal was legally significant because the will demanded a government order. Levi Eshkol's cabinet approved transfer on 15 March 1964. Jabotinsky and Johanna were reinterred at Mount Herzl on 9 July 1964 after public ceremonies in New York, Paris, Tel Aviv, and Jerusalem. The event normalized Revisionism within the state pantheon without ending partisan conflict over his legacy.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Revisionist succession

Jabotinsky's death produced no uncontested successor. The Irgun split with Avraham Stern's Lehi current in 1940; Raziel and later Begin led the Irgun. The NZO, Betar, American committees, and Palestine underground developed separate priorities. Schechtman, Akzin, Benzion Netanyahu, Eri Jabotinsky, and Hillel Kook preserved different aspects of the program.

Begin's Herut turned the underground tradition into parliamentary opposition. Ben-Gurion marginalized Herut as outside the governing consensus and invoked fascism and terrorism; Begin made Jabotinsky the absent father of a dispossessed half of Israel. Likud's 1977 victory converted that countermemory into state power.

### State commemoration

Jabotinsky is buried near Herzl on Mount Herzl. Israeli cities contain numerous Jabotinsky streets and squares. The **Jabotinsky Law of 2005** created state commemoration, and the Knesset marks his memorial day on 29 Tammuz. In 2022 Kyiv renamed a street for Volodymyr Zhabotynsky, adding a Ukrainian-national layer to a man formed in Russian imperial Odessa who supported Ukrainian independence but wrote mainly in Russian.

### Competing appropriations

- **Likud/Revisionist right:** prophet of Jewish power, territorial right, free enterprise, evacuation, and peace through deterrence.
- **Israeli liberal right:** defender of individual sovereignty, constitutional checks, Arab equality, free press, and welfare guarantees.
- **Labor tradition:** historic rival associated with strikebreaking, separatism, armed undergrounds, and fascist political style; later Labor governments helped integrate him into national memory.
- **Palestinian and anti-Zionist historiography:** unusually candid theorist of settler colonialism and military domination; progenitor of the “iron wall” security state.
- **Literary scholarship:** major Russian-Jewish Odessa writer, translator, and modernist whose politics long obscured *The Five*, *Samson*, and his verse.
- **Holocaust memory:** Cassandra who urged flight, complicated by the infeasibility of his plan and disputed prophecy text.

No single appropriation contains the whole record. The same archive contains native-resistance analysis and colonial coercion, Arab constitutional equality and late transfer notes, liberal individualism and militarized youth, opposition to dictatorship and collaboration with Fascist institutions.

## Chronology

- **17 Oct. 1880:** Born Vladimir Yevgenyevich Zhabotinsky in Odessa.
- **1885–1886:** Family goes to Germany for father's health; father dies; family returns to Odessa.
- **1898:** Leaves school and Odessa; reports from Bern and Rome; registers in law at Sapienza.
- **1901:** Returns to Odessa; literary and journalistic career expands.
- **Apr.–Jun. 1902:** Detained by tsarist authorities for radical journalism.
- **Apr. 1903:** Kishinev pogrom; organizes Jewish self-defense and enters full-time Zionist politics.
- **Aug. 1903:** Delegate to Sixth Zionist Congress; opposes Uganda proposal.
- **1904–1905:** Russian translation of Bialik's pogrom poem circulates; self-defense and minority-rights organizing during revolution.
- **1906:** Major role at Helsingfors Conference; national-cultural autonomy and “work in the present.”
- **Oct. 1907:** Marries Johanna Galperina.
- **1908–1909:** Works in Constantinople and Ottoman-oriented Zionist journalism.
- **16 Dec. 1910:** Son Eri born.
- **Jan. 1912:** Publishes “Horoscope,” anticipating a major mechanized European war.
- **1914:** War correspondent; Hebrew Poe translations published by this period.
- **Early 1915:** Meets Trumpeldor in Alexandria; campaigns for Jewish unit.
- **1915:** Zion Mule Corps formed; Jabotinsky does not serve in it.
- **May 1916:** Mule Corps disbanded; London campaign for Jewish combat unit continues.
- **Jan. 1917:** Enlists as private in London Regiment.
- **Aug. 1917:** British approval of Jewish battalion; later commissioned in 38th Royal Fusiliers.
- **1918:** Serves in Palestine/Transjordan campaign.
- **Sept. 1919:** Demobilized after dispute over Legion's future.
- **Dec. 1919:** Appointed MBE.
- **Apr. 1920:** Organizes Jerusalem defense during Nabi Musa violence; arrested.
- **1920:** Sentenced to fifteen years; imprisoned at Acre; released in July amnesty; elected to Yishuv Assembly.
- **Mar. 1921:** Joins Zionist Executive; Keren Hayesod and North American work.
- **1921:** Slavynsky/Petliura gendarmerie agreement creates major controversy.
- **1922:** Churchill White Paper separates Transjordan from Jewish-national-home provisions; Jabotinsky contacts Mussolini.
- **18 Jan. 1923:** Resigns from Zionist Executive.
- **Nov. 1923:** Publishes “The Iron Wall” and ethical sequel in Russian.
- **1923:** Riga activists establish Betar following his visit.
- **1924:** Moves organizational center to Paris.
- **Apr. 1925:** Hatzohar founded.
- **1925–1926:** “Zionist Fascism,” Revisionist program essays; expanding Congress representation.
- **1927:** *Samson the Nazarite*; denies advocating expulsion in *Ha-Olam*.
- **1928–1929:** Lives in Palestine; edits *Doar Hayom*.
- **1929:** Becomes *Rosh Betar*; leaves Palestine on tour.
- **1930:** British administration bars return; permanent life abroad.
- **1931:** Haganah split creates Haganah Bet/early Irgun under Tehomi.
- **1933:** Arlosoroff murdered; Revisionist suspects arrested; Jabotinsky leads defense campaign.
- **1934:** Stavsky's conviction overturned and defendants freed; Jabotinsky–Ben-Gurion agreements signed; Betar Naval Academy opens at Civitavecchia; Revisionist constitutional draft prepared.
- **1935:** Histadrut voters reject Ben-Gurion–Jabotinsky accords; Jabotinsky founds New Zionist Organization; writes warning on Jews and fascism.
- **1936:** Publishes *The Five* and Hebrew autobiography; launches Evacuation Plan; negotiates with Polish and Eastern European governments.
- **11 Feb. 1937:** Testifies to Palestine Royal Commission in London.
- **1937:** Rejects Peel partition; assumes supreme command over Revisionist Irgun remnant; Irgun ends restraint and attacks Arab targets; Af-Al-Pi immigration expands.
- **1938:** NZO adopts evacuation; famous “volcano” speech later attributed to this year, but authenticity disputed; Civitavecchia closes amid Italian racial turn.
- **1939:** White Paper; Poland–Irgun arms/training cooperation; appoints Begin head of Betar Poland; coded revolt plan; war cancels revolt; 11 November transfer notes.
- **1939–1940:** Campaigns for Jewish army against Nazi Germany; writes *The Jewish War Front* and postwar outlines.
- **May 1940:** Offers Churchill large Jewish volunteer corps; seeks Zionist unity.
- **3/4 Aug. 1940:** Dies at Camp Betar in Hunter, New York.
- **1949:** Johanna dies in New York.
- **1947–1959:** Eri and collaborators publish eighteen-volume Hebrew *Writings*.
- **1948:** Begin founds Herut; Jabotinsky's pseudonym given to the Irgun arms ship *Altalena*.
- **1956/1961:** Schechtman's two-volume biography consolidates sympathetic documentary legend.
- **15 Mar. 1964:** Eshkol government authorizes transfer of remains.
- **9 Jul. 1964:** Ze'ev and Johanna reinterred on Mount Herzl.
- **1965:** Herut and Liberal Party form Gahal.
- **1973:** Gahal becomes core of Likud.
- **1977:** Begin and Likud win government; Revisionist countermemory enters state power.
- **1996:** Benjamin Netanyahu first elected prime minister; Netanyahu family becomes principal contemporary bearer of Revisionist genealogy.
- **2005:** Israel enacts Jabotinsky commemoration law.
- **2015/2016:** *The Raven—Ze'ev Jabotinsky* documentary released.
- **2022:** Kyiv renames Muromska Street for Volodymyr Zhabotynsky.

## Atlas Connections

### Chaim Weizmann

- **[T3] Documented fact:** Jabotinsky corresponded extensively with Weizmann and served under him on the Zionist Executive from 1921 until his resignation in January 1923. Their written engagement continued through later disputes over British policy, immigration, and Zionist strategy; the Jabotinsky Institute catalog preserves, among other records, a [1917 letter from Jabotinsky to Weizmann](https://en.jabotinsky.org/archive/catalog-of-files/?itemId=134069).
- **[T5] Documented fact:** The two leaders met and worked together through the Zionist Executive and later political negotiations. In May 1940 Jabotinsky again approached Weizmann about cooperation on a Jewish army and wartime Zionist unity.

### David Ben-Gurion

- **[T3] Documented fact:** Jabotinsky and Ben-Gurion exchanged letters and written agreements during their attempt to reconcile Revisionist and Labor Zionism in 1934–1935. Surviving correspondence includes Jabotinsky’s request for approval to continue the negotiations; the resulting London agreements addressed political violence, labor disputes, and relations between their movements. [Jabotinsky Institute archival description](https://en.jabotinsky.org/archive/search-archive/item/?itemId=114175)
- **[T5] Documented fact:** Mediated by Pinhas Rutenberg, Jabotinsky and Ben-Gurion met repeatedly in London in 1934 and signed agreements on 26 October. Revisionists approved them, but Histadrut members rejected them in a 1935 referendum. The encounter and agreements are independently catalogued in the [Tel Aviv University research record](https://cris.tau.ac.il/en/publications/%D7%9C%D7%9C%D7%90-%D7%A4%D7%A9%D7%A8%D7%95%D7%AA-%D7%94%D7%A1%D7%9B%D7%9D-%D7%91%D7%9F-%D7%92%D7%95%D7%A8%D7%99%D7%95%D7%9F-%D7%96%D7%91%D7%95%D7%98%D7%99%D7%A0%D7%A1%D7%A7%D7%99-%D7%95%D7%9B%D7%A9%D7%9C%D7%95%D7%A0%D7%95-1934-1935/).

### Edgar Allan Poe

- **[T3] Documented fact:** Jabotinsky engaged directly with Poe’s writing by translating “The Raven” and “Annabel Lee” into Hebrew, with the translations published by 1914. The Jabotinsky Institute preserves his handwritten versions of “The Raven” in Hebrew and Russian transliteration. [Archive record](https://en.jabotinsky.org/archive/search-archive/item/?itemId=115670)

### Friedrich Nietzsche

- **[T3] Documented fact:** Jabotinsky read Nietzsche in his youth and subsequently discussed him in literary writing, including a comparison involving Maxim Gorky. Scholarship treats Nietzsche as one element in Jabotinsky’s early intellectual formation, while cautioning against reducing Jabotinsky’s political theory to Nietzschean influence. [David Ohana, *Nietzsche and Zion*](https://dokumen.pub/nietzsche-and-zion-0801437628-9780801437625.html)

### Giuseppe Garibaldi

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Jabotinsky wrote retrospectively about Garibaldi and the Italian Risorgimento as formative models encountered during his years in Rome, beginning in 1898. Garibaldi represented for him national liberation joined to civic courage and military voluntarism; this literary-political admiration preceded Italian Fascism and should not be conflated with it.

### Theodor Herzl

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Jabotinsky published the Russian essay “Dr. Herzl” in 1905, attempting a psychological and political portrait based on what he had “heard, seen, [and] read.” [Full text and bibliographic record](https://benyehuda.org/read/5107)
- **[T3] Documented fact:** At the Sixth Zionist Congress in Basel, 23–28 August 1903, Jabotinsky heard Herzl speak and later described the experience in his memoirs and the 1905 essay. It was the only Zionist Congress attended by both men. [National Library of Israel biography](https://www.nli.org.il/en/discover/israel/figures/zeev-jabotinsky)
- **[T4] Documented fact:** Both were delegates or principal participants at the Sixth Zionist Congress in Basel in August 1903, although Jabotinsky opposed Herzl’s Uganda proposal.
- **[T5] Reported fact:** Jabotinsky later recalled a personal encounter with Herzl at the congress, but no independent contemporaneous evidence confirming a private meeting has been located. The strongest secure claim is that Jabotinsky saw and heard Herzl there; historians dispute details of his retrospective account. [Discussion of the evidentiary problem](https://jewishreviewofbooks.com/articles/1968/your-time-is-up-jabotinsky-at-the-sixth-zionist-congress/)

### Benito Mussolini

- **[T3] Documented fact:** Jabotinsky wrote to Mussolini in Rome on 16 July 1922, seeking Italian support for Zionism and arguing against a pro-Arab Italian policy. The surviving letter predates Mussolini’s appointment as prime minister and is catalogued by the [Jabotinsky Institute](https://en.jabotinsky.org/archive/catalog-of-files/?itemId=146452); scholarship also records a copy dated 21 July in the Alfonso Pacifici Archive. [Vincenzo Pinto’s study](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/1353712042000242590)
- **[T3] Documented fact:** During the 1930s Jabotinsky’s movement pursued written and diplomatic engagement with Mussolini’s government, which permitted the Betar Naval Academy at Civitavecchia. This establishes contact with the regime but not a documented personal friendship or face-to-face meeting between Jabotinsky and Mussolini.

### Benjamin Netanyahu

- **[T1+] Documented fact:** Decades after Jabotinsky’s death, Netanyahu repeatedly wrote and delivered formal addresses about him, presenting Jabotinsky as an ideological source on Jewish military power, liberal nationalism, and deterrence. Netanyahu has also stated that he keeps and regularly reads Jabotinsky’s works; this is an intellectual-political reception rather than a personal relationship. [Jabotinsky Institute report](https://en.jabotinsky.org/media/9752/english52.pdf) and [Knesset record of Netanyahu’s Jabotinsky Day address](https://m.knesset.gov.il/en/News/PressReleases/Pages/Pr12208_pg.aspx)

No additional roster crossings were included where the available evidence showed only broad influence, shared subject matter, institutional succession, coincidental presence in the same country or city, or later comparison by third parties.
