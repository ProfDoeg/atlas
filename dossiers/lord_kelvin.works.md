## Works by William Thomson

### Scientific papers: a dispersed textual obra

- **1841–1908:** *The scientific-paper corpus*. Thompson’s standard bibliography records Thomson’s communications, articles, notes, replies, and abstracts individually; the contemporary biographical count is 661 papers. This is a body of work rather than a single text, and some entries are revised versions, summaries, or jointly authored manifestations rather than wholly independent works. [1][2]

- **1841:** *On Fourier’s Expansions of Functions in Trigonometrical Series*. Thomson’s first published paper, issued under the initials “P.Q.R.”; the pseudonym and his father’s role in submitting it complicate, but do not seriously dispute, its authorship. [1][3]

- **1842:** *On the Uniform Motion of Heat in Homogeneous Solid Bodies, and its Connection with the Mathematical Theory of Electricity*. A mathematical paper joining heat conduction to electrostatic analogy. [1][2]

- **1845:** *On the Elementary Laws of Statical Electricity*. A paper introducing what became known as the method of electrical images; it is a mathematical construction, not a material instrument. [1]

- **1846:** *On the Elementary Laws of Statical Electricity* and associated papers on electrical images. Their several journal manifestations resist treatment as wholly separate obras because Thomson repeatedly extended and republished the same investigation. [1][4]

- **1847:** *On the Theory of the Electric Telegraph*. An early investigation of signal propagation whose practical object was not merely descriptive: it helped constitute Thomson’s long telegraphic project. [1]

- **1848:** *On an Absolute Thermometric Scale Founded on Carnot’s Theory of the Motive Power of Heat, and Calculated from Regnault’s Observations*. A proposal for an absolute temperature scale—a conceptual and metrological obra, not an instrument or a book. The later kelvin scale developed from, but is not identical in formulation with, this first proposal. [1][5]

- **1849:** *An Account of Carnot’s Theory of the Motive Power of Heat*. An expository and reconstructive paper through which Thomson made Carnot’s work operative in British thermodynamics. [1][4]

- **1851:** *On the Dynamical Theory of Heat*. A thermodynamic memoir reconciling Carnot’s reasoning with Joule’s conservation of energy; it is both a theory-making text and part of a continuing project rather than a closed system. [1][4]

- **1852–1862:** *The Joule–Thomson experimental and theoretical papers*. A collaborative series by James Prescott Joule and Thomson on thermal effects in fluids, including what became the Joule–Thomson effect. The series should not be reassigned silently to either collaborator alone. [1][4]

- **1854–1857:** *Papers on the mathematical theory of submarine telegraphy*. A sequence of texts on retardation, signalling, conductor dimensions, and cable performance. Their status lies between theory, engineering protocol, and intervention in the Atlantic-cable controversy. [1][6]

- **1856:** *On the Electrodynamic Qualities of Metals: Effects of Magnetization on the Electric Conductivity of Nickel and of Iron*. A joint experimental paper with Joule; its authorship is explicitly collaborative. [1]

- **1856:** *On the Thermal Effects of Fluids in Motion*. A Joule–Thomson memoir reporting their porous-plug experiments and theory. It is one manifestation of the larger joint project, not the whole “Joule–Thomson effect.” [1][4]

- **1861:** *On the Electro-dynamic Qualities of Metals*. A continuation of Thomson and Joule’s joint experimental work rather than an entirely independent project. [1]

- **1862:** *On the Secular Cooling of the Earth*. A mathematical-geophysical paper estimating terrestrial age from heat conduction. Its conclusions became disputed because its model omitted heat later known to arise from radioactivity and because geological and biological timescales conflicted with it. [1]

- **1862:** *On the Age of the Sun’s Heat*. A physical argument for a finite solar age based on gravitational energy. It is a historically important model whose numerical conclusion did not survive nuclear physics. [1]

- **1863–1864:** *Reports on Electrical Standards*. Collaborative committee work toward reproducible electrical units. These reports resist individual authorship: Thomson was a principal maker, but the obra belonged institutionally to the British Association committee. [1]

- **1867:** *On Vortex Atoms*. A speculative dynamical model of matter presented to the Royal Society of Edinburgh. The “atoms” were conceptual and demonstrative constructions, often materialized with smoke rings, not discovered entities. [1][7]

- **1868:** *On Tidal Prediction*. A paper turning harmonic analysis into an operational programme for calculating tides, subsequently embodied in machines. [1][5]

- **1869:** *On Geological Dynamics*. An address extending Thomson’s cooling calculations into an intervention over the age and physical history of the Earth. It acts argumentatively and institutionally, approaching a scientific manifesto. [1]

- **1871:** *Inaugural Address to the British Association for the Advancement of Science*. A presidential address surveying physical science and asserting a dynamical programme for matter and energy; its public and programmatic action makes it more than a descriptive lecture. [1][8]

- **1873–1876:** *Kinetic-theory and dissipation papers*. A cluster of texts including discussions of the dissipation of energy and the reversibility paradox. They constitute an evolving project rather than a single settled theory. [1][4]

- **1879:** *Elasticity*. A long article made for the ninth edition of the *Encyclopædia Britannica*, later absorbed into Thomson’s collected papers. It is a reference work contribution rather than a freestanding book. [1][2]

- **1879:** *Heat*. Thomson’s substantial *Encyclopædia Britannica* article, likewise a commissioned component within another maker’s composite publication. [1][2]

- **1884:** *Steps towards a Kinetic Theory of Matter*. A lecture-text and speculative physical programme later collected in *Popular Lectures and Addresses*. [1][8]

- **1887:** *The Sun’s Heat*. A Royal Institution lecture and subsequently printed text revisiting Thomson’s solar-energy project. The lecture and printed paper are manifestations of one obra. [1][8]

- **1891:** *On a Decisive Test-Case Disproving the Maxwell–Boltzmann Doctrine regarding Distribution of Kinetic Energy*. A polemical theoretical paper; its title performs an argumentative act, although later physics did not accept its claimed decisiveness. [1]

- **1894:** *The Molecular Tactics of a Crystal*. A lecture and illustrated monograph using geometrical models to explain crystal structure. It crosses text, diagram, model, and performed demonstration, making it a small scientific Gesamtkunstwerk. [1][9]

- **1900:** *Nineteenth-Century Clouds over the Dynamical Theory of Heat and Light*. A Royal Institution discourse identifying two unresolved problems in classical physics. The familiar later story that its “clouds” straightforwardly predicted relativity and quantum theory is retrospective and disputed. [1][10]

- **1901–1907:** *Late papers on electrons, radioactivity, ether, and molecular dynamics*. A group of speculative papers included in the posthumous volumes of the collected works. Editorial selection and posthumous arrangement mean the resulting corpus is partly Larmor’s construction as editor. [4]

### Books and deliberately assembled textual works

- **1867:** *Treatise on Natural Philosophy, Volume I*. A jointly made textbook by Thomson and Peter Guthrie Tait intended to ground physical science in dynamics and energy. Only the first projected volume appeared, so the Treatise is both a book and an unfinished project. [1][11]

- **1873:** *Elements of Natural Philosophy, Part I*. A shorter instructional work by Thomson and Tait derived from the *Treatise*. Its derivative form is a deliberate pedagogical recasting, not merely a later printing. [1][12]

- **1872:** *Reprint of Papers on Electrostatics and Magnetism*. Thomson’s revised and annotated assembly of previously dispersed papers. It is simultaneously an obra of editorial self-construction and a collection of earlier works; its components should not be mistaken for texts first made in 1872. [1][12]

- **1882–1911:** *Mathematical and Physical Papers*. Six volumes collecting papers from 1841 onward. Thomson issued the first three volumes during his lifetime; Joseph Larmor completed the posthumous volumes, so the six-volume whole is not solely Thomson’s finished authorial object. [4][13]

- **1889:** *Popular Lectures and Addresses, Volume I: Constitution of Matter*. A curated collection of public addresses and papers rather than a continuous treatise. The lectures’ spoken occasions and printed forms are distinct manifestations. [1][8]

- **1891:** *Popular Lectures and Addresses, Volume II: Geology and General Physics*. A thematic collection containing Thomson’s public interventions in terrestrial and cosmical physics. [1][8]

- **1894:** *Popular Lectures and Addresses, Volume III: Navigation*. A collection joining lectures, protocols, instrument descriptions, and navigational advocacy; it belongs as much to Thomson’s maritime engineering project as to his literary output. [1][8]

- **1904:** *Baltimore Lectures on Molecular Dynamics and the Wave Theory of Light*. A substantially revised textual manifestation of lectures delivered at Johns Hopkins University in 1884. The twenty-year interval and extensive rewriting make the book neither a simple transcript nor an entirely new work. [1][12]

- **1912:** *Collected Papers in Physics and Engineering*. A posthumous collected edition prepared by editors from Thomson’s published work. It is a manifestation of his oeuvre, but the selection and final book-object were not made by Thomson alone. [14]

### Correspondence and notebooks that resist “work” status

- **1840s–1907:** *Scientific notebooks and calculation books*. Working documents containing calculations, sketches, experimental records, and developing theories. They resist being called finished works: their importance lies precisely in preserving projects before or without publication. [15]

- **1846–1901:** *Correspondence with George Gabriel Stokes*. A jointly produced epistolary corpus concerning mathematics, physics, experiments, publication, and scientific institutions. The modern two-volume edition is an editorial manifestation, not a book Thomson set out to write. [16]

- **1840s–1907:** *Scientific and engineering correspondence*. Thomson’s letters to Joule, Helmholtz, Maxwell, Tait, cable engineers, instrument makers, institutions, and family collectively form a major textual corpus, but neither the surviving archive nor any edition is complete. Individual letters often functioned as calculations, protocols, reports, or collaborative workspaces rather than private literary compositions. [1][15]

### Metrological creations and conceptual systems

- **1848 onward:** *The absolute thermodynamic temperature scale*. A metrological creation proposed in textual form and repeatedly reformulated. The modern SI kelvin is a later institutional descendant, not an object made by Thomson alone. [5]

- **1851 onward:** *The thermodynamic formulation associated with the second law*. Thomson made one of its foundational formulations and the dissipation-of-energy programme around it, but the law is not exclusively his obra: Carnot, Clausius, Rankine, Joule, and others were indispensable makers. [1][4]

- **1852 onward:** *The Joule–Thomson effect*. An experimentally manifested phenomenon discovered and interpreted collaboratively by Joule and Thomson. It resists classification as a “work” because the physical effect was not made, although the experiment, apparatus, measurements, and account were. [1][4]

- **1850s–1890s:** *Absolute electrical measurement*. A sustained project joining theory, laboratory procedures, standard units, instruments, and commercial practice. No single object exhausts it, and committee authorship complicates personal attribution. [1][6]

- **1860s–1890s:** *The vortex theory of matter*. A research project proposing knotted vortices in an ether as atoms. Its failure as physical theory does not make it unfinished in the same sense as the projected second volume of the *Treatise*: it survives as a completed speculative programme with abandoned claims. [1][7]

- **1860s–1900s:** *The mechanical-model programme*. Gyrostats, spinning bodies, springs, molecular arrays, fluids, and other demonstrators were used to make dynamical reasoning visible and tangible. It is a distributed Gesamtkunstwerk of lecture, calculation, apparatus, gesture, and model rather than a single machine. [1][17]

### Telegraphic instruments and systems

- **1855–1858:** *Submarine-cable theory and signalling system*. A combined project of equations, conductor specifications, operating practices, and instruments for long submarine cables. Commercial manufacture and cable-company implementation make authorship distributed among Thomson, instrument makers, electricians, and operators. [1][6]

- **1858:** *Mirror galvanometer*. A highly sensitive telegraphic receiver in which a lightweight mirror reflected a beam of light to display small current changes. Thomson devised the instrument, while manufacture and practical refinement involved firms and cable personnel. [5][6]

- **1867:** *Siphon recorder*. An automatic telegraphic receiver that traced signals in ink, patented by Thomson and manufactured commercially. It is both machine and writing system: the apparatus makes a text-like trace rather than merely measuring. [5][6]

- **1860s:** *Marine and cable galvanometers*. A family of related instruments rather than one fixed object; names, component arrangements, and commercial versions varied. Treating “the galvanometer” as one invention conceals this serial, workshop-made character. [1][17]

- **1850s–1870s:** *Cable-testing apparatus and protocols*. Bridges, resistance tests, condensers, and operating procedures used to locate faults and characterize submarine conductors. Many belong to collaborative practice and cannot be assigned cleanly to Thomson as solitary maker. [1][6]

### Electrometers and atmospheric-electricity apparatus

- **1850s–1860s:** *Absolute electrometer*. An instrument intended to measure electric potential in absolute mechanical units. Surviving examples and successive designs show it to be a family of manifestations, not a uniquely fixed machine. [1][17]

- **1850s–1860s:** *Quadrant electrometer*. A sensitive electrostatic measuring instrument whose divided quadrants and suspended element allowed comparison of potentials. Thomson repeatedly modified it, and commercial makers produced differing forms. [5][17]

- **1850s–1870s:** *Portable electrometers*. Compact instruments adapting Thomson’s electrostatic methods for practical testing. They form a product family rather than a single dated obra. [1][17]

- **1859:** *Water-dropper potential equalizer*. An apparatus using falling water drops to connect an electrometer effectively to atmospheric potential. It is often called the Kelvin water dropper, but that name can also denote the later electrostatic generator and should not erase the distinction. [18]

- **1859–1861:** *Photographic atmospheric-electricity recorder*. A system combining electrometer, water-dropper equalizer, optics, and photographic recording for continuous observations. Its identity is systemic and multimedia: instrument, light trace, photographic surface, and observational protocol belong together. [18]

- **1867:** *Kelvin water-dropper electrostatic generator*. A self-exciting apparatus that separates charge through streams and drops of water. Dating and nomenclature vary because it developed from Thomson’s earlier atmospheric-electricity apparatus. [17][18]

### Electrical standards and measuring instruments

- **1860s–1890s:** *Resistance standards and the British Association ohm project*. Thomson designed and evaluated apparatus and methods for realizing electrical resistance. The resulting standard was committee-made and historically revised, so it cannot properly be claimed as Thomson’s solitary invention. [1][6]

- **1861–1862:** *Thomson double bridge*. A bridge circuit for accurately measuring very low resistances while reducing errors from leads and contacts. It is a circuit and measurement protocol as much as a material instrument. [1][17]

- **1870s–1890s:** *Current balances*. Electrodynamic balances that related current to measurable mechanical force. Multiple absolute, centiampere, ampere, and laboratory forms make this a designed instrument family. [1][17]

- **1880s:** *Multicellular voltmeter*. A commercial electrostatic voltmeter using multiple cells to increase operating force. Workshop manufacture and subsequent product variants complicate the idea of a single original object. [1][17]

- **1880s–1890s:** *Electrostatic voltmeters*. A family of laboratory, switchboard, vertical, and portable instruments made from Thomson’s measuring principles. Their makers included James White and later Kelvin-associated firms, so design authorship and fabrication should be distinguished. [1][17]

- **1880s–1890s:** *Wattmeters and electrical power-measuring instruments*. Instruments and patented arrangements for measuring electrical power. They belong to Thomson’s commercial metrology project rather than constituting one uniform machine. [1][19]

- **1890s:** *Standard electric balances*. Precision instruments designed to embody electrical units through balanced mechanical forces. They act as material protocols: measurement depends on a prescribed operation as well as the apparatus. [1][17]

### Tidal computation and harmonic machines

- **1872–1873:** *Tide-predicting machine*. A mechanical analogue computer that summed harmonic constituents to draw predicted tides. Thomson supplied the governing design and theory, while Edward Roberts constructed the first machine; calling it solely Thomson’s machine suppresses that collaboration. [1][17]

- **1870s:** *Harmonic analyser*. A mechanical instrument for extracting Fourier coefficients from a drawn curve. Thomson devised the principle, but several builders and later designers produced materially different analysers. [1][17]

- **1870s:** *Harmonic synthesizer*. A mechanism for combining sinusoidal components into a resultant curve. It is the conceptual inverse of the analyser and part of the same analog-computation project. [1][17]

- **1870s–1900s:** *Tidal-analysis and prediction protocol*. Tables, observational reductions, harmonic coefficients, machines, and human operation formed one working system. Its results were collectively made by observers, calculators, builders, and Thomson rather than authored like a conventional text. [1][8]

### Navigation and marine engineering

- **1870s:** *Thomson sounding machine*. A compact deep-sea sounding system using wire rather than conventional rope and designed for use while a ship was moving. It is a navigational apparatus and operating protocol, not simply a measuring device. [1][8]

- **1870s–1890s:** *Improved mariner’s compass*. A compass system using a light card, multiple needles, low friction, and systematic correction for a ship’s magnetism. The design became commercially known as the Kelvin compass; its many manufactured forms resist singular dating. [1][8]

- **1870s–1890s:** *Compass-correction system*. Adjustable iron correctors, magnets, procedures, tables, and instruction together compensated for shipboard magnetic deviation. It is a distributed navigational protocol rather than one object. [1][8]

- **1870s–1890s:** *Compass binnacles and correctors*. Material housings and arrangements supporting Thomson’s compass and its magnetic compensation. Manufacture by specialist firms means surviving examples are authorized manifestations, not handmade originals. [1][17]

- **1870s:** *Depth recorder and sounding accessories*. Associated gauges, wire-handling apparatus, and recording arrangements extended the sounding machine into a navigational system. The bibliography and patent record divide this system into several legal inventions. [1][19]

- **1880s–1900s:** *Navigation instruments and instructional system*. Thomson joined compasses, sounding, tide prediction, charts, lectures, patents, and commercial advocacy into a maritime Gesamtkunstwerk. Its unity is functional rather than bibliographic. [1][8]

### Mechanical and physical models

- **1860s:** *Smoke-ring vortex demonstrations*. Experimental performances used to materialize the vortex-atom idea. A smoke ring is a transient manifestation rather than a durable work, and the apparatus and performance should not be confused with the theory itself. [1][7]

- **1870s–1890s:** *Gyrostats and gyrostatic models*. Spinning mechanical models used to demonstrate rigidity, precession, elastic behaviour, and possible ether dynamics. Many variants were constructed, often by assistants or instrument makers, so no complete single-author catalogue survives. [1][17]

- **1880s:** *Kinetic model of an elastic solid*. A mechanical model intended to show how concealed motion could produce apparently elastic behaviour. It is simultaneously experiment, analogy, and speculative ontology. [1][17]

- **1880s–1890s:** *Spring-and-bead molecular models*. Physical arrangements for reasoning about crystal lattices, molecular forces, and waves. Their particular names and dates vary across lectures, papers, and museum catalogues. [1][9][17]

- **1892–1893:** *Piezoelectric model*. A model constructed during Thomson’s work on crystalline structure and cleavage. Museum chronology records the activity, but “the model” should not be taken to mean that every surviving component is securely attributable to Thomson’s own hand. [5]

- **1890s:** *Models of crystal cleavage planes*. Three-dimensional pedagogical constructions joining geometry to physical crystallography. They are material companions to *The Molecular Tactics of a Crystal*, not illustrations subordinate to a book. [5][9]

- **1890s:** *“Mouse-mill” motor*. A small alternating-current motor associated with Thomson’s demonstrations and investigations. Its informal name underscores its status as a laboratory contrivance rather than a standardized commercial product. [17]

### Patented obra

- **1854–1907:** *The patent corpus*. Thompson’s appendix lists Thomson’s patents separately from his bibliography; Matthew Trainer’s modern survey counts seventy patents covering telegraphy, electrical measurement, navigation, and related engineering. A patent is both a legal text that acts and a project for possible embodiments: grant does not prove that every claimed machine was built or commercially realized. [1][19]

- **1854:** *Improvements in electrical conductors*. Thomson’s first patent involvement, shared with William John Macquorn Rankine and Peter Guthrie Tait. Its collaborative legal authorship should not be converted into a solitary Kelvin invention. [5][19]

- **1850s–1870s:** *Telegraphic-instrument patents*. A group covering sensitive receivers, recording, cable testing, and submarine communication. Individual patent specifications often protect several variants, while commercially sold instruments may combine claims from more than one patent. [1][19]

- **1860s–1890s:** *Electrical-measurement patents*. Specifications for electrometers, galvanometers, balances, voltmeters, and related apparatus. The patent documents are manifestos in the legal sense: they do not merely describe but claim and delimit rights. [1][19]

- **1867 onward:** *Siphon-recorder patents*. Patent texts protecting the automatic ink-recording telegraph and subsequent improvements. The legal family and the manufactured recorder are related but not identical obras. [5][19]

- **1870s–1900s:** *Marine-navigation patents*. A group covering compasses, deviation correction, sounding, and associated apparatus. Their boundaries are legal and chronological rather than the natural boundaries of the working navigation system. [1][19]

- **1870s–1900s:** *Tidal, mechanical, and miscellaneous patents*. A residual patent group including devices not adequately described as either telegraphy, electrical metrology, or navigation. “Miscellaneous” reflects the catalogue’s resistance to classification, not absence of design intent. [1][19]

### Institutional and industrial projects

- **1856–1866:** *The transatlantic-cable project*. Thomson’s contribution combined theory, testing, instruments, board work, sea voyages, and operational decisions. The completed cable was a collective infrastructure and cannot be catalogued honestly as an object made by Thomson alone. [1][5][6]

- **1850s–1907:** *Collaboration with James White and successor instrument firms*. A continuing design-and-manufacture relationship through which Thomson’s experimental arrangements became saleable instruments. Attribution must distinguish Thomson’s principles and designs from the draughting, construction, refinement, and production performed by workshop personnel. [1][17][19]

- **1846–1899:** *Glasgow natural-philosophy laboratory and teaching system*. Thomson reorganized teaching around experiment, quantitative measurement, student participation, and purpose-made apparatus. It is an institutional obra and continuing project, not a single building or text. [1][5]

- **1860s–1900s:** *Electrical standardization*. Committee reports, laboratory comparisons, instruments, names for units, and international negotiations formed a collective metrological project. Thomson was a powerful maker within it, but neither the ohm nor the wider unit system was his property alone. [1][6]
