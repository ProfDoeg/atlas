# Suzanne Perrottet: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

Suzanne Perrottet’s surviving archive is unusually rich but unevenly published. It contains photographs, correspondence, teaching notebooks, musical manuscripts, contracts, programs, drawings, dance notation, and later annotations. The principal modern biography derives from recorded conversations conducted by Giorgio J. Wolfensberger in 1981–1983 and was edited after Perrottet’s death. These recollections are indispensable but retrospective. Where an episode is known primarily from her memories, it is identified below as a **reported recollection** rather than treated as a contemporaneous record.

Labels used:

- **Documented fact:** supported by archival records, contemporary programs, correspondence, institutional records, or convergent scholarly sources.
- **Reported fact:** stated in a reputable secondary source but not independently confirmed here from the underlying record.
- **Recollection:** Perrottet’s or an associate’s later account.
- **Disputed or uncertain:** sources conflict or the surviving evidence is insufficient.
- **Myth or overstatement:** a recurrent claim exceeding the available documentation.

Research included a dedicated adversarial search in English, German, and French for controversy, scandal, criticism, allegations, lawsuits, fraud, accusations, conspiracy, and related terms, including material published in 2024–2026. No credible evidence was found that Perrottet was charged with a crime, sued for misconduct, investigated for fraud or corruption, or implicated in political violence or intelligence activity.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Susanne Luise Perrottet.
- **Usual professional name:** Suzanne Perrottet.
- **Familiar name:** Suzy.
- **Other recorded spellings/forms:** Susanne Perrottet; Suzanne P.; in authority files, Perrottet, Suzanne.
- **Nationality:** Swiss.
- **Born:** 13 September 1889.
- **Birthplace:** Rolle, Canton of Vaud, Switzerland, on Lake Geneva.
- **Died:** 10 August 1983.
- **Place of death:** Zürich, Switzerland.
- **Age at death:** 93.
- **Occupations:** musician and violinist; pianist and improviser; dancer; choreographer; teacher of rhythm, movement, pantomime, and musical improvisation; movement therapist in practice, although much of her work predated the professional category of dance or movement therapy; school director; collector and movement archivist.
- **Languages:** French was her mother tongue. Her career and surviving papers also demonstrate professional command of German.
- **Principal residences and workplaces:** Rolle and Geneva, 1889–1910; Dresden and Hellerau, 1910–1912; Vienna, 1912–1913; Monte Verità/Ascona and Geneva, 1913–1915; Hombrechtikon and Zürich, from 1915; Herrliberg for at least part of the postwar period; Zürich thereafter, including a senior residence late in life.
- **Marital status:** No marriage has been established. She was Rudolf von Laban’s partner and lover while he was married to Maja Lederer.
- **Child:** Adelar or Allar Perrottet, later André Perrottet von Laban (30 January 1916–24 February 1956).
- **Grandchildren:** André’s children included dancer and choreographer Claude Perrottet (1941–2001); the sources consulted also name a family archive maintained by Cosima Perrottet, but her exact place in the genealogy was not reconstructed here.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Father: Émile Jules Perrottet — documented fact.** He was a pharmacist. He maintained connections in Geneva’s artistic world and was a close friend of pianist Willy Rehberg, a teacher at the Geneva Conservatory. The Rehberg family lived in the same building as the Perrottets, creating an early musical environment for Suzanne and her sister. Perrottet later remembered encountering Edvard Grieg when he visited the Rehberg household.

**Mother: Adèle Julie Amélie Simon — documented fact.** She had studied at the Königin-Katharina-Stift in Stuttgart. According to the archival biography, she took her children to exhibitions and concerts and herself danced, sang, acted, and made costumes. Her correspondence with Jaques-Dalcroze survives. In 1909 she questioned whether revealing costumes and barefoot performance by the young women might damage the public reputation of Dalcroze’s method. His reply stated that barefoot movement had arisen during rehearsal because sandals impeded the exercises.

The family is characterized in the 2025–2026 Cabaret Voltaire archival reader as liberal and middle-class. No reliable evidence was located for grander ancestry or for a connection to the botanist George Samuel Perrottet.

### Sibling

**Lili or Lily Perrottet, later Odier — documented fact.** She was Suzanne’s elder sister and a musician. Sources disagree on whether her principal instrument was violin or cello. The Sächsische Biografie calls her a violinist; dance historian Selma Landen Odom describes archival photographs and letters identifying her as a cellist who continued her studies in Leipzig. This may reflect study of more than one string instrument, but the conflict cannot presently be resolved.

Suzanne’s letters to Lili describe her early touring work with Jaques-Dalcroze, including the sweets he gave the performers after successful presentations.

### Uncle

**Charles Simon — documented fact.** Perrottet’s maternal uncle was a restorer at Lausanne Cathedral and a professor at the Collège de Morges.

### Partner and triangular household

**Rudolf von Laban — documented intimate and professional relationship.** Perrottet met Laban while recuperating at the Lahmann Sanatorium at Weißer Hirsch near Dresden in 1912. He was married to the singer Maja Lederer. Perrottet became his lover, principal Swiss collaborator, and the mother of his son.

Later narratives describe a “largely harmonious” triangular relationship among Laban, Lederer, and Perrottet. Cohabitation and joint work are documented; the emotional characterization is a retrospective interpretation and should not be treated as proof that the arrangement was continuously harmonious. The three adults and their children lived and worked together in the Monte Verità/Hombrechtikon/Zürich milieu.

Perrottet and Laban separated as a couple relatively soon after their son’s birth, but continued to meet periodically and correspond until Laban’s death in 1958.

### Son

**Adelar/Allar Perrottet, later André Perrottet von Laban — documented fact.**

- Born 30 January 1916 at Hombrechtikon.
- His parents devised or used the name Allar; authority sources also give Adelar.
- The recent archival reader states that Laban could not officially recognize him. The exact legal circumstances have not been established from a civil-status record.
- Economic insecurity and Perrottet’s work obligations resulted in André being placed at different times in children’s homes, with foster parents, or within the Aryana-Bund community. This is documented in the recent archival synthesis, though the precise dates of every placement remain to be reconstructed.
- After leaving boarding school in 1933, his mother arranged an apprenticeship with Roman Clemens, newly engaged as a stage designer at the Zürich Stadttheater. He subsequently became a stage designer, sculptor, and experimental theatre architect.
- André married Julia von Wyss. Correspondence involving Suzanne, André, Julia, Émile and Adèle Perrottet, Rudolf von Laban, and their extended network is preserved in the Otto Wyss papers at the Swiss Social Archives.
- André died at Oensingen on 24 February 1956. A secondary biographical account reports suicide. The consulted institutional theatre account confirms the death date but not the manner; suicide should therefore be treated as a **reported fact requiring confirmation from a death or police record**, not as conclusively established here.

The death of her only child at forty was a major loss in Perrottet’s later life, but the consulted sources do not document her psychological response in sufficient detail to diagnose a crisis or illness.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1889–1905

Perrottet grew up between Rolle and Geneva in a household that combined disciplined education with exposure to music, theatre, visual art, and craft.

At approximately four years old, she played percussion and later violin in a children’s orchestra alongside Lili. At six, reportedly at her own request, she began solfège. At eight, she entered violinist Henri Marteau’s class.

Her father intended both daughters to become professional musicians. Their schedule combined morning attendance at a private school for girls with afternoon study at the Geneva Conservatory. Annual summer stays at Essert in France provided a recurring geographical break from the regimen.

No evidence was found for university education. Her formation instead combined private schooling, conservatory training, touring, apprenticeship, teaching responsibility, and experimental work.

## Education and Formation, 1902–1910

### Geneva Conservatory and Émile Jaques-Dalcroze

From approximately 1902, while studying violin at the Conservatoire de Musique de Genève, Perrottet joined the gifted group with which composer and educator Émile Jaques-Dalcroze developed rhythmic gymnastics.

The method combined:

- ear training;
- solfège;
- rhythmic walking and bodily realization of meter;
- breathing and relaxation;
- limb independence;
- balance;
- gesture songs;
- memory exercises;
- piano improvisation;
- what was called *plastique animée*, or bodily realization of music.

Perrottet became one of the children through whom Jaques-Dalcroze tested gesture and dance songs. She had absolute pitch and displayed enough aptitude to receive private lessons.

Her surviving notebooks, including three volumes of *Gymnastique rythmique* dated approximately 1905–1907, record exercises in solfège, breathing, mental counting, slow movement, muscular engagement, balance, anacrusis, gesture, and emotional “attitudes.” She also devised mimetic representations of harmony and musical themes, drawing on music by Franz Liszt, Robert Schumann, Claude Debussy, and Beethoven’s Seventh Symphony.

Her notes complemented those of Dalcroze’s assistant Nina Gorter but were less systematic. They are important because they preserve the workshop-like, provisional state of practices later presented in more formal published descriptions.

### Early performances and teaching

Perrottet soon served as an assistant, substitute teacher, performer, and touring demonstrator:

- **1906, Solothurn:** demonstration at a music festival.
- **May 1907, Paris:** lecture-demonstrations at the Conservatoire and before artists. A photograph identifies Perrottet among seven young women. Her later annotation states that Auguste Rodin came to thank them in tears.
- **1908, Basel:** presentation of the method.
- **1909, Amsterdam:** demonstration where Mary Wigman saw the work and subsequently enrolled.
- **1909:** a soirée included new works by Perrottet, Mitzi Steinwender, and Annie Beck.
- **Early 1910, Berlin:** Perrottet and Wigman appeared at the Königliche Akademische Hochschule für Musik at the invitation of critic Karl Storck or Stock—the surname varies in secondary transcription.

Perrottet received her diploma as a teacher of rhythmic gymnastics in 1909.

## Hellerau and the Institutionalization of Eurhythmics, 1910–1912

In 1910, Wolf and Harald Dohrn, Jaques-Dalcroze, and stage designer Adolphe Appia developed the educational institute later associated with Hellerau. Perrottet traveled from Geneva to Dresden with approximately forty students and teachers.

The Saxon Finance Ministry made two rooms in Dresden’s Ständehaus available. Courses began in October 1910. The teachers also conducted seminars for musicians of the court opera. A two-year diploma course cost 400 marks.

In October 1911, after the institute’s side wing was completed and the establishment received ministerial accreditation, instruction moved to Hellerau. Perrottet taught rhythmic gymnastics and bore major responsibility for piano improvisation—eight improvisation classes in a typical week, according to her papers.

Her colleagues included Jaques-Dalcroze, Nina Gorter, Marie Adama van Scheltema, Placido de Montoliu, Anna Epping, Christine Masing, and Clara Reynvaan. Students included:

- Mary Wigman;
- Marie Rambert, then Myriam Ramberg;
- Gustav Güldenstein;
- Eva Lippold;
- Elisabeth “Elsy” Knüpffer;
- Clara Brooke;
- Lily Braun;
- Annie van Deventer;
- Nina Macciacchini;
- Rudolf Bode.

Perrottet played violin in a Hellerau string quartet and participated in the 1912 school festival. She worked within Appia’s stage concepts and Alexander von Salzmann’s lighting environment.

Her 1912 income was reportedly 300 marks per month, a figure she described as a “brilliant career.” The figure supplies rare financial context: she had achieved substantial responsibility and a respectable professional salary at approximately twenty-two, although later periods of her life were economically precarious.

### Experiments against musical subordination

Even while teaching the Dalcroze method, Perrottet, Rambert, Annie Beck, and Wigman experimented with movement without music. Perrottet increasingly believed that Dalcroze’s system left the body subordinate to musical structure. Her later statement that she discovered herself to be an “anarchist”—not politically but artistically and culturally—belongs to this transition.

### Russia and illness

In 1912, probably through the Dohrn family’s aristocratic contacts, a group including Perrottet and Rambert traveled to Russia to introduce the method to Russian dance circles. The exact itinerary and institutions have not been established.

She returned weakened and entered the Lahmann Sanatorium at Weißer Hirsch. There she met Laban, who advocated movement governed by bodily and spatial principles rather than exclusively by music.

## Vienna, 1912–1913

After meeting Laban, Perrottet sought release from Jaques-Dalcroze. Following negotiations, his letter of 22 October 1912 authorized her to open a Dalcroze school in Vienna while allowing some room for independent experiments.

Through Louise Roy, an acquaintance from Rolle who taught French in aristocratic households, Perrottet gained access to Viennese patrons, including Maximilian Egon II, Prince of Fürstenberg.

She choreographed the Venusberg or Venus-scene dances for a production of Richard Wagner’s *Tannhäuser* at the Vienna Court Opera.

Perrottet returned to Hellerau in July 1913 to administer examinations. She then left the Dalcroze institutional world for Laban’s summer enterprise at Monte Verità. Dance historian Selma Odom notes that after this rupture she saw Jaques-Dalcroze only once again, in the 1930s, although she retained his letters, valued his teaching, and used its musical and improvisational foundations throughout her career.

## Monte Verità and the “Tanzfarm,” 1913–1915

At Monte Verità near Ascona, Laban, Maja Lederer, and Perrottet established a summer school associated with the colony’s life-reform culture. Their practices included barefoot or nude movement, loose clothing, outdoor work, improvisation, spoken text, rhythm, craft, and communal living.

Perrottet, Laban, and Lederer sought an art of movement grounded in individuality and in broad development across word, sound, form, and dance. Mary Wigman, Katja Wulff, and Charlotte Bara were among the dancers associated with the enterprise.

The group developed movement around principles such as *Anschwung*, *Aufschwung*, and *Drehschwung* and staged dance dramas based on words, myths, or conflicts among natural forces. Productions associated with this milieu include *Istvans Höllenfahrt* and *Sang an die Sonne*. Available sources do not permit a complete allocation of authorship among Laban, Perrottet, Lederer, and the dancers.

### Personal relationship

Perrottet’s relationship with Laban became both artistic and sexual while he remained married to Lederer. Surviving photographs show the adults together in Munich in 1913 and at Ascona in 1914. Later accounts call the arrangement harmonious, but the archival reader more cautiously describes an unconventional shared artistic and personal life.

When the First World War began, Perrottet initially returned to Geneva. Laban, an Austro-Hungarian reserve officer, remained in neutral Switzerland rather than return to Germany or Austria-Hungary.

## Hombrechtikon, Zürich, and the Laban School, 1915–1918

In 1915, Laban, Lederer, their children, Perrottet, and the child she was expecting settled in Canton Zürich. They continued spending summers in Ascona.

At Hombrechtikon they created what was called the *Labangarten*, with workshops for reform clothing, handicrafts, horticulture, household work, and decoration. The associated *Orchestischer Bund* offered training in breathing, ear training, improvisation, rhythm, song, and music theory.

Material conditions were precarious. The school later operated in the Urania building and, for a period, in rooms associated with the Kunsthaus Zürich. Its interdisciplinary curriculum was organized under:

- *Wort* — speech and acting;
- *Ton* — vocal and instrumental music;
- *Form* — visual art and craft;
- *Tanz* — movement art.

Perrottet’s musical training made her central to *Ton* and to improvisational teaching.

On 30 January 1916, she gave birth to Allar/Adelar. The child’s father was Laban, though the recent archival reader states that he was not officially recognized by him.

## Dada and Zürich Avant-Garde, 1916–1919

Sophie Taeuber introduced Perrottet to Zürich Dada circles. Her documented Dada role was chiefly musical, not that of a regular Dada dancer.

At least three appearances are documented:

- **1917, opening of Galerie Dada:** she played six piano pieces by Arnold Schönberg.
- **28 April 1917, Galerie Dada, “Abend Neuer Kunst”:** she improvised on themes by Laban, played her own piano and violin compositions, or both; surviving descriptions are not fully precise.
- **9 April 1919, eighth Dada soirée, Saal zur Kaufleuten:** she performed works by Cyril Scott, Schönberg, and Erik Satie. A surviving score identifies Scott’s *Poppies* among the music.

She recalled introducing Dada participants to modern atonal music and being stimulated by spontaneity, chance, and experimental “noise music.”

### Limits and later inflation of her Dada identity

**Documented fact:** Perrottet appeared as a musician at several Dada events and moved within the Taeuber/Laban/Wulff avant-garde network.

**Reported recollection:** motherhood, teaching, rehearsal, and earning a living limited her participation.

**Correction of a common overstatement:** some summaries call her an “active member” of Dada or imply extensive Cabaret Voltaire performance. The surviving documentation supports at least three musical appearances, but not a central organizational role or a sustained career as a Dada performer. A 1984 study states that dance was too serious or “sacred” for her to perform as a Dada dancer.

That same study mistakenly calls her son “Claude.” Claude was her grandson. This demonstrates the need to use the article cautiously even where its performance evidence is valuable.

## Independent School and Pedagogical Career, 1918–1938

When Laban left Switzerland for Germany after the war, Perrottet acquired or took over his Zürich school. She initially directed it with Katja Wulff and, from approximately 1921, alone.

The institution was successively known as:

- the Laban School for Movement Art;
- *Schule für Eurythmie*;
- *Bewegungs-Schule Suzanne Perrottet*.

The change from “Eurythmie” was intended to prevent confusion with Rudolf Steiner’s anthroposophical eurythmy. No evidence was found that Perrottet studied under Steiner or belonged to the Anthroposophical Society.

The school offered rhythmic and expressive gymnastics, movement, improvisation, pantomime, and dance with or without music. Perrottet’s central pedagogical idea was not simply to make pupils perform dances but to awaken them, stimulate imagination, and allow individual capacities to “flower.”

Mary Agnes Hougberg directed the gymnastic division. Teachers and guests included:

- Katja Wulff;
- Max Terpis;
- Eva Scanaria Henschel;
- Ria Ryser;
- Mary Wigman;
- later Kurt Jooss, Sigurd Leeder, Rosalia Chladek, and others in courses or exchanges.

Important pupils included Terpis, Vera Skoronel, Trudi Schoop, and many less publicly documented students. Max Terpis entered her school in 1922 after architecture studies and was subsequently directed by Laban toward Wigman’s Dresden school.

### Labour education and public teaching

In 1921, Perrottet and Terpis conducted summer courses at Villa Helbig on Lake Lucerne. On 1 December 1921 she gave the widely noticed lecture “Der vernachlässigte Körper und seine Forderungen” (“The Neglected Body and Its Demands”) to the education committee of the Workers’ Union.

She led children’s and adult classes for the Workers’ Union at Zürich’s Volkshaus. She also gave courses at:

- Zürich music institutions;
- the Zürich Stadttheater;
- girls’ boarding schools;
- the Jewish National Fund;
- later the ETH Zürich;
- the Zürcher Bühnenstudio.

Her clientele included teachers, musicians, architects, actors, industrial workers, and people referred for therapeutic reasons. Secondary biographical sources report that physicians Max Bircher-Benner and Carl Gustav Jung sent patients to her. This is plausible and widely repeated but was not independently confirmed here from patient-referral records.

### Choreography and performance

- **1923:** commissioned by the Zürich Stadttheater to choreograph dances for Gluck’s *Iphigénie en Aulide*.
- **1928:** appeared in the cabaret *Der Krater*.
- **1928:** archival photographs document a dance evening at the Zürich Schauspielhaus.
- **1930:** photographs document Perrottet dancing and participating in a Zürich dance evening.

Although she did perform, her long-term importance rested primarily on teaching, research, accompaniment, and choreography. She was not a touring star on Wigman’s model.

### Aryana-Bund and Mazdaznan environment

Perrottet and/or her son lived for a time in the Aryana-Bund settlement at Herrliberg. The Aryana-Bund was associated with Mazdaznan, a body-and-diet reform movement combining breathing, dietary practices, racialized terminology, and claims of ancient Zoroastrian wisdom.

**Documented or strongly reported fact:** the recent archival reader states that André was at times placed with the Aryana-Bund; scholarship on the settlement identifies Perrottet as a temporary resident.

**Unresolved:** the depth of her religious or ideological adherence. Residence or child placement does not by itself prove full acceptance of Mazdaznan doctrine. No writing by Perrottet endorsing its racial theories was found in the consulted sources.

## Institutional Career, 1936–1955

### ETH or EPFL discrepancy

German-language sources report that in 1936 Perrottet received a teaching appointment at the **ETH Zürich** in rhythmic gymnastics, expressive movement, and instruction in musical accompaniment. Her publisher’s biography likewise says she offered courses at the ETH.

French and English derivatives sometimes identify the institution as the **EPFL in Lausanne**. Given her Zürich residence and the consistent German accounts, ETH Zürich is the stronger reading. The precise appointment file should be checked in the ETH University Archives before the question is treated as finally settled.

She also taught pantomime, gymnastics, and dance at the Zürcher Bühnenstudio.

### Professional association

In 1939 Perrottet co-founded the Swiss professional association for dance and gymnastics, generally abbreviated SBTG. She served on its board until 1955. In 1939 she lectured to the new association on the history of movement teaching in Switzerland.

From 1946 she organized summer courses bringing international teachers—including Laban, Kurt Jooss, and Rosalia Chladek—into the Swiss dance and movement field.

### The Second World War

No evidence was found that Perrottet collaborated with Nazi institutions, engaged in fascist politics, or was investigated after the war. Conversely, no evidence was found for formal resistance activity.

Her former partner Laban’s career in Nazi Germany has produced extensive controversy concerning accommodation, state-sponsored mass choreography, and his eventual loss of official favor. Perrottet remained in neutral Switzerland and operated an independent school. The political record of Laban should not be transferred to her without evidence.

## Movement Theory, Therapeutic Practice, and Artistic Development

Perrottet’s work combined three major formations:

1. **Dalcroze:** musical hearing, rhythm, memory, solfège, improvisation, and bodily realization of music.
2. **Laban:** movement independent of musical command, spatial theory, effort, swings, direction, and bodily individuality.
3. **Her own pedagogy:** individualized combinations of music, games, pantomime, gymnastics, breathing, image prompts, and dance.

She rejected rigidly dogmatic music instruction and believed arrhythmia could be treated. She understood rhythm as a form of life conduct and self-determination.

For individual pupils, she developed different exercises rather than applying one standard choreography. Later observers described her as studying motion with something like a physicist’s attention while retaining improvisational imagination. Her piano playing reportedly compelled students to move through the entire room.

She studied classical ballet but did not make it the governing basis of her method. She continued to receive instruction and exchange ideas with Wigman, Laban, Kurt Jooss, and Sigurd Leeder. In 1938 she visited Leeder at Dartington Hall in England.

She recorded:

- foot positions;
- steps;
- pantomimes;
- scenic dances;
- expressive-dance sequences;
- spatial diagrams responding to Laban’s theories;
- observations of other dancers;
- pedagogical exercises;
- movement sequences preserved from early Dalcroze training.

Her notation was personal and experimental. It should not be conflated automatically with standardized Labanotation or Kinetography Laban.

## The Movement Image Archive

Over decades Perrottet collected tens of thousands of newspaper and magazine clippings depicting movement. Categories included:

- speaking;
- conducting;
- singing;
- playing wind instruments;
- walking;
- jumping;
- falling;
- dancing;
- lifting;
- throwing;
- balancing;
- carrying;
- athletic exertion;
- extremes of strength and flexibility.

The archive functioned as a teaching and therapeutic resource. Images supplied examples for pantomime, games, posture, expression, and individualized movement problems.

Wolfensberger called it a “subjective visual atlas of movement.” The description is interpretive but accurately identifies its hybrid character: neither a conventional art collection nor a neutral scientific database, it records how Perrottet selected and classified the moving human body.

## Later Career, 1947–1979

Perrottet first met Austrian dancer and educator Rosalia Chladek in 1947. They became close colleagues. They occasionally improvised together, with Chladek dancing and Perrottet playing piano.

In February 1969, at Chladek’s request, Perrottet prepared a fifteen-page typed account of the development of the Dalcroze method through 1913 and marked it “Definitive text.” She also assisted scholars and curators researching Jaques-Dalcroze, Appia, Laban, Ascona, and Zürich Dada.

A photograph documents her demonstration of a Dalcroze movement sequence at Restaurant Rigiblick in Zürich in August 1964. The sequence corresponded to a drawing she had made almost sixty years earlier in a *Gymnastique rythmique* notebook.

Perrottet’s work entered film in **Fifteen** (1968), directed by Alexander J. Seiler, photographed by Rob Gnant, and edited by June Kovach. The film followed Joan Sonberg, a fifteen-year-old American struggling with weight, family relations, self-esteem, and thoughts of death. Seiler later said the filmmakers believed the girl needed something like therapy and therefore approached Perrottet. The film includes Perrottet’s movement and music work with Joan. It received a Swiss federal quality award and was shown at Locarno and Florence.

At a 1979 meeting, dance historian Selma Odom described the nearly ninety-year-old Perrottet as approximately five feet tall, very thin, erect, agile, dark-haired, and dressed in trousers. Perrottet declined to be tape-recorded, so Odom reconstructed the conversation immediately afterward from memory. Perrottet was still conducting individual teaching and demonstrated gestures and songs during their French-language conversation.

She continued running or teaching through her school until 1979, an institutional career of approximately six decades.

## Final Years and Death, 1979–1983

On 17 September 1979, shortly after her ninetieth birthday, Perrottet participated in a public conversation with curator Harald Szeemann and writer Alfred A. Häsler at Zürich City Hall.

Between 1981 and 1983, photographer and researcher Giorgio J. Wolfensberger recorded extensive conversations in which Perrottet reconstructed her life. She entrusted him with her image archive. These recordings became the basis of the posthumous book *Suzanne Perrottet—Ein bewegtes Leben* and, later, *Die Befreiung des Körpers: Erinnerungen*.

Perrottet died in Zürich on 10 August 1983.

- **Cause of death:** not stated in the consulted archival or biographical sources.
- **Final illness:** not documented in the sources consulted.
- **Addiction, psychiatric diagnosis, accident, or injury:** none reliably documented.
- **Burial or cremation:** not established from the sources consulted.

### Business and income

Perrottet’s principal business was her privately operated movement school. No corporate structure, shareholding company, investment portfolio, patents, or significant property holdings were identified.

Available financial details include:

- 300 marks monthly income at Hellerau in 1912.
- A two-year Dalcroze teacher-training course in Dresden/Hellerau cost 400 marks; this was institutional tuition, not necessarily her personal income.
- A surviving Laban-school tariff from the period gives substantial fees for individual and specialist instruction, illustrating the partly bourgeois market for reform movement education.
- Later accounts emphasize recurring economic uncertainty.
- Raising André alone sometimes required institutional or foster placement.
- She supplemented school income with lectures and courses for theatres, music institutions, boarding schools, labour organizations, and other bodies.

No reliable estimate of her net worth at death was found.

### Technologies and inventions

Perrottet held no known patents. Her original technical contributions were pedagogical and notational:

- mimetic bodily representation of musical harmony;
- movement and pantomime notation;
- spatial diagrams responding to Laban’s theories;
- individualized movement-therapy strategies;
- a classified photographic clipping archive used as a visual teaching technology.

## Political, Religious, Philosophical, and Artistic Development

### Politics

Perrottet described herself retrospectively as an anarchist in an artistic and cultural—not party-political—sense. No evidence was found that she belonged to an anarchist organization or engaged in electoral politics, revolutionary conspiracy, or political violence.

Her work with the Workers’ Union and Volkshaus classes shows social engagement across class lines. Her courses for the Jewish National Fund constitute another institutional connection, but the nature and dates of that work require further archival investigation.

### Life reform

Monte Verità, the Labangarten, reform clothing, handicraft, gardening, outdoor or nude movement, vegetarian culture, and the Herrliberg Aryana settlement placed her within the broad Central European *Lebensreform* environment.

This environment included emancipatory body practices alongside pseudoscientific health claims, mystical systems, and occasionally racialized ideology. The surviving evidence supports Perrottet’s participation in movement, communal, dress, and body-reform practices. It does not establish her adherence to every doctrine circulating in those communities.

### Religion and spirituality

No conventional denominational commitment was established. Her language about bodily discovery could be ecstatic or quasi-religious: she recalled the first discovery of bending the knees or turning the hand as beautiful and “divine.” This describes artistic experience, not necessarily formal theology.

### Artistic philosophy

Her mature position placed movement neither entirely under music nor entirely apart from it. She retained Dalcroze’s ear training and improvisation while accepting Laban’s argument for autonomous bodily rhythm. Her teaching sought bodily awareness, personal invention, release from tension, and adaptation to the individual rather than production of a uniform style.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Émile Jaques-Dalcroze

Teacher, employer, collaborator, and formative influence. He trusted Perrottet with major teaching responsibility and touring demonstrations. Their professional break in 1913 was substantial, but not an erasure of influence. Thirty-four letters from him to Suzanne and eleven letters or cards to her mother survive in the Kunsthaus estate.

### Rudolf von Laban

Lover, father of her son, collaborator, mentor, and later correspondent. Their joint experiments were central to the emergence of Swiss and German-speaking modern movement culture. At the same time, later historiography often assigned theoretical authorship to Laban while treating Perrottet as assistant, companion, or transmitter.

### Maja Lederer

Laban’s wife, cohabitant, collaborator, and co-founder in the Monte Verità/Hombrechtikon enterprise. Sources have not recovered Lederer’s perspective on the triangular relationship in comparable depth.

### Mary Wigman

Initially Perrottet’s student in improvisation and rhythmic work; later a peer, guest teacher, and continuing correspondent. They experimented with movement without music and remained connected for decades.

### Marie Rambert

Roommate and colleague at Hellerau; participant in experiments with movement without music and in the 1912 Russian tour. Rambert later assisted Nijinsky in work on *Le Sacre du printemps* and became a major figure in British ballet.

### Katja Wulff

Collaborator at Monte Verità, in Zürich, and in the early independent school. Wulff and Perrottet jointly continued the school after Laban’s departure before Perrottet assumed sole direction.

### Sophie Taeuber-Arp

Member of the Laban/Monte Verità orbit and the person who introduced Perrottet to Zürich Dada. Their connection joined movement pedagogy, textile and visual practice, and avant-garde performance.

### Adolphe Appia

Theatre theorist and stage designer whose Hellerau staging concepts shaped Perrottet’s early choreographic environment. They later remained in exchange.

### Max Terpis

Student, assistant, summer-course collaborator, and later important choreographer and teacher. Perrottet recognized him while he was still studying architecture.

### Rosalia Chladek

Close postwar colleague from 1947. Their exchanges included teaching, documentation, and occasional dance-and-piano improvisation.

### Giorgio J. Wolfensberger

Late-life interviewer, editor, photographer, and custodian of her image archive. His work preserved her voice and collection but also shaped their posthumous presentation.

### Harald Szeemann

Curator who studied Perrottet and Monte Verità, interviewed her publicly, and mediated the heirs’ 1990 donation of her estate to the Kunsthaus Zürich.

### Patients and referrals

Secondary sources say Max Bircher-Benner and Carl Gustav Jung referred patients. It is unclear whether Perrottet herself was also treated or analyzed by Jung; some derivative biographies call her Jung’s patient, while other sources describe Jung sending patients to her. Without clinical or correspondence evidence, both propositions should remain **reported and unresolved**.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Contested Questions

### Affair with a married collaborator

**Documented fact:** Perrottet had a relationship with the married Rudolf von Laban and bore his son.

**Later characterization:** biographies often frame this as a harmonious ménage à trois with Maja Lederer. Shared residence and work are documented; emotional harmony is not independently measurable.

**Legal ambiguity:** the recent archival reader says Laban could not officially recognize Allar. The reason—marital status, nationality, civil law, or personal choice—is not established in the consulted material.

### Child placements

**Documented or strongly reported fact:** economic uncertainty and single parenthood led Perrottet to place André at times in homes, with foster parents, or at the Aryana-Bund.

This is rarely mentioned in short celebratory biographies. The sources do not support interpreting the placements either as neglect or as harmless educational arrangements without additional evidence.

### Aryana-Bund association

**Documented association:** Perrottet lived for a time in the Herrliberg settlement, and her son was placed there.

**Unresolved ideology:** no evidence located here proves that she accepted the movement’s racialized or pseudo-historical teachings. The connection should neither be erased nor expanded beyond documentation.

### Dada status

**Documented fact:** at least three musical appearances and direct association with Taeuber and other Zürich Dada figures.

**Overstatement:** calling Perrottet a leading or continuous Dada organizer is not supported by the presently available programs. Her motherhood and school work limited participation, and she apparently did not present her serious movement practice as Dada dance.

### Authorship and historical credit

Perrottet contributed materially to Dalcroze experiments and to Laban’s Swiss movement work. Jaques-Dalcroze himself later described the gifted students’ role as genuine collaboration and suggested that without them he might have abandoned his pedagogical career.

Nevertheless, later histories often credited male system-founders while describing Perrottet as pupil, assistant, muse, or lover. It would also overcorrect the record to assign her sole authorship of collectively developed Dalcroze or Laban concepts. The evidence supports collaborative development followed by a distinct independent pedagogy.

### Therapeutic claims

Perrottet believed arrhythmia and bodily or psychological tension could be treated through rhythm and movement. Doctors reportedly referred patients.

No controlled clinical evidence, diagnostic protocol, or outcome data was found. Claims that she “healed” psychiatric or physical conditions should therefore be understood as historical therapeutic claims, not clinically established results.

### Political and criminal matters

The adversarial search found:

- no lawsuit involving her;
- no indictment or criminal charge;
- no fraud or financial-misconduct allegation;
- no corruption, money-laundering, or intelligence connection;
- no documented political violence;
- no substantiated abuse allegation;
- no evidence of formal Nazi collaboration;
- no evidence of formal anti-Nazi resistance.

Searches sometimes returned unrelated criminal cases involving other people surnamed Perrottet. They have no connection to Suzanne Perrottet.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Founder of modern dance”

**Story:** Perrottet, Laban, and Wigman “founded” modern expressive dance.

**Circulation:** institutional biographies, publisher descriptions, dance histories, and exhibition materials.

**Evidence:** all three made documented contributions to the emergence of Central European *Ausdruckstanz*. Perrottet taught Wigman, worked with Laban, and developed an independent movement school.

**Qualification:** modern dance had multiple origins, including Delsartean practice, Dalcroze eurhythmics, Loïe Fuller, Isadora Duncan, Ruth St. Denis, and other reform-gymnastics movements. “Founder” is a shorthand for a distributed historical process.

### The harmonious triangle

**Story:** Perrottet, Laban, and Maja Lederer lived in a harmonious three-way relationship.

**Evidence:** cohabitation, artistic collaboration, photographs, and the acknowledged child.

**Against or limiting evidence:** no comparable detailed testimony from all three parties was examined. Economic stress and later separation complicate an idyllic reading.

**Reputational effect:** the story has made the household emblematic of Monte Verità sexual and social experimentation.

### Perrottet as a full Dadaist

**Story:** she was an active, central member of Zürich Dada and a Cabaret Voltaire performer.

**Evidence:** multiple documented musical appearances and personal connections.

**Limitation:** the best archival synthesis says her participation was limited; at least three appearances are known. Evidence for extensive Cabaret Voltaire activity is thin.

**Reputational effect:** attaching her to Dada has increased attention but can obscure her longer career in pedagogy and movement research.

### Naked dance as scandal or liberation

**Story:** Monte Verità dancers cast off clothing and convention in a pure revolution of bodily freedom.

**Evidence:** nude and lightly clothed outdoor movement occurred within the colony’s life-reform practices.

**Qualification:** the practice was embedded in structured teaching, communal discipline, health ideology, artistic experiment, and photographic self-representation—not only spontaneous liberation.

### “Anarchist”

**Story:** Perrottet was an anarchist.

**Evidence:** her own recollection that she was anarchistic in artistic and cultural form.

**Against a political reading:** no party membership, militant activity, or anarchist organizational work was found.

### The overlooked woman recovered intact

**Story:** recent exhibitions have simply restored a fully formed historical importance that earlier histories ignored.

**Evidence:** she was indeed overshadowed relative to Laban and Wigman, despite a sixty-year school career.

**Qualification:** her current image is also mediated by Wolfensberger’s editing, Szeemann’s curatorial work, archival survival, and contemporary interest in collective and female-authored avant-garde histories. Recovery is itself a historical reinterpretation.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Following Perrottet’s death, Wolfensberger devoted extensive work to preserving her archive and editing her recollections. Harald Szeemann mediated the donation of the principal estate by her heirs to the Kunsthaus Zürich Library in 1990.

The image-clipping portion did not initially accompany the core estate. Tens of thousands of clippings remained for approximately thirty years in boxes at Wolfensberger’s residence in Italy before becoming the basis of the 2014 *Bewegungen / Movements* volume.

Major posthumous moments include:

- **1989–1990:** Kunsthaus Zürich exhibition pairing Perrottet with Mary Wigman.
- **1991:** Marseille exhibition *De la danse libre vers l’art pur*.
- **1995:** republication of *Ein bewegtes Leben*.
- **2014:** publication of the image archive and edited memoir.
- **2025:** Kunsthaus Zürich’s ReCollect! program used Perrottet’s dance notations in a Wu Tsang installation examining intimate collaboration and collective artistic movement.
- **2025–17 May 2026:** Cabaret Voltaire exhibition *Suzanne Perrottet: After Dada, After Dance*, drawing on Kunsthaus, SAPA, family, Szeemann, and photographic archives.

Her legacy operates through several lines:

- Dalcroze eurhythmics and musical improvisation;
- Laban-related movement theory;
- Swiss expressive dance;
- individualized movement and body-awareness pedagogy;
- early movement-therapy practice;
- teacher training;
- the careers of Terpis, Skoronel, Schoop, and later generations;
- preservation of collective avant-garde history through photographs and correspondence;
- a visual taxonomy of bodily movement.

Her reputation has shifted from “assistant” or “companion” of major male and female modern-dance figures toward recognition as an independent pedagogue and archivist. The revision is supported by the extent of her school career and papers, although claims of sole invention remain unwarranted.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are included. Mere presence at Monte Verità, Zürich, Hellerau, or within modern-dance history was not treated as evidence of contact.

- **Achille-Claude Debussy — documented influence.** In her early Dalcroze work, Perrottet used Debussy’s new harmonic language when developing mimetic bodily representations of chords and musical themes. No personal meeting is documented.

- **Auguste Rodin — documented meeting, with recollected detail.** Rodin attended or responded to the May 1907 Paris demonstration involving Perrottet. Her annotation says he came to thank the performers in tears. His attendance is supported by the archival biography; the emotional detail is Perrottet’s later recollection.

- **Carl Gustav Jung — reported professional connection; details unresolved.** Biographical sources say Jung referred patients to Perrottet. Some derivative sources instead describe her as Jung’s patient. No clinical or correspondence record was examined, so neither direction should be stated more strongly.

- **Charlotte Bara — documented collaborator.** Bara supported or participated in Laban, Lederer, and Perrottet’s movement work at Monte Verità, approximately 1913–1914.

- **Erik Satie — documented repertoire connection.** Perrottet performed Satie at the eighth Dada soirée in Zürich’s Saal zur Kaufleuten on 9 April 1919. No personal meeting is documented.

- **Johannes Itten — reported shared institutional milieu, not a demonstrated personal meeting.** Both are associated with the Aryana-Bund/Mazdaznan settlement at Herrliberg. The sources consulted establish Perrottet’s residence or family placement there but do not document direct correspondence or collaboration with Itten. Accordingly this is not counted as a confirmed personal connection.

- **Katja Wulff — documented collaborator.** They worked at Monte Verità and Zürich; Wulff co-directed the school after Laban’s departure before Perrottet assumed sole leadership.

- **Mary Wigman — documented teacher-student, collaborator, and friendship network.** Wigman encountered the method after Perrottet’s 1909 Amsterdam presentation, studied in her Hellerau improvisation classes, joined movement-without-music experiments, worked at Monte Verità, later guest-taught at Perrottet’s school, and corresponded with her over many years.

- **Rudolf Laban — documented collaborator, partner, and father of her child.** They met at Weißer Hirsch in 1912, worked at Monte Verità, Hombrechtikon, and Zürich, and had a son in 1916. They separated as a couple but continued periodic contact and correspondence until his death in 1958.

- **Rudolf Steiner — documented institutional distinction, not a personal connection.** Perrottet renamed her *Schule für Eurythmie* partly to avoid confusion with Steiner’s eurythmy. No meeting, study, or correspondence is established.

- **Sophie Taeuber-Arp — documented artistic connection.** Taeuber belonged to the Laban/Monte Verità network and introduced Perrottet to Zürich Dada, approximately 1917.

- **Suzanne Perrottet — the subject herself appears in the supplied atlas roster.**

- **Émile Jaques-Dalcroze — documented teacher, employer, collaborator, and continuing influence.** Perrottet studied with him from approximately 1902, assisted and toured with him, taught at Dresden/Hellerau, and broke institutionally with him in 1913. Thirty-four letters from him to her and eleven to her mother survive.

- **François Delsarte — documented indirect intellectual lineage.** Scholarship identifies Delsartean traces in Dalcroze eurhythmics, through which they reached Perrottet. No direct contact was possible; Delsarte died before her birth.

- **Richard Wagner — documented repertoire/choreography connection.** Perrottet choreographed the Venus-scene dances for a Vienna Court Opera production of *Tannhäuser* in 1912–1913. No personal connection was possible.

No supported crossing was found with the remaining atlas names. In particular, sources that place Isadora Duncan, Hermann Hesse, Paul Klee, Otto Gross, or other figures within the broad Monte Verità story do not by themselves prove that they met Perrottet.
