## Works by Empress Theodora

### Performative manifestations

- **Before c. 522:** *Unnamed theatrical and mime performances*. Theodora reportedly worked as a mime performer before entering imperial society, but no script, role-list, or securely named production survives. Procopius’s hostile *Secret History* is the principal source, so the character and even some particulars of these ephemeral manifestations remain disputed rather than recoverable as a repertory.

### Institutional and architectural works

- **Probably 530s:** *Metanoia (“Repentance”)*. A palace on the Asian shore of the Bosporus was converted into an endowed residence or convent for women removed from prostitution; Procopius’s *Buildings* calls it the joint work of Justinian and Theodora, although his *Secret History* depicts the confinement as coercive. Modern patronage scholarship consequently identifies Metanoia—not Hagia Sophia or the many churches routinely credited to “Justinian and Theodora”—as Theodora’s one securely attested religious foundation.

- **Before 548:** *Hospices in the Houses of Isidorus and Arcadius*. Two Constantinopolitan shelters for poor and sick people, established opposite the rebuilt Hospice of Sampson; Procopius expressly says that Theodora assisted Justinian in this work. They are therefore joint philanthropic institutions, not texts and not securely separable into individual imperial designs.

- **c. 536–548:** *Miaphysite refuge in the Palace of Hormisdas*. Theodora maintained a protected community for displaced anti-Chalcedonian clerics—including Anthimus of Constantinople and, for a time, Theodosius of Alexandria—within an imperial palace complex. This resists classification as a discrete “work”: it was an improvised and continuing project of shelter, patronage, correspondence, ordination, and institutional survival rather than a formally titled foundation.

- **540s:** *Miaphysite clerical network associated with Jacob Baradaeus*. John of Ephesus credits Theodora’s patronage with enabling episcopal appointments and missionary activity from which a durable non-Chalcedonian hierarchy emerged. Calling that church hierarchy her creation would overstate individual authorship, but her sustained political and material intervention was constitutive of the project.

### Imperial protocols and counsel

- **535:** *Novel 8, on the sale of public offices*. This law was promulgated in Justinian’s name and is not a text authored by Theodora, but its preface uniquely states that the emperor took his wife as a partner in deliberation. It is therefore evidence for participation in making an imperial protocol, not grounds for assigning Theodora the *Novellae* collectively or the legislation concerning women often attributed to her.

- **527–548:** *Legislative interventions concerning actresses, prostitution, marriage, and divorce*. Modern biographies often describe these measures as Theodora’s work because they correspond strikingly to her reported experience and political concerns. Except where Justinian acknowledges her counsel, however, the enacted texts belong formally to his legislation; Theodora’s individual authorship cannot be demonstrated, so this is better classified as a disputed reform project than as a corpus of her writings.

### Reported letters and orders

- **536–537:** *Letter to Pope Silverius concerning Anthimus*. The *Liber Pontificalis* reports a letter in which Theodora demanded that Silverius come to Constantinople or restore the deposed patriarch Anthimus. No autograph or independent textual witness survives, and the partisan narrative surrounding Silverius’s deposition makes the transmitted wording and circumstances uncertain.

- **536–537:** *Instructions to Belisarius concerning Pope Silverius*. The *Liber Pontificalis* likewise attributes to Theodora written orders to find grounds for removing Silverius or send him to Constantinople. These are reported administrative acts rather than independently preserved documents, and their precise wording and responsibility remain contested.

- **After 537, before 548:** *Letter to Pope Vigilius concerning Anthimus*. The *Liber Pontificalis* presents Theodora as writing to Vigilius to demand fulfillment of his alleged promise to restore Anthimus. The document survives only within a hostile and problematic narrative tradition; scholarship disputes the associated correspondence and bargain, so it cannot be treated as an unquestionably authentic letter.

### Attributed speech

- **January 532:** *Speech before the imperial council during the Nika revolt*. Procopius’s *Wars* gives Theodora the decisive speech opposing flight from Constantinople. It is an attributed oral manifestation, not a surviving text by her: ancient historiography routinely composed speeches for historical actors, and scholars cannot determine how much, if any, reproduces Theodora’s words.
