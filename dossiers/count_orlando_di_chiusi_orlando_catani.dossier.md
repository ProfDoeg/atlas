# Count Orlando di Chiusi: Research Dossier

## Scope and Evidentiary Note

This dossier concerns the thirteenth-century Tuscan lord conventionally known as **Count Orlando di Chiusi**, not later people named Orlando Cattani or the Carolingian literary hero Roland/Orlando.

The surviving evidence is unusually thin. Only one presently identified contemporary record can be assigned to Orlando senior with reasonable confidence: a property transaction dated **6 May 1236**. The celebrated account of his meeting with Francis of Assisi was first written approximately a century after the supposed event. A purported 1274 confirmation of Orlando’s donation is considered a medieval forgery by modern specialists.

Accordingly, the reconstruction distinguishes:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary or near-contemporary documentary evidence.
- **Reported fact:** stated in later historical or institutional sources but not independently confirmed by contemporary evidence.
- **Tradition:** a long-established account whose exact details cannot be verified.
- **Legend or hagiography:** a story transmitted principally for devotional or symbolic purposes.
- **Scholarly inference:** a reasoned reconstruction from incomplete evidence.
- **Disputed or false document:** a text whose authenticity has been challenged or rejected by specialist scholarship.

The best modern treatment consulted is Pierluigi Licciardello’s study of the lords of Chiusi in *Storia, architettura ed arte alla Verna dalle origini al primo Quattrocento*. It corrects several persistent errors, particularly Orlando’s supposed comital title, the chronology of his death, confusion between Orlando senior and his son Orlando junior, and the authenticity of the 1274 confirmation.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Conventional name:** Count Orlando di Chiusi.

**Historically preferable form:** Orlando Catani of Chiusi; Italian, **Orlando Cattani da Chiusi**; Latin documentary form, **Orlandus de Catanis de castro Clusii**.

**Other forms:**

- Orlando di Chiusi
- Orlando da Chiusi
- Orlando Cattani
- Orlando Catani
- Orlando de’ Cattani
- Orlando dei Cattani
- Orlando de Catanis
- dominus Orlandus
- messer Orlando
- Urlandus, the spelling in the *Actus beati Francisci*
- Rolando, in some later geographical and antiquarian writing
- “Count Orlando,” a traditional but probably historically inaccurate title

**Status:** Castle-holder or territorial lord of Chiusi in the Casentino, probably holding Chiusi within the feudal jurisdiction of the bishop of Arezzo.

**Occupation or social function:** Rural seigneur, landholder, castle-holder, and member of the minor military aristocracy of eastern Tuscany.

**Religion:** Latin Christianity. His association with Francis and the alleged purpose of his gift—“for the salvation of my soul”—belong to later Franciscan narrative, although his material support for the early Franciscan establishment at La Verna is historically plausible.

**Period of activity:** Conventionally *fl.* circa 1213–1236. The only securely dated transaction consulted is from 6 May 1236.

**Birth:** Date and place unknown. He was probably born in the late twelfth century, but this is only inference from his adult prominence by approximately 1213.

**Death:** Between **6 May 1236** and **4 September 1237**, if the 1236 purchaser was Orlando senior. On the latter date his son is documented as “Orlando, son of the late lord Orlando.”

**Principal residence:** The castle of Chiusi in the Casentino, now Chiusi della Verna, Province of Arezzo, Tuscany.

**Principal historical association:** The grant or gift of Monte della Verna to Francis of Assisi and his companions.

## The Title “Count”

### Modern finding

The designation “Count Orlando” is traditional rather than documentary. Licciardello observes that the earliest narrative of the donation calls him **dominus**, “lord,” and describes him as rich and noble, but never calls him *comes*, “count.” The vernacular *Fioretti* correspondingly calls him **messere Orlando**.

The title probably arose because:

1. Orlando possessed or controlled a castle.
2. later writers treated “count” as a general synonym for an important nobleman;
3. the Casentino was dominated by genuinely comital houses, especially the Counts Guidi; and
4. the forged confirmation of 1274 repeatedly styled Orlando *comes* and embellished his status as an imperial palatine warrior.

Thus:

- **Documented fact:** Orlando was called *dominus* and *nobilis vir*.
- **Later title:** “Count.”
- **Unsupported embellishment:** “one of the emperor’s bravest warriors” or *imperatoris miles palatinus*.

[Licciardello’s discussion](https://www.esolibri.it/LIBRI%20ACCADEMIA%20TEMPLARE/TOSCANA/Storia,%20architettura%20ed%20arte%20alla%20Verna%20%20dalle%20origini%20al%20primo%20Quattrocento.pdf) concludes that the comital title is fictitious.

## Family and Ancestry

### The Cattani or Catani of Chiusi

Orlando belonged to the local lords of Chiusi, whose name appears as **Cattani**, **Catani**, or **de Catanis**. During Orlando’s lifetime the appellation was becoming a hereditary family name.

The family occupied a relatively modest place within the fragmented hierarchy of Casentino lordship. Licciardello ranks the lords of Chiusi below major houses such as the Counts Guidi, the Ubertini, and the lords of Montauto-Galbino. Their power was nevertheless locally significant because they controlled or administered the castle of Chiusi.

### Claimed descent from Gausfredo/Goffredo of Ildebrando

Local histories connect the family with **Gausfredo or Goffredo, son of Ildebrando**, beneficiary of an imperial privilege issued by Otto I on 7 December 967. That privilege concerned extensive lands, rights, dependants, and castles in the Apennine region, including territory extending toward *Petra Verna*.

The uninterrupted descent of Orlando’s family from Gausfredo is not demonstrated by the evidence consulted. It is best classified as:

- **Documented fact:** a tenth-century Gausfredo of Ildebrando held substantial regional rights under Otto I.
- **Reported genealogical claim:** Orlando was his direct descendant.
- **Unresolved question:** whether a continuous male-line genealogy connects Gausfredo to Orlando.

Modern municipal and tourism narratives tend to present the descent as certain; specialist medieval scholarship is more cautious.

### Parents and siblings

No reliable source consulted identifies Orlando’s father, mother, or siblings.

### Spouse or partner

No securely documented wife or partner of Orlando senior was identified.

Modern historical reenactments sometimes place a “Contessa Maria” beside him. This is reenactment tradition, not evidence that Orlando’s historical wife was named Maria.

### Children

One child is securely inferable:

- **Orlando junior:** documented on 4 September 1237 as *dominus Orlandus quondam domini Orlandi de Verona*—Orlando, son of the late lord Orlando of Verna.

The family genealogy assembled by Licciardello distinguishes Orlando senior, deceased in 1236–1237, from Orlando junior, documented from 1237 until 1289.

A purported 1274 confirmation names **Orlando, Cungio, Bandino, and Guglielmo/Guglielmino** as sons of the original donor. Some later sources add **Francesco**. The document is considered forged; its genealogical presentation cannot be used uncritically. Genuine later records show Cungio, Guglielmino, and Bandino within the descendants of an Orlando, but the precise generational arrangement has been distorted in popular accounts.

Other members of the documented later family include:

- Alberto
- Nicola
- Cungio
- Guglielmino
- Bandino
- Guiduccio
- Minuccio
- Bandinaglio

These belong principally to the lineage of Orlando junior and subsequent generations, not securely to Orlando senior’s immediate household.

## Childhood and Early Life

No source records Orlando’s childhood, date of birth, upbringing, fosterage, early military service, or youthful residence.

His later position implies formation within a castle-based aristocratic household. He would likely have learned the practical functions of rural lordship—land management, armed retainership, customary law, hospitality, and relations with ecclesiastical overlords—but this is social-historical inference rather than personal evidence.

No documentary support was found for claims that he was educated at a school, monastery, university, or court.

---

## Education, Teachers, Mentors, and Formative Influences

Nothing is known about Orlando’s formal education.

The *Actus beati Francisci*, written in the fourteenth century, presents Francis of Assisi’s preaching as a decisive spiritual influence. In that account Orlando already knew reports of Francis’s sanctity and miracles, desired to see and hear him, and sought private counsel about “the salvation of his soul.”

This can support only the following graded conclusions:

- **Hagiographical report:** Orlando had heard of Francis before their meeting.
- **Hagiographical report:** Francis’s sermon prompted him to seek spiritual counsel.
- **Tradition:** Francis became an important spiritual adviser or influence.
- **Not documented:** a continuing formal relationship of spiritual direction.

No other teacher or mentor is known.

---

## Political and Territorial Context

Chiusi lay in a fragmented political landscape between the Casentino and upper Tiber valley. Authority was divided among:

- the bishop of Arezzo;
- the commune of Arezzo;
- the Counts Guidi;
- the Ubertini;
- local castle-holding families;
- monasteries including Camaldoli, Santa Fiora, Prataglia, and Monte Coronaro.

Specialist reconstruction indicates that the lords of Chiusi held their castle under the bishop of Arezzo. Their position was therefore not that of sovereign counts but of local lords or vassals embedded in overlapping jurisdictions.

This background matters because later devotional histories transform Orlando into an almost autonomous territorial prince. The documentary setting suggests a more limited, though still substantial, lordship.

## Chronological Life History

### Before circa 1213

No dated record of Orlando has been identified.

By the time of the traditional encounter with Francis, Orlando is portrayed as an established, wealthy nobleman with a castle near Monte della Verna. The *Actus* calls him “a certain lord of Tuscany, Orlando by name, very rich and noble.”

Because this description appears approximately a century later, it is not independent proof of his exact wealth. His later ability to acquire a farm and his family’s control of Chiusi nevertheless confirm landed status.

### Circa 1213: the meeting at San Leo

#### The narrative

The earliest surviving narrative is chapter 9 of the *Actus beati Francisci et sociorum eius*, composed by Ugolino da Montegiorgio between approximately 1327 and 1340.

According to the *Actus*:

1. Francis was traveling from the Spoleto valley toward Romagna before receiving the stigmata.
2. He and Brother Leo approached the castle of Montefeltro, now San Leo.
3. A major celebration was being held for the making of a new knight.
4. Noblemen from several regions had gathered.
5. Orlando, a wealthy and noble Tuscan lord, was present.
6. Francis stood on a wall and preached using the vernacular refrain: “Tanto è il bene ch’io aspetto, ch’ogni pena m’è diletto”—approximately, “So great is the good I await that every suffering delights me.”
7. Orlando, moved by the sermon, asked to discuss the salvation of his soul.
8. Francis told him first to honor his hosts and dine with them.
9. After the meal, they conferred privately.
10. Orlando offered Francis a secluded Tuscan mountain called La Verna, suitable for contemplation and solitary life.
11. Francis accepted conditionally, proposing to send two companions to inspect it.
12. After Orlando returned to his castle, two friars came to Chiusi.
13. Orlando received them hospitably and escorted them to the mountain with approximately fifty armed men, supposedly because of wild animals.

The Latin text calls him *dominus Urlandus*, not count. It says that his castle stood near Monte della Verna. [The Latin *Actus* is available here](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Actus_beati_Francisci_et_sociorum_eius).

#### Dating problem

The *Actus* does not supply the now-standard exact date **8 May 1213**. The late-fourteenth-century vernacular version presents a confused chronological formulation, associating Francis’s age of forty-three with 1224.

The date 8 May 1213 is deeply embedded in Franciscan and local tradition, but its precise documentary foundation is weak. It should be given as:

- **Traditional date:** 8 May 1213.
- **Earliest narrative date:** only “before the stigmata,” within a text written circa 1327–1340.
- **Modern scholarly formulation:** “circa 1213.”

#### Location problem

The feast is placed at the castle of Montefeltro, normally identified with San Leo. Modern local tradition locates the private interview inside Palazzo Nardini. The current structure contains medieval fabric but was altered in later centuries.

- **Narrative location:** the castle and public space at Montefeltro/San Leo.
- **Local tradition:** Palazzo Nardini.
- **Not established:** the exact room now shown as the meeting room.

[San Leo’s municipal tourism authority](https://www.san-leo.it/monumenti-visita-san-leo/palazzo-nardini/) explicitly labels the room identification as traditional.

#### The knighting ceremony

Later accounts identify the new knight as **Montefeltrano II da Montefeltro**, son of Bonconte I. That identification is not supplied in the quoted *Actus* passage and should be treated as later reconstruction.

### The legal character of the gift

The story is often summarized as an outright transfer of ownership. That is legally misleading.

Franciscan poverty rules discouraged the friars from owning houses, places, or other property. The supposed 1213 act was therefore conceived in tradition as an oral grant of use or protection rather than a notarial conveyance of title.

No contemporary deed of gift survives. Zeffirino Lazzeri’s 1913 defense distinguished:

- an oral offer at San Leo; and
- a later written confirmation attributed to Orlando’s sons.

Modern scholarship accepts the absence of an original written conveyance but rejects the purported 1274 confirmation as forged.

### Circa 1214: inspection and first Franciscan occupation

The *Actus* says Francis sent two unnamed companions to inspect the mountain. Orlando supposedly met them and brought them to La Verna with approximately fifty armed men.

Later narratives interpret the escort as protection from beasts, brigands, or both. The *Actus* itself refers to wild animals. Claims that the mountain was deliberately ceded to pacify an area infested by criminals are modern speculation, not established evidence.

Local and Franciscan tradition dates Francis’s first ascent to 1214. The exact year cannot be independently confirmed from contemporary evidence.

### 1216–1218: Santa Maria degli Angeli

Institutional histories attribute the construction of the small chapel of Santa Maria degli Angeli to Orlando’s assistance, usually dating it between 1216 and 1218.

The claim is plausible within the patronage relationship but is not supported by a surviving contemporary building account or deed consulted here.

Later legend says Mary appeared to Francis and indicated the chapel’s location and dimensions. That is devotional tradition rather than documentary architectural history.

### 1224: Francis at La Verna

Francis’s culminating retreat at La Verna and the tradition of his receiving the stigmata there transformed the mountain from a remote hermitage into one of the central sites of Franciscan memory.

No source consulted places Orlando demonstrably at La Verna during Francis’s 1224 retreat. Later descriptions sometimes call him Francis’s host and continuing benefactor, but particular meetings after San Leo are not securely recorded.

The significance for Orlando’s biography is indirect but fundamental: his reputed grant became attached to the site of Francis’s most famous mystical experience.

### 6 May 1236: property acquisition

This is the clearest documentary event in Orlando’s recoverable secular life.

A record, preserved through Saturnino Mencherini’s *Codice diplomatico della Verna*, identifies:

> *Nobilis vir Orlandus de Catanis de castro Clusii*

The “noble man Orlando de Catanis of the castle of Chiusi” bought a farm or landed holding in the territory of Chiusi from **Guido, lord or count of Poppi**.

Licciardello explains that the purchaser could conceivably have been Orlando junior. However, documents concerning the son normally call him “Orlando, son of the late Orlando,” while this act does not describe his father as deceased. The stronger interpretation is therefore that the purchaser was Orlando senior and that he was alive on 6 May 1236.

The transaction confirms:

- Orlando’s noble status;
- his association with the castle of Chiusi;
- use of *de Catanis* as an emerging family name;
- his participation in the landed property market; and
- a financial relationship with the lord of Poppi.

It does not reveal the purchase price in the accessible regest.

### Between 6 May 1236 and 4 September 1237: death

On 4 September 1237 an Orlando is recorded as:

> *dominus Orlandus quondam domini Orlandi de Verona*

This is Orlando junior, son of the late lord Orlando of Verna. He and the knight Guido Carsidonii lent **11 lire and 10 soldi** to the abbey of Santa Fiora in Arezzo.

If the 1236 purchaser was Orlando senior, this establishes his death within a sixteen-month interval:

- alive: 6 May 1236;
- dead: by 4 September 1237.

No cause, place, circumstances, illness, accident, or violence associated with his death is known.

## Residences, Journeys, and Geographical Movements

### Chiusi

Orlando’s principal residence was the castle of Chiusi, approximately one mile from La Verna in the later Franciscan narrative. The surviving ruins, known as the **Cassero** or Castle of Count Orlando, stand above the historic settlement.

The castle’s association with Orlando is strong at the level of family lordship, although the present ruins cannot all be assigned specifically to his lifetime.

### San Leo or Montefeltro

The only major journey attributed to Orlando is his attendance at a knighting celebration in San Leo, across the Apennines in the Montefeltro region.

The route would have linked the Casentino to the Marecchia valley through the Apennine passes. His attendance would be consistent with aristocratic and military networks spanning Tuscany and Romagna, but no itinerary survives.

### La Verna

The *Actus* implies that Orlando personally escorted Francis’s emissaries to the mountain. Later tradition also has him welcome Francis on the saint’s first ascent.

No evidence documents foreign travel, pilgrimage, crusading, exile, imprisonment, or residence elsewhere.

## Wealth, Property, and Financial Relationships

The *Actus* describes Orlando as **very rich and noble**. This is a literary characterization but is consistent with his possession or administration of a castle, mountain, woodland, and agricultural land.

His recoverable assets and interests include:

- the castle of Chiusi;
- rights in or around Monte della Verna;
- woodland, cliffs, pasture, and approaches associated with the mountain;
- the farm purchased from Guido of Poppi in 1236;
- retainers or armed men, if the *Actus* escort story preserves a historical memory.

No account books, tax assessments, monetary fortune, debts, commercial partnerships, investments, or urban properties have been identified.

The grant to the Franciscans was probably a right of occupation and use supported by patronage, not an unencumbered modern-style transfer of ownership.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, and Projects

Modern corporate categories do not apply.

The principal institutions and projects associated with Orlando were:

- the **lordship and castle of Chiusi**;
- the emerging **Franciscan hermitage at La Verna**;
- the early chapel of **Santa Maria degli Angeli**, traditionally built with his assistance;
- a regional network involving the lords of Poppi and, indirectly, the bishopric of Arezzo.

No invention, patent, technology, company, formal guild membership, or banking enterprise is recorded.

## Political Activity

No political office held by Orlando senior is documented.

As lord of a castle subject to the bishop of Arezzo, he participated structurally in the feudal politics of the Casentino. His social position implied military capacity and judicial or administrative authority over dependants, but the exact extent of his jurisdiction is unknown.

Claims that he was:

- a palatine knight of the emperor;
- one of the emperor’s most valiant warriors;
- an imperial count; or
- an autonomous ruler of a large “county”

derive from later or forged material and should not be presented as fact.

Political conflicts involving Orlando junior after 1237—including the 1261 episcopal condemnation, allegations of aiding rebels, capture and torture of episcopal supporters, and the 1289 Campaldino campaign—belong to the son, not the donor himself.

---

## Religious and Intellectual Development

The only narrated turning point in Orlando’s inner life is his encounter with Francis.

The *Actus* portrays a sequence typical of Franciscan conversion narratives:

1. prior reports of Francis’s sanctity;
2. desire to see and hear him;
3. emotional penetration by the sermon;
4. private consultation about salvation;
5. an act of material generosity;
6. transformation of property into a site of penitence and contemplation.

The speech attributed to Orlando describes La Verna as “very devout and very solitary” and well suited to those desiring solitary life. This language reflects Franciscan eremitical ideals and may have been shaped by the fourteenth-century author.

No writing, theological position, philosophical doctrine, or membership in the Franciscan Third Order is securely attributable to Orlando.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Francis of Assisi

**Evidence:** Later narrative with strong and continuous tradition; not contemporary documentation.

**Nature:** Donor or patron of the La Verna retreat; alleged spiritual counselee of Francis.

**Date and place:** Traditionally 8 May 1213, San Leo.

The relationship is the defining fact of Orlando’s reputation, though its detailed dialogue comes from a source written about a century later.

### Brother Leo

**Evidence:** Hagiographical report.

The *Actus* says Leo accompanied Francis to the Montefeltro celebration. No direct conversation between Leo and Orlando is recorded.

### Two unnamed Franciscan companions

**Evidence:** Hagiographical report.

Francis supposedly sent them to inspect La Verna. Orlando received them and guided them to the mountain.

### Guido of Poppi

**Evidence:** Documentary regest.

On 6 May 1236 Guido sold a farm in the territory of Chiusi to Orlando. This is Orlando’s clearest known secular transaction.

### Orlando junior

**Evidence:** Documentary.

His son and successor. The identical name has caused extensive biographical contamination, particularly the attribution to Orlando senior of political and military actions occurring from 1237 to 1289.

### Bishop of Arezzo

Orlando senior’s family held Chiusi within the bishop’s sphere of lordship. No personal encounter with a named bishop is documented. The later conflict with Bishop Guglielmino Ubertini concerned Orlando junior and his brothers.

### Franciscan community at La Verna

**Evidence:** Strong institutional tradition; exact early legal arrangements uncertain.

Orlando’s patronage permitted or protected the community’s occupation of the mountain.

## Successes and Failures

### Successes

- Maintenance of a locally significant castle lordship at Chiusi.
- Acquisition of additional landed property in 1236.
- Creation or facilitation of the Franciscan presence at La Verna.
- Posthumous preservation of his name through one of the most important Franciscan sanctuaries.

### Failures or reversals

No personal failure of Orlando senior is securely documented.

Later reversals affecting the Cattani—loss or threatened loss of fiefs, episcopal condemnation, factional warfare, and alleged impoverishment—belong principally to his descendants. They should not be retroactively assigned to him.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

### No personal criminal record found

The dedicated adversarial searches combined Orlando’s names with Italian and English terms for scandal, accusation, fraud, lawsuit, conspiracy, criticism, crime, and controversy, including recent material from 2024–2026.

No credible source was found accusing Orlando senior of:

- fraud;
- corruption;
- criminal violence;
- unlawful seizure;
- heresy;
- financial misconduct;
- money laundering;
- sexual misconduct;
- imprisonment;
- prosecution; or
- participation in conspiracy.

This absence is not proof that his lordship was free of coercion; it means the surviving evidence does not support a specific accusation.

### Confusion with Orlando junior

A major historiographical hazard is the transfer of allegations against Orlando junior to his father.

On 29 October 1261 Bishop Guglielmino Ubertini publicly accused Orlando junior and his brothers of:

- violating fealty;
- aiding the bishop’s rebels and enemies;
- refusing entry to the episcopal notary Fructus;
- capturing, stripping, and torturing men loyal to the bishop.

The bishop deprived them of their episcopal fiefs. Licciardello notes that the proceeding may have stretched feudal procedure because the accused vassals were apparently not judged by a court of their peers. These are:

- **Documented accusations and episcopal sentence against Orlando junior and his brothers.**
- **Not allegations against Orlando senior**, who had been dead for approximately twenty-four years.

### The forged confirmation of 1274

The most important fraud connected with Orlando’s memory is not conduct by Orlando but a forged instrument created after his death.

The document purports to record, on 14 September 1274—other popular sources give 9 July, 9 June, or 2 July—confirmation by Orlando’s sons of their father’s donation of La Verna.

Modern specialists consider it false because:

- it survives only in copy;
- its formulae conflict with thirteenth-century practice;
- it consistently gives Orlando the anachronistic or inflated title *comes*;
- it calls him *imperatoris miles palatinus*;
- it incorporates genuine family names obtainable from archival documents into an invented text;
- it served the institutional interest of the friars by converting an oral tradition into written title.

The forgery existed by 1447, when it was inspected and accepted as authentic; in 1522 it was again judged visually ancient.

The older Franciscan defense by Zeffirino Lazzeri and G. Matteucci maintained its authenticity. D. Cresi, Luigi Pellegrini, and Pierluigi Licciardello rejected it. Licciardello states that scholarly agreement on its falsity is now effectively complete.

This finding does **not** by itself prove that the original grant never occurred. It shows that the later written confirmation cannot authenticate it.

### Status of the San Leo donation

The underlying gift remains a layered historical proposition:

- Franciscan occupation of La Verna is historical.
- Orlando’s family was associated with Chiusi and the mountain.
- Tradition consistently identifies Orlando as the enabling patron.
- No contemporary deed survives.
- The narrative was written about a century later.
- A later attempt to supply written validation was fraudulent.

The most economical reconstruction is that an informal grant or patronage arrangement probably existed, while the detailed San Leo dialogue and exact date remain traditional.

### Modern castle controversy

Recent local reporting concerns deterioration of the ruins called Orlando’s castle, including falling masonry, prolonged disputes over maintenance, and the consequences of an earlier agreement involving public and private parties. These are controversies over Orlando’s architectural legacy, not conduct by Orlando. They should not be misrepresented as medieval accusations against him.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The great imperial count”

**Story:** Orlando was a count, imperial palatine knight, and one of the emperor’s most valiant warriors.

**Appearance:** Most elaborately in the purported 1274 confirmation and later Franciscan histories.

**Evidence against:** The earliest donation narrative calls him *dominus*, never *comes*. The 1236 record calls him *nobilis vir*. Specialist analysis regards the grand titles as marks of forgery.

**Effect:** It elevated a minor castle lord into a chivalric prince suitable for devotional pageantry.

### The exact date: 8 May 1213

**Story:** Francis and Orlando met on 8 May 1213.

**Circulation:** Franciscan sanctuary histories, San Leo commemorations, pilgrimage routes, municipal tourism, historical reenactments, and a 2013 San Marino commemorative stamp.

**Evidence:** The earliest narrative lacks that precise date. Modern scholarship tends to say circa 1213.

**Effect:** The date structures annual celebrations and the Rimini–La Verna pilgrimage itinerary.

### The sermon from a love song

**Story:** Francis transformed the vernacular refrain “Tanto è il bene ch’io aspetto, ch’ogni pena m’è diletto” into a sermon on martyrdom, penance, and heavenly reward.

**Appearance:** The *Actus* and later *Fioretti* tradition.

**Evidence:** No contemporary sermon text survives. The refrain may preserve an authentic memory, but it is first documented about a century later.

**Effect:** It became the dramatic centerpiece of reenactments and artistic representations of the meeting.

### “The sentence at the table”

**Story:** Orlando wished to speak immediately with Francis, but Francis instructed him to dine first and honor his hosts. They discussed his soul after the meal.

**Appearance:** *Actus* chapter 9 and the *Fioretti*.

**Meaning:** The episode presents Francis as spiritually intense but socially discerning; Orlando’s patronage follows ordinary aristocratic hospitality rather than impulsive abandonment of obligations.

**Evidence:** Hagiographical report only.

### Fifty armed men

**Story:** Orlando escorted the friars to La Verna with approximately fifty armed retainers because of dangerous animals.

**Appearance:** *Actus* chapter 9.

**Later variants:** Protection from wild beasts, brigands, or a generally lawless landscape.

**Evidence:** No corroborating record. The number may be symbolic or narrative amplification.

**Effect:** It emphasizes both the wilderness of the mountain and Orlando’s military resources.

### Birds greeting Francis

**Story:** On Francis’s first ascent, birds surrounded him, sang, and perched on his head, shoulders, arms, and feet, confirming divine approval of the mountain.

**Appearance:** Later Franciscan narrative concerning the discovery or first occupation of La Verna.

**Evidence:** Hagiography.

**Effect:** It integrates Orlando’s gift into Francis’s broader mythology of communion with animals and creation.

### Orlando’s political motive

**Theory:** Orlando donated La Verna partly to establish order over a marginal area troubled by brigands and to reinforce his territorial authority.

**Circulation:** Modern local interpretive writing.

**Evidence:** The area was remote and difficult, but no contemporary text states this motive.

**Assessment:** Plausible political theory, not documented fact.

### Burial at Santa Maria degli Angeli

**Story:** Orlando was buried in the chapel he helped build, and his remains still rest there.

**Circulation:** Devotional biographies and poetry concerning La Verna.

**Evidence:** No contemporary burial record, identifiable tomb inscription, or osteological evidence was found in the consulted sources.

**Assessment:** Tradition or unverified claim.

### Francis’s bowl and drinking cup

A nineteenth-century ecclesiastical history says Orlando’s descendants delivered to the friars, together with their confirmation of the mountain, the bowl and cup Francis had used at Orlando’s table.

Because this story is tied to the problematic 1274 confirmation and appears in much later writing, the provenance of the objects is unverified. The tradition nevertheless reinforces the table scene and the idea of aristocratic hospitality converted into relic culture.

### The “Contessa Maria”

Modern reenactments sometimes pair Orlando with a Countess Maria. No medieval evidence consulted identifies Orlando senior’s wife by that name. This is best classified as pageant tradition.

### The castle as Orlando’s intact palace

The ruins at Chiusi are popularly called Orlando’s castle or palace. The site belonged to the medieval lordship, but it underwent rebuilding, abandonment, dismantling, and reuse. Stones were reportedly taken in 1486 for La Verna’s bell tower and later for other construction.

The ruins are a genuine material connection with the family’s center of power, not an untouched survival of Orlando’s personal residence.

## Material, Artistic, and Commemorative Legacy

### La Verna sanctuary

Orlando’s enduring significance derives from the later history of the land associated with his gift:

- early Franciscan huts or cells;
- Santa Maria degli Angeli, traditionally begun in 1216;
- Francis’s final retreat and stigmatization tradition in 1224;
- later chapels and conventual buildings;
- the Basilica of Santa Maria Assunta;
- major works by Andrea della Robbia and his workshop;
- continuous pilgrimage and Franciscan observance.

### Visual representations

A nineteenth-century painting by **Ferdinando Folchi**, dated 1877, in or near Santa Maria degli Angeli depicts Francis and Orlando at San Leo. It belongs to a paired commemorative program that also represents the chapel’s dedication.

The episode also appears in modern illustrations, stamps, devotional publications, historical processions, and promotional material for pilgrimage routes.

### Historical reenactment

Chiusi della Verna stages **Alla Corte del Conte Cattani**, a costumed reenactment of Orlando’s gift. The ceremony converts the literary account into civic memory and connects the village, castle ruins, and sanctuary.

### Commemorative stamp

In 2013 San Marino issued a €3.50 stamp commemorating the eight-hundredth anniversary of the donation. Designed by Antonella Napolione, it depicts Francis and Orlando and incorporates mapping and heraldic imagery. The issue fixed 8 May 1213 as the official commemorative date.

### Pilgrimage route

The Rimini–La Verna Way of Saint Francis, inaugurated during the 2013 commemorations, uses the San Leo encounter as a central historical link between the Adriatic and La Verna.

### Words attributed to Orlando

The *Actus* attributes to him an offer substantially meaning:

> “Brother Francis, I have in Tuscany a very devout and solitary mountain called La Verna, very suitable for those who desire solitary life. If it pleased you and your companions, I would gladly give it to you for the salvation of my soul.”

This is not an autograph or contemporaneous transcript. It is dialogue composed or transmitted approximately a century after the event.

The vernacular *Fioretti* version is a later translation and adaptation of the Latin narrative.

## Primary and Early Narrative Sources

### *Actus beati Francisci et sociorum eius*

- Language: Latin.
- Composition: approximately 1327–1340.
- Traditional author: Ugolino da Montegiorgio.
- Relevant section: chapter 9, *De inventione montis Alvernae*.
- Importance: earliest known narrative of Orlando’s meeting with Francis and offer of La Verna.
- Limitation: written approximately a century after the supposed meeting.
- Modern edition consulted indirectly: Paul Sabatier, *Actus beati Francisci et sociorum ejus*, Paris, 1902.
- [Latin text](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Actus_beati_Francisci_et_sociorum_eius)
- [Sabatier edition catalogue and scan access](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL23377259M/Actus_beati_Francisci_et_sociorum_ejus)

### *I Fioretti di san Francesco*, “Prima considerazione delle sacre stimmate”

- Language: Italian vernacular.
- Date: final quarter of the fourteenth century.
- Authorship: anonymous; dependent upon the *Actus*.
- Relevant traditional chapter: commonly printed as chapter LV in editions incorporating the Considerations.
- Importance: principal vehicle by which the Orlando story entered popular Franciscan memory.
- Limitation: later than the *Actus* and chronologically confused.
- [Text](https://it.wikisource.org/wiki/I_fioretti_di_Sancto_Francesco/Capitolo_LV)

### 6 May 1236 property act

- Original institutional provenance: Verna-related diplomatic collection.
- Published regest: Saturnino Mencherini, *Codice diplomatico della Verna e delle SS. Stimate di S. Francesco d’Assisi nel VII centenario del gran prodigio*, Florence, 1924, no. 211, p. 577.
- Key form: *Nobilis vir Orlandus de Catanis de castro Clusii*.
- Importance: only presently identified secular document likely referring to Orlando senior.

### 4 September 1237 Santa Fiora record

- Repository: Archivio Storico Diocesano di Arezzo, Santa Fiora, no. 791.
- Contents: Orlando junior, son of the late Orlando, and Guido Carsidonii lend 11 lire 10 soldi to the abbey.
- Importance: terminus ante quem for Orlando senior’s death.

### Purported confirmation of 1274

- Published by Mencherini, *Codice diplomatico*, no. 28, pp. 38–39.
- Status: forged.
- Historical significance: evidence for later Franciscan efforts to secure documentary legitimacy, not for the wording or legal form of Orlando’s original grant.

## Death and Burial

### Date

Orlando probably died between **6 May 1236 and 4 September 1237**.

### Place

Unknown. Chiusi is the likeliest place only because it was his principal seat.

### Cause

Unknown. No illness, accident, battle, execution, or other cause is documented.

### Burial

Later devotional tradition says he was buried at Santa Maria degli Angeli on La Verna and that his remains still lie there. No contemporary burial record or verified tomb was identified. The claim remains **unverified tradition**.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Orlando’s documentary footprint is extremely small, but his memorial footprint is large.

His later identity developed through several stages:

1. **Local castle lord:** the probable historical Orlando, known through property and lineage.
2. **Franciscan patron:** remembered as the man who made La Verna available.
3. **Spiritual penitent:** portrayed as seeking Francis’s guidance for his soul.
4. **Chivalric count:** elevated by later tradition and a forged charter into an imperial noble.
5. **Relic-associated host:** remembered through Francis’s supposed bowl, cup, and table.
6. **Civic ancestor:** central to Chiusi della Verna’s local identity.
7. **Pilgrimage figure:** linking San Leo, the Montefeltro, and La Verna.
8. **Pageant character:** annually reenacted in aristocratic costume.
9. **Philatelic symbol:** depicted in the 2013 anniversary issue.

Modern scholarship has reduced the certainty of the traditional narrative while clarifying Orlando’s actual historical setting. This has not displaced the devotional story; instead, the documentary Orlando and legendary Count Orlando coexist.

The most durable material legacy is not a text or dynasty but the landscape of La Verna. Its later significance—as Franciscan hermitage, alleged site of the stigmata, sanctuary, artistic center, and pilgrimage destination—gave Orlando a posthumous prominence far beyond that of comparable minor Tuscan lords.

**Evidentiary label:** Reported fact grounded in fourteenth-century Franciscan narrative; underlying patronage relationship historically probable, exact dialogue and date traditional.

**Evidentiary label:** No personal connection.

Orlando died decades before Dante’s birth; no meeting, correspondence, or direct acknowledged influence is possible.
