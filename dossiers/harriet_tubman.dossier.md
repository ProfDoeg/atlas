# Harriet Tubman — Research Dossier

**Subject:** Harriet Tubman (born Araminta Ross; c. March 1822–10 March 1913)  
**Scope:** life history, family, enslavement, self-emancipation, Underground Railroad operations, abolitionism, Civil War intelligence and military work, property and finances, suffrage, religion, health, myths and disputed narratives, works and oral testimony, historiography, film/media, and posthumous reputation.  
**Research date:** 19 August 2026

---

## Method and Evidentiary Conventions

This dossier distinguishes several kinds of evidence rather than flattening them into a single narrative:

- **Documented / contemporary record:** surviving legal records, advertisements, military and pension records, congressional records, deeds, letters, contemporaneous newspapers, photographs, government files, or other records created close to the event.
- **Tubman recollection / oral testimony:** statements attributed to Harriet Tubman in interviews, speeches, affidavits, or biographies. These are indispensable but may have been recorded years after the events and often passed through an editor or amanuensis.
- **Near-contemporary biographical tradition:** especially Sarah H. Bradford's *Scenes in the Life of Harriet Tubman* (title-page date 1869; copies circulated in 1868) and *Harriet, the Moses of Her People* (1886; revised 1901). Bradford knew Tubman and wrote partly to raise money for her, but her books are literary, devotional, and promotional as well as documentary.
- **Modern archival reconstruction:** conclusions reached by historians through comparison of censuses, manumission records, wills, runaway advertisements, deeds, military files, pension records, newspapers, local geography, and family history. Kate Clifford Larson, Catherine Clinton, Jean M. Humez, Milton C. Sernett, and Edda L. Fields-Black are particularly important here.
- **Disputed / unresolved:** surviving sources conflict or do not permit a confident conclusion.
- **Legend / myth / symbolic tradition:** a story whose historical truth is doubtful, unproved, exaggerated, or metaphorical but which has shaped Tubman's reputation and historical memory.

The older authorized biographies remain central because they preserve Tubman's voice, idiom, memories, religious worldview, and stories not preserved elsewhere. They are not treated as stenographic transcripts. Likewise, later correction of an exaggerated number does not make the older number historically irrelevant: this dossier records both the underlying evidence and the history of the claim.

## Basic Identifying Information

### Names and forms of name

- **Birth name:** **Araminta Ross**, commonly called **Minty**.
- **Later given name:** **Harriet**, adopted by the time of her first marriage, apparently in honor of her mother, Harriet “Rit” Green Ross.
- **First married name:** **Harriet Tubman**, after marriage to John Tubman around 1844. This is the name under which almost all of her public activity occurred.
- **Later legal/social form:** **Harriet Tubman Davis**, after her 1869 marriage to Nelson Davis. She continued to be publicly known principally as Harriet Tubman.
- **Abolitionist and popular epithets:** **Moses**, **the Moses of Her People**, **Black Moses**, **Black She-Moses** in some later popular writing.
- **John Brown's name for her:** **General Tubman**, a title of respect rather than a nineteenth-century military commission.
- **Family/community honorifics:** **Aunt Harriet** appears in later community memory.
- **Posthumous military title:** in 2024 the State of Maryland/Maryland National Guard posthumously commissioned her an honorary one-star brigadier general. This was a modern commemorative appointment, not a Union Army rank held during the Civil War.

### Birth

The exact date was not recorded in a surviving birth certificate. The strongest modern reconstruction favors **March 1822**, probably early in the month. National Park Service research now generally uses **March 1822**.

Competing historical dates include:

- **c. 1822:** favored by modern documentary reconstruction, including evidence from an 1822 midwife payment, ages in contemporary records, and the 1849 runaway advertisement.
- **1825:** an age/date Tubman herself gave on some occasions.
- **1820:** the year inscribed on her gravestone.
- **1815:** the year implied by her death certificate.

The conflicts are unsurprising for a person born enslaved, whose birth was recorded principally as the birth of property rather than through a civil vital-registration system, and who had no personal written birth record.

### Birthplace

Tubman was born enslaved in **Dorchester County, Maryland**, on Maryland's Eastern Shore. Popular accounts long placed her birth on the Edward Brodess farm near **Bucktown**. More recent reconstruction places her birth on the **Anthony Thompson plantation at Peter's Neck**, south of Madison, because her mother Rit was then working for Thompson and the Ross family was divided between the Thompson and Brodess holdings. Bucktown nevertheless remained central to Tubman's childhood and early labor.

### Death

- **Date:** 10 March 1913.
- **Place:** Auburn, Cayuga County, New York, at or associated with the Harriet Tubman Home for the Aged.
- **Cause:** pneumonia is the consistently reported immediate cause.
- **Burial:** Fort Hill Cemetery, Auburn, New York.
- **Age:** approximately 90 or 91 if the modern 1822 birth reconstruction is accepted; contemporary obituaries necessarily varied because her birth year was uncertain.

### Occupations and roles

Depending on period and documentary terminology, Tubman was an enslaved laborer, domestic worker, nursemaid, field and timber worker, self-emancipated fugitive, domestic worker in freedom, Underground Railroad conductor and organizer, abolitionist, fundraiser, farmer, household head and boarding-house operator, Civil War nurse, cook, laundress, relief worker, scout, intelligence operative/spy, recruiter and guide, public speaker, suffrage activist, AME Zion church supporter, and founder of a home for aged and indigent Black people.

She held no conventional salaried public office and never received the full formal military status or compensation she sought for her Civil War intelligence and scout work during her lifetime.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Father: Benjamin “Ben” Ross**

Ben Ross was enslaved by Anthony Thompson in Dorchester County. He was an experienced timberman and woodsman, and later a free man. Tubman's childhood work with or near him in forests, marshes, waterways, and timber operations helped give her the environmental knowledge that later became useful in escape operations. Thompson's manumission arrangements eventually freed Ben, although later than the age apparently contemplated in the relevant documents. He became legally free around **1840**.

Ben continued to live on the Eastern Shore after manumission and was known to assist people escaping slavery. In 1857, after the escape of a group later called the Dover Eight exposed him to possible arrest, Tubman removed Ben and Rit from Maryland and ultimately brought them to Canada. He later lived with Tubman in Auburn.

**Mother: Harriet “Rit” Green Ross**

Rit was enslaved by the Pattison/Brodess family. She worked as a cook and domestic laborer and bore at least nine children with Ben Ross. She was separated repeatedly from children who were hired out or sold. Tubman's later recollections gave Rit a central place in her moral formation and in stories of resistance to slave sales.

A famous family story, preserved through Tubman and Bradford, says that when an owner or trader intended to sell Rit’s youngest son Moses, Rit concealed him and threatened anyone who entered her cabin to seize him. The threatened sale did not occur. The story is plausible and important to Tubman's family memory, but it survives principally as later oral recollection rather than through a contemporaneous legal record.

Ben purchased Rit’s freedom in **1855**, making her legally free. The family's later inquiry into Pattison/Brodess wills suggested that Rit may have had a claim to freedom under an earlier testamentary arrangement, and Tubman reportedly paid a white lawyer **$5** to investigate the matter. Historians have reconstructed the documentary background, but it did not result in a successful lifetime legal emancipation suit for Tubman or her siblings.

### Siblings

The best-supported reconstruction identifies **nine children born to Ben and Rit Ross**. Order and exact birth years vary slightly among sources. Commonly listed are:

1. **Linah** (born c. 1808) — sold away from the family into the Deep South; later fate uncertain.
2. **Mariah Ritty** (born c. 1811) — sold away; later fate uncertain.
3. **Soph** (born c. 1813) — sold away; later fate uncertain.
4. **Robert** (born c. 1816) — escaped with Tubman; in freedom used the name **John Stewart**.
5. **Araminta “Minty” / Harriet** (born c. March 1822).
6. **Ben** — escaped with Tubman; later used **James Stewart**.
7. **Rachel** — remained enslaved; Tubman repeatedly tried to arrange her escape. Rachel died before Tubman could remove her and her children.
8. **Henry** — later **William Henry Stewart**; escaped with Tubman.
9. **Moses** — the youngest in the well-known family story about Rit preventing his sale.

Some NPS and older secondary accounts produce different family totals, including eleven children, because nieces or other close kin were sometimes remembered within the sibling group. Modern genealogical work generally treats nine as the biological children of Ben and Rit while recognizing the larger kin network.

### African ancestry

Family tradition remembered Tubman's maternal grandmother **Modesty** as African-born. Later retellings sometimes specify **Ashanti** or present-day Ghanaian ancestry. The African-born-grandmother tradition is historically significant; the more specific Ashanti identification is not established by a surviving documentary genealogy and should be treated as an oral or retrospective attribution rather than proven ethnic descent.

### First husband: John Tubman

Tubman married **John Tubman**, a free Black man, around **1844**. Under Maryland law, marriage to a free man did not free an enslaved woman, and the legal status of children followed the mother. There are no documented children of the marriage.

Around the marriage period Araminta began using the given name Harriet and the surname Tubman. John apparently did not join her first attempted escape in 1849. When she returned for him after establishing herself in freedom, probably in 1851, she discovered that he had married another woman, **Caroline**, and refused to leave Maryland with her. Tubman then turned the journey into another rescue effort and conducted other people north.

John Tubman was shot and killed in Dorchester County in **1867** in a roadside dispute. Contemporary/legal reporting identifies a white man, Robert Vincent, as the shooter; Vincent was tried and acquitted. The episode belongs to John Tubman's later life and did not involve Harriet directly.

### Second husband: Nelson Davis

Tubman married **Nelson Davis** on **18 March 1869** in Auburn. Davis had been born enslaved in North Carolina and served in the Union Army, commonly identified with Company G of the 8th United States Colored Troops. He was substantially younger than Tubman and worked as a bricklayer in Auburn.

The marriage lasted until Davis's death in **October 1888**. Surviving records and later summaries conflict over the exact day, often giving 4 October or 14 October. Tuberculosis is commonly reported as the cause. His veteran status later became the statutory basis for Tubman's widow's pension, even while she separately sought compensation for her own Civil War services.

### Adopted daughter: Gertie

Harriet and Nelson adopted an infant girl, **Gertie (Gertrude) Davis**, around **1874**. Family photographs survive showing Tubman, Nelson, and Gertie together. Gertie remained part of the Auburn family network.

### The Margaret episode — family memory and disputed parentage

Around 1859 Tubman brought a young light-skinned Black girl named **Margaret** from Maryland to New York and described her as a niece. Margaret later lived within Tubman's extended family. A later family account from Margaret's daughter Alice characterized the removal as a kind of kidnapping and said Margaret had been taken from a comfortable free household.

Historian Kate Clifford Larson at one stage considered whether Margaret might have been Tubman's undisclosed biological daughter, a hypothesis prompted by family testimony and the unusual circumstances. Subsequent genealogical research identified strong evidence that Margaret was one of the twin children of free Black Marylanders **Isaac and Mary Woolford**, neighbors within Tubman's wider community. Larson accordingly withdrew the secret-daughter hypothesis. The episode remains a useful example of how incomplete documentation, family memory, and later biographical reconstruction can generate and then revise a major claim.

### Kinship as an operating network

Tubman's Underground Railroad activity was never simply the work of an isolated heroic individual. Her parents, siblings, nieces, in-laws, free Black neighbors, mariners, timber workers, farmers, and trusted friends constituted an overlapping kinship and information network. Many of her rescue missions were specifically attempts to remove relatives. The danger created by sale, family separation, and slave-catching therefore shaped both her private motives and her operational priorities.

## Childhood and Early Life

### Enslaved childhood and hiring out

Tubman was born into a slave system in which children could be hired away from their parents. By approximately age five or six she was hired out as a domestic worker and nursemaid. In later recollections she described beatings, hunger, sleep deprivation, and punishment when an infant she was assigned to watch cried.

She was later hired for trapping muskrats in marshes, field labor, hauling, timber work, and other physically demanding jobs. The Eastern Shore environment of tidal rivers, creeks, swamps, forests, wharves, and roads became part of her practical education.

### Work in timber and waterways

As an adolescent and young adult Tubman performed outdoor labor unusually heavy for a girl or woman under the gender conventions of the period, including timber and field work. Work connected with **Stewart's Canal**, also called Parson's Creek in some local histories, placed her near free Black laborers and Black mariners. These mobile workers carried news, routes, and information between the Chesapeake, Delaware, Pennsylvania, and ports farther north.

This environment mattered later. Tubman learned to read landscape rather than text: currents, paths, vegetation, stars, seasonal changes, animal movement, work rhythms, and the behavior of people along transport routes. She also learned which free Black and white households might be approached, which waterways could conceal movement, and how hiring and transport systems worked.

### The head injury

At roughly age twelve or thirteen, while Tubman was at a store or worksite, an overseer attempted to stop another enslaved man who was leaving without permission. He threw a heavy metal weight, commonly described in Tubman's recollections as about two pounds. The projectile missed the intended man and struck Tubman in the head.

The injury was severe. She later reported prolonged unconsciousness or incapacity, headaches, buzzing or pressure sensations, and sudden episodes in which she fell asleep or became unresponsive. She also experienced vivid dreams, visions, and religious experiences that she interpreted as communications or warnings from God.

The exact neurological diagnosis cannot be made retrospectively. Popular biographies have often called the condition temporal-lobe epilepsy. A 2023 medical-history article by Peter A. Balmuth and Thomas E. Scammell instead argued that post-traumatic hypersomnia or narcolepsy-like symptoms may better fit the described episodes. None of these modern diagnoses can be proved from nineteenth-century evidence. What can be documented is a severe traumatic head injury followed by lifelong neurological symptoms and religiously interpreted visions.

### Religion and the formation of a prophetic worldview

Tubman's spirituality grew from an African American Christian world in which the Bible circulated not only through literacy but through sermons, songs, oral storytelling, remembered verses, dreams, and prayer. Her mother told biblical stories. Tubman developed an intensely personal providential theology in which God intervened in ordinary decisions and communicated through dreams and visions.

The **Exodus** story became particularly resonant in her life and in later descriptions of her. The name **Moses** was not merely decorative: abolitionists and freedom seekers applied the story of deliverance from bondage directly to her operations. Tubman herself prayed before and during missions and later described receiving warnings or guidance that she believed had practical consequences.

The historical fact is that Tubman reported and acted upon such visions. Whether a particular vision was supernatural, neurological, intuitive, or retrospectively organized is not a historical question the surviving documents can resolve.

## Education and Formation

Tubman received **no known formal schooling** and never became conventionally literate. She could not independently read a newspaper, map, legal document, or letter in the ordinary sense. This shaped the documentary archive: many surviving words attributed to her were dictated to other people, reported by journalists, recorded by biographers, or preserved through speeches and affidavits.

Lack of alphabetic literacy did not mean lack of specialized knowledge. Her practical education included:

- Eastern Shore geography, waterways, weather, stars, forests, marshes, roads, and seasonal work.
- The social geography of slavery: plantations, hired labor, patrols, auctions, slave traders, courts, jails, transport depots, and the timing of public notices.
- Free Black and maritime communication networks.
- Biblical stories, hymns, spirituals, prayer traditions, and Protestant religious culture.
- Concealment, disguise, observation, interpersonal judgment, coded communication through trusted intermediaries, and improvisation under surveillance.
- Later, direct exposure to leading abolitionists, reformers, soldiers, politicians, suffragists, and church leaders.

Her principal “teachers” in a conventional biographical sense were therefore family, work environments, religious communities, and operational collaborators rather than schools or named academic mentors.

## Enslavement, Ownership, and the Legal Setting

Tubman's childhood unfolded through overlapping claims by the **Brodess**, **Pattison**, and **Thompson** families. Because Ben Ross belonged to Anthony Thompson while Rit and the children were tied to the Pattison/Brodess line, the Ross household was vulnerable to physical separation even before individual children were hired or sold.

Slave status in Maryland was hereditary through the mother. John Tubman's free status therefore did not legally change Harriet's status after their marriage. The family had reason to fear sale to the Deep South, where separation would become much harder to reverse.

The death of **Edward Brodess in March 1849** intensified that danger. His estate had debts, and enslaved people represented assets that could be sold. Tubman's earlier experience of sisters being sold away made the threat concrete rather than abstract.

## Self-Emancipation, 1849

### First attempt with brothers

On or about **17 September 1849**, Harriet left with brothers **Ben** and **Henry**. Their enslaver, Eliza Brodess, placed a runaway advertisement in the *Cambridge Democrat*. An advertisement published **3 October 1849** described “Minty,” “Ben,” and “Harry” and offered rewards, including **$100 each if captured outside Maryland**, for a possible total of **$300**.

This surviving advertisement is important because it supplies a contemporary description and a real bounty figure. It is also the clearest documentary counterweight to the much later legend that slaveholders offered $40,000 for Tubman alone.

The first attempt failed because her brothers reconsidered and turned back. Harriet returned with them rather than continue alone.

### Second attempt alone

Later in **October or November 1849**, Harriet left again, this time alone. The exact path is not recoverable. Modern reconstructions place her moving north through Maryland and Delaware into Pennsylvania, using a combination of free Black, Quaker, maritime, and antislavery contacts. She later described crossing into a free state as an almost physical transformation of the world around her.

She reached **Philadelphia**, where she worked for wages, including domestic labor, while establishing contacts in the abolitionist and Vigilance Committee network. Self-emancipation did not make her legally safe. Under the 1793 federal fugitive-slave regime and especially after the **Fugitive Slave Act of 1850**, an escaped enslaved person could be seized in a free state and returned.

Tubman's response to freedom was therefore not permanent withdrawal from Maryland but repeated return.

## Underground Railroad Operations, 1850–1860

### What can be counted

The best modern documentary reconstruction supports approximately **thirteen rescue missions into Maryland** after Tubman's own escape. She personally conducted roughly **seventy people** to freedom and gave instructions or assistance to additional people who escaped without her physically accompanying the whole route. National Park Service pages vary between roughly fifty to sixty and roughly seventy additional people advised or instructed.

The famous older figures — **nineteen trips** and **three hundred people personally rescued** — grew from Sarah Bradford's authorized biography and later memorial culture. They are not supported by mission-by-mission reconstruction. They should not be confused with the **more than 750 people liberated during the Combahee River Raid** in 1863, an entirely different kind of wartime operation.

### December 1850 — Kessiah Bowley and children

Tubman's first securely documented family rescue involved her niece **Kessiah (Keziah) Bowley** and Kessiah's children. Kessiah was to be sold at auction in Cambridge, Maryland. Her husband, **John Bowley**, a free Black man, participated in an escape arrangement surrounding the auction, after which the family fled by water toward Baltimore. Tubman met them and helped move them north.

The episode illustrates several recurring characteristics of Tubman's work: family intelligence from Maryland, collaboration with a free Black husband, use of waterways, and a relay network rather than a single straight-line march.

### 1851 — brothers and husband

Tubman returned again in 1851 and helped remove relatives and other men. She also returned intending to bring **John Tubman** north. By then John had married Caroline and declined to leave. Tubman did not force him; she instead escorted other people who were ready to go.

Later in 1851 she led a larger group, often reconstructed as approximately **eleven people**, northward. Some accounts connect this party with a stay at Frederick Douglass's home in Rochester. Douglass certainly knew Tubman and sheltered freedom seekers, but exact rosters for individual early missions are not always recoverable.

### Canada and St. Catharines

After passage of the **Fugitive Slave Act of 1850**, Canada West became increasingly important because crossing the international border placed escapees beyond direct operation of U.S. fugitive-slave law. Tubman spent substantial time in **St. Catharines, Canada West (Ontario)**, and family members established themselves there. She continued to travel frequently through New York, Pennsylvania, New England, and Canada.

She did not simply transport every person permanently to Canada. Destinations depended on law, family, employment, and safety. Philadelphia, New York, Auburn, New England towns, and Canada all figured in the network.

### Christmas 1854 — the Jacob Jackson message and rescue of brothers

One of the best examples of Tubman's communication methods survives through a dictated coded letter sent in **December 1854** to **Jacob Jackson**, a free Black farmer and veterinarian in Dorchester County. The letter uses religious and family language rather than an explicit written escape plan and is associated with the signature “William Henry Jackson.”

The message alerted Tubman's brothers and allies. Around Christmas she removed brothers **Henry, Robert, and Ben**, Ben's fiancée **Jane**, and other companions. The group traveled more than a hundred miles north under severe winter conditions.

The episode is significant because it demonstrates that Tubman's operations did not depend on her own reading and writing. She could dictate a letter, rely on a literate intermediary, encode meaning through agreed language, and coordinate a complex rendezvous without committing an explicit plan to paper.

### 1857 — parents and the Dover Eight crisis

A group of freedom seekers later known as the **Dover Eight** was betrayed in Delaware in 1857 amid the prospect of a large reward. Ben Ross had helped shelter or assist people moving through the region, placing him in danger of arrest. Tubman returned to Maryland and removed both parents, Ben and Rit, ultimately bringing them to safety in Canada and later to Auburn.

The rescue of elderly parents required a different logistical problem from moving young adults. Tubman used transport rather than expecting them to travel the entire distance on foot.

### Rachel and her children

Tubman repeatedly tried to rescue her sister **Rachel** and Rachel's children. The plans failed. In one attempt, the money or terms demanded for the children could not be met; in another, circumstances changed before a safe removal could be arranged. By the time Tubman returned again, Rachel had died.

This is a central failure in an otherwise highly successful rescue record. The loss remained emotionally important in family memory and is an antidote to later portrayals in which every intended rescue succeeds through heroic will alone.

### November–December 1860 — last Maryland rescue

Tubman's last known rescue mission before the Civil War occurred in late **1860**. The group included members of the **Ennalls** family and other freedom seekers. They evaded pursuers and moved north through a network of safe places. On **28 December 1860**, a group associated with Tubman's final mission reached the Auburn area and the household of Martha and David Wright.

The precise composition and day-by-day route vary by reconstruction, but the mission marks the end of her decade of clandestine returns to Maryland. Within months, national civil war transformed the legal and military environment of slavery.

### Operating methods

Documented or strongly supported methods include:

- Choosing winter and periods of bad weather, when nights were long and surveillance could be reduced.
- Leaving on or near weekends, taking advantage of delays before fugitive advertisements could be printed or distributed.
- Moving primarily at night and hiding by day when required.
- Using stars, rivers, marshes, woods, roads, and local landscape knowledge rather than written maps.
- Using trains, wagons, boats, horses, and walking rather than a single stereotyped mode of travel.
- Changing clothing, posture, or apparent social role as disguise.
- Relying on free Black mariners, farmers, urban Vigilance Committees, Quakers, and antislavery households.
- Using songs or religious language as signals in some circumstances.
- Dictating coded messages through literate intermediaries.
- Carrying a small firearm according to Bradford and later family tradition.

The Underground Railroad was a **network metaphor**, not an actual subterranean railway. Tubman's work depended on people and institutions, not tunnels extending from plantation country to Canada.

### Firearm and discipline stories

Bradford's narratives describe Tubman carrying a pistol and threatening reluctant escapees who wanted to turn back because one returnee could expose an entire group. This is the source of many later “go on or die” stories. A firearm in her possession is credible within the documentary tradition. There is **no evidence that Tubman shot or killed a person she was conducting to freedom**.

Later depictions often turn the small pistol into a rifle, combine different episodes, and create polished dialogue that cannot be traced to a contemporary transcript.

### “Never lost a passenger”

At an 1896 suffrage convention Tubman used the railroad metaphor to describe herself as a conductor who had never run her train off the track and never lost a passenger. The line is best treated as **Tubman's own retrospective claim**. No known rescue mission provides a documented contradiction, but a secret operation cannot be audited trip by trip with the completeness of a formal passenger list.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Thomas Garrett

**Thomas Garrett**, the Wilmington, Delaware Quaker abolitionist, was among Tubman's most important logistical partners. Garrett's household and contacts formed a major station between Maryland and Pennsylvania. He supplied money, clothing, shelter, and onward connections. Garrett had already been prosecuted and financially punished for aiding fugitives before Tubman became active.

In a letter written for Sarah Bradford's fundraising biography in 1868, Garrett emphasized the unusual confidence Tubman inspired and the dangers she accepted. His testimony is valuable because he personally participated in the network; it is also retrospective praise written for a book intended to help Tubman financially.

### William Still

**William Still**, chair of Philadelphia's Vigilance Committee, systematically recorded freedom seekers passing through Philadelphia and later published much of that material in *The Underground Rail Road* (1872). Still and Tubman belonged to the same operational world of Black-led abolitionist assistance. His papers and book provide an essential independent framework for understanding the network in which she worked.

### Jacob Jackson

**Jacob Jackson** was a free Black Dorchester County farmer, veterinarian, and trusted friend. His ability to remain in Maryland, read correspondence, and communicate with Tubman's enslaved relatives made him an especially valuable intermediary. The surviving 1854 coded letter associated with him is among the clearest documentary traces of Tubman's clandestine planning.

### Frederick Douglass

Tubman and **Frederick Douglass** were allies and friends, although their public roles differed. Douglass's house in Rochester was part of the antislavery network, and Tubman's routes through New York brought them into direct contact.

On **29 August 1868**, Douglass wrote a letter for Bradford comparing his highly visible antislavery career with Tubman's clandestine work. He stressed that his own efforts had taken place before audiences while hers had taken place in darkness and danger with little public applause. The letter helped establish the enduring comparison between the most famous Black abolitionist orator and the secret “conductor.”

### John Brown

Tubman's relationship with **John Brown** is one of the most consequential and most mythologized of her abolitionist associations.

The National Park Service places their first documented meeting in **April 1858** at **St. Catharines, Canada West**. Brown was then developing an armed antislavery project that culminated in the 1859 raid on Harpers Ferry. He valued Tubman's knowledge of geography, Black communities, and clandestine movement and called her **“General Tubman.”**

Tubman assisted Brown by fundraising, discussing routes and recruitment, and helping him make contact with potential supporters. Brown's 1858 Chatham convention in Canada developed a provisional constitution for his revolutionary antislavery project. Tubman's precise presence at every Brown planning event is not securely documented, but her substantive involvement in his preparations is.

She **did not take part in the Harpers Ferry raid of 16–18 October 1859**. Later explanations include illness, her location in New York/New England, timing, and possible reservations about the operation. No surviving source permits a single definitive explanation. Her nonparticipation should therefore not be converted either into proof that she rejected Brown's project or proof that illness alone prevented her attendance.

A Brown letter preserved at Princeton, addressed to Frederick Douglass, sought money for Tubman and her parents, showing how Brown's relationship with her extended beyond the raid plan into practical concern for her household.

After Brown's execution Tubman continued to speak of him with respect and sometimes in explicitly religious language. Brown's own “General Tubman” epithet became a major component of her later military image.

### William H. and Frances Seward

In **1859**, Tubman acquired land in **Auburn, New York**, from **Frances Adeline Seward**, wife of U.S. senator and later secretary of state **William H. Seward**. The Sewards were prominent antislavery Whigs/Republicans and already connected to fugitive-aid networks.

The transaction was unusual and legally sensitive. Tubman was still a person whom federal law classified as an escaped slave, yet Frances Seward sold her approximately **seven acres** for **$1,200**, with a small down payment and private financing. The property gave Tubman's parents and extended family a stable base and became the nucleus of her later welfare work.

William Seward also later supported efforts to secure federal recognition or compensation for Tubman's wartime service.

### Martha Coffin Wright, Lucretia Mott, Susan B. Anthony, and reform networks

Auburn placed Tubman within the orbit of **Martha Coffin Wright**, a veteran women's-rights organizer and sister of Lucretia Mott. Wright's letters preserve evidence about Tubman's movements and postwar experiences. **Lucretia Mott**, **Susan B. Anthony**, and other abolitionist-suffragists became colleagues in the overlapping movements for emancipation, Black civil rights, and women's suffrage.

Tubman's friendship and political work with white suffragists did not erase conflicts within the suffrage movement over race, Reconstruction amendments, and political strategy. Her own participation increasingly centered on the proposition that Black women had claims both as Black citizens and as women.

### New England abolitionist circle

Tubman traveled and raised money through Boston and other New England reform centers. Her circle included or intersected with **William Lloyd Garrison**, **Franklin Benjamin Sanborn**, **Ralph Waldo Emerson**, **Bronson Alcott**, **Harriet Beecher Stowe**, the **Horace Mann** family, and other antislavery figures. The depth of individual friendship varied, and later memorial literature sometimes turns acquaintances into intimate relationships. The strongest evidence concerns fundraising, hospitality, introductions, and reform-network overlap.

### Black mariners, Gullah pilots, scouts, and freedom seekers

Many of Tubman's most operationally important collaborators are less famous than the reformers. During both the Underground Railroad years and the Civil War, mobile Black workers, pilots, fishermen, boatmen, refugees from slavery, and local guides supplied information unavailable to outsiders. The Combahee operation in particular depended on a network of Lowcountry Black intelligence rather than on Tubman acting alone.

## Auburn: Property, Household, Work, and Finances Before the Civil War

### The 1859 farm

Tubman purchased roughly **seven acres** on the southern edge of Auburn in 1859 for **$1,200**, with an initial payment commonly given as **$25**. The land transaction with Frances Seward gave her parents and relatives a permanent home after years of movement between Maryland, Canada, and the northern United States.

Tubman operated the property as more than a private residence. Family members, boarders, friends, homeless or destitute people, and people newly arrived from slavery passed through the household. The farm produced food and income. NPS accounts describe crops, fruit, livestock, pork, butter, and other household production. Tubman also continued paid domestic and service work when necessary.

### Chronic financial insecurity

Tubman's historical fame did not translate into wealth. Several factors kept her finances precarious:

- Maintaining a large dependent household.
- Supporting elderly parents and extended kin.
- Spending money on rescue operations and travel.
- Irregular employment and fundraising.
- Minimal federal compensation for Civil War service.
- Mortgage obligations on the Auburn property.
- Later investment in the Home for the Aged.
- A major fraud/robbery in 1873.

Her financial structure was therefore household- and property-based rather than corporate. There are no documented companies, patents, inventions, securities portfolios, or conventional business enterprises attributable to her.

## Civil War: Relief Work, Intelligence, Scouting, and Armed Operations, 1861–1865

### Transition from clandestine rescue to war work

The Civil War changed the legal and military meaning of Tubman's experience. The same skills that had allowed her to move secretly through slave territory — reading terrain, recruiting trusted guides, assessing strangers, building Black information networks, and operating without written records — had direct military intelligence value.

Tubman first performed relief and nursing work connected with displaced and self-emancipated people. NPS accounts place some early wartime service at or around **Fort Monroe, Virginia**, before she moved to the Union-occupied South Carolina Sea Islands.

### South Carolina and the Department of the South

By early **1862**, Tubman was in the **Port Royal–Beaufort** region of South Carolina. Massachusetts governor **John A. Andrew**, an important supporter of Black enlistment, helped recommend or facilitate her service. She worked under Union military authority but without a conventional enlisted status.

Her documented functions included:

- Nursing sick and wounded Black soldiers and civilians.
- Cooking and distributing food.
- Laundry and sanitation work.
- Teaching or organizing work for self-emancipated people.
- Gathering information from people escaping plantations.
- Recruiting and directing Black scouts.
- Acting as a guide and intelligence intermediary.
- Supporting military expeditions into Confederate-held territory.

Later popular summaries often reduce this to “Union spy.” That is true but incomplete: she moved among relief, intelligence, and military roles according to need, and the government never regularized her status in a way that matched her actual work.

### Intelligence organization

In pension and compensation claims Tubman later stated that she had directed **eight or nine scouts**. Names surviving in military, pension, and historical sources include men such as **William Plowden**, **Isaac Hayward**, **Charles Simmons**, and **Samuel Hayward**, although source lists differ and may refer to different phases of the network rather than a single fixed team.

The scouts' most important asset was local knowledge. Enslaved and formerly enslaved Lowcountry residents knew:

- Confederate troop positions and movements.
- Roads, causeways, waterways, and plantation landings.
- Locations of bridges and river obstructions.
- Placement of Confederate “torpedoes,” the nineteenth-century term for underwater mines.
- Which plantation workers could provide further information.
- Tidal and navigational conditions inaccessible to most northern officers.

**Francis Izzard**, a Black boat pilot/fisherman associated with the Combahee intelligence network, is among the people modern research highlights for knowledge of mines and navigation.

Tubman's intelligence role therefore joined two networks: her own practiced ability to evaluate and organize clandestine information, and the highly localized knowledge of Black South Carolinians living within the war zone.

### Combahee River Raid — 1–2 June 1863

The **Combahee River Raid** is the best-documented military operation associated with Tubman and the central basis for claims that she became the first woman to lead a major armed U.S. military operation.

#### Formal command and forces

The formal military commander was **Colonel James Montgomery**, commanding the **2nd South Carolina Volunteers (African Descent)**, later redesignated the 34th U.S. Colored Infantry, with artillery support. Tubman was not the colonel of the unit and did not hold a formal Army commission.

Sources vary on the exact troop strength. A contemporary newspaper account referenced roughly **300 Black soldiers**; some modern military histories give a force closer to **400**, depending on whom they include. The naval/transport force included the **John Adams**, **Harriet A. Weed**, and **Sentinel**; the Sentinel grounded and did not complete the upriver operation.

#### Planning and intelligence

Tubman and her Black scout network gathered information on the Combahee River, including the location of Confederate mines and plantation infrastructure. Recent research by Edda L. Fields-Black has substantially expanded the documentary reconstruction of the operation and the Black communities that made it possible.

Tubman's role was not merely ceremonial presence aboard a boat. The evidence supports her participation in intelligence preparation, scout organization, navigation/guidance, communication with freedom seekers, and the conduct of the expedition. At the same time, Montgomery retained formal command authority over the regiment and military orders.

This distinction explains why different historians use different verbs: **commanded**, **led**, **guided**, **planned**, **participated in**, or **served as intelligence leader for** the raid. “Led” is now common in NPS and public-history usage; “formally commanded” is too imprecise unless it is made clear that Montgomery held the military command.

#### The raid

The expedition moved up the Combahee during the night of **1–2 June 1863**, bypassing or avoiding Confederate river mines with local intelligence. Union troops landed at plantations and strategic points, destroyed or seized Confederate supplies and infrastructure, burned plantation buildings, rice mills and other property associated with the Confederate war economy, and encouraged enslaved people to leave.

Hundreds of people ran from plantations toward the gunboats. Tubman's later recollections preserved the chaotic scene of men, women, children, household goods, livestock, cries, whistles, and soldiers trying to embark everyone.

Government and historical sources generally state that the expedition liberated **more than 700** or **more than 750** enslaved people. Fields-Black's detailed modern reconstruction gives **756**. The difference is one of counting precision rather than disagreement over the scale of the operation.

More than **150 men** among the liberated population subsequently entered Union military service according to NPS reconstructions. Thus the operation simultaneously damaged Confederate resources, liberated enslaved laborers, and increased Union Black manpower.

#### Property destruction and violence

The raid was an armed Union operation in enemy territory. Plantation houses, agricultural installations, supplies, and other property were burned or destroyed. Confederates fired on the expedition, and Union troops operated with weapons and artillery. From Confederate and slaveholder perspectives the raid was described as destruction, theft, and incitement; Union and abolitionist press described it as a military and emancipatory success.

Tubman's role therefore belongs not only to humanitarian rescue history but to **wartime intelligence, armed coercion, destruction of enemy property, and military emancipation**. No evidence establishes that she personally killed anyone during the raid.

#### Press coverage and historical documentation

Contemporary Northern newspapers recognized that a Black woman had guided the expedition. Confederate newspapers condemned the raid. The surviving evidentiary record is dispersed: newspaper reports, military records, pension files, later statements, accounts of participants, and local records rather than a single comprehensive Tubman-authored after-action report.

Earlier historians sometimes treated the claim that Tubman “commanded” the raid skeptically because the official record foregrounded Montgomery. Recent archival work, especially Fields-Black's, has strengthened the case for Tubman's substantive operational leadership while also reconstructing the essential role of the Black Combahee community.

### Other wartime work

Tubman continued to nurse, scout, recruit, and perform relief work in the Department of the South after Combahee. Assertions that place her at every famous operation of the 2nd South Carolina should be treated cautiously unless separately documented. The regiment operated in South Carolina, Georgia, and Florida, but unit presence does not automatically prove Tubman's presence at every action.

### Compensation during the war

Congressional reconstruction of her claim indicates that Tubman received only about **$200** in direct federal payment across more than three years of work. She used some resources to create or support a **laundry operation** in which freedwomen could earn income. Much of her work was compensated irregularly, through rations, occasional pay, or private support rather than a stable military salary.

This ambiguous employment status became the foundation of a decades-long compensation dispute.

### 1865 train assault

After the war, while traveling north in **October 1865**, Tubman was ordered by railroad personnel to move to a smoking car despite possessing transportation connected with her government service. When she resisted, conductors or employees forcibly removed or moved her. Contemporary correspondence from Martha Coffin Wright's circle describes injuries to Tubman's arm, shoulder, or ribs.

The event illustrates that wartime Union service did not exempt her from racial segregation and violence in northern transportation. It also became part of the documentary record later used to demonstrate how little institutional protection accompanied her service.

## Civil War Intelligence Status: What Is and Is Not Established

### Established

- Tubman worked for Union forces in the Department of the South.
- She performed nursing, relief, cooking/laundry, scouting, and intelligence functions.
- She organized or directed a network of Black scouts.
- She gathered and transmitted military information obtained through local Black networks.
- She participated centrally in preparations for and execution of the Combahee River Raid.
- Federal officials and later congressional claim records acknowledged substantial service.

### Not established

- She was **not formally commissioned as a Union Army general** during the Civil War.
- No conventional enlistment record makes her a regularly enlisted soldier in the way Nelson Davis was.
- The title **“General Tubman”** was John Brown's honorific, not a federal rank.
- The **2024 brigadier-general commission** was a posthumous Maryland state/military honor.
- “Spy” is a reasonable modern description, but the surviving bureaucracy variously describes her as scout, nurse, cook, laundress, guide, or intelligence worker. Her irregular status is part of the historical problem, not evidence that the intelligence work did not occur.

## Postwar Life, Marriage, Property, and Household, 1865–1888

### Return to Auburn

Tubman returned to the Auburn household after the war, again taking responsibility for parents, relatives, boarders, and people in need. She traveled for lectures and fundraising but treated Auburn as her permanent home.

### *Scenes in the Life of Harriet Tubman* and fundraising

Financial strain led friends to encourage **Sarah H. Bradford** to write an authorized account of Tubman's life. *Scenes in the Life of Harriet Tubman* circulated in late 1868 with a 1869 title-page date. Its purpose was partly financial: Bradford, Douglass, Garrett, and others hoped book sales would help Tubman pay debts and support her household.

The book is one of the most important sources on Tubman's life because it contains stories and speech attributed directly to her and letters from people who knew her. It is also the origin or amplifier of several numerical exaggerations and dramatic scenes. Bradford wrote as an advocate, shaped episodes into a providential narrative, and did not operate under modern documentary-biography conventions.

### Marriage to Nelson Davis

On **18 March 1869**, Tubman married **Nelson Davis** in Auburn. Their household combined Tubman's elderly relatives, Nelson, boarders and dependents, and later adopted daughter Gertie. Davis's bricklaying wages and military-pension eligibility supplemented but did not solve the family's financial problems.

### The 1873 gold fraud and robbery

In **1873**, Tubman became the victim of an elaborate confidence scheme. Men approached her with a story that they possessed or knew the location of a cache of Southern gold and needed cash to secure it. Tubman assembled or borrowed approximately **$2,000**, a very large sum for her household, in expectation of receiving gold said to be worth about **$5,000**.

The men lured her away, assaulted or drugged her — accounts describe chloroform — took the money, and left her with worthless contents, often described as a trunk or package of rocks. The crime deepened her debt shortly after she had struggled to secure her property.

There is no evidence Tubman participated in fraud. She was the fraud victim. The episode is nevertheless important because it reveals both her financial vulnerability and the way her reputation for needing money could be exploited.

### Congressional claim, 1874

Tubman's friends pursued federal compensation. In **1874**, Representative **Clinton D. MacDougall** of New York introduced legislation for a payment of **$5,000** for her wartime services. House historical records show a committee recommending a smaller arrangement, reported as **$1,600 in trust**, but the proposal did not become law.

Older secondary accounts sometimes give different intermediate figures. The official House reconstruction is the preferable baseline. The important point is that Congress knew of the claim in the 1870s and did not enact the requested compensation.

### Fire and rebuilding

On **10 February 1880**, a fire caused by a careless boarder destroyed Tubman's wooden Auburn residence. The family and community rebuilt. The replacement was a more substantial **brick house**, completed around 1881–82 and associated with Nelson Davis's building trade and local Black labor.

The surviving Tubman residence within the national historical park reflects this later phase rather than the exact fabric of the original 1859 dwelling.

### Nelson Davis's death

Nelson died in **October 1888**, after nearly two decades of marriage. Tubman's later federal pension eligibility initially derived from being his widow, not from a government decision fully recognizing her own scout and intelligence service.

## Federal Compensation and Pension Dispute

Tubman's compensation claim is unusually important because it supplies a federal-paper trail for her Civil War work and exposes the mismatch between irregular wartime labor and nineteenth-century pension categories.

### Wartime and immediate postwar claims

Tubman repeatedly sought payment after 1865. Supporters obtained statements from officers and public figures attesting to her service. Her work did not fit neatly into a single bureaucratic category because she had not been formally enlisted, yet she had worked with military units and under military authority.

### 1874 legislation

As noted above, the 1874 attempt to secure **$5,000** did not become law. The failure left Tubman dependent on private fundraising and household income.

### Widow's pension

As the widow of Union veteran Nelson Davis, Tubman became eligible for a standard widow's pension. Official House history states that she began receiving **$8 per month in 1892**. Some later summaries give 1895 in connection with particular pension approvals or arrears; the federal legislative history should be preferred for the basic chronology while acknowledging the complexity of pension-file processing.

### Claim for her own service

In the late 1890s Tubman renewed claims for compensation based on her own Civil War work. Her affidavits and congressional submissions described approximately three years of nursing/cooking and her command or direction of eight or nine scouts. She sought back pay, often summarized at about **$1,800**, and a higher monthly pension.

Representative **Sereno E. Payne** introduced **H.R. 4982** in December 1897 for a pension of **$25 per month**. The House passed relief, but the Senate reduced the amount and grounded it more safely in the categories of widow and nurse rather than fully accepting a scout/spy back-pay theory.

President **William McKinley signed the measure on 28 February 1899**, increasing Tubman's pension to **$20 per month**.

### What the 1899 law did not do

The pension increase was substantial for Tubman, but it did **not** equal the full recognition she had sought. It did not award the claimed back pay on the theory that she had been a regularly compensated military scout or intelligence operative. National Archives and House records therefore support two simultaneous conclusions:

1. The federal government eventually paid Tubman an enhanced pension and recognized nursing/service evidence.
2. It never fully regularized or compensated her wartime scout and spy work on the terms she and her supporters requested.

The distinction is often lost in simplified statements that she “received a military pension.” She did receive federal pension money; the legal basis and scope were narrower than the full service claim.

## Harriet Tubman Home for the Aged and Institutional Work

### Origins

Tubman's Auburn household had functioned informally for decades as a refuge for relatives, travelers, poor people, and elderly Black residents. By the 1890s she tried to convert that practice into a permanent institution.

An incorporation certificate dated **3 December 1895** set out the purpose of a Harriet Tubman Home for aged or indigent Black people. The undertaking required land, buildings, trustees, church support, and money beyond what Tubman could provide alone.

### Additional land

In **1896**, Tubman acquired an adjoining property of roughly **twenty-five acres**, commonly reported at around **$1,200–$1,215**. Proceeds associated with the revised Bradford biography and other fundraising helped support the purchase.

### Transfer to AME Zion

Tubman eventually concluded that she could not sustainably operate the institution as a private undertaking. In **1903**, she transferred/deeded the Home property to the **African Methodist Episcopal Zion Church**, retaining a strong personal connection with the project.

The church imposed organizational and financial requirements, including admission arrangements that did not always match Tubman's wish to shelter people who had no money at all. The resulting compromises illustrate the difference between informal household charity and maintaining a legally incorporated residential institution.

### Opening

The **Harriet Tubman Home** formally opened on **23 June 1908**. It served elderly Black residents in Auburn and embodied a late-life shift from clandestine liberation and wartime work to organized mutual aid and elder care.

By **1911**, Tubman's own health had deteriorated enough that she became a resident of the Home she had worked to create.

## Political / Religious / Intellectual Development

### Abolitionism

Tubman's politics began in the material fact of slavery and family separation rather than in formal political theory. Her first political acts were self-emancipation and the repeated removal of relatives and others from bondage. By the 1850s she had become part of organized abolitionist networks in Philadelphia, New York, New England, and Canada.

She worked across several tendencies within abolitionism:

- Black-led Vigilance Committee and mutual-aid networks.
- Quaker and other white antislavery networks.
- Garrisonian reform circles.
- More militant abolitionism represented by John Brown.
- Wartime military emancipation and Black enlistment.

Her association with Brown shows that she did not regard all armed resistance as illegitimate. Her own military activity during the Civil War likewise involved intelligence used for armed raids and the destruction of Confederate resources.

### Religion

Tubman's religious language remained central throughout her life. She interpreted escape, danger, illness, military activity, and political work through prayer and providence. She was associated in Auburn with the **African Methodist Episcopal Zion Church**, a historically Black denomination with deep abolitionist connections.

Her visions were not peripheral ornament in her self-understanding. They functioned as a decision-making framework. She described warnings, foreknowledge, divine instruction, and answered prayer. Friends who were more conventionally rationalist or reformist sometimes recorded these experiences with a mixture of admiration and puzzlement.

In later life her religious commitments converged with institution-building through the AME Zion-supported Home for the Aged.

### Women's suffrage

Tubman increasingly appeared publicly in the women's-rights and suffrage movement after the Civil War. Her associations included **Susan B. Anthony**, **Lucretia Mott**, **Martha Coffin Wright**, and Black women's club organizers.

She spoke at meetings in New York and New England, frequently grounding the case for women's political rights in her own experience of labor and danger. At a **1896** suffrage gathering she delivered the famous railroad metaphor about having been a conductor who never lost a passenger.

In **1896** she also participated in the first convention of the **National Association of Colored Women**. Some NPS materials describe her as a co-founding member; the more conservative formulation is that she participated in the founding-era convention and became part of the Black women's club movement addressing suffrage, racial discrimination, education, segregation, and violence.

Tubman never held elective office. Her political influence came through testimony, public speaking, personal networks, institutional work, and symbolic authority.

### Civil rights after emancipation

The end of formal slavery did not end Tubman's involvement in racial justice. Her postwar concerns included:

- Payment and recognition for Black and female wartime labor.
- Education and employment for freedpeople.
- Protection from segregation and discrimination.
- Women's suffrage.
- Care for elderly and impoverished Black people.
- Black church and community institution-building.

Her 1865 railroad assault and decades-long compensation claim made federal emancipation and continuing discrimination parts of the same personal chronology.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

Tubman's life does not produce a conventional business or technology portfolio. No patents, inventions, incorporated commercial companies, stockholdings, industrial ventures, or financial-company directorships are documented.

### Relevant institutions and projects

- **Underground Railroad / Vigilance Committee networks:** decentralized clandestine assistance system, not a company.
- **Philadelphia Vigilance Committee:** William Still and associates; Tubman worked within its wider network.
- **Auburn farm and household:** agricultural production, boarding, family support, and relief.
- **Civil War laundry/work project:** federal records and later congressional histories indicate Tubman used wartime resources to help freedwomen earn income through laundry work.
- **Union Army / Department of the South:** irregular civilian and military-intelligence service.
- **African Methodist Episcopal Zion Church:** religious and institutional partner in Auburn.
- **Harriet Tubman Home for the Aged:** incorporated welfare project, eventually operated with AME Zion support.
- **National Association of Colored Women:** founding-era participant.
- **Women's suffrage organizations and conventions:** speaker and organizer rather than salaried officer.

### Property

The most consequential property relationships were:

- The **1859 seven-acre Auburn farm**, purchased from Frances Seward for $1,200 on favorable terms.
- Rebuilding after the **1880 fire**.
- The **c. 1896 twenty-five-acre parcel** acquired for the projected Home for the Aged.
- Transfer of the Home property to **AME Zion in 1903** to secure institutional continuity.

### Wealth

Tubman was never reliably wealthy. Even after becoming nationally famous, she combined pensions, farm production, family labor, speaking fees, book-related fundraising, donations, and credit. Her household obligations were large. The 1873 confidence fraud cost her approximately $2,000, and her federal compensation claims stretched across decades.

The repeated need for benefit publications and private appeals is documentary evidence against later romantic assumptions that national fame brought financial security.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Violence, and Financial Questions

Tubman's record contains relatively little evidence of personal corruption, criminal enrichment, or conventional scandal. The principal controversial material concerns actions criminalized under slave law, participation in armed conflict, disputed historical claims, and episodes in which she was a victim rather than perpetrator.

### “Criminality” under slavery law

From the standpoint of Maryland and federal fugitive-slave law, Tubman committed acts treated as crimes or civil wrongs: escaping enslavement, assisting other enslaved people to escape, concealing fugitives, and conspiring with antislavery networks. Slaveholders could seek her capture and return.

From a documentary perspective, it is important not to erase the legal fact simply because later law and public memory reversed the moral status of those acts. Her Underground Railroad activity was clandestine precisely because it violated enforceable law.

### John Brown and revolutionary violence

Tubman knowingly assisted John Brown during preparation for an armed antislavery project. She helped with fundraising, recruitment, information, and planning contacts. She did not participate in the Harpers Ferry attack itself.

The surviving evidence therefore supports **political association with planned revolutionary violence**, but not participation in the killings at Harpers Ferry.

### Combahee and wartime destruction

Tubman participated in an armed Union expedition that destroyed plantation and Confederate property, liberated enslaved laborers, and sought to weaken the Confederate war effort. To describe only the rescue aspect would sanitize the military nature of the event. To describe it simply as criminal destruction would ignore that it occurred as a Union wartime operation under Colonel Montgomery.

### Firearm threats on Underground Railroad journeys

Bradford's accounts say Tubman used or displayed a pistol to deter frightened escapees from turning back and exposing a group. No known source shows her actually shooting a passenger. The threat story is near-contemporary biographical testimony, not a police or court record.

### 1873 gold scam

Tubman lost approximately $2,000 to con men in a fake gold transaction. She was assaulted/drugged and robbed. The episode has sometimes been told as evidence of credulity or financial recklessness. The core fact remains that she was the victim of a premeditated confidence scheme; there is no basis to accuse her of fraud or money laundering.

### Federal pension controversy

The controversy lay in government classification of her work, not an accusation that she fabricated all service. Officers, supporters, congressional committees, and later federal historians accepted substantial evidence of wartime service. What remained contested in the nineteenth-century bureaucracy was the legal category under which that irregular service could be paid.

### Margaret's removal from Maryland

As discussed above, a later descendant described Tubman's removal of young Margaret as kidnapping. The child's parentage and circumstances have been debated by biographers. There is no known criminal prosecution. Modern genealogical work makes the earlier “secret biological daughter” hypothesis less likely and identifies Margaret with the Woolford family.

### No evidence found for conventional financial corruption

No substantial evidence was found of bribery for personal enrichment, embezzlement, corporate fraud, money laundering, organized crime, or misuse of public office by Tubman. This negative finding should not be confused with saying that every financial detail of her large informal household is recoverable.

## Health, Injuries, Medical History, and Psychological / Neurological Questions

### Childhood traumatic brain injury

The adolescent head injury produced lifelong symptoms. The nineteenth-century sources describe headaches, sudden sleep or loss of responsiveness, and vivid religious visions. Modern writers have proposed:

- Temporal-lobe epilepsy.
- Post-traumatic seizure disorder more generally.
- Narcolepsy or post-traumatic hypersomnia.
- Migraine or mixed post-traumatic neurological symptoms.

A 2023 medical paper specifically questioned the routine epilepsy label and proposed a hypersomnia/narcolepsy interpretation. Retrospective diagnosis remains uncertain.

### Visions and altered states

Tubman treated the experiences as religious, not psychiatric pathology. There is no good evidence that she regarded herself as mentally ill. The same visions that modern medicine might discuss neurologically were incorporated into a coherent Christian worldview and became a source of authority among supporters.

### Late-nineteenth-century head surgery

In the late 1890s Tubman underwent an operation at **Massachusetts General Hospital** to address chronic head pain. In her later account, a surgeon opened or elevated part of the skull. She said she declined anesthesia and bit down on a bullet during the procedure, comparing the method to what she had seen soldiers do.

The broad fact of surgery is accepted in major biographies and NPS accounts. The exact neurosurgical technique cannot be reconstructed from Tubman's colloquial description alone, and the “bit a bullet” detail comes through recollection.

### Aging and death

By the 1900s Tubman was increasingly frail and dependent. She entered the Harriet Tubman Home in 1911. Pneumonia preceded her death on 10 March 1913.

A reported deathbed line paraphrases John 14 — that she was going to prepare a place for those around her. It belongs to the near-contemporary memorial tradition and should be treated as reported last words rather than a stenographically verified transcript.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. “Nineteen trips and three hundred people rescued”

**Story:** Tubman returned to the South nineteen times and personally brought approximately 300 enslaved people to freedom.

**Emergence and circulation:** Sarah Bradford's authorized late-nineteenth-century biographies used large aggregate figures. Early twentieth-century memorials repeated them; an Auburn memorial tablet erected after Tubman's death stated that she had led more than 300 people to freedom. Schoolbooks, children's biographies, films, tourism literature, and older government pages reproduced the numbers.

**Evidence:** Modern archival reconstruction can identify roughly **thirteen Maryland rescue missions** and approximately **seventy people personally conducted**, with additional people receiving instructions or assistance. Secret operations inevitably leave gaps, so seventy is not necessarily an absolute maximum; it is a much better supported order of magnitude than three hundred.

**Why the myth may have grown:** Bradford may have combined people Tubman conducted, people she assisted indirectly, and the hundreds liberated in wartime, while commemorators rewarded round heroic numbers. Later writers copied earlier writers rather than reconstructing each mission.

**Effect on reputation:** The three-hundred figure helped create an almost superhuman lone-rescuer image. Correcting it does not diminish the scale of the actual record: repeated returns into slave territory, roughly seventy people personally conducted, and more than 750 liberated in the separate Combahee military operation.

### 2. The $40,000 bounty “dead or alive”

**Story:** Maryland slaveholders or the U.S. government placed a **$40,000 reward** on Tubman's head, sometimes specifically “dead or alive.”

**Emergence and circulation:** The figure appears in postwar fundraising and memorial literature and was amplified in twentieth-century biographies and popular culture. Historian Kate Clifford Larson traces an important appearance to an 1867 newspaper fundraising context associated with Tubman supporter **Sallie Holley**.

**Evidence against:** No fugitive-era advertisement offering $40,000 for Tubman has been found. The surviving **3 October 1849 Cambridge Democrat** notice offered **$100 each outside Maryland** for Minty, Ben, and Harry — $300 total for all three. Slaveholders also did not initially know that the mysterious conductor called “Moses” was the former “Minty” Ross.

**Effect on reputation:** The $40,000 claim dramatizes national notoriety and danger. Its persistence demonstrates how postwar hero-making can convert a real but localized fugitive advertisement into a vastly larger symbolic bounty.

### 3. Exact birth date and birthplace

**Story:** Tubman is often assigned a precise birth year — 1815, 1820, 1822, or 1825 — and a single birthplace, commonly Bucktown.

**Evidence:** No civil birth certificate settles the question. Tubman's own statements, her death certificate, gravestone, and later documentary reconstruction conflict. Current NPS and Larson-based research favors **March 1822** and birth on the **Anthony Thompson plantation at Peter's Neck**, while Bucktown remains central to her childhood.

**Effect:** The disagreement has repeatedly changed museum labels, anniversary observances, and “bicentennial” dating. The 2022 bicentennial relied on the modern 1822 reconstruction.

### 4. Ashanti ancestry

**Story:** Tubman was specifically descended from the Ashanti people of present-day Ghana through her maternal grandmother Modesty.

**Evidence:** A family tradition that Modesty was African-born is historically meaningful. The more specific Ashanti identification is not established by surviving genealogical documentation. Some modern interpretive sites have stated it more confidently than the evidence permits.

**Effect:** The claim supplies a direct named West African lineage to a person whose enslaved family records otherwise obscure African origins. It is better classified as a specific ancestral tradition than as proved genealogy.

### 5. Quilt codes on the Underground Railroad

**Story:** Quilts hung outside houses encoded secret directions — patterns told Tubman and escapees when to leave, what route to take, or what supplies to carry.

**Emergence:** The “quilt code” became highly popular in late-twentieth-century Underground Railroad folklore and educational material.

**Evidence:** No contemporary Tubman document, William Still record, Thomas Garrett correspondence, or Bradford account establishes that Tubman used a standardized quilt code. Historians of the Underground Railroad generally regard the elaborate code system as unsupported.

**Effect:** The story provides an attractive visual language for museums and classrooms but can displace better-documented communication methods: human couriers, free Black networks, coded letters, songs, oral instructions, clothing/disguise, and local geography.

### 6. “Swing Low,” “Wade in the Water,” and “Follow the Drinking Gourd” as Tubman's codebook

**Story:** Tubman used a standard repertory of spirituals — especially “Wade in the Water,” “Swing Low, Sweet Chariot,” and “Follow the Drinking Gourd” — as precise coded instructions.

**Evidence:** Bradford does connect Tubman with songs and religious singing, including **“Go Down Moses”** and forms of **“Bound for the Promised Land.”** The specific modern package of songs commonly taught as Tubman's escape code is not supported by contemporary Tubman evidence. “Follow the Drinking Gourd” in particular has a complicated twentieth-century publication/performance history.

**Effect:** The song-code narrative turns a flexible oral signaling practice into a standardized secret system. The documented point is narrower: Tubman sang and used religious language/signals; particular modern song attributions require independent evidence.

### 7. The pistol, rifle, and “go on or die”

**Story:** Tubman carried a rifle or pistol and threatened to shoot anyone who turned back.

**Evidence:** Bradford's biographies describe a **pistol** and a threat against retreat because a returning person could betray a whole party. Later art often transforms the pistol into a long gun or creates cinematic dialogue. There is no evidence she shot a freedom seeker.

**Effect:** The story has two opposite uses: heroic iconography of an armed liberator and hostile portrayals of coercive leadership. The documentary core is a firearm and stated willingness to prevent catastrophic retreat; the lethal follow-through is unproved.

### 8. “Never lost a passenger”

**Story:** Tubman never lost anyone she personally conducted to freedom.

**Source:** Unlike many Tubman slogans, this has a traceable basis in **her own 1896 public statement** using railroad language.

**Evidence:** No known mission contradicts the claim, but the clandestine archive is incomplete. It is properly identified as Tubman's retrospective self-description rather than an independently complete statistical record.

**Effect:** It became one of the most durable expressions of her operational competence and is often repeated without identifying it as her own claim.

### 9. “First woman to command an American military expedition”

**Story:** Tubman was the first woman to command or lead an armed U.S. military raid.

**Evidence and dispute:** Colonel James Montgomery held formal command of the Combahee force. Tubman was a civilian/irregular Union operative who gathered intelligence, directed scouts, helped plan and guide the raid, and participated aboard the expedition. Earlier historian Milton Sernett warned against turning “commanded the raid” into wishful heroic memory. More recent archival work by Edda L. Fields-Black has strengthened the evidence for Tubman's operational leadership. NPS now commonly describes her as having planned and led the raid.

**Best formulation:** She played a central intelligence and operational leadership role in the Combahee River Raid, an armed U.S. military expedition; she did not hold Montgomery's formal regimental command.

**Effect:** The phrasing is central to her military legacy and to recent Army, Navy, and state honors.

### 10. Prophetic visions

**Story:** God directly warned Tubman of danger, showed her future events, and guided escape routes through visions.

**Evidence:** Numerous recollections by Tubman and her associates establish that **she experienced and believed in visions** and made decisions with them in mind. A childhood brain injury and lifelong neurological symptoms are independently central to her medical history. Historical method cannot prove or disprove supernatural causation.

**Circulation:** Bradford presented the visions in a providential Christian framework. Later secular biographies often medicalized them; recent writers such as Tiya Miles have returned to their religious and experiential significance without requiring a supernatural historical verdict.

**Effect:** The visions made Tubman simultaneously a Christian prophetic figure, a subject of neurological speculation, and a mythic “Moses.”

### 11. “I freed a thousand slaves; I could have freed a thousand more if only they knew they were slaves”

**Story:** A quotation widely attributed to Tubman claims that the enslaved failed to recognize their own slavery.

**Evidence:** No nineteenth- or early-twentieth-century primary source has been identified for the line. Historian Milton Sernett and other Tubman specialists have treated it as a late fabrication or misattribution. It became especially prominent in modern political rhetoric and internet quotation culture; a version circulated in a 2008 political context through writer Robin Morgan and thereafter became ubiquitous.

**Effect:** The fake quotation is useful to almost any ideology that wants to portray opponents as unconsciously enslaved. Its rhetorical versatility explains part of its persistence. It should not be quoted as Tubman's words.

### 12. Margaret as Tubman's secret daughter

**Story:** Margaret, the child Tubman brought north around 1859, was really Tubman's biological daughter, concealed from the historical record.

**Emergence:** The unusual removal story and later family testimony led Kate Clifford Larson to consider the hypothesis.

**Evidence against / revision:** Subsequent genealogical research strongly identified Margaret as a Woolford child from a free Black family near Tubman's Maryland community. Larson revised her position.

**Effect:** The episode demonstrates responsible historiographic revision and the power of descendant memory to open questions that records may then answer differently.

### 13. “General Tubman” as Civil War rank

**Story:** Tubman was literally a Union Army general.

**Evidence:** John Brown called her **General Tubman** before the Civil War. She never held a federal general's commission in the 1860s. Maryland posthumously commissioned her an honorary **brigadier general in 2024**.

**Effect:** The same title now has three layers: Brown's revolutionary honorific, popular symbolic military memory, and a real but posthumous state commission.

### 14. Tubman as a solitary Underground Railroad operator

**Story:** A lone Tubman repeatedly walked into Maryland, collected groups, and single-handedly moved them to Canada.

**Evidence:** Tubman's courage and direct leadership are well documented, but every successful mission depended on networks: family intelligence, free Black communities, sailors, guides, Vigilance Committees, Quakers, safe houses, train and boat workers, donors, and border communities.

**Effect:** The lone-hero version increases dramatic simplicity while obscuring Black collective action and the infrastructure of resistance.

### 15. Tubman film mythology

The **2019 feature film *Harriet*** deliberately combines historical and fictional elements. Its central outline — enslavement, escape, rescue missions, visions, abolitionist work, Civil War service — is historical. Individual characters, confrontations, timelines, and narrative devices were compressed or invented for drama. NPS explicitly cautions that the film is **not a documentary**.

The film significantly expanded visual public memory of Tubman and re-centered her as the protagonist of a major theatrical feature, but film scenes should not be cited as documentary evidence.

## Works by Harriet Tubman, Dictated Material, Speeches, and Attributed Texts

Tubman did **not** write and publish a conventional autobiography under her own hand. She was not conventionally literate. Her textual corpus is therefore primarily **dictated, reported, sworn, transcribed, or editorially mediated**.

### Dictated correspondence

**Coded letter to Jacob Jackson, December 1854**  
A dictated message intended to prepare relatives for an escape. It uses biblical/family language and is associated with the signature “William Henry Jackson.” The physical text was produced by a literate intermediary. It is genuine evidence of Tubman's planning but not autograph writing.

### Oral testimony in Sarah H. Bradford

**Material incorporated into *Scenes in the Life of Harriet Tubman* (1868/1869)**  
Bradford interviewed Tubman and people who knew her. Tubman's stories, dialect speech, prayers, songs, and memories are embedded throughout. Authorship is therefore layered: Tubman is the oral source for much content; Bradford selected, organized, rewrote, and narrated it.

**Material incorporated into *Harriet, the Moses of Her People* (1886; revised 1901)**  
Bradford rewrote and expanded the earlier biography, again with Tubman's cooperation. It is not a Tubman-authored memoir, although it is sometimes cataloged or described loosely as her life story.

### Affidavits and pension testimony

**Civil War service affidavits / general affidavits, 1890s**  
Tubman supplied sworn statements in support of compensation and pension claims, describing nursing, cooking, scouting, and direction of a scout network. These records were dictated or completed on official forms and authenticated by witnesses/notaries rather than written in Tubman's hand.

### Public speeches and reported remarks

Tubman spoke at abolitionist, church, relief, women's-rights, and suffrage gatherings. Most speeches survive through newspaper reports or recollection rather than manuscripts.

Especially important:

- **1896 suffrage convention remark** using the Underground Railroad “conductor” metaphor and claiming never to have lost a passenger.
- Speeches at Black women's club and suffrage meetings in the 1890s.
- Fundraising appearances concerning the Harriet Tubman Home.

### Interviews

Tubman gave recollections to journalists, historians, and visitors. Among the important late-nineteenth-century collectors of Underground Railroad testimony was **Wilbur H. Siebert**, whose papers and publications contain route and operative information derived from interviews with surviving participants.

### What is not known to exist

No authenticated Tubman-authored diary, handwritten memoir, published essay collection, patent, scientific paper, technical paper, musical composition, or business prospectus is known. Later books printed “by” or “in the words of” Tubman must be checked for editorial mediation; many are compilations of reported speech rather than autograph authorship.

## Primary and Near-Primary Documentary Corpus

The following are indispensable to serious research on Tubman even when she is not their formal author:

1. **Sarah H. Bradford, *Scenes in the Life of Harriet Tubman* (Auburn: W. J. Moses, title-page 1869; copies in circulation 1868).** Authorized fundraising biography based on interviews, correspondence, and testimonial material. Essential but hagiographic and numerically unreliable in places.
2. **Sarah H. Bradford, *Harriet, the Moses of Her People* (New York: George R. Lockwood & Son, 1886; revised edition 1901).** Expanded/reworked authorized biography.
3. **William Still, *The Underground Rail Road* (Philadelphia, 1872).** Documentary compilation from Philadelphia Vigilance Committee records; crucial for the network surrounding Tubman.
4. **Frederick Douglass to Sarah H. Bradford, 29 August 1868.** Testimonial letter about Tubman's secret work.
5. **Thomas Garrett testimonial correspondence, 1868.** Firsthand network evidence used by Bradford.
6. **John Brown correspondence concerning Tubman and her family, 1858–59.** Evidence of Brown's respect, fundraising, and planning relationship.
7. **1849 Cambridge *Democrat* runaway advertisement for “Minty,” Ben, and Harry.** Central contemporary evidence for the first escape and the real bounty.
8. **Tubman pension and compensation records, National Archives / congressional records, 1870s–1899.** Core evidence for Civil War service claims, pay, witnesses, and congressional treatment.
9. **Contemporary Combahee River Raid press coverage, June 1863.** Northern and Confederate reporting on the expedition, including recognition of a Black woman guide/leader.
10. **Auburn deeds, incorporation records, census records, and AME Zion institutional records.** Evidence for property, household composition, and the Home for the Aged.

## Books and Major Works About Harriet Tubman

### Nineteenth-century and early documentary biographies

**Sarah H. Bradford, *Scenes in the Life of Harriet Tubman* (1868/1869).**  
The first book-length account. Bradford was a white Auburn author and Tubman supporter. The book was explicitly designed to raise money. It includes Tubman's recollections, testimonial letters, songs, religious material, Underground Railroad stories, and early versions of numerical claims later absorbed into legend. It is indispensable and must be read critically.

**Sarah H. Bradford, *Harriet, the Moses of Her People* (1886; revised 1901).**  
A rewritten and expanded version of Bradford's earlier book, again produced with Tubman's cooperation and for her financial benefit. It deepened the Moses/providential framework through which much of Tubman's early public reputation was constructed.

**William Still, *The Underground Rail Road* (1872).**  
Not a Tubman biography, but one of the essential primary compilations on the Philadelphia network. Still's recordkeeping provides context and corroboration for the Black-led infrastructure through which Tubman and many other freedom seekers moved.

### Twentieth-century biographies and literary treatments

**Earl Conrad, *Harriet Tubman* (1943).**  
The first major twentieth-century adult biography to attempt a systematic reconstruction beyond Bradford. Conrad amassed a large research archive of notes, correspondence, clippings, photographs, and manuscript material now held by the Schomburg Center/New York Public Library. His work helped fix Tubman in modern biography while still inheriting some older numerical traditions.

**Dorothy Sterling, *Freedom Train: The Story of Harriet Tubman* (1954).**  
An influential biography for younger readers. Like much mid-century children's historical writing, it helped standardize the heroic Underground Railroad narrative for generations of students.

**Ann Petry, *Harriet Tubman: Conductor on the Underground Railroad* (1955).**  
A widely read juvenile biography by novelist Ann Petry. Important not only as biography but as a vehicle of twentieth-century public memory.

**Marcy Heidish, *A Woman Called Moses* (1976).**  
Historical novel rather than documentary biography. It supplied the basis for the 1978 television miniseries starring Cicely Tyson. It should be classified as fictionalized interpretation, not primary evidence.

### Modern archival biographies and historiography

**Jean M. Humez, *Harriet Tubman: The Life and the Life Stories* (University of Wisconsin Press, 2003).**  
A major scholarly intervention that separates Tubman's historical life from the “life stories” told by Tubman and others. Humez collects and analyzes a broad corpus of oral and textual representations, making the book especially useful for questions of voice, memory, and mediation.

**Kate Clifford Larson, *Bound for the Promised Land: Harriet Tubman, Portrait of an American Hero* (2004; later editions).**  
One of the foundational modern archival biographies. Larson reconstructed family genealogy, Maryland geography, rescue missions, property, and networks and is central to the revised figures of roughly thirteen missions and about seventy people personally conducted.

**Catherine Clinton, *Harriet Tubman: The Road to Freedom* (2004).**  
A major modern biography emphasizing Tubman's life within slavery, abolitionism, Civil War, and women's-rights history. Often read alongside Larson because the two books appeared almost simultaneously and made independent archival contributions.

**Milton C. Sernett, *Harriet Tubman: Myth, Memory, and History* (Duke University Press, 2007).**  
The principal monograph on the creation of the Tubman icon. Sernett tracks Bradford, memorials, children's literature, political uses, visual culture, and recurring false quotations or exaggerations. Essential for distinguishing the historical person from the layers of American memory built around her.

**Beverly Lowry, *Harriet Tubman: Imagining a Life* (Doubleday, 2007; Anchor paperback 2008).**  
A literary biography that explicitly confronts the incompleteness of the archive. Useful for understanding the boundary between documented reconstruction and biographical imagination.

**Kristen T. Oertel, *Harriet Tubman: Slavery, the Civil War, and Civil Rights in the 19th Century* (Routledge, 2015).**  
A concise scholarly biography supplemented by primary documents and particularly useful for placing Tubman's later civil-rights and women's-rights work in continuity with the better-known Underground Railroad period.

**Erica Armstrong Dunbar, *She Came to Slay: The Life and Times of Harriet Tubman* (37 Ink/Simon & Schuster, 2019).**  
An accessible modern biography emphasizing the full life rather than ending the story with the Underground Railroad or Civil War.

**Dorothy Wickenden, *The Agitators: Three Friends Who Fought for Abolition and Women's Rights* (2021).**  
A group biography focused on Harriet Tubman, Martha Coffin Wright, and Frances Seward. Particularly useful for Auburn's reform network and the social relationships surrounding Tubman's property and women's-rights activity.

**Kate Clifford Larson, *Harriet Tubman: A Reference Guide to Her Life and Works* (Rowman & Littlefield, 2022).**  
A reference work incorporating decades of Tubman scholarship, chronology, people, places, controversies, and memorialization. Useful for resolving names, dates, and older myths.

**Clarence Lusane, *Twenty Dollars and Change: Harriet Tubman and the Ongoing Fight for Racial Justice and Democracy* (City Lights, 2022).**  
Not principally a full biography. It uses the campaign to put Tubman on the $20 note as a route into historical memory, currency, political symbolism, race, and democracy.

**Tiya Miles, *Night Flyer: Harriet Tubman and the Faith Dreams of a Free People* (Penguin Press, 2024).**  
A modern interpretive biography emphasizing Tubman's inner religious life, ecology, dreams, kinship, and the experiential worlds of enslaved Black women. It deliberately treats faith and visions as historical evidence about Tubman's world rather than reducing them to decorative legend.

**Edda L. Fields-Black, *COMBEE: Harriet Tubman, the Combahee River Raid, and Black Freedom during the Civil War* (Oxford University Press, 2024).**  
The most extensive modern study of the Combahee River Raid and the communities involved. Fields-Black integrates military records, pension files, plantation records, genealogy, landscape, and descendant history. It substantially revises the older image of Combahee as a short isolated Tubman episode by reconstructing a long intelligence operation and the agency of the people who freed themselves during the raid.

### Specialized themes and source collections

**National Archives, Harriet Tubman pension and service-claim files.**  
These are not a single published book but form the crucial federal documentary corpus for her wartime employment and compensation struggle.

**Earl Conrad Research Material on Harriet Tubman, Schomburg Center for Research in Black Culture, New York Public Library.**  
A major twentieth-century biographer's working archive. Useful for tracing sources, correspondence with descendants, and the evolution of modern Tubman scholarship.

**Library of Congress Harriet Tubman research guides and Chronicling America guide.**  
Gateways to photographs, newspapers, manuscripts, and digitized primary materials.

**Harriet Tubman National Historical Park / Harriet Tubman Underground Railroad National Historical Park collections.**  
Archaeology, site studies, genealogy, property history, cultural landscape, and interpretive research surrounding Auburn and Maryland.

### Fiction, children's literature, and myth-making as historical evidence

A complete bibliography of Tubman children's books would be enormous. The following are especially influential examples whose importance is partly cultural rather than evidentiary:

- Dorothy Sterling, *Freedom Train* (1954).
- Ann Petry, *Harriet Tubman: Conductor on the Underground Railroad* (1955).
- Marcy Heidish, *A Woman Called Moses* (1976), historical novel.
- Faith Ringgold, *Aunt Harriet's Underground Railroad in the Sky* (1992), picture-book fantasy linking a modern child to Tubman's memory.

Such works frequently preserve outdated figures or invent dialogue, but they are important sources for **how Tubman was remembered**, especially by children and in education.

## Films, Television, Opera, Documentaries, Interviews, and Archival Material

### Film and television

**A Woman Called Moses (NBC, 1978)**  
Two-part television miniseries starring **Cicely Tyson** as Tubman, directed by **Paul Wendkos**, based on Marcy Heidish's novel. The production belongs to historical drama rather than documentary and was one of the most important twentieth-century screen representations of Tubman before the 2019 film.

**Harriet (Focus Features, 2019)**  
Feature film directed and co-written by **Kasi Lemmons**, starring **Cynthia Erivo** as Harriet Tubman. Released in the United States on **1 November 2019**. It was the first major feature-length theatrical film centered entirely on Tubman's life. The film uses real people and events but also composite/fictional characters, compressed chronology, invented confrontations, and dramatized visions. National Park Service interpretive material explicitly warns audiences not to treat it as documentary evidence.

**Harriet Tubman: Visions of Freedom (PBS / Maryland Public Television, 2022)**  
Documentary produced and directed by **Stanley Nelson and Nicole London**, narrated by **Alfre Woodard**. PBS announced it as part of a pair of films on Tubman and Frederick Douglass. It integrates historians, archival material, and location photography and is among the strongest recent documentary introductions to Tubman's full life.

### Opera and musical works

**Thea Musgrave, *Harriet, the Woman Called Moses* (1984; premiered 1 March 1985, Virginia Opera).**  
Opera based freely on Tubman's life. The official composer/publisher description illustrates the persistence of older public-memory numbers, including versions of the nineteen-missions/three-hundred-rescued tradition. Its value is therefore both artistic and historiographical.

**Thea Musgrave, *The Story of Harriet Tubman***  
A later reduced/reworked version for smaller forces, designed to make the material more performable in chamber/educational settings.

**Nkeiru Okoye, *Harriet Tubman: When I Crossed That Line to Freedom* (2014).**  
Two-act opera with words and music by Okoye, dramatizing Tubman's transformation from an enslaved girl into an Underground Railroad conductor through Black musical idioms including spiritual, gospel, blues, and jazz-inflected language. The chamber version received its world premiere in 2014 through American Opera Projects.

### Photographs and visual archive

A small number of photographs survive, spanning Tubman's middle and old age. Particularly important are:

- A youthful portrait, probably **c. 1868–69**, held by the Library of Congress and associated with an album acquired by the Library and Smithsonian.
- Later formal portraits used in suffrage and memorial culture.
- Family imagery of **Harriet Tubman, Nelson Davis, and Gertie**, held by the National Museum of African American History and Culture.
- A 1944 photograph of the launch/christening of the Liberty ship **SS Harriet Tubman**.

The youthful portrait became especially significant because much twentieth-century iconography relied on photographs of an elderly Tubman; rediscovery and circulation of younger images changed the public visual vocabulary around her.

**National Archives and Records Administration (NARA)**  
Pension, service claims, congressional documents, and related federal records.

**Library of Congress**  
Photographs, newspapers, research guides, and digitized materials.

**Schomburg Center for Research in Black Culture, NYPL**  
Earl Conrad research materials and extensive African American historical collections.

**Princeton University Library**  
John Brown–Frederick Douglass correspondence concerning Tubman and her family.

Photographic and object collections, including family images and material relating to the Combahee raid and SS *Harriet Tubman*.

Residence, Home for the Aged, Thompson Memorial AME Zion Church, landscape and archaeology.

Birthplace landscape, Eastern Shore sites, Underground Railroad interpretation, and current Tubman scholarship.

## Death and Funeral

By 1911 Tubman's health had declined and she moved into the Harriet Tubman Home for the Aged. She remained surrounded by family, church members, and the institution she had created.

She died of **pneumonia on 10 March 1913** in Auburn. Reports of her final moments describe family and friends singing and praying. A later-reported final statement invokes John 14 and the preparation of a place for others in the afterlife.

Her funeral was held at **Thompson Memorial African Methodist Episcopal Zion Church**. Civic, church, women's-club, and veteran/military associations participated. She was buried at **Fort Hill Cemetery** in Auburn with commemorative military elements reflecting her Union service even though the federal government had never formally commissioned her during the war.

Her first grave marker was later replaced. In **1937**, the **Empire State Federation of Women's Clubs** installed a new memorial stone.

## Posthumous Reputation, Cult, Influence, Historical Reinterpretation, and Legacy

### 1913–1940s: local saint, national heroine, and numerical legend

Immediately after Tubman's death, memorializers emphasized “Moses,” Christian faith, the Underground Railroad, Civil War service, and personal sacrifice. A **1914 Auburn memorial tablet** repeated the large “more than 300” liberation figure, demonstrating how quickly Bradford-era numbers hardened into public inscription.

The memorial language often transformed Tubman into a near-hagiographic figure: a selfless deliverer, “Moses,” and Christian servant. This preserved genuine aspects of her faith but could also flatten her into a saintly stereotype and obscure strategy, anger, armed politics, and material hardship.

### Earl Conrad and modern biography

Earl Conrad's **1943** biography and research program marked a transition toward modern source-based reconstruction. His papers later became an archive in their own right. Mid-century juvenile biographies by Sterling and Petry then carried Tubman's story to a mass school-age readership.

### World War II: SS *Harriet Tubman*

A Liberty ship named **SS *Harriet Tubman*** was launched in **1944**. Tubman's relatives participated in the launch, and a surviving Smithsonian photograph documents the event. The naming linked her Civil War service with wartime American patriotic memory during World War II. It was the first Liberty ship named for an African American woman according to standard maritime histories.

### 1978 postage stamp

On **1 February 1978**, the U.S. Postal Service issued a **13-cent Harriet Tubman stamp**, the first stamp in the Black Heritage series. Smithsonian National Postal Museum records identify her as the **first African American woman honored on a U.S. postage stamp**.

This was a major transition from regional and African American commemorative memory into a recurring federal visual canon.

### Historic preservation and National Park Service sites

Tubman's Maryland landscapes and Auburn properties became major public-history sites. The two principal federal units are:

- **Harriet Tubman Underground Railroad National Historical Park** in Maryland, preserving and interpreting the Eastern Shore landscape connected with her birth, enslavement, and escape network.
- **Harriet Tubman National Historical Park** in Auburn, New York, preserving the Tubman residence, Harriet Tubman Home for the Aged, Thompson Memorial AME Zion Church, and related landscape. Congress authorized the Auburn park in 2014 and it was formally established in **2017**.

Preservation shifted historical interpretation away from abstract “Underground Railroad” legend toward geography: marshes, waterways, roads, farms, churches, houses, and communities.

### Combahee River Collective

The **Combahee River Collective**, a Black feminist organization formed in Boston in **1974**, took its name from Tubman's 1863 raid. The naming connected Tubman's history to twentieth-century Black feminist politics and to a collective model of liberation rather than only a great-individual narrative.

The Collective's 1977 statement became a foundational text in Black feminist and intersectional political thought. Tubman's Combahee legacy therefore entered modern intellectual history indirectly through the name of an organization whose political vocabulary reached far beyond Civil War memory.

### Military intelligence recognition

Tubman's espionage and scouting work received increasing formal military recognition in the twenty-first century.

The U.S. Army Military Intelligence Hall of Fame listings identify **Harriet Ross Tubman** among the **2021** members. This recognition belongs to retrospective military intelligence history, not to a nineteenth-century rank award.

### U.S. Navy: future USNS *Harriet Tubman* (T-AO 213)

In **2023**, the Secretary of the Navy announced that a future **John Lewis-class fleet replenishment oiler**, **T-AO 213**, would be named **USNS *Harriet Tubman***.

In June 2025, reports that the Defense Department was considering renaming the vessel generated controversy as part of a wider review of ship names. Subsequent Pentagon reporting said there were no plans at that time to extend the renaming action to the other ships under review. The Navy's current fact file, updated in **December 2025**, continued to list **Harriet Tubman (T-AO 213)** as a ship in production. As of this dossier's research date, **19 August 2026**, the official Navy listing still supports the Tubman name.

### 2024 Maryland brigadier-general commission

On **11 November 2024**, Maryland posthumously commissioned Tubman as a **one-star brigadier general** in the Maryland National Guard in recognition of her military service. The ceremony involved Governor Wes Moore and Maryland National Guard leadership.

This modern state commission has sometimes generated confused headlines implying that a suppressed nineteenth-century Army rank was finally “restored.” It was instead a new posthumous honorary commission. During the Civil War Tubman remained an irregular federal worker without a general officer's commission.

### 2024 Harriet Tubman commemorative coins

The United States Mint issued a **2024 Harriet Tubman Bicentennial Commemorative Coin Program** authorized by Public Law 117-163. It included a **$5 gold coin, $1 silver coin, and half-dollar clad coin**, with designs representing phases of Tubman's life. Surcharges were designated to support Tubman-related institutions and sites.

The program translated a contested and complex life into official numismatic symbolism: Underground Railroad conductor, Civil War worker, and later-life activist.

### The $20 bill

On **20 April 2016**, Treasury Secretary **Jacob J. Lew** announced that Harriet Tubman would be placed on the front of a redesigned **$20 Federal Reserve note**, replacing Andrew Jackson as the principal face while Jackson imagery would remain on the reverse in connection with the White House.

The redesign became politically contested and repeatedly delayed. The first Trump administration did not proceed on the timetable expected under the Obama administration. The Biden administration stated that it would accelerate or revive work, but currency redesign also involves long security and production cycles.

As of **7 July 2026**, Reuters reported that Treasury Secretary **Scott Bessent** said the Trump administration was **not at present proceeding** with the Tubman $20 plan. On **30 July 2026**, Democratic senators publicly urged Treasury to revive it. Therefore, as of **19 August 2026**, the accurate status is not that a Tubman $20 is scheduled for imminent release; the redesign remains **on ice / without a current implementation commitment from Treasury**.

The currency controversy has become one of the most visible arenas in which Tubman's image functions as a proxy for arguments over slavery, federal memory, Andrew Jackson, race, gender, and the symbolic meaning of money.

### 2025 National Park Service webpage controversy

In **April 2025**, changes to a National Park Service Underground Railroad webpage removed or deemphasized Tubman's image/quotation and altered language about slavery. The changes drew public criticism and were reported nationally. NPS subsequently restored material and characterized the edits as unauthorized.

This incident is part of Tubman's modern afterlife: public disputes are now not merely about whether she should be remembered, but about the language, imagery, and historical framing through which federal institutions remember slavery and emancipation.

### Reinterpretation of the Combahee Raid

Older biographies often treated Combahee as a spectacular single night starring Tubman. Twenty-first-century scholarship, especially **Edda L. Fields-Black's 2024 *COMBEE***, has recast it as the culmination of long intelligence work and as the history of hundreds of Black South Carolinians who supplied knowledge, fled plantations, enlisted, and created freedom in the context of Union military power.

The reinterpretation does not remove Tubman from the center; it changes the center from a lone heroic individual to an operational network and a Black community.

### Reinterpretation of faith

For much of the twentieth century, biographers often treated Tubman's visions as either quaint religious language or symptoms to be medically explained. Recent work, especially **Tiya Miles's *Night Flyer***, has insisted that religion, dreams, ecology, and embodied knowledge are themselves historical domains. This allows the archive to preserve both neurological inquiry and Tubman's own spiritual categories without forcing one to erase the other.

### The historical icon versus the historical person

Tubman's posthumous image has accumulated several simplified figures:

- the infallible “conductor”;
- the Christian Moses;
- the rifle-bearing revolutionary;
- the secret spy;
- the feminist suffragist;
- the patriotic military heroine;
- the saintly caretaker of the aged;
- the face proposed for American currency.

All derive from real parts of her life. Problems arise when one icon is allowed to replace the chronology. The archival record instead shows a person whose roles changed across nine decades: enslaved child, injured adolescent worker, wife, self-emancipated fugitive, family rescuer, abolitionist, militant Brown associate, farmer, Civil War intelligence operative, fraud victim, widow, pension claimant, suffragist, churchwoman, welfare institution founder, and finally a national symbol shaped by people who often disagreed about what she symbolized.

## Chronology

| Date / Year | Event |
|---|---|
| **c. March 1822** | Best-supported modern birth date for Araminta “Minty” Ross, enslaved in Dorchester County, Maryland, probably on Anthony Thompson's Peter's Neck plantation. Other historical sources give 1815, 1820, or 1825. |
| **c. 1827–28** | Hired out as a young child for domestic/nursemaid work; later recollections describe beatings, hunger, and sleep deprivation. |
| **1830s** | Performs muskrat trapping, field, timber, and other outdoor labor; develops extensive knowledge of Eastern Shore geography and Black maritime/labor networks. |
| **c. 1835** | Severe head injury after an overseer throws a heavy weight at another enslaved man and strikes Tubman. Lifelong headaches, sleep/seizure-like episodes, and visions follow. |
| **1840** | Father Ben Ross becomes legally free under Anthony Thompson's manumission arrangements. |
| **c. 1844** | Marries free Black man John Tubman; begins using Harriet Tubman. |
| **March 1849** | Edward Brodess dies; debt and possible sale intensify danger to the Ross/Tubman family. |
| **17 Sept. 1849** | First escape attempt with brothers Ben and Henry. They turn back and Harriet returns with them. |
| **3 Oct. 1849** | *Cambridge Democrat* runaway advertisement offers rewards for “Minty,” Ben, and Harry, including $100 each if captured outside Maryland. |
| **Oct.–Nov. 1849** | Harriet escapes alone to Pennsylvania and establishes herself in Philadelphia. |
| **1850** | Fugitive Slave Act increases danger in free states and the importance of Canada. |
| **Dec. 1850** | Rescue of niece Kessiah Bowley and children from Maryland, Tubman's first securely documented family rescue after her own escape. |
| **1851** | Additional rescue trips. Returns for husband John, discovers he has remarried and will not leave; conducts other freedom seekers north instead. |
| **1851–57** | Operates repeatedly between Maryland, Philadelphia, New York, New England, and St. Catharines, Canada West. |
| **Dec. 1854** | Dictates coded message to Jacob Jackson; rescues brothers and companions around Christmas. |
| **1855** | Ben Ross purchases Rit Ross's freedom. |
| **1857** | Dover Eight crisis exposes Ben Ross to danger; Tubman removes parents from Maryland and ultimately north to safety. |
| **April 1858** | First documented meeting with John Brown at St. Catharines according to NPS reconstruction. Brown later calls her “General Tubman.” |
| **1858–59** | Assists Brown with fundraising, intelligence, recruitment contacts, and planning for armed antislavery activity. |
| **1859** | Purchases roughly seven acres in Auburn, New York, from Frances Seward for $1,200; moves parents/family there. |
| **16–18 Oct. 1859** | John Brown attacks Harpers Ferry. Tubman does not participate; reasons for absence remain unresolved beyond evidence of illness/location/logistics. |
| **1859** | Returns to Maryland in an unsuccessful attempt to save sister Rachel and/or her children; Rachel dies before a successful rescue can be arranged. |
| **Nov.–Dec. 1860** | Final known Maryland rescue mission, including members of the Ennalls family; group reaches Auburn region by 28 December. |
| **1861** | Civil War begins. Tubman enters wartime relief work and later Union service. |
| **early 1862** | In Union-occupied South Carolina/Port Royal–Beaufort region; works as nurse, cook, laundress, relief worker, scout and intelligence organizer. |
| **1862–63** | Builds/works with network of Black scouts and Lowcountry informants; gathers military intelligence on rivers, mines, roads and Confederate positions. |
| **1–2 June 1863** | Combahee River Raid under formal command of Col. James Montgomery. Tubman plays central intelligence, guide and operational-leadership role. More than 750 people are liberated; Fields-Black reconstructs 756. |
| **1863–65** | Continues Union nursing, scouting, intelligence, recruitment and relief work in the Department of the South. |
| **1865** | War ends. Tubman receives only limited direct compensation for years of federal service. |
| **Oct. 1865** | Railroad employees forcibly move/remove Tubman in a segregation dispute, injuring her. |
| **1867** | First husband John Tubman is shot and killed in Maryland. |
| **1868** | Sarah Bradford prepares fundraising biography; Frederick Douglass and Thomas Garrett supply testimonial letters. |
| **1868/1869** | *Scenes in the Life of Harriet Tubman* published/circulated. |
| **18 Mar. 1869** | Marries Union veteran Nelson Davis in Auburn. |
| **c. 1874** | Harriet and Nelson adopt infant Gertie Davis. |
| **1873** | Tubman loses about $2,000 in fraudulent gold scheme; assaulted/drugged and robbed. |
| **1874** | Rep. Clinton D. MacDougall introduces federal compensation legislation; no payment bill becomes law. |
| **10 Feb. 1880** | Auburn wooden residence destroyed by fire caused by a boarder. |
| **1881–82** | Brick residence rebuilt. |
| **1886** | Bradford publishes *Harriet, the Moses of Her People*. |
| **Oct. 1888** | Nelson Davis dies; exact day varies in sources. |
| **1892** | Official House history places beginning of Tubman's $8/month widow's pension here. |
| **3 Dec. 1895** | Incorporation documentation for Harriet Tubman Home project. |
| **1896** | Acquires additional property for the Home; active in suffrage and Black women's club movement; makes famous “conductor” statement at suffrage convention. |
| **late 1890s** | Undergoes head surgery at Massachusetts General Hospital; renews federal compensation/pension claims based on her own Civil War work. |
| **Dec. 1897** | Rep. Sereno E. Payne introduces H.R. 4982 seeking $25/month pension. |
| **28 Feb. 1899** | President William McKinley signs law increasing Tubman's pension to $20/month; full scout/spy back-pay claim remains unpaid. |
| **1901** | Revised edition of Bradford's *Harriet, the Moses of Her People*. |
| **1903** | Tubman deeds Home property to AME Zion Church to secure institutional operation. |
| **23 June 1908** | Harriet Tubman Home for the Aged formally opens. |
| **1911** | Tubman becomes a resident of the Home as her health declines. |
| **10 Mar. 1913** | Dies of pneumonia in Auburn; buried at Fort Hill Cemetery. |
| **1914** | Auburn memorial tablet helps canonize heroic memory and repeats large rescue figures. |
| **1937** | Empire State Federation of Women's Clubs installs replacement grave marker. |
| **1943** | Earl Conrad publishes major modern biography *Harriet Tubman*. |
| **1944** | Liberty ship SS *Harriet Tubman* launched; relatives participate. |
| **1954–55** | Dorothy Sterling and Ann Petry publish influential youth biographies. |
| **1974** | Boston Black feminist organization Combahee River Collective takes its name from the 1863 raid. |
| **1 Feb. 1978** | 13-cent Harriet Tubman stamp issued; first Black Heritage series stamp and first U.S. stamp honoring an African American woman. |
| **Dec. 1978** | NBC broadcasts *A Woman Called Moses*, starring Cicely Tyson. |
| **1985** | Thea Musgrave's opera *Harriet, the Woman Called Moses* premieres. |
| **2003–07** | Humez, Larson, Clinton, Sernett and Lowry publish major modern biographies/historiographical works, substantially revising popular narratives. |
| **2014** | Auburn national historical park authorized; Nkeiru Okoye's *Harriet Tubman: When I Crossed That Line to Freedom* premieres. |
| **20 Apr. 2016** | Treasury announces plan for Tubman to become principal face of redesigned $20 note. |

| **2021** | U.S. Army Military Intelligence Hall of Fame lists Harriet Ross Tubman among 2021 members. |
| **2022** | Tubman bicentennial commemorations based on 1822 birth reconstruction; PBS airs *Harriet Tubman: Visions of Freedom*. |
| **2023** | Navy announces future John Lewis-class oiler T-AO 213 will be named USNS *Harriet Tubman*. |
| **2024** | U.S. Mint issues Harriet Tubman commemorative coins; Fields-Black publishes *COMBEE*; Tiya Miles publishes *Night Flyer*. |
| **11 Nov. 2024** | Maryland posthumously commissions Tubman an honorary/state brigadier general. |
| **Apr. 2025** | NPS webpage edits removing/deemphasizing Tubman and slavery language are reversed after public controversy. |
| **June 2025** | Possible Navy ship renaming reviewed/reported; later official Navy listings retain T-AO 213 *Harriet Tubman*. |

| **30 July 2026** | Democratic senators urge Treasury to revive the Tubman $20 redesign. |
