# Tertullian: Research Dossier

**Subject:** Tertullian of Carthage  
**Conventional Latin name:** *Quintus Septimius Florens Tertullianus*  
**Life:** probably born c. 150–170 CE; active as a Christian author c. 196–212/220+; death date and circumstances unknown  
**Prepared:** 30 August 2026  

---

## Scope, Method, and Evidentiary Limits

Tertullian is documented in an unusual way: thirty-one Latin treatises survive, but they contain remarkably little secure autobiography. The fullest ancient biography is chapter 53 of Jerome's *De viris illustribus*, written c. 392–393—roughly 170 years after Tertullian's literary career. Eusebius, writing in the early fourth century, preserves several Greek translations or paraphrases of Tertullian but supplies little reliable personal information. Augustine's report about the “Tertullianists” was written around 428, more than two centuries after Tertullian's active period.

This dossier therefore separates:

- **Direct evidence:** statements in Tertullian's surviving works, dated where possible by internal historical references.
- **Early external testimony:** Eusebius, Jerome, Augustine, and other ancient witnesses.
- **Modern reconstruction:** deductions from language, rhetoric, law, social setting, and intertextual dependence.
- **Tradition or myth:** familiar assertions that cannot be independently established.

Dates attached to individual treatises are approximate. The “standard” chronology inherited from Harnack, Monceaux, and the *Corpus Christianorum* differs substantially from Timothy D. Barnes's reconstruction. No exact birth date, conversion date, date of adherence to the New Prophecy, or death date survives.

### The conventional full name

The familiar *Quintus Septimius Florens Tertullianus* should not be treated like a modern birth-certificate name. Tertullian calls himself *Septimius Tertullianus* in the textual tradition, while the expanded form appears in medieval manuscript headings. Its components are credible Roman names, but the complete combination is not independently confirmed. There is no secure basis for deriving ancestry, citizenship history, or family relationships from the names alone.

### “First Latin theologian”

Tertullian is the **first surviving Christian author to leave a large theological corpus in Latin**, and the first major architect of Latin Christian technical prose. He was not literally the first Christian ever to use Latin. The *Acts of the Scillitan Martyrs* (180) is the earliest precisely dated Latin Christian document; early Latin biblical translations also preceded or accompanied him. Victor of Rome reportedly wrote in Latin, and the date of Minucius Felix's *Octavius* relative to Tertullian remains debated. “First Latin theologian” is thus a useful shorthand if “first major, extensively surviving” is understood.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and ancestry

- **Father:** Jerome says Tertullian was the son of a *centurio proconsularis*. Older biographies render this “a centurion attached to the proconsul of Africa.” Modern objections are substantial: no Roman military rank with that exact title is otherwise known, and Jerome may have drawn an erroneous biographical inference from a corrupted reading of *Apologeticum* 9.2. James B. Rives, Philippe Henne, and Heinrike Maria Zilling have resisted complete dismissal of the tradition. The father's name is unknown.
- **Mother:** not named or described in any surviving source.
- **Religion of household:** Tertullian's convert rhetoric—most famously “Christians are made, not born” (*Apologeticum* 18)—and his descriptions of former pagan life support, but do not prove in a modern biographical sense, a non-Christian upbringing.
- **Ethnicity:** modern descriptions as “Berber,” “Punic,” “Phoenician,” “African,” or “Roman” often exceed the evidence. He was culturally fluent in elite Latin letters and lived in Roman Africa. David E. Wilhite argues that his writing can express a resistant African identity under Roman rule; other scholars emphasize the hybridity of Roman-African elite culture. No source supplies a genealogical ethnic identity.
- **Siblings and extended kin:** none securely known.

### Marriage and household

Tertullian was married. *Ad uxorem* (“To His Wife”), two books addressed directly to an unnamed Christian wife, contemplates what she should do if he dies first. He advises her preferably not to remarry and, if she does, to marry only a Christian. This is the single clearest fact about his private life.

No source names his wife, dates the marriage, describes her family, or records her death. The treatise does not prove that he was already dying, that she became a widow, or that he himself later became widowed. No children are securely attested. Claims that he had children are conjectures, sometimes inferred from his discussions of baptism or family life, which are not autobiographical.

A first-person plural passage in *De resurrectione carnis* 59 has been read as an admission that Tertullian had committed adultery; Kyle Hughes, following modern scholarship, notes the possibility. The grammar and polemical register do not make it a secure personal confession. It should remain classified as a disputed autobiographical inference.

### Wealth and social position

No property records, inheritance documents, accounts, patronage contracts, or financial figures survive. His extensive education, access to books, capacity to write for publication, and command of elite rhetoric point toward a literate and probably privileged urban milieu. That does not establish senatorial, equestrian, or municipal-office status. Wilhite interprets *De cultu feminarum* and *De pallio* as evidence of a writer negotiating and sometimes rejecting markers of Roman elite identity.

## Childhood and Early Life

There is no ancient narrative of Tertullian's childhood. The conventional picture—born c. 155/160 to a pagan military household in Carthage, educated there, sent to Rome for law, and returned after a dramatic conversion—is a synthesis of Jerome, Eusebius, and nineteenth-century inference rather than a documented sequence.

The secure setting is late-second-century Carthage: capital of Africa Proconsularis, a major port, judicial center, and, after Rome, one of the largest and wealthiest cities in the Latin West. Its public life included temples, imperial cult, baths, theater, circus, amphitheater, schools of rhetoric, legal administration, commerce, and multilingual populations. Tertullian's writing assumes intimate knowledge of this environment.

North African Christianity is securely visible before his literary debut. Twelve Scillitan Christians were tried and executed at Carthage on 17 July 180. Their *Acta* are the earliest dated Latin Christian text. The *Passion of Perpetua and Felicity* records arrests and executions in Carthage in 203. These texts show a Christian population crossing status divisions, including slaves and the well-born, and a culture in which visions, martyrdom, baptism, and discipline carried exceptional weight.

No evidence establishes that Tertullian personally witnessed the Scillitan trial, knew Perpetua or Felicity, or converted because he saw a martyr die. His *Apologeticum* does argue that exemplary Christian endurance provokes inquiry and conversion, and later biographers converted that general argument into a personal conversion scene.

## Education and Formation

### What the writings demonstrate

Tertullian had an advanced literary education. His works display:

- trained Latin rhetoric: invective, irony, forensic arrangement, paradox, antithesis, apostrophe, and compressed epigram;
- detailed acquaintance with Roman religion, antiquarian learning, spectacles, military ritual, and public institutions;
- substantial knowledge of Roman legal vocabulary and forensic procedure;
- reading of Greek and Latin philosophy, especially Stoic categories, together with Plato, Aristotle, Epicureans, and medical or psychological theories;
- knowledge of Virgil, Cicero, Seneca, Varro, and other classical authors;
- access to Greek Christian apologetic and anti-heretical writing, especially Justin and Irenaeus;
- extensive use of Scripture. He cites nearly the whole emerging New Testament corpus and often appears to translate Greek biblical texts himself, while sometimes drawing on existing Latin versions.

### Lawyer or jurist?

Eusebius describes Tertullian in terms that later readers took to mean a distinguished expert in Roman law at Rome. The *Digest* preserves excerpts from a Roman jurist named Tertullianus. Earlier biographies identified the theologian with that jurist and built a Roman legal career around the identification.

Timothy Barnes and David Rankin dismantled the identification: the name was not unique; the chronology and career do not align securely; legal language was part of elite rhetorical education and public culture; and Tertullian's legal usages do not require professional juristic office. Modern positions range from “Christian sophist” or professional rhetorician, through an advocate with some legal training, to Eric Osborn's description of him as a philosopher. **No surviving pleading, client, appointment, juristic opinion, or official record proves a legal career.**

### Teachers and mentors

No teacher, school, or formal mentor is named. Justin Martyr and Irenaeus are literary predecessors, not demonstrable personal teachers. Tertullian explicitly names Irenaeus as a source in *Adversus Valentinianos* 5.1. His engagement with Stoicism is appropriative and adversarial rather than evidence of membership in a philosophical school.

### Rome and travel

No firm evidence places him in Rome or anywhere outside Carthage. His awareness of Roman controversies and his account in *Adversus Praxean* of a Roman bishop's dealings with the New Prophecy can derive from correspondence, documents, travelers, or church networks. A Roman education, legal practice, and return to Africa remain possible but unproved. Claims of journeys through Greece and Asia Minor are later reconstruction without direct documentation.

## Early Career and Conversion

### Before Christianity

Tertullian occasionally uses the language of having once shared pagan error, sexual sin, or delight in spectacles. Such passages fit conversion rhetoric and cannot be mined uncritically as a diary. There is no documented pre-Christian job, publication, criminal case, military service, or civic office.

### Conversion

He was Christian by the time of his earliest securely datable works. *Ad nationes* refers to Septimius Severus's victory over Clodius Albinus on 19 February 197, placing it after that date; the *Apologeticum* is normally assigned to later in 197. Conversion must precede these publications, commonly estimated c. 193–196. An exact day, church, baptizer, and motive are unknown.

The familiar explanation that martyrs' courage converted him is plausible as a psychological inference from *Apologeticum* 50, not recorded fact. His maxim “fiunt, non nascuntur Christiani” (“Christians are made, not born,” *Apologeticum* 18) describes Christian identity in general and is not a dated conversion notice.

### First publications, c. 196–198

The earliest cluster probably includes:

- *Ad martyras*, an exhortation to imprisoned Christians awaiting trial or death;
- *Ad nationes*, two books exposing what he presents as pagan inconsistency and false accusation;
- *Apologeticum*, addressed rhetorically to Roman provincial governors and demanding lawful examination of Christians;
- *De testimonio animae*, arguing that spontaneous human speech bears witness to one God and judgment.

These works established the principal traits of his public voice: forensic framing, aggressive reversal of accusations, an insistence on Christian loyalty to the emperor short of worship, and a theology of martyrdom as victory.

## Chronological Life History

### c. 150–170: Birth and formation

Probably born and educated in Carthage. Exact year, parentage, and educational itinerary are unknown. By adulthood he possessed elite rhetorical and bilingual literary skills.

### Before c. 196: Marriage and Christian conversion

He married an unnamed woman who was Christian by the composition of *Ad uxorem*. Whether the marriage preceded or followed his conversion is not known. He entered the Carthaginian Christian community before his first dated writings.

### c. 196–198: Apologist under Septimius Severus

In *Ad nationes* and *Apologeticum*, Tertullian objects that Christians are condemned for the name without investigation. He denies charges of incest and infant murder, attacks analogous or worse practices in Roman myth and society, distinguishes prayer for the emperor from sacrifice to him, and depicts Christianity as numerous throughout the empire.

The works are literary addresses, not surviving briefs filed in a known case. They do not prove that a governor formally received or answered them. They nonetheless document a Christian intellectual confronting Roman coercive power in the idiom of Roman public argument.

The celebrated ending of *Apologeticum* 50 reads in Latin: *Plures efficimur, quotiens metimur a vobis; semen est sanguis Christianorum*—approximately, “We become more numerous whenever we are mown down by you; the blood of Christians is seed.” “The blood of the martyrs is the seed of the Church” is an expanded later paraphrase.

### c. 198–203: Church discipline and anti-heretical method

Works commonly placed in this period address prayer, baptism, repentance, patience, spectacles, dress, marriage, and the authority of apostolic churches. In *De praescriptione haereticorum*, Tertullian uses the Roman legal concept of *praescriptio*: opponents classed as heretics have no standing to claim Scripture against churches that possess apostolic teaching and succession.

The approach was institutionally conservative. It also authorized exclusion: doctrinal opponents were not simply mistaken debate partners but people whose claim to the common documents could be denied in advance.

*De baptismo* is the earliest surviving Latin treatise devoted to baptism. It defends water baptism, describes preparation and associated rites, refuses a woman's right to teach or baptize, and recommends delaying baptism in some cases—especially for small children and the unmarried—because of the gravity of post-baptismal sin. This is important evidence that infant baptism was practiced or proposed in his environment, even though he opposed haste.

### 203 and the Perpetua circle

Perpetua, Felicity, and companions were executed at Carthage in 203. The surviving *Passio* shares Tertullian's interest in visions, the Spirit, martyrdom, and rigor. He was long proposed as its editor. Linguistic and textual scholarship no longer regards that attribution as established, and the work must be catalogued as anonymous. No personal meeting with the martyrs is documented.

### c. 204–208: Increasing allegiance to the New Prophecy

The New Prophecy began in Phrygia in the later second century around Montanus and the women prophets Prisca/Priscilla and Maximilla. It emphasized continuing prophetic speech, rigorous fasting and sexual discipline, and the authority of the Paraclete. Opponents challenged the prophets' ecstatic manner and authority; later heresiology turned a varied movement into a more uniform “Montanism.”

Tertullian increasingly cites or defends the New Prophecy. Lost works including *De ecstasi* treated prophetic ecstasy, while extant later works invoke the Paraclete and attack less rigorous Christians as *psychici* (“soulish” or “carnal” people). No text records a ceremony of joining, an excommunication, a new congregation, or a self-description equivalent to “I became a Montanist.”

### 207–208: The Marcion project

*Adversus Marcionem* survives in five books and is the largest extant work. Tertullian says it was his third version; an earlier version was stolen or improperly issued, and he rewrote it. An internal reference places an edition in the fifteenth year of Severus, c. 207–208. He attacks Marcion's distinction between the just creator God of the Hebrew Bible and the good Father proclaimed by Jesus, defends the unity of the two Testaments, and preserves extensive evidence for Marcion's Gospel and Pauline collection. Because Tertullian quotes an opponent's otherwise lost scriptural system, the work remains indispensable for reconstructing Marcionite Christianity, although every quotation is mediated through hostile controversy.

### c. 208–212: Anthropology, Christology, resurrection, discipline

In *De anima*, Tertullian surveys philosophical, medical, and Christian theories of the soul. He holds that the soul is corporeal in a refined Stoic sense, possesses form, and is propagated from parents—a position later called traducianism. He reports a Christian “sister” in the congregation who received visions during worship, including a vision of a soul's bodily form. The passage is direct evidence for female visionary authority in his community, though he uses her vision to confirm his own theory.

*De carne Christi* and *De resurrectione carnis* defend Christ's real human flesh and the bodily resurrection against docetic and Gnostic positions. The former contains the paradox later transformed into *credo quia absurdum*.

*De corona* discusses a Christian soldier who refused a ceremonial crown and was detained. Tertullian defends the provocation, attacks idolatrous military rites, and asks whether warfare is compatible with Christian allegiance at all. His corpus also acknowledges Christians serving in armies. Scholars consequently debate whether his position was an absolute pacifism, an objection primarily to idolatry and killing, or a sharpening rigorism.

*Ad Scapulam*, conventionally dated after the solar eclipse of 14 August 212, addresses Scapula, proconsul of Africa. It warns against persecution, cites disasters that befell persecutors, and states that it is a human and natural right to worship according to one's conviction: religion should be adopted voluntarily, not by force. The argument seeks freedom for Christians in a plural imperial setting; it does not amount to a developed modern constitutional program.

### After c. 212: Open conflict over discipline

The standard chronology places *De fuga in persecutione*, *Adversus Praxean*, *De virginibus velandis*, *De monogamia*, *De ieiunio*, and *De pudicitia* after c. 212; some older datings put *De pudicitia* as late as 222–223. Barnes compressed the extant corpus and placed the end of Tertullian's activity around 212.

In these works Tertullian:

- rejects flight or payment to avoid persecution;
- demands veiling of unmarried as well as married women;
- treats remarriage after a spouse's death as forbidden;
- defends intensified fasts associated with new prophecy;
- denies ecclesiastical power to absolve certain grave post-baptismal sexual sins;
- formulates the relationship of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit against Praxeas.

*De pudicitia* mocks an unnamed prelate's “peremptory edict” granting reconciliation after adultery and fornication. Tertullian calls him *pontifex maximus* and *episcopus episcoporum* (“bishop of bishops”). Since the nineteenth century many scholars have identified the target as Callistus, bishop of Rome (c. 217–222), partly by comparison with Hippolytus; others identify a Carthaginian bishop or regard the location as unresolved. The target's identity materially affects the work's date and whether it documents a direct Roman conflict.

### Last years and death

No surviving account describes Tertullian's last residence, health, office, reconciliation, trial, imprisonment, or death. Jerome says he “is reported to have lived to a decrepit age,” a formula that advertises hearsay. Barnes's suggestion that he died soon after c. 212 and perhaps as a martyr is possible but unsupported by a martyr act, cult, tomb, feast day, or contemporary testimony. Traditional death dates from c. 220 to c. 240 are estimates, not records.

## Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Carthaginian church

Tertullian's principal institution was the Christian community at Carthage. His works presuppose bishops, presbyters, deacons, widows, virgins, catechumens, baptized laity, confessors, martyrs, worship assemblies, fasting practices, discipline, and communication with churches elsewhere. The exact congregation, building, office, and chain of command cannot be recovered.

Jerome calls him a presbyter. Against this, Tertullian's late works speak as a layman and argue that laity possess priestly capacity when ordained clergy are absent. Possible resolutions include: Jerome was wrong; Tertullian was ordained only during part of his life; “lay” was rhetorical; or local offices did not map neatly onto later categories. No appointment or dismissal document survives.

### New Prophecy networks

Tertullian knew traditions attributed to Montanus, Prisca, and Maximilla and defended prophetic revelation. Whether he belonged to an organized African branch, an informal prophetic current within the Carthaginian church, or merely appropriated written oracles is contested. He names no Montanist bishop, clergy, church building, initiation, or financial organization.

### Publication as a project

His corpus implies copying and circulation networks. *Adversus Marcionem* explicitly refers to competing editions and unauthorized handling of a prior draft. Greek versions of *De baptismo*, *De spectaculis*, and *De virginibus velandis*, and apparently a Greek *Apologeticum*, show deliberate multilingual dissemination. No publisher, scribe, patron, sale price, royalty, or book-trade account is known.

### Businesses, investments, technology, and property

There is no evidence for businesses, investments, patents, inventions, estates, slaves owned by him, or financial relationships. His “technology” was literary and conceptual: adaptation of forensic rhetoric, creation or stabilization of Latin Christian terminology, and systematic polemical use of textual comparison.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Empire and civil loyalty

Tertullian rejects emperor worship but repeatedly presents Christians as politically loyal. In *Apologeticum* 30–33 he says Christians pray for the emperor's safety, armies, senate, people, and the stability of the world, while worshiping only God. He denies that Caesar is divine and insists that Christians cannot lie by calling him a god.

His rhetoric is not revolutionary in program: he does not organize rebellion or propose replacement government. It nevertheless creates a jurisdictional boundary—imperial authority cannot command worship or conscience. *Ad Scapulam* 2 contains one of antiquity's most cited arguments against religious compulsion.

### Persecution and martyrdom

Tertullian treats martyrdom as witness, combat, sacrifice, and victory. *Ad martyras* consoles prisoners; *Scorpiace* rejects evasion of confession; *De fuga* condemns flight from persecution and payments to authorities; *Apologeticum* argues that executions recruit rather than extinguish Christians.

The scale of empire-wide, continuous persecution depicted by Christian apologetic must be distinguished from evidence for episodic, locally administered proceedings. The Scillitan and Perpetua cases are historical anchors; a universal “edict of Septimius Severus” forbidding conversion is not preserved independently and remains disputed. Tertullian's refusal of flight could expose adherents to arrest; his opponents within Christianity evidently judged such rigor imprudent.

### Scripture, tradition, and “heresy”

He defends apostolic churches, the rule of faith, and the unity of Scripture against Marcionites, Valentinians, followers of Hermogenes and Apelles, and Praxeas. His knowledge of opponents is extensive but hostile. Descriptions of their sexual behavior, instability, secrecy, or absurdity are accusations in polemic, not neutral ethnography.

The irony of Tertullian's later reputation is real: an author who helped define and attack “heresy” was himself classified by later catholic writers as a Montanist heretic. Yet the institutional fact behind the label is unsettled. His trinitarian and Christological arguments remained usable in later orthodoxy even when his discipline and prophetic allegiance did not.

### Trinity and Christology

Theophilus of Antioch had earlier used Greek *trias*. Tertullian provides the earliest surviving major Latin deployment of *trinitas*. In *Adversus Praxean* 2 he describes Father, Son, and Holy Spirit as three *personae*, not divided in *substantia* but ordered within one divine reality or economy. The familiar formula “one substance, three persons” accurately summarizes his vocabulary but is not a freestanding creed written by him in precisely the later scholastic sense.

His account is pre-Nicene. It includes developmental and subordinating language that does not simply equal the fourth-century Nicene settlement. Nevertheless, *substantia*, *persona*, *trinitas*, *status*, *gradus*, and *oeconomia* became basic resources for later Latin theology.

He vigorously defends two realities in Christ—divine and human—and Christ's genuine birth, flesh, suffering, death, and resurrection. These formulations anticipated later debates but should not be retroactively treated as a complete Chalcedonian doctrine.

### Anthropology, sin, and the afterlife

- The soul is a real, subtle body rather than an incorporeal Platonic entity.
- Souls are transmitted through generation (traducianism), helping transmit a *vitium originis*, a defect originating in Adam. Augustine later developed a more systematic doctrine of original sin; Tertullian is a predecessor, not its final architect.
- The dead normally await final resurrection in Hades; martyrs receive privileged access to paradise in his lost *De paradiso* as cited in *De anima*.
- He defended bodily resurrection and a millennial hope in the lost *De spe fidelium*.
- He accepts demons, visions, prophecy, exorcism, and the Enochic story of fallen angels as real explanatory categories.

### Sacraments and church practice

Tertullian provides early Latin vocabulary for baptism, Eucharist, confession, penance, anointing, laying on of hands, fasting, prayer, and ecclesial discipline. He uses *sacramentum*, a Latin term with military and legal resonances, in ways important to later sacramental language. His Eucharistic language includes “figure” (*figura*) but also realist and devotional expressions; later confessional disputes selectively enlist him on opposing sides.

### Philosophy: rejection and appropriation

“What indeed has Athens to do with Jerusalem?” (*De praescriptione* 7) attacks philosophical systems as sources of Christian heresy. It is not evidence that Tertullian abandoned reason. His arguments use Stoic ontology, rhetorical probability, dialectic, natural-law reasoning, and classical exempla. Robert D. Sider showed that the alleged irrationalist *credo quia absurdum* distorts a contextual argument resembling Aristotelian reasoning from improbability.

### Sexual ethics and remarriage

Tertullian's trajectory moves toward increasingly restrictive practice. *Ad uxorem* still permits a Christian widow to remarry another Christian while discouraging it. *De exhortatione castitatis* and *De monogamia* oppose second marriage, with the latter treating it as a form of adultery or fornication. *De pudicitia* denies church absolution for adultery and fornication after baptism, against a bishop willing to reconcile penitents. The movement is from severe counsel to claimed obligation, justified through the Paraclete's progressive discipline.

### Women

The adverse evidence is explicit:

- *De cultu feminarum* 1.1 addresses women collectively as “the devil's gateway,” associates them with Eve's transgression, and uses inherited blame to demand plain dress and rejection of cosmetics and jewelry.
- *De baptismo* 17 denies women authority to teach and baptize, attacking a text involving “Thecla” used to authorize female practice.
- *De virginibus velandis* demands veiling for unmarried women.
- His sexual discipline places heavy restrictions on widows and remarriage.

Countervailing evidence does not erase those texts but complicates a single label. He praises an ideal Christian marriage as shared prayer, fasting, instruction, Eucharist, and mutual support (*Ad uxorem* 2.8). He accepts the authority of Prisca and Maximilla's prophetic utterances and reports a female visionary speaking in church (*De anima* 9). Modern studies divide between describing a systematic misogyny and reading the invective as ascetic rhetoric aimed at luxury and pagan status-display. Both the gendered blame and its polemical-ascetic context are documentable.

The phrase “a temple built over a sewer,” often attributed to Tertullian in modern discussions, is not securely located in his extant corpus and should not be quoted as his without a primary reference.

### Jews and Judaism

*Adversus Iudaeos* argues that the law's observances were temporary, that Gentiles inherit the promises, and that Jesus fulfills Hebrew prophecy. Chapters 9–14 substantially overlap *Adversus Marcionem* III; scholars dispute whether the work is unfinished, composite, completed from Tertullian's notes by another hand, or an integral Tertullianic composition. Geoffrey Dunn has defended its rhetorical unity, while critical editions have often doubted the second half.

Tertullian knew some contemporary Jewish practices and lived in a city with a Jewish population, but the staged dispute at the work's opening is not proof of an actual recorded debate. His supersessionist claims and hostile collective rhetoric belong to the formation of Latin Christian anti-Judaism and were available to later traditions. They should not be converted into an undocumented account of personal violence or state action against Jews.

### Spectacle and punitive imagination

*De spectaculis* prohibits Christian attendance at circus, theater, athletic contests, and amphitheater because of idolatrous origins and the passions they excite. Chapter 30 reverses Roman spectacle: Tertullian imagines the Last Judgment as the supreme spectacle in which persecuting rulers, philosophers, actors, charioteers, wrestlers, and enemies of Christ suffer in fire. The passage is future eschatological fantasy, not evidence that he carried out violence, but it is one of the starkest examples of retaliatory imagination in early Christian polemic.

| Unnamed “bishop of bishops” | Opponent over absolution | Traditionally Callistus I of Rome; alternatives include a Carthaginian bishop. Identity unresolved. |
| Roman clergy | Alleged enemies | Jerome says their envy and insults pushed Tertullian to Montanism. This is late, possibly Jerome's projection, and not corroborated. |
| Cyprian of Carthage | Major posthumous reader | Jerome transmits a chain of oral testimony that Cyprian asked daily for Tertullian by saying “Give me the master.” Cyprian never names Tertullian in surviving works, but extensive influence is widely recognized. |
| Perpetua and Felicity | Near-contemporary Carthaginian martyrs | Shared setting and themes; no secure meeting or authorship link. |

No named patron, employer, collaborator, student, child, business associate, or political ally is securely recoverable.

## Controversies, Accusations, and Potential Harms

### Status key

- **Documented:** present in Tertullian's text or a securely identified ancient witness.
- **Later testimony:** ancient but substantially later and uncorroborated.
- **Scholarly inference:** reasoned reconstruction, not direct record.
- **Disputed:** significant evidence or scholarly positions conflict.
- **Legend/myth:** circulating story that lacks adequate evidence as literal biography.

### Montanist “heresy”

- **Documented:** Tertullian defended ongoing prophecy, cited the Phrygian prophets, used “Paraclete” language, and embraced rigorous fasting, monogamy, and discipline.
- **Later classification:** Jerome says he “lapsed to Montanus”; Augustine calls the Tertullianists heretics and says Tertullian passed to the Cataphrygians, later separated, and propagated his own congregations.
- **Disputed:** whether he formally left or was expelled from the catholic church. Christine Trevett calls him a “Montanist catholic.” William Tabbernee and David Wilhite argue that there is no evidence of schism in Tertullian's own time. The label “heretic” is secure as a fact of later reception, not as a documented contemporary verdict delivered to him.

### Rigorism and exposure to persecution

*De fuga in persecutione* treats flight as resistance to God's will and condemns buying safety. *De corona* defends a soldier whose public refusal attracted arrest. These were disputed ethical prescriptions within Christianity and could increase adherents' exposure to punishment. There is no evidence that Tertullian coerced a particular person, controlled a prison group, or engineered an arrest.

### Sexual discipline and exclusion

His late rejection of remarriage and absolution for grave sexual sins attacks Christians he calls *psychici*. The controversy concerned who could be restored to communion, by whose authority, and after what penance. Tertullian's position was rejected by emerging catholic practice. No secular lawsuit or criminal proceeding resulted.

### Gendered invective

The “devil's gateway” passage is genuine and collective. Arguments that it is merely anti-luxury rhetoric do not make its attribution of Eve's guilt to women disappear. Conversely, the unsupported “temple over a sewer” quotation should not be added to the case as though genuine. His acceptance of women prophets coexists with restrictions on women teaching, baptizing, appearing unveiled, and displaying wealth.

### Anti-Jewish rhetoric

His writings advance supersession, collective displacement, and polemical representations of Jewish failure to recognize Christ. These are documented theological claims with a long reception history. An actual public debate, personal Jewish enemy, or violent act by Tertullian is not documented.

### Claims about pagan crime and Christian innocence

The apologetic works answer accusations of Christian cannibalism and incest by accusing Roman religion and society of sacrifice, sexual disorder, exposure of infants, and consumption of blood. These are forensic and satirical reversals using mythology, reported customs, and selective examples. They are evidence of polemical strategy, not a statistical survey of Roman behavior.

### Violence, crime, corruption, and finance

No credible evidence connects Tertullian personally with homicide, assault, political conspiracy, espionage, corruption, fraud, money laundering, theft, abuse of office, prosecution, conviction, imprisonment, or financial scandal. He lived under a coercive imperial system and wrote about arrest, torture, execution, military service, and eschatological punishment, but no personal criminal file survives.

### Health and psychology

No illness, disability, addiction, accident, or diagnosed psychological crisis is documented. In *De patientia* he calls himself “most wretched” and “sick with the fever of impatience”; this is moral self-deprecation and authorial posture, not a clinical record. Jerome's “keen and vehement” characterization and modern descriptions of a violent temperament are literary judgments based on polemical style.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. “The blood of the martyrs is the seed of the Church”

1. **Story:** Tertullian coined the familiar sentence.
2. **Appearance and circulation:** It derives from *Apologeticum* 50.13 (197), but the Latin says, “the blood of Christians is seed.” Later Christian quotation expanded “Christians” to “martyrs” and supplied “of the Church.”
3. **Evidence:** The underlying thought and compact seed metaphor are authentic; the common wording is not verbatim.
4. **Reputation:** It became one of Christianity's central slogans about persecution producing growth, often detached from Tertullian's forensic argument and his more complex acknowledgment that people do not desire suffering as such.

### 2. *Credo quia absurdum*—“I believe because it is absurd”

1. **Story:** Tertullian confessed that Christian faith is true because it is irrational.
2. **Appearance and circulation:** *De carne Christi* 5 actually says *credibile est, quia ineptum est* and *certum est, quia impossibile* in a paradoxical anti-Marcionite argument about the scandalous birth, death, and resurrection of Christ. Peter Harrison traces a seventeenth-century “I believe because it is impossible” version and Voltaire's eighteenth-century addition of “absurd.”
3. **Evidence:** Tertullian never wrote the standard maxim. Robert Sider relates the original argument to rhetorical reasoning, including Aristotle's argument that an extremely improbable report may be credible precisely because no inventor would choose it.
4. **Reputation:** The misquotation made Tertullian an emblem of fideism for Enlightenment critics, theologians, philosophers, Freud, and popular dictionaries. Its usefulness in disputes over faith and reason preserved it despite repeated correction.

### 3. The Roman lawyer and jurist

1. **Story:** Tertullian studied and practiced law at Rome and is the jurist Tertullianus quoted in Justinian's *Digest*.
2. **Appearance and circulation:** Developed from Eusebius's description, Tertullian's legal style, and the shared name; standard in older patrologies.
3. **Evidence:** No professional record links them. Barnes and Rankin regard the identification as disproved or highly improbable. Legal-rhetorical education remains likely.
4. **Reputation:** It explains his theology as “legalistic” and supplies a neat career-before-conversion story, but risks circularly reading every forensic metaphor as autobiography.

### 4. Son of a proconsular centurion

1. **Story:** His father was a Roman centurion serving the African proconsul.
2. **Appearance and circulation:** Jerome, *De viris illustribus* 53.
3. **Evidence:** Late testimony; the exact rank is otherwise unattested and may rest on textual corruption. Rives and some others preserve the possibility.
4. **Reputation:** The story supports portraits of Tertullian as Roman, military, affluent, or disciplined and has encouraged unsupported ethnic narratives about the father.

### 5. Priest driven into heresy by jealous Roman clergy

1. **Story:** Tertullian was a Catholic priest until Roman clerical envy and abuse caused his Montanist apostasy.
2. **Appearance and circulation:** Jerome, c. 392–393.
3. **Evidence:** Tertullian's late self-positioning as lay and the lack of any Roman episode weigh against it. Frend summarizes Barnes's proposal that Jerome projected his own Roman clerical conflicts of 382–385 backward.
4. **Reputation:** It created a psychological tragedy—brilliant orthodox priest becomes embittered schismatic—that structures many devotional biographies.

### 6. Founder of a separate Tertullianist sect

1. **Story:** After joining the Montanists, Tertullian quarreled with them and founded his own sect, whose Carthaginian basilica survived until Augustine.
2. **Appearance and circulation:** Augustine, *De haeresibus* 86, c. 428; repeated by *Praedestinatus* and later heresiology.
3. **Evidence:** Augustine personally knew of people called Tertullianists and says their last members transferred a basilica to the Catholics. That establishes a late-fourth/early-fifth-century group, not when or how it began. Douglas Powell concludes that the date of formal schism is unknowable; “Tertullianists” may have been an African name for Cataphrygians/Montanists.
4. **Reputation:** It secured Tertullian's place in heresy catalogues and helps explain why he is revered as a Father but not a saint.

### 7. Editor of the *Passion of Perpetua and Felicity*

1. **Story:** Tertullian edited Perpetua's prison diary, Saturus's vision, and a narrator's martyr account into the surviving *Passio*.
2. **Appearance and circulation:** Proposed in modern scholarship because of Carthaginian setting, date, vocabulary, visionary interest, and affinity with the New Prophecy.
3. **Evidence:** No manuscript names him. Stylistic and theological parallels are insufficient, and current scholarship generally treats the editor as anonymous.
4. **Reputation:** The attribution places Tertullian at the center of the best-known early Christian woman's text and can misleadingly make Perpetua's voice dependent on him.

### 8. Inventor of the Trinity

1. **Story:** Tertullian invented the doctrine and the exact formula “one substance, three persons.”
2. **Appearance and circulation:** Modern summaries of *Adversus Praxean* and his use of *trinitas*, *substantia*, and *persona*.
3. **Evidence:** Triadic Christian language and Greek *trias* predate him. His Latin synthesis is exceptionally early and influential, but pre-Nicene and not identical in every respect with later creeds.
4. **Reputation:** Both trinitarian apologetics and anti-trinitarian polemics use him as an origin point, sometimes anachronistically.

### 9. Berber or Phoenician ancestry

1. **Story:** Tertullian was specifically ethnically Berber, Punic, or Phoenician.
2. **Appearance and circulation:** Modern African, colonial, nationalist, and popular biographies.
3. **Evidence:** Carthage and African identity are secure contexts; genealogy is not. Roman Africa contained layered identities, and a Latin name does not settle ancestry.
4. **Reputation:** Competing appropriations make him either a Roman civilizational figure or a colonized African voice. Wilhite's argument concerns social identity expressed in texts, not a genetic pedigree.

### 10. Martyrdom

1. **Story:** Tertullian ultimately died a martyr.
2. **Appearance and circulation:** A modern hypothesis associated especially with Barnes's compressed chronology.
3. **Evidence:** No martyr act, ancient cult, feast, relic, or explicit witness. Jerome instead reports extreme old age.
4. **Reputation:** It reconciles his advocacy of martyrdom with an exemplary death but must remain speculation.

### 11. Portraits of Tertullian

Renaissance and early-modern engravings, including André Thevet's 1584 portrait, are imaginative reception images produced more than a millennium after his death. No contemporary likeness, physical description, tomb image, or securely identified ancient portrait survives.

### Spurious, disputed, and formerly attributed works

- **Pseudo-Tertullian, *Adversus omnes haereses*:** a short heresy catalogue appended to *De praescriptione* in the Cluny tradition and early editions. Modern editors reject Tertullianic authorship; probably composed at Rome from a Greek heresiological source related to Hippolytus.
- ***De execrandis gentium diis*:** fragment in Vaticanus Latinus 3852. Traditionally dated much later and rejected; Marie Turcan and Anne-Marie Turcan-Verkerk argue it may preserve excerpts from Tertullian's lost *De superstitione saeculi*. Unresolved minority proposal.
- ***Carmen adversus Marcionitas*:** 1,302 Latin hexameters in five books, probably fifth century (proposals vary); certainly not Tertullian, though it uses his anti-Marcionite work.
- ***Passio sanctarum Perpetuae et Felicitatis*:** near-contemporary, Carthaginian, and once attributed editorially to Tertullian; anonymous and normally edited separately.
- **Poems *Genesis*, *De Sodoma*, *De Iona propheta/De Nineva*, *Ad senatorem*, *De iudicio Domini*:** fourth- or later-century Pseudo-Cyprianic/Cyprianus Gallus material, not Tertullian.
- **Novatian, *De trinitate* and *De cibis Iudaicis*:** transmitted under Tertullian's name in some manuscripts or early editions; now assigned to Novatian.
- **Thecla material:** Tertullian attacks a writing circulating under Paul's name that authorized women to teach and baptize (*De baptismo* 17). This is evidence for the reception of the *Acts of Paul and Thecla*, not a work by Tertullian.

### Manuscript transmission

Five major collection families are conventionally named Cluniacensis, Corbeiensis, Trecensis, Agobardinus, and Ottobonianus. The earliest and most important surviving codex is the ninth-century **Codex Agobardinus**, Paris, Bibliothèque nationale de France, Latin 1622. Its damaged final portion is lost, but its table of contents records works no longer extant, including *De spe fidelium*, *De paradiso*, and *De superstitione saeculi*. It preserves the sole manuscript witness to *Ad nationes*. The Corbie manuscripts are lost, although their readings survive through early printed editions.

## Death, Burial, Relics, and Cult

The date, place, cause, and manner of death are unknown. No reliable source records illness, execution, suicide, accident, or natural cause. Carthage is the presumed place because it is his last secure setting.

There is no securely identified grave, epitaph, body, relic, shrine, feast day, or ancient cult. He is commonly called a Church Father because of antiquity and influence, but not “Saint Tertullian” in official Catholic or Orthodox calendars. The traditional explanation is his later association with a condemned movement and absence of a recorded reconciliation. That explanation belongs to reception history; no deathbed statement survives.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Third and fourth centuries

Cyprian's theology, ecclesial language, biblical Latin, and style show extensive Tertullianic influence. Jerome's anecdote—transmitted through an old man named Paul of Concordia, who had known Cyprian's former secretary—says Cyprian daily requested Tertullian with “Da magistrum,” “Give me the master.” The chain is late and oral, but it captures genuine literary dependence.

Later Latin theology absorbed Tertullian's vocabulary while rejecting or ignoring his New-Prophecy rigor. Novatian's *De trinitate* drew so heavily on him that Jerome described it as an epitome. Augustine engaged ideas of soul, sin, church, and heresy in a North African tradition shaped by Tertullian, while reporting the disappearance of the Tertullianist congregation.

### Medieval survival

His corpus survived unevenly in a small number of manuscript collections. Some works disappeared after being listed in the ninth-century Codex Agobardinus. Medieval readers knew selected treatises, excerpts, and misattributed works rather than a stable modern canon. His difficult, compressed Latin both attracted learned readers and limited broad circulation compared with Augustine.

### Renaissance and Reformation

The 1521 Beatus Rhenanus edition gave the corpus a major printed form. Catholic and Protestant controversialists mined Tertullian for early evidence about Scripture, tradition, apostolic succession, baptism, Eucharist, penance, clerical power, and Roman primacy. Because his positions changed or contain tensions, opposing confessions could cite him selectively.

### Enlightenment and modern philosophy

The altered *credo quia absurdum* turned him into a symbol of faith against reason. Voltaire's formulation and later repetition by philosophers and psychologists often eclipsed his actual argumentation. Modern patristic scholarship has reversed this portrait by emphasizing his rhetorical and philosophical sophistication.

### Modern theology and law

- Trinitarian history continues to treat *Adversus Praxean* as a foundational Latin text.
- Histories of religious freedom cite *Apologeticum* 24 and *Ad Scapulam* 2 for *libertas religionis*, natural right, and opposition to compelled worship.
- Pacifist and just-war traditions dispute the meaning of *De corona* and *De idololatria*.
- Feminist scholarship treats *De cultu feminarum*, *De virginibus velandis*, and his response to women prophets as a central archive for gender and authority.
- Jewish-Christian studies examine *Adversus Iudaeos* and related passages as evidence for both lived contact and literary construction of Jews during Christian identity formation.
- Postcolonial and African Christian scholarship debates whether he should be read chiefly as Romanized elite, African resistor, or a hybrid subject.
- Pentecostal and charismatic histories revisit his New-Prophecy allegiance as evidence for prophecy and visions in early Latin Christianity.

### The durable paradox of reception

Tertullian supplied language later central to catholic orthodoxy while later catalogues placed him outside that orthodoxy. His works remained authoritative enough to copy, cite, and imitate, but his person did not acquire a saint's cult. That double reception—master and heretic, institutional traditionalist and prophetic rigorist—is historical evidence, not a contradiction that can be resolved by choosing only one half.

## Chronology

|---|---|
| c. 150–170 | Probable birth range; Carthage is the traditional and most likely birthplace. |
| 17 July 180 | Scillitan Christians executed at Carthage; earliest dated Latin Christian document and background to Tertullian's church. No personal connection proved. |
| before c. 196 | Receives advanced rhetorical education; marries; converts to Christianity. Exact order and dates unknown. |
| c. 196–197 | Probable *Ad martyras*. |
| after 19 Feb. 197 | *Ad nationes* refers to Severus's victory over Albinus. |
| later 197 | Conventional date of *Apologeticum*; “the blood of Christians is seed.” |
| c. 197–198 | *De testimonio animae*. |
| c. 198–203 | Conventional early cluster: *De praescriptione*, *De spectaculis*, *De baptismo*, *De oratione*, *De patientia*, *De paenitentia*, *De cultu feminarum*, *Ad uxorem*, possibly *Adversus Iudaeos* and *Adversus Hermogenem*. Order disputed. |
| 203 | Perpetua, Felicity, and companions executed at Carthage. Tertullian's editorship of their *Passio* is unproved. |
| c. 204 onward | Increasingly visible support for the New Prophecy; exact beginning and institutional status unknown. |
| c. 207–208 | Dated edition of *Adversus Marcionem* under Severus; surviving five-book project developed across multiple editions. |
| c. 208–212 | Likely composition of major anti-Gnostic, anthropological, Christological, resurrection, and disciplinary works. |
| c. 211 | Conventional date of *De corona*. |
| after 14 Aug. 212 | Conventional date of *Ad Scapulam*, based on eclipse reference. |
| c. 212–219 | Standard chronology places *De fuga*, *Adversus Praxean*, *De virginibus velandis*, *De monogamia*, and *De ieiunio* here. Barnes dates the end of the extant corpus earlier. |
| c. 212–223 | Possible range for *De pudicitia*; depends partly on whether its “bishop of bishops” was Callistus of Rome. |
| after c. 212/220 | Literary record ends. Date and circumstances of death unknown. |
| c. 250s | Cyprian's writings show strong Tertullianic influence; later “Give me the master” anecdote attached to this reception. |
| c. 325 | Eusebius's *Church History* preserves early external notices and Greek Tertullian material. |
| c. 392–393 | Jerome's *De viris illustribus* 53 creates the standard biographical narrative: Carthage, centurion father, presbyter, Roman clerical quarrel, Montanism, old age. |
| late 4th/early 5th c. | Augustine observes the final Carthaginian Tertullianists enter Catholic communion and surrender their basilica. |
| c. 428 | Augustine records the event and sectarian tradition in *De haeresibus* 86. |
| 9th c. | Codex Agobardinus copied, preserving unique text and titles of works subsequently lost. |
| 1521 | Beatus Rhenanus publishes major Basel edition; early-modern recovery accelerates. |
| 1844 | Migne's *Patrologia Latina* collection begins publication, including authentic and spurious works. |
| 1954 | Tertullian's collected works appear in *Corpus Christianorum Series Latina* 1–2. |
| 1971/1985 | Barnes publishes and revises the critical historical-literary reconstruction that transforms modern biography and chronology. |
| 2007 | Wilhite's *Tertullian the African* reframes identity in Roman-African and postcolonial terms. |
| 2017 | Harrison documents the early-modern and Enlightenment invention of *credo quia absurdum*. |
| 2026 | Binder's study revisits Tertullian and Jews in early Christian literary imagination. |
