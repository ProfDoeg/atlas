## Works by Dido

### Foundational projects

- **Traditionally 814/813 BC:** *Carthage (“New City”)*. Dido/Elissa’s defining obra is the Phoenician settlement she founded in Libya; this is legendary rather than securely historical, since archaeology has not yet established occupation as early as the traditional date. Timaeus is the earliest surviving witness, while Justin preserves the fullest foundation narrative ([Oxford Classical Dictionary, “Dido”](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548725644); [Oxford Classical Dictionary, “Carthage”](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548508399); [Timaeus, FGrHist 566 F 82](https://www.uvm.edu/~jbailly/courses/CLAS24TrojanWar/virgilBackground.html)).

- **Traditionally 814/813 BC:** *The Tyrian migration to Libya*. This was a collective political project organized by Elissa after her husband’s murder: she recruited disaffected Tyrian nobles, obtained ships and treasure, stopped in Cyprus, and established a new community in Africa. It resists classification as a discrete work because it was simultaneously a conspiracy, journey, refugee community, and colonial foundation ([Justin, *Epitome* 18.4–5](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/the-founding-of-carthage/)).

- **Traditionally 814/813 BC:** *The feigned transfer to Pygmalion*. In Justin’s account Elissa pretended that she wished to move into her brother’s household, thereby obtaining royal servants and ships for her escape; it is an authored stratagem rather than a material object ([Justin, *Epitome* 18.4.9–15](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/the-founding-of-carthage/)).

- **Traditionally 814/813 BC:** *The sandbag-and-treasure ruse*. Elissa had bags filled with sand and cast into the sea as though sacrificing her murdered husband’s riches, then used the participants’ apparent complicity to persuade them to share her exile. This is best classified as a staged political fiction: a performance that acted upon its audience rather than a “work” in the ordinary sense ([Justin, *Epitome* 18.4.15–18](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/the-founding-of-carthage/)).

- **Traditionally 814/813 BC:** *The Byrsa land bargain and oxhide enclosure*. Dido purchased—or requested—the land that an oxhide could encompass, cut the hide into narrow strips, and enclosed the hill that became Carthage’s citadel. The enclosure is an ingenious spatial protocol and foundation myth, not an archaeologically attributable construction; its Greek etymology and widespread folktale parallels make its Punic historicity doubtful ([Justin, *Epitome* 18.5](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/the-founding-of-carthage/); [Scheid and Svenbro, “Byrsa: la ruse d’Élissa et la fondation de Carthage”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/annales-histoire-sciences-sociales/article/abs/byrsa-la-ruse-delissa-et-la-fondation-de-carthage/86B980EB5BD81630C58E434B8B4948E3)).

- **Traditionally 814/813 BC:** *Byrsa, the citadel*. Ancient tradition treats the oxhide enclosure as the nucleus of the city and, once Carthage expanded, as its citadel. It may therefore be counted as Dido’s built obra only within the legend; neither the surviving fabric nor the historical founding can be assigned personally to her ([Justin, *Epitome* 18.5](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/the-founding-of-carthage/); [Smith, *Dictionary of Greek and Roman Geography*, “Carthago”](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.04.0064%3Aentry%3Dcarthago-geo)).

### Buildings in Virgil’s Carthage

- **Poetic chronology, traditionally c. 1182–1180 BC:** *The walls and rising city of Carthage*. Virgil makes Dido the royal author of an urban Gesamtkunstwerk—walls, gates, streets, houses, harbour works, theatre, civic spaces, and institutions—although the physical labour belongs collectively to the Tyrian settlers. Dido later calls the city and walls her accomplishment; the Aeneas-era date is Virgil’s deliberate folding together of chronologically incompatible legends ([Virgil, *Aeneid* 1.418–440, 4.655](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.02.0054%3Abook%3D1); [Oxford Classical Dictionary, “Dido”](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548725644)).

- **Poetic chronology, traditionally c. 1182–1180 BC:** *Temple of Juno*. Virgil specifically says that Sidonian Dido was founding or building this immense, richly ornamented temple; its Trojan-War images turn architecture, cult, and pictorial narrative into a single work. It belongs to the fictional Carthage of the *Aeneid*, not to an identifiable excavated sanctuary commissioned by a historical queen ([Virgil, *Aeneid* 1.441–493](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.02.0054%3Abook%3D1)).

### Political and legal creations

- **Poetic chronology, traditionally c. 1182–1180 BC:** *The laws, decrees, and allocation of civic labour*. In Virgil, Dido gives laws and judgments, apportions work, and assigns responsibilities by decision or lot. These are protocols and performative governmental texts rather than literary works; no wording survives independently of Virgil’s representation ([Virgil, *Aeneid* 1.494–508](https://www.aeneid.co/text/book-1/494-508)).

- **Poetic chronology, traditionally c. 1182–1180 BC:** *Carthage’s civic order*. The settlers establish magistracies and a sacred senate while Dido directs the emerging kingdom. Virgil leaves the division of authorship deliberately collective, so the institutions belong to her foundation project but cannot safely be catalogued as her sole creations ([Virgil, *Aeneid* 1.418–440](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.02.0054%3Abook%3D1)).

### Vows, ritual projects, and performative speech

- **Legendary reign, undated:** *Vow of fidelity to her murdered husband*. The pre-Virgilian tradition defines Dido’s last project as preserving fidelity to Acherbas rather than marrying the Libyan king; Virgil recasts this as her vow never to remarry after Sychaeus. It is a binding speech-act rather than a surviving text, and its precise formulation is not recoverable ([Timaeus, FGrHist 566 F 82](https://www.uvm.edu/~jbailly/courses/CLAS24TrojanWar/virgilBackground.html); [Justin, *Epitome* 18.6](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/the-founding-of-carthage/); [Virgil, *Aeneid* 4](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.02.0054%3Abook%3D4)).

- **Legendary reign, undated:** *The ceremonial pyre*. In Timaeus and Justin, Dido constructs a pyre under the pretext of rites that would release her from her former vows, then uses it for her suicide rather than marry the Libyan ruler. It is at once architecture, ritual apparatus, deceptive project, and final political manifesto ([Timaeus, FGrHist 566 F 82](https://www.uvm.edu/~jbailly/courses/CLAS24TrojanWar/virgilBackground.html); [Justin, *Epitome* 18.6](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/the-founding-of-carthage/)).

- **Poetic chronology, traditionally c. 1182–1180 BC:** *The pyre for Aeneas’s effects*. Virgil substantially remakes the older pyre: Dido devises it, supplies a deceptive ritual explanation, and orders Anna to build it with Aeneas’s weapons, clothing, and bed, but Anna’s hands perform the construction. Its authorship is therefore divided between Dido’s project and Anna’s material execution ([Virgil, *Aeneid* 4.474–521](https://reader.humanitext.ai/en/read/urn%3Acts%3AlatinLit%3Aphi0690.phi003.humanitext-lat2%3A4.472-4.550)).

- **Poetic chronology, traditionally c. 1182–1180 BC:** *Rites at the pyre*. Dido designs and begins a composite ceremony of altars, offerings, invocations, effigies, and objects associated with Aeneas. It masquerades as a magical protocol for ending or reversing love but becomes preparation for suicide; Virgil does not present it as an independently transmitted liturgical text ([Virgil, *Aeneid* 4.504–521, 4.630–640](https://www.theoi.com/Text/VirgilAeneid4.html)).

- **Poetic chronology, traditionally c. 1182–1180 BC:** *Curse upon Aeneas and his descendants*. This is Dido’s clearest manifesto: a spoken act demanding perpetual enmity between her people and Aeneas’s, including the advent of an avenger, and functioning etiologically as the poem’s prefiguration of the Punic Wars. It exists only as Virgil’s poetry spoken through Dido, not as a historical Carthaginian document ([Virgil, *Aeneid* 4.584–629](https://www.theoi.com/Text/VirgilAeneid4.html); [Oxford Classical Dictionary, “Dido”](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548725644)).
