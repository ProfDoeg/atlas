## Works by Martin Luther

### Academic lectures and biblical expositions

- **1513–1515:** *Dictata super Psalterium* (“First Lectures on the Psalms”). A university lecture-cycle surviving in Luther’s manuscript and student notes; its status lies between authored commentary, teaching performance, and documentary residue (WA 3–4).
- **1515–1516:** *Lectures on Romans*. A university exposition reconstructed from Luther’s scholia and glosses rather than issued by him as a finished book (WA 56).
- **1516–1517:** *Lectures on Galatians*. The first of Luther’s two major Galatians lecture-cycles; the published 1519 commentary is a subsequent reworking (WA 57/2).
- **1517–1518:** *Lectures on Hebrews*. A lecture-course preserved principally through student transcriptions and therefore not wholly an author-controlled text (WA 57/3).
- **1518:** *Explanation of the Ninety-Five Theses*. A sustained Latin defense and interpretation of the propositions on indulgences (WA 1).
- **1519:** *Commentary on Galatians*. A revised, printed manifestation of the 1516–1517 lectures rather than a simple transcript (WA 2).
- **1519–1521:** *Operationes in Psalmos*. An unfinished second Psalms commentary, ending with Psalm 22; incompleteness is part of the work’s historical form (WA 5).
- **1523–1524:** *Lectures on Deuteronomy*. An academic exposition subsequently printed from the lecture material (WA 14).
- **1524–1526:** *Lectures and sermons on the Minor Prophets*. A series of teaching and preaching performances, preserved in editorially mediated records rather than one unitary book (WA 13; 20; 23).
- **1527–1530:** *Lectures on 1 John*. An exposition transmitted through notes taken by hearers (WA 20).
- **1527–1530:** *Lectures on Titus and Philemon*. University lectures surviving through student records and later editorial shaping (WA 25).
- **1528–1530:** *Lectures on Isaiah*. An extended lecture-cycle whose printed form was prepared with assistance from Luther’s colleagues (WA 25; 31/2).
- **1529–1530:** *Lectures on 1 Timothy*. A teaching-performance known from notes rather than an autograph finished treatise (WA 26).
- **1530–1531:** *Lectures on the Song of Songs*. A biblical exposition whose received text depends on auditors’ records (WA 31/2).
- **1532:** *Annotations on Ecclesiastes*. Notes from Luther’s lectures, edited for publication by others; authorship of the teaching is Luther’s, but the textual object is collaborative (WA 20).
- **1532–1535:** *Lectures on Psalms 2, 45, 51, 90, 101, 110–118 and other selected psalms*. Distinct lecture and sermon cycles rather than a single continuous commentary (WA 31/1; 40/2).
- **1531/1535:** *Lectures on Galatians*. Luther’s second Galatians course, delivered in 1531 and published from student notes in 1535; the published commentary is consequently a collaborative manifestation (WA 40/1–2).
- **1535–1545:** *Lectures on Genesis*. Luther’s last great lecture-project, extending across a decade; the received text was assembled and published from notes by Veit Dietrich, Caspar Cruciger, and others, so its exact verbal fidelity remains disputed (WA 42–44).
- **1537–1540:** *Sermons and annotations on Matthew 18–24*. A sequence of expositions preserved in notes and much later printed forms, resisting classification as a single author-finished work (WA 47; 52).
- **1543:** *On the Last Words of David*. An anti-Jewish exposition of 2 Samuel 23:1–7, presented as philological theology (WA 54).

### Disputations, theses, and academic manifestos

- **1516:** *Disputation against Scholastic Theology*. Ninety-seven academic theses designed for debate; a manifesto-like intervention rather than a descriptive treatise (WA 1).
- **1517:** *Disputation on the Power and Efficacy of Indulgences* (“Ninety-Five Theses”). A Latin disputation text that became a public manifesto through printing and circulation; the traditional story that Luther nailed it to the Wittenberg church door is disputed (WA 1).
- **1518:** *Heidelberg Disputation*. Twenty-eight theological and twelve philosophical theses, together with their proofs, made to govern an academic disputation (WA 1).
- **1519:** *Theses for the Leipzig Disputation*. Propositions prepared for Luther’s debate with Johann Eck; they functioned as a protocol for oral contest as well as a printed intervention (WA 2).
- **1520:** *The Fourteen Consolations*. A pastoral devotional work organized as fourteen images or topics of consolation (WA 6).
- **1535:** *Theses concerning Faith and Law*. Academic propositions whose proper medium was disputation rather than continuous prose (WA 39/1).
- **1536:** *Disputation concerning Man*. Forty theses defining the human being theologically and philosophically (WA 39/1).
- **1536:** *Disputations concerning Justification*. Several related sets of theses and oral disputation records, not one stable author-finished treatise (WA 39/1).
- **1537–1540:** *Antinomian Disputations*. Six thesis-series and records directed against antinomian teaching; the surviving complex combines Luther’s propositions with reports of spoken debate (WA 39/1–2).
- **1540:** *Disputation on the Divinity and Humanity of Christ*. Christological theses made for academic defense (WA 39/2).
- **1543:** *Disputation on John 1:14: “The Word Was Made Flesh.”* A set of academic propositions and discussion records rather than a conventional book (WA 39/2).

### Reformation manifestos, polemics, and public interventions

- **1518:** *Sermon on Indulgence and Grace*. A vernacular intervention that condensed the indulgence controversy for a broad reading public (WA 1).
- **1518:** *Proceedings at Augsburg*. Luther’s account and documentary presentation of his examination by Cardinal Cajetan (WA 2).
- **1519:** *The Leipzig Debate*. Luther’s printed account and defense of propositions contested at Leipzig (WA 2).
- **1519:** *Two Kinds of Righteousness*. A sermon-treatise distinguishing alien and proper righteousness (WA 2).
- **1520:** *On the Papacy in Rome*. A polemic against Augustin von Alveld’s account of papal authority (WA 6).
- **1520:** *To the Christian Nobility of the German Nation*. A vernacular manifesto summoning secular rulers to reform the church (WA 6).
- **1520:** *The Babylonian Captivity of the Church*. A Latin sacramental polemic dismantling much of the medieval sacramental system (WA 6).
- **1520:** *The Freedom of a Christian*. A paired theological manifesto and pastoral treatise, issued in German and Latin forms whose wording is not identical (WA 7).
- **1520:** *Why the Books of the Pope and His Disciples Were Burned*. A justification of Luther’s public burning of canon law and polemical books; the text acts as an extension of that symbolic action (WA 7).
- **1521:** *Defense and Explanation of All the Articles*. Luther’s defense of propositions condemned in the papal bull *Exsurge Domine* (WA 7).
- **1521:** *Against Latomus*. A Latin theological polemic against the Louvain theologian Jacobus Latomus (WA 8).
- **1521:** *Judgment on Monastic Vows*. A Latin dismantling of the theological basis of binding monastic vows (WA 8).
- **1522:** *Against the Spiritual Estate of the Pope and the Bishops Falsely So Called*. A vernacular attack on episcopal authority and clerical coercion (WA 10/2).
- **1522:** *The Estate of Marriage*. A reforming treatise on marriage, impediments, divorce, and pastoral jurisdiction (WA 10/2).
- **1522:** *The Misuse of the Mass*. A polemic against the mass understood as sacrifice (WA 8).
- **1523:** *That a Christian Assembly Has the Right and Power to Judge All Teaching and to Call, Appoint, and Dismiss Teachers*. A congregational manifesto arguing from scripture for communal authority (WA 11).
- **1523:** *Temporal Authority: To What Extent It Should Be Obeyed*. A political-theological treatise distinguishing spiritual and temporal government (WA 11).
- **1523:** *That Jesus Christ Was Born a Jew*. A missionary and exegetical tract containing a qualified appeal for humane treatment of Jews; it does not cancel the violently anti-Jewish writings Luther later made (WA 11).
- **1523:** *On the Ministry*. A Latin argument concerning ordination, ministry, and the priesthood of believers (WA 12).
- **1524:** *Letter to the Princes of Saxony concerning the Rebellious Spirit*. A public letter against Thomas Müntzer and radical reform (WA 15).
- **1524–1525:** *Against the Heavenly Prophets in the Matter of Images and Sacraments*. A two-part polemic against Andreas Karlstadt and sacramental radicalism (WA 18).
- **1525:** *Admonition to Peace: A Reply to the Twelve Articles of the Peasants in Swabia*. A political-pastoral intervention addressed to both rulers and peasants (WA 18).
- **1525:** *Against the Robbing and Murdering Hordes of Peasants*. A notorious manifesto urging violent suppression during the Peasants’ War; its title, date, and operative rhetoric are essential to its classification (WA 18).
- **1525:** *An Open Letter on the Harsh Book against the Peasants*. Luther’s defense and partial qualification of the preceding tract (WA 18).
- **1525:** *The Bondage of the Will*. A Latin polemical treatise replying to Erasmus on free choice (WA 18).
- **1526:** *Whether Soldiers, Too, Can Be Saved*. A pastoral-political treatise on military vocation and legitimate war (WA 19).
- **1526:** *The Sacrament of the Body and Blood of Christ—Against the Fanatics*. A polemic defending Christ’s bodily presence in the Eucharist (WA 19).
- **1527:** *That These Words of Christ, “This Is My Body,” Still Stand Firm against the Fanatics*. An extended sacramental polemic against Zwingli and Oecolampadius (WA 23).
- **1527:** *Whether One May Flee from a Deadly Plague*. A public pastoral letter balancing civic duty, medical precaution, and conscience during epidemic disease (WA 23).
- **1528:** *Confession concerning Christ’s Supper*. A sacramental treatise ending with a broad confession of Luther’s doctrine (WA 26).
- **1528:** *Concerning Rebaptism*. A letter-treatise against Anabaptist rebaptism (WA 26).
- **1529:** *On War against the Turk*. A political-theological argument distinguishing defensive war from crusade (WA 30/2).
- **1530:** *Admonition to All Clergy Assembled at Augsburg*. A public intervention addressed to the imperial diet and Catholic clergy (WA 30/2).
- **1530:** *Commentary on the Alleged Imperial Edict*. A polemical response to reports of an imperial mandate against evangelical teaching (WA 30/2).
- **1531:** *Warning to His Dear German People*. A manifesto concerning resistance should imperial or ecclesiastical authorities wage war to suppress the evangelical confession (WA 30/3).
- **1531:** *Against the Assassin at Dresden*. A polemic against Duke George of Saxony and writings associated with him (WA 30/3).
- **1533:** *The Private Mass and the Consecration of Priests*. An autobiographically framed polemic against private masses and sacerdotal ordination (WA 38).
- **1535:** *Against the Sabbatarians*. A polemic against Jewish teaching and Christians whom Luther believed adopted Jewish observances (WA 50).
- **1538:** *Against the Antinomians*. A public letter-polemic affirming a continuing role for the law in preaching (WA 50).
- **1539:** *On the Councils and the Church*. A historical and ecclesiological treatise asking how councils and the church may be recognized (WA 50).
- **1541:** *Against Hans Worst*. A polemical defense of the evangelical churches against Duke Henry of Brunswick-Wolfenbüttel (WA 51).
- **1542:** *On the Jews and Their Lies*. A violently anti-Jewish polemic advocating coercive and destructive measures; it must be classified as an actionable persecutory manifesto, not merely “theology” (WA 53).
- **1543:** *On the Ineffable Name and on the Lineage of Christ* (*Vom Schem Hamphoras*). An anti-Jewish polemic employing abusive and scatological rhetoric (WA 53).
- **1543:** *On the Last Words of David*. Besides being an exposition, this is part of Luther’s late anti-Jewish polemical project (WA 54).
- **1545:** *Against the Roman Papacy, an Institution of the Devil*. Luther’s final major anti-papal manifesto (WA 54).
- **1545:** *Against the Thirty-Two Articles of the Louvain Theologians*. A late polemic answering doctrinal condemnations issued at Louvain (WA 54).
- **1545:** *Against the Asses of Paris and Louvain*. A brief, abusive polemical manifestation associated with the same controversy (WA 54).

### Pastoral works, catechisms, and protocols

- **1519:** *A Sermon on the Sacrament of Penance*. A short vernacular sacramental instruction (WA 2).
- **1519:** *A Sermon on Holy Baptism*. A vernacular pastoral explanation of baptism (WA 2).
- **1519:** *A Sermon on the Blessed Sacrament of the Holy and True Body of Christ and the Brotherhoods*. A Eucharistic and ecclesial instruction (WA 2).
- **1520:** *Treatise on Good Works*. A systematic vernacular account of faith and the commandments expressed through the category of good works (WA 6).
- **1520:** *A Treatise on the New Testament, That Is, the Holy Mass*. A reforming account of the mass as promise and testament (WA 6).
- **1522:** *A Brief Instruction on What to Look for and Expect in the Gospels*. A hermeneutical protocol first attached to Luther’s *Church Postil* (WA 10/1/1).
- **1522:** *Personal Prayer Book* (*Betbüchlein*). A changing devotional compilation made from prayers, catechetical instruction, and related texts; successive editions make it a project rather than one fixed text (WA 10/2).
- **1523:** *Order of Baptism* (*Taufbüchlein*). A vernacular liturgical protocol adapted from the inherited Latin rite (WA 12).
- **1523:** *Formula Missae et Communionis*. A Latin protocol for evangelical reform of the mass (WA 12).
- **1526:** *German Mass and Order of Divine Service*. A vernacular liturgical protocol integrating words, music, movement, and congregational action; in that limited sense it approaches a Gesamtkunstwerk rather than a merely written treatise (WA 19).
- **1526:** *Revised Order of Baptism*. A shortened revision of Luther’s baptismal protocol (WA 19).
- **1528:** *Instructions for the Visitors of Parish Pastors in Electoral Saxony*. An ecclesiastical visitation protocol principally composed by Melanchthon but issued with Luther’s preface and authority; it should not be silently treated as Luther’s sole-authored work (WA 26).
- **1529:** *Small Catechism*. A compact instructional protocol for household teaching, appearing as wall charts and booklets and incorporating words, images, and liturgical forms (WA 30/1).
- **1529:** *Large Catechism*. An expanded catechetical work developed from Luther’s sermons and intended especially for clergy and teachers (WA 30/1).
- **1529:** *Exhortation to Confession*. A pastoral defense and reformulation of private confession (WA 30/1).
- **1530:** *A Sermon on Keeping Children in School*. A public exhortation to parents and magistrates concerning education (WA 30/2).
- **1537:** *Smalcald Articles*. A confessional memorandum prepared for a projected church council; it was not formally adopted at Smalcald in the manner originally intended, so the failed project is part of the work’s status (WA 50).
- **1539:** *The Keys*. A doctrinal-pastoral account of absolution and ecclesiastical authority (WA 50).
- **1542:** *Comfort for Women Who Have Had a Miscarriage*. A short pastoral text whose precise circumstances and textual transmission require caution (WA 53).
- **1542:** *A Short Order of Confession before the Priest for the Common Man*. A practical protocol for confession and absolution (WA 53).

### Biblical translations and translation theory

- **1517:** *The Seven Penitential Psalms*. A German translation with explanations; Luther revised it substantially in 1525, so no single wording exhausts the obra (WA 1; 18).
- **1521:** *Magnificat, Translated and Explained*. A German translation and exposition dedicated to Prince John Frederick (WA 7).
- **1522:** *September Testament*. Luther’s first printed German New Testament, translated at the Wartburg and revised with colleagues; it is both a translation and a designed print object with prefaces and woodcuts (WA DB 6–7).
- **1522:** *December Testament*. A corrected second manifestation of the German New Testament, not simply another copy of the September text (WA DB 6–7).
- **1523:** *The Pentateuch in German*. The first installment of Luther’s Old Testament translation, made within an increasingly collaborative Wittenberg translation project (WA DB 8).
- **1524:** *The Historical Books in German*. The second installment of the translated Old Testament, covering Joshua through Esther (WA DB 9).
- **1524:** *The Psalter in German*. A translation produced and repeatedly revised by Luther and the Wittenberg circle (WA DB 10/1).
- **1524:** *Job, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and the Song of Songs in German*. The third major Old Testament installment; collaborative philological work complicates the notion of solitary authorship (WA DB 10).
- **1526:** *The Prophet Jonah Interpreted*. A translation and exposition of Jonah (WA 19).
- **1528:** *The Prophet Zechariah Interpreted*. A German translation and exposition (WA 23).
- **1528:** *The Prophet Isaiah Interpreted*. A German translation and explanatory project distinct from Luther’s Latin lectures on Isaiah (WA 25).
- **1529:** *Revised Latin Psalter*. A revision of the Vulgate Psalms rather than an independent translation from scratch (WA DB 5).
- **1530:** *Open Letter on Translating*. A defense of Luther’s translation practice and a polemic about idiomatic German (WA 30/2).
- **1531:** *The Psalter Newly Revised*. A major collaborative revision documented in surviving revision protocols (WA DB 3–4; 10/1).
- **1532:** *The Prophets in German*. The Wittenberg translation of Isaiah through Malachi, made collaboratively under Luther’s direction (WA DB 11).
- **1534:** *The Complete German Bible*. The first complete Wittenberg Bible associated with Luther, including the Apocrypha; it is a collaborative, continuously revised Gesamtkunstwerk of translation, prefaces, typography, and images rather than Luther’s unaided production (WA DB 6–12).
- **1534:** *The Apocrypha in German*. A separately identifiable portion of the complete Bible, translated collaboratively and described as useful though not equal to canonical scripture (WA DB 12).
- **1534–1546:** *Revisions of the German Bible*. A continuing project documented in Luther’s annotated copies and the Wittenberg revision minutes; it resists treatment as a succession of wholly separate “books” (WA DB 3–4).
- **1538:** *Defense of the Translation of the Psalms*. A translation-theoretical defense of the Wittenberg Psalter (WA 38).
- **1522–1546:** *Prefaces to the books of the German Bible*. A changing paratextual corpus that directs how readers should classify and interpret biblical books; prefaces were revised, omitted, and relocated across editions (WA DB 6–12).

### Sermon-books and preaching corpora

- **1514–1546:** *Individual sermons*. Luther made hundreds of preaching events, outlines, autograph sermon texts, and revised print sermons. “The sermons” are not one authored book: many survive only in notes by hearers, whose accuracy and editorial contribution vary (WA 1–54).
- **1521–1522:** *Church Postil* (*Kirchenpostille*). A collection of model sermons on the epistle and gospel readings, begun at the Wartburg and repeatedly expanded and revised (WA 10/1/1–2).
- **1525:** *The Three Righteous and Holy Orders*. A sermon-broadsheet presenting household, civil, and ecclesiastical vocations (WA 17/2).
- **1525–1526:** *Sermons on Exodus*. A preaching-cycle preserved through print and reporters’ notes (WA 16).
- **1528–1529:** *Sermons on John 16–20*. Preached expositions, some published from notes by others rather than from Luther’s own finished copy (WA 28).
- **1528–1532:** *Sermons on the Gospel of John 1–4*. A sustained preaching-cycle whose received text depends heavily upon Caspar Cruciger’s transcription and editing (WA 46).
- **1530–1532:** *Sermon on the Mount and related Matthew sermons*. A preaching and exposition complex preserved in edited notes (WA 32).
- **1532–1534:** *Sermons on John 6–8*. A preaching-cycle issued through the mediation of Luther’s recorders and editors (WA 33).
- **1537–1540:** *Sermons on John 14–16*. A sustained preaching-cycle, textually mediated by hearers’ notes (WA 45).
- **1544:** *House Postil* (*Hauspostille*). A collection arranged by Veit Dietrich from Luther’s sermons for household use; Luther supplied the preaching, but the book as book is Dietrich’s editorial construction. A competing edition by Andreas Poach underscores that no single “House Postil” transparently reproduces Luther’s words (WA 52).

### Hymns, melodies, and sung manifestations

- **1523:** *Ein neues Lied wir heben an*. A narrative hymn about the execution of the Augustinian friars Hendrik Vos and Jan van Essen; both text and likely melody are attributed to Luther (WA 35).
- **1523/1524:** *Nun freut euch, lieben Christen g’mein*. A congregational narrative hymn; Luther’s authorship of the text is secure, while transmission of its early melodies is more complicated (WA 35).
- **1524:** *Ach Gott, vom Himmel sieh darein*. A metrical paraphrase of Psalm 12 (WA 35).
- **1524:** *Aus tiefer Not schrei ich zu dir*. A metrical paraphrase of Psalm 130, made in four- and five-stanza forms; Luther is also associated with its principal melody (WA 35).
- **1524:** *Es spricht der Unweisen Mund wohl*. A metrical paraphrase of Psalm 14 (WA 35).
- **1524:** *Es wollt uns Gott genädig sein*. A metrical paraphrase of Psalm 67 (WA 35).
- **1524:** *Wär Gott nicht mit uns diese Zeit*. A metrical paraphrase of Psalm 124 (WA 35).
- **1524:** *Nun komm, der Heiden Heiland*. A German adaptation of the Latin Advent hymn *Veni redemptor gentium* (WA 35).
- **1524:** *Christum wir sollen loben schon*. A German adaptation of the Latin hymn *A solis ortus cardine* (WA 35).
- **1524:** *Gelobet seist du, Jesu Christ*. An expanded German Christmas hymn built upon an older stanza (WA 35).
- **1524:** *Christ lag in Todesbanden*. An Easter hymn reworking older German and Latin materials; both words and musical form participate in the creation (WA 35).
- **1524:** *Jesus Christus, unser Heiland, der den Tod überwand*. An Easter hymn distinct from Luther’s later Eucharistic hymn with a similar opening (WA 35).
- **1524:** *Komm, Gott Schöpfer, Heiliger Geist*. A German adaptation of *Veni creator spiritus* (WA 35).
- **1524:** *Komm, Heiliger Geist, Herre Gott*. Luther expanded and adapted an existing German Pentecost stanza, so the obra is partly transformation rather than wholly new composition (WA 35).
- **1524:** *Jesus Christus, unser Heiland, der von uns den Gotteszorn wandt*. A Eucharistic hymn adapted from medieval antecedents (WA 35).
- **1524:** *Gott sei gelobet und gebenedeiet*. A Eucharistic hymn expanding an older German stanza (WA 35).
- **1524:** *Mitten wir im Leben sind*. A German expansion of the medieval antiphon *Media vita in morte sumus* (WA 35).
- **1524:** *Mensch, willst du leben seliglich*. A catechetical hymn on the Ten Commandments (WA 35).
- **1524:** *Dies sind die heilgen zehn Gebot*. A second, more extensive hymn on the Ten Commandments (WA 35).
- **1524:** *Jesaja, dem Propheten, das geschah*. A German Sanctus paraphrasing Isaiah 6 (WA 35).
- **1524:** *Mit Fried und Freud ich fahr dahin*. A metrical rendering of the *Nunc dimittis*, also functioning as a hymn for death and burial (WA 35).
- **1524:** *Christ, unser Herr, zum Jordan kam*. A baptismal hymn; although often dated 1541 from its first known printing, scholarship has also connected its conception with earlier liturgical work, so its dating should not be treated as certain (WA 35).
- **1524:** *Eyn Enchiridion oder Handbüchlein* and the Wittenberg choir hymnbook. Luther did not singly author these hymnals, but his hymns and programmatic prefaces helped make the earliest evangelical songbook project; Johann Walter and the printers were indispensable co-makers (WA 35).
- **1526:** *Isaiah 6 Sanctus and music for the German Mass*. Liturgical words and melodies assigned to worship actions; attribution of every individual musical detail to Luther is not secure (WA 19; 35).
- **1529:** *Verleih uns Frieden gnädiglich*. A German prayer-song adapted from the antiphon *Da pacem, Domine* (WA 35).
- **1529:** *Gib unserm Fürsten und aller Obrigkeit Fried und gut Regiment*. A stanza commonly joined to *Verleih uns Frieden*; its attribution to Luther has been questioned and should not be presented as certain (WA 35).
- **1529:** *Herr Gott, dich loben wir*. A German adaptation of the *Te Deum* (WA 35).
- **1535:** *Sie ist mir lieb, die werte Magd*. An ecclesiological hymn drawing on the woman of Revelation 12 (WA 35).
- **1535:** *Was fürchtst du, Feind Herodes, sehr*. A German adaptation of the Latin Epiphany hymn *Hostis Herodes impie* (WA 35).
- **1537/1538:** *Vater unser im Himmelreich*. A catechetical hymn on the Lord’s Prayer (WA 35).
- **1538:** *Erhalt uns, Herr, bei deinem Wort*. A polemical prayer-hymn first directed explicitly against pope and Turk; later textual alterations belong to its reception rather than Luther’s original wording (WA 35).
- **1541:** *Christ, unser Herr, zum Jordan kam*. The first securely documented printing of Luther’s baptismal hymn (WA 35).
- **1542:** *Vom Himmel hoch, da komm ich her*. A Christmas hymn probably first made for domestic performance; the familiar melody associated with it appeared after an earlier tune (WA 35).
- **1543:** *Vom Himmel kam der Engel Schar*. A Christmas hymn based on Luke 2 (WA 35).
- **1543:** *Der du bist drei in Einigkeit*. A German adaptation of the Latin evening hymn *O lux beata Trinitas* (WA 35).
- **1543:** *Nun bitten wir den Heiligen Geist*. Luther expanded an older German stanza with three new stanzas, making the hymn an explicitly collaborative object across time (WA 35).
- **1543:** *Des wir sollen alle froh sein*. A hymn sometimes assigned to Luther in older lists but of doubtful attribution; it cannot securely be counted as his obra (WA 35, editorial discussion).
- **1545:** *Die deutsche Litanei*. A German litany adapted for sung congregational use; it is a ritual protocol and musical-verbal manifestation rather than simply a poem (WA 35).
- **1523–1545:** *The body of Luther’s hymns*. The critical tradition generally recognizes roughly three dozen texts, but totals differ because adaptations, alternate forms, melodies, and doubtful attributions are counted differently; “Luther’s hymnal” is therefore not one fixed authorial object (WA 35).

### Letters, prefaces, opinions, and administrative writings

- **1501–1546:** *Correspondence*. Approximately 2,600 surviving letters by Luther, plus drafts, enclosures, and documents known only through copies, form an epistolary corpus rather than one work; dating and addressee attribution remain uncertain in individual cases (WA Br 1–18).
- **1519:** *Letter to Pope Leo X*. A diplomatic-theological letter associated with the Latin form of *The Freedom of a Christian*; its conventional dating and relation to the treatise have required editorial scrutiny (WA Br 2; WA 7).
- **1521:** *Declaration at the Diet of Worms*. Luther’s spoken defense survives in variant Latin and German reports rather than an uncontested autograph text; the famous later wording is not wholly secure (WA 7).
- **1522–1545:** *Prefaces to other writers’ books*. Dozens of paratexts made to authorize, commend, frame, or redirect works by colleagues and predecessors; each is a small intervention, while together they form a distinct corpus (WA 10–54; *Luther’s Works*, vols. 59–60).
- **1524:** *To the Councilmen of All Cities in Germany That They Establish and Maintain Christian Schools*. A civic-educational manifesto addressed to municipal governments (WA 15).
- **1525:** *Open Letter to Cardinal Albrecht of Mainz*. A public letter opposing the clerical marriage and related policies of the archbishop (WA 18).
- **1529:** *Marburg Articles*. Fifteen doctrinal articles produced and signed at the Marburg Colloquy; the document is a negotiated collective creation, not Luther’s sole-authored confession (WA 30/3).
- **1530:** *Exhortation to All Clergy Assembled at Augsburg*. Besides being a polemic, it is an open letter designed to act upon a specific political assembly (WA 30/2).
- **1536:** *Wittenberg Concord*. A negotiated agreement concerning the Eucharist; Luther was a principal participant, but the text is a collective diplomatic creation (WA 50).
- **1539:** *Advice on the Bigamy of Philip of Hesse*. A confidential opinion signed by Luther, Melanchthon, and Bucer permitting a secret second marriage under exceptional conditions; it is a jointly made counsel, not Luther’s solitary work (WA Br 8).
- **1541–1542:** *Documents concerning Nicolaus von Amsdorf’s election and consecration as bishop of Naumburg*. Opinions, instructions, and a model consecration order forming an administrative-liturgical project with several contributors (WA 53).
- **1542:** *A New Ordinance for the Common Chest at Wittenberg*. An institutional protocol associated with Luther and Wittenberg’s reformers; exact compositional responsibility is shared (WA 53).
- **1542:** *Last Will and Testament*. A legal-family document made by Luther and witnessed by colleagues; its validity was initially complicated because it departed from ordinary Saxon legal form (WA Br 9; WA 50).
- **1545:** *Preface to the Complete Edition of Luther’s Latin Writings*. An autobiographical account of his reforming development, written as paratext to a collected edition he had once resisted (WA 54).
- **1546:** *The Eisleben papers*. Luther’s final letters, notes, and mediation documents concerning the Mansfeld dispute; these are products of an unfinished diplomatic project rather than a single titled work (WA Br 11).

### Emblems, performances, and works not wholly reducible to text

- **1519–1530:** *Luther’s seal (“Luther rose”)*. A personal emblem whose elements Luther described in a 1530 letter; the surviving design developed through seals, rings, woodcuts, and workshop execution, so Luther supplied the symbolic project but cannot be credited as sole fabricator of every visual manifestation (WA Br 5).
- **1520:** *Burning of the canon law and papal books at Wittenberg*. A deliberately staged symbolic action, followed by a printed justification. It resists being called a text or stable object, but it was a made public manifestation with manifesto force (WA 7).
- **1521:** *Appearance and declaration at Worms*. An embodied legal and rhetorical performance known through variant reports; it is a historical act rather than a reliably fixed authored text (WA 7).
- **1522–1546:** *The Wittenberg Bible-revision sessions*. Collaborative spoken workshops involving Luther, Melanchthon, Bugenhagen, Cruciger, Aurogallus, and others. Surviving protocols document a collective translation process, not a separable book wholly “by Luther” (WA DB 3–4).
- **1529:** *Marburg Colloquy*. A theological encounter, set of negotiations, chalked formulae, and signed articles. As an obra it is collective and performative; Luther made interventions within it but not the event as a sole author (WA 30/3).
- **1530:** *The Coburg correspondence-and-writing campaign*. Sermons, translations, letters, counsels, and polemics produced while Luther was confined at Coburg form a coordinated political-theological project, but not a titleable single work (WA 30/2; WA Br 5).
- **1534 and after:** *The Luther Bible as designed artifact*. Translation, typography, woodcut illustration, page architecture, prefaces, and workshop production form a multimedia whole. Calling it solely Luther’s work erases the contributions of translators, printers, artists, and editors; calling it merely an edition misses its Gesamtkunstwerk character (WA DB 6–12).
- **1546:** *Mediation of the Mansfeld dispute*. Luther’s final journey produced proposals, conferences, letters, and draft settlements. The intended peace was the project; because negotiations continued until shortly before his death, its incompleteness is constitutive.

### Recorded speech and posthumous constructions that resist authorship

- **1531–1546:** *Table Talk* (*Tischreden*). Remarks attributed to Luther and written down by Veit Dietrich, Anton Lauterbach, Johannes Mathesius, Hieronymus Weller, Johannes Schlaginhaufen, and others. These sayings appeared, but most are not texts Luther wrote or authorized; variant notebooks, retrospective recollection, and Johannes Aurifaber’s posthumous editing make “Luther’s Table Talk” a documentary creation of multiple makers (WA TR 1–6).
- **1510s–1546:** *Students’ and hearers’ lecture notes*. These manuscripts preserve Luther’s teaching but mingle his speech with selection, abbreviation, misunderstanding, and editorial reconstruction. They should be cited as witnesses to performances, not silently converted into autograph works (WA, passim).
- **1514–1546:** *Sermon reports*. Many printed “sermons by Luther” derive from shorthand or expanded notes by other people. The preaching event is Luther’s manifestation; the surviving verbal object may be jointly made or principally a reporter’s construction (WA, passim).
- **1544:** *House Postil as edited by Veit Dietrich*. The sermons originate in Luther’s preaching, but the collection’s arrangement and wording are editorial; Luther later complained about unauthorized or inaccurate sermon publication, leaving the work’s authority contested (WA 52).
- **1566 and later:** *Aurifaber’s Table Talk*. A posthumous anthology that powerfully created the later public object called “Luther’s Table Talk.” It is not itself an obra made by Luther, even though it contains material attributed to him (compare WA TR 1–6).
