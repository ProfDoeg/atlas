# John Brown — Research Dossier

**Subject:** John Brown (1800–1859), American abolitionist, militant organizer, guerrilla commander in Kansas, and leader of the October 1859 seizure of the federal armory at Harpers Ferry, Virginia.

**Research note:** This dossier separates contemporary documentary evidence, later recollection, historical interpretation, and legend wherever the distinction matters. Dates and claims are given at the level of precision supported by the available record. Brown left a large body of letters and statements but no conventional autobiography or book-length work published in his lifetime; much of the surviving documentary corpus was assembled posthumously by allies, descendants, journalists, collectors, and historians. Early biographies are themselves part of the history of Brown's reputation and sometimes conceal, rationalize, or exaggerate particular episodes.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** John Brown.
- **Common designations:** Captain John Brown; Captain Brown; Old Brown; Old Brown of Osawatomie; Osawatomie Brown. “Captain” derived from militia/guerrilla usage in Kansas rather than a commission in the regular United States military.
- **Operational aliases / assumed names:** Brown used aliases during clandestine operations. Well-attested examples include **Shubel Morgan** during the 1858–1859 planning period and **Isaac Smith** while renting the Kennedy Farm near Harpers Ferry. Specialist Brown literature also records **Nelson Hawkins** among names used in covert travel; surviving sources vary in how consistently individual aliases can be tied to particular journeys.
- **Born:** **May 9, 1800**, Torrington, Litchfield County, Connecticut.
- **Died:** **December 2, 1859**, Charles Town, Virginia (now Charles Town, West Virginia).
- **Cause of death:** Judicial execution by hanging after conviction in a Virginia state court.
- **Burial:** Brown family farm, North Elba, New York; now John Brown Farm State Historic Site.
- **Occupation(s):** Tanner and currier; farmer; livestock breeder and shepherd; surveyor; land speculator; wool dealer; postmaster; businessman; abolitionist organizer; armed antislavery guerrilla; clandestine revolutionary planner.
- **Religious identification:** Protestant, formed in a strongly Calvinist/Congregationalist milieu. Brown described himself as a firm believer in the divine authority of the Bible and understood antislavery action as a religious obligation.
- **Freemasonry:** Initiated in Hudson Lodge No. 68, Hudson, Ohio, in 1824; lodge records give Entered Apprentice January 13, Fellow Craft February 10, and Master Mason May 11. He later became alienated from Masonry and is reported to have publicly opposed it. The Masonic period belongs to his early civic life and should not be projected onto his later abolitionist organization.
- **Principal historical roles:** militant participant in Bleeding Kansas; commander associated with the Pottawatomie killings of 1856; organizer of the League of Gileadites; planner of a projected mountain-based antislavery campaign; leader of the 1859 Harpers Ferry raid; defendant in *Virginia v. John Brown*.

### A note on terminology

Brown was convicted of **treason against the Commonwealth of Virginia**, not federal treason against the United States. The other principal charges were conspiracy with enslaved people to produce insurrection and murder. Modern descriptions of Brown as a “terrorist,” “guerrilla,” “revolutionary,” “insurrectionist,” “freedom fighter,” “fanatic,” or “martyr” are interpretive classifications rather than the names of a single uncontested historical category.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and ancestry

Brown was the son of **Owen Brown (1771–1856)** and **Ruth Mills Brown (1772–1808)**. He grew up in a large family in the Connecticut–Western Reserve migration stream. Brown later told Henry L. Stearns that he had been born to “poor but respectable parents,” claimed paternal descent from a passenger in the Mayflower migration, and said his maternal ancestry included an early immigrant from Amsterdam. He also stated that both of his grandfathers served in the American Revolution and that his paternal grandfather died in New York in 1776 while in service. These ancestry claims come from Brown’s own 1857 autobiographical letter; genealogical writers have attempted to reconstruct the lines, but the letter itself should be treated as Brown’s family tradition rather than as a modern genealogical proof.

Owen Brown was a tanner, farmer, cattleman, community figure, and committed opponent of slavery. The family moved from Connecticut to the Western Reserve in Ohio in **1805**, settling in the area that became Hudson. The Western Reserve’s New England religious culture, antislavery networks, and frontier economy became central to John Brown’s formation.

John’s mother Ruth died in **December 1808**, when he was eight. Owen married **Sally Root** in 1809. Brown later wrote that his stepmother was intelligent and estimable but that he never emotionally replaced his mother and “continued to pine” for Ruth for years. His description is autobiographical and retrospective but unusually intimate.

### Siblings and extended family

Brown belonged to a large Brown family network in northeastern Ohio. Several brothers, half-siblings, children, sons-in-law, and later grandchildren became important custodians of family memory. Archival collections preserve material from or concerning his brother **Oliver Owen Brown**, half-brother **Jeremiah Root Brown**, and half-sister **Florella Brown Adair**, among others. Descendants became major informants for early biographers such as Franklin B. Sanborn and Oswald Garrison Villard.

### First marriage: Dianthe Lusk

Brown married **Dianthe Lusk** in **1820**. Brown’s own later description of her called her industrious, economical, pious, practical, and capable of moderating his “haughty obstinate temper.” National Park Service family research notes that she suffered episodes described by contemporaries as mental illness and that Brown attended her through periods of illness. Dianthe died in **1832**, shortly after the birth and death of an infant son.

Children of John and Dianthe Brown:

1. **John Brown Jr.** — born July 25, 1821, Hudson, Ohio; educated in part at Grand River Institute; married Wealthy Hotchkiss in 1847; active in Kansas antislavery politics; served in a Kansas cavalry unit during the Civil War; died May 3, 1895, Put-in-Bay, Ohio.
2. **Jason Brown** — born January 19, 1823, Hudson; married Ellen Sherbondy in 1847; participated in the family’s Kansas migration; died December 24, 1912, Akron, Ohio.
3. **Owen Brown** — born November 4, 1824, Hudson; fought in Kansas; participated in the Harpers Ferry conspiracy and escaped after the raid; died January 8, 1889, near Pasadena, California.
4. **Frederick Brown I** — born January 9, 1827, New Richmond, Pennsylvania; died in childhood, about age four.
5. **Ruth Brown** — born February 18, 1829, New Richmond; attended Grand River Institute; married Henry Thompson on September 26, 1850; died January 18, 1904, Pasadena, California.
6. **Frederick Brown II** — born December 31, 1830, New Richmond; active with the Brown family in Kansas; shot and killed near Osawatomie on August 30, 1856, by proslavery settler Martin White.
7. **Unnamed infant son** — born August 7, 1832; died after three days. Dianthe died shortly afterward and was buried with the infant.

### Second marriage: Mary Ann Day Brown

Brown married **Mary Ann Day** in **1833**. She was about seventeen at marriage; he was thirty-three. Their marriage lasted until his death. Mary managed households repeatedly disrupted by Brown’s commercial ventures, migrations, absences, political work, and eventually armed conspiracy. She opposed or feared aspects of his militancy but remained connected to him and to the family project. She visited him in prison on December 1, 1859, received his body after execution, and later became an important guardian of family memory. She moved west after the Civil War and died **February 29, 1884**, in Saratoga, California.

Children of John and Mary Ann Brown:

1. **Sarah Brown I** — born May 11, 1834; died of dysentery in 1843, age nine.
2. **Watson Brown** — born October 7, 1835; married Isabella Thompson in September 1858; mortally wounded at Harpers Ferry, died October 19, 1859. His body was used for anatomical study at Winchester Medical College; his remains were eventually recovered and reburied at North Elba in 1882.
3. **Salmon Brown** — born October 2, 1836; married Abbie C. Hinckley in September 1858; active in Kansas but did not take part in Harpers Ferry; later suffered severe disability after a horse accident and died by suicide May 10, 1919, in Portland, Oregon.
4. **Charles Brown** — born November 3, 1837; died September 11, 1843, during a family dysentery outbreak.
5. **Oliver Brown** — born March 9, 1839; married Martha Brewster April 7, 1858; participated in Harpers Ferry; mortally wounded and died October 17 or 18, 1859. His remains were later recovered and returned to North Elba.
6. **Peter Brown** — born December 7, 1840; died September 22, 1843, of dysentery.
7. **Austin Brown** — born September 14, 1842; died September 27, 1843, of dysentery.
8. **Annie Brown** — born December 23, 1843; served as a lookout and household cover at the Kennedy Farm during Harpers Ferry preparations; married Samuel Adams; died October 5, 1926, in California.
9. **Amelia Brown** — born June 22, 1845; accidentally scalded and died October 30, 1846.
10. **Sarah Brown II** — born September 11, 1846; never married; died in 1916 in Saratoga, California.
11. **Ellen Brown I** — born April 26, 1848, Springfield, Massachusetts; died April 30, 1849, of consumption, reportedly in her father’s arms.
12. **Unnamed infant son** — born April 26, 1852; died of whooping cough after about three weeks.
13. **Ellen Brown II** — born September 25, 1854; married James Fablinger in 1876; died July 16, 1916, Saratoga, California.

Brown thus fathered **twenty children** by two wives. Nine died in childhood. Multiple adult sons became participants in his armed antislavery activities, making the Brown family both a domestic household and, eventually, part of his political-military network.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1800–1816

### Torrington to the Western Reserve

John Brown was born in Torrington on May 9, 1800. In 1805 the family undertook the long migration to Ohio, much of it by ox team. Brown’s 1857 autobiographical letter remembers frontier travel, driving cattle, encounters with rattlesnakes, Native people, wild animals, leather work, and considerable freedom to range through the woods.

Owen Brown learned and practiced tanning and deerskin dressing. John later remembered being put to leather work as a small child and acquiring enough practical skill to dress animal skins and make whip lashes. This childhood craft training became economically important: tanning was Brown’s most consistent skilled trade in early adulthood.

### Mother’s death and religious formation

Ruth Brown’s death in 1808 was one of the central losses in Brown’s own account of childhood. He later linked his emotional reserve, shyness around women, and dependence on older mentors partly to that loss. These are Brown’s own retrospective psychological interpretations, not clinical findings.

Brown described an early period of religious skepticism followed by conversion in adolescence. He said that he became convinced of Christianity and thereafter a firm believer in the Bible, with an unusually strong memory for scripture. His mature rhetoric drew heavily on Hebrew Bible narratives, prophetic judgment, covenantal obligation, sacrifice, and New Testament commands concerning the poor and oppressed.

### War of 1812 and the enslaved-boy episode

During the War of 1812, Owen Brown supplied beef cattle to the army, and young John participated in cattle drives. Brown later said the experience disgusted him with conventional military display and drilling; he avoided militia training for years and paid fines rather than drill.

The most famous formative story in Brown’s own account occurred during this wartime cattle-driving period. He described staying with a prosperous man who treated young Brown as an admired guest while keeping an enslaved Black boy of approximately Brown’s age poorly fed, poorly clothed, exposed to cold, and beaten with iron shovels or whatever object was at hand. Brown wrote in 1857 that the contrast caused him to ask whether enslaved children could call God their father and eventually led him to swear “eternal war” against slavery.

**Evidentiary status:** the event is known from Brown’s autobiographical letter written roughly four decades later. It is therefore primary evidence for Brown’s remembered self-understanding and self-presentation, but not an independently corroborated contemporaneous report. Later biographers and documentaries frequently turn it into a single conversion scene; the original letter presents it as one event in a longer religious and moral development.

### Education

Brown had little regular schooling. In his own account he admitted that he disliked school confinement and preferred work, wrestling, outdoor activity, and travel. He claimed that he learned no grammar in school and less arithmetic than the four basic rules. He nevertheless became a serious reader, helped by an older acquaintance who gave him access to a library around age ten.

At roughly sixteen or seventeen he went east for further study, associated in local histories with **Morris Academy in Litchfield, Connecticut**, and with preparatory study connected to the ministry. A separate family/local tradition places him briefly in **Plainfield, Massachusetts**, studying under Rev. Moses Hallock, a noted preparatory teacher. Brown suffered recurring inflammation of the eyes and lacked money; his formal educational project ended and he returned to Ohio. The exact sequence and institutional labels vary slightly among biographical sources, but there is broad agreement that he contemplated ministerial preparation and abandoned it because of health and finances.

Brown subsequently taught himself practical arithmetic and surveying from books. He remained a self-educated reader rather than a college-trained intellectual.

## Education and Formation

Brown’s formation was not centered in a university or seminary. The principal formative structures were:

- his father Owen Brown’s antislavery Protestant household;
- New England Calvinist/Congregationalist religious culture transplanted to the Western Reserve;
- tanning and livestock work;
- frontier travel and cattle driving;
- self-directed reading, especially the Bible, history, and practical surveying/arithmetic;
- the antislavery culture of northeastern Ohio;
- later direct contact with Black abolitionists, fugitive-slave networks, Frederick Douglass, Gerrit Smith, and militant resistance to the Fugitive Slave Act;
- the political violence of Kansas Territory.

Brown’s adult intellectual vocabulary remained biblical and practical more than academic. He was capable of drafting constitutions, organizational rules, military plans, propaganda, and extensive correspondence. His prose could be compressed, idiosyncratically punctuated, and highly scriptural.

## Early Career, Marriage, Tanning, and Pennsylvania, 1816–1835

### Tanner and foreman

From about age fifteen into early adulthood, Brown spent much of his time in his father’s tanning and currying business. He described himself as cook and bachelor-housekeeper for work crews and, for much of the period, foreman under Owen Brown. He later admitted that early responsibility made him accustomed to being obeyed and contributed to an imperious manner.

### Marriage and first independent business

After marrying Dianthe Lusk in 1820, Brown tried to establish an independent household and business. He developed a lasting interest in high-quality cattle, horses, sheep, and swine and regarded livestock and especially wool as possible routes to economic independence.

In **1826** Brown moved his family to **New Richmond, Pennsylvania**, near the Ohio border. There he built or operated a tannery and became a recognizable local civic figure. National Park Service accounts state that the tannery contained a concealed space used to shelter people escaping slavery; the Brown household and tannery are accordingly remembered as part of the Underground Railroad geography. Precise numbers of people assisted are not documented.

Brown helped organize a church and served as a local postmaster. He cultivated land, raised animals, and pursued the combination of skilled craft, farming, and civic responsibility that characterized his early ambitions.

### Freemasonry

In Hudson in 1824, before the Pennsylvania move, Brown had joined Hudson Lodge No. 68 and advanced through the first three Masonic degrees. His later political identity was not Masonic; sources report that he became a critic and severed ties. The episode is nevertheless useful evidence of how conventional his early community participation could be before his later revolutionary reputation.

### Dianthe’s death and remarriage

Dianthe’s death in 1832, following the death of their newborn child, left Brown with surviving children and a working household to manage. He remarried Mary Ann Day in 1833. The second marriage produced thirteen additional children and became the domestic framework for all of his later migrations.

## Chronological Life History

### 1835–1842: Ohio speculation, expansion, panic, and bankruptcy

Brown returned to northeastern Ohio in the mid-1830s. He entered a period of ambitious commercial activity involving tanning, surveying, livestock, land, and real estate speculation. In the Franklin Mills/Kent area he worked with **Zenas B. Kent** and became involved in land and canal-related ventures. Paul Finkelman’s National Archives study identifies Brown with the **Franklin Land Company**, which controlled approximately 700 acres.

Brown was not merely a subsistence farmer who later turned political. He repeatedly sought large-scale commercial success. The expansion exposed him to credit risk. The **Panic of 1837** and the collapse of speculative land values produced a prolonged financial crisis. Brown tried to hold properties against creditors, became entangled in lawsuits, and was briefly jailed during conflict over property seizure. In **1842** he went through formal bankruptcy proceedings and lost most of his property.

Accounts of the bankruptcy often emphasize either incompetence or integrity. The documentary picture is more specific: Brown overextended himself in speculative and credit-based ventures, was sued repeatedly, and lost heavily. Contemporary and later researchers have not established a pattern of fraudulent enrichment. Finkelman emphasizes that even business opponents did not generally accuse him of dishonesty.

### 1837: Elijah Lovejoy and a remembered vow

The murder of antislavery editor **Elijah P. Lovejoy** by a proslavery mob at Alton, Illinois, on November 7, 1837 became a major milestone in Brown family memory. Later recollections say that at a memorial meeting Brown stood and publicly dedicated his life to the destruction of slavery.

**Evidentiary status:** the vow is a significant family/abolitionist tradition but rests on later recollection rather than a known contemporaneous transcript of the Hudson meeting. It fits Brown’s documented antislavery trajectory but should not be treated as stenographically certain.

### 1843: family epidemic

The Brown household suffered an especially severe period in 1843 when several children died of dysentery in a short span. Sarah I, Charles, Peter, and Austin died during this period. Repeated childhood death was a defining fact of the family’s domestic history.

### 1844–1851: Simon Perkins, sheep, wool, Springfield, and England

Brown entered a partnership with wealthy Akron businessman **Simon Perkins Jr.** in 1844. An agreement placed Brown in charge of Perkins’s sheep and wool interests; the Brown family rented a Perkins property in Akron. Brown’s expertise in animal husbandry and wool grading was real, and the venture expanded.

By **1846** the partnership operated as **Perkins & Brown** with a warehouse in **Springfield, Massachusetts**, a major wool market. Brown wanted to break what he considered exploitative merchant control of wool prices by organizing growers and marketing wool more directly. The family moved to Springfield.

Springfield transformed Brown’s political network. He encountered a strong Black community, abolitionist clergy, fugitive-slave activists, and Frederick Douglass. The city also placed him closer to New England reform circles capable of supplying money and publicity.

Brown’s commercial strategy failed. He accumulated wool in expectation of better prices and eventually tried to open an English market. In **1849–1850** he traveled to **England**, attempting to sell large quantities of American wool. Buyers did not accept his pricing strategy, and the trip ended disastrously. The partnership became enmeshed in litigation. Summit County historical material places losses from the wool enterprise and associated disputes at roughly **$40,000**, a large nineteenth-century sum; exact accounting differs among narratives.

The failure did not produce evidence of personal enrichment. It did reinforce a long pattern: Brown repeatedly attempted ambitious projects, relied on partners’ capital, proved technically knowledgeable in some domains, but often misjudged markets, timing, leverage, and other people’s willingness to follow his plans.

### 1847–1848: Frederick Douglass and the emerging mountain plan

Brown met **Frederick Douglass** in Springfield in the late 1840s. Douglass later remembered Brown as already thinking beyond ordinary abolitionist petition and speech toward armed resistance. Brown outlined a concept in which small disciplined bands would operate in mountain country, assist enslaved people to escape, make slavery economically insecure, and gradually weaken the institution.

The precise form of the plan evolved. Harpers Ferry in 1859 was not simply the sudden idea of an aging militant; its elements had been incubating for more than a decade. Douglass admired Brown’s sincerity and racial egalitarianism but repeatedly remained more skeptical of suicidal or strategically impossible armed plans.

### 1848–1849: Gerrit Smith, Timbuctoo, and North Elba

New York abolitionist and landowner **Gerrit Smith** distributed Adirondack land to Black New Yorkers. One motive was to create farms and communities; another was political: New York imposed a property qualification on Black male voters, and land ownership could help qualify recipients to vote.

Brown offered to settle among the Black grantees and assist with surveying, clearing, farming, and livestock. He acquired land at **North Elba**, near present-day Lake Placid. National Park Service accounts give a purchase of **244 acres at $1 per acre**; the present historic site encompasses a somewhat different acreage because later site boundaries are not identical to Brown’s original transaction.

Brown called the Black settlement project **Timbuctoo/Timbukto** in correspondence. The actual settlement was dispersed, difficult to farm, and only partially successful, but North Elba became Brown’s most important permanent family home and eventually his burial place.

### 1850–1851: Fugitive Slave Act and the League of Gileadites

The **Fugitive Slave Act of 1850** radicalized many Northern antislavery networks by empowering federal recapture of people accused of escaping slavery and penalizing assistance to fugitives.

In Springfield on **January 15, 1851**, Brown helped organize a Black self-defense group generally known as the **League of Gileadites**. His written “Words of Advice” urged collective resistance to slave catchers, secrecy, discipline, mutual aid, legal attention, and force when necessary. The surviving text names twenty-seven participants and refers to additional people, producing a total often reconstructed as forty-four.

The original signed manuscript has not been continuously traceable; the text was first printed years later and preserved by Brown biographers. Nevertheless, the organization is supported by Springfield community history and Brown’s known activities. It is important because it shows Brown explicitly advocating organized armed resistance years before Kansas and Harpers Ferry.

### 1851–1854: return to Ohio and North Elba

After the English wool failure, Brown spent time in Ohio disentangling obligations to Perkins and continuing agricultural work. He returned intermittently to North Elba. His finances remained precarious. By the mid-1850s he was not a wealthy independent revolutionary; he depended increasingly on family labor and on donations from abolitionist supporters for political projects.

## Bleeding Kansas, 1854–1857

### Kansas-Nebraska Act and family migration

The **Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854** opened Kansas Territory to a contest over whether slavery would be permitted under popular sovereignty. Proslavery Missourians, Northern emigrant-aid settlers, antislavery militants, speculators, and national political organizations converged on the territory. Elections were marked by fraud and intimidation, and rival governments emerged.

Several of Brown’s sons migrated to Kansas. They requested weapons and assistance. Brown traveled west in **1855**, bringing arms and joining them. At this stage he entered sustained armed political conflict.

### Wakarusa War, late 1855

Brown and his sons appeared during the **Wakarusa War**, a confrontation around the free-state town of Lawrence. Although large-scale battle was avoided, Brown’s reputation among militant free-state settlers increased. He criticized leaders who accepted compromise rather than confronting proslavery forces.

### Sack of Lawrence and Sumner caning, May 1856

On **May 21, 1856**, proslavery forces sacked Lawrence, destroying newspaper presses and damaging buildings. The next day in Washington, Representative Preston Brooks brutally caned Senator Charles Sumner after Sumner’s antislavery speech. News and rumor intensified a sense among Brown and other militants that proslavery violence was advancing with impunity.

### Pottawatomie killings, May 24–25, 1856

On the night of **May 24–25, 1856**, Brown led a party that included four of his sons, a son-in-law, and other associates into the Pottawatomie Creek area. They took five proslavery settlers from cabins and killed them with broadswords/knives:

- **James P. Doyle**
- **William Doyle**
- **Drury Doyle**
- **Allen Wilkinson**
- **William Sherman**

Brown’s men spared women, children, and some men they questioned. James Doyle’s wife and a younger son were not killed. The selective character of the killings does not change the documentary fact that the five men were deliberately seized and killed outside formal combat or judicial process.

Sources differ about Brown’s direct physical role in each death. The strongest reconstruction is that he commanded the expedition and selected or approved the targets; witnesses and later accounts indicate that his sons and associates performed most of the sword attacks, while Brown fired into the wounded James Doyle after the attack. Brown’s command responsibility is not seriously in doubt in modern scholarship.

#### Motive and target selection

Brown and his defenders characterized the action as retaliation and preemptive self-defense against men associated with threats to free-state settlers. Later hostile accounts characterized the victims as harmless political opponents murdered solely for their views. The surviving evidence indicates that the area was saturated with threat, intimidation, and partisan violence, but the exact conduct of each victim before the killings is unevenly documented.

#### Brown’s denial and early biography

Brown did not publicly take clear responsibility for Pottawatomie during his lifetime. Early sympathetic biographer **James Redpath** defended him in ways that obscured his responsibility. Later documentary work, including Sanborn, Villard, Kansas research, and modern historians, established the Brown party’s role.

**Status:** documented political killing, clandestinely carried out. It was never adjudicated against Brown in a Kansas murder trial because he was not tried for it. Modern legal or moral labels such as massacre, assassination, reprisal, or terrorism vary by historian; the underlying killings and Brown’s command role are well established.

### Battle of Black Jack, June 2, 1856

On **June 2, 1856**, Brown’s group fought proslavery forces commanded by **Henry Clay Pate** near Black Jack. After fighting, Pate and several of his men surrendered. Brown’s success enhanced his reputation as a field commander among militant free-state settlers.

### Osawatomie, August 30, 1856

A much larger proslavery force attacked **Osawatomie** on August 30. Brown organized a defense but was badly outnumbered. The town was burned. Brown’s son **Frederick Brown II** was killed before or during the action by proslavery settler Martin White. Brown’s stand, even though tactically unsuccessful, produced the enduring epithet **“Osawatomie Brown.”**

### Kansas reputation

By the end of 1856 Brown had become simultaneously:

- a hero to militant antislavery settlers and sympathetic Northern press;
- a wanted killer and symbol of Northern aggression to proslavery Kansans and Missourians;
- a useful fundraising figure for Eastern antislavery committees;
- an increasingly independent operator who distrusted moderate political leadership.

The Kansas experience altered his strategic thinking. He became convinced that small, disciplined groups could survive against larger forces if they possessed mobility, local knowledge, weapons, and moral commitment.

## Eastern Fundraising, Revolutionary Planning, and the “Secret Six,” 1857–1859

### Fundraising tours

In **1857** Brown traveled through New England and New York seeking money, arms, and political support. He spoke in Concord, Boston, and other reform centers. His Kansas fame gave him access to figures far wealthier and more socially connected than he was.

### Major supporters later called the Secret Six

The label **“Secret Six”** was applied to a group of six prominent Brown supporters:

- **Gerrit Smith** — wealthy New York abolitionist and land reformer.
- **George Luther Stearns** — Massachusetts businessman and major financier of antislavery Kansas activity.
- **Franklin Benjamin Sanborn** — young Concord reformer and later Brown biographer.
- **Thomas Wentworth Higginson** — minister, militant abolitionist, and later Union officer.
- **Samuel Gridley Howe** — physician, reformer, and husband of Julia Ward Howe.
- **Theodore Parker** — influential radical minister.

These men were not a neatly incorporated revolutionary directorate. They supplied money, weapons, introductions, encouragement, and in some cases knowledge that Brown was preparing an armed antislavery operation. The surviving record does **not** show that every member possessed the same detailed advance knowledge of the final Harpers Ferry plan.

The distinction mattered after the raid. Some fled temporarily to Canada or Europe; some denied knowing the target; Senate investigators tried to determine whether an organized Northern conspiracy had knowingly financed an invasion of Virginia. The Senate committee’s minority report found no sufficient evidence that absent Northern citizens had knowingly participated in the October 1859 raid as accessories, while acknowledging that some associates had known of earlier schemes. The term “Secret Six” therefore describes a support network better than a uniform chain of command.

### George L. Stearns

Stearns was especially important financially. He had become involved with Brown through Massachusetts antislavery support for Kansas and supplied weapons and money. Brown’s correspondence sometimes requested funds under intentionally vague descriptions such as “secret service.” After Brown’s execution, Stearns also supported Brown family members and attempted to recover bodies of raiders from medical institutions.

### Hugh Forbes: trainer, critic, and security breach

Brown hired **Hugh Forbes**, an English military adventurer and former soldier, to provide drill and tactical instruction. Forbes quarreled with Brown over money and strategy. He opposed aspects of Brown’s plan and threatened to expose it. Information reached abolitionist allies and politicians, contributing to postponement and anxiety about Brown’s secrecy. Forbes’s role demonstrates that Brown’s conspiracy was never completely secure.

### Weapons

Brown accumulated arms originally associated with Kansas antislavery aid and later procured additional weapons for a larger slave-liberation campaign. Material associated with the Harpers Ferry plan included:

- approximately **200 Sharps rifles/carbines** linked to antislavery Kansas support;
- revolvers;
- roughly **950–1,000 pikes**, manufactured for Brown’s project.

The pikes were intended as inexpensive weapons that could be distributed to people who joined after the operation began. Surviving Harpers Ferry pikes became highly charged relics and were later used in political propaganda about Northern abolitionist aggression.

## Harriet Tubman and Black Abolitionist Networks

Brown’s relationship with **Harriet Tubman** is among the most important connections in the final planning period. National Park Service research places their first documented meeting in **April 1858** at **St. Catharines, Canada West**. Brown addressed or referred to Tubman as **“General Tubman.”**

Tubman’s value to Brown was practical: she knew Underground Railroad routes, border geography, Black communities in Canada, people who had escaped slavery, and possible recruits. She assisted his efforts to establish contacts and understood his willingness to use force.

Tubman did not take part in the Harpers Ferry raid. Later accounts commonly say illness prevented her from joining. A famous recollection says that before the raid Tubman dreamed of an old bearded man with two younger men struck down and later interpreted the dream as Brown and his sons. This belongs to Tubman/Brown memory and hagiography; it is not evidence of a verifiable prediction.

Brown also depended on Black abolitionists and activists including **Frederick Douglass**, **Martin R. Delany**, **William C. Nell**, **Jermain Loguen**, and communities in Springfield, New York, Canada West, and elsewhere. The degree of agreement with Brown’s tactics varied sharply.

## Chatham Convention and Provisional Government, May 1858

On **May 8, 1858**, Brown convened a clandestine meeting at **Chatham, Canada West**. Participants included Black and white supporters; surviving reconstructions commonly give approximately twelve white and thirty-four Black attendees, although the precision of participant lists varies.

The convention adopted Brown’s **“Provisional Constitution and Ordinances for the People of the United States.”** The text set out an alternative governing structure for territory or communities liberated from slavery, including offices, legislative procedures, military organization, rules for property, and conduct.

Brown was chosen **commander-in-chief** of the projected organization. The constitution is crucial evidence against interpreting Harpers Ferry as merely an impulsive suicidal gesture. Brown imagined an extended campaign with political administration, not a one-night symbolic protest.

The document did not purport to become the ordinary Constitution of the United States through legal amendment. Its exact constitutional theory is debated. Legal historian Robert L. Tsai treats it as evidence of a serious alternative constitutional project; critics saw it in 1859 as proof of treasonous intent.

## Missouri Liberation Raid, December 1858–March 1859

On the night of **December 20–21, 1858**, Brown led men from Kansas into **Missouri**. Separate parties entered slaveholding properties. The operation liberated **eleven enslaved people**; one enslaved woman was pregnant and later gave birth during the long journey north. Slaveholder **David Cruise** was killed during the raid by a member of Brown’s force.

The party seized horses, wagons, supplies, and other property useful for flight. Proslavery authorities described the action as murder, theft, and slave stealing; Brown understood it as liberation and wartime confiscation.

Brown then undertook an extended movement through free territory to deliver the group beyond the reach of slave catchers. The fugitives traveled more than a thousand miles. In **Iowa**, **Chicago**, and **Detroit**, antislavery contacts assisted them. Private detective and antislavery sympathizer **Allan Pinkerton** helped raise funds and transportation in Chicago. On **March 12, 1859**, the group crossed into **Canada**, where slavery was not legal and U.S. fugitive-slave process could not compel their return.

This raid was a tactical success in the narrow sense that the people Brown removed from slavery reached Canada. It also deepened the price on his head and confirmed his willingness to use armed interstate incursions and property seizure.

---

## Final Harpers Ferry Preparations, 1859

### Return to the target area

Brown selected **Harpers Ferry, Virginia**, because it contained a major federal armory, arsenal, and rifle works and lay near mountain routes extending through the Appalachians. He did not plan simply to hold the town indefinitely against the United States Army. His broader concept was to seize arms, attract enslaved people, withdraw into mountain terrain, organize mobile bands, raid plantations, move liberated people north or into defensible zones, and make slave property increasingly insecure.

Whether that larger plan had any realistic chance of success is a separate question from whether Brown possessed one. Frederick Douglass famously concluded it did not.

### Kennedy Farm

On **July 3, 1859**, Brown arrived in the Harpers Ferry area with sons and associates. Using the name **Isaac Smith**, he rented the **Kennedy Farm** in Maryland, about five miles from the town. Men, arms, and supplies were concealed there.

Brown’s daughter **Annie Brown** and daughter-in-law **Martha Brown** stayed at the farm during much of the summer, helping make the household appear ordinary and watching for suspicious neighbors. They left before the raid.

### Chambersburg meeting with Frederick Douglass

In **August 1859**, Brown met **Frederick Douglass** near Chambersburg, Pennsylvania, and urged him to join. Douglass later remembered warning that an attack on a federal armory in a town enclosed by heights would be a **“perfect steel-trap.”** Douglass refused.

**Shields Green**, a formerly enslaved man associated with Douglass, chose to go with Brown. Douglass’s refusal is significant because it demonstrates that deep admiration for Brown did not imply agreement with Harpers Ferry strategy.

---

## Harpers Ferry Raid, October 16–18, 1859

### Composition of the force

Brown’s immediate Harpers Ferry organization consisted of Brown and **twenty-one male followers**, commonly described as sixteen white followers and five Black followers in addition to Brown depending on counting convention. The five Black raiders were:

- **Dangerfield Newby**
- **Lewis Sheridan Leary**
- **John Anthony Copeland Jr.**
- **Shields Green**
- **Osborne Perry Anderson**

The white raiders included Brown’s sons **Watson** and **Oliver**, his son **Owen**, and men such as **John E. Cook Jr.**, **Aaron D. Stevens**, **Jeremiah G. Anderson**, **Albert Hazlett**, **Edwin Coppoc**, **Barclay Coppoc**, **William H. Leeman**, **William Thompson**, **Dauphin Thompson**, **Stewart Taylor**, **John Henry Kagi**, **Charles Plummer Tidd**, and **Francis Jackson Meriam**.

Not every member occupied the same location during the action; some were used as guards, messengers, or rear support. This accounts for small differences in contemporary counts of how many physically entered the town at a particular hour.

### October 16: seizure

On the evening of **Sunday, October 16**, Brown’s force moved from the Kennedy Farm. By late evening they had seized key points including the railroad bridge, the federal armory/arsenal complex, and the rifle works. They cut or controlled communications imperfectly and detained local residents as hostages.

Detachments went into the countryside and seized prominent slaveholder **Lewis Washington**, a great-grandnephew of George Washington, and **John Allstadt**, bringing them and enslaved people from their properties into Harpers Ferry.

### Heyward Shepherd

Around the railway station during the early hours of October 17, **Heyward Shepherd**, a free Black baggage master/railroad employee, was shot and mortally wounded by a member of Brown’s party. He died after lingering for hours.

Shepherd’s death became politically symbolic. Decades later, white Southern heritage organizations built a memorial portraying him as representative of loyal Black opposition to Brown. The monument belongs to the twentieth-century memory struggle and does not establish Shepherd’s political views toward slavery; he was a free railroad worker caught in the armed confrontation.

### Brown allows a train to depart

Brown detained a Baltimore & Ohio train but eventually allowed it to continue. This decision was strategically catastrophic because the train carried news of the seizure eastward. Brown’s motives are reconstructed as a mixture of reluctance to harm uninvolved passengers, concern for normal traffic, and poor operational judgment.

### October 17: town and militia mobilization

By daylight Harpers Ferry residents and nearby militia units began firing on Brown’s positions. The raiders became separated. Local men and militia killed or captured several of Brown’s followers.

**Dangerfield Newby** was killed. **John Henry Kagi** and others at the rifle works were driven into or toward the Shenandoah River; Kagi was killed. **Lewis Leary** was mortally wounded. **John Copeland** was captured. Other raiders were killed in street fighting.

Mayor **Fontaine Beckham** was shot and killed. In retaliation, an enraged crowd murdered captured raider **William Thompson** and threw his body from the bridge/riverside area. This was an extrajudicial killing by townspeople during the battle.

Brown’s sons **Watson** and **Oliver** were mortally wounded. Men attempting to communicate under flags of truce were fired upon; the treatment of truce parties later appeared in defense argument and historical controversy.

### Local enslaved participation: a disputed question

A central controversy immediately arose over whether enslaved people voluntarily joined Brown.

**White Virginian and official narratives:** many witnesses insisted that local enslaved people did not voluntarily rise and that those brought into Harpers Ferry had been coerced or remained loyal to owners. This claim became politically important because a failed slave uprising could be presented as proof of enslaved contentment and Brown’s delusion.

**Osborne P. Anderson’s account:** Anderson, the only Black Harpers Ferry raider to escape and survive, published *A Voice from Harper’s Ferry* in 1861. He explicitly described enslaved local men as taking up arms and assisting Brown’s force.

**Documentary conclusion:** Brown’s men unquestionably removed enslaved people from nearby slaveholding properties and brought some into the operation. Some were armed. The precise number who acted voluntarily, under what circumstances, and for how long cannot be reconstructed with certainty. The categorical claim that “no enslaved person joined” is not the only firsthand account and should not be repeated as settled fact.

### Failure to withdraw

Brown’s greatest operational failure was remaining in Harpers Ferry after the initial seizure. The armory was in a low river town surrounded by heights. As militia arrived, escape routes closed. Brown became increasingly tied to hostages and wounded men and withdrew into the small armory fire-engine house, later known as **John Brown’s Fort**.

### Federal response and Robert E. Lee

President James Buchanan’s government sent U.S. Marines under overall command of **Colonel Robert E. Lee**, with **Lieutenant J. E. B. Stuart** serving as Lee’s aide. Approximately ninety Marines reached Harpers Ferry late October 17.

On the morning of **October 18**, Stuart delivered a surrender demand. Brown refused. Marines battered through the engine-house door and stormed the building.

Lieutenant **Israel Green** attacked Brown with his sword. Brown received head wounds and was stabbed/struck during capture but survived. Raiders **Jeremiah Anderson** and **Dauphin Thompson** were killed in the final assault. **Shields Green** and **Edwin Coppoc** were among the men captured.

No hostages were killed in the final Marine assault.

### Casualties and immediate outcome

Ten of Brown’s men were killed or mortally wounded during the raid; others were captured; five escaped the immediate area. Brown was captured alive. Civilian, railroad, militia, and Marine deaths made the action deadly beyond Brown’s own force.

The raid lasted roughly **thirty-six hours** from initial seizure to final capture.

---

## Capture, Interrogation, and Public Statements

Brown was badly injured but mentally alert after capture. Virginia officials, journalists, and politicians questioned him. Governor **Henry A. Wise** personally observed and spoke with him and came away impressed by his courage and coherence even while condemning his actions.

Brown denied that his purpose had been indiscriminate killing, robbery for private gain, or a generalized massacre of whites. He said his goal was to free enslaved people. He did not deny organizing an armed invasion.

These interviews immediately began changing his public position. A military defeat became a national publicity event. Brown’s calmness under interrogation and willingness to accept death became as politically consequential as the raid itself.

---

## Trial: *Virginia v. John Brown*, October–November 1859

### Jurisdiction and charges

Brown was held in **Charles Town**, Jefferson County, Virginia. Virginia chose to prosecute quickly in state court rather than leave the case to federal authorities.

The grand jury indicted him on charges commonly summarized as:

1. **Treason against the Commonwealth of Virginia**;
2. **Conspiring with enslaved people and others to produce insurrection**;
3. **Murder** arising from deaths during the raid.

The treason was **state treason**. Brown was a New York resident/citizen, and his lawyers challenged the proposition that he owed Virginia the allegiance necessary for treason.

### Judge and counsel

- **Judge:** Richard Parker.
- **Principal prosecutor:** Andrew Hunter, with county prosecutor Charles B. Harding involved in the proceedings.
- **Early/court-appointed defense:** Lawson Botts and Thomas C. Green; Charles J. Faulkner also appeared in the earliest preliminary stage.
- **George H. Hoyt:** young Massachusetts lawyer sent by Northern supporters; joined the defense but had limited Virginia criminal-law experience.
- **Samuel Chilton:** experienced Washington lawyer brought in for the defense.
- **Hiram Griswold:** Cleveland lawyer and abolitionist; delivered important defense argument.

Brown became dissatisfied with Botts and Green and declared that he had no confidence in them; they withdrew. Chilton and Griswold then carried much of the defense.

### Physical condition and requests for delay

Brown entered proceedings soon after severe wounds. At points he lay on a cot in the courtroom. Defense requests for additional delay—based on his injuries, hearing difficulty, absent witnesses, and the arrival of chosen counsel—were largely denied or limited. A physician examined him and the court concluded he was able to proceed.

The speed of the trial became controversial at the time and remains part of legal historical analysis. Speed alone does not nullify the formal verdict; it is relevant to evaluating procedure and defense preparation.

### Defense arguments

The defense advanced several arguments:

- Brown, as a non-Virginian, could not commit treason against Virginia because he owed the Commonwealth no allegiance.
- His aim was liberation of enslaved people rather than “insurrection” understood as indiscriminate riot, arson, or murder.
- Responsibility for individual killings had to be proven rather than inferred solely from leadership.
- Violence directed at Brown’s men, including firing on persons under flags of truce, complicated the prosecution’s narrative of the battle.

The court rejected the key legal arguments.

### Verdict

On **October 31, 1859**, the jury found Brown guilty on all principal counts. The jury deliberated only a short period.

### Allocution and sentence, November 2

On **November 2**, before sentence, Brown delivered one of the most famous courtroom statements of the nineteenth century. He freely admitted that he had intended to free enslaved people. He denied that he had intended murder, treason in the sense charged, destruction of property for its own sake, or a generalized slave rebellion. He grounded his conduct in the biblical command to remember those in bonds and declared that he believed he had acted on behalf of the poor.

Judge Parker sentenced him to hang on **December 2, 1859**.

---

## The Insanity Question

After conviction, supporters and relatives sent affidavits describing mental illness in branches of Brown’s maternal family and proposed that hereditary insanity might support clemency or a legal challenge. Attorney George Hoyt collected some of this material.

Brown **rejected an insanity defense**. He objected to being represented as insane and insisted on the rational and moral character of his actions.

### Evidentiary status

- **Documented:** affidavits and claims about mental illness in Brown’s family existed and were transmitted to the defense/governor.
- **Documented:** Brown refused to rely on insanity as an explanation.
- **Not documented:** a nineteenth-century court or qualified examining physician finding Brown legally insane.
- **Later interpretation:** historians, biographers, political enemies, and admirers have repeatedly used psychological labels—monomania, fanaticism, paranoia, grandiosity, messianism, depression, bipolarity, and others. Retrospective diagnosis from fragmentary nineteenth-century evidence is inherently uncertain and should not be presented as a medical fact.

Brown’s prison interviews, correspondence, trial participation, and practical planning demonstrate sustained coherence. Coherence does not by itself settle all psychological questions, but it makes the simple contemporary caricature of an incoherent “madman” historically inadequate.

---

## Prison, Correspondence, and Transformation into a Public Symbol

Brown spent approximately six weeks awaiting execution. During this period he wrote extensively to family members, supporters, journalists, and acquaintances. He carefully framed his actions and death.

His letters combined:

- concern for Mary and the children;
- instructions about debts, property, and burial;
- biblical reflection;
- confidence that his execution would advance abolition;
- rejection of rescue attempts that might sacrifice others;
- willingness to die.

Brown recognized that the spectacle of his execution could do what the failed raid had not. One prison statement expressed the view that he might be “worth inconceivably more to hang” than for another purpose. The line became part of the martyr narrative, but it also reflects his own political calculation.

Northern visitors, letters, newspaper reprints, sermons, and public meetings made Charles Town a media center. Southern authorities feared rescue attempts and insurrection; armed forces guarded the region.

---

## Death, December 2, 1859

### Final family meeting

Mary Brown arrived and met John in prison on **December 1**. They discussed family and burial arrangements. Their final meeting was controlled by authorities and limited in time.

### Last written note

On the morning of December 2 Brown wrote a short note predicting that the “crimes” of the land would not be purged without blood. The statement is authentic and survives in documentary tradition. It was written seventeen months before the Civil War began and later acquired the status of prophecy.

### Execution

Brown was taken from the jail to a gallows outside Charles Town under heavy military guard. Virginia militia and cadets were present. **Thomas J. “Stonewall” Jackson**, then an instructor at Virginia Military Institute, attended with cadets. **John Wilkes Booth**, later Abraham Lincoln’s assassin, was also present after joining a militia company for the occasion; surviving correspondence supports his attendance.

Brown was hanged on **December 2, 1859**, aged fifty-nine.

### Body and burial

Mary Brown received the body. It was returned north and brought to the family farm at North Elba. Brown was buried there on **December 8, 1859**, near a large boulder on the property. The grave became a pilgrimage site.

Several raiders did not receive comparable treatment. Bodies of some Black and white raiders were delivered to medical schools for dissection or anatomical display. Later efforts by families and supporters recovered some remains, including those of Brown’s son Watson.

---

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Tanning enterprises

Brown’s most durable technical trade was tanning and currying leather. His father trained him in Ohio; Brown later operated or managed tanneries in Ohio and Pennsylvania. The New Richmond tannery combined business, home, local civic participation, and reported fugitive-slave assistance.

### Surveying and land speculation

Brown taught himself surveying and used it professionally. During the 1830s he participated in rapidly appreciating and then collapsing Western Reserve land markets. His activities included the Franklin Mills/Kent area and the Franklin Land Company. He also pursued canal-related opportunities.

### Bankruptcy and lawsuits

Brown’s 1830s expansion was heavily credit-dependent. The Panic of 1837 damaged him severely. Creditor conflict produced litigation in multiple jurisdictions; formal bankruptcy followed in 1842. He lost most of his assets.

This is a documented history of financial failure and legal conflict, **not a documented history of fraud or embezzlement**. Sources used here do not establish that Brown secretly enriched himself at partners’ expense.

### Perkins & Brown wool enterprise

With Simon Perkins Jr., Brown created a wool-growing and marketing operation intended to improve returns to producers and weaken commission merchants’ market power. The venture had real technical sophistication—Brown was considered knowledgeable about sheep and wool—but failed commercially.

The attempt to force higher wool prices by withholding supply and selling in England misread market conditions. Litigation and losses followed. Brown’s later revolutionary dependence on donors cannot be separated from this collapse of personal commercial independence.

### Timbuctoo / North Elba

Brown’s North Elba project was partly agricultural, partly social, and partly political. He intended to live among Black land grantees and help them become economically secure landowners. The project intersected directly with New York’s discriminatory property qualification for Black voting rights.

### League of Gileadites

A political self-defense organization rather than a company. Brown’s instructions show attention to operational security, collective discipline, rapid mobilization, witnesses, legal aid, and physical resistance.

### Provisional government

The 1858 Chatham Constitution created a paper governmental structure for liberated territory. It included executive, legislative, judicial, and military roles and a commander-in-chief. It never became an operating territorial government on the scale Brown envisioned.

### Harpers Ferry armament project

Brown’s military procurement involved Sharps firearms, pistols, ammunition, and a large run of pikes. No invention or patent by Brown is known. His use of pikes was a logistical choice: they were cheaper and simpler than firearms and could be supplied to recruits after a seizure of more advanced weapons.

---

## Political / Religious / Intellectual Development

### Antislavery as religious obligation

Brown did not separate theology from politics. Slavery was, for him, a sin under divine judgment. He read biblical injunctions concerning justice and the oppressed as commands requiring action rather than private belief.

His thought drew especially on:

- covenantal responsibility;
- Old Testament judges, prophets, and warriors;
- the Golden Rule;
- identification with prisoners and the oppressed;
- the concept that bloodguilt could bring national judgment.

### From antislavery conviction to organized force

Brown’s militancy developed incrementally:

1. antislavery family formation and personal vow narratives;
2. assistance to fugitives and participation in abolitionist communities;
3. armed resistance to the Fugitive Slave Act through the Gileadites;
4. guerrilla fighting and retaliatory killings in Kansas;
5. interstate liberation raid into Missouri;
6. constitutional and logistical planning for a sustained slave-liberation war;
7. Harpers Ferry.

### Racial equality and paternalism

Brown’s racial attitudes were unusual among many white abolitionists of his era. He lived among Black settlers, sat and ate with Black people, solicited Black political advice, worked with Black armed participants, addressed Tubman as “General,” and envisaged Black officeholders in his provisional structure. Frederick Douglass and later Black writers repeatedly emphasized Brown’s willingness to treat Black people as human equals.

At the same time, some of Brown’s writing used forms now recognized as racial impersonation and stereotype. In **“Sambo’s Mistakes,”** written for the Black abolitionist newspaper *The Ram’s Horn*, Brown adopted a Black persona/dialect to criticize accommodation and ineffective forms of antislavery behavior. The piece is evidence both of his desire to intervene in Black political debate and of a paternalistic white-authored ventriloquism characteristic of its period. The two aspects need not be collapsed into one judgment.

### Political parties

Brown was not a conventional Republican Party operative. Republicans opposed slavery’s extension but party leaders rejected Brown’s invasion and denied organizational responsibility. Abraham Lincoln publicly separated the Republican Party from Brown, and the 1860 Republican platform denounced armed invasion of states or territories “under whatever pretext.”

Brown’s project was extra-electoral and revolutionary: he did not believe ordinary party politics would abolish slavery quickly enough.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Owen Brown

Father, employer, religious influence, and source of early antislavery conviction. Owen’s economic and moral example shaped John’s combination of craft work, frontier independence, and Protestant duty.

### Dianthe Lusk Brown

First wife and stabilizing domestic influence. Brown later credited her with moderating his temper and behavior.

### Mary Ann Day Brown

Second wife, mother of thirteen of his children, manager of a frequently unstable household, and custodian of the family after his death. Her role was logistical and emotional rather than command participation in Harpers Ferry.

### Brown’s sons

John Jr., Jason, Owen, Frederick, Watson, Salmon, and Oliver linked family and political action. Several fought in Kansas; Frederick was killed there; Watson and Oliver died from Harpers Ferry wounds; Owen escaped Harpers Ferry. Brown’s willingness to expose sons to lethal risk became a central theme in both hostile and admiring biographies.

### Simon Perkins Jr.

Business partner whose capital supported Brown’s most ambitious commercial enterprise. Their relationship ended in major financial loss but not a demonstrated finding of fraud by Brown.

### Gerrit Smith

Wealthy abolitionist, land donor, political reformer, North Elba connection, and later financial supporter. Smith’s exact prior knowledge of Harpers Ferry became a post-raid controversy.

### Frederick Douglass

Friend, political interlocutor, admirer, and strategic critic. Douglass accepted Brown’s seriousness and egalitarianism, helped him at points, but refused to join Harpers Ferry and later explained why he believed the operation was doomed.

### Harriet Tubman

Underground Railroad leader, recruiter/contact, and respected militant ally. Brown’s “General Tubman” designation became emblematic of their relationship.

### George L. Stearns

Financier, supplier, Massachusetts abolitionist organizer, and later supporter of Brown’s family and memory.

### Franklin B. Sanborn

Young supporter and later major documentary biographer. His access to Brown and the family gave him invaluable materials but also placed him inside the sympathetic memory tradition he helped create.

### Thomas Wentworth Higginson

Militant abolitionist supporter, one of the Secret Six, later Union officer commanding Black troops. He had previously been involved in attempts to resist fugitive-slave enforcement.

### Theodore Parker

Radical minister, intellectual and financial supporter, part of Brown’s New England network.

### Samuel Gridley Howe

Reformer and supporter; later investigated over Brown connections.

### Hugh Forbes

Military instructor turned critic and security threat. His dispute with Brown exposed divisions over strategy, pay, and competence.

### Martin R. Delany

Black abolitionist, physician, emigrationist, and political thinker who met Brown in Canada. Brown attempted to build support among Black leaders with different views of strategy and emigration.

### John Henry Kagi

One of Brown’s most capable younger lieutenants, secretary in the provisional organization, and a major organizer in the final conspiracy. Killed during the Harpers Ferry raid.

### Robert E. Lee and J. E. B. Stuart

U.S. Army officers who ended the raid. Their participation later became historically resonant because both became Confederate commanders.

### Henry A. Wise

Governor of Virginia. Wise condemned Brown but publicly acknowledged his courage and composure, contributing inadvertently to the image of Brown as formidable rather than insane.

### Andrew Hunter and Richard Parker

Hunter led the prosecution; Parker presided over the trial. Both became central figures in the legal end of Brown’s life.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, and Legal Questions

### 1. Pottawatomie killings

**Status:** documented killings; Brown’s command role established by accumulated testimony and scholarship. No Kansas criminal conviction of Brown occurred.

**Dispute:** precise culpability of victims, Brown’s personal physical role in each killing, and the proper analytical label—massacre, reprisal, assassination, guerrilla action, terrorism.

### 2. Missouri raid

**Status:** documented armed interstate incursion to free enslaved people, with seizure of private property and the killing of David Cruise by Brown’s party.

**Legal view at the time:** Missouri authorities and slaveholders treated the action as murder, theft, and slave stealing; Brown treated the seized property as resources for liberation.

### 3. Harpers Ferry

**Status:** documented armed seizure of federal property, hostage taking, interruption of railroad operations, deaths, and an attempt to initiate a broader antislavery campaign.

Brown did not personally shoot every person killed; as commander he bore organizational responsibility for the raid. Individual deaths require separate attribution where evidence permits.

### 4. Treason conviction

**Court finding:** guilty of treason against Virginia. The conviction was entered by a state court and sentence carried out.

**Legal dispute:** defense argued Brown, as a non-Virginian, did not owe the allegiance required for Virginia treason. The court rejected the argument. Later legal historians continue to debate the doctrinal soundness of the treason count separately from the murder and conspiracy charges.

### 5. Insanity allegations

**Status:** family-history affidavits and political claims existed; no court finding of insanity; Brown refused the defense. Later psychiatric diagnoses are retrospective speculation.

### 6. “Secret Six” conspiracy

**Documented:** six major Northern supporters supplied varying forms of assistance.

**Not established as a uniform fact:** that all six knew the exact target, date, tactical plan, and intended seizure in advance. Post-raid congressional investigation produced competing reports and political claims.

### 7. Financial misconduct

Brown had extensive debts, bankruptcies, failed ventures, property disputes, and lawsuits. The researched record does **not** establish embezzlement, fraudulent securities activity, bribery, money laundering, or corrupt public-office enrichment. His financial history is primarily one of overextension, failed speculation, litigation, and dependence on patrons.

### 8. Violence against noncombatants

At Pottawatomie, Brown’s party deliberately killed men at their homes rather than in an open battlefield. At Harpers Ferry, civilian and nonmilitary people were killed, including Heyward Shepherd and Mayor Beckham, although Brown’s stated plan was not indiscriminate massacre and he took hostages rather than killing them. These facts are central to later terrorism debates.

### 9. Hostage treatment

Brown took hostages, including slaveholders and civilians, as leverage and to control the town. Surviving hostages were not executed. Brown’s sparing of hostages has been used by historians who distinguish his operation from indiscriminate terrorism; hostage taking itself was coercive and illegal.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### Myth 1: Brown kissed a Black child on the way to the gallows

#### The story

A famous story says that as Brown was taken from jail to execution, a Black mother presented her infant and Brown stooped or leaned down to kiss the child.

#### Appearance and circulation

The story appeared in post-execution newspaper circulation and quickly entered sympathetic biography, poetry, prints, and painting. It became visually canonical through works such as **Thomas Hovenden’s *The Last Moments of John Brown*** and earlier popular imagery, including a painting by **Louis Ransom** exhibited in the 1860s.

#### Evidence for and against

Detailed study of execution logistics and contemporary witness accounts provides no reliable evidence that such an encounter occurred. Security was extreme; access to Brown on the route was restricted. The story’s early newspaper forms are inconsistent and appear after the event rather than as a securely corroborated eyewitness report.

#### Historical effect

The kiss transformed Brown’s execution into a compact abolitionist icon: white martyr, Black child, slavery, sacrifice, and future liberation in a single scene. Its power in art outlasted doubts about its factual basis.

**Classification:** probable post-execution legend, historically influential but not established fact.

### Myth 2: Brown was simply insane

#### Story

A hostile nineteenth-century explanation treated Harpers Ferry as the act of a lunatic or monomaniac rather than a political project.

#### Circulation

Virginia newspapers, political opponents, some Northern critics, and later biographers used insanity language. After conviction, actual affidavits concerning mental illness in Brown’s extended family gave the claim documentary material.

#### Evidence

Brown’s family history included reported mental illness, but Brown was never adjudicated insane. He rejected that defense. The Chatham constitution, multi-year fundraising, weapons procurement, coded travel, operational planning, prison correspondence, and courtroom arguments demonstrate an organized political program.

#### Effect

The “madman” image allowed critics to depoliticize the raid, while Brown’s admirers responded by exaggerating his serenity or prophetic rationality. Modern retrospective diagnoses remain disputed.

**Classification:** historical accusation and later interpretive tradition; no established diagnosis.

### Myth 3: Brown was “America’s first terrorist”

#### Story

Modern commentators sometimes label Brown the first American domestic terrorist, especially because of Pottawatomie and Harpers Ferry.

#### When it emerged

“Terrorist” in its modern security-policy sense is an anachronistic category when applied directly to 1850s legal language. The label became especially prominent in late twentieth- and early twenty-first-century debates about political violence.

#### Evidence and competing interpretations

At Pottawatomie Brown used clandestine lethal violence to intimidate a political enemy; at Harpers Ferry he deliberately initiated armed violence for political transformation. Those facts make the terrorism analogy intelligible.

Historian/legal scholar **Paul Finkelman**, however, argues that Brown does not fit a definition centered on indiscriminate killing and terror as the primary goal. Brown selected targets, spared some people, attacked a military arsenal, took rather than massacred hostages, and pursued a concrete military-political objective.

Other writers regard Pottawatomie’s exemplary violence—intended in part to frighten proslavery settlers—as terroristic regardless of Harpers Ferry’s military target.

**Classification:** contested modern analytical label, not a nineteenth-century court finding.

### Myth 4: No Black person would join Brown

#### Story

Southern post-raid narratives emphasized that enslaved people rejected Brown and remained loyal to slaveholders.

#### Evidence

Five Black men were members of Brown’s organized raiding force. Local enslaved people were brought into Harpers Ferry; some were armed. White witness testimony often described coercion or nonparticipation. Black raider **Osborne P. Anderson** described local enslaved people actively aiding the raid.

#### Effect

The “faithful slave” narrative became part of proslavery and later Lost Cause memory. The 1931 Heyward Shepherd memorial further transformed the first victim into a symbol of Black loyalty to the old order, although Shepherd’s own political views are not documented that way.

**Classification:** categorical claim disproved as to Brown’s force and disputed as to local enslaved participation.

### Myth 5: The Secret Six were a unified board that ordered Harpers Ferry

#### Story

A compact conspiracy narrative portrays six wealthy New England abolitionists as fully informed sponsors who collectively authorized the raid.

#### Evidence

All six were meaningful supporters, but their knowledge differed. Brown deliberately compartmentalized information. Post-raid testimony, flight, denials, private letters, and Senate investigation reveal uncertainty about who knew the exact target and timing.

#### Effect

The image of an organized Northern elite conspiracy was politically powerful in the South and useful to Democratic attacks on Republicans in 1859–1860.

**Classification:** simplification built on a real support network.

### Myth 6: Harriet Tubman prophetically foresaw Brown’s death

#### Story

Later Tubman narratives say she dreamed of a bearded old man and two younger heads struck down, later identifying them as Brown and his sons.

#### Evidence

The dream survives in later recollection, not contemporaneous documentation before the raid.

#### Effect

It integrated Brown into Tubman’s sacred biography and the broader prophetic memory of abolition.

**Classification:** remembered visionary narrative / hagiographic tradition; not independently verifiable prophecy.

### Myth 7: Brown “predicted the Civil War”

#### Story

Brown’s December 2 note saying the nation’s crimes would be purged with blood is frequently described as a prophecy of the Civil War.

#### Evidence

The note is authentic. Civil War began in April 1861. Brown did anticipate more bloodshed over slavery, but many Americans already feared sectional war. The “prophecy” is therefore genuine as a prediction but mythologized in hindsight by its apparent fulfillment.

**Classification:** authentic prediction; prophetic status is retrospective interpretation.

### Myth 8: Brown started the Civil War

#### Story

Frederick Douglass later said, in effect, that Brown began the war that ended slavery; later writers called him a meteor or herald of war.

#### Evidence

Harpers Ferry did not mechanically cause secession. Sectional conflict already involved slavery expansion, fugitive-slave enforcement, Kansas, party collapse, Dred Scott, and decades of constitutional conflict. The raid nevertheless intensified Southern fears of armed Northern abolitionism and became a major issue in the 1860 political atmosphere.

#### Effect

Brown became a symbolic causal figure even where direct political causation was more complex.

**Classification:** historically influential interpretive claim, not a literal single-cause explanation.

### Myth 9: Brown as saint, Christ, Moses, or Old Testament prophet

After his capture, sympathetic intellectuals rapidly sacralized Brown. **Henry David Thoreau** publicly defended him before execution. **Ralph Waldo Emerson** reportedly compared his coming gallows to the cross. **Victor Hugo** appealed for his life and warned of the consequences of execution. **Frederick Douglass** later contrasted his own willingness to live for the enslaved with Brown’s willingness to die. Black and white abolitionist memorial culture repeatedly compared Brown with biblical liberators and martyrs.

These were political-religious analogies, not claims of literal sainthood. They helped make Brown’s execution more culturally durable than his military operation.

### Myth 10: Brown as bloodthirsty monster

Proslavery and later hostile imagery collapsed Pottawatomie, Harpers Ferry, imagined slave massacre, and racial panic into a figure of indiscriminate bloodlust. The five Pottawatomie killings provide real evidence of deliberate lethal violence. The broader image becomes inaccurate when it implies that Brown’s strategy was random killing: he repeatedly spared people, protected hostages, and articulated a specific liberation program.

**Classification:** hostile political mythology built around documented violent acts but enlarged beyond them.

### Myth 11: Brown as flawless egalitarian modern hero

Sympathetic memory can flatten contradictions in the opposite direction. Brown’s unusual commitment to Black equality coexisted with patriarchal family authority, secretive command, willingness to expose followers and sons to lethal risks, and paternalistic forms of racial representation such as “Sambo’s Mistakes.” His historical distinctiveness does not require treating him as socially identical to a twenty-first-century egalitarian.

**Classification:** retrospective hagiographic simplification.

## Works by John Brown

Brown did not publish a conventional book during his lifetime. His significant works consist mainly of letters, political documents, organizational instructions, statements, and speeches. Dates below refer to composition or first known publication when identifiable.

### “Sambo’s Mistakes” — c. 1847; published 1848

A satirical/polemical piece written for the Black abolitionist newspaper **The Ram’s Horn**. Brown wrote in an assumed Black voice/persona to criticize what he regarded as errors of accommodation, misplaced trust, and ineffective antislavery conduct. Manuscript material survives in Brown’s letter book at the Maryland Center for History and Culture.

**Authorship:** genuine Brown text; not a later attribution.

### Autobiographical letter to Henry L. Stearns — July 15, 1857

Usually called Brown’s autobiographical letter. Written in the third person under the device of telling the story of “a certain boy” named John. Covers birth through early adulthood, childhood losses, education, War of 1812 experience, the enslaved-boy episode, religion, work, character faults, and first marriage.

**Authorship:** genuine Brown letter. Some later printings/transcriptions contain date irregularities; the 1857 date is supported by the addressee/context and early publication tradition.

### “Words of Advice” / League of Gileadites document — January 15, 1851

Operational rules for resisting fugitive-slave capture in Springfield. It advocates organization, mutual support, secrecy, witnesses, legal attention, and physical resistance.

**Authorship:** attributed to and consistent with Brown; surviving transmission is through later print rather than an easily traceable original signed manuscript.

### *Provisional Constitution and Ordinances for the People of the United States* — 1858

Drafted by Brown and adopted at the Chatham convention. Creates governing offices and rules for a projected liberated antislavery polity.

**Authorship:** genuine Brown political document; surviving federal archival copies became evidence in investigation and trial discourse.

### *A Declaration of Liberty by the Representatives of the Slave Population of the United States of America* — 1859

A revolutionary declaration associated with Brown’s planned campaign, consciously echoing the Declaration of Independence and indicting slavery. Survives through archival/edited traditions.

**Authorship:** generally attributed to Brown and his circle; Brown was the central authorial force.

### Letters requesting and accounting for support — 1850s

Brown wrote numerous fundraising and operational letters to Stearns, Smith, Sanborn, family members, Kansas committees, and others. These are essential evidence for his finances, secrecy, procurement, and strategy.

### Prison interviews and letters — October–December 1859

A large body of correspondence from Charles Town addresses Mary Brown, children, supporters, ministers, journalists, and strangers. Some were immediately printed in newspapers. They were later collected by Redpath, Sanborn, Villard, and modern editors.

**Authorship:** individual letters vary in manuscript survival; many are firmly authenticated, while newspaper versions should be checked against manuscripts where possible.

### Courtroom address — November 2, 1859

Brown’s statement before sentence. Survives in contemporary court/newspaper publication and broadside form.

**Authorship:** oral statement transcribed contemporaneously; wording varies slightly among reports.

### Last written note — December 2, 1859

The short “crimes of this guilty land…with Blood” note written on the morning of execution.

**Authorship:** genuine and widely reproduced from the original tradition.

### Posthumous collections — not books authored as such by Brown

- **James Redpath, ed., *The Public Life of Capt. John Brown* (1860).** Contains Brown documents but is Redpath’s biography and editorial construction.
- **F. B. Sanborn, ed., *The Life and Letters of John Brown* (1885).** Major letter/document collection embedded in a sympathetic biography.
- **Louis A. DeCaro Jr., ed., *John Brown Speaks: Letters and Statements from Charlestown* (2015).** Modern scholarly edited collection of Brown’s final-period texts.

These should not be catalogued as lifetime books “written by John Brown” simply because they contain his words.

## Books and Major Works About John Brown

### Immediate and nineteenth-century works

1. **Richard D. DeWitt, *The Life, Trial and Execution of Captain John Brown* (1859).** Immediate trial/execution compilation; valuable for contemporary texts but sensational and rapidly produced.

5. **Frederick Douglass, “John Brown” lectures (1860 and later) and the Storer College address (1881).** First-person recollection and Black abolitionist interpretation.

## Films, Television, Documentaries, Art, Music, and Archival Material

### Film and television

- ** *Santa Fe Trail* (1940), directed by Michael Curtiz.** Raymond Massey plays Brown. Major Hollywood fictionalization; compresses chronology and uses Brown as a fanatic antagonist within a story centered on future Civil War officers.
- ** *Seven Angry Men* (1955), directed by Charles Marquis Warren.** Raymond Massey again plays Brown in a more biographical treatment.
- **PBS *American Experience: John Brown’s Holy War* (2000), produced by Robert Kenner.** Dedicated documentary combining historians, dramatized readings, family/business history, Kansas, Harpers Ferry, and the martyr/madman debate.
- **PBS *American Experience: The Abolitionists* (2013).** Multi-part documentary series in which Brown appears within the broader abolitionist movement alongside Douglass, Garrison, Stowe, and others.
- ** *The Good Lord Bird* (2020).** Limited television series starring Ethan Hawke as Brown, adapted from James McBride’s novel. Deliberately comic, grotesque, and fictionalized rather than documentary.

### Visual art

- **Thomas Hovenden, *The Last Moments of John Brown* (1880s).** Famous painting of the apocryphal child-kiss scene; major artifact of Brown hagiography.
- **Jacob Lawrence, *The Legend of John Brown* (1941).** Twenty-two-panel narrative series. Lawrence later recreated the cycle as screenprints in the 1970s. One of the most important Black visual reinterpretations of Brown.
- **Louis Ransom, *John Brown on His Way to Execution* / related last-kiss image (1860s exhibition).** Helped popularize the child-kiss legend.
- **John Steuart Curry, *Tragic Prelude* (1930s–1940s), Kansas State Capitol.** Brown appears as an enormous, armed, biblical-looking figure amid tornado, prairie fire, troops, and sectional conflict—an iconic visualization of “Bleeding Kansas.”
- Numerous Currier & Ives and other popular prints transformed Brown’s capture and execution into mass visual culture.

### Music

#### “John Brown’s Body”

The marching song **“John Brown’s Body”** became one of the most recognizable Union songs. Its origin is complicated: an early camp song was connected with a Massachusetts soldier also named John Brown, then rapidly fused with the abolitionist’s name and martyr legend. Union troops sang versions asserting that Brown’s “soul is marching on.”

#### “Battle Hymn of the Republic”

**Julia Ward Howe** wrote new lyrics to the already circulating “John Brown’s Body” tune, producing **“The Battle Hymn of the Republic.”** The tune thereby carried Brown’s musical afterlife into one of the dominant patriotic hymns of the Civil War and later American political culture.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Immediate Northern reaction

Northern reaction was divided. Many newspapers and political leaders condemned the raid as criminal and reckless. Republican leaders urgently denied party involvement. At the same time, abolitionist meetings, church bells, sermons, poems, and memorial gatherings transformed Brown into a martyr.

**Henry David Thoreau** was among the fastest and most uncompromising defenders. **Ralph Waldo Emerson**, **Bronson Alcott**, and other New England intellectuals participated in a culture of sanctification. **Frederick Douglass**, while retaining his criticism of the Harpers Ferry strategy, later made Brown central to the history of emancipation.

### Immediate Southern reaction

In the South, Harpers Ferry confirmed fears that Northern antislavery ideology could turn into armed invasion and slave revolt. Militias expanded. Southern newspapers sought evidence connecting Brown to Republican leaders and wealthy abolitionists. The pikes became vivid propaganda objects.

Brown’s raid did not create sectional conflict, but it made the prospect of Northern-supported slave insurrection concrete in a way that speeches and books had not.

### Election of 1860

Democrats and Southern political speakers repeatedly invoked Brown to attack Republicans. Republicans denied complicity. **Abraham Lincoln**, at Cooper Union and elsewhere, rejected Brown’s invasion while maintaining opposition to slavery’s expansion. The Republican platform condemned armed invasion of states and territories.

Brown therefore entered electoral politics posthumously as both accusation and symbol.

### Civil War

Once war began, “John Brown’s Body” turned the executed prisoner into a marching presence in Union armies. The Emancipation Proclamation and enlistment of Black soldiers altered the meaning further. To many Union soldiers and abolitionists, Brown appeared to have anticipated the war’s emancipatory turn.

### African American memory

Brown’s reputation in Black abolitionist and later Black historical writing was often more durable than in white mainstream memory. Douglass, Du Bois, Jacob Lawrence, and later scholars emphasized that Brown not only opposed slavery abstractly but accepted Black political and military partnership.

The five Black Harpers Ferry raiders—especially Shields Green and John Copeland, who were executed—have increasingly been restored to the narrative as actors rather than appendages to Brown.

### Lost Cause counter-memory and Heyward Shepherd

In the twentieth century the death of free Black railroad worker **Heyward Shepherd** was appropriated into a counter-narrative. The **Heyward Shepherd Memorial** at Harpers Ferry, promoted by Confederate heritage organizations, presented Shepherd as proof of Black loyalty and Brown as a dangerous outsider. Civil-rights and Black organizations challenged the ideological use of Shepherd’s death.

The monument illustrates how Brown’s memory became a proxy battlefield over slavery, emancipation, race, and Civil War memory.

### Historic preservation

Brown’s North Elba farm became a New York state historic site in **1896**. His grave and farm developed into sites of pilgrimage and commemoration. The Harpers Ferry engine house—“John Brown’s Fort”—was repeatedly moved, exhibited, reconstructed, and returned near its historic setting, becoming a relic in its own right.

### Historiographical oscillation

Broadly, Brown’s reputation has moved through recurring interpretive poles:

- martyr and saint;
- criminal murderer;
- madman;
- guerrilla;
- revolutionary;
- terrorist;
- radical democrat;
- religious zealot;
- precursor of Civil War emancipation;
- failed strategist whose death succeeded politically where his raid failed militarily.

No one of these labels contains the complete documentary record. The major modern scholarly shift has been away from treating Harpers Ferry as an isolated eruption and toward reconstructing Brown’s long development through family religion, business failure, Black abolitionist networks, Kansas violence, financing, constitutional planning, and revolutionary strategy.

## Assessment of Successes and Failures — Documentary Summary

### Documented successes

- Established himself as a skilled tanner, livestock handler, wool grader, and practical surveyor despite little formal education.
- Built durable alliances across racial lines uncommon among white nineteenth-century reformers.
- Assisted fugitive-slave resistance and Black self-defense organizing.
- Became an effective small-unit leader in some Kansas engagements, notably Black Jack.
- Successfully removed eleven enslaved people from Missouri to freedom in Canada in 1858–1859.
- Raised significant money and arms from elite abolitionist networks despite personal poverty.
- Forced slavery and the legitimacy of violent resistance into national political discussion after Harpers Ferry.
- Converted his trial and execution into an extraordinarily effective abolitionist propaganda event.

### Documented failures

- Repeated commercial overextension and bankruptcy.
- Major failure of the Perkins & Brown wool strategy and English sales venture.
- Inability to maintain secrecy from Hugh Forbes and other observers.
- Pottawatomie killings produced lasting legal, political, and moral controversy and gave enemies strong evidence of clandestine lethal violence.
- The Harpers Ferry plan failed tactically: Brown did not withdraw rapidly, did not trigger the mass uprising he hoped for, and lost most of his immediate force to death or capture.
- Two sons died at Harpers Ferry and one had already been killed in Kansas; the family paid a severe personal cost for his political program.
- His provisional government never operated on the scale envisioned.

## Atlas Connections

### Abraham Lincoln

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Lincoln discussed Brown after Brown’s death in his February 27, 1860, Cooper Union address. He rejected Republican responsibility for Harpers Ferry while warning that suppressing antislavery politics might enlarge rather than diminish the number of “John Browns.” No personal meeting or correspondence between them is documented. [Contemporary text in the Library of Congress](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/rbc/lprbscsm/scsm0237/scsm0237.pdf)

### George Washington

- **[T9-]** **Documented fact, with disputed provenance:** During the October 1859 raid, Brown’s party seized from Lewis Washington two pistols associated with the Marquis de Lafayette and a ceremonial sword reputedly presented by Frederick the Great to George Washington. Brown carried the sword during the raid, creating an object-mediated connection between Washington and Brown; the sword’s claimed royal provenance is disputed, but its passage through the Washington family and seizure by Brown’s force are documented. [New York State Museum](https://nysm.nysed.gov/about/press/new-york-state-museum-expands-commemoration-americas-250th-anniversary-exhibit)

### Harriet Tubman

- **[T5]** **Documented fact:** Brown and Tubman first met in April 1858 at St. Catharines, Canada West. Brown sought her advice and recruiting assistance for his projected antislavery campaign, called her “General Tubman,” and subsequently remained in contact with her network. [National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/hafe/learn/historyculture/when-john-brown-met-harriet-tubman.htm)
- **[T3]** **Documented fact:** On April 8, 1858, immediately after meeting Tubman, Brown wrote to John Brown Jr. describing her enthusiastic support and praising her abilities. This is a surviving one-sided written engagement concerning a living counterpart, distinct from their personal meeting. [St. Catharines Museum](https://stcatharinesmuseumblog.com/2021/02/13/general-tubman-st-catharines-1858-part-ii/)
- **[T2- prediction]** **Reported fact / visionary tradition:** In a later-recorded account, Tubman said that before meeting Brown she repeatedly dreamed of a bearded older man and two younger men being struck down; after meeting Brown, she identified the figures with Brown and two of his sons. Because the account was recorded retrospectively and no causal mechanism is alleged, it is a retrofitted prediction rather than documented foreknowledge. [National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/hafe/learn/historyculture/when-john-brown-met-harriet-tubman.htm)

### Jesus of Nazareth

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Brown invoked Jesus Christ explicitly in his prison correspondence. In a November 22, 1859, letter to his older children, for example, he urged them to trust in God “and Jesus Christ whom he hath sent.” This is documentary evidence of Brown writing about and appealing to the earlier religious figure, not evidence of any visionary or supernatural encounter. [Transcription of the letter](https://civilwarnotebook.blogspot.com/2018/09/john-brown-to-his-older-children.html)

### Karl Marx

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Writing to Friedrich Engels from London on January 11, 1860, Marx identified the American movement of enslaved people “started by the death of John Brown” as one of the world’s most momentous contemporary developments. The connection is posthumous intellectual commentary; no meeting or correspondence between Brown and Marx is documented. [Marx–Engels correspondence](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/download/Marx_Engels_Correspondence.pdf)

### Victor Hugo

- **[T3]** **Documented fact:** From exile on Guernsey, Hugo issued an open appeal concerning Brown’s prosecution and impending execution in late 1859. Although not private correspondence with Brown, it was a direct, one-sided written intervention addressed to Brown’s case while Brown was alive, urging clemency and warning about the political consequences of execution. No personal meeting is documented. [Library of Congress copy](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/rbc/lcrbmrp/t1302/t1302.pdf)
- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** After Brown’s execution, Hugo continued writing about him and authorized publication of his own image of the hanged Brown, helping establish Brown’s international martyr iconography. This later commentary constitutes writing about a past figure. [Victor Hugo correspondence](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Correspondance_de_Victor_Hugo/1861)
