# Leonardo di ser Piero da Vinci: Research Dossier

## Scope, Method, and Evidentiary Labels

This dossier reconstructs Leonardo’s life from surviving archival documents, contracts, financial records, correspondence, manuscripts, contemporary and near-contemporary testimony, museum catalogues, and modern scholarship.

Evidence is labeled where uncertainty matters:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a surviving contemporary record, manuscript, contract, account, artwork, or authenticated correspondence.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a reasonably placed contemporary or near-contemporary source but not independently documented.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** inference drawn by modern specialists from multiple pieces of evidence.
- **Disputed attribution or interpretation:** supported by some specialists and rejected or qualified by others.
- **Allegation:** claim formally made but not proved.
- **Rumor or myth:** story unsupported by adequate contemporary evidence.
- **Legend:** culturally influential narrative whose factual basis is absent, uncertain, or substantially embellished.

Leonardo’s surviving papers are extensive but incomplete. More than 4,000 sheets survive, probably less than half the original corpus. They are working notes rather than a continuous diary. No intimate memoir, systematic autobiography, or uncontested contemporary portrait of his private life survives.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name:** Leonardo di ser Piero da Vinci.

The name means “Leonardo, son of Ser Piero, from Vinci.” “Ser” marked his father’s professional status as a notary. “Da Vinci” was geographical, not a modern hereditary surname.

**Contemporary spellings and names:**

- Lionardo di ser Piero da Vinci
- Lionardo da Vinci
- Leonardo da Vinci
- Leonardo Vinci
- Leonardo Fiorentino
- Ma(o)stro Leonardo
- Léonard de Vinci in French
- “Painter and engineer of the king” or comparable formulations in late French records

**Born:** Night of 14–15 April 1452, conventionally 15 April 1452.

**Birthplace:** Vinci or its immediate countryside, Republic of Florence, now Tuscany, Italy.

The traditional birthplace is the house at Anchiano outside Vinci. **Unresolved question:** no surviving birth record identifies that building. Leonardo’s grandfather recorded the date and baptism, but not an exact house. Carlo Vecce has proposed that a property at Anchiano may have been used discreetly for the birth; this remains a reconstruction.

**Baptism:** Shortly after birth at the parish church of Santa Croce in Vinci. His grandfather’s memorandum names ten godparents, an unusually large group suggesting that the child, though illegitimate, was publicly incorporated into local society.

**Died:** 2 May 1519, aged sixty-seven.

**Place of death:** Clos Lucé, then Château du Cloux, Amboise, Kingdom of France.

**Burial:** Collegiate church of Saint-Florentin at the Château d’Amboise, according to his will. The church was later damaged and demolished. Bones recovered in the nineteenth century and reinterred in the Chapel of Saint-Hubert are traditionally identified as Leonardo’s, but their identity has not been scientifically established.

**Citizenship and political setting:** Born a subject of the Florentine Republic. He subsequently worked under Medici, Sforza, French, Venetian, papal, and Borgia authorities without holding a stable modern nationality or political allegiance.

**Occupations:**

- Painter and draftsman
- Workshop master
- Court artist
- Festival and theatrical designer
- Military and civil engineer
- Architect and urban planner
- Anatomist
- Investigator of mechanics, hydraulics, optics, geology, botany, flight, and proportion
- Cartographer
- Sculptural designer
- Writer on painting and natural philosophy
- Musician and improviser, according to early testimony

He was not a university-trained physician, natural philosopher, or engineer and held no known degree.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal family

Leonardo’s paternal line was an established family of notaries and landholders associated with Vinci.

- **Father:** Ser Piero di Antonio da Vinci, born about 1426/27, died 9 July 1504. He developed a successful notarial practice in Florence and worked for important civic, monastic, and mercantile clients.
- **Paternal grandfather:** Antonio di ser Piero da Vinci, who recorded Leonardo’s birth in a family book.
- **Paternal grandmother:** Lucia di ser Piero di Zoso, associated with the Toia family and probably descended from ceramic workers.
- **Uncle:** Francesco di Antonio da Vinci, substantially younger than Leonardo’s father. He appears to have maintained a close relationship with Leonardo and later made him beneficiary of land.

Leonardo was born outside marriage. Ser Piero did not marry Leonardo’s mother and apparently did not formally legitimate Leonardo in a way that would place him on equal inheritance terms with later legitimate sons. Nevertheless, Leonardo lived within or near the paternal household for part of his childhood and was treated publicly as Ser Piero’s son.

Ser Piero married four times:

1. **Albiera di Giovanni Amadori**, married in 1452; she died childless in 1464.
2. **Francesca di ser Giuliano Lanfredini**, also apparently childless.
3. **Margherita di Francesco Giulli**, mother of several legitimate children.
4. **Lucrezia di Guglielmo Cortigiani**, mother of additional children.

Genealogical research identifies at least twenty-two paternal half-siblings or possible half-siblings, although counts depend on the treatment of children who died young and documentary ambiguities. Leonardo had no documented biological children. Present-day members of the Vinci paternal line descend from his half-brothers, not from Leonardo.

### Mother: Caterina

The birth memorandum calls his mother simply **Caterina**.

The long-standing reconstruction identifies her as a local woman, probably **Caterina di Meo Lippi**, who subsequently married **Antonio di Piero del Vacca**, called **Accattabriga**, a kiln worker or agricultural laborer. This identification was developed from tax and local family records.

A competing reconstruction announced by Carlo Vecce in 2023 identifies Leonardo’s mother with an enslaved Circassian woman named Caterina. A Florentine document dated 2 November 1452, drawn up by Ser Piero, emancipated a Circassian slave named Caterina belonging to the household of Donato di Filippo di Salvestro Nati. Vecce connected this Caterina to other members of the Vinci network and argued that she was Leonardo’s mother.

**Evidence assessment:** the emancipation document is authentic and Ser Piero’s involvement is documented. The proposition that its Caterina was Leonardo’s mother is a **scholarly reconstruction**, not an explicit statement in the document. Critics have noted that Caterina was a very common baptismal name among both free and enslaved women and that Vecce initially presented much of the reconstruction through a historical novel before publishing a full scholarly biography. The older Caterina di Meo Lippi identification also relies on linkage rather than an explicit birth record. The question remains contested.

### Marriages, partners, and children

Leonardo never married and had no documented children.

His household included young male pupils and assistants, notably Gian Giacomo Caprotti, called Salaì, and Francesco Melzi. Their relationships with Leonardo were emotionally and materially significant. Whether either was his sexual partner cannot be proved from surviving records.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1452–1466/69

Leonardo’s grandfather’s memorandum is the chief documentary anchor for his birth. It states that a grandson, son of Ser Piero, was born on Saturday, 15 April 1452, at the third hour of the night.

He probably spent his earliest years around Vinci, in a landscape of streams, ravines, rock formations, mills, fields, and workshops. Later notebooks show intense attention to water, erosion, plants, geological strata, agricultural machinery, and animal movement. Connecting these interests directly to childhood is plausible but remains biographical inference.

A tax declaration from 1457 places the five-year-old Leonardo in Antonio’s household. His father was frequently in Florence. Albiera, Ser Piero’s first wife, may have participated in raising him, but the surviving evidence does not permit a detailed domestic reconstruction.

As an illegitimate son, Leonardo was barred from following the conventional hereditary path into the Florentine notarial guild. His literacy was acquired in the vernacular rather than through a full Latin humanist curriculum. He later called himself an **omo sanza lettere**, a “man without letters,” meaning one without formal classical education. This did not mean illiteracy: his notebooks show enormous reading, experimentation with technical vocabulary, and persistent attempts to teach himself Latin, mathematics, and geometry.

His early schooling probably included basic reading, writing, commercial arithmetic, and abacus methods. No schoolmaster has been securely identified.

## Education and Formation

### Verrocchio’s workshop

Between roughly 1466 and 1469, Ser Piero placed Leonardo in the Florentine workshop of **Andrea del Verrocchio**. The precise starting date is unknown.

Verrocchio’s bottega trained artists across media. Leonardo would have encountered:

- Drawing from models and casts
- Preparation and grinding of pigments
- Panel preparation
- Tempera and emerging oil techniques
- Drapery studies
- Perspective
- Anatomy as needed for representation
- Metalworking, casting, and engineering
- Sculpture in clay, marble, and bronze
- Workshop accounting and collaborative production
- Design for festivals, armor, banners, and court objects

Leonardo’s formation was therefore technical and collective rather than narrowly “fine artistic.”

### Early intellectual influences

His notebooks and book lists document engagement with or pursuit of works by:

- Aristotle and medieval Aristotelian commentators
- Euclid
- Archimedes
- Pliny the Elder
- Vitruvius
- Ptolemy
- Albertus Magnus
- Roger Bacon
- Leon Battista Alberti
- Roberto Valturio
- Luca Pacioli
- Marsilio Ficino
- Francesco di Giorgio Martini
- Medical and anatomical authorities including Mondino de’ Liuzzi and Avicenna

Leonardo’s access was often through Italian translations, excerpts, oral explanation, or collaboration with educated associates. His intellectual method increasingly emphasized observation and repeated experience, but he did not reject inherited authorities wholesale.

### Guild standing

In 1472 Leonardo was recorded in the **Compagnia di San Luca**, the painters’ confraternity associated with the Florentine guild of physicians and apothecaries. He remained connected to Verrocchio’s workshop after becoming professionally eligible to work independently.

## Early Career in Florence, approximately 1469–1482

### Workshop collaborations

Leonardo’s hand is generally recognized in parts of Verrocchio’s **Baptism of Christ**, especially the left-hand angel and portions of the landscape. Vasari’s story that Verrocchio abandoned painting after seeing Leonardo surpass him is a **legendary embellishment**: Verrocchio continued to direct painting production, and collaborative workshop practice better explains the picture.

Other early works include drapery studies, the **Annunciation**, the **Madonna of the Carnation**, and the **Benois Madonna**, though dates and degrees of workshop participation vary among catalogues.

### First independent records

In January 1478 Leonardo received a commission for an altarpiece for the Chapel of Saint Bernard in Florence’s Palazzo della Signoria. The commission was not completed. This is the first securely recorded independent painting commission.

In 1481 the Augustinian monks of San Donato a Scopeto commissioned the **Adoration of the Magi**. Leonardo developed an exceptionally complex underdrawing but left the panel unfinished when he departed Florence.

The unfinished Saint Bernard altarpiece and *Adoration* began a recurrent pattern: extraordinary planning, revision, and experimentation accompanied by missed delivery or indefinite postponement.

### The 1476 sodomy proceedings

On 9 April 1476 an anonymous denunciation submitted to the Florentine authorities accused Leonardo and three other men of sexual relations with Jacopo Saltarelli, a young goldsmith’s assistant who had himself been the subject of sexual accusations.

The case was handled by the **Ufficiali di Notte**, the magistracy policing male sodomy. The proceeding was suspended or dismissed because the accusation lacked the required signed or corroborated evidence. A second denunciation followed in June, and the matter was again dismissed.

**Evidence classification:**

- Leonardo’s accusation: **documented fact**.
- Sexual conduct alleged in the denunciation: **allegation, not proved**.
- Acquittal after evidentiary hearing: not precisely the modern procedure; “dismissal” or “release from accusation” is more accurate.
- Theory that Medici influence protected the defendants because Leonardo Tornabuoni belonged to a powerful family: **unproved theory**.
- Claim that the record proves Leonardo was homosexual: **interpretation, not documentary proof**.
- Claim that Leonardo was celibate or asexual: likewise **unproved interpretation**.

No other surviving judicial record establishes his sexual conduct.

### Medici connections

Leonardo worked within Florence’s Medici-dominated cultural economy. Lorenzo de’ Medici may have facilitated his move to Milan, and later sources say Leonardo carried a silver lyre as a gift or diplomatic offering. The exact scope of Lorenzo’s patronage is uncertain; Leonardo was not documented as a resident member of Lorenzo’s household in the manner of the young Michelangelo.

Leonardo drew the corpse of Bernardo Bandini Baroncelli after the conspirator’s execution in Florence in December 1479. The drawing is a documentary study, not evidence that Leonardo participated politically in the Pazzi conflict.

## First Milanese Period, approximately 1482–1499

### Move to Milan

Leonardo moved from Florence to Milan around 1482. The reasons probably included access to a larger court, major engineering and sculptural commissions, and dissatisfaction with his Florentine prospects.

His famous letter to **Ludovico Sforza**, preserved in the *Codex Atlanticus*, offers skills in bridges, siege devices, artillery, tunnels, ships, architecture, sculpture, and painting. It is often called the first résumé, but that is a modern analogy. The surviving draft may date somewhat later than his arrival.

The document is revealing but promotional. Many proposed devices were conceptual, adapted from earlier engineering traditions, or never built.

### Employment and court work

Leonardo entered the Sforza court as artist, engineer, designer, and impresario. Payments and references are intermittent, and his precise contractual status varied.

He designed:

- Court entertainments, costumes, mechanical effects, and allegorical pageants
- Architectural and urban schemes
- Canal and hydraulic projects
- Fortification studies
- Weapons and military devices
- Portraits and devotional paintings
- A monumental equestrian memorial to Francesco Sforza

His association with musicians, engineers, physicians, mathematicians, and artisans in Milan profoundly expanded his work.

### Virgin of the Rocks and payment dispute

On 25 April 1483 Leonardo and the brothers **Ambrogio and Evangelista de Predis** contracted with the Confraternity of the Immaculate Conception for an altarpiece for San Francesco Grande.

The first version of the **Virgin of the Rocks**, now in the Louvre, appears to have been diverted or sold after the artists disputed the confraternity’s valuation. The National Gallery version was associated with the eventual fulfillment of the commission. Documents from the 1490s and 1500s record disagreements over price and completion.

This was a **documented commercial dispute**, not evidence of fraud. The surviving contracts and petitions show that Leonardo and Ambrogio sought more than the originally stated amount, contending that the work and materials justified it.

### Cecilia Gallerani and court portraiture

The **Lady with an Ermine** is widely identified as Cecilia Gallerani, Ludovico’s young mistress. The ermine operates as a personal, political, and linguistic emblem. Leonardo also produced portraits associated with the Milanese court, including the disputed or partially collaborative **Portrait of a Musician** and **La Belle Ferronnière**.

### Anatomical and scientific work

Leonardo’s first extensive anatomical campaigns date from Milan. He studied skulls, proportion, muscles, and the mechanics of the body. Access to corpses is incompletely documented, but later he explicitly described dissections.

He treated vision, light, mechanics, water, and anatomy as interrelated problems of form and movement. He planned books but published none.

### The Sforza monument

Leonardo spent years designing an immense bronze equestrian monument to Francesco Sforza. A full-scale clay horse was displayed by the early 1490s. The bronze reserved for casting was reportedly redirected to cannon production during the Italian Wars. French forces entering Milan in 1499 used or damaged the clay model, which was eventually destroyed.

The monument was a conspicuous failure of completion, but responsibility was divided among technical ambition, patronal delay, war, and diversion of material.

### The Last Supper

Ludovico commissioned the **Last Supper** for the refectory of Santa Maria delle Grazie, probably around 1494–95. Leonardo completed it approximately 1497–98.

Rather than true fresco, he used an experimental mixed technique on dry wall, permitting revision and subtle modeling but causing rapid deterioration. Deterioration was reported within decades. Later damp, repainting, occupation, a doorway cut through the wall, and bombing compounded the damage.

The work established his contemporary fame while also becoming the clearest example of technical experimentation producing conservation failure.

### Luca Pacioli

The mathematician **Luca Pacioli** lived and worked at the Milanese court. Leonardo illustrated the polyhedra for Pacioli’s *Divina proportione*, completed in manuscript in 1498 and printed in 1509. Pacioli helped deepen Leonardo’s mathematical education.

### Fall of Milan

French forces overthrew Ludovico in 1499. Leonardo’s patron fled and was later captured. Leonardo left Milan with Pacioli and others, traveling through Mantua and Venice.

## Mantua, Venice, and Return to Florence, 1499–1502

### Mantua

At Mantua Leonardo encountered **Isabella d’Este**, marchioness and a determined collector. He drew her profile portrait. Isabella repeatedly sought a painted portrait or devotional work from him through agents, but no delivered painting is documented.

Letters from Fra Pietro da Novellara in 1501 report that Leonardo was absorbed in mathematics and reluctant to paint. These letters are contemporary evidence for the tension between his investigations and patrons’ demands.

### Venice

In 1500 Leonardo advised Venice on defenses against possible Ottoman attack. Designs associated with this episode include movable barriers, flooding strategies, and diving apparatus. Whether specific devices were built is unknown.

### Florence

Leonardo returned to Florence by April 1500 and lodged for a period with the Servite friars at Santissima Annunziata. A large cartoon of the Virgin, Child, Saint Anne, and Saint John attracted public attention according to Fra Pietro and Vasari. Whether this was precisely the surviving Burlington House Cartoon remains debated.

---

## Cesare Borgia and Central Italy, 1502–1503

In 1502 Leonardo entered the service of **Cesare Borgia**, commander and papal prince, as military architect and engineer. A surviving safe-conduct dated 18 August 1502 authorized him to inspect fortifications throughout Borgia’s territories.

He traveled through parts of Romagna and the Marche, including:

- Urbino
- Pesaro
- Rimini
- Cesena
- Cesenatico
- Imola
- Faenza and surrounding territories, with individual stops reconstructed from notes and maps

He surveyed fortresses, waterways, terrain, and roads. His map of Imola is a landmark of measured urban cartography.

Leonardo was present in the political-military environment in which Borgia eliminated enemies and imposed control by force. There is no documentary evidence that Leonardo planned murders or personally committed violence. His service nevertheless supplied technical expertise to a coercive military regime.

He probably encountered **Niccolò Machiavelli**, then a Florentine envoy observing Borgia. Their later involvement in a scheme to divert the Arno makes acquaintance highly likely, though the documentary record does not provide a detailed personal relationship.

Leonardo left Borgia’s employment by early 1503, as the political position of Borgia and Pope Alexander VI deteriorated.

---

## Florence, 1503–1506

### Mona Lisa

Leonardo began the portrait conventionally identified as **Lisa Gherardini del Giocondo**, wife of the Florentine merchant Francesco del Giocondo, around 1503.

A note written by Agostino Vespucci in October 1503 compares Leonardo with Apelles and states that he was working on a head of “Lisa del Giocondo.” This is the strongest contemporary identification.

Leonardo retained the painting and continued revising it for years. Competing identifications have included Isabella of Aragon, Pacifica Brandani, Giuliano de’ Medici’s mistress, and idealized or composite figures. The Lisa Gherardini identification has the strongest documentary basis, but the precise relationship between the 1503 portrait and the final Louvre object has still generated debate.

### Battle of Anghiari

In 1503 the Florentine government commissioned Leonardo to paint the **Battle of Anghiari** in the Palazzo della Signoria’s Great Council Hall. Michelangelo received the opposing-wall commission for the **Battle of Cascina**.

Leonardo prepared extensive studies and transferred part of the design to the wall. The painting was never completed. Early accounts say his experimental wall technique failed, causing colors to run or detach. Later remodeling under Vasari covered or destroyed the surviving material.

Searches for a hidden Leonardo beneath Vasari’s mural have produced inconclusive physical evidence. The claim that Vasari secretly preserved Leonardo’s painting behind a false wall is an **unresolved theory**, not an established discovery.

### Rivalry with Michelangelo

Leonardo and **Michelangelo Buonarroti** were simultaneously commissioned in the Great Council Hall. Contemporary and near-contemporary sources describe personal tension. One reported street encounter has Michelangelo mocking Leonardo over the unfinished Sforza horse.

The exact words and circumstances are **reported fact from later testimony**, not a verbatim contemporary transcript. Their rivalry was real at the institutional and reputational level, but later narratives may exaggerate constant personal hostility.

### Arno diversion project

Leonardo studied a plan to divert the Arno near Pisa, potentially depriving rebellious Pisa of river access while improving Florentine navigation. Machiavelli was involved administratively. Excavation began but engineering, financing, and hydrological difficulties defeated the project.

The plan combined civil hydraulics with military pressure. It was a significant practical failure.

---

## Second Milanese Period, 1506–1513

In 1506 French governor **Charles d’Amboise** requested Leonardo’s return to Milan. Florence initially resisted because of the unfinished *Battle of Anghiari*, but Leonardo obtained leave and increasingly detached himself from the commission.

Under French rule he served Louis XII and his representatives as painter and engineer. He traveled between Milan, Florence, and other northern Italian locations.

### Francesco Melzi

Around 1506–07 the young Milanese nobleman **Francesco Melzi** entered Leonardo’s household. Melzi became pupil, secretary, companion, and eventually literary executor. His handwriting appears in manuscripts, and he remained with Leonardo until his death.

### Salaì

**Gian Giacomo Caprotti da Oreno**, called Salaì or Salaino, had joined Leonardo in 1490 at about age ten. Leonardo’s notes call him a thief, liar, obstinate, and glutton while recording clothes, gifts, and repeated thefts. He nevertheless remained associated with the household for decades, received property jointly with Battista de Vilanis, and developed an artistic career.

Claims that Salaì was Leonardo’s lover or the model for the *Mona Lisa* are **unproved interpretations**. His long-term intimacy and favored status are documented; the sexual nature of the relationship is not.

### Anatomy with Marcantonio della Torre

Around 1510–11 Leonardo collaborated with the anatomist **Marcantonio della Torre** at Pavia. Leonardo made detailed studies of bones, muscles, organs, the fetus, heart valves, and comparative anatomy.

He claimed to have dissected more than thirty human bodies. This statement appears in his own notes. Conditions were difficult, and access likely depended on hospitals and medical collaborators.

His anatomical drawings included observations of exceptional accuracy, but also inherited errors and speculative physiology. Because the projected treatise was never completed or printed, these studies had little direct influence on sixteenth-century medicine.

### Inheritance disputes

Ser Piero died intestate in 1504. Leonardo apparently received no share of the paternal estate, consistent with his illegitimacy and the claims of legitimate sons.

His uncle Francesco’s will of 12 August 1504 left Leonardo an interest in lands near Vinci. After Francesco’s death around 1506/07, Leonardo’s half-brothers contested the arrangement. The dispute continued for years and led Leonardo to seek assistance from French officials and Italian patrons.

This was a **documented civil inheritance dispute**, not a criminal case. A later accommodation appears to have secured Leonardo use or possession of part of the property.

### Financial position

Leonardo was not consistently poor. He received court salaries, commissions, gifts, and benefits; kept deposits with the Hospital of Santa Maria Nuova in Florence; owned or held interests in land; and maintained servants, pupils, horses, books, instruments, and specialized materials.

His finances were nevertheless irregular. Patronal arrears, unfinished commissions, litigation, travel, workshop costs, and dependence on court favor produced periods of uncertainty. No evidence establishes a large commercial firm or investment enterprise in the modern sense.

---

## Rome, 1513–1516

Leonardo moved to Rome in September 1513 under the patronage of **Giuliano de’ Medici**, brother of Pope Leo X. He lived in the Vatican Belvedere complex with assistants.

Rome was dominated artistically by Raphael and Michelangelo. Leonardo obtained no comparable monumental commission. He continued studies in optics, geometry, water, anatomy, mirrors, and industrial processes.

A German mirror-maker, Georg, worked with him. Leonardo complained in drafts or notes about interference, surveillance, and denunciations by workshop personnel. Anatomy may have been curtailed after complaints to papal authorities, but no surviving formal prosecution against Leonardo has been identified.

Vasari’s account that Pope Leo X withdrew a commission after Leonardo began distilling varnishes before starting the picture is anecdotal and shaped to illustrate procrastination. No corresponding contract survives.

Giuliano died in March 1516, removing Leonardo’s principal Roman patron.

---

## France, 1516–1519

### Migration to Amboise

Leonardo accepted the invitation of **Francis I of France** and crossed the Alps in 1516, probably accompanied by Melzi and Battista de Vilanis. Salaì may initially have traveled with him or remained connected to his affairs, but was not part of the final household in the same way as Melzi.

Leonardo settled at the Château du Cloux, now Clos Lucé, near the royal residence at Amboise. French records describe him as the king’s first painter, engineer, and architect. He received a substantial pension, commonly recorded as 700 écus annually.

He brought paintings including the *Mona Lisa*, *Saint Anne*, and *Saint John the Baptist*, as well as manuscripts, drawings, and instruments.

### French projects

He advised on:

- A projected royal palace and ideal settlement at Romorantin
- Canal and water-control schemes
- Court festivities and mechanical spectacles
- Architectural planning
- Continued revision of paintings and scientific manuscripts

The Romorantin project was not realized, though surviving drawings establish serious design work.

### Health

Antonio de Beatis, secretary to Cardinal Luigi d’Aragona, visited Leonardo at Cloux on 10 October 1517. He reported that Leonardo could no longer color with his former sweetness because his right hand was paralyzed, although he could still draw and teach.

Because Leonardo normally wrote and drew left-handed, the impairment’s consequences are debated. A modern medical analysis proposed traumatic ulnar neuropathy rather than stroke. Others have suggested stroke or another neurological event. No clinical evidence can settle the diagnosis.

### Will

Leonardo dictated his will on 23 April 1519 before royal notary Guillaume Boreau.

Its provisions included:

- Burial at Saint-Florentin in Amboise
- Funeral processions, masses, chaplains, friars, and sixty poor men carrying candles
- Books, instruments, drawings, and materials relating to painting to Francesco Melzi
- Clothing and other effects to Melzi
- A Milanese garden divided between Salaì and Battista de Vilanis
- Rights in a canal to de Vilanis
- Money and property in Florence to his paternal half-brothers
- Clothing and two ducats to his servant Mathurine

The will’s Catholic provisions are documentary evidence of conventional religious observance at death. They do not by themselves settle the character of Leonardo’s private belief.

### Death

Leonardo died on 2 May 1519.

No contemporary medical record gives a cause. Stroke, recurrent vascular disease, and other causes are modern hypotheses.

Francesco Melzi wrote to Leonardo’s family describing the death as a permanent grief and Leonardo’s affection for him as deeply devoted.

## Deathbed, Burial, and Dispersal of the Estate

Vasari’s revised biography says Leonardo died in the arms of King Francis I after expressing religious contrition. Francis was probably at Saint-Germain-en-Laye on 2 May 1519.

**Assessment:** the king-cradling-Leonardo scene is a **legend**, probably designed to symbolize royal recognition of genius. It was later immortalized in paintings by François-Guillaume Ménageot, Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres, and others.

Melzi carried the manuscripts and drawings to the family villa at Vaprio d’Adda. He attempted to organize Leonardo’s notes on painting into the **Libro di pittura**. After Melzi’s death, the archive dispersed through heirs, collectors, gifts, sales, and theft.

The sculptor **Pompeo Leoni** acquired and rearranged many sheets, sometimes cutting and mounting them. The resulting collections included the *Codex Atlanticus* and the group eventually entering the Royal Collection at Windsor.

Other manuscripts reached:

- Biblioteca Ambrosiana, Milan
- Institut de France, Paris
- British Library, London
- Victoria and Albert Museum, London
- Biblioteca Nacional de España, Madrid
- Biblioteca Reale, Turin
- Biblioteca Trivulziana, Milan
- Private ownership, including the *Codex Leicester*

Napoleonic seizure, nineteenth-century collecting, and the thefts of Guglielmo Libri further disturbed provenance. The Madrid codices were effectively lost within the library catalogue until rediscovered in the twentieth century.

## Residences, Travels, and Geographic Movements

- **1452–approximately 1466:** Vinci and its countryside; possibly Anchiano.
- **By late 1460s–1482:** Florence, with possible returns to Vinci.
- **Approximately 1482–1499:** Milan; journeys connected to Lombard court and engineering work.
- **Late 1499:** Mantua.
- **1500:** Venice, then Florence.
- **1502–early 1503:** Urbino, Pesaro, Rimini, Cesena, Cesenatico, Imola, and other Borgia territories.
- **1503–1506:** primarily Florence, with engineering journeys in Tuscany.
- **1506–1513:** primarily Milan and Lombardy, with visits to Florence and Pavia.
- **1513–1516:** Rome.
- **1516–1519:** Amboise, France, with work relating to Romorantin and the Loire court.

Claims that he traveled to the Ottoman Empire, Armenia, Egypt, or the Near East are unsupported by secure travel records. Some landscape descriptions and a draft letter concerning a supposed journey in Armenian territory have generated theories, but most scholars treat the material as literary or projective rather than proof of travel.

## Technologies, Inventions, and Projects

Leonardo left no patents in the modern sense and few devices securely documented as constructed. His technical sheets mix practical commissions, improvements on existing mechanisms, teaching diagrams, fantasies, thought experiments, and copied or adapted precedents.

### Engineering fields

- Bridges, including rapidly deployable military bridges
- Fortifications and angled defenses
- Artillery and multiple-barrel weapons
- Siege machines
- Armored vehicle concepts
- Giant crossbow designs
- Canal locks, dredges, pumps, sluices, and water-lifting devices
- Textile and manufacturing machinery
- Bearings, gears, cams, screws, and transmission mechanisms
- Parachute concept
- Ornithopters and other flying-machine concepts
- Gliding and controlled-flight studies
- Diving and underwater-breathing apparatus
- Theatrical automata and mechanical animals
- Measuring instruments and odometers
- Urban sanitation and multilevel city schemes

### Priority claims

Popular accounts often call Leonardo the inventor of the helicopter, tank, parachute, submarine, bicycle, robot, or automobile.

These formulations are misleading:

- Several mechanisms had ancient or medieval predecessors.
- Leonardo frequently synthesized existing engineering traditions.
- Many designs could not have worked as drawn or lacked suitable materials and power sources.
- The “bicycle” drawing in the *Codex Atlanticus* is widely disputed and may be a later addition.
- His aerial screw resembles a conceptual rotary-wing device but is not an operational helicopter.
- His armored vehicle drawing contains mechanical problems, possibly accidental, pedagogical, or deliberate.
- His automata and theatrical machines are better grounded in Renaissance court engineering traditions than claims that he created modern robotics.

His historical importance lies less in isolated “first inventions” than in visual analysis of mechanisms, systematic comparison of natural and mechanical motion, and integration of drawing with investigation.

## Artistic and Intellectual Development

### Painting as knowledge

Leonardo argued that painting was an intellectual and scientific discipline because it represented all visible things through perspective, light, shadow, color, anatomy, and motion. He ranked sight above other senses and treated the painter as an investigator of nature.

### Experience and authority

His recurring appeal to **esperienza** did not amount to modern experimental science in a fully institutionalized sense. He combined observation, controlled trials, analogy, received theory, geometrical aspiration, and metaphysical speculation.

### Mathematics

Association with Luca Pacioli intensified his interest in Euclidean geometry, proportion, transformations, and regular solids. His mathematical work was visually powerful but sometimes limited by incomplete formal training.

### Anatomy

His development moved from artist’s anatomy toward mechanical and physiological inquiry. Major achievements included:

- Layered representation of muscles
- Sectional and exploded views
- Studies of skull cavities
- Spinal curvature
- Fetal position
- Heart chambers and valves
- Comparative anatomy
- Mechanical analysis of limbs and posture

Errors included the persistence of Galenic or medieval ideas, imperfect reproductive anatomy, and analogical schemes not always supported by observation.

### Geology and water

Leonardo correctly recognized that marine fossils found in mountains were remains of organisms rather than products of a single biblical flood. He studied sedimentation, erosion, water vortices, river systems, and large-scale earth processes.

### Cosmology and philosophy

He often conceived the human body as a microcosm and the earth as a living macrocosm. His thought combined Aristotelian, Platonic, mechanical, artisanal, and personal observational traditions without forming a finished philosophical system.

### Religion

Leonardo produced major Christian works and arranged a Catholic funeral. His notebooks also display independence from clerical authority and occasionally satirical or skeptical observations.

Claims that he was atheist, secret pagan, Cathar, Hermetic initiate, or covert anti-Christian are not established by contemporary evidence. Likewise, conventional religious imagery does not prove private orthodoxy in every respect.

### Animals and diet

Early sources portray him as compassionate toward animals and tell of his buying caged birds to release them. Some later authors inferred vegetarianism. The bird-release story is **reported fact** from early biography; strict lifelong vegetarianism is **plausible but unproved**.

## Successes and Failures

### Major successes

- Creation of an exceptionally influential small corpus of paintings
- Transformation of portraiture through psychological presence and atmospheric modeling
- *Last Supper* as a new model of narrative organization
- Integration of art, optics, anatomy, and motion
- A visual language for technical and scientific investigation
- Court success under Sforza and Francis I
- Cartographic innovations, especially the Imola plan
- Anatomical drawings of lasting scientific and artistic importance
- Formation of a Milanese school of followers
- Early posthumous elevation as the model of the universal artist

### Failures, abandoned work, and limitations

- Saint Bernard altarpiece: undelivered
- *Adoration of the Magi*: unfinished
- Sforza monument: never cast
- *Battle of Anghiari*: unfinished and technically compromised
- Arno diversion: failed
- Romorantin: unrealized
- Numerous treatises: never completed or published
- Anatomical findings: largely unavailable to contemporary medicine
- Flying machines and many military devices: unbuilt or impractical
- *Last Supper* technique: rapid deterioration
- Repeated patronal frustration over delayed or undelivered paintings

“Leonardo never finished anything” is an exaggeration, but incompletion was a defining feature of his working life.

## Finances, Property, and Business Relationships

Leonardo operated within patronage and workshop economies rather than a modern incorporated business.

Documented or probable assets and financial arrangements included:

- Court salaries and pensions
- Payments for paintings, engineering, and festivities
- Joint contractual obligations with the de Predis brothers
- A Florentine bank account or deposit relationship with Santa Maria Nuova
- Land inherited or contested near Vinci
- A garden outside Milan shared or transferred through his estate
- French royal pension
- Workshop equipment, books, clothing, horses, servants, and pupils
- Advances and expenses recorded for household members, especially Salaì

The *Virgin of the Rocks* dispute concerned valuation and payment. Leonardo’s inheritance litigation concerned civil property rights. No evidence indicates money laundering, systematic fraud, embezzlement, or corrupt public office.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Contested Questions

### Sodomy accusation, 1476

**Status:** documented accusation; dismissed without proof.

The case is the only direct legal evidence concerning Leonardo’s sexual life. Modern biographies range from cautious agnosticism to interpretations of Leonardo as predominantly homosexual. His unmarried status, male household, aesthetic interests, and relationships with Salaì and Melzi can inform interpretation but do not independently establish sexual conduct.

### Service to violent rulers

**Status:** documented employment.

Leonardo offered military technology to Ludovico Sforza, advised Venice, served Cesare Borgia, and worked on Florentine wartime hydraulics. Some devices depicted methods of killing, siege, and terror.

Whether the most extreme weapons were practical offers, intellectual exercises, inherited motifs, or deterrent fantasies varies by sheet. His service to Borgia materially associated him with conquest, but claims that he was Borgia’s assassin, intelligence chief, or secret operational spy lack evidence.

### Dissection and grave-robbing stories

**Status:** dissections documented through Leonardo’s notes; grave robbery unproved.

Human dissection was regulated but not uniformly prohibited. Leonardo likely worked through hospitals and medical contacts. Sensational stories of clandestine tomb robbery are later embellishments.

### Incompletion and alleged attention disorder

Modern medical and psychological writers have proposed ADHD, dyslexia, autism, bipolarity, or other diagnoses to explain mirror writing, wide interests, procrastination, and incomplete projects.

**Status:** retrospective diagnostic speculation. No diagnosis can be established from the evidence. Mirror writing is readily explained by habitual left-handed writing and is not itself proof of dyslexia or secrecy.

### Weapons pacifist paradox

A notebook phrase describes war as **pazzia bestialissima**, “most bestial madness,” yet Leonardo repeatedly sought military employment.

**Status:** both the condemnation and military work are documented. Claims that he sabotaged every weapon design to prevent its use are unsupported. Some drawings contain errors, but deliberate disabling has not been demonstrated.

### Salvator Mundi attribution and market controversy

The painting sold in 2017 for approximately US$450.3 million after being restored and attributed to Leonardo by several specialists. Other specialists regard it as substantially workshop-made or reject full autograph status. The Prado later categorized it with attributed, workshop, or supervised works rather than firmly autograph paintings.

The Louvre’s unpublished 2019 scientific booklet reportedly supported attribution to Leonardo, while the work was absent from the Louvre exhibition after disagreements concerning display or status.

Its recent market history became part of litigation involving Dmitry Rybolovlev, Yves Bouvier, and Sotheby’s over alleged undisclosed markups. Those proceedings concerned modern intermediaries, not misconduct by Leonardo.

**Status:** authorship remains a genuine scholarly dispute; modern financial controversy is documented but posthumous.

### The mother’s enslaved Circassian identity

**Status:** authentic emancipation document plus contested scholarly identification.

It should neither be reported as settled fact nor dismissed as fabrication. The decisive missing evidence is an explicit contemporary statement connecting the emancipated Circassian Caterina to Leonardo’s birth.

### Sexualized or erotic drawings

Leonardo’s circle produced erotic and grotesque imagery, and some works attributed to Salaì or the workshop have been linked to Leonardo. Claims of a suppressed royal cache of obscene Leonardo drawings remain unverified.

### “Self-portrait” identification

The red-chalk head in Turin is traditionally called Leonardo’s self-portrait. Its date, identity, and even relationship to Leonardo’s appearance remain disputed. It should not be treated as a securely documented likeness.

### Battle of Anghiari search

Claims that Vasari preserved Leonardo’s mural behind his own wall painting remain unresolved. Investigations have been criticized for risking damage and for overstating ambiguous pigment or structural findings.

### Adversarial search findings, 2024–2026

A dedicated search combining Leonardo’s full name with controversy, scandal, allegations, accusations, lawsuits, fraud, conspiracy, and criticism produced no credible new allegation of previously unknown personal criminality.

Recent contested material predominantly concerns:

- Continuing *Salvator Mundi* attribution and provenance disputes
- Modern litigation over the painting’s resale and intermediary markups
- Debate over Caterina’s identification as an enslaved Circassian
- Public exaggeration of Leonardo’s inventions and secret codes
- Retrospective psychiatric diagnosis
- Reassessment of Vasari-derived anecdotes

No sourced 2024–26 finding establishes fraud, corruption, money laundering, criminal conspiracy, or political violence personally committed by Leonardo.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Propaganda

### Death in the arms of Francis I

**Story:** Leonardo died while held by the French king.

**Appearance and circulation:** Vasari’s revised *Lives* of 1568; later French and Italian royalist art.

**Evidence against:** Francis was probably elsewhere on the date of death; Melzi’s immediate letter does not mention royal presence.

**Reputational effect:** converted Leonardo’s death into a scene of monarchy honoring genius.

**Label:** legend.

### Verrocchio broke his brushes after Leonardo’s angel

**Story:** Leonardo painted an angel in the *Baptism of Christ* so superior that Verrocchio abandoned painting.

**Source:** Vasari.

**Evidence:** Leonardo’s contribution is widely accepted, but Verrocchio continued overseeing painted works.

**Effect:** early foundation of the “effortless divine genius” narrative.

**Label:** embellished workshop legend.

### Leonardo as secret grand master of the Priory of Sion

**Story:** he headed an ancient secret society preserving knowledge about Jesus and Mary Magdalene.

**Modern circulation:** Pierre Plantard-related dossiers, *Holy Blood, Holy Grail*, Dan Brown’s *The Da Vinci Code*, film and television.

**Evidence:** the purported Priory lineage rests on twentieth-century fabricated documents. No Renaissance evidence connects Leonardo to such an organization.

**Effect:** globally recast paintings as encrypted theological testimony.

**Label:** modern conspiracy myth.

### Mary Magdalene in the Last Supper

**Story:** the figure traditionally identified as John is Mary Magdalene.

**Evidence against:** youthful, beardless representations of John were conventional. Preparatory and early interpretive traditions identify the apostolic group, not a hidden spouse of Jesus.

**Label:** modern fictional interpretation.

### Mirror writing as secret code

**Story:** Leonardo reversed writing to hide heresy, inventions, or occult teaching.

**Evidence:** he was left-handed and wrote reversed script naturally and efficiently; some ordinary notes, accounts, and reminders use it. He could write conventionally when necessary.

**Label:** popular myth with no need for conspiratorial explanation.

### Mona Lisa as Leonardo in disguise

**Story:** the portrait is an encoded self-portrait or “male Mona Lisa.”

**Evidence:** superficial morphing comparisons and wordplay; against this stands Vespucci’s 1503 reference to Lisa del Giocondo and the portrait’s early provenance.

**Label:** speculative modern theory.

### Mona Lisa’s smile conceals pregnancy, illness, or secret identity

Medical and biographical readings have proposed pregnancy, postpartum status, thyroid disease, facial paralysis, high cholesterol, dental loss, or psychological codes.

**Status:** generally untestable visual diagnosis. Some may be compatible with the image but lack confirming evidence.

### First modern scientist and inventor of everything

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century nationalism, industrial culture, museums, and popular science elevated Leonardo as inventor of nearly every later technology.

**Evidence:** genuine visionary studies coexist with copied precedents, impractical designs, and an absence of constructed prototypes.

**Label:** heroic technological mythology built on authentic notebooks.

### Vegetarian prophet and animal-rights activist

Early reports of compassion toward animals and bird release exist. The comprehensive modern identity of vegan or animal-rights campaigner projects later categories onto fragmentary evidence.

**Label:** partly grounded reputation, expanded into modern hagiography.

### Hidden journey to the East

Draft writings describing the Taurus region and geographical phenomena have been read as records of travel to Armenia or the Ottoman world.

**Evidence:** no independent travel documentation; literary exercise, proposed employment, or imagined eyewitness narration remains more likely.

**Label:** disputed travel theory.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Andrea del Verrocchio

Teacher and workshop master. The relationship provided Leonardo’s multidisciplinary technical formation. Claims that Verrocchio ceased painting out of defeat are legendary.

### Ludovico Sforza

Principal Milanese patron. Supported the *Last Supper*, court productions, engineering activity, and Sforza monument. Patronal delays and war contributed to the monument’s failure.

### Isabella d’Este

Prospective patron and correspondent through agents. She sought a painted work repeatedly; Leonardo did not satisfy her requests. Their exchanges document his reputation and aversion to deadline-driven painting.

### Luca Pacioli

Friend and mathematical collaborator. Leonardo illustrated *Divina proportione*. Their travels after Milan’s fall are documented or strongly reconstructed.

### Cesare Borgia

Military employer in 1502–03. Leonardo’s safe-conduct and maps document the relationship. No evidence proves participation in Borgia’s assassinations.

### Niccolò Machiavelli

Political-administrative associate in overlapping Borgia and Florentine contexts; connected particularly to the Arno diversion. Details of personal intimacy are sparse.

### Michelangelo

Rival artist. Their opposing Great Council Hall commissions created direct competition. Anecdotes of insults should be treated cautiously.

### Salaì

Pupil, household member, assistant, possible model, and beneficiary. His misconduct is recorded in Leonardo’s own expense notes. Sexual partnership is possible but unproved.

### Francesco Melzi

Pupil, companion, secretary, executor, and principal heir to the intellectual estate. His preservation work is essential to Leonardo’s survival as a writer.

### Francis I

Final royal patron. Provided residence, pension, title, and access to court. The deathbed embrace is legendary.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Sixteenth and seventeenth centuries

Vasari constructed Leonardo as a divinely gifted, beautiful, gracious, intellectually limitless man whose very abundance prevented completion. This became the central biographical template.

Leonardo’s followers transformed Milanese painting through enigmatic expression, soft modeling, devotional types, and imitative compositions. The category “Leonardeschi” includes artists of widely varying independence.

Melzi’s compilation of the *Book on Painting* and its later printed abridgment made Leonardo an authority in European academic art theory.

### Eighteenth and nineteenth centuries

Collecting, connoisseurship, Romantic ideas of genius, and national cultural politics expanded his reputation. The *Mona Lisa* became increasingly canonical. Nineteenth-century writers emphasized mystery, and Walter Pater’s famous prose helped turn the sitter into an emblem of timeless psychological ambiguity.

The theft of the *Mona Lisa* from the Louvre in 1911 and its recovery in 1913 generated unprecedented mass publicity.

### Twentieth century

Fascist Italy’s 1939 Leonardo exhibition mobilized him as a national and technological ancestor. Elsewhere he became the archetypal “Renaissance man,” a symbol of creativity crossing art and science.

Freud’s study shaped psychological and sexual interpretations despite its linguistic and evidentiary problems.

Facsimile publication and manuscript scholarship replaced many technological legends with more precise histories. The rediscovery of the Madrid codices expanded knowledge of his mechanics.

### Twenty-first century

Leonardo remains central to global museum culture, art markets, popular science, design education, and fictional conspiracy narratives.

The *Salvator Mundi* sale made a disputed Leonardo attribution a symbol of secrecy, sovereign wealth, restoration ethics, and extreme art-market value.

Current scholarship increasingly emphasizes:

- Workshop collaboration rather than solitary genius
- Manuscript dispersal and editorial intervention
- Material and conservation history
- Dependence on medieval and Renaissance predecessors
- Court labor and military patronage
- Sexuality without overclaiming
- Slavery and Mediterranean mobility in the debate over Caterina
- Distinction between conceptual drawings and operational inventions
- The productive role of incompletion

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the reviewed sources are included. Mere chronological overlap is excluded.

### Leon Battista Alberti

**Nature:** documented intellectual and professional influence, not a securely documented personal meeting.

Leonardo encountered Alberti’s theory of painting, perspective, architecture, mechanics, and the status of the artist as learned practitioner. Later claims that Leonardo personally accompanied Alberti to Medici gatherings are not securely documented.

**Label:** documented influence; personal relationship unresolved.

### Lorenzo di Piero de’ Medici

**Nature:** patronage-network and possible diplomatic connection in Florence, approximately 1470s–1482.

Leonardo worked in the Medici cultural sphere. Early tradition associates Lorenzo with sending Leonardo to Milan bearing a silver lyre. Direct long-term employment by Lorenzo is not firmly documented.

**Label:** reported patronage connection; exact scope disputed.

### Michelangelo di Lodovico Buonarroti Simoni

**Nature:** rivalry and parallel civic commissions, Florence, 1503–06.

Leonardo’s *Battle of Anghiari* and Michelangelo’s *Battle of Cascina* were commissioned for the same hall. Later testimony reports personal hostility and a public insult over the unfinished Sforza monument.

**Label:** documented institutional rivalry; reported personal confrontation.

### Aristotle

**Nature:** intellectual influence through texts and the Aristotelian natural-philosophical tradition.

Leonardo’s book lists and notes show engagement with Aristotelian subjects and commentators, although he increasingly tested inherited propositions against observation.

**Label:** documented intellectual influence; no personal connection possible.

### Plato

**Nature:** intellectual and reputational influence, often mediated through Renaissance Platonism, Pacioli, Ficino, and mathematical traditions.

The Louvre’s interpretive account places Leonardo’s view of mathematically governed nature within a Pythagorean-Platonic lineage. Direct depth of reading in Plato is uncertain.

**Label:** documented cultural/intellectual influence; extent disputed.

**Label:** documented textual connection.

### Archimedes of Syracuse

**Nature:** mathematical and mechanical influence.

Leonardo sought Archimedean works and worked within a Renaissance revival of Archimedean mechanics.

**Label:** documented intellectual influence.

### Roger Bacon

**Nature:** indirect textual and scientific influence.

Bacon belonged to the medieval optical and natural-philosophical traditions available to Leonardo. Direct use of particular texts is less certain than the broader tradition.

**Label:** reported or reconstructed intellectual influence.

### Plinio el Viejo

**Nature:** textual influence.

Leonardo’s lists include Pliny’s *Natural History*, a source for natural history, art, and ancient anecdote.

**Label:** documented textual influence.
