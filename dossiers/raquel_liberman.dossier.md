# Raquel Liberman: Research Dossier

## Scope, Method, and Evidentiary Note

Raquel Liberman left no memoir and did not become publicly known until the last decade of her life. Her biography must therefore be reconstructed from several bodies of evidence that do not always agree:

1. Her passport, marriage contract, family photographs, and Yiddish correspondence with her first husband, preserved by descendants and studied by Nora Glickman and Myrtha Schalom.
2. Her police statements and the judicial summary of the Zwi Migdal proceeding published in *La Gaceta del Foro* in November 1930.
3. Contemporary newspapers, particularly *Crítica*, *La Prensa*, *La Razón*, and *El Mundo*.
4. Julio L. Alsogaray’s retrospective police account, *Trilogía de la trata de blancas* (1933), which contains firsthand information but also reflects his institutional interests and sometimes antisemitic criminological language.
5. Records of Ezras Noshim, the Buenos Aires branch of the Jewish Association for the Protection of Girls and Women.
6. Later scholarship, especially Nora Glickman and historian Mir Yarfitz.
7. Novels, journalism, commemorations, films, and political campaigns that transformed Liberman into a symbol.

The reconstruction below uses the following labels:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a surviving contemporary record or multiple independent records.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a generally credible secondary source but not independently verified here from the underlying record.
- **Testimony/allegation:** asserted in a police or judicial statement, without necessarily having been judicially proved.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** a historian’s analysis of incomplete or conflicting evidence.
- **Disputed claim:** contradicted by other evidence or scholars.
- **Rumor, legend, or myth:** culturally significant but unsupported or demonstrably fictionalized.

The familiar expression “white slavery” appears in contemporary titles and quotations. It was historically used for trafficking and coerced prostitution but also carried racial, moral-panic, anti-immigrant, and sometimes antisemitic meanings.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and siblings

Liberman was born into a poor, reportedly large Jewish family. Her parents’ names and occupations have not been established securely in the publicly accessible sources consulted. Later biographies say the family moved from Berdychiv to Warsaw while she was a child, apparently for economic reasons.

Claims that she was born in Łódź or Warsaw occur in older popular histories. Her recovered passport identifies Berdychiv, and the Berdychiv identification is now the strongest available version.

The number and names of her siblings remain uncertain. Some retellings refer vaguely to a sister or to relatives implicated in the sex trade, but the best-documented Argentine relative was her husband’s sister, not necessarily Liberman’s own sister.

### First husband: Yaacov Ferber

**Documented fact:** Liberman married the Warsaw tailor **Yaacov—or Iakov/Iaacov/Yákov—Ferber** under Jewish law on **21 December 1919**. Their surviving *ketubah* supports the marriage.

The marriage appears to have been genuine rather than one of the fraudulent “ritual marriages” later associated with trafficking recruitment. Their Yiddish letters record ordinary marital concerns: money for passage, Ferber’s health, their children, family accommodation, and their hoped-for reunion in Argentina.

Ferber migrated alone to Argentina in 1921, joining his sister and brother-in-law at Tapalqué in Buenos Aires Province. His surviving letters referred to illness, hospital stays, the birth or circumcision of his younger son, and the difficulty of financing his family’s voyage.

### Children

The couple had two sons:

- **Joshua David Ferber**, born in 1920; Spanish-language accounts commonly call him **José**.
- A second son, born in 1921 after his father had departed Poland, usually called **Moisés** in recovered-family accounts. Some summaries supply different names, including Jaime or Salomón; these are weakly supported and probably reflect confused retelling.

The younger child was initially registered with his mother’s surname because his father was abroad.

After their father’s death and their mother’s move to Buenos Aires, the boys remained in or around Tapalqué with relatives or a foster family. Liberman concealed their existence in her best-known judicial testimony, almost certainly to protect them from stigma or retaliation.

Her sons reportedly grew up without being told that their mother had engaged in prostitution or had been the complainant in the Zwi Migdal affair. In 1993 a granddaughter, **Raquel Ferber de Romeo**, recognized Liberman’s face when Myrtha Schalom displayed her identity-card photograph on the television program *Siglo XX, cambalache*. The family then produced letters, photographs, the passport, and marriage documentation that materially revised the established biography.

### Ferber family and the prostitution economy

**Documented fact:** Contemporary press material identifies **Elke/Elka/Helke Ferber**, Yaacov’s sister in Tapalqué, as having been arrested as a member of Zwi Migdal during the 1930 proceedings.

**Scholarly interpretation:** Myrtha Schalom and later writers infer that Helke or her household may have facilitated Raquel’s entry into prostitution. Another reconstruction, associated with José Luis Scarsi, argues that Yaacov and Raquel themselves operated or benefited from a brothel at Tapalqué after Yaacov sold a barbering business.

These possibilities complicate the standard story but do not prove that Liberman entered prostitution without pressure. Poverty, family dependence, regulated sex work, procurer control, and coercion could coexist. The accessible evidence does not establish a single definitive mechanism of entry.

### Later partner: Natalio Vainstok

Ezras Noshim records summarized by Mir Yarfitz indicate that, in **1932**, **María Vainstok** complained that her husband, **Natalio Vainstok**, had abandoned his family or failed in his obligations while maintaining a relationship with Raquel Liberman.

This is a **documented contemporary allegation**, not a known criminal charge against Liberman. Ezras Noshim supported María Vainstok in seeking divorce and maintenance. The episode shows that Liberman’s later private life did not fit the simplified picture of uninterrupted seclusion after the Zwi Migdal prosecution.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1900–1919

Liberman was born in Berdychiv, an important center of Jewish life in the western Russian Empire. The city had a large Yiddish-speaking Jewish population and was associated with commerce, crafts, Hasidism, and recurrent economic insecurity.

Her family subsequently moved west to Warsaw. The exact date is unknown.

Myrtha Schalom, relying on recovered family documents, reports that Liberman attended Warsaw’s **Jerusalén/Jerusalem school**. The identification of the institution and extent of her attendance require further archival confirmation. Her surviving Yiddish letters nevertheless establish that she was literate. Their organized handwriting, grammar, household detail, and references to Jewish observance contradict depictions of her as wholly uneducated.

No university education, formal professional credential, teacher, or intellectual mentor is documented. Sewing or dressmaking appears to have been her practical training. The search for viable work as a seamstress later became part of accounts explaining her move into prostitution.

Warsaw during her adolescence underwent the First World War, German occupation, food shortages, the collapse of imperial authority, and the emergence of an independent Polish state. Her letters reportedly refer to the hardship from which she hoped Argentina would provide an escape.

## Marriage, Separation, and Migration, 1919–1923

### Marriage and children

Liberman and Yaacov Ferber married in Warsaw on 21 December 1919. Joshua/José was born in 1920. Their second son, usually identified as Moisés, followed in 1921.

### Yaacov Ferber’s departure

Ferber traveled to Argentina in 1921, apparently seeking employment and relying on his sister Helke’s household in Tapalqué. His health was already poor. The surviving correspondence describes pulmonary illness and hospitalization; later biographies identify tuberculosis.

Liberman pressed him to send passage money. Her letters express urgency, anxiety over his health, gratitude to the relatives housing him, and uncertainty about life in Argentina.

### Raquel and the children’s voyage

Two arrival dates circulate:

- **30 September 1922** appears in several later biographies.
- **22 October 1922** is given by Myrtha Schalom’s document-based account.

The discrepancy may distinguish arrival in Argentina from disembarkation or may reflect differing passenger evidence. The exact date should therefore be treated as unresolved within late September–October 1922.

She traveled with both small children, reunited with Ferber, and lived at Tapalqué. This documentary family migration disproves the older legend that a trafficker named Jaime Cissinger/Kissinger recruited her in Poland with a false marriage proposal and brought her directly to Buenos Aires.

### Death of Yaacov Ferber

Ferber died within months, probably in **1923**, usually reported from tuberculosis. Liberman was left with two small children, limited Spanish, no secure income, and dependence on Ferber’s relatives.

Some accounts say Yaacov was already unemployed and gravely ill when she arrived. Others, particularly Scarsi’s reconstruction, report that he had engaged in a barbering business and that the couple participated briefly in a Tapalqué brothel. The second version remains a **reported but contested reconstruction**.

## Entry into Prostitution and Zwi Migdal, circa 1923–1928

Liberman left her children with relatives or foster carers at Tapalqué and went to Buenos Aires in search of work. Accounts commonly say she tried to obtain sewing work within the Yiddish-speaking community but could not support herself and the boys.

She eventually worked in regulated prostitution and became connected to **Jaime Cissinger**, whose surname also appears as Kissinger, Cynsiger, or similar variants. He was described as her **caftán**—a procurer, pimp, or protector. She apparently paid him part of her earnings but did not always reside with him.

The degree of coercion at her initial entry remains disputed:

- **Traditional victim narrative:** Cissinger or another trafficker deceived, abducted, purchased, or immediately imprisoned her.
- **Court-derived reconstruction:** Her known 1922 migration was to her genuine husband; there is no evidence that Cissinger recruited her in Europe. Her first involvement in prostitution appears to have occurred later in Argentina.
- **Yarfitz’s interpretation:** Her initial sex work was “apparently consensual” in the limited historical sense that no kidnapping or seduction was documented. This does not exclude poverty, family pressure, economic compulsion, violence, or exploitation.
- **Scarsi/Butierrez interpretation:** She entered sex work knowingly and moved in and out of it while developing considerable knowledge of the organization.
- **Unresolved question:** Whether Helke Ferber, another relative, a fellow passenger named Bronya Coyman, or Cissinger introduced her to the Buenos Aires network.

Liberman’s name was entered in the municipal police register of prostitutes. In regulated Buenos Aires prostitution, registration subjected women to identification, medical inspection, residence rules, and police surveillance. It did not make coercion or theft lawful.

For several years she earned and accumulated money. The widespread claim that she secretly saved for six years is plausible but not precisely documented. Nor is there conclusive evidence that a sympathetic client staged a fictional “purchase” by pretending to employ her in a Mendoza brothel; that episode belongs primarily to later narrative tradition.

## First Exit, Shop Ownership, and Marriage to José Salomón Korn, 1928

By 1928 Liberman had accumulated enough capital to attempt to leave prostitution. She opened a shop at **Callao 515**, near the Once district. Sources variously describe it as:

- an antique shop;
- a store of art objects;
- a shop selling sheet music and decorative goods.

The shop’s existence and address are supported by court-derived research. It was located within a few blocks of numerous brothels and homes associated with members of Sociedad Varsovia, so it was neither socially nor geographically outside their orbit.

### 2 June 1928 police appearance

**Documented fact:** On **2 June 1928**, Liberman went to Commissioner **Julio L. Alsogaray** of the Seventh Police District. She stated that she had left prostitution and wanted her name removed from the municipal register.

The 1928 approach is frequently omitted in commemorative accounts, which present 31 December 1929 as her first contact with Alsogaray.

Liberman also complained that **José Salomón Korn**, a Sociedad Varsovia member, had deceived her into marriage and appropriated substantial property. Her alleged losses included clothing, jewelry, and cash. Later accounts place the amount near **15,000 Argentine pesos moneda nacional**, but the precise total is not consistently reported and should not be treated as settled.

According to her account, Korn entered her shop as a stranger, courted her, and proposed marriage. The ceremony was religious rather than a securely registered civil marriage. Later narratives say it took place at the society’s premises or synagogue at **Córdoba 3280**, before ten witnesses associated with the organization.

Liberman claimed she did not initially recognize Korn as a procurer or Zwi Migdal associate. Yarfitz notes that her previous proximity to the organization makes this claim difficult to assess, but no evidence proves she knew his identity before the marriage.

### Theft, pressure, and return to prostitution

Liberman alleged that Korn used the marital relationship to acquire her money and valuables and, with organizational pressure, compelled her back into prostitution. She said she was subjected to threats including death and facial disfigurement.

She first sought restitution inside the organization. According to the 1930 judicial summary, she appealed to at least five members and eventually approached the society’s president. Only after those approaches failed did she pursue police action.

This sequence matters: her initial grievance was not an abstract denunciation of all prostitution. It concerned Korn’s deception, appropriation of her assets, coercion, and the organization’s protection of him.

Liberman temporarily sought to withdraw or soften her complaint and returned to the prostitution register. Traditional accounts explain this as the result of threats and a bribe of cash or jewelry. Pressure is credible and appears in the case record, but the exact mixture of fear, negotiation, economic necessity, and strategic retreat is unresolved.

## Renewed Complaint and the Zwi Migdal Case, 1929–1931

### Varsovia becomes Zwi Migdal

The organization had operated legally as the **Sociedad Israelita de Socorros Mutuos Varsovia**, a mutual-aid society serving Jewish procurers, brothel owners, and some sex workers excluded from mainstream Jewish institutions.

Following protests by Polish diplomatic representatives over its use of “Warsaw,” it adopted the name **Zwi Migdal** in 1929, honoring an early associate or founder. Sources differ on whether the formal change occurred on 23 May or 20 August 1929.

The society maintained premises, a synagogue, burial arrangements, mutual assistance, legal-defense funds, membership registers, and business networks. These genuine mutual-aid functions coexisted with the organization of prostitution and protection of procurers.

### 31 December 1929 complaint

On **31 December 1929**, Liberman returned to Alsogaray and made or renewed the complaint that became the foundation of the major case. Later tradition gives her one of two defiant replies:

- “Sólo se muere una vez; la denuncia no la retiro.”
- “Todo lo que usted quiera.”

Neither quotation has been verified here as a verbatim contemporary transcript. They are best treated as **reported recollections that became legendary formulas**.

Liberman identified herself in parts of the proceeding as single, omitted her children, and altered or obscured her arrival history. Recovered family records demonstrate that those statements were false in literal terms. The likely explanation is concealment for her sons’ safety and reputation, not fabrication of the Korn complaint. Alsogaray and the investigating judge may have known or tolerated the omissions, but that remains inferential.

### Investigation

Fiscal agent **Luis A. Barberis** formed an investigative group that included police officials **Mario Otamendi** and **Horacio Amadeo Vieyra**, together with Yiddish interpreter **Selig Ganapol**, an investigator for Ezras Noshim.

On **12 March 1930**, investigators visited the society headquarters at Córdoba 3280. Bookkeeper **Jacobo Saltzman** gave access to records including:

- minute books;
- membership registers;
- account books;
- applications for membership;
- telephone notebooks;
- statutes;
- documentation of legal recognition.

These records connected the society’s members with a large body of prior arrests and cases. Yarfitz’s reading of the judicial summary finds more than **120 earlier trials** and over **2,000 arrests** involving traceable members, contradicting the legend that Liberman was the first person ever to generate legal action against the organization.

### Testimony before Judge Rodríguez Ocampo

Judge **Manuel Rodríguez Ocampo** took charge of the judicial inquiry. Liberman ratified her account before him; one source dates this ratification to **24 May 1930**.

The prosecution argued that Zwi Migdal operated collectively: procurement, transport, control, sale or transfer of women, and legal defense were organized under disciplined leadership while masquerading as lawful mutual aid.

Liberman was a uniquely useful witness because she described the relationship between an individual act—Korn’s alleged deception and theft—and the institutional machinery protecting him.

### Arrests and preliminary ruling

On **27 September 1930**, Rodríguez Ocampo ordered the prosecution and preventive detention of **108 members**. Contemporary and later accounts also mention warrants or cases involving roughly **334 additional fugitives**, producing totals near 442.

These figures are frequently conflated. “442 arrests” is not accurate: 108 is the commonly documented number actually subjected to the major initial detention or processing order; the larger figure included people sought, absent, or otherwise named.

The society’s legal personality was revoked, its headquarters exposed, and its ability to operate openly was gravely damaged.

### Appeal and collapse of the prosecution

The defendants appealed. During the judicial recess, an appellate panel reviewed an evidentiary record reportedly running to approximately 5,000 pages.

By January 1931, most defendants were released. Sources commonly say only **three minor participants** remained in preventive detention or subject to proceedings. The appellate judges held that the evidence did not sufficiently prove unlawful association against the mass of defendants and noted that other purported victims had not testified.

Therefore:

- **Documented:** Liberman’s evidence helped trigger a massive investigation, arrests, loss of legal status, and public exposure.
- **Not established:** that her complaint produced final convictions of all or most Zwi Migdal members.
- **Misleading popular claim:** that a single denunciation legally “destroyed” the entire international network.
- **More defensible interpretation:** the case broke the society’s open institutional power through police action, publicity, political change, immigration measures, and reputational collapse, even though the mass prosecution largely failed.

Alsogaray said he was removed from the police in December 1930 without adequate explanation. Claims that his removal and the appellate reversal prove bribery or a coordinated conspiracy are plausible within the broader record of police and political corruption but were not judicially established in the sources consulted.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Callao 515 shop

Liberman’s principal documented independent business was the shop at Callao 515. Its inventory may have included antiques, art objects, decorative items, and sheet music.

The shop represents both a financial achievement and vulnerability. Her accumulated sex-work earnings were converted into a small commercial enterprise, but Korn allegedly acquired or dissipated its capital through marriage, theft, or coercion.

No surviving corporate registration, complete inventory, balance sheet, property deed, or final accounting has been identified in the consulted material.

### Sex-work earnings and savings

The record indicates that Liberman retained enough money during her relationship with Cissinger to finance the shop. This suggests a more complex economic position than total confinement: she was exploited but retained some earning and saving capacity.

The sum allegedly taken by Korn is often reported as 15,000 pesos plus jewels and clothes. It should be labeled an **alleged amount**, derived from complaint narratives rather than a known final civil judgment.

### Zwi Migdal

Liberman was not shown to have held office in Zwi Migdal. Her relationship was instead as a prostitute under the protection or control of associated procurers, the wife or ritual wife of member José Salomón Korn, and later a complainant.

The society’s mutual-aid structure included burial, religious observance, financial support, and legal defense. Its records also provided the evidentiary map used against it.

### Property

No verified real estate owned by Liberman has been identified. References to “buying” the Callao premises sometimes mean acquiring the business rather than the building itself.

No patents, inventions, industrial technologies, formal investments, or corporate directorships are documented.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

No party membership, political office, ideological manifesto, or organized feminist affiliation is documented.

Liberman’s action later became political because abolitionists, Jewish communal organizations, conservative moral reformers, journalists, police officials, feminists, and sex-worker-rights writers all interpreted it through competing programs.

Her recovered letters show continuing Jewish cultural literacy. The letters reportedly mention holidays, family rites, and Jewish history. Her first marriage was contracted under Jewish law. Her marriage to Korn was also religious, although later accounts characterized it as fraudulent or legally ineffective.

Mainstream Jewish organizations in Argentina excluded procurers and many prostitutes as **tme’im/temeim**, “impure” people. They could be barred from communal institutions and ordinary cemeteries. Zwi Migdal consequently maintained religious and burial institutions of its own.

Liberman cooperated at times with Ezras Noshim, but its support was conditional. It opposed traffickers and coercion while also enforcing conventional family and sexual morality. Its 1932 support for María Vainstok against Liberman demonstrates that the relationship was not one of uncomplicated patronage.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Yaacov Ferber

First husband, correspondent, father of her children, and the reason for her migration to Argentina. His illness and death were decisive economic and personal ruptures.

### José and Moisés Ferber

Her sons. Protecting them appears to explain her concealment of her family in court and probably her effort to return to Poland near the end of her life.

### Helke/Elke Ferber

Sister-in-law and initial host at Tapalqué; arrested as a Zwi Migdal member. Her role in introducing Liberman to prostitution is probable in some reconstructions but not conclusively documented.

### Jaime Cissinger

Liberman’s procurer or protector during much of her sex-work period. The extent of his violence and control is incompletely documented. The popular story transforming him into the man who recruited her in Europe conflicts with her passport, genuine marriage, and family voyage.

### José Salomón Korn

Second husband or purported husband; Zwi Migdal associate; accused by Liberman of deception, theft, threats, and coercion back into prostitution. Her conflict with him became the immediate foundation of the wider case.

### Julio L. Alsogaray

Commissioner of the Seventh Police District. He received Liberman’s statements, pursued the organization, and made her central to his later narrative. He is conventionally described as incorruptible.

Yarfitz cautions that Alsogaray had a preexisting campaign or vendetta against the society, institutional ambitions, and a criminological discourse that could become antisemitic. His assistance to Liberman was real, but his retrospective self-presentation should not be treated as neutral.

### Manuel Rodríguez Ocampo

Investigating judge who converted the complaint into an association-wide prosecution and ordered the processing and detention of 108 people.

### Selig Ganapol and Ezras Noshim

Ganapol acted as Yiddish interpreter and anti-trafficking investigator. Ezras Noshim initially supported Liberman and helped bridge the linguistic divide between complainant and authorities.

### Natalio and María Vainstok

Natalio was reportedly Liberman’s later partner. María’s 1932 complaint brought Liberman into conflict with Ezras Noshim over family abandonment, divorce, and support.

### Simon Brutkievich

Zwi Migdal president during the crisis. Later accounts say he or the organization offered Liberman money and jewels to withdraw her complaint. This is best classified as a **reported allegation in the case narrative**, not an independently proven bribery conviction.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Evidentiary Findings

### Was Liberman abducted or deceived into migrating?

**Disputed claim:** A trafficker met her in Poland, promised marriage, and brought her directly to Argentina for prostitution.

**Evidence against:** Passport, *ketubah*, letters, two children, and migration to reunite with Yaacov Ferber at Tapalqué.

**Finding:** The direct-abduction migration narrative is not compatible with the strongest family documentation.

### Did she enter prostitution voluntarily?

“Voluntary” is too blunt for the surviving evidence.

- There is no documented European recruitment fraud.
- Poverty, widowhood, lack of Spanish, childcare separation, and constrained employment choices are documented or strongly supported.
- Her capacity to retain savings and leave prostitution does not negate exploitation.
- Her allegations against Korn explicitly included coercion and threats.

**Finding:** Her initial entry mechanism is unresolved; her later coercion and economic victimization formed the substance of her complaint.

### Was she a passive prisoner for six or eleven continuous years?

**Disputed claim.** She appears to have moved between prostitution and independent commerce, dealt directly with organizational leaders, accumulated capital, married Korn, used police and communal channels, and later had a relationship with Vainstok.

This evidence demonstrates agency but does not show absence of victimization.

### Did she lie in court?

**Documented fact:** She concealed her widowhood, sons, and actual arrival history by presenting herself as single and without Argentine family.

**Likely explanation:** protection of her children and separation of the proceeding from the potentially compromising Ferber family connection.

There is no basis for treating those omissions as proof that every substantive allegation against Korn or Zwi Migdal was false.

### Did Zwi Migdal invent the second marriage?

Liberman alleged that Korn’s courtship and religious marriage were an organizational device designed to seize her assets and return her to prostitution. The larger pattern of religious-only marriages used by procurers is documented. Whether every participant in her ceremony knowingly took part in a prearranged plot was not established by a final conviction.

### Did her complaint single-handedly destroy Zwi Migdal?

**Mythic overstatement.** Her evidence was a catalyst and supplied a narratively powerful witness. The organization had already faced thousands of arrests, previous cases, Jewish communal opposition, international anti-trafficking campaigns, diplomatic pressure, and police scrutiny.

Its loss of legal status and public operating capacity followed the 1930 case, but most defendants were released on appeal. Political upheaval, immigration enforcement, publicity, internal weakness, and later changes to prostitution law also contributed.

### Police and judicial corruption

Contemporary critics and later historians describe Zwi Migdal as protected by bribes and relationships with police, judges, politicians, and municipal officials. The frequency of previous arrests followed by release and the organization’s collective legal-defense fund support the existence of substantial protection.

Specific allegations against named officials require care. Claims that the appellate judges were directly bribed, or that Alsogaray’s removal was ordered by the organization, were not proved in the consulted sources.

### Antisemitism and the case

Zwi Migdal was a real organization involving Jewish procurers and sex workers. Jewish organizations also led sustained opposition to it.

At the same time, police writing, moral-reform journalism, and nationalist politics sometimes treated Jewish criminality as inherently foreign or racial. Alsogaray’s Spanish book opened with material assigning distinctive responsibility to “the Hebrew people”; its French edition omitted some of this language.

Later Nazi propaganda incorporated Jewish trafficking into a generalized conspiracy image. The existence of Jewish offenders does not establish the conspiratorial proposition that Jews collectively controlled international prostitution.

### 1932 Vainstok complaint

**Documented allegation:** María Vainstok accused Natalio of neglecting his family during a relationship with Liberman. Ezras Noshim supported María.

No evidence consulted shows that Liberman was prosecuted, sued to judgment, or found legally responsible.

### 1934 inspection and suspicion of renewed prostitution

When Liberman sought documents for travel to Poland, officials asked Ezras Noshim to establish that she was not then engaged in prostitution. An investigator reportedly visited without warning; Liberman refused to open the door, producing suspicion that she had returned to sex work.

That refusal is not proof. Her occupation in the final year of her life remains unresolved.

### Dedicated adversarial-search finding, 2024–2026

Searches combining Liberman’s name with controversy, scandal, accusations, lawsuits, fraud, conspiracy, criticism, and related Spanish and English terms found no credible newly disclosed criminal case or personal scandal from 2024–2026.

Recent criticism instead concerns:

- the political use of her memory by abolitionist campaigns;
- the erasure of her agency as a sex worker;
- the oversimplified claim that all prostitution is identical to trafficking;
- the participation of conservative, antisemitic, police, and Jewish communal interests in the campaign against Zwi Migdal;
- exaggeration of her complaint’s judicial success.

No credible evidence located supports conspiracy narratives that Liberman’s existence, the proceeding, or the organization itself was wholly fabricated.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The innocent bride sold on arrival”

**Story:** A prosperous-looking trafficker named Jaime Cissinger/Kissinger seduced Liberman in Poland, married or promised to marry her, transported her to Buenos Aires, and immediately imprisoned her in a brothel.

**Appearance and circulation:** Popular histories of “white slavery,” later newspaper features, institutional commemorations, and fiction.

**Evidence:** Contradicted by the recovered correspondence, *ketubah*, children, and family migration to Yaacov Ferber.

**Effect:** Made Liberman fit the archetypal “marriage ruse” and removed the less easily narrated period of widowhood, poverty, and possible knowing entry into sex work.

### “She was sold twice”

**Story:** A compassionate client and his brother pretended to buy her for a Mendoza brothel so that she could escape; Zwi Migdal later recaptured her through Korn.

**Evidence:** The Korn marriage and renewed coercion rest on her complaint; the Mendoza purchase has not been corroborated from the strongest court-derived scholarship.

**Effect:** Structured her biography as captivity, liberation, recapture, and heroic final escape.

### “One woman destroyed a worldwide mafia”

**Story:** Liberman’s single complaint immediately dismantled Zwi Migdal and sent its hundreds of members to prison.

**Evidence against:** Earlier prosecutions existed; only 108 were initially processed or detained in the principal ruling; most were released on appeal; few remained under detention.

**Historical residue:** Her testimony was nonetheless central to the organization’s public delegitimation and loss of open legal existence.

### “Alsogaray the incorruptible policeman”

**Story:** Liberman found the one honest officer in a completely corrupted force.

**Evidence:** Alsogaray pursued the case vigorously and left a detailed account. His conduct in this matter contrasts with documented police tolerance and protection of procurers.

**Qualification:** His account is self-interested, shaped by an older personal campaign, moralizing criminology, and ethnic generalization. “Incorruptible” is a reputation, not an independently audited description of his whole career.

### “Sólo se muere una vez”

The sentence has become Liberman’s signature declaration. Its exact documentary origin is uncertain. It may derive from Alsogaray, press paraphrase, or later biography.

Its repetition helped transform a complainant pursuing theft, freedom, and safety into an archetype of fearless testimony.

### Heroine, victim, sex worker, or “accidental heroine”

These identities reflect competing memorial regimes:

- **Abolitionist memory:** exemplary survivor who exposed sexual slavery.
- **Jewish communal memory:** a woman whose resistance helped isolate criminal elements wrongly generalized to the entire community.
- **Sex-worker-rights revision:** a working woman with agency whose central grievance was theft, coercion, and loss of autonomy rather than sex work itself.
- **Scarsi’s formulation:** an “accidental heroine” whose case became larger than her private objective.
- **Popular culture:** “La Polaca,” a tragic national heroine.

None alone captures the full record.

### Antisemitic conspiracy narratives

The Zwi Migdal has been invoked in propaganda alleging a transhistorical Jewish conspiracy controlling prostitution. The organization’s existence and crimes are documented; the collective ethnic conspiracy is not.

Historical evidence instead shows sharp conflict within Jewish Argentina: mainstream congregations, newspapers, women’s organizations, and international Jewish associations ostracized and investigated the *temeim*. That opposition sometimes adopted its own punitive or stigmatizing treatment of prostitutes.

## Later Life, 1931–1935

After the prosecution, Liberman reestablished contact with her sons and lived in Buenos Aires. The exact household sequence and source of income are uncertain.

The 1932 Vainstok episode documents an intimate relationship and continued contact with Ezras Noshim.

By 1934 she was seriously ill and sought a Polish visa to travel with her sons. A letter from the IWO/YIVO-associated archive to the Polish consulate records the effort. The intended journey was probably an attempt to return to surviving family and remove the children from danger or stigma.

Officials required reassurance that she was not practicing prostitution. Ezras Noshim’s unsuccessful surprise visit left the question unresolved. Bureaucratic delay and declining health prevented the voyage.

---

## Death and Burial

Liberman died on **7 April 1935**, reportedly in **Hospital Argerich**, Buenos Aires, from thyroid cancer. She was 34. Some secondary literature gives 17 April or “cancer of the throat”; 7 April and thyroid cancer are the better-supported modern consensus.

Her sons were approximately fourteen and fifteen.

For decades her grave was said to be unknown. Later documentary work identified her burial under her married name, reportedly **Rujel Lea L. de Ferber**, in the cemetery at Villa Domínico/Avellaneda associated with the *temeim* and Zwi Migdal milieu.

The burial is historically significant. Although she had denounced the organization, mainstream communal exclusion of prostitutes reportedly left her in the stigmatized cemetery also used by procurers. Gabriela Böhm initially reported difficulty locating her grave; subsequent researchers stated that it had been found through burial documentation.

## Works by Raquel Liberman

Liberman was not a published author. No books, essays, patents, artworks, manifestos, or formally issued memoirs are known.

### Surviving correspondence

- **Letters between Ruchla Laja Liberman and Yaacov Ferber**, 1921–1922, in Yiddish.  
  **Status:** genuine private correspondence preserved by descendants; portions translated, quoted, or summarized by Nora Glickman and Myrtha Schalom. No complete critical edition was identified.

The letters concern:

- Ferber’s health and hospital stays;
- passage money;
- the children;
- the younger son’s *bris*;
- relatives in Tapalqué;
- hardship in Warsaw;
- anticipated life in Argentina;
- Jewish holidays and family observance.

### Police and judicial testimony

- Statement to Julio L. Alsogaray, 2 June 1928.
- Renewed complaint or denunciation, 31 December 1929.
- Testimony and ratification before Manuel Rodríguez Ocampo, 1930.

**Status:** authentic legal speech as summarized in police and court materials, but not an authored publication. The principal contemporary printed vehicle is the November 1930 *Gaceta del Foro* case summary.

### Attributed quotations

“Sólo se muere una vez; la denuncia no la retiro” and “Todo lo que usted quiera” are widely attributed to her but should not be treated as securely verified verbatim statements without examination of the original transcript.

## Books and Major Works About Raquel Liberman

### Documentary and scholarly works

- **Julio L. Alsogaray**, *Trilogía de la trata de blancas: rufianes, policía, municipalidad* (Buenos Aires: L. J. Rosso, 1933). Firsthand police account; indispensable but self-interested and ethnically polemical.
- **Julio L. Alsogaray**, *La Prostitution en Argentine: étude documentaire* (Paris: Denoël et Steele, 1935), translated by Charles Louis Constant. French adaptation omitting some material in the Spanish edition.
- **Gerardo Bra**, *La organización negra: la increíble historia de la Zwi Migdal* (Buenos Aires: Corregidor, 1982). Influential popular history; José Luis Scarsi criticizes it as insufficiently documented.
- **Donna J. Guy**, *Sex and Danger in Buenos Aires: Prostitution, Family, and Nation in Argentina* (University of Nebraska Press, 1991). Contextual history of prostitution, law, nationalism, and gender; not a biography of Liberman.
- **Nora Glickman**, *The Jewish White Slave Trade and the Untold Story of Raquel Liberman* (Garland, 2000; later Routledge edition). Major documentary-literary reconstruction using family correspondence and descendants.
- **Isabel Vincent**, *Bodies and Souls: The Tragic Plight of Three Jewish Women Forced into Prostitution in the Americas* (William Morrow, 2005). Journalistic history; reinforces the trafficking-victim framework.
- **Mir Yarfitz**, *Impure Migration: Jews and Sex Work in Golden Age Argentina* (Rutgers University Press, 2019). Court-record-based revision emphasizing migration, labor, agency, and the ideological construction of “white slavery.”
- **José Luis Scarsi**, *Cómo y por qué se formó la Zwi Migdal* (publication details vary by edition). Revisionist study drawing on court and press material.
- **Edward J. Bristow**, *Prostitution and Prejudice: The Jewish Fight against White Slavery, 1870–1939* (Oxford/Clarendon Press, 1982). International Jewish anti-trafficking context.
- **Andrés Carretero**, *Prostitución en Buenos Aires* (1998). General historical context.
- **Raúl A. Schnabel**, *Historia de la trata de personas en Argentina como persistencia de la esclavitud*. Institutional historical survey repeating parts of the standard Liberman narrative.

### Fiction, poetry, and drama

- **Myrtha Schalom**, *La Polaca* (Buenos Aires, 2003; subsequent editions). Biographical novel based partly on family documents. It should not be used as an unmarked factual transcript.
- **Humberto Costantini**, *Rapsodia de Raquel Liberman*. Unfinished novel/poetic project; two portions or volumes were reportedly drafted before the author’s death.
- **Humberto Costantini**, “Milonga de una mujer.” Poetic commemoration.
- **Carlos Luis Serrano**, *Raquel Liberman: una historia de Pichincha*. Stage play connecting her story with prostitution districts and the Zwi Migdal network.
- **Elsa Drucaroff**, *El infierno prometido* (2006). Historical novel about trafficking and Jewish migration; Liberman’s world is contextual rather than a documentary biography.
- **Ilan Sheinfeld**, *Ma’aseh ba-taba’at* / *The Tale of a Ring* (2007). Hebrew historical novel involving the trafficking milieu.
- **Edgardo Cozarinsky**, *El rufián moldavo* / *The Moldavian Pimp* (2004). Fiction about Jewish prostitution, memory, and performance.
- **María Inés Krimer**, *Sangre kosher* (2010). Crime fiction drawing on the Zwi Migdal historical memory.
- **Talia Carner**, *The Third Daughter* (2019). Historical novel inspired by Eastern European Jewish trafficking narratives, not a biography of Liberman.
- **Gerardo H. Badoglio**, *Mistonga, florcita de lis* (2017). Fiction referring explicitly to Liberman and the trafficking milieu.
- **Mariel Rosciano**, *En el nombre de Raquel* (stage adaptation, first presented circa 2013), derived from Schalom’s novel.

## Films, Television, Interviews, and Archival Material

### Documentary

- **Gabriela Böhm**, *Raquel: A Marked Woman* (also described in publicity as *In Raquel’s Footsteps*), documentary short, approximately 33 minutes, released during the 2010s. Uses photographs, letters, expert interviews, reenactments, and research into Liberman’s descendants and grave.

### Television and fiction

- *Siglo XX, cambalache* (Argentina), 1993 episode or discussion concerning the 1930s and Zwi Migdal. Myrtha Schalom’s display of Liberman’s identity-card photograph prompted the granddaughter’s identification.
- *Argentina, tierra de amor y venganza* (2019). The character **Raquel Novack**, played by Eugenia “China” Suárez, was inspired by Liberman but is fictionalized.
- *La malavida* (Hugo Fregonese, 1974). Fictional treatment of organized prostitution and corruption, part of the cinematic environment through which the Zwi Migdal story was remembered.
- *Proceso a la infamia* (Alejandro Doria, produced in the 1970s; release history variously dated 1974/1978). Fictionalized treatment of prostitution networks and political corruption.

### Archives and documentary repositories

- Family archive: passport, *ketubah*, photographs, and Yiddish correspondence preserved by Liberman’s descendants.
- *La Gaceta del Foro*, November 1930: published judicial summary of the Zwi Migdal case.
- IWO/YIVO-related holdings in Buenos Aires: Ezras Noshim papers, anti-trafficking reports, and the 1934 Polish-visa correspondence.
- Contemporary press archives: *Crítica*, *La Prensa*, *La Razón*, *El Mundo*, and Jewish/Yiddish periodicals.
- Zwi Migdal institutional records seized at Córdoba 3280: minutes, membership books, financial records, applications, statutes, and telephone lists. Their complete present archival location and survival status require specialized archival inquiry.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Liberman was not continuously famous after 1935. Her public memory passed through several phases.

### Contemporary notoriety

In 1930 she was presented as a courageous prostitute confronting a powerful organization. Newspapers used both admiring and stigmatizing language, including “mujer de vida airada.”

### Partial disappearance

For decades, popular histories of Zwi Migdal repeated a compressed or fictionalized version while Liberman’s family history remained hidden. Her sons’ silence and the family’s inability or unwillingness to read the Yiddish letters contributed to the gap.

### Documentary rediscovery

The 1993 recognition by her granddaughter connected the public complainant to the Warsaw-Tapalqué family archive. This established:

- her genuine first marriage;
- her two sons;
- her true migration route;
- her literacy and correspondence;
- the deliberate family concealment in her testimony.

### Feminist and anti-trafficking commemoration

Buenos Aires created the **Premio Raquel Liberman** through Law No. 3460 in 2010 to recognize individuals and organizations protecting or promoting the rights of women who survived violence.

Her name became a reference point in Argentine anti-trafficking campaigns and comparisons with later cases such as that of Marita Verón.

### Callao–Raquel Liberman station

The Buenos Aires Legislature approved adding her name to the **Callao station on Subte Line D** in September 2023, associating the station with the nearby Callao 515 shop and the anti-trafficking message.

Mir Yarfitz and Marce Butierrez criticized the commemoration’s abolitionist framing. They argued that it risked reducing Liberman to a passive trafficking victim, ignoring her agency, her business activity, her voluntary movements into and out of sex work, and the politically mixed coalition that used her case.

### Competing modern meanings

Liberman has become simultaneously:

- a survivor of procurer violence;
- a whistleblower against organized sexual exploitation;
- an immigrant widow navigating constrained labor markets;
- a sex worker who accumulated capital and opened a business;
- a mother who concealed her children for their protection;
- a witness used by police, judicial, communal, and political campaigns;
- an emblem of abolitionism;
- a figure through whom sex-worker-rights historians challenge abolitionist simplification;
- a case study in how genuine criminal evidence can be absorbed into antisemitic propaganda.

Her historical importance does not require accepting every heroic legend. The documented core is substantial: she challenged Korn, supplied evidence against a protected association, persisted amid threats, and materially contributed to the loss of Zwi Migdal’s legal and public power.
