# Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

This dossier concerns the German philosopher Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel (1770–1831). It distinguishes:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary records, surviving correspondence, manuscripts, institutional records, or convergent historical scholarship.
- **Reported fact:** found in an early biography, memoir, or credible later reconstruction but not independently established here from a surviving primary document.
- **Disputed interpretation:** scholars agree on the relevant texts or events but disagree about their meaning.
- **Allegation or theory:** a substantive claim advanced by an identified critic but not established as fact.
- **Rumor or myth:** a circulating story unsupported or contradicted by the documentary record.

The most important editorial caution is that several works conventionally published under Hegel’s name—especially the lectures on history, art, religion, and the history of philosophy—were assembled after his death from Hegel’s manuscripts and students’ notes. They cannot always be treated as verbatim authorial texts. The modern historical-critical *Gesammelte Werke* therefore supersedes many nineteenth-century editorial constructions.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name:** Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel  
**Baptismal and standard published name:** Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel  
**Family name in private use:** “Wilhelm”  
**Common scholarly forms:** G. W. F. Hegel; Georg Friedrich Wilhelm Hegel occasionally appears through inversion or error  
**Titles and offices:** Magister of philosophy; Privatdozent at Jena; außerordentlicher Professor at Jena; editor of the *Bamberger Zeitung*; rector of the Egidiengymnasium at Nuremberg; professor at Heidelberg; royal Prussian professor at the University of Berlin; rector of the University of Berlin  
**Nationality in modern shorthand:** German  
**Political identity during his lifetime:** born a subject of the Duchy of Württemberg within the Holy Roman Empire; subsequently resident in territories repeatedly reorganized during the Napoleonic period; finally a Prussian state professor  
**Religion:** baptized and educated as a Württemberg Lutheran; trained for the Protestant ministry but never ordained or employed as a pastor  
**Profession:** philosopher, university lecturer, school administrator, teacher, newspaper editor  
**Born:** 27 August 1770, Stuttgart  
**Died:** 14 November 1831, Berlin  
**Officially recorded cause of death:** cholera  
**Modern qualification:** the diagnosis is disputed; the reported clinical course was atypical, and a chronic or acute gastric disorder has been proposed  
**Burial:** Dorotheenstädtischer Friedhof, Berlin, close to Johann Gottlieb Fichte’s grave  
**Known pseudonyms:** none securely documented  
**Major portraits:** the 1831 portrait by Jakob Schlesinger; contemporary sketches and a death mask also survive or are attributed to him.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal family

Hegel’s father, **Georg Ludwig Hegel** (1733–1799), was a Württemberg fiscal official. He served as *Rentkammersekretär* and ultimately *Rentenkammer-Expeditionsrat*. The family belonged to the educated Protestant administrative stratum rather than the aristocracy.

The *Neue Deutsche Biographie* traces the paternal line through:

- Hegel’s grandfather **Georg Ludwig Hegel** (1708–1761), an *Oberamtmann* at Altensteig;
- great-grandfather **Georg Ludwig Hegel**, a municipal official at Rosenfeld;
- several generations of Württemberg pastors, administrators, and court officials.

A family coat of arms was reportedly granted in 1643. It did not make Hegel an aristocrat.

### Maternal family

His mother was **Maria Magdalena Louisa Hegel, née Fromm** (1741–1783), daughter of Stuttgart lawyer Ludwig Albrecht Fromm and Regine Dorothea Schnepff. She came from a prosperous family connected with Württemberg clergy, lawyers, and officials. She was unusually well educated and taught Hegel elementary Latin.

### Parents’ marriage and children

Georg Ludwig Hegel and Maria Magdalena Fromm married on 29 September 1769. Their surviving children were:

1. **Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel** (1770–1831).
2. **Christiane Luise Hegel** (1773–1832). She worked for a period as a governess, experienced serious psychological illness, was confined in an institution in 1820–1821, and died by suicide through drowning in 1832.
3. **Georg Ludwig Hegel** (1776–1812), an army officer who died during Napoleon’s Russian campaign.

Four additional children reportedly died in infancy.

### Partner and extramarital child

While living in Jena, Hegel had a relationship with his landlady and housekeeper **Christiane Charlotte Johanna Burkhardt, née Fischer**, a married or separated woman whose domestic and legal situation is described inconsistently in later biographies.

She gave birth on 5 February 1807 to Hegel’s son:

- **Georg Ludwig Friedrich Fischer**, usually called **Ludwig Fischer** (1807–1831).

**Documented fact:** Hegel acknowledged Ludwig, paid toward his support, and brought him into his household in Heidelberg in 1817.

**Reported and partly documented family conflict:** Hegel’s treatment of Ludwig was materially responsible but emotionally strained. Ludwig did not enjoy the same position as the two sons born within Hegel’s marriage. Relations deteriorated; he entered Dutch military service and traveled to the East Indies. He died of fever at Batavia/Jakarta on 28 August 1831. News apparently did not reach Hegel before Hegel’s own death.

Some accounts say Hegel had contemplated marriage to Burkhardt and withdrew or abandoned a promise when he left Jena. The precise legal and documentary status of this alleged promise remains uncertain.

### Marriage

Hegel married **Marie Helena Susanna von Tucher** (1791–1855) at Nuremberg on 15 or 16 September 1811; sources differ by one day. She belonged to the old Nuremberg patrician Tucher family. Her father was **Jobst Wilhelm Karl von Tucher** (1762–1813), a senator and municipal official.

Their children were:

- **Susanna Hegel**, born 27 June 1812 and died 8 August 1812.
- **Karl Friedrich Wilhelm Hegel** (1813–1901), later a prominent historian and professor, particularly known for research on medieval and municipal history.
- **Immanuel Thomas Christian Hegel** (1814–1891), later a Prussian civil servant and senior administrator in the Protestant Church.

Marie survived Hegel by nearly twenty-four years. Karl and Immanuel preserved manuscripts, correspondence, and family traditions, while also exercising influence over posthumous representations of their father.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1770–1788

Hegel was born in the house now identified as Eberhardstraße 53 in Stuttgart. The family lived there for roughly his first six or seven years, later moving—after an incompletely documented intermediate residence—to what is now Lange Straße 7.

Stuttgart was the capital of the Lutheran Duchy of Württemberg. Its political culture combined ducal authority, a strong territorial church, established representative estates, and an educated civil-service class. Hegel grew up within that administrative Protestant milieu.

He attended the German school, Latin school, lower gymnasium, and Stuttgart’s *Gymnasium illustre*. His surviving school notebooks and excerpts demonstrate extensive reading rather than childhood philosophical originality. He copied passages on history, literature, religion, mathematics, psychology, and the classical world.

Important school influences included:

- **Friedrich Wilhelm Löffler**, a teacher who reportedly gave eight-year-old Hegel an eighteen-volume German Shakespeare edition;
- Greek and Roman writers, especially Sophocles, Euripides, Thucydides, Livy, Cicero, and Epictetus;
- Enlightenment histories and popular philosophy;
- Shakespeare, Goethe, Schiller, Lessing, Rousseau, and contemporary religious controversy.

In September 1783 his mother died from an illness described as *Gallenfieber*, often translated as bilious fever. Hegel became seriously ill in the same outbreak but survived.

His early compositions included school exercises, translations, diary material, excerpts, and the 1788 graduation address concerning the deficient state of arts and sciences among the Turks. These texts should not be retroactively treated as a finished philosophical system.

## Education and Formation at Tübingen, 1788–1793

### Tübinger Stift

Hegel entered the **Tübinger Stift**, the Württemberg Protestant seminary attached to the University of Tübingen, in October 1788. The institution educated scholarship students for the Lutheran ministry under strict discipline.

He studied philosophy for two years, receiving the degree of **Magister philosophiae** in 1790, and then theology from 1790 to 1793. His formal teachers included representatives of the Tübingen theological establishment, notably **Gottlob Christian Storr**, whose supernaturalist biblical theology Hegel and his friends resisted.

His official academic record was respectable but not brilliant. A frequently repeated report characterizes him as competent in theology and philology but deficient in philosophy. This judgment came from institutional assessment and has acquired ironic force in later retellings; it does not mean contemporaries unanimously regarded him as unintelligent.

### Hölderlin and Schelling

Hegel formed decisive friendships with:

- **Friedrich Hölderlin**, poet and fellow seminarian;
- **Friedrich Wilhelm Joseph Schelling**, five years younger but intellectually precocious.

They read Greek literature, Kant, Rousseau, Jacobi, Spinoza, and writings arising from the French Revolution. Later tradition calls them the “Tübingen Three.”

The story that Hegel, Hölderlin, and Schelling planted a “tree of liberty” and danced around it in celebration of the French Revolution is famous but poorly documented. It appears in later recollection rather than a secure contemporary record. Their sympathy for the Revolution’s early ideals, however, is documented.

### “Oldest System-Programme”

The short manuscript known as **“Das älteste Systemprogramm des deutschen Idealismus”** was written in Hegel’s handwriting, probably in 1796 or 1797. Its authorship remains disputed among Hegel, Schelling, Hölderlin, or some collaborative intellectual context. Handwriting proves that Hegel copied the surviving text, not that he composed it.

### Religious and political formation

The young Hegel opposed authoritarian theological orthodoxy and inherited religion based only on command. His developing ideal was a communal or “folk” religion joining reason, imagination, freedom, and ethical life. He admired ancient Greek civic unity while recognizing that it could not simply be restored.

He passed the theological consistory examination in 1793 but did not enter the ministry.

## Early Career: Bern and Frankfurt, 1793–1800

### Bern, 1793–1796

Hegel became a private tutor in the household of **Karl Friedrich von Steiger** near Bern. The position provided income and access to an extensive library but left him socially isolated.

His Bern manuscripts addressed:

- the life and teaching of Jesus;
- “positive religion,” meaning religion imposed through external authority;
- Christianity’s transformation into an institutional system;
- Württemberg politics and constitutional reform;
- Kantian morality and the relationship between reason and faith.

Important manuscripts later given editorial titles include *The Life of Jesus* and *The Positivity of the Christian Religion*. These titles and textual boundaries were imposed or stabilized by later editors.

Hegel traveled in Switzerland, including to the Bernese Oberland. A diary of the journey survives. His response to Alpine scenery was more observational than conventionally sublime.

### Frankfurt, 1797–1800

Through Hölderlin’s assistance, Hegel obtained another tutoring position in Frankfurt, in the household of the merchant **Johann Noë Gogel**. He was reunited with Hölderlin and encountered the intellectual world around the Homburg circle.

Frankfurt marked an important transition:

- from an essentially Kantian critique of positive religion;
- toward themes of love, life, reconciliation, division, and social totality;
- toward political economy and the structure of modern civil society;
- toward a philosophy no longer reducible to theology.

Manuscripts from this period include material later titled *The Spirit of Christianity and Its Fate*, constitutional writings, fragments on religion and love, and the unfinished essay on the constitution of Germany.

### Father’s death and inheritance

Georg Ludwig Hegel died in 1799. Hegel inherited a modest sum, often estimated at approximately 3,000 gulden. It did not make him wealthy, but it gave him enough independence to attempt an academic career at Jena.

## Jena, 1801–1807

### Entry into university life

Hegel moved to Jena in January 1801. Jena was then a center of post-Kantian philosophy and Romanticism. Schelling was already celebrated there.

Hegel’s first printed book was:

- *Differenz des Fichte’schen und Schelling’schen Systems der Philosophie* (1801).

It defended Schelling’s position against interpretations that reduced it to Fichtean subjective idealism, while beginning to articulate Hegel’s own conception of philosophical system.

He submitted the Latin habilitation dissertation:

- *Dissertatio philosophica de orbitis planetarum* (1801).

The dissertation has often been mocked for discussing planetary distances shortly before the asteroid Ceres became widely known. The common claim that Hegel “proved there could be no planet” between Mars and Jupiter is an exaggeration: his speculative treatment was unsuccessful and poorly timed, but the legend simplifies its actual argument.

Hegel became a **Privatdozent**, living primarily from student fees. His first announced course reportedly attracted a very small audience.

### Collaboration with Schelling

In 1802–1803 Hegel and Schelling jointly edited the *Kritisches Journal der Philosophie*. Hegel contributed essays on:

- philosophical criticism;
- skepticism;
- natural law;
- faith and knowledge;
- relations among contemporary systems.

Schelling left Jena for Würzburg in 1803. Their intellectual paths increasingly diverged.

### Academic and financial difficulties

Hegel’s Jena income was insecure. In 1805 he obtained appointment as an unpaid extraordinary professor. He complained when **Jakob Friedrich Fries** obtained preferential advancement and sought support from **Johann Wolfgang von Goethe**, an influential figure in the university’s administration.

Hegel’s relationship with Goethe was respectful and institutionally important, but they were not intimate philosophical collaborators. Goethe helped his career; Hegel later lectured on and defended aspects of Goethe’s theory of color.

### Phenomenology and Napoleon

During 1805–1806 Hegel completed *Phänomenologie des Geistes*. The work developed forms of consciousness through perception, understanding, self-consciousness, recognition, reason, ethical life, religion, and absolute knowing.

On 13 October 1806, immediately before the Battle of Jena–Auerstedt, Hegel saw Napoleon in Jena and wrote to Friedrich Immanuel Niethammer that he had seen the emperor—“this world-soul”—riding through the city. The authentic letter refers to Napoleon as a world-soul concentrated at a point. The popular phrase “the World Spirit on horseback” is a later paraphrase.

French occupation disrupted the university and Hegel’s household. He worked to send the final manuscript pages to his publisher and temporarily sought safety elsewhere.

### Christiane Burkhardt and Ludwig

Hegel’s relationship with Christiane Burkhardt occurred during the Jena years. Their son Ludwig was born on 5 February 1807, when Hegel was moving toward employment in Bamberg. The episode was concealed or minimized in older respectable biographies but is securely documented.

---

## Bamberg, 1807–1808

Hegel’s *Phänomenologie des Geistes* appeared in 1807, with the original title identifying it as the first part of a “system of science.” Its hostile or dismissive reference to an Absolute in which “all cows are black” was understood by Schelling as directed against his philosophy. Their close friendship did not recover.

Through **Friedrich Immanuel Niethammer**, Hegel became editor of the **Bamberger Zeitung**. He handled political and military news under Napoleonic censorship. He was reportedly an efficient editor.

A censorship dispute arose after the newspaper published sensitive military information. Hegel was questioned, but there was no criminal conviction, imprisonment, or established espionage. He disliked the insecurity of journalism and left when Niethammer arranged a school position.

---

## Nuremberg, 1808–1816

### Rector of the Egidiengymnasium

In November 1808 Hegel became rector of the Nuremberg **Egidiengymnasium**. He taught philosophy, religion, German, Greek, and mathematics and administered curriculum, examinations, discipline, staffing, and ceremonies.

His surviving addresses show a demanding but institutionally serious educator. He argued that education required disciplined mediation rather than spontaneous self-expression. Materials later published as the *Philosophische Propädeutik* derive from manuscripts and outlines used in this work; the book is a posthumous editorial compilation, not a volume published by Hegel.

In 1813 Hegel also became local school councillor. He helped establish or reorganize teacher training.

### Marriage and household

Hegel married Marie von Tucher in 1811. The marriage brought him into Nuremberg’s patrician social world. Their daughter Susanna died in infancy; Karl and Immanuel survived.

Ludwig Fischer remained elsewhere during most of these years. After his mother’s death, he was cared for through arrangements involving associates of publisher Karl Friedrich Frommann. He entered Hegel’s household only in 1817.

### *Science of Logic*

Hegel completed his major logical work in Nuremberg:

- *Wissenschaft der Logik*, Book I, *Die objektive Logik*, Volume I: Doctrine of Being (1812);
- second part of the Objective Logic: Doctrine of Essence (1813);
- Book II: *Die subjektive Logik oder die Lehre vom Begriff* (1816).

The work examines categories such as being, nothing, becoming, quality, quantity, essence, appearance, actuality, concept, judgment, syllogism, objectivity, life, cognition, and the absolute idea. Hegel began revising it near the end of his life; only the revised Doctrine of Being was completed for posthumous publication.

---

## Heidelberg, 1816–1818

Hegel received possible or actual offers from Erlangen, Berlin, and Heidelberg. He accepted Heidelberg’s chair of philosophy in 1816, restoring him to university life.

His Heidelberg lectures covered logic, metaphysics, philosophy of nature, philosophy of spirit, natural law, political philosophy, history of philosophy, anthropology, psychology, and aesthetics.

In 1817 he published:

- *Enzyklopädie der philosophischen Wissenschaften im Grundrisse*.

This textbook presented his system in three parts:

1. Logic;
2. Philosophy of Nature;
3. Philosophy of Spirit.

It was designed as a lecture outline, not a self-sufficient exposition. Hegel extensively enlarged it in 1827 and 1830.

### Heidelberg controversy and politics

Hegel published an essay on the Württemberg Estates proceedings, advocating constitutional reform while criticizing entrenched corporate privilege. He favored representation, codified institutions, professional administration, and a constitutional monarchy, not democratic republicanism or restorationist absolutism.

In 1817 Ludwig Fischer, aged ten, entered the Hegel household. Evidence suggests persistent friction involving status, discipline, and inheritance expectations.

Previously unknown transcripts of Hegel’s Heidelberg lectures were identified in the Munich–Freising diocesan archive in 2022 by Klaus Vieweg, demonstrating that the lecture corpus remains incomplete and capable of significant revision.

---

## Berlin, 1818–1831

### Appointment and residences

Hegel arrived in Berlin on 29 September 1818 to occupy the leading philosophy chair at the University of Berlin, vacant since Fichte’s death. He was recruited under the reform-minded Prussian minister **Karl vom Stein zum Altenstein**.

He first lived near Leipziger Straße and Friedrichstraße. From 1820 he lived at **Am Kupfergraben 4a**.

His inaugural lecture represented philosophy as understanding its own age in thought. He soon became a major academic attraction, although the image of instant, uncontested dominance exaggerates a gradual rise.

### Teaching

Hegel repeatedly lectured on:

- logic and metaphysics;
- philosophy of nature;
- subjective spirit;
- philosophy of right;
- philosophy of history or world history;
- aesthetics or philosophy of art;
- philosophy of religion;
- history of philosophy;
- proofs of the existence of God.

Students and auditors included Eduard Gans, Heinrich Gustav Hotho, Karl Ludwig Michelet, Hermann Hinrichs, Leopold von Henning, Ludwig Feuerbach, David Friedrich Strauss, Bruno Bauer, and, reportedly, Felix Mendelssohn. Heinrich Heine encountered the Hegelian milieu and later supplied influential recollections, some literary rather than stenographically reliable.

### *Philosophy of Right*

Hegel published *Grundlinien der Philosophie des Rechts* in 1820, bearing the publication year 1821. It treated:

- abstract right and property;
- contract and wrong;
- morality;
- family;
- civil society;
- poverty and the “rabble” problem;
- corporations;
- police and public administration;
- constitutional monarchy;
- international law;
- world history.

The preface’s formula “What is rational is actual; and what is actual is rational” became the foundation of the myth that Hegel sanctified everything that existed. In Hegel’s technical vocabulary, *wirklich* did not mean every existing fact; it referred to actuality as the realization of a rational structure. Nevertheless, the formulation was politically ambiguous and readily usable as an apology for established power.

### Carlsbad reaction and surveillance

The assassination of August von Kotzebue in 1819 led to the Carlsbad Decrees, censorship, surveillance of universities, and persecution of nationalist student organizations.

Hegel was not imprisoned, dismissed, or formally charged. His situation was nonetheless politically delicate:

- students and associates came under suspicion;
- his student Karl Ludwig Carové was denied an academic future;
- Hegel assisted or intervened for some people investigated as “demagogues”;
- he revised the *Philosophy of Right* under censorship;
- his polemics against Fries and subjective political enthusiasm aligned in part with governmental attacks on radical nationalism.

**Disputed interpretation:** Critics present him as accommodating Prussian reaction. Revisionist scholarship notes that his constitutional model resembled the unfulfilled Prussian reform program more than the Restoration state that actually existed. Both accommodation and guarded reformism are visible in the record.

### Institutional power and networks

Hegel acquired influence through:

- Altenstein and the Prussian education ministry;
- ministry official Johannes Schulze;
- university appointments and examining bodies;
- pupils who reviewed books, taught his philosophy, and edited journals;
- the *Jahrbücher für wissenschaftliche Kritik*, founded in 1827 by a circle associated with Hegel.

This network has sometimes been characterized as an academic “school” that distributed patronage and attacked rivals. It did exercise institutional influence, but no evidence establishes a covert organization or unlawful conspiracy.

### Travel

Hegel’s major later journeys included:

- **Dresden, 1820:** viewing art collections;
- **the Netherlands, 1822:** travel through Dutch cities and contact with modern commercial society;
- **Vienna, 1824:** opera, theater, art, and society;
- **Paris, 1827:** accompanied or hosted by Victor Cousin; attended theaters and visited collections;
- **Prague, 1824**, in connection with the Vienna journey;
- repeated visits to spas, including Karlsbad.

His travel letters reveal enthusiasm for painting, opera, city life, wine, cards, and sociability, complicating the image of an exclusively abstract recluse.

### Rectorate

Hegel served as rector of the University of Berlin in 1829–1830. His administrative role included formal speeches and university governance during a period of political anxiety.

### July Revolution and final writings

The French Revolution of July 1830 interested and unsettled him. He neither reverted to simple counterrevolution nor welcomed uncontrolled popular sovereignty. His final political essay, **“Über die englische Reformbill”** (1831), criticized the unreformed British electoral system and aristocratic privilege but warned that the proposed reform might empower atomized interests without adequate institutions.

The essay appeared in installments in the *Allgemeine Preußische Staats-Zeitung*. Publication of the final part was stopped following royal or official intervention. Thus the claim that Hegel was an unrestricted mouthpiece of the Prussian state is inconsistent with his having been censored by it.

## Finances, Property, and Material Position

Hegel was born into a secure but not wealthy civil-service family.

His principal financial phases were:

- scholarship-supported seminary education;
- salaried household tutoring in Bern and Frankfurt;
- modest paternal inheritance after 1799;
- precarious fee income at Jena;
- borrowed assistance from friends, especially Niethammer;
- a regular editorial salary at Bamberg;
- a stable salary, housing, and administrative standing in Nuremberg;
- professorial income at Heidelberg;
- substantially improved salary and honoraria in Berlin.

Reported figures place his Nuremberg compensation around 1,500 gulden with housing, Heidelberg income at a broadly similar level, and Berlin income near 3,000 thalers, though comparisons are complicated by currencies, allowances, and changing purchasing power.

He was not a business owner, major investor, inventor, or patent holder. No significant corporate interests, secret financial arrangements, fraud, money laundering, or corrupt enrichment are documented. His important “projects” were intellectual and institutional: journals, curricula, lecture programs, philosophical publications, and the formation of an academic school.

## Philosophical, Religious, and Political Development

### From Enlightenment religion to speculative philosophy

Hegel’s intellectual development can be schematized without treating it as mechanically predetermined:

1. **Stuttgart:** classical education, Enlightenment excerpting, Protestant upbringing.
2. **Tübingen:** Kant, Rousseau, Greek civic religion, the French Revolution, resistance to theological orthodoxy.
3. **Bern:** critique of positive Christianity and externally imposed religion.
4. **Frankfurt:** love, life, reconciliation, division, and historical community.
5. **Jena:** system, recognition, ethical life, logic, nature, spirit, and the phenomenological “education” of consciousness.
6. **Nuremberg:** mature logic.
7. **Heidelberg and Berlin:** encyclopedic system and specialized philosophies of law, art, religion, history, and philosophy.

### Dialectic

The popular formula “thesis–antithesis–synthesis” is not Hegel’s standard method and rarely appears in his texts. It derives more directly from later pedagogical simplifications of Fichtean and Hegelian development.

Hegel’s actual procedures include determinate negation, immanent contradiction, mediation, *Aufhebung*—simultaneously cancellation, preservation, and elevation—and movement from abstract categories toward more concrete conceptual structures.

### Freedom

A central thread is that freedom is not mere arbitrary choice. It requires social recognition and institutions in which persons can understand laws and practices as rationally their own. This underlies his accounts of property, family, civil society, law, constitutional government, history, art, religion, and philosophy.

### Religion

Hegel did not simply abandon Christianity. He treated Christianity, especially the doctrines of incarnation and Trinity, as representational expressions of speculative truth. Philosophy translated religious representation into conceptual form.

This produced incompatible receptions:

- orthodox followers portrayed him as a Christian philosopher;
- critics accused him of pantheism;
- Left Hegelians extracted atheistic and humanistic conclusions;
- Schelling was later invited to Berlin partly to counter the perceived religious and political danger of Hegelianism.

### Politics

Hegel supported:

- constitutional government;
- the rule of law;
- legal personality and property;
- an organized civil service;
- representation through institutions;
- reform of obsolete privileges;
- religious inclusion in civil status.

He rejected:

- atomistic contract theories of the state;
- direct democracy as a universal model;
- romantic nationalism based on feeling or ethnicity;
- restoration of feudal privileges;
- unmediated revolutionary voluntarism.

He accepted or defended:

- hereditary constitutional monarchy;
- social hierarchy and estates;
- war as a persistent feature of interstate life;
- restrictions incompatible with modern egalitarian democracy.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Friedrich Hölderlin

Close friend from Tübingen; helped secure Hegel’s Frankfurt position. Hölderlin’s ideas about unity, separation, beauty, and Greek modernity influenced Hegel’s development. Their later contact diminished as Hölderlin’s mental health deteriorated.

### Friedrich Wilhelm Joseph Schelling

Friend, roommate, intellectual collaborator, and later rival. Hegel initially appeared as Schelling’s ally. The *Phenomenology* publicly marked their divergence. Their friendship never fully recovered, although the picture of lifelong personal hatred is exaggerated.

### Friedrich Immanuel Niethammer

One of Hegel’s most dependable friends and patrons. Niethammer found or facilitated positions in Bamberg and Nuremberg, lent money, corresponded extensively, and became godfather to Immanuel Hegel.

### Johann Wolfgang von Goethe

Institutional patron at Jena and respected literary figure. Goethe supported Hegel’s advancement. Hegel admired Goethe and defended elements of his scientific work.

### Friedrich Schiller

A formative literary and philosophical influence, especially through writings on aesthetic education, freedom, and modern fragmentation. Schiller died before Hegel’s mature Berlin period; the relationship was primarily intellectual rather than a close personal friendship.

### Johann Gottlieb Fichte

A major philosophical predecessor whose idealism Hegel criticized and incorporated. Hegel later occupied the Berlin chair left vacant after Fichte’s death. They were not close associates.

### Jakob Friedrich Fries

Academic and philosophical rival. Hegel opposed Fries’s reliance on immediate conviction and attacked the political culture associated with Friesian nationalism. Hegel’s language concerning Fries in the *Philosophy of Right* preface helped associate him with governmental reaction after 1819.

### Friedrich Schleiermacher

Berlin colleague and rival in theology, philosophy, university politics, and academy elections. Hegel mocked Schleiermacher’s emphasis on religious feeling. Their antagonism was intellectual and institutional, not a criminal or violent feud.

### Eduard Gans

Student, jurist, and important interpreter. Gans’s lectures and editorial work helped push Hegelian legal philosophy in more liberal directions. His Jewish background is relevant to Hegel’s support for Jewish civil rights.

### Victor Cousin

French philosopher, acquaintance, and host during Hegel’s Paris visit. Hegel intervened after Cousin’s arrest in Germany on political suspicion. Cousin helped disseminate German philosophy in France.

### Family network

Marie von Tucher provided household stability and access to Nuremberg patrician society. Relations involving Christiane Hegel and Ludwig Fischer were sources of domestic strain. Karl and Immanuel later helped control manuscripts and family memory.

## Controversies, Accusations, and Contested Material

## The illegitimate son and family treatment

**Documented fact:** Hegel fathered Ludwig Fischer outside marriage, acknowledged him, financed his care, and received him into the marital home.

**Contested moral and biographical issue:** Ludwig appears to have experienced inferior status and conflict in the household. Later letters indicate resentment and emotional estrangement. Hegel eventually consented to or encouraged his departure into Dutch service. The evidence supports a troubled relationship but not claims that Hegel wholly abandoned or denied his son.

No lawsuit, paternity prosecution, or criminal proceeding is documented.

## Race, Africa, and colonialism

This is the most substantial modern controversy.

In surviving lecture materials, Hegel advanced hierarchical claims about human “races,” attributed fixed physical and spiritual characteristics to populations, and described sub-Saharan Africa as outside “world history” in the relevant sense. Some lecture versions present slavery as a disciplinary transition toward freedom and recommend gradual rather than immediate abolition. His discussion drew on travel literature, anthropology, and racial classifications available in his period, sometimes selectively and inaccurately.

**Documented textual fact:** Hegel expressed propositions that current scholarship characterizes as racial essentialism and hierarchy.

**Disputed interpretation:**

- Robert Bernasconi, Emmanuel Eze, Alison Stone, Nathaniel Coleman, and other critics argue that racial hierarchy is structurally connected to Hegel’s philosophy of spirit and history.
- Other scholars contend that these claims are inconsistent with Hegel’s more universal account of rational freedom and are corrigible rather than system-defining.
- Recent historical-critical work stresses differences between lecture years and warns against relying solely on composite posthumous editions.
- Scholarship on Egypt shows that Hegel did not exclude every African element from historical development; this qualification does not erase his derogatory statements about sub-Saharan Africans.
- Susan Buck-Morss proposed that the Haitian Revolution and contemporary reports of slavery may have informed the master–slave dialectic. The chronological possibility is significant, but no surviving Hegel text explicitly identifies Haiti as its source. The claim remains an influential historical theory, not a proven genealogy.

Hegel neither owned plantations nor participated directly in colonial administration or slave trading.

## Jews and Judaism

Hegel’s early theological manuscripts describe ancient Judaism in severe terms, emphasizing separation, domination, and alienation. Some passages reproduce longstanding Christian anti-Jewish constructions.

In mature political philosophy, however, he opposed excluding Jews from civil rights. In *Philosophy of Right* §270 and associated materials, he argued that Jews are human beings and citizens whose civil recognition assists social integration.

**Documented tension:** negative theological representation of Judaism coexists with support for Jewish civic emancipation.

Whether to classify Hegel personally as “antisemitic” depends on whether the term is used for theological anti-Judaism, racial-political antisemitism, or opposition to Jewish civil equality. The third is contradicted by his published position; the first is supported by early texts.

## Women, marriage, and gender

Hegel assigned men and women differentiated social roles. He associated women primarily with family life and men with civil society, scholarship, and the state. A notorious passage compares the difference between men and women to that between animals and plants and suggests women are not suited to the highest forms of science or government.

**Documented textual fact:** his mature philosophy contains hierarchical and exclusionary gender claims.

Interpretations that derive an egalitarian theory of recognition from Hegel must therefore distinguish the broader logical resources of his philosophy from his stated social judgments.

## War and international relations

Hegel held that sovereign states exist without a final world authority and that conflict cannot be eliminated by moral exhortation alone. He also wrote that war can prevent peoples from becoming rigidly isolated in private life.

**Documented fact:** Hegel did not advocate permanent peace as Kant did and attributed an ethical-historical role to war.

**Disputed interpretation:** Critics treat this as glorification of violence; defenders emphasize that Hegel regarded war as a tragic structural possibility and supported international law, not perpetual aggression.

## Prussian apologetics and authoritarianism

Rudolf Haym, Bertrand Russell, Karl Popper, and others portrayed Hegel as a court philosopher who rationalized Prussian monarchy.

Evidence supporting the accusation includes:

- his state appointment and ministerial connections;
- support for monarchy and bureaucracy;
- attacks on Friesian political subjectivism;
- cautious behavior under censorship;
- language treating the state as the actuality of ethical life.

Evidence against the strongest version includes:

- his defense of constitutional and legal reforms not realized in Restoration Prussia;
- advocacy of Jewish civil rights;
- assistance to politically suspect associates;
- criticism of aristocratic privilege;
- censorship of his 1831 Reform Bill essay;
- the later Prussian appointment of Schelling to combat radical Hegelian consequences.

**Assessment:** “Conservative,” “liberal reformer,” and “Prussian apologist” each capture parts of the record but become misleading when treated as complete descriptions. The claim that Hegel’s system directly caused Nazism is a twentieth-century polemical genealogy, not a documented chain of influence.

## Totalitarianism and fascism

Popper’s *The Open Society and Its Enemies* accused Hegel of obscurantism, intellectual dishonesty, nationalism, historicism, and proto-totalitarian state worship. Russell made related charges.

Walter Kaufmann, Herbert Marcuse, and subsequent historians challenged Popper’s quotation practices, anachronism, and reliance on guilt by association. Modern political scholarship generally rejects a direct line from Hegel to National Socialism, while continuing to debate whether his institutionalism leaves insufficient safeguards for individual dissent.

**Evidentiary label:** influential allegation and disputed interpretation; no evidence shows that Hegel advocated twentieth-century one-party dictatorship, racial state policy, concentration camps, or total administrative control.

## Obscurantism, jargon, and intellectual fraud

Schopenhauer, Haym, Russell, and Popper alleged that Hegel’s difficulty concealed vacuity or deliberate deception.

**Documented fact:** Hegel’s prose is frequently compressed, terminologically specialized, and difficult; his lecture delivery was halting.

**Allegation:** that he consciously fabricated nonsense to obtain power or money. No documentary evidence of fraud supports that psychological accusation.

## Natural science

Hegel’s philosophy of nature contains obsolete or incorrect scientific judgments, including speculative treatments of planetary organization, chemistry, magnetism, and organism. He criticized Newtonian presentations and supported Goethe’s color theory.

These errors are documented intellectual failures, not scientific fraud. Claims that Hegel denied all empirical research are contradicted by his extensive engagement with contemporary scientific literature, although he sometimes subordinated evidence to speculative classification.

## Criminality, corruption, and intelligence connections

No credible evidence was found of:

- criminal conviction;
- imprisonment;
- fraud or financial corruption;
- espionage or intelligence service;
- money laundering;
- political violence;
- patent theft;
- plagiarism adjudicated through legal proceedings;
- membership in a violent underground organization.

The Bamberg censorship inquiry and Berlin political surveillance environment were administrative-political matters, not convictions.

## Illness, Psychological History, and Death

Hegel suffered the serious childhood fever that killed his mother. His correspondence mentions recurrent gastric complaints and ordinary periods of illness, but no documented addiction or sustained psychiatric disorder.

His sister Christiane’s illness and suicide belong to the family history; they should not be projected onto Hegel without evidence.

During the Berlin cholera epidemic, Hegel became acutely ill on 13 November 1831 and died on 14 November after approximately a day and a half.

### Competing accounts

**Official finding:** physicians recorded “cholera in its most concentrated form.” His body was reported to the epidemic authorities, and his apartment was disinfected.

**Family and contemporary description:** Marie Hegel described a gentle, relatively painless death, without some conspicuous external symptoms associated with severe cholera.

**Modern reconstruction:** Klaus Vieweg and others have suggested that Hegel probably died from an acute exacerbation of longstanding gastric disease rather than cholera.

No modern autopsy evidence can resolve the question. The accurate formulation is: **officially cholera; medically disputed on retrospective symptom analysis**.

Friends secured permission to modify rules governing the rapid and isolated burial of cholera victims. Hegel was buried on 16 November 1831 in the Dorotheenstadt cemetery near Fichte, reportedly in accordance with his wishes.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Propaganda

### “The World Spirit on horseback”

**Story:** Hegel saw Napoleon and called him “the World Spirit on horseback.”

**Evidence:** an authentic letter of 13 October 1806 calls Napoleon “this world-soul” and describes him riding through Jena. The familiar formulation is a compressed paraphrase.

**Reputational effect:** it supports images of Hegel as either a perceptive theorist of world-historical agency or an admirer of conquest.

### The liberty tree

**Story:** Hegel, Hölderlin, and Schelling planted a liberty tree at Tübingen and danced around it.

**Evidence:** later testimony and tradition; no secure contemporary record establishes the event. Their revolutionary sympathies are independently documented.

**Effect:** central to the romantic image of the three seminarians as secret Jacobins.

### “Thesis–antithesis–synthesis”

**Story:** Hegel’s universal dialectical formula was thesis, antithesis, synthesis.

**Evidence against:** the triad is not his standard terminology and does not accurately describe the varied transitions in his major works.

**Effect:** probably the most widespread pedagogical simplification of Hegel.

### The “end of history”

**Story:** Hegel declared that history ended in his own philosophy or in the Prussian state.

**Evidence against:** he described historical development as open to new conflicts and forms and never identified the actual Prussia of 1820 as a perfect terminus. His system does claim retrospective conceptual completion in a stronger philosophical sense, which helped generate the legend.

**Effect:** revived through Marxist debates and Francis Fukuyama’s differently constructed “end of history” thesis.

### The perfect Prussian state

**Story:** Hegel believed the existing Prussian monarchy was the final and wholly rational state.

**Evidence against:** *Philosophy of Right* does not name Prussia as such a culmination; several institutions it prescribes did not exist there. Evidence of accommodation and monarchism remains.

### The missing planet

**Story:** Hegel deductively proved no planet could exist between Mars and Jupiter just as Ceres was discovered.

**Evidence:** his 1801 dissertation speculated unsuccessfully about planetary organization and used a sequence that omitted a planet in that interval. The stronger claim that he definitively disproved Ceres is caricature.

### Freemason or Illuminatus

**Story:** Hegel belonged to the Freemasons, Bavarian Illuminati, or a secret occult conspiracy that devised dialectic as a program of world control.

**Evidence:** some acquaintances had belonged to Masonic or Illuminist circles, and Hegel studied mysticism. No reliable membership record establishes that he joined either organization. The historical Bavarian Illuminati had largely ceased organized activity before Hegel’s mature career.

**Label:** rumor and modern conspiracy mythology.

### Hermetic magician

Glenn Alexander Magee and other interpreters have emphasized parallels between Hegel and Hermetic, theosophical, mystical, Boehmean, and Neoplatonic traditions.

**Documented foundation:** Hegel knew Jacob Boehme, Meister Eckhart, mysticism, and speculative theology.

**Disputed interpretation:** whether “Hermeticism” identifies a formative structural source of his system or retrospectively overstates affinities. Claims that Hegel practiced ritual magic are unsupported.

### Direct father of Marxism, fascism, or both

Hegel’s concepts profoundly influenced Marxism, while nationalist and conservative thinkers also appropriated selected ideas. The claim that one philosophical text secretly generated both communist and fascist regimes is a reception myth that substitutes genealogy for demonstrated institutional causation.

## Authoritative modern edition

The historical-critical ** *G. W. F. Hegel: Gesammelte Werke* ** has been published by Felix Meiner in cooperation with the Hegel-Archiv and scholarly institutions since 1968. It separates:

- writings and drafts;
- lecture manuscripts;
- independently transmitted student transcripts;
- editorial variants and textual apparatus.

It is the preferred basis for questions of wording, chronology, and authorship.

## Death, Burial, and Immediate Aftermath

Hegel died at his apartment at Am Kupfergraben 4a on 14 November 1831. Johannes Schulze and other associates handled official and funeral arrangements. He was buried near Fichte in the Dorotheenstadt cemetery.

His widow, sons, students, and colleagues rapidly formed a **“Verein von Freunden des Verewigten”** to publish his works. Between 1832 and 1845 they issued the first collected edition. Their work preserved a vast body of material but also created synthetic texts whose seams were frequently hidden.

Christiane Hegel died by suicide in 1832. Ludwig Fischer had already died in Batavia in August 1831. Marie Hegel died in 1855.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Division of the school

After Hegel’s death, his followers divided—imprecisely—into:

- **Right or Old Hegelians**, emphasizing compatibility with Christianity and the Prussian order;
- a **Hegelian center**;
- **Young or Left Hegelians**, using criticism and dialectic against religion and the state.

David Friedrich Strauss’s *Life of Jesus* accelerated the religious division. Feuerbach transformed theology into anthropology. Bruno Bauer radicalized criticism. Marx absorbed and attacked Hegelian philosophy, claiming to turn its dialectic “right side up.”

### International reception

Hegelianism spread through:

- British Idealism: T. H. Green, F. H. Bradley, Bernard Bosanquet;
- Italian Idealism: Benedetto Croce and Giovanni Gentile;
- Russian philosophy and revolutionary theory;
- French twentieth-century interpretation through Kojève, Hyppolite, Sartre, Lacan, and later Derrida;
- American St. Louis and educational Hegelianism;
- theology, Marxism, existentialism, phenomenology, critical theory, pragmatism, political theory, and contemporary analytic philosophy.

### Periods of rejection

Schopenhauer made Hegel a symbol of professorial charlatanism. Early analytic philosophy defined itself partly against British Idealism. Russell and Popper treated Hegel as an enemy of liberal clarity and the open society.

The association with Nazism became especially influential during and after the Second World War. Later archival and contextual scholarship substantially weakened the simple “Hegel-to-Hitler” genealogy without eliminating questions about hierarchy, nationalism, Eurocentrism, and state authority.

### Later revivals

Major twentieth- and twenty-first-century revivals include:

- Marxist and Western Marxist reinterpretation;
- French master–slave and recognition readings;
- Frankfurt School critical theory;
- social-practical accounts of reason;
- Pittsburgh neo-Hegelianism associated with John McDowell and Robert Brandom;
- renewed metaphysical readings;
- feminist, postcolonial, decolonial, and race-critical reassessment;
- new historical-critical lecture editions.

### Material commemoration

Hegel is commemorated through:

- the Hegel-Haus museum;
- monuments, plaques, streets, schools, prizes, and societies;
- German postage stamps, including a 2020 issue for his 250th birthday;
- his Berlin honorary grave;
- international Hegel societies and congresses.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by documented personal contact, institutional succession, correspondence, collaboration, or explicit intellectual reception are included.

- **Aristotle — documented intellectual influence.** Hegel treated Aristotle as one of the highest predecessors of speculative philosophy and lectured extensively on him.
- **Plato — documented intellectual influence.** Hegel’s accounts of Greek ethical life, dialectic, and the history of philosophy repeatedly engage Plato.
- **Aurelius Augustinus — documented intellectual engagement.** Discussed in lectures on religion and the history of philosophy.
- **Jacob Boehme — documented intellectual influence.** Hegel read and praised Boehme as an important speculative thinker, while criticizing his symbolic form.
- **Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz — documented intellectual influence.** Central predecessor in Hegel’s histories of metaphysics, substance, individuality, and contradiction.
- **Jean-Jacques Rousseau — documented formative influence.** Read at Tübingen; important for general will, freedom, modern social division, and revolutionary politics.
- **Adam Smith — documented intellectual engagement.** Hegel absorbed British political economy, including division of labor and civil society; the route included Smith and later interpreters.
- **Immanuel Kant — documented formative influence and principal philosophical predecessor.** Hegel’s system developed through sustained criticism of Kantian epistemology, morality, aesthetics, and religion.
- **Johann Gottlieb Fichte — documented personal/intellectual and institutional connection.** Hegel worked at Jena in the immediate Fichtean intellectual environment, criticized Fichte’s system in 1801, and in 1818 took the Berlin chair vacant since Fichte’s death.
- **Johann Wolfgang von Goethe — documented personal and patronage connection.** Goethe supported Hegel’s Jena career; they met and corresponded. Hegel later defended Goethe’s color theory and discussed his literary work.
- **Friedrich Schiller — documented intellectual influence and shared Jena milieu.** Schiller’s aesthetic and political writings shaped Hegel; evidence for a close personal friendship is lacking.
- **Friedrich Wilhelm Joseph Schelling — documented friendship, co-residence, collaboration, and rivalry.** Tübingen friendship; joint editorship of the *Kritisches Journal* at Jena in 1802–1803; philosophical rupture surrounding the 1807 *Phenomenology*.
- **Napoleon Bonaparte — documented sighting and political-historical interpretation.** Hegel saw Napoleon in Jena on 13 October 1806 and described him as a “world-soul.” No personal meeting is documented.
- **Giambattista Vico — reported intellectual parallel/influence.** Hegel knew traditions of philosophical history in which Vico was important, but a decisive direct dependence is harder to establish than later comparisons imply.
- **Martin Luther — documented intellectual and religious influence.** Hegel treated the Reformation as decisive for subjective freedom and the modern state.
- **Karl Marx — documented posthumous influence; no meeting.** Marx was thirteen when Hegel died. He studied, appropriated, and criticized Hegel intensively during the 1840s and afterward.
- **Søren Aabye Kierkegaard — documented posthumous critical reception; no established meeting.** Kierkegaard attacked Hegelian system-building, mediation, and the absorption of individual existence into speculative history.
- **Friedrich Nietzsche — documented later critical reception.** Nietzsche engaged Hegelian historical and dialectical legacies mostly through the nineteenth-century intellectual environment; no personal connection was possible.
- **Martin Heidegger — documented posthumous interpretation.** Heidegger lectured and wrote extensively on Hegel’s ontology, time, experience, and negativity.
- **Walter Benjamin — documented posthumous reception.** Benjamin’s philosophy of history was formed partly against historicist and progressivist readings associated with Hegel.
- **Theodor W. Adorno — documented posthumous critical appropriation.** Adorno’s negative dialectics both depended on and opposed Hegelian identity thinking.
- **Herbert Marcuse — documented posthumous interpretation.** *Reason and Revolution* defended Hegel against fascist genealogy and linked him to critical social theory.
- **Jean-Paul Sartre — documented posthumous influence.** Sartre’s accounts of recognition, conflict, and being-for-others developed partly through French Hegelianism.
- **Jacques Marie Émile Lacan — documented posthumous influence.** Lacan encountered Hegel especially through Kojève’s Paris seminars.
- **Jacques Derrida — documented posthumous critical engagement.** *Glas* and other works deconstruct Hegelian dialectic, family, religion, and absolute knowledge.
- **Gilles Louis René Deleuze — documented posthumous antagonistic reception.** Deleuze constructed philosophies of difference partly against Hegelian contradiction and negation.
- **Paul-Michel Foucault — documented posthumous reception.** Foucault acknowledged the difficulty of escaping Hegel, especially through Jean Hyppolite’s teaching.
- **Jürgen Habermas — documented posthumous influence.** Habermas’s accounts of modernity, recognition, and communicative reason repeatedly engage Hegel.
- **Slavoj Žižek — documented posthumous interpretation.** Žižek combines Hegel with Lacanian psychoanalysis and has been a prominent contemporary advocate of dialectical materialism.
- **Rudolf Steiner — documented posthumous reception.** Steiner wrote on German Idealism and situated his spiritual philosophy in relation to Hegel, though not as straightforward discipleship.
- **Karl Popper is not on the supplied roster.** His adversarial importance is nevertheless covered above.
- No documentary basis was found for a personal or organizational connection between Hegel and the roster’s Freemasonic, Illuminati, occult, banking, intelligence, or modern political figures merely linked to him in conspiracy literature.

## Compact Chronology

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 27 Aug. 1770 | Born in Stuttgart. |
| 1777–1788 | Classical schooling at Stuttgart’s gymnasium. |
| 20 Sept. 1783 | Mother dies; Hegel survives the same fever outbreak. |
| Oct. 1788 | Enters the Tübinger Stift. |
| 1790 | Receives Magister degree; begins advanced theology. |
| 1793 | Passes theological examination; becomes tutor near Bern. |
| 1796 | Leaves Bern. |
| 1797 | Begins tutoring in Frankfurt; renews contact with Hölderlin. |
| 1799 | Father dies; Hegel receives modest inheritance. |
| Jan. 1801 | Moves to Jena. |
| 1801 | Publishes *Difference between Fichte’s and Schelling’s Systems*; habilitates with planetary dissertation. |
| 1802–1803 | Co-edits *Kritisches Journal der Philosophie* with Schelling. |
| 1805 | Appointed unsalaried extraordinary professor at Jena. |
| 13 Oct. 1806 | Sees Napoleon in Jena; writes “world-soul” letter. |
| 1807 | *Phenomenology of Spirit* published. |
| 5 Feb. 1807 | Ludwig Fischer born. |
| Mar. 1807 | Moves to Bamberg; edits the *Bamberger Zeitung*. |
| Nov. 1808 | Becomes rector of the Egidiengymnasium, Nuremberg. |
| Sept. 1811 | Marries Marie von Tucher. |
| 1812 | Daughter Susanna born and dies; first part of *Science of Logic* published. |
| 1813 | Karl Hegel born; second logical volume published. |
| 1814 | Immanuel Hegel born. |
| 1816 | Completes *Science of Logic*; becomes Heidelberg professor. |
| 1817 | Publishes first *Encyclopaedia*; Ludwig joins household. |
| 29 Sept. 1818 | Arrives in Berlin as professor. |
| 1819 | Carlsbad Decrees intensify censorship and university surveillance. |
| 1820/1821 | *Elements of the Philosophy of Right* published. |
| 1820 | Moves to Am Kupfergraben; travels to Dresden. |
| 1822 | Travels in the Netherlands. |
| 1824 | Travels to Prague and Vienna. |
| 1827 | Second *Encyclopaedia*; visits Paris; *Jahrbücher für wissenschaftliche Kritik* begins. |
| 1829–1830 | Rector of Berlin University. |
| 1830 | Third *Encyclopaedia*. |
| 1831 | Writes essay on the English Reform Bill; final installment suppressed. |
| 28 Aug. 1831 | Ludwig Fischer dies in Batavia, unknown to Hegel. |
| 14 Nov. 1831 | Hegel dies in Berlin, officially of cholera. |
| 16 Nov. 1831 | Buried in Dorotheenstadt cemetery near Fichte. |
| 1832–1845 | Friends and pupils issue the first collected works. |
| 1832 | Sister Christiane dies by suicide. |
| 1844 | Rosenkranz publishes the first major biography. |
| 1968 onward | Historical-critical *Gesammelte Werke* begins publication. |
| 2020 | Hegel’s 250th anniversary; renovated Hegel-Haus reopens. |
| 2022 | Previously unknown Heidelberg lecture transcripts identified in the Munich–Freising archive. |
| 2024–2026 | New scholarship concentrates on race, colonialism, slavery, editorial transmission, and political reception. |
