# José Antonio de la Sierra: Research Dossier

## Scope, Identification, and Evidentiary Limits

This dossier concerns the late-colonial metallurgical officer whose assayer mark **J** appears on the coins struck at Potosí in 1813 for the **Provincias Unidas del Río de la Plata**.

The strongest specialist and archival literature identifies him as **José Antonio de Sierra**, without *la*. The expanded form **José Antonio de la Sierra** is common in later Argentine institutional and popular accounts, including material published by the Banco Central de la República Argentina. It is retained in the title because it is the requested form, but it should not automatically be assumed to be the form he used himself.

The surviving reconstruction is unusually uneven. Sierra is well documented as an officer of the royal fiscal and mint establishments and as the defendant in an infidelity proceeding after the royalist reconquest of Potosí. His domestic life, education, final exile, and death remain largely undocumented in the published sources consulted.

The principal modern reconstruction is Arnaldo J. Cunietti-Ferrando’s archival study of the Potosí mint during the wars of independence. Later investigations by Roberto A. Biazzi and collaborators, and Luciano Pezzano, checked Cunietti-Ferrando’s identification against documents in the **Archivo Histórico de la Casa Nacional de Moneda de Potosí** (AHCNMP). They located Sierra’s own signed certificate, payroll evidence, the instrument recording his promotion, and the 1815 sentence clearing him of *infidencia*.

### Evidentiary labels used

- **Documented fact:** supported directly by a contemporary document or by a modern researcher identifying a specific archival record.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a generally reliable secondary source, but the underlying document was not independently accessible during this research.
- **Inference:** a conclusion drawn from documented dates or circumstances.
- **Disputed:** materially conflicting versions exist.
- **Incorrect or superseded attribution:** an older identification contradicted by later archival evidence.
- **Unverified:** no adequate supporting document was found.
- **Unknown:** the consulted record does not establish the fact.

### Birth-date problem

Two incompatible traditions appear in the literature:

1. **Lima, 1755.** Cunietti-Ferrando’s later synthetic history says Sierra was born in Lima in 1755.
2. **Circa 1745–1746.** Cunietti-Ferrando’s earlier account described him as about seventy years old when his proceedings ended in January 1815. A numismatic synthesis described him as a sixty-seven-year-old fundidor in 1813. Both estimates indicate a birth around 1745–1746.

No baptismal record was located in the accessible sources. The atlas date “c. 1746” is therefore plausible, but the contradiction remains unresolved.

### Name problem

The archival and specialist sources consistently favor **José Antonio de Sierra**. The Banco Central and some later scientific or commemorative publications use **José Antonio de la Sierra**. No evidence was found that “de la Sierra” was a pseudonym, nickname, or formally adopted alternative name.

He must not be confused with:

- **Juan Palomo y Sierra**, a royalist Potosí assayer once incorrectly identified as the “J” of 1813;
- a different José Antonio de Sierra associated with the 1769 Wayuu uprising in New Granada;
- José Antonio de Sierra y Sotomayor of Lima;
- later officials and businessmen bearing similar names.

## Family and Ancestry

### Father

**Miguel Lamberto de Sierra Parafita** — **reported fact, supported by Cunietti-Ferrando’s archival reconstruction**.

He was a Spaniard who became an important royal fiscal officer in Potosí. Offices attributed to him include:

- minister of the Real Hacienda;
- treasurer of the Reales Cajas of Potosí from 1781;
- honorary member or officer of the Tribunal Mayor and Real Audiencia de Cuentas;
- recipient of a reported salary of 3,000 pesos while treasurer;
- retirement in June 1804 with an annual pension of 2,000 pesos.

He died in Potosí in October 1808.

Miguel Lamberto is also remembered for compiling a statistical account of the wealth extracted from the Cerro Rico and the royal fifths collected through 31 December 1800. That manuscript was later published as *Manifiesto de la plata extraída del cerro de Potosí, 1556–1800*, with a prologue by Humberto F. Burzio, by the Academia Nacional de la Historia in 1971.

The father’s offices, salary, and reputation place the Sierra household within the upper stratum of Potosí’s royal fiscal bureaucracy. This does not prove nobility or independently owned mining wealth, but it establishes substantial institutional standing.

### Mother

**Unknown.** No reliable name was found in the consulted material.

### Siblings

Cunietti-Ferrando states that Miguel Lamberto de Sierra Parafita left three sons:

- **José Antonio de Sierra**, the subject;
- **Juan de Sierra**;
- **Miguel Lamberto de Sierra**, an attorney.

The political histories of the brothers became relevant to José Antonio’s 1813–1815 prosecution.

#### Juan de Sierra

**Reported fact:** Juan initially supported José Manuel de Goyeneche’s royal army but subsequently joined or followed Manuel Belgrano’s retreating revolutionary forces toward Tucumán after the 1813 reverses.

No fuller occupational or domestic biography was found.

#### Miguel Lamberto de Sierra

**Documented or strongly reported fact:** Miguel Lamberto was an attorney and a royal treasury official. He appears as treasurer of the Reales Cajas during the revolutionary period.

His political trajectory was unstable:

- he initially supported the royalist army;
- after the royalist recovery following Huaqui, a cabildo abierto reportedly selected him as interim governor of Potosí;
- on 25 August 1811 he sent a declaration of submission to Goyeneche;
- he later passed to the revolutionary side and followed Belgrano’s army toward Tucumán;
- when José Rondeau’s forces occupied Potosí in 1815, he returned and served on a tribunal collecting funds for the revolutionary administration, including through confiscation of property belonging to royalist émigrés.

This record made José Antonio’s defense more difficult when the latter was prosecuted for collaboration.

### Spouse and children

Cunietti-Ferrando says José Antonio remained in Potosí “with his family” when the revolutionary army withdrew in November 1813. This establishes some domestic household, but the consulted sources do not identify:

- his wife;
- whether the union was a formal marriage;
- any children;
- descendants;
- household property;
- inheritance arrangements.

Assertions about a named spouse or descendants would presently be speculative.

## Childhood and Early Life

If the Lima birthplace is correct, Sierra was born in the capital of the Viceroyalty of Peru, either around 1745–1746 or in 1755. His father’s eventual service in Potosí provides an evident route for the family’s migration from Lima or Spain-connected administrative circles into Upper Peru.

No documentation was found concerning:

- baptism;
- childhood residence;
- mother or maternal ancestry;
- literacy training;
- apprenticeship;
- university attendance;
- early travel from Lima to Potosí;
- date of arrival in Potosí.

His later ability to pass a technical examination and work in silver accounting, balance control, smelting, alloying, and assaying indicates sustained practical formation in fiscal metallurgy. It cannot presently be determined whether that training came through a formal apprenticeship, household patronage, instruction inside the mint, or employment in the Reales Cajas.

## Education and Formation

No school, university, named teacher, or apprenticeship contract has been identified.

His documented or reported professional competencies included:

- weighing precious metals;
- accounting for silver;
- refining and smelting;
- preparing prescribed metallic alloys;
- testing fineness;
- certifying bars presented for coinage;
- working within the documentary and accountability procedures of the royal treasury and mint.

On 1 October 1795, Governor-Intendant **Francisco de Paula Sanz** appointed him on an interim basis to the offices of balanzario, fundidor, and ensayador of the Reales Cajas. The appointment reportedly followed a notably successful examination. Sanz stated that Sierra possessed the birth status, ability, conduct, and intelligence prescribed by law.

This examination is the clearest evidence for Sierra’s formal technical competence. It does not establish that he was originally trained as a full mint assayer; his usual specialty was smelting and alloy preparation.

## Early Career

### Silver accounting

Before October 1795, Sierra served as **contador de plata**, or silver accountant, in the Potosí fiscal establishment. The exact beginning of this service is unknown.

The office required more than ordinary bookkeeping. Potosí’s fiscal-industrial system received silver from mines and refining works, evaluated its weight and fineness, levied royal charges, and converted accepted bullion into coin. Silver accounting therefore placed Sierra inside the administrative chain linking the Cerro Rico, the royal treasury, the Banco de Rescates de San Carlos, and the mint.

### Interim technical offices, 1795–1799

On **1 October 1795**, Francisco de Paula Sanz appointed Sierra interim:

- **balanzario**;
- **fundidor**;
- **ensayador** of the Reales Cajas.

The appointment was to last until the Crown selected permanent officeholders or incorporated the posts directly into royal administration.

The combination of offices gave Sierra responsibility for several connected stages of precious-metal processing: weighing, melting and alloying, and testing fineness. The arrangement was temporary, not a permanent proprietary title.

### Removal in 1799

Viceroy **Gabriel de Avilés** removed Sierra from the interim appointment in 1799 and installed **Salvador José María de Matos** as permanent officeholder.

Cunietti-Ferrando states that this produced a long-lasting hostility between the Sierra and Matos families. The later political conflict intensified it:

- Sierra’s household was associated first with royal fiscal service;
- Salvador de Matos supported the Potosí rising of 10 November 1810;
- Matos became síndico procurador of the cabildo and supported the revolutionary program;
- Matos’s son, Francisco José de Matos, became an assayer of the second United Provinces coinage in 1815.

There is no evidence that José Antonio carried out violence, litigation, or sabotage against the Matos family. “Enmity” is the characterization of the modern biographer.

## Chronological Life History

## 1808–1810: Return to the smelting office

The death of the mint fundidor **Blas Garcés** created a vacancy. Sierra began exercising the office of interim fundidor on **27 February 1809**.

In February 1810, he was confirmed as interim fundidor, reportedly at half the salary of the permanent officeholder. He was thus already performing an essential metallurgical role before the revolutionary armies obtained control of Potosí.

The fundidor was responsible for carrying out the assayers’ instructions and adjusting bullion to the legally required alloy. The job was distinct from designing or engraving coin dies.

### Political setting

Potosí was one of the central industrial and fiscal nodes of the Spanish monarchy. Its institutional system included:

- the Cerro Rico mines;
- the Reales Cajas;
- the Banco de Rescates de San Carlos;
- the customs administration;
- the Casa Real de Moneda.

During 1810, these institutions supplied royal military expenditure. Potosí’s treasury and mint consequently became strategic objectives of both royalist and revolutionary forces.

### The revolution of 10 November 1810

After the revolutionary victory at Suipacha, Potosí overthrew Governor Francisco de Paula Sanz on 10 November 1810 and accepted the authority of the Buenos Aires junta. Juan José Castelli later ordered the execution of Sanz, Vicente Nieto, and José de Córdoba.

The sources do not describe José Antonio de Sierra’s personal actions during the 10 November uprising. Cunietti-Ferrando characterizes him during this period as loyal to the king. His brother Miguel Lamberto’s later political shifts, and the revolutionary allegiance of the Matos family, formed part of the divided political environment surrounding the mint.

## 1811–1812: Royalist recovery and family division

After the revolutionary defeat at Huaqui in June 1811, royalist forces recovered Potosí. José Antonio remained Fundidor Mayor through the subsequent realignments.

His brothers Juan and Miguel Lamberto reportedly began on the royalist side but later moved into the revolutionary camp. Miguel Lamberto briefly occupied a prominent local position under the restored royal order before following the revolutionary forces south.

José Antonio, by contrast, remained at the Potosí mint. No evidence was found that he held a military commission, participated in armed combat, or made a public ideological declaration in this period.

## 1813: Promotion to Ensayador Mayor

### Revolutionary occupation

The second United Provinces expedition into Upper Peru, commanded by **Manuel Belgrano**, advanced after the victories of Tucumán and Salta.

The chronology requires care:

- revolutionary authorities controlled the Potosí mint from early March 1813;
- the army’s vanguard under Eustoquio Díaz Vélez entered Potosí on 17 May;
- Belgrano personally reached the city in June—Pezzano dates his entry to 19 June;
- consequently, mint reorganization and Sierra’s promotion preceded Belgrano’s personal arrival.

Many senior royalist mint officials had withdrawn. The new administration filled vacancies using employees who remained.

### Appointment

The payroll for May 1813 paid Sierra:

- fourteen days as fundidor;
- seventeen days as ensayador.

From this, Biazzi and collaborators infer that he took up the assayer’s position on **15 May 1813**.

A title issued to **Lucas Morales**, also dated 15 May, states that the post of Fundidor Mayor was vacant because José Antonio de Sierra had been promoted to ensayador. Although researchers have not located Sierra’s own formal title, this collateral document directly confirms the promotion.

Initially he signed as:

> “Fundidor Mayor en ausencia de los ensayadores”

By **31 May 1813**, a surviving certificate concerning bars of silver was signed by him as:

> “Ensayador Mayor”

The certificate is held in the AHCNMP series covering assayers’ certifications of silver bars, **CRM-2153**.

### Complaint by Hipólito Barroso

A primary-document catalogue published by Argentina’s Archivo General de la Nación records a complaint dated **2 October 1813** by **Hipólito Barroso**, master of the mint’s workshops for ironwork, locksmithing, bronze casting, and refining copper used to alloy gold and silver.

Barroso invoked his years of service and complained about the appointment of José Antonio Sierra as fundidor and Ensayador Mayor. The document is catalogued as **AGC XIII 10-1742, document 60**.

This is evidence of an internal employment or precedence dispute. It establishes that Sierra’s elevation was contested by at least one senior technical employee. The accessible description does not reveal:

- whether Barroso alleged technical incompetence, favoritism, or merely superior seniority;
- whether an inquiry followed;
- whether Sierra answered;
- whether any authority upheld the complaint.

It is therefore inaccurate to convert the complaint into a finding of misconduct.

## The 1813 coinage

### Legislative origin

On **13 April 1813**, the Asamblea General Constituyente in Buenos Aires ordered the Potosí mint to replace royal imagery with new devices while retaining the weight and fineness used under Carlos IV and Fernando VII.

The authorized silver design included:

- the seal of the Assembly;
- the legend **PROVINCIAS DEL RÍO DE LA PLATA**;
- a sun;
- the motto **EN UNIÓN Y LIBERTAD**;
- denomination, date, mint mark, and assayer’s mark.

Gold and silver designs were transmitted to Potosí. A later executive act ordered the coins to be received as current money at their legal value.

### Sierra’s role

Sierra did **not** originate the political decision, national emblems, or principal artistic design.

The division of labor reconstructed by Pezzano and other specialists was:

- **Pedro José Agrelo:** principal intellectual or legislative author of the coinage measure;
- **Juan de Dios Rivera:** probable maker of the designs sent from Buenos Aires;
- **Pedro Venavides/Benavides:** Talla Mayor responsible for opening the dies;
- **José Antonio de Sierra:** Ensayador Mayor responsible for testing and certifying fineness;
- **Buenaventura Salinas**, later **Apolinario Figueroa:** superintendent-level authority;
- the mint’s numerous subordinate officers and laborers: manufacture and accounting.

Later BCRA language calls Sierra the “supervisor” of the coinage. That is broadly understandable but imprecise. The superintendent was the mint’s highest authority. Sierra’s narrower responsibility was the legally critical assay of the metal.

### First issues

The first firmly reconstructed rendition of the new silver coinage occurred on **22 June 1813**. Potosí annals reported that by 25 June, 40,000 pesos bearing the new devices had left the mint for the bullion-rescue bank.

Silver production under the United Provinces design continued until **18 November 1813**.

The 1813 series included:

- silver: ½, 1, 2, 4, and 8 reales;
- gold: 1, 2, 4, and 8 escudos, according to the specialist reconstruction, although some popular catalogues omit individual rare denominations.

All carried:

- the intertwined PTS mint mark of Potosí;
- the date 1813;
- the initial **J**, identifying the responsible assayer.

The silver coinage used a laurel-patterned edge, while gold pieces used diagonal reeding.

### Assayer’s responsibility and technical quality

An assayer’s initial was an official accountability mark. It indicated the officer who certified that the precious metal met the prescribed fineness. It was not an artist’s signature or declaration that the assayer personally struck every coin.

Some 1813 coins display spelling mistakes, doubled numerals, or other die varieties. These are attributable primarily to die preparation and hurried production rather than automatically to Sierra’s assays. An example in the BCRA museum has a doubled numeral “3” over “8.”

One modern synthesis describes the remaining personnel as insufficiently trained and connects this to numerous errors. That judgment should be qualified:

- Sierra had prior technical experience and had passed a formal examination;
- engraving errors were outside the assayer’s primary function;
- no consulted record establishes that the coinage was condemned for fraudulent fineness or that Sierra was charged with debasement.

### End of production

Defeats at Vilcapugio and Ayohuma forced Belgrano’s army to withdraw. The United Provinces administration left Potosí on 18 November 1813.

Before retreating, the army attempted to deny the mint to the royalists. A plan to detonate gunpowder reportedly failed after a man identified as Anglada cut the fuse. Other damage was inflicted on mint offices and equipment.

Sierra did not accompany the withdrawing revolutionary army. He remained in Potosí with his family.

## 1813–1815: Arrest and prosecution for infidelity

### Arrest

After the royalist recovery, Sierra was:

- dismissed from his mint offices;
- arrested as a collaborator;
- prosecuted for his role in producing the United Provinces coinage.

The proceeding was not an accusation of counterfeiting, embezzlement, or adulteration. It concerned **infidencia**—political disloyalty to the king.

The accusation was legally serious. The 1813 coins had removed the royal portrait and titles, substituted revolutionary emblems, and asserted an autonomous political authority. Serving as the assayer publicly attached Sierra’s official mark to that act.

His brothers’ movement into the revolutionary camp worsened his position.

### Duration of imprisonment

The literature says he remained imprisoned for more than a year. Since the revolutionary evacuation occurred on 18 November 1813 and he was cleared on 25 January 1815, this duration is chronologically credible.

### Defense

Sierra denied voluntary political disloyalty. He argued that his acts resulted from:

- fear of the armed forces of the Río de la Plata government;
- absence of freedom to refuse the revolutionary authorities’ orders;
- coercive circumstances amounting to force majeure.

No complete transcript of the evidence or witness examinations was accessible. It is therefore impossible to determine exactly which witnesses supported him or what additional evidence the court considered.

### Judgment of 25 January 1815

The sentence, preserved as **AHCNMP CRM-2196**, cleared Sierra.

In substance, the deciding authority held that:

- Sierra had purified or vindicated his conduct concerning the acts alleged;
- his conduct had resulted from fear of the United Provinces forces;
- he lacked freedom to resist the orders of the “intruding” authorities;
- he was free of the offense of infidelity against the king;
- his prior good name and reputation were restored.

This was an **acquittal or exonerating court determination**, not merely an untested declaration of innocence by Sierra.

The Conde de Casa Real de Moneda ordered him restored as **Fundidor Mayor**, with full salary from the date of the decision.

### Interpretation

Two interpretations remain possible:

1. **Coerced royal servant:** Sierra had remained loyal to the Crown, accepted the revolutionary appointment under duress, and told the truth in 1815.
2. **Pragmatic survivor or concealed sympathizer:** he cooperated willingly or at least opportunistically in 1813 and invoked coercion when royal authority returned.

The first interpretation was accepted by the royalist tribunal. The second remains conjectural. Cunietti-Ferrando explicitly acknowledged that Sierra’s true political sympathies could not be established.

## 1815: Restoration and final departure

Sierra resumed the position of Fundidor Mayor after 25 January 1815.

José Rondeau’s army then advanced into Upper Peru, and the revolutionary forces occupied Potosí again in April or May. This time Sierra withdrew with the royalist army.

This final movement is the last reported episode in his life. The consulted published literature does not establish:

- his route;
- whether his family accompanied him;
- where he settled;
- whether he resumed government or mint employment elsewhere;
- when or where he died.

The atlas description “after 1815” is therefore the safest death-date formulation.

He was not the assayer of the United Provinces’ 1815 issues. Those coins carry the initials associated with **Francisco José de Matos** and **Leandro Ozio**.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

## Institutions

### Reales Cajas of Potosí

Sierra’s early offices belonged to or interacted with the royal treasury apparatus. The Reales Cajas:

- received and accounted for fiscal revenue;
- managed precious metals and royal dues;
- financed government and military expenditures;
- interacted with the mint and bullion-rescue system.

### Casa Real de Moneda de Potosí

Sierra’s principal employer was the Potosí mint, one of the major monetary-industrial establishments of Spanish America.

The second mint building, operating from the 1770s, used mechanized rolling and screw-press technology rather than the older hammer-struck process. Work remained highly specialized and divided among:

- treasury and accounting offices;
- assayers;
- weighing officers;
- smelters;
- engravers and die cutters;
- coinage-room officers;
- guards and material custodians.

### Banco de Rescates de San Carlos

The bullion-rescue bank purchased or received precious metal and formed another part of the production chain. No evidence was found that Sierra personally owned an interest in it.

## Metallurgical responsibilities

As fundidor, Sierra had to:

- melt bullion;
- follow the assayers’ instructions;
- adjust alloy composition;
- prepare metal suitable for coinage;
- account for precious-metal loss and residue.

As assayer, he had to:

- sample bullion or prepared metal;
- determine fineness;
- certify compliance with legal standards;
- assume official responsibility through his initials.

## Personal businesses, mines, or investments

No reliable evidence was found that Sierra personally owned:

- mines;
- refining mills;
- merchant houses;
- bank shares;
- workshops independent of the mint;
- patents;
- agricultural estates;
- urban rental properties.

The family’s official salaries indicate a comfortable bureaucratic position, but no defensible estimate of Sierra’s personal wealth can be made.

## Salary

Known salary evidence is limited:

- in February 1810 he was reportedly confirmed as interim fundidor at half the permanent officeholder’s salary;
- the May 1813 payroll distinguished his days paid as fundidor and ensayador;
- in January 1815 he was restored with full salary from the date of judgment.

The accessible summaries do not supply Sierra’s exact annual or monthly amounts.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Technical Development

## Political allegiance

Sierra left no known political manifesto, memoir, or correspondence explaining his beliefs.

His record shows service under alternating regimes:

- royal fiscal administration before 1813;
- revolutionary administration in 1813;
- restored royal administration in early 1815;
- withdrawal with royalist forces during the second revolutionary occupation.

This sequence proves institutional adaptation but not ideological conversion.

Unlike his brothers, whose movement between camps is reported, José Antonio did not publicly commit himself in surviving records except through official service. His 1815 defense explicitly asserted loyalty to the king and involuntary obedience to the revolutionary government.

## Religion

As a Spanish imperial officer in late-colonial Potosí, Sierra operated within an officially Catholic order. No personal evidence was found concerning:

- devotional practice;
- confraternity membership;
- clerical relationships;
- religious patronage;
- heterodoxy;
- anticlericalism.

## Intellectual or artistic authorship

Sierra was not the designer or engraver of the 1813 coin. His historical role was technical and fiduciary. The association of his **J** with the coin’s imagery has sometimes blurred the distinction between:

- political authorship;
- graphic design;
- die engraving;
- metallic assay.

The evidence assigns those functions to different people.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Miguel Lamberto de Sierra Parafita

Father and probable institutional patron. His senior fiscal offices placed the family inside Potosí’s royal administrative elite.

### Juan de Sierra and Miguel Lamberto de Sierra

Brothers whose movement from royalist to revolutionary associations affected José Antonio’s prosecution.

### Francisco de Paula Sanz

Governor-intendant who appointed Sierra to the combined interim technical offices on 1 October 1795 after his examination.

### Gabriel de Avilés

Viceroy who removed Sierra from the interim office in 1799 and installed Salvador José María de Matos.

### Salvador José María de Matos

Professional rival and head of a family described as having a long enmity with the Sierras. The rivalry arose from officeholding and was sharpened by divergent political alignments.

### Blas Garcés

His death opened the fundidor vacancy that Sierra began filling in February 1809.

### Juan Palomo y Sierra

Royalist assayer and later historiographical double. There is no evidence that he was the **J** on the United Provinces coinage. The erroneous identification joined his surname “Sierra” and initial circumstances to the wrong official.

### Buenaventura Salinas

Governor-intendant and mint superintendent during the early 1813 revolutionary administration. Sierra served under this authority.

### Apolinario Figueroa

Succeeded Salinas in September 1813 as governor-intendant and superintendent-level official.

### Lucas Morales

Appointed to the fundidor vacancy created by Sierra’s promotion on 15 May 1813. His title provides collateral documentary proof of Sierra’s elevation.

### Pedro Venavides/Benavides

Talla Mayor responsible for directing the opening of the dies. His artistic-mechanical contribution complemented Sierra’s metallurgical and assay responsibilities.

### Hipólito Barroso

Senior workshop master who complained on 2 October 1813 about Sierra’s appointment. This is the only located documentary evidence of a contemporary professional challenge to Sierra.

### Manuel Belgrano

Commander of the revolutionary army controlling Potosí in 1813. Sierra worked within the mint administration sustained by Belgrano’s campaign, although his promotion occurred before Belgrano personally arrived in the city.

### Conde de Casa Real de Moneda

The royal mint authority who signed or issued the January 1815 exoneration and restoration order. The accessible summaries do not consistently give his personal name.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

## 1. The 1799 office dispute

**Status: documented administrative replacement; reported family rivalry.**

Sierra lost his interim technical offices when Viceroy Avilés appointed Salvador José María de Matos permanently. No evidence was found that Sierra was removed for corruption, failed assays, or incapacity.

## 2. Hipólito Barroso’s complaint

**Status: documented complaint; outcome unknown.**

Barroso complained about Sierra’s appointment in October 1813 and invoked his own years of service. The available catalogue description does not show that Sierra was found unqualified or that the complaint succeeded.

## 3. Collaboration with the revolutionary government

**Status: documented service and prosecution.**

Sierra served as Ensayador Mayor under the United Provinces administration. His **J** appeared on coins replacing royal authority with revolutionary symbols.

## 4. Charge of infidelity to the king

**Status: formal prosecution followed by acquittal.**

Royalist authorities imprisoned and prosecuted Sierra after November 1813. On 25 January 1815, the court accepted his coercion defense, declared him free of *infidencia*, restored his reputation, and returned him to office.

The allegation should not be presented as a conviction.

## 5. Possible political opportunism

**Status: unresolved interpretation.**

His successive service under revolutionary and royal administrations can be described factually. Claims that he was secretly a patriot, secretly an unwavering royalist, or purely opportunistic are not established by the surviving evidence.

## 6. Coin quality and errors

**Status: documented die varieties; no documented personal fraud.**

The 1813 issues contain varieties and engraving errors. No reliable source consulted accuses Sierra of:

- deliberate debasement;
- theft of bullion;
- false assay;
- counterfeiting;
- bribery;
- money laundering.

The famous seventeenth-century Potosí mint fraud involving Felipe Ramírez de Arellano and Francisco Gómez de la Rocha occurred long before Sierra and must not be attached to him.

## 7. Adversarial search findings, including 2024–2026

A dedicated search combined both name forms with Spanish and English terms for controversy, scandal, fraud, corruption, conspiracy, accusation, litigation, criticism, and crime. It also restricted searches to the most recent two years.

The only materially relevant negative findings were:

- the documented 1813 Barroso employment complaint;
- the royalist prosecution for collaboration and *infidencia*;
- general statements that hastily promoted mint personnel produced errors.

No recent investigation, newly reported scandal, fraud allegation, lawsuit, or financial-crime claim concerning this historical Sierra was located. Several search results referred to unrelated homonyms or to the seventeenth-century Potosí mint scandal.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## Myth 1: Juan Palomo y Sierra was the assayer “J”

### Story

Earlier numismatic writing attributed the initial **J** on the 1813 coins to royalist assayer **Juan Palomo y Sierra**.

### Appearance and circulation

Jorge N. Ferrari repeated this identification in his 1963 work *Sesquicentenario de la Primera Moneda con el Sello de la Patria*. It entered older numismatic discussion because Palomo was a known Potosí assayer and “Sierra” appeared in his name.

### Evidence against

Cunietti-Ferrando identified José Antonio de Sierra through archival research. Later examination of the Potosí records found:

- the 15 May instrument noting Sierra’s promotion;
- the May payroll dividing his fundidor and assayer service;
- Sierra’s 31 May certificate signed as Ensayador Mayor;
- the 1815 sentence explicitly linking him to the revolutionary coinage.

### Reputation effect

Correcting the attribution transformed Sierra from an obscure smelter into a remembered participant in the first United Provinces coinage.

### Status

**Incorrect or superseded attribution.**

## Myth 2: Sierra personally designed or minted the first Argentine coin

### Story

Popular accounts sometimes speak as if the person whose initial appears on a coin designed or personally manufactured it.

### Evidence

The available division of labor assigns:

- the legislative concept to Agrelo and the Assembly;
- the probable transmitted drawing to Juan de Dios Rivera;
- die cutting to Pedro Venavides;
- assay responsibility to Sierra;
- production to the mint staff.

### Reputation effect

The simplification has increased Sierra’s visibility but obscured the institutional character of coin production.

### Status

**Popular simplification, not documented fact.**

## Myth 3: “Supervisor of the coinage”

### Story

The BCRA calls José Antonio de la Sierra the supervisor of the 1813 coin.

### Evidence

Sierra was Ensayador Mayor, but the superintendent was the highest authority of the mint. His role was essential but technically narrower: certifying metallic fineness.

### Status

**Modern institutional shorthand requiring qualification.**

## Myth 4: The “J” proves Sierra was a committed patriot

### Story

Because the initial appears beside revolutionary emblems, Sierra is sometimes absorbed into patriotic commemoration as an intentional adherent of independence.

### Evidence for

- He accepted the assayer’s office.
- He signed as Ensayador Mayor.
- He remained in the role throughout the 1813 production period.

### Evidence against or qualifying the story

- He later testified that he had acted under coercion.
- A royal tribunal accepted that defense.
- He remained in Potosí rather than retreating with Belgrano.
- In 1815 he withdrew with the royalist army.
- His exact political beliefs are unknown.

### Status

**Unresolved interpretation and later patriotic mythology.**

## Myth 5: Sierra was merely an unqualified elderly fundidor

### Story

Some summaries describe him as “simply” a sixty-seven-year-old fundidor promoted because qualified personnel had fled, linking the promotion to production errors.

### Evidence for

- He was principally serving as fundidor immediately before promotion.
- The revolutionary administration faced personnel shortages.
- The coinage exhibits varieties and mistakes.

### Evidence against or qualifying the story

- He had earlier served in silver accounting.
- He had passed an examination for combined weighing, smelting, and assay duties in 1795.
- He had previous interim experience as ensayador.
- Die-lettering mistakes were not primarily the assayer’s responsibility.

### Status

**Partly factual but reductive characterization.**

## Myth 6: His full name was unquestionably “José Antonio de la Sierra”

### Story

Later Argentine references commonly give “de la Sierra.”

### Evidence

The archival-specialist literature repeatedly uses **José Antonio de Sierra**, and the surviving official descriptions use Sierra without *la*. The longer form appears to be a later normalization or variant.

### Status

**Disputed nomenclature.**

## Myth 7: He was involved in the historic “great Potosí mint fraud”

### Story

Because Sierra was an assayer at Potosí, generic internet searches for the mint and fraud can misleadingly place him near accounts of the massive seventeenth-century debasement scandal.

### Evidence

The historic fraud centered on officials including Felipe Ramírez de Arellano and Francisco Gómez de la Rocha in the seventeenth century. It preceded Sierra by more than a century.

### Status

**False association produced by contextual conflation.**

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

For much of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, Sierra’s identity was effectively hidden behind the isolated initial **J**.

Jorge Ferrari’s 1963 attribution to Juan Palomo y Sierra illustrates the extent of the uncertainty. Cunietti-Ferrando’s 1975 study restored José Antonio de Sierra to the record, and later archival work confirmed the identification.

His legacy now rests on several levels:

- **Technical:** he certified the fineness of the 1813 gold and silver issues.
- **Institutional:** his career shows how an imperial technical bureaucracy was reused by revolutionary governments.
- **Political:** his prosecution records the hazards faced by civilian officials serving cities repeatedly captured by opposing armies.
- **Numismatic:** his initial appears on one of the foundational issues of Argentine monetary history.
- **Symbolic:** the 1813 coin supplied imagery reproduced on modern Argentine money.

In 2013, the BCRA issued 154 million commemorative one-peso coins marking the bicentenary of the first patriotic coinage. The center reproduced the 1813 design, including Sierra’s **J**. His initial thus circulated again on a vastly larger scale two centuries after the original issue.

The BCRA’s modern description uses “José Antonio de la Sierra” and calls him supervisor of the coinage. Specialist work favors “José Antonio de Sierra” and the more exact office of Ensayador Mayor.

The 1813 eight-escudo gold piece is now among the best-known rarities of Argentine numismatics. Only a very small number of examples are known, and the BCRA has described the denomination as one of the outstanding pieces in its museum collection. The rarity of the surviving gold issues magnifies public interest in everyone associated with them, including Sierra.

His posthumous patriotic incorporation should remain distinct from the unresolved question of his personal allegiance.

### Manuel Belgrano

**Evidentiary label: documented institutional connection; personal meeting not documented.**

Belgrano commanded the United Provinces army whose campaign secured revolutionary control of Potosí in 1813. Sierra was promoted inside the mint administration on 15 May, while the army’s vanguard occupied the city; Belgrano personally arrived in June.

Belgrano’s military command enabled the revolutionary government to control the industrial and fiscal system in which Sierra served. Sierra certified the coinage ordered by the Assembly during that occupation. Pezzano emphasizes, however, that Sierra’s promotion and preparations for the new coinage began before Belgrano personally entered Potosí. No source consulted documents a face-to-face meeting or correspondence between them.
