# Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi: Research Dossier

**Prepared:** 24 August 2026  
**Subject:** Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi (1804–1851)  
**Scope:** Documentary reconstruction of Jacobi’s life, family, career, intellectual network, mathematics, finances, politics, illness, death, works, reception, and associated myths.  

## Research note and evidentiary conventions

This dossier uses Jacobi’s own publications and collected works; the biographical notice by his friend Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet; Leo Königsberger’s 554-page centenary biography (1904), which drew on correspondence and family material; the *Neue Deutsche Biographie*; the *Dictionary of Scientific Biography*; institutional records; and specialist histories of elliptic functions and Hamilton–Jacobi dynamics. Short modern biographies are used only where they preserve or point toward material in those stronger sources.

Labels used below:

- **Documented:** supported by contemporaneous records, Jacobi’s works or correspondence, or convergent scholarly sources.
- **Reported:** present in a serious secondary source, but not independently verified here against the original document.
- **Disputed / uncertain:** sources disagree or the evidence does not establish the proposition.
- **Anecdote / later tradition:** a story circulated after the event, sometimes without a traceable contemporary source.
- **No evidence found:** the requested category was investigated but yielded no substantiated relevant material.

The spelling **Königsberg** refers to the East Prussian city now called Kaliningrad, Russia. Monetary figures in the consulted sources are incomplete; no speculative modern equivalents are supplied.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and household

Jacobi was the second son in a prosperous, educated Jewish household in Potsdam.

- **Father:** **Simon Jacobi** (1772–1832), born in Beelitz. The *Neue Deutsche Biographie* describes him as a money changer/banker and board member of Potsdam’s Jewish community. His business supplied the family’s initial prosperity and helped make Carl financially independent early in his career.
- **Mother:** **Rachel Jacobi**, née Lehmann (1774–1848), daughter of a *Generalprivilegierter* Lehmann—that is, a member of the small class of specially protected Prussian Jews. Her brother was Jacobi’s first teacher.
- **Maternal uncle:** usually identified simply as **Lehmann** in English biographies. He educated Jacobi at home in languages and elementary mathematics before gymnasium.

The family’s Jewish status mattered legally and professionally in Restoration Prussia. Jacobi nevertheless passed the state teaching examination and was offered a school position in 1825. He converted to Christianity during the transition from student to university lecturer. Sources often infer that conversion removed a professional barrier; they do not preserve a definitive confession by Jacobi that career advancement was his sole or principal motive.

### Siblings

- **Moritz Hermann Jacobi** (1801–1874), later known in Russia as **Boris Semyonovich Jacobi** and ennobled as Moritz Hermann von Jacobi. He became an engineer and physicist in St Petersburg, noted for galvanoplastics/electrotyping and electric motors.
- **Therese Jacobi**, sister. The consulted sources provide little biographical detail.
- **Eduard Jacobi**, younger brother, who continued or assumed the paternal banking business after Simon’s death. The failure of that business destroyed Carl’s inherited capital.

### Marriage and children

Jacobi married **Marie Schwinck** (1809–1901) in Königsberg in **1831**. She was a daughter of a wealthy Königsberg merchant and commercial councillor. Marie accompanied Jacobi and Friedrich Wilhelm Bessel on the 1842 British journey. During Jacobi’s 1849 financial crisis, she and the children relocated to less expensive Gotha while Jacobi remained in Berlin.

The couple had **eight children—five sons and three daughters**. The best documented in general reference sources is:

- **Leonhard Jacobi** (1832–1900), lawyer and later professor in Berlin.

The consulted authoritative summary sources do not consistently enumerate all seven other children. Rather than import uncertain genealogical-tree names, this dossier records that gap explicitly.

No reliable evidence was found of extramarital partners, illegitimate children, or a marital scandal.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1804–1821

Jacobi grew up in Potsdam amid financial comfort and cultural ambition. His maternal uncle taught him before he entered the Potsdam Gymnasium in **November 1816**, shortly before his twelfth birthday. Within months he was promoted to the highest class. He had already met much of the curriculum, but the University of Berlin would not admit him before sixteen; consequently, he remained in the senior class for approximately four years.

This enforced delay became an unusually intensive period of self-education. School reports and later biographies describe outstanding achievement in Greek, Latin, and history. He read Leonhard Euler’s *Introductio in analysin infinitorum* and began private mathematical investigations, including an attempt to solve the general quintic by radicals. The attempt failed—as it had to in the unrestricted case, later shown through the work of Abel and Galois—but it shows that algebraic equations were among his formative problems before university.

Claims that the adolescent Jacobi had already “invented the Jacobian” are retrospective exaggerations. His mature work on functional determinants came later, and Cauchy had used such determinants in 1815 before the object acquired Jacobi’s name.

## Education and Formation, 1821–1826

### University of Berlin

Jacobi entered the new University of Berlin in **1821**. For roughly two years he divided his attention among:

- philosophy;
- classical philology and ancient languages;
- mathematics.

He eventually chose mathematics. German university instruction in advanced mathematics was then thin compared with Paris, so Jacobi largely formed himself through Euler, Lagrange, Laplace, Gauss, and other printed authorities. **Enno Dirksen** was the faculty member most directly associated with his doctorate, but “doctoral adviser” can misleadingly imply a later research-supervision system. Jacobi’s higher mathematical formation was substantially self-directed.

In autumn **1824** he passed the state *Oberlehrer* examination. It qualified him to teach mathematics, Greek, and Latin at all secondary levels and history at lower levels. In 1825 the Joachimsthalsches Gymnasium in Berlin offered him a post.

### Doctorate, habilitation, conversion

Jacobi submitted **_Disquisitiones analyticae de fractionibus simplicibus_** (“Analytical investigations concerning partial fractions”) and received the doctorate in **1825**. He quickly completed the habilitation process and converted from Judaism to evangelical Christianity. In the winter semester **1825–26**, aged twenty, he lectured in Berlin on curves and surfaces in space.

The lecture’s reception brought ministerial advice that his promotion would be quicker at Königsberg. In 1826 he accepted a salaried *Privatdozent* position there. This move—not exile—began the career for which he became known as “Königsberg’s great teacher.”

## Early Career and the Elliptic-Function Breakthrough, 1826–1829

### Appointments

- **1826:** salaried *Privatdozent*, University of Königsberg.
- **1827:** extraordinary professor.
- **1829:** ordinary professor, an exceptionally rapid elevation for a man in his mid-twenties.

### Number theory and Gauss

Soon after arriving, Jacobi informed **Carl Friedrich Gauss** of work on cubic residues. His first paper in that area appeared in 1827. Gauss, reportedly impressed, asked Bessel about the young mathematician and sent Jacobi a letter; the letter and later letters from Gauss to Jacobi are lost, and no sustained correspondence is documented. Gauss remained an intellectual model and occasional critical authority rather than a close mentor.

### Abel, Legendre, and elliptic functions

On **5 August 1827**, Jacobi opened a long correspondence with **Adrien-Marie Legendre**, the senior master of elliptic integrals. At nearly the same moment **Niels Henrik Abel** and Jacobi independently published discoveries that transformed the subject from a theory of elliptic integrals into a theory of their inverse, doubly periodic functions.

Their routes differed:

- Abel began centrally from multiplication and addition questions.
- Jacobi began centrally from transformation theory.
- Both understood that complex arguments and double periodicity were fundamental.

The relation was a priority race, but not adequately described as a personal feud. Abel feared that Jacobi might pre-empt parts of his work and wrote sharply in private; Jacobi credited Abel’s discoveries after Abel’s death. Legendre, initially the revered elder, also complained that Jacobi’s early announcements sometimes gave striking results without proofs.

Jacobi’s synthesis was **_Fundamenta nova theoriae functionum ellipticarum_** (Königsberg, 1829). It systematized transformations, modular equations, inverse functions, infinite products and series, theta-function methods, and number-theoretic applications. It fixed Jacobi’s international standing before he was twenty-five.

### 1829 journey: Göttingen and Paris

During the summer of **1829**, Jacobi traveled west. He visited Gauss in Göttingen and proceeded to Paris, where he met Legendre, **Joseph Fourier**, **Siméon-Denis Poisson**, and other leading mathematicians. This was an important passage between the relatively isolated Prussian university world and the French center of analysis.

Fourier and Jacobi were acquaintances through this visit, not long-term collaborators. Their most famous association is intellectual disagreement: Fourier emphasized usefulness and natural phenomena; in a letter to Legendre dated **2 July 1830**, Jacobi argued that “the unique aim of science is the honor of the human mind” and that a number-theory problem was, on that ground, as worthy as a problem concerning the world system. The original context makes this a defense of pure mathematics, not a claim that mathematics never has applications.

## Königsberg as a Research and Teaching Center, 1829–1842

### Teaching method

Jacobi did not merely present finished theory. He brought students to the frontier and assigned research problems. In a period when mathematics lectures often consisted of exposition from texts, he treated active investigation as trainable practice.

With the physicist **Franz Ernst Neumann**, he established the Königsberg mathematical-physical seminar in **1833**, modeled partly on philological seminars. Students learned through supervised problem solving and original work. Together with the astronomer **Friedrich Wilhelm Bessel**, Jacobi and Neumann created the celebrated “Königsberg school.”

Students and younger mathematicians directly shaped by Jacobi include:

- **Friedrich Julius Richelot**;
- **Ludwig Otto Hesse**;
- **Carl Wilhelm Borchardt**;
- **Johann Georg Rosenhain**;
- **Eduard Heine**;
- **Philipp Ludwig von Seidel**;
- **Gustav Kirchhoff**, who attended the Neumann–Jacobi seminar from 1843 to 1846;
- **Paul Gordan**, whose formal doctoral relationship belongs to the later Berlin legacy of Jacobi’s school.

Later portrayals call Jacobi the most inspiring mathematical teacher of his era. The claim is evaluative, but it rests on concrete institutional innovation and a remarkable student network.

### Work rhythm and personality

Accounts describe extraordinary memory, speed with long formulas, and a capacity to connect remote areas of mathematics. His teaching load could be extreme: the *Dictionary of Scientific Biography* notes ten hours a week on elliptic functions in 1835–36. He was ambitious, self-conscious about priority, socially energetic, and willing to cultivate powerful correspondents. Those traits generated both an unusually productive network and occasional resentment.

### Major research programs

#### Elliptic and theta functions

Jacobi made theta functions a foundation for elliptic-function theory. His notation and functions—later standardized as **sn**, **cn**, and **dn**—remain in use. He studied transformations, modular equations, product and Fourier-series expansions, and addition theorems. His methods connected analysis to arithmetic identities.

#### Abelian functions and inversion

Jacobi generalized the inversion problem from elliptic integrals to Abelian integrals. The complex torus attached to an algebraic curve is now called its **Jacobian variety**; the **Abel–Jacobi map/theorem** commemorates the combined lineage. Riemann and Weierstrass later rebuilt and extended the subject with stronger general foundations.

#### Number theory

Jacobi worked on quadratic, cubic, and higher reciprocity; introduced the **Jacobi symbol**; developed **Jacobi sums**; studied continued fractions; and used theta identities to prove formulas for representations of integers as sums of squares. His four-square identity supplied a proof of Lagrange’s theorem; his formulas also counted representations by two, four, six, and eight squares under specified conditions.

#### Determinants and the “Jacobian”

Jacobi systematized determinants and functional determinants, including what became the **Jacobian determinant**, crucial in changes of variables, differential equations, and geometry. He did **not** originate every precursor: Cauchy used a functional determinant in 1815. The eponym reflects Jacobi’s extensive development and propagation of the theory.

He established identities now called the **Jacobi identity**, the **Desnanot–Jacobi identity**, and related determinant formulas. The Jacobi identity in modern Lie algebra is historically connected to his work on Poisson brackets, though its present abstract formulation is later.

#### Mechanics and differential equations

Jacobi extended **William Rowan Hamilton’s** 1834–35 “general method in dynamics.” Jacobi’s 1836 work on the three-body problem and 1837 paper on first-order partial differential equations assimilated Hamilton’s characteristic function into a broader method of integration. The modern **Hamilton–Jacobi equation** accurately names a sequential contribution: Hamilton supplied the principal-function formulation; Jacobi recast, extended, and taught it as a general analytical method.

Jacobi and Hamilton met during Jacobi’s 1842 British journey. Their relationship is better described as mathematical succession plus personal contact than as a long joint collaboration. Jacobi’s later lectures, published posthumously as *Vorlesungen über Dynamik*, became the main nineteenth-century vehicle for the method.

Other mechanics contributions include the **Jacobi integral** in the restricted three-body problem, the **last multiplier**, canonical methods, geodesics, rotating fluid figures (**Jacobi ellipsoids**), and celestial mechanics.

#### Geometry and numerical algebra

He studied geodesics on an ellipsoid and introduced elliptic coordinates. His 1846 procedure for diagonalizing a real symmetric matrix by successive plane rotations is now the **Jacobi eigenvalue algorithm**. A 1842 theorem on the spherical image of the normal directions of a closed space curve followed a correction by Thomas Clausen to an earlier Jacobi paper—an instructive documented case in which Jacobi’s published work required correction but led to another result.

#### Notation

In 1841 Jacobi reintroduced Legendre’s partial-derivative symbol **∂**, helping establish its standard use. Calling him the “inventor” of ∂ is false; the notation predates him.

## Illness, Financial Crisis, and Italian Convalescence, 1843–1844

In early **1843**, Jacobi became seriously ill. Contemporary and near-contemporary accounts diagnose **diabetes**; some biographical retellings also speak of collapse from overwork. These are compatible but not identical claims: diabetes is the documented medical diagnosis, while overwork was an aggravating biographical interpretation.

Jacobi could not personally finance the prescribed stay in Italy. His inherited capital had been lost in the failure of the paternal banking business after his brother Eduard assumed it, amid a wider commercial depression. **Dirichlet**, after spending roughly two weeks with Jacobi in April 1843, appealed through **Alexander von Humboldt** to King **Friedrich Wilhelm IV**. The king supplied a grant.

Jacobi traveled south with Dirichlet and **Carl Wilhelm Borchardt**; Dirichlet’s wife also traveled with the party. At the scientific meeting in **Lucca**, Jacobi reportedly discovered that few Italian mathematicians had seriously read his papers—a revealing but secondary-source observation. The party arrived in **Rome on 16 November 1843**. **Jakob Steiner** and **Ludwig Schläfli** joined the circle; Schläfli’s linguistic abilities made him useful as interpreter.

Jacobi’s health improved. In the Vatican Library he collated manuscripts of Diophantus’s *Arithmetica*, an episode that shows his continuing classical and historical interests. He resumed publication and returned to Berlin by the end of June 1844.

Because physicians judged the Königsberg climate dangerous, the crown allowed him to move permanently to Berlin as a state pensioner and gave him a salary supplement for the capital’s higher cost and his medical expenses. As a Prussian Academy member he could lecture at the university without being obliged to do so. Poor health limited his teaching.

## Berlin Years, Politics, and the Crisis of 1848–1850

### Royal patronage and liberal activity

Jacobi’s political position does not fit a simple republican/monarchist label. In **1846**, volume I of his *Opuscula mathematica* carried a dedication to Friedrich Wilhelm IV, expressing loyalty and gratitude. During the **1848 Revolution**, however, he participated in Berlin’s liberal **Constitutional Club**, spoke in political debate, and unsuccessfully stood as a candidate associated with a liberal club.

The exact wording of the crucial impromptu speech was not recovered in the consulted primary material. Biographical sources agree that it angered both monarchists and republicans: too liberal for conservative authorities and insufficiently radical for the left. It should not be embellished into a revolutionary manifesto.

### Administrative retaliation and finances

The Ministry of Education rejected Jacobi’s petition for a secure formal association with the University of Berlin. In **June 1849**, the state withdrew his special salary supplement. The sequence strongly supports political disfavor, although the consulted records do not amount to a judicial finding of unlawful retaliation.

Because the failed family bank had already consumed his inherited wealth, the loss of the supplement forced drastic measures:

- Jacobi gave up his Berlin home and lived in an inn.
- Marie and the eight children moved to Gotha, where living costs were lower.
- The family remained divided for roughly two years.

No evidence indicates fraud by Jacobi, hidden investments, corrupt enrichment, or financial crime. He was a creditor/victim of a family-business failure, not a documented perpetrator. The exact value of his inheritance, losses, salary, royal grant, and supplement was not established in the consulted sources.

### Vienna offer and Prussian retention

Late in **1849**, Jacobi received an offer of a professorship in Vienna and accepted it provisionally. The prospect that Prussia would lose its most internationally famous active mathematician caused the government to make concessions. Jacobi’s preference to remain in his native country also mattered; he withdrew his acceptance.

His position improved in 1850. He lectured on number theory in the summer semester, visited his family during vacations, and worked with the Gotha astronomer **Peter Andreas Hansen**. Marie and the children remained in Gotha until the eldest son completed Gymnasium.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Religion

The record supports three propositions:

1. Jacobi was born and raised in a prominent Jewish household.
2. He converted to evangelical Christianity in 1825.
3. The conversion coincided with entry into a Prussian academic career in which Jewish status could be disabling.

It does **not** establish whether his inner motives were religious conviction, assimilation, professional necessity, or a mixture. Later accounts that treat the conversion as purely cynical or purely devotional go beyond the available evidence.

### Pure mathematics and utility

Jacobi’s 1830 letter to Legendre opposing Fourier’s utilitarian emphasis is his clearest intellectual declaration. He asserted the equal dignity of arithmetic and cosmological problems under the “honor of the human mind.” Yet his own career included mechanics, astronomy, rotating fluids, and numerical algorithms. His position was not anti-application; it denied that immediate utility was the sole measure of mathematical worth.

### Rigor

A widely repeated remark has Jacobi say that only Dirichlet—“not I, nor Cauchy, nor Gauss”—knew what a completely rigorous proof was: when Gauss said he had proved something it was very probable, when Cauchy said so one could wager either way, but when Dirichlet said it, it was certain. The statement survives through later testimony and is best treated as a reported classroom/intellectual anecdote, not a verbatim stenographic record. It captures Jacobi’s esteem for Dirichlet and nineteenth-century concerns about proof.

### Politics

Jacobi was a constitutional liberal in 1848, not a professional politician. No evidence was found of party office, underground organization, intelligence work, political violence, or advocacy of terror. His candidacy failed, and his political intervention damaged his patronage without producing an elected career.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet

Close friend, mathematical peer, and practical protector. Dirichlet helped secure the Italian grant, traveled with Jacobi, and delivered the Berlin Academy memorial on **1 July 1852**, calling him the Academy’s greatest mathematician since Lagrange. Their friendship joined two centers of nineteenth-century analysis and number theory.

### Alexander von Humboldt

Patron and correspondent. Correspondence began in **1828** and became regular after 1839; forty-four letters are reported to survive. Humboldt mediated royal assistance and represented the courtly-scientific patronage structure on which Jacobi depended during illness.

### Friedrich Wilhelm Bessel and Franz Neumann

Bessel, the Königsberg astronomer, and Neumann, the physicist, were Jacobi’s institutional partners. Bessel supplied observations and a bridge to astronomy; Neumann co-founded the mathematical-physical seminar. The “Königsberg school” was their joint ecology, not Jacobi’s isolated creation.

### Crelle

**August Leopold Crelle**, engineer, administrator, and founder of the *Journal für die reine und angewandte Mathematik*, provided the principal publication vehicle for Jacobi and Abel. “Crelle’s Journal” was essential infrastructure for the new German mathematical community.

### Legendre, Abel, Gauss, Fourier, and Poisson

- **Legendre:** sustained correspondent and elder interlocutor; praised, criticized, and transmitted Jacobi’s elliptic-function work.
- **Abel:** independent co-founder and priority competitor; no substantial personal meeting is documented.
- **Gauss:** early object of emulation, occasional correspondent, critic of missing proofs; the surviving relationship is thinner than later genealogies sometimes imply.
- **Fourier:** met in Paris in 1829; intellectual foil in Jacobi’s 1830 defense of pure science.
- **Poisson:** met in Paris; Jacobi later emphasized the power of what became known as Poisson’s theorem concerning brackets of integrals of motion.

### Hamilton

Hamilton’s dynamical papers preceded Jacobi’s assimilation and extension. They met in Britain in 1842. The compound name “Hamilton–Jacobi” is historically defensible, but there was no jointly authored theory or long laboratory-style collaboration.

### Galois

There is **no evidence of a personal meeting or correspondence** between Jacobi and **Évariste Galois**, who died in 1832. A draft letter by Galois’s brother Alfred provides slight evidence that Évariste studied Jacobi’s elliptic-function work. Modern editor Peter Neumann warns that the reference may be courtesy rather than proof of deep reading. Claims that Galois’s manuscripts were routinely sent to Jacobi for judgment were not substantiated by the manuscript history consulted: Auguste Chevalier preserved/copied material; Joseph Liouville received it in 1843 and published it in 1846.

The meaningful connection is mathematical: both emerged from the theory of equations and elliptic functions; Jacobi’s transformation/modular work formed part of the environment in which Galois worked; later algebra unified themes neither man expressed in modern group-theoretic language.

### Cayley

**Arthur Cayley** was twenty-nine when Jacobi died. No substantial personal correspondence, collaboration, or meeting was established. Their connection is primarily **posthumous and structural**:

- Cayley’s determinant, matrix, invariant, and algebraic-geometry work developed in a field Jacobi helped transform.
- Cayley used and extended determinant methods, but the familiar square array notation for determinants/matrices and modern matrix algebra belong to Cayley’s own trajectory.
- “Jacobi–Cayley” should not be presented as a documented personal pair analogous to Hamilton–Jacobi.

### Students and editors

Borchardt became both student and friend. After Jacobi’s death, **Alfred Clebsch**, **Carl Wilhelm Borchardt**, and **Karl Weierstrass**, among others, edited lectures, manuscripts, correspondence, and collected works. Jacobi’s posthumous authorial corpus therefore contains layers: authentic manuscripts, student lecture notes, editorial reconstruction, and later compilation.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Institutions

- **University of Berlin:** student, doctorate/habilitation, lecturer (1825–26); later limited lecturer through Academy membership.
- **University of Königsberg:** salaried lecturer, extraordinary professor, ordinary professor (1826–1843/44).
- **Königsberg mathematical-physical seminar:** co-founded with Neumann (1833); a training institution for research.
- **Prussian Academy of Sciences:** member; source of institutional status and posthumous publication.
- **Berlin Constitutional Club:** participant during 1848.
- **British Association for the Advancement of Science:** official Prussian representative at Manchester in 1842.

### Business and property

Jacobi did not operate a mathematical company, seek patents, or commercialize inventions in the modern sense. His material base had three phases:

1. **Family wealth:** early independence through Simon Jacobi’s banking business.
2. **Academic salary and inheritance:** Königsberg professorship plus inherited capital.
3. **Dependence on state patronage:** after the bank failure and illness, royal travel support, pension, and cost-of-living/medical supplement; then severe hardship after the supplement’s withdrawal.

No surviving evidence in the consulted sources establishes significant landholding, securities, patents, royalties, or independent commercial investments by Carl Jacobi.

### Technologies and practical methods

Jacobi’s work was theoretical rather than patented technology. Long-term practical descendants include:

- Jacobian matrices in coordinate transformation, optimization, robotics, statistics, and machine learning;
- Jacobi iteration and rotation algorithms in numerical linear algebra;
- elliptic functions in pendulum motion, mechanics, conformal mapping, and mathematical physics;
- Hamilton–Jacobi equations in optics, mechanics, control theory, and semiclassical physics.

These are legacies, not businesses or technologies monetized by Jacobi.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Failures

### Priority and proof in elliptic functions

**Documented controversy:** Jacobi and Abel independently reached essential features of elliptic functions in 1827. Fast announcements and incomplete proofs produced priority anxiety. Legendre and Gauss criticized Jacobi for initially publishing results without adequate demonstrations. *Fundamenta nova* supplied systematic proofs and exposition.

**Assessment:** neither a plagiarism finding nor fraud. The best-supported account is independent discovery under competitive communication constraints.

### The family bank failure

**Documented:** the banking business inherited/continued by Eduard failed, and Jacobi lost his capital.

**Not documented:** embezzlement, fraud, money laundering, or criminal proceedings by or against Carl Jacobi. Later shorthand that “Jacobi went bankrupt” can blur the distinction between loss through a family business collapse and personal fraudulent insolvency.

### 1848 politics and state retaliation

**Documented sequence:** liberal political speech/activity; hostility from political factions; refusal of a secure Berlin appointment; withdrawal of supplement; near-departure for Vienna.

**Unresolved:** the precise administrative reasoning and exact speech text. The evidence supports political disfavor but not every dramatic retelling of persecution.

### Errors and corrections

Thomas Clausen corrected an error in Jacobi’s 1836 geometric paper. Jacobi responded in 1842 with a striking theorem. This is a normal documented mathematical correction, not misconduct.

### No evidence found

No substantiated record was found of lawsuits, arrest, imprisonment, academic plagiarism findings, sexual scandal, substance addiction, espionage, intelligence connections, political violence, abuse, corruption, or criminal activity.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### “Invert, always invert” (*Man muss immer umkehren*)

1. **Story:** Jacobi supposedly gave “Invert, always invert” as his universal problem-solving maxim.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** common in twentieth- and twenty-first-century mathematical and investment writing; often attributed without a primary citation.
3. **Evidence:** Jacobi certainly used inversion as a central mathematical strategy, especially inverse elliptic functions. The exact aphorism’s contemporary documentary provenance is weak in the sources consulted.
4. **Reputation:** it recasts Jacobi as a patron saint of contrarian reasoning and is frequently transported far outside mathematics.

**Status:** plausible summary of method; exact quotation unverified.

### “One should always generalize” (*Man muss immer generalisieren*)

1. **Story:** Jacobi’s habitual advice was always to generalize.
2. **Circulation:** later quotation collections and mathematical memoir literature.
3. **Evidence:** consistent with his work, but the standard web versions usually cite secondary quotation books rather than a dated letter or lecture transcript.
4. **Reputation:** supports the image of Jacobi as a formal generalizer.

**Status:** reported aphorism, not securely authenticated verbatim.

### “There is no great mathematician in England”

1. **Story:** after visiting Britain in 1842, Jacobi was asked who the greatest English mathematician was and replied, “There is none.”
2. **Circulation:** twentieth-century histories, notably retellings descending through E. T. Bell and Carl Boyer.
3. **Evidence:** no contemporaneous letter or witness account was located. The story is awkward beside Jacobi’s recognition of Hamilton’s work and their meeting.
4. **Reputation:** used to dramatize the perceived weakness of early Victorian British mathematics and Jacobi’s arrogance.

**Status:** later anecdote; not established fact.

### The Dirichlet rigor quotation

1. **Story:** Jacobi ranked Dirichlet above himself, Gauss, and Cauchy in reliability of proof.
2. **Circulation:** histories of Berlin mathematics and quotation collections.
3. **Evidence:** associated with later testimony rather than a Jacobi autograph located here.
4. **Reputation:** reinforces Dirichlet’s status as a founder of modern rigor and shows Jacobi capable of generous comparison.

**Status:** credible reported remark; wording may be polished.

### “The sole aim of science is the honor of the human mind”

Unlike the aphorisms above, this is securely anchored in Jacobi’s **2 July 1830 letter to Legendre**, though English translations vary (“sole,” “unique,” or “real” aim; “mind,” “intellect,” or “human spirit”). The mythic distortion lies not in attribution but in stripping away its polemical context against Fourier’s criterion of utility.

### Born “Carl” versus born “Jacques Simon”

The serious biographical tradition reports **Jacques Simon** as the birth name and Carl Gustav Jacob as the Christian/adult name. Some catalogs normalize him retrospectively as Karl/Carl Gustav Jakob from birth. This is a cataloging simplification, not proof against the family-name account.

### “Inventor of the Jacobian”

The eponym tempts a lone-inventor story. Cauchy used the relevant functional determinant earlier; Jacobi developed it extensively and made it central. The accurate legacy is systematizer and namesake, not uncontested first inventor.

## Works by Jacobi

### Authorship categories

- **Lifetime authored publications:** books, memoirs, and journal articles issued under Jacobi’s name while alive.
- **Posthumous works from manuscripts or lecture notes:** mathematically Jacobi’s, but edited and sometimes reconstructed by others.
- **Collected editions:** editorial compilations, not new works authored in the publication year.
- **Correspondence editions:** Jacobi’s letters are genuine documents; selection, transcription, and annotation are editorial.

### Principal lifetime books and separately issued works

1. **_Disquisitiones analyticae de fractionibus simplicibus_** (Berlin, 1825). Doctoral dissertation on partial fractions.
2. **_Fundamenta nova theoriae functionum ellipticarum_** (Königsberg, 1829). Foundational monograph on the new theory of elliptic functions.
3. **_Commentatio de transformatione integralis duplicis indefiniti in formam simpliciorem_** (1832). Prize memoir on transforming an indefinite double integral.
4. **_Canon arithmeticus, sive tabulae quibus exhibentur pro singulis numeris primis vel primorum potestatibus infra 1000 numeri ad datos indices et indices ad datos numeros pertinentes_** (Berlin, 1839). Extensive arithmetic/index tables.
5. **_Opuscula mathematica_**, 3 vols. (Berlin, 1846–1857). Volumes began during Jacobi’s life; later portions are posthumous. Volume I was dedicated to Friedrich Wilhelm IV.

### Major papers and memoirs (selective but substantial)

Jacobi published scores of articles, principally in *Journal für die reine und angewandte Mathematik* (Crelle’s Journal), *Astronomische Nachrichten*, Academy proceedings, and related venues. A paper-by-paper definitive bibliography is the contents/index apparatus of the eight-volume *Gesammelte Werke*. The following are the major landmarks and recurrently cited papers:

- **“Extraits de deux lettres de M. Jacobi de Königsberg à M. Schumacher”** (1827), early announcements on elliptic functions in *Astronomische Nachrichten*.
- Early papers on **cubic and biquadratic residues** (from 1827).
- Memoirs on transformation and multiplication of elliptic functions (late 1820s–1830s).
- Papers on theta functions, infinite products, series, and sums of squares.
- **“De determinantibus functionalibus”** (1841), a major treatment of functional determinants.
- **“De formatione et proprietatibus determinantium”** (1841), systematic determinant theory.
- **“Über eine neue Methode zur Integration der hyperelliptischen Differentialgleichungen und über die rationale Form ihrer vollständigen algebraischen Integralgleichungen”** and related Abelian-function papers.
- **“Über die Reduction der Integration der partiellen Differentialgleichungen erster Ordnung zwischen irgend einer Zahl Variabeln auf die Integration eines einzigen Systems gewöhnlicher Differentialgleichungen”** (1837), central to Hamilton–Jacobi theory.
- Papers on the three-body problem and celestial mechanics, including the integral now bearing his name (1836 onward).
- Work on the **last multiplier** and analytical dynamics.
- Papers on geodesics and confocal quadrics.
- **“Über ein leichtes Verfahren, die in der Theorie der Säcularstörungen vorkommenden Gleichungen numerisch aufzulösen”** (1846), source of the Jacobi rotation/eigenvalue method.
- **“Über die vierfach periodischen Functionen zweier Variabeln, auf die sich die Theorie der Abelschen Transcendenten stützt”** (1847), on multiply periodic functions and Abelian transcendents.
- Historical essay **“Zur Geschichte der elliptischen und Abelschen Transcendenten”**, composed probably in 1847 and published posthumously.
- Late astronomical work with or connected to Peter Andreas Hansen.

### Posthumous lecture and manuscript publications

1. **_Vorlesungen über Dynamik_**, edited by Alfred Clebsch (Berlin, 1866; later edition by A. Wangerin). Based on Jacobi’s lectures/manuscripts; not a lifetime-authored finished book.
2. **_Vorlesungen über analytische Mechanik: Berlin 1847/48_**, reconstructed from Wilhelm Scheibner’s notes; modern edited publication. Genuine lecture content mediated by a student transcript and later editors.
3. Lectures on elliptic functions, number theory, and related subjects published from manuscripts or student notes in the collected works and later documentary editions.

### Collected works

**_C. G. J. Jacobi’s Gesammelte Werke_**, 8 vols. (Berlin: G. Reimer, 1881–1891), issued at the initiative of the Royal Prussian Academy of Sciences and edited principally by Carl Wilhelm Borchardt and Karl Weierstrass. This is the essential comprehensive corpus. It contains:

- reprints of published papers;
- posthumous manuscripts;
- lecture-derived texts;
- historical notes;
- selected correspondence and editorial material.

Because it is an editorial collection produced thirty to forty years after his death, bibliographic entries should cite both the original publication date and the collected-works volume/page whenever possible.

### Correspondence

Important published or archived bodies include letters with:

- Adrien-Marie Legendre;
- Alexander von Humboldt (44 surviving letters reported);
- Dirichlet;
- Bessel;
- Gauss (Jacobi’s side survives incompletely; Gauss’s letters to Jacobi are reported lost);
- Schumacher;
- Crelle;
- Borchardt;
- Peter Andreas Hansen;
- P. H. von Fuss concerning a projected Euler edition.

No complete modern critical edition of all Jacobi correspondence was located. Letters are dispersed through the *Gesammelte Werke*, Königsberger’s biography, Academy archives, and specialist editions/articles.

### Projected but unrealized works

- A book-length synthesis of his number theory remained unrealized.
- In the 1840s Jacobi designed a detailed plan for an edition of **Euler’s collected works**, corresponding with P. H. von Fuss in St Petersburg. The nineteenth-century project did not reach publication in Jacobi’s lifetime.
- Several lecture cycles and manuscripts were left unfinished or unpublished and were edited after his death.

## Death and Immediate Aftermath

Early in **1851**, after visiting his family in Gotha, Jacobi contracted **influenza**. Before fully recovering, he developed **smallpox** and died within approximately a week on **18 February 1851** in Berlin. Diabetes and years of poor health formed the medical background, but the proximate fatal disease was smallpox following influenza.

He died before reuniting his household in Berlin. Marie survived until 1901.

Dirichlet delivered the Prussian Academy memorial on **1 July 1852**. Jacobi’s manuscripts then entered a long editorial afterlife through students, colleagues, and the Academy.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Mathematical eponyms

The name Jacobi now attaches to a very large set of objects and methods, including:

- Jacobi elliptic functions and Jacobi amplitude;
- Jacobi theta functions and identities;
- Jacobi polynomials;
- Jacobi symbol and Jacobi sums;
- Jacobian matrix, determinant, and variety;
- Jacobi identity;
- Jacobi fields and Jacobi equation in differential geometry;
- Jacobi coordinates;
- Jacobi integral and Jacobi constant;
- Jacobi ellipsoid;
- Jacobi transformation;
- Jacobi method / iteration and Jacobi eigenvalue algorithm;
- Jacobi’s last multiplier;
- Hamilton–Jacobi equation and Hamilton–Jacobi theory;
- Abel–Jacobi map and theorem;
- Desnanot–Jacobi identity and related determinant formulas.

Not every eponym corresponds to sole invention, and some names were assigned by later authors. The density of eponyms reflects both the breadth of his work and nineteenth-century naming practice.

### The Königsberg teaching model

Jacobi’s strongest institutional legacy was the research seminar: advanced students were trained by doing mathematics near the frontier. The Königsberg seminar helped establish a German model later associated with the research university. Through Hesse, Richelot, Borchardt, Heine, Kirchhoff, Seidel, Rosenhain, and others, this pedagogy spread beyond East Prussia.

### From Abel and Jacobi to Riemann and Weierstrass

Jacobi and Abel made inverse elliptic functions and double periodicity central. Riemann’s theory of complex functions and Abelian integrals and Weierstrass’s analytic theory later recast the foundations. Jacobi was sometimes portrayed as a brilliant formal manipulator surpassed in rigor by the next generation; recent histories treat his formalism, computational power, and seminar practice as constitutive of that next generation rather than merely preliminary.

### Algebra and Cayley

Cayley’s matrices, invariants, and algebraic geometry grew in a mathematical world reshaped by Jacobi’s determinant and transformation work. The connection is lineage rather than personal collaboration. Modern presentations sometimes place them together under “classical invariant/determinant theory,” but the dossier finds no basis for inventing a Jacobi–Cayley partnership.

### Mechanics and physics

Hamilton–Jacobi theory became a bridge among classical mechanics, geometrical optics, partial differential equations, and later quantum mechanics. In modern symplectic geometry, optimal control, and semiclassical analysis, Jacobi’s part of the Hamiltonian tradition remains active, though expressed in concepts developed after him.

### Jewish history and assimilation

Older biographies often mentioned Jacobi’s Jewish origin briefly and treated conversion as a career footnote. Recent scholarship has revisited how Prussian emancipation limits, baptism, family communal status, and academic professionalization interacted. The appropriate historical position is neither to erase his Jewish birth nor to claim an undocumented enduring religious identity after conversion.

### Archives, portraits, and screen media

Jacobi’s record is predominantly textual. Portrait engravings and later reproductions survive. Major repositories and documentary access points include:

- Berlin-Brandenburg/Prussian Academy materials and the Academy-sponsored *Gesammelte Werke*;
- Heidelberg University Library’s digitized Königsberger biography;
- Internet Archive scans of the collected works;
- Göttingen digitization of the dissertation and nineteenth-century journals;
- correspondence published in collected works and specialist studies.

No major theatrical film, television biopic, or authoritative feature documentary devoted solely to Jacobi was identified. Short educational internet videos exist, but they generally add no archival evidence and are not included as authorities.

## Books and Major Works About Jacobi

### Primary and near-primary biography

1. **Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet**, “Gedächtnisrede auf Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi” / memorial address, delivered to the Berlin Academy, 1 July 1852. Near-primary testimony by a close friend and peer; laudatory genre.
2. **Leo Königsberger**, **_Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi: Festschrift zur Feier der hundertsten Wiederkehr seines Geburtstages. Mit einem Bildnis und dem Faksimile eines Briefes_** (Leipzig: B. G. Teubner, 1904). The foundational full biography, 554 pages; indispensable but written in a commemorative register half a century after Jacobi’s death.

### Scholarly reference and historical studies

3. **Kurt-R. Biermann**, “Jacobi, Carl Gustav Jacob,” in *Dictionary of Scientific Biography*. Concise, source-based life and technical survey.
4. **Kurt-R. Biermann**, *Die Mathematik und ihre Dozenten an der Berliner Universität 1810–1933* and related studies of Berlin mathematics. Institutional context.
5. **Hans Niels Jahnke**, editor, studies of analysis and nineteenth-century mathematical teaching, including Jacobi’s criticism of Lagrange and the changing role of mathematics in physics.
6. **Michiyo Nakane and Craig G. Fraser**, “The Early History of Hamilton–Jacobi Dynamics, 1834–1837,” *Centaurus* 44 (2002), 161–227. Detailed reconstruction of Hamilton’s and Jacobi’s sequential contributions.
7. **Jean-Pierre Kahane**, “Fourier, Legendre and Jacobi.” Focused study of Jacobi’s French network and the utility/pure-science dispute.
8. **Jeremy Gray**, works on nineteenth-century algebra, complex functions, and geometry, useful for Jacobi’s placement between Abel/Gauss and Riemann/Weierstrass.
9. **Peter M. Neumann**, **_The Mathematical Writings of Évariste Galois_** (European Mathematical Society, 2011). Critical source for the narrow and uncertain Galois–Jacobi link.
10. **Thomas Hawkins**, studies of Lie groups, determinants, and the emergence of modern algebraic structures, including the later meaning of Jacobi identities and brackets.
11. **André Weil**, historical essays on number theory and Abelian functions, placing Jacobi in the Gauss–Abel tradition.
12. **E. T. Bell**, *Men of Mathematics* (1937), chapter pairing Jacobi and Hamilton. Influential popularization but highly anecdotal; should not be used uncritically for quotations or private-life claims.
13. **Carl B. Boyer** and later revisers, *A History of Mathematics*. Broad synthesis; source of several widely repeated anecdotes.
14. **Neue Deutsche Biographie**, “Jacobi, Carl Gustav Jacob.” Authoritative compressed German biographical and genealogical record.
15. **MacTutor History of Mathematics**, “Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi.” Modern secondary narrative with bibliography and quotations; useful orientation rather than final authority.

### Hostile or critical treatments

There is no major evidence-based “hostile biography” comparable to those of political figures. Critical material instead occurs in:

- Abel’s private priority anxieties;
- Legendre’s and Gauss’s criticism of unsupported announcements;
- later contrasts between Jacobi’s formal calculation and Dirichlet/Weierstrass rigor;
- political criticism from both monarchist and republican factions in 1848;
- E. T. Bell’s dramatized personality portrait.

These are perspectives, not findings of misconduct.

## Films, Documentaries, Interviews, and Archival Material

Jacobi died before recorded sound or film. Therefore:

- **Interviews:** none.
- **Contemporary photographs:** portraits reproduced online are commonly engravings, paintings, or later photographic reproductions of artworks; captions should be checked before calling an image a life photograph.
- **Feature films / television:** no significant dedicated production identified.
- **Documentaries / podcasts:** scattered educational episodes and videos exist, but no source-rich canonical documentary was identified in the search.
- **Archival texts:** the digitized Königsberger biography, *Gesammelte Werke*, dissertation, journal papers, and Academy memorial are the principal documentary corpus.

## Material Gaps and Cautions

- A fully enumerated list of all eight children was not established from the strongest consulted sources.
- Exact sums for inheritance, bank losses, salary, royal grant, medical supplement, and Vienna offer remain unverified.
- The exact text and occasion of Jacobi’s controversial 1848 speech require archival confirmation.
- Exact election dates for every European academy were not reconstructed from each academy register.
- Jacobi’s complete article-level bibliography is best obtained from the tables of contents and indexes of the eight-volume *Gesammelte Werke*; later catalogs sometimes merge originals and reprints.
- The Galois link is indirect and slight; the Cayley link is primarily posthumous.
- Famous aphorisms other than the 1830 Legendre letter should not be treated as autograph quotations without further provenance.

## Atlas Connections

### Arthur Cayley

- **[T1+] Documented fact:** Cayley’s *Elementary Treatise on Elliptic Functions* (1876) explicitly presented and developed “Jacobi’s theory.” This is a posthumous intellectual connection: Jacobi is the earlier, written-about figure, not a documented personal acquaintance of Cayley. [MacTutor’s transcription of Cayley’s discussion](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Extras/Cayley_elliptic/)

### Charles Babbage

- **[T5] Documented fact:** During Jacobi and Friedrich Wilhelm Bessel’s 1842 British visit, they met Babbage and questioned him about the proposed Analytical Engine. Babbage’s own account records Jacobi participating in a discussion of how the machine would detect an incorrectly supplied logarithm. [Reproduction of Babbage’s account](https://repository.ias.ac.in/38342/1/13-PUB.pdf)

### René Descartes

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Jacobi delivered a well-attended public lecture about Descartes in Berlin in January 1846. Jacobi is therefore the later writer/speaker discussing the earlier figure. [*Dictionary of Scientific Biography* notice, pp. 4–5](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/DSB/Jacobi.pdf)

### Leonhard Euler

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Jacobi wrote historically about Euler’s role in elliptic-function theory and, during the 1840s, prepared a detailed plan for an edition of Euler’s works in consultation with P. H. von Fuss of the St Petersburg Academy. The proposed comprehensive edition was not realized during Jacobi’s lifetime. [*Dictionary of Scientific Biography* notice, pp. 4–5](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/DSB/Jacobi.pdf)

### Joseph Fourier

- **[T5] Documented fact:** Jacobi met Fourier during his summer 1829 visit to Paris. The sources establish personal acquaintance on that journey, not an extended collaboration. [MacTutor biography](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Jacobi/)
- **[T3] Documented fact:** In his letter to Legendre of 2 July 1830, Jacobi directly engaged Fourier’s utilitarian conception of mathematics, defending the independent value of pure inquiry. This was written engagement with Fourier’s position, although the surviving communication was addressed to Legendre rather than Fourier.

### Évariste Galois

- **[T3] Documented fact, with evidentiary caution:** In his testamentary letter to Auguste Chevalier of 29 May 1832, Galois asked that Jacobi or Gauss be invited to judge the importance of his results. A draft letter by Galois’s brother Alfred also supplies slight evidence that Évariste had studied Jacobi’s work. Peter M. Neumann cautions that the latter reference may have been little more than courtesy; no response, correspondence, or meeting between Jacobi and Galois is documented. [Neumann’s critical discussion](https://ems.press/content/book-chapter-files/20742)

### Carl Friedrich Gauss

- **[T3] Documented fact:** Jacobi wrote to Gauss from Königsberg in 1827 about his work on cubic residues. Gauss responded through Bessel, but Gauss’s letters to Jacobi are lost, and the exchange did not become a regular correspondence. [*Dictionary of Scientific Biography* notice, pp. 1–2](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/DSB/Jacobi.pdf)
- **[T5] Documented fact:** Jacobi visited Gauss in Göttingen while traveling to Paris in the summer of 1829. [MacTutor biography](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Jacobi/)

### Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel

- **[T3] Documented fact:** As a University of Berlin student, Jacobi attended Hegel’s philosophy lectures. Hegel also served on the examining board for Jacobi’s 1825 doctorate, establishing an institutional encounter beyond mere contemporaneity. [*Dictionary of Scientific Biography* notice, p. 1](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/DSB/Jacobi.pdf)

### Alexander von Humboldt

- **[T3] Documented fact:** Jacobi and Humboldt maintained a substantial correspondence beginning in 1828; a modern scholarly edition is devoted to their exchange, and surviving records include letters sent from Königsberg, Rome, Berlin, and Potsdam. [Edition Humboldt correspondence records](https://edition-humboldt.de/chronologie/index.xql?cs=on&jahr=&offset=8551)
- **[T5] Documented fact:** Humboldt became Jacobi’s personal patron and intermediary at the Prussian court. Most consequentially, in 1843 he helped Dirichlet obtain royal funding for Jacobi’s medical journey to Italy; in 1846 he also asked Jacobi for material on ancient Greek mathematics for *Kosmos*. [Jacobi memorial](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Extras/Jacobi_memorial/)

### William Rowan Hamilton

- **[T3] Documented fact:** Jacobi explicitly took Hamilton’s canonical formulation of dynamics as a starting point, extended it in his work on first-order partial differential equations and analytical mechanics, and publicly credited Hamilton during his 1842 British Association address. [Text of Jacobi’s Manchester address](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Extras/Jacobi_BA_lecture/)
- **[T5] Documented fact:** Hamilton and Jacobi met at the British Association meeting in Manchester in 1842. A later contemporary scientific notice described this as apparently their first and only personal meeting. [1902 *Nature* report](https://dbc.wroc.pl/Content/21717/PDF/23807.pdf)

### Joseph-Louis Lagrange

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Jacobi repeatedly analyzed and criticized Lagrange’s foundations of analytical mechanics, while treating Lagrange’s work as a central predecessor to his own investigations. His posthumously edited dynamics lectures preserve this sustained historical and technical engagement. [*Dictionary of Scientific Biography* notice, pp. 3–4](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/DSB/Jacobi.pdf)

### Siméon-Denis Poisson

- **[T5] Documented fact:** Jacobi became personally acquainted with Poisson during his 1829 visit to Paris. [*Dictionary of Scientific Biography* notice, p. 2](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/DSB/Jacobi.pdf)
- **[T3] Documented fact:** Jacobi engaged Poisson’s published mechanics, particularly bracket relations and the French canonical tradition, in developing his own analytical dynamics. This establishes written intellectual engagement, but no surviving Jacobi–Poisson correspondence was identified. [*Dictionary of Scientific Biography* notice, pp. 3–4](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/DSB/Jacobi.pdf)

No additional roster member was included solely because of shared chronology, general mathematical influence, later use of a Jacobi eponym, or unverified attendance at the same institution or event.
