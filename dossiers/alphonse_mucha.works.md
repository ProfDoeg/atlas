## Works by Alphonse Mucha

### Early drawings, paintings, and studies

- **c. 1868:** *Crucifixion*. An early drawing, catalogued by the Mucha Foundation as the earliest surviving work attributed to Mucha.
- **c. 1880:** *Portrait of Mucha’s Sister, Angela*. A painted family portrait.
- **1882:** *Fantaz*. A magazine-cover design rather than an autonomous painting.
- **1882:** *Diploma of Ivančice*. A mock ceremonial diploma honouring a local celebrity, incorporating an illustration inspired by Karel Hynek Mácha’s poem *May*; it is both graphic design and a socially performative document.
- **c. 1885:** *Portrait of Mucha’s Sister, Anna*. A painted family portrait.
- **1885:** *Krokodil*. A design for a magazine masthead or cover heading.
- **1885–1887:** *Study of a Male Nude*. An academic figure study.
- **c. 1887–1888:** *The Devil and Mr Twardowsky*. A study for an illustration to the Faust-related *Tale of Pan Twardowsky*, not necessarily a finished independent work.
- **1889:** *In the Desert*. A study for an illustration in the Czech magazine *Světozor*.
- **1890:** *Page’s Costume*. A study made for *Le Costume au Théâtre*.
- **1891:** *At the Sight of the Two Storks the Owl Rose*. A study for an illustration in Xavier Marmier’s *Contes de grand’mères*; its title derives from the illustrated episode rather than an autonomous title supplied by Mucha.

### Illustrated books, portfolios, and graphic Gesamtkunstwerke

- **1892:** *Scènes et épisodes de l’histoire d’Allemagne*. Charles Seignobos’s history book with illustrations by Mucha; authorship is divided between Seignobos’s text and Mucha’s images.
- **1893:** *Les Aventures de Gil Blas*. An illustrated edition for which Mucha supplied drawings; the underlying literary work is not his.
- **1894:** *Adamites*. An illustrated literary publication rather than a text authored by Mucha.
- **1896:** *La Dame aux Camélias: special programme*. A coordinated cover-and-interior programme design associated with Sarah Bernhardt’s production.
- **1897:** *Ilsée, princesse de Tripoli*. Robert de Flers’s book, transformed by Mucha’s extensive lithographic illustration and page design into a collaborative book-object.
- **1897:** *L’Année qui vient*. A calendar or almanac whose graphic identity and ornament were designed by Mucha.
- **1898:** *Documents décoratifs*. A portfolio of seventy-two plates offering figures, botanical forms, ornaments, jewellery, furniture, tableware, and other patterns. It is best understood as a protocol for applied artists and craftsmen as well as a body of graphic work.
- **1899:** *Le Pater*. An illustrated and ornamented interpretation of the Lord’s Prayer issued in a small edition. Text, image, typography, symbolism, and book design operate together, making it a Gesamtkunstwerk rather than merely an illustrated book.
- **1902:** *Combinaisons ornementales*. A pattern portfolio produced with Maurice Verneuil and Georges Auriol; Mucha’s individual contribution belongs to a collaborative design manual.
- **1902:** *Figures décoratives*. A portfolio of forty figure studies intended as practical models for artists and designers—a visual protocol rather than a conventional narrative book.
- **1906:** *The New York Daily News: Mucha supplement*. A newspaper supplement conceived as a presentation of Mucha’s art; individual authorship is complicated by its editorial and reproductive production.
- **1922:** *Svatý Václav*. A book project concerning Saint Wenceslas, with graphic and illustrative work by Mucha; it remains a collaborative publication rather than a text wholly authored by him.
- **1927:** *Světová revoluce*. Tomáš Garrigue Masaryk’s book with cover and decorative design by Mucha; only its visual apparatus is Mucha’s obra.

### Posters for theatre and performance

- **1894:** *Gismonda*. A colour lithographic poster for Sarah Bernhardt at the Théâtre de la Renaissance; its elongated format helped establish Mucha’s public style.
- **c. 1895:** *Théâtre de la Renaissance*. A poster design advertising Bernhardt’s theatre.
- **1895:** *Amants*. A theatrical poster for a production starring Sarah Bernhardt.
- **1896:** *La Dame aux Camélias*. A colour lithographic poster for Bernhardt’s production.
- **1896:** *Lorenzaccio*. A theatrical poster presenting Bernhardt in the title role.
- **1897:** *La Samaritaine*. A poster for Sarah Bernhardt’s production.
- **1897:** *Sarah Bernhardt as La Princesse lointaine*. A poster made for *La Plume*, turning a theatrical characterization into an art-periodical image.
- **1898:** *Médée*. A theatrical poster for Bernhardt; the prominently depicted snake bracelet also generated a related jewellery object.
- **1899:** *Hamlet*. A theatrical poster representing Sarah Bernhardt in the title role.
- **1908:** *The Woman of a Thousand Faces*. A poster for the American performer and impersonator Cissy Loftus.
- **1909:** *Maude Adams as Joan of Arc*. A theatrical poster and idealized performer portrait.
- **1911:** *Princezna Hyacinta*. A poster for Oskar Nedbal and Ladislav Novák’s ballet-pantomime, also functioning as a portrait of actress Andula Sedláčková.

### Advertising and exhibition posters

- **1895:** *Luchon*. A lithographic travel advertisement for the French resort and railway destination.
- **1895:** *Judgement of Paris*. A calendar image for the Vieillemard printing company; commercial calendar, mythological scene, and display print overlap.
- **1896:** *Imprimerie Cassan Fils*. A printer’s advertising poster.
- **1896:** *Biscuits Champagne Lefèvre-Utile*. A food-advertising poster.
- **1896:** *Salon des Cent, 20e exposition*. An exhibition poster that was also marketed as an art print.
- **1896:** *JOB*. A cigarette-paper advertisement; Mucha subsequently produced a second distinct JOB poster in 1898.
- **1896:** *Champagne Ruinart*. A champagne advertisement.
- **1896:** *Lance Parfum Rodo*. A perfume advertisement.
- **1896–1897:** *L’Illustration: Christmas number*. A magazine-cover design occupying the border between periodical illustration and poster art.
- **1897:** *Chocolat Idéal*. A chocolate advertisement.
- **1897:** *Bières de la Meuse*. A beer advertisement.
- **1897:** *Monaco–Monte-Carlo*. A PLM railway and resort poster.
- **1897:** *Nestlé’s Food for Infants*. A commercial poster.
- **1897:** *Hommage respectueux de Nestlé*. A commemorative advertising image for Queen Victoria’s Diamond Jubilee.
- **1897:** *Salon des Cent: Exposition Mucha*. A poster for Mucha’s own exhibition.
- **1897:** *Société populaire des beaux-arts*. An exhibition or arts-organization poster.
- **1897:** *F. Guillot-Pelletier, Orléans*. A commercial calendar.
- **1897:** *La Trappistine*. A liqueur advertisement.
- **1897:** *Biscuits Lefèvre-Utile*. A separate advertising design for the biscuit manufacturer.
- **1897:** *Savonnerie de Bagnolet*. A soap advertisement.
- **1898:** *JOB*. Mucha’s second major poster for the cigarette-paper brand, distinct from the 1896 design.
- **1898:** *Waverley Cycles*. A bicycle advertisement.
- **1898:** *Bénédictine*. An advertisement for the liqueur.
- **1898:** *Au Quartier Latin*. A poster for the illustrated periodical.
- **1898:** *Letem českým světem*. A poster or cover design for the Czech illustrated publication.
- **1899:** *Moët & Chandon: Crémant Impérial*. A champagne advertisement.
- **1899:** *Moët & Chandon: White Star*. A companion champagne advertisement.
- **1901:** *Heidsieck & Co.* A champagne advertisement.
- **1902:** *Cycles Perfecta*. A bicycle advertisement.
- **1911:** *Moravian Teachers’ Choir*. A poster for a musical ensemble.
- **1912:** *Sixth Sokol Festival*. A poster for the Prague Sokol gathering, combining publicity with a nationalist civic image.
- **1913:** *Lottery of the Union of Southwestern Moravia*. A fundraising or lottery poster.
- **1914:** *Regional Exhibition at Ivančice*. An exhibition poster for Mucha’s birthplace.
- **1918:** *Insurance Bank Slavia*. A commercial poster whose personification of Slavia also carries national symbolism.
- **1928:** *Tenth Anniversary of the Republic of Czechoslovakia*. A commemorative poster acting as civic manifestation as much as advertisement.

### Decorative panels and serial allegories

- **1896:** *The Seasons*. A four-panel decorative series—*Spring*, *Summer*, *Autumn*, and *Winter*—issued without advertising text for domestic display.
- **1896:** *The Seasons, screen-format variation*. A rearranged manifestation of the 1896 series; it is not simply another impression of the original configuration.
- **1896:** *Zodiac*. A decorative panel first designed for a calendar and subsequently issued in other commercial forms, so no single use exhausts the work.
- **1897:** *Fruit and Flower*. A pair of decorative panels.
- **1897:** *Byzantine Heads*. A paired decorative work comprising *The Blonde* and *The Brunette*.
- **1897:** *The Times of the Day*. A four-panel cycle comprising *Morning Awakening*, *Brightness of Day*, *Evening Contemplation*, and *Night’s Rest*.
- **1897:** *The Months*. A calendar cycle; its status lies between functional calendar and decorative print series.
- **1898:** *Rêverie*. A decorative image initially associated with the Champenois printing firm’s calendar, later circulated independently.
- **1898:** *The Arts*. A four-panel cycle comprising *Dance*, *Painting*, *Poetry*, and *Music*.
- **1898:** *The Flowers*. A four-panel cycle comprising *Carnation*, *Iris*, *Lily*, and *Rose*.
- **1899:** *The Precious Stones*. A four-panel cycle comprising *Amethyst*, *Emerald*, *Ruby*, and *Topaz*.
- **1900:** *The Seasons*. A second, compositionally distinct four-panel treatment of the seasons.
- **1902:** *The Stars*. A four-panel cycle comprising *The Morning Star*, *The Evening Star*, *The Moon*, and *The Pole Star*.
- **1911:** *The Moon and the Stars*. A decorative celestial cycle closely related to Mucha’s earlier serial personifications; titles and configurations vary between catalogues and editions.

### Painting cycles, murals, and architectural Gesamtkunstwerke

- **1899–1900:** *Bosnia-Herzegovina Pavilion*. Mucha’s historical mural and interior-decoration scheme for the 1900 Paris Exposition. Because architecture, painting, ornament, exhibits, and imperial representation were integrated, it is properly a commissioned Gesamtkunstwerk; only fragments of the original decoration survive.
- **1900:** *Austria Pavilion*. Decorative contributions for the Paris Exposition; these formed part of a larger collaborative building and should not be treated as a wholly independent Mucha structure.
- **1900:** *Boutique Fouquet*. The interior, façade, furniture, lighting, ornament, and display environment designed for jeweller Georges Fouquet. It is a Gesamtkunstwerk realized with craftsmen and later reconstructed in the Musée Carnavalet, so “the work” cannot be reduced to a single surviving original object.
- **1910–1911:** *Lord Mayor’s Hall, Municipal House, Prague*. An integrated civic interior comprising ceiling and wall paintings, decorative designs, furnishings, and architectural ornament; its program centres on Slav unity and Czech civic identity.
- **1910–1911:** *The Slavonic Concord*. The central ceiling painting for the Lord Mayor’s Hall, accompanied by surviving studies.
- **1910–1911:** *The Eight Civic Virtues*. A mural cycle for the Lord Mayor’s Hall; it functions within the room’s larger Gesamtkunstwerk rather than as a set of independent easel paintings.
- **1912:** *The Slavs in Their Original Homeland: Between the Turanian Whip and the Sword of the Goths*. Canvas no. 1 of *The Slav Epic*.
- **1912:** *The Celebration of Svantovít: When Gods Are at War, Salvation Is in the Arts*. Canvas no. 2 of *The Slav Epic*.
- **1912:** *Introduction of the Slavonic Liturgy: Praise God in Your Mother Tongue*. Canvas no. 3 of *The Slav Epic*.
- **1914:** *The Sacrifice at Szigetvár by Nikola Zrinski: The Shield of Christendom*. Canvas no. 14 of *The Slav Epic*.
- **1914:** *The Brethren School in Ivančice: The Printing of the Czech Bible—God Gave Us the Gift of Language*. Canvas no. 15 of *The Slav Epic*.
- **1914:** *The Abolition of Serfdom in Russia: To Work in Freedom Is the Foundation of a State*. Canvas no. 19 of *The Slav Epic*.
- **1916:** *Jan Milíč of Kroměříž: The Magic of the Word—A Brothel Converted into a Convent*. Canvas no. 7 of *The Slav Epic*.
- **1916:** *Master Jan Hus Preaching at the Bethlehem Chapel: The Magic of the Word—Truth Prevails*. Canvas no. 9 of *The Slav Epic*.
- **1916:** *The Meeting at Křížky: The Magic of Words—Sub utraque*. Canvas no. 10 of *The Slav Epic*.
- **1918:** *Petr Chelčický at Vodňany: Do Not Repay Evil with Evil*. Canvas no. 12 of *The Slav Epic*.
- **1918:** *Jan Amos Komenský: A Glimmer of Hope*. Canvas no. 16 of *The Slav Epic*.
- **1923:** *Tsar Simeon of Bulgaria: The Dawn of Slav Literature*. Canvas no. 4 of *The Slav Epic*.
- **1923:** *Tsar Štěpán Dušan: The Slavic Code of Law*. Canvas no. 6 of *The Slav Epic*.
- **1923:** *After the Battle of Vítkov: God Is Found in Truth, Not Force*. Canvas no. 11 of *The Slav Epic*.
- **1923:** *The Hussite King Jiří of Poděbrady: Treaties Are to Be Respected*. Canvas no. 13 of *The Slav Epic*.
- **1924:** *The Bohemian King Přemysl Otakar II: The Union of Slav Dynasties*. Canvas no. 5 of *The Slav Epic*.
- **1924:** *After the Battle of Grunewald: The Solidarity of the Northern Slavs*. Canvas no. 8 of *The Slav Epic*.
- **1926:** *Holy Mount Athos: Vatican of Orthodox Christianity, Sheltering the Oldest Slav Literary Monuments*. Canvas no. 17 of *The Slav Epic*.
- **1926, unfinished:** *Oath of the “Youth” under the Slav Linden Tree: The Slav Revival*. Canvas no. 18 of *The Slav Epic*. The Mucha Foundation explicitly catalogues it as unfinished, so the project—not a presumed completed state—is the work.
- **1926:** *Apotheosis: Slavs for Humanity—Four Stages of Slav History in Four Colours*. Canvas no. 20 and the culmination of *The Slav Epic*.
- **1911–1926:** *The Slav Epic*. The project and completed twenty-canvas cycle are also a single obra: a monument conceived to narrate Slav history across media-scale painting, historical research, staged photography, exhibition, and civic gift.
- **c. 1923:** *Song of Bohemia*. A symbolic painting; catalogues have also assigned variants of the date, so “c. 1923” should not be hardened into an exact year.
- **1925:** *Portrait of Jiří Mucha*. A painted portrait of the artist’s son.
- **1925:** *Portrait of Jaroslava Mucha*. A painted portrait of the artist’s daughter.
- **1931:** *Stained-glass window for St Vitus Cathedral*. A monumental stained-glass composition for Archbishop’s Chapel in Prague Cathedral, designed by Mucha and executed through specialist glass manufacture.
- **1934:** *Three Ages*. A triptych project—*The Age of Reason*, *The Age of Wisdom*, and *The Age of Love*—left unfinished. It resists classification as a completed painting cycle: its surviving studies and cartoons manifest an unrealized philosophical project.

### Jewellery, objects, packaging, and national design

- **1895:** *Diadem for La Princesse lointaine*. A theatrical head ornament designed for Sarah Bernhardt; manufacture and performance complicate singular authorship.
- **1898–1899:** *Snake bracelet and ring*. A jewellery design developed from the serpent in the *Médée* poster and executed by Georges Fouquet; the designed obra and the jeweller’s manufactured object are related but not identical acts of making.
- **1899–1901:** *Jewellery designed for Georges Fouquet*. A body of pendants, necklaces, brooches, chains, rings, and combs designed by Mucha and materially executed in Fouquet’s workshop. Individual pieces are collaborative objects, not paintings translated into metal.
- **1896 onward:** *Lefèvre-Utile packaging and brand designs*. A body of biscuit boxes, labels, wrappers, calendars, and promotional graphics. It resists reduction to a single work because designs were repeatedly adapted across packages and editions.
- **1896 onward:** *Moët & Chandon packaging and menu designs*. A body of labels, menus, postcards, and promotional material extending beyond the named posters.
- **1900:** *Fouquet display objects and shop furnishings*. Cases, furniture, sculpture, lighting, and ornament designed as constituents of the Boutique Fouquet interior; separating them into autonomous works partly falsifies their intended unity.
- **1918:** *First Czechoslovak postage stamps*. Designs for the Hradčany stamp issue. These are state instruments and mass manifestations rather than singular art objects.
- **1919:** *Czechoslovak banknotes*. Mucha designed denominations and ornamental systems for the new state; individual notes exist in numerous proofs and issues, making the design program more stable than any one printed specimen.
- **1918–1931:** *Czechoslovak state documents and insignia*. A body of stamps, banknotes, bonds, revenue papers, certificates, and official graphics. Authorship is sometimes shared with engravers, printers, and state authorities, and catalogues differ over whether every adaptation counts as a separate Mucha work.
- **1920s:** *Slavia insurance documents and securities*. A group of commercial and financial designs using Mucha’s Slavia imagery; they are operative documents as well as graphic art.

### Photography and staged source-images

- **early 1880s–1930s:** *Photographic œuvre*. Roughly 4,000 photographs survive in the Mucha Trust archive, including portraits, landscapes, documentary images, family photographs, and working studies. Many have no fixed authorial title, and some resist the status of finished works because they were working instruments, records, or collaborative sittings rather than exhibition photographs.
- **early 1890s:** *Self-portrait in a Russian shirt in the Rue de la Grande-Chaumière studio*. A staged photographic self-image.
- **1892:** *Self-portrait in the Rue de la Grande-Chaumière studio*. A photographic studio portrait.
- **1893:** *Paul Gauguin posing in Mucha’s studio*. A photograph by Mucha documenting and staging a fellow artist.
- **c. 1893–1894:** *Mucha with friends in his studio*. A group photograph whose authorship may involve an unidentified person operating Mucha’s camera; possession of the negative does not by itself settle who released the shutter.
- **c. 1893–1894:** *Paul Gauguin playing Mucha’s harmonium*. A staged studio photograph. The Foundation’s catalogue has elsewhere displayed an evident “1893–1994” typographical range; the accepted context is the early 1890s, but an exact date should not be invented.
- **1894:** *Funeral procession of President Sadi Carnot*. Documentary photographs of the procession; the catalogue contains more than one image under the same descriptive title.
- **c. 1896:** *Model posing with a silk hat*. A studio photograph used as working material.
- **c. 1896:** *Self-portrait with a silk hat*. A staged studio photograph.
- **c. 1897:** *Mucha’s studio decorated with his collection of objets d’art*. A photographic record of the studio as a constructed artistic environment.
- **c. 1898:** *Amilcare Cipriani in Mucha’s studio*. A photographic portrait of the Italian revolutionary.
- **1910–1926:** *Photographic studies for The Slav Epic*. A body of posed figures, costumes, gestures, landscapes, and documentary observations used in constructing the canvases. These images resist being called autonomous works, but excluding them would conceal a central part of Mucha’s making process.

### Sculpture and unrealized projects

- **1890s–1900s:** *Sculptural models and decorative reliefs*. A small body of modeled heads, figures, and relief elements connected with jewellery and interior commissions. Mucha often designed while specialist makers executed the durable object, so attribution must distinguish conception from fabrication.
- **1900:** *La Nature*. A sculptural bust conceived by Mucha and executed in collaboration with the sculptor Auguste Seysses. It should not be assigned to Mucha alone without acknowledging Seysses.
- **1900s:** *Woman on a Rock*. A modeled sculptural composition associated with Mucha’s decorative and jewellery practice; versions and materials vary.
- **1930s, unfinished:** *Monument to the Slavs*. A late monumental project known through designs rather than a completed monument. Its resistance to object-status is essential: the intention and surviving project documents are the obra.
- **1930s, unrealized:** *Pavilion or temple for The Slav Epic*. Mucha envisioned a permanent architectural setting for the cycle, but it was not realized. It is therefore a project rather than a lost building.

### Writings, lectures, and manifestos of artistic purpose

- **c. 1900–1930s:** *Lectures on Art*. A body of lecture notes and diagrams later edited and published posthumously. Their boundaries and final authorial form are uncertain: they document an evolving pedagogical protocol rather than a book completed for publication by Mucha.
- **1899:** *Le Pater: explanatory commentary*. Mucha’s prose explanations form part of the book’s symbolic program. They describe the images but also act as a spiritual-artistic manifesto.
- **1928:** *Presentation of The Slav Epic to the City of Prague*. The gift is not a conventional art object, yet it is a civic act that completed the project’s intended public function; the cycle was formally presented by Mucha and Charles Richard Crane.
- **1930s:** *Statements and plans for Three Ages*. Surviving notes and studies articulate a late humanist project. They do not constitute a securely finalized manifesto, but they record an intention whose incomplete state is part of the work.

- Mucha Foundation, [Mucha at a Glance](https://www.muchafoundation.org/en/gallery/mucha-at-a-glance-46). Institutional chronology and account of Mucha’s poster, decorative-design, Paris Exposition, and *Slav Epic* projects.
- Mucha Foundation, [Bibliography](https://www.muchafoundation.org/en/about/bibliography). Bibliographic guide identifying the standard catalogues and specialist studies used to delimit the œuvre.
- Ann Bridges, ed., with Jiří Mucha, Marina Henderson, and Anna Dvořák, *Alphonse Mucha: The Complete Graphic Works* (London: Academy Editions, 1991). The standard collected catalogue for Mucha’s graphic production; it does not purport to catalogue every painting, photograph, interior, or unrealized project.
- Jack Rennert and Alain Weill, *Alphonse Mucha: The Complete Posters and Panels* (Uppsala: Hjert & Hjert, 1984). Catalogue raisonné for posters and decorative panels, including variant states and commercial adaptations.
- Alain Weill, *Alphonse Mucha: The Complete Postcards* (Uppsala: Hjert & Hjert, 1983). Specialist catalogue for the postcard corpus; postcard manifestations are numerous and often reproduce or adapt other designs, so they are not silently counted here as new autonomous compositions.
- Graham Ovenden, *Alphonse Mucha Photographs* (London: Academy Editions; New York: St. Martin’s Press, 1974). Foundational catalogue and study of Mucha’s photographic practice.
- Josef Moucha, *Alfons Mucha: Foto Torst 3* (Prague: Torst, 2005). Specialist study of the photographic archive and the uncertain boundary between finished photograph, private record, and working study.
- Alphonse Maria Mucha, *Lectures on Art: A Supplement to the Graphic Work of Alphonse Mucha* (London: Academy Editions; New York: St. Martin’s Press, 1975). Posthumously edited source for Mucha’s artistic teaching; its editorial status prevents treating it uncritically as a finished lifetime book.
- Gabriel Mourey, *The Art Nouveau Style Book of Alphonse Mucha: All 72 Plates from Documents décoratifs* (New York: Dover, 1980). Complete reproduction and identification of the *Documents décoratifs* portfolio.
- Anna Dvořák, *Mucha’s Figures décoratives: 40 Plates by Alphonse Mucha* (New York: Dover, 1981). Complete reproduction and identification of the *Figures décoratives* portfolio.
- Sarah Mucha, ed., with Ronald F. Lipp, Victor Arwas, Anna Dvořák, Jan Mlčoch, and Petr Wittlich, *Alphonse Mucha* (London: Mucha Limited, 2000). Multi-author scholarly survey used for work types beyond the graphic catalogues.
