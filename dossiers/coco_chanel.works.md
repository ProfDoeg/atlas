## Works by Gabrielle Bonheur "Coco" Chanel

### Fashion obra: garments and collections

- **1910–1971:** *The Chanel fashion oeuvre*. An open-ended body of hats, couture garments, sportswear, knitwear, evening wear and accessories rather than a finite series of individually titled works. No catalogue raisonné exists, many early pieces are lost, and surviving garments are normally identified by object type, season or museum accession number; moreover, “Chanel” can mean Gabrielle Chanel’s design, the collective labour of her ateliers, or merely an attribution to the House. [S1][S2][S3]

- **1910–1912:** *Chanel hats*. Millinery was Chanel’s first commercial design practice: she opened an independent shop at 21 rue Cambon in 1910, and contemporary American reporting mentioned her hats by 1912. These were changing models rather than a single titled obra, and few securely documented early examples survive. [S2][S4]

- **1912–1915:** *Deauville sportswear*. A project comprising marinières, sweaters, blouses, jackets and relaxed seaside clothes, subsequently extended at Biarritz. It is best understood as an evolving design system for bodily freedom, not as one work with a fixed title or form. [S2][S4]

- **1916:** *Marinière blouse*. One of the earliest surviving Chanel garments, a cream silk-jersey blouse with sailor collar; its generic museum title describes the object rather than supplying an authorial title. [S3][S5]

- **ca. 1919:** *Dress*. A belted black silk-jersey day dress preserved by the V&A and presented in its retrospective as an early surviving instance of Chanel’s reduction of dress to soft, functional construction. “Dress” is a catalogue description, not a fixed title. [S3][S5]

- **1922:** *Afternoon ensemble from the “Russian” collection*. A blouse-and-skirt ensemble covered with Kitmir machine chain-stitch embroidery, now in the Metropolitan Museum of Art. The object embodies collaboration: Chanel directed the design, while Russian émigré workshops executed its embroidery. [S6][S7]

- **ca. 1922:** *Day dress*. A surviving dress catalogued by the Kunstgewerbemuseum and exhibited in *Picasso/Chanel*; it is an individual material object but has no distinctive authorial title. [S8]

- **ca. 1924:** *Dress*. A surviving Chanel dress in the Metropolitan Museum’s Costume Institute, identified by house, designer, object type and approximate date rather than by a unique title. [S7]

- **1926:** *Model 817 (“Ford” dress / little black dress)*. A simple black crêpe-de-chine day dress published by American *Vogue* and compared there to the universally available Ford automobile. “Little black dress” names a reproducible type and continuing Chanel project, not one stable object; numerous later Chanel dresses belong to the type without being copies of Model 817. [S2][S7]

- **1926–1927:** *Evening dress*. A black silk evening dress attributed by the Metropolitan Museum to both Gabrielle Chanel and the House of Chanel. The museum’s qualified attribution should not be silently converted into certain sole authorship. [S7]

- **ca. 1927:** *Day ensemble*. A black-and-ivory silk-charmeuse jacket, blouse and skirt in the Metropolitan Museum. Its title is classificatory, and the finished ensemble was necessarily the product of Chanel’s couture atelier as well as her direction. [S9]

- **ca. 1927:** *Day ensemble*. A black wool-jersey and satin dress with jacket and scarf in the Metropolitan Museum, distinct from the similarly dated silk-charmeuse ensemble despite their identical catalogue titles. [S9]

- **ca. 1927:** *Theater coat*. A white-and-black ombré-silk coat in the Metropolitan Museum; “theater coat” describes use and object type, not an authorial literary title. [S9]

- **ca. 1927:** *Coat*. A surviving Chanel coat catalogued by the Metropolitan Museum. Its generic name and approximate date exemplify why Chanel’s fashion oeuvre cannot honestly be converted into a neat list of uniquely titled works. [S7]

- **ca. 1927:** *Ensemble*. A surviving Chanel ensemble catalogued by the Metropolitan Museum; it is separate from the day ensembles above, although the catalogue language does little to individuate it outside its accession record. [S7]

- **1929:** *Ensemble*. A Chanel ensemble preserved by the Metropolitan Museum and dated to 1929. As with most couture, the museum object is one manifestation of a model that may originally have been made for more than one client. [S7]

- **1930–1935:** *Overblouse*. A surviving Chanel overblouse in the Metropolitan Museum. Its broad date range records the limits of present knowledge rather than a date to be “corrected.” [S7]

- **1936:** *Evening ensemble*. A surviving Chanel evening ensemble in the Metropolitan Museum; its soft decoration complicates the reductive identification of Chanel solely with austere daywear. [S7]

- **Autumn–winter 1938–1939:** *Evening ensemble*. A documented seasonal couture ensemble in the Metropolitan Museum. It belongs to a collection and atelier system, so “made by Chanel” denotes creative direction rather than fabrication by one pair of hands. [S7]

- **1930s:** *Watteau-derived suit*. A women’s suit reworked from the costume Chanel wore to a masquerade ball as a figure from a Watteau painting. The Metropolitan Museum records the transformation, but the surviving suit lacks a secure independent authorial title. [S7]

- **1954:** *Comeback couture collection*. Chanel’s reopening collection, shown on 5 February, was a project to reassert her pre-war principles against the corseted post-war silhouette. Contemporary reception was divided, and its many looks—not the collection as one material object—constitute the obra. [S1][S2][S10]

- **1954–1971:** *The Chanel suit*. A continuing family of cardigan-like jackets and coordinated skirts, refined through repeated fittings, materials and seasons rather than one invention with one date. The trimmed tweed version entered Chanel’s collections in 1956, but surviving examples must be dated and catalogued separately. [S2][S7][S10]

- **1956:** *Trimmed tweed suit*. The emblematic braided tweed suit introduced after Chanel’s return to couture. It is a design protocol repeatedly manifested in different textiles and colours, not a single unique garment. [S10]

- **Spring–summer 1971:** *Final collection*. Chanel worked on this couture collection before her death on 10 January; it was completed and presented posthumously by the House. It therefore resists attribution as either wholly finished by Chanel or wholly posthumous. [S3][S11]

### Jewellery and wearable objects

- **1922:** *Jewellery for Antigone*. Diadems set with semi-precious stones for the principal characters have been described as Chanel’s first jewellery designs. None of the original costumes survives, so the project is known through documentation rather than a complete extant object group. [S8]

- **1920s–1971:** *Costume-jewellery oeuvre*. Necklaces, cuffs, brooches, chains, crosses and other ornaments combining imitation stones, glass, gilt metal and pearls form a continuing body of work rather than a closed titled collection. Workshop authorship is essential: many post-war pieces associated with Chanel were materially designed or made by Robert Goossens and other specialist makers. [S1][S2][S12]

- **1932:** *Bijoux de Diamants*. A high-jewellery collection presented at Chanel’s Paris residence from 7 to 19 November, with astronomical motifs including comets, stars and fringes. Nearly all the jewels were subsequently dismantled; surviving drawings, photographs and one complete diamond comet preserve manifestations of a largely lost collection whose exact original item count is not secure. [S2][S13]

### Handbags, footwear and accessories

- **1955:** *2.55 handbag*. A quilted shoulder bag with a sliding chain, named for February 1955 and conceived to leave the wearer’s hands free. It is both a particular historical model and a continuing manufactured design; later versions are manifestations of the protocol, not objects personally made by Chanel. [S10][S14]

- **1957:** *Two-tone slingback shoe*. A beige shoe with black toe, developed with the specialist shoemaker Massaro, who added the elastic heel strap. The proper authorship is collaborative: Chanel supplied the visual and functional project, while Massaro resolved its shoemaking construction. [S2][S10]

### Fragrance, cosmetics and skincare

- **1921:** *Chanel N°5*. A perfume project arising from Chanel’s collaboration with perfumer Ernest Beaux: Beaux composed the fragrance, while Chanel selected, named, presented and marketed it. Calling the olfactory formula Chanel’s unaided creation would erase its documented technical author. [S15]

- **1922:** *Chanel N°22*. A perfume composed by Ernest Beaux and named for its year. It belongs to Chanel’s branded olfactory oeuvre but should not be attributed to her as perfumer. [S15]

- **1924:** *First Chanel makeup collection*. Collections of face powders and lipsticks launched with the newly formed Société des Parfums Chanel. This is a commercial design programme rather than a single stable object, and surviving packages or shades are manifestations of it. [S15]

- **1925:** *Gardénia*. A fragrance developed jointly by Chanel and Ernest Beaux; the House explicitly credits both. Later reformulations and the 1983 reissue are not necessarily identical to the 1925 olfactory object. [S15]

- **1927:** *Cuir de Russie*. A leather fragrance composed by Ernest Beaux. Its inclusion here records Chanel’s commissioning and branded creative programme, not personal authorship of the formula. [S15]

- **1927:** *First Chanel skincare range*. A system of fifteen beauty products issued under the Chanel name. Product development was corporate and technical, so the range resists classification as fifteen individually authored Chanel works. [S15]

- **1928:** *Bois des Îles*. A woody fragrance composed by Ernest Beaux. It is part of Chanel’s Gesamtkunstwerk-like construction of dress, body, scent and image, but the composition itself is Beaux’s work. [S15]

- **1955:** *Pour Monsieur*. A men’s fragrance composed by Henri Robert and launched during Chanel’s lifetime. Its place in her oeuvre is institutional and curatorial; the House identifies Robert, not Chanel, as its creator. [S10]

- **1970:** *Chanel N°19*. A green floral fragrance composed by Henri Robert and named for Chanel’s birthday, 19 August. Chanel approved the project, but the surviving corporate record credits Robert with the formula. [S11]

### Theatre and ballet costumes

- **1922:** *Antigone*. Costume designs for Jean Cocteau’s adaptation of Sophocles, staged with sets and masks by Pablo Picasso. Chanel used archaic Greek forms and heavy wool in Picasso’s palette; no original costume survives, so this is an absent theatrical project documented through images and reports rather than a recoverable set of objects. [S8][S16]

- **1924:** *Le Train bleu*. Costumes for Serge Diaghilev’s one-act Ballets Russes production, with scenario by Cocteau, music by Darius Milhaud, choreography by Bronislava Nijinska and décor by Henri Laurens and Picasso. Chanel adapted contemporary sporting clothes, sometimes at the expense of dancers’ ease of movement; the production is a collective Gesamtkunstwerk, not hers alone. [S16][S17]

- **1926:** *Orphée*. Costumes for Cocteau’s play. Documentation assigns the production’s clothes to Chanel, but the project survives less completely than her couture, and individual costume titles and dates of fabrication are not securely recoverable from the retrospective catalogue. [S2]

- **1929:** *Apollon Musagète*. Costumes for George Balanchine’s ballet to music by Igor Stravinsky. The attribution is to Chanel’s costume project, while each performed costume was an atelier object made for movement and replacement rather than an autonomous titled work. [S2]

- **1937:** *Œdipe-Roi*. Costumes for Cocteau’s production of Stravinsky’s opera-oratorio. Like the earlier theatrical projects, it is a designed costume group embedded in a collective performance rather than a freestanding Chanel work. [S2]

### Film costume projects

- **1931:** *Tonight or Never*. Costumes for Gloria Swanson as the principal character in Mervyn LeRoy’s film. Chanel designed a star wardrobe rather than the film as a whole, and studio execution separates design authorship from manufacture. [S18]

- **1931:** *Palmy Days*. A Hollywood costume assignment associated with Chanel’s contract with Samuel Goldwyn. The extent of her realised contribution is less securely documented than the publicity surrounding her Hollywood appointment, so it should be treated as a disputed or incomplete project rather than a securely itemised wardrobe. [S2]

- **1932:** *The Greeks Had a Word for Them*. A film wardrobe project arising from Chanel’s Hollywood work. Individual garments and the division of labour with studio costume departments are not comprehensively established in the standard retrospective catalogue. [S2]

- **1939:** *La Règle du jeu (The Rules of the Game)*. Costumes for Jean Renoir’s film, credited to the House of Chanel. The institutional credit makes sole personal authorship by Gabrielle Chanel less certain than the convenient label “costumes by Coco Chanel” suggests. [S18]

- **1961:** *L’Année dernière à Marienbad (Last Year at Marienbad)*. Clothes for Delphine Seyrig’s character, supplied by Chanel for Alain Resnais’s film. These are character-defining couture looks within a collaborative film rather than authorship of the film’s complete costume design. [S2]

### Interior and architectural project

- **1928–1930:** *La Pausa*. Chanel’s villa at Roquebrune-Cap-Martin, commissioned from architect Robert Streitz and closely shaped by her preferences, including a staircase recalling the Aubazine convent of her childhood. It is properly a collaborative architectural and interior project—not a building drawn or constructed by Chanel alone—and thus a form of patronal authorship rather than an uncomplicated personal obra. [S15]

### A manifesto without a single text

- **ca. 1912–1971:** *The Chanel style*. The retrospective catalogue treats Chanel’s sustained vocabulary—freedom of movement, jersey, restrained geometry, black and beige, masculine tailoring, costume jewellery, the suit and coordinated accessories—as a “fashion manifesto.” It is manifesto-like because it acts upon bodies and social conduct, but it is not one written manifesto: its text is distributed across garments, shows, shops, perfumes, images and Chanel’s cultivated public persona. [S1][S2][S3]
