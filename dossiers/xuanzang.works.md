## Works by Xuanzang

Xuanzang's output falls into two very different categories. One is a single geographical-historical book he produced about his own journey. The other, far larger in volume, is decades of Buddhist scriptural translation that has nothing to do with travel writing as such but is the reason the Tang court valued him enough to fund that journey's record in the first place. Treating only the travel book as "his work" would misrepresent a man whose own sense of vocation was overwhelmingly as a translator of Sanskrit philosophy, not as an author of memoir.

### The Great Tang Records on the Western Regions

After returning to Chang'an in 645 following roughly sixteen years away, Xuanzang was asked by Emperor Taizong to set down an account of the lands he had crossed between China and India. As with Ibn Battuta two centuries earlier in a different setting, Xuanzang did not simply sit and write: he supplied oral and written material that a disciple, Bianji, organized and composed into finished literary form over more than a year, completing the work in 646. The result, conventionally called the Great Tang Records on the Western Regions, runs to twelve scrolls and covers around a hundred and ten named kingdoms, regions, and city-states across Central Asia, Afghanistan, Pakistan, India, and Sri Lanka, describing terrain, climate, routes, products, government, religion, and custom for each. It is not a diary of Xuanzang's own day-to-day movements; many entries report places he knew from other travelers rather than places he personally entered, and the text was shaped throughout by its purpose as a presentation to the throne, mixing political geography with sacred legend.

No early autograph survives, but because the work entered the Chinese Buddhist canon and the imperial historical record early, it was copied continuously and never underwent the long manuscript gap that affects several of the other travelers compared here. It is available in the Taisho Buddhist canon and other standard print editions descended from that transmission. The major modern translations are Samuel Beal's two-volume Buddhist Records of the Western World (1884), Thomas Watters' annotated study On Yuan Chwang's Travels in India (1904-05), and Li Rongxi's twentieth-century rendering for the Numata translation series (1996).

### Translator of the Buddhist canon

The far larger body of work is Xuanzang's translation career, carried out mainly at the Ci'en Monastery translation bureau in Chang'an from 645 until his death in 664. He had gone to India specifically because existing Chinese versions of Yogacara philosophical texts disagreed with each other, and he returned with hundreds of Sanskrit manuscripts intending to settle those disagreements by translating the sources directly. Working with a large team of monks who checked his Sanskrit, recorded his Chinese, and polished the resulting prose, he is traditionally credited with translating seventy-five separate works running to well over a thousand scrolls, including the enormous six-hundred-scroll Great Perfection of Wisdom Sutra, versions of the Heart Sutra and Diamond Sutra, the foundational Yogacara treatise the Yogacarabhumi, and a wide range of Abhidharma and logic texts. He also produced the Cheng Weishi Lun, a synthesis of ten Indian commentaries on a short verse treatise by Vasubandhu, which is closer to an edited compilation under his doctrinal direction than a plain translation. This translation work, not the travel record, is why Xuanzang is a central figure in East Asian Buddhist history independent of the journey itself.
