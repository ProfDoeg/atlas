# Julio Argentino Roca: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

This dossier concerns **Alejo Julio Argentino Roca** (1843–1914), Argentine army officer, minister of war, political organizer, and twice president of Argentina.

Labels used for contested material:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary records, official publications, archival descriptions, or convergent scholarship.
- **Reported fact:** present in reputable secondary literature but not independently confirmed here from a primary record.
- **Contested interpretation:** facts are substantially accepted, but their legal, political, or moral classification remains disputed.
- **Contemporary allegation:** an accusation made during Roca’s lifetime without a demonstrated judicial finding.
- **Later allegation or theory:** a retrospective claim requiring separation from established fact.
- **Rumor or myth:** a culturally significant story whose factual basis is absent, weak, or uncertain.

No known criminal court convicted Roca of genocide, corruption, fraud, or another offense. That procedural fact does not resolve the historical evidence concerning mass killing, captivity, forced displacement, coerced labor, electoral coercion, patronage, or public-land concentration.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Alejo Julio Argentino Roca.
- **Common form:** Julio Argentino Roca; frequently Julio A. Roca.
- **Surname form in genealogical usage:** Roca Paz.
- **Military and official titles:** general; brigadier general; later lieutenant general; minister of War and Navy; president of the Argentine Republic.
- **Political designation:** principal leader of the **Partido Autonomista Nacional** (PAN) and of the political system commonly called the *orden conservador*, *República Conservadora*, or *roquismo*.
- **Political slogan:** **“Paz y Administración”**, used for his first presidency.
- **Nicknames and hostile epithets:** “el Zorro” is often attributed to his reputation for reserve and political maneuvering, although its precise origin is poorly documented. Later admirers called him an architect or “founder” of modern Argentina; critics called him a “genocida.” These are reputational designations, not neutral biographical descriptions.
- **Born:** 17 July 1843.
- **Birthplace:** the family property known as **El Vizcacheral**, San Miguel de Tucumán, Tucumán, Argentine Confederation.
- **Died:** 19 October 1914, aged 71.
- **Place of death:** Buenos Aires, Argentina.
- **Burial:** La Recoleta Cemetery, Buenos Aires.
- **Nationality:** Argentine.
- **Professions:** career army officer, politician, landowner, political organizer, diplomat.
- **Presidential terms:** 12 October 1880–12 October 1886 and 12 October 1898–12 October 1904.

The Argentine General Archive preserves a large Roca family archive—approximately 40,000 items in its older description—including official and private correspondence, telegrams, diaries of travel, political propaganda, photographs, maps, projects, and documents extending beyond Roca’s own lifetime.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and family environment

Roca was the third of nine children of:

- **José Segundo Roca**, a military officer associated with the Argentine wars of independence and subsequent civil conflicts.
- **Agustina Paz**, sister of **Marcos Paz**, who became vice-president under Bartolomé Mitre.

The family belonged to the provincial military-political stratum of Tucumán. Roca’s paternal background supplied a tradition of army service; his maternal Paz connections tied him to a prominent Tucumán political family.

Agustina Paz died in 1855, when Julio was twelve. José Segundo Roca then dispersed or “distributed” several children among schools and relatives, a practice not unusual in nineteenth-century elite families facing bereavement and financial or logistical pressures.

Known brothers included:

- **Celedonio Roca**, who studied with Julio at Concepción del Uruguay.
- **Marcos Roca**, likewise sent to the college.
- **Ataliva Roca**, a businessman and political intermediary whose enrichment became a principal target of accusations against the wider Roca network.
- **Rudecindo Roca**, army officer, governor of Misiones, and proprietor of the Ingenio San Juan, where captured Indigenous people were employed under conditions officially described today as slavery.
- Other siblings belonged to the same extended military and political network; surviving sources differ in how completely they enumerate the nine children.

José Segundo and two of Julio’s brothers reportedly died during the Paraguayan War. The war thus combined Roca’s rapid professional advancement with severe family loss.

### Marriage

Roca married **Clara Dolores del Corazón de Jesús Funes y Díaz**, usually known as **Clara Funes**, in Córdoba in 1872. She came from a prominent Córdoba family. Her sister Elisa married **Miguel Juárez Celman**, making Juárez Celman Roca’s brother-in-law and later political protégé, presidential successor, and rival.

Clara was born in Córdoba on 29 March 1849 and died in Buenos Aires on 2 May 1890, aged 41. She served as first lady during Roca’s first presidency.

### Children

Sources consistently identify a large family, though lists differ over an infant who died young. The children generally recorded are:

- **Julio Argentino Pascual Roca Funes** (1873–1942), called “Julito”; lawyer, legislator, governor of Córdoba, vice-president of Argentina, diplomat, and co-signatory of the 1933 Roca–Runciman Agreement.
- **Alejandro Roca Funes**, born in 1874 and dead in infancy or early childhood.
- **Elisa Clara Genoveva Roca Funes**.
- **María Marcela Roca Funes**.
- **Clara del Corazón de Jesús Roca Funes**.
- **Agustina Eloísa Roca Funes**.
- **Elena Josefina Roca Funes**.

Because family compilations inconsistently count Alejandro, accounts alternately say that the marriage produced six surviving children or seven children in total.

### Extramarital relationship

**Reported fact:** A long clandestine relationship is attributed to Roca and **Guillermina de Oliveira Cézar de Wilde**, wife of his friend and political associate **Eduardo Wilde**. Published accounts rely substantially on private correspondence and later family testimony. The relationship appears more firmly grounded than ordinary political gossip, but its exact duration and the degree of Wilde’s knowledge remain uncertain. A story that an unidentified woman wept conspicuously at Roca’s funeral has been connected retrospectively to Guillermina; this particular funeral anecdote belongs closer to family legend unless corroborated from contemporary evidence.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1843–1855

Roca grew up in Tucumán in a family shaped by military service and the instability of the post-independence Argentine provinces. His early childhood coincided with the final years of Juan Manuel de Rosas’s ascendancy and the ensuing reorganization of Argentina.

The family property, El Vizcacheral, is given as his precise birthplace in the Museo Casa Rosada account. No detailed childhood diary is known. Most reconstructions depend on later biographical narratives and family papers.

The death of his mother in 1855 was the decisive early disruption. Julio, Celedonio, and Marcos were sent as boarders to the **Colegio del Uruguay** at Concepción del Uruguay, Entre Ríos.

---

## Education and Formation, 1855–1859

### Colegio del Uruguay

Roca received a scholarship associated with President **Justo José de Urquiza** and entered the Colegio del Uruguay, then one of the Confederation’s leading educational institutions.

His education included general secondary subjects and military instruction. The institution placed him in the political orbit of Urquiza’s Argentine Confederation and exposed him to youths from several provinces, an important foundation for his later interprovincial network.

No evidence supports the claim that Roca completed a university degree. His principal professional formation was military and experiential.

### Formative influences

Documented or strongly supported influences included:

- His father’s army career.
- His maternal uncle Marcos Paz.
- The Urquiza political and educational network.
- The Colegio del Uruguay’s national rather than narrowly provincial environment.
- The civil wars, which made advancement in arms and political allegiance inseparable.
- The emerging belief among state-building elites that permanent national institutions, communications, settlement, and a professional army were necessary to end recurrent provincial war.

## Early Military Career, 1859–1877

### Cepeda and Pavón

In 1859, at about sixteen, Roca asked leave from the college to join the army during renewed war between the Argentine Confederation and the State of Buenos Aires. He participated in the **Battle of Cepeda** on 23 October 1859.

He also fought at **Pavón** on 17 September 1861. Accounts differ in detail over his unit and conduct, but agree that these campaigns began his continuous army career. Pavón ended Urquiza’s effective national predominance and opened the way to Bartolomé Mitre’s reunified government.

### Campaign against the federal montoneras

Roca served in operations against provincial federalist resistance, including the campaign that ended with the killing of **Ángel Vicente “Chacho” Peñaloza** in 1863. Roca was not the officer who personally killed Peñaloza, but he belonged to the national military apparatus that defeated the movement.

He subsequently operated against other interior uprisings. These campaigns taught him mobile warfare, provincial patronage, intelligence gathering, and negotiation with governors and commanders.

### Paraguayan War, 1865–1870

Roca served in the War of the Triple Alliance against Paraguay. He participated in major operations conventionally associated with:

- Uruguayana.
- Yatay or the surrounding campaign.
- Tuyutí.
- Curupaytí and subsequent operations.

Exact lists of his engagements vary among biographies. His bravery and survival contributed to promotion. The deaths of his father and brothers during the conflict became part of the family’s military memory.

The Paraguayan War exposed him to large-scale logistics, modern firearms, railway and telegraph communications, entrenched warfare, and the capacity of a centralized state to mobilize men and material.

### Repression of provincial revolts

After Paraguay, Roca served against uprisings connected to federalist and provincial opposition. His assignments brought him into Cuyo, the northwest, and Córdoba.

In 1871 he became commander of the southern Córdoba frontier, headquartered at **Río Cuarto**. There he developed relationships with provincial families, officers, merchants, landholders, and governors. Recent regional research emphasizes that his later national power did not arise solely from battlefield reputation: it grew through a dense patronage and correspondence network linking the interior provinces.

### Marriage and Córdoba network

His 1872 marriage to Clara Funes reinforced his place in Córdoba society. Through the Funes family and Miguel Juárez Celman, Roca acquired durable political access in Córdoba, which became one of the foundations of the later PAN coalition.

### Revolution of 1874

When Bartolomé Mitre’s supporters rebelled against the election of Nicolás Avellaneda, Roca remained loyal to the national government. On 7 December 1874, at **Santa Rosa**, Mendoza, his forces defeated General **José Miguel Arredondo**.

A frequently repeated story says Roca altered or creatively interpreted orders so that he could attack Arredondo from an unexpected direction. Whether every tactical detail is exact, the victory is documented and decisive. It broke the western revolutionary army and led to Roca’s promotion to general at only thirty-one.

### Frontier policy before 1878

As a frontier commander, Roca observed treaty making, commerce, cattle raiding, captive taking, military expeditions, and negotiation between state agents and politically diverse Indigenous communities. The frontier was not a simple line between two homogeneous peoples. It contained forts, settlers, traders, soldiers, *indios amigos*, autonomous groups, mixed families, captives, missionaries, and trans-Andean networks.

Roca became a critic of War Minister **Adolfo Alsina’s** primarily defensive system, whose emblem was the ditch or *Zanja de Alsina*. Roca advocated a rapid offensive to the Río Negro, made possible by disciplined columns, the telegraph, rail supply, and Remington rifles.

## Minister of War and the Conquest of the Desert, 1878–1879

### Appointment

After Alsina’s death in December 1877, President **Nicolás Avellaneda** appointed Roca minister of War and Navy, effective in January 1878.

Roca secured congressional funding of approximately **1.6 million pesos** for the campaign. The financing was connected to anticipated sales of the lands the army would occupy. Law No. 947 authorized expenditures and a land-finance mechanism in 1878.

### Objectives

Roca publicly and privately presented several connected objectives:

- End raids on frontier settlements and the taking of livestock and captives.
- Destroy or compel submission of autonomous Indigenous military power.
- Occupy territory to the Río Negro and beyond.
- establish effective Argentine sovereignty in a region also implicated in boundary competition with Chile.
- Open land to settlement, ranching, transport, and export production.
- Replace negotiated frontier coexistence with direct territorial administration.

The description “desert” was political and strategic language, not a literal demographic fact. The territory was inhabited by Mapuche, Ranquel, Pampa, Tehuelche, and other communities with changing identities and alliances.

### Preliminary offensives, 1878

Before the main 1879 advance, twenty-three expeditions attacked Indigenous groups. Figures reproduced from contemporary military reports record:

- 398 Indigenous people killed.
- 901 warriors captured.
- 3,668 family members captured.

These are the army’s categories and numbers, not a complete mortality register. They exclude many deaths from hunger, exposure, flight, disease, and later confinement.

### Main campaign, 1879

Five army divisions advanced south and west in April 1879. Roca personally led the principal column to the Río Negro and reached **Choele Choel**.

The campaign used superior firearms, coordinated columns, telegraphy, river transport, supply depots, and intelligence. It targeted mobile settlements, food supplies, livestock, and leadership networks, not merely organized forces on formal battlefields.

Roca’s order of the day at Carhué on 26 April 1879 described the pursuit and destruction of Indigenous centers of resistance in language that treated the communities as “nests” and “swarms” of “savages.” This documentary vocabulary is important evidence of the campaign’s dehumanizing official ideology.

A later official summary credited the operations with:

- 1,313 “Indians of the lance” killed.
- 1,271 fighting men taken prisoner.
- Approximately 10,513 or 10,539 women, children, elderly people, and other noncombatants captured under the military category *chusma*.
- 1,049 people characterized as voluntarily “reduced” or submitted.
- Capture or defeat of principal chiefs, including Pincén and members of the Catriel leadership; Baigorrita was killed.

Numerical variations result from different report dates and transcription traditions. None should be treated as a complete demographic balance sheet.

### Continuation after the 1879 expedition

The “Conquest” did not end when Roca returned from the Río Negro. Subsequent campaigns into Neuquén, the Andes, and Patagonia continued under his political authority during his presidency, especially from 1880 to 1885. Manuel Namuncurá’s resistance ended in submission in 1884–1885.

### Captivity, family separation, and forced labor

**Documented fact:** Captured Indigenous people were confined in military camps, barracks, prisons, islands, missions, and other holding sites. Families were separated. Adults and children were distributed to households, estates, sugar plantations, vineyards, military units, naval service, and other employers.

Research on Mendoza documents at least roughly 3,000 prisoners sent into the province. Contemporary newspaper notices publicly offered Indigenous captives for placement. Scholars describe these arrangements as coerced labor, servitude, or slavery in substance despite the absence of a new formal slave status in Argentine law.

Rudecindo Roca’s **Ingenio San Juan** in Misiones employed Indigenous prisoners in conditions that an Argentine government heritage description calls slavery; revolts occurred there. This establishes direct benefit within the Roca family network, though it does not by itself prove Julio personally managed the enterprise.

Captives were also subjected to religious conversion, renaming, domestic placement, and removal of children from kin. Mortality during forced marches and confinement is documented, but no comprehensive list survives.

### Land transfer

Military occupation opened tens of millions of hectares to state disposition. Contemporary laws financed the campaign through advance subscriptions and later sales. Very large parcels went to financiers, military officers, ranchers, companies, and politically connected families. Secondary estimates range widely according to geography and dates; claims of approximately 40–50 million hectares refer to the broader incorporation and disposition process, not necessarily grants personally signed over by Roca in one transaction.

**Documented fact:** Landholding became highly concentrated.

**Contested question:** How much concentration resulted from deliberate oligarchic design, how much from the financing statutes, and how much from subsequent resale and consolidation? The available evidence supports all three mechanisms to some degree.

Roca himself acquired or developed major rural property, notably **La Larga** near present-day Daireaux. Accounts differ over whether particular holdings were direct rewards, purchases, exchanges, or later acquisitions. The claim that every hectare of his estate was simply a personal “prize” for killing Indigenous people is too categorical unless traced parcel by parcel.

### Was it genocide?

**Contested interpretation grounded in documented conduct:** A substantial body of modern scholarship classifies the campaigns and their aftermath as genocide or genocidal practice because they combined killing, destruction of community subsistence, forced transfer, family separation, mass confinement, coerced labor, child removal, and policies intended to eliminate autonomous Indigenous societies.

Other historians reject or qualify the legal label, citing:

- The term “genocide” was coined only in 1944.
- The government often sought submission, incorporation, labor, or territorial control rather than the physical death of every Indigenous person.
- Indigenous alliances with the army complicate a binary ethnic-war model.
- Frontier violence included raids, killings, cattle theft, captive taking, and warfare by multiple parties.

These objections concern classification; they do not negate the documented killings, mass captivity, deportation, coerced labor, or territorial dispossession. No court adjudicated Roca’s responsibility under modern genocide law.

## Election of 1880 and Federalization of Buenos Aires

Roca’s military prestige made him the leading presidential candidate of the provincial coalition opposed to continued Buenos Aires dominance. Governors, officers, and political brokers supported him through what became the PAN.

Buenos Aires governor **Carlos Tejedor** resisted. The 1880 presidential succession produced armed confrontation between provincial forces and the national government. Fighting at Olivera, Puente Alsina, Barracas, and Corrales caused heavy casualties.

Roca did not command every engagement personally, but he was the victorious presidential candidate supported by the national coalition. Tejedor resigned, and Congress federalized the city of Buenos Aires. The Province of Buenos Aires later established La Plata as its capital.

The federalization settlement was one of the most consequential institutional changes of Roca’s career: it gave the national government a capital independent of provincial control and broke the cycle of armed struggle over the city’s political status.

## First Presidency, 1880–1886

### Accession and political system

Roca took office on 12 October 1880 with **Francisco Bernabé Madero** as vice-president.

His slogan “Paz y Administración” expressed a program of political order, national bureaucracy, territorial organization, public works, immigration, foreign investment, and export growth.

The regime was constitutional in its formal institutions but operated through:

- Restricted male suffrage.
- Public rather than secret voting.
- Electoral intimidation and fraud.
- Intervention in provincial politics.
- Government control over candidate selection.
- Patronage linking the presidency to governors and local elites.

Roca’s title as the PAN’s political “elector” reflected his influence over succession, not a formal constitutional office.

### Territorial and administrative organization

Major measures included:

- Organization of the national territories of La Pampa, Río Negro, Neuquén, Chubut, Santa Cruz, Tierra del Fuego, Chaco, Formosa, and Misiones through legislation and administration developed during the period.
- Expansion of federal courts, customs, postal services, telegraphs, military posts, surveys, and land offices.
- Nationalization of the University of Buenos Aires.
- Development of a national currency framework.
- Creation or development of the Banco Hipotecario Nacional.
- Enactment of mining and penal legislation.
- Expansion of census, statistical, and civil-record functions.

The state’s increased capacity served public education and administration while also enabling surveillance, military conscription, labor control, territorial appropriation, and electoral management.

### Education and church-state conflict

The **Law 1,420 of Common Education** was enacted in 1884. It established free, compulsory, gradual primary education under national jurisdiction and is conventionally described as secular, though the exact institutional meaning of *laica* and the permitted place of religious teaching were debated.

The law was a collective product of educators, legislators, ministers, and earlier initiatives—especially those associated with Domingo F. Sarmiento—not the personal invention of Roca. His government nevertheless sponsored and implemented it.

The establishment of civil registries transferred registration of births, marriages, and deaths away from exclusive church control. Conflict with the Catholic hierarchy led to a diplomatic rupture with the Holy See. The Civil Marriage Law was enacted in 1888 under Juárez Celman, not during Roca’s first term, though it extended the same secularizing program.

Roca was culturally Catholic and participated in conventional rites; he was not an anti-religious doctrinaire. Politically, he supported state supremacy over education and civil status.

### Economy, infrastructure, and foreign capital

The government promoted:

- Railway construction.
- Port improvements.
- Agricultural and livestock exports.
- Immigration.
- Foreign borrowing and investment, especially British capital.
- Sale and leasing of public land.
- Urban public buildings and administrative infrastructure.

Work began on projects associated with the modern Casa Rosada and the Port of Buenos Aires. Italian architect **Francesco Tamburini** became an important state architect during the era. Claims that Roca personally designed these works are false; his administration authorized, financed, or reorganized them.

Growth was substantial, but so were external indebtedness, land concentration, speculation, and dependence on export earnings and foreign finance. The later 1890 crisis arose under Juárez Celman, but its structural antecedents included borrowing and speculative development begun or accelerated during Roca’s administration.

### Foreign relations

The **1881 Boundary Treaty with Chile** recognized the Andes watershed/divide framework and assigned Patagonia east of the Andes to Argentina, while leaving difficult boundary questions unresolved. It reduced immediate conflict but generated later disputes requiring arbitration.

### Attempted assassination, 1886

On 10 May 1886, while proceeding to open the congressional session, Roca was struck on the head with a stone by **Ignacio Monjes** or Monge—the surname appears in variant spellings. His hat and surrounding officials limited the injury. Contemporary imagery made the attack part of his political legend.

There is no strong evidence of a large conspiracy behind the assailant. Treating it as an organized opposition assassination plot would exceed the available record.

### Succession

Roca promoted his brother-in-law **Miguel Juárez Celman**, who became president on 12 October 1886. The succession illustrates both Roca’s political power and the familial basis of elite networks.

## Between Presidencies, 1886–1898

### European travel and political distance

After leaving office, Roca spent time in Europe and temporarily reduced his public visibility. Travel served diplomatic, social, and personal purposes and allowed him to observe European armies and political institutions.

### Break with Juárez Celman

Juárez Celman attempted to free himself from Roca’s tutelage and concentrate leadership in the *Unicato*. Their alliance deteriorated.

The late 1880s brought speculative expansion, public borrowing, monetary instability, corruption accusations, and opposition from the Unión Cívica.

### Revolution of the Park, 1890

The **Revolución del Parque** began in Buenos Aires on 26 July 1890. The rebels failed militarily but Juárez Celman resigned in August.

Roca supported the constitutional forces and emerged strengthened. He helped arrange the transfer of power to Vice-President **Carlos Pellegrini**.

**Contested interpretation:** Opponents and later writers described Roca as a backstage beneficiary who allowed or manipulated the crisis to remove his estranged brother-in-law. The documentary record establishes political maneuvering and benefit but not that Roca secretly organized the uprising.

### Minister of the Interior

Roca served as minister of the Interior under Pellegrini in 1890–1891. He helped reconstruct the ruling coalition after the financial and political crisis.

### The Mitre–Roca agreement

In 1891 Roca negotiated with **Bartolomé Mitre**, producing an elite accord around the candidacy of **Luis Sáenz Peña** and blocking the stronger independent candidacy of Roque Sáenz Peña.

The agreement divided the Unión Cívica and contributed to the formation of the Unión Cívica Radical. It is a central example of controlled succession through negotiation among party chiefs rather than open electoral competition.

### Senate and provincial revolts

Roca returned to the Senate and became its president during José Evaristo Uriburu’s presidency. During the radical revolts of 1893 he again exercised military and political influence in defending the national order.

### Return to the presidency

By 1897–1898, the PAN coalition again selected Roca. Elections remained managed and non-secret. He took office for a second term on 12 October 1898, with **Norberto Quirno Costa** as vice-president.

## Second Presidency, 1898–1904

### Economic and monetary policy

Roca inherited continuing fiscal and currency problems. His government adopted monetary stabilization, including the 1899 Conversion Law, fixing a relationship between paper pesos and gold.

Foreign trade, railway mileage, immigration, agricultural output, and urban construction expanded. Benefits were unevenly distributed, while dependence on commodity exports and British capital persisted.

An attempt to unify or restructure external debt provoked opposition in 1901. Critics feared excessive concessions to foreign bondholders and bankers. Public demonstrations and political resistance forced modification or abandonment of the original proposal.

No evidence located establishes that Roca personally embezzled proceeds from the debt plan. The controversy concerned public policy, financial influence, and sovereignty rather than a judicially proven personal fraud.

### Army reform and conscription

War Minister **Pablo Riccheri** professionalized the army. Law No. 4,031 of 1901 established compulsory military service.

The reform:

- Reduced dependence on provincial guards.
- Supported standardized training.
- Expanded the national government’s reach into male citizenship.
- Functioned as an institution of nationalization and discipline.
- Was criticized as militarization and as a means of social control.

The Escuela Superior de Guerra was created during this broader professionalization process.

### Electoral reform

Law No. 4,161 of 1902, associated with Interior Minister **Joaquín V. González**, introduced single-member constituencies. It did not establish a secret or universally honest ballot, but it allowed the socialist **Alfredo Palacios** to win election in Buenos Aires in 1904.

The reform was repealed after Roca left office. Full secret and compulsory male voting came with the Sáenz Peña Law of 1912.

### Labor conflict and Law of Residence

Rapid immigration and urbanization produced stronger socialist and anarchist labor movements. Major strikes occurred in 1901–1902.

In November 1902 Congress enacted **Law No. 4,144**, the Law of Residence, allowing the executive to expel foreigners deemed dangerous to public order without an ordinary criminal trial.

**Documented fact:** Roca’s government used coercive state power against labor militants and deported immigrant activists.

**Legal-political characterization:** The law was constitutional legislation at the time, but it denied robust due process and became a durable instrument of political repression. It was not merely a response to proven violent crimes; it could be used against organizers on executive assessment.

Roca’s administration also commissioned a comprehensive labor-law project after **Juan Bialet Massé’s** investigation into working conditions. The bill proposed regulation but failed in Congress. Repression and reform therefore coexisted within the government’s labor policy.

### Chile: brinkmanship and settlement

Argentina and Chile engaged in an expensive naval and military competition over unresolved Andean and Patagonian boundaries.

Roca met Chilean president **Federico Errázuriz Echaurren** in the **Abrazo del Estrecho** at Punta Arenas in February 1899. The encounter had major symbolic value but did not by itself settle every dispute.

The **Pactos de Mayo** of 1902 established arbitration and naval limitation mechanisms and helped avert war. King Edward VII’s arbitral award later resolved major boundary sections.

Roca’s diplomacy combined military preparedness, personal presidential contact, and negotiated settlement. The claim that he single-handedly “saved” the countries from war is hagiographic; diplomats, ministers, arbitrators, and the Chilean government were indispensable.

### Brazil and regional diplomacy

Roca traveled to Rio de Janeiro in 1899 and cultivated Brazilian president **Manuel Ferraz de Campos Sales**. His later friendship with Brazilian statesman **José Maria da Silva Paranhos Júnior, Baron of Rio Branco**, supported efforts to reduce Argentine-Brazilian rivalry.

### Drago Doctrine

During the 1902–1903 European blockade of Venezuela, Foreign Minister **Luis María Drago** articulated the doctrine that public debt should not justify armed intervention or territorial occupation in the Americas.

The doctrine belongs intellectually and textually to Drago, though it was issued by Roca’s government.

### Antarctica

In 1904 Argentina assumed operation of the meteorological observatory on Laurie Island in the South Orkneys. This began the country’s uninterrupted official Antarctic presence. Later nationalist memory credits Roca with strategic foresight; operational responsibility was distributed among Argentine officials and Scottish expeditionary arrangements.

### End of presidency

Roca supported **Manuel Quintana**, who succeeded him on 12 October 1904. By then Roca’s relationship with Carlos Pellegrini had deteriorated, partly over financial policy and presidential succession.

## Later Life, 1904–1914

### Retirement, estates, and continuing influence

Roca divided his time among Buenos Aires residences, family properties, and rural estates, especially La Larga. He remained a consulted political elder but no longer exercised his earlier control over the PAN.

He supervised agricultural and livestock development and benefited from the rise in land values and railway access. A complete modern balance sheet of his wealth has not been established. His position as a major landowner is documented; precise separation between salary, purchase, inheritance, political reward, and appreciation requires parcel-level archival research.

### Brazil missions

In March 1907 Roca traveled to Brazil on a diplomatic mission intended to reduce tensions. Photographic albums document the visit and his contacts with Campos Sales and Rio Branco.

He undertook another extraordinary mission to Brazil in 1912. Argentine foreign-ministry records list him as envoy extraordinary and minister plenipotentiary.

### Political change

Roca lived to see:

- Continued radical opposition.
- The fragmentation of the old PAN system.
- Roque Sáenz Peña’s presidency.
- Enactment of the secret-ballot reform in 1912.
- The opening of electoral politics that would bring Hipólito Yrigoyen and the Radical Party to power after Roca’s death.

He did not lead a last major campaign to reverse the Sáenz Peña reform. His declining political role reflected both age and structural change.

---

## Death

Roca died in Buenos Aires on 19 October 1914, aged seventy-one.

The public record consistently gives the date and place but readily available authoritative sources do not provide a sufficiently precise medical cause to state one confidently. Later accounts sometimes mention a sudden illness, heart failure, or complications following a coughing episode; without a death certificate or clinical record, the immediate cause should be treated as **not securely established here**.

He received official honors and was buried in La Recoleta Cemetery.

## Residences, Travels, and Geographical Movements

Principal documented locations included:

- **1843–1855:** Tucumán, especially San Miguel de Tucumán and El Vizcacheral.
- **From 1855:** Concepción del Uruguay, Entre Ríos, for schooling.
- **1859–1861:** military movement during the Cepeda and Pavón campaigns.
- **1860s:** interior campaigns and the Paraguayan theater.
- **1871 onward:** Río Cuarto and the southern Córdoba frontier.
- **1872:** Córdoba marriage and strengthening of local family ties.
- **1874:** Mendoza campaign and Santa Rosa.
- **1878–1879:** frontier headquarters, Carhué, Río Negro, and Choele Choel.
- **1880–1886:** Buenos Aires as president, with official travel through the provinces.
- **After 1886:** extended European travel; archival holdings include letters from Paris, Bern, and other locations.
- **1899:** Punta Arenas for the meeting with Errázuriz; Uruguay and Brazil during presidential diplomacy.
- **1904 onward:** Buenos Aires, La Larga, Europe, and diplomatic travel.
- **1907 and 1912:** Brazil.
- **1914:** death in Buenos Aires.

The archive contains travel diaries capable of supporting a more granular itinerary than has been published in standard biographies.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Personal and family interests

- **La Larga estate:** Roca’s principal documented rural property near present-day Daireaux; developed as a modern agricultural and livestock establishment.
- **Family land network:** Ataliva and other Roca relatives acquired substantial property and participated in land and commercial transactions.
- **Ingenio San Juan:** Established by Rudecindo Roca in Misiones in 1883; used captive Indigenous labor under coercive conditions. It was Rudecindo’s enterprise, not proven to have been Julio’s company.
- **No known patents or inventions:** Roca was a user and promoter of military, transport, and communications technologies rather than an inventor.

### Technologies and state projects associated with his career

- Remington rifles in frontier warfare.
- Military telegraph networks.
- Railways for supply, settlement, exports, and national integration.
- Port construction and dredging.
- Surveying and cadastral systems.
- Civil registries and centralized statistics.
- Professional military education.
- Gold-conversion monetary institutions.
- Antarctic meteorological presence.

Roca’s relationship to these projects was political and administrative. Attributing every institutional innovation personally to him obscures the roles of ministers, legislators, engineers, educators, financiers, and civil servants.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Political outlook

Roca did not leave a systematic philosophical treatise. His outlook must be reconstructed from speeches, correspondence, appointments, and policy.

Recurring principles included:

- A strong national executive.
- Subordination of provincial military autonomy.
- Political order before mass electoral competition.
- Negotiated elite coalition building.
- Territorial sovereignty and military preparedness.
- Public education as an instrument of citizenship and cultural integration.
- State authority over civil registration and education.
- Export-led growth financed by foreign capital.
- European immigration.
- Gradual reform when necessary to preserve order.
- Willingness to use intervention, patronage, electoral manipulation, deportation, and military force.

### Liberalism and conservatism

Roca’s system was liberal in economic policy, secular public institutions, constitutional vocabulary, and encouragement of immigration. It was conservative and oligarchic in electoral practice and social power.

Neither “liberal modernizer” nor “military dictator” alone adequately describes the regime. He twice served constitutionally defined terms and transferred office, but elections were not free or secret by later democratic standards.

### Religion

Roca did not advocate abolition of religion. His conflicts concerned jurisdiction: whether church institutions or the national state would control education, marriage, and civil status. His government’s secularization generated fierce Catholic opposition and a rupture with the papacy, followed by eventual normalization.

### Race and civilization

His official language shared the era’s hierarchical “civilization and barbarism” framework. Indigenous autonomy was presented as incompatible with the desired nation-state. Assimilation, dispersal, conversion, labor placement, and military defeat were treated as legitimate instruments of incorporation.

This documentary ideology is central to interpreting the Conquest and should not be erased by later descriptions limited to “territorial integration.”

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Nicolás Avellaneda

President, patron, and political superior who appointed Roca minister of War. Avellaneda authorized the campaign and shared responsibility for its policy and financing. Roca’s military success then enabled him to succeed Avellaneda.

### Domingo Faustino Sarmiento

Correspondent, occasional supporter, critic, and rival elder statesman. Roca wrote Sarmiento respectfully about his military reputation in 1875. Sarmiento later criticized Roca’s family patronage and Ataliva’s enrichment.

### Bartolomé Mitre

Military and political adversary in 1874; later negotiating partner in the 1891 accord. The relationship shifted between rivalry and elite accommodation.

### Carlos Pellegrini

Military-political associate, minister in Roca’s first administration, president after Juárez Celman, and later rival. Their rupture grew over financial and succession issues.

### Miguel Juárez Celman

Brother-in-law, Córdoba ally, chosen successor, then political rival after Juárez’s *Unicato* challenged Roca’s leadership.

### Eduardo Wilde

Physician, writer, minister, diplomat, and close political associate. The alleged affair between Roca and Wilde’s wife, Guillermina, adds a private dimension whose effect on their friendship is insufficiently established.

### Ataliva Roca

Brother, intermediary, landholder, and symbol of alleged influence peddling. Contemporary critics treated Ataliva’s fortunes as inseparable from Julio’s power.

### Rudecindo Roca

Brother, officer, governor of Misiones, and plantation owner. His treatment of Indigenous captives links the family network materially to the coerced-labor aftermath of the Conquest.

### Provincial governors

Roca’s “League of Governors” was not a single permanently constituted organization but a shifting interprovincial alliance. Córdoba, Tucumán, Santa Fe, Entre Ríos, and other provincial elites supplied electoral votes, officers, legislators, and administrative support.

### Indigenous adversaries and intermediaries

Principal leaders affected by or resisting the campaigns included:

- Manuel Namuncurá.
- Pincén.
- Epumer.
- Baigorrita.
- Catriel leaders.
- Sayhueque.

The relationships ranged from declared war and pursuit to surrender, negotiated submission, military alliance, and state-managed resettlement. They should not be compressed into an undifferentiated “Indian enemy.”

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Corruption, and Political Violence

### 1. Mass violence and Indigenous destruction

**Documented fact:** Roca designed, secured financing for, and personally commanded the main 1879 offensive. His government continued campaigns that produced mass death, captivity, forced transfer, family separation, coerced labor, and destruction of autonomous societies.

**Contested interpretation:** Whether this constitutes genocide under a retrospective historical or legal framework. The evidence establishes practices included in modern genocide analysis; there was no contemporaneous or later criminal adjudication of Roca.

### 2. Executions and unlawful killing

The newspaper *La Nación* denounced the killing of approximately sixty disarmed Indigenous prisoners by troops commanded by Rudecindo Roca in 1878 as contrary to humanity and the laws of war.

**Documented contemporary accusation:** The denunciation existed and concerned Rudecindo’s command.

**Unresolved attribution:** Julio was minister and strategic superior, but proof that he personally ordered that particular killing has not been established here.

### 3. Captive distribution and slavery

**Documented fact:** Prisoners were distributed as laborers and domestic servants, sometimes publicly advertised. Current scholarship and an Argentine government heritage description characterize specific arrangements as slavery or virtual slavery.

The absence of formal slave deeds does not make the labor voluntary. Conversely, the exact conditions varied between institutions and households and should be reconstructed case by case.

### 4. Public-land concentration

**Documented fact:** Campaign financing and land laws transferred enormous areas into relatively few hands. Soldiers’ warrants were often sold, enabling consolidation by investors.

**Contested claim:** That Roca designed the entire campaign principally to enrich himself and his family. Private enrichment and family advantage are strongly relevant, but military, fiscal, territorial, diplomatic, and settler-security motives are also documented.

### 5. “Atalivar” and Ataliva Roca

Domingo F. Sarmiento coined or popularized **atalivar**, derived from Ataliva Roca’s name, to mean profiting improperly through influence, commissions, or political connections.

**Contemporary allegation:** Ataliva operated as his brother’s collector of commissions and beneficiary of land transactions.

**Documented reputational fact:** The word circulated as a synonym for corrupt enrichment.

**Evidentiary limit:** No located judicial conviction establishes a specific bribery offense by Julio or Ataliva. Later claims that Ataliva received precisely 160,000 hectares require confirmation from land registers before being treated as exact.

### 6. Electoral fraud and provincial control

**Documented systemic practice:** Elections under Roca lacked secret ballots and were routinely shaped by official lists, police, governors, local bosses, intimidation, and manipulation of returns.

Roca was not uniquely responsible for a system predating him, but he organized and benefited from it extensively.

### 7. Nepotism

The advancement of Juárez Celman, the commercial roles of Ataliva and Rudecindo, and the later political careers of Roca descendants show strong family patronage.

**Documented fact:** Kinship and public power overlapped.

**Qualification:** Family relationship alone does not prove each appointment or transaction corrupt; individual acts require their own evidence.

### 8. 1890 political maneuvering

**Later allegation or theory:** Roca covertly facilitated the Revolution of the Park to destroy Juárez Celman.

Evidence confirms estrangement, maneuvering, and that Roca benefited. It does not conclusively demonstrate that he planned the rebellion.

### 9. External debt and foreign influence

Roca’s governments favored foreign investment, especially British railway and financial capital. Critics alleged national dependence and excessive benefits for financiers.

**Documented fact:** Foreign capital was structurally important and received guarantees and concessions.

**Not established:** A personal secret financial relationship between Roca and a named foreign bank amounting to bribery or money laundering. No money-laundering framework or proven scheme appears in the record.

### 10. Law of Residence

**Documented fact:** His second government acquired and used authority to expel immigrant activists without ordinary judicial trial.

Descriptions of this as political repression are supported by the law’s structure and application. Claims that every expelled person was innocent, or that all were convicted terrorists, are both unsupported generalizations.

### 11. Personal relationships

The Guillermina de Oliveira Cézar relationship was scandalous because she was married to Eduardo Wilde. It remained substantially private during Roca’s life. It was not a criminal proceeding or public corruption case.

### 12. Dedicated adversarial review, including 2024–2025 material

Recent searches combining Roca’s name with *controversia*, *genocidio*, *crítica*, *corrupción*, *acusaciones*, *fraud*, *lawsuit*, and *conspiracy* chiefly produced:

- Renewed conflict over monuments and place names.
- Defenses of Roca by the current Argentine government and institutes devoted to his memory.
- Indigenous and left-wing descriptions of the Conquest as genocide.
- Repetition of the Ataliva corruption allegations.
- Polemics linking contemporary landholding to nineteenth-century grants.
- Claims that critics are erasing Argentine sovereignty.
- No newly reported criminal file, financial investigation, lawsuit, or authenticated private ledger changing the evidentiary record of Roca’s own lifetime.

A 2025 article revived the history of *atalivar*; it did not present a newly discovered conviction. A 2024–2025 government-sponsored rehabilitation emphasized sovereignty and state construction, while critics renewed attention to coercion and captivity. These are present-day memory struggles, not new adjudications of Roca’s legal liability.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The desert was empty”

- **Story:** Roca conquered uninhabited land.
- **Origin and circulation:** Official and settler language using *desierto*, later school narratives and nationalist commemoration.
- **Evidence:** False if taken literally. Numerous Indigenous societies inhabited, traded across, cultivated, grazed, and governed the territory.
- **Effect:** Naturalized state occupation and marginalized Indigenous sovereignty.

### “Roca exterminated every Indigenous inhabitant”

- **Story:** The campaign simply killed all Indigenous people in Patagonia.
- **Evidence:** False as a literal statement. Thousands survived through submission, captivity, flight, military incorporation, missions, reservations, labor placement, and reorganized communities.
- **Historical core:** Mass killing, social destruction, deportation, and family separation are documented.
- **Effect:** The exaggeration can obscure the longer history of survivors and continuing Indigenous communities.

### “The Mapuche were merely Chilean invaders”

- **Story:** Roca expelled foreigners who had recently invaded Argentina.
- **Circulation:** Nationalist defenses of the campaign, especially in present-day social media and monument debates.
- **Evidence:** Trans-Andean movement and political expansion occurred, but the Andes did not function as a modern national border for these societies. Communities had complex Mapuche, Tehuelche, Pampa, Ranquel, and mixed identities. Reducing all campaign targets to foreign Chileans is historically misleading.
- **Effect:** Converts a war against autonomous peoples into a simple defense against foreign invasion.

### “Without Roca, Patagonia would certainly be Chilean”

- **Story:** Chile would inevitably have annexed all Patagonia absent the campaign.
- **Evidence:** Argentine-Chilean rivalry and uncertain sovereignty were real. The 1881 treaty was important. Inevitability cannot be proved, and the argument does not determine whether the methods used against Indigenous inhabitants were necessary.
- **Effect:** Central claim in Roca’s patriotic commemoration.

### “Roca invented public education”

- **Story:** Roca personally created free public schooling.
- **Evidence:** Law 1,420 was enacted under him, but it resulted from decades of provincial schooling, Sarmiento’s programs, the 1882 Pedagogical Congress, ministerial work, and legislative debate.
- **Effect:** Concentrates a collective institutional history in presidential biography.

### “Argentina became the richest country in the world under Roca”

- **Story:** His presidency made Argentina literally the world’s wealthiest nation.
- **Evidence:** Argentina experienced rapid growth and later ranked very high in per-capita income estimates. Rankings depend on year, dataset, and reconstruction; they do not establish highest total wealth, equal prosperity, or causation by one president.
- **Effect:** Key element of contemporary pro-Roca mythology.

### “Roca was a dictator”

- **Story:** His government was equivalent to a twentieth-century military dictatorship.
- **Evidence:** He served constitutional terms, worked with Congress, and transferred office. Yet the electoral regime was coercive, restricted, and systematically manipulated.
- **Effect:** The label highlights exclusion but can obscure the specific mechanics of the nineteenth-century oligarchic republic.

### “Roca was only a front for Britain”

- **Story:** British finance secretly controlled him personally.
- **Evidence:** British capital strongly shaped railways, debt, trade, and public policy. No evidence located proves that Roca was a paid British agent.
- **Classification:** Conspiracy theory when stated as personal covert control; documented structural dependence when referring to the economy.

### “The anonymous mourner”

- **Story:** At Roca’s funeral, a mysterious woman wept with such intensity that his daughter realized she had been his father’s lover.
- **Circulation:** Family reminiscence and later popular biographies.
- **Evidence:** Possible connection to Guillermina de Oliveira Cézar, but contemporary confirmation is weak.
- **Effect:** Humanized Roca and sustained interest in his clandestine private life.

### “The fox”

- **Story:** Roca was universally known as *el Zorro* for cunning.
- **Evidence:** The epithet appears in later political memory; a precise contemporary point of origin is elusive.
- **Effect:** Encapsulates his image as taciturn, patient, and manipulative.

### Presidential chair mythology

A chair commonly called the “Sillón de Rivadavia” is not Rivadavia’s chair. Casa Rosada research dates the current presidential chair to approximately 1885 and Roca’s first presidency. The object became part of a retrospective myth of uninterrupted presidential succession.

## Offices, Appointments, and Honors

Principal positions included:

- Army service from 1859.
- Frontier and provincial commands during the 1860s and 1870s.
- Commander on the southern Córdoba frontier.
- General following Santa Rosa, 1874.
- Minister of War and Navy, 1878–1879.
- President of Argentina, 1880–1886.
- Minister of the Interior under Carlos Pellegrini, 1890–1891.
- National senator and president of the Senate during the 1890s.
- President of Argentina, 1898–1904.
- Extraordinary diplomatic envoy to Brazil, including 1907 and 1912.

He received domestic military and civic honors and foreign decorations. The surviving archive includes at least one Venezuelan government decoration. A definitive list would require systematic examination of the Roca archival fonds and military service file.

## Works by Julio Argentino Roca

Roca was not primarily a book author. His authentic corpus consists mainly of official messages, military orders, speeches, correspondence, travel diaries, memoranda, and proclamations. Many volumes issued under his name are posthumous editorial collections.

### Contemporary official and military works

- **Orders, reports, and correspondence on frontier operations** (1870s). Genuine official authorship or dictation; dispersed in War Ministry records and later compilations.
- **Proyecto and ministerial presentations concerning transfer of the frontier to the Río Negro** (1878). Official ministerial texts; often printed in congressional or War Ministry documents.
- **Orden del día, Carhué, 26 de abril de 1879.** Genuine military address/order associated with the opening of the principal expedition.
- **Reports on the expedition to the Río Negro** (1879). Official authorship or responsibility; some wording may have been prepared by staff.
- **Discurso del Brigadier General D. Julio A. Roca al recibirse de la Presidencia de la República ante el Congreso Argentino el 12 de octubre de 1880.** Contemporary official publication.
- **Mensajes del Presidente de la República al Honorable Congreso de la Nación** (annual, 1881–1886). Official presidential texts; institutionally drafted and delivered under Roca’s authority.
- **Mensaje al Honorable Congreso relativo a la inconversión de billetes bancarios**. Genuine official message; exact edition varies in catalogs.
- **Speech inaugurating the Mendoza railway and honoring Tomás Aragón**, 10 April 1885. Archival item described as a discourse in verse.
- **Second-presidency messages to Congress** (1899–1904). Official texts, including discussion of finances, foreign relations, labor policy, and administration.
- **Speeches connected with the Abrazo del Estrecho, Brazil, Uruguay, public works, and military ceremonies** (1898–1904). Authentic official speeches, sometimes published separately or in newspapers.
- **1904 message recommending national labor legislation.** Official presidential text.

### Correspondence and diaries

- **Private and official correspondence** with Nicolás Avellaneda, Domingo F. Sarmiento, Miguel Juárez Celman, Carlos Pellegrini, Lucio V. Mansilla, Pablo Riccheri, political governors, family members, and foreign statesmen. Genuine manuscripts; much remains unpublished.
- **Travel diaries.** Genuine archival manuscripts in the Roca family fonds; not a single standardized commercial edition.
- **Roca–Riccheri correspondence.** Selections published posthumously in *La amistad Roca–Richeri a través de su correspondencia*, edited by Rosendo Fraga, 1996.
- Letters to Sarmiento, including that of 31 July 1875, have been quoted or reproduced in historical publications.

### Posthumous and edited collections

- **Discursos, escritos y homenajes.** Various commemorative compilations; contents require edition-by-edition verification because some combine Roca’s texts with speeches about him.
- **Mensajes presidenciales de Julio A. Roca.** Parliamentary and historical reproductions of the original annual messages.
- Archival catalogs and museum publications reproduce individual letters, speeches, photographs, and orders.

### Works not by Roca

- **Soy Roca** by Félix Luna is a 1989 historical novel written in Roca’s imagined first-person voice. It is not memoir, dictation, autobiography, or ghostwritten testimony.
- Commemorative biographies titled simply *Roca* are works about him, not authored by him.
- Statements issued by ministers—most notably the Drago Doctrine—belong to their authors even when issued under his administration.

A fully exhaustive bibliography would require item-level searching of the National Library, Congressional Library, General Archive, Museo Roca, provincial newspaper collections, and nineteenth-century government printers. Many official speeches appeared as pamphlets without durable standardized titles.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Early commemoration

For much of the twentieth century, official memory emphasized:

- National territorial consolidation.
- Federalization of Buenos Aires.
- Public education.
- Secular civil institutions.
- Railway and export growth.
- Peace with Chile.
- A strong national army.
- Political stability after decades of civil war.

Monuments, streets, towns, schools, army units, and public buildings bore his name. Major memorials include the 1941 monument in Buenos Aires and the equestrian monument in Bariloche.

### Currency

Roca and the Conquest appeared on Argentina’s 100-peso banknote. The reverse reproduced Juan Manuel Blanes’s *La Conquista del Desierto*. The note transformed a military occupation scene into an everyday national icon.

Its replacement became part of a larger dispute over whether public symbols should celebrate territorial state formation or acknowledge Indigenous dispossession.

### Revision and “Desmonumentar”

From the late twentieth century, Indigenous organizations, historians, human-rights activists, and particularly **Osvaldo Bayer** campaigned to remove Roca’s monuments and names. Bayer’s *Desmonumentar a Roca* campaign proposed replacing honorific monuments with memorials to Indigenous peoples.

Monuments in Río Gallegos and elsewhere were removed or relocated; streets and schools were renamed. The Bariloche monument became a repeated site of paint, performance, protest, legal action, and municipal controversy.

### Bariloche controversy

In 2023 the proposal to move the equestrian statue from the center of Bariloche’s Civic Center produced national controversy and litigation. A court temporarily restrained alterations. Supporters called Roca a sovereign state-builder; opponents regarded the monument’s privileged location as an ongoing celebration of conquest.

The dispute is not merely about nineteenth-century facts. It concerns municipal heritage law, Indigenous presence, national identity, and control of public space.

### Rehabilitation under Javier Milei

President **Javier Milei** cited Roca approvingly during his December 2023 inauguration and his government publicly commemorated Roca in July 2024 as a leading state-builder.

This produced a visible rehabilitation within official discourse. In 2024–2025, defenders associated Roca with national sovereignty, fiscal-administrative order, and economic expansion, while critics connected that rehabilitation with denial or minimization of Indigenous state violence.

### Historical balance of influence

Roca’s durable institutional consequences include:

- A federal capital separated from Buenos Aires Province.
- A more centralized army and state bureaucracy.
- National territorial administration.
- Expansion of public schooling and civil registration.
- Consolidation of an export economy and concentrated rural landholding.
- A political machine based on governors, controlled elections, and presidential succession.
- Territorial dispossession, coerced incorporation, and demographic dispersal of Indigenous peoples.
- A model of presidential diplomacy.
- Policies of both labor investigation and political deportation.

These consequences remain subjects of empirical research and competing classification.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted record are included. Mere contemporaneity is excluded.

### Domingo Faustino Sarmiento

- **Label:** Documented fact.
- **Nature:** Correspondent, political elder, occasional supporter, later critic.
- **Dates and places:** Roca corresponded with Sarmiento by at least 1875. A letter dated 31 July 1875 defended Sarmiento’s military reputation. Sarmiento participated in national political life during Roca’s ascent and later attacked the Roca family’s patronage through the term *atalivar*.
- **Evidence:** The Roca archive contains Sarmiento correspondence; a 1875 letter is reproduced by the Academia Nacional de la Historia.
- **Qualification:** Roca implemented national education legislation building partly on Sarmiento’s earlier work, but Law 1,420 was not a simple personal collaboration between the two men.

### José Rafael Hernández

- **Label:** Reported fact requiring more precise primary confirmation.
- **Nature:** Political supporter of Roca’s 1880 candidacy and legislator within the federalization-era coalition.
- **Dates and places:** Buenos Aires political struggles, approximately 1879–1880.
- **Evidence:** Later historical accounts list José Hernández among Roca’s political supporters.
- **Qualification:** No direct Roca–Hernández correspondence was confirmed in the sources consulted, so the connection should not be overstated as friendship.

### Torcuato Antonio de Alvear

- **Label:** Documented fact.
- **Nature:** Presidential appointee and municipal administrator.
- **Dates and places:** Buenos Aires, 1883–1887. Roca appointed Torcuato de Alvear as the first mayor or *intendente* of the federal capital under the reorganized municipal system.
- **Significance:** Alvear’s urban works formed part of the capital-building program enabled by federalization.

### Francesco Tamburini

- **Label:** Documented institutional connection.
- **Nature:** Architect employed by the Argentine state during Roca’s first presidency.
- **Dates and places:** Buenos Aires, especially the mid-1880s.
- **Significance:** Tamburini worked on the Casa Rosada’s consolidation and other state architecture. The connection is governmental rather than evidence of personal friendship.

### Ceferino Namuncurá

- **Label:** Documented indirect historical connection; no verified personal meeting.
- **Nature:** Ceferino was the son of Manuel Namuncurá, whose people resisted the campaigns launched by Roca and were displaced and subordinated in their aftermath.
- **Dates and places:** Manuel’s resistance continued into 1884–1885; Ceferino was born at Chimpay in 1886.
- **Qualification:** The relationship is consequential and familial, not a documented direct encounter between Roca and Ceferino. A Río Negro legislative record places Roca at Chimpay for the railway inauguration on 1 July 1899, but it does not establish that he met the twelve-year-old Ceferino.

### Carlos Gardel

- **Label:** No documented personal connection found.
- **Explanation:** Gardel later sang works associated with labor and deportation themes, and he attended a Salesian environment sometimes connected in popular narratives with Ceferino Namuncurá. No reliable evidence consulted establishes a meeting, correspondence, collaboration, or acknowledged influence between Gardel and Roca.

### Ángel della Valle

- **Label:** Documented representational connection, not confirmed personal association.
- **Nature:** Della Valle painted *La vuelta del malón* (1892), one of the most influential images of frontier conflict during the post-Conquest period.
- **Qualification:** The painting helped shape the visual culture that justified or remembered frontier war, but no personal commission from Roca or direct relationship was established here.

### Javier Gerardo Milei

- **Label:** Documented posthumous influence.
- **Nature:** Milei publicly invoked Roca as one of Argentina’s leading presidents and quoted his 1880 inaugural formulation in December 2023; his government promoted official commemoration in 2024.
- **Qualification:** This is a posthumous political appropriation, not a lifetime relationship.

### Juan Domingo Perón, Eva Duarte de Perón, Cristina Fernández de Kirchner, and other later Argentine figures on the roster

- **Label:** No direct lifetime connection possible or found.
- **Explanation:** Later governments and movements participated in changing national narratives, currency, monuments, and state institutions, but the sources consulted did not establish sufficiently specific person-to-person influence to add them as individual connections.

### Absence finding

No evidence was found of direct, source-supported connections between Roca and the overwhelming majority of the 896-person atlas roster. Shared nationality, residence in Buenos Aires, later artistic treatment, use of institutions created during his governments, or participation in later debates about his reputation was not treated by itself as a connection.
