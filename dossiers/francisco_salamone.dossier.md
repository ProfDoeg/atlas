# Francisco Salamone: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

Francisco Salamone left very little published writing about himself or his architecture. Much of his personal archive was reportedly lost or discarded, while municipal documentation remains dispersed across numerous towns. The most reliable reconstruction therefore comes from university records, municipal files and council minutes, surviving plans, building inscriptions, government heritage inventories, family-held drawings, contemporary newspapers, later interviews with associates, and specialist architectural research.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by official records, plans, legislation, archival documentation, or strong contemporaneous evidence.
- **Reported fact:** stated by credible researchers or witnesses but not independently verified here from the underlying record.
- **Allegation or disputed claim:** asserted in litigation, political controversy, or later commentary without a conclusive supporting record.
- **Rumor or myth:** circulated as part of Salamone’s reputation but unsupported or contradicted by available evidence.
- **Interpretation:** a scholarly or critical reading rather than a biographical fact.

Conflicting figures for his output—roughly sixty, seventy, or one hundred works—usually count different things: completed buildings only; buildings plus plazas and urban furniture; or all known designs, including unbuilt projects and minor interventions.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Birth name:** Francesco Salamone.
- **Common Argentine name:** Francisco Salamone.
- **Extended form sometimes used:** Francisco Salamone D’Anna, incorporating his mother’s surname.
- **Professional designation:** “Ing. Arq. Francisco Salamone,” although his formal qualifications were in engineering. Argentine engineers of his period could legally design and direct architectural works.
- **Other titles:** engineer-architect; civil engineer; constructor; public-works designer and director.
- **Later epithets:** “el arquitecto de las pampas,” “el Gaudí de las pampas,” “el loco de las torres,” “architect of the pampas,” and “lost futurist.” These are later journalistic or professional labels, not names he is known to have used.
- **Birth:** 5 June 1897, Leonforte, central Sicily, Kingdom of Italy. Some secondary sources mistakenly give 15 June, 1898, or locate Leonforte in Catania; Leonforte is now in the province of Enna.
- **Death:** 8 August 1959, Buenos Aires, Argentina, aged sixty-two. One architectural website gives 28 August, but the specialist literature and the majority of records give 8 August.
- **Nationality:** Italian by birth; subsequently naturalized Argentine. A 1922 university record described him as an “argentino naturalizado.”
- **Occupations:** engineer, architect in professional usage, building and paving contractor, municipal-works consultant, designer, caricaturist.
- **Principal places of residence:** Leonforte; Buenos Aires; La Plata or its environs during early employment; Córdoba; the Punilla Valley; Villa María; Buenos Aires again; temporary residence or exile in Uruguay; a country or weekend property in Martínez.
- **Burial:** initially Recoleta Cemetery, Buenos Aires; his remains were reportedly transferred in 1992 to Jardín de Paz, Pilar.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and siblings

**Documented/repeated in specialist literature:** Salamone was a son of **Salvatore Salamone**, a Sicilian builder or construction tradesman, and **Antonia D’Anna**. The family’s construction background is the clearest identifiable vocational influence on him.

The five children are generally identified as:

- Josefa Salamone.
- Ángel Salamone.
- José Salamone.
- Carlos Salamone.
- Francesco/Francisco Salamone.

Salvatore transmitted building-related trades to his sons. Ángel later became Francisco’s business associate in paving work. Carlos participated in Francisco’s projects as an *sobrestante*, or works supervisor. Less has been recovered about José’s professional activity.

### Migration

The Salamone family emigrated from Italy to Argentina when Francisco was a child. Sources place the migration between **1903 and 1906**; one academic reconstruction gives 1903. They initially settled in Buenos Aires. The precise arrival date, vessel, and port-entry record were not established in the sources consulted.

### Marriage and Croft family

The fullest documented name of Salamone’s wife was **Emilia Adolfina Teresa Croft** (1906–1975), usually called **Adolfina**, **Finita**, or Adolfina Croft.

She was the only daughter of:

- **Joseph/José Oliver Croft**, a Bahía Blanca merchant associated with the firm Diego Meyer and vice-consul of the Austro-Hungarian Empire.
- **Adolfina Vlieghe**.

Secondary sources sometimes incorrectly call Croft the British or English consul. The recent specialist reconstruction identifies him more precisely as Austro-Hungarian vice-consul.

A 2024 account based on Bahía Blanca social reporting dates Francisco and Finita’s engagement to September 1924 and says their wedding occurred later that year in Buenos Aires. Most architectural biographies instead date the marriage to **1928**. The discrepancy remains unresolved without the civil or ecclesiastical marriage record.

Their four children were:

- Ricardo Salamone.
- Roberto Salamone.
- Ana María Salamone.
- Stella Maris Salamone.

Roberto later preserved some of his father’s “arquicaricaturas.” Some of the children participated in the postwar family construction company SAFRRA, although the sources consulted do not consistently identify which children held which formal roles.

### Family property

Adolfina was the registered owner of the Salamones’ Martínez property. Municipal cadastral research identified plans captioned:

- “Hormigón armado en la propiedad de la Sra. Adolfina C. de Salamone.”
- “Casa de campo ‘Villa Finita.’”
- “Casa de campo ‘Villa Finita’… Ampliación.”

The house stood at approximately Pueyrredón 1338, Martínez. “Finita” was Adolfina’s nickname. The original stage appears to date from 1940, with a later addition. The property is useful evidence that the family’s wealth from the provincial building period was invested in real estate.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1897–c.1916

Salamone was born into a family already connected with construction. After migration, he grew up in Buenos Aires and entered the city’s leading industrial school, the **Escuela Industrial de la Nación Otto Krause**.

He graduated as a **maestro mayor de obras** or technical master builder. This training combined drafting, building technology, structures, and practical construction. It helps explain the later unity in his work between architectural form, structural experimentation, mechanical function, signage, furniture, paving, and minute hardware.

After graduation, he reportedly worked on construction projects in La Plata. The nature and dates of those jobs have not been fully reconstructed.

## Education and Formation

### University studies

Salamone reportedly began higher studies at the **Universidad Nacional de La Plata** and then transferred to the **Universidad Nacional de Córdoba**.

The most detailed chronology gives:

- **December 1920:** diploma as **Ingeniero Arquitecto** or engineer qualified for architectural practice, Faculty of Exact, Physical and Natural Sciences, National University of Córdoba.
- **August 1922:** sworn in as **Ingeniero Civil**. The university record described him as “Francisco Salamone, argentino naturalizado, soltero, de 25 años de edad.”

Some compressed biographies say he graduated in 1917 or obtained both degrees in 1920. The dated university reconstruction—1920 and 1922—is better supported.

His engineering qualification was professionally important. Salamone did not rely on architecture solely as facade composition: he coordinated structures, drainage, paving, municipal machinery, reinforced concrete, spatial circulation, sanitary requirements, and construction logistics.

### Teachers and formative influences

No securely documented individual teacher or mentor emerged from the reviewed sources. The following influences are better treated at different evidentiary levels:

- **Documented formative environment:** Otto Krause technical education, Córdoba engineering culture, municipal modernization, construction work with relatives.
- **Strong stylistic interpretation:** Art Deco, functionalism, reinforced-concrete modernism, German or Central European Expressionism, Italian Futurism, Constructivism, neocolonial architecture, monumental classicism, and graphic design.
- **Repeated but unproven personal influence:** Fritz Lang’s 1927 film *Metropolis*. Researchers and journalists frequently compare its city imagery with Salamone’s towers, but no writing or interview by Salamone confirming that he consciously used the film as a source has been found.
- **Unverified influence claims:** Bauhaus, Czech Cubism, Fascist Italian architecture, Soviet Constructivism, Hollywood scenery, Las Vegas, and Disneyland. These can illuminate visual resemblance but do not demonstrate study, travel, or ideological adherence.

There is no evidence in the consulted record that Salamone studied in Europe after emigrating, met leading European modernists, or joined an architectural avant-garde.

### Early prizes and competitions

The record is inconsistent:

- Some biographies state that in **1919** he received medals for designs shown in Milan and Barcelona.
- A later academic publication identifies a **gold medal and grand prize at the 1929 Barcelona International Exposition**.
- The 1919/1929 discrepancy may reflect conflation of separate exhibitions or repeated copying of an erroneous date. No award certificate or official exhibition catalogue was located in this research.

Documented professional registrations reported in an Italian exhibition catalogue include:

- **Centro Argentino de Ingenieros**, registration no. 605, in 1922.
- **Sociedad Central de Arquitectos**, registration no. 957, in 1924.

## Early Career in Córdoba, c.1921–1933

### Architectural practice

After graduating, Salamone opened a practice in Córdoba. He associated with a local constructor identified as Ferreyra and designed or built residential projects. Early and incompletely documented work includes:

- An apartment building for Ricardo Villela at Deán Funes 168/174, Córdoba; its realization or survival is uncertain.
- Residences in Córdoba.
- A cinema in Valle Hermoso, attributed mainly through oral history.
- Housing or land-development work in the Punilla Valley, including areas called El Dominador and El Rincón; attribution is not uniformly documented.
- A proposed *Panteón del Maestro*.
- Competition projects for Córdoba public and commercial buildings.

### Competitions and professional dispute

In 1924 he reportedly:

- Placed second in a competition to design the cover of the journal of the Sociedad Central de Arquitectos.
- Received a special mention in the competition for an extension to the National University of Córdoba.

In 1925 he submitted a design for the South Market in Córdoba.

In 1926 he published or circulated a sharp open letter criticizing the jury decision in the competition for the Córdoba Stock Exchange. Later accounts say he accused the organizers of fraudulent handling and that the dispute damaged his standing with the Sociedad Central de Arquitectos and its influential journal.

**Evidentiary assessment:** his competitive activity and polemical letter are reported in specialist reconstructions. The broader story that the Society deliberately erased him from architectural history for decades is plausible as professional memory but has not been established through institutional disciplinary records. His association with the politically discredited 1930s government and the architectural academy’s later stylistic preferences are alternative or complementary explanations for his neglect.

### Political activity

In **1923**, Salamone sought the Radical nomination for provincial senator for Córdoba’s Punilla Department.

- **Documented/reported fact:** he was active in the **Unión Cívica Radical** and failed to secure election or nomination.
- An Italian catalogue says his oratory and progressive arguments failed against a party apparatus controlled by Amadeo Sabattini.
- He then withdrew from sustained active politics.

This episode is important because it conflicts with later claims that Salamone was straightforwardly a fascist ideologue. Membership in the UCR in 1923 does not prove his later views, but it does show a political history distinct from Fresco’s conservative corporatism.

### Artistic work: “arquicaricaturas”

Around 1924, and apparently again in the early 1930s, Salamone made angular, faceted caricature portraits of architects, professionals, politicians, and public figures. He called them **“arquicaricaturas.”**

A contemporary notice recalled by architect Ángel Lo Celso reported an exhibition in Córdoba’s Confitería Oriental. A 1932 newspaper item in *La Voz del Interior* reportedly reviewed another exhibition favorably. Several drawings remained with his family, particularly Roberto Salamone.

These are among the only known autonomous artworks securely attributable to him. Their geometric fragmentation has been compared with the faceted Christs and angel figures later integrated into his cemeteries.

## Villa María, Las Varillas, and the Transition to Monumental Public Work, 1933–1936

### Villa María

Under mayor **Eugenio Parajón Ortiz**, Salamone became involved in an ambitious municipal modernization program. His assignments included or contemplated:

- Urban cadastral survey.
- Leveling and drainage study.
- Street and boulevard paving.
- Landscaping or ornamentation of boulevards España, Argentino, Vélez Sarsfield, Italia, and Córdoba.
- Plaza Centenario.
- Plaza Independencia and Plaza San Martín projects.
- Cemetery park or improvements.
- Municipal slaughterhouse.
- Municipal palace or administrative project.
- Possibly plans connected with the public-assistance building, although that attribution is not certified.

The **Plaza Centenario** is sometimes dated to a 1925 initial remodeling and elsewhere to the 1930s municipal campaign. This may indicate more than one phase of work.

The **Matadero Modelo** was designed under Salamone’s direction and constructed by engineer Antenor Villagra. It was inaugurated unfinished on **16 February 1936**. Its design reportedly changed from a neocolonial scheme at tender stage to an Art Deco treatment during construction.

### Failures and controversy in Villa María

The Villa María works generated genuine contemporary criticism:

- The paving deteriorated prematurely and was repaired during Salomón Deiver’s 1940–1943 administration, then reconstructed again in 1953.
- The slaughterhouse reportedly exceeded its initial cost estimate.
- Municipal commissioner Juan Blanco closed or inactivated it for alterations on **13 March 1936**, only weeks after inauguration.
- Later historical work describes public protests, denunciations, questions about cost and transparency, and scandals involving the Parajón Ortiz administration.

**Evidentiary distinction:** these matters document defects, overruns, and political controversy around works in which Salamone participated. They do not, by themselves, prove that Salamone embezzled money or personally engaged in criminal corruption. The consulted material did not identify a conviction of Salamone arising from Villa María.

### Las Varillas

Salamone designed the municipal building at **Las Varillas**, begun in 1934 and completed around 1936. It marks the transition from his Córdoba practice to the monumental towered municipal architecture for which he became known.

### Alta Gracia

A slaughterhouse or water-tower complex at Alta Gracia is often attributed to Salamone and described as an early Art Deco work. Catalogues warn that it may instead be the work of **Antonio Medina Allende**. It should remain classified as a disputed attribution.

## The Buenos Aires Province Campaign, 1936–1940

### Political and financial framework

Salamone’s most productive period coincided with the governorship of **Manuel Fresco**, conservative governor of Buenos Aires Province from 1936 until federal intervention in 1940.

The program operated through provincial and municipal public-works legislation, particularly **Law 4017 of 1928**, which authorized municipal public-works bonds. Municipalities without large technical staffs could obtain financing, call tenders, and contract designers, directors, and construction firms.

Fresco’s administration pursued highly visible modernization: roads, health and sanitary works, schools, municipal buildings, squares, cemeteries, and slaughterhouses. Its rhetoric emphasized state authority, order, labor, hygiene, “Dios, Patria y Hogar,” and the conversion of rural settlements into modern towns.

Fresco openly admired aspects of European authoritarian regimes and governed during Argentina’s **Década Infame**, characterized by electoral manipulation. In 1940, President Roberto M. Ortiz ordered federal intervention in Buenos Aires Province after the notoriously fraudulent election associated with Fresco’s intended successor, Alberto Barceló.

### How Salamone obtained the work

Later stories often say Fresco personally selected Salamone and gave him unlimited authority. The surviving municipal record presents a more complicated picture:

- Salamone had a relationship with the provincial political network and benefited from a government committed to large-scale public construction.
- Municipalities formally issued contracts and tenders.
- In Adolfo Alsina, council minutes record an $800,000 national-currency loan and a contested tender. The opposition denied a quorum; officials used fines and police compulsion to assemble the council. Three firms competed, with **S.A.D.O.P.** receiving the work as the lowest bidder. Salamone served as technical director.
- His capacity to produce designs, estimates, and completed works rapidly made him attractive to small municipalities.
- His established administrative and technical organization allowed him to supervise many geographically separated projects.
- He often worked as designer and technical director rather than as the sole builder. Different construction companies executed the contracts.

A 2025 study calculates that approximately 30 percent of the contracts examined were awarded in 1936 and 60 percent in 1937. Activity fell sharply after 1938.

### Scale and logistics

Depending on counting method, between 1936 and 1940 Salamone:

- Designed about one hundred municipal buildings, spaces, or interventions.
- Completed approximately sixty to seventy works.
- Worked across seventeen to twenty-eight partidos or municipalities.
- Operated at eighteen or more sites separated by hundreds of kilometers.

A persistent report states that an aircraft was made available to him so he could inspect widely scattered construction sites. This is credible and repeated by researchers, but aircraft logs or ownership records were not located.

His office used standardized administrative methods and recurring functional types while giving major buildings individually recognizable towers and symbolic profiles. His brother Carlos acted as a supervisor on some sites. Sculptor **Santiago José Chierico** executed several religious figures and reliefs from Salamone’s designs.

### Principal architectural types

#### Municipal palaces and delegations

The city halls established the visual dominance of the secular municipality. Clock towers frequently rose above church towers, presenting timekeeping, bureaucracy, and public authority as the organizing center of town life.

Major examples include:

- Adolfo Gonzales Chaves.
- Alberti.
- Carhué, Partido de Adolfo Alsina.
- Coronel Pringles.
- Guaminí.
- Laprida.
- Pellegrini.
- Rauch.
- Tornquist.
- Vedia.
- Tres Lomas.
- Chascomús, whose attribution and precise scope appear in the national heritage decree.
- Delegations at Cacharí, Chillar, Casbas, Garré, Laguna Alsina/Bonifacio, Alberdi, El Dorado, Los Pinos, San Agustín, Miranda, Rivera, San Miguel Arcángel, Saldungaray, and other settlements.

The **Carhué Municipal Palace**, inaugurated 3 December 1938, is frequently treated as the most elaborate example, combining a large clock tower, functional planning, geometric ornament, and theatrical massing.

#### Slaughterhouses

Municipal slaughterhouses stood on town margins for sanitary and logistical reasons. They incorporated open or flexible working areas, reinforced concrete, water systems, ventilation, animal circulation, and towers visible from afar.

Major examples or remains include:

- Azul.
- Balcarce.
- Carhué/Villa Epecuén.
- Chillar.
- Coronel Pringles.
- Guaminí.
- Laprida.
- Rivera.
- Salliqueló.
- Saldungaray.
- Tres Lomas.
- Vedia.
- Alberdi.
- Alem.
- Villa María in Córdoba.

The towers have often been read as symbolic knives. The Coronel Pringles tower has a thin fin resembling a blade; Guaminí’s tiered forms suggest machinery or a spacecraft. Whether Salamone intended every tower as a literal knife is not documented.

#### Cemeteries and religious sculpture

The cemetery entrances are among his most dramatic works:

- **Azul:** monumental portal, giant black “R.I.P.” letters, and a colossal faceted angel commonly identified as the Archangel Gabriel or an “angel of death.”
- **Saldungaray:** circular portal framing a monumental crucified Christ.
- **Laprida:** towering cross and full-size Christ, with additional funerary structures.
- **Coronel Pringles:** monumental cemetery cross and related works.
- **Balcarce, Carhué, Guaminí, and other towns:** crosses, portals, chapels, or related religious interventions.

Santiago Chierico physically modeled or executed a number of these sculptures. The 2014 federal decree specifically recognizes the Laprida Christ as created by Salamone together with Chierico.

One Carhué roadside Christ reportedly collapsed soon after installation because its pedestal was inadequate. It was replaced; the damaged original was repaired and moved to an estate near Ramón Arano. This is a documented local account and an example of a structural failure within the broader program.

#### Plazas, parks, and street furniture

Salamone treated public squares as integrated design systems. Work included:

- Paving patterns.
- Geometric fountains and planters.
- Benches.
- Lamp standards.
- Pergolas.
- Flagpoles and monuments.
- Entry portals.
- Signage and lettering.
- Door handles, fittings, and furniture.

Important ensembles survive at Azul, Balcarce, Coronel Pringles, Guaminí, Laprida, Pellegrini, Rauch, Tornquist, Tres Lomas, Vedia, and Alberti.

At **Azul’s Plaza San Martín**, black, white, and gray zigzag paving converges on a central equestrian monument to José de San Martín. Laboratory research has examined the original tiles and materials.

### Materials and technology

Salamone’s preferred medium was reinforced concrete—later romanticized as **“piedra líquida,”** or liquid stone—combined with brick masonry, plaster, stone veneers, metalwork, terrazzo or manufactured tiles, glass, and sculptural cement.

Technical characteristics included:

- Towers constructed as signs as much as occupied space.
- Thin fins and cantilevers.
- Curved or circular concrete elements.
- Faceted monumental sculpture.
- Open-span industrial interiors.
- Custom concrete furniture.
- Integrated clocks and mechanical systems.
- Graphic use of letters and abbreviations.
- Repeated modular elements adaptable to different municipalities.

The scale of his production depended on modern materials, repeatable drawings, delegated site supervision, and a centralized office capable of tender and cost documentation.

### Financial success

The provincial campaign made Salamone affluent. Evidence includes:

- Purchase of a three-story *petit hôtel* in Barrio Norte, Buenos Aires, usually identified as Uruguay 1237 or 1239.
- Transfer of his family residence, office, and business operations to that building.
- Purchase of land on or near Pueyrredón Street in Martínez.
- Construction and enlargement of Villa Finita.
- Capacity to form later family companies and invest in apartment buildings.

No complete balance sheets, bank records, or probate inventory were located. Claims that he accumulated “a great fortune” are reasonable but remain imprecise.

### Contraction after 1938

Several factors reduced the program:

- European tensions increased the cost of imported materials.
- Provincial municipal bonds fell in value, reportedly reaching a 40-percent discount by 1940.
- Existing contracts were reduced.
- Some authorized projects never began.
- Political conflict between Ortiz and Fresco weakened the provincial regime.
- The 1940 federal intervention ended the governmental framework.

Thus the end of the campaign cannot be explained solely as punishment for Salamone, flight from scandal, or immediate political proscription.

## Later Career, Tucumán Proceedings, Uruguay, and Return, 1940–1959

### Move to Buenos Aires

In 1940 Salamone settled permanently in Buenos Aires with his family. He entered a new professional phase with his brother Ángel, concentrating on paving and civil works.

He continued to practice until his death, although he never again received a program comparable to the Buenos Aires municipal campaign.

### Tucumán paving case

This episode is frequently simplified into a story that Salamone was accused, fled to Uruguay, and was completely cleared by 1945. The surviving reconstruction is more complicated.

- In 1940 Salamone was hired as technical adviser to the **Oficina General de Pavimentación Municipal** in San Miguel de Tucumán.
- His function was to control a contract for paving approximately **600 suburban blocks**.
- In 1943, after the military coup, the municipal legal adviser initiated criminal proceedings involving former mayor **Lozano Muñoz**, Salamone, and contractor **W. Boccazzi**.
- On his lawyer’s advice, Salamone went to Uruguay, reportedly to avoid preventive detention.
- He remained there approximately two years.
- Biographical sources say he returned after being acquitted or having the charges dismissed and that he successfully pursued payment of professional fees.
- However, archival research published in 2025 states that on **26 July 1947**, Tucumán investigating judge **Marcelino Suñen** issued an adverse ruling against Lozano Muñoz, Salamone, and Boccazzi.

**Unresolved question:** the accessible sources do not reconcile the claimed earlier exoneration with the adverse 1947 ruling. It is possible that the 1947 decision was interlocutory, later reversed, concerned a different procedural stage, or was followed by dismissal. Without the complete court file, it is unsafe to state either that Salamone was convicted or that every charge had conclusively disappeared by 1945. No final criminal conviction was found.

### SAFRRA and property-horizontal development

After returning to Argentina, Salamone formed **SAFRRA** with members of his family. Sources expand the name inconsistently or do not expand it at all; it should therefore be retained as the company’s sigla unless corporate records are recovered.

The company undertook:

- Urban paving and road work.
- Apartment houses.
- Reinforced-concrete construction.
- Projects enabled by Argentina’s 1948 horizontal-property law, which made individual ownership of apartments practicable.

Known late work includes the apartment building at **Avenida Alvear 1917**, dated around 1950. Other rationalist apartment buildings and a 1954 private house in Mar del Plata are attributed to him. His late style was comparatively restrained and lacks the cemetery and slaughterhouse monumentalism of the 1930s.

A 1953 project or monument commemorating **José de San Martín** in Mar del Plata appears in work catalogues, but its realized status requires further archival verification.

### Social circle and home

In his later years Salamone held or participated in gatherings at his Barrio Norte house. Reported visitors included:

- Historian **Ricardo Levene**.
- Writer **Arturo Capdevila**.
- Physician and politician **Tiburcio Padilla**.
- Archbishop **Fermín Emilio Lafitte**.

Former associate Corradino recalled Salamone as sociable and “campechano” but forceful and accustomed to directing many workers. Another recollection describes a large cheese and whisky being offered to people waiting at his office while Salamone remained in meetings.

The office reportedly displayed his Art Deco caricatures rather than photographs of his celebrated provincial buildings. This suggests either personal disengagement from the 1930s work, a change in professional identity, or simply a decorative preference; the evidence does not permit a firm conclusion.

### Health and death

Salamone suffered from **diabetes** and repeated heart attacks. One account claims eight infarctions, but the precise number is not independently documented.

He died in Buenos Aires on **8 August 1959**, surrounded by his immediate family. Diabetes and cardiovascular disease are the reported medical context; no death certificate was examined.

His death received little public notice. Later narratives say it was overshadowed by the death of Archbishop Lafitte and by the arrival of an international celebrity—identified inconsistently as Greta Garbo or Marlene Dietrich. Lafitte did die on 8 August 1959; the celebrity detail varies and belongs to the mythology of Salamone’s neglected death unless corroborated by contemporary newspapers.

- Barrio Norte *petit hôtel*, Uruguay Street, used as residence, office, and company headquarters.

### Patents and inventions

No verified patent issued to Francisco Salamone was located. His innovations were chiefly design, constructional adaptation, office organization, reinforced-concrete expression, and integration of municipal functions—not documented patented inventions.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Politics

The most securely documented personal political action is his unsuccessful 1923 candidacy within the Unión Cívica Radical.

His later public career depended on municipalities participating in Fresco’s conservative public-works program. That professional relationship does not establish party membership or ideological agreement.

No private political diary, manifesto, party card, public defense of Fascism, correspondence with Fascist officials, or record of membership in an Argentine or Italian Fascist organization was located.

### Religion and symbolism

Salamone designed explicitly Catholic imagery: monumental crucifixions, angels, cemetery chapels, and crosses. His social relationship with Archbishop Lafitte may indicate Catholic connections.

Nevertheless, no personal devotional writing, formal religious office, or reliable evidence of theological beliefs was found. His religious structures can be documented as public and funerary commissions without assuming the intensity or character of his private faith.

### Artistic position

No architectural manifesto or theoretical treatise by Salamone is known. Scholars consequently reconstruct his ideas from buildings and drawings.

Recurring characteristics include:

- Monumental vertical towers placed against the horizontal pampas.
- Geometric abstraction.
- Graphic symbolism and enormous letters.
- Fusion of functional planning with theatrical facades.
- Repetition with variation.
- Integration of architecture, sculpture, landscape, furnishings, and civic ceremony.
- Deliberate contrast with low-scale nineteenth-century town fabric.
- Celebration of machinery, hygiene, administration, and municipal presence.

Critical classifications include Art Deco, Futurism, Expressionism, rationalism, functionalism, neocolonial work, “eclectic modernity,” and Mario Sabugo’s phrase “futurismo populista bonaerense.” No single label adequately covers the work.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Salvatore Salamone

Father and foundational construction influence. The relationship is documented at family level, although no correspondence survives in the reviewed sources.

### Ángel and Carlos Salamone

Ángel became a paving-business associate; Carlos served as a works supervisor. Their participation made the provincial output partly a family enterprise.

### Adolfina “Finita” Croft

Spouse, property owner, and participant in the family’s social and business world. Villa Finita was named for her; cadastral plans carry both spouses’ signatures in their respective capacities.

### Manuel Fresco

Governor during Salamone’s extraordinary public-works period. Their exact personal closeness is disputed. Some accounts call Fresco his patron or *mecenas*; municipal documentation shows contracts and tenders mediated through local governments rather than a single direct gubernatorial commission.

### Municipal officials

Mayors, commissioners, and councils were essential patrons. Among the documented political environments were Eugenio Parajón Ortiz in Villa María and the contested council proceedings in Adolfo Alsina.

### Santiago José Chierico

The best-documented artistic collaborator. Chierico translated Salamone’s designs into monumental sculpture, including the Laprida Christ and related works.

### Professional rivals and institutional opponents

Salamone competed with numerous architects, engineers, and contracting companies. His strongest reported institutional conflict was with the Sociedad Central de Arquitectos after his challenge to a competition jury. No single personal “enemy” can be established from the available record.

### Late intellectual circle

Ricardo Levene, Arturo Capdevila, Tiburcio Padilla, and Fermín Lafitte were reported visitors at his Buenos Aires home. The nature and frequency of each friendship are not fully documented.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### 1. Villa María construction controversy

**Status: documented defects and political controversy; personal criminal wrongdoing unproven.**

Paving failed prematurely, the slaughterhouse required immediate alteration, and costs and contracting were publicly questioned. These events damaged Salamone’s local position and coincided with accusations against the municipal administration. No court judgment convicting him of fraud was found.

### 2. Preferential access to Fresco

**Status: disputed claim.**

The scale of Salamone’s success encouraged claims that Fresco personally selected or enriched him. Their political association is real at the level of the program. Yet tenders, competing bidders, municipal votes, and locally executed contracts are documented. A personal patronage arrangement may have existed, but it cannot replace the tender record as an explanation.

### 3. Fascism

**Status: allegation and later interpretation; not established as personal organizational affiliation.**

Fresco’s corporatist and pro-Fascist sympathies are well documented. Salamone’s monumental architecture was built within that government’s civic program and can be analyzed as state propaganda.

Evidence against simply identifying Salamone as a Fascist includes:

- His earlier UCR activity.
- Absence of pro-Fascist writings or organizational records.
- Lack of proof that Fresco directly dictated his forms.
- The international breadth of his stylistic sources.

Evidence supporting a political reading includes:

- Monumentality serving an authoritarian and fraud-tainted government.
- Civic towers embodying centralized state authority.
- Public commissions carrying Fresco-era social rhetoric.
- Formal parallels with interwar authoritarian architecture.

The justified conclusion is institutional association and ideological availability of the architecture, not proven personal Fascist conviction.

### 4. Tucumán criminal proceedings

**Status: documented investigation and adverse 1947 judicial ruling; ultimate disposition unresolved.**

The case arose from supervision of a 600-block paving contract. Salamone went to Uruguay during the proceedings. Biographies say he was cleared; newer archival research identifies an adverse ruling dated 26 July 1947. No final conviction has been demonstrated.

### 5. Professional ostracism

**Status: reported interpretation.**

His conflict with the Sociedad Central de Arquitectos, association with Fresco, distance from Buenos Aires academic networks, and eclectic style may all have contributed to decades of neglect. The stronger legend—that the profession formally banned discussion of him—has not been documented.

### 6. Wealth and corruption narratives

**Status: wealth documented in property; corrupt acquisition not established.**

He bought valuable real estate after receiving numerous public contracts. That shows commercial success, not by itself illicit enrichment. No hidden-company network, offshore structure, money-laundering allegation, or documented unexplained fortune was found.

### Dedicated recent adversarial search, 2024–2026

Searches combining Salamone’s name with scandal, fraud, corruption, accusation, lawsuit, conspiracy, Fascism, criticism, and recent-year filters produced:

- Renewed academic documentation of Villa María defects and protests.
- The 2025 archival identification of the 1947 Tucumán ruling.
- Continued debate over whether “Fascist architect” is an accurate label.
- Preservation controversies involving neglected, altered, or endangered buildings.
- No new criminal investigation, lawsuit against his estate, documented fraud network, or credible new allegation of personal political violence.
- No evidence of intelligence-service connections, imprisonment, homicide, abuse, addiction, or money laundering.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The Gaudí of the Pampas”

**Story:** Salamone was an Argentine equivalent of Antoni Gaudí.

**Origin and circulation:** tourism publicity, newspapers, and exhibitions, especially during the twenty-first-century revival.

**Evidence:** both produced highly individual landmark architecture and later became cultural-tourism icons.

**Against:** there is no documented relationship, direct influence, shared structural philosophy, or close stylistic equivalence.

**Effect:** increased international recognition while obscuring Salamone’s engineering, Art Deco, and Argentine municipal context.

### “The madman of the towers”

**Story:** colleagues called him *el loco de las torres* because of his repeated monumental towers.

**Status:** reported professional nickname; its earliest source has not been identified.

**Effect:** contributed to the image of an isolated eccentric genius.

### Fresco’s handpicked architect

**Story:** Fresco personally commissioned Salamone to remake the pampas and gave him unchecked funds and an airplane.

**Evidence for:** extraordinary concentration of work under Fresco; later reports of a personal relationship; stylistic consistency with the government’s monumental ambitions; aircraft story.

**Evidence against/supplement:** individual municipalities conducted documented tender and borrowing procedures; contractors competed; some opposition councils actively resisted.

**Effect:** fused Salamone’s identity with Fresco and the Década Infame.

### Secret Fascist or Francoist

**Story:** Salamone was a committed Fascist, Mussolini admirer, or Franco sympathizer whose buildings encoded totalitarian symbols.

**Evidence:** political context and visual analogies.

**Against:** no membership, correspondence, speech, manifesto, or direct profession of Fascist or Francoist belief has emerged; earlier Radical political activity is documented.

**Effect:** helped produce both hostile neglect and later cult fascination.

### Buildings inspired directly by *Metropolis*

**Story:** Fritz Lang’s film supplied the visual program for Salamone’s towers.

**Evidence:** striking formal similarities and the film’s availability after 1927.

**Against:** no statement by Salamone confirms it.

**Effect:** established a durable retrofuturist and science-fiction reading of his architecture.

### Esoteric or Masonic codes

**Story:** the geometric crosses, circles, stars, lettering, axes, and towers contain secret Masonic or occult messages.

**Status:** rumor or speculative interpretation. No lodge record, esoteric writing, or consistent decoded program was found.

**Effect:** fuels tourism, photography, comics, and conspiracy-oriented readings.

### The supernatural pampas

Salamone’s abandoned slaughterhouses and cemetery portals are commonly described as alien landing sites, spacecraft, Gotham City sets, or structures from another civilization.

These are cultural metaphors, not historical claims. They became historically significant through photography, film, blogs, tourism, and fiction.

### The vanished master archive

**Story:** the family threw away his entire archive after decades of neglect.

**Evidence:** local accounts say substantial material deteriorated and was discarded; only limited family drawings survived.

**Qualification:** municipal plans, photographs, technical files, and family items remain dispersed, and the Province is now assembling a digital Salamone collection. “Entire archive” is therefore an exaggeration, although major loss is credible.

### Sixty buildings in forty months

This formula captures the speed of the campaign but varies by definition. Counts range from more than sixty completed buildings to around seventy completed works and roughly one hundred designed spaces or structures. It should not be treated as an exact audited total.

## Works by Francisco Salamone

Salamone was principally a designer and builder, not an author. No substantial book, manifesto, diary, memoir, theoretical essay, or technical treatise by him is known.

### Visual and documentary works

- **Arquicaricaturas**, c.1924–1932: faceted caricatures and portrait drawings; genuine authorship, partly family-preserved.
- Competition drawings for the Sociedad Central de Arquitectos journal cover, 1924.
- Competition design for extension of the Universidad Nacional de Córdoba, 1924.
- Córdoba South Market competition design, 1925.
- Open letter criticizing the Córdoba Stock Exchange competition decision, 1926; reported in specialist literature, but full text was not recovered here.
- Architectural plans, estimates, tender submissions, technical specifications, correspondence, and site drawings held in municipal or provincial collections.
- Designs for municipal furniture, lettering, clocks, paving, fountains, monuments, sculpture, and hardware.
- Designs executed sculpturally with Santiago Chierico; these should be treated as collaborative works, not sculptures carved solely by Salamone.

### Selected built and designed oeuvre

#### Córdoba Province

- Early residences and apartment projects, Córdoba city.
- Valle Hermoso cinema and housing developments, disputed or orally attributed.
- Plaza Centenario, Villa María.
- Villa María paving, boulevard, drainage, cadastral, cemetery, plaza, and slaughterhouse works.
- Las Varillas Municipal Palace.
- Alta Gracia slaughterhouse/water tower, disputed attribution.

#### Buenos Aires Province

- Municipal palaces, delegations, or civic ensembles at Adolfo Gonzales Chaves, Alberti, Carhué, Coronel Pringles, Guaminí, Laprida, Pellegrini, Rauch, Tornquist, Tres Lomas, Vedia, Saldungaray, Cacharí, Chillar, Casbas, Garré, Bonifacio/Laguna Alsina, Alberdi, El Dorado, Los Pinos, San Agustín, Miranda, Rivera, and other settlements.
- Cemetery portals, crosses, chapels, or sculptures at Azul, Laprida, Saldungaray, Coronel Pringles, Balcarce, Carhué, and related sites.
- Slaughterhouses at Azul, Balcarce, Carhué/Villa Epecuén, Chillar, Coronel Pringles, Guaminí, Laprida, Salliqueló, Saldungaray, Tres Lomas, Vedia, Alberdi, Alem, Rivera, and others.

- Villa Finita, Martínez, c.1940 and later enlargement.

Catalogues identify park portals in Santa Fe and Venado Tuerto, including one identical or closely related to the Alberti park gate. Attributions and dates should be checked against municipal plan archives. The Juan de Garay Municipal Park gate in Santa Fe was reportedly demolished in 1987.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

For decades Salamone remained familiar locally but marginal in Argentine architectural history. Contributing factors included political association with Fresco, the remoteness of the buildings, loss and dispersal of records, deterioration of slaughterhouses, the profession’s preference for more canonical modernism, and his absence from influential architectural publications.

Recovery accelerated from the late twentieth century through:

- Photographic surveys.
- University research.
- Municipal preservation campaigns.
- Tourism routes.
- Documentary film.
- Exhibitions.
- Heritage legislation.
- Digital mapping and social media.

### Heritage recognition

- **Buenos Aires Provincial Law 12,854:** declared Salamone’s provincial public works cultural heritage.
- **National Decree 1138/2014:** declared numerous works national historic monuments or properties of national historical and artistic interest.

The decree protects ensembles in Adolfo Alsina, Adolfo Gonzales Chaves, Alberti, Azul, Balcarce, Chascomús, Coronel Pringles, Guaminí, Laprida, Leandro N. Alem, Pellegrini, Rauch, Salliqueló, Tornquist, and Tres Lomas.

### Preservation problems

The surviving works range from functioning municipal buildings and active slaughterhouses to abandoned ruins.

Threats include:

- Concrete corrosion.
- Water penetration.
- Biological growth.
- Incompatible repainting and alteration.
- Loss of original windows, furniture, clocks, fixtures, and tiles.
- Abandonment after changes in slaughterhouse technology.
- Flood damage at Villa Epecuén.
- Municipal archival disposal.
- Restoration that preserves silhouette while replacing original material.

Villa Epecuén’s 1985 inundation transformed the Carhué-area slaughterhouse ruins into part of a dark-tourism landscape. This posthumous setting intensified the science-fiction and apocalyptic readings of Salamone.

### Cultural influence

His buildings now influence:

- Architectural tourism.
- Photography.
- Comics and graphic novels.
- Film and television locations.
- Science-fiction imagery.
- Regional identity and municipal branding.
- Heritage conservation.
- Contemporary debates over monumentality, state power, and noncanonical modernism.

His reception has shifted from obscure provincial engineer, to “Fascist architect,” to cult modernist, and finally to a more complicated figure situated between municipal modernization, political propaganda, industrial function, entrepreneurial organization, and personal artistic invention.

## Chronology

- **5 June 1897:** Francesco Salamone born in Leonforte, Sicily, to Salvatore Salamone and Antonia D’Anna.
- **1903–1906:** family emigrates to Argentina and settles initially in Buenos Aires.
- **c.1910s:** studies at the Escuela Industrial Otto Krause; qualifies as maestro mayor de obras.
- **c.1916–1919:** reportedly works on construction in La Plata; begins higher studies.
- **1919 or 1929:** medals or major prize connected with Milan and Barcelona exhibitions; exact date and event disputed.
- **December 1920:** receives engineering/architectural qualification from the National University of Córdoba.
- **1921–1922:** begins Córdoba practice and early residential work.
- **August 1922:** sworn in as civil engineer; recorded as a naturalized Argentine.
- **1923:** unsuccessful UCR political candidacy in Punilla.
- **1924:** joins or is registered with the Sociedad Central de Arquitectos; wins recognition in design competitions; produces arquicaricaturas.
- **September 1924:** reported engagement to Adolfina Croft; one source dates the marriage to late 1924.
- **1925:** competition and municipal designs in Córdoba; early Villa María plaza chronology sometimes begins here.
- **1926:** publicly challenges the Córdoba Stock Exchange competition decision.
- **1928:** conventional date for marriage to Emilia Adolfina Teresa “Finita” Croft.
- **Late 1920s–early 1930s:** works in Córdoba and the Punilla Valley; exhibits caricatures.
- **1932:** documented press notice of a Córdoba arquicaricature exhibition.
- **1933–1936:** Villa María cadastral, paving, plaza, cemetery, and slaughterhouse work.
- **1934:** work begins on Las Varillas Municipal Palace.
- **16 February 1936:** Villa María slaughterhouse inaugurated unfinished.
- **13 March 1936:** slaughterhouse closed temporarily for alterations.
- **1936:** Salamone begins concentrated Buenos Aires Province municipal campaign under the framework of Fresco-era public works.
- **1936–1937:** first major municipal, cemetery, slaughterhouse, plaza, and delegation contracts; highest rate of new awards.
- **1937–1938:** Azul, Laprida, Coronel Pringles, Guaminí, Carhué, Balcarce, Rauch, Tornquist, Saldungaray, and related work advances.
- **3 December 1938:** Carhué Municipal Palace inaugurated.
- **1938–1940:** contracts decline because of costs, bond depreciation, cancellations, and political crisis.
- **1940:** Fresco government removed by federal intervention; Salamone relocates to Buenos Aires.
- **1940:** begins Villa Finita in Martínez; undertakes Tucumán paving-supervision assignment.
- **1943:** criminal proceedings initiated concerning the Tucumán paving contract; Salamone goes to Uruguay.
- **c.1943–1945:** resides in Uruguay.
- **c.1945:** returns to Buenos Aires; biographies report acquittal or dismissal, though later judicial chronology remains unresolved.
- **26 July 1947:** Judge Marcelino Suñen issues an adverse ruling involving Salamone, Lozano Muñoz, and W. Boccazzi.
- **1948 onward:** SAFRRA develops paving and apartment-building projects under the horizontal-property regime.
- **c.1950:** Avenida Alvear 1917 apartment building.
- **c.1953:** San Martín commemorative project in Mar del Plata, realization uncertain.
- **1954:** private house in Mar del Plata reported.
- **1950s:** declining health; diabetes and repeated infarctions; social gatherings continue at his Uruguay Street residence.
- **8 August 1959:** dies in Buenos Aires.
- **1959:** initially buried in Recoleta Cemetery.
- **1992:** remains reportedly transferred to Jardín de Paz, Pilar.
- **1990s:** systematic rediscovery through photography and preservation research gathers pace.
- **2001:** major patrimonial survey published; Province declares his public work cultural heritage through Law 12,854.
- **2004:** Longoni and Molteni publish a major archival biography and study.
- **2009:** fiftieth anniversary of his death prompts commemorations.
- **2014:** National Decree 1138 protects extensive groups of his buildings.
- **2015:** *Mundo Salamone* receives public screenings and expands audiovisual recognition.
- **2017:** LEMIT publishes a major technical and historical volume.
- **2020s:** new tourism catalogues, restoration studies, exhibitions, archival discoveries, and the provincial digital fund extend research.
- **2022–2024:** Argentina–Italy exhibitions and new heritage publications reinforce international recognition.
- **2025:** archival study of Villa Finita adds property and Tucumán-case evidence.
- **2026:** Salamone remains the subject of preservation campaigns, tourism, scholarship, and competing political and mythic interpretations.
