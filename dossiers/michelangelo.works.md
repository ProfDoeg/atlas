## Works by Michelangelo

Michelangelo's sculpture, painting, and architecture are documented elsewhere in this dossier and in his journey file. This note covers only what he put into words: his letters and his poems. It also flags, briefly, the Sistine ceiling, a made thing of a different order entirely, not a text but worth naming here precisely because it sits so close to the writing in both time and feeling.

### Letters

Roughly 1,400 letters involving Michelangelo survive, written and received between his youth and his death in 1564, making him one of the best-documented artists of the sixteenth century. Most concern family business: as the effective patriarch of the Buonarroti after his brother Buonarroto's death, he sent money, arranged marriages and employment, bought property, and rebuked relatives he thought careless with the family name. Others cover the practical grind of his commissions, including the Sistine ceiling and the endless quarrying and shipping of marble, and his friendships, especially with Tommaso de' Cavalieri and Vittoria Colonna. The letters were first collected by Gaetano Milanesi (*Le lettere di Michelangelo Buonarroti*, 1875); the modern scholarly edition is *Il carteggio di Michelangelo*, begun by Giovanni Poggi and completed by Paola Barocchi and Renzo Ristori in five volumes (1965-1983). Deborah Parker's *Michelangelo and the Art of Letter Writing* (2010) is a standard English-language study of the correspondence as its own body of work.

### Poetry (sonnets and madrigals)

More than three hundred poems survive: mostly sonnets and madrigals, plus capitoli, epigrams, epitaphs, and comic verse, nearly all circulated in manuscript during his life rather than published as a finished book. About sixty are addressed to men, chiefly to Cavalieri, a Roman nobleman Michelangelo met in 1532; scholars have called this the first substantial body of modern love poetry from one man to another. A second major strand addresses Vittoria Colonna, marchioness of Pescara and a poet herself, whose friendship with Michelangelo in the 1530s and 1540s turned on shared religious concerns; her death in 1547 affected him deeply, and his later poems grow more penitential. A smaller, lighter vein includes the comic sonnet to Giovanni da Pistoia describing the physical misery of painting the ceiling. His grandnephew's 1623 edition regularized the verse and altered the gender of some male beloveds to female; Cesare Guasti's 1863 edition began restoring the manuscript readings. The standard modern critical edition is Enzo Noè Girardi's *Rime* (1960). English translations include Creighton Gilbert's *Complete Poems and Selected Letters* (1980), James M. Saslow's *The Poetry of Michelangelo: An Annotated Translation*, and Joseph Tusiani and Gianluca Rizzo's *The Complete Poems of Michelangelo* (2022).

### The ceiling, as a made thing that is not a text

The Sistine ceiling belongs to a wholly different category of making: paint and plaster, not words, and covered elsewhere in this project. It is named here only because Michelangelo himself linked it to his writing, in the comic sonnet above about painting it, and because any account of what he made with words sits incomplete without a nod to what he made, at the same time, with everything else.
