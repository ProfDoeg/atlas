# Joseph-Marie Jacquard: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

Joseph-Marie Jacquard’s documented life is unevenly preserved. Civil, patent, institutional, property, and honors records establish the principal dates, but roughly three decades of his early adulthood remain obscure. Biographies published after his death progressively converted those gaps into a standardized story of poverty, solitary genius, worker persecution, and eventual triumph.

The labels used below are:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary administrative, civil, patent, institutional, or material records.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a generally reliable secondary source but not independently established here from the underlying record.
- **Disputed claim:** supported by conflicting accounts or challenged by archival scholarship.
- **Rumor or myth:** a culturally consequential story lacking adequate contemporary confirmation.
- **Unresolved question:** the surviving evidence does not permit a firm conclusion.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Documented name at birth:** Joseph-Marie Charles.
- **Usual historical name:** Joseph-Marie Jacquard.
- **Alternative forms:** Joseph Marie Charles dit Jacquard; Joseph-Marie Charles, dit Jacquard; Joseph Marie Jacquard; occasionally “Jacquart” in nineteenth-century sources.
- **Nature of “Jacquard”:** originally a hereditary family sobriquet rather than the family’s civil surname. A paternal ancestor appears in records as Barthélemy Charles *dit Jacquard*. Joseph-Marie became internationally known under the sobriquet.
- **Titles and descriptions:** mécanicien; inventeur; fabricant or tisseur in some biographical accounts; chevalier de la Légion d’honneur; municipal councillor of Oullins.
- **Born:** 7 July 1752, Grande-rue de l’Hôpital, Lyon, France.
- **Baptized:** parish church of Saint-Nizier, Lyon.
- **Died:** 7 August 1834, Oullins, Rhône, France, aged eighty-two.
- **Buried:** 8 August 1834, Oullins cemetery.
- **Nationality and political jurisdictions:** French; born under the Bourbon monarchy, lived through the Revolution, First Republic, Consulate, First Empire, Bourbon Restoration, and July Monarchy.
- **Principal occupations:** drawboy or *tireur de lacs*, silk worker, binder, printer or printing worker, type founder, quarry or lime-kiln worker, mechanic, inventor, and—according to later records—teacher of mechanics.
- **Principal significance:** gave a commercially and institutionally successful form to a programmable shedding attachment for figured weaving. The mature “Jacquard” mechanism combined elements previously developed by Basile Bouchon, Jean-Baptiste Falcon, and Jacques de Vaucanson and was subsequently made reliably operational by other mechanics, especially Jean-Antoine Breton.

The Bibliothèque nationale de France authority record confirms the standard dates and describes him as a mechanic who invented a loom without manual drawboy operation. Lyon’s historical study gives the fuller civil name and family setting. [BnF authority record](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb12083432j); [Bruno Benoit, Grand Lyon study](https://millenaire3.grandlyon.com/dossiers/2015/la-soierie-lyonnaise-entre-tradition-et-innovation-archives/joseph-marie-jacquard-1752-1834)

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal family

- **Father:** Jean-Charles Charles, called Jacquard, a master worker or manufacturer of figured fabrics in gold, silver, and silk. He reportedly possessed three large drawlooms.
- **Paternal grandfather:** Isaac-Charles Jacquard/Charles, a resident and stonecutter of Couzon-au-Mont-d’Or.
- **Earlier paternal ancestry:** a marriage record reportedly identifies Isaac Charles as the son of Barthélemy Charles *dit Jacquard* and Anne Garin, both associated with stonecutting. This supports the conclusion that “Jacquard” began as a sobriquet distinguishing a branch of the Charles family.
- **Inherited property:** on his father’s death in 1772, Joseph-Marie inherited the workshop on the quai de Retz—now approximately quai Jean-Moulin—and houses, vineyards, and quarries at Couzon-au-Mont-d’Or.

### Maternal family

- **Mother:** Antoinette Rive, born about 1724 and dead in 1762.
- **Occupation:** *liseuse de dessins*, a specialist who interpreted textile designs and translated them into the instructions required for figured weaving.
- **Origin:** reported as Saint-Maurice-de-Rotherens in Savoy.

Both parents therefore participated directly in Lyon’s silk manufacture: the father operated figured-silk looms, while the mother performed skilled pattern-reading work.

### Siblings

Joseph-Marie was the fifth of nine children. Only he and an elder sister, **Clémence**, reportedly survived childhood. Clémence married into printing and later managed the Barret printing establishment after her husband’s death. Her husband or household helped educate Joseph-Marie and exposed him to printing, bookbinding, typography, and mechanical trades.

The names and exact life dates of the other seven children were not recovered in the consulted sources.

### Marriage and child

- **Wife:** Claudine Boichon, daughter of an armorer.
- **Marriage:** 26 July 1778, church of Notre-Dame-de-la-Platière, Lyon.
- **Residence around marriage:** rue Bouteille, Lyon.
- **Child:** Jean-Marie Charles, called Jacquard, born in 1779; no other child is securely documented.

Claudine’s mother died in 1784. Claudine inherited, jointly with a half-sister, a house on the quai de la Pêcherie and other landed property. During Joseph-Marie’s absence in the 1780s, Claudine remained in Lyon, raised their son, and operated a straw-hat workshop.

### Son’s fate

**Unresolved question:** Traditional biographies say Jean-Marie accompanied his father into military service and died in his arms during a battle on the Rhine or in Germany. The Grand Lyon historical reconstruction notes that no sufficiently firm record establishes this scene. Jean-Marie disappears from the recoverable record during the Revolutionary period; his precise place, date, and manner of death remain uncertain.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1752–1772

Joseph-Marie was raised inside the highly specialized Lyon silk economy. As a child he learned first to be a *tireur de cordes* or *tireur de lacs*—the assistant who pulled the cords controlling selected warp threads on a drawloom—and then to weave in his father’s workshop.

A drawboy normally worked above or beside the loom, following the pattern reader’s instructions and raising the appropriate groups of warp threads. The labor could be repetitive, physically taxing, and error-prone. This first-hand experience became important to the later biographical interpretation of Jacquard’s work, although no surviving youthful statement from him describes a childhood resolution to abolish drawboy labor.

His mother died when he was ten. He received no documented formal schooling comparable to a lycée, technical college, or academy. His education appears to have been familial and artisanal. Through his printer brother-in-law, he acquired some literacy and worked in trades variously reported as:

- bookbinding;
- printing;
- typography;
- type founding;
- cutlery or metalworking in some later biographies;
- quarrying or lime production.

These trades exposed him to precision fabrication, repeated patterns, movable components, and the mechanical organization of information. Any claim that printing directly “inspired” punched-card programming, however, is retrospective inference rather than documented testimony.

When his father died in 1772, the twenty-year-old inherited the family workshop and property.

## Education and Formation

### Formal education

No formal school, degree, or named academic teacher is documented. Accounts describing him as “uneducated” should be understood as meaning without advanced institutional schooling, not illiterate or mechanically ignorant.

### Practical teachers and influences

- **Jean-Charles Charles dit Jacquard:** taught him drawloom work and weaving.
- **Antoinette Rive:** her skilled work reading textile designs formed part of the household’s technical environment.
- **Printer brother-in-law and sister Clémence:** reportedly gave him instruction and access to printing trades.
- **Lyon silk workshops:** supplied his practical understanding of drawlooms and figured weaving.
- **Basile Bouchon:** in 1725 used perforated paper to control warp selection.
- **Jean-Baptiste Falcon:** replaced Bouchon’s continuous paper with separately replaceable cards linked in a chain and expanded the number of controlled hooks.
- **Jacques de Vaucanson:** constructed an automated loom mechanism employing needles, hooks, and perforated paper. A model was preserved at the Conservatoire des arts et métiers in Paris.
- **Jean-Antoine Breton:** later transformed Jacquard’s difficult early apparatus into a more practical mechanism through changes to the needles, card motion, batten, press, and card-reading or punching equipment.

Jacquard’s contribution belongs to this cumulative technical lineage. The 1911 *Encyclopædia Britannica* accurately summarized the machine as “not the work of one man” but the product of several inventors over approximately three-quarters of a century. [1911 Britannica technical account](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:EB1911_-_Volume_28.djvu/462)

## Early Career and Finances, 1772–1791

The period from 1772 to about 1800 contains major documentary gaps.

### Workshop and unsuccessful business

Later biographies say Jacquard attempted to operate the inherited figured-silk workshop but lacked aptitude for business management. He incurred debts and disposed of looms and real estate. The modern Lyon reconstruction finds evidence that he paid obligations by selling parts of his inheritance and drawing upon Claudine Boichon’s dowry.

He never appears in the surviving professional register as a recognized *maître-fabricant*, despite later claims that he held that status. This is a significant correction to the traditional occupational description “master silk manufacturer.”

### Residences

- **1776:** rue Neyret, Lyon.
- **1778:** rue Bouteille, Lyon.
- **1780s:** absent from Lyon for at least part of the decade, working in Bresse or Bugey.
- **By 1791:** returned to Lyon and living in the Boichon family property on quai de la Pêcherie.

### Work outside Lyon

Jacquard reportedly worked as a *chaufournier*—a lime-burner or lime-kiln worker—in Bresse or Bugey. Other biographies refer to employment in a gypsum quarry, type foundry, cutlery shop, or printing works. The exact sequence and duration of these employments are not securely established.

### Financial position

The familiar portrait of utter destitution is overstated. He experienced debt and sold inherited assets, but:

- his father had left workshops and real property;
- his wife inherited a house and other land;
- his sister controlled a printing establishment;
- in 1797 he described himself as a *rentier*.

The evidence is most consistent with fluctuating fortunes within the property-owning artisan or lower-bourgeois world, rather than uninterrupted pauperism.

## Revolution and Military Service, 1791–1795

Jacquard appears to have initially welcomed at least some Revolutionary ideas. His conduct during the 1793 crisis nevertheless aligned him with the Lyon uprising against the radical National Convention.

### Siege of Lyon

- **Documented or strongly reported fact:** Jacquard participated in Lyon’s defense in 1793 with the rank of sergeant.
- The insurgent city was besieged by Republican forces and fell in October 1793.
- After the defeat, Jacquard hid to avoid denunciation or punishment.

### Service under an assumed identity

The Grand Lyon reconstruction reports that he enlisted under a false name and fought in Alsace in forces commanded by Lazare Hoche and Jean-Charles Pichegru.

The assumed name has not been recovered from the consulted sources. Nor is it established whether he was formally discharged, deserted, or simply left after the immediate danger passed.

### Jean-Marie’s alleged battlefield death

Traditional biographies present a vivid scene: father and son enlist together, and Jean-Marie is mortally wounded and dies in Jacquard’s arms.

**Evidentiary status: disputed claim.** A version was already circulating in an obituary published shortly after Jacquard’s death, giving the story some early provenance. Modern archival reconstruction nonetheless regards the son’s fate as obscure and the death-in-his-father’s-arms scene as unverified.

### Return to Lyon

Jacquard returned around 1794–1795. Accounts variously say he:

- wove for the silk manufacturer Camille Pernon;
- joined his wife’s straw-hat operation;
- worked at assorted manual trades;
- developed mechanisms at night.

No single employment sequence can be reconstructed confidently.

## Chronological Life History: Invention and Public Career

## 1795–1800: Experimental mechanics

During the silk industry’s recovery after the Terror, Jacquard worked on mechanisms intended to reduce or eliminate manual drawboy operation. His early experiments were not yet the punched-card apparatus later bearing his name.

The economic problem was clear: figured weaving required a worker to select warp threads for each pass of the weft. A mechanism that stored the selection sequence could reduce labor, errors, and the difficulty of repeating complex designs.

## 1800–1801: First patent and exhibition

On **13 Messidor Year VIII—2 July 1800**, Jacquard lodged a patent application at the Rhône prefecture for a “machine destinée à suppléer le tireur de lacs, dans la fabrication des étoffes brochées et façonnées.”

The ten-year patent was approved by Interior Minister Jean-Antoine Chaptal and delivered to Jacquard on **18 January 1801**. Other catalogues date the formal patent to **23 December 1801**, probably reflecting a later administrative or publication stage. The original specification was subsequently printed in volume VIII of *Description des machines et procédés... dont la durée est expirée*.

This first invention did **not** embody the mature chain-of-cards Jacquard mechanism. It used a limited number of treadles to produce relatively small geometric or floral figures suitable for waistcoats and dresses. The INPI archive identifies the application as file 1BA134. [INPI patent image and description](https://artsandculture.google.com/asset/le-m%C3%A9tier-%C3%A0-tisser-joseph-charles-jacquard-brevet-d-invention-d%C3%A9pos%C3%A9-le-02-07-1800-pour-une-machine-destin%C3%A9e-%C3%A0-suppl%C3%A9er-le-tireur-de-lacs-dans-la-fabrication-des-%C3%A9toffes-broch%C3%A9es-et-fa%C3%A7onn%C3%A9es-1ba134/pQHjCND0-52A3A?hl=en)

At the second Exposition des produits de l’industrie française in the Louvre in September 1801, Jacquard exhibited this apparatus and received a bronze medal.

## 1802–1804: Fishing-net machine and Paris

The Société d’encouragement pour l’industrie nationale offered a prize for a machine capable of manufacturing fishing nets. Jacquard constructed an apparatus and was brought to Paris for examination in 1803. Commissioners associated with the Société and Conservatoire included Nicolas-Jacques Conté, Claude-Pierre Molard, Bardel, and Louis Costaz.

The commission considered the machine incomplete but promising. Jacquard received encouragement and reportedly a 300-franc advance toward patent expenses. He obtained a patent for the net-making machine, usually dated **13 December 1804** or described as issued in 1805. Later technical reviewers judged that it contained useful ideas but was not itself a commercially decisive net loom.

### Conservatoire and Vaucanson’s machine

Jacquard was attached for a period to the Conservatoire des arts et métiers, reportedly to repair or maintain machines. There he could inspect Vaucanson’s preserved automatic loom.

This encounter is central because the mature apparatus combined:

- Vaucanson’s overhead arrangement and automated needle-and-hook mechanism;
- Falcon’s chain of individual punched cards and polygonal card cylinder;
- Jacquard’s reconfiguration of these elements into a compact attachment suited to existing Lyon looms.

Claims that Jacquard invented punched cards are therefore incorrect. Falcon had used linked cards in the 1720s. Jacquard’s achievement was a new integration and institutional deployment of established principles.

## 1804–1806: Formation of the “Jacquard” mechanism

By 1804–1805, Jacquard had removed much of the bulky cordage associated with earlier drawlooms, placed the selection mechanism above the loom, and used cards whose holes governed whether needles and hooks were displaced.

In simplified operation:

1. A card representing one transverse line of the intended design was pressed against a field of needles.
2. A hole allowed the corresponding needle or feeler to pass.
3. Unperforated areas pushed other needles sideways.
4. The selected hooks engaged or disengaged from the lifting frame.
5. The resulting group of warp threads rose for one pass of the shuttle.
6. The card cylinder advanced to the next card and repeated the sequence.

A linked card chain could be looped, making the pattern repeat. Cards could be replaced, corrected, stored, and reused. The mechanism was an interchangeable program for controlling a physical process, although calling it a “computer” would be anachronistic: it performed no general calculation and contained no conditional branching.

## 12 April 1805: Napoleon and Joséphine

**Documented or strongly reported fact:** Emperor Napoleon Bonaparte and Empress Joséphine visited Lyon’s Palais Saint-Pierre and inspected Jacquard’s apparatus during their stay in the city. The event greatly increased his public recognition.

The familiar dialogue in which Napoleon or Carnot asks whether Jacquard is the man who can “do the impossible”—sometimes expressed as tying a knot in a taut string—belongs to later anecdotal literature and cannot be treated as a verified transcript.

On **27 August 1805**, the Lyon academy awarded him its inventors’ prize.

## 1805–1811: State acquisition and compensation

An imperial act placed the invention at Lyon’s disposal or in the public domain so it could spread through the silk industry. Sources disagree on whether the decisive act occurred on 15 April 1805 or under a Berlin decree of 27 October 1806; the discrepancy may reflect separate municipal, patent, and imperial actions.

Jacquard’s compensation is consistently reported as:

- an annual life pension of **3,000 francs**;
- a payment or royalty of **50 francs for every machine installed**, commonly limited to the years 1805–1811.

This arrangement made him a beneficiary of state-sponsored diffusion rather than the proprietor of a vertically integrated loom business. No evidence was found that he founded a major manufacturing company, accumulated outside investors, issued shares, or controlled the international trade in machines carrying his name.

## Practical failure and Jean-Antoine Breton’s improvements

The apparatus first associated with Jacquard was difficult to operate. Research using Lyon prud’hommes records concludes that the initial mechanism did not work reliably enough for ordinary workshop use.

Jean-Antoine Breton, a carpenter and mechanic from Privas, was introduced to Jacquard through the weaving teacher Pierre-Henri Laselve. Breton and other mechanics supplied essential improvements, including:

- alterations to spring-loaded needles and hook heels;
- improved registration of cards on the four faces of the cylinder;
- replacement of Vaucanson’s heavy sliding carriage with a lighter swinging batten around 1807;
- machinery for transferring the design reading to cards around 1812;
- a press bringing the cylinder firmly against the needles;
- later card-reading and punching equipment;
- improvements to card-chain movement, often dated to 1817.

The historically successful machine was consequently a collective and evolving artifact. Jacquard established the organizing configuration and secured institutional backing; Breton and other Lyon mechanics made it dependable and adaptable.

## Adoption

Adoption was initially slow. According to François Jarrige’s synthesis:

- in 1815 Lyon had about 14,500 looms within the city and several thousand in its suburbs;
- approximately 2,000 were Jacquard-equipped;
- by the middle of the nineteenth century the broader Lyon industry operated 59,733 looms, about 9,000 of them Jacquard machines.

The term came to include many later modifications. The attachment was applied not only to silk but to woolen shawls, mixed silk-and-cotton textiles, furnishing fabrics, ribbons, carpets, and other figured weaving.

## Later Life, 1810–1834

### Residences

- **1810–1817:** 1 rue Puits-d’Ainay, now rue Adélaïde-Perrin, Lyon.
- **After 1817:** 2 rue Vaubecour, Lyon, as tenant of his friend Jean-Marie Benoît Bouchard.
- **From approximately 1810 onward, intermittently or increasingly:** Clos Fleury, Oullins.

He lived with Claudine and a servant or younger cousin, Marguerite Vignard, during at least part of the 1810–1817 period.

### Clos Fleury and property relations

Jacquard obtained use of the Oullins country property known as Clos Fleury together with nieces after settling debts owed by their parents. Later hagiography sometimes described this as a humble cottage bestowed by grateful authorities. The evidence instead points to a family financial arrangement and a relatively comfortable country residence.

### Institutions

- Member of the Société des amis du commerce et des arts de Lyon.
- Adviser to a mechanics commission presided over by Gensoul.
- Elected on **19 December 1817** to the Société royale d’agriculture, histoire naturelle et arts utiles de Lyon, following nomination by his friend Bouchard.
- Invited in 1822 by Guillard-Lièvre to teach mechanics at a newly founded school of commerce near the Pont Morand.

Whether he maintained a regular teaching schedule, and for how long, is not established in the consulted material.

### Honors

- Bronze medal, Exposition des produits de l’industrie française, 1801.
- Inventors’ prize from the Lyon academy, 27 August 1805.
- Gold medal, fifth Exposition des produits de l’industrie française, 1819, although his invention was apparently not physically exhibited there.
- Knight of the Legion of Honor, **17 November 1819**.
- Municipal councillor of Oullins, appointment generally dated 1824 or 1826.

The difference between 1824 and 1826 in secondary sources may represent proposal versus formal appointment. The mayor’s recorded welcome in 1826 called Jacquard’s name “European” and associated it with national wealth.

### Politics after 1830

Following the July Revolution of 1830, municipal government became elective and Jacquard was not returned to the Oullins council.

He took no documented part in the Lyon silk workers’ uprising of November 1831. A later rhetorical suggestion that he might have sympathized privately is unsupported. He was by this point an honored property holder and state pensioner, not an organizer within the canut mutualist or republican networks.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Family and personal enterprises

- Father’s workshop of three large drawlooms: inherited in 1772, later partly or wholly liquidated.
- Properties at Couzon-au-Mont-d’Or: houses, vineyards, and quarries inherited from his father and subsequently reduced through sales.
- Claudine Boichon’s straw-hat workshop in Lyon.
- Claudine’s jointly inherited quai de la Pêcherie house and other real estate.
- Clos Fleury at Oullins: occupied through a family debt-settlement arrangement.

### Patent interests

1. **Machine replacing the drawboy in brocaded and figured fabrics**
   - Application: 2 July 1800.
   - Approval/delivery: January 1801.
   - Formal patent date often catalogued as 23 December 1801.
   - Term: ten years.
   - Archival file: INPI 1BA134.
   - Not the mature punched-card Jacquard machine.

2. **Machine for manufacturing fishing nets**
   - Developed and tested in Paris, 1803.
   - Patent generally dated 13 December 1804 or 1805.
   - Technically interesting but not a decisive commercial success.

3. **Punched-card loom attachment**
   - Material form developed around 1804–1805.
   - Frequently but imprecisely described as an 1804 patent.
   - State or Lyon acquisition/public release in 1805–1806.
   - Continuously altered thereafter.

### Income

Known or credibly reported sources of income included:

- proceeds from inherited property;
- wife’s family property and business;
- craft or industrial wages;
- rents, as suggested by his 1797 declaration as *rentier*;
- 3,000-franc state pension;
- 50-franc payments for installed mechanisms for a defined period;
- possible teaching remuneration;
- occupation or use of Clos Fleury.

No reliable total for his estate at death was found. “Died rich” and “died poor” are both oversimplifications; “relative ease” or comfortable provincial respectability is better supported for his final decades.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Social Development

### Politics

Jacquard’s politics were not recorded in a developed body of writing.

- He reportedly welcomed the early Revolution.
- He defended insurgent Lyon against the Convention’s army in 1793.
- He then served in a Republican army under an assumed identity.
- He benefited from Consular and Imperial industrial policy.
- He accepted honors under the Bourbon Restoration.
- He held municipal office but failed to win election after the 1830 revolution.
- He did not publicly join the 1831 canut revolt.

These actions suggest accommodation to successive regimes combined with local civic conservatism, but no systematic ideology can be established.

### Religion

He was baptized Catholic. His funeral included the curé of Oullins, and a memorial was erected in the local church. Funeral rhetoric portrayed him as forgiving his enemies. No private devotional writings, conversions, heterodox affiliations, or developed religious philosophy were identified.

### Intellectual development

Jacquard’s intellectual activity was applied and mechanical rather than literary. His principal modes of knowledge were:

- workshop observation;
- practical recombination;
- prototyping;
- institutional demonstration;
- adaptation of preserved machinery;
- collaboration with skilled mechanics.

No diary, memoir, manifesto, or theoretical treatise is known.

### Family and household

- **Jean-Charles Charles dit Jacquard:** father and first weaving instructor.
- **Antoinette Rive:** mother; skilled pattern reader.
- **Clémence Charles:** surviving elder sister; printer and important family support.
- **Claudine Boichon:** wife, property holder, and straw-hat manufacturer.
- **Jean-Marie Charles dit Jacquard:** only documented child; fate unresolved.
- **Marguerite Vignard:** servant and younger cousin in the Lyon household.
- **Unnamed nieces:** shared use of Clos Fleury following Jacquard’s settlement of their parents’ debts.

- **Basile Bouchon:** predecessor in perforated-paper control.
- **Jean-Baptiste Falcon:** predecessor in chained punched cards.
- **Jacques de Vaucanson:** predecessor whose preserved loom Jacquard studied.
- **Jean-Antoine Breton:** decisive practical improver and at least occasional collaborator.
- **Pierre-Henri Laselve:** weaving teacher who reportedly introduced Breton to Jacquard.
- **Albert Dutillieu:** developed a regulator in 1811.
- **Guigo and Lantérès:** later mechanics who added stopping, compensation, and gauging devices.

### Patrons and institutional supporters

- **Jean-Antoine Chaptal:** interior minister who approved the first patent.
- **Claude-Pierre Molard, Nicolas-Jacques Conté, Bardel, and Louis Costaz:** evaluated or assisted the fishing-net project.
- **Napoleon Bonaparte and Empress Joséphine:** imperial visitors whose inspection publicized the machine.
- **Gensoul:** president of the Lyon mechanics commission.
- **Jean-Marie Benoît Bouchard:** friend, landlord, and sponsor for membership in the Société royale d’agriculture.
- **Guillard-Lièvre:** recruited Jacquard to teach mechanics.
- **Mayor Prunelle:** commissioned Jean-Claude Bonnefond’s portrait of Jacquard.

### Rivals and critics

No documented personal “enemy” comparable to a litigating business rival emerged. Later priority critics included:

- **Isidore Hedde**, who argued that local vanity had exaggerated Jacquard’s originality and emphasized Vaucanson.
- Historians and technical writers who credited Falcon, Vaucanson, Breton, and workshop improvers more fully.
- The Lyon prud’hommes, who criticized the reliability or utility of Jacquard’s early apparatus; later legend recast this technical conflict as persecution by ignorant workers.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

## Inventorship and alleged plagiarism

### Documented fact

Jacquard did not invent:

- perforated media as a means of controlling a loom;
- the chain of individual punched cards;
- the needle-and-hook principle;
- every practical feature of the mature machine.

Those components preceded him in the work of Bouchon, Falcon, and Vaucanson.

### Documented contribution

Jacquard combined Falcon’s card chain with a Vaucanson-derived overhead selection mechanism, simplified the relationship between hooks and harness, promoted a mountable attachment, obtained state support, and gave the system a recognizable configuration capable of further development.

### Disputed characterization

Calling him either the wholly original “inventor of the programmable loom” or merely a fraudulent plagiarist is unsupported. Technical invention here was cumulative. Jacquard deserves credit for synthesis, rearrangement, demonstration, and diffusion; Breton and later mechanics deserve credit for operational reliability.

### No formal fraud finding

No evidence was found of:

- prosecution for patent fraud;
- a civil judgment declaring him a plagiarist;
- criminal misrepresentation;
- falsified financial accounts;
- bribery or corruption;
- money laundering;
- intelligence activity;
- political assassination or violence initiated by him.

Nineteenth-century criticism was primarily a priority dispute, not a judicial finding of theft.

## Accuracy of Jacquard’s occupational claims

Jacquard sometimes presented himself as a master manufacturer. The relevant professional registers apparently contain no entry establishing that status.

**Evidentiary label: documented discrepancy.** This may reflect loose occupational usage or social self-presentation rather than deliberate fraud. There is insufficient evidence to characterize it as criminal or financially deceptive.

## Debts and wife’s property

Jacquard incurred debts and used both inherited property and part of his wife’s dowry to meet them. Claudine’s family reportedly objected.

**Documented or strongly reported financial fact:** unsuccessful workshop management contributed to asset sales.

No surviving evidence consulted establishes embezzlement, fraudulent conveyance, coercion, or litigation between Jacquard and the Boichon family.

## Early machine’s unreliability

Archival research by Alain Cottereau, summarized by François Jarrige, indicates that the prud’hommes’ adverse response was substantially technical: Jacquard’s apparatus did not yet work reliably. The council promoted competing improvements.

This conflicts with the later account that officials destroyed an effective machine solely to placate an irrational mob. [François Jarrige, “Le martyre de Jacquard ou le mythe de l’inventeur héroïque”](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/30439019_Le_martyre_de_Jacquard_ou_le_mythe_de_l%27inventeur_heroique_France_xixe_siecle)

## Labor displacement

The machine eliminated or reduced the role of the drawboy and lowered the skill and labor required for some figured designs. Worker concern over displacement was therefore economically intelligible.

Longer-term expansion of the silk industry created or preserved employment in other functions, including weaving, design reading, card punching, machinery construction, and maintenance. This does not establish that every displaced worker benefited, nor that mechanization alone caused Lyon’s later industrial growth.

## Relationship to the canut revolts

A widespread claim makes Jacquard’s machine a cause of the canut revolts of 1831 and 1834.

**Evidentiary label: misleading later association.** The uprisings principally concerned piece rates, tariffs, working conditions, debt, commercial organization, and political authority. Mechanization formed part of the broader restructuring of silk labor, but the 1831 revolt did not arise as a direct campaign against Jacquard personally or his original machine. He took no documented part in it.

7. benefit from imperial patronage and state compensation;
8. uncertain claims about worker attacks;
9. later use of his biography as propaganda against labor resistance.

Recent online claims largely repeat either the traditional lone-genius story or the punched-card-to-computer genealogy. No credible evidence of lawsuits, fraud convictions, personal corruption, sexual scandal, or clandestine political activity was found.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## “A starving, uneducated poor boy”

### Story

Jacquard rose from extreme childhood poverty, had no education, repeatedly failed, and worked alone at night until genius overcame deprivation.

### Appearance and circulation

The story was elaborated in nineteenth-century biographies, schoolbooks, moral tracts, and works such as Henri Baudrillart’s *Vie de Jacquard* and Sarah Knowles Bolton’s *Lives of Poor Boys Who Became Famous*.

### Evidence

His family was artisanal but owned three drawlooms and real property. Jacquard inherited houses, vineyards, quarries, and a workshop; his wife inherited property; he called himself a rentier in 1797.

### Assessment

**Myth built around a factual core.** He did suffer business setbacks and debt, but uninterrupted destitution is contradicted by property evidence.

### Effect

It made him an exemplary figure of meritocratic self-help and industrial perseverance.

## “He invented the punched card”

### Story

Jacquard independently invented the punched card around 1801 or 1804.

### Evidence

Bouchon used perforated paper in 1725; Falcon developed chained cards shortly afterward; Vaucanson automated aspects of the system.

### Assessment

**False as stated.** Jacquard recombined and promoted earlier control technologies.

### Effect

The claim simplified the ancestry of programmable machinery and made Jacquard a direct, singular “father of computing.”

## “The Jacquard loom was a power loom”

### Story

Jacquard invented a fully powered automatic loom.

### Evidence

His device was principally a programmable shedding or warp-selection attachment mounted over a loom. It did not, by itself, supply motive power to every weaving operation.

### Assessment

**Terminological myth or simplification.** “Jacquard machine” is technically preferable to “Jacquard power loom” for the original apparatus.

## Public destruction by the prud’hommes

### Story

The Lyon council of prud’hommes ordered Jacquard’s completed loom smashed on the Place des Terreaux; its iron was sold as scrap and its wood as firewood.

### Earliest circulation

A version appeared in the *Magasin pittoresque* in 1833, while Jacquard was alive. Léon Faucher gave the narrative influential form in 1835, shortly after Jacquard’s death.

### Evidence for

The story emerged relatively early, and later writers attributed the iron-and-firewood phrase to Jacquard.

### Evidence against

No corroborating archival record has been found for the ceremonial destruction as described. Press denials appeared in Lyon soon after Faucher’s article. Prud’hommes records instead show criticism of the machine’s poor operation and efforts to stimulate technical improvement.

### Assessment

**Disputed story, probably heavily dramatized.**

### Effect

It became the foundational scene of Jacquard’s “martyrdom” at the hands of anti-technology workers.

## Attempted drowning in the Rhône or Saône

### Story

Workers seized Jacquard, beat him, dragged him to the river, and nearly drowned him before police or manufacturers rescued him.

### Development

Faucher wrote that Jacquard’s life was threatened three times. Later versions placed the attack at Port Saint-Clair and alternated between the Rhône and Saône.

### Evidence

Pierre Cayez found no archival confirmation that workers attempted to throw him into the river. The story became visually fixed in the 1894–1895 Diorama Jacquard.

### Assessment

**Rumor or myth.** Some hostility to labor-saving machinery is plausible, but the specific assault is unverified.

### Effect

It portrayed the inventor as a secular martyr and workers as a violent, ignorant crowd.

## “The canuts destroyed Jacquard machines in the 1831 revolt”

### Story

The 1831 canut uprising was an anti-Jacquard machine-breaking revolt.

### Evidence

The revolt was centered on wages and enforcement of a minimum tariff. Jacquard machines were already embedded in the industry. Machine destruction occurred in different Lyon crises, particularly 1848, but the blanket description of 1831 as anti-Jacquard Luddism is inaccurate.

### Assessment

**Later conflation.**

## The dead son in his father’s arms

### Story

Jean-Marie joined Jacquard in the army and died in his arms after battle.

### Evidence

Early posthumous notices repeat the episode, but modern biographical reconstruction cannot trace the son’s death.

### Assessment

**Disputed claim; unresolved underlying disappearance.**

### Effect

The episode supplied the hero’s biography with Revolutionary sacrifice and personal tragedy.

## “Napoleon challenged him to do the impossible”

### Story

Napoleon—or in some variants Carnot—asked Jacquard whether he truly proposed to tie a knot in a taut cord, and Jacquard demonstrated the machine.

### Evidence

The imperial inspection is credible; the dialogue is not documented as a contemporary transcript.

### Assessment

**Anecdotal embellishment.**

## A humble cottage given by a grateful city

### Story

Jacquard retired to a small house and garden at Oullins, donated in gratitude.

### Evidence

His use of Clos Fleury arose from paying family debts and sharing rights with nieces.

### Assessment

**Hagiographic simplification.**

## “Direct father of the computer”

### Story

A continuous line runs from Jacquard’s cards through Charles Babbage and Ada Lovelace to Hollerith, IBM, and modern computers.

### Evidence

Babbage explicitly adopted separate operation and variable cards for his Analytical Engine and used Jacquard weaving as an analogy. Ada Lovelace likewise described the Analytical Engine as weaving algebraic patterns as the Jacquard loom wove flowers and leaves. Later punched-card data systems form part of the same broad history of machine-readable media.

### Qualification

Jacquard’s machine did not calculate, execute conditional instructions, or embody a general-purpose computer architecture. Hollerith’s cards also drew on railway-ticket practices and independent engineering decisions.

### Assessment

**Documented influence surrounded by teleological mythology.** The connection to Babbage is real; describing Jacquard himself as a computer programmer is anachronistic.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

## Funeral and first memorials

Jacquard died at Oullins at approximately one in the morning on 7 August 1834 and was buried the following morning. A mulberry tree—symbol of silk production—was planted at the head of his grave.

His funeral was not a large public triumph. François Jarrige reports sparse attendance. The curé Bez and members of the Société d’agriculture praised him, emphasizing forgiveness of jealous enemies.

On 26 August 1834, the Lyon prud’hommes opened a subscription for a public monument and contributed 200 francs. A church monument was erected at Oullins on 10 September 1835.

No medically specific cause of death was located. Claims of a particular fatal disease should therefore be treated as unestablished.

## Statue and civic cult

A bronze statue by Jean-Marie Bonnassieux’s contemporary sculptural milieu—usually identified as the work of Denis Foyatier—was inaugurated on Place Sathonay, Lyon, on **16 August 1840**. The ceremony was reportedly subdued; subscriptions from Lyon itself had been disappointing.

In **1901**, the statue was moved to Place de la Croix-Rousse, fixing Jacquard geographically and symbolically within the canut district.

His name was attached to:

- streets and public squares;
- schools and technical institutions;
- textile machines and methods;
- fabrics described as “jacquard”;
- computer, engineering, and data projects using “Jacquard” as a symbol of programmable material.

France issued a commemorative postage stamp in 1934 for the centenary of his death.

## Bonnefond and Carquillat portraits

In 1832, Lyon mayor Victor-Gabriel Prunelle commissioned Jean-Claude Bonnefond to paint Jacquard for a gallery of celebrated Lyonnais. The portrait showed the elderly inventor seated in a workshop.

In 1839, Michel-Marie Carquillat, working for Didier, Petit et Cie, translated the portrait into woven silk using approximately 24,000 cards. The object displayed both Jacquard’s image and the extraordinary informational capacity of the technology bearing his name. Copies circulated internationally; Charles Babbage possessed one and regarded it as an outstanding example of automated control.

The portrait became one of the most reproduced images in the prehistory of computing. [SILKNOW object record](https://ada.silknow.org/object/04c9b5a4-985f-3a7f-beb8-eb438ec29e8b); [Computer History Museum discussion](https://computerhistory.org/blog/artifact-adopted-preserving-the-legend-of-j-m-jacquard/)

## Industrial legacy

The Jacquard principle enabled complex designs to be reproduced without manually reselecting every warp grouping. Its consequences included:

- faster preparation and repetition of figured patterns;
- separation of pattern information from loom structure;
- easier correction and reuse of designs;
- growth of card-reading, card-punching, and machine-maintenance occupations;
- application to silk, wool, cotton, linen, carpets, ribbons, labels, and knitted fabrics;
- later electrical and electronic Jacquard controls.

The mechanism did not remain frozen in Jacquard’s form. Its longevity resulted from repeated redesign by mechanics, manufacturers, and users.

## Historiographical reinterpretation

Nineteenth-century biographies presented:

1. a poor artisan;
2. an unprecedented invention;
3. persecution by ignorant workers;
4. state recognition;
5. industrial prosperity vindicating the inventor.

Twentieth- and twenty-first-century research shifted attention toward:

- collective invention;
- workshop knowledge;
- institutional patronage;
- property and class position;
- the technical weakness of the early machine;
- labor’s rational concern over displacement;
- political uses of inventor mythology.

Jarrige argues that Jacquard’s “martyrdom” helped employers and civic elites present resistance to mechanization as ignorance while obscuring technical defects and institutional responsibility. The myth was invoked during the 1848 machine-breaking disturbances and disseminated in schools during the Third Republic.

## Works by Joseph-Marie Jacquard

Jacquard was not a literary author. No securely attributed book, memoir, diary, manifesto, philosophical treatise, or substantial published correspondence was found.

### Patent and technical works

1. **“Machine destinée à suppléer le tireur de lacs, dans la fabrication des étoffes brochées et façonnées.”**
   - Patent application lodged 2 July 1800.
   - Ten-year patent approved and delivered in 1801.
   - Formal date often given as 23 December 1801.
   - Published posthumously as an expired patent specification in *Description des machines et procédés...*, vol. VIII, 1824.
   - Genuine technical authorship, although not the mature punched-card machine.

2. **Machine à fabriquer les filets pour la pêche.**
   - Constructed and demonstrated around 1803.
   - Patent dated 13 December 1804 in later technical records.
   - Genuine technical project; evaluated by the Société d’encouragement.
   - Not a major commercial success.

3. **Model or mechanism for figured weaving using chained cards.**
   - Developed around 1804–1805.
   - Genuine Jacquard technical contribution, but collaborative and derivative in the neutral patent-historical sense.
   - Surviving models attributed to him are held by the Conservatoire national des arts et métiers. [Musée des Arts et Métiers object notice](https://www.arts-et-metiers.net/musee/modele-metier-original-de-jacquard)

### Attributed sayings

The line that the destroyed machine’s “iron was sold as old iron, and the wood as firewood” was repeatedly attributed to Jacquard. No autograph or contemporary verbatim record was established; it should be cited as an attributed remark embedded in the martyr legend.

### Correspondence

Individual autograph letters may survive in institutional or private collections, but no comprehensive scholarly edition of Jacquard’s correspondence was identified. No claim should be made that a complete correspondence exists.

## Death

Jacquard died at Oullins on 7 August 1834. The precise medical cause has not been established in the consulted sources. He was eighty-two, and no well-supported account identifies a terminal accident, addiction, psychiatric crisis, or chronic disease.

He was buried on 8 August. The mulberry planted at his grave converted the burial site into a symbol of the silk industry.

His wife’s precise death date was not securely established in the consulted material. Some narratives imply that she predeceased his final retirement, but the date should remain open absent a civil record.
