# Empress Joséphine: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier concerns Marie-Josèphe-Rose Tascher de La Pagerie, better known retrospectively as Joséphine de Beauharnais and during the First French Empire as Empress Joséphine.

Evidence is labeled where necessary:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a civil, ecclesiastical, governmental, archival, or contemporaneous record.
- **Reported fact:** supported by reputable secondary scholarship but not independently verified here from the underlying document.
- **Allegation or disputed claim:** asserted in sources, but evidence is incomplete, contradictory, or contested.
- **Rumor or myth:** a story primarily preserved through memoir, folklore, propaganda, retrospective anecdote, or popular culture.

Names and dates vary because eighteenth-century spelling was inconsistent and Joséphine herself understated her age in her 1796 marriage record.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full baptismal name:** Marie Josèphe Rose Tascher de La Pagerie. Variants include Marie-Joseph-Rose, Marie-Josèphe-Rose, Marie Rose Joseph, and Marie-Rose.

**Usual early name:** Rose. “Joséphine” was not her normal childhood or first-marriage name. Napoleon derived it from her second given name and made it her customary name after their relationship began.

**Common historical name:** Joséphine de Beauharnais. This is convenient but partly retrospective: while married to Napoleon she was Madame Bonaparte and later Joséphine, Empress of the French, rather than ordinarily “Joséphine de Beauharnais.”

**Other styles and titles:**

- Mademoiselle de Tascher de La Pagerie
- Vicomtesse de Beauharnais
- Citoyenne Beauharnais during the Revolution
- Madame Bonaparte
- Empress of the French, 18 May 1804–16 December 1809
- Queen of Italy, 1805–1809
- Empress Joséphine after the divorce
- Duchess of Navarre, created in 1810

**Birth:** 23 June 1763, on the Tascher family’s sugar estate at Les Trois-Îlets, Martinique, then a French slave colony.

**Baptism:** 27 July 1763, parish church of Notre-Dame-de-la-Bonne-Délivrance, Les Trois-Îlets.

**Death:** Around noon on 29 May 1814, Château de Malmaison, Rueil, west of Paris.

**Cause of death:** The postmortem diagnosis was an inflammatory throat and respiratory illness described as pneumonia with “angine gangréneuse” or gangrenous angina. Later retellings commonly say that she caught cold while walking with Tsar Alexander I, but a single causal exposure cannot be medically established.

**Burial:** Church of Saint-Pierre-Saint-Paul, Rueil-Malmaison. Her remains were transferred into the completed monumental tomb on 28 September 1825.

**Physical descriptions:** Revolutionary police records described her as approximately 1.63 meters tall, with brown hair and eyes, a brown complexion, and an oval face. Portraits emphasize her slender figure, dark eyes, soft expression, and elaborate clothing. She reportedly kept her lips closed because of damaged or discolored teeth, a feature often invoked to explain her restrained smile and quiet speaking style.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal family

Her father was **Joseph-Gaspard Tascher de La Pagerie** (1735–1790), a member of a minor noble family established in Martinique. The Taschers originated in the Perche region of France. Joséphine’s grandfather Gaspard-Joseph Tascher had gone to Martinique in 1726.

Joseph-Gaspard had served as a page to the dauphine Marie-Josèphe of Saxony, became an artillery lieutenant, and was a chevalier of the Order of Saint Louis. Accounts describe him as sociable and frequently absent from the practical management of the plantation.

His sister **Marie-Euphémie-Désirée Tascher de La Pagerie**, usually called Désirée Renaudin, lived in France and maintained an intimate relationship with François de Beauharnais, father of Joséphine’s future husband. That relationship was central to arranging the Beauharnais marriage.

### Maternal family

Her mother was **Rose-Claire des Vergers de Sannois** (1736–1807), from an established white Creole family. She managed much of the plantation’s day-to-day business. Through her colonial ancestry, Joséphine belonged to Martinique’s slaveholding planter society.

### Siblings

- **Catherine-Désirée Tascher de La Pagerie** (1764–1777). She was originally considered as a bride for Alexandre de Beauharnais but died before the match could be completed.
- **Marie-Françoise Tascher de La Pagerie** (1766–1791), sometimes called Manette.

A woman called **Euphémie**, who accompanied Joséphine to France and remained attached to her household, has sometimes been described as an illegitimate mixed-race half-sister. This identification is **disputed**. The surviving evidence supports Euphémie’s existence and close service but does not conclusively establish her parentage.

### The plantation and enslaved population

The La Pagerie property produced sugar and other crops through enslaved labor. A hurricane in 1766 badly damaged the estate and sugar works; the family thereafter occupied part of the surviving buildings and experienced continuing financial difficulty.

The exact number of enslaved people held at every stage of Joséphine’s childhood is not securely established in the sources consulted. The essential point is documented: she was born and raised in a slaveholding household whose economic status depended on colonial land and coerced labor.

### First marriage and children

Joséphine married **Alexandre François Marie, vicomte de Beauharnais** (1760–1794) at Noisy-le-Grand on 13 December 1779.

Their children were:

1. **Eugène Rose de Beauharnais** (3 September 1781–21 February 1824), later Napoleon’s adopted son, viceroy of Italy, prince of Venice, and duke of Leuchtenberg.
2. **Hortense Eugénie Cécile de Beauharnais** (10 April 1783–5 October 1837), later wife of Napoleon’s brother Louis Bonaparte, queen consort of Holland, and mother of Napoleon III.

Through Eugène, Joséphine became an ancestor of later Swedish, Norwegian, Danish, Belgian, Luxembourgish, Portuguese, Brazilian, and other European dynastic lines. Through Hortense, she was grandmother of Napoleon III.

### Second marriage

She married **Napoleon Bonaparte** in a civil ceremony in Paris on 9 March 1796, 19 Ventôse Year IV.

The record made Napoleon approximately eighteen months older and Rose four years younger than their actual ages, narrowing an age difference of more than six years. Whether the falsification was deliberate convenience, vanity, or clerical accommodation cannot be decisively established.

A religious marriage was performed secretly at the Tuileries during the night of 1–2 December 1804, immediately before the coronation, reportedly after Joséphine informed Pope Pius VII that the union had never received a Catholic blessing.

The civil marriage was dissolved in December 1809. An ecclesiastical tribunal declared the religious marriage null in January 1810, principally on grounds concerning defective canonical form and Napoleon’s claimed lack of valid consent.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1763–1779

Joséphine grew up on the La Pagerie estate near Les Trois-Îlets. Her upbringing combined provincial noble status, tropical plantation culture, financial insecurity, Catholic observance, and daily proximity to slavery.

From approximately 1773 to 1777 she was educated by the **Dames de la Providence** at Fort-Royal, present-day Fort-de-France. Her education appears to have been conventional rather than extensive: religion, reading, writing, music, dancing, sewing, deportment, and household skills. Alexandre later criticized her spelling and education. Her surviving letters show an expressive but frequently irregular orthography.

No secure evidence supports later stories that she received sophisticated scientific training in Martinique. Her serious patronage of botany developed later at Malmaison.

The death of Catherine-Désirée changed Joséphine’s future. Désirée Renaudin and François de Beauharnais substituted Rose in the projected marriage alliance with Alexandre. In 1779 she crossed the Atlantic with her father, reaching Brest after a difficult voyage, then traveled to the Paris region.

---

## Marriage to Alexandre de Beauharnais, 1779–1785

The marriage contract was signed on 10 December 1779; the church wedding followed three days later.

The match joined two financially strained colonial-noble families. Alexandre was educated, politically ambitious, conscious of metropolitan manners, and disappointed by what he considered Rose’s provincial formation. He spent long periods away and had extramarital relationships.

Eugène was born in Paris in 1781 and Hortense in 1783. Alexandre questioned Hortense’s paternity and accused Rose of infidelity. His accusations were influenced, according to later reconstruction, by his mistress Laure de Longpré. The allegations were not judicially established.

Late in 1783 Rose entered the **Abbey of Penthemont**, a fashionable religious residence used by aristocratic women during marital disputes. This was not imprisonment in the criminal sense. The separation proceedings generated documentary testimony about Alexandre’s conduct and Rose’s reputation. Arbitrators found much of his case unproved; a formal separation was arranged in 1785, with financial provision and custody arrangements.

Claims that Alexandre routinely raped or violently beat Joséphine appear in modern popular retellings but are not established to the standard warranted for documentary fact by the sources consulted. Their marriage was unquestionably hostile and included accusations, coercive pressure, abandonment, and litigation-like family arbitration.

---

## Separation, Martinique, and Return to France, 1785–1792

After leaving Penthemont, Rose developed a more independent Parisian social existence. She lived at different addresses, relied on family funds and credit, and moved in aristocratic and literary circles connected with the Beauharnais family.

In 1788 she returned to Martinique with Hortense. Eugène remained principally in France with his father. Motives included money, family circumstances, and the possibility of settling colonial property questions.

She stayed in Martinique during the early French Revolution. In 1790 disturbances involving rival political and racial factions spread around Fort-Royal. Rose and Hortense left amid the disorder and sailed for France, arriving in late 1790. Popular accounts sometimes dramatize their departure as a narrowly escaped massacre; danger was real, but precise cinematic versions are retrospective.

Alexandre became active in revolutionary politics. He was president of the National Constituent Assembly during the royal family’s flight to Varennes in June 1791. Despite their separation, Rose remained tied to his name, finances, children, and political exposure.

---

## Revolution, Arrest, and the Terror, 1792–1794

Alexandre served as a general of the revolutionary armies. After the fall of Mainz in 1793, he was accused of military failure and arrested under the increasingly expansive laws against suspects.

Rose attempted to defend him and maintained contacts across aristocratic and revolutionary networks. On 19 April 1794 she was accused; an imprisonment order followed, and she was confined in the former Carmelite convent known as the **Prison des Carmes**.

Alexandre was tried by the Revolutionary Tribunal and guillotined on 23 July 1794, five days before Robespierre’s fall. He was buried in a mass grave.

Joséphine remained incarcerated for more than three months. Later memoir literature supplied dramatic stories of prison romance, imminent execution, prophetic reassurance, and messages smuggled through a dog. Some Carmes anecdotes may preserve authentic prison practice, but many cannot be independently verified.

She was released on 6 August 1794, after 9 Thermidor ended Robespierre’s government. The often-repeated assertion that her fellow prisoner Thérésa Cabarrus, later Madame Tallien, personally secured her release through Tallien is plausible but simplified; the general collapse of the Terror’s detention system was decisive.

The experience left Rose a widow, with two children, compromised property, debts, and unusually valuable social connections among survivors of several political factions.

---

## Thermidorian Society and Relationship with Napoleon, 1794–1796

After release, the widow Beauharnais rebuilt her finances and public life. Some confiscated assets were restored, but her income remained insecure. She entered the fashionable post-Terror world of salons, theaters, contractors, generals, politicians, and financiers.

Important associates included:

- **Thérésa Tallien**, friend and fellow prisoner.
- **Paul Barras**, a leading member of the Directory.
- **Lazare Hoche**, general; claims of an affair exist but are not conclusively documented.
- **Pauline de Villeneuve**, **Fortunée Hamelin**, and other salon figures.
- Members of the Beauharnais, Renaudin, Tallien, and emerging Bonaparte networks.

### Barras

The claim that Joséphine was Barras’s mistress is a persistent contemporary and retrospective allegation. Barras himself encouraged damaging versions in memoirs written after his political fall. Their association is documented; a sexual relationship is possible but not proved by unequivocal surviving evidence.

The cruder legend that Barras “gave” Joséphine to Napoleon to dispose of an expensive mistress belongs to hostile political mythology. Barras supported Napoleon’s career and knew Joséphine, but the transactional formulation rests chiefly on retrospective gossip.

### Meeting Napoleon

Napoleon probably met Rose in the autumn of 1795 in the social circle surrounding Barras and the Directory. The sentimental story that Eugène approached Napoleon to recover his executed father’s sword and thereby introduced him to Rose is **legendary or embellished**. It appears in later Bonapartist narrative and lacks firm contemporary corroboration.

Napoleon fell intensely in love and renamed her “Joséphine” in his correspondence. She was older, socially experienced, and useful as an entrée into political society. She appears initially to have regarded the marriage more pragmatically than romantically.

They signed a marriage contract and married on 9 March 1796. Two days later Napoleon departed to command the Army of Italy.

---

## Italy, the Hippolyte Charles Affair, and Financial Speculation, 1796–1798

Napoleon’s letters from Italy are among the best-known love letters in European history. They record desire, jealousy, demands that Joséphine join him, and frustration at her infrequent replies.

After delays attributed variously to illness, reluctance, pregnancy claims, social commitments, and her relationship with **Hippolyte Charles**, she traveled to Italy in June 1796. She moved through Milan, Brescia, Bologna, Genoa, and other centers within the French military-political sphere.

### Hippolyte Charles

Charles was a young hussar officer attached to General Leclerc. Joséphine’s affair with him is a **documented fact**, supported by surviving letters and contemporary reports. It began by 1796 and continued, intermittently, into 1798 or 1799.

The relationship became entangled with army contracting. Charles was connected to the supplier **Jean-Pierre Bodin**. Napoleon’s brothers Joseph and Louis suspected that Joséphine had assisted or concealed speculative dealings and supply contracts involving Charles and Bodin.

The surviving material supports:

- Joséphine’s intimate attachment to Charles.
- Her fear that Napoleon would discover it.
- Charles’s involvement in contracting and financial activity.
- Bonaparte family suspicions of improper influence.

It does **not** establish a formal criminal conviction or prove that Joséphine personally organized a fraud. The episode should be described as a mixture of adultery, conflict of interest, patronage, and suspected profiteering, not as an adjudicated corruption case.

Napoleon learned fuller details during the Egyptian expedition in 1798. A private letter discussing her infidelity was intercepted by the British and published, internationalizing the scandal. On returning unexpectedly from Egypt in October 1799, he initially refused to admit her. Eugène and Hortense helped secure a reconciliation.

The reconciliation altered the marriage. Napoleon began or continued affairs of his own, while Joséphine became more emotionally dependent on preserving the union.

---

## The Consulate and Malmaison, 1799–1804

Joséphine purchased the **Château de Malmaison** in April 1799 for approximately 325,000 francs while Napoleon was in Egypt. The price, repairs, furnishings, gardens, and purchases produced enormous debts. Napoleon was angry but retained the property and repeatedly paid or restructured her obligations.

After the coup of 18 Brumaire, 9–10 November 1799, Napoleon became First Consul. Joséphine did not formulate the coup, but her salons, social tact, and contacts contributed to the regime’s social consolidation.

Malmaison became simultaneously:

- A private residence.
- An informal political salon.
- A setting for ministerial meetings.
- A center of music, amateur theater, fashion, and art.
- A horticultural and zoological experimental establishment.
- A showcase for the new ruling household.

Architects **Charles Percier** and **Pierre-François-Léonard Fontaine** redesigned interiors; **Louis-Martin Berthault** played a major role in the gardens and later buildings.

Joséphine solicited favors, appointments, pensions, pardons, and returns from emigration. Numerous petitions passed through her household. This constituted court patronage, not a defined ministerial office. Her reputation for intercession earned the later epithet *la bonne Joséphine*, though petitioners’ gratitude and Bonapartist commemoration magnified it.

### Dynastic family policy

Napoleon incorporated the Beauharnais children into his regime:

- Eugène became an aide-de-camp, imperial prince, and eventually viceroy of Italy.
- Hortense married Napoleon’s brother Louis on 4 January 1802. The marriage was politically arranged and unhappy.
- Their sons strengthened the apparent union of the Bonaparte and Beauharnais families.

The death in 1807 of Hortense’s eldest son, Napoleon-Charles, was a dynastic blow because Napoleon had considered the child a possible heir.

---

## Empress of the French, 1804–1809

Napoleon proclaimed the Empire on 18 May 1804. Joséphine became **Empress of the French**.

### Religious marriage and coronation

Because Pope Pius VII was to attend the coronation, Joséphine disclosed that her civil marriage had not been blessed by the Church. Cardinal Fesch performed a private ceremony immediately before the coronation.

On 2 December 1804 at Notre-Dame de Paris, Napoleon crowned himself and then crowned Joséphine. Jacques-Louis David’s enormous painting depicts her kneeling before him. David idealized the participants, rearranged positions, included Napoleon’s absent mother, and converted ceremony into dynastic visual propaganda.

Napoleon was crowned king of Italy in Milan in May 1805; Joséphine held the corresponding title of queen, although she was not separately crowned there.

### Court and household

As empress, Joséphine maintained a large household under regulated etiquette. Important women included Adélaïde de La Rochefoucauld, Émilie de Beauharnais Lavalette, Madame d’Arberg, Madame de Rémusat, Madame de Talhouët, and her chamber attendant Marie-Jeanne-Pierrette Avrillion.

Joséphine performed ceremonial receptions, charitable visits, dynastic hospitality, gift exchange, and patronage. Her political influence was personal and intercessory rather than constitutional. She sometimes advocated for émigrés and people threatened by the regime, but she could not consistently override Napoleon.

### Travels

Her imperial movements included:

- Italian journeys associated with Napoleon’s campaigns and political settlements.
- Strasbourg and Mainz during imperial progresses.
- Milan, Venice, Mantua, and other Italian cities.
- Brussels and territories incorporated into or allied with the Empire.
- Plombières and other spas, partly in attempts to treat infertility.
- Fontainebleau, Saint-Cloud, the Tuileries, Malmaison, and other imperial residences.

Precise day-by-day itineraries are preserved in specialist chronologies, court records, and correspondence but are too numerous for full reproduction here.

### Infertility and succession

Joséphine had borne two children but never conceived a surviving child with Napoleon. Proposed explanations have included complications from Hortense’s birth, infection, age-related decline, stress, or other gynecological causes. No diagnosis can be established retrospectively.

Napoleon initially faced uncertainty about whether he was infertile. The births of children attributed to him by Éléonore Denuelle de La Plaigne in 1806 and Maria Walewska in 1810 persuaded him that he could father an heir.

### Expenditure and debt

Joséphine’s expenditures were extraordinary even by court standards. Major categories included:

- Dresses, shawls, shoes, hats, and textiles.
- Jewelry.
- Furniture and interior decoration.
- Art and antiquities.
- Plants, greenhouses, animals, and gardening staff.
- Gifts and personal pensions.
- Malmaison construction and maintenance.

Contemporary and later totals vary because invoices, imperial payments, household budgets, private debts, and arrears were calculated differently. Figures exceeding one million francs recur at several moments; Napoleon repeatedly settled accounts. The conduct was financially damaging and concealed from him at times, but it was not prosecuted as theft or fraud.

## Malmaison: Science, Art, Collecting, and Imperial Resources

Joséphine was a patron and collector, not a trained botanist. Her importance lies in assembling money, people, diplomatic connections, specimens, gardens, images, and institutions.

### Botanical work

She worked with or patronized:

- **Aimé Bonpland**, botanist and explorer.
- **Étienne Pierre Ventenat**, botanist and author of *Jardin de la Malmaison*.
- **Pierre-Joseph Redouté**, flower painter.
- **André Thouin** and other figures associated with the Muséum national d’Histoire naturelle.
- Nurserymen including the Kennedy and Lee nursery networks in Britain.
- Garden designer Louis-Martin Berthault.

Approximately 200 plants reportedly flowered for the first time in France at Malmaison. The estate cultivated magnolias, camellias, tree peonies, hibiscus, dahlias, and many plants from the Americas, Africa, Asia, and Australia.

The museum at Malmaison estimates that she assembled more than 250 rose species and varieties. The familiar claim that she created a modern, formally organized “rose garden” is misleading: roses were grown in pots, beds, forcing houses, and mixed displays. The word and specialized layout *roseraie* belong mainly to a later period.

### Zoology

The menagerie included black swans, kangaroos, emus, ostriches, parrots, zebras, and other animals. Some were eventually transferred to the Muséum because specialist care was expensive.

### Art and books

Joséphine patronized painters, sculptors, porcelain makers, textile manufacturers, jewelers, and cabinetmakers. Artists associated with her collections included Redouté, Prud’hon, Gérard, Isabey, Girodet, and Vallayer-Coster.

Malmaison’s library reportedly held approximately 13,000 books. She also collected minerals, antiquities, paintings, sculpture, porcelain, and luxury objects.

Her privileged access had a public-private ambiguity. Museum research notes that she used ministers, diplomats, explorers, public institutions, and specimens from state collections to build a private collection. This was not then prosecuted as unlawful appropriation, but it demonstrates how imperial office enabled personal collecting.

---

## Divorce and Annulment, 1809–1810

By 1809 Napoleon had decided that the dynasty required a biological heir and an alliance with a reigning house.

On 30 November he informed Joséphine of the decision. A formal family assembly occurred on 15 December. Both spouses read statements presenting the separation as a mutual sacrifice for France. Joséphine’s distress and resistance are amply reported; the ceremonial language should not be taken as evidence that she wanted the divorce.

The Sénat conservateur enacted the civil dissolution on 16 December 1809. An ecclesiastical tribunal declared the religious marriage null on 10 January 1810.

Napoleon’s opponents described the process as repudiation dressed in constitutional and religious forms. The ecclesiastical ruling has long been criticized as politically compliant. Its legal basis was a claimed absence of proper canonical form and defects of consent, not infertility itself.

Joséphine retained:

- The title and precedence of empress.
- Malmaison and its furnishings.
- The Élysée Palace as a Paris residence.
- The Château de Navarre near Évreux.
- A very large annual allowance, conventionally given as three million francs, although payments and debts complicated her actual liquidity.
- Continued place for her children in the imperial dynasty.

Napoleon married Archduchess Marie-Louise of Austria in 1810. Joséphine withdrew from the wedding ceremonies but followed reports of the new marriage and the 1811 birth of the king of Rome.

---

## Later Life, 1810–1814

Joséphine divided her time principally between Malmaison and Navarre. Navarre gave rise to a small post-imperial court of relatives, attendants, artists, and visitors. She maintained correspondence with Napoleon, Eugène, Hortense, relatives, and court figures.

Her household remained costly. The title Duchess of Navarre was created for her in April 1810 with arrangements intended to make it hereditary through Eugène’s line.

She traveled to Switzerland in 1810 and visited places associated with Hortense. She continued collecting, gardening, charitable patronage, music, and theater.

Relations with Napoleon remained affectionate but controlled. He sent news, gifts, and money and at times restricted her movements or contacts for reasons of etiquette and politics. Joséphine preserved many of his letters. Far fewer letters from her to Napoleon survive, possibly because of uneven preservation or deliberate family destruction; a comprehensive destruction campaign is suspected but not proved.

### Collapse of the Empire

In 1814 allied armies entered France. Joséphine left Malmaison temporarily for Navarre because of military danger, then returned after Napoleon’s abdication.

Tsar **Alexander I of Russia** treated her and her children favorably. He visited Malmaison, as did King Frederick William III of Prussia and members of the allied royal entourage. Alexander’s protection was politically important because Eugène’s status and property were uncertain.

Later sentimental accounts claim that Joséphine asked to accompany Napoleon to Elba. Evidence shows continued concern for him, but no practical plan for her to join his exile was implemented.

---

## Death and Funeral

Joséphine was unwell in May 1814 but continued receiving visitors. Her condition worsened after receptions and walks involving Alexander I and allied royalty.

She developed fever and severe throat symptoms. On 29 May Abbé Bertrand administered the last rites. Joséphine died around noon in her bedroom at Malmaison, with Eugène and Hortense nearby.

The autopsy on 30 May identified “angine gangréneuse”; other records and modern summaries call the illness pneumonia. These descriptions are compatible with a severe respiratory and throat infection but do not permit a precise modern diagnosis.

Her funeral occurred at Rueil on 2 June. Reports claimed approximately 20,000 mourners, but this is a contemporary estimate rather than a verified count. Eugène and Hortense did not participate publicly because court etiquette excluded close royal family members from the procession.

Her temporary burial was replaced by the monument designed by Berthault and sculpted by Pierre Cartellier. Restoration authorities resisted imperial insignia; Cartellier represented her kneeling in the pose of David’s coronation painting and arranged her headdress so that it evoked a diadem without explicitly violating the restriction.

## Wealth, Property, and Financial Relationships

### Principal property

- Family interest in La Pagerie, Martinique.
- Paris residences, including the house in rue Chantereine, renamed rue de la Victoire.
- Château de Malmaison, bought in 1799.
- Use of the Tuileries, Saint-Cloud, Fontainebleau, and other state residences as consort.
- Élysée Palace after divorce.
- Château de Navarre and associated ducal endowment.

### Sources of income

- Marriage settlements and Beauharnais property.
- Recoveries after revolutionary confiscation.
- Napoleon’s personal and state-backed payments.
- Imperial household allocations.
- Post-divorce pension and landed endowment.
- Credit supplied by merchants who expected imperial payment.

### Debts

Her suppliers repeatedly extended credit far beyond ordinary private means because she was Napoleon’s wife and later empress. She concealed invoices and shifted obligations between household accounts. Napoleon periodically ordered investigations, reprimanded suppliers, and paid debts.

No evidence consulted establishes that Joséphine was convicted of embezzlement, fraudulent accounting, money laundering, or another financial crime. Her finances are better characterized as chronic overconsumption sustained by court credit, influence, and repeated rescue.

### Colonial financial interest

Her natal family’s plantation economy rested on slavery. The extent to which Joséphine personally received income derived directly from enslaved labor at particular dates is difficult to reconstruct from general biographies. Her family identity and potential inheritance were nevertheless bound to slave property and the planter economy.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Political position

Joséphine did not publish a political program. Her loyalties were relational and adaptive:

- Born into colonial royalist nobility.
- Married to a revolutionary constitutional politician.
- Imprisoned during the Terror.
- Integrated into Thermidorian and Directory society.
- Consort of the First Consul and emperor.
- Intercessor for émigrés and former nobles.
- Host to victorious Allied sovereigns after Napoleon’s fall.

This adaptability was interpreted by admirers as tact and mercy, and by critics as opportunism.

### Religion

She was baptized and educated as a Catholic. Her practice was flexible during the Revolution and Consulate. She insisted on a religious marriage before the coronation and received Catholic last rites. Her religion should not be confused with the fortune-telling legends surrounding her.

### Political influence

Her strongest influence was exercised through:

- Social introductions.
- Petitions and recommendations.
- Marriage alliances.
- Protection of the Beauharnais family.
- Reconciliation between the regime and portions of the old aristocracy.
- Court ceremonial and cultural patronage.

There is little evidence that she determined military, diplomatic, colonial, or legislative policy.

### Artistic identity

She did not claim professional authorship or artistic production. Her historical importance in art came through taste, collecting, commissions, architecture, gardens, clothing, portraiture, and the display of imperial femininity.

---

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Napoleon Bonaparte

Husband, political protector, lover, co-head of the Consular and imperial household, and ultimately the ruler who dissolved their marriage. Their correspondence documents intense early passion, jealousy, estrangement, reconciliation, dynastic calculation, and continuing post-divorce affection.

### Alexandre de Beauharnais

First husband and father of her children. Their marriage produced litigation, separation, competing accusations, reconciliation during the revolutionary period, and shared exposure to the Terror.

### Eugène and Hortense

Her most durable intimate relationships. Both children participated in her reconciliation with Napoleon in 1799. Their marriages and descendants spread the Beauharnais connection across Europe.

### Thérésa Tallien

Friend from the Carmes prison and Thermidorian society. Later memoir and popular culture made the women emblematic of post-Terror fashion.

### Paul Barras

Political associate and alleged lover. He linked the worlds of Thermidorian power, society, and Napoleon’s advancement. Sexual and transactional interpretations remain disputed.

### Hippolyte Charles

Documented lover and participant in a network of military contracting and speculative finance.

### Napoleon’s family

Joséphine faced sustained hostility from Napoleon’s mother Letizia and several siblings, who distrusted her age, debts, children, alleged infidelity, and influence. Relations varied individually. She promoted marriages and positions for Beauharnais relatives, contributing to Bonaparte-Beauharnais rivalry.

### Madame de Rémusat

Lady-in-waiting and memoirist. Her memoir is a major source for court life but was reconstructed after the original manuscript was destroyed; it requires comparison with correspondence and official records.

### Pierre-Joseph Redouté and Étienne Ventenat

Scientific-artistic collaborators whose publications made Malmaison’s plants internationally visible.

### Alexander I

Post-imperial protector and guest during her final weeks. Later memory romanticized their meetings, but his practical favor toward the Beauharnais family is documented.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### Family participation in slavery

**Documented fact:** Joséphine was born into a white Creole planter family that owned enslaved people and operated a sugar estate in Martinique.

**Unresolved financial question:** General biographies do not fully quantify what income or property she personally received from enslaved labor at each stage of her life.

### Alleged responsibility for Napoleon’s restoration of slavery

France abolished slavery in its colonies in 1794. The law of 20 May 1802 maintained slavery where abolition had not taken effect, while subsequent measures and military force restored it elsewhere under French control.

**Documented fact:** Napoleon’s government restored and maintained slavery and sent forces against Black and mixed-race resistance in the Caribbean.

**Widely circulated allegation:** Joséphine persuaded Napoleon to restore slavery to protect her family’s Martinique interests.

**Evidence assessment:** No surviving order, letter, cabinet record, or reliable contemporaneous testimony consulted establishes that Joséphine initiated or dictated the policy. Martinique was under British occupation when abolition occurred and slavery had continued there; it returned to France under the Treaty of Amiens. The policy had broader military, colonial, commercial, racial, and planter-lobby causes.

**Conclusion:** Her slaveholding background is documented. Her decisive personal authorship of the 1802 policy is an **unproved and probably apocryphal attribution**, though it became politically and memorially powerful.

### Barras relationship

**Documented fact:** Joséphine and Barras belonged to the same political-social network; Barras supported Napoleon.

**Allegation:** They were lovers and Barras transferred her to Napoleon.

**Assessment:** A sexual relationship is possible but not conclusively proved. The “transfer” story reflects misogynistic and anti-Directory polemic as much as recoverable fact.

### Hippolyte Charles and contracting

**Documented fact:** Joséphine had an affair with Charles and exchanged compromising letters.

**Documented context:** Charles and Bodin were involved in army supply business.

**Allegation:** Joséphine used Napoleon’s position to assist fraudulent or corrupt military contracts.

**Assessment:** Surviving correspondence raises serious conflict-of-interest and concealment questions. No criminal adjudication against Joséphine established fraud.

### Extramarital relationships

The Charles affair is documented. Relationships with Barras, Hoche, and several others range from plausible allegation to gossip. Napoleon also had numerous affairs. Later imperial and anti-imperial writers selectively emphasized one spouse’s conduct for dynastic or polemical purposes.

### Spending and unpaid suppliers

**Documented fact:** She incurred exceptionally large debts, concealed purchases, and repeatedly required Napoleon to settle accounts.

**Not established:** Theft, criminal fraud, or formal embezzlement.

### Political patronage

She used access to obtain appointments, pardons, pensions, and favors. This was normal court practice but involved unequal access and could overlap with supplier interests. No modern distinction between public and private influence was consistently applied by the imperial household.

### Annulment

The civil dissolution followed the regime’s dynastic requirements. The Catholic annulment was produced by a tribunal operating under overwhelming political pressure. It was legally formal but remains historically controversial as an example of ecclesiastical accommodation to imperial power.

### Current and recent controversy

The most visible modern controversy concerns her representation in Martinique. Her statue at Fort-de-France became a focal point for criticism of colonial memory:

- Inaugurated in 1859 under Second Empire influence.
- Decapitated in 1991 and later marked with red paint.
- Reinterpreted in scholarship and performance art as a counter-monument.
- Pulled down and destroyed by activists on 26 July 2020.

Arguments for removal linked her to planter society, slavery, and an imperial memory that marginalized the enslaved. Arguments against the specific historical accusation stressed the absence of evidence that she caused the 1802 restoration. Recent scholarship distinguishes those questions: the causal story may be apocryphal while the monument’s colonial political function remains historically demonstrable.

A dedicated search combining her name with controversy, scandal, allegations, lawsuit, fraud, accusations, conspiracy, and criticism, including results published or indexed in 2024–2026, found no credible new criminal allegation or newly discovered lawsuit against her. Recent discussion continues to center on slavery, monument removal, colonial memory, spending, sexual reputation, and fictional distortion.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### The fortune teller who predicted a crown

**Story:** A Martinican woman—usually named Euphémia or described as an old Black or mixed-race fortune teller—told young Rose that she would become greater than a queen but later fall.

**Appearance and circulation:** Nineteenth-century biographies, memoir literature, popular histories, novels, and screen drama.

**Evidence:** No contemporary childhood record verifies the prophecy. Its wording closely matches knowledge available only after Joséphine’s rise and divorce.

**Assessment:** **Myth or retrospective prophecy.**

**Reputational effect:** It made her life appear fated, exotic, and romantically tragic while turning Martinique into a mystical origin scene.

### The dog Fortuné

**Story:** Joséphine’s pug Fortuné carried messages during her imprisonment and later bit Napoleon in the bridal bed.

**Evidence:** Joséphine owned dogs, and the bridal-night anecdote was repeated in memoir culture. The prison-message and biting details lack firm contemporaneous confirmation.

**Assessment:** **Reported anecdote with legendary elaboration.**

**Effect:** Fortuné became a durable comic element in biographies and popular culture.

### Eugène’s sword introduces Napoleon to Joséphine

**Story:** After disarming Paris, Napoleon allowed young Eugène to retain or recover Alexandre’s sword; Joséphine thanked him, and romance followed.

**Evidence:** The episode is poorly supported and appears mainly in later heroic narratives.

**Assessment:** **Bonapartist legend.**

**Effect:** It supplied a virtuous, sentimental origin for a relationship probably formed through Directory society.

### “Barras gave Joséphine to Napoleon”

**Story:** Barras wished to discard his aging, expensive mistress and married her off to an ambitious subordinate.

**Evidence:** Association is documented; ownership-like transfer is not. Barras’s hostile retrospective memoir and anti-Directory gossip helped circulate it.

**Assessment:** **Hostile mythology resting on an unproved sexual allegation.**

### Joséphine as sole author of restored slavery

**Story:** She used sexual influence over Napoleon to reverse abolition.

**Evidence:** Her background in planter society is undeniable; documentary evidence of decisive intervention has not been found.

**Assessment:** **Unverified political legend**, historically significant because of colonial memory and monument politics.

### The perfect imperial love story

**Story:** Napoleon and Joséphine remained mutually faithful soulmates separated only by the state’s need for an heir.

**Evidence against:** Both had extramarital relationships; their marriage involved jealousy, financial conflict, and political calculation.

**Evidence for a narrower claim:** Their correspondence and post-divorce behavior show enduring attachment.

**Assessment:** **Romantic simplification of a documented but unstable bond.**

### Joséphine as exclusively benevolent

Nineteenth-century panegyrics presented her as a universal intercessor and charitable mother of France. She did make interventions and gifts, but the hagiographic image suppresses slavery, patronage, debts, and court privilege.

### Joséphine as empty-headed spendthrift

Hostile accounts reduce her to clothing, adultery, and debt. Her spending was real, but the caricature omits her political mediation, dynastic role, sustained collecting, and sophisticated mobilization of botanical, artistic, and diplomatic networks.

### The formal rose garden

**Story:** Malmaison contained a modern roseraie in which every known rose was systematically planted.

**Evidence:** Joséphine maintained an exceptional rose collection, but the museum notes that the formal concept of a roseraie emerged later. Her roses were distributed among pots, forcing houses, beds, and mixed plantings.

**Assessment:** **Later horticultural myth built on a genuine collection.**

### Napoleon’s last word was “Joséphine”

Napoleon’s reported last words at Saint Helena are variously transcribed as fragments including “France,” “army,” “head of the army,” and “Joséphine.”

**Assessment:** **Reported but textually unstable deathbed tradition.** Even if a witness heard the name, the precise final sequence cannot be securely reconstructed.

## Works by or Attributed to Joséphine

Joséphine was not a professional author. Her genuine literary corpus consists overwhelmingly of letters, notes, dictated messages, formal declarations, and signatures.

### Authentic and edited correspondence

- **Private letters to Napoleon Bonaparte**, 1796–1814. Only a fraction survive. They exist across archives, sales, family collections, and edited compilations.
- **Letters to Eugène de Beauharnais**, numerous dates; major source for family and political relations.
- **Letters to Hortense de Beauharnais**, family and court correspondence.
- **Letters to Napoleon’s ministers, diplomats, household officers, artists, suppliers, botanists, and petitioners.**
- **Letters connected with Hippolyte Charles**, crucial evidence for their affair and financial anxieties.
- **Formal divorce declaration**, read 15 December 1809. An official political document delivered in her name; the degree of drafting by imperial officials is uncertain.

### Major printed collections

- *Lettres de Napoléon à Joséphine pendant la première campagne d’Italie, le Consulat et l’Empire; et lettres de Joséphine à Napoléon et à sa fille* (Paris, 1833). Posthumous edited collection.
- *Mémoires et correspondance de l’impératrice Joséphine* (Paris, 1820), published by Regnault-Warin. **Not an authenticated autobiography.** It is an early posthumous compilation with literary shaping and should not be treated as a memoir written by Joséphine.
- Jean Hanoteau, ed., *Les Beauharnais et l’Empereur: lettres de l’impératrice Joséphine et de la reine Hortense au prince Eugène* (Paris: Plon, 1936). Posthumous scholarly collection.
- Jean Hanoteau, ed., *Le Ménage Beauharnais* (Paris, 1935). Documentary correspondence concerning Joséphine and Alexandre.
- Jean-Michel Laot, ed., *Napoléon et Joséphine: correspondance, lettres intimes* (2012). Modern edited compilation.
- *Correspondance (1782–1814)*, modern cataloged editions under the authorship heading “Impératrice Joséphine de Beauharnais.”

### Works commissioned by, but not authored by, Joséphine

- Étienne-Pierre Ventenat, *Jardin de la Malmaison* (1803–1804), with plates by Pierre-Joseph Redouté.
- Pierre-Joseph Redouté, *Les Liliacées* (1802–1816), supported in part through her patronage.
- Pierre-Joseph Redouté, *Les Roses* (1817–1824). Published after her death; associated with the Malmaison legacy but not a catalogue authored by Joséphine.
- Architectural, garden, decorative, and artistic commissions at Malmaison. These were patronage projects, not her authored designs in a strict professional sense.

No patents, technical inventions, signed scientific papers, novels, memoirs demonstrably written by her, or independently authored political manifestos have been identified.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Immediate memory

After 1814 pamphlets and funeral accounts presented Joséphine as charitable, gracious, wronged by divorce, and beloved across political divisions. This image contrasted her with Marie-Louise and assisted the Beauharnais family after Napoleon’s fall.

Napoleon’s reported continuing attachment, his preservation of the memory of Malmaison, and traditions about his last words transformed their marriage into a central Napoleonic love story.

### Dynastic legacy

Joséphine had no child with Napoleon, but her descendants occupied or married into numerous European thrones. Her grandson Napoleon III ruled France from 1852 to 1870. Her granddaughter Joséphine of Leuchtenberg became queen of Sweden and Norway. Through Eugène’s descendants, her lineage entered the royal houses of Sweden, Norway, Denmark, Belgium, and Luxembourg.

The Second Empire institutionalized her cult. Napoleon III promoted Malmaison, family relics, portraits, publications, and the 1859 Martinique statue.

### Botanical legacy

Malmaison became a foundational site in the cultural history of nineteenth-century horticulture. Her patronage accelerated the circulation, acclimatization, description, and illustration of plants. Redouté’s flower images made her garden an enduring symbol even after the living collection dispersed.

Her name survives in rose cultivars, plant names, garden histories, exhibitions, perfumes, decorative arts, and commercial branding. Not every plant called “Joséphine” can be traced directly to her collection.

### Fashion and material culture

The high-waisted white dresses, shawls, cameos, classical references, and luxury interiors associated with Joséphine helped define the visual language of the Directoire, Consulate, and Empire. She was not the sole originator of the style, but court portraiture made her one of its chief icons.

### Colonial reinterpretation

In Martinique, the Second Empire’s image of a local girl who became empress coexisted with the memory of plantation slavery. From the late twentieth century, activists and scholars increasingly treated her monument as an artifact of white Creole and French imperial power.

The decapitated statue became a palimpsest: simultaneously the original imperial monument and visible evidence of anticolonial rejection. Its destruction in 2020 shifted the debate from reinterpretation of a damaged monument to removal from public space.

Current historical work generally separates three propositions:

1. Joséphine belonged to and benefited from a slaveholding colonial family: **documented fact**.
2. Napoleon restored slavery and pursued violent colonial war: **documented fact**.
3. Joséphine personally caused that policy: **unproved claim**.

### Historical reinterpretation

Successive portrayals have emphasized different identities:

- Creole Cinderella.
- Revolutionary survivor.
- Seductive and unfaithful widow.
- Social broker of the Directory.
- Beloved imperial consort.
- Financially irresponsible consumer.
- Botanical and artistic patron.
- Victim of dynastic repudiation.
- Beneficiary and symbol of colonial slavery.
- Ancestress of European royalty.

No single one of these categories reconstructs her entire life.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are included. Mere contemporaneity has been excluded.

### Napoleon Bonaparte — documented fact

Second husband, political partner, patron, and emperor. They probably met in Paris in 1795, married there on 9 March 1796, were crowned emperor and empress at Notre-Dame on 2 December 1804, and formally separated in December 1809. They continued corresponding until 1814.

### Louis XVI — documented indirect institutional connection

Joséphine’s first husband Alexandre de Beauharnais was president of the National Constituent Assembly during Louis XVI’s flight to Varennes in June 1791. Joséphine was linked to the revolutionary political world through Alexandre, but no significant personal meeting between her and Louis XVI was established in the sources consulted.

### Aimé Jacques Alexandre Goujaud, called Aimé Bonpland — documented fact

Bonpland, botanist and explorer, participated in the scientific network supplying and interpreting exotic plants associated with Malmaison after returning from the American expedition with Alexander von Humboldt. Joséphine acquired and cultivated material connected with that network.

### Alexander von Humboldt — documented influence/network connection; direct meeting not established here

Humboldt’s and Bonpland’s American collections contributed to the European botanical world from which Malmaison benefited. Bonpland had a more direct working relationship with Joséphine. A face-to-face meeting between Humboldt and Joséphine is plausible but was not established by the sources consulted and is therefore not asserted as fact.

### Pierre-Simon Laplace — reported fact

Laplace served in Napoleon’s political and scientific circle and is named in accounts of ministerial gatherings at Malmaison. This supports presence in the same institutional and domestic-political setting. The exact dates and content of personal exchanges with Joséphine were not recovered.

### Joseph-Louis Lagrange — reported fact

Lagrange appears among scientific figures received in the Consular and imperial social world associated with Malmaison. Evidence consulted supports shared institutional setting rather than a close individual relationship.

### Marie-Joseph Paul Yves Roch Gilbert du Motier, Marquis de Lafayette — reported social connection

Older biographical accounts name Lafayette among political and social acquaintances shared by the Beauharnais circle during the early Revolution. The precise depth of Joséphine’s individual relationship with him remains unclear.

### Marie Adrienne Françoise de Noailles, Marquise de Lafayette — no substantiated direct connection found

She occupied an overlapping aristocratic and revolutionary social world, but the consulted sources did not establish a significant personal relationship with Joséphine.

### Johann Wolfgang von Goethe — no substantiated direct connection found

Goethe interacted with Napoleon and the Napoleonic cultural world, but no reliable direct meeting or correspondence with Joséphine was established.

### Pierre-Joseph Redouté — absence from atlas roster

Redouté was among Joséphine’s most important documented collaborators, but he is not listed in the supplied atlas roster.

The remaining roster names produced no documented meeting, correspondence, close institutional relationship, family tie, or explicitly acknowledged personal influence in the sources consulted. Absence from this section should not be interpreted as proof that two people never occupied the same event; it means that no dossier-level connection was established.

- **2024–2026:** Scholarship and public discussion continue to focus on the Fort-de-France monument, slavery, colonial memory, and distinctions between her documented background and alleged personal responsibility for the 1802 policy.
