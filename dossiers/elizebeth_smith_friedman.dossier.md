### Evidentiary caution on numerical claims

Frequently repeated totals—12,000 rumrunner messages, 650 prosecutions, 33 court appearances, 4,000 wartime messages on 48 circuits, and 8,398 soldiers aboard *Queen Mary*—come from different periods and source traditions. They are useful measures of scale but are not interchangeable. “Messages solved,” “messages processed,” “cases supported,” “defendants,” “indictments,” “prosecutions,” and “convictions” are often collapsed in later retellings. This dossier preserves those distinctions where the underlying record permits.

## Family and Ancestry

Elizebeth was born into the large family of **John Marion Smith** and **Sopha/Sophia Strock Smith**. John Smith was described in government and family-derived accounts as a Quaker dairyman or dairy farmer, banker, and local politician. The family lived near Huntington, Indiana, and had sufficient property and business standing to be locally secure, but it was not an environment in which higher education for daughters was assumed. Later biographies describe John as resisting Elizebeth’s wish to attend college. The conflict is consistent across her autobiographical tradition, although later inspirational accounts sharpen it into a categorical hostility to educating women.

Sources differ in their count because the couple had ten children but only nine survived infancy. Elizebeth was the youngest of the nine surviving children; a younger infant, Anna M. Smith, died in 1896 in genealogical reconstructions. Identified siblings in family records include Edna, Ethel/Eathel Leona, Lena Alcinda, Harvey Clair, and others; a Marshall Foundation photograph labels the “Smith sisters” Edna, Ethel, Elizebeth, and Lena. Only Elizebeth and one other sibling are said to have attended college.

The surname Smith and the limited digitization of local Quaker and civil records make a complete, independently verified genealogy difficult. Public genealogical sites extend the paternal line to Atchison Smith and Melissa Smith and supply “Clare” as an additional given name. Those details are not used here as settled facts without civil or meeting records.

### Marriage and household

At Riverbank Laboratories, Elizebeth met **William Frederick Friedman**, a Russian-born Jewish immigrant trained in genetics at Cornell University. William was then employed by George Fabyan as a plant geneticist and photographer. Elizebeth brought him into the Bacon-cipher work; their professional courtship became a romantic partnership. They married in Chicago on **21 May 1917**, shortly after American entry into the First World War.

Their marriage was simultaneously domestic, intellectual, and operational. They taught one another, devised problems and demonstrations, collected cryptologic books, and sometimes published jointly. Their domains later separated: William became the Army’s preeminent cryptologist, while Elizebeth led Treasury/Coast Guard work against smugglers and later German clandestine networks. The common description of her as merely “William Friedman’s assistant” is contradicted by her supervisory positions, personnel file, court record, and the operational histories of her unit.

They had two children: **Barbara**, born in 1923, and **John Ramsay**, born in 1926. Photographs place the family at a Military Road home in Washington by 1927, in Rock Creek Park in 1929, and later at their Capitol Hill home, whose garden was photographed in 1956. Elizebeth combined federal work, travel, and repeated courtroom assignments with primary responsibility for much of the household.

William suffered recurrent depression, exhaustion, and psychiatric collapse during the 1930s and 1940s. In January 1941 he entered the neuropsychiatric section of Walter Reed General Hospital and remained for roughly two and a half months. His condition was officially associated with anxiety and overwork on highly secret cryptanalytic projects. Elizebeth protected his privacy, maintained the household, and continued her own work during his illnesses. Later accounts sometimes turn this into a simple heroic-caregiver story; the letters and biographical record instead show an enduring marriage strained by secrecy, money, illness, professional inequality, and extreme work.

## Childhood and Early Life

Elizebeth grew up on or near the family farm at Huntington. She read poetry and literature intensively and developed ambitions that exceeded the expected local occupations for women. Her surviving college-era journal shows restlessness, humor, impatience with prescribed behavior, and attraction to intellectual and personal risk. In a 1913 entry she asked why “something with a risk in it” gave her an exuberant feeling and recorded that people told her she “should have been born a man.” These are contemporary self-observations, not later biographical reconstruction.

Her mother’s naming decision became part of family mythology: *Elizebeth*, not *Elizabeth*, allegedly because Sopha disliked “Eliza.” The story was repeated by Friedman and by the NSA. Its circulation explains both the spelling and her later irritation at its constant misspelling.

Accounts also describe her as skilled at sewing, hairdressing, and fashion advice. The NSA’s biographical notice characterizes her as energetic, impatient, strongly opinionated, and contemptuous of stupidity, while noting that friends exploited these practical talents. These details derive from acquaintances and the Friedman papers rather than employment records.

## Education and Formation

### College of Wooster, 1911–1913

In 1911, aged nineteen, Smith entered the **College of Wooster** in Ohio. She studied literature and languages. Her coursework is summarized in later records as English, Greek, Latin, and German; some biographies also mention French. In 1913, when her mother became ill, she left Wooster and transferred closer to Indiana.

### Hillsdale College, 1913–1915

Smith completed her degree at **Hillsdale College**, Michigan, graduating in 1915 with a major in English literature. She belonged to **Pi Beta Phi**, edited or worked on *The Collegian*, wrote poetry, and continued classical and modern-language study. The later phrase that she “minored in a great many other things” reflects the breadth of her study rather than a formal transcript category.

Hillsdale later awarded her an honorary Doctor of Laws. Sources disagree on whether the year was **1938 or 1939**: older biographies and derivative summaries often give 1938, whereas Hillsdale’s own recent official lists give **1939**. The institutional date, 1939, is preferred pending inspection of the commencement program.

### Formation without a cryptography degree

No American university then offered the professional course of study she ultimately needed. Her technical formation came through:

- Elizabeth Wells Gallup’s practical use of Francis Bacon’s biliteral cipher;
- William Friedman’s statistical and scientific habits;
- Parker Hitt’s *Manual for the Solution of Military Ciphers* and his visits to Riverbank;
- Joseph Mauborgne’s and the Army’s need for workable military systems;
- historical cryptographic literature accumulated at Riverbank;
- daily solution of real intercepted messages;
- the necessity of teaching officers almost as soon as she and William learned a method.

In her 1976 NSA oral history, Friedman rejected a neat retrospective curriculum. Riverbank’s cryptanalysts learned from the limited material available, including Hitt’s manual, and improvised under wartime pressure. She called them, with deliberate irony, “instant cryptanalysts.”

## Early Career

### Teaching, 1915–1916

After graduation, Smith became a substitute principal or administrator at a public high school in **Wabash, Indiana**, in autumn 1915. She disliked the conventional path available to a female literature graduate and left in spring 1916. Her goal was unusual literary research rather than routine teaching.

### The Newberry Library encounter, 1916

In Chicago in the summer of 1916, she visited the **Newberry Library**, reportedly seeking access to Shakespeare’s 1623 First Folio and asking about research work. A librarian knew that the wealthy eccentric **George Fabyan** required an assistant for a Shakespeare-cipher project. The librarian telephoned him. Fabyan arrived in a limousine and took Smith to his Riverbank estate at Geneva, Illinois.

In her 1976 oral history, Friedman told the scene as a personal origin story. Fabyan asked, “Well! What do you know?” She answered, “That remains, sir, for you to find out.” She later called it perhaps the most “immoral” remark she had made, meaning audacious rather than unethical. The story is first-person testimony recorded sixty years later; its dialogue may be polished by repetition, but the meeting and immediate recruitment are well documented.

## Chronological Life History

### 1916–1921: Riverbank Laboratories, Bacon, and the First World War

Fabyan’s estate, **Riverbank**, combined private wealth, scientific experimentation, publicity, and personal autocracy. Its projects included acoustics, genetics, photography, and Fabyan’s campaign to prove that Francis Bacon wrote works attributed to Shakespeare. Smith was assigned to **Elizabeth Wells Gallup**, who claimed that two distinguishable typefaces in early printed books encoded Bacon’s biliteral cipher and revealed a hidden royal and literary history.

Smith initially took the project seriously enough to test it. Her growing conclusion was that Gallup’s readings were not reproducible: ambiguous type could be classified as “a” or “b” to force a desired result. William Friedman, recruited from Riverbank’s genetics department to photograph type and assist with the analysis, reached a similar conclusion. The project trained both in the difference between discovering a cipher and imposing one.

When the United States entered the First World War in April 1917, Fabyan offered Riverbank’s services to Washington. Because the federal government had almost no standing civilian codebreaking institution, Riverbank became an unofficial cryptologic bureau. Government departments forwarded suspicious messages; Army and Navy personnel came for instruction. The Friedmans and colleagues solved messages, constructed systems, built teaching materials, and trained officers. **Agnes Meyer Driscoll**, later a major Navy cryptanalyst, was among those associated with Riverbank training.

The exact division of authorship in the **Riverbank Publications** is important. Most cryptographic pamphlets numbered 15–22 are credited to William F. Friedman. Elizebeth was explicitly coauthor of No. 21, *Methods for the Reconstruction of Primary Alphabets* (1918), and contributed intellectually and operationally to the environment in which the series was produced. Later attempts to credit all eight equally to the couple are not bibliographically supported; the opposite claim, that she merely typed William’s work, is also incompatible with the unit’s operational record.

Fabyan’s patronage was controlling. The Friedmans later alleged that he intercepted correspondence and obstructed attempts to find other employment. Elizebeth described him in her NSA oral history as a “vile creature” in many ways while also insisting that he launched projects important to the country. That ambivalence is more accurate than later versions casting him solely as visionary benefactor or comic eccentric.

William entered Army service in 1918; Elizebeth continued cipher work at Riverbank. After his demobilization in 1919, the couple tried to establish an independent life. They finally left in 1921.

### 1921–1926: Washington, War Department and Navy work

The Friedmans moved to **Washington, D.C.**, in 1921 for civilian cryptographic employment with the War Department. William became a central figure in Army cryptology. Elizebeth’s personnel record shows a sequence of federal appointments rather than an uninterrupted shared post. She worked as a cryptanalyst for the U.S. Navy beginning in the early 1920s—commonly dated 1923—and also withdrew from regular employment for pregnancy, childcare, and family needs.

The births of Barbara in 1923 and John Ramsay in 1926 did not end her technical career. Federal agencies had accumulated intercepted radio traffic from maritime smugglers but lacked analysts capable of systematic solution. Her availability as one of the country’s few experienced cryptanalysts made her repeatedly indispensable.

### 1926/1927–1930: Treasury and Coast Guard anti-smuggling work

During Prohibition, liquor syndicates used ship-to-shore radio to coordinate cargoes from Canada, Newfoundland, the Bahamas, Cuba, Mexico, and British Honduras. They employed code words, substitution alphabets, transpositions, mixed systems, call signs, and operational slang. Coast Guard intercept stations collected traffic, but collection without cryptanalysis produced warehouses of unread material.

The Treasury Department engaged Friedman temporarily in **1926 or 1927**; administrative accounts differ over the exact start because the work passed among Customs, Prohibition, and Coast Guard structures. She and a clerk-typist attacked a backlog measured in years. Her own *History of Work in Cryptanalysis, 1927–1930* records a scope extending across the Atlantic, Gulf, Pacific, and international narcotics routes. Between 1928 and 1930, she and one assistant are credited in institutional histories with solving approximately **12,000 messages**. The often-added claim that these produced **650 prosecutions** is plausible as an aggregate of cases supported, but should not be read as 650 convictions personally secured by Friedman.

In June 1928 she went west to train **C. A. Housel**, associated with Pacific Coast coordination. Later summaries credit Housel with decoding about 3,300 messages over twenty-one months after her instruction. In October–November 1929 she traveled to **Houston, Texas**, where a U.S. attorney had subpoenaed roughly 650 messages. She reportedly reconstructed twenty-four systems. Her work supported cases in Houston and Galveston and against the narcotics-trafficking **Ezra brothers**, members of an international network originating in Shanghai/Hong Kong and linked by later scholarship to American organized crime.

Friedman repeatedly emphasized weak cipher discipline. Rumrunners chose predictable keys such as “Havana,” reused systems, and mixed operational information with recognizable language. Her maxim—reported in her writings—was never to choose a keyword associated with the operation being concealed.

### 1930–1931: Creation of a permanent unit

The workload was structurally impossible for one analyst and a typist. In a 1930 memorandum, Friedman proposed a seven-person cryptanalytic organization. The government approved a permanent unit in 1931. She recruited and trained its staff and became its civilian head, commonly titled **Cryptologist-in-Charge**. It is frequently described as the first U.S. government codebreaking unit headed by a woman and the only such American unit then led by one.

The unit sat within a shifting bureaucratic environment: Treasury, Customs, the Bureau of Prohibition, the Coast Guard Intelligence Division, and later Navy communications intelligence all appear in its administrative history. Its identity would eventually include **Coast Guard Unit 387**, **OP-20-GU**, and wartime Navy designations. Treating these as separate units founded from nothing obscures institutional continuity.

### 1931–1934: Consolidated Exporters and the New Orleans trials

The most public anti-smuggling work concerned **Consolidated Exporters Corporation** of Vancouver, a large international liquor syndicate. The network used radio stations, mother ships, smaller boats, warehouses, and American distribution. Prosecutors described it as perhaps the most powerful international smuggling syndicate then operating.

Friedman’s decrypts connected vessels, stations, cargoes, and individuals. In the 1933 New Orleans proceedings usually identified with **United States v. Morrison**, she explained monoalphabetic substitution and her reconstruction process to the jury and withstood detailed cross-examination. The archive preserves trial transcripts and exhibits. Colonel **Amos W. W. Woodcock**, formerly federal Prohibition director and involved in the prosecution, later stated that the case could not have been won without the unit’s translations and her testimony.

Later totals conflict. One tradition says twenty-three defendants were tried and five ringleaders convicted; the NSA gives thirty-five bootlegging ringleaders convicted through her 1933 work. These likely refer to different indictments or the wider series of cases, not a single verdict. The claim that Friedman “convicted Al Capone” is inaccurate. Capone had already been convicted of federal income-tax evasion in 1931. The New Orleans ring included people described as Capone associates or members of his network; Friedman’s evidence did not prosecute Capone personally.

Her courtroom visibility made her briefly famous. Newspapers used gendered titles such as “Smiling Lady Proves Smugglers’ Nemesis,” while *Reader’s Digest* later called her the “Key Woman of the T-Men” and *Detective Fiction Weekly* presented her as “Lady Manhunter.” She testified as an expert in an often-repeated **33 cases**, traveling to Gulf and Pacific jurisdictions and Canada.

### 1929–1935: The *I’m Alone* international dispute

On 22 March 1929, after a pursuit, the U.S. Coast Guard sank the Canadian-registered schooner **I’m Alone** in the Gulf of Mexico. Canada protested; one crewman had died, and the episode became an international arbitration over jurisdiction and ownership.

Friedman decoded a set of approximately twenty-three relevant messages. The intelligence supported the American argument that, despite Canadian registration, the vessel was controlled by American rumrunners and had operated within U.S. anti-smuggling jurisdiction. Later popular accounts say her work “won the case” and erased a $350,000 claim. The legal result was mixed: the joint commission criticized the sinking and awarded compensation for the vessel and the dead crewman, while rejecting the larger Canadian presentation of innocent ownership. Her decrypts were important to the beneficial-ownership question but were not the sole legal basis of the award.

### 1937–1938: Canadian opium and arms case

The Royal Canadian Mounted Police sought Friedman’s help against the network of **Gordon Lim**. The evidence included material enciphered through Chinese characters or Chinese-language code groups. Friedman did not speak Chinese; she solved the cryptographic structure while working with linguistic support. The episode is often simplified to “she broke a Mandarin code without knowing Mandarin.” The accurate point is methodological: cryptanalytic regularities can be reconstructed without fluency, but identification of plaintext meaning still requires language knowledge or external context.

Friedman traveled to **Vancouver, British Columbia**, and testified for the Crown. Gordon Lim and associates were convicted in January 1938 in a case involving opium trafficking and arms shipments. Her celebrity was sufficient that she reportedly traveled or registered under “Mrs. William Friedman” to reduce press attention.

### Late 1930s–1940: From smugglers to clandestine agents

After repeal of Prohibition, the unit’s targets shifted to narcotics, customs fraud, and foreign intelligence. Coast Guard high-frequency direction finding and monitoring supplied metadata and intercepts. By 1939–1940, unusual clandestine radio circuits in Latin America required the same combined skills the unit had developed against mobile maritime networks: intercept organization, call-sign analysis, traffic analysis, cipher solution, and identification of operators.

Friedman also faced chronic overwork and family strain. William’s mental health deteriorated while he worked against foreign diplomatic systems. The pair were financially constrained relative to the national value and secrecy of their work. There is no evidence that Elizebeth accumulated significant business wealth, owned commercial cryptographic enterprises, or profited from patents. Her career was salaried civil service and later consultancy.

### 1940–1941: German networks in Latin America

Beginning in 1940, Friedman’s group studied German clandestine traffic between agents in South America and controllers in Europe. The networks reported Allied shipping, defense production, military technology, politics, and local influence operations. German intelligence activity later grouped under **Operation Bolívar** included Abwehr and Sicherheitsdienst/SS elements and locally recruited agents.

The principal adversary in the later narrative was **Johannes Siegfried Becker**, code-named **SARGO**, an SS intelligence organizer working across Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Paraguay, and neighboring states. Another operator, “LUNA,” handled radio functions. Network labels and machine colors—Green, Red, and Blue—appear in British and American files.

### 1941–1945: Navy control, Unit 387/OP-20-GU, Enigma, and FBI relations

After American entry into the war, the Coast Guard operated under Navy authority. Friedman’s unit became part of Navy communications intelligence, associated with **OP-20-GU** and **Unit 387**. As a civilian woman she could not retain the same formal command position within the Navy hierarchy, but she remained the central cryptanalytic authority for clandestine Latin American traffic.

The unit attacked hand systems and several commercial-type or intelligence **Enigma** systems. Its technical history and liaison cables show exchanges with Britain’s **Intelligence Service Knox (ISK)** at Bletchley Park. The two sides sometimes arrived independently at solutions and then exchanged settings, indicators, machine information, and decrypts. The record therefore supports neither “Friedman alone broke Enigma” nor “Bletchley did it all.” Her unit was the American center for these particular non-military intelligence Enigma networks; it did not solve every German Enigma system.

Wartime summaries credit the group with reading approximately **4,000 messages on 48 circuits**. It supplied intelligence to the FBI because the Bureau had responsibility for counterespionage in the Western Hemisphere but lacked equivalent cryptanalytic capacity at the outset.

#### March 1942 Brazil roundup

The most consequential interagency conflict followed an FBI-directed roundup in Brazil in March 1942. Coast Guard history complained that the FBI made “much too free use” of verbatim decrypts and acted prematurely, compromising sources and methods. Arrests alerted German controllers that their traffic was readable. Important agents escaped or withdrew, and the Germans changed procedures and systems. The 1943 *History of OP-20-GU* records this criticism contemporaneously; it is not merely a later biographer’s inference.

#### *Queen Mary*

In March 1942, decrypted agent traffic reported the movement of RMS **Queen Mary** near the Brazilian coast. The ship carried **8,398 American servicemen** in the account used by PBS; German U-boats sought high-value Allied troopships, and a large bounty on *Queen Mary* was widely reported. The decrypt was real and operationally significant. It enabled Allied authorities to know that the ship was being observed and to protect routing information.

The later formula that Friedman “saved 8,000 lives” is stronger than the evidence. The surviving public record establishes detection of hostile reporting and the ship’s safe passage, but not a documented counterfactual in which a named U-boat was diverted at the last moment solely because of her solution. The statement is best classified as a commemorative causal inference built on a documented decrypt.

#### Renewed SARGO networks and Enigma solutions, 1943–1944

Becker returned to Argentina with funds, personnel, and revised systems. The network collected information about American weapons and shipping and sought political influence. Friedman’s unit and ISK recovered the new Enigma systems. Intelligence was passed more cautiously after the 1942 breach. Latin American governments closed radio stations and arrested or expelled agents; diplomatic pressure and changing military fortunes also contributed to Bolivia, Chile, and Argentina severing or reducing Axis relations.

Later biographies sometimes credit Friedman with causing fascist regimes to fall or single-handedly moving entire states into the Allied camp. The decrypts materially enabled counterespionage and diplomacy, but governmental alignment resulted from multiple domestic and international pressures. Argentina did not declare war on Germany and Japan until March 1945.

#### J. Edgar Hoover and attribution

The documentary case for misattribution is strong. The FBI received decrypted material and operational leads from Friedman’s unit. Hoover then published articles such as “How the Nazi Spy Invasion Was Smashed” (*The American Magazine*, September 1944), promoted FBI-centered accounts, and supported publicity films including *The Battle of the United States*. These accounts omitted Unit 387 and Friedman and sometimes implied that Bureau cryptographers had performed the decisive technical work.

Because Friedman had signed secrecy obligations and the operational records remained classified, she could not publicly correct the story. “Hoover stole all the credit” is rhetorically compressed but rests on a documented asymmetry: FBI publicity claimed institutional success while concealing the Coast Guard/Navy cryptanalytic source. The FBI still performed investigations, ran agents, coordinated arrests, and used the intelligence; it was not absent from the operations.

### 1944: Velvalee Dickinson, the “Doll Woman”

**Velvalee Dickinson**, a New York doll dealer, sent letters that used doll customers, names, and transactions as open code for naval information. Friedman prepared an analysis connecting the apparently innocent language to ships and military facts. Open code is inherently less mechanically demonstrable than a substitution cipher, and Friedman stated her conclusions cautiously. Dickinson ultimately pleaded guilty to violating censorship laws and received a ten-year sentence; the espionage charge was not tried to verdict.

Later FBI publicity described Bureau cryptographers or a Navy check as solving the messages and omitted Friedman. Her role is documented in her papers and later archival reconstruction. Claims that she alone “convicted” Dickinson similarly overstate the relation between expert analysis, investigation, plea negotiation, and judgment.

### 1945–1949: Retirement from federal service and International Monetary Fund

Friedman retired from wartime federal service in 1945. From approximately **1946 to 1949**, she served as a **Consultant in Communications** to the newly established **International Monetary Fund**. She designed or established its communications-security system, described in later accounts as using one-time tapes or related one-time cryptographic material. Detailed specifications remain obscure in the public record.

The IMF work shows that her career included cryptography—designing secure systems—as well as cryptanalysis. No patent or private corporate interest is associated with this system. It was consultancy for an international institution, not a commercial venture she owned.

### 1950s–1960s: Shakespeare, cipher controversies, and private research

The Friedmans returned to the question that had brought them together. Their manuscript **“The Cryptologist Looks at Shakespeare”** won the 1955 Folger Shakespeare Library literary prize and was published by Cambridge University Press in 1957 as *The Shakespearean Ciphers Examined*. It systematically tested cipher claims by Ignatius Donnelly, Elizabeth Wells Gallup, Orville Ward Owen, and other anti-Stratfordian writers.

The book did not prove metaphysically that no hidden message can exist in any Shakespeare text. It demonstrated that the proposed systems lacked reproducibility, allowed uncontrolled choices, used unstable typography, or could generate arbitrary results. Its conclusion supported Shakespearean authorship because the presented cryptographic evidence for alternatives failed.

The authors inserted a genuine typographic joke: a Baconian biliteral message stating **“I did not write the plays. F. Bacon.”** This is an intentional cipher, not a confession that they secretly believed Bacon wrote Shakespeare.

In 1959 they published **“Acrostics, Anagrams, and Chaucer”** in *Philological Quarterly*. The paper attacked unconstrained anagram methods and included its own elaborate anagrammatic device, with a solution reportedly deposited under seal with the editor. Elizebeth later wrote **“‘The Most Mysterious Manuscript’ Still an Enigma”** for *The Washington Post* (5 August 1962), about the Voynich Manuscript.

The Friedmans studied the **Voynich Manuscript** for decades after John Manly sent them photographs in the 1920s. William organized a study group in the 1940s. They did not solve it. They also examined the **Beale ciphers/Beale Papers** and other historical cipher puzzles. Popular treatments sometimes imply that every puzzle associated with them was solved; the archival importance of these projects often lies in their disciplined refusal to claim a solution.

### William’s illness and death, 1955–1969

William suffered a heart attack in April 1955 and continued to experience declining health. He retired from the National Security Agency that year. Elizebeth increasingly managed care, correspondence, and their collections while continuing research and speaking.

On 26 June 1959, NSA personnel visited the Friedman home and removed or reclassified material that had previously been treated as unclassified, including First World War documents. The Friedmans resented the action and feared that donating their papers to the government would bury them permanently. This experience shaped their choice of the private **George C. Marshall Research Library** as repository.

William died of a heart attack on **2 November 1969**. Elizebeth continued the inventory and transfer they had planned.

### 1970–1980: Archival work, oral histories, and final years

In 1971, Elizebeth supervised the packing and transfer of the couple’s library, papers, desk, and a small safe to the Marshall Foundation in Lexington, Virginia, following the moving truck by car and overseeing unloading. She also donated her own papers. The resulting William F. Friedman and Elizebeth Smith Friedman collections are central archives of American cryptologic history.

She gave extensive interviews and orientations: a long conversation with Marshall historian Forrest Pogue in 1973; a five-tape series at the Marshall Library in June 1974; an interview with William’s biographer Ronald Clark in March 1975; and NSA oral histories in 1976 with Virginia T. Valaki and R. Louis Benson. Her memory was sometimes halting, and interview transcripts preserve uncertainty and self-correction. These records remain indispensable because she could describe Riverbank and the emergence of American cryptology from direct experience.

Her 1966 partial memoir, dictated onto tape and assembled as a manuscript, ends before her secret wartime work. It was never commercially published during her life. The omission reflects classification as well as incompletion.

Friedman remained active in the **League of Women Voters**, supported statehood for the District of Columbia, spoke publicly, and organized the historical record of her husband’s career. No evidence found indicates party office, electoral candidacy, radical political organization, or intelligence activity after the IMF consultancy.

She eventually entered Abbott Manor Nursing Home in Plainfield, New Jersey. She died there on 31 October 1980 of arteriosclerosis.

### Technologies and methods

- Francis Bacon’s biliteral cipher and typographic steganography.
- Monoalphabetic and polyalphabetic substitution.
- Transposition systems and codebooks.
- Traffic analysis, call-sign analysis, radio intercept correlation, and high-frequency direction-finding support.
- Reconstruction of primary alphabets.
- Open-code analysis, as in the Dickinson letters.
- Commercial/intelligence Enigma solution in collaboration and parallel work with ISK.
- One-time systems for IMF communications.
- Manual statistical and linguistic analysis. Most celebrated work was done with paper, pencil, cards, worksheets, and clerical support, although the wartime unit also used specialized communications and tabulating resources.

### Patents, investments, and wealth

No patent issued in Elizebeth Friedman’s name has been identified in the consulted sources. No private code-machine company, equity holding, or licensing business is documented. The Friedmans collected books and manuscripts of scholarly value, but this library was built for research rather than financial speculation and was donated.

Contemporaneous and institutional accounts describe the couple as financially strained and undercompensated. Their income came primarily from government salaries, book royalties of uncertain size, honoraria, and Elizebeth’s IMF consultancy. Claims that she died “poor” appear in popular memorialization, but no probate inventory or estate accounting was located; “not wealthy” and “financially constrained during much of their service” are supportable, while destitution is not established.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

Friedman’s Quaker upbringing supplied a cultural vocabulary of conscience and public service but did not prevent her from building military and intelligence capabilities in two world wars. No developed pacifist theology survives in the sources consulted. Her working ethic was institutional and empirical: solve the message, show the method, document what the evidence permits.

Her central intellectual development ran from literary enthusiasm through disillusionment with unfalsifiable cipher claims to a scientific standard of reproducibility. Gallup’s Bacon theory mattered because it taught her how pattern-seeking can manufacture evidence. That lesson later joined practical cryptanalysis: a genuine solution must explain repeated features, survive independent checking, and predict unread material.

Politically, the documented record is modest. She participated in the League of Women Voters and campaigned for District of Columbia statehood or full representation. She served federal administrations of both major parties without a publicly identified partisan program. Wartime work placed her inside U.S. counterintelligence against Nazi and Japanese networks; this is an employment and national-security connection, not evidence of covert partisan activity.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### William F. Friedman

Husband, collaborator, student, teacher, and at times the more publicly credited half of the partnership. Elizebeth introduced William to cipher work; William supplied statistical and scientific approaches that shaped modern cryptology. Their intellectual partnership produced Riverbank work, the Shakespeare book, the Chaucer article, demonstrations, and the archive. His institutional prestige sometimes obscured her independent career.

### George Fabyan

Patron and employer who created the circumstances for her career, funded Riverbank, and offered its services to the government. Also a controlling employer accused of intercepting correspondence and impeding the Friedmans’ departure. Friedman’s own verdict was mixed: personally destructive in important respects, historically catalytic in others.

### Elizabeth Wells Gallup

Immediate supervisor and practical initiator into Bacon’s biliteral cipher. Gallup’s claims became the object of the Friedmans’ later refutation. She was formative less as a successful cryptanalyst than as a case study in non-reproducible interpretation.

### Parker and Genevieve Young Hitt; Joseph Mauborgne; John Manly

Parker Hitt’s manual and visits supplied early military cryptanalytic technique. Mauborgne represented the Army’s institutional demand for modern cryptography. University of Chicago scholar John Matthews Manly, himself a wartime cryptologist and a critic of Baconian claims, connected the Friedmans to historical problems including the Voynich Manuscript.

### Agnes Meyer Driscoll

Navy cryptanalyst associated with the Riverbank training network. Later retellings sometimes flatten the relationship into direct pupil and teacher; Riverbank’s institutional role is established, while the exact duration and content of personal instruction should not be exaggerated.

### Coast Guard and Treasury colleagues

**Leonard T. Jones** wrote the 1943 unit history and documented its contribution and frustration with the FBI. **C. A. Housel** extended work on Pacific traffic after Friedman’s training. **Charles Root** and later Coast Guard intelligence officers enabled the early unit. **Amos W. W. Woodcock** publicly credited her evidence in the Consolidated Exporters prosecution.

### J. Edgar Hoover and the FBI

Institutional customer, rival claimant to credit, and source of operational conflict. No evidence indicates a close personal friendship or direct private feud. The conflict is best reconstructed through the unit’s memoranda, FBI publicity, and omission of the source organization.

### Johannes Siegfried Becker/SARGO

Adversarial intelligence relationship rather than a meeting. Friedman reconstructed the communications of his networks; there is no evidence that they met. The later formulation that she was “his nemesis” is a biographer’s summary of this remote contest.

### Forrest Pogue, Virginia Valaki, R. Louis Benson, Ronald Clark, and Marshall Foundation staff

Interviewers, historians, and archivists who helped preserve her testimony. Their work also reveals the limits of late memory and the way classification shaped what could be said.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

### No documented personal criminality or corruption

No evidence was found that Friedman was investigated, indicted, charged, or convicted of a crime; participated in fraud, money laundering, political violence, or corruption; or profited from the criminal networks she studied. The controversies concern institutional authority, secrecy, credit, evidence in prosecutions, and later exaggeration.

### Surveillance and prosecution work

Her career supported state interception, surveillance, customs enforcement, Prohibition, narcotics policing, wartime censorship, and counterespionage. These activities involved expansive federal power and secret evidence. The surviving record treats her primarily as a technical analyst; it does not show her setting national policy on prohibition, drug sentencing, censorship, or civil liberties. Defendants challenged her methods in court, making her courtroom explanations important to the admissibility and credibility of cryptanalytic evidence.

### FBI operational compromise, 1942

**Documented interagency accusation:** The Coast Guard/Navy unit’s 1943 history criticized the FBI for premature arrests in Brazil and overly free use of verbatim decrypts, which exposed the source and led German networks to change systems. This is a contemporaneous institutional judgment from Friedman’s unit, not a court finding. FBI investigators had operational reasons for arrests, but their public and investigative handling endangered continuing cryptanalytic access.

### Hoover’s credit claims

**Documented publicity dispute:** Hoover and FBI media products credited the Bureau with smashing Nazi networks while omitting the cryptanalytic unit that supplied decisive material. It is fair to call this institutional appropriation of credit. It is not accurate to say the FBI did nothing: it investigated, recruited sources, coordinated with foreign police, and conducted arrests.

### Classification and erasure

Friedman’s silence was legally and professionally constrained. The public could see her Prohibition work but not the wartime record. The later claim that all revealing documents were “declassified in 2008” is imprecise: materials emerged in stages from U.S. and British archives, and some oral history material was released later. **2008** is a useful milestone in popular recovery, not a single opening of every file.

### Gender and rank

The Navy’s wartime command structure displaced a civilian woman from formal control of the organization she had built. Newspaper coverage exoticized her as a smiling lady, wife, or suburban mother. A 1950s *Time* description called her an “assistant cipher clerk,” a demonstrably inadequate account. These are documentary examples of institutional and media diminution; they do not mean that every lack of recognition resulted from gender rather than classification, bureaucratic rivalry, or William’s different achievements.

### Prohibition and organized-crime associations

Friedman analyzed criminal communications and testified against members of trafficking organizations. She had no documented alliance with Al Capone, Joseph P. Kennedy, Lucky Luciano, or other figures later inserted into retellings. Assertions that Consolidated Exporters had investors or customers among prominent Americans require case-specific corporate and financial evidence; her West Coast narrative describes the syndicate but does not by itself establish every later claim about beneficial ownership.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “She convicted Al Capone”

1. **Story:** Friedman broke the code that convicted Al Capone.
2. **Appearance and circulation:** Modern institutional biographies, commemorative articles, social media, and simplified histories of women in intelligence.
3. **Evidence:** Her decrypts and testimony helped prosecute Consolidated Exporters and persons described as Capone associates. Capone’s own 1931 conviction was for tax evasion and did not depend on her testimony.
4. **Assessment:** **Misleading/false in its direct form.** A documented connection to a ring with Capone-linked figures became a claim that she convicted Capone himself.
5. **Reputational effect:** Connects an unfamiliar cryptanalyst to the most famous Prohibition gangster, increasing recognition at the cost of legal precision.

### “She saved 8,000 lives aboard *Queen Mary*”

1. **Story:** Friedman’s swift solution revealed a U-boat ambush and directly saved more than 8,000 troops.
2. **Appearance and circulation:** Recent biographies, PBS, History, Smithsonian commemoration, Coast Guard and shipbuilder publicity.
3. **Evidence:** Unit 387 read real agent reports of *Queen Mary*’s movement; the ship carried 8,398 servicemen in the cited voyage and passed safely. German forces sought the ship.
4. **Assessment:** **Documented warning; causal claim unresolved.** No public operational record proves that her decrypt alone caused a last-minute course change that otherwise would certainly have prevented or produced a sinking.
5. **Reputational effect:** Supplies a vivid, quantifiable wartime rescue narrative and has become the dominant summary of her World War II work.

### “She single-handedly broke Enigma”

1. **Story:** Friedman personally broke the Nazi Enigma machine.
2. **Circulation:** Popular headlines and short biographies.
3. **Evidence:** Her unit solved several intelligence-service Enigma networks used in Latin America; ISK at Bletchley Park worked in parallel and exchanged solutions. Military Enigma comprised many networks and variants.
4. **Assessment:** **True only in a narrowly specified sense.** She directed or performed central American work against particular spy Enigmas; she did not alone solve the total Enigma problem.
5. **Effect:** Corrects her erasure but risks replacing a collaborative history with a lone-genius legend.

### “The other half” or “William’s assistant”

1. **Story:** Elizebeth was either the uncredited equal half of everything William did or merely his assistant.
2. **Circulation:** The assistant version appeared in mid-century profiles; the equal-half corrective appears in modern commemoration.
3. **Evidence:** They collaborated profoundly but held different posts and produced distinct achievements. Purple, SIGABA-related work, and Army SIS organization belong principally to William and his teams; rumrunner and Latin American clandestine work belong principally to Elizebeth and hers.
4. **Assessment:** **Both extremes distort.** Their partnership was real; their careers were not interchangeable.
5. **Effect:** The corrective recovers Elizebeth but can blur the very independent record that establishes her stature.

### Bacon secretly wrote Shakespeare; the Friedmans confessed in code

1. **Story:** Although their 1957 book rejected Baconian ciphers, the Friedmans secretly believed Francis Bacon wrote Shakespeare and placed a confession in their Arlington memorial: “FRANCIS BACON IS SHAKESPEARE.”
2. **Appearance and circulation:** Francis Bacon Society writers, Baconian websites, cryptographic puzzles surrounding the gravestone, and hostile descriptions of “fraudulent Friedmans.”
3. **Evidence for:** Their memorial includes “Knowledge is Power,” a Bacon-associated motto, and deliberately cryptographic design. Their book contains intentional concealed messages.
4. **Evidence against:** Their published analysis explicitly rejects the claimed evidence; the known hidden message in the book says “I did not write the plays. F. Bacon.” The tombstone interpretations depend on selecting and rearranging features using methods resembling those the book criticized.
5. **Assessment:** **Conspiracy narrative/hostile mythology; not established.** The couple enjoyed cryptographic play, which invites overinterpretation.
6. **Effect:** Keeps them inside the Shakespeare-authorship controversy and demonstrates the persistence of the pattern-imposition problem that began their careers.

### “America’s first female cryptanalyst”

1. **Story:** Friedman was the first woman in American cryptanalysis.
2. **Circulation:** NSA Hall of Honor, Coast Guard, museums, biographies.
3. **Evidence:** She was among the earliest professional American women employed continuously in federal cryptanalysis and the first known woman to lead a permanent U.S. government cryptanalytic unit.
4. **Qualification:** Women had practiced codes, ciphers, and intelligence analysis earlier, and exact “firsts” depend on definitions of professional, federal, military, and cryptanalyst.
5. **Assessment:** **Defensible honorific with definitional limits.** The leadership claim is more precise than an absolute first-ever claim.

### Hoover as sole villain

1. **Story:** Hoover personally erased Friedman and fabricated the entire South American victory.
2. **Evidence:** FBI publicity omitted her unit and implied Bureau technical authorship; the unit documented serious frustration.
3. **Qualification:** Wartime secrecy would have prevented open technical credit in any event, and FBI field operations were substantive. Navy command and classification also concealed her.
4. **Assessment:** **Core appropriation documented; sole-cause narrative overstated.** Hoover’s publicity was a major mechanism of erasure, not the only one.

## Death and Later Life

After William’s death in 1969, Friedman devoted extensive labor to preserving the documentary record that the secrecy system had fragmented. She continued interviews and archival consultation but did not publish a completed autobiography. Her own wartime achievement remained largely inaccessible to the public.

She died on 31 October 1980 at Abbott Manor Nursing Home in Plainfield, New Jersey. The contemporary obituary gives arteriosclerosis as the cause. No documented addiction, suicide attempt, major accident, or diagnosed psychiatric disorder of her own was found. Institutional histories mention chronic fatigue and the burden of her husband’s recurrent psychiatric illness; those should not be converted into an unsupported diagnosis for Elizebeth.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Friedman’s reputation developed in three phases.

**During life**, she was publicly known chiefly as a Prohibition-era expert witness and half of the cryptologic couple that wrote the Shakespeare book. Newspaper fame in the 1930s did not reveal the scale of her later intelligence work.

**From 1980 through the early 2000s**, cryptologic institutions honored her but the full Latin American record remained specialist knowledge. The Marshall collections allowed researchers to reconstruct her work outside official publicity traditions. NSA Hall of Honor induction in 1999 and joint naming of NSA spaces marked institutional correction.

**After major archival releases and the 2017 biographies**, she became a widely narrated hidden figure: the woman who smashed rumrunner codes, hunted Nazi spies, and lost credit to Hoover. That recovery is supported by real records, but it has also produced hagiographic compression—Al Capone, 8,000 lives, “the Enigma,” and single-handed destruction of Axis influence.

Her technical legacy lies less in one named theorem than in an integrated model of intelligence work: organize intercepts, reconstruct systems, combine cryptanalysis with traffic analysis, train a team, document solutions for court or operations, and protect sources. Her institutional legacy runs from Riverbank through the Coast Guard unit to modern American signals intelligence. The claim that she “created the NSA” is too direct; she helped establish personnel, methods, and organizational precedents inherited by later agencies.

Her literary legacy is unusually coherent with her intelligence career. She began inside a myth-making machine devoted to Bacon, learned how unconstrained pattern recognition produces false certainty, and ended by publishing reproducible tests of hidden-message claims. The Baconian conspiracy surrounding her own gravestone repeats the epistemic problem she spent decades exposing.

The planned cutter *Friedman* became a posthumous irony. Naming in 2020 and fabrication in 2021 appeared to give material form to Coast Guard recognition; the contract was terminated in June 2025 after delay and cost dispute. The cancellation does not rescind the naming honor, but no commissioned USCGC *Friedman* exists as of 30 August 2026.

### Francis Bacon

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** From her 1916 employment at Riverbank Laboratories through the jointly authored *The Shakespearean Ciphers Examined* (1957), Friedman studied Bacon’s biliteral cipher and critically evaluated claims that Bacon encoded evidence of his authorship in Shakespearean texts. The book concluded that the proposed Baconian decipherments were not cryptographically reproducible; its concealed joke, “I did not write the plays. F. Bacon,” reinforced that conclusion. [Folger Shakespeare Library exhibition documentation](https://folgerpedia.folger.edu/Decoding_the_Renaissance_exhibition_material), [Folger catalog record](https://catalog.folger.edu/record/52477)

### Ignatius Loyola Donnelly

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Friedman and William F. Friedman devoted part of *The Shakespearean Ciphers Examined*—notably chapter III—to Donnelly’s nineteenth-century claim that a numerical cipher in Shakespeare revealed Francis Bacon’s authorship. They tested and rejected Donnelly’s method as cryptographically uncontrolled. [Bibliographic identification of the Donnelly chapter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignatius_L._Donnelly), [Folger catalog record for the Friedmans’ book](https://catalog.folger.edu/record/52477)

### William Frederick Friedman

- **[T5] Documented fact:** Elizebeth Smith met William Friedman at Riverbank Laboratories in Geneva, Illinois, in 1916 after drawing the staff geneticist and photographer into the Bacon-cipher investigation. Their personal and intellectual relationship developed there, and they married in Chicago on 21 May 1917; they remained married until William’s death on 2 November 1969. [NSA biographical history](https://www.nsa.gov/History/Cryptologic-History/Historical-Figures/Historical-Figures-View/Article/1623028/elizebeth-s-friedman/), [Folger exhibition documentation](https://folgerpedia.folger.edu/Decoding_the_Renaissance_exhibition_material)

- **[T3] Documented fact:** The Friedmans maintained a decades-long written and scholarly exchange documented by their correspondence, working papers, and joint publications. Their formal collaborations included *Methods for the Reconstruction of Primary Alphabets* (Riverbank Publication No. 21, 1918), *The Shakespearean Ciphers Examined* (1957), and “Acrostics, Anagrams, and Chaucer” (1959). Their surviving correspondence includes letters exchanged during William’s 1918 military service and later professional and family papers. [Elizebeth Smith Friedman Collection guide](https://www.marshallfoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Friedman_Elizabeth.pdf), [Folger catalog record](https://catalog.folger.edu/record/52477)
