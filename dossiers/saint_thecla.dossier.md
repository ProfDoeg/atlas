# Saint Thecla: Research Dossier

**Prepared:** 22 August 2026  
**Scope:** the fullest source-critical reconstruction reasonably possible, distinguishing a possible first-century woman from the second-century literary protagonist and the historically documented late-antique and later cult.

## Executive Source-Critical Finding

Thecla is not named in the canonical New Testament, Paul's undisputed letters, Acts, or any securely first-century source. The earliest surviving narrative is the Greek *Acts of Paul and Thecla* (usually abbreviated ATh or APT), a self-contained portion of the *Acts of Paul*, conventionally dated to the later second century. Tertullian of Carthage, writing about 198–203, supplies the earliest datable external notice: he says an Asian presbyter composed a writing about Paul that allowed the example of Thecla to be used for women teaching and baptizing, and that the presbyter was removed from office after confessing that he wrote it “out of love for Paul” (*De baptismo* 17). This is unusually early evidence for the text, but it is not independent evidence for the events it narrates.

Accordingly, three objects must be kept separate:

1. **A possible historical Thecla:** a first-century Christian woman of Iconium may lie behind the tradition, but her existence, dates, family, meeting with Paul, trials, and movements cannot be independently demonstrated.
2. **The literary Thecla:** the young elite Iconian convert, celibate disciple of Paul, condemned twice and miraculously delivered, self-baptizer, teacher, and apostolic missionary described in the second-century *Acts*.
3. **The cultic Thecla:** a saint demonstrably invoked, represented, visited, and institutionally venerated across the late Roman, Byzantine, medieval, and modern Christian worlds. This cult has a reconstructable history whether or not the first-century biography is factual.

The dossier therefore does not convert hagiographic detail into undocumented biography. “According to the *Acts*” means that the source says it; it does not certify the event.

## Evidence Map and Method

### Primary or near-primary textual strata

- **Later second century:** *Acts of Paul and Thecla*, the basic narrative.
- **About 198–203:** Tertullian, *De baptismo* 17, the earliest securely datable external witness and the source of the Asian-presbyter authorship report.
- **Late third century:** Methodius of Olympus, *Symposium* (*Banquet of the Ten Virgins*), makes Thecla the foremost of ten learned virgins and gives her the climactic discourse and hymn.
- **Fourth century:** Gregory of Nazianzus, Gregory of Nyssa, Macarius Magnes, and other writers treat her as a recognized ascetic/martyrial exemplar; Gregory of Nazianzus knew her Seleucian sanctuary.
- **About 381–384:** Egeria's *Itinerarium* gives eyewitness evidence for the sanctuary near Seleucia, its monks and virgins, wall, church/martyrium, and a liturgical reading of Thecla's acts.
- **About 445–474:** anonymous *Life and Miracles of Saint Thecla*, formerly attributed to Basil of Seleucia, rewrites her life and records forty-six miracles centered on her sanctuary.
- **Material evidence:** churches, caves, cisterns, inscriptions, lamps, pilgrim objects, textiles, paintings, and funerary images demonstrate the cult, not the first-century adventures.

### Limits

Ancient biography and hagiography freely use typology, romance, miracle, rhetorical invention, and local cult aetiology. The absence of independent evidence does not prove that no woman named Thecla existed; it does prevent precise dates, dialogue, legal procedure, family status, and miracles from being reported as established fact.

## Family and Ancestry

No genealogy or independently verifiable ancestry survives.

### Literary family

In the *Acts*, Thecla is a young, apparently prosperous woman of Iconium:

- **Mother:** Theocleia (also transliterated Theoclia/Theokleia). She owns or controls the house from whose adjoining window Thecla hears Paul. Theocleia resists her daughter's rejection of marriage and, in the trial scene, demands that she be burned so that other women will fear similar conduct. In the ending, Thecla returns to Iconium and finds Thamyris dead; she seeks her mother and announces that the God who saved her can also save Theocleia. The text does not clearly narrate the mother's conversion.
- **Father:** absent and unnamed. Scholars have variously read the absence as widow-headed household, narrative economy, or displacement of paternal authority; none can be proved.
- **Siblings:** none named.
- **Fiancé:** Thamyris, a prominent Iconian man. He is not a husband: the story consistently treats the marriage as prospective. Thecla's turn to celibacy breaks the alliance and household expectations.
- **Spouse, partner, children:** none in the tradition. Her perpetual virginity is constitutive of the cultic persona.

Later devotional accounts add “rich and illustrious parents,” extraordinary beauty, and an age of eighteen. These are hagiographic elaborations, not independent records.

## Childhood and Early Life

Nothing is known of a historical childhood. The *Acts* begins when Thecla is marriageable and betrothed. Her literacy is not explicitly established in the earliest narrative. She listens from a window for three days and nights while Paul preaches at the neighboring house of Onesiphorus. The household's architecture, attendants, jewelry, and advantageous betrothal mark her as elite within the story.

The window becomes an enduring iconographic symbol: Thecla is frequently pictured in a tower or upper window listening to Paul. The scene also reverses domestic confinement—the place meant to enclose her becomes the aperture through which she enters a translocal religious life.

## Education and Formation

### Recoverable fact

No school, teacher, or curriculum can be established for a historical Thecla.

### Formation in the *Acts*

Paul is her teacher. At the Iconian house he proclaims “the word of God concerning abstinence and the resurrection,” with beatitudes blessing purity, continence, those who possess wives as though they had none, those who fear God, and bodies kept chaste. Thecla receives this teaching through prolonged listening rather than a formal catechumenate.

She then bribes Paul's jailer with bracelets and gives the gatekeeper a silver mirror, enters the prison, sits at Paul's feet, kisses his bonds, and hears “the great things of God.” These payments are literary evidence of personal property or luxury objects; they cannot support a historical balance sheet.

### Later intellectual persona

Methodius's late-third-century *Symposium* transforms her into an accomplished Christian intellectual capable of biblical exegesis, natural philosophy, and argument against astrological determinism. This is a reception history of Thecla, not evidence for first-century education. In the fifth-century *Life*, the author similarly magnifies her eloquence, orthodoxy, and victory over pagan cults.

## Chronological Life History: Earliest Narrative

The following sequence reconstructs the plot of the *Acts of Paul and Thecla*, not independently documented events.

### 1. Paul arrives in Iconium

Paul enters Iconium and is welcomed by Onesiphorus, his wife Lectra, and sons Simmias and Zeno. Onesiphorus recognizes Paul from a divinely supplied description: small, bald, bow-legged, strongly built, with joined eyebrows and a prominent nose, “full of grace,” sometimes appearing human and sometimes angelic. This famous portrait belongs to the literary Paul tradition.

Paul preaches in Onesiphorus's house. Thecla, next door, remains at her window for three days, “bound by faith,” refusing food and movement. Theocleia summons Thamyris.

### 2. Thamyris mobilizes opposition

Thamyris questions Paul's hosts and encounters Demas and Hermogenes, presented as hypocritical companions jealous of Paul. Over a lavish dinner, they advise him to accuse Paul before the governor of teaching Christians “not to marry,” a charge with civic and household implications. They falsely offer knowledge of resurrection doctrine in return for money and hospitality.

### 3. Paul's arrest and Thecla's prison visit

Thamyris brings Paul before the governor, Castelius/Cestilius in variant forms. The governor imprisons Paul pending fuller inquiry. Thecla gives her jewelry and mirror as bribes, enters the jail, and listens at Paul's feet. Her family finds them together. Paul is scourged and expelled from Iconium; Thecla is tried.

### 4. First condemnation: the fire at Iconium

Asked why she will not marry according to Iconian law, Thecla looks toward Paul and remains silent. Theocleia calls for her burning. The governor weeps, yet condemns her. Thecla is stripped; the crowd supplies wood. She makes the sign of the cross and mounts the pyre. The fire does not touch her. A storm of hail and rain extinguishes it, killing or endangering spectators in the narrative.

**Legal-historical caution:** no court record exists. Roman provincial criminal procedure did not normally punish a broken engagement by burning. The scene uses martyr-act conventions and casts household revolt as a public crisis.

### 5. Reunion and travel

Paul, Onesiphorus, and family have fasted in a tomb outside the city. A child, sent to buy bread, encounters Thecla; she rejoins the group. She proposes cutting her hair and following Paul. He fears another temptation and postpones giving her the “seal” (normally baptism). The group proceeds toward Antioch, usually identified in the narrative tradition as Antioch of Pisidia, though geography and textual transmission are not entirely stable.

### 6. Alexander's assault

At Antioch, Alexander, a powerful local Syrian/“first man” or magistrate, sees Thecla and desires her. Believing her to belong to Paul, he offers Paul money and gifts. Paul denies knowing the woman—an unsettling episode interpreted as cowardice, protection, strategic disavowal, or narrative abandonment. Alexander embraces Thecla publicly. She resists, tears his cloak, removes or knocks off his crown, and makes him ridiculous. He prosecutes her for assaulting a civic notable.

### 7. Second condemnation: beasts at Antioch

The governor condemns Thecla to the wild beasts. At her request she is kept with Tryphaena, a wealthy woman and relative of Caesar whose daughter Falconilla has died. Falconilla appears to Tryphaena in a dream and asks her mother to accept Thecla so that Thecla may pray for her translation to the righteous. Tryphaena becomes patron, surrogate mother, and intercessory client.

In the arena a lioness licks Thecla's feet and fights a bear and a male lion, dying in her defense. Thecla sees a tank containing seals or sea-calves and declares, “In the name of Jesus Christ I baptize myself on the last day.” She leaps in; lightning kills or blinds the animals and a cloud/fire covering protects her nakedness. Further beasts and bulls fail to kill her. Tryphaena faints; fearing that a relative of Caesar has died and the city will suffer, the governor halts the spectacle.

Thecla tells the governor that she is a servant of the living God and has survived through Christ. He orders clothing and releases her. She stays with Tryphaena for eight days and teaches so successfully that most of the household believes. Tryphaena gives her gold and clothing: some for the poor, some for Paul.

### 8. Changed clothing, reunion with Paul, commission

Thecla searches for Paul in Myra. She alters her mantle into a male-style cloak, travels with young men and maidservants, and finds Paul. He marvels that she survived. She reports her self-baptism and offers him Tryphaena's wealth; Paul declines or directs its use. In the common Greek ending Paul tells her, “Go and teach the word of God.” The episode became central in disputes about women's authority.

The gendered clothing is temporary and practical in the earliest text, not a claim to male identity. Later women-disguised-as-monks legends repeatedly echo it.

### 9. Return to Iconium and settlement at Seleucia

Thecla returns to Onesiphorus's house, prostrates herself where Paul taught, and weeps. She learns Thamyris has died. She seeks Theocleia and proclaims God. She then goes to Seleucia, “enlightens many,” and in the shortest early ending “slept a good sleep.” Manuscripts and versions expand this conclusion greatly.

## Later-Life and Death Traditions

No cause or date of death is documented.

### Seleucian cave and healing life

Expanded endings and the fifth-century *Life* locate Thecla in a cave near Seleucia for decades. She practices asceticism, preaches, defeats pagan divine powers, and heals illnesses. A common arithmetic makes her eighteen at conversion and ninety at disappearance, hence seventy-two years of ascetic life. These numbers are traditional, not archival.

### Assault by physicians and disappearance into rock

One expanded story says pagan physicians, angry that Thecla's free cures destroyed their income, concluded that the virgin goddess Artemis served her and that sexual violation would remove her healing power. They sent violent young men to rape the aged saint. Thecla fled; a rock opened at her prayer, she entered, and it closed. A veil or part of her clothing remained visible. The story explains an empty or inaccessible tomb and converts threatened sexual violence into inviolable disappearance.

This is a hagiographic accusation against professional rivals, not documented economic sabotage or a criminal case. It nevertheless preserves late-antique tensions around healing markets, sanctuary medicine, gender, and pagan-Christian competition.

### Death and burial

Other recensions say she died naturally at Seleucia and was buried there. Still others send her to Rome, where she dies near Paul's tomb or lies beside him. The traditions are incompatible. The massive Seleucian cult did not require universally agreed possession of a body.

## Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

Thecla held no historically documented employment, office, company, business, patent, investment, or institutional appointment.

### Wealth inside the narrative

The *Acts* associates her with bracelets, a silver mirror, clothing, servants, and an elite marriage. Tryphaena supplies money and garments. Thecla directs wealth toward the poor and Paul. These are plot details illustrating renunciation, patronage, and redistribution, not auditable finances.

### Hagia Thekla/Meriamlik as an institution

The historical institution grew about 1.5–2 km south of Seleucia. By Egeria's visit in the early 380s it contained numerous cells for men and women, a wall protecting the sanctuary from raiders, a church/martyrium, clergy, and a deaconess named Marthana whom Egeria already knew from Jerusalem. Egeria attended prayer, communion, and the reading of Thecla's acts.

The surviving archaeological zone includes the cave church, large basilicas conventionally called the Basilica of Thecla and the Domed Church, smaller churches, baths, cisterns, tombs, monastic buildings, and defensive works. Dating and identifications have been revised by successive archaeologists; not every ruin can be securely tied to a named patron or function.

Imperial patronage is traditionally associated with Emperor Zeno (r. 474–491), who was linked to construction or rebuilding at the shrine after crediting Thecla with protection or victory. The fifth-century sanctuary was therefore not only devotional but a substantial pilgrimage economy involving lodging, water, votives, healing, clergy, monks, artisans, and imperial/local finance. Exact accounts do not survive.

The anonymous author of the *Miracles* competes explicitly with local physicians and pagan sanctuaries, presenting Thecla as a superior healer. Patients sleep at the shrine, receive dream appearances, prescriptions, rebukes, or cures. The tales preserve named clients and social transactions but remain crafted miracle literature rather than clinical records.

## Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

Because her historical psychology is inaccessible, “development” means development of the persona across sources.

### Second-century persona

The earliest Thecla moves from immobilized listener to active disciple. She repudiates marriage, buys access to prison teaching, survives execution, resists sexual aggression, baptizes herself, adopts travel clothing, teaches a household, receives Paul's commission, and evangelizes. Authority arises outside household and ordinary ecclesial gatekeeping.

### Ascetic and ecclesial domestication

Later sources preserve her prominence while regulating its implications. Tertullian attacks use of her example to license women to teach or baptize. Some versions soften or omit self-baptism and preaching. Methodius makes her intellectually preeminent but places her within an orthodox literary symposium devoted to virginity. Fifth-century hagiography aligns her with developed doctrine, sanctuary hierarchy, and episcopal society.

### Combat with pagan gods

The *Life and Miracles* portrays her expelling or defeating powers associated with Sarpedon, Athena, Aphrodite, and Zeus. This is Christianization mythology mapped onto local sacred geography. It may preserve memories of competition with pre-Christian cults, but demons' speeches are not historical records.

### Visual types

Thecla appears:

- listening to Paul from a window;
- between or among beasts in an orant prayer posture;
- paired with Paul;
- dressed as a richly robed virgin martyr holding a cross;
- in manuscript menologia and icons as protomartyr;
- on lamps, ampullae, combs, textiles, wall paintings, reliquaries, and altarpieces.

A disputed late-fourth-century fresco in the so-called Grotto of Paul at Ephesus depicts Paul and a woman often identified as Thecla; damage to the woman's eyes and hand has prompted arguments about iconoclasm or hostility toward female authority, but the identity, date of damage, and motive cannot all be established.

## Political Activity, Law, Violence, and Controversy

No historical political office or activity can be assigned to Thecla. The narrative is nevertheless political in its depiction of household governance, civic order, elite male privilege, judicial spectacle, and bodily autonomy.

### Judicial violence

The two trials, burning, exposure to animals, binding to bulls, and threatened rape are literary episodes. They resemble martyr-romance conventions and cannot be synchronized with a named emperor or verified governor. Thecla is called protomartyr despite surviving; “martyr” here means witness and attempted martyr, not necessarily one killed by execution.

### Women's teaching and baptism

Tertullian's complaint is a documented controversy around AD 200. He rejects the use of Thecla to authorize women to teach and baptize and alleges that the presbyter-author was convicted by Asian churches and deposed. What is documented is Tertullian's assertion. No independent deposition record survives, the presbyter is unnamed, and scholars debate whether he composed the whole *Acts of Paul*, the Thecla unit, or edited prior oral/written material.

The passage shows that readers very early took Thecla as actionable precedent, not merely entertainment. Later textual transmission sometimes restricts her authority. The Ethiopic version, for example, removes or weakens crucial references to her baptizing and preaching.

### Authorship and “forgery”

Calling the *Acts* a modern-style forgery oversimplifies ancient pseudonymous religious writing, but Tertullian plainly regarded attribution to Paul or the use of Paul's authority as illegitimate and says the author confessed. The work was not included in the eventual New Testament canon. It nevertheless circulated widely in Greek, Latin, Syriac, Armenian, Coptic, Ethiopic, Arabic, and other traditions.

### Sexual violence

Alexander's public seizure and the physicians' rape plot are distinct literary episodes. The first is in the early narrative; the second belongs to expanded Seleucian endings. Neither should be euphemized, and neither has independent legal corroboration.

### Suppression thesis

A popular modern claim says “the Church erased Thecla” because she proved women could preach and baptize. Evidence supports a narrower claim: Tertullian opposed precisely such use; some translations modified empowering details; the text remained noncanonical. Evidence also shows the opposite of total erasure: bishops and theologians praised her, churches institutionalized her feast, artists represented her, and enormous shrines carried her cult across centuries. “Contested, edited, and domesticated while continuously venerated” fits the record better than either complete suppression or uncomplicated empowerment.

## Illness, Healing, Psychological States, Injuries, and Death

- The earliest text records fasting, trance-like absorbed listening, imprisonment, stripping, threatened burning, assaults, and arena exposure, but no lasting injury or diagnosed illness.
- Interpreting her window vigil as catatonia, anorexia, mania, dissociation, or another modern diagnosis would exceed the evidence; the genre portrays religious absorption.
- Later sources make her a healer of blindness, paralysis, wounds, fever, reproductive illness, and other conditions. They document beliefs and healing practices at the sanctuary, not medically verified outcomes.
- No addiction is alleged.
- Cause, date, and place of biological death remain unknown; disappearance into rock is legend.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### Historical existence

1. **Story:** Thecla was an actual first-century Iconian disciple of Paul.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** first extant in the later-second-century *Acts*, circulated from Asia Minor throughout Christianity.
3. **Evidence for:** early and geographically rooted tradition; Tertullian's notice close to the text's composition; plausible presence of women coworkers around Paul.
4. **Evidence against/limits:** silence of Paul's letters and canonical Acts; romance structure; theological anachronism; no inscription, grave, or document contemporary with her.
5. **Effect:** allowed the saint to function as apostolic-era authority. Verdict: **possible, unverified historical nucleus**.

### Paul commissioned her to preach

1. **Story:** Paul tells Thecla to go teach God's word.
2. **Appearance:** Greek *Acts* ending; altered in some versions.
3. **Circulators:** readers, ascetic women, later churches and critics.
4. **Evidence:** textually present in important witnesses; no first-century corroboration.
5. **Effect:** foundational symbol for women's ministry and a target of restriction. Verdict: **ancient literary claim, not independently verified event**.

### Self-baptism among murderous seals

1. **Story:** expecting death, Thecla baptizes herself in an arena pool; lightning kills the seals and a cloud/fire preserves modesty.
2. **Appearance:** earliest narrative stratum.
3. **Circulation:** widespread manuscripts, art, and controversy.
4. **Evidence:** multiple textual traditions attest the story's antiquity; naturalistic verification is absent.
5. **Effect:** supplied precedent for baptism without male clergy and produced centuries of debate. Verdict: **early hagiographic miracle narrative**.

### Tryphaena was Queen Antonia Tryphaena

1. **Story:** Thecla's patron was the historically attested Pontic client queen Antonia Tryphaena, related to the imperial house.
2. **Appearance:** scholarly attempts to anchor the *Acts* through its phrase “kinswoman of Caesar.”
3. **Evidence for:** name, wealth, and imperial connection.
4. **Evidence against:** chronological and geographic difficulties; Tryphaena was not a rare invented possibility; romance frequently borrows prestigious names.
5. **Effect:** made the narrative appear historically anchored. Verdict: **disputed identification, not established**.

### The rock swallowed her alive

1. **Story:** a fissure opened and protected the ninety-year-old Thecla from rapists; she remains within or passed bodily into the earth.
2. **Appearance:** expanded endings and Seleucian hagiography, later than the short ending in which she dies.
3. **Circulators:** Seleucian shrine personnel, hagiographers, pilgrims.
4. **Evidence:** cultic cave and topography exist; no physical evidence proves disappearance.
5. **Effect:** explained an empty/inaccessible tomb and made landscape itself a relic. Verdict: **cult aetiology/legend**.

### Physicians plotted rape because she ruined their business

1. **Story:** jealous doctors hire assailants, believing virginity powers her cures.
2. **Appearance:** late expanded narrative.
3. **Circulators:** hagiographers in a competitive healing environment.
4. **Evidence:** sanctuary and medical competition are historically plausible; individuals and plot are uncorroborated.
5. **Effect:** delegitimized rival medicine and presented free sacred healing as victorious. Verdict: **polemical legend**.

### Seleucia versus Maaloula tomb

1. **Story:** both Silifke and Maaloula preserve caves identified with Thecla's last dwelling/tomb.
2. **Appearance:** Seleucia is ancient and documented by the fourth century; Maaloula's localized tradition is later.
3. **Circulators:** local churches, monasteries, pilgrims.
4. **Evidence:** both are genuine cult places; neither verifies a first-century burial. The early literary trajectory favors Seleucia.
5. **Effect:** produced distinct pilgrimage landscapes and living local identities. Verdict: **competing sacred geographies**.

### Relics, especially the arm at Tarragona

1. **Story:** an arm relic obtained from the East reached Tarragona in 1321 and is preserved in the cathedral.
2. **Appearance:** medieval diplomatic and civic tradition; elaborate reception in Tarragona.
3. **Circulators:** Aragonese monarchy, archbishops, cathedral chapter, civic festival.
4. **Evidence:** the translation and custody of an object are historical; its biological identity has not been scientifically established, and disappearance traditions complicate corporeal relic claims.
5. **Effect:** established Thecla as Tarragona's patron and shaped one of Catalonia's major festivals. Verdict: **historical medieval relic tradition; attribution unverified**.

### “Female apostle deliberately forgotten”

1. **Story:** ecclesiastical authorities systematically erased Thecla.
2. **Appearance:** modern popular scholarship, journalism, videos, and advocacy.
3. **Evidence for:** noncanonical status, Tertullian's opposition, textual attenuation.
4. **Evidence against:** continuous feast, titles, shrines, art, manuscripts, liturgy, and patristic praise.
5. **Effect:** revived Thecla as a symbol in debates over ordination and authority. Verdict: **overstated as total erasure; grounded in real ancient contestation**.

## Cult and Historical Reception

### Late antiquity

By the late third century, Methodius could assume audience recognition. Fourth-century Cappadocians invoked her as the archetypal virgin. Gregory of Nazianzus lists her among martyrs and withdrew near her sanctuary; Gregory of Nyssa praised her death to worldly passions. Macrina the Younger was reportedly first named Thecla by her mother, testimony to the name's prestige.

Egeria's account provides the first detailed eyewitness picture at Seleucia. She traveled three stages from Tarsus, climbed to the sanctuary, found monastic cells and a protective wall, met Marthana, prayed, received communion, and had the saint's acts read. This proves an organized, literate pilgrimage cult by the early 380s.

The fifth-century *Life and Miracles* localized Thecla in the civic life of Seleucia. Its forty-six miracle accounts present her appearing in dreams, healing, disciplining clergy, rescuing devotees, deciding disputes, and protecting the sanctuary. The text also preserves rivalry with Bishop Basil of Seleucia; the saint can overrule or correct living ecclesiastical authorities in narrative form.

### Egypt

Thecla became important among Egyptian ascetic women and at pilgrimage centers, including a shrine traditionally located near Alexandria/Mareotis. Coptic texts, wall paintings, funerary stelae, textiles, lamps, combs, and ampullae connect her to healing, burial hope, virginity, and women's devotional networks. Some artifacts pair Thecla with Paul; others show her among beasts. Precise identification of unlabeled images is sometimes disputed.

### Syria and Maaloula

The convent of Mar Takla at Maaloula centers on a cave and spring identified with the saint. Its Thecla geography conflicts with the ancient Seleucian endpoint but constitutes a major later tradition. During the Syrian war, thirteen nuns and three attendants associated with the Maaloula convent were seized in December 2013 by rebels including the al-Nusra Front and released in March 2014 in a negotiated exchange involving Syrian women prisoners. Reports disputed ransom claims and aspects of treatment. This episode belongs to the modern history of a Thecla institution, not to her biography.

### Medieval West and Tarragona

Thecla entered Latin martyrologies, calendars, churches, and relic networks. Tarragona's patronal tradition received decisive political form in the restored medieval archbishopric. A relic identified as her arm arrived in 1321 after royal and ecclesiastical negotiations. The annual Santa Tecla festival, processions, dances, bestiary, spoken dances, and human towers combine liturgical, civic, and folkloric layers. The cathedral chapel constructed 1760–1775 houses the relic and an iconographic cycle of her acts.

### Modern devotional status

Eastern Orthodox churches commemorate her chiefly on 24 September as Protomartyr and Equal-to-the-Apostles. Western calendars commonly use 23 September. She remains patron of Tarragona and of numerous churches and communities. Her names are widespread personal names across Orthodox and Mediterranean cultures.

## Awards, Honors, Offices, and Appointments

No secular award or office is documented. Her “honors” are posthumous cult titles rather than appointments:

- Protomartyr among women;
- Equal-to-the-Apostles;
- virgin and martyr;
- wonderworker;
- patroness of Tarragona and numerous local institutions;
- titular dedication of churches, monasteries, wells, schools, and feast days.

## Works by or Attributed to Thecla

No surviving text can be securely attributed to Thecla, and the earliest narrative does not depict her writing a work.

### Ancient attributed speech

- Speeches and prayers in the *Acts of Paul and Thecla*—compositions of the anonymous narrator, not transcripts.
- Thecla's Discourse VIII and concluding hymn in Methodius, *Symposium* (late third century)—explicitly literary dialogue written by Methodius.
- Prayers, teachings, and anti-pagan speeches in the anonymous fifth-century *Life and Miracles*—pseudohistorical literary composition.

There are no genuine letters, diaries, memoirs, patents, artworks, interviews, recordings, or independently preserved sermons by her.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Thecla's durable significance lies in the coexistence of mutually useful images:

- apostolic disciple and authorized teacher;
- celibate woman escaping marriage and household control;
- exemplary virgin safely incorporated into episcopal and monastic Christianity;
- arena martyr who does not biologically die;
- self-baptizer cited for female ritual authority;
- healer competing with physicians and older gods;
- guardian of cities, pilgrims, graves, and women's ascetic communities;
- portable saint localized in rival caves, relics, festivals, and nations.

Her story influenced female martyr acts and the large hagiographic family of women who cut their hair, put on male monastic dress, flee marriage, and enter monasteries as men. Direct literary dependence varies case by case, but the Thecla pattern was widely available.

Modern historians have successively treated the story as pious fiction, a remnant of women's oral storytelling, evidence for conflict over Pauline authority, ascetic propaganda, ancient romance, a script for women's devotion, and a text repeatedly “domesticated” by transmission. No single model explains every version or use.

## Research Conclusions and Unresolved Questions

- **Established:** a Thecla narrative existed by about AD 200; it was contested over women's ritual authority; a major Seleucian sanctuary flourished by the fourth century; the cult spread widely and continuously.
- **Probable:** the surviving *Acts* is a later-second-century composition from an Asia Minor environment and reflects real debates over celibacy, household rupture, women's patronage, teaching, and baptism.
- **Possible but unproved:** the author used memories of an actual Iconian woman associated with Pauline mission.
- **Not established:** AD 30 birth, age eighteen, noble ancestry, exact governor, two historical trials, self-baptism miracle, Paul’s commission, seventy-two years in a cave, age ninety, disappearance into rock, bodily relic identifications, or Rome burial.
- **Open:** precise relationship between oral tradition and written composition; textual boundaries within the *Acts of Paul*; extent to which variants deliberately restrict women's authority; exact sequence and patronage of Meriamlik buildings; identification of certain images and relics.

## Atlas Connections

### Paul the Apostle

- **[T5]** The later-second-century *Acts of Paul and Thecla* portrays Paul as meeting Thecla at Iconium during his missionary travels, teaching her ascetic Christianity, reuniting with her after both attempted executions, and eventually commissioning her to teach. Narrative setting: approximately AD 45–60, principally Iconium and Myra. **Rumor or myth:** this teacher–disciple relationship is the foundational hagiographic claim, but neither Paul’s letters nor canonical Acts mentions Thecla, and no independent first-century evidence verifies their meeting. [Acts of Paul and Thecla](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0816.htm)

### Tertullian

- **[T1+]** Tertullian wrote about Thecla in *De baptismo* 17 at Carthage, approximately AD 198–203. He opposed Christians who invoked her example to authorize women to teach and baptize and reported that an Asian presbyter had composed the underlying Pauline narrative “out of love for Paul” and subsequently lost office. **Documented fact:** the surviving treatise is the earliest securely datable external notice of Thecla and her narrative; the presbyter’s confession and deposition remain **reported facts** known only through Tertullian. [Tertullian, *De baptismo* 17](https://tertullian.org/books/tertulliansouter_prayerbapt.pdf)

### Egeria

- **[T1+]** Egeria wrote about “holy Thecla” and her sanctuary in *Itinerarium* 22–23 after visiting Seleucia in Isauria, approximately AD 381–384. She records praying there, receiving communion, meeting the deaconess Marthana, and having the saint’s acts read. **Documented fact:** Egeria’s surviving travel narrative is eyewitness evidence for the late-fourth-century cult and institution, not for Thecla’s alleged first-century biography. [Egeria’s itinerary and shrine visit](https://portal.sds.ox.ac.uk/articles/online_resource/E05227_The_pilgrim_Egeria_in_her_Itinerary_writes_of_the_sanctuary_of_Thekla_follower_of_the_Apostle_Paul_S00092_in_Seleucia_ad_Calycadnum_Isauria_southern_Asia_Minor_where_she_prayed_and_read_a_passage_from_the_Acts_of_Thecla_Written_in_La/13867643)

- **[T9+]** The sanctuary of Hagia Thekla near Seleucia connects the earlier saint and the later pilgrim without demonstrated shared occupancy: Thecla was the site’s attributed sacred occupant, while Egeria physically visited its church, martyrium, monastic cells, and protected precinct centuries later. **Documented fact:** Egeria occupied the cult site; **rumor or myth:** Thecla’s personal residence, death, or bodily presence there cannot be independently verified. [Egeria and Hagia Thekla](https://www.ccel.org/m/mcclure/etheria/etheria.htm)

### Augustine of Hippo

- **[T1+]** Augustine invoked Thecla in North Africa around AD 400. In *Contra Faustum* 30.4 he discussed Paul’s alleged conversion of the betrothed Thecla to perpetual virginity at Iconium; in *De sancta virginitate* 45 he used her as a paradigmatic virgin prepared for martyrdom. **Documented fact:** Augustine wrote these references and treated Thecla as an established ascetic exemplar; the Pauline episode he repeated remains **rumor or myth** derived from the apocryphal tradition. [Augustine, *Contra Faustum* 30](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/140630.htm) [Augustine, *De sancta virginitate* 45](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1310.htm)

### Jacobus de Voragine

- **[T1+]** In the thirteenth-century *Golden Legend*, Jacobus de Voragine included Thecla in his life of Saint Martin, repeating a story in which Martin said that Agnes, Thecla, and Mary had visited him, as had Peter and Paul. **Documented fact:** Jacobus compiled this written notice; **rumor or myth:** the posthumous visitation is hagiographic tradition, not a historically verifiable encounter. [Jacobus de Voragine, *Golden Legend*, Life of Saint Martin](https://ccel.org/ccel/voragine/goldleg6.xvi.html)

No other roster member produced a sufficiently specific, source-supported crossing under the six defined connection types. Mere shared Christian tradition, comparable legends, namesakes, coappearance in art, or presence in the same broad geography was excluded.
