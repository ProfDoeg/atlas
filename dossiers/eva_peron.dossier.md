# Eva Duarte de Perón: Research Dossier

## Scope and Evidentiary Conventions

Eva Perón’s life is unusually entangled with political propaganda, family secrecy, deliberate destruction or alteration of records, state censorship, hostile mythology, hagiography, and disputes over authorship and medical treatment. This dossier uses the following labels:

- **Documented fact:** supported by primary records, legislation, archival holdings, contemporary recordings, or convergent high-quality scholarship.
- **Reported fact:** supported by credible testimony or scholarship but not independently established by surviving primary documentation.
- **Allegation or theory:** a specific claim exists, but the evidence remains incomplete, contested, or circumstantial.
- **Rumor or myth:** a culturally significant story lacking adequate substantiation or contradicted by stronger evidence.

Dates are Gregorian. Argentine places retain their contemporary names where possible.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

**Best-attested full name:** María Eva Duarte de Perón.

**Names appearing in records or public life:**

- Eva María Ibarguren: reportedly the name on her original birth registration, reflecting her mother’s surname and her status as a child born outside her father’s legal marriage.
- María Eva Duarte: the name used in later civil documentation and during her acting career.
- Eva Duarte: professional name.
- María Eva Duarte de Perón: married and official form.
- Eva Perón: standard political and historical name.
- Evita: affectionate diminutive that became her principal popular and political identity.
- “Jefa Espiritual de la Nación” (“Spiritual Leader of the Nation”): title granted by Congress shortly before her death.
- “Abanderada de los Humildes” (“Standard-Bearer of the Humble”): Peronist honorific.
- “Primera Peronista” (“First Peronist”): political designation used by Eva herself.
- “Santa Evita”: popular devotional and literary name; she has never been canonized by the Roman Catholic Church.

**Birth:** 7 May 1919.

**Probable birthplace:** the rural settlement of La Unión, near Los Toldos, partido of General Viamonte, Buenos Aires Province.

A later civil record gave Junín as her birthplace and 7 May 1922 as her birth date. The prevailing scholarly reconstruction regards the 1919 Los Toldos-area birth as genuine and the 1922 Junín entry as a replacement created before her marriage. The original registration reportedly disappeared or was destroyed. Exact documentary reconstruction is consequently impossible.

**Death:** 26 July 1952, 8:25 p.m., presidential residence, Buenos Aires.

**Age:** 33.

**Cause of death:** metastatic cervical cancer, commonly described in contemporary official language as uterine cancer. She had undergone surgery, radiotherapy, medication including nitrogen mustard, and, according to later medical research, probably a prefrontal lobotomy.

**Burial:** Duarte family tomb, Recoleta Cemetery, Buenos Aires, since 1976.

**Nationality:** Argentine.

**Occupations and functions:**

- Stage, radio, and film actress
- Radio-union organizer
- Political campaigner
- First lady of Argentina, 1946–1952
- President of the Eva Perón Foundation
- President and organizer of the Partido Peronista Femenino
- Principal intermediary between Juan Perón’s government, organized labor, women’s organizations, and petitioners seeking social assistance

She never held an elected national office.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Father: Juan Duarte** (approximately 1872–1926), a cattle-estate administrator and locally influential conservative political figure.

**Mother: Juana Ibarguren** (1894–1971), of Basque ancestry, who worked as a seamstress after the family’s economic decline.

Juan Duarte was legally married to Adela D’Huart and maintained a recognized household in Chivilcoy. His relationship with Juana Ibarguren produced five children but was not a legal marriage. Contemporary Argentine law classified Eva and her siblings as illegitimate, a status carrying legal and social disabilities.

### Full siblings

- Blanca Duarte (1908–2005)
- Elisa Duarte (1910–1967)
- Juan Ramón Duarte (1914–1953)
- Erminda Luján Duarte (1916–2012)
- Eva María Duarte (1919–1952)

Juan Duarte also had children with his legal wife. The two households were separate.

### Paternal loss and funeral episode

Juan Duarte died in an automobile accident in 1926. According to Eva’s sisters and later biographers, Juana Ibarguren brought her children to his funeral in Chivilcoy, where members of the legal family initially resisted their entrance. Some accounts say they were ultimately allowed to participate briefly.

- **Reported fact:** the Ibarguren-Duarte family attended under humiliating circumstances.
- **Unresolved detail:** exact words, actions, and whether the children were literally barred at the church door vary among witnesses.
- **Historical importance:** the episode became an explanatory story about Eva’s resentment of class hierarchy and legal discrimination against children born outside marriage.

### Siblings in later political life

Eva’s brother Juan Duarte became Juan Perón’s private secretary and accumulated conspicuous wealth. He died from a gunshot wound in April 1953.

- The official finding was suicide.
- Opposition circles alleged assassination or a cover-up connected to corruption.
- No court finding established that Eva participated in his financial dealings or that his death was murder.
- Because he died after Eva, later scandals involving him should not automatically be projected backward onto her without evidence.

### Marriage and children

Eva met Colonel Juan Domingo Perón in January 1944. They entered a relationship soon afterward and married civilly in Junín on 22 October 1945. A Catholic ceremony followed at the Church of Saint Francis of Assisi in La Plata, generally dated 10 December 1945.

The marriage produced no children. No credible documentary evidence establishes that Eva had children by another partner.

Perón was more than twenty years older and had been widowed since the death of Aurelia Tizón in 1938.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1919–1934

Eva spent her earliest years on or near the estate at La Unión. After Juan Duarte withdrew from the Ibarguren household, and especially after his death, the family’s financial position deteriorated. They moved into Los Toldos and later, in 1930, to Junín.

Juana Ibarguren supported the household through sewing and by taking in boarders. The family was poor but not continuously destitute in the absolute sense later suggested by both hostile and heroic narratives. Their situation combined economic insecurity with the stigma of illegitimacy and provincial social exclusion.

Eva attended primary school in Los Toldos and Junín. She did not receive a university education or sustained advanced schooling. Her school records and recollections describe an interest in recitation, performance, and school theatre. In 1933 she appeared in the school production *Arriba estudiantes*.

Formative circumstances commonly identified by biographers include:

- Legal and social discrimination against “illegitimate” children
- Her mother’s effort to maintain the household independently
- Provincial class divisions
- Popular cinema, radio drama, and celebrity magazines
- The migration of rural and provincial Argentines to Buenos Aires during the Depression era
- A desire for professional visibility and economic independence

Eva later minimized precise childhood detail in *La razón de mi vida*, which is political autobiography rather than a reliable chronological memoir.

## Move to Buenos Aires: Competing Accounts

Eva arrived in Buenos Aires in early January 1935, aged fifteen.

### The Agustín Magaldi story

The best-known story says tango singer Agustín Magaldi encountered her in Junín and took her to Buenos Aires.

- **Where circulated:** early biographies, hostile accounts, popular journalism, and later the musical *Evita*.
- **Evidence:** weak. Magaldi’s precise presence in Junín at the relevant time and the logistics of the story have been disputed.
- **Assessment:** **rumor or myth**, not established biography.
- **Effect:** it became the archetypal tale of the ambitious provincial girl using a male celebrity to enter the capital.

### Family-assisted move

Eva’s sisters said their mother accompanied her to Buenos Aires, helped arrange an audition, and placed her with family acquaintances.

- **Assessment:** **reported fact**, broadly more plausible than the Magaldi story, although surviving accounts were recorded later and are not identical.

The exact circumstances cannot now be conclusively established.

---

## Education and Formation

Eva’s formal education ended comparatively early. Her professional formation occurred through:

- Touring theatre companies
- Rehearsal and repertory work
- Radio melodrama
- Publicity photography
- Film studios
- Trade-union organization among radio performers
- Her relationship with Perón and exposure to labor politics after 1944

Actors and directors associated with her early career included Eva Franco, José Franco, Pierina Dealessi, Pascual Pellicciotta, Marcos Zucker, and Mario Soffici. No single person can confidently be called her principal teacher.

Her later political speaking style combined radio technique, melodramatic emotional address, Catholic vocabulary, labor militancy, and intensely personal loyalty to Perón.

## Early Career: Stage, Radio, Film, and Union Activity, 1935–1944

### Theatre

Eva made her documented professional stage debut on 28 March 1935 in *La señora de Pérez* with Eva Franco’s company at the Teatro Comedia. Reviews did not identify her as a major new talent, and her early roles were small.

She subsequently worked with several companies and toured provincial cities. Employment was intermittent. Early photographs and theatre notices document steady efforts to gain publicity.

### Radio

Radio became her most successful medium. Among her reported programs and serials were:

- *Oro blanco* (1937)
- *Los jazmines del ochenta*
- *Muy bien* (from 1942)
- *Grandes mujeres de todos los tiempos* (Radio Belgrano, from 1943)
- *Una rosa en el río*
- *Hacia un futuro mejor* (1944–1945), which promoted the government formed after the 1943 military coup and particularly Perón’s labor policies

In historical dramas she portrayed figures including Elizabeth I, Sarah Bernhardt, Catherine the Great, and Alexandra Feodorovna. By 1943–1944 she had become a recognized and well-paid radio performer, although published income figures vary.

### Radio union

On 3 August 1943, Eva participated in founding the Asociación Radial Argentina. In May 1944 the Secretariat of Labor recognized it as the representative organization for radio workers, with Eva as president.

This is important because her labor affiliation predated her marriage and was not wholly invented after she became first lady. Her proximity to Perón, however, clearly strengthened the union’s institutional position.

### Film

Her screen appearances included:

- *¡Segundos afuera!* (1937), small role; some credits rendered her name as “Eva Durante”
- *La carga de los valientes* (1940)
- *El más infeliz del pueblo* (1941)
- *Una novia en apuros* (1942)
- *La cabalgata del circo* (filmed 1944; released 1945)
- *La pródiga* (filmed 1945; withheld from release until 1984)

The Museo Evita preserves production material for *La cabalgata del circo* and *La pródiga*, as well as photographs from *La carga de los valientes*. Her film career was modest; radio, not cinema, made her a national performer.

### Sexual-patronage allegations

Hostile biographies claimed Eva advanced through affairs with directors, producers, military officers, or entertainers.

- **Documented fact:** she formed professional and personal relationships in the entertainment industry, and details of her private life before Perón are incomplete.
- **Allegation:** systematic “casting couch” advancement or prostitution.
- **Evidence:** no surviving documentary record establishes the lurid versions. They largely emerged or expanded after her political rise.
- **Historical significance:** such stories became central to anti-Peronist representations of her as socially and sexually illegitimate.

## Meeting Juan Perón and the 1944 Earthquake Campaign

On 15 January 1944, an earthquake devastated San Juan. The military government organized a public fundraising campaign led by entertainers. On 22 January, a major benefit took place at Luna Park in Buenos Aires.

Eva and Perón probably met at or through the earthquake-relief campaign. Precise accounts differ over whether the decisive introduction occurred at Luna Park or in related organizing meetings. They became partners shortly afterward.

Perón was then secretary of Labor and Welfare, minister of War, and later vice president. Eva used radio to publicize the military government’s social program. Her relationship with him gave her unprecedented political access; her entertainment experience gave the emerging Peronist movement a skilled mass-media advocate.

The couple lived together openly, violating elite expectations for a senior officer. This intensified opposition within the armed forces and conservative society.

---

## October 1945 and Perón’s Political Rise

Military opponents forced Perón from office and detained him in October 1945, ultimately holding him on Martín García Island and then at the Military Hospital.

### Eva’s role in 17 October

Peronist mythology often depicts Eva as the principal organizer of the enormous labor mobilization that secured Perón’s release on 17 October 1945.

- **Documented fact:** she sought information, contacted associates, and remained emotionally and politically committed to Perón.
- **Strong scholarly conclusion:** organized labor leaders, workers, and local political networks—not Eva alone—were decisive in mobilizing the demonstration.
- **Myth:** Eva personally summoned the working masses or directed the entire operation.
- **Effect:** the story retrospectively established her as co-founder of Peronism and heroine of its foundational day.

Eva and Perón married five days later, on 22 October.

During the 1946 election campaign she traveled with Perón, spoke publicly, and appeared beside him—unusual conduct for an Argentine presidential candidate’s wife. Perón defeated the Unión Democrática coalition and took office on 4 June 1946.

---

## First Lady and Political Operator, 1946–1947

Eva rejected the traditional ceremonial limitations of the first lady’s role. She established offices at the Secretariat of Labor, received delegations, mediated disputes, recommended appointments, answered petitions, and cultivated direct relations with union leaders.

Her political power was substantial but informal. It depended on:

- Her marriage to the president
- Personal access to Perón
- Labor-union support
- Radio and controlled press exposure
- The welfare apparatus she later institutionalized
- Her leadership of Peronist women

She was neither a cabinet minister nor constitutionally accountable for much of this authority.

Opposition newspapers portrayed her as ignorant, vindictive, sexually disreputable, and dictatorial. Peronist media portrayed her as self-sacrificing, incorruptible, and uniquely compassionate. Both images became self-reinforcing political constructions.

---

## The 1947 “Rainbow Tour”

From June to August 1947 Eva undertook an official and semi-official European journey, popularly called the *Gira del Arco Iris*. Its diplomatic purpose included improving Argentina’s postwar image and presenting the Perón government as Catholic, socially reformist, and independent of both Soviet communism and old oligarchic capitalism.

### Spain

Francisco Franco’s government received her with exceptional ceremony. She traveled through Madrid, Toledo, Granada, Seville, Santiago de Compostela, and other cities, receiving the Grand Cross of the Order of Isabella the Catholic.

Argentina supplied economically isolated Spain with food and credit. The tour therefore combined humanitarian display, bilateral diplomacy, and support for an authoritarian regime excluded from the new United Nations order.

Eva met Franco and Carmen Polo and appeared before large crowds. She also visited working-class neighborhoods and welfare institutions. Peronist accounts emphasized her appeal to release or aid political prisoners; the extent and effectiveness of such intervention remain debated.

### Italy and the Vatican

She visited Rome and met Pope Pius XII on 27 June 1947. The audience was respectful but less ceremonially extravagant than her Spanish reception. She did not receive a papal title or endorsement of sainthood.

### Portugal, France, Monaco, and Switzerland

She met António de Oliveira Salazar in Portugal and visited France, Monaco, and Switzerland. In Paris she met government officials and participated in public events.

Her Swiss stay was troubled by hostile demonstrations and accusations concerning Argentina’s ties to Axis fugitives and wartime wealth.

### Britain

A proposed British visit did not occur. Disagreement persists over whether the British government avoided an official invitation, whether Buckingham Palace scheduling was decisive, or whether Eva declined an insufficiently prestigious reception.

### Nazi-money allegations arising from the tour

Later journalists alleged that Eva opened Swiss accounts, moved Nazi assets, negotiated protection for fugitives, or even met Martin Bormann.

- **Documented context:** Perón’s government subsequently admitted numerous Nazi and fascist fugitives; Argentine, Vatican-linked, Red Cross, and European channels assisted escape networks.
- **Documented context:** German-Argentine financial and political networks existed.
- **Allegation:** Eva personally deposited bribes or Nazi loot in Swiss accounts.
- **Evidence:** press reports and investigative claims, but no publicly established account record or judicial finding proves that Eva owned such an account.
- **Bormann meeting:** **rumor or conspiracy theory**; Bormann’s presence in Europe in 1947 is not established, and no reliable record documents such a meeting.
- **Conclusion:** the Perón government’s Nazi-refuge policy is historical fact; Eva’s personal banking or courier role remains unproved.

## Women’s Suffrage and the Partido Peronista Femenino

Argentine feminists, socialists, radicals, and provincial reformers had campaigned for women’s political rights for decades before Eva entered politics. She did not originate the cause.

She nevertheless made suffrage a major government priority, campaigned publicly for it, negotiated within the Peronist structure, and became the regime’s principal public symbol of the legislation.

Law 13,010 was enacted on 9 September and promulgated on 23 September 1947. It placed women’s national political rights and obligations on the same legal basis as men’s and ordered preparation of the female electoral roll. The [statutory text](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/normativa/nacional/ley-13010-47353/texto) survives online.

In July 1949 Eva founded and presided over the Partido Peronista Femenino (PPF), an autonomous branch of the Peronist movement. It organized through thousands of *unidades básicas femeninas* and “census delegates.” Membership estimates vary; contemporary and later accounts frequently cite several hundred thousand women.

The PPF:

- Registered and politically educated newly enfranchised women
- Distributed welfare and government information
- Recruited candidates
- Built a nationwide female patronage and mobilization network
- Remained centralized under Eva’s personal leadership

In the 11 November 1951 election, Argentine women voted nationally for the first time. Peronist women won seats in Congress and provincial legislatures; no rival party matched the scale of female candidacies.

## The Eva Perón Foundation

### Creation and structure

Eva began dispensing assistance informally from government offices. The María Eva Duarte de Perón Social Aid Foundation was formally created in 1948 and soon renamed Fundación Eva Perón (FEP).

Eva served as president. The foundation occupied an ambiguous space between private charity, public welfare, organized labor, and presidential government. It was not simply a personal business, but neither was it subjected to the ordinary transparency expected of a state ministry.

### Financing

Sources included:

- Union donations
- Employer and corporate contributions
- Individual gifts
- Lottery, casino, and racetrack revenues
- Public transfers and statutory funds
- Contributions associated with wage agreements
- Donated property, goods, and services

Law 13,992 transferred specified social-welfare funds to the foundation and defined public functions associated with it. The [official statutory text](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/normativa/nacional/ley-13992-296368/texto) confirms that at least part of its resources had an explicit legislative basis.

### Activities and projects

The foundation funded or operated:

- Hospitals and polyclinics
- Nursing education
- Homes for older people
- The Hogar de la Empleada for working women
- Orphanages and *hogares-escuela*
- Schools and dining facilities
- Housing projects
- Holiday complexes at Chapadmalal and Embalse
- Sporting competitions, notably the Evita children’s tournaments
- Scholarships
- Medical aid
- Sewing machines, furniture, clothing, shoes, food, toys, and cash relief
- International disaster assistance and shipments

The Archivo General de la Nación holds a Fundación Eva Perón fonds, and government architectural collections preserve hundreds of plans and project documents. These holdings establish that the organization’s building and welfare programs were extensive and materially real.

### Administrative style

Eva personally received petitioners for long sessions, sometimes late into the night. Applicants described rapid, direct grants that bypassed conventional bureaucracy. This produced both genuine relief and a personalized system in which access to the leader replaced stable legal entitlement.

### Financial controversies

Critics alleged that:

- Businesses and unions were coerced into donating.
- Workers’ deductions were not always voluntary.
- Firms that refused support risked regulatory or commercial retaliation.
- Accounting was opaque.
- Foundation resources doubled as political patronage and regime publicity.
- Purchases and contracts benefited political associates.

Evidence supports opacity and political pressure in at least some fundraising. The foundation did not publish transparent, independently audited accounts comparable to modern public charities. Its closeness to regulatory power made “voluntary” corporate contributions inherently difficult to assess.

No court convicted Eva of embezzlement, fraud, or personal enrichment. The foundation’s hospitals, schools, housing, and mass distribution programs are independently documented. It is therefore inaccurate either to treat it as a fictitious charity or to assume that all funds were transparently and voluntarily obtained.

After the 1955 coup, the military government dissolved the foundation, confiscated its property, and investigated it. Those investigations occurred in an openly anti-Peronist political environment and did not produce a judicial conviction of the deceased Eva.

## Labor Relations and Political Network

Eva developed unusually close relations with the Confederación General del Trabajo (CGT). Union leaders treated her as their principal access point to the presidency. She intervened in wage disputes, appointments, social-benefit requests, and internal labor politics.

Important associates included:

- Juan Domingo Perón
- José Espejo, CGT secretary-general
- Ángel Borlenghi, interior minister and former labor leader
- Domingo Mercante, governor of Buenos Aires Province, later marginalized
- Ramón Carrillo, health minister
- Juan Duarte, presidential private secretary
- Raúl Apold, state propaganda official
- Father Hernán Benítez, Jesuit priest, confessor, and adviser
- Delia Parodi, organizer and later leader within the PPF
- Elena Caporale and other female census delegates
- Pedro Ara, anatomist who preserved her body

Her relationships were frequently transactional and hierarchical. Allies who lost Perón’s confidence could also lose access to Eva.

### Press and cultural power

The Peronist government progressively restricted opposition newspapers, radio stations, and political speech. Eva publicly attacked the “oligarchy,” anti-Peronist press, and political opponents. The acquisition of newspapers and radio interests by pro-government groups narrowed independent media space.

Eva was not solely responsible for the government’s censorship machinery, but she participated in and benefited from a system that used state authority, newsprint controls, licensing, acquisition, and intimidation against critics.

## Political, Religious, and Ideological Development

Eva’s ideology was not a systematic philosophical doctrine. Its recurring components were:

- Personal and political loyalty to Perón
- Social justice framed through labor rights and redistribution
- Opposition between *pueblo* or *descamisados* and the “oligarchy”
- Catholic social vocabulary
- Economic nationalism
- Anticommunism
- Hostility toward liberal elite institutions
- A corporatist understanding of society organized through workers, women, the state, and the leader
- Acceptance of concentrated executive power

She described Peronism as a “cause” and Perón as the indispensable leader. Her rhetoric could be conciliatory toward individual petitioners and violently antagonistic toward political enemies.

### Feminism

Eva’s relationship to feminism was complex.

- She secured or accelerated women’s political incorporation and created an unprecedented national women’s party.
- She publicly rejected some established feminists as socially elite and anti-Peronist.
- *La razón de mi vida* often defined women through spiritual, maternal, domestic, and supportive roles.
- The original manuscript by Manuel Penella de Silva reportedly contained more autonomous feminist proposals, later removed by government editors.
- The PPF expanded women’s participation while remaining vertically subordinate to Eva and Perón.

It is therefore possible to document both a major expansion of women’s public power and a movement structured around personal leadership rather than autonomous feminist pluralism.

### Catholicism

Eva identified as Catholic, maintained a close relationship with Father Hernán Benítez, visited churches and the Vatican, and framed welfare as Christian social justice. Her popular cult later borrowed Catholic imagery, but she was not formally beatified.

---

## Wealth, Property, Clothing, and Personal Finances

Eva’s early radio success gave her financial independence before her marriage. By the early 1940s she reportedly earned a high salary and acquired property or a residence in Buenos Aires, though published monthly-income figures are inconsistent.

As first lady she wore expensive European couture, jewelry, and furs, including garments associated with Christian Dior and Jacques Fath. Peronist interpretations presented this display as the working classes’ symbolic conquest of spaces formerly monopolized by elites. Critics treated it as hypocrisy beside her rhetoric about poverty.

Reliable distinctions are needed:

- **Documented fact:** she possessed and wore luxury clothing and jewelry.
- **Documented fact:** the foundation handled very large resources.
- **Unresolved:** the exact division among personal purchases, official gifts, loans from fashion houses, and foundation or state expenditure.
- **Not established:** that the foundation was merely a vehicle for her personal fortune.
- **Allegations:** overseas personal accounts and Nazi-derived wealth remain unproved.

---

## 1950–1951: Illness and the Vice-Presidential Crisis

Eva fainted and experienced abdominal symptoms in January 1950. An appendectomy was performed, but later accounts suggest the deeper disease was already developing.

By 1951 she suffered bleeding, weakness, weight loss, and severe pain. The government concealed the diagnosis’s gravity.

### The Cabildo Abierto, 22 August 1951

The CGT organized an enormous rally on Avenida 9 de Julio to demand that Eva become Perón’s vice-presidential running mate. Crowd estimates range widely and cannot be treated as precise.

Eva repeatedly asked for time rather than accepting. The University of La Plata preserves a recording and descriptive record of her speech at the [Cabildo Abierto](https://sedici.unlp.edu.ar/handle/10915/29472).

### “Renunciation”

On 31 August 1951 she announced by radio that she would not be a candidate, an event commemorated in Peronist history as the *Renunciamiento*.

Reasons included:

- Her advanced illness
- Armed-forces resistance to a woman and labor-backed political figure becoming vice president and potentially commander in chief
- Resistance within Peronist political circles
- Perón’s unwillingness to risk institutional conflict

The popular story that she freely rejected power solely from humility is incomplete. So is the claim that illness alone decided the matter.

### First women’s national vote

On 11 November 1951, Eva cast her ballot from her hospital bed. Perón won reelection. The event combined genuine female enfranchisement with carefully staged political imagery.

## Medical History and Final Months

### Diagnosis and surgery

A biopsy in 1951 disclosed cervical cancer. On 6 November 1951 American surgeon George T. Pack performed a radical hysterectomy at the Policlínico Presidente Perón in Avellaneda, assisted by an Argentine medical team.

Some accounts say Eva was not fully informed of the diagnosis. Medical secrecy around political leaders was then common, but the exact information given to her remains unresolved.

The cancer had advanced or recurred. Treatment reportedly included radiotherapy, analgesics, transfusions, and nitrogen mustard chemotherapy.

### Lobotomy

A 2012 peer-reviewed medical-historical study examined testimony from neurosurgeon George Udvarhelyi, statements connected to Eva’s physicians, skull radiographs, and records involving Boston neurosurgeon James L. Poppen. It concluded that evidence supports a prefrontal lobotomy or related neurosurgical procedure in 1952. The [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22079825/) summarizes the evidence.

A subsequent study proposed two possible purposes:

1. Relief of otherwise uncontrollable metastatic-cancer pain, anxiety, and agitation.
2. Behavioral modification intended to reduce Eva’s political militancy or belligerence.

Assessment:

- **Probable documented medical event:** a neurosurgical operation consistent with prefrontal lobotomy.
- **Likely medical indication:** extreme pain and agitation.
- **Allegation or theory:** Perón secretly ordered it primarily to suppress her political behavior.
- **Unresolved:** Eva’s knowledge and consent, exact operative date, exact technique, and the relative medical versus political motives.

The evidence does not justify presenting involuntary political lobotomy as conclusively proved. Nor is it responsible to dismiss the operation as pure rumor.

### Final public appearance

On 4 June 1952 Eva accompanied Perón during his second inauguration. Severely emaciated and reportedly supported by a concealed plaster-and-wire framework under her clothing, she stood in the presidential car and appeared before crowds.

### Death

Eva died at 8:25 p.m. on 26 July 1952. She reportedly weighed approximately 36–38 kilograms, though exact figures vary.

A national radio announcement said that the “Spiritual Leader of the Nation” had entered immortality. Public clocks and later official commemorations sometimes marked 8:25. Nationwide mourning followed; enormous crowds filed past her body, and crowd compression caused injuries and some deaths.

---

## Embalming, Funeral, and the Intended Monument

Spanish anatomist Dr. Pedro Ara embalmed Eva’s body using an elaborate preservation procedure. His intention was not ordinary burial preparation but long-term preservation with a lifelike appearance.

The body lay in state and was then kept at CGT headquarters while plans advanced for a monumental tomb associated with the projected Monumento al Descamisado. The planned structure was never completed.

The preserved corpse became simultaneously:

- A family body
- A state relic
- A Peronist political symbol
- An object of devotional behavior
- A security problem for later governments

---

## The Posthumous Odyssey of the Body, 1955–1976

After Perón’s overthrow in September 1955, the military dictatorship banned Peronist symbols and sought to prevent Eva’s body from becoming a resistance shrine.

On the night of 23 November 1955, Lieutenant Colonel Carlos Eugenio Moori Koenig and other intelligence personnel removed the body from CGT headquarters. The [CEDINPE documentary history](https://cedinpe.unsam.edu.ar/sites/default/files/pdfs/dicc_del_peronismo_final_final.pdf) traces the removal and subsequent dispute within the military government.

The body was moved secretly among military and intelligence locations. Stories accumulated about mistreatment and obsessive behavior by custodians.

### Burial in Milan

Under an operation generally associated with Colonel Héctor Cabanillas and with ecclesiastical assistance, the body was transported to Italy and buried in Milan’s Cimitero Maggiore under the false name “Maria Maggi de Magistris” in 1957.

### Return to Perón

In 1971 President Alejandro Agustín Lanusse’s government returned the body to Juan Perón at his residence in Puerta de Hierro, Madrid. Pedro Ara and others examined it. Reports described cuts, pressure damage, and other injuries.

### Return to Argentina

After Juan Perón died in 1974, his widow and successor María Estela Martínez de Perón arranged Eva’s return to Argentina. For a period, the bodies of Juan and Eva were displayed at the presidential residence.

Following the 1976 military coup, Eva was buried in the Duarte family vault at Recoleta Cemetery. The tomb was reinforced to deter theft or further political seizure.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Political Abuses

### Authoritarian government

Eva operated inside a government that:

- Pressured and closed hostile media
- Imprisoned political opponents
- Used state employment and resources for partisan organization
- Built a leader cult
- Restricted institutional checks
- Intervened in unions and universities

She publicly endorsed concentrated leadership and attacked opponents in absolutist terms. The surviving evidence does not permit separating her welfare work from the government’s authoritarian political system.

### Coercive fundraising

**Reported fact:** some businesses and workers experienced foundation contributions as compulsory or politically unavoidable.

**Unresolved:** the scale of direct threats, the degree of Eva’s personal instruction, and the proportion of coerced versus voluntary resources.

**No conviction:** Eva was never judicially convicted of extortion, corruption, or misappropriation.

### Political retaliation in entertainment

A famous story says actress Libertad Lamarque slapped Eva during the making of *La cabalgata del circo*, after which Eva destroyed Lamarque’s Argentine career and forced her into exile.

- Lamarque denied that a slap occurred and disputed being formally banned.
- She did move to Mexico and built a successful career there.
- The Peronist state exercised real power over studios and media.
- Direct documentary proof of an Eva-issued blacklist order has not surfaced.
- **Assessment:** the slap is **rumor or myth**; political pressure against perceived enemies in entertainment is plausible and sometimes documented more broadly, but this personal revenge narrative is unproved.

### Nazi connections

As noted above, the admission of Nazi fugitives under Perón is established. Eva’s personal role in financial transfers, passport sales, or secret Swiss accounts is not.

No evidence establishes that Eva was formally a Nazi agent.

### Arms for workers

Late speeches and *Mi mensaje* express militant hostility toward the oligarchy. Accounts claim she arranged or intended to arrange the purchase of weapons for trade unions.

- **Reported fact:** Eva favored a strongly mobilized labor base and used quasi-military rhetoric.
- **Allegation or unresolved question:** whether she completed an operational plan to arm workers. Accounts differ about weapons purchases and later storage.
- This allegation has been invoked in theories that Perón ordered her lobotomy, but the causal link is unproved.

### Personal violence and revenge stories

Hostile literature attributed beatings, sexual coercion, imprisonment orders, and individual vendettas directly to Eva. Some opponents of Peronism were unquestionably censored, jailed, dismissed, or harassed. Individual stories require case-specific documentation; they cannot all be accepted merely because the broader regime was repressive.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The saint of the people”

**Story:** Eva sacrificed herself entirely for the poor, answered prayers, performed favors after death, and deserves beatification.

**Circulation:** spontaneous mourning, union culture, Peronist imagery, prayer cards, household shrines, petitions to the Church, and literature such as *Santa Evita*.

**Evidence:** extensive evidence of popular devotion and of her welfare activity; no Vatican recognition of miracles or sanctity.

In 2019 Argentine union leaders formally requested advancement of a beatification cause. As a 2024 [Associated Press account](https://apnews.com/article/7c23a6d3cb53cf35dc13a951b79ebfbd) documented, popular devotion persists, but Eva is not a Catholic saint.

### “The woman with the whip”

Mary Main’s hostile 1952 biography, published as *The Woman with the Whip*, portrayed Eva as a vindictive adventuress who manipulated sex, violence, and dictatorship. It helped establish the international anti-Evita archetype and influenced later popular culture.

Some underlying facts—political patronage, hostility to opponents, authoritarian government—are real. The sexualized psychological narrative often outruns demonstrable evidence.

### “She single-handedly won women the vote”

This Peronist simplification erases decades of organizing by Julieta Lanteri, Alicia Moreau de Justo, Elvira Rawson, socialist women, radicals, and provincial reformers.

Eva was nevertheless a decisive government advocate and organizer at the point of enactment and mass implementation.

### “She organized 17 October”

Labor organizations and workers were the decisive mobilizers. Eva’s retrospective centrality grew with her later political stature.

### “The Dior-clad champion of the poor”

The contrast between luxury couture and welfare became a durable symbol used in opposite ways: hypocrisy to opponents, collective vindication to followers. Both depend on interpretation; the clothing and welfare work are factual.

### “Her corpse was replaced by copies”

Numerous stories describe wax replicas, multiple bodies, substitutions, or an uncertain corpse in Recoleta.

Pedro Ara did prepare casts and preservation materials, and the military used secrecy and deception. No reliable evidence shows that the body in Recoleta is a substitute.

### Sexual violation of the corpse

Accounts alleging necrophilic abuse or sexual assault by Moori Koenig or other custodians circulate in memoir, fiction, and oral history.

- The corpse suffered documented damage.
- Custody was secret and abusive in the political sense.
- Specific sexual acts have not been established by a court or conclusive forensic record.
- Tomás Eloy Martínez’s *Santa Evita* intentionally blended history, testimony, and fiction and should not be read as a literal archive.

### The concealed lobotomy

Once treated as sensational rumor, the operation now has significant medical-historical support. The political motive remains contested.

### “Twenty-six years old”

Official posthumous propaganda sometimes altered age, origin, or biography, reflecting the replacement civil record. The strongest evidence supports birth in 1919 and death at thirty-three.

## Works by or Under the Name of Eva Perón

### Books

#### *La razón de mi vida* — 1951

Published by Peuser in Buenos Aires on 15 October 1951.

**Authorship status:** dictated or discussed by Eva but substantially ghostwritten by Spanish journalist Manuel Penella de Silva and revised by Raúl Mendé, Armando Méndez San Martín, and other government hands. It should not be cited as unmediated autobiography.

The original Penella manuscript reportedly contained more independent discussion of women’s political power. The official text transformed the work into a devotional political autobiography centered on Perón, the people, and Eva’s mediating role.

An accessible scan is available through [UPCN](https://upcndigital.org/~ciper/biblioteca/Eva/Evita%2B-%2BLa%2Brazon%2Bde%2Bmi%2Bvida%2B%28completo%29%2B%281%29.pdf).

Translations include:

- *My Mission in Life*
- *Evita by Evita*
- Numerous Spanish-language editions and political reprints

#### *Mi mensaje* — composed or dictated 1952; published posthumously

A more militant final text attacking bureaucracy, privilege, clerical indifference, and internal enemies.

**Attribution history:**

- Appeared publicly decades after her death.

- Litigation and expert examination eventually supported authenticity.

## Death, Burial, and Present Status

Eva Perón has been dead since 26 July 1952. Her remains are interred in the Duarte family tomb in Recoleta Cemetery.

She left no children, no completed memoir written entirely in her own hand, and no conventional personal archive open enough to settle every biographical controversy. Her surviving record is dispersed among state institutions, Peronist archives, family collections, newspapers, recordings, medical testimony, diplomatic sources, and the archives of political opponents.

---

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Eva’s posthumous identities include:

- Working-class benefactor
- Architect of women’s mass political incorporation
- Welfare patron
- Authoritarian propagandist
- Feminist precursor
- Anti-feminist maternalist
- Catholic social symbol
- Secular saint
- Dictator’s wife
- Revolutionary icon
- Fashion figure
- Corpse-relic of Argentine political conflict
- International musical character detached from Argentine historical detail

After 1955, Decree-Law 4161 prohibited Peronist names, symbols, songs, and images. The attempt to erase Eva increased her symbolic power.

The Peronist resistance invoked her as a martyr. Later revolutionary Peronists emphasized her final militant speeches; orthodox unions emphasized loyalty and welfare; conservative Peronists emphasized Catholic charity; feminists debated whether her vertical organization enabled or constrained women.

Her image has appeared on currency, stamps, murals, monuments, union banners, domestic shrines, and campaign materials. A large steel portrait is installed on the former Ministry of Public Works building on Avenida 9 de Julio.

The 1996 film and Lloyd Webber–Rice musical globalized an image shaped partly by Mary Main’s hostile biography. Within Argentina, academic scholarship since the 1970s has increasingly separated the historical actor from both “saint” and “whore” archetypes.

No stable consensus exists because her documented career genuinely joined social redistribution, female mobilization, personal compassion, political coercion, theatrical self-construction, and authoritarian government.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted record are included. Shared nationality, chronology, or symbolic comparison alone is excluded.

### Juan Domingo Perón — **Documented fact**

Eva met Colonel Juan Domingo Perón during the San Juan earthquake-relief campaign in Buenos Aires in January 1944. They became partners, married civilly in Junín on 22 October 1945, and remained married until Eva’s death. She campaigned for him, led the women’s branch of his movement, operated the foundation under his government, and made loyalty to him the center of her public doctrine.

### Christian Dior — **Reported fact**

Eva was a prominent client and wearer of Christian Dior designs during her first-lady years, particularly after the house opened in 1947. Dior is widely reported to have said that the only queen he dressed was Eva Perón, but the precise original source and wording are difficult to establish. No consulted source established a personal friendship or meeting; the supported connection is client–couturier/house.

### Enrique Santos Discépolo — **Documented fact**

Discépolo was a major Argentine songwriter, actor, and committed public advocate for Perón. He moved within the Peronist cultural and broadcasting network during Eva’s political years and delivered the famous “Mordisquito” radio talks supporting the government in 1951. The connection is political and institutional; the consulted sources do not establish a close private friendship with Eva.

### María Estela Martínez de Perón — **Documented posthumous connection**

Eva never met María Estela Martínez, whom Juan Perón encountered years after Eva’s death. As president and Perón’s widow, Martínez arranged for Eva’s body to be returned from Madrid to Argentina in 1974. This is a posthumous custodial and dynastic connection, not a lifetime relationship.

### José López Rega — **Documented posthumous connection**

López Rega belonged to Juan and María Estela Perón’s Madrid and later presidential circle. He participated in the political-religious environment surrounding the repatriated bodies of Juan and Eva during the 1970s. He had no documented relationship with Eva during her lifetime.

### Cristina Fernández de Kirchner — **Documented influence, posthumous**

Fernández de Kirchner has publicly invoked Eva as a political and historical antecedent for Argentine women. The relationship is one of acknowledged posthumous political influence; they could not have met.

### Benito Mussolini — **Theory of ideological influence; no direct connection established**

Perón studied Italian fascist corporatism and observed Mussolini’s Italy before meeting Eva. Eva later functioned within a centralized, corporatist, leader-centered movement. No consulted evidence documents a meeting, correspondence, or explicit personal acknowledgment by Eva that Mussolini influenced her. A direct Atlas connection is therefore not established.

### Ernesto “Che” Guevara — **No documented personal connection**

The narrator called “Che” in the Lloyd Webber–Rice musical encouraged a widespread association. Guevara was a young Argentine during Eva’s rise, but no reliable source establishes that they met. The theatrical character is composite and symbolic.

### Victoria Ocampo — **Political and cultural opposition; direct contact unestablished**

Ocampo’s liberal, cosmopolitan, and anti-Peronist milieu stood in opposition to the Perón government. No consulted source establishes a significant personal meeting or correspondence with Eva, so a direct Atlas connection cannot be asserted.

### Ada Falcón, Carlos Gardel, Jorge Luis Borges, Frida Kahlo, Fidel Castro, and other plausible contemporaries on the roster

No sufficiently documented meeting, correspondence, collaboration, or acknowledged influence with Eva was found in the sources consulted. The Gardel and Magaldi entertainment mythology does not establish a Gardel connection. Mere participation in Argentine or Latin American cultural history is insufficient.
