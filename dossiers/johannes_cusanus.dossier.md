# Johannes Cusanus: Research Dossier

## Scope, Identification, and Evidentiary Limits

This dossier concerns **Johannes Enclen de Cusa**, the early-sixteenth-century itinerant teacher of arithmetic, artificial memory, and diagrams of kinship—not the much better documented fifteenth-century cardinal and philosopher **Nicholas of Cusa (Nicolaus Cusanus, 1401–1464)**.

Johannes Cusanus survives principally through:

- university matriculation and faculty records;
- municipal and cathedral-chapter evidence;
- title pages, prefaces, colophons, and dedicatory verses in early printed books;
- annotations made by students in surviving copies;
- bibliographical records of lost or inaccessible editions;
- a small body of modern scholarship, especially the reconstruction by Farkas Gábor Kiss and Rafał Wójcik in *The Art of Memory in Late Medieval East Central Europe*.

The documentary record establishes activity between **1493 and 1533**. No contemporary birth record, death record, portrait, private correspondence, will, property inventory, marriage record, or continuous narrative biography has been identified.

Consequently, this is the fullest presently reconstructable life, but not a complete private biography. Statements below are labeled where necessary:

- **Documented fact**: directly attested by a contemporary record or surviving publication.
- **Probable reconstruction**: a conclusion drawn from multiple contemporary traces.
- **Reported fact**: stated in historical scholarship based on cited records not independently reproduced online.
- **Hypothesis**: plausible but not demonstrable from the surviving evidence.
- **Unresolved**: available evidence does not permit a reliable conclusion.

## Family and Ancestry

### Father

**Documented fact:** The 1529 Cracow record calls him *Johannes Kusanus Petri Henklen de Kusan*, identifying his father as **Peter Henklen**, with the surname variously normalized as Henklen, Enklen, or Enclen.

Nothing else is securely known about Peter Henklen’s occupation, legal status, wealth, property, or ancestry.

### Mother, siblings, spouse, and descendants

**Unresolved:** No reliable source located for this dossier identifies:

- his mother;
- siblings;
- spouse or partner;
- legitimate or illegitimate children;
- descendants;
- a household of his own.

His academic and possibly clerical career may help explain the absence of family evidence, but celibacy cannot be inferred from silence.

### Social origin

**Documented fact:** When matriculating at Cologne in October 1493, Cusanus was entered as a **pauper**, exempting him from the registration fee.

This establishes limited means at matriculation or, at minimum, officially recognized eligibility for the university’s poverty concession. It does not prove lifelong poverty. His later ability to finance editions suggests that he eventually commanded income or credit from teaching, book sales, patronage, or some combination of these.

---

## Childhood and Early Life

No direct evidence survives for Cusanus’s childhood.

His later identification as *de Kusan* and as belonging to the diocese of Trier makes birth or upbringing at **Kues on the Moselle** the strongest reconstruction. Modern scholarship places his birth approximately between 1475 and 1480, principally by working backward from his university entry in 1493.

If born around 1478, he would have been approximately fifteen when he matriculated. A birth closer to 1475 would make him about eighteen. Neither possibility can be preferred conclusively.

There is no documentary evidence concerning:

- his childhood home;
- elementary or Latin-school education;
- whether his father was connected with the Church, commerce, agriculture, navigation, or local government;
- whether his family had any relationship to the foundations or surviving relatives of Nicholas of Cusa;
- the circumstances by which he obtained the Latin education necessary for university entry.

## Education and Formation

## University of Cologne, 1493–1496

**Documented fact:** Cusanus matriculated in the Faculty of Arts at the University of Cologne in **October 1493**, as a poor student exempt from the normal fee.

### Teachers and examinations

- In **1494**, he completed his *determinatio* under **Theodericus de Buscoducis**, also known as Dirk van ’s-Hertogenbosch.
- He continued toward the master’s degree in **1495**.
- In **1496**, he received the Master of Arts under **Remigius de Malmandario**, or Rémy de Malmedy.

The Cologne curriculum would ordinarily have involved logic, natural philosophy, moral philosophy, arithmetic, and related liberal arts, although no personal course list survives.

### Formation in artificial memory

Cusanus’s earliest direct teacher in mnemonic practice is uncertain.

**Scholarly hypothesis:** Henricus Vibicetus—an obscure Cologne-associated memory teacher—may have instructed him around 1500. This rests on the following chain of evidence:

1. A mnemonic handbook attributed to Vibicetus apparently appeared near Cologne around 1500.
2. Anonymous or differently titled versions appeared at Cologne in 1501 and Zwolle in 1502.
3. Cusanus was connected with Zwolle in 1502.
4. From 1510 onward he published substantially the same mnemonic text under his own name.
5. The same or closely related woodblocks passed through the editions.

This makes a teacher-successor relationship plausible, but **no document explicitly calls Cusanus a pupil of Vibicetus**.

### Intellectual sources

Cusanus’s mnemonic teaching belonged to the late-medieval and Renaissance tradition of *ars memorativa*. His printed apparatus drew on or transmitted material associated with:

- the classical rhetorical distinction between natural and artificial memory;
- Cicero’s *De oratore*;
- the pseudo-Ciceronian *Rhetorica ad Herennium* tradition;
- Simonides as the legendary inventor of ordered spatial memory;
- Aristotle’s discussions of memory and recollection;
- medieval and Renaissance “places and images” systems;
- the pictorial alphabet and numeral tradition printed by **Jacobus Publicius**;
- methods associated with **Peter of Ravenna** and **Conrad Celtis**;
- German and Central European university mnemonic instruction.

Cusanus was a practical teacher rather than a speculative philosopher of memory. His publications present memory as an acquired technique serving students, preachers, lawyers, and other learned professions.

## Early Career and the Beginning of the “Philosophical Pilgrimage”

In the preface to his Lübeck publication of 1523, Cusanus dated that year as the twenty-second year of his wandering undertaken through love of philosophical knowledge:

> *Anno … 1523, anno peregrinationis suae philosophicae amore scientiae vicesimo secundo.*

His 1529 Cracow preface similarly called that year the twenty-seventh of his itinerant life. Both calculations point to **1501–1502** as the beginning of his travels.

The phrase was partly autobiographical and partly self-presentation. It cast paid academic itinerancy as a **philosophical pilgrimage**. Surviving records confirm extensive travel, although they do not verify every one of the nineteen universities or seven kingdoms later claimed.

## Chronological Life History

## 1493–1501: Cologne

Cusanus studied at Cologne between 1493 and 1496 and probably remained in or returned to the Rhineland during the later 1490s.

Around 1500–1501, mnemonic publications connected with Henricus Vibicetus and Hermann von dem Busche appeared at Cologne. Cusanus may have learned the mnemonic trade in this setting.

**Unresolved:** No securely identified appointment or publication under his own name survives from 1496–1501.

## 1501–1503: Zwolle and Deventer

### Zwolle, 1502

**Documented fact:** On **20 October 1502**, Arnoldus Kempen printed Cusanus’s:

*Algorismus proiectilium de integris novus magistri Johannis de Cusa…*

This is the first securely known printed work bearing his name. It was a compact Latin manual of reckoning with counters or counting tokens.

The booklet explained arithmetic operations and proofs. Its examples included:

- seven old women going to Rome, each associated with a recursively multiplying sequence of seven animals or objects;
- twenty-five women at a market, each with a basket containing hens and young birds;
- addition, multiplication, and progression problems;
- practical forms of calculation relevant to instruction and commerce.

The recurring “seven old women” problem belongs to the long transmission history of the “seven houses/cats/sacks” family of arithmetic puzzles, also known from Fibonacci and later European reckoning books. Cusanus did not invent the underlying problem.

A mnemonic booklet, *Aureum reminiscendi memorandique perbreve opusculum*, was also printed by Kempen at Zwolle in September 1502. It was anonymous but used materials closely connected with the earlier Vibicetus text. Modern scholars consider Cusanus’s involvement probable because:

- he was demonstrably working with the same printer;
- the mnemonic woodblocks connect the Zwolle and Cologne editions to his later publications;
- his itinerant career began at precisely this time.

That involvement remains a **bibliographical reconstruction**, not an explicit contemporary attribution.

### Deventer, 1503

**Reported fact:** An edition of the arithmetic manual was printed at Deventer by Richard Paffraet in **1503**. This connects Cusanus with the city’s prominent Latin-school and printing environment.

Whether he formally taught at the Deventer school has not been demonstrated. The edition establishes a publishing connection, not necessarily an institutional appointment.

## 1505: Erfurt

**Documented fact:** Cusanus matriculated at the University of Erfurt for the winter semester of **1505** under the form *Ioannes Ordenn de Cusa*. Specialists regard “Ordenn” as a likely corruption of Enclen.

He was already a master, so the matriculation probably facilitated teaching, study, academic privileges, or access to the university community. The specific courses he offered at Erfurt have not survived.

## 1506–1509: Untraced interval

No fixed residence has been securely established for these years.

His later claim to nineteen universities implies additional stops, but naming them would be speculation. Manuscript mnemonic witnesses circulating in Germany in 1505–1506 demonstrate the vitality of his professional field, not necessarily his physical presence at their places of production.

## 1510: Frankfurt an der Oder

At the recently founded University of Frankfurt an der Oder, Cusanus issued the first surviving mnemonic handbook explicitly under his name:

*Tractatulus artificiose memorie omnibus cuiuscumque etatis studiosis admodum utilis et necessarius.*

It was printed by **Johannes Hanaw**.

The volume contained:

- a short treatise on artificial memory;
- a diagram of the mnemonic house;
- a figurative alphabet;
- images for numerals;
- a passage from Cicero;
- commendatory verse by Hermann von dem Busche;
- verse by **Eberhard Verberius Dantiscus**, who called himself a listener or pupil in Cusanus’s artificial-memory course;
- verse by **Hermann Trebelius**, a poet and legal student associated with Frankfurt.

### Teaching method

The system required the learner to construct ordered mental places and install vivid images within them. Cusanus described nested structures:

- large houses;
- medium-sized houses located within them;
- smaller houses or compartments;
- images placed inside the smallest units.

He combined spatial architecture with pictorial letters and numbers. The aim was reliable ordered recall rather than generalized “photographic memory.”

### Student evidence

**Documented fact:** Eberhard Verberius’s printed designation—*auditor artificiose memorie M. Joannis Cusani*—establishes that he attended Cusanus’s mnemonic instruction.

## 1512–1514: Vienna

Cusanus may have reached Vienna by 1512, but the first direct evidence is from 1514.

A 1512 geographical edition prepared by Johannes Camers contains a discussion of indexes and memory using examples closely resembling the paratexts of Cusanus’s earlier book. This might reflect a local connection or shared source; it does not conclusively place Cusanus in Vienna in 1512.

### 22 March 1514

**Documented fact:** Hieronymus Vietor and Johannes Singrenius printed a Vienna edition of the *Tractatulus artificiose memorie* on **22 March 1514**.

He probably taught as a *professor extraneus*, an outside or non-regent lecturer rather than a salaried member of the permanent faculty.

### 11 July 1514

The same printers issued:

*Algorithmus linealis proiectilium de integris… diligenti opera per Magistrum Joannem Cusanum in hanc formam collectus.*

The formulation “collected into this form” is important: it presents Cusanus as compiler or redactor rather than necessarily the inventor of all its arithmetic content.

The manual covered the basic operations, numerical progressions, proofs, and some commercial reckoning. Surviving annotated copies suggest active classroom use.

### Finances and publishing

The Vienna books illustrate his business model:

1. compile a concise teaching text;
2. pay or arrange for a local edition;
3. use the copies as course manuals;
4. lecture privately or semi-publicly;
5. sell or supply books to students;
6. move to another university town when the audience or stock was exhausted.

The exact division of risk and profit between Cusanus and his printers is unknown.

## 1515–1516: Vienna or travel

He appears to have remained in Vienna for some time, but no continuous sequence of residence records has been recovered.

A 1513 Vienna edition of a lecture text on the trees of consanguinity has sometimes been tentatively connected to him. That attribution is **unresolved**.

## 1517: Ingolstadt and probably Nürnberg

### Ingolstadt

**Documented fact:** On **6 May 1517**, *Ioannes Cusanus Trevirensis diocesis Coloniensis* matriculated at the University of Ingolstadt under rector Martinus Otingensis and vice-rector **Johannes Eck**.

Matriculation in the same administration as Eck does not itself prove personal collaboration.

A copy of the 1514 mnemonic book preserved at Wolfenbüttel contains a student’s note stating that in 1517 he heard the treatise from Master Johannes Cusanus, “a man not unlearned,” and paid **three talents/pounds** to hear it.

This is important evidence for:

- actual oral instruction;
- the portability of Cusanus’s printed textbook;
- the relatively substantial fee charged;
- a favorable contemporary student judgment.

The monetary unit and its purchasing power require local numismatic clarification. The note nevertheless treats the payment as significant.

### Nürnberg

A 1517 Nürnberg edition appeared under the title:

*Arborum consanguinitatis, affinitatis, cognationis spiritualis et legalis…*

It bears the initials **D.P.J.C.**, corresponding closely to *Domino Professore Magistro Johanne Cusano* in his mnemonic book and to the same initials in the securely attributed Lübeck edition.

**Probable reconstruction:** Cusanus prepared or corrected this edition and spent time teaching in Nürnberg. Neither proposition is absolutely proven by the initials alone.

## 1519: Leipzig

Cusanus arranged a new printing of the *Tractatulus artificiose memorie* at Leipzig through Johann Thanner.

The title explicitly says that it was printed:

> *impensis Joannis Cusani, eiusdem artis professoris*

—that is, **at the expense of Johannes Cusanus, professor of that art**.

This is direct evidence that he invested his own resources in publication and publicly claimed professional status as a teacher of artificial memory.

Surviving copies show:

- reuse of the mnemonic illustrations;
- new typesetting that closely imitated the Vienna edition;
- annotations by users or students;
- one Oxford copy with the notation *Mag. Io. Cusanus*.

## 1520–1522: Northward movement

No precise itinerary survives. By 1523 he had reached Lübeck. Additional universities among his claimed nineteen may belong to this interval.

## 1523–1527: Lübeck

### Publication and public teaching

On **22 October 1523**, Vitus Blancken printed Cusanus’s:

*Textus lecture arborum consanguinitatis, affinitatis, cognationis spiritualis et legalis…*

The book was printed at Cusanus’s expense and identified him as a **public professor of the trees**.

It dealt with four kinds of legally significant relationship:

1. blood relationship or consanguinity;
2. affinity created by marriage;
3. spiritual kinship;
4. legal kinship.

The diagrams served canon-law, matrimonial, pastoral, and educational purposes. They helped users determine prohibited degrees of marriage and other relational consequences.

The book’s title says that Cusanus corrected and purged an earlier text of many mistakes and added explanatory riddles or problems. Its subject matter derived substantially from the medieval jurist **Giovanni d’Andrea**, so Cusanus’s role was that of editor, compiler, lecturer, and pedagogical adapter.

The personal preface calls 1523 the twenty-second year of his philosophical wandering.

### Copenhagen, 1524

On **9 October 1524**, Cusanus registered at the University of Copenhagen. The university record says he was admitted to communicate or teach the “measure” of:

- the four trees of consanguinity, affinity, spiritual kinship, and legal kinship;
- artificial memory.

This establishes the combined curriculum he marketed across northern Europe.

### Lübeck religious controversy

**Documented accusation:** Lübeck Cathedral Chapter authorities accused or suspected Cusanus of using lectures on artificial memory to gather and instruct adherents of the Protestant Reformation.

**Documented administrative action:** On **18 August 1527**, the Cathedral Chapter expelled him from Lübeck.

A later history of the Danish Reformation describes him as apparently a cleric who was suspected of secretly assembling Lutherans under the cover of mnemonic teaching. The episode occurred while Lutheran preaching and organization were advancing in Lübeck and while the “Martinian” current sought opportunities to hear evangelical preaching.

### Evidentiary assessment

- It is a **documented fact** that ecclesiastical authorities expelled him.
- It is a **documented accusation or suspicion** that his memory lectures concealed Lutheran organization or preaching.
- The accusation is not equivalent to a trial finding.
- No indictment, interrogation transcript, confession, or theological work by Cusanus has been found.
- No surviving document establishes his precise doctrinal position.
- It remains possible that the accusation accurately reflects Protestant activity, that mnemonic teaching provided a genuine meeting structure later used for reform discussion, or that authorities interpreted an unconventional itinerant lecture circle as religious subversion.
- There is no evidence of imprisonment, torture, formal heresy conviction, or execution.

## 1528–1529: Return to Central Europe

After expulsion from Lübeck, Cusanus traveled southeast.

### Cracow, 1529

**Documented fact:** In 1529 he appeared among the teachers of the University of Cracow as:

> *Johannes Kusanus Petri Henklen de Kusan d. Triverensis, magister Coloniensis, lector artificiose memorie in decem novem universitatibus et septem regnis.*

This provides his fullest contemporary identification and his celebrated claim to have taught artificial memory in **nineteen universities and seven kingdoms**.

Only approximately nine centers can presently be associated with him with varying confidence:

1. Cologne;
2. Zwolle;
3. Erfurt;
4. Frankfurt an der Oder;
5. Vienna;
6. Ingolstadt;
7. Leipzig;
8. Copenhagen;
9. Cracow.

Probable or possible additions include Deventer, Nürnberg, and Lübeck, although not all were necessarily “universities” in a strict institutional sense. The remaining locations have not been recovered.

The numerical claim may have counted temporary lectures, schools, or jurisdictions as universities and constituent states or duchies as kingdoms. It is a contemporary self-description, not an independently verified total.

### Cracow publication

Maciej Szarffenberg printed, at the expense of Marek Szarffenberg:

*Textus lecture quatuor arborum consanguinitatis, affinitatis, cognationis spiritualis ac legalis, a Joanne Cusano ex diversis harum arborum scriptoribus noviter, clare et compendiose congestus.*

The preface was dated **9 March 1529**. Cusanus dedicated the work to the doctors and masters of the Collegium Maius and described them as his patrons. He called 1529 the twenty-seventh year of his philosophical wandering.

This edition claimed compilation from several writers, making the derivative character of the work explicit.

## 1530–1533: Western Europe, Antwerp, and Paris

After Cracow, Cusanus returned westward.

### Antwerp, 1532–1533

Two editions associated with Johannes de Ghelen appeared:

- *Argumenta singulorum (nempe 89) capitum generalia quatuor evangelistarum, auctore Joanne Cusano* (1532);
- *Argumenta singulorum nempe 89 capitum generalia quatuor evangelistarum… carmine, soluta oratione, imaginibus descripta* (1533).

These works supplied arguments or summaries for eighty-nine Gospel chapters in verse, prose, and images. They were related to the pictorial mnemonic Bible commonly known as the *Figurae Evangelistarum* and sometimes falsely attributed to Peter of Rosenheim.

The two editions suggest that Cusanus’s biblical-mnemonic explanations found a viable audience.

### Paris, 1533

A Paris edition appeared as:

*Declaratio arboris consanguinitatis, affinitatis, hoc est propinquitatis tam civilis quam spiritualis per Joannem Cusanum jam recens magna diligentia edita.*

The identified copy is held by the Bibliothèque nationale de France under Rés. E*-645.

### Disappearance

After these 1532–1533 publications, Cusanus disappears from the known record.

No evidence establishes:

- whether he remained in Paris or Antwerp;
- whether he returned to the Rhineland;
- whether he continued teaching;
- whether he entered a religious institution;
- when or where he died;
- whether his disappearance resulted from illness, age, poverty, religious conflict, or ordinary documentary loss.

He was probably in his fifties in 1533.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

## Institutions

Cusanus is documented or plausibly connected with:

- University of Cologne;
- Zwolle printing and teaching milieu;
- Deventer printing and Latin-school milieu;
- University of Erfurt;
- University of Frankfurt an der Oder;
- University of Vienna;
- University of Ingolstadt;
- Leipzig University environment;
- Lübeck public lecture culture;
- University of Copenhagen;
- University of Cracow;
- Collegium Maius, Cracow;
- printers and booksellers in Cologne, Zwolle, Deventer, Frankfurt, Vienna, Nürnberg, Leipzig, Lübeck, Cracow, Antwerp, and Paris.

## Technologies and pedagogical projects

### Counter arithmetic

His *Algorismus* taught calculation with counters on a lined reckoning board. This technology preceded widespread universal adoption of written Hindu-Arabic calculation and remained important in commerce and education.

### Mnemonic architecture

His artificial-memory system used:

- nested imagined buildings;
- ordered loci;
- striking active images;
- pictorial alphabets;
- visual number symbols;
- chains of interacting images;
- grammatical or categorical differentiation through alterations to figures.

### Kinship diagrams

The four kinship trees converted complicated legal relationships into structured visual schemas. Their utility extended to:

- canon law;
- marriage impediments;
- confession and pastoral counseling;
- legal education;
- memory training.

### Illustrated Gospel summaries

His late Gospel publications joined image, prose, and verse to make biblical chapter structures memorable.

## Finances and business structure

No accounts, contracts, investments, or property records have been located. Nevertheless, the books yield several financial facts:

- He entered Cologne as a fee-exempt poor student.
- A student reported paying him three pounds/talents for a 1517 course.
- The Leipzig *Tractatulus* was printed at Cusanus’s expense.
- The Lübeck kinship book was also printed at his expense.
- The Cracow edition involved the Szarffenberg family as printer and financier.
- His repeated reprinting suggests that courses and book distribution generated sufficient demand to justify new editions.

He appears to have operated as an independent intellectual entrepreneur rather than as a continuously salaried professor.

No evidence has been found for banking interests, landed property, patents, manufacturing businesses, or inheritance.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

## Political activity

No political office, diplomatic mission, party allegiance, or service to a prince has been documented.

His career was nevertheless shaped by:

- the fragmented political geography of the Holy Roman Empire;
- university privileges;
- printing networks;
- canon-law jurisdiction;
- the Reformation;
- ecclesiastical control of teaching in Lübeck.

## Religious position

Cusanus’s earliest and middle publications function comfortably within Catholic educational culture. His kinship diagrams assisted canon-law determinations, and his late Gospel books were Christian devotional-pedagogical works.

The 1527 Lübeck expulsion indicates at least suspected involvement with Lutheran reform.

The evidence supports three conclusions and no more:

1. Church authorities regarded his teaching circle as religiously dangerous.
2. He was expelled on that basis.
3. His personal confessional theology remains undocumented.

It would be premature to call him either a confirmed Lutheran reformer or a Catholic victim of baseless suspicion.

## Intellectual orientation

His work combined medieval university practice with humanist print culture. Important characteristics include:

- practical utility over theoretical originality;
- compression of learning into portable handbooks;
- visual organization;
- mobility between universities;
- compilation and correction of existing texts;
- adaptation to local print shops;
- self-advertisement through degrees, professorial titles, and claims of extensive travel;
- use of classical authorities and humanist dedicatory verse.

The label “humanist” describes his Latin teaching and itinerant print culture, but his legal diagrams and memory systems retained strongly medieval forms.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

## 1. Protestant organizing under cover of memory lectures

**Status:** Documented accusation; documented expulsion; underlying activity incompletely established.

Lübeck Cathedral Chapter authorities believed Cusanus’s artificial-memory lectures concealed the gathering or instruction of Lutherans. They expelled him on 18 August 1527.

No surviving criminal conviction or heresy judgment has been identified. The case should therefore not be described as proof that he committed a crime.

## 2. Authorship of the *Tractatulus artificiose memorie*

**Status:** Major modern attribution dispute.

All known editions of 1510, 1514, and 1519 name Cusanus. Textual and material evidence, however, indicates that the core work existed around 1500 under the name of **Henricus Vibicetus** and subsequently in anonymous editions.

The evidentiary sequence is:

- approximately 1500: a now-unavailable Cologne edition attributed to Vibicetus;
- 1501: an anonymous Cologne *Aureum reminiscendi opusculum*;
- 1502: an anonymous Zwolle version, probably connected with Cusanus;
- 1510 onward: Cusanus names himself as author or responsible professor.

Modern researchers propose that Cusanus learned the system from Vibicetus, transmitted and republished it, and eventually assumed authorship after the predecessor disappeared from view.

No contemporary plagiarism accusation against Cusanus has been found. “Plagiarism” in its modern legal and professional sense should not be mechanically imposed on early print culture. Nevertheless, the evidence makes **sole original authorship by Cusanus doubtful**.

## 3. Authorship of the kinship-tree books

**Status:** Compilation and correction, not wholly original authorship.

Cusanus’s editions depend on Giovanni d’Andrea and other legal writers. His own Cracow title acknowledges compilation from diverse authors. His original contribution appears to consist of:

- selection;
- correction;
- condensation;
- arrangement;
- explanatory riddles;
- lecture use;
- possibly some humanistic Latin rewriting.

## 4. The nineteen-universities claim

**Status:** Contemporary self-representation, only partly corroborated.

The claim appears in a university record, but apparently derives from the way Cusanus presented himself. Approximately nine to twelve locations can be traced, depending on standards of proof. The full nineteen have not been identified.

The statement may be accurate if brief lecture visits and non-university schools were included. It may also contain promotional inflation. The evidence does not decide between those possibilities.

## 5. Financial self-promotion

He repeatedly styled himself professor and advertised his works as useful or necessary to students of every age. Such language was normal for early printed textbooks.

No evidence of fraud, unpaid debt, litigation with printers, counterfeit degrees, or deceptive financial schemes has been found.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## Confusion with Nicholas of Cusa

**Story:** Johannes Cusanus is sometimes implicitly or algorithmically conflated with Cardinal Nicholas of Cusa.

**Origin and circulation:** The shared Latin geographical designation *Cusanus*, combined with search-engine indexing and catalog abbreviation, produces the confusion. Modern searches for “Cusanus controversy,” philosophy, mathematics, or theology overwhelmingly return Nicholas.

**Evidence against:** Johannes was active as a poor Cologne student beginning in 1493 and died after 1533. Nicholas was born in 1401 and died in 1464. Their careers, works, and offices are entirely distinct.

**Effect:** The confusion obscures Johannes’s actual identity and wrongly transfers Nicholas’s philosophy, ecclesiastical offices, wealth, controversies, and relationships to him.

## “Nineteen universities and seven kingdoms”

**Story:** Cusanus spent approximately three decades teaching at nineteen universities in seven kingdoms.

**First known form:** The claim survives in his 1529 Cracow identification.

**Evidence:** His books and matriculations confirm remarkable mobility across the Empire, Low Countries, Denmark, and Poland. They do not yet recover all nineteen institutions.

**Interpretation:** The phrase is both a genuine biographical datum and a piece of professional self-fashioning.

**Effect:** It has become the central image of Cusanus as the archetypal wandering memory master.

## The philosophical pilgrim

**Story:** Cusanus wandered through Europe out of love of philosophical knowledge.

**Origin:** His own prefaces of 1523 and 1529.

**Evidence:** His movements are real. The language interprets them in elevated intellectual terms.

**Competing explanation:** His mobility may also have been driven by the economics of private teaching, the need for new student markets, printing opportunities, institutional insecurity, and—in 1527—expulsion.

## Master of extraordinary memory

No direct contemporary demonstration records Cusanus performing feats of prodigious recall. He taught a system for improving memory; that is not evidence that he personally possessed supernatural or infallible memory.

## Secret Lutheran mnemonic circle

**Story:** Memory lessons were a cover for Lutheran instruction.

**Circulators:** Lübeck ecclesiastical authorities and later Reformation historians.

**Evidence for:** The formal expulsion and the historical context of covert evangelical organization.

**Evidence against or limiting it:** No surviving transcript proves what he taught behind closed doors.

**Effect:** The episode makes him a minor figure in accounts of the prehistory of the Lübeck Reformation.

## Successor who appropriated his master’s work

**Story:** Cusanus learned the mnemonic art from Vibicetus, inherited or obtained the woodblocks, and later issued the master’s work under his own name.

**Origin:** Modern textual and book-historical reconstruction.

**Evidence for:** Earlier Vibicetus attribution, near-identical incipits and structure, woodblock continuity, and chronological sequence.

**Limit:** No letter, contract, accusation, or confession explains the transfer.

## Works by or Published Under the Name of Johannes Cusanus

## Arithmetic

### *Algorismus proiectilium de integris novus magistri Johannis de Cusa*

- Zwolle: Arnoldus Kempen, 20 October 1502.
- Deventer: Richard Paffraet, 1503.
- Subject: counter arithmetic, integer operations, proofs, progressions, and practical problems.
- Authorship status: Cusanus named as author/compiler; much content belongs to the wider algorithmic tradition.

### *Algorithmus linealis proiectilium de integris perpulchris Arithmetrice artis regulis…*

- Vienna: Hieronymus Vietor and Johannes Singrenius, 11 July 1514.
- Authorship status: “collected into this form” by Cusanus.
- Digital copy cataloged by e-rara, Zentralbibliothek Zürich, shelf mark Ry 318,2.

## Artificial memory

### *Tractatulus artificiose memorie omnibus cuiuscumque etatis studiosis admodum utilis et necessarius*

Known Cusanus editions:

- Frankfurt an der Oder: Johannes Hanaw, 1510.
- Vienna: Hieronymus Vietor and Johannes Singrenius, 22 March 1514.
- Leipzig: Johann Thanner, at Cusanus’s expense, 1519.

Authorship status: published under Cusanus’s name, but the core treatise probably originated with Henricus Vibicetus.

Related predecessors:

- *Tractatulus de facili et ordinato modo memorandi sive reminiscendi…*, Cologne?, approximately 1500; attributed to Vibicetus; known through description, with cited copies inaccessible or missing.
- *Aureum reminiscendi opusculum*, Cologne: Ludovicus de Renchen, 1501; anonymous.
- *Aureum reminiscendi memorandique perbreve opusculum…*, Zwolle: Arnoldus Kempen, September 1502; anonymous but probably connected with Cusanus.

The printed *Tractatulus* includes pictorial alphabets, numeral images, a mnemonic house, classical testimony, and commendatory verse.

## Kinship and canon-law diagrams

### *Arborum consanguinitatis, affinitatis, cognationis spiritualis et legalis…*

- Nürnberg: Hieronymus Höltzl, 1517.
- Attribution: probable Cusanus edition based on initials and close textual relationship; not absolutely secure.

### *Textus lecture arborum consanguinitatis, affinitatis, cognationis spiritualis et legalis…*

- Lübeck: Vitus Blancken, 22 October 1523.
- Printed at Cusanus’s expense.
- Authorship status: Cusanus as corrector, compiler, lecturer, and contributor of problems; underlying legal text derived from Giovanni d’Andrea and related authorities.

### *Textus lecture quatuor arborum consanguinitatis, affinitatis, cognationis spiritualis ac legalis…*

- Cracow: Maciej Szarffenberg, at the expense of Marek Szarffenberg, 1529.
- Preface dated 9 March 1529.
- Authorship status: explicitly compiled by Cusanus from several writers.

### *Declaratio arboris consanguinitatis, affinitatis, hoc est propinquitatis tam civilis quam spiritualis…*

- Paris, 1533.
- Authorship status: issued under Cusanus’s name as a newly and carefully prepared declaration.

## Gospel mnemonics

### *Argumenta singulorum (nempe 89) capitum generalia quatuor evangelistarum*

- Antwerp: Johannes de Ghelen, 1532.
- Attributed on the title page to Johannes Cusanus.

### *Argumenta singulorum nempe 89 capitum generalia quatuor evangelistarum…*

- Antwerp: Johannes de Ghelen, 1533.
- Presents Gospel material in verse, prose, and images.
- Related to the illustrated mnemonic-Gospel tradition sometimes falsely attributed to Peter of Rosenheim.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Cusanus did not acquire a durable personal cult or philosophical school. His name was gradually detached from the books and practices he transmitted.

His historical importance lies in five areas:

1. **Itinerant teaching:** He exemplifies the mobile lecturer who lived by carrying specialized knowledge between university towns.
2. **Print-supported instruction:** His small books were simultaneously advertisements, manuals, student texts, and portable professional capital.
3. **Transition in arithmetic:** His counter-arithmetic books document practical calculation during the coexistence of reckoning boards and written algorithms.
4. **Late medieval memory culture:** His mnemonic system shows the continued vitality of architectural places, images, alphabets, and number symbols before the more ambitious systems of Giulio Camillo and Giordano Bruno.
5. **Visual legal pedagogy:** His kinship trees joined legal doctrine, diagram, riddling problems, and memory training.

Modern reassessment has also reduced the certainty of his claimed authorship. He is now better understood as a transmitter, editor, entrepreneur, and teacher working inside a collective textual tradition.

The dominant obstacle to his reputation has been confusion with Nicholas of Cusa and the survival of only a few rare copies of his publications.

## Atlas Connections

The roster was checked for documented personal, institutional, or intellectual crossings. Only the following are supportable.

### Aristotle

**Evidentiary label: Documented intellectual invocation.**

An epigram printed in Cusanus’s mnemonic textbook names Aristotle among authoritative figures who treated memory as an exceptional or divine capacity. Aristotelian memory theory also belonged to the learned background of the tradition Cusanus taught.

This is intellectual reception, not a personal relationship.

### Marcus Tullius Cicero

**Evidentiary label: Documented textual influence.**

The *Tractatulus* prints a passage attributed to Cicero concerning Carneades and Metrodorus and the use of images impressed in ordered places. Cicero is explicitly invoked against critics of artificial memory.

### Simonides of Ceos

**Evidentiary label: Documented legendary authority.**

The paratexts of Cusanus’s book name Simonides as an inventor or exemplary master of artificial memory. The connection is to the classical foundation legend of the art, not a historically possible personal relationship.

### Peter of Ravenna

**Evidentiary label: Reported intellectual influence.**

Modern scholarship places Cusanus among itinerant memory teachers who followed the professional and textual path established by Jacobus Publicius and Peter of Ravenna. Specific elements of the wider mnemonic tradition—especially spatial places and vivid images—were circulating through Peter’s *Phoenix* before Cusanus’s career.

No meeting is documented.

### Ramon Llull

**Evidentiary label: Reported contextual connection, not direct influence.**

Modern studies compare the diagrammatic organization of Cusanus’s kinship trees with the wider use of trees in Llullian knowledge organization. No source located here establishes that Cusanus read Llull or explicitly acknowledged him. The relationship is therefore contextual only.

### Giordano Bruno

**Evidentiary label: Reported later textual parallel or reception.**

Studies of Bruno’s art of memory identify image chains and pictorial encoding comparable to, and in some cases transmitted through, the same late-medieval mnemonic corpus represented by Cusanus. A direct line from Cusanus personally to Bruno has not been demonstrated.

### Frances Amelia Yates

**Evidentiary label: Historiographical connection.**

Yates’s *The Art of Memory* helped create the modern scholarly field in which Cusanus’s neglected mnemonic books were subsequently reassessed. She is a historian of his broader tradition, not a contemporary connection.

### Nicholas of Cusa

**Evidentiary label: Shared geographical name; no documented personal or family connection.**

Both men used *Cusanus* because of an association with Kues. Nicholas died decades before Johannes’s documented university career. No evidence establishes ancestry, patronage, institutional succession, or acknowledged intellectual influence between them.

### Johannes Reuchlin, Johannes Trithemius, Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa, Martin Luther, Paracelsus, Giulio Camillo, and other contemporaries on the roster

**Evidentiary label: No documented connection found.**

They inhabited intersecting humanist, reform, occult-philosophical, or university worlds, but mere contemporaneity, shared cities, or related subject matter does not establish contact. No supported meeting, correspondence, collaboration, rivalry, or explicit acknowledged influence was located.

### Absence finding

Despite the breadth of the roster, Cusanus’s surviving personal network is mostly composed of people not listed there: his Cologne examiners, Vibicetus, Hermann von dem Busche, Hermann Trebelius, Eberhard Verberius, his printers, and Lübeck ecclesiastical authorities. The atlas connections are therefore principally textual and historiographical rather than interpersonal.
