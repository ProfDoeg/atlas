# Andrew Jackson: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

This dossier concerns **Andrew Jackson (1767–1845), seventh president of the United States**.

Labels used for contested material:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary records, official documents, correspondence, court records, or strong archival evidence.
- **Reported fact:** reported by contemporaries or later witnesses but not independently documented to the same degree.
- **Allegation:** an accusation made at the time or afterward without an adjudication establishing it.
- **Court or legislative finding:** a formal judicial or legislative determination.
- **Disputed interpretation:** historians agree on the underlying events but differ over their meaning, responsibility, legality, or classification.
- **Rumor or myth:** a story lacking reliable contemporary substantiation, whether or not it reflects a broader historical reality.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Andrew Jackson.
- **Common titles:** General Andrew Jackson; Major General Jackson; President Jackson.
- **Nickname:** **“Old Hickory,”** acquired from soldiers who associated his physical endurance and rigidity with hickory wood.
- **Hostile political names:** **“King Andrew,” “King Andrew the First,” “American Caesar,”** and related monarchical imagery employed by National Republican and Whig opponents.
- **Birth:** March 15, 1767.
- **Birthplace:** The Waxhaws, a Scots-Irish settlement straddling the North Carolina–South Carolina boundary. The precise cabin and state remain disputed. Jackson consistently identified South Carolina as his birthplace; North Carolina traditions place it at or near the George McCamie house.
- **Death:** June 8, 1845.
- **Place of death:** The Hermitage, near Nashville, Tennessee.
- **Burial:** Garden tomb at The Hermitage beside his wife, Rachel.
- **Nationality:** British subject at birth; American after independence.
- **Religion:** Raised in a Presbyterian environment. For much of his adult life he avoided formal church membership, partly because he believed his conduct did not meet the obligations of profession. He joined the Presbyterian Church late in life after consulting the Reverend James Smith.
- **Professions:** Lawyer, public prosecutor, merchant, land speculator, planter, enslaver, horse breeder and racer, judge, militia officer, U.S. Army general, territorial governor, congressman, senator, and president.
- **Principal residences:** Waxhaws; Salisbury, North Carolina; Jonesborough and Nashville in the western district; Hunter’s Hill near Nashville; The Hermitage; Washington, D.C.; military headquarters and encampments across Tennessee, Alabama, Mississippi, Louisiana, and Spanish Florida.
- **Presidency:** March 4, 1829–March 4, 1837; seventh president; vice presidents John C. Calhoun and, in the second term, Martin Van Buren.
- **Party affiliations:** Jeffersonian Democratic-Republican; later central symbol and organizer of the Democratic Party.

The Library of Congress holds approximately 26,000 Jackson-related items, chiefly from 1785–1845, including correspondence, military orders, treaty journals, financial material, speeches, and presidential drafts.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and ancestry

Jackson’s parents, **Andrew Jackson Sr.** and **Elizabeth Hutchinson Jackson**, were Presbyterian migrants from the north of Ireland, generally described as Scots-Irish or Ulster Scots. They left the Carrickfergus area of County Antrim for the Carolinas in 1765 with two sons.

Andrew Sr. died shortly before his youngest son’s birth, reportedly after an accident while clearing land. His grave was unmarked or later could not be identified. Elizabeth, commonly called Betty, and her sons depended heavily on Crawford relatives in the Waxhaw community.

### Siblings

- **Hugh Jackson:** Eldest brother. Joined Patriot forces and died in 1779 after the Battle of Stono Ferry, probably from heat exhaustion or illness rather than a battle wound.
- **Robert Jackson:** Served or rode with Andrew in the partisan conflict of the Revolution. Captured with him in 1781; died soon after their release, probably from smallpox aggravated by wartime privation.
- **Andrew Jackson:** Youngest and ultimately the only surviving member of his immediate family.

Elizabeth Jackson died in 1781 after traveling to Charleston to nurse or retrieve imprisoned relatives or soldiers. Accounts differ on her exact mission and place of burial. She was probably buried in an unmarked grave in or near Charleston. By approximately fourteen, Andrew had lost both parents and both brothers.

### Marriage

Jackson met **Rachel Donelson Robards**, daughter of frontier surveyor and Nashville founder John Donelson and Rachel Stockley Donelson, while boarding with the Donelson household.

Rachel was separated from her husband, **Lewis Robards**, but not yet legally divorced. Jackson and Rachel began living as husband and wife and apparently underwent a marriage ceremony in 1791. Robards subsequently obtained a Kentucky divorce on grounds involving Rachel’s relationship with Jackson. After learning that the earlier union was legally defective, Andrew and Rachel married again in January 1794.

**Documented fact:** Their first marriage occurred before Rachel’s divorce was complete and was therefore legally invalid.

**Disputed question:** Jackson’s defenders said the couple mistakenly believed Robards had already obtained a divorce. Daniel Feller and other modern historians consider complete innocent misunderstanding unlikely, although the legal situation was confusing and Nashville society largely accepted the couple.

The episode became a major weapon during the elections of 1824 and 1828. Anti-Jackson publications called Rachel an adulteress and bigamist; Jackson blamed political attacks for worsening the illness that preceded her death. The causal assertion cannot be medically demonstrated.

Rachel died on December 22, 1828, after suffering severe chest pain and illness, commonly described as heart disease or a heart attack. She was buried at The Hermitage in the dress prepared for the presidential inauguration.

### Children, adoptees, wards, and household kin

Andrew and Rachel had no known biological children.

Their extended household included:

- **Andrew Jackson Jr.**, born one of twin sons of Rachel’s brother Severn Donelson. The Jacksons took him into their household and treated him as an adopted son. He inherited The Hermitage and much of Jackson’s property but accumulated substantial debts.
- **Lyncoya**, a Creek infant found after the 1813 Battle of Tallushatchee, where Jackson’s troops and allied fighters killed much of the Red Stick population. Jackson sent the child to Rachel, and Lyncoya was raised at The Hermitage. Jackson initially considered training him at West Point or as a saddler. Lyncoya died of tuberculosis on June 1, 1828, while still a teenager.
- **Andrew Jackson Hutchings**, a grandnephew of Rachel, who was raised in the household.
- **Andrew Jackson Donelson**, Rachel’s nephew, whom Jackson mentored; Donelson became his military aide, private secretary, political intermediary, and later a diplomat.
- Children of General Edward Butler and of Rachel’s brother Samuel Donelson, for whom the Jacksons acted as guardians or quasi-parents.

Jackson’s care for Lyncoya has often been cited as evidence of personal affection crossing ethnic boundaries. It coexisted with Jackson’s military campaigns, coercive treaties, and program of mass Indigenous removal. The adoption does not negate those documented policies; neither does the policy establish that Jackson’s household relationship with Lyncoya was fictitious.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1767–1787

Jackson grew up among Scots-Irish farming families in a violent borderland marked by property disputes, Indigenous dispossession, religious community, and resistance to British authority. He received an irregular elementary education at local “old-field” schools. Later claims that he was intended for the ministry are possible but not firmly documented. He acquired basic literacy, some Latin, and a lifelong insecurity about spelling and formal composition.

During the Revolutionary War, the Waxhaws became a theater of civil and irregular warfare. Jackson served as a mounted messenger or courier with Patriot irregulars rather than as a regularly enlisted adult soldier.

In 1781 Andrew and Robert Jackson were captured by British or Loyalist forces. According to the established family story, a British officer ordered Andrew to clean his boots. Jackson refused, whereupon the officer struck him with a sword, cutting his hand and head. The scars were real and Jackson repeatedly attributed them to this incident. The exact exchange rests primarily on later recollection, but the capture, injuries, and bitter anti-British consequences are well supported.

The brothers contracted smallpox. Their mother secured their release through a prisoner exchange, but Robert soon died. Elizabeth’s death later that year left Andrew orphaned.

After the war Jackson lived irregularly with relatives, spent an inheritance, gambled, raced horses, and briefly taught school. His youthful reputation included quick temper, practical jokes, betting, and a sensitivity to insults.

Around 1784 he moved to Salisbury, North Carolina, to read law. His principal instructors were attorneys **Spruce Macay** and **John Stokes**. Legal education consisted of apprenticeship, copying documents, reading English legal authorities, and observing court practice. Jackson was admitted to the North Carolina bar in 1787.

## Legal, Commercial, and Political Formation, 1788–1804

### Migration to Tennessee

In 1788 Jackson accepted appointment as public prosecutor for North Carolina’s western **Mero District**, centered on Nashville. He traveled west through dangerous roads and settlements and established himself in a rapidly expanding society where legal claims, debts, violence, land titles, and Indigenous cessions were intertwined.

He prosecuted criminal cases, represented creditors, acquired property through fees and debt proceedings, and developed a network among the Donelsons and other leading families. His advancement was assisted by ability, aggression, family alliance, and the scarcity of trained lawyers.

### Business and land

Jackson:

- Speculated in military bounty warrants and western land.
- Bought and sold town lots, farms, and larger tracts.
- Operated stores or mercantile partnerships, including ventures with Samuel Donelson and John Hutchings.
- Traded along the Cumberland, Mississippi, and Natchez routes.
- Bred, raced, and wagered on horses.
- Bought and sold enslaved people.
- Extended and incurred credit in a cash-poor frontier economy.

He acquired **Hunter’s Hill**, a plantation near Nashville, and became part of Tennessee’s slaveholding planter and officeholding elite. His self-presentation as a frontier outsider obscured his relatively early admission into this regional ruling class.

### Glasgow land-fraud investigation

As a lawyer and officeholder, Jackson helped expose fraudulent North Carolina military land grants associated with North Carolina secretary of state **James Glasgow** and cooperating land agents.

**Documented fact:** Jackson provided evidence and participated in proceedings connected to forged or improperly issued grants.

**Interpretive qualification:** Jackson was simultaneously an active land speculator whose advancement depended on the same volatile land system. Exposure of one fraudulent network did not make his own speculative interests disinterested.

### Early offices

- **1796:** Delegate to Tennessee’s constitutional convention.
- **1796–1797:** Tennessee’s first member of the U.S. House of Representatives. He opposed the Jay Treaty and voted against a resolution praising George Washington’s administration.
- **1797–1798:** U.S. senator from Tennessee; resigned after roughly one year.
- **1798–1804:** Judge of the Tennessee Superior Court.
- **1802:** Elected major general of the Tennessee militia, defeating former governor John Sevier by a tie-breaking vote.

### Conflict with John Sevier

Jackson and Tennessee governor **John Sevier** became bitter rivals. Jackson accused Sevier of misconduct, while Sevier invoked allegations concerning Jackson’s marriage and accused him of land-related impropriety. In 1803 they nearly fought a duel at Southwest Point. The confrontation dissolved into insults and disorder rather than a formal exchange of shots.

### Financial collapse and The Hermitage

A failed commercial arrangement with Philadelphia merchant **David Allison** left Jackson responsible for major debts after Allison defaulted. Jackson sold Hunter’s Hill, merchandise, enslaved people, and large speculative holdings. In 1804 he purchased a smaller tract that became **The Hermitage**, initially approximately 425 acres and subsequently enlarged.

The episode made Jackson suspicious of distant credit, banks, paper instruments, and debt, although he continued to use credit and commercial networks. It was an important formative influence on his later bank politics but does not by itself explain the Bank War.

## Violence, Honor, Burr, and Militia Ambition, 1804–1812

### Charles Dickinson duel

A dispute involving a horse race, financial obligations, and insults to Rachel escalated into a duel with **Charles Dickinson**, a celebrated marksman, on May 30, 1806, near the Kentucky–Tennessee border.

Dickinson fired first and struck Jackson in the chest near the ribs. Jackson remained standing, took deliberate aim after a misfire or half-cock complication, and fatally shot Dickinson. The ball in Jackson’s body could not safely be removed and contributed to chronic pain and pulmonary problems.

**Documented fact:** Jackson killed Dickinson under the conventions of a formally arranged duel.

**Legal status:** He was not convicted of homicide. Duels were socially regulated affairs of honor but were increasingly subject to legal prohibition and criticism.

**Hostile account:** Opponents portrayed Jackson’s second, deliberate attempt to fire as murder.

**Defensive account:** Jackson’s supporters argued that the agreed rules permitted him to recock and fire.

Jackson participated in or threatened numerous affairs of honor, but popular claims that he fought scores or more than a hundred completed duels are exaggerations. Many incidents were challenges, aborted encounters, brawls, or political stories rather than exchanges of fire.

### Aaron Burr connection

Jackson welcomed former vice president **Aaron Burr** in Tennessee, admired his opposition to eastern political elites, and provided boats or logistical assistance for Burr’s projected western enterprise. When President Jefferson accused Burr of conspiracy, Jackson defended Burr’s character and testified at his 1807 trial.

**Documented fact:** Jackson associated with Burr and aided preparations before the full character of Burr’s plans was clear.

**Allegation:** Jackson knowingly joined a treasonous plan to detach western territories or invade Spanish possessions.

**Finding:** Burr was acquitted of treason; no court established Jackson’s participation in a criminal conspiracy. Jackson’s correspondence shows enthusiasm for a possible campaign against Spain but does not conclusively prove knowledge of a separatist plot.

### Militia ambitions

Jackson repeatedly offered Tennessee forces for national service. His hostility toward Britain, Spain, and Indigenous groups resisting U.S. expansion was linked to childhood trauma, frontier security, land expansion, nationalism, and personal ambition.

## War of 1812 and Creek War, 1812–1814

### Natchez expedition

In late 1812 Jackson led Tennessee volunteers south toward Natchez after the federal government accepted their service. The War Department then ordered the force disbanded far from home. Jackson refused to abandon sick or unpaid soldiers, marched them back to Tennessee, and reportedly pledged or used personal credit for provisions.

This episode helped produce the **“Old Hickory”** image: the commander who endured hardship with his men. It also deepened his resentment of the Madison administration and regular-army bureaucracy.

### Benton street fight

A dispute with brothers **Thomas Hart Benton** and **Jesse Benton** culminated in a violent Nashville hotel brawl in September 1813. Jackson was shot in the shoulder or upper arm and nearly died. Surgeons advised amputation; he refused. Thomas Hart Benton later became one of Jackson’s most important Senate allies.

### Creek War

The Creek civil war divided Red Stick resistance forces from Creek groups aligned with the United States. The Fort Mims attack of August 1813 intensified U.S. intervention. Jackson commanded Tennessee troops in a campaign characterized by supply collapse, desertion, hunger, internal mutiny, attacks on Creek towns, and cooperation with Cherokee and Lower Creek allies.

Important engagements included:

- **Tallushatchee, November 3, 1813:** John Coffee’s troops surrounded and destroyed the town; combatants and noncombatants were killed. Lyncoya was found afterward.
- **Talladega, November 9, 1813:** Jackson relieved allied Creeks besieged by Red Sticks.
- **Emuckfaw and Enotachopo Creek, January 1814:** Inconclusive, costly fighting.
- **Horseshoe Bend, March 27, 1814:** Jackson’s combined force, including Cherokee and allied Creek warriors, attacked the Red Stick fortified position. Approximately 800 Red Stick men were killed; the battle broke organized resistance.

### Discipline and executions

Jackson used threats, arrests, and courts-martial to hold his army together. Militiaman **John Woods** was executed in 1814 for mutiny after a court-martial, despite questions about his mental capacity and the proportionality of the sentence. Jackson later approved executions of six militiamen convicted after desertion or mutiny proceedings.

**Documented fact:** The executions occurred under Jackson’s command or approval after military proceedings.

**Controversy:** Critics described them as unnecessary judicial killings used to impose obedience. Defenders emphasized repeated desertion and the risk of army collapse. No later court reversed the sentences.

### Treaty of Fort Jackson

On August 9, 1814, Jackson imposed the Treaty of Fort Jackson on the Creek Nation. The cession encompassed approximately **23 million acres** in present-day Alabama and Georgia. It took land not only from Red Stick enemies but also from Creek communities that had allied with Jackson.

The treaty opened territory to white settlement, cotton cultivation, and slave-labor expansion. It established the pattern of pairing military victory with exceptionally large land cessions.

## New Orleans, Martial Law, and National Fame, 1814–1817

Commissioned a major general in the regular U.S. Army, Jackson was placed in command of the Seventh Military District. He seized Pensacola from Spanish and British forces in November 1814 and then moved to defend New Orleans.

Jackson assembled regulars, Tennessee and Kentucky militia, free Black troops, Choctaw fighters, local Creoles, and men associated with privateer **Jean Lafitte**. The climactic British assault on January 8, 1815, ended in a decisive U.S. victory with disproportionate British casualties. The Treaty of Ghent had been signed but not yet ratified or communicated to the combatants.

The victory made Jackson the most celebrated American military figure of his generation and converted a mixed earlier career into a viable national political identity.

### Martial law controversy

Jackson had declared martial law in New Orleans. After news of peace approached, Louisiana legislator **Louis Louaillier** published criticism of continued military rule. Jackson ordered him arrested. When federal judge **Dominick A. Hall** issued a writ of habeas corpus, Jackson had Hall arrested and expelled from the city.

After civil authority resumed, Hall found Jackson in contempt and fined him **$1,000**. Jackson paid. Congress refunded the sum with interest in 1844.

**Court finding:** Jackson was formally fined for contempt.

**Disputed constitutional issue:** Supporters argued military necessity; critics regarded the arrests and defiance of habeas corpus as a dangerous assertion of arbitrary power.

## Indigenous Treaties, Florida, and the First Seminole War, 1816–1821

Jackson negotiated or helped impose treaties with the Cherokee, Chickasaw, Choctaw, and Creek nations. These agreements transferred millions of acres to the United States. His commissions often overlapped with his regional political relationships and knowledge of land markets.

### Salt Lick Reservation controversy

The 1818 Chickasaw treaty reserved land containing valuable salt deposits. Jackson and associates were accused of structuring or exploiting the arrangement for speculative advantage.

**Documented fact:** Jackson was a treaty commissioner and had relationships with persons interested in western land.

**Allegation:** The reservation provision was corruptly engineered for private enrichment.

**Assessment:** Surviving evidence establishes conflict-of-interest concerns and speculation around treaty-derived land, but it does not yield a criminal conviction or conclusive proof that Jackson secretly wrote the provision solely for personal profit.

### First Seminole War

In 1818 Jackson entered Spanish Florida while pursuing Seminole fighters, Red Stick refugees, and people escaping slavery. He captured St. Marks and Pensacola, removed Spanish authorities, destroyed settlements, and widened a border campaign into an invasion of foreign territory.

Jackson seized British subjects **Alexander Arbuthnot** and **Robert Ambrister**, accusing them of aiding Seminoles and inciting war. A military court convicted both. Arbuthnot was hanged. The court sentenced Ambrister to death, reconsidered, and reduced the sentence to whipping and imprisonment; Jackson restored the death penalty, and Ambrister was shot.

**Documented fact:** Jackson ordered the executions.

**Legal controversy:** Critics in Congress and Britain argued that the tribunal lacked jurisdiction, the evidence was inadequate, and Jackson unlawfully reversed Ambrister’s reduced sentence. Later authorities on military law have described the execution as legally indefensible or even murder.

**Political outcome:** Secretary of State **John Quincy Adams** defended Jackson diplomatically. The Monroe administration returned captured posts but used the crisis to pressure Spain. The Adams–Onís Treaty of 1819 transferred Florida to the United States.

### Florida governorship

Jackson resigned his army commission and became governor of Florida Territory in 1821. He oversaw the transfer from Spain but quarreled with former Spanish governor **José María Callava**, whom he briefly imprisoned during a dispute over records and property claims. Jackson’s governorship lasted only a few months; he resigned and returned to Tennessee.

## Presidential Rise, 1822–1828

Tennessee allies nominated Jackson for president and returned him to the U.S. Senate in 1823. His candidacy joined military fame to resentment of established Washington politicians.

### Election of 1824

Jackson won a plurality of both the popular and electoral votes but not the required electoral majority. The House of Representatives chose among Jackson, John Quincy Adams, and William H. Crawford. **Henry Clay**, excluded from the final vote, supported Adams; Adams subsequently appointed Clay secretary of state.

Jacksonians alleged a **“corrupt bargain.”**

- **Documented fact:** Clay supported Adams and afterward became secretary of state.
- **Allegation:** Adams explicitly promised Clay the appointment in exchange for votes.
- **Evidence:** No documentary proof establishes a bargain. Adams and Clay denied it. The sequence was politically damaging and became the central Jacksonian explanation for the result.

Jackson resigned from the Senate in 1825 and organized for a rematch. Political managers included **Martin Van Buren, John Henry Eaton, William B. Lewis, Andrew Jackson Donelson, Amos Kendall, Duff Green**, and later **Francis Preston Blair**.

### Election of 1828

The campaign was intensely personal. Jackson’s side attacked Adams as aristocratic, corrupt, and morally suspect. Adams supporters circulated the “coffin handbill,” emphasizing Jackson’s executions, duels, brawls, and alleged murders; they also revived Rachel’s marital history.

Jackson decisively defeated Adams. Rachel died after the election but before the inauguration. Jackson entered Washington grieving and convinced that opponents had persecuted her to death.

## Presidency, First Term, 1829–1833

### Inauguration and popular politics

Jackson’s March 4, 1829, inauguration drew an unusually large crowd. The White House reception became crowded and disorderly, furnishing elite observers with an image of “King Mob.”

**Documented fact:** The reception was heavily attended and damage or disorder occurred.

**Mythic enlargement:** Later accounts turned the scene into an invasion by drunken frontiersmen destroying the White House. Contemporary evidence supports crowding and disruption but not every lurid detail.

Jackson asserted that the president uniquely represented the whole people. He expanded use of the veto from a largely constitutional objection into a policy instrument and relied heavily on informal advisers—the so-called **Kitchen Cabinet**.

### Office rotation and patronage

Jackson removed officeholders and appointed loyalists under the theory that rotation prevented an entrenched bureaucracy.

The phrase **“to the victor belong the spoils”** was not coined by Jackson. Senator **William L. Marcy** used a related formulation in 1832. Jackson’s administration nevertheless became permanently associated with the **spoils system**.

Removals were less universal than hostile rhetoric implied, but patronage became more overtly tied to party organization. Appointee **Samuel Swartwout**, collector of the Port of New York, later absconded after embezzling more than $1 million. The theft was discovered after Jackson left office.

**Documented fact:** Swartwout was a Jackson appointee and embezzler.

**Responsibility dispute:** The scandal demonstrated serious patronage and oversight failures; it does not establish that Jackson shared the proceeds.

### Peggy Eaton affair

Secretary of War **John Henry Eaton** married **Margaret “Peggy” O’Neale Timberlake Eaton** soon after her first husband’s death. Cabinet wives, led socially by Vice President Calhoun’s wife Floride, ostracized Peggy amid rumors of premarital adultery.

Jackson, remembering attacks on Rachel, treated Peggy Eaton’s defense as a test of loyalty. The affair divided the administration, intensified Jackson’s break with Calhoun, and contributed to a cabinet reorganization in 1831. Van Buren’s willingness to socialize with the Eatons strengthened his position as Jackson’s successor.

**Allegation:** Peggy and Eaton committed adultery before her first husband’s death.

**Evidence:** Widely circulated gossip and suspicious chronology existed, but no judicial or documentary finding established adultery.

### Internal improvements

Jackson vetoed federal subscription to the Maysville Road in 1830, arguing that a project wholly within Kentucky lacked sufficient national character. He did not oppose every road, harbor, or improvement; his policy distinguished projects he considered national from local subsidies and emphasized debt reduction.

### Indian Removal Act

Removal was Jackson’s leading domestic objective. Congress narrowly passed the **Indian Removal Act**, which Jackson signed on May 28, 1830. It authorized exchanges of eastern Indigenous land for territory west of the Mississippi and appropriated implementation funds.

Jackson publicly represented removal as voluntary, protective, and benevolent. In private and official communications, he told Native leaders that state jurisdiction was inevitable and that refusal would make them responsible for resulting harm.

The administration negotiated or enforced removal agreements affecting Choctaw, Chickasaw, Muscogee Creek, Cherokee, and Seminole peoples. Coercion included denial of federal protection against state laws, pressure on leaders, fraudulent contracting and land transactions, military force, and recognition of minority treaty factions.

Approximately 100,000 Indigenous people were displaced in the broader removal process between 1830 and 1850. The most famous Cherokee forced marches occurred under Martin Van Buren in 1838–1839, but the legal and treaty machinery was substantially created and driven by Jackson.

### Cherokee sovereignty and the Supreme Court

In **Cherokee Nation v. Georgia** (1831), the Supreme Court held that the Cherokee Nation was a “domestic dependent nation” and lacked standing as a foreign nation. In **Worcester v. Georgia** (1832), it ruled that Georgia law had no force within Cherokee territory.

Jackson did not issue the famous statement, “John Marshall has made his decision; now let him enforce it,” in any known contemporary record. A version appeared decades later, notably in Horace Greeley’s 1864 history.

A more credible contemporary formulation attributed to Jackson was that the Court’s decision had “fell still born” because it could not compel Georgia to comply.

**Documented policy:** Jackson declined to protect Cherokee sovereignty against Georgia and continued pursuing removal.

**Legal qualification:** Worcester did not directly invalidate the Indian Removal Act, and the federal government was not procedurally ordered to march troops against Georgia in that case. The popular story compresses a more complicated but still consequential refusal of executive protection.

### Nullification crisis

South Carolina declared the tariffs of 1828 and 1832 null and void within the state. Vice President Calhoun defended a theory of state interposition and nullification.

Jackson issued his **Proclamation to the People of South Carolina** on December 10, 1832, denying that a state could nullify federal law or secede unilaterally. He prepared military enforcement and supported the Force Bill while accepting Henry Clay’s compromise tariff.

The crisis ended without combat. It broke the Jackson–Calhoun alliance and established Jackson as an emphatic defender of federal union, despite his states’ rights positions in other contexts.

### Bank veto and reelection

The Second Bank of the United States, led by **Nicholas Biddle**, sought early recharter in 1832. Jackson vetoed the bill. His message combined constitutional objections, suspicion of concentrated financial power, hostility to foreign stockholders, and egalitarian rhetoric about government making “the rich richer.”

Supporters regarded the veto as resistance to monopoly and privilege. Opponents regarded it as demagoguery and an attack on a useful stabilizing institution. Jackson defeated Clay in the 1832 election, treating the result as popular ratification of the veto.

## Presidency, Second Term, 1833–1837

### Removal of federal deposits and Senate censure

Jackson ordered federal deposits removed from the Bank of the United States. Treasury secretary **Louis McLane** resisted and was moved; successor **William J. Duane** refused and was dismissed. **Roger B. Taney** carried out the policy by redirecting deposits to selected state banks.

On March 28, 1834, the Senate censured Jackson for assuming authority “not conferred by the Constitution and laws.” Jackson filed a formal protest. The Senate refused to place it in the journal. After Democrats regained control, the censure was expunged on January 16, 1837.

- **Legislative finding:** The 1834 Senate majority formally censured Jackson.
- **Later legislative action:** A different majority expunged the resolution.
- **Unresolved constitutional question:** No court adjudicated the deposit-removal dispute.

The conflict contributed to formation of the Whig Party, whose “King Andrew” imagery accused Jackson of executive usurpation.

### Financial policy, debt, and the Panic of 1837

The national debt was temporarily extinguished in January 1835—the only such episode in U.S. history. Land sales, tariff revenues, and fiscal restraint contributed.

The collapse of the national bank was followed by expansion among state-chartered banks, land speculation, and unstable credit. Jackson’s **Specie Circular** of 1836 generally required payment for public land in gold or silver. Congress also distributed the federal surplus among the states.

The Panic of 1837 began as Jackson left office and became Van Buren’s crisis.

**Disputed interpretation:** Jackson’s bank destruction, deposit distribution, and Specie Circular contributed materially to monetary instability. International specie flows, British credit contraction, cotton prices, and state-bank behavior were also major causes. Assigning the panic exclusively to Jackson is not supported.

### Indigenous removal treaties and wars

The administration recognized the **Treaty of New Echota** of December 1835, signed by a small unauthorized Cherokee minority rather than the elected government led by John Ross. More than 15,000 Cherokee protested. The Senate ratified it by one vote in May 1836; Jackson approved it and rejected Ross’s attempts to stop removal.

The treaty allowed two years for emigration. Van Buren ordered the final military roundup after the deadline, but Jackson bears direct responsibility for obtaining, submitting, and defending the disputed treaty.

The **Second Seminole War** began in 1835 after Seminole resistance to removal. Jackson’s government’s enforcement of the Treaty of Payne’s Landing and pressure to deport Seminoles and associated Black communities were central causes. The war continued until 1842.

The Muscogee Creek removals included detention, dispossession by fraudulent land purchasers, military suppression, and forced migration, sometimes in chains.

### Abolitionist mail crisis

In 1835 southern mobs and postmasters intercepted abolitionist publications sent through the U.S. mail. Postmaster General **Amos Kendall** tolerated local nondelivery. Jackson recommended federal legislation prohibiting circulation of “incendiary” antislavery material in slave states.

**Documented fact:** Jackson supported suppression and wrote Kendall that postmasters should withhold such papers except from known subscribers and record recipients’ names.

Congress did not enact Jackson’s proposed censorship law. The episode connected his defense of slavery with federal communications policy.

### Foreign affairs

Jackson’s administration:

- Settled or pursued claims against Denmark, Portugal, Naples, and Spain.
- Pressed France to pay indemnities under an 1831 treaty, at one point producing a serious diplomatic confrontation.
- Recognized or expanded relations with new Latin American states.
- Pursued trade agreements, including arrangements with Britain concerning West Indian commerce.
- Sought unsuccessfully to purchase Texas from Mexico.
- Recognized the Republic of Texas on the final full day of his presidency, after delaying recognition to avoid appearing to promote annexation during the election.

### Assassination attempt

On January 30, 1835, unemployed house painter **Richard Lawrence** attempted to shoot Jackson outside the Capitol. Both pistols misfired. Jackson attacked Lawrence with his cane before bystanders restrained them.

Lawrence believed himself entitled to the British throne and blamed Jackson for withholding money. Prosecuted by Francis Scott Key, he was found not guilty by reason of insanity and confined for the remainder of his life.

Jackson suspected a political conspiracy and focused suspicion on Senator **George Poindexter** and anti-administration forces.

- **Documented fact:** Lawrence attempted the shooting and was adjudged insane.
- **Allegation:** A Whig, bank, or Poindexter conspiracy directed him.
- **Evidence:** No reliable evidence established accomplices; the supported conclusion is that Lawrence acted alone under delusions.

Claims that the pistols’ simultaneous failure had odds of exactly 125,000 to one derive from much later testing and should be treated as illustrative rather than a scientifically reconstructable probability.

## Plantation, Slavery, Business, and Property

### The Hermitage enterprise

The Hermitage expanded into a cotton plantation of roughly 1,000 acres. Its mansion was built and remodeled over time; a major fire in 1834 damaged the house, which was reconstructed.

Jackson’s income depended on:

- Cotton produced by enslaved labor.
- Landownership.
- Horse breeding and racing.
- Commodity sales.
- Investments and credit relationships.
- Earlier mercantile and slave transactions.
- Political and legal networks that increased the value of western land.

### Enslaved population

Jackson brought approximately nine enslaved people to the original Hermitage property. By his death, he owned approximately **150** people. Research by The Hermitage has identified more than 500 enslaved persons and descendants associated with Jackson properties across generations.

Named people in the surviving record include **Hannah, George, Alfred, Betty, Gracy, Louisa, Rachel, Nancy, Gilbert**, and others. Enslaved people performed agricultural work, cooking, nursing, blacksmithing, carpentry, carriage driving, domestic service, and other skilled labor.

Jackson bought and sold human beings throughout his adult commercial life. Evidence places some transactions within interstate trading networks extending from Virginia and Tennessee toward Mississippi and New Orleans. Historians differ over whether “professional slave trader” accurately characterizes his principal occupation; there is no serious dispute that he repeatedly purchased, sold, transported, and profited from enslaved people.

### Punishment and flight

A surviving 1804 runaway advertisement offered a reward for an enslaved man’s return and “ten dollars extra, for every hundred lashes any person will give him, to the amount of three hundred.”

**Documented fact:** Jackson placed or authorized the advertisement.

The Hermitage’s records also show payments to people who captured escapees. Jackson sometimes permitted enslaved families to live in household units and allowed limited garden or livestock activity. Such paternal practices remained components of coerced ownership; they did not provide legal freedom or protection from sale and punishment.

### White House slavery

Jackson brought enslaved workers to Washington, including household servants connected with the Hermitage. He also purchased enslaved women and children in or near Washington during his presidency.

### Wealth and debt

Jackson possessed valuable land, livestock, furnishings, horses, and enslaved people but was frequently cash-poor. He had experienced near-ruin from early land and mercantile debts. During and after his presidency, Andrew Jackson Jr.’s plantation investments and undisclosed obligations placed further pressure on family finances.

Jackson sold land and used personal assets to cover debts. Thus, “wealthy planter” and “financially strained debtor” are both accurate at different levels: his net worth rested largely in land and enslaved human property rather than liquid funds.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

Jackson was not a systematic political theorist and often relied on advisers to draft polished public documents. His ideas developed from several overlapping traditions:

- Revolutionary republican suspicion of corruption and concentrated power.
- Jeffersonian agrarian and states’ rights language.
- Militant nationalism and defense of federal union.
- White male political equality.
- Executive leadership claiming direct democratic legitimacy.
- Territorial expansion.
- Defense of slavery and racial hierarchy.
- A paternal theory that represented Indigenous removal as protection while denying enduring Native sovereignty inside state boundaries.
- Suspicion of paper credit, monopoly, and privileged corporations.
- Presbyterian providentialism, especially pronounced in later life.

His program expanded democratic participation and party organization primarily for white men. It simultaneously strengthened institutions and markets built on Indigenous land cessions and enslaved labor.

Jackson’s important presidential drafts were often composed or extensively revised by **John Henry Eaton, Amos Kendall, Roger B. Taney, Edward Livingston, Andrew Jackson Donelson**, and other advisers. Authorship of a document issued over Jackson’s name therefore ranges from personal composition to collaborative or ghost-drafted state paper.

- **Winfield Scott:** Professional and political rival.

- **John Henry Eaton:** Biographer, senator, secretary of war, and loyal friend.

## Controversies, Accusations, Investigations, and Findings

### Duels and personal violence

**Established:** Jackson killed Charles Dickinson in a duel, was involved in the Benton brawl, threatened or participated in other affairs of honor, and frequently used violent language.

**Exaggerated claim:** He fought more than one hundred completed duels. The number conflates challenges, threatened encounters, brawls, and folklore.

### Military executions

**Established:** Jackson approved executions for desertion or mutiny, including John Woods and six militiamen, under military-court procedures.

**Dispute:** Whether they were lawful necessities of command or disproportionate acts designed to terrorize volunteers into obedience.

### New Orleans civil-rights violations

**Court finding:** Jackson was held in contempt and fined after arresting a critic and removing the judge who issued habeas corpus.

**Later political action:** Congress refunded the fine; that action did not erase the historical court finding.

### Florida invasion and executions

**Established:** Jackson exceeded or expansively interpreted orders, invaded Spanish territory, displaced authorities, and executed Arbuthnot and Ambrister.

**Dispute:** Monroe administration officials privately debated whether he had been authorized. Adams defended the outcome. Contemporary congressional efforts to censure him failed.

### Indigenous dispossession

**Established:** Jackson was a central military, diplomatic, and presidential agent of southeastern land cession and removal. He supported Georgia against Cherokee sovereignty, signed the Indian Removal Act, recognized coercive or minority-supported treaties, and created the policy framework for forced migrations.

**Classification dispute:** Many modern historians and public institutions describe removal as ethnic cleansing; some scholars analyze it under settler-colonial genocide. Other historians reserve “genocide” for policies demonstrating an intent to physically destroy a protected group and emphasize that removal’s formally stated objective was territorial expulsion rather than extermination. Mortality, coercion, dispossession, and destruction of political autonomy are not disputed; the legal-historical label is.

### Slavery and slave trading

**Established:** Jackson owned, bought, sold, inherited, mortgaged, transported, disciplined, and sought fugitives from slavery. His plantation and social position depended upon enslaved labor. He defended slave-state censorship interests during the abolitionist mail crisis.

**Historiographical issue:** Older biographies frequently marginalized slavery or framed Jackson chiefly as a paternal planter. Later archival scholarship has placed slavery and the domestic trade at the center of his economic and political life.

### Patronage and corruption

**Established:** Jackson linked office rotation to party loyalty and appointed officials later implicated in major theft or corruption, including Samuel Swartwout. Post Office and land-office scandals occurred under his administration.

**Not established:** That Jackson personally shared embezzled funds or organized a criminal patronage conspiracy for his own enrichment.

### Bank War and executive power

**Senate finding:** Censure in 1834 for unconstitutional assumption of authority.

**Counteraction:** Expungement in 1837 by Jacksonian senators.

**Disputed interpretation:** Democratic defense of elected executive authority and opposition to monopoly versus destabilizing destruction of institutional checks and financial governance.

### Abolitionist-mail censorship

**Established:** Jackson supported restricting abolitionist publications and endorsed local withholding practices. The proposed national ban did not become law.

### “Corrupt bargain”

**Allegation:** Adams and Clay secretly traded the presidency for the State Department.

**Evidence:** Political sequence and shared opposition to Jackson; no documentary proof of an explicit bargain.

### Burr conspiracy

**Allegation:** Jackson knowingly aided treason or western secession.

**Evidence:** Association and material assistance to Burr’s preparations are documented. Knowledge of a treasonous design is not established. Burr was acquitted.

### Assassination conspiracy

**Rumor:** Lawrence was an agent of the Bank, Whigs, Poindexter, or foreign powers.

**Finding:** No substantiating evidence; Lawrence was found insane.

### Recent adversarial and critical reassessment, 2024–2026

A targeted search of recent scholarship and reporting found no credible new criminal accusation against Jackson distinct from the established record. Recent work instead emphasizes neglected evidence and revised classifications:

- A 2024 *Humanities* survey stressed that there were multiple “Trails of Tears,” not only the Cherokee march, and called for attention to Native decision-making, regional diversity, and the limits of a single victimization narrative.
- Recent settler-colonial scholarship places Jacksonian removal within a longer Scots-Irish and U.S. program of territorial occupation and white supremacy.
- A 2024 announcement from The Hermitage reported the probable rediscovery of the burial ground of people enslaved on Jackson’s plantation. Archaeological work identified a likely cemetery zone; individual graves and identities remain under investigation.
- Contemporary legal commentary continues using Jackson and *Worcester* as an example of the judiciary’s dependence on executive compliance, while also acknowledging that the famous Marshall quotation is apocryphal.
- Current scholarship continues debating “genocide” versus “ethnic cleansing” without disputing the forced, state-sponsored character of removal.

## Health, Injuries, and Psychological Strain

Jackson’s documented or strongly reported medical history included:

- Smallpox contracted during Revolutionary captivity.
- Sword scars on his hand and head.
- Chronic digestive disease, diarrhea, and dysentery-like episodes.
- Recurrent malaria or “ague.”
- A chest wound and retained bullet from the Dickinson duel.
- A severe arm or shoulder wound from the Benton brawl.
- Chronic pain, coughing, hemorrhage, weakness, and breathing difficulty.
- Dental loss.
- Edema or “dropsy” late in life.
- Possible chronic lead poisoning from retained bullets, proposed retrospectively but not demonstrable as a complete diagnosis.
- Later descriptions of bronchiectasis, heart disease, and other chronic organ damage.

He periodically coughed blood and experienced physical collapses. Physicians repeatedly bled, purged, and medicated him according to contemporary practice.

No reliable evidence establishes an alcohol or drug addiction. Jackson drank and gambled socially in his youth and used common nineteenth-century medicines, but claims of addiction exceed the evidence.

Rachel’s death produced prolonged grief, anger, and loneliness. Jackson preserved her portrait and blamed enemies for her death. These reactions are documented; retrospective psychiatric diagnoses are speculative.

## Later Life, 1837–1845

Jackson retired to The Hermitage in March 1837 but remained the Democratic Party’s most powerful elder.

He:

- Advised Van Buren through the Panic of 1837.
- Defended the Independent Treasury and hard-money principles.
- Opposed re-creation of a national bank.
- Supported John Tyler’s bank vetoes after William Henry Harrison’s death.
- Promoted annexation of Texas.
- Broke with Van Buren when the latter opposed immediate Texas annexation.
- Helped propel James K. Polk to the 1844 Democratic nomination.
- Lived to see Polk inaugurated and annexation approved.
- Organized his papers and cooperated with projected biographies by Amos Kendall and others.
- Returned to New Orleans in 1840 for the twenty-fifth-anniversary celebration of his victory.
- Joined the Presbyterian Church late in life.

His declining finances were aggravated by Andrew Jr.’s debts and plantation investments. Visitors continued treating The Hermitage as a political shrine.

Jackson died on June 8, 1845, surrounded by household members. Contemporary descriptions attribute death to accumulated chronic disease, heart or pulmonary failure, and dropsy rather than a single acute event.

He was buried beside Rachel. His will distributed property that included enslaved human beings; he did not emancipate them.

## Myths, Legends, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “John Marshall has made his decision; now let him enforce it”

- **Story:** Jackson openly dared Chief Justice Marshall to enforce *Worcester v. Georgia*.
- **Appearance:** No known contemporary Jackson document contains it. A version appeared after Jackson’s death, notably in Horace Greeley’s 1864 history.
- **Evidence against:** Absence from contemporary records; procedural circumstances make the exact remark improbable.
- **Underlying reality:** Jackson supported Georgia, did not protect Cherokee sovereignty, and described the ruling as having “fell still born.”
- **Reputational effect:** Became the most famous shorthand for executive defiance of the Supreme Court.

### “Old Hickory”

- **Story:** Soldiers compared Jackson’s toughness first to oak and then to hickory.
- **Status:** Contemporary or near-contemporary nickname rooted in his military reputation, though the exact first utterance is uncertain.
- **Effect:** Central element in campaign songs, biographies, souvenirs, and the self-made frontier-warrior persona.

### British officer’s sword

- **Story:** Thirteen-year-old Jackson refused to clean a British officer’s boots and was slashed.
- **Evidence:** Jackson bore scars and repeatedly told the story; independent eyewitness documentation is sparse.
- **Status:** Strong autobiographical tradition, not independently reconstructable in every detail.
- **Effect:** Explained his lifelong Anglophobia and became a standard patriotic childhood episode.

### Rachel killed by campaign slander

- **Story:** Anti-Jackson attacks caused Rachel’s death.
- **Evidence:** She was distressed and had serious heart symptoms; no medical evidence can establish slander as the cause.
- **Status:** Jackson’s sincere belief and a potent hagiographic narrative, not a medical finding.
- **Effect:** Intensified the tragic-romantic image of the marriage and Jackson’s hostility to opponents.

### The inauguration mob

- **Story:** A drunken frontier multitude destroyed the White House.
- **Evidence:** A large, disorderly reception and property damage are documented; later versions exaggerated scale and barbarity.
- **Circulators:** Elite observers and anti-Jackson writers.
- **Effect:** Symbolized either mass democracy’s arrival or mob rule.

### The giant cheese

New York dairyman **Thomas S. Meacham** presented Jackson with an enormous cheese, commonly reported at approximately 1,400 pounds. Jackson opened the White House to the public in 1837 to consume it.

- **Status:** Documented event.
- **Mythic afterlife:** Became a comic emblem of Jacksonian populism and inspired “Big Block of Cheese Day” in *The West Wing* and later White House publicity.
- **Frequent confusion:** It was not the source of every earlier presidential “mammoth cheese” story; Thomas Jefferson had received a separate giant cheese in 1802.

### Jackson’s swearing parrot

- **Story:** Jackson’s parrot had to be removed from his funeral because it shouted profanities.
- **Source problem:** The familiar version comes from a later reminiscence attributed to Reverend William Menefee Norment, published many decades afterward.
- **Status:** Reported family or local tradition; not securely contemporary.
- **Effect:** Reinforces the folkloric Jackson as profane, violent, and charismatic even in death.

### Hundreds of duels

- **Story:** Jackson fought 100 or more duels and carried numerous bullets.
- **Evidence:** Many quarrels and challenges are documented, but only a smaller number of actual armed encounters can be demonstrated.
- **Status:** Exaggerated legend derived from a genuinely violent honor culture.

### “To the victor belong the spoils”

- **Story:** Jackson coined the doctrine of partisan patronage.
- **Evidence:** The famous wording belongs to William L. Marcy.
- **Underlying fact:** Jackson institutionalized rotation and partisan appointment sufficiently to make the phrase adhere to his administration.
- **Status:** Misattributed quotation attached to a substantially relevant policy.

### The providential assassination escape

- **Story:** Divine intervention caused both of Lawrence’s pistols to misfire.
- **Evidence:** Both guns did misfire; Jackson and admirers interpreted survival providentially. Later tests reportedly found the weapons functional.
- **Status:** Theological interpretation, not an empirically testable finding.
- **Effect:** Strengthened the belief among supporters that Jackson was preserved for national purposes.

### “King Andrew”

- **Story:** Jackson was a would-be monarch or Caesar.
- **Circulation:** National Republican and Whig cartoons, pamphlets, speeches, and campaign propaganda.
- **Evidence:** Jackson enlarged presidential power and claimed direct popular authority; he also left office at the constitutional limit then established by custom and submitted to elections.
- **Status:** Hostile political metaphor based on real constitutional conflict, not a literal plan for monarchy.

## Works by or Under the Name of Andrew Jackson

Jackson was a prolific correspondent and issuer of official documents, not the sole literary author of conventional books. Many state papers under his name were collaboratively drafted.

### Contemporary and official writings

- Congressional speeches and motions, 1796–1797.
- Military orders, court-martial approvals, campaign reports, and correspondence, especially 1812–1821.
- Journals and documents from Indigenous treaty negotiations, 1816 and 1818.
- Florida territorial proclamations and administrative correspondence, 1821.
- Presidential inaugural addresses, 1829 and 1833.
- Annual Messages to Congress, 1829–1836.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Jackson’s reputation has passed through several overlapping forms.

### Democratic hero

Nineteenth-century Democrats presented him as the self-made soldier-president who defeated Britain, broke elite privilege, defended the Union, and enlarged popular rule. Democratic organizations held commemorations, displayed hickory poles, and treated The Hermitage as a shrine.

### Constitutional danger

Whigs and later critics portrayed him as an executive usurper whose vetoes, deposit removal, patronage, martial-law record, and personal violence weakened legal restraint.

### National military icon

The Battle of New Orleans became a national patriotic legend, celebrated through songs, anniversaries, prints, biographies, and films. Jackson Square in New Orleans and Clark Mills’s equestrian statues monumentalized this image.

### Symbol of removal and slavery

During the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, public attention shifted toward the experiences of Cherokee, Muscogee, Seminole, Choctaw, Chickasaw, and enslaved people. Jackson increasingly became a symbol of forced removal, settler expansion, slaveholding power, and racially bounded democracy.

### Currency

Jackson has appeared on the U.S. $20 Federal Reserve note since 1928. His placement is historically ironic because of his war on the national bank and suspicion of paper money. Proposals to place Harriet Tubman on a redesigned $20 have made the note a focal point in disputes over commemoration. Announced designs have contemplated retaining Jackson imagery elsewhere on the note, but implementation has repeatedly been delayed.

### Monuments and protest

Equestrian Jackson statues stand in Washington, D.C., New Orleans, Nashville, and Jacksonville. The Lafayette Square statue, dedicated in 1853, became a protest site, including an attempted toppling in June 2020. Disputes concern whether monuments commemorate New Orleans and unionism or glorify removal and slaveholding.

### Hermitage reinterpretation

The Hermitage was long interpreted primarily as the home of a military hero and president. Its current scholarship gives far greater attention to the hundreds of enslaved people connected to the plantation. The reported 2024 rediscovery of their cemetery represents an important change in whose lives the site commemorates.

### Political appropriation

Jackson has been claimed by:

- Democrats emphasizing popular organization and economic anti-monopoly.
- Unionists invoking the Nullification Proclamation.
- Presidents defending strong executive power.
- Populists attacking banks and political establishments.
- Critics using Jackson as a warning about demagoguery, ethnic expulsion, and executive defiance.
- Modern conservative politicians, notably Donald Trump, who displayed Jackson’s portrait and invoked his populist image.
- Indigenous activists and historians who treat his name and image as symbols of state-sponsored removal.

These appropriations often select one Jacksonian strand while ignoring contradictory ones.

## Compact Chronology

- **1765:** Andrew and Elizabeth Jackson migrate from County Antrim to the Waxhaws.
- **March 15, 1767:** Andrew Jackson born shortly after his father’s death.
- **1779:** Brother Hugh dies following the Stono Ferry campaign.
- **1781:** Andrew and Robert captured; Andrew scarred; both contract smallpox; Robert and their mother die later that year.
- **1784–1787:** Reads law in Salisbury under Spruce Macay and John Stokes.
- **1787:** Admitted to the North Carolina bar.
- **1788:** Moves west as public prosecutor for the Mero District; settles in Nashville.
- **1791:** Begins living as Rachel Robards’s husband under a legally defective marriage.
- **January 1794:** Andrew and Rachel marry after Robards’s divorce.
- **1796:** Tennessee constitutional delegate; elected first U.S. representative from Tennessee.
- **1797:** Enters U.S. Senate.
- **1798:** Resigns Senate seat; becomes Tennessee Superior Court judge.
- **1802:** Elected major general of Tennessee militia.
- **1803:** Near-duel and political rupture with John Sevier.
- **1804:** Resigns judgeship; financial reverses; acquires The Hermitage.
- **May 30, 1806:** Kills Charles Dickinson in duel and receives permanent chest wound.
- **1806–1807:** Assists Aaron Burr’s preparations; later testifies at Burr’s trial.
- **1808:** Takes Andrew Jackson Jr. into household.
- **1812–1813:** Natchez expedition and return march.
- **September 1813:** Severely wounded in Benton brawl.
- **November 1813:** Creek campaign; Tallushatchee and Talladega.
- **March 27, 1814:** Victory at Horseshoe Bend.
- **August 9, 1814:** Imposes Treaty of Fort Jackson, acquiring approximately 23 million acres.
- **November 1814:** Captures Pensacola.
- **January 8, 1815:** Defeats British assault at New Orleans.
- **March 1815:** Arrests Louis Louaillier and Judge Hall; later fined for contempt.
- **1816–1818:** Conducts major Indigenous treaty negotiations.
- **1818:** Invades Spanish Florida; orders executions of Arbuthnot and Ambrister.
- **1819:** Adams–Onís Treaty provides for Florida’s transfer.
- **1821:** Briefly serves as governor of Florida Territory.
- **1823:** Returns to U.S. Senate; presidential candidacy develops.
- **1824:** Wins electoral and popular pluralities but loses contingent election to John Quincy Adams.
- **1825:** Resigns Senate seat and organizes opposition.
- **1828:** Defeats Adams; Rachel dies December 22.
- **March 4, 1829:** Inaugurated president.
- **1830:** Vetoes Maysville Road bill; signs Indian Removal Act; issues removal message.
- **1831:** *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia*; Eaton affair contributes to cabinet reorganization.
- **1832:** *Worcester v. Georgia*; vetoes Bank recharter; reelected; issues Nullification Proclamation.
- **1833:** Force Bill and compromise tariff end acute nullification crisis; federal deposits removed from national bank.
- **March 28, 1834:** Senate censures Jackson.
- **1834:** Hermitage mansion damaged by fire; administration confronts removal fraud and bank crisis.
- **January 1835:** National debt temporarily extinguished.
- **January 30, 1835:** Survives Richard Lawrence assassination attempt.
- **1835:** Supports suppression of abolitionist mail; Treaty of New Echota signed by Cherokee minority; Second Seminole War begins.
- **1836:** Treaty of New Echota ratified by one Senate vote; Specie Circular issued; Creek removals intensify.
- **January 16, 1837:** Senate expunges censure.
- **March 1837:** Leaves presidency; Van Buren succeeds him.
- **1837–1839:** Panic and depression; Cherokee forced migration implemented under Van Buren.
- **1840:** Visits New Orleans for victory anniversary.
- **1841:** Supports John Tyler’s national-bank vetoes.
- **1844:** Helps displace Van Buren and secure James K. Polk’s nomination; Congress refunds New Orleans fine.
- **1845:** Sees Polk inaugurated and Texas annexation approved; dies June 8 at The Hermitage.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by sources or the established documentary record are included. Mere chronological overlap has been excluded.

### Thomas Jefferson — documented fact

Jackson and Jefferson served in national politics during overlapping periods, corresponded, and shared elements of Republican political language. Surviving correspondence includes Jackson’s letters of August 3, 1803, December 8, 1823, and May 31, 1825, and Jefferson’s March 21, 1807 letter to Jackson. Jefferson’s 1823 response recalled their “joint labors” in the Senate era and invited continued contact.

Their relationship was not simple discipleship. Jackson defended Aaron Burr after Jefferson’s administration prosecuted him. During the 1824–1828 conflict, rival factions circulated contradictory reports of Jefferson’s opinion of Jackson, including Daniel Webster’s recollection that Jefferson considered him dangerously temperamental. The reliability and precise wording of those retrospective reports are disputed.

### Alexis-Charles-Henri Clérel de Tocqueville — documented fact

During Tocqueville and Gustave de Beaumont’s 1831–1832 American journey, they visited Washington and met President Jackson. Tocqueville observed Jacksonian political and social life while gathering material that informed *De la démocratie en Amérique*. The connection was a documented meeting and observation, not a friendship or collaboration.

### Abraham Lincoln — documented influence; no personal meeting

Lincoln was twenty when Jackson became president and there is no evidence they met. As a Whig, Lincoln operated within a party created partly in opposition to Jacksonian executive politics. During the secession crisis, however, Lincoln and his advisers studied or invoked Jackson’s 1832 Nullification Proclamation. Scholarship has identified Jackson’s unionist reasoning and rhetoric as an influence on Lincoln’s First Inaugural Address.

### George Washington — documented institutional overlap and correspondence; personal meeting unestablished

Jackson entered the House during Washington’s final presidential year and wrote concerning a Tennessee judicial appointment. Jackson also voted against a congressional resolution praising Washington’s administration. No reliable source consulted establishes a personal meeting. Later Jacksonian commemoration deliberately positioned New Orleans and Jackson’s farewell address alongside Washingtonian national mythology.

### Alexander Hamilton — documented ideological and institutional legacy; no personal connection established

Jackson’s Bank War took place against the inherited Hamiltonian model of federally supported public finance and concentrated fiscal administration. Jacksonians represented the Second Bank as part of a privileged system descended from Hamilton. No evidence consulted establishes a meeting or correspondence; Hamilton died in 1804 before Jackson became nationally prominent.

### Napoleon Bonaparte — hostile comparison and political mythology

Jackson’s opponents compared him to Napoleon or Caesar to depict a military hero converting popularity into executive despotism. “American Napoleon” was hostile analogy and propaganda, not evidence of a meeting or acknowledged personal influence.

### Albert Pike — no supported direct connection found

Pike lived in Arkansas during Jackson’s presidency and later became prominent in law, politics, and Freemasonry. The sources consulted did not establish a meeting, correspondence, command relationship, or documented influence between Pike and Jackson. Later conspiratorial literature sometimes places both inside sweeping Masonic lineages, but that does not demonstrate a personal connection.

### Other roster findings

No reliable direct intersections were found with the overwhelming majority of the supplied atlas roster. Shared nationality, political era, symbolic comparison, later commentary, or residence in the same broad region was not treated as a connection without documentary support.
