# Viola Farber: Research Dossier

**Prepared:** 24 August 2026  
**Scope:** Documentary reconstruction of Viola Farber’s life, work, networks, institutions, reception, and surviving record, 1931–1998, with posthumous legacy.  
**Name note:** Sources consistently use **Viola Farber**. Genealogical indexes give **Viola Anna Farber**; some records and press indexes use **Viola Farber-Slayton** during and after her marriage. One enthusiast site reverses the names as “Anna Viola Faber” and misspells the surname; this is not corroborated by institutional records.

## Research status and evidentiary limits

Farber left a substantial performance record but no published autobiography and no known unified personal archive readily searchable online. The documentary base is dispersed among the Merce Cunningham Dance Foundation records and choreography database, the New York Public Library’s Dance Division and recorded-moving-image holdings, Black Mountain College archives, Sarah Lawrence College records, Robert Rauschenberg Foundation records, filmed performances, oral histories, contemporary criticism, and Jeff Slayton’s privately published biography. Her career is therefore far better documented than her household finances, extended ancestry, private relationships, or childhood.

Several chronological conflicts recur in reference literature:

- The Merce Cunningham Dance Company is sometimes dated to 1952, but its own archive and the NYPL place the first company program at Black Mountain College in August **1953**.
- Farber’s company is called founded in **1968**, **1969**, or operational from **1970**. These are reconcilable as stages: initial choreography/company formation in 1968–69, a named and sustained New York company by 1970.
- The Angers appointment is described as **1981–83**, **1981–84**, or “three years.” The strongest contemporary recollections place the final Farber-company engagement in summer 1983 and her departure before the first semester of 1984; an institutional directorship list may count the transition through 1984.
- Sarah Lawrence notices variously say she arrived or became chair in **1987** or **1988**. The college magazine records a new-faculty notice in fall/winter 1987 and a chair profile in spring/summer 1988.
- The French honor is sometimes described as posthumous, but Sarah Lawrence’s 1998 magazine index records her as an **Officier de l’Ordre des Arts et des Lettres** before her death. The likely explanation is that the award was announced or recorded in 1998 and publicized again after her death, not that it was first conferred posthumously.

Where no reliable evidence was found, this dossier says so rather than inferring.

## Family and Ancestry

Farber was born in Heidelberg to **Eduard Farber** and **Dora Schmidt Farber**. [Genealogical search results](https://www.ancestry.com/genealogy/records/results?firstName=viola&lastName=farber) supply the mother’s maiden name; Jeff Slayton’s biographical materials name two sisters, **Elisabeth** and **Irene**. No reliable source consulted gives the sisters’ full vital histories.

Eduard Farber was a chemist and historian of chemistry. A biographical summary credits him with industrial research, consulting, about eighty-five U.S. patents, roughly fifty industrial-chemistry papers, and historical writing. This matters to Viola Farber’s formation: the household joined scientific, musical, and historical interests rather than emerging from a theatrical family alone. Dorothea Rockburne remembered that Farber was an accomplished pianist and that her family performed as a quartet.

The family’s migration was forced by Nazism. [Slayton reports](https://www.ladancechronicle.com/dance-history-needs-to-remember-viola-farber-1931-1998/) that Eduard was Jewish and that his employer helped the family leave Germany in 1938, shortly before emigration became still more difficult. They first spent time in New Haven, Connecticut, then settled in the Washington, D.C., area. This is documentary family history, not a later metaphor: Farber was a seven-year-old refugee from Nazi Germany. The consulted sources do not identify the employer, vessel, immigration category, exact arrival date, or whether Dora and the children were categorized as Jewish under Nazi law. Those remain research gaps.

No reliable evidence was located for Farber’s grandparents, earlier ancestry, family wealth in Germany, inheritance, or property lost in emigration. No source located describes her as observantly Jewish or assigns her a mature religious affiliation.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1931–1948

Farber began dancing as a small child in Heidelberg. A recurrent story says her parents stopped formal dance lessons when she was about six, after which she danced privately and concentrated on piano. The story is reproduced in standard biographical summaries but is not independently documented in the open archival sources consulted. It is plausible and consistent with Rockburne’s recollection of her musicianship.

In 1938 the family escaped Germany. After a brief New Haven period it settled near Washington, D.C. Farber grew up there and studied with Washington modern-dance teacher **Erika Thimey**. She also pursued piano seriously. A secondary German-language biography says she became a U.S. citizen in 1944; no naturalization document was located.

The record does not support claims of childhood imprisonment, internment, abuse, addiction, or political activity. Her childhood crisis was displacement from Nazi Germany; the particulars of persecution beyond her father’s Jewish status and the family’s flight remain incompletely documented.

## Education and Formation, c. 1948–1953

### University of Illinois and George Washington University

Farber spent about a year at the **University of Illinois**, studying music and taking dance with **Margaret Erlanger**. She then transferred to **George Washington University**, combining music and dance. Degree completion at either institution has not been established.

### Black Mountain College

Farber encountered a photograph of **Merce Cunningham** dancing with **Anneliese Widman** and, according to Slayton, decided to transfer to **Black Mountain College** in North Carolina. The Black Mountain College Museum dates her student residence to 1951 and her return with the Cunningham group to 1953.

At Black Mountain she studied dance principally with **Katherine Litz**, encountered Cunningham’s teaching, and studied music in the experimental environment associated with **Lou Harrison**, **John Cage**, and **David Tudor**. Slayton also names poet **Charles Olson** among her teachers. In a [2002 oral-history interview](https://www.blackmountaincollege.org/1-1-dorothea-rockburne-interview/), Rockburne, briefly Farber’s roommate, remembered that both initially studied with Litz rather than Cunningham and described Litz’s movement as shimmering and irregular, “like a broken butterfly.”

Black Mountain placed music, visual art, poetry, theater, and movement in daily contact. Farber’s peers and collaborators included Rockburne, **Robert Rauschenberg**, **Cy Twombly**, Remy Charlip, Cage, Tudor, and Cunningham. Sources associate her with Cage’s 1952 **Theater Piece No. 1**, later mythologized as the first “happening.” The event’s status and exact participant actions vary across recollections, but Farber’s presence in the Black Mountain circle is well supported.

Her mature work retained several lessons from this formation:

- dance and music could coexist without illustration;
- silence could function as a compositional environment;
- visual design, electronic sound, and movement could be made independently and joined in performance;
- performers could have sharply individual movement identities;
- humor, awkwardness, and discontinuity could be compositional materials.

## Early Career and the Merce Cunningham Dance Company, 1953–1965

### Founding company member

The Cunningham company’s first program took place at Black Mountain College on **21 August 1953**. The [Cunningham Trust’s production record for *Banjo*](https://www.mercecunningham.org/the-work/choreography/banjo) documents an original cast of Carolyn Brown, Remy Charlip, Cunningham, Farber, Jo Anne Melsher, and Paul Taylor; David Tudor played Louis Moreau Gottschalk’s music. Farber thus belongs to the founding generation of the company, not merely to a later repertory cast.

She remained for approximately thirteen years, through 1965. The early company operated with little money and limited institutional security. Dancers rehearsed and toured while teaching and taking other work. Farber taught at **Adelphi University** (reported 1959–67), the **Cunningham Studio** (reported 1961–69), and later **Bennington College** (1967–68). She continued ballet study with **Margaret Craske** and **Alfred Corvino** and retained links to Erika Thimey.

### Roles and repertory

Farber originated or became closely associated with roles in Cunningham works including:

- *Banjo* (1953)
- *Dime a Dance* (1953)
- *Minutiae* (1954)
- *Suite for Five in Space and Time* / *Suite for Five* (1956–57 version history)
- *Nocturnes* (1956)
- *Changeling* (1957)
- *Summerspace* (1958)
- *Antic Meet* (1958), including “Sports & Diversions #3”
- *Rune* (1959)
- *Crises* (1960)
- *Suite de Danses* (1961)
- *Aeon* (1961)
- *Field Dances* (1963)
- *Story* (1963)
- *Winterbranch* (1964)
- *Cross Currents* (1964)
- *Paired* (1964)
- Cunningham “Events,” including the 24 June 1964 Museum Event in Vienna

The Cunningham Trust’s [centennial reconstruction materials](https://www.mercecunningham.org/activities/night-of-100-solos-all-performances/) preserve specific Farber solos from *Antic Meet*, *Summerspace*, *Rune*, *Field Dances*, and *Story*. This is unusually concrete evidence of her choreographic embodiment: later dancers learned movement identified not only by the work but by “Viola Farber” and short archival cues such as “determined” and “walk and think.”

### Stage identity

Contemporaries consistently describe Farber as forceful, angular, alert, musical, comic, and individual. She was not a neutral executor. By the time she left, she and Carolyn Brown were the company’s leading women. Her compact power and abrupt changes of direction became integral to the look of early Cunningham choreography.

### John Cage

Cage was Cunningham’s musical director and life partner, and Farber worked inside the Cage–Cunningham principle that choreography and score could be composed independently. Her association was practical as well as aesthetic. She was one of the pianists in Cage’s 9–10 September 1963 presentation of Erik Satie’s **Vexations**, an approximately eighteen-hour relay performance. Accounts often call her the only woman among the pianists; the exact roster should be checked against the event program before treating that detail as definitive.

Farber later used silence, new scores, electronic sound, and classical music without treating any one approach as dogma. Cage’s influence was therefore structural rather than a requirement to imitate Cagean chance operations.

### Other performing work

Farber also danced for Katherine Litz, notably in Litz’s *Dracula* (1959), and worked with Paul Taylor’s early group. This complicates a simple “Cunningham dancer” identity: her early performing life crossed the small, overlapping experimental-dance networks of 1950s New York.

### Departure in 1965

Farber left the Cunningham company in 1965. The consulted open sources do not document a scandal, firing, or permanent feud. The evidence points to artistic independence and the transition from interpreter to choreographer. She later returned to Cunningham repertory, including a 1970 revival of *Crises*, and remained part of the company’s oral-history and reconstruction community.

## Chronological Life History After Cunningham

### 1965–1969: transition to choreography

After leaving Cunningham, Farber continued teaching and performing while making dances. Her earliest independent works include *Excerpt* (1968), *Duet for Mirjam and Jeff*, *Quota*, *Passage*, and *Standby* (listed as 1969). Jeff Slayton recalled that she asked him to dance in her work in 1969; he left Cunningham in 1970 because her movement felt more natural to him.

The “founding date” of the Viola Farber Dance Company depends on definition. Black Mountain College and several reference works say 1968; Slayton says she started the company in 1969; press summaries describe it as active from 1970. The safest reconstruction is: independent choreography and a nascent ensemble began in 1968, company formation occurred in 1969, and sustained operations under the company name were visible by 1970.

### 1970–1974: Viola Farber Dance Company

The company developed amid New York’s underfunded experimental dance ecology. It maintained a studio at Broadway and Bleecker Street and toured in the United States and Europe. Dancers included, at different times, **Jeff Slayton, Mirjam Berns, Willi Feuer, June Finch, Susan Matheke, Anne Koren, Larry Clark, Jumay Chu, Michael Cichetti, Ed Henry, Karen Levey, Joël Leucht, Andé Peck**, and others.

Works from the period include *Tendency*, *Area Code*, *Curriculum*, *Co-Op*, and *Mildred* (1970); *Survey* and *Patience* (1971); *Default*, *Route 6*, *Dune*, and *Poor Eddie* (1972); *Soup* and *Spare Change* (1973); and *Willi I*, *Some of the Symptoms*, *Dinosaur Parts*, *No Super, No Boiler*, *Defendant*, and *Houseguest* (1974).

The dances used difficult partnering, abrupt attacks, off-center balances, pedestrian action, collisions, and a dry theatricality. Some early reviewers perceived a violent or sadomasochistic atmosphere; Christopher Caines’s encyclopedia entry summarizes this as “dark, violent intensity” interrupted by tenderness. This is critical reception, not evidence of abuse within the company.

Farber also made site-responsive work in 1974 at the **New York Botanical Garden** and the **Staten Island Ferry** waiting area. Such projects placed her alongside broader 1970s experiments in moving dance outside the proscenium.

### Marriage to Jeff Slayton

Farber married dancer **Jeff Slayton** in 1971. They were artistic partners as well as spouses; Slayton was one of the defining performers of her repertory and later its principal memoirist and restager. Sources date the end of the marriage to 1980, although the exact legal divorce date was not located. They continued to work together afterward, including in France. There is no reliable evidence in the consulted record of children, domestic violence, litigation, or a public marital scandal.

Because Slayton wrote the only full biography, his testimony is indispensable but interested. *The Prickly Rose* incorporates letters, journals, interviews, and company experience, yet it is an ex-spouse’s reconstruction and was self-published. Claims found only there are labeled accordingly in this dossier.

### 1971 Paris prize

Farber and Slayton won a gold medal or Grand Prix at the **Ninth International Dance Festival in Paris** in 1971. Secondary accounts do not consistently state the exact institutional title or category. The award helped establish her unusually strong French reception.

### 1975–1980: mature New York period

Works include *Motorcycle/Boat*, *Night Shade*, and *Duet for Willi and Susan* (1975); *Five Works for Sneakers*, *Some Things I Can Remember*, and *Sunday Afternoon* (1976); *Brazos River*, *Lead Us Not Into Penn Station*, and *Solo* (1977); *Turf*, *Doublewalk*, *Private Relations*, *Dandelion*, and *Local* (1978); *Duet*, *Ledge*, and *Tide* (1979); and *Tracks* and *Bright Stream* (1980).

This repertory expanded her palette. *Night Shade* used Beethoven’s “Moonlight” Sonata; other dances were silent or used commissioned music. *Sunday Afternoon* and *Private Relations* were described as more relaxed than the tense early work. The title *Lead Us Not Into Penn Station* exemplifies her verbal humor, but the dance should not be reduced to its title.

#### *Brazos River* (1977)

*Brazos River* was a major media collaboration produced for television by the Fort Worth Art Museum and KERA, Dallas/Fort Worth. The [Rauschenberg Foundation performance history](https://www.rauschenbergfoundation.org/performance-history) identifies Farber as choreographer, **Robert Rauschenberg** as set-and-costume designer, **David Tudor** as composer of the electronic score, and **Dan Parr** as director. The cast comprised members of the Viola Farber Dance Company. It was previewed at Leo Castelli Gallery in New York and Galerie Ileana Sonnabend in Paris. The project reunited Black Mountain relationships in a new technological format and is one of the clearest examples of her work in film/video rather than only onstage.

Rauschenberg also designed costumes for *Dandelion* (1978) and *Tracks* (1980); the Rauschenberg Foundation flags parts of the premiere data for those works as unconfirmed.

### Financing and company structure

The Viola Farber Dance Company was an artist-led nonprofit-scale touring ensemble, not a large commercial business. Available records and recollections point to grants, university bookings, festival engagements, teaching revenue, and touring fees. Farber received National Endowment for the Arts support and a Guggenheim Fellowship (reported for 1985), but a complete grant ledger was not found.

No reliable balance sheets, salaries, tax filings, real-estate holdings, investments, or net-worth estimates were located. There is no evidence of patents, equity investments, money laundering, corporate fraud, or unusual financial structures. The meaningful financial history is precarity: sustaining a modern-dance company through teaching, grants, touring, and institutional appointments.

### 1981–1983/84: Centre national de danse contemporaine, Angers

The French Ministry of Culture appointed Farber to succeed **Alwin Nikolais** as director of the **Centre national de danse contemporaine (CNDC)** in Angers in 1981. The center combined a permanent performing company, choreographic production, and professional training. Farber assembled a mixed American–French ensemble and instituted teacher training.

Her Angers circle included **Douglas Nielsen, Claire Verlet, Didier Deschamps**, and young artists who became central to French contemporary dance, including **Mathilde Monnier, Angelin Preljocaj, François Verret**, and **Jean-François Duroure**. Exact claims that she “discovered” any one of them should be treated cautiously; she provided training, employment, and institutional visibility within a larger French dance network. [Numeridanse’s CNDC record](https://numeridanse.com/en/publication/classe-au-cndc/) confirms the post-Farber interim run by Nielsen, Verlet, and Deschamps.

Works associated with the French period include *Bequest* (1981) and later company repertory not consistently cataloged online. Slayton identifies an Arles festival engagement in summer 1983 as the final performance of Farber’s French company. Numeridanse states that she had left the directorship prematurely before the first semester of 1984 and that senior members temporarily managed the center. French institutional chronologies sometimes list her through 1984. The departure was therefore real and somewhat abrupt, but the consulted sources do not document misconduct, dismissal for cause, litigation, or a political purge. “Prematurely” describes timing, not an established scandal.

### 1984–1987: London and independent work

Farber taught at the **London Contemporary Dance School/The Place** from roughly 1984 to 1987. In 1985 she formally dissolved the Viola Farber Dance Company. She used a Guggenheim Fellowship that year for collaborative work, according to Slayton’s account. She also made photographs; Sarah Lawrence’s magazine indexed an exhibition, *Newfane Flea Market*, in winter 1985.

### 1987/88–1998: Sarah Lawrence College

Farber joined Sarah Lawrence in 1987 and succeeded the influential **Bessie Schönberg** as head/chair of dance. College records profile her as new chair in 1988. She led the program for approximately a decade, received tenure by 1995, and continued international teaching and choreography.

Her pedagogy emphasized compositional problem-solving, detailed attention, individuality, and embodied knowledge rather than reproduction of a fixed style. **Pam Tanowitz**, later a major choreographer, has repeatedly said [Farber “changed my life”](https://www.dancereflections-vancleefarpels.com/en/pam-tanowitzs-choreographic-career-blooming) and retaught her how to think about dance. Farber’s influence also passed through generations of Cunningham-based and European contemporary-dance teaching.

Late projects included:

- *Ainsi de suite* (1992), made with Mathilde Monnier and presented in Avignon, Paris, and Brest;
- teaching in France and at the Vienna dance festival;
- participation in Cunningham oral-history and reconstruction events;
- a return to performance with **Ralph Lemon** in *Threestep (Shipwreck)* at the Joyce Theater in 1995;
- appearances in the late-1990s intergenerational project *From the Horse’s Mouth* (reported in reference sources, though exact 1998 performance documentation should be checked against program archives).

The 1995 Lemon duet is important to her late image: she re-entered public performance as an older body with a long movement history, rather than only administering or teaching dance.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Politics

Farber’s childhood was directly shaped by fascist racial persecution and forced migration. Nevertheless, no evidence was found that she joined a political party, held political office, worked for an intelligence service, financed political groups, or participated in political violence. Her appointment by the French Ministry of Culture made her part of state cultural policy, but not a party functionary.

Her work’s institutional politics were those of postwar experimental art: collective production, dancer individuality, nonhierarchical stage space, public subsidy, and the creation of training structures. Interpreting these as a specific partisan program would go beyond the evidence.

### Religion and spirituality

Eduard Farber was Jewish, and the family fled Nazi Germany. Farber’s own observance is undocumented. A Meher Baba enthusiast site places her among “his close ones,” but the consulted institutional biographies, obituaries, and Slayton summaries do not establish discipleship, conversion, or sustained involvement. This should be treated as an unverified association pending letters, diaries, or records from a Meher Baba archive.

### Artistic development

Farber’s artistic course can be schematized without imposing a moral narrative:

1. **Music-centered childhood:** piano and family chamber music gave her strong rhythmic and structural awareness.
2. **Black Mountain interdisciplinarity:** dance, poetry, painting, experimental music, and event structure were treated as neighboring practices.
3. **Cunningham performance:** she learned exacting coordination, independent musical time, spatial complexity, and performer agency.
4. **Early choreography:** tense partnering, abruptness, discontinuity, tenderness, aggression, and black humor.
5. **1970s expansion:** site work, commissioned scores, silence, classical music, video, and less continuously febrile social atmospheres.
6. **European institutional period:** training and repertory creation within a state-supported national center.
7. **Late pedagogy:** composition and attention became central; her legacy increasingly resided in dancers and students rather than a continuously touring company.

Farber was not simply a Cunningham imitator. She inherited Cunningham’s separation of arts but created more overt interpersonal friction, theatrical titles, unstable partnering, and emotional ambiguity.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Merce Cunningham

Cunningham was teacher, employer, choreographer, and the person whose image drew Farber to Black Mountain. He made roles around her distinctive physical intelligence. Her thirteen years in his company helped define his early repertory; his institution gave her a technical and professional base. She left without a documented public rupture and later returned to the repertory.

### John Cage

Cage provided the musical and philosophical environment of the Cunningham company and Black Mountain. Farber participated in Cage-related events, including *Vexations*. Her later willingness to use silence and autonomous sound reflects this formation, while her use of Beethoven and dramatic interpersonal structures shows independence from Cagean orthodoxy.

### Carolyn Brown

Brown and Farber were the two defining women of early Cunningham. They shared founding repertory but projected different stage identities. No evidence was found of a personal feud; narratives that cast them as rivals are mostly retrospective comparisons of style.

### Robert Rauschenberg

Friendship began at Black Mountain and became professional collaboration in *Brazos River*, *Dandelion*, and *Tracks*. Rauschenberg’s records also preserve the later Sarah Lawrence Viola Farber residency program.

### David Tudor

Tudor was a Black Mountain and Cunningham musician and later created the electronic score for *Brazos River*. The relationship linked Farber’s earliest experimental-music environment to her mature company.

### Katherine Litz

Litz was Farber’s consequential early dance teacher at Black Mountain and later employed her in *Dracula*. Her eccentric humor and unstable movement quality offer a second lineage beside Cunningham.

### Jeff Slayton

Slayton was dancer, spouse, artistic partner, later ex-spouse, biographer, archivist, and restager. His testimony preserves otherwise unavailable company history. It must be read both as eyewitness evidence and as a subjective reconstruction.

### Mathilde Monnier and the French network

Farber trained and employed artists who became leaders of France’s “nouvelle danse.” Her later collaboration with Monnier demonstrates a relationship that moved from teacher/director to cross-generational peer exchange.

### Ralph Lemon

Lemon’s *Threestep (Shipwreck)* returned Farber to the stage in 1995 and placed her Cunningham-era body in dialogue with a younger choreographer’s work.

### Pam Tanowitz

Tanowitz’s repeated testimony is among the clearest markers of Farber’s posthumous pedagogical influence. Farber encouraged compositional imagination rather than imitation; Tanowitz carries that approach into twenty-first-century choreography.

## Illness, Injury, Psychological Crisis, and Death

No reliable public record was found of addiction, psychiatric hospitalization, chronic illness, or career-ending dance injury. This absence should not be converted into a claim that none existed; it means the consulted sources do not document them.

In late November 1998 Farber suffered **two cerebral hemorrhages** and entered a coma. In his [documentary remembrance](https://www.ladancechronicle.com/dance-history-needs-to-remember-viola-farber-1931-1998/), Jeff Slayton says he remained at her bedside during the month. She died on **24 December 1998** in Bronxville, New York. The immediate medical mechanism is reported as cerebral hemorrhage; no autopsy report, hospital record, underlying vascular diagnosis, or death certificate was consulted.

She was 67 and still chairing the Sarah Lawrence dance program. Her death was therefore sudden in professional terms, even though it followed a month-long coma.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

No substantiated record was found of criminal charges, civil litigation, fraud, corruption, abuse, money laundering, intelligence work, political violence, or professional discipline involving Farber.

Three matters require careful distinction:

1. **Violence in the choreography.** Critics described some early dances as dark, violent, or sadomasochistic. This is an interpretation of stage imagery, not an allegation of offstage violence.
2. **Angers departure.** Numeridanse calls her departure premature. No accessible evidence establishes dismissal for cause, financial irregularity, or scandal. The matter remains administratively underdocumented.
3. **“Prickly” temperament.** Slayton’s biography and later reminiscences portray Farber as exacting, blunt, difficult, funny, and generous. These are personality testimonies, not findings of mistreatment. No documented pattern of abuse was located.

Financial transparency is limited because company accounts were not found online. Lack of records is not evidence of wrongdoing.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The prickly rose”

1. **Story:** A critic supposedly compared Farber to a prickly rose, capturing beauty combined with sharpness.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** Farber told Slayton about the phrase in or around 1995; he used it as the title of his 2006 biography and repeated it in 2020.
3. **Evidence:** Slayton searched but could not identify the critic or article.
4. **Assessment:** Unverified quotation; verified self-description/recollected story.
5. **Effect:** It became the dominant compact myth of her personality and risks replacing analysis of her choreography with temperament lore.

### “Founding member in 1952”

1. **Story:** Farber helped found the Cunningham company in 1952.
2. **Circulation:** Obituaries and short biographies sometimes give 1952.
3. **Evidence:** Cunningham and NYPL records place the company’s inaugural program at Black Mountain on 21 August 1953. Cunningham, Cage, and the dancers were already collaborating before that date.
4. **Assessment:** 1952 describes the pre-company network; 1953 is the documented company founding.
5. **Effect:** The earlier date emphasizes continuity with Cage’s 1952 Black Mountain event but blurs institutional chronology.

### “First happening” participant

1. **Story:** Farber participated in Cage’s *Theater Piece No. 1*, the first Happening.
2. **Circulation:** Black Mountain histories and exhibition catalogs.
3. **Evidence:** Participant memories and college histories support her presence in the circle; the event’s exact score, actions, and even date have been reconstructed differently.
4. **Assessment:** Participation is plausible and widely accepted; “the first Happening” is a later art-historical label, not a title Cage used at the time.
5. **Effect:** It places Farber at a canonical origin point of performance art, sometimes overshadowing her own choreography.

### “Only female pianist” in *Vexations*

1. **Story:** Farber was the sole woman in Cage’s 1963 relay performance of Satie’s *Vexations*.
2. **Circulation:** Biographical reference summaries.
3. **Evidence:** Her participation is well established; roster details should be verified against the original program.
4. **Assessment:** Likely but not independently confirmed for this dossier.
5. **Effect:** It reinforces the image of Farber as a musician-dancer inside a male experimental-music network.

### “Forgotten modern-dance legend”

1. **Story:** Farber was once a legend/icon but has been erased from dance history.
2. **Circulation:** Slayton’s biography and L.A. Dance Chronicle essays, especially “Dance History Needs to Remember Viola Farber.”
3. **Evidence:** Her repertory is seldom staged and no major institutional biography followed her death; conversely, NYPL holdings, Cunningham reconstructions, French institutional memory, Sarah Lawrence programs, scholarship, and a contemporary award preserve her name.
4. **Assessment:** “Forgotten” is relative: public visibility declined, specialist memory persisted.
5. **Effect:** The narrative has motivated archival preservation, restaging, teaching, and memorial awards.

### Meher Baba association

1. **Story:** A devotional website lists Farber among Meher Baba’s close women followers.
2. **Circulation:** Meher Baba Travels website.
3. **Evidence:** No corroborating institutional biography, obituary, interview summary, or archival citation was located.
4. **Assessment:** Unverified; possibly based on private contact or mistaken identity.
5. **Effect:** Minimal in mainstream dance reception, but potentially significant to spiritual-biography communities.

## Death and Immediate Aftermath

Farber died on Christmas Eve 1998 after two cerebral hemorrhages and a month in a coma. Obituaries emphasized four identities: foundational Cunningham dancer, independent choreographer, teacher, and transatlantic institution-builder.

Sarah Lawrence recorded her death in its spring 1999 magazine. A named artist-in-residence program followed; Rauschenberg’s papers document activity from 1999 to 2004. Slayton undertook preservation personally by writing the biography, transferring or duplicating performance video, and restaging dances.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Performance legacy

Farber’s most visible surviving performances are often inside Cunningham works rather than her own repertory. This produces an archival asymmetry: the dancer remains highly visible while the choreographer is harder to see. The 2019 Cunningham centennial restored specific Farber solos to public performance, confirming how strongly individual dancers shaped ostensibly abstract repertory.

### Choreographic legacy

Her company’s dances survive through NYPL video, private recordings, notation or dancer memory, photographs, programs, reviews, and occasional restaging. The repertory’s survival is fragile because Farber dissolved the company, died without a permanent trust devoted solely to her work, and created movement closely tied to individual performers.

### Pedagogical legacy

Her largest living legacy may be pedagogical. Students and colleagues carried her approach through American universities, The Place, French conservatories and companies, and contemporary choreography. Pam Tanowitz’s testimony provides a direct twenty-first-century line of influence.

### French legacy

Farber helped consolidate the CNDC as a hybrid training and production institution during a formative period of French contemporary dance. Artists associated with the Angers period later directed companies, festivals, conservatories, and national institutions. France recognized her as an Officier de l’Ordre des Arts et des Lettres.

### Institutional commemoration

Sarah Lawrence established a Viola Farber residency initiative. The Foundation for Contemporary Arts now gives an annual Viola Farber Award “in recognition of outstanding achievement” reflecting her innovation and dedication. These programs shift her name from repertory history to ongoing artist support.

### Reinterpretation

Early criticism often treated Farber through the categories of intensity, aggression, eccentricity, and temperament. Later writing increasingly places her within:

- the women who materially built Cunningham’s repertory;
- refugee histories of American modernism;
- Black Mountain interdisciplinarity;
- dance-for-camera and electronic-music collaboration;
- institutional exchange between American postwar dance and France’s nouvelle danse;
- genealogies of women choreographers and teachers whose companies did not become permanent legacy organizations.

No evidence supports a “cult” in the religious or mass-cultural sense. There is, however, a durable specialist community of former dancers, students, archivists, and choreographers who treat her as a foundational artistic ancestor.
