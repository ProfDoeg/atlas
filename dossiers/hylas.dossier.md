# Hylas: Research Dossier

**Subject:** Hylas, Roman pantomime dancer of the Augustan age; pupil and rival of Pylades  
**User-supplied research tag:** `san_vito` (retained as metadata; no ancient evidence located connecting Hylas with Saint Vitus, the cult of San Vito, or the later disorder called St. Vitus's dance)  
**Period:** Active under Augustus (reigned 27 BCE–14 CE); more exact dates uncertain  
**Evidence base:** Two literary dossiers—Suetonius, *Divus Augustus* 45.4, and Macrobius, *Saturnalia* 2.7.12–19—plus a possible epigraphic identification in CIL VI 10115 = ILS 5197  
**Date of dossier:** 25 August 2026

---

## Executive Documentary Summary

Hylas is one of the earliest named stars of Roman imperial pantomime, but he is not the subject of an ancient biography. His recoverable life consists of a handful of notices. Macrobius says that Pylades trained Hylas until the pupil became his master's equal and rival; Roman popular opinion divided between them; Hylas danced at least Agamemnon and Oedipus; Pylades publicly criticized details of both portrayals; and disturbances arose from their rivalry. Suetonius says that Augustus, acting after a praetor complained, had Hylas flogged in the atrium of Hylas's own house without excluding spectators. The complaint, underlying act, exact date, and later result are not stated.

An inscribed terracotta object found near Rome, CIL VI 10115, praises the pantomime Theoros as victor over four named dancers: Pylades of Cilicia, Nomius of Syria, Hylas of Salmacis, and Pierus of Tibur. If this “Hylas of Salmacis” is the same performer, it supplies Hylas's probable origin: Salmacis, the spring and district adjoining Halicarnassus in Caria, in modern Bodrum, Turkey. The identification is plausible because the inscription also names the famous Pylades and is dated by modern scholars to the late first century BCE or broadly the first century CE. It is not certain: Hylas was a reusable theatrical name, and the inscription itself does not call its Hylas a pupil of Pylades.

Nothing securely establishes Hylas's birth name, legal status, patron or owner, parents, family, date of birth, date or place of death, burial, wealth, property beyond the house implied by Suetonius, or career after the Augustan notices. No work written by him survives or is attributed to him. No evidence connects him with the mythological Hylas except the shared name and the broader theatrical practice of adopting mythic names.

### The name

The name is Greek and identical to that of the beautiful companion of Heracles taken by the nymphs at a spring. Edith Hall notes that imperial dancers repeatedly bore mythological names—Pylades, Hylas, Paris—which could allow a performer to identify himself with the mythic figures his art animated. That makes a stage name possible, perhaps likely, but no surviving source states that “Hylas” was adopted professionally rather than given at birth. The name alone cannot establish ethnicity, birthplace, sexuality, servile status, or a biographical relationship to the myth.

### Persons who must not be conflated with him

- **Mythological Hylas**, companion of Heracles and an Argonaut. Juvenal's *Satires* 1.164 refers to this mythic Hylas, not the Augustan dancer.
- **Marcus Ulpius Hylas**, a performer of the Trajanic period. The nomen *Ulpius* and later date distinguish him.
- **Publius Pomponius Hylas**, associated with the Roman columbarium bearing his name, constructed in the first century CE. No evidence identifies him as the dancer.
- **Hyllus/Hylas the writer on gods or augury**, an uncertain literary name in later antiquarian citations.
- Later performers who may have reused **Hylas** as a professional name.

## State of the Evidence and Source Criticism

### 1. Suetonius, *Divus Augustus* 45.4

Written around the early second century CE, roughly a century after the events. In a discussion of Augustus's regulation of performers, Suetonius reports:

> *Hylan pantomimum querente praetore in atrio domus suae nemine excluso flagellis verberarit.*

Literal sense: Augustus had the pantomime Hylas beaten with whips in the atrium of his own house, after a praetor complained, with no one excluded. The clause *nemine excluso* makes the punishment deliberately visible; English translations commonly render it “publicly.” Suetonius does not name the praetor, specify the complaint, state whether there was a trial, or date the incident. The passage establishes imperial corporal punishment, not the unknown conduct that prompted it.

Suetonius juxtaposes this with Augustus's punishment of Stephanio and expulsion of Pylades. The juxtaposition does not prove that all three cases occurred together or arose from the same disturbance.

### 2. Macrobius, *Saturnalia* 2.7.12–19

Composed in late antiquity, probably in the early fifth century CE. This is much later than Hylas but preserves the only connected narrative about his artistic career. Macrobius states that Pylades, famous under Augustus, trained Hylas “up to a contest of equality” (*usque ad aequalitatis contentionem*). The people then divided in support of the two. Macrobius narrates two pieces of artistic criticism and finally says that popular unrest arose from the rivalry between Pylades and Hylas.

The anecdotes are literary table talk, not contemporary reportage. Their pointed sayings and neat contrasts may have been polished in transmission. Their technical specificity—Greek phrases, named roles, contrast between literal size and moral greatness, correction of the gait of blind Oedipus—nevertheless makes them important evidence for ancient ideas about pantomime interpretation.

### 3. CIL VI 10115 = ILS 5197

An inscribed terracotta token or plaque reportedly found near Rome and first published in the nineteenth century. One side praises Gaius Theoros as “light and victor of the pantomimes”; another calls him victor over Pylades of Cilicia, Nomius of Syria, Hylas of Salmacis, and Pierus of Tibur. Modern publications date it either to the late first century BCE or more broadly to the first century CE.

Its Hylas may be Pylades's pupil: the pairing with a Cilician Pylades, chronology, and profession all fit. The inscription remains independent evidence only if the identification is not assumed. It does not name a teacher, Augustus, Agamemnon, Oedipus, or Rome as the contest site.

### 4. Cassius Dio, *Roman History* 54.17.4–5

Dio dates the restoration of an exiled Pylades to 18 BCE. He says Augustus rebuked Pylades for quarreling with **Bathyllus**, not Hylas, and gives Pylades's famous reply that it benefited Caesar for the people to occupy themselves with the dancers. This conflicts with or preserves a different episode from Macrobius, who assigns the disturbances to a Pylades–Hylas rivalry. Dio does not mention Hylas in this passage.

### 5. What cannot be recovered

No papyrus, libretto, contract, letter, household record, legal dossier, portrait securely labeled as Hylas, or contemporary narrative has been identified. Modern accounts therefore become unreliable when they supply an exact birth year, make him a freedman without qualification, assign him to a patron, or state that Augustus exiled him.

## Family and Ancestry

No ancient source names Hylas's parents, ancestors, siblings, spouse, partner, or children. No source supplies a civic affiliation except the possible *Salmacidensis* of CIL VI 10115. If the inscription belongs to him, it indicates geographic origin or civic designation, not ancestry.

The mythological Hylas had elaborate genealogies, most commonly as son of Theiodamas. Those genealogies belong to the mythic figure and cannot be transferred to the dancer.

## Childhood and Early Life

Unknown. Macrobius begins with Hylas already as Pylades's pupil. No age is supplied, and the word *discipulus* does not establish childhood training. Ancient professional dancers could begin bodily training young, but this general fact cannot be converted into a biographical statement about Hylas.

If Hylas of Salmacis is identical with him, his early geographical horizon may have been Caria, specifically the famous spring or suburban district of Salmacis beside Halicarnassus. A move from the Greek-speaking eastern Mediterranean to Rome would then be required by his career. No route, date, reason, owner, troupe, or intermediary is known.

## Education and Formation

### Training by Pylades

Macrobius is explicit that Pylades trained Hylas and brought him to equality and rivalry. Pylades was associated in ancient tradition with the new tragic form of pantomime that rose under Augustus. The art centered on a solo, normally masked and non-speaking dancer who embodied successive mythic characters while a chorus sang a libretto and musicians accompanied the action.

The surviving anecdotes indicate areas in which Hylas had been trained and judged:

- physical characterization of rank, age, blindness, and emotional state;
- semantic translation of sung language into bodily sign;
- synchronization with a sung *canticum*;
- the transformation of a single performer into major tragic characters;
- the ability to sustain a recognizable role without speech;
- sensitivity to a sophisticated audience capable of criticizing particular gestures.

At least one of Hylas's libretti ended in Greek words, *ton megan Agamemnona* (“the great Agamemnon”). This supports performance in or close engagement with Greek, but does not prove that Hylas himself was literate or a native Greek speaker. John Jory observes that the anecdote turns on conveying the meaning “great,” a meaning recognizable whether the sung word was Greek or Latin.

### Formation within the Pyladean tradition

Pylades was later remembered as an innovator who replaced older, rougher dancing with a more elegant form and integrated flutes, pipes, and human voices. Hylas's repertoire—Agamemnon and Oedipus—belongs to tragic myth rather than the comic or erotic strand later associated with Bathyllus. It is therefore reasonable to place him within Pylades's tragic school. No ancient text says that Hylas founded a school of his own.

## Early Career

No debut, first employer, or first performance survives. The safest sequence is:

1. Pylades became prominent in Augustan Rome.
2. Hylas studied under him.
3. Hylas achieved enough mastery to be considered Pylades's equal.
4. The public divided into rival followings.

If CIL VI 10115 concerns the same man, Hylas also competed against Theoros and was remembered as one of four distinguished opponents whom Theoros claimed to have defeated. The word “defeated” may refer to formal competition, promotional commemoration, or retrospective ranking; the object does not preserve the occasion or judging body.

## Chronological Life History

### Possible eastern origin and migration to Rome

The inscribed phrase *Hyla[m] Salmacid[ensem]* is the only candidate for a birthplace or origin. Salmacis lay at Halicarnassus in Caria, on the southwest coast of Asia Minor. If the identification is correct, Hylas joined a conspicuously trans-Mediterranean profession: the same inscription labels Pylades Cilician, Nomius Syrian, and Pierus Tiburtine. This suggests performers were marketed partly through geographic identities.

The migration itself is inferred. There is no evidence that Hylas was captured, sold, manumitted, recruited by an impresario, attached to an elite household, or independently contracted.

### Apprenticeship under Pylades

Macrobius's phrase makes Pylades both instructor and the standard Hylas eventually met. The teacher–pupil relation became competitive rather than remaining hierarchical. No evidence dates the beginning or length of the apprenticeship.

### Emergence as an equal and rival

Macrobius says Hylas advanced to “equality” and that the Roman people divided their votes or favor between him and Pylades. This is the clearest testimony to Hylas's success. It does not record an official award; “equality” is the narrator's characterization of artistic standing.

The divided public implies organized or at least strongly identified followings. Later imperial sources document theatrical partisans, paid claques, regulations, and violence. For Hylas specifically, Macrobius attests popular division and *seditio* but not the size, organization, leadership, or social composition of his faction.

### The Agamemnon performance

Hylas danced a *canticum* ending “the great Agamemnon” (*τὸν μέγαν Ἀγαμέμνονα*). He represented greatness through a gesture or posture marking height and bulk—Macrobius says he seemed to measure something lofty and immense. Pylades, seated in the audience, called out in Greek: *Σὺ μακρὸν οὐ μέγαν ποιεῖς*—“You make him tall/long, not great.”

The audience then compelled Pylades to perform the same piece. At the disputed phrase, Pylades represented Agamemnon thinking, because a great commander must think on behalf of everyone. The anecdote establishes:

- Hylas performed Agamemnon in public;
- teacher and pupil could perform the same libretto or scenario;
- the audience could demand an immediate comparative performance;
- the artistic dispute contrasted literal gestural illustration with ethical or intellectual characterization;
- Pylades's correction, not Hylas's version, controls the surviving narrative.

The title and full source of the sung text are unknown. It need not have been Aeschylus's *Agamemnon*; pantomime libretti recombined tragic myth, and only the closing phrase survives.

### The Oedipus performance

Macrobius next says that while Hylas danced Oedipus, Pylades criticized the dancer's *securitas*—assurance, carelessness, or unconstrained ease—by shouting *Σὺ βλέπεις*, “You are looking/You can see.” The point is that Hylas moved with more visual assurance than a blind Oedipus should.

Which Oedipus episode was danced is unknown. The correction strongly favors a post-blinding Oedipus, but no surviving title identifies Sophocles's *Oedipus Tyrannus*, *Oedipus at Colonus*, or another adaptation. Modern retellings that describe this as a rehearsal add a circumstance absent from Macrobius; Pylades is not explicitly said to be rehearsing Hylas.

### Rival factions and public disorder

Macrobius 2.7.19 states that a *populi seditio* was stirred by the rivalry between Pylades and Hylas and that Augustus became angry with Pylades. Pylades answered in Greek: “You are ungrateful, emperor; let them occupy themselves with us.” The remark casts theatrical partisanship as a diversion from politics.

Two cautions are necessary:

1. Cassius Dio gives the same political thought in connection with Pylades's quarrel with Bathyllus and Pylades's restoration in 18 BCE.
2. Macrobius does not date the Pylades–Hylas disturbances or say that Hylas was exiled.

Possibilities include two separate rivalries, a later substitution of Hylas for Bathyllus in anecdotal transmission, or the compression of several episodes. The sources do not decide among them.

### The imperial flogging

At an unknown date Augustus had Hylas flogged in Hylas's own atrium after a praetor complained. The open atrium and admission of onlookers converted corporal punishment into a public assertion of authority inside the performer's domestic space.

What is documented: complaint by an unnamed praetor; Augustus's order or agency; flogging with *flagella*; Hylas's own house; spectators not excluded.

What is not documented: the charge; a judicial hearing; guilt; relation to the factional disturbances; number of blows; injuries; confiscation; exile; dismissal; death; Hylas's legal status. Calling the incident a conviction would exceed the source.

### Possible defeat by Theoros

CIL VI 10115 commemorates Theoros as victor over Hylas of Salmacis and three other pantomimes. If identical, this is Hylas's only securely named competitive defeat. It also places him in a professional network reaching Cilicia, Syria, Caria, Tibur, and Rome. The commemorative object speaks in Theoros's voice and should be read as victory publicity, not a neutral league table.

### Later life and death

No source follows Hylas into retirement, old age, or death. The absence of later notices does not establish early death, disgrace, exile, or loss of fame. No tomb is securely his.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Professional organization

No named company, guild, troupe, or contractual patron is attached to Hylas. His training under Pylades may imply membership in a school or performing organization, but Macrobius describes a pedagogical relationship rather than a legal entity.

### Performance apparatus

The form in which Hylas worked normally combined:

- a solo dancer who did not speak;
- a sung narrative or libretto performed by a chorus or singer;
- instrumental accompaniment, especially pipes or flutes;
- masks with closed mouths and costume changes;
- codified or conventional gesture capable of carrying narrative meaning;
- mythological and tragic subject matter.

These are features of the art around him, not inventions attributable to Hylas. No technology, patent, authored libretto, choreography notation, mask, costume, or musical composition can be assigned to him.

### House and property

Suetonius's *atrium domus suae* implies that Hylas occupied a Roman house with an atrium. The grammar ordinarily means “his own house,” but does not prove ownership rather than possession or residence. It gives no address, value, dimensions, slaves, furnishings, or title history. It is the sole evidence touching his material circumstances.

### Earnings and wealth

Unknown. Pantomime stars could receive high fees and elite favor, but no amount, inheritance, business interest, patronal gift, or financial dispute survives for Hylas. His fame and house suggest some material establishment; a net worth cannot be reconstructed.

## Political / Religious / Intellectual / Artistic Development

### Political position

No political opinions or offices are recorded. Hylas became politically consequential through mass spectatorship: his rivalry with Pylades produced public division and, according to Macrobius, unrest sufficient to draw Augustus's intervention.

Pylades's reply to Augustus frames celebrity rivalry as politically useful displacement. It is Pylades's reported argument, not evidence that Hylas or his supporters consciously served an Augustan strategy.

### Religious life

Unknown. His repertoire drew on Greek heroic myth. Performing Agamemnon or Oedipus does not establish personal cult, philosophical belief, or religious affiliation.

### Artistic development

The recoverable arc is pedagogical and competitive: training by Pylades, equality with him, then public rivalry. The two criticisms preserved by Macrobius present Hylas as technically accomplished but too literal or insufficiently embodied in specific moments:

- he rendered *great* Agamemnon as physically tall rather than mentally burdened with command;
- he rendered blind Oedipus with movements that disclosed sight.

Because both stories are transmitted in a Pylades-centered sequence, they may function as master-artist anecdotes that magnify Pylades. They do not establish that Hylas was generally an inferior dancer; the same text first calls him Pylades's equal.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Pylades

Teacher, artistic model, rival, public critic, and opposing center of theatrical partisanship. This is the only securely documented personal relationship in Hylas's life.

### Augustus

Imperial regulator and punisher. Augustus personally or administratively caused Hylas to be flogged after a praetor's complaint. Macrobius's riot story places Hylas's rivalry within Augustus's direct concern, although the emperor's dialogue is with Pylades.

### The unnamed praetor

Complainant whose status gave the grievance access to Augustus. Identity and grounds are unknown. No responsible prosopography can select a candidate.

### The Roman audience

Collective patron, judge, and partisan force. The audience divided between Hylas and Pylades and compelled Pylades to demonstrate his alternative Agamemnon. The spectators were active participants in artistic adjudication, not passive consumers.

### Theoros, Nomius, Pierus

Possible competitors known from CIL VI 10115. The inscription says Theoros defeated Pylades, Nomius, Hylas, and Pierus; it does not state that the four competed simultaneously or maintained personal relationships.

### Bathyllus

Not securely a direct associate of Hylas. Bathyllus was Pylades's rival in Cassius Dio's version of the unrest tradition. Later summaries often combine the Pylades–Bathyllus and Pylades–Hylas rivalries. The ancient texts should remain distinct.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, and Violence

### Complaint and corporal punishment

- **Documented event:** Augustus had Hylas flogged publicly in his own atrium.
- **Documented initiating act:** a praetor complained.
- **Unresolved:** the alleged misconduct, evidentiary procedure, legal basis, and whether punishment followed formal adjudication.
- **Not documented:** fraud, corruption, financial crime, conspiracy, sexual misconduct, or a criminal conviction.

Roman actors carried degraded civic status and had historically been exposed to magistrates' corporal discipline. Suetonius places the episode within Augustus's effort to restrain performers' *licentia*, but that authorial category is not a specific charge.

### Factional violence

- **Macrobius's version:** public disturbance arose from rivalry between Pylades and Hylas.
- **Dio's version:** Pylades had been exiled for sedition and quarreled with Bathyllus; Augustus restored him in 18 BCE.
- **Later synthesis:** some modern scholars connect Hylas directly with the disturbance dated to 18 BCE and occasionally state that both Pylades and Hylas were expelled.
- **Assessment:** Hylas's connection to disturbances is textually documented by Macrobius; his exile is not. The exact year 18 BCE belongs securely to Dio's Pylades episode, not expressly to Hylas.

No ancient source says Hylas personally attacked anyone, ordered violence, paid a claque, or was prosecuted for sedition. The agency of performers, fans, and organizers remains unresolved.

### Rivalry as propaganda or promotion

The Theoros object is self-promotional commemoration: it constructs Theoros's stature by naming defeated celebrity opponents. Macrobius's anecdotes likewise center Pylades's superior interpretive intelligence. Both preserve Hylas's fame while subordinating him to someone else's victory narrative.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Hylas of Salmacis”

1. **Story:** The Augustan dancer came from Salmacis near Halicarnassus.
2. **Appearance:** CIL VI 10115 names a defeated pantomime Hylas of Salmacis; modern databases and scholars connect him with Pylades's pupil.
3. **Circulation:** Epigraphists and historians of Roman theater, including the Theatrum database and contributors to *New Directions in Ancient Pantomime*.
4. **Evidence:** Matching rare professional context, compatible date, and association with Pylades support the identification. Reuse of theatrical names and lack of an explicit teacher or emperor prevent certainty.
5. **Reputational effect:** It converts an otherwise originless Roman celebrity into an eastern Mediterranean migrant and places him in a named competitive circuit.

### Hylas as an exiled dancer in 18 BCE

1. **Story:** Hylas and Pylades were both banished after their factions rioted in 18 BCE.
2. **Appearance:** Modern reconstructions sometimes merge Macrobius's Hylas rivalry with Dio's dated account of Pylades's exile and Bathyllus quarrel.
3. **Circulation:** Modern histories of pantomime and Senecan performance.
4. **Evidence:** Macrobius supports Hylas-related unrest; Dio supports Pylades's exile and restoration in 18 BCE. Neither source says Hylas was exiled.
5. **Reputational effect:** It gives Hylas a dramatic fall and return that the evidence does not independently preserve.

### The “tall, not great” Agamemnon

1. **Story:** Hylas embodied greatness as height; Pylades corrected him and demonstrated greatness as thought for the army.
2. **Appearance:** Macrobius, *Saturnalia* 2.7.13–14, early fifth century CE.
3. **Circulation:** Macrobius and later histories of dance, acting, gesture, and the reception of Greek tragedy.
4. **Evidence:** Only Macrobius preserves it. The Greek punch line and performance detail may derive from an older anecdotal collection; no contemporary corroboration survives.
5. **Reputational effect:** Hylas becomes the foil in a foundational parable about literal gesture versus interpretive acting.

### The seeing Oedipus

1. **Story:** Hylas's movement betrayed that he could see while portraying blind Oedipus; Pylades cried, “You are looking.”
2. **Appearance:** Macrobius, *Saturnalia* 2.7.15.
3. **Circulation:** Modern acting and pantomime histories.
4. **Evidence:** Single late source; compatible with the technical demands of masked dance.
5. **Reputational effect:** It fixes Hylas in reception as a gifted but correctable pupil and provides rare evidence for ancient standards of bodily verisimilitude.

### Confusion with mythological Hylas

1. **Story:** Biographical treatments occasionally absorb the youth, beauty, water-nymph episode, or Heraclean relationship of mythological Hylas into the dancer's identity.
2. **Appearance:** Usually through name-based search contamination rather than an ancient biographical claim.
3. **Circulation:** General web pages, image databases, and undifferentiated indexes.
4. **Evidence:** None for identity. They belong to categorically different figures.
5. **Reputational effect:** The mythic Hylas overwhelmingly dominates modern art and film, making the historical dancer difficult to retrieve.

### “San Vito” / St. Vitus's dance

No ancient, medieval, or modern scholarly source consulted connected the Augustan Hylas with Saint Vitus, the cult at San Vito, tarantism, choreomania, or the medical label “St. Vitus's dance.” The supplied phrase is therefore retained only as a research tag, not developed into a historical claim.

## Books and Major Works About the Person

No full-length ancient or modern biography devoted solely to the Augustan Hylas was identified. The principal scholarly works are studies of pantomime, actors, gesture, and tragedy reception in which he appears.

### Primary texts and corpora

- Suetonius, *De vita Caesarum*, “Divus Augustus” 45.4. Earliest surviving explicit notice of Hylas's punishment.
- Macrobius, *Saturnalia* 2.7.12–19. Principal narrative of apprenticeship, rivalry, roles, and disturbances.
- CIL VI 10115 = ILS 5197 = *Carmina Latina Epigraphica* 925. Possible contemporary or near-contemporary inscription naming Hylas of Salmacis.
- Cassius Dio, *Roman History* 54.17.4–5. Necessary comparator for the conflicting Bathyllus version and the date 18 BCE.
- Tacitus, *Annals* 1.54 and 1.77. Context for Augustan indulgence toward pantomime and Tiberian regulation of theatrical factions; Hylas is not named.

- William Smith, ed., *A Dictionary of Greek and Roman Antiquities*, article “Pantomimus” (1875). Useful historiographically but now dated. Its citation of Juvenal 6.63 concerns Bathyllus, not Hylas; Juvenal 1.164's Hylas is mythological.

- *Prosopographia Imperii Romani*, first edition, H 171. Briefly registers Hylas as a famous Augustan pantomime, pupil of Pylades, flogged by Augustus.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Hylas's survival is paradoxical. Macrobius states that he attained equality with the founding master Pylades, yet nearly every preserved anecdote places him as Pylades's foil. He survives as:

- evidence that the first generation of imperial pantomime already reproduced itself through teachers and pupils;
- an early example of a celebrity pupil becoming a rival brand;
- a focal point of audience factionalism and urban disorder;
- a case in imperial discipline of a performer inside his own domestic space;
- a rare named interpreter of Agamemnon and Oedipus through dance;
- a central example in modern scholarship on the semiotics of gesture.

The Agamemnon story has had the strongest theoretical afterlife. It is used to distinguish picturing a word from interpreting a character: Hylas gives “great” a spatial body, while Pylades turns it into the mental labor of command. The Oedipus story is used to show that pantomime's credibility depended upon the entire body, including the dancer's relation to an unseen gaze.

Hylas also illustrates the structure of the archive. His name is preserved by an emperor's punishment, a teacher's criticism, a rival's victory monument, and a report of civil disturbance—not by his own voice. The record documents celebrity and subordination simultaneously.
