# Micaela Bastidas Puyucahua: Research Dossier

## Scope and Evidentiary Note

Micaela Bastidas left no memoir and no securely identified body of writing in her own hand. Her reconstructable life rests primarily on:

- her 1760 marriage record;
- correspondence, passes, orders, and administrative papers produced under her authority;
- testimony from prisoners, associates, enemies, and royal officials;
- her interrogation and criminal proceeding of April–May 1781;
- contemporaneous accounts of the rebellion and executions;
- later historical, literary, political, and commemorative interpretations.

The surviving judicial record was created by authorities prosecuting a defeated rebellion. It contains authentic administrative facts but also hostile terminology, coerced evidence, hearsay, and testimony by prisoners who had incentives to minimize their own culpability. Statements from that record are therefore identified below as judicial findings, prosecution allegations, witness testimony, or documentary facts rather than treated interchangeably.

A dedicated adversarial search combined her name with Spanish and English terms for controversy, accusations, crimes, fraud, scandal, conspiracy, criticism, and litigation, including searches covering 2024–2026. It found no plausible allegations of private fraud, corruption, money laundering, sexual misconduct, or ordinary criminality. The substantive adverse material concerns conduct during the rebellion, threats against dissenters, coercive recruitment, the treatment of prisoners and Europeans, rebel violence, the political purpose of the uprising, and modern disputes over her birthplace and historical representation.

### The name “Puyucahua”

The maternal surname appears in inconsistent colonial spellings. Modern forms such as **Phuyuqhawa** represent attempts to regularize it according to Quechua orthography. No evidence establishes that Micaela herself preferred one surviving written form.

She is sometimes called “Micaela Túpac Amaru,” especially in popular or literary treatments. That was not her normal documented personal name; it derives from her marriage to José Gabriel Condorcanqui, Túpac Amaru II.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Documented fact:** Her parents were Manuel Bastidas and Josefa Puyucahua. The marriage record is the central documentary source.

The record’s description of her legitimacy and social classification has been read differently. Some transcriptions and studies describe her as a legitimate daughter of parents classified as *españoles* or criollos. Other modern studies call her a *hija natural*, mestiza, chola, zamba, or a woman of combined Andean and African ancestry.

These descriptions cannot safely be harmonized:

- **Documented colonial classification:** At least one scholarly transcription of the marriage record says her parents were presented as criollos or “Spaniards.”
- **Modern historical interpretation:** Numerous biographies identify Josefa Puyucahua as Andean and Manuel Bastidas as African-descended or Afro-Spanish.
- **Unresolved question:** No surviving baptismal record has been securely produced to establish her birth status, caste designation, or exact ancestry.
- **Contextual caution:** Colonial racial categories were legal and reputational classifications, not precise genealogical descriptions. Families could be recorded differently across documents.

A 2025 retrospective citing José Antonio del Busto characterizes the maternal Puyucahua family as part of a locally established “aristocracia pueblerina,” related to families including the Cusihuamán, Cahuaypata, and Cápac Tinta. This is a reported scholarly interpretation, not a demonstrated line-by-line genealogy.

### Siblings and extended family

**Documented fact:** Antonio Bastidas was Micaela’s brother or, in royal documentation, José Gabriel’s brother-in-law. He participated in the rebellion and was executed with the principal prisoners on 18 May 1781.

Other Bastidas, Castro, and Puyucahua relatives appear in the wider rebellion’s records, but the precise relationship of every person to Micaela is not always established. Marcela Castro Puyucahua is associated with the insurgent family network in later accounts. This dossier does not treat every shared surname as proof of a specific relationship.

### Marriage

**Documented fact:** Micaela married José Gabriel Condorcanqui Noguera—later Túpac Amaru II—on 25 May 1760 in the church of Nuestra Señora de la Purificación at Surimana. She was approximately fifteen or sixteen. The priest was Antonio López de Sosa.

José Gabriel belonged to an Andean chiefly family and became curaca of Surimana, Tungasuca, and Pampamarca. He operated a substantial mule-transport business along the southern Andean commercial routes connecting Cusco with the Upper Peruvian/Potosí economy.

The marriage was simultaneously domestic, commercial, political, and eventually military. Her correspondence addresses him intimately as **“Chepe mío”**, while also criticizing his operational decisions.

### Children

The couple had three sons:

1. **Hipólito Condorcanqui Bastidas**, born in 1761. He participated in the rebellion and was executed in Cusco on 18 May 1781 after being forced, with his parents, through the public execution ceremony.

2. **Mariano Condorcanqui Bastidas**, born in 1762. He escaped the April 1781 capture and continued within the surviving insurgent and family network. He died in 1784, reportedly while displaced in Portuguese territory/Brazil; precise circumstances vary among secondary accounts.

3. **Fernando Condorcanqui Bastidas**, born in 1768. Aged about twelve at his parents’ execution, although royal records sometimes calculated him as ten or eleven. He was forced to witness the punishment and was condemned to perpetual exile. He was eventually transported to Spain, confined for years, and died impoverished in Madrid in 1798. Sixteen of his later petitions and letters were recovered and published in the twenty-first century.

The colonial reprisals extended beyond the condemned leaders to children and relatives. The principal sentence declared descendants infamous and legally disabled, ordered exile or confinement for surviving family members, and attempted to destroy the lineage’s material and symbolic position.

## Childhood and Early Life

Almost nothing securely documentary survives about Micaela’s childhood.

### Birth date

The authorized PARES record gives **23 June 1744**. Many modern Peruvian works follow it. Some library authority records use **1745**. Because no uncontested baptismal record is presently available, 23 June 1744 is the conventional date rather than an independently verified civil-style registration.

### Birthplace controversy

Three formulations appear:

#### Pampamarca, Canas, Cusco

**Documentary support:** During her confession of 22 April 1781, Micaela answered that she was “natural del pueblo de Pampamarca.” This is the strongest first-person documentary statement. Advocates of Pampamarca also cite the marriage entry and the origins of her parents.

#### Tamburco, Abancay, Apurímac

**Documentary and traditional support:** A royal list of principal prisoners identified her as “natural de Abancay.” Later historians and Peruvian state publications have placed her in Tamburco, then within the Abancay jurisdiction. Tamburco and the Apurímac regional government maintain a strong civic tradition identifying it as her birthplace.

#### Why the dispute persists

The 1781 compilation itself contains both descriptions: her confession says Pampamarca, while a later list says Abancay. A congressional proposal supporting Pampamarca argued that the Abancay entry resulted from an error by the officer Francisco Cuéllar. Apurímac advocates reject that conclusion and cite local family traditions and historical research.

In a 2017 congressional debate, legislators could not resolve the regional dispute and removed a specific birthplace from the operative text rather than legislate one version.

**Finding:** Pampamarca has the stronger direct first-person evidence. Tamburco/Abancay has longstanding documentary, historiographical, and local institutional support. The matter is not universally settled.

### Economic circumstances

Popular accounts sometimes portray her as an impoverished orphan. The marriage she entered, however, became part of a relatively prosperous curaca and transport household owning houses, animals, and movable goods. Evidence about her exact childhood wealth is inadequate.

No source consulted establishes a childhood illness, disability, accident, addiction, psychological crisis, or extended journey.

## Education and Formation

No school attendance, formal teacher, or documented course of study has been established for Micaela.

### Literacy

Her judicial proceedings repeatedly record that she did not sign because she did not know how. This is stronger evidence than later statements that she received elementary literary education.

Her inability to sign does not imply political or administrative incapacity. She dictated orders and letters through a secretarial staff that included, according to prosecution testimony, Felipe Bermúdez, Mariano de la Banda, Diego Ortigosa, Esteban Escarcena, and Diego Berdejo/Verdejo.

### Language and oral formation

Witnesses said she spoke in “lengua índica,” meaning Quechua, and that interpreters rendered her words into Spanish. Whether she could understand or speak some Spanish is unresolved. Her administrative competence indicates extensive practical formation in household finance, regional commerce, kinship politics, oral negotiation, provisioning, and the authority structures of the curacazgo.

### Religious formation

Her church marriage and judicial oath establish conventional Catholic participation. The rebellion operated in an intensely Catholic society and sometimes invoked religion and the monarch while opposing colonial officials. Conversely, Bishop Juan Manuel Moscoso y Peralta and other clergy deployed excommunication and royalist communications against the insurgents.

Claims that she personally followed a clearly articulated secular Enlightenment ideology are not documented. Ideas attributed to her should be derived primarily from her orders and correspondence: opposition to oppressive corregidores and fiscal burdens, mobilization of the provinces, defense of the insurgent project, and—in testimony contested by her—aspiration to establish a new sovereign order.

## Marriage, Household Economy, and Commercial Life, 1760–1780

After the 1760 marriage, Micaela lived within José Gabriel’s network in Surimana, Tungasuca, Pampamarca, Tinta, and the larger southern Andean transport corridor.

José Gabriel inherited or obtained recognition as curaca of Surimana, Tungasuca, and Pampamarca around 1764. Accounts differ on the size of his mule enterprise:

- one modern essay describes more than sixty mules and horses;
- wider biographies of José Gabriel give approximately 350 mules inherited or controlled by the family.

The discrepancy may reflect different dates, definitions, or exaggeration. A precise household inventory before the uprising has not been established here.

### Houses and property

A Peruvian government publication reports that the family possessed:

- a house in Tinta, on the commercial route connecting Lima, Cusco, Potosí, and Buenos Aires;
- a house in Tungasuca, which became a rebel headquarters.

The judicial record mentions silver plate, vessels, textiles, religious objects, books, and other goods associated with rebel households, but ownership of every listed article is not always clear. Some property held at rebel headquarters belonged to prisoners or third parties.

Micaela’s pre-rebellion managerial responsibilities are inferred from her later demonstrated ability to procure food, organize transport, issue payments, supervise personnel, and maintain communications. No separate company, mine, patent, formal investment vehicle, or independent commercial registration under her name has been located.

### Formative grievances

The family lived amid:

- compulsory Indigenous labor drafts, particularly the mining *mita*;
- Indigenous tribute;
- customs and sales taxes;
- abuses connected with the compulsory distribution of goods by corregidores;
- conflicts over curaca authority and succession;
- commercial restrictions and Bourbon fiscal reforms.

José Gabriel pursued petitions and litigation before open rebellion. Micaela’s precise role in those earlier legal efforts is not documented, although she shared their economic consequences.

## Chronological Life History

## 1760–1779: Marriage, motherhood, and regional management

Between 1760 and 1768 she married and gave birth to Hipólito, Mariano, and Fernando. The family circulated among its curaca communities and commercial properties. José Gabriel’s long journeys for transport and legal business left Micaela with practical household and local managerial responsibilities.

No surviving evidence documents extramarital partners, separation, lawsuits between the spouses, or personal enemies from this period.

## 1780: From clandestine planning to rebellion

### Preparations

Royalist witnesses later claimed that Micaela said the uprising had been contemplated three or four years before 1780. One witness said she spoke of seven agents in Lima. She denied or evaded these assertions at trial.

- **Witness allegation:** Micaela was involved in planning well before November 1780.
- **Her judicial position:** She said her husband had not disclosed the project to her in advance.
- **Assessment:** Her central participation after 4 November is indisputable; the length and nature of her prior knowledge remain unresolved.

### 4 November: Capture of Antonio de Arriaga

José Gabriel’s adherents seized Antonio de Arriaga, corregidor of Tinta. The capture initiated the public phase of the rebellion.

### 10 November: Execution of Arriaga

Arriaga was publicly hanged after being compelled to issue orders that helped the insurgents obtain money, arms, and personnel. Micaela’s exact physical role in the capture and hanging is not independently established, but she became a principal organizer immediately afterward.

### Headquarters at Tungasuca

Micaela operated from the household and rebel headquarters at Tungasuca. Her responsibilities included:

- gathering and distributing food, coca, clothing, money, weapons, ammunition, and animals;
- recruiting and ordering communities to provide fighters;
- coordinating messengers and mounted couriers;
- issuing passes and safe-conducts;
- supervising secretaries;
- relaying intelligence;
- detaining or releasing prisoners;
- corresponding with caciques, governors, clergy, local authorities, and commanders;
- maintaining the rear while José Gabriel campaigned;
- publicizing rebel victories and extending the movement geographically.

A letter dated 14 November 1780 from Melchor Castelo addresses her with formal deference and reports compliance with her command concerning María, wife of the imprisoned Francisco Cisneros. This is direct evidence that commanders treated her as an authority in her own right.

### 18 November: Sangarará

The insurgents defeated a royalist force at Sangarará. Fire in the church where royalist troops had taken position caused heavy deaths and became a major propaganda and political turning point. Royal authorities and clergy depicted the episode as sacrilege and massacre; insurgents blamed royalists for militarizing the church and emphasized their broader grievances.

Micaela did not personally command the battlefield force at Sangarará, so responsibility for individual killings cannot be assigned to her merely from her leadership position. She helped manage the movement benefiting from the victory.

### Strategic disagreement over Cusco

After Sangarará, Micaela repeatedly urged an immediate advance on Cusco. Her surviving letters criticized José Gabriel for delay. In one, she complained that he was moving slowly and giving the defenders time to prepare; she warned that their children and followers were endangered.

Her correspondence shows:

- urgency about attacking Cusco;
- concern over desertion and troop morale;
- fear of poisoning and insistence that José Gabriel eat food prepared by trusted people;
- frustration that her recommendations were ignored;
- continued emotional attachment to him.

The later proposition that she alone identified the decisive strategy is supported by the letters’ content, though the counterfactual claim that Cusco certainly would have fallen cannot be proved.

### December movements

Micaela moved between Tungasuca, Pomacanchi, and other centers while coordinating commanders who reported shortages of weapons and men. She sent proclamations toward Arequipa and sought support in Urubamba, Paucartambo, Quispicanchi, Paruro, Tinta, Lampa, Azángaro, Carabaya, Chucuito, and other provinces.

## December 1780–January 1781: The siege of Cusco

The insurgent forces approached and besieged Cusco around late December and early January. By then the city had organized its defense, received reinforcements, and mobilized royalist Indigenous forces.

Mateo García Pumacahua, curaca of Chinchero, became an important royalist opponent. His participation demonstrates that the conflict was not simply Indigenous people against Spaniards: Andean communities, curacas, mestizos, creoles, clergy, and Europeans divided between and within the two camps.

The siege failed. The insurgents withdrew in January 1781. Historians commonly identify the delay after Sangarará as a major lost opportunity, but the size of the city’s potential loyalist population, rebel provisioning problems, internal divisions, and uncertainty about urban support also mattered.

## Early 1781: Widening war and deteriorating control

The movement spread through a large part of the southern Andes. Rebel and royalist forces used coercive recruitment, confiscation, threats, exemplary punishment, and propaganda.

Micaela continued provisioning and correspondence. Witnesses described her giving money, coca, and food to mobilized people. Other testimony accused her of threatening death and confiscation against noncompliant persons.

As royalist forces advanced, the insurgent leadership’s control over dispersed adherents weakened. Reports of attacks on Spaniards, creoles, mestizos, priests, property, and commercial establishments accumulated. Not all such acts were ordered by Micaela or José Gabriel.

## March–April 1781: Defeat and capture

Royalist armies attacked the rebel center. Pumacahua led Indigenous royalist forces against the Tinta region. On or about 6 April the rebel army suffered a decisive defeat.

José Gabriel was betrayed and captured through the actions of former or wavering associates, commonly named as Francisco Santa Cruz, Ventura Landaeta, and the priest Antonio Martínez, though accounts allocate responsibility differently.

Micaela, warned of the disaster, attempted to escape with family members along the Livitaca route, reportedly intending to reach the La Paz region. She was intercepted and captured, with Hipólito and Fernando among the prisoners. She was brought to Cusco and confined in the former Jesuit complex used as a military prison.

## Political, Religious, and Ideological Development

### Initial program

The earliest public phase attacked the corregidores and their abuses. The rebellion proclaimed the abolition or reduction of burdens such as the *reparto*, customs charges, Indigenous tribute, and the *mita*. Its declarations attempted to recruit Indigenous people, mestizos, creoles, and enslaved or free Afro-descended people.

The insurgent program evolved and was not perfectly consistent. Different proclamations appealed variously to:

- loyalty to the king against corrupt officials;
- restoration of justice;
- abolition of oppressive exactions;
- Inca legitimacy;
- expulsion or punishment of Europeans;
- emancipation from slavery;
- a new political order under Túpac Amaru.

### Was the rebellion “independence”?

This remains a major historiographical dispute.

- **Documented fact:** Some proclamations professed loyalty to the Spanish monarch while attacking “bad government.”
- **Prosecution finding:** The leaders intended to detach Peru from the Crown, seize the kingdom, and reign.
- **Witness allegation:** Micaela spoke of their intention to reign and of rounding up Europeans.
- **Micaela’s defense:** She denied knowledge of an earlier conspiracy and denied or refused to confirm many incriminating conversations.
- **Later nationalist interpretation:** The movement was the beginning of Peruvian or continental independence.
- **Revisionist interpretation:** It began as an anti-corregidor, anti-fiscal, and reformist movement whose separatist dimensions became stronger as war escalated.
- **Finding:** It cannot be reduced to either pure royal loyalty or a fully formed nineteenth-century national-independence program. Its surviving texts contain multiple, changing political languages.

### Religion and excommunication

The rebels did not generally present themselves as opponents of Christianity. Nevertheless, they challenged clergy who supported royal authority and ignored ecclesiastical censures. The prosecution specifically charged Micaela with contempt for “the sacred arms” of excommunication. Royalists used this as proof of sacrilege; later historians view it as evidence that she refused to let church discipline terminate the political and military movement.

There is no documentary basis for attributing atheism, occult practice, or membership in Freemasonry to Micaela. Claims of Masonic contacts attach primarily—and speculatively—to José Gabriel’s time in Lima.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Household commercial organization

The Condorcanqui-Bastidas household participated in the muleteering economy joining Cusco and Upper Peru. Its assets reportedly included houses, livestock, pack animals, cultivated property, household silver, and commercial goods.

Micaela’s work cannot be reconstructed through modern corporate categories. It was embedded in:

- marriage and kinship;
- curaca authority;
- community obligations;
- transport contracts;
- household labor;
- regional patronage and credit.

### Rebel administrative system

Her most consequential organizational project was the insurgent logistics and communications apparatus. It used:

- mounted couriers and chasquis;
- written and oral orders;
- safe-conducts;
- requisitioning;
- community recruitment;
- dispersed depots and kitchens;
- local officials and caciques;
- household secretaries;
- intelligence supplied by supporters.

Calling this a “technology” would be anachronistic if taken to mean an invention. It was an adaptive administrative system combining Andean messenger practices with colonial correspondence, transport, and jurisdictional networks.

### Confiscation

The prosecution demanded forfeiture of all her property to the royal treasury. José Antonio de Areche’s sentence ordered her goods confiscated, her body divided and publicly displayed, and her memory used as an example. The wider judgment ordered insurgent houses demolished or symbolically salted and attempted to disable the descendants from office and status.

No reliable valuation of Micaela’s personal net worth at death has been located.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### José Gabriel Condorcanqui, Túpac Amaru II

Husband, business partner, political collaborator, and fellow commander. The relationship was affectionate but strategically contentious. Her letters show she did not merely transmit his decisions; she proposed policy, reprimanded delay, and exercised independent authority.

### Hipólito, Mariano, and Fernando

Her sons were both family members and participants or victims of the rebellion. Her letters repeatedly show concern that José Gabriel’s military decisions endangered them.

### Tomasa Tito Condemayta

Curaca of Acos, landowner, and prominent insurgent leader. She provided material support and led forces, including resistance around the Pilpinto bridge. She was tried and executed with Micaela.

### Antonio Bastidas

Her brother and insurgent associate, executed on 18 May 1781.

### Diego Cristóbal Túpac Amaru

José Gabriel’s cousin and a major commander who continued the rebellion after the principal leaders’ capture. Micaela belonged to the same political-family command network, though no detailed personal correspondence between them was located in the consulted sources.

### Cecilia Túpac Amaru

A female member of José Gabriel’s family active in the movement and subsequently punished. Some secondary sources call her a sister, half-sister, or cousin; the precise relationship should be checked against the relevant genealogical record.

### Secretaries and intermediaries

Trial testimony names Felipe Bermúdez, Mariano de la Banda, Diego Ortigosa, Esteban Escarcena, and Diego Berdejo/Verdejo among people who wrote for or served the leadership. Because some later testified against her, they belong both to her working network and to the prosecution’s evidence network.

### Melchor Castelo

A subordinate correspondent who addressed her respectfully and reported compliance with her instruction in November 1780.

### Marcos de la Torre and other local commanders

They sent reports to her concerning men, weapons, and regional conditions, demonstrating her position within the command structure.

### Antonio López de Sosa

The priest who married the couple and reportedly remained associated with them during the rebellion.

### Antonio de Arriaga

Corregidor of Tinta and principal initial enemy. His capture and execution transformed the movement from organized grievance and conspiracy into open rebellion.

### José Antonio de Areche

Royal visitador and superintendent. He directed the repression, signed the exemplary sentences, and represented Micaela as a principal author and executor of rebellion.

### Benito de la Mata Linares

Royal audiencia judge who conducted her interrogation, witness confrontations, and preliminary criminal proceeding.

### Pablo Figueroa

Prosecuting official who sought a death designed to cause public “terror and fright.”

### Gregorio Murillo

Court-appointed defense lawyer. Appointed on 4 May 1781, only days before judgment, he argued on her behalf within a process controlled by the royal commission.

### Mateo García Pumacahua

Curaca of Chinchero and royalist military opponent. There is no documented personal meeting, but his forces helped defeat the rebellion.

### Francisco Cisneros and Manuel de San Roque

Prisoners or witnesses whose testimony portrayed her as more rigorous than her husband, accused her of coercion, and described her support for armed mobilization.

### Francisco Santa Cruz, Ventura Landaeta, and Antonio Martínez

Named in later accounts as betrayers or facilitators of the insurgent leaders’ capture. Their respective roles are reported differently and should not be treated as one uncontested act.

## Trial, Sentence, and Execution

## Interrogation: 22 April 1781

Micaela appeared before Benito de la Mata Linares in Cusco. Asked her identity, origin, age, status, and reason for detention, she called herself Micaela Bastidas and said she was from Pampamarca. The age entry is incomplete or imprecise in surviving transcription, describing her as over twenty rather than recording the conventional thirty-six.

She denied:

- advance knowledge of her husband’s plan;
- knowing many of the alleged collaborators;
- incriminating communications attributed to her;
- aspects of the alleged Lima and Cusco networks.

She frequently answered that her husband might know. She did not disclose the names or positions sought by interrogators.

Witness confrontations were held with Esteban Escarcena, Diego Ortigosa, Francisco Túpac Amaru, Francisco Cisneros, Andrea Esquivel, and others. In several, the witness maintained an accusation while Micaela maintained her denial.

### Torture

Secondary narratives commonly say Micaela and José Gabriel were tortured for intelligence. The consulted judicial extracts conclusively show coercive imprisonment, an expedited capital proceeding, confrontations, and a predetermined exemplary objective. They do not, in the passages inspected, provide a precise session-by-session record of physical torture inflicted on her before execution. Physical torture before trial should therefore be classified as **widely reported but not specifically reconstructed here from a primary protocol**.

## Prosecution

Pablo Figueroa accused her of being a principal author and executor of rebellion, causing enormous royal expenses and innumerable deaths. He asked for an aggravated death that would create “terror” and serve as an example.

The prosecution alleged that she:

- issued rigorous orders to gather people;
- invaded or subjected provinces;
- ordered death for noncompliance;
- aspired to reign;
- disregarded excommunication;
- publicized victories;
- aimed to free the kingdom from taxes and burdens;
- mobilized people with money, coca, and food.

## Defense

On 4 May Gregorio Murillo was appointed her defense counsel. The compressed schedule—appointment on 4 May and sentence later that month—limited the possibility of a prolonged defense. The legal formality did not produce an independent tribunal or meaningful appellate review.

## Sentence

Areche condemned her to death. The written order required that she be:

1. taken from the prison;
2. dragged with an esparto rope around her neck, hands and feet tied;
3. accompanied by a herald announcing her crime;
4. seated on a platform and killed by garrote;
5. subjected to removal of the tongue;
6. displayed on the gallows;
7. dismembered;
8. deprived of her property.

Her head was to be displayed at Piccho/Callanca. Limbs were assigned to Tungasuca and Arequipa; another distribution list records the dispersal of remains across rebel territory.

## 18 May 1781

Micaela, José Gabriel, Hipólito, Tomasa Tito Condemayta, Antonio Bastidas, Francisco Túpac Amaru, Diego Berdejo/Verdejo, Andrés Castelo, Antonio Oblitas, and other condemned people were taken to Cusco’s principal square.

She was made to witness executions including that of Hipólito. José Gabriel and the surviving child Fernando were present.

### Contradictory execution details

The sources disagree on the timing of mutilation:

- **Official execution certification:** says her tongue was cut out, the garrote killed her, and she was then hanged.
- **Another contemporary relation:** says she refused to extend her tongue and it was cut out after death.
- **Detailed contemporary narrative:** says her neck was too thin for the new iron garrote to work; executioners tightened ropes from both sides and kicked her stomach and breasts until she died.

The points shared across the principal accounts are execution by garrote/strangulation, public display, dismemberment, and distribution of remains. The resistance, failed mechanism, ropes, and kicks are contemporaneously reported, not merely a modern legend, but belong to narrative relations rather than the terse official certification.

Her body was dismembered that afternoon. Portions of the bodies of Micaela and José Gabriel were burned at Piccho; remains were sent to localities for exemplary display.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, and Financial Questions

## 1. Treason and rebellion against the Crown

- **Formal charge and court finding:** Principal authorship and execution of *lesa majestad*—rebellion against the king, kingdom, and Cusco.
- **Disposition:** Convicted and executed.
- **Modern legal status:** This was a colonial political judgment, not an unresolved accusation. Peru later officially recognized her as a precursor, patriot, and martyr.

## 2. Threats and capital punishment for noncompliance

Francisco Cisneros testified that she issued written and oral orders more rigorously than José Gabriel and directed that people who disobeyed or spoke against the rebellion suffer death and loss of property.

- **Evidentiary status:** Sworn hostile witness testimony incorporated into a capital prosecution.
- **Corroboration:** Surviving documents establish her authority to order recruitment and punish disobedience; the exact number of people personally condemned or killed under her instructions is not established.
- **Defense position:** She denied broad responsibility and many attributed conversations.
- **Finding:** Coercive commands are credible within a civil war and are reflected in the accusation, but no reliable victim-by-victim list can be assigned directly to her from the consulted record.

## 3. Failure to restrain attacks

Manuel de San Roque alleged that complaints about abuses by insurgents against Spaniards and mestizos were presented but Micaela did not remedy them.

- **Evidentiary status:** Witness allegation.
- **Context:** The rebellion’s early leadership sometimes tried to limit uncontrolled looting and retain non-Indigenous support. Control deteriorated as the conflict spread.
- **Unresolved:** Whether she knowingly approved particular abuses or lacked the means to stop them cannot be determined globally.

## 4. Plan to collect or eliminate Europeans

Witnesses attributed to her statements that Europeans had to be “collected” or removed so the insurgent ruler could reign. Later narratives portray the rebellion as an indiscriminate racial war.

- **Evidentiary status:** Contested witness testimony; she did not admit it.
- **Documentary context:** Rebel proclamations sometimes distinguished American-born people from peninsular Spaniards and threatened hostile Europeans. Other appeals sought support across caste lines.
- **Finding:** Anti-European policy existed within parts of the insurgent movement, but a single, stable extermination program authored by Micaela is not proven.

## 5. Responsibility for wartime deaths

The prosecutor attributed “innumerable” deaths and royal expenses to her as a principal leader.

- **Evidentiary status:** Formal prosecutorial attribution and judicial finding of collective responsibility.
- **Limitation:** It does not establish that she personally ordered every killing committed by dispersed rebels.

## 6. Aspiration to become queen

Royalist witnesses and the sentence represented Micaela as aspiring to reign and being obeyed more rigorously than her husband.

- **Evidentiary status:** Prosecution allegation supported by disputed testimony.
- **Interpretive possibility:** This may record a genuine insurgent monarchy project, royalist fear of female sovereignty, or both.
- **No documentary proof:** No authenticated proclamation in which Micaela personally declares herself queen was found.

## 7. Birthplace controversy

This is the principal continuing civic controversy. Cusco/Canas advocates cite her 1781 confession and their reading of the marriage record. Apurímac/Tamburco advocates cite the “natural de Abancay” listing, family geography, and local historical tradition.

The dispute has affected legislation, public monuments, regional ceremonies, tourism, and the identity of institutions named after her. Congress avoided fixing the birthplace in its 2017 final formulation.

## 8. Racial identity

“Mestiza,” “chola,” “zamba,” “Indigenous,” “Quechua,” “Afro-Indigenous,” and “criolla” appear across different periods.

- **Documented fact:** Her maternal surname and Quechua language connect her to the Andean social world.
- **Reported interpretation:** Her father was of African or mixed African-Spanish descent.
- **Contrary documentary classification:** The marriage record has been read as classifying her parents as Spaniards or criollos.
- **Finding:** Modern identity labels capture different aspects of a socially fluid and politically charged colonial record. Exact biological ancestry remains incompletely documented.

## 9. Wealth and status

Heroic narratives sometimes portray her as born in extreme poverty, whereas the married household had curaca status, transport assets, houses, livestock, and silver goods.

- **Finding:** A poor childhood is possible but insufficiently documented. As an adult she belonged to a locally elite or prosperous Indigenous/mestizo household, though one exposed to commercial debt, colonial taxation, and political litigation.

## 10. No evidence found for modern-style scandals

The adversarial search produced no credible evidence of:

- personal embezzlement;
- commercial fraud;
- money laundering;
- secret foreign intelligence employment;
- sexual scandal;
- ordinary civil litigation in her own name;
- private corruption unrelated to the insurgency.

The absence is unsurprising for a woman who died in 1781 and whose surviving papers were preserved principally because of a treason prosecution.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## “The true strategist whom her husband ignored”

**Story:** Micaela alone understood that the rebels had to seize Cusco immediately after Sangarará; José Gabriel ignored her, thereby losing the revolution.

**Appearance and circulation:** Built from her authentic letters and repeated by nationalist, feminist, popular, and academic histories.

**Evidence for:** Her letters undeniably and repeatedly urged speed and criticized his delays.

**Evidence against or limiting:** The certainty that an immediate attack would have succeeded is counterfactual. The rebels faced problems of supply, discipline, urban alliances, geography, and unclear support.

**Reputational effect:** Repositioned her from “wife of Túpac Amaru” to autonomous strategist and has sometimes reduced José Gabriel to the role of indecisive commander.

## “More implacable than Túpac Amaru”

**Story:** She was fiercer, more rigorous, or more commanding than her husband.

**Origin:** Statements by Francisco Cisneros and other prosecution witnesses; language in the sentence emphasizing her rigor and “masculine” resolve.

**Evidence:** She issued independent commands and could be severe. The comparison rests heavily on hostile witnesses and gendered expectations.

**Reputational effect:** Royalist propaganda used it to portray her as unnatural and dangerous. Later commemorative narratives inverted the same image into evidence of extraordinary leadership.

## The “thin neck” and failed garrote

**Story:** The garrote could not close sufficiently around her narrow neck; executioners used ropes and kicks.

**Origin:** Contemporary accounts of the 18 May execution, later reproduced in histories.

**Evidence:** A detailed contemporary relation in the documentary collection supports it; the official certification records only death by garrote.

**Assessment:** Reported contemporary fact with some procedural details uncorroborated by the terse certificate—not a purely modern myth.

## Last words about patria, equality, and liberty

A quotation circulated online and in popular biographies has Micaela lamenting that she would not see her sons grow because of her sacrifice for “patria,” “igualdad,” and “libertad.”

**Evidence:** No authenticated contemporary execution record consulted contains those words.

**Assessment:** Unverified or apocryphal quotation, probably a later patriotic construction.

**Reputational effect:** It translates the rebellion into the vocabulary of later republican nationalism and gender equality.

## Romantic “love letter” attributed to Túpac Amaru

A purported letter dated January 1782 describes love, “revolutionary” men, nature, and idealism.

**Evidence against:** José Gabriel died in May 1781. A letter dated January 1782 from him is chronologically impossible unless misdated, fictional, or posthumously composed. Its modern ideological diction is also anomalous.

**Assessment:** False or literary attribution. It should not be included in the genuine correspondence.

## “Mother of Peruvian independence”

**Story:** Micaela and José Gabriel began the national independence movement later completed by San Martín and Bolívar.

**Evidence:** Their uprising challenged colonial authority on an immense scale and became a precedent remembered during the independence era.

**Qualification:** The insurgents’ language was not identical to the later republican project; some texts invoked loyalty to the king. Direct organizational continuity with the campaigns of the 1820s is not established.

**Assessment:** A consequential retrospective national interpretation, not a literal contemporary office or title.

## African ancestry as settled genealogy

**Story:** Manuel Bastidas was definitively African and Micaela therefore definitively “zamba.”

**Evidence:** Repeated modern historical descriptions and the colonial use of racialized terms in accounts of the family.

**Counterevidence:** The marriage document’s reported classification of her parents as Spaniards/criollos and the absence of a fully demonstrated paternal genealogy.

**Assessment:** Plausible and widely accepted, but not established with the precision often implied.

## Royalist “monstrous woman” mythology

Colonial descriptions emphasized that she commanded with greater rigor than a man, ignored ecclesiastical authority, and desired to reign. Female political command was treated as an inversion of the expected social order.

**Evidence:** This language appears in judicial and testimonial material.

**Effect:** It helped justify an execution intended explicitly to terrorize spectators. Modern scholarship has studied how the same hostile archive inadvertently preserves evidence of her agency.

## Local birthplace traditions

Pampamarca and Tamburco both maintain physical, ceremonial, and family traditions presenting themselves as her true birthplace.

**Assessment:** Historically important civic memory. Neither a memorial house nor sustained oral tradition independently overrides contradictory eighteenth-century records.

## Works by Micaela Bastidas

Micaela was not an author in the conventional print sense. Her documentary corpus consists of dictated letters, commands, notices, passes, and safe-conducts transmitted through scribes. Authorship here means intellectual or administrative responsibility, not handwriting.

### Genuine or substantially authenticated documentary corpus

1. **Letters to José Gabriel Condorcanqui/Túpac Amaru II**, 23 November 1780–23 March 1781.  
   Approximately nineteen letters are commonly identified for this period. They concern security, poisoning fears, recruitment, propaganda, food, weapons, desertion, family danger, and the advance on Cusco. Published in documentary collections of the rebellion.

2. **Letter/order to Governor José Torres**, 1780.  
   Calls for immediate mobilization, enumerates provinces claimed for the cause, and frames the objective as eliminating harmful corregidores and burdens.

3. **Letters and orders to caciques, provincial authorities, commanders, and clergy**, 1780–1781.  
   These include recruitment instructions, requests for arms and supplies, intelligence communications, and political appeals.

4. **Passes and safe-conducts**, 1780–1781.  
   Issued under her authority to regulate movement through insurgent territory.

5. **Administrative and supply orders**, 1780–1781.  
   Payments and distributions involving silver, coca, food, animals, clothing, ammunition, and personnel.

6. **Messages received and annotated within her command network**, including correspondence with Melchor Castelo, Marcos de la Torre, Pedro Venero, Justo Gallegos, Fernando Gurpide, Agustín Aucagualpa, and others.

7. **Confesión de Micaela Bastidas**, 22 April 1781.  
   A judicially mediated oral testimony recorded by a colonial scribe. It is her speech under interrogation, not a voluntarily composed memoir.

8. **Careos and supplementary answers**, April–May 1781.  
   Recorded confrontations with witnesses. Again, these are judicial transcripts rather than authored essays.

### Publication history

The principal modern documentary publication is the **Colección Documental de la Independencia del Perú**, series *La rebelión de Túpac Amaru*, beginning in 1971 under the editorship/introduction of Carlos Daniel Valcárcel and collaborators. The Peruvian Congress’s bicentennial **Nueva Colección Documental de la Independencia del Perú** reproduces the rebellion, trial, and execution materials in searchable volumes.

The Biblioteca Nacional del Perú catalogs, for example, **“Cartas de y a Micaela Bastidas,” 9–12 December 1780**, as a documentary unit published in volume 2, pages 340–345.

### Disputed, apocryphal, or posthumous material

- The romantic “1782” letter from Túpac Amaru to Micaela is impossible as dated and should be treated as apocryphal.
- Modern monologues placed in her voice—especially in historical novels and plays—are literary reconstructions.
- Patriotic last words attributed to her are not established by the primary execution documents consulted.
- Modern compilations titled *Micaela Bastidas* are edited studies about her, not books authored by her.

## Death and Immediate Aftermath

Micaela died at approximately thirty-six years of age. No burial site containing an intact body exists: the state deliberately dismembered, burned, dispersed, and displayed her remains.

The punishment was intended to accomplish several objectives:

- kill the insurgent command;
- obtain or suppress information;
- terrorize potential supporters;
- desecrate the leaders’ political bodies;
- eliminate dynastic claims;
- confiscate family resources;
- erase the name and status of Túpac Amaru.

The objective was not fully achieved. Diego Cristóbal Túpac Amaru and other leaders continued the rebellion. Further campaigns, negotiations, executions, deportations, and forced marches followed through 1782–1783.

Her children’s fates extended the punishment across generations. Fernando’s petitions from Spanish confinement repeatedly argued that birth alone had been treated as his crime.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Colonial suppression

After the uprising, authorities attacked Inca political symbolism, clothing, genealogical claims, public performances, and cultural practices associated with insurgent mobilization. The sentence attempted to make the family name infamous.

### Nineteenth-century nationalism

Republican histories gradually repositioned the condemned rebels as precursors of independence. This interpretation linked the 1780 uprising to the later struggles associated with San Martín and Bolívar, even though there was no personal contact and the political programs differed.

Micaela was frequently subordinated in these narratives as “the wife of Túpac Amaru.” Her own letters and trial record later enabled a recovery of her independent command.

### Twentieth-century reinterpretation

Indigenismo, labor politics, agrarian reform, regional nationalism, and women’s history all adopted her as a symbol. Representations emphasized different aspects:

- Indigenous and anti-colonial resistance;
- Afro-Andean ancestry;
- peasant and agrarian struggle;
- national independence;
- women’s military and political leadership;
- motherhood and family sacrifice;
- strategic intelligence.

The Lima monument of 1967–1969 became associated with vindication of women’s rights and the political culture surrounding agrarian reform.

### Official recognition

- **Law No. 21705 (1976):** established the historical importance of commemorating the bicentenary of the Túpac Amaru and Micaela Bastidas rebellion.
- **Law No. 23225 (4 November 1980):** officially recognized Micaela Bastidas as precursor, prócer, and martyr of Peruvian emancipation alongside José Gabriel Condorcanqui.
- **Universidad Nacional Micaela Bastidas de Apurímac:** created in 2000 and named in her honor.
- **District of Micaela Bastidas:** one of the districts of Grau Province, Apurímac.
- Numerous schools, women’s organizations, streets, plazas, and public programs bear her name.

### Twenty-first-century memory

The 2020–2021 LUM exhibition emphasized the absence of an authenticated portrait and the many artistic faces created for her.

The recovery and 2025 publication of Fernando Túpac Amaru’s letters renewed attention to the destruction and exile of her family. A symbolic repatriation of material associated with Fernando to Peru in 2025 was staged through ritual and civic commemoration.

As recently as 2024–2026, public institutions in Tamburco and Apurímac continued to identify her strongly with that region, while Cusco/Canas institutions maintained the Pampamarca claim. The birthplace dispute is therefore part of her active political geography, not merely an antiquarian disagreement.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by consulted sources are listed. Shared geography, similarity, or later inclusion in the same patriotic canon is not treated as a personal connection.

### José Gabriel Condorcanqui Noguera

**Evidentiary label: Documented fact.**

Micaela married José Gabriel Condorcanqui—Túpac Amaru II—at Surimana on 25 May 1760. They had three sons, participated in the household transport and curaca economy, jointly led the 1780–1781 rebellion, exchanged strategic and personal correspondence, were captured in April 1781, tried by royal authorities, and executed in Cusco on 18 May 1781.

This is the central relationship of her documented adult life. Her letters show both emotional intimacy and independent political judgment.

### Simón Bolívar

**Evidentiary label: Posthumous ideological association, not personal contact.**

Micaela died approximately forty-two years before Bolívar entered Peru. They did not meet or correspond. Later Peruvian nationalist histories placed both within a continuous genealogy of continental emancipation. A reported 1825 letter from the surviving Túpac Amaru family network to Bolívar concerns the family’s later search for recognition, but it is not a connection involving Micaela during her lifetime.

### José de San Martín

**Evidentiary label: Posthumous ideological association, not personal contact.**

Micaela died decades before San Martín’s Peruvian campaign. Later histories and commemorations classified her as a precursor whose rebellion preceded the 1820–1821 independence process. No personal or organizational connection has been established.

### Atahualpa

**Evidentiary label: Dynastic-symbolic association through the Inca past, not a personal connection.**

Atahualpa died more than two centuries before Micaela’s birth. The Túpac Amaru movement used Inca legitimacy and memory, but no direct genealogical relationship between Micaela and Atahualpa is established. General claims that “Inca blood” ran in her veins often conflate her ancestry with José Gabriel’s asserted descent from Túpac Amaru I.

### El Inca Garcilaso de la Vega

**Evidentiary label: Reported indirect intellectual influence through José Gabriel; no direct evidence for Micaela.**

Biographies of José Gabriel state that he knew the *Comentarios reales*. No source consulted proves that Micaela read, heard, or explicitly cited Garcilaso. Her inability to sign makes direct literary influence possible through oral transmission but undocumented.

### Francisco Pizarro González

**Evidentiary label: Later symbolic opposition only.**

Pizarro belonged to the foundational history of Spanish conquest opposed by later Inca-restoration narratives, but he died long before Micaela’s birth. No direct family, documentary, or specifically acknowledged intellectual connection was found.

### Other roster members

No source consulted documented a meeting, correspondence, collaboration, rivalry, family relationship, patronage bond, shared institution, or explicit acknowledged influence involving Micaela and any other named atlas subject. In particular, mere later comparison with Manuela Sáenz, Juana Azurduy, Policarpa Salavarrieta, María Remedios del Valle, or other women commemorated as independence figures does not establish a lifetime connection.
