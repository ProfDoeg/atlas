## Family and Ancestry

Heidegger was the first child of **Friedrich Heidegger** (1851–1924), a master cooper and sexton of St Martin’s Catholic church in Meßkirch, and **Johanna Heidegger, née Kempf** (1858–1927). Both families were rooted in rural Catholic Baden. His siblings were **Marie** (1892–1956) and **Fritz** (1894–1980). Fritz, a bank employee, later typed and discussed many manuscripts and became an important familial interlocutor.

The household was respectable but financially limited. Church service gave Martin access to ecclesiastical scholarships and schools. The bell tower, liturgy, Alemannic landscape and craft vocabulary later became elements of his autobiographical geography; how far these memories illuminate rather than stylize his formation is interpretive.

On 21 March 1917 Heidegger married **Elfride Petri** (1893–1992), a Lutheran from a Prussian military family, in a Catholic ceremony performed by Engelbert Krebs; a Protestant ceremony followed. Their marriage was intellectually and sexually non-exclusive in practice, though its precise agreements changed and remain incompletely documented. Their household included:

- **Jörg Heidegger** (1919–2019), generally treated as Martin’s biological son.
- **Hermann Heidegger** (1920–2020), raised and legally acknowledged by Martin. Family correspondence published much later establishes that his biological father was the physician and family friend **Friedel Caesar**; this was not publicly known during Martin’s life. Hermann became a historian, soldier and later executor/editor of his father’s estate.

Documented or strongly evidenced extramarital relationships include the philosopher **Hannah Arendt**, the educator **Elisabeth Blochmann**, and other relationships discussed in correspondence and biographies. The evidence does not justify reducing each intellectual friendship to a sexual one.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1889–1909

Heidegger attended local primary and civic schools in Meßkirch. In 1903 he entered the humanistic Gymnasium in Konstanz with church support and lived at the **Konradihaus**, an archiepiscopal seminary. From 1906 he attended the Berthold-Gymnasium in Freiburg and lived in the archiepiscopal seminary there. His intended vocation was the priesthood.

A decisive early book was Franz Brentano’s dissertation **Von der mannigfachen Bedeutung des Seienden nach Aristoteles** (1862), reportedly given to him in 1907 by the future archbishop Conrad Gröber. The question of the multiple meanings and unity of “being” supplied Heidegger with a retrospective origin story for his lifelong problem. Aristotle, scholasticism, Thomas Aquinas, Duns Scotus, Catholic apologetics and German idealism formed the initial horizon.

## Education and Formation, 1909–1916

### Theology, illness, and change of course

In September 1909 Heidegger entered the Jesuit novitiate at Tisis near Feldkirch but was released after roughly two weeks, reportedly because of a heart condition. He then enrolled in Catholic theology at Freiburg. In 1911 another health episode interrupted training; he abandoned the clerical course and shifted toward philosophy, mathematics and natural science. Later portrayals sometimes turn this into a clean “break with Catholicism.” The record instead shows a gradual intellectual and institutional separation: Catholic scholarship and theology remained structurally important even after confessional orthodoxy became untenable for him.

### Degrees and mentors

- **1913 doctorate:** *Die Lehre vom Urteil im Psychologismus. Ein kritisch-positiver Beitrag zur Logik* under Arthur Schneider; the work opposed psychologism and engaged neo-Kantian logic and Husserl.
- **1915 habilitation:** *Die Kategorien- und Bedeutungslehre des Duns Scotus*, supervised by Heinrich Rickert and associated with the Catholic philosopher Martin Honecker. The text treated a work then attributed to Duns Scotus, now assigned substantially to Thomas of Erfurt. The historical misattribution was conventional at the time, not Heidegger’s invention.
- **1915 inaugural lecture:** *Der Zeitbegriff in der Geschichtswissenschaft*.

Principal formative figures were **Franz Brentano**, Aristotle, Augustine, Luther, Kierkegaard, Dilthey, Rickert, Emil Lask and especially **Edmund Husserl**, appointed to Freiburg in 1916. Heidegger’s path led from neo-scholastic and neo-Kantian problems through Husserlian phenomenology toward a hermeneutics of “factical life.”

### Military service

Heidegger was called up in 1915, initially assigned to postal and weather duties, and served in a meteorological unit near the western front in 1918. His cardiac classification limited combat service. Claims that he was a front-line fighting soldier are unsupported. He was discharged after the Armistice.

## Early Career, 1916–1923

Heidegger became Husserl’s assistant in Freiburg in 1919. Early lecture courses on phenomenology, religion, Aristotle and factical life developed a style that made him a celebrated teacher before he possessed a major book. He corresponded with Karl Jaspers after their 1920 meeting and shared a project of renewing philosophy against academic system-building. His students included Karl Löwith, Hans-Georg Gadamer, Leo Strauss, Günther Anders and Herbert Marcuse.

Around 1918–19 he told Engelbert Krebs that his epistemological convictions made the “system of Catholicism” problematic or unacceptable, while insisting that Christianity and metaphysics remained important. This letter is stronger evidence than later legends of either an abrupt atheistic conversion or an unchanged secret Catholicism.

In 1922 he wrote the “Natorp Report,” an extraordinarily compressed prospectus on Aristotle, to support appointment efforts. It helped secure an associate professorship at **Marburg**, beginning in 1923. Elfride arranged construction of the small mountain hut at **Todtnauberg** in the Black Forest; it became Heidegger’s writing retreat and a central element of his public image.

## Chronological Life History

### Marburg, Arendt, and *Being and Time*, 1923–1928

At Marburg Heidegger worked alongside the Protestant theologian **Rudolf Bultmann**, participated in seminars on Paul and Greek thought, and influenced theologians as well as philosophers. His intense lectures stressed practical involvement, finitude, temporality and the historical destruction (*Destruktion*) of inherited ontology.

In 1924 the eighteen-year-old student **Hannah Arendt** met the married thirty-five-year-old professor. Their clandestine affair is documented by surviving letters and lasted in its first phase roughly from 1925 to 1926/28. The pedagogical power imbalance and secrecy are facts; claims about coercion are interpretive and no criminal allegation or proceeding is known. Heidegger encouraged her intellectual work but arranged meetings covertly. Arendt moved to Heidelberg and completed her doctorate with Jaspers. They renewed contact in 1950, and their emotional-intellectual relationship, with long gaps, continued until shortly before Heidegger’s death. Elfride eventually knew of it.

Heidegger also maintained a close correspondence with **Elisabeth Blochmann**, a German-Jewish educator and Elfride’s friend; their relationship became erotic by 1929. He later helped her indirectly while she was in exile and renewed the friendship after the war.

**Sein und Zeit** appeared in Husserl’s *Jahrbuch für Philosophie und phänomenologische Forschung* in February 1927 and as a separate volume. Published under pressure to qualify for a chair, it was dedicated to Husserl “in reverence and friendship.” It announced a two-part project but printed only the first two divisions of Part I. Heidegger never completed the promised remainder. The book analyzed *Dasein* as being-in-the-world: care, equipment, being-with, thrownness, projection, anxiety, conscience, authenticity, being-toward-death, historicity and temporality. It rapidly transformed German philosophy.

### Freiburg succession and international prominence, 1928–1932

Heidegger succeeded Husserl in Freiburg in 1928. His 1929 inaugural lecture, **Was ist Metaphysik?**, treated the nothing and anxiety; Carnap later attacked its language as exemplary metaphysical pseudo-statement. In March–April 1929 the **Davos disputation** set Heidegger against Ernst Cassirer over Kant, finitude and Enlightenment humanism. *Kant und das Problem der Metaphysik* followed in 1929.

Relations with Husserl deteriorated over philosophical divergence, professional competition and personal grievance. The claim that Heidegger, as rector, personally barred Husserl from the university library is not supported by a surviving Heidegger order and is generally treated as legend; anti-Jewish state rules affected retired Jewish academics. Heidegger did, however, fail publicly to defend Husserl, and the 1941 edition of *Being and Time* omitted the printed dedication under publisher pressure, while retaining the footnote acknowledging Husserl’s contribution.

### Nazi accession and rectorate, 1933–1934

The verified sequence is unusually important:

- 21 April 1933: elected rector of Freiburg after the Social Democrat Wilhelm von Möllendorff resigned under political pressure.
- 1 May: joined the NSDAP. He remained a dues-paying member until 1945.
- 27 May: delivered **Die Selbstbehauptung der deutschen Universität**, tying university “self-assertion,” labor, military and knowledge services to the historical mission of the German people and new state.
- Summer–autumn: participated in Gleichschaltung, promoted a leader principle within university reform, attended Party-linked events, and issued political addresses supporting Hitler and the new order.
- 3 November: a student-aid appeal excluded “Jewish or Marxist” students and those deemed non-Aryan under the regime’s rules while privileging SA/SS and other service. Whether this circular originated nationally does not remove Heidegger’s official transmission of it.
- 11 November: signed/spoke within the Leipzig professors’ electoral rally supporting Hitler’s withdrawal from the League of Nations and plebiscite.
- December: wrote a damaging political assessment of former student Eduard Baumgarten, mentioning his association with “the Jew Fränkel.” Baumgarten survived professionally; the letter later entered denazification evidence.

Heidegger intervened in some individual cases, including support for certain Jewish or politically vulnerable students and colleagues. These interventions are documented but coexist with his implementation and advocacy of the regime’s transformation. Evidence does **not** establish that he joined the SA or SS, participated in genocide, served an intelligence agency, or committed a prosecutable war crime.

He resigned the rectorate effective 23/27 April 1934. His later explanation stressed conflict with Party officials over deans and his attempt to protect academic autonomy. Archival scholarship shows real conflicts, but disputes his presentation of the year as fundamentally a protective maneuver. He did not leave the Party, publicly repudiate Hitler, or cease all ideologically aligned language.

### 1934–1945: teaching under the dictatorship

Heidegger resumed ordinary professorial work. Party ideologues distrusted his opaque philosophy; he was not a leading official philosopher of the Reich. Yet marginalization within Nazi institutions is not equivalent to resistance. He lectured on Hölderlin, Nietzsche, metaphysics and the history of Being. In the 1935 course later published as *Einführung in die Metaphysik*, he referred to the “inner truth and greatness” of the movement. The 1953 edition added a parenthesis identifying this with the encounter between global technology and modern humanity; whether that gloss reflects the 1935 meaning is disputed.

In 1936 Heidegger met former student **Karl Löwith**, a Jewish émigré, in Rome while wearing a swastika badge. Löwith later reported that Heidegger linked his political commitment to his philosophy and still expressed confidence in Hitler. This is a contemporaneous-participant memoir written in 1940, not a court finding, but is important counterevidence to the claimed 1934 break.

The private **Schwarze Hefte** from the 1930s and 1940s, published beginning in 2014, contain multiple antisemitic passages. They associate “world Jewry,” calculation, uprootedness and modernity within Heidegger’s history-of-Being narrative, while also attacking Christianity, liberalism, Bolshevism, Americanism and vulgar Nazi racial biology. The existence and wording of the passages are documented. Scholars disagree over their philosophical centrality: Peter Trawny speaks of “being-historical antisemitism”; Donatella Di Cesare and Emmanuel Faye argue for deep implication; Friedrich-Wilhelm von Herrmann, François Fédier and others resist treating the entire philosophy as antisemitic. The notebooks defeat the older claim that Heidegger harbored no antisemitism, but do not by themselves settle how every published concept functions.

Heidegger’s response to the Holocaust was sparse and indirect. In a 1949 Bremen lecture he placed mechanized agriculture, atomic weapon manufacture, and the production of corpses in gas chambers/extermination camps within the same technological essence. The comparison has been read as recognition of technological extermination, as leveling victim and process, or as evasion. He never issued an explicit public apology for his political engagement or a sustained public analysis of Jewish persecution.

Near the war’s end he was briefly assigned to Volkssturm labor/fortification work; the university had earlier protected prominent faculty from full deployment. Freiburg was bombed, and manuscripts were moved for safety.

### Collapse, denazification, and psychological crisis, 1945–1951

French occupation authorities and Freiburg committees investigated Heidegger. He submitted apologetic accounts emphasizing resignation and philosophical opposition. **Karl Jaspers**, asked for an assessment, distinguished Heidegger’s intellectual stature from his political unreliability and recommended temporary exclusion from teaching while allowing pension and writing. The commission ultimately prohibited him from teaching; he was classified in the denazification process in a manner that allowed retirement support but not immediate instruction.

In winter 1945–46 Heidegger suffered a documented mental/psychosomatic collapse and received treatment at the sanatorium of psychiatrist **Viktor von Gebsattel** in Badenweiler. Biographers describe depression or nervous breakdown; a precise retrospective diagnosis is not available. No evidence establishes addiction.

French philosopher **Jean Beaufret** sent questions that elicited the 1946 **Brief über den Humanismus**, in which Heidegger distinguished his thought from Sartrean existentialism and rearticulated the “truth of Being,” language as the house of Being, and humanism. Private seminars and contacts resumed. Arendt visited in 1950; reconciliation involved Elfride and helped restore international connections. In 1951 the teaching ban ended and Heidegger became emeritus, giving influential lectures.

### Later career, 1951–1976

The postwar works developed technology as **Gestell** (enframing), dwelling and the fourfold, poetic language, *Gelassenheit* (releasement), and **Ereignis** (appropriating event). Major lectures included “Building Dwelling Thinking,” “The Thing,” “What Is Called Thinking?,” “The Question Concerning Technology,” “The Principle of Reason,” and “Time and Being.” He held seminars with **Eugen Fink**, **Medard Boss**, French poets and philosophers, including sessions at Le Thor hosted by **René Char**.

The Zollikon seminars with psychiatrist Medard Boss (1959–69) applied phenomenological questioning to psychiatry and psychotherapy, while Heidegger criticized Ludwig Binswanger’s use of *Being and Time*. There was no patented technology, commercial company or conventional invention attached to Heidegger; “technology” was an object of ontological analysis, not engineering practice.

He traveled to Greece in 1962 and 1967 after earlier imagined/aborted encounters with Greece, and visited Provence repeatedly (1966, 1968, 1969). He received visitors at Freiburg and Todtnauberg, among them poets, architects, theologians and philosophers. In 1967 the poet **Paul Celan**, whose parents were murdered in the Holocaust, visited the hut and wrote “Todtnauberg.” The poem’s “hope, today, for a thinker’s coming word” has commonly been understood as hoping for a reckoning, though the exact conversation is unknown. Claims that Celan received either a full apology or a definitive refusal are not documented.

On 23 September 1966 Heidegger gave Rudolf Augstein and Georg Wolff a long **Der Spiegel** interview on condition of posthumous publication. “Nur noch ein Gott kann uns retten” defended the rectorate as an effort to save the university, acknowledged an initial “awakening,” claimed disillusionment, and framed the planetary problem as technology. It appeared 31 May 1976, five days after his death. Because Heidegger stipulated delayed publication and helped shape the record, it is a primary source for his late self-defense, not an independent adjudication.

He arranged the **Gesamtausgabe** with Vittorio Klostermann as an edition “of last hand,” ordered by thematic series rather than strict chronology. Publication began in 1975. Editorial omissions, silent variants and family control produced a continuing textual controversy.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

Heidegger’s economic life was that of a German professor rather than an entrepreneur. Income came from salary, pension, lecture fees and royalties. Relevant institutions were the Archdiocese of Freiburg (school support), Universities of Freiburg and Marburg, NSDAP, Vittorio Klostermann publishing house, Neske and Niemeyer publishers, and the German Literature Archive.

His significant property was the family house in Freiburg-Zähringen and the Todtnauberg hut/land arranged by Elfride. No reliable evidence reviewed for this dossier establishes substantial businesses, stockholdings, trusts, patents, corrupt payments, money laundering or litigation over commercial interests. Exact wealth figures are unavailable. Posthumous publication rights and archive access were administered by the family, especially Hermann and later Arnulf Heidegger.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Religious trajectory

1. **Catholic formation (1889–c.1917):** altar and sexton’s household, seminary education, theology, scholastic dissertation.
2. **Break with Catholic system (c.1918–19):** rejection of binding neo-scholastic epistemology; continued engagement with Augustine, Paul, Luther and religious experience.
3. **Methodological atheism/phenomenology:** early courses treated philosophy as independent of confessional theology.
4. **Later language of gods and the holy:** Hölderlin, “the last god,” withdrawal and the “god beyond metaphysics.” This is not an evidenced return to Catholic doctrine.
5. **Death rites:** Catholic burial after conversations with Bernhard Welte. Whether this signifies personal reconciliation with the Church is unresolved.

### Philosophical trajectory

- **Early:** logic, psychologism, categories, medieval ontology.
- **Factical phenomenology:** pre-theoretical life, hermeneutics, Aristotle, temporality.
- ***Being and Time*:** fundamental ontology through Dasein; destruction of metaphysics; finitude.
- **After 1929 / the “turn” (*Kehre*):** truth as unconcealment, Being’s historical disclosure, artwork, Hölderlin, Nietzsche and nihilism. Heidegger denied a simple reversal; scholars debate continuity.
- **Late:** technological enframing, language, dwelling, fourfold, releasement, Ereignis, another beginning.

### Political development

Before 1933 Heidegger was culturally conservative, anti-liberal and anti-modern in important respects but not known as a Party activist. In 1933 he committed publicly and institutionally to National Socialism and imagined a spiritual transformation deeper than ordinary Party doctrine. After the failed rectorate he withdrew from high office but retained membership and elements of revolutionary-national and civilizational diagnosis. After 1945 he criticized modernity, technology, Americanism, Soviet communism and mass democracy in ontological terms, not through a public democratic conversion or explicit recantation.

### Intellectual lines to the figures named in the brief

- **Kant:** Heidegger interpreted the *Critique of Pure Reason* as grounding metaphysics in finite temporality, a disputed reading dramatized at Davos.
- **Hegel:** both target and resource in Heidegger’s history of metaphysics; Heidegger rejected absolute knowing while repeatedly lecturing on experience, negativity and Being.
- **Nietzsche:** interpreted in 1936–46 as the “last metaphysician,” whose will to power consummates Western nihilism; Nietzsche helped shape Heidegger’s account of values and technology.
- **Michel Foucault:** credited Heidegger (along with Nietzsche) as decisive, though he wrote little direct exegesis; archaeology/genealogy, subjectivation and critique developed through a transformed inheritance.
- **Gilles Deleuze:** engaged Heidegger’s ontological difference and univocity while building a philosophy of difference that did not accept Heidegger’s history-of-Being framework.

## Controversies, Allegations, and Evidentiary Status

### Established documentary facts

- Voluntary NSDAP application and membership from 1 May 1933 to 1945.
- Rector of Freiburg, April 1933–April 1934; public speeches endorsing Hitler’s state and university transformation.
- Participation in discriminatory university administration and a denunciatory Baumgarten report.
- Continued Party membership after resignation.
- Antisemitic passages in private notebooks and some correspondence.
- No public apology or comprehensive public Holocaust reckoning.

### Documented but interpretation disputed

- The philosophical meaning of the rectoral address: an attempt at university self-assertion inside Nazism, a distinct Heideggerian Nazism, or resistance to crude Party control. Its explicit allegiance is not disputed; its exact ideological structure is.
- How centrally antisemitism informs the ontology/history of Being.
- Whether post-1934 Nietzsche lectures offered coded resistance. Evidence shows criticisms of biological racism and Party vulgarity, but also continued political-metaphysical investment.
- The 1949 extermination-camp comparison: technological diagnosis versus ethically leveling evasion.
- Editorial manipulation of the GA: specific omissions and variants are documented; claims of a single total conspiracy exceed demonstrated evidence.

### Unsupported or overstated claims

- **Personal library ban on Husserl:** widely repeated, not established by an extant order from Heidegger.
- **Removal of the *Being and Time* dedication solely by Heidegger out of antisemitism:** the dedication disappeared in 1941 under publishing constraints, but an acknowledgment footnote remained. His broader abandonment of Husserl is separately documented.
- **SS membership, espionage, direct participation in killing:** no supporting evidence.
- **Complete resistance after 1934:** contradicted by Party membership, Löwith’s account, lectures and notebooks.
- **A proven causal line from *Being and Time* to genocide:** an interpretive thesis, not an event demonstrated by criminal or archival causation.

### Affairs and professional ethics

The Arendt relationship involved a married professor and a very young student under his authority. It was secret and documented. No known lawsuit, university discipline, police investigation or formal charge resulted. Retrospective ethical criticism should not be mislabeled as a contemporaneous adjudication.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The peasant sage of the Black Forest”

**Story:** Heidegger derived an elemental philosophy directly from rural soil, craft and solitude, refusing metropolitan modernity. **Appearance/circulation:** cultivated in his essays, hut photographs, dress, speeches and admirers’ recollections from the 1920s onward. **Evidence:** the hut and work rhythm were real; he was also a career academic, internationally networked author and strategic manager of reputation. **Effect:** made Todtnauberg a pilgrimage site and supplied both hagiography and parody.

### “Heidegger was Hitler’s official philosopher”

**Story:** the regime adopted his system as its doctrine. **Circulation:** émigré polemic, postwar journalism and shorthand accounts. **Evidence:** he sought leadership in 1933 and philosophically endorsed the revolution; however Rosenberg’s circle and other Party authorities distrusted him, he held no national ideological command, and his rectorate lasted one year. **Effect:** compresses real complicity into a false institutional title.

### “He resigned and became a resistance thinker”

**Story:** resignation proved principled opposition. **Circulation:** Heidegger’s postwar statements and defenders. **Evidence:** real administrative conflicts and loss of office; no Party resignation, public repudiation or resistance organization. **Effect:** central to postwar rehabilitation.

### “Husserl was banned from the library by Heidegger”

**Story:** Heidegger personally ordered his Jewish mentor excluded. **Circulation:** early postwar accounts and repeated biographies. **Evidence:** no such personal order has been produced; national/regional discriminatory rules are the likely substrate. Heidegger’s failures toward Husserl remain independently documented. **Effect:** became a vivid emblem of betrayal because it personalized a broader institutional persecution.

### “Only a god can save us” as religious conversion or prophecy

**Story:** Heidegger predicted civilizational collapse and returned to faith. **Appearance:** the 1966 interview, published 1976. **Evidence:** the phrase concerns readiness through thinking and poetry for a god’s appearance or absence, not a confession of Christian belief or timed prophecy. **Effect:** perhaps his most circulated late aphorism, appropriated by religious, ecological, conservative and technological critiques.

### Celan’s demanded apology

**Story:** Celan went to the hut seeking one word of remorse; Heidegger refused. **Circulation:** readings of “Todtnauberg,” memoirs and literary criticism. **Evidence:** the guestbook, poem and visit are documented; no transcript proves what was requested or said. **Effect:** the encounter functions as a symbolic tribunal over German philosophy and Jewish catastrophe.

### Arendt as absolver

**Story:** a Jewish former lover’s reconciliation cleared Heidegger of antisemitism/Nazism. **Circulation:** apologetic reception. **Evidence:** reconciliation and assistance are facts; Arendt was one individual with complex motives and never possessed authority to adjudicate collective crimes. **Effect:** biographical intimacy is repeatedly used as political evidence beyond its scope.

The works below are genuine Heidegger texts unless marked otherwise. Many lecture courses and notebooks were **posthumously edited from manuscripts or student transcripts** and were not books Heidegger published in that form. The GA, planned during his lifetime and begun in 1975, is not a historical-critical edition: it follows Heidegger’s arrangement and editorial instructions. ### Gesamtausgabe structure

The publisher’s current plan is the authoritative working bibliography: **I. published writings 1910–76; II. lecture courses 1919–44; III. unpublished treatises, lectures and notes; IV. notes and records**, projected at 100+ volumes. Rather than falsely treating every GA volume as a lifetime-authored “book,” this dossier directs researchers to the continuously updated Klostermann Editionsplan in Sources.

## Books and Major Works About Heidegger

### Biographical and documentary

- Rüdiger Safranski, *Ein Meister aus Deutschland: Heidegger und seine Zeit* (1994; English 1998). Broad intellectual biography.

## Films, Documentaries, Interviews, and Archival Material

- **“Nur noch ein Gott kann uns retten”**: filmed/recorded *Der Spiegel* interview transcript, conducted 23 September 1966, published 31 May 1976; primary self-presentation.
- **Martin Heidegger und seine Heimat** (1969 television conversation with Richard Wisser): rare filmed interview material.
- **Human, All Too Human: “Martin Heidegger—Thinking the Unthinkable”** (BBC/RM Arts, 1999), popular documentary episode; useful orientation, not an archival authority.
- **The Ister** (David Barison and Daniel Ross, 2004), documentary essay following Heidegger’s Hölderlin lectures and the Danube.
- **Being in the World** (Tao Ruspoli, 2010), documentary on practical skill inspired by Heidegger and Hubert Dreyfus; not a biography.
- **Heidegger’s Black Notebooks: A Conference** (2014, public conference recordings), multi-scholar reception record.
- Deutsches Literaturarchiv Marbach: manuscripts and estate catalogues.
- Freiburg University Library Heidegger portals/bibliographies: extensive primary and secondary bibliographic records.
- Messkirch Martin-Heidegger-Museum and municipal holdings: local reception and material culture; museum framing should be checked against archives.

## Illness, Later Life, Death, and Burial

Documented health events include the cardiac complaint that ended the Jesuit novitiate and limited military service; recurring strain; and the 1945–46 psychological/psychosomatic crisis treated by von Gebsattel. Late photographs and correspondence show declining vigor, but public sources do not establish a single detailed terminal diagnosis. Heidegger died at home in Freiburg on 26 May 1976. General biographical sources describe natural causes; claims of a precisely documented cause should be treated cautiously unless supported by a death certificate or medical file.

At his request Bernhard Welte conducted the Catholic burial in Meßkirch. Heidegger’s grave, near his parents, became a site of philosophical pilgrimage. The posthumous *Spiegel* interview appeared almost immediately.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Heidegger’s impact is institutional and transdisciplinary: Gadamer’s philosophical hermeneutics; Sartrean and post-Sartrean existentialism; Bultmann’s theology; Arendt’s political thought; Merleau-Ponty, Levinas, Derrida and deconstruction; Foucault’s and Deleuze’s transformed Nietzschean ontologies; Japanese Kyoto School philosophy; psychoanalysis and Daseinsanalysis; architecture, ecology, literary theory and science-and-technology studies.

Reception has proceeded in waves:

1. **1927–45:** rapid canonization of *Being and Time*, accompanied by political commitment and émigré criticism.
2. **1945–70s:** teaching ban then international rehabilitation; French reception; political facts known but often bracketed.
3. **1980s–90s:** Ott and Farías revived archival controversy; correspondence and GA materials complicated biography.
4. **2005 onward:** Faye controversy over philosophy’s Nazification.
5. **2014 onward:** Black Notebooks established explicit antisemitic writing and reopened debates over the entire corpus and editorial estate.

There is no settled scholarly verdict on whether the philosophical achievement can or should be separated from the politics. There is broad factual agreement on Party membership, rectoral activism and antisemitic notebook passages; disagreement concerns conceptual penetration, methods of reading, curriculum and commemoration. Chairs, societies and conferences have reconsidered names and missions; Günter Figal resigned as chair of the Martin-Heidegger-Gesellschaft in 2015 after the notebooks’ publication. The GA remains indispensable and textually contested.
