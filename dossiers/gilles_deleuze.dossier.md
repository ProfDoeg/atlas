# Gilles Louis Rene Deleuze: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

This dossier concerns the French philosopher conventionally known as **Gilles Deleuze** (1925–1995). The spelling “Gilles Louis Rene Deleuze” in the request corresponds to **Gilles Louis René Deleuze**.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact**: supported by institutional records, publications, contemporary reporting, recorded interviews, or multiple scholarly sources.
- **Reported fact**: reported by a biographer, colleague, family member, or reliable secondary source, but not independently confirmed here by a primary record.
- **Allegation or theory**: a contested interpretation or accusation.
- **Rumor or myth**: a story lacking adequate documentary support, included because it has circulated and influenced reception.
- **Unresolved**: available evidence does not establish a single version.

The biographical record is unusually uneven. Deleuze resisted conventional autobiography, kept much of his family life private, and reportedly destroyed or declined to preserve many personal papers. His books, interviews, recorded courses, institutional history, and François Dosse’s extensively researched dual biography provide the principal reconstruction.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Gilles Louis René Deleuze.
- **Usual published name:** Gilles Deleuze.
- **Alternative spellings:** Gilles Louis Rene Deleuze, without French diacritics.
- **Pseudonyms:** No established literary pseudonym.
- **Titles and positions:** French philosopher; secondary-school philosophy teacher; university assistant and lecturer; researcher at the Centre national de la recherche scientifique; professor at the University of Lyon; professor at the Centre universitaire expérimental de Vincennes/Université Paris VIII; professor emeritus after retirement.
- **Born:** **18 January 1925**, Paris, France.
- **Birthplace:** Conventionally identified as the **17th arrondissement of Paris**.
- **Died:** **4 November 1995**, Paris.
- **Place of death:** His home in the 17th arrondissement, generally identified as **84 avenue Niel**.
- **Cause and manner of death:** Suicide by defenestration, according to contemporary reporting and the standard biographical account, amid advanced pulmonary disease and severe respiratory incapacity. No reliable evidence of homicide has emerged.
- **Nationality:** French.
- **Primary fields:** Metaphysics, epistemology, history of philosophy, political philosophy, aesthetics, psychoanalysis criticism, literature, cinema, painting.
- **Languages of publication:** Principally French.
- **Religion:** No documented adherence to an organized religion in adult life. His philosophy was secular and strongly associated with Spinozist immanence, though he studied theology and religious philosophers as conceptual resources.
- **Political orientation:** Generally situated on the non-party revolutionary or libertarian left after 1968; sympathetic to prison activism, Palestinian political claims, Italian autonomism, and other extra-parliamentary movements. He was not a consistent member or theorist of a conventional political party.

Institutional summaries by the [Bibliothèque nationale de France](https://comitehistoire.bnf.fr/dictionnaire-fonds/gilles-deleuze), [Éditions de Minuit](https://www.leseditionsdeminuit.fr/auteur-Deleuze_Gilles-1381-1-1-0-1.html), and the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/deleuze/) corroborate the central dates and appointments.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Louis Deleuze**, Gilles’s father, was an engineer. Biographical accounts describe the household as conservative, bourgeois, patriotic, and politically right-wing. In *L’Abécédaire*, Deleuze himself characterized his family as uncultivated bourgeois people of the right. The exact degree of his parents’ involvement with particular nationalist organizations is less secure than the general description of their politics.

**Odette Camauër**, his mother, managed the household. Some genealogical accounts identify her as a granddaughter of the Belgian-born composer and musician **Godefroid Camauër**, but this ancestry is not central to the surviving documentary biography and should be treated as reported rather than fully reconstructed fact.

The family’s finances appear to have been comfortable but not continuously affluent. Deleuze recalled that financial pressures influenced his schooling and prevented the more expensive private Catholic education initially contemplated for him.

### Sibling: Georges Deleuze

Gilles had one known elder brother:

- **Georges Gabriel Jean Deleuze**, born in 1922.
- Resistance alias reported as **Gabriel Dorset** or **Dorsay**.
- Arrested by German authorities in 1944 after activity in the French Resistance.
- Deported in a transport leaving the Nantes region.
- Died during transport, near **Revigny-sur-Ornain**, before reaching the camp system.

A surviving biographical record compiled by a French deportee-memory association documents Georges’s addresses, Resistance effort, attempted passage into Spain, arrest, deportation, and death. It corrects a recurring compressed account that simply says he “died on the way to Auschwitz.” The destination and transport history are more complicated; Buchenwald also appears in biographical summaries. The safest finding is that Georges died during deportation before reaching his intended camp destination.

François Dosse interprets Georges’s death as a defining family trauma. According to Dosse’s reconstruction, the parents transformed Georges into a heroic lost son, leaving Gilles with the sense that his intellectual achievements could never equal his brother’s sacrifice. That psychological consequence is a **biographical interpretation**, not a clinical fact, though it is consistent with Deleuze’s later remarks about the family.

### Marriage and children

In **1956**, Deleuze married **Denise Paule Grandjouan**, known as **Fanny Deleuze**.

Fanny had worked in fashion, reportedly for **Pierre Balmain**, and later became a translator, notably of works associated with D. H. Lawrence. She also helped preserve, transcribe, and organize parts of her husband’s oral teaching. Both Fanny and Gilles signed the controversial January 1977 petition discussed below.

They had two children:

- **Julien Deleuze**, born in 1960.
- **Marie Émilie Deleuze**, born in 1964 at Nogent-sur-Marne; later a film and television director.

No reliable evidence located in this research documents extramarital partnerships, secret children, major inherited wealth, or a family business controlled by Deleuze.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1925–1944

Deleuze was born and raised largely in Paris’s 17th arrondissement. The district remained his principal geographical anchor throughout his life. Apart from school appointments, wartime displacement, university posts, travel, and periods of convalescence, his biography repeatedly returns to this part of Paris.

His childhood was marked by respiratory weakness. Later biographies connect his lifelong pulmonary vulnerability to illnesses beginning early in life, although the precise childhood diagnosis is not consistently documented.

### Schooling and wartime Normandy

Deleuze attended the **Lycée Carnot** in Paris. During the German invasion and occupation, the family was in Normandy. He was left at school in **Deauville** for a period when travel and wartime conditions separated him from his parents.

A decisive intellectual encounter occurred there with **Pierre Halbwachs**, a young philosophy or literature teacher and son of sociologist Maurice Halbwachs. Deleuze later credited this teacher with awakening him intellectually through literature and philosophy. Writers such as Anatole France and Charles Baudelaire entered his formation through this encounter.

After returning to Paris, Deleuze continued at Lycée Carnot and also attended preparatory philosophy instruction associated with **Lycée Henri-IV**, where **Jean Beaufret**—later known as a major French interpreter of Martin Heidegger—was among his teachers.

### Georges’s Resistance and death

On **2 July 1944**, according to a commonly cited arrest date, Georges Deleuze was arrested for Resistance activity. His subsequent death during deportation became the central catastrophe of the Deleuze household.

Gilles himself was not a documented Resistance fighter. Later attempts to cast his entire philosophy retrospectively as a direct continuation of Resistance experience overstate the evidence. The documented link is familial and formative: his brother resisted and died; Gilles lived with the consequences.

### Earliest publication

In 1944, the young Deleuze published **“Description de la femme: pour une philosophie d’autrui sexuée.”** The essay was a stylized philosophical-literary exercise, often described as a pastiche of Jean-Paul Sartre. Its later republication showed an early fascination with alterity, embodiment, description, and the philosophical possibilities of contemporary literature.

## Education and Formation, 1944–1948

Deleuze studied philosophy at the **Sorbonne**, then part of the University of Paris.

His teachers and examiners included several of the most important French historians of philosophy of the period:

- **Ferdinand Alquié**, specialist in Descartes and surrealism.
- **Georges Canguilhem**, historian and philosopher of science.
- **Maurice de Gandillac**, historian of medieval and Renaissance philosophy.
- **Jean Hyppolite**, translator and interpreter of Hegel.
- **Jean Wahl**, associated with pluralist metaphysics, Kierkegaard, and Anglo-American philosophy.
- **Jean Beaufret**, a formative secondary/preparatory teacher rather than a principal Sorbonne supervisor.

Fellow students and early intellectual companions included **François Châtelet**, **Michel Tournier**, **Michel Butor**, **Claude Lanzmann**, and **Olivier Revault d’Allonnes**. The precise intimacy of each relationship varied.

Deleuze completed the **agrégation de philosophie in 1948**, qualifying him to teach philosophy in the French secondary system.

### Sartre as formative influence

Deleuze’s generation encountered **Jean-Paul Sartre** as a model of the contemporary philosopher operating outside purely academic history. Deleuze later said that Sartre had been the “Outside” or fresh air of his generation. Nevertheless, he did not become an orthodox existentialist. His later work displaced consciousness, personal responsibility, lack, and negation with impersonal processes, multiplicities, difference, desire-production, and immanence.

### Philosophical ancestors

During and after his formal education, Deleuze constructed a counter-canon:

- **Lucretius**
- **Duns Scotus**
- **Spinoza**
- **Leibniz**
- **Hume**
- **Nietzsche**
- **Bergson**
- Later, **Whitehead** and **Simondon**

He treated these figures not merely as historical objects but as resources against philosophies founded on identity, transcendence, judgment, and representation.

## Early Career, 1948–1963

### Secondary teaching

After the agrégation, Deleuze taught philosophy in lycées, including appointments commonly listed as:

- **Amiens**, 1948–1952.
- **Orléans**, 1952–1955.
- **Lycée Louis-le-Grand**, Paris, 1955–1957.

Some institutional summaries compress or slightly vary these dates. The progression from provincial lycée teaching to Paris and then university employment is secure.

### First major book

In **1953**, he published:

- **_Empirisme et subjectivité: essai sur la nature humaine selon Hume_**

The book argued that the subject is produced through habits, associations, institutions, and social arrangements rather than existing as a pre-given foundation. It established several durable Deleuzian concerns: empiricism, production of subjectivity, relations external to their terms, and institutions as organized satisfactions of tendencies.

### Anthologies and editorial projects

Deleuze prepared or contributed to pedagogical anthologies:

- **_Instincts et institutions_** (1955).
- **_Mémoire et vie_**, selected texts from Bergson (1957).

These were editorial and pedagogical works, not wholly authored philosophical monographs. Bibliographies sometimes list them without making that distinction.

### Sorbonne and CNRS

Deleuze served as an assistant at the Sorbonne in the later 1950s. Éditions de Minuit gives 1955 as the beginning of his assistantship; other accounts identify 1957–1960 as his principal Sorbonne teaching period.

From **1960 to 1964**, he held a research appointment at the **Centre national de la recherche scientifique (CNRS)**.

### Marriage, domestic life, and intellectual production

Deleuze married Fanny Grandjouan in 1956. Their son Julien was born in 1960. Domestic life remained intentionally shielded from intellectual publicity, although Fanny’s translation and transcription work made her an important practical collaborator.

### Nietzsche and the break with orthodox history

**_Nietzsche et la philosophie_** appeared in **1962**. It presented Nietzsche as a systematic thinker of force, value, affirmation, and active versus reactive life. The book helped revive philosophical Nietzsche studies in France and opposed both psychological and narrowly philological readings.

Deleuze later worked with Michel Foucault on the French reception and editing of the Colli–Montinari critical Nietzsche edition. He participated in the **1964 Royaumont colloquium on Nietzsche** and the **1972 Cerisy colloquium**, where Derrida, Lyotard, Pierre Klossowski, and others also took part.

### Failed or unrealized appointment

In 1962, **Michel Foucault** reportedly proposed Deleuze as successor to Jules Vuillemin at Clermont-Ferrand. **Roger Garaudy**, then favored by the ministry, obtained the position. This was an institutional disappointment, not a dismissal or formal disciplinary action.

## Lyon, Doctoral Work, Illness, and 1968, 1964–1969

### University of Lyon

Deleuze taught at the **University of Lyon from 1964 to 1969**. His daughter Émilie had been born in 1964.

During this period he published a rapid series of studies:

- _Proust et les signes_ (1964).
- _Nietzsche_ (1965).
- _Le Bergsonisme_ (1966).
- _Présentation de Sacher-Masoch: le froid et le cruel_ (1967).

These books moved from history of philosophy toward Deleuze’s independent conceptual system.

### Doctoral theses

Under the older French doctorate system, Deleuze submitted:

- Principal thesis: **_Différence et répétition_**.
- Complementary thesis: **_Spinoza et le problème de l’expression_**.

The principal thesis was supervised by **Maurice de Gandillac**. Both were published in **1968**.

_Différence et répétition_ argued for difference not as variation from prior identity but as productive in itself. It developed repetition, intensity, individuation, virtuality, and critique of the “image of thought.” _Spinoza et le problème de l’expression_ reconstructed expression as the relation among substance, attributes, and modes.

### Pulmonary crisis

In 1968 Deleuze suffered a grave pulmonary illness, generally described as tuberculosis. Biographical accounts say he underwent major surgery involving removal of part or all of one lung. The exact surgical description varies—“lung removal,” “partial pneumonectomy,” and “thoracoplasty” appear in secondary accounts—so the precise procedure remains **unresolved** without a medical record.

What is documented is that:

- The illness was severe.
- Surgery and convalescence interrupted his activity.
- He lived thereafter with substantially reduced respiratory capacity.
- He continued smoking for much of his life, worsening an already precarious condition.
- His lung disease became disabling in his final years.

### May 1968

Deleuze was not among the most publicly visible street leaders of May 1968. His illness limited direct participation. Nevertheless, May altered the institutional and political conditions of his work. His philosophy after 1968 became more explicitly political, and he joined the experimental university established at Vincennes.

The notion that _Difference and Repetition_ was simply “the philosophy of May ’68” is retrospective. The book had a longer conceptual genesis, although its publication in 1968 and its assault on identity and representation made it emblematic of the period.

## Vincennes and the Guattari Collaboration, 1969–1980

### Appointment at Vincennes

In October **1969**, Deleuze joined the philosophy department of the experimental **Centre universitaire de Vincennes**, later **Université Paris VIII–Vincennes–Saint-Denis**.

Important colleagues included:

- **Michel Foucault**, involved in the department’s creation before leaving for the Collège de France.
- **François Châtelet**.
- **Jean-François Lyotard**.
- **Alain Badiou**, whose Maoist politics produced both institutional conflict and later philosophical debate.
- **René Schérer** and others associated with the experimental department.

The university admitted nontraditional students, challenged conventional curricula, and became an institutional center of post-1968 radical thought. Deleuze’s lectures attracted philosophers, artists, psychiatrists, militants, foreigners, and listeners without standard academic credentials.

### Meeting Félix Guattari

Deleuze met **Pierre-Félix Guattari** in 1969 through mutual contacts, often identified as psychiatrist and activist **Jean-Pierre Muyard**.

Reported meeting locations include:

- A house in the Limousin where Deleuze was convalescing.
- **Dhuizon**, near the La Borde psychiatric clinic, during subsequent work sessions.

The collaboration joined markedly different working styles:

- Deleuze: systematic reader, historian of philosophy, careful organizer and rewriter.
- Guattari: activist, institutional psychotherapist, prolific generator of notes, diagrams, neologisms, and political interventions.

They described their collaboration as producing something between them rather than preserving two individual voices. Dosse calls this a kind of “third man.” Surviving accounts indicate that Guattari sent large quantities of notes and Deleuze reorganized, selected, and rewrote them; this does not establish that either was a ghostwriter for the other. Their jointly signed books are genuine coauthored works.

### _L’Anti-Œdipe_

Published in **1972**, _L’Anti-Œdipe: Capitalisme et schizophrénie 1_ became a French bestseller and succès de scandale.

Its principal claims included:

- Desire is productive, not fundamentally founded in lack.
- The Oedipal family triangle improperly reduces social and political desire to familial representation.
- Capitalism decodes social flows while continually reterritorializing them.
- Psychoanalysis often reproduces authority it claims to interpret.
- Fascism involves investments of desire, not merely obedience to an external state.

The book brought Deleuze much greater public visibility. It also attracted criticism for rhetorical violence toward psychoanalysis, its use of “schizophrenia,” its sweeping historical claims, and ambiguity about the relationship between clinical psychosis and “schizoanalysis.”

### Political activism

Deleuze’s political activity was mostly undertaken through committees, petitions, interviews, and support networks rather than party office.

Documented or strongly reported engagements include:

- Participation in or association with the **Groupe d’information sur les prisons (GIP)**, created around Foucault, Daniel Defert, and others.
- Public discussion with Foucault on intellectuals and power.
- Support for prisoners’ ability to speak in their own names.
- Support for Palestinian claims and criticism of Israeli state policy.
- Later support for Italian autonomist militants, including interventions connected with **Toni Negri**.
- Opposition to state repression and to the traditional Communist Party’s monopoly on left politics.

He did not hold elected office and is not known to have directed a political party or clandestine armed organization.

### Foucault relationship

Deleuze and **Michel Foucault** were friends, colleagues, mutual readers, and political collaborators. Foucault wrote the famous preface to the English translation of _Anti-Oedipus_, calling it an “Introduction to the Non-Fascist Life.” Deleuze wrote _Foucault_ after Foucault’s death.

Their friendship cooled in the late 1970s. Biographical accounts connect the distance to political disagreement over the Klaus Croissant affair and attitudes toward political violence, as well as intellectual divergence. The relationship should not be reduced to a simple feud: Deleuze continued to treat Foucault as a major philosopher and delivered remarks at his funeral in 1984.

### Kafka, rhizomes, and “minor literature”

In **1975**, Deleuze and Guattari published _Kafka: Pour une littérature mineure_. They defined “minor literature” not as literature in a minor language but as what a minority does within a major language: deterritorializing it, connecting individual expression to politics, and constructing collective enunciation.

The concept became influential well beyond Kafka studies but was criticized for abstracting Kafka from specific Jewish, German, Czech, and Austro-Hungarian histories.

### Dialogues and theatre

With former student and journalist **Claire Parnet**, Deleuze published _Dialogues_ in 1977. The book combined interview, philosophical exposition, and jointly arranged dialogue. A revised edition appeared in 1996 with “L’Actuel et le virtuel.”

In 1979, he collaborated with theatre director and actor **Carmelo Bene** on _Superpositions_, containing Bene’s _Richard III_ and Deleuze’s “Un manifeste de moins.”

### _Mille plateaux_

Published in **1980**, _Mille plateaux: Capitalisme et schizophrénie 2_ developed:

- Rhizome.
- Assemblage/agencement.
- Deterritorialization and reterritorialization.
- Becoming-animal and becoming-minoritarian.
- Smooth and striated space.
- War machine and state apparatus.
- Faciality.
- Nomadology.
- Geology and stratification.
- Micropolitics and segmentarity.

Unlike _Anti-Oedipus_, it was not organized as a linear argument. Its interdisciplinary borrowings from linguistics, anthropology, biology, music, mathematics, political history, and ethology contributed both to its influence and to later criticism.

## Saint-Denis, Cinema, Art, and Late Career, 1980–1987

Vincennes was dismantled and Paris VIII relocated to **Saint-Denis** in 1980. Deleuze continued teaching there until retirement on **30 September 1987**.

### Teaching practice

Recorded courses show a distinctive pedagogy:

- Long sessions, sometimes approximately three hours.
- No conventional examination-centered lecture style.
- Repetition of a conceptual problem across multiple weeks.
- Movement among philosophy, mathematics, painting, music, literature, and cinema.
- Direct engagement with questions from a heterogeneous audience.
- Extensive preparation but relatively free oral delivery.

The recordings—hundreds of hours—became a major posthumous archive. They cover Spinoza, Leibniz, cinema, painting, Foucault, state apparatuses, war machines, and related subjects. The courses are preserved through Paris VIII, Gallica, WebDeleuze, and the Purdue Deleuze Seminars project.

### Francis Bacon

**_Francis Bacon: Logique de la sensation_** appeared in 1981. Deleuze analyzed Bacon’s use of the Figure, sensation, deformation, diagram, color, and forces acting on the body. The book was produced in dialogue with Bacon’s paintings but not through a sustained personal collaboration with the painter.

### Cinema books

Deleuze published:

- **_Cinéma 1: L’Image-mouvement_** (1983).
- **_Cinéma 2: L’Image-temps_** (1985).

Drawing heavily on Henri Bergson and Charles Sanders Peirce, these books constructed a philosophical taxonomy of cinematic signs and images. The movement-image was associated with sensory-motor organization; the time-image with postwar breakdowns in action, chronology, and perception.

Filmmakers discussed include Eisenstein, Vertov, Chaplin, Hitchcock, Welles, Rossellini, De Sica, Antonioni, Resnais, Godard, Ozu, Kurosawa, Dreyer, Bresson, and many others. Deleuze did not make films himself, but the cinema books became foundational in film philosophy.

### Foucault’s death

After Foucault died in June 1984, Deleuze spoke at the funeral, reading from Foucault’s work. He published **_Foucault_** in 1986, presenting Foucault’s thought through the relations among strata, diagrams, power, knowledge, subjectivation, and the “Outside.”

### Retirement

Deleuze retired in 1987 and became professor emeritus. Retirement was connected both to normal career chronology and to declining health. It was not a dismissal.

## Final Period, 1988–1995

### _Le Pli_

In **1988**, Deleuze published _Le Pli: Leibniz et le baroque_. The fold served as a concept connecting Leibnizian monadology, Baroque architecture and art, matter, perception, and subjectivity.

### _L’Abécédaire_

Between approximately **1988 and 1989**, Deleuze recorded a long filmed conversation with Claire Parnet, directed by **Pierre-André Boutang**. Organized alphabetically from “A comme Animal” through “Z comme Zigzag,” it became known as **_L’Abécédaire de Gilles Deleuze_**.

Deleuze agreed on the condition that it be broadcast only after his death. The agreement was later relaxed, and Arte began broadcasting selections in 1995 while he was still alive. The INA catalogue documents an Arte broadcast on 16 April 1995.

The filmed conversations are primary evidence for:

- His account of childhood and family politics.
- His dislike of travel and television.
- His admiration for teachers and writers.
- His views on alcohol, animals, desire, friendship, illness, opera, resistance, and philosophy.
- His stylized self-presentation as a philosopher interested in impersonal life rather than confession.

It should not be treated as an unmediated autobiography: the alphabetic format, friendship with Parnet, editing, and Deleuze’s cultivated anti-autobiographical stance shaped the result.

### Shorter late works

- _Périclès et Verdi: La philosophie de François Châtelet_ (1988).
- _Pourparlers, 1972–1990_ (1990).
- _Qu’est-ce que la philosophie?_ with Guattari (1991).
- “L’Épuisé,” accompanying Samuel Beckett’s television plays (1992).
- _Critique et clinique_ (1993).
- “L’Immanence: une vie…” (1995), among his final published texts.

### Guattari’s death

Félix Guattari died in 1992. Their final joint book, _Qu’est-ce que la philosophie?_, had appeared the previous year. The extent to which Guattari’s death aggravated Deleuze’s final decline is interpretive; it unquestionably ended a collaboration of more than two decades.

### Terminal pulmonary illness

By the early 1990s Deleuze’s respiratory condition had become severe. Accounts describe:

- Greatly diminished lung capacity.
- Dependence on oxygen.
- Difficulty speaking and writing.
- Periods of tracheal or respiratory intervention.
- Increasing confinement.
- Inability to sustain his previous teaching and writing practices.
- A diet restricted near the end of life; Jean-Luc Marion later recalled discussing theology with him while Deleuze could eat little beyond rice pudding.

No reliable evidence found here establishes drug addiction, psychiatric hospitalization, psychosis, or a diagnosed major depressive disorder. His heavy smoking is documented as behavior relevant to his lung disease, but retrospective diagnosis of “addiction” beyond nicotine dependence would require medical evidence.

## Death

On **4 November 1995**, Deleuze died after falling or jumping from a window of his Paris apartment. Contemporary reporting in *Le Monde* stated that he “gave himself death” by throwing himself from the window and explicitly connected the act to his long respiratory illness.

The standard conclusion is **suicide by defenestration**.

### Date discrepancy

One *Le Monde* retrospective referred to “the night of Saturday to Sunday” and elsewhere misdated Saturday as 3 November. The calendar and the principal contemporary obituary support **Saturday, 4 November 1995**.

### Accident theory

A later **rumor or theory** claims that Deleuze did not intend suicide but reached toward the window for air and accidentally fell. Variants have circulated in online discussions and have sometimes been attributed to friends or to Slavoj Žižek.

Evidence against the accident theory:

- Contemporary reporting consistently described suicide.
- Biographical accounts connect the act to terminal respiratory incapacity.
- No police, medical, witness, or family document establishing an accident has surfaced in the sources consulted.

Evidence offered for it:

- The extremity of his breathlessness could make reaching a window plausible.
- Some admirers considered suicide inconsistent with their image of his temperament or philosophy.

Assessment: **unverified rumor**. Philosophical claims that suicide was “un-Deleuzian” are not forensic evidence.

### Suicide note

No authenticated suicide note was located. Claims about a note or final philosophical declaration should therefore be treated as **unverified** unless tied to a document in a recognized archive.

## Residences, Travel, and Geographical Movements

Deleuze was less geographically mobile than many French intellectuals of his generation.

### Documented or strongly reported places

- **Paris, 17th arrondissement:** Birth, childhood home, much of adult residence, and death.
- **Deauville/Normandy:** Wartime schooling and formative encounter with Pierre Halbwachs.
- **Amiens:** Secondary teaching appointment.
- **Orléans:** Secondary teaching appointment.
- **Paris:** Lycée Louis-le-Grand, Sorbonne, CNRS, domestic and publishing life.
- **Lyon:** University appointment, 1964–1969.
- **Limousin:** Convalescence after pulmonary surgery; early meeting with Guattari is reported there.
- **Dhuizon/La Borde region:** Work sessions with Guattari.
- **Vincennes:** Teaching from 1969 until the campus relocation.
- **Saint-Denis:** Paris VIII teaching after 1980.
- **Royaumont and Cerisy-la-Salle:** Nietzsche colloquia.
- **Italy:** Intellectual and political relations with Italian philosophers and autonomist militants; exact journeys require reconstruction from correspondence and conference records.
- **United States:** His thought became influential there, but Deleuze himself disliked extensive travel and did not build his career through long American residencies.

In *L’Abécédaire*, he treated travel skeptically, distinguishing conceptual movement from tourism. Accounts portraying him as a cosmopolitan wanderer confuse the global circulation of his work with his own relatively Paris-centered life.

No exile or imprisonment is documented.

## Employment, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Employment chronology

- French public secondary-school system: philosophy teacher, 1948–1957.
- University of Paris/Sorbonne: assistant or lecturer in the later 1950s.
- CNRS: research appointment, 1960–1964.
- University of Lyon: faculty appointment, 1964–1969.
- Vincennes/Paris VIII: professor, October 1969–30 September 1987.
- Paris VIII: professor emeritus after retirement.

### Publishing relationships

Deleuze published with:

- **Presses Universitaires de France**, especially earlier monographs.
- **Éditions de Minuit**, his central later publisher.
- **Flammarion**, for _Dialogues_ with Claire Parnet.
- Various journals, collective volumes, and activist publications.

His relationship with Minuit and publisher **Jérôme Lindon** was a major professional and financial relationship, but no available public record provides a comprehensive accounting of advances, royalty percentages, lifetime income, or estate value.

### Wealth and property

No evidence located of:

- Large commercial investments.
- Corporate directorships.
- Patents or inventions in the legal or engineering sense.
- Shell companies, trusts, or offshore structures.
- Significant real-estate speculation.
- Financial crimes, tax investigations, or bankruptcy.

He earned a public academic salary and author royalties. _Anti-Oedipus_ sold unusually well for a philosophical book, but no reliable lifetime net-worth estimate was found. Claims that he became wealthy from “French Theory” are unsupported without accounts or contracts.

### Technologies and archives

Deleuze’s technological legacy is archival rather than proprietary:

- Audio recording of his Paris VIII courses.
- Television recording of _L’Abécédaire_.
- Posthumous digitization by Paris VIII, Gallica, WebDeleuze, and Purdue.
- No patents are associated with him.

## Political, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

### 1940s–1950s: literature, existentialism, and empiricism

The early Deleuze moved from Sartrean contemporary philosophy toward Humean empiricism. The subject became an effect of relations and habits rather than a sovereign origin.

### 1960s: counter-history of philosophy

His studies of Nietzsche, Kant, Bergson, Spinoza, Proust, and Sacher-Masoch constructed a selective philosophical lineage. He often described his method provocatively as producing a “monstrous child” through the history of philosophy: remaining textually grounded while making a thinker generate unfamiliar consequences.

### Difference, repetition, and immanence

The mature metaphysics rejected the subordination of difference to identity. Key terms included:

- Difference-in-itself.
- Repetition distinct from generality.
- Virtual and actual.
- Intensive individuation.
- Event.
- Univocity of being.
- Plane of immanence.
- Transcendental empiricism.

### Collaboration and political desire

With Guattari, Deleuze shifted from largely individual philosophical construction to a theory of social machines, desire, assemblages, and collective enunciation. Marx entered into combination with Nietzsche, Freud, anthropology, linguistics, and institutional psychotherapy.

### Arts

Deleuze treated the arts as autonomous forms of thought:

- Philosophy creates concepts.
- Science creates functions or propositions.
- Art creates percepts and affects.

His analyses of Proust, Kafka, Lewis Carroll, Beckett, Bacon, cinema, and music were not applications of a fixed theory; he presented them as sites where concepts were generated.

### Religion and theology

Deleuze did not advocate a personal God or ecclesiastical religion. His Spinozism identified immanence with a plane excluding transcendent judgment. Yet his writing engaged:

- Duns Scotus on univocity.
- Spinoza’s God/Nature.
- Leibnizian theodicy.
- Kierkegaard and Pascal.
- Christian painting.
- The relation between atheism and philosophy.

Labels such as atheist, Spinozist, vitalist, materialist, or metaphysical naturalist each capture only part of the record.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Félix Guattari — coauthor and political-intellectual collaborator

Documented collaboration, 1969–1992. Joint authorship produced _Anti-Oedipus_, _Kafka_, _Rhizome_, _Mille Plateaux_, and _What Is Philosophy?_

### Michel Foucault — friend, colleague, collaborator, later estranged friend

They collaborated institutionally and politically, edited Nietzsche, publicly discussed power, and interpreted one another’s work. Their friendship cooled but intellectual respect continued.

### Claire Parnet — student, interlocutor, coauthor

Coauthor of _Dialogues_ and interviewer in _L’Abécédaire_. She became one of the chief mediators of his oral self-portrait.

### François Châtelet — friend and colleague

A fellow student and later colleague at Vincennes/Paris VIII. Deleuze published a short philosophical tribute, _Périclès et Verdi_, about Châtelet.

### Jean-François Lyotard — colleague and interlocutor

Shared the Vincennes environment and Nietzsche colloquia. Lyotard later contributed to commemorative discussion after Deleuze’s death.

### Jacques Derrida — philosophical contemporary

They met in conferences and shared overlapping intellectual networks, including the 1972 Cerisy Nietzsche colloquium. Derrida wrote an elegiac text after Deleuze’s death. They were neither simple allies nor direct doctrinal rivals.

### Alain Badiou — colleague and later critic

At Vincennes, Badiou’s Maoist group opposed aspects of Deleuzian teaching and politics. His later book _Deleuze: La clameur de l’Être_ argued that Deleuze, usually considered a philosopher of multiplicity, was fundamentally a thinker of the One. Critics accused Badiou of selective reconstruction; the dispute remains a major interpretive controversy.

### Jean-Pierre Muyard — mediator

Frequently credited with introducing Deleuze and Guattari in 1969.

### Pierre Klossowski — Nietzschean collaborator and influence

Participated in Nietzsche’s French revaluation and influenced Deleuze’s thought on drives, signs, simulacra, and repetition.

### Richard Pinhas — student, musician, archivist

Pinhas attended Deleuze’s courses, incorporated philosophical material into electronic music culture, and helped establish WebDeleuze.

### David Lapoujade — student, editor, posthumous curator

Edited posthumous collections, including _L’Île déserte_, _Deux régimes de fous_, _Lettres et autres textes_, and published courses.

### Fanny Deleuze — spouse and practical textual collaborator

Translator and transcriber who assisted with preservation of oral materials. Assertions that she secretly authored Deleuze’s books are unsupported.

## Controversies, Allegations, Criticism, and Contested Material

### 1. The 1977 petition concerning sexual offenses against minors

**Documented fact:** On **26 January 1977**, *Le Monde* published “À propos d’un procès,” a petition concerning three men detained before trial in the Versailles case. Signatories included Gilles and Fanny Deleuze, Sartre, Beauvoir, Barthes, Guattari, and numerous other intellectuals and professionals.

The petition argued that approximately three years of pretrial detention were disproportionate to the conduct as presented and invoked the minors’ reported statements that they had consented. It emerged during a broader campaign to revise French sexual-offense law.

A later May 1977 text concerning reform of laws governing minors and sexuality also circulated among intellectuals, with overlapping but nonidentical signatory lists.

**Important distinctions:**

- Signing is documented.
- It is inaccurate to infer from the signature alone that Deleuze was charged with or personally committed child sexual abuse.
- No criminal charge, investigation, lawsuit, or victim allegation against Deleuze personally was found.
- The petitions did minimize or contest the legal significance of adult–minor sexual relations and relied on concepts of child consent now broadly rejected.
- Gabriel Matzneff later claimed authorship of the January text. This affects responsibility for wording but not the documented act of signing.
- Later investigation of the Versailles case suggested that the facts were more serious than the petition’s presentation and included sexual photographs.
- No public retraction by Deleuze was located.

Evidentiary classification: **documented political act; no documented personal abuse allegation**.

### 2. Appropriation of schizophrenia

Critics in psychiatry and disability studies argue that _Anti-Oedipus_ converted schizophrenia into a revolutionary metaphor while inadequately representing the suffering and incapacity of people with psychotic illness.

Deleuze and Guattari distinguished the clinical schizophrenic from “schizophrenia as process,” and Guattari worked in institutional psychiatry at La Borde. Nevertheless, the language frequently slides between clinical, social, and metaphysical registers.

Classification: **documented scholarly and clinical criticism**, not a finding of professional malpractice. Deleuze was not treating patients as a physician.

### 3. Feminist criticism

During the 1970s and 1980s, critics including **Luce Irigaray** and **Alice Jardine** questioned whether becoming-woman, bodies without organs, and deterritorialized desire appropriated sexual difference while ignoring women’s historically embodied positions.

Later feminist scholars, including Rosi Braidotti, Elizabeth Grosz, Claire Colebrook, and others, used Deleuzian concepts constructively. There is no single feminist verdict.

Classification: **documented interpretive controversy**.

### 4. Scientific and mathematical language

Critics associated with analytic philosophy and the “Science Wars” accused Deleuze and Guattari of using mathematics, physics, biology, and linguistics metaphorically or incorrectly. Alan Sokal and Jean Bricmont’s broader criticism of French theory is often invoked here, though Deleuze was not the sole or always the principal target.

Defenders respond that these scientific references operate in philosophical concept creation rather than as empirical scientific proofs. Individual passages still require discipline-specific evaluation.

Classification: **documented scholarly dispute**, not scientific fraud.

### 5. Badiou’s “philosopher of the One” thesis

Badiou argued that beneath the rhetoric of multiplicities lies a Spinozist ontology of univocal Being or the One. Other scholars say this collapses Deleuze’s distinction between univocity and numerical unity.

Classification: **philosophical interpretation**, neither exposure nor factual allegation.

### 6. Relations with psychoanalysis

_The Logic of Sense_ and earlier work were engaged with psychoanalysis; _Anti-Oedipus_ attacked Oedipal and structural-Lacanian authority. Deleuze nevertheless retained concepts from Freud, Melanie Klein, and Lacan.

Claims that he simply “refuted psychoanalysis” are misleading. His position was a selective appropriation combined with institutional and theoretical attack.

### 7. Palestine, Israel, and allegations of antisemitism

Deleuze supported Palestinian political claims and described the Palestinian situation through dispossession, colonization, and a “people missing.” Critics have alleged that some anti-Zionist or state-form arguments risk erasing Jewish historical specificity or reproducing hostile political figures.

No evidence was located of Deleuze endorsing racial antisemitism, Nazi policy, or attacks on Jews as Jews. His major works include sustained affirmative engagement with Jewish writers including Spinoza, Kafka, and Proust. That does not, by itself, settle criticism of particular political formulations.

Classification: **documented pro-Palestinian politics; contested allegation of antisemitic implications; no established finding of racial antisemitism**. A 2026 Berlin academic event on “philo- and antisemitic figurations” confirms that this remains an active research question, not a settled biographical fact.

### 8. “French Theory,” post-truth, and contemporary culture-war claims

Deleuze, Derrida, and Foucault are frequently blamed for relativism, identity politics, “wokism,” rejection of truth, or contemporary illiberalism.

Problems with the claim:

- “French Theory” was partly an American academic construction combining thinkers with substantial disagreements.
- Deleuze did not argue that every statement is equally true.
- His work contains explicit theories of problems, signs, sense, falsification, creation, and evaluation.

Possible basis:

- He attacked representation, universal subjectivity, fixed identity, and conventional philosophical judgment.
- Later movements selectively adapted his concepts.

Classification: **reception narrative and political polemic**, not a demonstrable causal chain.

### 9. Authorial imbalance in the Guattari collaboration

A persistent claim says Deleuze supplied the philosophy while Guattari supplied raw material, or conversely that Deleuze appropriated Guattari’s ideas and received disproportionate credit.

Surviving working descriptions show an asymmetrical but genuine collaboration: Guattari generated extensive notes; Deleuze selected, structured, rewrote, and connected arguments to philosophical history. Both authorized joint publication.

Classification: **documented asymmetry; allegation of appropriation unproven**.

### 10. Suicide as philosophical contradiction

Some commentators treat Deleuze’s suicide as refutation of a philosophy associated with affirmation, joy, and life. Others interpret it as an assertion of agency against terminal suffocation.

Neither claim establishes motive. Philosophy cannot substitute for a medical or personal record.

Classification: **posthumous interpretation**.

### 11. Lawsuits, fraud, criminality, and corruption

No credible evidence located of Deleuze being:

- Indicted or convicted of a crime.
- Investigated for financial fraud, corruption, money laundering, or tax crime.
- A party to a significant personal lawsuit.
- A member of an intelligence service.
- An organizer of political violence.
- Accused by an identified victim of sexual assault or abuse.

The principal documented controversy with a legal subject is his signature on the 1977 petition.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Self-Created Mythology

### “A fingernail like the claw of a philosopher”

Photographs and _L’Abécédaire_ made Deleuze’s unusually long fingernails part of his public iconography. Stories attributed them to aristocratic eccentricity, protective talismans, or deliberate rebellion.

Deleuze offered mundane or semi-playful explanations involving lack of protective fingerprints or discomfort from cutting them. The fingernails became a visual shorthand for the eccentric French philosopher.

Classification: **documented physical trait; symbolic explanations largely anecdotal**.

### “The philosopher who never traveled”

The story says Deleuze hardly left the 17th arrondissement and built a nomadic philosophy without traveling.

Evidence supports his Paris-centered life and dislike of tourism. But he taught in Amiens, Orléans, and Lyon, visited conferences, convalesced elsewhere, and maintained international connections.

Classification: **myth built around a documented preference**.

### “Guattari wrote everything; Deleuze polished it”

This hostile version exaggerates their division of labor. Guattari’s notes were indispensable, but Deleuze had an extensive independent oeuvre before and after their collaboration.

Classification: **unsupported reduction of a documented asymmetric collaboration**.

### “Deleuze was a Maoist”

He worked alongside Maoists, supported prisoner movements, and shared some extra-parliamentary causes. Alain Badiou and organized Maoist groups at Vincennes sometimes opposed him.

Classification: **misleading label**. He was a radical left intellectual but not securely documented as an orthodox Maoist party member.

### “Foucault said the century would be Deleuzian”

Foucault wrote that “perhaps one day, this century will be known as Deleuzian.” The remark is genuine, from “Theatrum Philosophicum,” but became mythic through removal from its playful and philosophical context. Deleuze himself reportedly treated it as a joke directed at admirers and detractors.

Classification: **authentic quotation with mythologized meaning**.

### “Rhizome predicted the Internet”

Later digital culture adopted the rhizome as an image for networks. Deleuze and Guattari did not invent packet switching, hypertext, or the Internet, nor did they make a technical prediction equivalent to the modern web.

Classification: **retrospective symbolic association**.

### “He died accidentally while seeking air”

Discussed above: no adequate documentary support.

Classification: **unverified rumor**.

### The anti-biographical philosopher

Deleuze cultivated the claim that philosophers’ lives are largely uninteresting and that writing concerns impersonal becomings rather than private anecdote. This was partly methodological and partly self-protective. _L’Abécédaire_ simultaneously resists biography and supplies a carefully staged autobiographical mythology.

Classification: **documented self-presentation**.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Academic institutionalization

By the time of his death, Deleuze’s work had become internationally influential, especially in:

- Philosophy.
- Literary theory.
- Film and media studies.
- Art history.
- Architecture.
- Geography and urban theory.
- Anthropology.
- Feminist and queer theory.
- Political theory.
- Science and technology studies.
- Music and sound studies.
- Performance studies.
- Education.
- Digital and network culture.

The expansion was particularly strong in Anglophone universities, where Deleuze and Guattari became central components of “French Theory.”

### Concepts in circulation

Widely borrowed terms include:

- Rhizome.
- Assemblage/agencement.
- Deterritorialization.
- Becoming.
- Body without organs.
- Affect.
- Multiplicity.
- Nomadology.
- Minor literature.
- Smooth and striated space.
- Control society.
- Virtuality.
- Plane of immanence.

Translation created conceptual problems. “Agencement,” for example, became “assemblage” in English, a useful but incomplete equivalent that can obscure agency, arrangement, fitting, and process.

### Art, architecture, and design

Architects and artists appropriated the fold, smooth space, diagram, and rhizome. Some applications remained closely argued; others reduced the concepts to visual motifs or branding.

### Politics

Deleuze influenced autonomism, post-anarchism, alter-globalization, networked activism, affect theory, and analyses of control. Critics argue that the vocabulary of fluidity and networks was also absorbed by flexible capitalism, management, marketing, and digital platforms.

This double reception—revolutionary vocabulary and corporate adaptability—became a major question in later Deleuze studies.

### Cinema

The two cinema books helped establish film philosophy as a field not reducible to ideological criticism or semiotics. Their taxonomies remain debated but continue to organize scholarship on modern cinema.

### Continuing disputes

Posthumous interpretation divides among:

- Metaphysical Deleuze.
- Political Deleuze.
- Vitalist or materialist Deleuze.
- Spinozist monist Deleuze.
- Pluralist and process philosopher.
- Aesthetic philosopher.
- Philosopher compatible with science.
- Speculative thinker whose scientific borrowings require skepticism.

No consensus has replaced this plurality.

### Recent developments

The publication of edited courses from 2023 onward has renewed attention to Deleuze as a teacher rather than only as an opaque writer. The 2024 Spinoza course and 2025 course volumes reveal the oral construction of concepts and the interaction with students.

Recent controversy continues to concentrate on the 1977 petition, sexuality debates of the French 1970s, Israel/Palestine, the political legacy of “French Theory,” and whether concepts designed for emancipation have been absorbed by capitalism and digital control.

## Atlas Connections

Only documented or explicitly reported connections with people in the supplied atlas roster are included. Mere shared interest or contemporaneity is excluded.

### Pierre-Félix Guattari

**Documented fact.** Met in 1969 through Jean-Pierre Muyard; collaborated in the Limousin, Dhuizon/La Borde milieu, Paris, and through correspondence until Guattari’s death in 1992. Coauthored five principal books and additional texts.

### Paul-Michel Foucault

**Documented fact.** Academic colleague, friend, Nietzsche editorial collaborator, Vincennes associate, GIP-era political collaborator, and interlocutor in “Les intellectuels et le pouvoir.” Their friendship cooled in the late 1970s. Deleuze spoke at Foucault’s funeral in 1984 and published _Foucault_ in 1986.

### Jacques Derrida

**Documented fact.** Shared French philosophical and conference networks, including the 1972 Cerisy Nietzsche colloquium. Derrida wrote a memorial text after Deleuze’s death. Their connection was collegial and philosophical, not a sustained coauthorship.

### Jacques Marie Émile Lacan

**Documented intellectual relationship.** Deleuze and Guattari worked in a field transformed by Lacan; Guattari had direct Lacanian institutional connections. _Anti-Oedipus_ criticized Oedipal and signifier-centered psychoanalysis while appropriating selected Lacanian concepts. No close personal friendship between Deleuze and Lacan is established here.

### Jean-Paul Sartre

**Documented influence and political association.** Sartre was a formative philosophical model for Deleuze’s generation. Deleuze wrote “Il a été mon maître.” Both later appeared among signatories of political petitions, including the January 1977 text, but this does not establish close personal friendship.

### Friedrich Nietzsche

**Documented intellectual influence.** Subject of Deleuze’s 1962 monograph and 1965 introductory book. Deleuze participated in the Royaumont and Cerisy Nietzsche colloquia and assisted French work on the Colli–Montinari edition. This is influence across time, not personal contact.

### Henri-Louis Bergson

**Documented intellectual influence.** Deleuze edited Bergson selections, wrote _Le Bergsonisme_, and used duration, memory, and the virtual throughout his later work, particularly the cinema books.

### Baruch Spinoza

The roster lists no entry under the conventional name Baruch Spinoza, so no formal roster connection is recorded, despite Spinoza’s central importance to Deleuze.

### Immanuel Kant

**Documented intellectual relationship.** Subject of _La Philosophie critique de Kant_. Deleuze criticized Kant’s representational and juridical structures while adapting transcendental philosophy into “transcendental empiricism.”

### Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel

**Documented intellectual relationship.** Deleuze studied under Jean Hyppolite and wrote on Hyppolite’s Hegel. His mature work opposed dialectical negation and contradiction, although scholars dispute how completely he escaped Hegelian structures.

### Karl Marx

**Documented intellectual influence.** Marxian political economy is central to _Capitalisme et schizophrénie_. Deleuze and Guattari combined Marx’s analysis of capitalism with Nietzschean genealogy and criticism of psychoanalysis. No personal connection was possible.

### Sigmund Freud

**Documented intellectual relationship.** Freud was a major object of appropriation and attack. _Anti-Oedipus_ criticized Oedipal reduction while retaining engagement with drives, repression, and the unconscious.

### Marcel Proust

**Documented intellectual influence.** Subject of _Proust et les signes_, repeatedly expanded from 1964 through 1976. Deleuze read the _Recherche_ as an apprenticeship in signs rather than primarily a memoir.

### Antonin Artaud

**Documented intellectual influence.** Artaud’s phrase “body without organs” became central to Deleuze and Guattari. Deleuze also discussed Artaud in _Difference and Repetition_, _Logic of Sense_, and later work.

### William Seward Burroughs II

**Documented intellectual influence.** Burroughs’s term “control” helped shape Deleuze’s “Post-scriptum sur les sociétés de contrôle.” Burroughs also figured in the broader literary and countercultural network invoked by Deleuze and Guattari. No close personal relationship is established.

### Claude Lévi-Strauss

**Documented intellectual relationship.** Lévi-Straussian structural anthropology formed part of the intellectual field addressed and criticized in _Anti-Oedipus_ and _Mille plateaux_. No close collaboration was found.

### Jean Baudrillard

**Reported institutional/intellectual intersection.** Both were associated with post-1968 French theory and Parisian academic culture. Their ideas were later grouped under “French Theory,” but no substantial personal collaboration is established. Classification: **reported shared scene, not demonstrated close relationship**.

### Roland Gérard Barthes

**Documented shared intellectual and political milieu.** Both were leading figures in postwar French theory and appeared among signatories of the January 1977 petition. No sustained collaboration was found.

### Guy-Ernest Debord

No documented personal meeting, correspondence, or collaboration was established from the consulted sources. Their theories have often been compared, but conceptual comparison is not a biographical connection.

### Albert Camus

No documented close personal connection was established. Both belonged to successive generations of Parisian intellectual life, but contemporaneity is insufficient.

### Avram Noam Chomsky

No direct biographical connection was established. Later debates contrast Chomskyan linguistics with Deleuze and Guattari’s pragmatics, but no supported meeting or correspondence was found.

### Walter Benjamin

No personal connection was possible in practical terms: Benjamin died in 1940 when Deleuze was fifteen. A circulating online story claiming a 1985 meeting is chronologically impossible and is therefore **false/modern fabricated rumor**.

### Slavoj Žižek

**Documented posthumous critic.** Žižek wrote _Organs Without Bodies_ about Deleuze’s legacy and has circulated interpretations of his death. This is a posthumous reception connection, not a lifetime relationship established here.

### Mark Fisher

**Documented posthumous influence.** Fisher drew on Deleuze and Guattari in cultural criticism. No lifetime meeting is established.

### Nick Land

**Documented posthumous/intellectual reception.** Land’s 1990s work at Warwick used Deleuze and Guattari intensively. No reliable direct personal relationship with Deleuze was established.

### Donna Jeanne Haraway

No documented personal relationship was found. Later scholarship compares assemblages, becoming, companion species, and situated knowledge, but conceptual affinity alone is not an atlas crossing.

### Remaining roster

No supported meeting, correspondence, collaboration, rivalry, institutional bond, family tie, or explicitly acknowledged influence was found for the great majority of the supplied 896-person roster. Absence of a listed connection does not mean Deleuze never read or mentioned the person; it means the research did not establish an atlas-level biographical crossing strong enough to record.

## Chronology

- **18 Jan. 1925:** Born in Paris’s 17th arrondissement.
- **1928:** Family photographs and later biographies place Gilles with elder brother Georges during family holidays.
- **1930s:** Educated in Paris; family household described as conservative and bourgeois.
- **1940:** Wartime displacement to Normandy; schooling at Deauville.
- **Early 1940s:** Pierre Halbwachs becomes a formative teacher.
- **1943–1944:** Philosophy preparation in Paris, including instruction associated with Jean Beaufret.
- **1944:** Publishes “Description de la femme.”
- **2 July 1944:** Georges Deleuze arrested for Resistance activity.
- **1944:** Georges dies during deportation near Revigny-sur-Ornain.
- **1944–1948:** Studies philosophy at the Sorbonne.
- **1948:** Passes the agrégation in philosophy.
- **1948–1952:** Teaches at Amiens.
- **1952–1955:** Teaches at Orléans.
- **1953:** Publishes _Empirisme et subjectivité_.
- **1955–1957:** Teaches at Lycée Louis-le-Grand; begins Sorbonne assistantship according to institutional chronology.
- **1955:** Publishes/edits _Instincts et institutions_.
- **1956:** Marries Denise “Fanny” Grandjouan.
- **1957:** Publishes Bergson anthology _Mémoire et vie_.
- **1957–1960:** Teaches history of philosophy at the Sorbonne.
- **1960:** Son Julien born.
- **1960–1964:** Researcher at CNRS.
- **1962:** Publishes _Nietzsche et la philosophie_; Foucault reportedly proposes him for Clermont-Ferrand appointment, unsuccessfully.
- **1963:** Publishes _La Philosophie critique de Kant_.
- **1964:** Daughter Émilie born; Deleuze begins teaching at Lyon; publishes _Proust et les signes_.
- **1964:** Participates in Royaumont Nietzsche colloquium.
- **1965:** Publishes _Nietzsche_.
- **1966:** Publishes _Le Bergsonisme_.
- **1967:** Publishes _Présentation de Sacher-Masoch_.
- **1968:** Publishes doctoral theses _Différence et répétition_ and _Spinoza et le problème de l’expression_.
- **1968:** Severe pulmonary illness and major surgery.
- **1969:** Publishes _Logique du sens_.
- **1969:** Meets Félix Guattari.
- **Oct. 1969:** Joins experimental university at Vincennes.
- **1970:** Publishes Spinoza text selection.
- **1971–1972:** Associated with prison-information activism and Foucault’s political network.
- **1972:** Publishes _L’Anti-Œdipe_ with Guattari.
- **4 Mar. 1972:** Recorded political-intellectual conversation amid GIP-era prison struggles.
- **1972:** Participates in Cerisy Nietzsche colloquium.
- **1972:** Publishes discussion “Les intellectuels et le pouvoir” with Foucault.
- **1975:** Publishes _Kafka: Pour une littérature mineure_ with Guattari.
- **1976:** Publishes _Rhizome_ with Guattari.
- **26 Jan. 1977:** Gilles and Fanny Deleuze sign controversial Versailles-case petition.
- **1977:** Publishes _Dialogues_ with Claire Parnet.
- **1977:** Publicly attacks the “nouveaux philosophes.”
- **Late 1970s:** Friendship with Foucault cools amid political and intellectual disagreement.
- **1979:** Publishes _Superpositions_ with Carmelo Bene.
- **1980:** Publishes _Mille plateaux_ with Guattari.
- **1980:** Paris VIII moves from Vincennes to Saint-Denis.
- **1981:** Publishes _Spinoza: Philosophie pratique_ and _Francis Bacon_.
- **Mar.–June 1981:** Delivers course later published as _Sur la peinture_.
- **1983:** Publishes _Cinéma 1_ and “Grandeur de Yasser Arafat.”
- **1984:** Foucault dies; Deleuze speaks at his funeral.
- **1985:** Publishes _Cinéma 2_.
- **1986:** Publishes _Foucault_.
- **1987:** Gives “Qu’est-ce que l’acte de création?” lecture; retires 30 September.
- **1988:** Publishes _Le Pli_ and _Périclès et Verdi_.
- **1988–1989:** Records _L’Abécédaire_ with Claire Parnet and Pierre-André Boutang.
- **1990:** Publishes _Pourparlers_ and “Post-scriptum sur les sociétés de contrôle.”
- **1991:** Publishes _Qu’est-ce que la philosophie?_ with Guattari.
- **1992:** Guattari dies; Deleuze publishes “L’Épuisé.”
- **1993:** Publishes _Critique et clinique_.
- **16 Apr. 1995:** INA records an Arte broadcast segment of _L’Abécédaire_.
- **1995:** Publishes “L’Immanence: une vie…”
- **4 Nov. 1995:** Dies by defenestration at his Paris home; death reported as suicide.
- **1996:** Revised _Dialogues_ published posthumously.
- **1997:** Badiou publishes _Deleuze: La clameur de l’Être_.
- **2002:** _L’Île déserte et autres textes_ published.
- **2003:** _Deux régimes de fous_ published.
- **2007:** François Dosse publishes the major dual biography.
- **2015:** _Lettres et autres textes_ published.
- **2023:** _Sur la peinture_ published from course recordings.
- **2024:** _Sur Spinoza_ published.
- **2025:** Further edited courses on the state/war machine and lines of life published.
- **2026:** Scholarship and political controversy continue, including research into Jewish and antisemitic figurations in Deleuze’s reception.
