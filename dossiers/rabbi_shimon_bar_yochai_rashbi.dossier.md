# Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai: Research Dossier

## Scope and Method

Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai is a historical second-century Jewish sage whose biography survives almost entirely in texts compiled generations or centuries after his lifetime. No contemporary inscription, letter, Roman record, securely dated manuscript, property document, or archaeological object names him.

This dossier therefore distinguishes:

- **Historically probable core:** conclusions cautiously reconstructed from recurrent tannaitic traditions and the political setting.
- **Rabbinic report:** material in the Mishnah, Tosefta, tannaitic midrash, Palestinian Talmud, Babylonian Talmud, or classical midrash. Such evidence is historically valuable but not contemporary biography.
- **Late tradition or hagiography:** medieval and early-modern accounts elaborating his life, death, miracles, and tomb.
- **Traditional attribution:** a claim accepted within important religious traditions but not demonstrated by contemporary evidence.
- **Modern scholarly finding:** conclusions based on philology, textual history, manuscript evidence, and historical comparison.
- **Unverified claim:** a claim for which adequate supporting evidence has not been found.

“Rabbi Shimon” in rabbinic literature is not invariably Shimon bar Yochai; identification depends on textual context.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

**Principal Hebrew name:** רבי שמעון בן יוחאי, *Rabbi Shimʿon ben Yoḥai*  
**Aramaic form:** רבי שמעון בר יוחאי, *Rabbi Shimʿon bar Yoḥai*  
**Common English forms:** Shimon bar Yochai; Simeon ben Yohai; Simon bar Yoḥai  
**Honorific abbreviation:** רשב״י, *Rashbi*  
**Usual Mishnaic designation:** רבי שמעון, “Rabbi Shimon”  
**Religious title:** *Tanna*, a sage associated with the strata underlying the Mishnah  
**Later mystical designation:** “the holy tanna,” or *ha-tanna ha-Elohi*, in devotional literature

### Dates

- **Birth:** unattested.
- **Death:** unattested.
- **Secure chronological description:** flourished in the second century CE, especially in the generation after Rabbi Akiva.
- **Frequently printed dates such as approximately 100–160 CE:** modern estimates, not documentary dates.
- **Death on 18 Iyyar/Lag BaOmer:** late tradition; not stated in the Mishnah, either Talmud, or an independent contemporary record.

### Places associated with him

- The Land of Israel under Roman rule.
- Yavne: associated with a possible youthful appearance in a late rabbinic narrative; historically disputed.
- Bnei Brak: later tradition places prolonged study under Rabbi Akiva there.
- Southern Judea: associated with Rabbi Akiva’s post-crisis circle.
- A cave: the Palestinian and Babylonian traditions agree on concealment but do not securely establish its modern location.
- Tiberias: associated in rabbinic tradition with his purification of areas suspected of grave impurity.
- Tekoa: associated with a later teaching circle, although its identification—Judean Tekoa or a Galilean locality—is disputed.
- Meron: traditional burial place; the identification is medieval, not contemporary.
- Peki’in: widely promoted as the cave’s location in later local tradition, but the ancient narratives do not identify it securely.
- Rome: destination of a legendary diplomatic mission.

## Family and Ancestry

### Father

His patronymic identifies his father as **Yohai/Yoḥai**. Nothing independently verifiable is known about Yohai’s ancestry, profession, residence, wealth, or public office.

A Babylonian anecdote has Shimon, while demanding instruction from the imprisoned Rabbi Akiva, threaten to inform his father; another passage says that had Yohai still been alive, the sages could not lightly have sent his son on a dangerous mission. Older biographers inferred that Yohai possessed influence with Roman authorities. That inference is possible but not established.

### Mother, ancestry, and siblings

No reliable source identifies his mother, grandparents, siblings, tribe, or broader lineage. Claims of distinguished ancestry belong to later embellishment unless separately documented.

### Wife

The Babylonian cave narrative mentions an **unnamed wife** who brought bread and water to Shimon and his son while they initially hid in a study hall. No secure name or biography survives. The narrative’s disparaging generalization that women might yield under torture belongs to the literary characterization of the story and is not evidence about his wife’s conduct.

### Children

**Rabbi Eleazar/Elazar son of Rabbi Shimon** is consistently presented as his son and principal companion. In the Babylonian cave story, father and son hide together, practice extreme austerity, and initially burn agricultural laborers or their surroundings through their destructive gaze. Eleazar later became an important rabbinic figure in his own right.

No other child can be identified with confidence.

### Pinhas ben Yair

Babylonian Shabbat 33b calls **Rabbi Pinhas ben Yair** Shimon’s *ḥatanei*. The term and the relationship are interpreted inconsistently: translations often call Pinhas Shimon’s son-in-law, while other traditions reverse or otherwise reconstruct the relationship. A family relationship is reported, but its exact form is uncertain.

---

## Childhood and Early Life

No childhood narrative can be dated close to Shimon’s lifetime. His birthplace is not known. “Born in Galilee,” repeated in older reference works, is a reconstruction rather than a securely transmitted datum.

A story in Babylonian Berakhot 28a has a young Shimon participating in events involving Rabban Gamaliel II and Rabbi Joshua at Yavne. Nineteenth-century scholars already questioned whether its chronology could be reconciled with the later traditions concerning Yohai and Rabbi Akiva. It should not be treated as a dated eyewitness record.

There is no evidence concerning childhood wealth, occupation, literacy training, illnesses, or marriage age.

---

## Education and Formation

### Rabbi Akiva

**Rabbinic report; historically probable relationship:** Shimon belonged to the school of **Rabbi Akiva**. Babylonian Yevamot 62b names Rabbi Meir, Rabbi Judah, Rabbi Yose, Rabbi Shimon, and Rabbi Eleazar ben Shammua as the five later students through whom Akiva restored Torah learning after the deaths of an earlier body of disciples.

Later tradition says Shimon studied with Akiva for thirteen years at Bnei Brak. The exact duration is not independently verifiable.

A prison narrative portrays Shimon demanding instruction after Akiva’s arrest. Its threat—“If you will not teach me, I will tell Yohai my father”—may be comic, polemical, or preserve a memory of Yohai’s standing. It cannot establish a governmental connection without corroboration.

### Intellectual formation

Shimon’s transmitted rulings belong broadly to Akiva’s interpretive school. Babylonian Sanhedrin 86a associates anonymous material in the **Sifrei** with Shimon following Akiva’s method. This identifies a school or stream of tradition, not personal authorship of the surviving compilation.

His legal reasoning is often characterized by attention to intention, purpose, or underlying rationale. The formula “Rabbi Shimon interprets the reason of Scripture” became associated with his jurisprudence, although reducing hundreds of rulings to one method is misleading.

---

## Historical Context

Shimon’s career belongs to Roman Palestine after the destruction of the Jerusalem Temple in 70 CE and around or after the Bar Kokhba revolt of 132–135.

Rabbinic literature associates Rabbi Akiva with Bar Kokhba, but it does **not securely document Shimon personally commanding troops, financing the revolt, meeting Bar Kokhba, or serving in a rebel government**. Calling Shimon a military revolutionary or formal “leader of the revolt” exceeds the evidence.

Roman repression following the revolt provides a credible setting for stories of arrests, hiding, and disrupted schools. Nevertheless, the detailed cave narrative survives in sources edited long afterward and must be analyzed as literature as well as possible historical memory.

## Chronological Life History

### Before approximately 130 CE: formation

Shimon likely entered the learned network associated with Rabbi Akiva. The exact sequence among Yavne, Bnei Brak, and southern academies cannot be reconstructed.

Traditions place him among Akiva’s intimate pupils and preserve disputes with other major second-century sages, especially Rabbi Judah bar Ilai, Rabbi Yose ben Halafta, Rabbi Meir, and Rabbi Ishmael’s school.

### The Akivan crisis and Roman persecution

Yevamot 62b says Akiva’s earlier students died between Passover and Shavuot because they failed to honor one another; it identifies the cause of death as a severe epidemic or diphtheria. Later interpreters connected this account to wartime losses in the Bar Kokhba revolt, but the Talmudic passage itself does not state that they were soldiers.

Shimon appears as one of the later five disciples who maintained Akiva’s teaching. This is an important institutional memory, though not a contemporary enrollment record.

### Anti-Roman conversation

Babylonian Shabbat 33b relates a discussion among:

- Rabbi Judah bar Ilai,
- Rabbi Yose ben Halafta,
- Rabbi Shimon,
- Judah son of converts.

Judah praises Roman markets, bridges, and bathhouses. Shimon replies that Rome built markets for prostitution, bathhouses for self-indulgence, and bridges to collect tolls. The conversation reaches the authorities:

- Judah is promoted;
- Yose is exiled to Sepphoris;
- Shimon is condemned to death.

**Evidentiary status:** rabbinic report with plausible Roman-period political content, but not a contemporaneous trial record. No Roman decree naming Shimon survives.

The story establishes Shimon’s later literary image as the uncompromising critic of empire. It does not prove membership in an organized revolutionary party.

### Hiding and the cave

#### Palestinian version

The Palestinian tradition says Shimon and Eleazar hid in a cave for thirteen years during persecution, ate carobs, and emerged after interpreting birds caught or released in response to heavenly utterances as evidence that providence governed their safety.

#### Babylonian version

Shabbat 33b–34a presents a more elaborate sequence:

1. Shimon and Eleazar first hide in a study hall.
2. Shimon’s unnamed wife brings food and water.
3. Fearing torture might expose them, they move to a cave.
4. A miraculous carob tree and spring sustain them.
5. To preserve their clothing, they sit unclothed in sand while studying, dressing only for prayer.
6. After twelve years, Elijah announces that the emperor has died and the decree ended.
7. On emerging, they see people plowing and sowing.
8. Regarding agriculture as abandonment of eternal Torah for temporary life, their gaze burns whatever it strikes.
9. A heavenly voice orders them back into the cave for destroying God’s world.
10. After twelve additional months they emerge again.
11. Eleazar still harms what he encounters; Shimon heals it.
12. An elderly man carrying two bundles of myrtle in honor of the Sabbath reconciles them to ordinary Jewish life.

**Historical assessment:** concealment during Roman repression is possible. The exact twelve-plus-one-year chronology, miraculous food, Elijah, fiery gaze, and heavenly voice are hagiographic elements. The story’s principal literary arc is Shimon’s movement from absolute withdrawal to partial reconciliation with work, embodiment, and community.

A modern comparative study by Michal Bar-Asher Siegal identifies affinities between the Babylonian presentation and late-antique Christian monastic narratives. This does not demonstrate that Shimon never hid; it shows that the surviving story was shaped within a wider ascetic literary environment.

### Bodily injury and recovery

The Babylonian account says prolonged burial in sand left Shimon’s skin fissured. Pinhas ben Yair took him to a bathhouse and wept when his tears caused pain in the wounds. Shimon interpreted the suffering as the source of intensified learning.

**Status:** literary report, not clinical evidence. No independent medical diagnosis or later health history is available.

### Purification of Tiberias

Both Palestinian and Babylonian materials connect his emergence with a public act in **Tiberias**. Areas believed to contain human remains created ritual-impurity problems. Shimon reportedly used lupines to identify buried corpses, removed remains, and marked pure and impure areas.

A hostile observer accused him of declaring a cemetery pure. Shimon answered that the man had attended the original deliberation and, in some versions, fixed him with a lethal gaze or reduced him to a heap of bones.

**Historically possible core:** a rabbinic authority participated in defining burial zones and urban purity.  
**Legendary development:** the diagnostic plants and fatal gaze.  
**Controversial aspect:** the story depicts coercive or lethal supernatural retaliation against a critic; no historical death can be demonstrated.

### Teaching circle

Later sources place Shimon’s school at **Tekoa**. Pupils or associates attributed to his circle include his son Eleazar and figures such as Judah ha-Nasi in some traditions. The geographical identification and chronology are uncertain.

Shimon’s statements became deeply embedded in the Mishnah, Tosefta, Sifrei, both Mekhiltot, and later Talmuds. This demonstrates the authority of traditions transmitted under his name, but not that every attribution is verbatim.

### Mission to Rome

Babylonian Meilah 17b reports anti-Jewish decrees prohibiting Sabbath observance, circumcision, and family purity. Shimon, regarded as “accustomed to miracles,” travels toward Rome with **Rabbi Eleazar son of Rabbi Yose**.

The story says the demon Ben Temalion possesses the emperor’s daughter; Shimon exorcises it, gains access to an imperial archive, and has the decrees annulled. It also portrays Shimon angrily cursing or punishing his younger companion for answering a legal question before him.

**Assessment:**

- Jewish delegations to Roman authorities are historically plausible.
- The demon, possession, miraculous cure, and archive episode are hagiography.
- The emperor and daughter are unnamed.
- Attempts to identify the daughter with Lucilla and the emperor with Marcus Aurelius are conjectural.
- The episode cannot securely establish that Shimon personally visited Rome.

### Final years

No source permits a reliable year-by-year reconstruction. He continued to function in rabbinic memory as teacher, disputant, exegete, miracle worker, and model of total Torah study.

There is no reliable evidence of salaried employment, land ownership, commercial investment, institutional office, or personal wealth. His transmitted opposition to routine labor as an ideal should not be mistaken for a financial biography.

## Religious and Intellectual Development

### Torah study and labor

In Babylonian Berakhot 35b, Rabbi Ishmael reads “you shall gather your grain” as requiring Torah study combined with worldly occupation. Shimon asks how Torah could survive if a person plowed, sowed, harvested, threshed, and winnowed in their seasons. Under ideal conditions, he argues, Israel’s work should be done by others.

The later Babylonian narrator, through Abaye, adds that many followed Ishmael successfully, while those who adopted Shimon’s program did not succeed. The tradition thus preserves both Shimon’s radical position and an internal critique of its practicality.

### Asceticism

The cave account intensifies this position into separation from food production, clothing, public life, and family. Its conclusion limits the extremism: ordinary people’s Sabbath devotion becomes the evidence that worldly activity need not exclude holiness.

### Attitude toward Rome

His attributed criticism is economic and moral:

- marketplaces linked to prostitution,
- bathhouses to elite pleasure,
- bridges to taxation.

Other traditions portray him rejecting Roman power more broadly. These sayings made him usable by later nationalist and anti-imperial movements, although his own political program is unknown.

### Law and jurisprudence

Subjects on which traditions attributed to him survive include Sabbath law, ritual purity, sacrifices, marriage and divorce, damages, criminal procedure, vows, agriculture, prayer, and scriptural interpretation.

His prominence in the Mishnah makes him a major transmitter or emblem of the Akivan school. The majority rule does not invariably follow him, and later legal tradition frequently records his position as a dissent.

### Mysticism

Early rabbinic literature gives Shimon access to miracles and esoteric prestige, but it does not contain the medieval **Zohar** as a second-century book. His transformation into the central master of Kabbalah occurred through medieval pseudepigraphy and later reception.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Rabbi Akiva

Teacher and formative authority. **Rabbinic report; historically probable.**

### Rabbi Meir, Rabbi Judah bar Ilai, Rabbi Yose ben Halafta, Rabbi Eleazar ben Shammua

Fellow members of Akiva’s later circle. They appear as colleagues and disputants rather than a fixed organization. **Rabbinic report.**

### Rabbi Judah bar Ilai

Intellectual and political foil in the Roman-works narrative: Judah praises imperial infrastructure; Shimon exposes its exploitative purposes. **Rabbinic report.**

### Rabbi Yose ben Halafta

Colleague in the same narrative and father of Shimon’s purported companion on the Roman mission. **Rabbinic report.**

### Rabbi Eleazar son of Rabbi Yose

Companion on the legendary Roman embassy; also a target of Shimon’s anger in that story. **Late rabbinic report with miraculous elements.**

### Rabbi Eleazar son of Shimon

Son, cave companion, student, and in later literature an independent sage. **Rabbinic report; family relationship highly probable.**

### Pinhas ben Yair

Relative by marriage and healer after the cave episode. Exact kinship is textually uncertain. **Rabbinic report.**

### Judah son of converts

The hearer through whom Shimon’s criticism allegedly reaches the Roman state. Later versions punish or stigmatize him. **Rabbinic literary figure; historicity uncertain.**

### Roman authorities

Collective adversary in the cave narrative and petitioned government in the embassy narrative. No named official can be securely connected to Shimon.

---

## Institutions, Projects, Property, and Financial Interests

### Rabbinic schools

Shimon is associated with Rabbi Akiva’s school and a later teaching center at Tekoa. These were networks of teachers and disciples, not documented corporations or endowed academies in the modern sense.

### Tiberias purification project

The closest thing to a documented civic project is the rabbinic tradition that he mapped grave impurity in Tiberias, enabling settlement or priestly movement through previously suspect areas.

### Personal finance

No evidence survives concerning:

- income,
- debts,
- investments,
- houses or agricultural land,
- slaves or employees,
- inheritance,
- patronage,
- taxes paid,
- manuscripts owned.

Claims that he lived solely on miraculous carobs belong to the cave hagiography, not an economic ledger.

### Tomb property

The modern property, donation, construction, and management arrangements at Meron concern religious trusts, governments, charities, and pilgrims centuries after Shimon’s death. They are part of his cult and legacy, not his personal estate.

## Controversies, Accusations, Violence, and Financial Questions

## 1. Anti-Roman sedition

**Claim:** Shimon publicly denounced Rome and was sentenced to death.

**Evidence:** Babylonian Shabbat 33b, compiled centuries after the presumed event.

**Finding:** possible memory of political repression; no Roman indictment, judgment, or execution order survives. His actual participation in revolt remains unresolved.

## 2. Destructive supernatural violence

The cave story says Shimon and Eleazar burned farmers with their gaze; another narrative makes a critic die or become bones.

**Label:** hagiographic supernatural violence, not demonstrable homicide.  
**Historical importance:** the stories preserve anxiety about charismatic severity. The Babylonian cave narrator explicitly rebukes the destruction through a divine voice.

## 3. Temper and punitive authority

Meilah 17b describes Rabbi Yose fearing that Shimon might punish his son, despite Shimon’s undertaking not to do so. The story says Shimon nevertheless pronounced words interpreted as causing the younger scholar not to return alive to his father.

**Label:** rabbinic moral narrative. It contributed to an image of Shimon as brilliant, dangerous, and exacting; it cannot establish a prosecutable death.

## 4. Zohar authorship

**Traditional claim:** Shimon revealed or authored the Zohar with his companions, sometimes during the cave years.

**Documentary problem:** the principal Zoharic literature appears in late-thirteenth-century Castile, more than a millennium after Shimon. Its language, geography, literary concerns, and citation history belong substantially to medieval Iberian Kabbalah.

**Moses de León:** he circulated Zoharic materials and represented them as ancient. A report preserved by Isaac of Acre says Moses’s widow or another household informant denied the existence of an ancient manuscript and said he wrote the work for financial gain. The report is indirect and survives through later transmission, so the widow story is not independently conclusive.

**Modern scholarly position:** the Zohar is a composite medieval corpus, often connected to Moses de León and a Castilian kabbalistic circle. Not every stratum need have one author. *Tikkunei ha-Zohar* and *Raʿya Meheimna* are commonly treated as later or separate strata.

**Finding:** personal second-century authorship by Shimon is unsupported by manuscript history and rejected in critical scholarship. Traditional attribution remains religiously authoritative in many communities.

Calling the work simply a “fraud” can obscure the established medieval convention of pseudepigraphic revelation; conversely, devotional acceptance cannot substitute for historical evidence.

## 5. Tomb identification

The Meron shrine is Shimon’s **traditional**, not archaeologically proven, grave. Ancient burial remains exist in the region, but no contemporary inscribed marker identifies his remains.

A thirteenth-century travel tradition is among the earliest evidence explicitly placing Shimon and Eleazar’s tombs at Meron. Earlier pilgrimage geography in the area involved other purported biblical and rabbinic graves.

## 6. Meron administration, money, and legal disputes

The shrine became associated with competing Sephardi and Ashkenazi religious endowments, donation boxes, building rights, lighting privileges, and governmental management.

A 2020 Israeli Supreme Court proceeding concerned control of the “candle room.” The petition was withdrawn subject to recognition of the authority of a court-created five-member management committee. This litigation concerned modern trustees and institutions, not Shimon.

Israel’s State Comptroller reported unauthorized construction, confused authority, inadequate oversight, and insufficient preparation at the compound well before the 2021 disaster.

## 7. The 2021 Mount Meron crowd crush

On Lag BaOmer 2021, 45 men and boys died in a crowd crush at the pilgrimage.

The Israeli State Commission of Inquiry’s final report, released in 2024, attributed the disaster to longstanding failures of governance, safety regulation, planning enforcement, and divided responsibility. It assigned personal responsibility to living officials and administrators.

**Finding:** this was a modern institutional disaster at a cult site bearing Shimon’s name. It is not evidence of misconduct by the historical Shimon. It is central to the history of his posthumous cult.

## 8. Recent adversarial-search findings, 2024–2026

Searches combining Shimon’s name with fraud, scandal, accusations, corruption, lawsuits, conspiracy, criticism, and Hebrew equivalents produced:

- continuing arguments over Zoharic authorship;
- implementation of the Meron commission’s recommendations;
- disputes over access, construction, management, and lighting rights;
- crowd-control restrictions and resistance to them;
- allegations concerning modern religious or political actors using the pilgrimage for money, authority, or publicity;
- a 2024 police-force incident near the shrine that resulted in disciplinary proceedings;
- renewed safety disagreements surrounding later pilgrimages.

No credible source produced a new historical criminal accusation against the second-century sage himself. Modern allegations attach to institutions or persons invoking his shrine and reputation and must not be retroactively assigned to him.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

## The thirteen-year cave

**Story:** Shimon and Eleazar lived on miraculous carobs and spring water, studied while buried in sand, received news from Elijah, and emerged with fiery power.

**Appearance:** Palestinian and Babylonian rabbinic traditions; the Babylonian version is more elaborate.

**Evidence:** agreement on hiding may preserve a historical core. Exact duration and miracles are unverifiable.

**Impact:** created the dominant image of Shimon as an ascetic recipient of secret Torah.

## The modern “Peki’in cave”

**Story:** a specific cave and carob tree at Peki’in are the places described in the Talmud.

**Evidence:** later localization; the Talmud does not securely name Peki’in.

**Impact:** pilgrimage, tourism, devotional geography, and claims of physical continuity with the narrative.

## The fiery gaze

**Story:** Shimon’s eyes burn workers, places, or opponents.

**Meaning:** later interpreters understand it as miraculous power, dangerous judgment, or a metaphor for ascetic intolerance.

**Evidence:** literary only.

**Impact:** made him both protector and potentially terrifying saint.

## Ben Temalion and the emperor’s daughter

**Story:** a demon enters an imperial daughter so Shimon can exorcise it and obtain cancellation of decrees.

**Evidence:** supernatural rabbinic narrative; proposed parallels with Christian saints’ lives exist.

**Impact:** established Shimon as miracle-working advocate for all Israel.

## Authorship of the Zohar

**Story:** Shimon and his circle recorded heavenly mysteries in the second century; the text remained concealed until medieval discovery.

**Evidence against literal authorship:** late medieval emergence, medieval linguistic and cultural features, composite textual strata, absence from earlier citation chains.

**Evidence invoked by traditional defenders:** the work’s self-presentation, traditions of concealment, early acceptance by kabbalists, and the possibility of later redaction of ancient teachings.

**Impact:** transformed a tannaitic legal sage into the paradigmatic master of Kabbalah.

## Death on Lag BaOmer

**Story:** Shimon died on 18 Iyyar while revealing the *Idra Zuta*; fire surrounded the house, and his death became a “wedding” or union with the divine.

**Appearance:** Zoharic and later Safedian kabbalistic tradition, not early rabbinic biography.

**Evidence:** no contemporary date; the association of Lag BaOmer with Shimon becomes visible in late medieval and especially sixteenth-century practice.

**Impact:** generated one of Judaism’s largest annual pilgrimages.

## Meron burial

**Story:** Shimon and his son are buried in the paired tomb structure at Meron.

**Evidence:** medieval travel and devotional traditions; no identifying second-century inscription.

**Impact:** permanent sacred geography, endowed property, donation economy, mass pilgrimage, legal conflict, and national regulation.

## Protective merit and miracle claims

Devotees invoke Shimon for healing, fertility, livelihood, children, protection, and rescue. Stories of answered prayer circulate through oral tradition, printed collections, donation campaigns, and contemporary media.

**Status:** religious testimony, not independently verified causal evidence.

## “Bar Yochai” hymn

The famous hymn beginning **“Bar Yoḥai, nimshaḥta ashrekha”** was not composed by Shimon. It is attributed to the sixteenth-century kabbalist **Shimon Lavi** and organizes its praise around the ten sefirot.

Its diffusion in Hebrew and Jewish languages helped standardize Shimon’s mystical persona far beyond specialist kabbalistic circles.

## Works by or Attributed to Shimon

No autograph manuscript or securely personal book survives.

### Authentic or early transmitted material

#### Sayings and rulings under “Rabbi Shimon”

Hundreds of legal and interpretive positions are transmitted in:

- the Mishnah;
- Tosefta;
- Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael;
- Mekhilta de-Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai;
- Sifra;
- Sifrei Numbers and Deuteronomy;
- Palestinian Talmud;
- Babylonian Talmud;
- classical midrash.

These are **traditions attributed to him**, not a recoverable personally written bibliography.

### Works associated with his school

#### *Mekhilta de-Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai*  
Hebrew: מכילתא דרבי שמעון בר יוחאי

A tannaitic halakhic midrash on Exodus associated with Shimon’s interpretive school. The extant work is textually reconstructed from medieval citations and Cairo Geniza fragments after largely disappearing from circulation.

**Attribution:** school or tradition attribution; not proven autograph composition.

#### *Sifrei*

Babylonian Sanhedrin 86a associates anonymous Sifrei material with Shimon in accordance with Akiva. The surviving collections are composite redacted works.

**Attribution:** traditional association with his school, not sole personal authorship.

### Medieval pseudepigrapha

#### *Sefer ha-Zohar*  
Hebrew/Aramaic: ספר הזוהר, “Book of Radiance”

First circulated in late-thirteenth-century Castile, prominently by Moses de León.

**Attribution:** traditionally Shimon and his circle; critically medieval and composite.

Major internal units include:

- *Zohar ʿal ha-Torah*;
- *Sifra di-Tsniʿuta*;
- *Idra Rabba*;
- *Idra Zuta*;
- *Midrash ha-Neʿlam*;
- *Heikhalot* materials;
- *Raʿya Meheimna*;
- *Tikkunei ha-Zohar*.

These units differ in date and authorship. They should not all be assigned automatically to either Shimon or Moses de León.

#### *Nistarot de-Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai* / *The Secrets of Rabbi Simon ben Yohai*

An early-medieval Jewish apocalypse presented as Shimon’s revelation and used to interpret the rise of Islam and subsequent empires.

**Attribution:** pseudepigraphic; not written by the second-century sage.

#### Other magical, apocalyptic, and mystical texts

Various prayers, secrets, angelological texts, and predictions circulate under Shimon’s name. Attribution generally rests on charismatic authority rather than manuscript proximity to his lifetime.

## Death and Burial

No early source supplies a death date, age, cause, final illness, or verifiable place of death.

The widely repeated account that he died at Meron on Lag BaOmer while revealing secrets is part of later mystical tradition. In the *Idra Zuta*, Shimon’s death is portrayed as an ecstatic culmination surrounded by fire and esoteric disclosure.

There is no evidence that he was executed by Rome. The cave narrative instead says the imperial decree lapsed when an emperor died.

No securely identified physical remains have been examined. The Meron structure is a traditional cenotaph or burial shrine whose historical identification cannot presently be demonstrated archaeologically.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Rabbinic sage

In early reception, Shimon was principally:

- an eminent student of Akiva;
- a major legal disputant;
- a transmitter of Akivan exegesis;
- a critic of Rome;
- an exemplar of uncompromising Torah study;
- a miracle worker in developing narrative tradition.

### Mystical master

The Zohar made “Rabbi Shimon” the leader of the *ḥavrayya*, a fellowship wandering through an idealized Galilee while revealing the hidden structure of Scripture and divinity. This literary Shimon is not simply a biography of the tanna; he is a medieval mystical persona constructed from rabbinic memory.

### Saint and intercessor

Safedian Kabbalah and later Hasidic, Sephardi, Mizrahi, and North African traditions made his tomb a place for prayer, vows, charity, first haircuts, music, bonfires, and appeals for fertility or healing.

### Lag BaOmer

Lag BaOmer is absent as Shimon’s death anniversary from the Bible, Mishnah, and Talmuds. Its origins are obscure. From the fifteenth and especially sixteenth centuries, Safed kabbalists increasingly connected the date to Shimon. Hayim Vital records visits by Isaac Luria and his circle around 1570–1571.

The pilgrimage later attracted Jews from many regions and also neighboring Arab, Druze, and Bedouin participants. Zionist culture reworked Lag BaOmer around Bar Kokhba, bows and arrows, national resistance, and signaling fires.

### Modern tensions

The cult’s scale generated:

- competing custodial claims;
- substantial charitable revenue;
- unlicensed construction;
- gender-segregation disputes;
- political patronage;
- resistance to state regulation;
- environmental criticism of bonfires;
- public-health and crowd-safety concerns;
- litigation and disaster inquiries.

The 2021 deaths permanently altered discussion of the shrine, shifting attention from devotional spontaneity toward capacity limits, engineering, accountability, and state responsibility.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the supplied roster and consulted sources are included.

### Moses de León — posthumous attribution and literary construction

**Nature:** Moses de León circulated major portions of the Zohar in late-thirteenth-century Castile under a framework presenting Shimon bar Yochai and his companions as ancient revelatory authorities.

**Dates and places:** approximately the 1280s–1305; Castile, including Guadalajara, Ávila, Valladolid, and surrounding literary networks.

**Label:** **documented medieval textual relationship; no lifetime meeting possible.** Shimon had been dead for roughly a millennium. The relationship consists of attribution, literary representation, and reception.

### Moses — literary association only

The Zohar repeatedly presents Shimon’s revelations in relation to the biblical Moses and sometimes elevates Shimon as an esoteric interpreter capable of disclosing Mosaic secrets.

**Label:** **mythic/literary connection, not a historical meeting.**

### Publius Aelius Hadrianus — imperial context, not a demonstrated encounter

Hadrian ruled during the Bar Kokhba revolt and the repression forming the probable background to Shimon’s generation. No consulted source documents a meeting, correspondence, appointment, or direct order exchanged between Hadrian and Shimon.

**Label:** **reported historical context; direct connection unverified.**

### Maimonides, Rashi, Abraham Abulafia, Moses, and later kabbalists on the roster

Their intellectual worlds intersect traditions later associated with Shimon, but no source consulted established a qualifying personal meeting or correspondence. Mere engagement with rabbinic or mystical traditions is insufficient for an Atlas crossing.

**Absence finding:** no other roster member was found to have a securely documented lifetime relationship with Shimon.
