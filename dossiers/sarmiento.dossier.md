# Domingo Faustino Sarmiento: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier reconstructs the documented life, writings, political activity, relationships, controversies, and posthumous reputation of Domingo Faustino Sarmiento.

Evidentiary labels used:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary records, Sarmiento’s writings or correspondence, official archives, or strong scholarly documentation.
- **Reported fact:** repeated in credible institutional or scholarly accounts, but the underlying record was not directly inspected here.
- **Disputed claim:** interpretations or factual claims for which significant competing versions exist.
- **Allegation or theory:** an accusation or explanatory theory not established by a judicial or documentary finding.
- **Rumor or myth:** a story circulating without adequate supporting evidence.
- **Retrospective classification:** a later analytical category—such as genocide or scientific racism—applied to nineteenth-century conduct or texts.

Sarmiento’s autobiographical works, especially *Mi defensa* and *Recuerdos de provincia*, are indispensable primary sources but are also acts of political self-fashioning. Statements drawn from them require comparison with correspondence, official records, and independent histories.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full registered name:** Faustino Valentín Quiroga Sarmiento.

**Usual name:** Domingo Faustino Sarmiento.

**Alternative forms:** Domingo F. Sarmiento; D. F. Sarmiento; Faustino Sarmiento.

**Name problem:** Official cultural institutions report that he was born on 14 February 1811 and entered in the baptismal or civil record on 15 February. The established commemorative date is 15 February. His family called him “Domingo,” reportedly because of his mother’s devotion to Saint Dominic and a family naming tradition.

**Titles and offices:**

- Teacher and school director
- Journalist and editor
- Member of the University of Chile
- Provincial legislator and senator
- Director of Schools of Buenos Aires
- Governor of San Juan, 1862–1864
- Argentine minister plenipotentiary to the United States, 1865–1868
- President of Argentina, 12 October 1868–12 October 1874
- National senator, 1874–1879
- Minister of the Interior, 1879
- Superintendent-General of Schools
- Military officer, ultimately holding the rank of general
- Freemason and, for a period, Grand Master

**Birth:** 14 or 15 February 1811, Carrascal neighborhood, city of San Juan, then within the Viceroyalty’s successor revolutionary territory, now Argentina.

**Death:** 11 September 1888, Asunción, Paraguay.

**Burial:** La Recoleta Cemetery, Buenos Aires. His funeral became a major civic commemoration.

**Nationality and political identity:** Argentine; associated successively with provincial Unitarian opposition, the anti-Rosas exile community, Buenos Aires liberalism, and the national state-building coalition usually grouped under the “historical presidencies.”

**Principal professions:** Teacher, journalist, author, editor, educational administrator, diplomat, military and political official.

**Known pseudonymous or collective signatures:** Sarmiento used initials, unsigned journalism, editorial voices, and occasional polemical personae, but no single stable literary pseudonym comparable to his public name. Attribution of anonymous newspaper articles often depends on later editorial collection.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Father:** José Clemente Cecilio Sarmiento Funes y Morales.

- **Documented/reported fact:** A soldier and provincial patriot who served in forces associated with the independence movement and the Army of the Andes.
- He possessed less economic stability than the family’s older lineage might suggest.
- Sarmiento’s later presentation of him alternated between affection, embarrassment over limited means, and incorporation into a patriotic genealogy.

**Mother:** Paula Zoila de Albarracín e Irrazábal, commonly Paula Albarracín.

- Born into an established San Juan family but lived under constrained economic circumstances.
- Supported the household through weaving and domestic production.
- Her loom and the family fig tree became central objects in Sarmiento’s autobiographical mythology.
- The Casa Natal de Sarmiento in San Juan preserves the domestic setting associated with her.

### Ancestry and siblings

Sarmiento represented both the Sarmiento and Albarracín lines as old provincial families whose standing had declined. His autobiographical genealogies link family history to San Juan’s colonial and revolutionary history.

Paula Albarracín reportedly bore fifteen children, most dying young. The children surviving into significant portions of adult life included Domingo and sisters commonly identified as Bienvenida, Procesa, María del Rosario, and Paula. Exact lists vary because some children died in infancy and family names recur in records.

**Procesa Sarmiento de Lenoir** became a painter and teacher and maintained an important correspondence with him. Letters to Procesa, Bienvenida, and Rosario constitute a major source for his domestic life.

### Partners, marriage, and children

#### María Jesús del Canto

- **Documented fact:** During Sarmiento’s first Chilean exile, he formed a relationship with María Jesús del Canto, described in many accounts as a pupil at the school where he taught.
- Their daughter, **Ana Faustina Sarmiento**, was born in Chile on 18 June 1832.
- They did not marry.
- The imbalance implicit in a teacher-pupil relationship should be noted, although surviving sources do not permit a full reconstruction of consent, ages, or the relationship’s duration.

#### Ana Faustina Sarmiento

- Sarmiento’s only generally accepted biological child.
- Became a teacher and later joined his household.
- Married Jules Belin, also rendered Julio Belín.
- Her children included **Augusto Belín Sarmiento**, later an important editor of Sarmiento’s writings, and **Eugenia Belín Sarmiento**, a painter responsible for a prominent portrait of her grandfather.
- Ana died in Buenos Aires in 1904.

#### Benita Agustina Martínez Pastoriza

- A San Juan-born widow living in Chile.
- Previously married to the merchant and mining entrepreneur Domingo Castro y Calvo.
- Sarmiento married her in 1848.
- Their marriage deteriorated during his political and journalistic activity and amid evidence of his intimate relationship with Aurelia Vélez Sarsfield.
- Separation occurred without a modern divorce proceeding; Catholic marriage law and nineteenth-century practice constrained formal dissolution.

#### Domingo Fidel Castro/Sarmiento, “Dominguito”

- Born in Santiago, Chile, 17 April 1845.
- Biological son of Benita Martínez Pastoriza and Domingo Castro y Calvo.
- Adopted or socially acknowledged as a son by Sarmiento after the latter’s marriage to Benita.
- Served as an Argentine officer in the Paraguayan War.
- Killed at Curupayty on 22 September 1866, aged twenty-one.
- His death profoundly affected Sarmiento, then in the United States. Sarmiento later assembled letters, recollections, and biography into *Vida de Dominguito*.

#### Aurelia Vélez Sarsfield

- Daughter of jurist and politician Dalmacio Vélez Sarsfield.
- **Documented fact:** Sarmiento and Aurelia maintained an extensive intimate and intellectual correspondence.
- Their relationship is generally described by biographers as romantic and sexual, although the surviving record is necessarily incomplete.
- Discovery of their correspondence contributed to the breakdown of Sarmiento’s marriage.
- Aurelia acted as confidante, political interlocutor, travel companion in later life, and guardian of aspects of his memory.
- Sensational accounts sometimes reduce her to “the president’s mistress”; the correspondence demonstrates a sustained intellectual and political relationship.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1811–1826

Sarmiento was born during the opening phase of the Spanish American revolutions. San Juan was a small, commercially connected provincial society whose political and economic position changed dramatically through independence and civil war.

His family home combined signs of older status with material scarcity. Paula Albarracín’s weaving sustained the household. Sarmiento later transformed the home, loom, patio, and fig tree into symbols of industrious provincial republican virtue.

In 1816 he entered one of San Juan’s **Escuelas de la Patria**. He later recalled being named “Primer Ciudadano” for school performance. The phrase belongs partly to his self-constructed exemplary childhood, but his precocious schooling is well supported.

He received no continuous university education. Failed opportunities to obtain scholarships for study in Buenos Aires or Córdoba became part of his account of exclusion from elite education. He compensated through intensive self-education.

Major formative reading included:

- The Bible and religious instructional literature
- Enlightenment political and historical works
- Rousseau
- Benjamin Franklin
- French revolutionary and liberal writing
- European histories and biographies
- Later, Alexis de Tocqueville and American educational literature

Family clergy were important teachers, especially his uncle, the priest **José de Oro**.

## Education and Formation

### San Francisco del Monte

Around 1825–1827 Sarmiento accompanied José de Oro to San Francisco del Monte de Oro, San Luis. They established or operated a small school. Sarmiento, still an adolescent, taught children and studied Latin.

This episode became the foundational story of “Sarmiento the teacher.” The later national cult often presents him as a fully formed educational reformer at fifteen; the evidence instead shows an adolescent provincial teacher acquiring experience under a clerical relative.

### Autodidactic formation

Sarmiento’s education was discontinuous and self-directed. He learned through family libraries, borrowed books, newspapers, political debate, translation, and journalism. His French was initially reading-based; he later acquired working knowledge through travel. His English remained limited and often required mediation, notably from Mary Mann.

### Intellectual mentors and models

- **José de Oro:** Teacher, uncle, and early political influence.
- **Manuel Montt:** Chilean patron and government sponsor.
- **Andrés Bello:** Intellectual authority, institutional colleague, and sometimes opponent in linguistic and educational disputes.
- **José Victorino Lastarria:** Chilean liberal collaborator and interlocutor.
- **Horace Mann:** Principal model for common-school organization.
- **Mary Peabody Mann:** Translator, correspondent, recruiter, and educational collaborator.
- **Benjamin Franklin:** Model of the self-made civic educator.
- **Alexis de Tocqueville:** Major interpretive source for the United States, though Sarmiento revised Tocqueville through direct observation.
- **François Guizot and French liberal historians:** Sources for his understanding of civilization, institutions, and historical development.
- **Jean-Jacques Rousseau:** Important to his republican language, although Sarmiento rejected rural primitivism.
- **Johann Heinrich Pestalozzi:** Part of the transatlantic pedagogical tradition informing common-school reform.

## Early Political and Military Formation, 1827–1840

### Civil wars and Unitarian affiliation

The wars between Unitarians and Federalists shaped Sarmiento’s adolescence. He entered provincial military and political struggles on the Unitarian side. He later portrayed Federalist rule as personal despotism and the suppression of literate civic life.

He was arrested or detained during provincial conflicts, although accounts differ on precise dates and circumstances. Stories of his defiance in prison belong both to documentary memory and to his later heroic self-presentation.

### First Chilean exile, 1831–1836

After the defeat of Unitarian forces, Sarmiento crossed the Andes into Chile.

His occupations included:

- Schoolteacher in Los Andes and Pocuro
- Shop or commercial employee
- Mining work or management near Copiapó
- Private tutor

He fathered Ana Faustina during this exile.

Illness—usually identified as typhoid fever—prompted his return to San Juan in 1836. Accounts agree that his health was seriously compromised, though detailed medical records are unavailable.

### San Juan cultural initiatives

Back in San Juan, Sarmiento helped organize the **Sociedad Literaria** and became involved in educational and cultural projects associated with the Generation of 1837’s broader liberal-romantic movement.

In 1839 he founded **El Zonda**, a newspaper critical of provincial authority. Financial pressure, censorship, and political hostility forced its closure.

### Second exile, 1840

In November 1840, under renewed Federalist pressure, he crossed again into Chile. Tradition holds that he wrote on the rock or wall at the Zonda passage:

> “On ne tue point les idées” — “Ideas cannot be killed.”

**Status: disputed anecdote with a probable autobiographical source.** Sarmiento circulated the story himself in *Recuerdos de provincia*. It became one of the central images of his civic legend. The act cannot be independently verified, and the French phrase’s exact literary origin has been debated.

## Chilean Career and Intellectual Emergence, 1841–1851

### Journalism

Chile offered Sarmiento a relatively stable press and political environment. He wrote for or helped direct:

- *El Mercurio* of Valparaíso
- *El Nacional*
- *El Progreso*
- *La Crónica*
- Other periodicals and educational publications

He became known for combative journalism, literary criticism, educational advocacy, and attacks on Juan Manuel de Rosas.

His prose was rapid, personal, polemical, and sometimes factually careless. Opponents criticized grammatical irregularity and aggression; admirers treated the same qualities as signs of originality.

### Educational administration

In 1842 the Chilean government entrusted him with the new **Escuela Normal de Preceptores** in Santiago, often described as the first normal school for male teachers in Latin America.

He produced instructional books, advocated teacher training, and engaged in the Chilean orthography controversy. He favored spelling reform designed to align writing more closely with American pronunciation and simplify literacy. Andrés Bello supported reform in principle but differed over methods and institutional pace.

### University of Chile

Sarmiento became associated with the University of Chile at its founding. His participation connected him with Bello, Lastarria, Montt, and Chile’s principal intellectual networks.

### *Facundo*

In 1845, *Civilización i barbarie. Vida de Juan Facundo Quiroga* first appeared serially in *El Progreso*, then as a book.

The work combined:

- Biography of the caudillo Facundo Quiroga
- Attack on Rosas
- Geography and social typology
- Political program
- Travel-derived and secondhand testimony
- Fictionalized scenes
- A theory of conflict between urban “civilization” and pastoral “barbarism”

Its documentary details are uneven. Sarmiento did not personally know many events he narrated and sometimes privileged dramatic structure over verification. The book remains both a primary political intervention and a hybrid literary-historical work.

### Government-sponsored journey, 1845–1848

Manuel Montt commissioned Sarmiento to investigate primary education abroad. He sailed from Chile on 28 October 1845.

Documented itinerary included:

- France
- Spain
- Algeria and Oran
- Italy
- Switzerland
- German states
- Belgium
- Holland
- Britain
- United States
- Canada
- Cuba
- Panama
- Return along the Pacific coast to Chile

In France he met **José de San Martín** at Grand Bourg in 1846. Sarmiento’s recollection is an important record of the aged liberator.

In Italy he obtained an audience with Pope Pius IX.

He reached New York on 15 September 1847 and spent roughly two and a half months in the United States and Canada. He visited New York, Boston, Washington, Philadelphia, parts of the Great Lakes region, Montreal, Quebec, and New Orleans.

His meeting with **Horace and Mary Mann** in Massachusetts was decisive. Mary translated between the men. Sarmiento observed schools and treated Massachusetts as an institutional model.

His expense notebook survives, documenting the practical costs of travel. His letters became *Viajes por Europa, África i América*.

### Marriage and family reorganization

After returning to Chile, Sarmiento married Benita Martínez Pastoriza in 1848 and incorporated Dominguito into his household.

### Major educational publications

His Chilean period produced *Método de lectura gradual*, *De la educación popular*, schoolbooks, translations, and reports on popular instruction.

---

## Anti-Rosas Coalition, Caseros, and Break with Urquiza, 1851–1855

In 1851 Sarmiento joined Justo José de Urquiza’s anti-Rosas coalition and served in the Ejército Grande’s press and military apparatus.

He was present during the campaign culminating in the **Battle of Caseros**, 3 February 1852, which overthrew Rosas.

Sarmiento soon broke with Urquiza. He opposed the political organization emerging under Urquiza and returned to Chile. His *Campaña en el Ejército Grande* presented his own role and criticized Urquiza.

### Polemic with Juan Bautista Alberdi

The dispute produced:

- Alberdi’s *Cartas quillotanas*
- Sarmiento’s *Las ciento y una*

Issues included:

- The Constitution of 1853
- Buenos Aires’s separation from the Confederation
- Press conduct
- Immigration and economic development
- The relative priority of institutional order and political liberty
- Personal legitimacy and authorship of the anti-Rosas victory

The exchange was both a major constitutional debate and an exceptionally personal feud.

---

## Buenos Aires and National Ascendancy, 1855–1861

Sarmiento settled in Buenos Aires in the mid-1850s and aligned with the State of Buenos Aires against the Argentine Confederation.

He held legislative office and became head of the Buenos Aires school department. He promoted:

- School construction
- Teacher professionalization
- Educational statistics
- Graded classrooms
- Women’s participation as teachers
- Public libraries
- Textbook and inspection systems

He acquired property in the Paraná Delta at Carapachay, Tigre, and promoted settlement, agriculture, navigation, and technological development in the islands. The preserved “Casa Sarmiento” reflects his interest in corrugated or prefabricated construction and landscape experimentation.

He participated politically and militarily in the conflicts ending with Buenos Aires’s national ascendancy after Pavón in 1861.

---

## Governor of San Juan, 1862–1864

Sarmiento became governor of San Juan in 1862 under the national political order led by President Bartolomé Mitre.

His administration pursued:

- Expansion of public education
- Roads and public works
- Mining development
- Fiscal and administrative organization
- Provincial military consolidation
- Suppression of Federalist resistance

### Chacho Peñaloza

The rebellion of Ángel Vicente “Chacho” Peñaloza became the defining controversy of Sarmiento’s governorship.

Peñaloza led Federalist provincial forces in resistance to the post-Pavón order. Sarmiento supported forceful suppression and treated the montoneras as banditry or internal barbarism rather than a legitimate political army.

Peñaloza surrendered and was killed at Olta on 12 November 1863 by forces associated with Colonel Pablo Irrazábal. His head was displayed.

**Documented fact:** Peñaloza was killed after capture rather than in ordinary combat.

**Responsibility dispute:** Sarmiento did not personally carry out the killing, and historians distinguish between direct orders, command responsibility, political authorization, and subsequent approval. His writings defended the suppression and denied Peñaloza the status of a lawful belligerent. This made him politically and morally associated with the killing even where an explicit execution order is not demonstrated.

José Hernández’s *Vida del Chacho* condemned the killing and presented Peñaloza as a victim. Sarmiento’s later *El Chacho* countered with the state’s version. The two texts became rival memory traditions.

### “No economizar sangre de gauchos”

A letter attributed to Sarmiento, generally dated 20 September 1861 and addressed to Mitre, contains the line:

> “No trate de economizar sangre de gauchos. Este es un abono que es preciso hacer útil al país.”

**Status: substantially documented quotation, often stripped of context.** It appears in archival and collected-document contexts, not merely modern memes. The language advocates treating gaucho combatants’ deaths as politically useful. Context can clarify its immediate military reference but does not erase the statement’s violence.

### Departure from the governorship

Sarmiento resigned in 1864 amid political conflict with national authorities and disputes over provincial administration and military policy.

---

## Diplomatic Mission to the United States, 1865–1868

Mitre appointed Sarmiento Argentine minister plenipotentiary in the United States.

He arrived after Abraham Lincoln’s assassination. He studied:

- Federal institutions
- Civil War reconstruction
- Public schools and normal schools
- Agriculture and industrial technology
- Telegraphy
- Libraries
- Immigration
- Scientific institutions

### Mary Mann network

Mary Mann became his closest American collaborator. Their correspondence documents politics, education, family matters, emotional intimacy, and recruitment of teachers.

Sarmiento and Mann promoted the migration of American teachers to Argentina. The frequently repeated claim that he personally “brought sixty-five teachers” compresses a longer program extending across later administrations. Recruitment encountered resistance, refusals, illness, cultural conflict, and financial difficulties. Approximately seventy teachers, mostly women, eventually participated across the broader program, depending on counting criteria.

### Lincoln

Sarmiento wrote and promoted a Spanish-language life of Abraham Lincoln, presenting him as an exemplary self-made republican. He did not meet Lincoln: the president had been assassinated before Sarmiento’s mission began.

### Death of Dominguito

Dominguito died at Curupayty in 1866. Sarmiento received the news in the United States. His grief is documented in correspondence. He later made the son’s death part of a patriotic family narrative, but contemporary letters reveal less controlled mourning.

### Presidential candidacy

While abroad, Sarmiento emerged as a compromise presidential candidate. Colonel Lucio V. Mansilla and military-political networks promoted his candidacy. Adolfo Alsina became the vice-presidential candidate.

Sarmiento was elected without possessing a strong personal party organization. The election was indirect and conducted under the restricted, coercive, and factional electoral practices of nineteenth-century Argentina; it was not a mass democratic election in the modern sense.

---

## Presidency of Argentina, 1868–1874

Sarmiento assumed office on 12 October 1868, with Adolfo Alsina as vice president.

### Governing context

His administration inherited:

- The Paraguayan War
- Federalist insurgencies
- Weak national administrative reach
- Limited transport and communication
- High illiteracy
- Provincial autonomy disputes
- Indigenous-controlled territories beyond the effective frontier
- Fiscal dependence on customs and foreign credit

### Cabinet and associates

Important officials included:

- Nicolás Avellaneda, minister of Justice, Worship, and Public Instruction
- Dalmacio Vélez Sarsfield, minister of the Interior
- Mariano Varela and Carlos Tejedor in foreign affairs
- Adolfo Alsina, vice president and political ally with independent ambitions

The administration was not a one-man educational project: Avellaneda, provincial governments, legislators, teachers, immigrant educators, and local communities carried much of the work.

### First National Census

The first national census was conducted in 1869. It counted approximately 1.83 million inhabitants, excluding or incompletely counting many Indigenous peoples and residents beyond effective state control.

The census documented extremely high illiteracy. It also served state-building, taxation, military, immigration, and territorial purposes.

### Education

Measures included:

- Federal subsidies for provincial education
- Expansion of primary schools
- Normal schools, including the Escuela Normal de Paraná
- Recruitment of foreign teachers
- Teacher training and inspection
- Textbook distribution
- School statistics
- Libraries and reading promotion
- Support for scientific and technical education

Enrollment reportedly increased from roughly 30,000 to approximately 100,000 during or around his administration, but figures vary by inclusion criteria and should not be treated as perfectly comparable.

The **Law of Subsidies of 1871** helped fund provincial education. The **Commission for Popular Libraries**, ancestor of CONABIP, was established in 1870.

**Important correction to popular memory:** Sarmiento did not enact Law 1420, the national law of common, free, compulsory primary education. It was enacted in 1884 under President Julio Argentino Roca. Sarmiento participated in the later educational conflict and helped shape the institutional and ideological groundwork.

### Science and professional institutions

His presidency created or consolidated:

- National Academy of Sciences in Córdoba
- Córdoba Astronomical Observatory, associated with Benjamin Apthorp Gould
- Meteorological institutions
- Colegio Militar de la Nación
- Escuela Naval Militar
- Agricultural and experimental institutions
- National exhibitions, especially the Córdoba Exposition of 1871

These projects involved foreign scientists and professionals and reveal Sarmiento’s effort to attach state authority to measurement, technical education, and classification.

### Telegraph and communications

The government extended thousands of kilometers of telegraph line, connected provincial capitals, and pursued links to Chile and the international cable system.

Sarmiento regarded telegraphy as a political technology: it enabled administrative coordination and rapid military response as well as commerce and information exchange.

### Railways and navigation

Railway mileage increased substantially. Projects connected Córdoba and western or northern regions and encouraged port and river development.

Not all projected routes were completed. Costs, foreign capital, geography, provincial rivalry, and the 1873 international financial crisis constrained expansion.

### Immigration and colonization

Sarmiento supported European immigration and agricultural colonization, influenced by the United States. His ideal was not simply immigration but the transplantation of literate family farming, municipal institutions, and technical habits.

His preferences were ethnically and culturally hierarchical. Northern European and Anglo-American settlers often appeared in his writing as preferred agents of “civilization,” while Indigenous, Afro-descended, gaucho, and mestizo populations were frequently described as obstacles or deficient populations.

### Public health crises

The administration faced cholera and, most dramatically, the **Buenos Aires yellow-fever epidemic of 1871**.

Sarmiento temporarily left the capital during the epidemic, an action criticized by opponents as presidential abandonment. Governmental response was fragmented among national, municipal, medical, charitable, and citizen bodies. Later heroic narratives emphasizing only state modernization can obscure the administration’s limited capacity during the crisis.

### Paraguayan War

The war began under Mitre but continued into Sarmiento’s presidency, ending militarily in 1870.

Sarmiento had a personal connection through Dominguito’s death and publicly supported the war effort. His correspondence also called it a “maldita guerra” and revealed frustration with its cost.

**Disputed historical classification:** Some Paraguayan national narratives and later critical scholarship describe the war as genocide. Other historians reject a planned extermination interpretation, emphasizing interstate conflict, battlefield mortality, disease, demographic uncertainty, and Solano López’s decisions. Catastrophic Paraguayan population loss is not disputed, but prewar population figures and intentionality remain contested.

No judicial body charged or convicted Sarmiento of genocide. Applying the word to him is a **retrospective political-historical classification**, not a contemporary legal finding. His dehumanizing racial and civilizational language remains directly documented and relevant to the debate.

### Indigenous frontier policy

Sarmiento’s presidency funded military frontier operations, forts, communications, and territorial expansion. It preceded Roca’s 1878–1879 Conquest of the Desert but helped increase the national state’s frontier capacity.

His writings contain incompatible strands:

- Advocacy of schooling, incorporation, treaties, and agricultural transformation
- Descriptions of Indigenous societies as racially or culturally inferior
- Support for military coercion and territorial occupation
- Occasional criticism of corrupt frontier agents and ineffective warfare

Claims that Sarmiento personally “ordered the Conquest of the Desert” are false as a matter of chronology. Claims that his ideology and institutions contributed to its intellectual and administrative preconditions are historically supportable.

### Jordanist rebellions and Urquiza’s death

Ricardo López Jordán’s rebellion followed the 1870 assassination of Urquiza. Sarmiento’s government conducted major military campaigns against the rebellion.

The national army used new weaponry, telegraph coordination, and professional organization. Repression included emergency powers and contested treatment of prisoners and provincial civilians.

### Attempted assassination

On 23 August 1873, attackers fired at Sarmiento’s carriage in Buenos Aires. A firearm reportedly exploded in an assailant’s hand. Sarmiento, already severely hearing-impaired, did not hear the explosion.

The attackers were linked in contemporary investigation to opposition or Jordanist networks, but broad conspiracy claims were never established to modern evidentiary standards.

### 1873 economic crisis

The global Panic of 1873 affected Argentine credit, trade, and public finance. Infrastructure ambitions and debt exposure met fiscal limits. Narratives presenting the presidency as uninterrupted material progress omit this late-term contraction.

### Succession and Mitre’s rebellion

Sarmiento supported Nicolás Avellaneda’s succession. Bartolomé Mitre’s supporters challenged the 1874 election and launched a rebellion.

The revolt began near the end of Sarmiento’s term and was defeated under Avellaneda. The episode exposed the coercive and factional foundations of electoral succession despite the constitutional transfer of office.

Sarmiento completed his term and handed power to Avellaneda on 12 October 1874.

## Later Political Life, 1874–1888

### Senator

Sarmiento served as national senator for San Juan from 1874 to 1879. He remained combative, frequently criticizing both former allies and new governing factions.

### Minister of the Interior

President Avellaneda appointed him minister of the Interior in 1879. His tenure was brief. Political disagreements, including disputes surrounding succession and relations with provincial and military leaders, led to his departure.

### Conflict with Roca-era politics

Sarmiento supported some state-consolidating and educational policies associated with Julio Argentino Roca but criticized political management, corruption, personalism, and the emerging governing machine.

His attitude toward Roca was therefore neither simple alliance nor constant opposition.

### Superintendent of Schools and educational debate

He served in senior educational administration and participated in debates leading toward common-school legislation.

He favored secular state authority over public education. “Secular,” however, did not necessarily mean anti-religious: Sarmiento’s educational writings frequently treated Christianity or biblical morality as culturally formative while opposing clerical control of state schools.

### Law 1420

Sarmiento supported the common-school cause surrounding the 1884 law, but the law was enacted under Roca. His exact relationship to every provision should not be replaced by the later slogan that he single-handedly created free, secular, compulsory education.

### Final writings

His late work included:

- *Conflicto y armonías de las razas en América*
- Educational journalism
- Political commentary
- *Vida de Dominguito*
- Autobiographical and memorial writings
- Articles later collected as *El Carapachay*
- The unfinished continuation of his racial-historical study

*Conflicto y armonías* intensified his racial interpretation of American history. It used categories now associated with nineteenth-century scientific racism, environmental determinism, and civilizational hierarchy.

### Final residence in Paraguay

Declining health led Sarmiento to spend time in Asunción’s warmer climate. Aurelia Vélez Sarsfield and family members remained important in his last years.

He continued writing and receiving visitors. His choice to die in Paraguay—the country devastated in the war he had supported—later acquired symbolic and polemical meanings not clearly established as his conscious intention.

---

## Death and Medical History

Sarmiento suffered progressive deafness. The condition became severe enough that he did not hear the 1873 assassination attempt.

Other reported late-life illnesses included cardiovascular and respiratory problems. He traveled to Paraguay partly for health reasons.

He died in Asunción on 11 September 1888. Accounts generally attribute death to heart failure or a cardiovascular collapse complicated by declining health. A precise modern diagnosis cannot be made from the accessible documentation.

His body was transported to Buenos Aires and buried in Recoleta Cemetery. The funeral procession became a national civic ritual.

There is no credible evidence that he was murdered or poisoned.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Newspapers and publishing ventures

Sarmiento founded, directed, or wrote extensively for:

- *El Zonda*
- *El Progreso*
- *La Crónica*
- *El Nacional*
- *El Mercurio* of Valparaíso
- Educational journals and government publications

Ownership structures varied. *El Progreso*, for example, was connected to Chilean proprietors rather than being solely Sarmiento’s private company.

### Educational institutions

He was directly involved in:

- Escuela Normal de Preceptores, Santiago
- Buenos Aires school administration
- Escuela Normal de Paraná
- Common-school and normal-school networks
- Popular libraries
- Teacher-recruitment programs
- Educational inspection and statistical systems

### Scientific and military institutions

His government sponsored or founded:

- Córdoba Observatory
- National Academy of Sciences
- Meteorological services
- Colegio Militar
- Escuela Naval
- Agricultural stations and exhibitions

### Technologies

Sarmiento did not hold a known major patent and was not an inventor in the narrow technical sense. He was a political promoter and adopter of:

- Telegraph networks
- Railways
- Steam navigation
- Modern printing and mass journalism
- Prefabricated and corrugated construction
- Agricultural machinery
- Meteorological and astronomical measurement
- Educational standardization

### Property

Documented or strongly reported property included:

- Interests in the family house in San Juan
- His house and land in the Paraná Delta
- Urban residences associated with his public career

The Delta property was both a retreat and a model project for island settlement and cultivation.

### Wealth and finance

Sarmiento’s livelihood came principally from teaching, journalism, public salaries, diplomatic office, publishing, and property. He complained frequently about money and travel expenses.

No strong evidence uncovered in this research establishes a clandestine business empire, systematic embezzlement, money laundering, or unexplained fortune.

His government relied on public borrowing and foreign capital for infrastructure. Criticism of debt and expenditure is a legitimate policy controversy but is not evidence of personal theft.

## Political, Religious, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

### Republican liberalism

Sarmiento’s central political commitments were:

- Constitutional republican government
- Mass primary education
- Literacy as a condition of citizenship
- Immigration and population growth
- Communications and transport infrastructure
- Scientific administration
- Urban and municipal institutions
- Suppression of personalist caudillo power

### Centralization versus federalism

Although he opposed Rosas’s central dominance, Sarmiento repeatedly supported coercive national authority against provincial movements. His federalism was institutional and municipal rather than sympathetic to autonomous armed provincial caudillismo.

### Civilization and barbarism

The opposition was never merely descriptive. It organized people, landscapes, economies, and political legitimacy.

“Civilization” included cities, schools, print, European and North American immigration, commerce, and state institutions.

“Barbarism” included the open interior, pastoral mobility, caudillos, montoneras, Indigenous autonomy, and populations insufficiently incorporated into literate institutions.

The binary could be unstable: *Facundo* also exhibits fascination with the vitality, knowledge, and charisma of figures classified as barbarous.

### Race

Sarmiento’s racial thinking hardened and became more systematized over time. He used hierarchies involving Europeans, Indigenous peoples, Africans, gauchos, and mixed populations.

**Documented fact:** Racist and eliminationist language appears in authentic texts and correspondence.

**Interpretive caution:** His racial positions were not perfectly consistent. He advocated universal schooling and sometimes civic assimilation while also supporting coercion and predicting or desiring the disappearance of populations he classified as obstacles. Educational universalism did not cancel racial hierarchy; the two coexisted in his state-building project.

### Religion and secularism

Sarmiento was raised Catholic, used Christian moral vocabulary, translated or promoted religious instructional texts, and met Pope Pius IX.

He opposed ecclesiastical control over public education and supported secular civil institutions. His Freemasonry intensified clerical suspicion.

It is inaccurate to describe him simply as an atheist. His position was anticlerical or jurisdictionally secular rather than consistently irreligious.

### Freemasonry

Institutional museum records document his membership and leadership.

- He was active in Argentine Freemasonry.
- He held high office, including Grand Master.
- On 18 April 1882 he affiliated with Logia Obediencia a la Ley No. 13.

**Conspiracy claims:** Assertions that Freemasonry secretly dictated every major decision of his presidency are unsupported. Membership and networks are documented; totalizing theories of hidden command are not.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### José de San Martín

Met in France in 1846. Sarmiento left a written portrait of the aging liberator. The meeting is documented, though dialogue survives through Sarmiento’s retrospective account.

### Bartolomé Mitre

Ally in national consolidation, correspondent, military-political superior, and later rival. Their relationship encompassed shared opposition to Federalist uprisings, cooperation, presidential succession conflict, and disagreement over the 1874 election.

### Justo José de Urquiza

Temporary ally against Rosas; commander of the Ejército Grande; then target of Sarmiento’s criticism after Caseros.

### Juan Manuel de Rosas

Primary political enemy during exile. Rosas served as the tyrannical endpoint of the argument in *Facundo*, even though the nominal subject was Quiroga.

### Juan Bautista Alberdi

Intellectual ally in opposition to Rosas and later bitter rival over constitutional organization. Their polemic is among the major documentary exchanges of nineteenth-century Spanish American political thought.

### Andrés Bello

Institutional colleague and intellectual rival in Chile. They disagreed over language reform, pedagogy, literary authority, and cultural development.

### Manuel Montt

Chilean patron who provided Sarmiento with government employment and sponsored his educational journey.

### Horace Mann

Educational model and personal acquaintance. Their meeting in Massachusetts shaped Sarmiento’s common-school program.

### Mary Peabody Mann

Correspondent, translator, advocate, and organizer. Their letters form one of the richest transnational archives connected to Sarmiento.

### Nicolás Avellaneda

Education minister during Sarmiento’s presidency, successor as president, and later political associate whose independent authority complicated their relationship.

### Dalmacio Vélez Sarsfield

Minister, jurist, author of the Civil Code, collaborator, and father of Aurelia. Sarmiento’s relationship with Aurelia introduced a private dimension into the political association.

### José Hernández

Journalistic and political opponent over Peñaloza, gaucho society, and the meaning of state violence. Hernández’s *Martín Fierro* later became the cultural counter-symbol most frequently set against Sarmiento’s *Facundo*.

### Ángel Vicente Peñaloza

Political and military enemy whose killing became a permanent stain in hostile accounts of Sarmiento and a test case for the limits of national state-building.

### Ricardo López Jordán

Federalist rebel suppressed during Sarmiento’s presidency. Suspected Jordanist connections surrounded the 1873 assassination attempt.

### Benjamin Apthorp Gould

American astronomer recruited for scientific institution-building in Argentina.

## Controversies, Allegations, Violence, and Financial Questions

### Racism and eliminationist rhetoric

**Documented fact:** Sarmiento wrote derogatorily about Indigenous peoples, Africans, gauchos, mixed populations, Paraguayans, Spaniards, and others. Several passages imagine demographic replacement or disappearance as progress.

Authentic passages must be distinguished from internet quote compilations. Some popular quotations are paraphrased, truncated, incorrectly dated, or attributed to the wrong newspaper. The underlying worldview does not depend on every viral quotation being authentic.

### Chacho Peñaloza’s killing

- **Documented fact:** Peñaloza was killed after capture.
- **Documented fact:** Sarmiento supported harsh suppression and defended the outcome politically.
- **Unresolved/document-specific question:** Whether he gave an explicit prior order to execute Peñaloza.
- **Later charge:** José Hernández and revisionist historians treated the death as state murder for which Sarmiento bore central responsibility.

### Gaucho deaths

The “no economizar sangre de gauchos” letter is substantially documented. Attempts to dismiss it as wholly fabricated are not supported by the available archival evidence. Conversely, modern versions sometimes omit that it was written in the context of armed civil conflict.

### Paraguay and genocide accusation

- **Documented fact:** Sarmiento supported continuation of the Triple Alliance war as president.
- **Documented fact:** Paraguay suffered catastrophic military and civilian mortality.
- **Documented fact:** His writings include dehumanizing racial and civilizational judgments.
- **Disputed classification:** Whether the war as a whole constituted genocide.
- **Disputed individual attribution:** Whether Sarmiento should be called a “genocidaire.” No contemporary or later court made such a finding; the label is historiographical and political.

The theory that Britain ordered Argentina, Brazil, and Uruguay to destroy a uniquely self-sufficient Paraguay remains disputed. British financial and commercial influence was real, but the strongest modern scholarship generally treats regional state interests, border disputes, Uruguayan civil conflict, imperial Brazilian policy, and Solano López’s decisions as independent causal forces rather than proof of a single British command conspiracy.

### Indigenous dispossession

Sarmiento’s government strengthened the frontier state and supported territorial expansion. It did not conduct Roca’s later Conquest of the Desert, but its military, telegraphic, census, and ideological projects contributed to the state capacity used in subsequent campaigns.

### Electoral coercion

Sarmiento’s rise and government operated within a system of restricted suffrage, provincial intervention, armed factionalism, and electoral manipulation. Describing his presidency as democratic requires that qualification.

### Press aggression

He regularly used ridicule, invective, and personal accusation. His journalism blurred analysis and political combat. Opponents accused him of dishonesty and fabrication; some errors are demonstrable, but a general judicial finding of fraud does not exist.

### Marital infidelity

His relationship with Aurelia Vélez Sarsfield during his marriage to Benita is supported by correspondence and family history. It was a private scandal with political resonance, not a criminal offense.

### Yellow fever departure

Opponents accused Sarmiento of cowardice for leaving Buenos Aires during the 1871 epidemic. The departure is documented; interpretations differ over whether it was prudent executive relocation or abandonment.

### Corruption and personal enrichment

No substantial documentary evidence located in the dedicated adversarial search supports claims of systematic personal corruption, embezzlement, fraud, money laundering, or illicit corporate ownership.

Policy controversies over foreign borrowing, contracts, railway concessions, military procurement, and public spending require project-specific research and should not be converted into personal criminal accusations without records.

### Recent controversies, 2024–2026

The 2024 renaming of the former Centro Cultural Kirchner as the **Palacio Libertad Domingo Faustino Sarmiento** renewed partisan disputes over his legacy.

Recent online debate recirculated:

- Racist quotations, some authentic and some poorly sourced
- Claims that he personally organized the Conquest of the Desert
- Genocide accusations related to Paraguay
- Defense of him as the sole founder of Argentine education
- Comparisons with contemporary politicians

These are modern memory conflicts rather than new evidence about his life. Searches through the most recent two years disclosed no new criminal investigation, lawsuit, financial revelation, or authenticated archive fundamentally altering the established biography.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Ideas cannot be killed”

**Story:** While fleeing to Chile in 1840, Sarmiento wrote “On ne tue point les idées” at the Zonda passage.

**Origin and circulation:** Sarmiento’s autobiographical account; later schoolbooks, monuments, and civic biographies.

**Evidence:** Self-report, without independent contemporary confirmation.

**Reputational effect:** Made exile into a tableau of intellect resisting dictatorship.

### The wholly self-made poor boy

**Story:** A destitute child rose by unaided genius to create Argentine education.

**Evidence:** The family had genuine economic hardship, and Sarmiento lacked a conventional higher education. But he benefited from clerical relatives, old family networks, Chilean patrons, journalism, state appointments, and transnational collaborators.

**Effect:** Central to school hagiography and to Sarmiento’s own Franklin-inspired self-image.

### “Father of the classroom”

**Story:** Sarmiento personally created Argentine public education.

**Evidence:** He was a decisive advocate and administrator, but school systems predated him, and expansion depended on provincial authorities, Avellaneda, teachers, communities, women educators, later governments, and Law 1420 after his presidency.

**Effect:** Produced a durable national teacher cult and obscured collective institutional labor.

### He enacted secular education

**Story:** Sarmiento enacted Law 1420.

**Evidence against:** The law passed in 1884 under Roca. Sarmiento influenced the debate and institutional development.

### Sarmiento ordered the Conquest of the Desert

**Story:** He personally initiated Roca’s 1879 campaign.

**Evidence against:** He was not president in 1879 and did not command the campaign. His ideology and earlier frontier policies remain relevant antecedents.

### Sarmiento as British agent

**Story:** His policies, including the Paraguayan War, were directly controlled by Britain.

**Evidence:** Argentina had British commercial, credit, and infrastructure relationships. No adequate documentary evidence establishes Sarmiento as a British intelligence agent or proves a British command chain.

**Status:** Political theory/propaganda unless tied to a specific documented transaction.

### Masonic hidden government

**Story:** Sarmiento’s documented Freemasonry proves that a secret lodge controlled the state.

**Evidence:** Membership and leadership are documented. Total institutional control is not.

### He hated all gauchos and Indigenous people without qualification

**Story:** Every action followed an undifferentiated personal hatred.

**Evidence:** His derogatory and violent language is genuine. His educational and incorporation projects introduce complexity but do not neutralize it. The categorical psychological claim goes beyond the evidence.

### Sarmiento as flawless liberal democrat

**Story:** He consistently defended peaceful constitutional liberty.

**Evidence against:** He defended education and constitutional institutions while endorsing coercive suppression, emergency authority, and violent language against political and racialized populations.

### Death in Paraguay as conscious repentance

**Story:** He chose Paraguay to atone for the war.

**Evidence:** He went primarily for health and personal reasons. No adequate document proves a penitential intention.

## Works by Domingo Faustino Sarmiento

### Authorship warning

Sarmiento’s output includes books, pamphlets, reports, speeches, translations, schoolbooks, newspaper articles, letters, government papers, and posthumous compilations. The canonical *Obras* were edited principally by Augusto Belín Sarmiento and Luis Montt between 1884 and 1903. Editorial inclusion is not always equivalent to original publication as a standalone Sarmiento book.

### Major lifetime publications

- *Mi defensa* (1843): Autobiographical and polemical self-defense.
- *Método gradual de enseñar a leer el castellano* (1845; variant publication dates occur): School reading method.
- *Civilización i barbarie. Vida de Juan Facundo Quiroga* (1845): First serialized in *El Progreso*; revised editions followed. Common modern title: *Facundo: civilización y barbarie*.
- *Viajes por Europa, África i América, 1845–1847* (published in installments/volumes, 1849–1851): Travel letters and institutional observations.
- *De la educación popular* (1849): Comparative report and educational program.
- *Recuerdos de provincia* (1850): Autobiography, family genealogy, and political self-construction.
- *Argirópolis o la capital de los Estados Confederados del Río de la Plata* (1850): Proposal for regional political organization and a capital on Martín García Island.
- *Campaña en el Ejército Grande aliado de Sud América* (1852): Memoir and political account of the anti-Rosas campaign.
- *Las ciento y una* (1853): Open letters answering Alberdi.
- *Comentarios de la Constitución de la Confederación Argentina* (1853): Constitutional commentary.
- *Memoria sobre educación común* (1856): Educational administration and comparative policy.
- *El Chacho, último caudillo de la montonera de los Llanos* (1860s; publication history includes newspaper and later collected forms): Political biography and defense of state suppression.
- *Las escuelas, base de la prosperidad y de la república en los Estados Unidos* (1866): Educational argument from the American example.
- *Vida de Abraham Lincoln* / *La vida de Abraham Lincoln* (1860s–1873 editions): Adapted biographical work presenting Lincoln as a republican model.
- *Ambas Américas* (journal, 1867–1868): Edited during his United States mission.
- *Conflicto y armonías de las razas en América* (volume I, 1883; continuation posthumous/incomplete): Racial-historical interpretation of the Americas.
- *Vida de Dominguito* (serialized in *El Censor* in 1886; later book/collected form): Biography and memorial compilation concerning Domingo Fidel.
- *El camino de Lacio* and late political/linguistic writings: Publication histories vary across collected editions.

### Educational texts, translations, and adaptations

Works associated with Sarmiento’s educational program include:

- *Método de lectura gradual*
- *Educación popular*
- *Educación común*
- Orthographic reform essays
- Reading books and moral instruction
- *Vida de Jesucristo*
- *Conciencia de un niño*
- *Manual de historia de los pueblos*
- *El porqué o la física popularizada*
- Works on the metric system
- Reports on teacher training, school architecture, inspection, women’s education, and libraries

Several are translations or adaptations rather than wholly original compositions. Sarmiento sometimes presented translation as a practical instrument for supplying Spanish American schools.

### Journalism

The complete attribution of unsigned journalism is unresolved. His collected works include articles from:

- *El Zonda*
- *El Mercurio*
- *El Progreso*
- *La Crónica*
- *El Nacional*
- *Ambas Américas*
- *El Censor*
- Other political and educational papers

### Correspondence

Important bodies include:

- Letters to family members
- Correspondence with Mary Mann
- Letters to Mitre
- Letters involving Alberdi, Montt, Bello, Lastarria, Avellaneda, Vélez Sarsfield, and Aurelia Vélez
- Presidential letters and telegrams
- Diplomatic dispatches from the United States

Published selections are editorial compilations; they should not be mistaken for books designed by Sarmiento.

### Speeches and state papers

His presidential messages, parliamentary speeches, decrees, memoranda, educational reports, and diplomatic papers appear across the collected works and archival holdings.

### Posthumous compilations

- *El Carapachay*: Articles on the Paraná Delta collected posthumously.
- *Obras de Domingo F. Sarmiento*, edited by Augusto Belín Sarmiento and Luis Montt, 52/53 volumes, 1884–1903.
- *Obras completas*, Luz del Día edition, 1948–1956.
- Later 54-volume and digital editions.
- Numerous thematic anthologies: education, politics, travel, correspondence, autobiography, and literary prose.

The varying totals—52, 53, or 54 volumes—reflect differences in edition, supplementary volumes, and counting, not distinct bodies of original lifetime books.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Civic canonization

Sarmiento’s death was followed by institutional commemoration:

- Schools and streets named for him
- Statues and monuments
- Preservation of his birth house
- National educational rituals
- Publication of massive collected works
- “Father of the classroom” iconography

Argentina observes **Teachers’ Day on 11 September**, the anniversary of his death. The date also became Pan-American Teachers’ Day through a twentieth-century inter-American decision.

### Monument by Auguste Rodin

Rodin’s monument to Sarmiento in Buenos Aires was controversial because its physiognomy did not conform to conventional heroic likeness. The dispute illustrates the tension between modernist artistic commemoration and schoolbook iconography.

### Literary legacy

*Facundo* remains central to Latin American literature, political thought, sociology, and cultural criticism. It shaped enduring binaries:

- City/interior
- Literacy/orality
- State/caudillo
- Europe/America
- Civilization/barbarism

Later writers frequently inverted, criticized, or repurposed those categories.

### Counter-canon: *Martín Fierro*

José Hernández’s gaucho poem became the principal symbolic alternative to Sarmiento’s civilizing program. Argentine cultural debate often staged Sarmiento versus Hernández, or *Facundo* versus *Martín Fierro*, even though their positions and texts were more complex than the binary.

### Revisionism

Nationalist, Federalist, Peronist, Paraguayan, Indigenous, and left-wing revisionisms attacked Sarmiento as:

- Agent of Buenos Aires centralism
- Enemy of gaucho and Indigenous peoples
- Advocate of foreign cultural dependence
- Participant in internal repression
- Defender of the Paraguayan War

Liberal and educational traditions defended him as:

- Founder of common schooling
- Modernizer of communications and science
- Opponent of dictatorship
- Advocate of immigration and civic literacy

### Twenty-first-century reinterpretation

Current scholarship increasingly studies the coexistence of:

- Educational inclusion and racial exclusion
- Republican institutions and state violence
- Cosmopolitan learning and cultural hierarchy
- Autobiographical self-creation and archival biography
- Scientific institution-building and racial science
- Women’s educational labor and male heroic memory

The 2024 naming of the Palacio Libertad Domingo Faustino Sarmiento demonstrated that his image remains usable in present political competition.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources or well-established primary documentation are included. Shared nationality, broad intellectual resemblance, or mere contemporaneity was not treated as a connection.

### Abraham Lincoln — documented influence, no personal meeting

Sarmiento arrived for his diplomatic service after Lincoln’s assassination and therefore did not meet him. He studied Lincoln’s life, wrote or adapted a Spanish-language biography, and promoted Lincoln as a model of the self-educated republican citizen.

### Alexis-Charles-Henri Clérel de Tocqueville — documented intellectual influence

Sarmiento read *De la démocratie en Amérique*. His 1847 observations of the United States used Tocqueville as a point of departure but stressed mass education, settlement, technology, and economic mobility in his own way. There was no documented personal meeting.

### Benjamin Franklin — documented intellectual and autobiographical influence

Franklin supplied a model for Sarmiento’s self-presentation as an autodidact, printer-journalist, practical educator, and civic improver. No personal connection was possible.

### Charles Robert Darwin — reported/documented textual relationship

Sarmiento engaged evolutionary language in his late racial and social thought and publicly commemorated Darwin in Argentina. This is a textual and commemorative connection, not a documented personal meeting.

### Johann Heinrich Pestalozzi — documented pedagogical influence

Pestalozzian methods belonged to the European and American educational reform network studied by Sarmiento. No personal meeting occurred.

### Jean-Jacques Rousseau — documented intellectual influence

Rousseau formed part of Sarmiento’s early republican reading. Sarmiento nevertheless rejected any simple celebration of natural or rural life and placed literacy and institutional discipline above pastoral autonomy.

### José de San Martín — documented personal meeting

Sarmiento met San Martín at Grand Bourg, France, in 1846 during his government-sponsored journey. Sarmiento recorded the encounter in his travel and later writings.

### José Rafael Hernández — documented rivalry

Hernández condemned the treatment and killing of Chacho Peñaloza; Sarmiento defended the national government’s suppression and produced a counter-biography. Their writings became rival interpretations of gauchos, provincial resistance, and state formation.

### Juan Manuel José Domingo Ortiz de Rozas y López de Osornio — documented political enmity

Juan Manuel de Rosas was Sarmiento’s principal political enemy during exile and the ultimate target of *Facundo*. They did not have a documented personal meeting central to the relationship; it was political, textual, and military.

### Julio Argentino Roca — documented political association and tension

Sarmiento interacted with the younger military and political leader during the 1870s. He supported some state and educational measures but criticized aspects of Roca’s political organization. Sarmiento did not command Roca’s Conquest of the Desert.

### Simón Bolívar — documented comparative influence, no meeting

Sarmiento treated Bolívar as a major Spanish American figure and used the independence generation in constructing republican history. No personal connection was possible.

### Simón Rodríguez — reported intellectual comparison, not a documented personal relationship

Sarmiento and Rodríguez are frequently paired as major nineteenth-century South American educational thinkers. The consulted material did not establish a meeting or correspondence. This is an intellectual-historical comparison, not a personal connection.

### Alexander von Humboldt — reported intellectual influence, no meeting established

Humboldt’s geographical and civilizational description of the Americas formed part of the intellectual environment from which Sarmiento drew. No reliable evidence of a personal encounter was found.

### Adam Smith — documented broader intellectual influence, no personal connection

Political economy and the relationship among labor, commerce, population, and improvement in Sarmiento’s work reflect the liberal political-economic tradition associated with Smith. No direct personal relationship existed.

### Jorge Luis Borges — documented posthumous interpretive connection

Borges wrote an influential prologue to *Facundo* and described it as a foundational interpretation of Argentina. This was posthumous literary reception, not a lifetime relationship.

### Giuseppe Maria Garibaldi — no adequately documented direct connection found

Both participated in the wider nineteenth-century world of liberal nationalism and Río de la Plata conflict, but the consulted sources did not establish a meeting, correspondence, or direct collaboration. Absence is therefore recorded rather than inferred.

### Remaining roster

No adequately sourced direct meeting, correspondence, collaboration, family connection, institutional relationship, or explicitly acknowledged influence was established for the other listed Atlas figures. Many were remote historical figures who may appear incidentally in Sarmiento’s enormous collected writings; incidental mention alone was not counted as a life connection.

## Chronology

- **1811, 14/15 February:** Born in the Carrascal neighborhood of San Juan as Faustino Valentín Quiroga Sarmiento.
- **1816:** Enters an Escuela de la Patria.
- **1820s:** Studies under relatives, especially José de Oro; begins intensive self-education.
- **c. 1825–1827:** Teaches with José de Oro at San Francisco del Monte de Oro, San Luis.
- **1827–1831:** Participates in provincial Unitarian political and military struggles.
- **1831:** Exiled to Chile.
- **1832, 18 June:** Birth of daughter Ana Faustina to María Jesús del Canto.
- **1830s:** Works as teacher, clerk, tutor, and in the Copiapó mining region.
- **1836:** Returns to San Juan following serious illness.
- **1839:** Founds *El Zonda*.
- **1840:** Forced into second Chilean exile; later recounts writing “On ne tue point les idées.”
- **1841–1842:** Writes for the Chilean press.
- **1842:** Directs the Escuela Normal de Preceptores in Santiago.
- **1843:** Publishes *Mi defensa*.
- **1845:** Publishes *Facundo*; departs on educational mission to Europe and North America.
- **1846:** Meets José de San Martín in France.
- **1847:** Travels through Europe and North Africa; reaches New York in September; meets Horace and Mary Mann in Massachusetts.
- **1848:** Returns to Chile; marries Benita Martínez Pastoriza; assumes paternal role toward Dominguito.
- **1849:** Publishes *De la educación popular* and educational works.
- **1850:** Publishes *Recuerdos de provincia* and *Argirópolis*.
- **1851:** Joins Urquiza’s Ejército Grande.
- **1852, 3 February:** Participates in the Caseros campaign; Rosas falls.
- **1852–1853:** Breaks with Urquiza; publishes *Campaña en el Ejército Grande* and polemicizes with Alberdi.
- **1855:** Establishes himself in Buenos Aires; enters legislative and school administration.
- **Late 1850s:** Expands Buenos Aires schools; develops property and projects in the Paraná Delta.
- **1861:** Writes the letter containing “No trate de economizar sangre de gauchos.”
- **1862:** Becomes governor of San Juan.
- **1863, 12 November:** Chacho Peñaloza killed after capture; Sarmiento defends the suppression.
- **1864:** Leaves the governorship.
- **1865:** Begins diplomatic mission in the United States.
- **1866, 22 September:** Dominguito killed at Curupayty.
- **1867–1868:** Publishes *Ambas Américas*; presidential candidacy organized in Argentina.
- **1868, 12 October:** Assumes presidency with Adolfo Alsina.
- **1869:** First National Census.
- **1870:** Paraguayan War ends; Urquiza assassinated; Jordanist rebellion begins; Popular Libraries Commission established.
- **1871:** Yellow-fever epidemic; Córdoba National Exposition; education subsidy measures.
- **1872:** Continued railway, telegraph, military-school, and scientific expansion.
- **1873, 23 August:** Survives assassination attempt; global economic crisis affects Argentina.
- **1874, 12 October:** Completes presidential term; Avellaneda succeeds him; Mitre rebellion follows the disputed election.
- **1874–1879:** National senator.
- **1879:** Briefly minister of the Interior.
- **Early 1880s:** Senior educational work; conflict and cooperation with the Roca political order.
- **1882, 18 April:** Affiliates with Logia Obediencia a la Ley No. 13; remains prominent in Freemasonry.
- **1883:** Publishes first volume of *Conflicto y armonías de las razas en América*.
- **1884:** Participates in educational controversies surrounding Law 1420.
- **1886:** *Vida de Dominguito* serialized in *El Censor*.
- **1888:** Travels to/resides in Asunción for health.
- **1888, 11 September:** Dies in Asunción.
- **1888:** Body returned to Argentina and buried at Recoleta.
- **1884–1903:** His family and collaborators publish the monumental collected works.
- **1910–1911:** Birth house declared a national monument and opened as a museum.
- **1944:** Release of *Su mejor alumno*.
- **1943 onward:** 11 September institutionalized across the Americas as a teachers’ commemorative date.
- **2024:** Former CCK renamed Palacio Libertad Domingo Faustino Sarmiento, renewing public controversy.
- **2026:** Casa Natal publishes renewed digital access to the collected works.
