# Marta María Ezcurra Real de Azúa: Research Dossier

## Scope, method, and evidentiary note

Marta María Ezcurra Real de Azúa was an Argentine Catholic organizer, social worker, author, institution-builder, international social-service official, and government appointee. She was active from the 1910s through at least the 1960s and is especially associated with:

- the organization of the women’s youth branch of Acción Católica Argentina;
- Catholic labor and social-service initiatives;
- the professionalization of social work in Argentina and Venezuela;
- the foundation and early direction of the Asociación para la Lucha contra la Parálisis Infantil (ALPI);
- service connected with the Pan American Union, Organization of American States, and United Nations;
- the post-coup intervention in institutions formerly administered by the Fundación Eva Perón in 1955;
- the former Catholic observance of 2 July as Argentina’s Social Worker’s Day.

The surviving online record is fragmented and politically polarized. Acción Católica Argentina supplies a short commemorative account, while social-work scholarship provides institutional reconstruction. Much of the public discussion of Ezcurra’s conduct in 1955 derives from Peronist histories and later journalism. Several dramatic allegations are frequently repeated without an accessible copy of the originating order, report, inventory, or testimony.

Accordingly, this dossier uses the following labels:

- **Documented fact:** supported by an institutional record, contemporaneous publication, scholarly reconstruction citing archival material, or mutually independent sources.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a generally credible secondary source, but the underlying primary document was not accessible in this research.
- **Allegation:** a specific accusation for which decisive supporting documentation was not located.
- **Disputed claim:** competing versions exist or the available sources conflict.
- **Rumor or myth:** a reputational story whose transmission can be documented but whose factual basis cannot presently be established.

No accessible evidence was found that Ezcurra was criminally charged, tried, or convicted for her official conduct.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name:** Marta María de Ezcurra y Real de Azúa; more commonly Marta María Ezcurra Real de Azúa or Marta Ezcurra.

**Alternative forms:**

- Marta María Ezcurra Real de Azúa
- Marta María de Ezcurra y Real de Azúa
- Marta Ezcurra
- M. Ezcurra in bibliographical references

**Honorific or professional designation:** Asistente Social.

**Reported nickname:** “El azote de Dios.” This appears in hostile retrospective accounts and was not found in a contemporaneous Acción Católica document. It should be treated as an **unverified reputational attribution**, not an established internal nickname.

**Nationality:** Argentine.

**Religion:** Roman Catholic; a lay organizer within Catholic Action and related institutions.

**Profession:** Social worker, educator, Catholic social-action organizer, author, administrator, and international social-service specialist.

**Marital status:** Genealogical compilations identify her as unmarried and without descendants. This is consistent with Catholic institutional portrayals but has not been independently verified through civil records.

### Birth

The date is disputed:

- **1897:** Acción Católica Argentina’s 2026 institutional commemoration gives “1897–1988.” This is presently the strongest institutional statement.
- **2 May 1900, Buenos Aires:** supplied by a hostile biographical narrative later reproduced in blogs and political commentary.
- **1901:** appears in some derivative accounts and encyclopedia entries without convincing documentary support.

**Best-supported conclusion:** born in Buenos Aires, probably in 1897; the exact date remains unverified. The frequently repeated 2 May 1900 date should not be accepted without a civil or baptismal record.

### Death

The year is likewise disputed:

- **1988:** given by Acción Católica Argentina.
- **1996:** asserted by a hostile narrative without a cited death record.

**Best-supported conclusion:** died in 1988, location and exact date presently unestablished. No obituary, civil death entry, burial notice, or cemetery record was located in the consulted online sources.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

Genealogical compilations identify her parents as:

- **Pedro Tomás Dionisio Ezcurra Pardo** (1859–1913), engineer and Argentine public official.
- **Marta Real de Azúa y Muñoz.**

Pedro Ezcurra served as national minister of agriculture during the presidency of José Figueroa Alcorta. His name became associated with the development of state petroleum exploration around the 1907 discovery at Comodoro Rivadavia. A naval tanker, *Ministro Ezcurra*, was later named for him.

The parental identification is consistent across several genealogical compilations, but those databases are user-generated and should ultimately be checked against parish and civil registers.

### Siblings

Available genealogical trees identify a large sibling group, including:

- Pedro Tomás Ezcurra Real de Azúa
- Federico Lorenzo Ezcurra Real de Azúa
- Carlos Pablo Ezcurra Real de Azúa
- Tomás Manuel Ezcurra Real de Azúa
- Martín José Ezcurra Real de Azúa
- Julia Victoria Ezcurra Real de Azúa
- Margarita or María Margarita Ezcurra Real de Azúa

One hostile account calls Marta the fourth of twelve children. The exact birth order and complete sibling list could not be verified.

### Extended family and social position

Ezcurra belonged to an established upper-class Buenos Aires family. Later narratives describe kinship with the nineteenth-century Ezcurra family that included Encarnación Ezcurra, wife and political associate of Juan Manuel de Rosas, and María Josefa “Pepa” Ezcurra, who had a son by Manuel Belgrano.

A genealogical relationship is plausible, but the exact degree—sometimes rendered as “tataranieta del hermano de Encarnación”—was not independently reconstructed from primary records. Broad statements that she was related to the Rosas–Ezcurra family should be labeled **reported genealogical fact**; elaborate degrees of relationship remain provisional.

No spouse, partner, or children are identified in the available record. Genealogical databases mark her unmarried and “without posterity.”

### Wealth and property

Her family background placed her among economically comfortable Catholic women able to undertake unpaid philanthropic work. A reported remark attributed to Ezcurra describes herself and her early coworkers as “señoritas de posición económica holgada” who had time to help the poor.

No personal estate, property portfolio, investments, company holdings, inheritance file, or probate record was located. Assertions about her wealth therefore concern family and class position rather than a quantified personal fortune.

## Childhood and Early Life

Little direct documentation survives concerning her childhood.

She was raised in Buenos Aires in a Catholic household and attended the Colegio del Sagrado Corazón in Almagro, reportedly as a boarding pupil. She graduated in **1915**.

The social world reflected in her later career was that of upper- and upper-middle-class Catholic women whose public activity was initially organized through Marian congregations, parish charities, workshops, visiting societies, and maternalist welfare associations.

No childhood diaries, correspondence, medical records, or first-person memoirs were located.

## Education and Formation

### Colegio del Sagrado Corazón

**Documented or strongly reported fact:** Ezcurra completed secondary studies at the Colegio del Sagrado Corazón in Almagro in 1915.

Immediately afterward, she joined the **Congregación de las Hijas de María**. Acción Católica’s commemoration says that she visited poor families, worked in communal food stores, and came to believe that almsgiving alone was inadequate.

### Early Catholic and charitable training, 1915–1925

A detailed social-work history associates her with:

- Congregación de las Hijas de María
- Liga de Damas Católicas
- Apostolado de la Oración
- Cooperadoras Salesianas
- Congregación de los Padres Vicentinos
- Conferencia de Damas de la Sociedad de San Vicente de Paúl
- Taller de Santa Rita of the Augustinians
- Unión Noelista Argentina
- Sisters of the Assumption
- the non-confessional association El Centavo

These affiliations are **reported facts**, drawn from retrospective reconstruction rather than separately verified membership registers.

She contributed to or helped produce the periodical *Noel*, apparently her first editorial experience. Precise issues and bylines remain unidentified.

Around **1925**, she reportedly became president of the Congregación de las Hijas de María. She also worked with *El Centavo*, which assisted poor women through food distribution and sewing work.

### Juvenile welfare

Ezcurra reportedly served as an honorary delegate and probation inspector under the regulations implementing Argentina’s Law 10,903, the 1919 child-welfare or *Patronato de Menores* law.

This experience placed her at the junction of Catholic philanthropy, juvenile justice, family surveillance, and emerging professional social work.

### Museo Social Argentino

In **1930**, Ezcurra entered the recently founded Escuela de Servicio Social of the Museo Social Argentino, directed by physician and hygienist Alberto Zwanck.

An institutional history reports that she initially received recognition as an auditor or non-regular student and later completed the requirements. She graduated as an **asistente social in 1933**.

Claims that Luis Agote and Francisco Pascasio Moreno were personally her teachers appear in a polemical history but were not corroborated through a course register. They should be treated as **reported, not fully verified**.

Her training emphasized social diagnosis, hygiene, family casework, labor organization, and a more systematic alternative to traditional charitable visiting.

## Early Career and Catholic Action, 1931–1940

### Founding of the women’s youth organization

In **1931**, while still studying social work, Ezcurra was asked by Bishop Santiago Luis Copello to organize the young women’s branch of Acción Católica Argentina.

She became president of its first provisional superior council. Sources variously describe this as founding the **Asociación de Jóvenes de la Acción Católica** or the women’s youth branch of the ACA.

This was an important national organizing assignment. She traveled, trained local cadres, and helped establish diocesan and parish groups. Acción Católica later remembered her as tall, thin, distinguished in appearance, and cordial in manner.

### Rome journey and Pius XI

In **1932**, Ezcurra traveled to Rome carrying a report on the newly organized Argentine association. She reportedly received a private audience with **Pope Pius XI** and presented a memorandum bound in white leather.

The journey is consistently reported in Catholic and social-work histories, though no Vatican audience register or contemporary travel report was located in the consulted online material.

The visit exposed her to European Catholic social programs and the principle, frequently associated with her subsequent work, that providing organized employment was preferable to giving alms.

### Catholic social doctrine

Ezcurra’s intellectual development followed the Catholic social teaching derived especially from:

- Leo XIII’s *Rerum novarum*;
- Pius XI’s *Quadragesimo anno*;
- the theology of Catholic Action;
- the corporatist or occupational organization of labor;
- the family as the fundamental social unit;
- private property subject to social duties;
- opposition to communism, secularism, and liberal divorce;
- a hierarchical understanding of relations between Church, state, employers, workers, and professional associations.

Her later publications indicate a systematic rather than merely devotional interest in wages, labor history, class relations, authority, property, production, occupational organization, and state power.

### Secretariado Económico-Social

Between approximately **1936 and 1946**, Ezcurra worked in the Secretariado Económico-Social of Acción Católica Argentina.

Important ecclesiastical and intellectual associates included:

- Bishop Miguel de Andrea
- Cardinal Santiago Luis Copello
- Gustavo Franceschi
- Atilio Dell’Oro Maini
- Gustavo Martínez Zuviría, known as Hugo Wast

The evidence supports institutional collaboration; it does not establish identical political views on every question.

### Catholic labor organizing

Ezcurra helped develop labor programs for women, especially homeworkers and garment workers. She taught cutting and sewing and assisted in distributing garments to commercial outlets.

She became technical adviser to the **Sindicato Argentino de Obreras de la Confección** after offering her services at a federal assembly of the women’s Catholic youth organization.

The union:

- organized female garment workers and homeworkers;
- used Acción Católica premises at Montevideo 850 in Buenos Aires;
- offered occupational and religious training;
- participated in wage commissions connected with the Secretaría de Trabajo y Previsión;
- formally emphasized labor responsibility while recognizing the Church’s moral and religious authority.

A 2019 academic study reports approximately 1,000 members before Peronism. Acción Católica’s 2026 commemoration gives a peak of 17,000 affiliates. These figures probably refer to different dates or definitions, but the institutional source does not specify the year.

Ezcurra’s work combined genuine vocational organization and wage advocacy with the Catholic aim of drawing workers away from communist, socialist, and secular union cultures.

## Chronological Life History

### 1934: International Eucharistic Congress

Ezcurra participated in the organization of the **XXXII International Eucharistic Congress**, held in Buenos Aires in October 1934.

Her work appears to have centered on the mobilization of young women and children. Later Catholic history regarded the congress as a demonstration of national Catholic organization. Critical historians interpret the mass mobilization as part of a campaign to identify Argentine nationality with Catholicism and prepare the return of religious instruction to public schools.

Ezcurra was also associated with the children’s periodical *Primeras Armas*. Exact articles and dates have not been recovered.

### 1936–1940: Garment workers and women’s social action

During these years, she expanded social-service and labor initiatives involving home seamstresses. In 1937–1938 she served as technical adviser while Acción Católica women helped administer the union and its training activities.

Her published articles in *Anhelos* addressed marriage, divorce, communism, religious authority, and social duty.

### 1941–1945: Director of a Catholic school of social service

Ezcurra became technical director of the Escuela de Servicio Social of the Instituto Superior de Cultura Religiosa or Católica, serving approximately **1941–1945**.

This placed her among the early women directing formal social-work education in Argentina. María Encarnación Zurano, another Museo Social Argentino graduate, also worked there.

### 1943: Child-welfare conference

She reportedly helped organize the **Second National Conference on Abandoned and Delinquent Childhood** under the Patronato de la Infancia.

The conference belonged to a wider field in which child protection, juvenile courts, medical expertise, institutional care, family investigation, and moral reform overlapped.

### 1943–1950: ALPI

On **17 December 1943**, the Asociación para la Lucha contra la Parálisis Infantil was founded in Buenos Aires. Ezcurra served as its first or early president.

ALPI brought together physicians and socially prominent women to assist children affected by poliomyelitis. Its program involved rehabilitation, medical-social support, fundraising, and expansion through provincial branches.

A scholarly study describes the organization as an alliance of doctors and female social activists, sustained partly by Christian ideas that legitimized women’s intervention in public health and family life.

Later accounts state that businessman Juan Bernardo Sullivan and Juana Berthet supported the organization and that Sullivan financed premises at Paseo Colón 221. Sullivan’s reported connection to La Forestal has produced hostile interpretations of ALPI as elite charity financed from extractive wealth. The underlying financial records were not located.

ALPI’s Santa Fe branch traced its formation to a telephone call from Ezcurra. It was formally constituted on 19 January 1946. A Salta historical source records the creation of its branch in 1954 and identifies Ezcurra as president of ALPI Buenos Aires.

### 1945: Political contacts

A critical retrospective source asserts that Ezcurra established relationships in 1945 with:

- **Spruille Braden**, United States ambassador and prominent opponent of Juan Domingo Perón;
- **Angélica Fuselli**, Argentine delegate to the Inter-American Commission of Women.

Fuselli’s professional connection is plausible in light of Ezcurra’s later inter-American work. The assertion of personal friendship or political coordination with Braden rests on a polemical source and should be labeled **reported but unverified**.

### 1946–1947: Caracas

In **1946**, Ezcurra moved to Venezuela to direct the new Escuela Católica de Servicio Social in Caracas, created through Venezuelan Catholic women’s organizations with support from the apostolic nuncio and international Catholic social-service networks.

She served until **1947**. Margarita Calvento, another Argentine social worker whom Ezcurra reportedly recommended, later assumed leadership.

This appointment demonstrates Ezcurra’s position in a transnational Catholic network connecting Argentina, Venezuela, Belgium, Rome, and inter-American women’s organizations.

### 1948–1950: Publications and regional work

By the late 1940s Ezcurra was publishing manuals of Catholic social doctrine. Her institutional biography also records invitations to the United States, Brazil, and Venezuela, but dates and sponsoring organizations are incomplete.

### 1950–1954: Pan American Union, OAS, and United Nations

Sources report that Ezcurra became head of a social-service section connected with the **Pan American Union/OAS in 1950** and subsequently worked with the **United Nations between 1952 and 1954**.

She reportedly lived in New York for approximately four years. The institutional history supports international appointments but does not provide exact job titles, contract dates, or departments. No UN personnel file was located.

Her international work appears to have concerned social welfare, women, children, community organization, and the development of professional social services in Latin America.

## The 1955 Coup and the Fundación Eva Perón

### Appointment

Following the military overthrow of President Juan Domingo Perón in September 1955, Ezcurra was appointed **Director of Asistencia Integral** in the Ministry of Social Assistance and Public Health.

Some later accounts call her the “interventora” or liquidator of the Fundación Eva Perón. Institutionally, the transition was more complex: the coup government intervened in the Foundation, created new administrative bodies, investigated the organization, and transferred its properties and programs.

Ezcurra unquestionably held a senior social-assistance post during the initial intervention. The precise wording, date, and jurisdiction of her appointment require confirmation in the *Boletín Oficial* and ministry archives.

### Occupation of the Hogares-Escuela

A scholarly study of the Hogar Escuela General Juan Perón in Córdoba reports that Ezcurra, acting as director of Asistencia Integral, ordered the military occupation of the country’s Hogares-Escuela on **23 September 1955**.

Other sources give 24 September. The one-day discrepancy may concern the signing and implementation dates.

The occupation and administrative intervention are **documented or strongly supported facts**. The institutions were renamed, transferred, reorganized, or closed to varying degrees; not every former Fundación facility suffered the same fate.

### “Excessive” care

A report attributed to Ezcurra criticized the former Foundation’s treatment of children as:

> “suntuosa, incluso excesiva” and insufficiently adjusted to the “sobriedad republicana” appropriate to austere formation.

The same reported passage cited fish and poultry in menus and replacement of children’s clothing every six months.

The quotation is widely reproduced in legislative, academic, and journalistic sources. An image or archival citation to the original signed report was not located during this research. Its status is therefore **reported documentary quotation**, pending inspection of the original report.

### Destruction of Peronist symbols

There is substantial evidence that the post-coup government removed names, portraits, busts, insignia, and other Peronist symbols from public institutions. This was part of the broader official campaign of *desperonización*.

Accounts specifically attribute to Ezcurra or her department orders to occupy the schools and remove objects bearing Fundación Eva Perón insignia. The broad policy and her administrative role are documented. The degree to which she personally ordered every local act is not established.

### Blankets, sheets, mattresses, balls, and toys

Numerous later accounts state that articles carrying the FEP emblem—blankets, sheets, mattresses, balls, and toys—were burned in front of children.

**Evidentiary status:** repeated allegation with partial contextual support. Destruction and removal of insignia are well established, but no contemporaneous nationwide order signed by Ezcurra, complete inventory, photograph, or independently identified eyewitness testimony was located. Later sources often repeat nearly identical lists, suggesting dependence on a common narrative source.

### Blood banks and “Peronist blood”

A particularly famous story says that Ezcurra ordered blood-bank containers destroyed because they held “Peronist blood.”

**Evidentiary status:** allegation and hostile mythology, not established fact.

Evidence located:

- repeated in political journalism, commemorative speeches, and later historical narratives;
- sometimes presented as her literal statement;
- no signed order, hospital record, laboratory report, contemporary newspaper report, named direct witness, or criminal investigation was located;
- the wording is symbolically perfect for the memory of violent *desperonización*, which may explain its persistence.

The claim should be investigated in Ministry of Health records, hospital archives, oral histories, and the documentary base used by Néstor Ferioli. On currently accessible evidence, it cannot be presented as proven.

### Pulmotors or iron lungs

Another recurring allegation states that Ezcurra ordered iron lungs removed or destroyed because their plaques bore the Foundation’s initials, forcing the government to purchase replacements during a poliomyelitis outbreak.

**Evidentiary status:** allegation, not independently verified.

The irony of attributing this conduct to a former president of ALPI has made the story especially powerful. No equipment inventory, procurement record, destruction certificate, or signed directive was located.

### Escuela de Enfermeras

The Fundación Eva Perón’s nursing school was occupied and eventually closed under the new authorities. Former nurses later recalled the destruction of materials bearing Foundation markings.

The institutional closure is a **documented or strongly reported fact**. Ezcurra’s personal authorship of every destructive action remains unproven.

### Termas de Reyes

Later accounts claim that children were expelled from the Clínica de Recuperación Infantil at Termas de Reyes, Jujuy, and that the property soon became a luxury casino.

**Evidentiary status:** reported allegation requiring property and institutional records. No direct order bearing Ezcurra’s signature was found.

### Furniture and alleged private appropriation

Sources state that furniture was confiscated from hospitals, homes, and schools and that some pieces ended up in the houses of members of Catholic civil-command groups.

**Evidentiary status:** allegation. Néstor Ferioli is cited as a source, but the responsible individuals, inventories, transfer records, and legal findings are not specified in the accessible material.

No evidence was found that Ezcurra personally appropriated furniture or received financial benefit.

### Findings concerning Foundation finances

Accounts of the military government’s investigation report that the investigators failed to prove criminal irregularities in the Foundation’s purchasing and contracting procedures. A statement attributed to Adela Caprile similarly says that Eva Perón could not be accused of keeping money personally.

These findings concern accusations against the Fundación Eva Perón, not a legal proceeding against Ezcurra. No evidence was found that Ezcurra faced prosecution for the liquidation.

## Later Career

### Return to professional and Catholic social work

After the immediate post-coup period, Ezcurra continued in international Catholic social-service networks.

She became an important Latin American officer of the **Unión Católica Internacional de Servicio Social** (UCISS), described in later academic sources as its Latin American secretary and vice president.

Her exact terms of office require confirmation from UCISS archives.

### Social Worker’s Day

In **1961**, Ezcurra reportedly proposed **2 July** as Argentina’s Día del Asistente Social or Día del Trabajador Social. In the pre–Vatican II liturgical calendar, 2 July was the Feast of the Visitation of the Virgin Mary.

The observance reflected both:

- the historically Catholic institutional base of much Argentine social-work education;
- the professional image of the social worker as a “visitor.”

In 2012, the Federación Argentina de Asociaciones Profesionales de Servicio Social changed the national date to **10 December**, Human Rights Day. The accompanying resolution criticized the confessional origins of the former observance.

A historical controversy followed over whether Ezcurra alone established the date, whether UCISS influenced it, and whether the later resolution accurately described the early institutional history. The safest conclusion is that she played a prominent role in promoting the 2 July observance, but the precise decision-making process remains incompletely documented.

### Conflict with reconceptualization

By the late 1960s, Latin American social work was being transformed by the *Movimiento de Reconceptualización*, which criticized charity, casework, developmentalism, ecclesiastical control, and imported professional models.

A scholarly history reports that Ezcurra, acting as UCISS’s Latin American secretary, accused or criticized the ECRO group associated with professional reconceptualization. The exact text of her report or accusation was not accessed.

This episode placed her on the conservative-confessional side of a major disciplinary conflict.

### Final decades

Reliable information about Ezcurra’s life after the 1960s is sparse. No detailed account was found of:

- her residences after New York and Caracas;
- retirement;
- health;
- finances;
- intimate relationships;
- continued writing;
- institutional offices after UCISS;
- final illness;
- exact death place or cause.

Acción Católica’s dates imply that she died in **1988**.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

Ezcurra did not appear to regard religion, social work, and politics as separate fields.

Her development can be divided into four phases:

1. **1915–1929: devotional charity.** Marian congregations, home visiting, food assistance, workshops, and juvenile welfare.
2. **1930–1945: professionalized Catholic social action.** Formal social-work education, Catholic Action, women’s labor organization, public mobilization, and teaching.
3. **1946–1954: transnational professional work.** Venezuela, inter-American organizations, New York, and the United Nations.
4. **1955 onward: government intervention and conservative professional leadership.** Administration after the anti-Peronist coup, UCISS leadership, Catholic professional commemoration, and opposition to radical reconceptualization.

Her writings show adherence to:

- Roman Catholic orthodoxy;
- the social authority of the Church;
- the natural family and sacramental marriage;
- opposition to divorce;
- anti-communism;
- private property with social obligations;
- occupational or professional organization;
- organized welfare rather than unstructured almsgiving;
- a hierarchical relationship between religious morality and labor organization.

### Fascism and authoritarianism

Critical authors place Ezcurra in the milieu of Argentine “integral Catholicism,” nationalism, anti-liberalism, and movements that supported military intervention.

What is documented:

- she was a senior Catholic Action organizer;
- she worked with clerics and intellectuals associated with Catholic nationalism;
- she accepted office after the 1955 coup;
- her department participated in *desperonización*.

What is not documented:

- membership in a fascist party;
- operational participation in political violence;
- command responsibility for the 1930, 1943, 1951, 1962, or 1966 military movements;
- membership in an intelligence service.

A polemical author’s assertion that she bore responsibility for multiple Argentine coups is an **accusation or ideological interpretation**, not a demonstrated chain of command.

### Christian Democracy

Some recent hostile profiles call Ezcurra a cofounder of Argentina’s Partido Demócrata Cristiano. No party founding document or reliable party history confirming this role was found. This remains an **unverified claim**.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Santiago Luis Copello

**Documented institutional relationship.** Copello asked Ezcurra to organize the young women’s branch of Acción Católica and placed her at the head of its provisional council.

### Miguel de Andrea

**Reported institutional relationship.** Ezcurra worked in Catholic social-action structures associated with De Andrea. Later accounts say he recommended her for educational leadership.

### Pius XI

**Reported meeting.** Private audience in Rome in 1932, during which Ezcurra presented the first report of the young women’s organization.

### Gustavo Franceschi

**Documented institutional association.** Franceschi was connected with the Catholic social secretariat and *Criterio*, within the environment in which Ezcurra worked and published.

### Atilio Dell’Oro Maini and Gustavo Martínez Zuviría

**Reported collaboration/shared Catholic network.** They participated in the same Catholic intellectual and Eucharistic Congress milieu.

### Angélica Fuselli

**Reported professional connection.** Both operated in inter-American women’s and social-service networks.

### Cristine de Hemptinne

**Reported international Catholic connection.** Involved in the network through which Ezcurra was selected for the Caracas social-work school.

### Margarita Calvento

**Reported colleague and successor.** Ezcurra reportedly recommended Calvento for leadership of the Caracas school.

### Juan Bernardo Sullivan and Juana Berthet

**Reported patrons or hosts of ALPI’s formative work.** Sullivan reportedly helped finance ALPI’s first premises. The exact financial structure was not established.

### Spruille Braden

**Disputed relationship.** Later hostile accounts describe friendship or strategic contact in 1945. No correspondence, diary, diplomatic cable, or contemporaneous report confirming a close political partnership was located.

### Enemies and rivals

No personal enemy is clearly documented. Her institutional and ideological opponents included:

- Peronist officials and beneficiaries displaced after 1955;
- secular, socialist, and communist labor organizations competing for women workers;
- later advocates of social-work reconceptualization;
- critics of Catholic control over professional associations.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### Established or strongly supported

- Ezcurra accepted a senior welfare appointment from the military government installed in September 1955.
- She ordered or administered the intervention and military occupation of Hogares-Escuela formerly attached to the Fundación Eva Perón.
- Her administration participated in the removal of Peronist symbolism.
- A report attributed to her criticized the Foundation’s material standards for children as excessive or insufficiently austere.
- The Foundation’s institutions were reorganized, transferred, renamed, and in some cases closed.

### Alleged but unproven in the accessible record

- personally ordering the burning of blankets, mattresses, toys, and sports equipment;
- ordering destruction of blood because it was “Peronist”;
- ordering destruction or seizure of iron lungs solely because they bore FEP plaques;
- personally ordering children expelled from Termas de Reyes;
- directing confiscated furniture into private Catholic homes;
- personally profiting from liquidation;
- belonging to or directing armed comandos civiles;
- intelligence connections;
- operational responsibility for multiple coups;
- cofounding the Partido Demócrata Cristiano;
- committing a prosecutable offense.

### Criminal and civil proceedings

No lawsuit, indictment, criminal charge, conviction, disciplinary judgment, or formal corruption finding against Ezcurra was located.

### Adversarial search findings, including 2024–2026 material

Searches combining her full name with Spanish and English terms for scandal, fraud, accusations, litigation, corruption, conspiracy, and criticism yielded almost entirely repetitions of the 1955 Fundación Eva Perón narrative.

Recent articles published in 2025–2026 repeat claims concerning blood banks, iron lungs, burned supplies, furniture, and Termas de Reyes. They do not supply newly disclosed court files, signed orders, hospital inventories, or contemporary testimony. The recent material therefore expands the circulation of the accusations but does not materially strengthen their documentary foundation.

No separate financial scandal, fraud case, sexual scandal, corruption prosecution, or money-laundering allegation was found.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “El azote de Dios”

**Story:** Ezcurra was supposedly known within Acción Católica as “the scourge of God.”

**Appearance and circulation:** modern hostile biographies and blogs.

**Evidence:** no contemporaneous letter, personnel file, memoir, or Acción Católica publication using the nickname was found.

**Assessment:** **rumor or hostile mythology**.

**Reputational effect:** it converts a disciplined religious organizer into a punitive archetype and frames later stories of destruction.

### “Peronist blood”

**Story:** she ordered blood-bank stocks destroyed because the blood was Peronist.

**Circulation:** Peronist journalism, speeches, blogs, and secondary histories.

**Evidence for:** persistence across numerous sources and compatibility with documented campaigns to remove FEP insignia.

**Evidence against or limiting:** no primary order, medical record, named witness, or contemporaneous investigation located.

**Assessment:** **allegation with mythic amplification**.

**Reputational effect:** it became the most vivid symbol of class hatred and irrational *desperonización*.

### Destroyed iron lungs

**Story:** iron lungs were destroyed or seized because they carried the Foundation’s name; new machines then had to be purchased during a polio emergency.

**Evidence:** repeated narrative; no located equipment or procurement documentation.

**Assessment:** **unverified allegation**.

**Reputational effect:** particularly damaging because Ezcurra had led ALPI.

### “She hated children”

**Story:** her unmarried and childless life is interpreted as evidence that she hated children.

**Origin:** openly hostile retrospective commentary.

**Evidence:** none. Her career involved extensive child-welfare and polio work.

**Assessment:** **hostile psychologizing and rumor**, not historical evidence.

### Hidden biography

**Story:** her life was intentionally concealed to protect her family and prevent accountability.

**Evidence:** she is indeed poorly documented in general reference works; however, her career appears in Catholic histories, social-work scholarship, and institutional records.

**Assessment:** **conspiracy narrative unsupported by evidence of a coordinated suppression effort**. Archival neglect, gendered historiography, institutional embarrassment, and the limited preservation of professional records are sufficient alternative explanations.

### The Catholic pioneer

**Story:** a selfless woman who transformed charity into organized, dignifying professional service.

**Circulation:** Acción Católica commemorations and institutional memory.

**Evidence:** substantial evidence supports her organizational and professional achievements.

**Limitations:** this hagiographic version omits her post-coup office and the coercive side of confessional social intervention.

**Assessment:** **institutional hagiography resting on real achievements but selective in scope**.

### The destroyer of Evita’s work

**Story:** Ezcurra was personally the chief architect of the complete destruction of the Fundación Eva Perón.

**Evidence:** she held an important administrative position and directed early interventions.

**Limitations:** the dissolution involved the military executive, ministries, investigative commissions, provincial governments, and successor institutions. Attributing the entire national process to one official exaggerates individual control.

**Assessment:** **historically grounded hostile mythology that personalizes a wider state policy**.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Ezcurra left several distinct legacies:

### Catholic Action

Acción Católica remembers her as a formative organizer who converted personal charity into disciplined social institutions. Her maxim is given as:

> “Dejar lo de atrás e ir para adelante.”

The institution emphasizes her professionalism, international work, and organization of women workers.

### Social work

She was an early professionally trained Argentine social worker, school director, international organizer, and professional association leader. Her career illustrates how the field emerged from a combination of:

- women’s philanthropy;
- public health;
- juvenile welfare;
- Catholic social doctrine;
- labor organization;
- state administration;
- international technical assistance.

Her role in choosing a Marian feast for Social Worker’s Day later became a symbol of the profession’s confessional origins. Replacing it with 10 December marked a shift toward a secular and human-rights-centered professional identity.

### Disability and rehabilitation

Her presidency of ALPI places her among the early organizers of systematic poliomyelitis rehabilitation in Argentina. Provincial branches remembered her direct role in encouraging their establishment.

### Peronist memory

In Peronist public memory, she is a principal personification of the destruction or confiscation of Eva Perón’s social institutions. Stories of burned toys, destroyed blood, seized iron lungs, expelled children, and appropriated furniture have made her an emblem of class vengeance.

Some of these narratives reflect documented *desperonización*. Others remain allegations whose repetition has exceeded their accessible evidentiary base.

### Historical reinterpretation

Recent scholarship has moved away from both hagiography and demonology. It studies Ezcurra as a node connecting:

- elite Catholic women;
- social-work professionalization;
- women’s labor organizing;
- medical rehabilitation;
- international welfare networks;
- Catholic anti-communism;
- the coercive reorganization of welfare after 1955.

## Chronology

- **1897:** Probable birth year according to Acción Católica Argentina; probably Buenos Aires.
- **2 May 1900:** Alternative birth date circulated by hostile retrospectives; unverified.
- **1913:** Death of her father, Pedro Tomás Dionisio Ezcurra Pardo.
- **1915:** Graduates from Colegio del Sagrado Corazón, Almagro; joins Hijas de María.
- **1915–1925:** Participates in Catholic congregations, charitable visiting, workshops, and women’s assistance.
- **Circa 1925:** Reportedly becomes president of Hijas de María; works with El Centavo.
- **Late 1920s:** Honorary juvenile-welfare and probation activity under Law 10,903.
- **1930:** Enters the Escuela de Servicio Social, Museo Social Argentino.
- **1931:** Organizes the women’s youth branch of Acción Católica at Copello’s request; heads its provisional council.
- **1932:** Travels to Rome; reportedly presents the organization’s report to Pius XI.
- **1933:** Graduates as an asistente social.
- **1934:** Participates in the International Eucharistic Congress in Buenos Aires.
- **1936–1946:** Works in Acción Católica’s Secretariado Económico-Social.
- **1937–1938:** Advises women garment workers’ organization; publishes in *Anhelos*.
- **1939:** A text by her is reprinted in *Revista Costarricense*.
- **1941–1945:** Technical director of a Catholic school of social service in Buenos Aires.
- **1943:** Helps organize a national conference on abandoned and delinquent children.
- **17 December 1943:** ALPI founded in Buenos Aires; Ezcurra becomes president.
- **1945:** Reported contacts with Angélica Fuselli and Spruille Braden.
- **19 January 1946:** ALPI Santa Fe formally constituted after an initiative attributed to Ezcurra.
- **1946–1947:** Directs the Escuela Católica de Servicio Social in Caracas.
- **1950:** Publishes *Principios básicos de la Doctrina Social Católica*; reported Pan American Union/OAS appointment.
- **1952–1954:** Reported United Nations work; period of New York residence.
- **12 June 1954:** ALPI Salta branch opens while Ezcurra is identified as president of ALPI Buenos Aires.
- **September 1955:** Appointed director of Asistencia Integral after the coup.
- **23–24 September 1955:** Orders or administers military occupation/intervention of the Hogares-Escuela.
- **Late 1955:** Removal of Peronist symbols and restructuring of former Fundación Eva Perón institutions; more extreme destructive acts remain disputed or unverified.
- **Circa 1961:** Promotes 2 July as Social Worker’s Day.
- **1960s:** Latin American secretary and vice president of UCISS; opposes or criticizes social-work reconceptualization.
- **1988:** Probable death year according to Acción Católica.
- **1996:** Alternative, poorly sourced death year in hostile writing.
- **2012:** Argentine professional federation replaces 2 July with 10 December as Social Worker’s Day.
- **2017 onward:** Academic work reexamines her role in ALPI, Catholic social work, labor organization, and the post-1955 welfare system.
- **2025–2026:** Renewed polemical journalism repeats allegations about the Fundación Eva Perón intervention without producing major new primary documentation.
- **2026:** Acción Católica Argentina publishes a commemorative profile giving her lifespan as 1897–1988.
