# Jules (Julio) Dormal: Research Dossier

**Subject:** Belgian-born architect, engineer, designer, teacher, municipal official, and arts organizer active in Argentina  
**Life:** 1846–22 March 1924  
**Principal places:** Liège; Paris; Puerto Ruiz/Gualeguay, Entre Ríos; Buenos Aires; La Plata; Mar del Plata  
**Prepared:** 20 August 2026  

## Scope, method, and evidentiary limits

This dossier reconstructs Dormal's life from government heritage records, institutional histories, contemporaneous or near-contemporaneous professional material, academic studies, architectural surveys, newspapers, genealogical compilations, and later commemorative material. No personal archive, birth act, marriage act, naturalization file, probate file, university matriculation register, or complete catalogue raisonné was located online. Accordingly:

- **Documented** means supported by an institutional, contemporaneous, or scholarly source.
- **Probable** means repeated in multiple sources but not verified here against the originating record.
- **Attributed** identifies a project commonly assigned to Dormal where the surviving online evidence does not establish the exact division of labor.
- **Unverified genealogical lead** means a claim from a user-contributed family tree, not a civil or church record.
- **Legend** identifies a story whose circulation can be documented but whose factual premise is not supported by evidence located here.
- Silence is not treated as proof. Where no evidence was found concerning religion, party membership, criminality, illness, or finances, that absence is stated rather than filled by inference.

The online record repeatedly conflates designing a building, revising another architect's design, directing construction, completing interiors, and producing a later alteration. Those roles are separated below.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and ancestry

The only names located for Dormal's parents are **Philippe Dormal** and **Julie Godet**, supplied by the collaborative Geneanet tree. Because that tree expressly warns that its data may contain errors and does not reproduce civil certificates for these claims, the parentage should be treated as an **unverified genealogical lead**. No siblings, grandparents, family occupation, household wealth, or religious affiliation were securely identified.

The use of **Godet** as a second surname in Argentine biographical sources is consistent with a maternal family name, but Belgian civil naming practice did not ordinarily create the Spanish-style double surname implied by "Dormal Godet." It may be a later Argentine genealogical form.

### Marriages and children

The family record is unusually contradictory:

1. **First marriage:** Argentine sources name **Amalia** or **Amelia Peña Lezica**, sometimes expanded to **Amalia Peña Lezica y Lezica**. The marriage connected Dormal to a prominent Buenos Aires family. The government heritage record for the Peña buildings confirms that Dormal later worked for the Peña family, but it does not establish the marriage.
2. **Children of the first marriage:** a Buenos Aires provincial legislative heritage report names **Carmen, Julia, and Carlos**. Geneanet instead lists **Julia Dormal Peña** (1881–1970) and **Carmen Dormal Peña**, while another collaborative genealogy has circulated an **Alberto Julio Jorge Dormal Peña**, born 14 July 1878. No birth acts were located; the complete sibship is therefore unresolved.
3. **Widowhood and second marriage:** the legislative report says that after being widowed Dormal married **Elina Sosa Díaz**, from a prominent Uruguayan family. Other sources call her **Elena** and Geneanet expands the name to **Elina Sosa Díaz Maderna**.
4. **Children attributed to the second marriage:** Geneanet lists **Roberto Julio Emilio Jorge Dormal Sosa Díaz** (born 1890) and **Julio Enrique Jorge Dormal Sosa Díaz** (born 1892). The legislative account instead names no children from this union. These are genealogical leads, not established civil facts.

No reliable evidence was found for extramarital partners. Reports that descendants organized or attended the 2024 centenary commemoration at the Teatro Colón demonstrate a surviving family network but do not resolve the nineteenth-century records.

### Family property

Dormal designed and occupied a large building at **San Martín 1137**, Buenos Aires. Sources differ on whether the house dates to 1913 or about 1920. A 2024 property report describes a six-level, approximately 1,700-square-meter building with thirty rooms on a 315-square-meter lot and records later ownership by the Nougués family and another private owner. Such figures describe the building in 2024, not Dormal's personal fortune. No probate inventory, purchase price, mortgage, or estate valuation was found.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1846–c. 1863

Dormal was born in Liège in 1846. Beyond the probable parental names above, no documentary account of his childhood, neighborhood, schooling, religion, family business, siblings, or early travels was located. Later biographies present him as technically educated and culturally fluent, but none reproduces childhood correspondence or a first-person memoir.

Belgium's industrial and engineering culture and Liège's technical institutions formed the background to his later combination of engineering and architecture. That is contextual inference, not evidence of his household circumstances.

## Education and Formation, c. 1860s

The educational record is the largest unresolved biographical question.

### Competing versions

| Popular architectural writing | Training at the **École Polytechnique**. | Unsupported by a located roll or diploma and likely a conflation of elite Paris technical schools. |

An academic study of the Beaux-Arts model in Latin America cautions that several European practitioners in the region were described as formal graduates when the evidence may show atelier attendance, lectures, or another form of study. No Dormal dossier from the École des Beaux-Arts, École Centrale, École Spéciale d'Architecture, École Polytechnique, or University of Liège was located in this research. It is therefore safest to say:

- Dormal had **engineering education in Liège** and **architectural formation in Paris**;
- association with Trélat, Chabat, and Viollet-le-Duc is repeatedly reported;
- the precise institution, status, dates, and degree remain unverified;
- calling him an École des Beaux-Arts or École Polytechnique **graduate** exceeds the present evidence.

His later work shows a durable command of French academic planning, historical styles, theater design, structure, and decorative coordination. Viollet-le-Duc's name may indicate direct instruction or a broader formative influence; no personal correspondence proving mentorship was located.

## Migration to Argentina and Early Career, 1868–1875

### 1868–1870: Puerto Ruiz, failure, and permanent migration

The most detailed reconstruction says that in **1868**, aged about twenty-two, Dormal traveled to Argentina to install or manage a **meat-extract/refrigeration enterprise at Puerto Ruiz**, the river port near Gualeguay in Entre Ríos Province. The business failed. He returned to Europe to close his affairs and then settled permanently in Buenos Aires in **1870**. A Buenos Aires government page instead dates his arrival to **1869**. These versions can be reconciled only tentatively: an initial 1868 trip, a temporary return, and permanent settlement around 1870.

The enterprise is sometimes called a *frigorífico*, but the sources do not identify a company name, investors, patent, process, capitalization, or insolvency proceeding. It should not be treated as a documented industrial corporation without further archival evidence.

Later accounts say the French plenipotentiary representative helped introduce Dormal to Argentine leaders, especially Presidents **Domingo F. Sarmiento** and **Nicolás Avellaneda**. Hodge calls both men close friends. The introductions are plausible and consistent with Dormal's early public commissions, but no letter recording the first meeting was located.

### 1870–1875: office and public-works network

Dormal reportedly opened an architectural office in Buenos Aires in **1870**. His early network included Sarmiento, Avellaneda, engineer **Jordán Czeslaw Wysocki**, and the emerging municipal/public-works establishment. He also became connected with **Carlos Thays**, **Eduardo Schiaffino**, **Eduardo and Alejandro Sívori**, and other figures in urban improvement and the arts.

### Parque Tres de Febrero and Palermo, 1874–1876

Short biographies often say Dormal "designed Parque Tres de Febrero." The project history is more complex:

- A competition was held for the new Palermo park. Architectural historian Daniel Schávelzon reports that the winning scheme was too costly; Dormal's second-prize proposal also exceeded available resources; Wysocki ultimately assumed a central execution role.
- A contemporaneous *Revista del Jardín Zoológico de Buenos Aires* later remembered a team including Wysocki, Dormal, Rosse, and gardeners Van Geert and Mauduit.
- Dormal is consistently associated with the **Portones de Palermo**, monumental entrance gates, and the early avenue system, including the Avenida de las Palmeras.
- The park opened in 1875–1876. The gates were later demolished, usually dated to 1917.

The defensible formulation is that Dormal was an important designer and engineer in the park's formative phase, not its sole author.

## Chronological Life History

### 1876–1885: artistic organization, sculpture, and public commissions

In **1876**, Dormal helped found the **Sociedad Estímulo de Bellas Artes**. Laura Malosetti Costa's reconstruction names a circle including Eduardo and Alejandro Sívori, Eduardo Schiaffino, Alfredo Paris, Carlos Gutiérrez, Giuseppe Agujari, Carlos E. Zuberbühler, and Juan Camaña. Early meetings took place in warehouse space connected to the Sívori family business near the port. The society promoted instruction, exhibitions, criticism, and institutional support for modern artistic culture in Buenos Aires.

A plaster bust signed and dated by Dormal in **1881**, depicting painter **Eduardo Sívori**, was preserved by descendants of Dr. Luis Govi, restored by TAREA at the Universidad Nacional de San Martín, and exhibited at the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes in 2023. This is the strongest evidence that Dormal practiced sculpture as more than an architectural decorator. No larger sculptural oeuvre has yet been identified.

#### San Martín's cathedral tomb/mausoleum, c. 1877–1880

The remains of General **José de San Martín** were returned to Buenos Aires and placed in the Metropolitan Cathedral in 1880. Official Argentine material identifies French sculptor **Albert-Ernest Carrier-Belleuse** as the maker of the mausoleum's sculptural ensemble. Later biographical and Belgian commemorative accounts assign Dormal the architectural tomb or enclosure. No original contract, signed drawing, or commission minute was located here to define the division precisely. The probable allocation is **Carrier-Belleuse: sculpture; Dormal: architectural setting/design coordination**, but the latter attribution remains less firmly documented.

#### Casa de Gobierno, La Plata, 1882–1892

The new provincial capital generated a major commission. The national heritage record states that the Architecture Section of the provincial Department of Engineers produced the building's general plan and distribution, while Dormal designed the **facades and interiors**. The work combines French and Flemish Renaissance references, Louis XIII brickwork, and neo-Baroque window frames. This official account corrects the common claim that Dormal alone designed the entire government palace.

### 1886–1899: professional consolidation and theater work

#### Sociedad Central de Arquitectos

On **18 March 1886**, Dormal was among the founders of the **Sociedad Central de Arquitectos (SCA)**. Lists generally include Ernesto Bunge, Joaquín Mariano Belgrano, Adolfo Büttner, Carlos Altgelt, Otto von Arnim, Juan Martín Burgos, Juan Antonio Buschiazzo, Enrique Joostens, and Fernando Moog. The association later lapsed or weakened. On **26 June 1901**, Dormal and **Alejandro Christophersen** invited colleagues to reconstitute it; revised statutes followed in 1902.

This institutional history matters because architecture in Argentina was becoming a regulated, credentialed profession. A later study of professional exclusion notes that several influential foreign practitioners operated with qualifications not formally revalidated in Argentina. This was a broad professional conflict, not a located accusation that Dormal falsified a diploma or practiced fraudulently.

#### Teatro Ópera, 1888

The national monuments register identifies the earlier Teatro Ópera as an 1872 building by **Émile Landois**, substantially modified by Dormal in **1888** in an opulent Beaux-Arts manner. It was demolished in 1935 for the present theater. Dormal therefore altered a predecessor building; he did not originate the first Ópera, and his work does not survive intact.

#### Other late-nineteenth-century attributions

Sources associate Dormal with early facilities at the **Hipódromo Argentino de Palermo**, zoo pavilions, and private residences. The Hipódromo opened in 1876 and passed to Jockey Club administration in 1883. The present grandstand was designed by **Louis Faure-Dujarric** and inaugurated in 1908, replacing earlier structures. Dormal may have designed original or early stands, but the exact work and dates have not been established here; the surviving principal stand is not his.

### 1900–1903: elite residences, teaching, and municipal cultural policy

Dormal became a professor in the architecture program at the **University of Buenos Aires**, whose Escuela de Arquitectura dates from 1901, and Hodge reports that he taught until his death. Architect **Alejandro Virasoro** is among the students whom Buenos Aires institutional material places under Dormal and René Sergent's associate **Louis Le Monnier**. Dormal's teaching helped transmit French academic composition at the moment Argentine architectural education was becoming institutionalized.

The **Palacio Ortiz Basualdo**, completed in **1904** opposite Plaza San Martín, is securely attributed to Dormal by the UBA architectural inventory. The residence was a family wedding gift for an Ortiz Basualdo/Castellanos alliance; sources disagree about the exact donor-client formulation. It won or was associated with a **1905 municipal facade prize**. The building was demolished after 1969.

By **1903**, Dormal was serving as a municipal architectural official, described as subdirector of Architecture. A city art-works commission created under Mayor **Alberto Casares** included Dormal, park director Carlos Thays, museum director Eduardo Schiaffino, and artist Ernesto de la Cárcova. It selected and purchased European and Argentine works for public space during 1905–1906. This activity placed Dormal at the junction of architecture, municipal beautification, and the fine-arts establishment.

### 1904–1908: National Congress and Teatro Colón

#### Palacio del Congreso Nacional

Italian architect **Vittorio Meano** won the Congress competition in 1895, and construction began in 1898. After Meano was murdered in 1904, Dormal assumed responsibility for completing the project. The building was inaugurated on **12 May 1906**, although work continued and the complex was not considered fully completed until 1946. The national heritage record calls Dormal the architect who concluded Meano's work. It does not support later shorthand making Dormal the original designer of Congress.

#### Teatro Colón: inherited project

The new Teatro Colón had begun with **Francesco Tamburini**. Its cornerstone was laid on **25 May 1890**. Tamburini died in 1891; Meano continued and modified the project; financial crisis halted work in 1894; and Meano's murder in 1904 created another succession. The municipality hired Dormal in 1904, with active direction generally dated from late 1904 or early 1905.

Hodge's archival study cites *La Prensa* reports of Dormal's selection and action in June and November 1904 and later inspections in 1907–1908. Dormal reviewed structural and safety conditions, revised circulation and rooms, completed major interior and decorative work, and gave the building a more pronounced French academic character. The official Teatro Colón history explicitly credits him with structural modifications and a French decorative imprint.

The roles can be separated as follows:

| Architect | Documented role |
|---|---|
| Francesco Tamburini | Initial project and start of construction. |
| Vittorio Meano | Continuation and substantial modification after 1891. |
| Jules/Julio Dormal | Structural review and modifications, interior/decorative resolution, coordination, and completion after Meano's death. |

The first theater lease was awarded in late 1907. The hall opened on **25 May 1908** with an Italian opera company, although the Salón Dorado and exterior iron-and-glass marquees were not yet complete. Dormal's work was crucial to the building as opened, but "creator of the Teatro Colón" is inaccurate if it erases Tamburini and Meano.

#### Contractor dispute and safety claims

A later history associated with the Pellizzari construction family says Dormal considered some existing work unsafe and at one point favored demolishing and rebuilding more extensively; he ordered mobile floor/stall work reconstructed. Because this account is derivative and no signed Dormal report was located, it should be treated as a **family-history claim with a plausible documentary core**, not a verbatim record of his position. Construction involved tensions among municipal authorities, architects, and contractors, but no evidence was found that Dormal was accused, investigated, or charged with corruption.

### 1908–1915: maturity, city politics, Mar del Plata, and commercial work

After the Colón opening, Dormal continued private and civic practice. He served on the **Buenos Aires Concejo Deliberante from 1910 to 1915**. Sources say he concentrated on building and urban questions; one scholarly discussion links him to proposals for widening Corrientes and allowing taller buildings. No secure party affiliation, election totals, voting dossier, or evidence of national political office was located.

#### Mar del Plata Sud station

Dormal designed the terminal known as **Estación Mar del Plata Sud**. A legislative preservation report dates the first train to provisional platforms to **1 December 1910** and the definitive station opening to **1 December 1911**. Short summaries often give 1910 alone. The station later became a bus terminal and operated in that role until 2009. Municipal and provincial preservation measures, redevelopment, and reuse debates have kept the building in public attention.

The **old wooden rambla of Mar del Plata** is also repeatedly attributed to Dormal, but no original drawing, contract, or precise completion date was located in the consulted sources. It remains a probable attribution rather than a fully documented catalogue entry.

#### Peña buildings

At **Florida 466**, Dormal designed the rental **Edificio Peña**, inaugurated in **1913**. The neighboring Peña-Blaye residence, now used by the Sociedad Rural Argentina, was also his work. The government heritage page distinguishes these adjacent commissions, which are often conflated. The Confitería Richmond entered the Edificio Peña's ground floor in 1917.

Other secondary catalogues attribute apartment or commercial buildings at Avenida Belgrano 456, Triunvirato 4802, Esmeralda and Santa Fe, and Esmeralda and Tucumán to Dormal. These were not verified against permits or signed plans and are listed as lower-confidence attributions below.

### 1916–1924: Palacio Pereda, own residence, and final years

#### Palacio Pereda

The Palacio Pereda is frequently called a Dormal design, but the official Argentine account names French architect **Louis Martin** as the original designer. Client **Celedonio Pereda**, dissatisfied with aspects of the project, asked Dormal to correct it. The mansion's composition drew on French palatial models, including a monumental terrace and stair associated with Fontainebleau. Dormal died before the much-protracted building was fully completed. Later claims that a son completed the work were not verified from a strong source. The residence eventually became associated with the Brazilian state and is now the Brazilian ambassador's residence.

#### San Martín 1137

Dormal's own Buenos Aires residence at San Martín 1137 belongs to his last period. One source dates purchase and construction to 1913; another property history gives about 1920. Its dense eclectic ornament and large internal program made it both family residence and an architectural statement. Dormal reportedly died there on 22 March 1924.

#### Final professional activity

Hodge says Dormal remained a University of Buenos Aires professor until his death. He was still remembered as a man of broad culture and as a senior authority of the French academic school. No retirement, dismissal, bankruptcy, major illness, accident, addiction, or psychological crisis was found in the consulted record.

### Technology and design practice

Dormal's technical reputation rested on combining engineering review with academic composition. The Colón commission required evaluation of existing structure, theater circulation, sightlines, stage/service systems, fire and audience safety, and coordination of imported and local decorative work. Later journalism sometimes says he "invented" the Colón's acoustics. No patent, acoustic treatise, experimental notebook, or sole-inventor evidence was found. The hall's performance resulted from the cumulative Tamburini–Meano–Dormal design and construction process.

No patents, incorporated architectural company, industrial technology ownership, securities holdings, or invention registrations were located.

### Wealth and finances

Dormal secured prestigious public commissions, married into established families, and owned a substantial central Buenos Aires residence. These facts indicate professional and social success but do not quantify net worth. The Puerto Ruiz venture failed; no later insolvency is known. No reliable figures were found for fees, salaries, investments, landholdings, shares, debts, taxes, estate, or inheritance.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Political activity

Dormal's only documented elective political role is his 1910–1915 service on the Buenos Aires Concejo Deliberante. His known concerns were architecture, building regulation, street planning, and urban modernization. His municipal arts work linked him to the civic elite of the conservative-order period, including Alberto Casares, Schiaffino, de la Cárcova, Thays, and national leaders who were patrons or contacts. No reliable source located a party card, campaign platform, legislative bloc, intelligence affiliation, revolutionary organization, or role in political violence.

### Artistic and intellectual position

Dormal's architecture belongs broadly to **French academic eclecticism/Beaux-Arts culture**, but his work deployed different historical vocabularies: Franco-Flemish and Louis XIII references at La Plata, monumental classicism for state architecture, lavish theater interiors, and eclectic domestic facades. His organizing work in the Sociedad Estímulo de Bellas Artes, municipal art acquisition, friendship with artists, sculpted Sívori bust, and unrealized museum project show participation in a wider program of institutionalizing fine art in Argentina.

With Christophersen and other émigré professionals, he helped make academic architecture teachable and professionally organized. His practice was neither a single pure style nor simply imported decoration; it joined planning, engineering, pedagogy, art administration, and patronage.

### Religion and philosophy

No dependable evidence was found for Dormal's religious practice, denomination, freemasonry, anti-clericalism, devotional life, or formal philosophical commitments. Work on a cathedral tomb does not by itself establish religious belief.

## Successes, Failures, Appointments, and Honors

### Major successes

- Establishing a long architectural career after the failure of the Puerto Ruiz industrial venture.
- Participation in the formation of Palermo's public landscape and Buenos Aires art institutions.
- Founding roles in both the Sociedad Estímulo de Bellas Artes and Sociedad Central de Arquitectos.
- Senior municipal architectural and public-art responsibilities.
- University teaching from the creation period of the architecture school to his death.
- Completion of two national monuments after Meano's death: Congress and Teatro Colón.
- A 1905 municipal recognition for the Ortiz Basualdo facade, as reported in later sources.
- Election/appointment to the Buenos Aires Concejo Deliberante, 1910–1915.

### Failures and unrealized work

- The Puerto Ruiz meat-extract/refrigeration enterprise failed around 1868–1870.
- Dormal's competition proposal for Palermo was reportedly too expensive for execution in full.
- A monumental Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes project was not built.
- Dormal died before the prolonged Palacio Pereda project reached final completion.
- Several important works were later demolished, including the Palermo gates, the 1888 Ópera interior/building, and Palacio Ortiz Basualdo.

### Honors and memorials

- Jules Lagae modeled a bust of Dormal in **1909**.
- The bronze bust, donated by the sculptor's son, was installed in the Teatro Colón foyer in **September 1962** under municipal Decree-Ordinance no. 9966 of 12 August 1962. The plaque reads: "JULIO DORMAL (1846–1924) ARQUITECTO–INGENIERO / JULES LAGAE / 1909."
- A centenary commemoration was held at the Teatro Colón in **2024**, involving descendants and Belgian-Argentine institutional representatives.

No national order, Belgian decoration, honorary doctorate, or formal academy membership was verified.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

### Findings

No sourced allegation, investigation, indictment, civil judgment, criminal charge, conviction, corruption case, money-laundering matter, intelligence connection, political violence, or abuse allegation involving Dormal personally was found in the consulted material.

That negative finding is limited by the incomplete digitization of Argentine municipal and court records. It should not be converted into a claim that no dispute ever occurred.

### Project controversies distinguished from personal wrongdoing

1. **Teatro Colón financing and delay:** the project endured financial interruption, political criticism, changing contracts, and public safety concerns over nearly two decades. Most difficulties predated Dormal. No evidence found here assigns him criminal or corrupt conduct.
2. **Construction disagreements:** later contractor-family material records Dormal's severe assessment of parts of the inherited work and orders to rebuild elements. This is evidence of a technical/contractual conflict, not proven misconduct by either side.
3. **Foreign credentials and professional regulation:** early Argentine architectural organizations included foreign-trained practitioners whose degrees were not always revalidated. Scholars treat this as a professional-boundary conflict. No located record charges Dormal with forged credentials.
4. **Authorship inflation:** later publicity repeatedly credits Dormal as sole "creator" of buildings he completed or revised. This is a historiographical issue, not fraud demonstrably committed by Dormal.
5. **Mar del Plata station redevelopment:** later preservation and real-estate controversies concern the fate of Dormal's building after his death, not his conduct.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. The “curse of the architects” at the Teatro Colón

**Story:** Tamburini and Meano both died before the theater opened, each at about forty-four. The story says architects feared taking the commission; Dormal accepted because he was already older than the supposedly fatal age, or because the curse affected only the earlier Italian designers.

**Appearance and circulation:** the basic sequence of deaths was available from the opening era, but the fully formed “curse” is most visible in twenty-first-century tourism, newspaper, and popular-history accounts. An online travel narrative from 2008 called the project ill-fated; Argentine popular press and cultural sites later repeated the age motif.

**Evidence for/against:** Tamburini's death and Meano's murder are facts. No contemporaneous refusal letter, municipal minute, Dormal statement, or newspaper report was located saying that candidates declined from supernatural fear or that Dormal calculated his safety by age.

**Effect:** the legend makes the protracted project memorable and turns Dormal into the survivor/completer. It also obscures financial suspension, institutional turnover, engineering revision, and ordinary project management.

**Classification:** factual deaths; later supernatural/psychological explanation **unverified legend**.

### 2. Dormal as the sole “creator” of the Teatro Colón

**Story:** Headlines and short biographies call Dormal “the creator,” “designer,” or inventor of the Colón and its acoustics.

**Origin/circulation:** celebratory journalism, property publicity, tourism, and commemorations that foreground the Belgian contribution.

**Evidence:** official records and Hodge's study show a three-architect sequence. Dormal made important structural and interior changes and brought the theater to opening, but Tamburini and Meano created and developed the prior project. No acoustic patent or sole-invention document was found.

**Effect:** the claim strengthens Dormal's public name while flattening the institution's collaborative and conflict-ridden construction history.

**Classification:** **commemorative simplification**, false if read as sole authorship.

### 3. “Dormal wanted to demolish the entire theater”

**Story:** On inspecting Meano's unfinished Colón, Dormal supposedly declared it so unsafe that it should be demolished and restarted.

**Appearance/circulation:** later reconstruction based on the Pellizzari contractor family's history and repeated in popular accounts.

**Evidence:** Dormal unquestionably made structural modifications and ordered some work redone. No original report located here proves that he formally proposed total demolition. The strongest version may dramatize a real safety dispute.

**Effect:** it portrays Dormal as the uncompromising engineer who rescued a dangerous building, while casting predecessors or contractors as incompetent.

**Classification:** **disputed later claim with a plausible technical core**.

### 4. Sole author of Parque Tres de Febrero

**Story:** Dormal designed the whole Palermo park.

**Evidence:** competition and execution histories show Wysocki, Dormal, other engineers, and gardeners; Dormal's scheme was not executed in full. His gates and design participation were real.

**Effect:** the myth substitutes an individual author for a changing state project and multidisciplinary team.

**Classification:** **overstatement based on genuine participation**.

### 5. Sole architect of Congress, La Plata Government House, and Palacio Pereda

These are three related authorship legends:

- **Congress:** Meano designed it; Dormal completed it after 1904.
- **La Plata Government House:** the provincial Architecture Section supplied general plan/distribution; Dormal designed facades and interiors.
- **Palacio Pereda:** Louis Martin originated it; Dormal revised/corrected it.

The repeated assignment of whole buildings to Dormal reflects biographical compression. It materially changes the history of labor and should not be repeated without qualification.

### 6. The missing grave

**Story:** Dormal's Recoleta grave disappeared, and his remains can no longer be located.

**Appearance/circulation:** a specialist Recoleta Cemetery blog reports that his second wife's mausoleum no longer exists, says records show cremation in 1989, and proposes a possible transfer to another family vault without confirming it.

**Evidence:** the blog cites cemetery records but does not reproduce a complete documentary chain. No official cemetery response was obtained for this dossier.

**Effect:** the unresolved burial has become a posthumous mystery attached to a man whose buildings remain prominent.

**Classification:** **document-based but unresolved claim**, not established disappearance.

### 7. Educational prestige inflation

**Story:** Dormal was a graduate of whichever elite Paris school best fits the retelling—École des Beaux-Arts, École Centrale, École Spéciale, or École Polytechnique—and a direct disciple of Viollet-le-Duc.

**Evidence:** Paris training and a Liège engineering background are consistent across serious sources, but the institution and diploma vary, and no roll was located. The named teachers may reflect courses or atelier influence rather than formal discipleship.

**Effect:** the label legitimizes Dormal within a Beaux-Arts genealogy and Argentine professional history.

**Classification:** **unresolved educational claim; Polytechnique version especially weak**.

## Death and Disposition of Remains

Dormal died in Buenos Aires on **22 March 1924**, reportedly at his residence at San Martín 1137, aged about seventy-seven or seventy-eight. No medical cause of death was located. No evidence was found of a prolonged final illness, accident, suicide, homicide, addiction, or psychiatric crisis.

He was reportedly interred at Recoleta Cemetery, associated with the mausoleum of his second wife, Elina/Elena Sosa Díaz. The AfterLife cemetery-history project says cemetery records indicate cremation in **1989** and suggests, without proving, that the ashes may have gone to another family vault. The original mausoleum and proposed receiving vault are no longer identifiable in the form described. Until official register entries or transfer authorization are published, the present location of his remains should be recorded as **unresolved**.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Dormal's posthumous image has developed along four lines:

1. **The completer of monuments.** Congress and Teatro Colón made him the architect called when a nationally important work had lost its original designer. This is accurate if “completion” includes substantive revision, but misleading when converted to sole authorship.
2. **The Belgian carrier of French academicism.** Belgian-Argentine commemoration emphasizes his Liège origin and Paris formation. The stylistic characterization is sound; the precise school credential remains unsettled.
3. **Institution builder and teacher.** His roles in the Sociedad Estímulo de Bellas Artes, SCA, municipal arts commission, and UBA show influence beyond surviving buildings. The recovery of the Sívori bust expanded his known practice into sculpture.
4. **Architect of a vanished Buenos Aires.** The demolished Palermo gates, old Ópera, and Ortiz Basualdo palace encourage nostalgic treatment. At the same time, the Colón, Congress, La Plata Government House, Peña buildings, Palacio Pereda, Mar del Plata station, and his own residence preserve substantial material evidence.

The Lagae bust's installation in the Colón in 1962 canonized him inside the building most associated with his name. The 2024 centenary renewed Belgian diplomatic, family, and Buenos Aires interest. Recent real-estate publicity for his house and preservation debate around Mar del Plata have further broadened attention.

Historical reinterpretation has moved from great-man lists toward **shared authorship**: Dormal as participant in a Palermo team; facade/interior designer at La Plata; successor to Meano at Congress; third architect and reviser at the Colón; and corrective architect after Louis Martin at Palacio Pereda. This does not diminish the volume of his work; it specifies it.

## Atlas Connections

### Alejandro Christophersen

- **[T3] Documented fact:** Dormal and Christophersen jointly issued the **26 June 1901** written call that revived the Sociedad Central de Arquitectos in Buenos Aires; they subsequently participated in its reorganization and revised statutes. This is direct documentary/professional engagement, not merely membership in the same milieu. [Architectural-history study](https://architecture-history.org/books/Breve%20historia%20de%20la%20arquitectura%20Argentina.pdf)

### Carlos Thays

- **[T4] Documented fact:** By **1903–1906**, Dormal and Thays served together in Buenos Aires municipal cultural administration: Dormal as a senior architecture official and Thays as director of parks, both participating in the commission that considered and acquired artworks for public space. The evidence establishes a shared official milieu but does not, by itself, prove friendship. [UNLP study of the commission](https://sedici.unlp.edu.ar/bitstream/handle/10915/38780/Documento_completo.pdf?sequence=1)

### Juan Antonio Buschiazzo

- **[T3] Documented fact:** Dormal and Buschiazzo were among the ten architects who signed the constitutive statutes of the Sociedad Central de Arquitectos in Buenos Aires on **18 March 1886**. Their joint signature and organizational work constitute written professional engagement. [History of the SCA](https://www.palermo.edu/dyc/publicaciones/creacion.produccion/pdf/creacion09/09-completo.pdf)

### Domingo Faustino Sarmiento

- **[T5] Reported fact:** Later biographical accounts describe Sarmiento as an early contact or friend of Dormal after the latter settled in Buenos Aires around **1870**. A more specific tradition says Sarmiento personally advised Dormal about the arrangement of trees and promenades during the **1874–1876 Parque Tres de Febrero** project. The relationship is repeatedly reported, but no correspondence or contemporary record of their first meeting was located. [Biographical account](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julio_Dormal)

### Francesco Tamburini

- **[T9-] Documented fact:** Dormal became the final architect in the succession attached to the new **Teatro Colón**, whose original project and construction had been initiated by Tamburini in **1890**. Tamburini died in 1891, long before Dormal assumed control in 1904, so this is a connection through an inherited building project rather than a documented meeting or collaboration. [Teatro Colón institutional history](https://teatrocolon.org.ar/el-teatro/)

### Vittorio Meano

- **[T9-] Documented fact:** After Meano’s murder in **1904**, Dormal inherited two major projects associated with him in Buenos Aires: the **Palacio del Congreso Nacional**, which Dormal carried toward its 1906 inauguration, and the unfinished **Teatro Colón**, which he structurally reviewed, modified, decorated, and completed for its 1908 opening. No personal meeting between the two architects is established here; their documented crossing is the transfer of plans, works, and sites from predecessor to successor. [National Congress heritage record](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/capital-humano/cultura/monumentos/congreso-nacional), [Teatro Colón institutional history](https://teatrocolon.org.ar/el-teatro/)

### José de San Martín

- **[T9-] Reported fact / qualified attribution:** Dormal is traditionally credited with the architectural setting or enclosure made for San Martín’s remains in the Metropolitan Cathedral around **1877–1880**, while Albert-Ernest Carrier-Belleuse supplied the sculptural mausoleum. The shared object is therefore San Martín’s funerary monument and relics; Dormal’s precise contractual role remains incompletely documented. [Argentine legislative heritage account](https://intranet.hcdiputados-ba.gov.ar/asuntos/asuntos1382.pdf)

### Daniel Schávelzon

- **[T1+] Documented fact:** Architectural historian and archaeologist Daniel Schávelzon, born after Dormal’s death, wrote about Dormal’s participation in the competition and formative works for **Parque Tres de Febrero**, distinguishing his proposal from the park’s later execution by a broader team. On Dormal’s dossier the sign is positive because he is the earlier figure written about. [Schávelzon’s landscape study](https://josefinaoliver.com/arc/books/arbolCementoEs.pdf)

No additional roster crossings were included where the evidence showed only broad contemporaneity, presence in the same city, stylistic affinity, or later use of a Dormal-associated building.
