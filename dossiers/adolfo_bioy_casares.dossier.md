# Adolfo Bioy Casares: Research Dossier

## Scope, method, and evidentiary labels

This dossier reconstructs Adolfo Bioy Casares's life, work, relationships, finances, politics, and posthumous record from Argentine institutional chronologies, his own autobiographical and diary publications, contemporary newspapers, archives, publishers' and agents' catalogues, and literary scholarship. It does not treat later recollection as equivalent to a civil or contemporaneous record. The following labels are used where needed:

- **Documented:** supported by a dated publication, archive catalogue, official chronology, legal/succession reporting based on a court file, or multiple mutually consistent sources.
- **Attributed recollection:** a claim made by Bioy or a named witness after the event.
- **Allegation:** a named person's claim for which independent corroboration was not located.
- **Disputed or unresolved:** sources give materially different versions, or the available record cannot decide the question.
- **Myth/legend:** a culturally influential story whose historical effect is separable from its literal truth.

Dates of first book publication are given rather than dates of later editions. Bioy repudiated much of his work before *La invención de Morel*; those books are nevertheless included because they remain part of the documentary bibliography. No evidence was located that he was imprisoned, exiled, formally charged with a crime, investigated for corruption, held patents, managed an industrial company, or maintained an intelligence-service relationship.

### Pseudonyms, collective personae, and nicknames

- **Martín Sacastrú:** pseudonym on *17 disparos contra lo porvenir* (1933). Daniel Martino's critical bibliography confirms the signature.
- **Javier Miranda:** name used for the first edition of *Breve diccionario del argentino exquisito* (1971); Bioy later issued revised versions under his own name.
- **H. (Honorio) Bustos Domecq:** collective pseudonym of Bioy and Jorge Luis Borges, most famously for *Seis problemas para don Isidro Parodi*. “Bustos” and “Domecq” came from family names in their respective genealogies. It was not Bioy's individual pseudonym, despite occasional chronologies assigning one half of the persona to each man.
- **B. (Benito) Suárez Lynch:** another collective Borges–Bioy signature, used for *Un modelo para la muerte*. “Suárez” and “Lynch” likewise drew on family surnames.
- **B. Lynch Davis** and **C. I. Lynch:** bibliographic sources sometimes attach these names to minor collaborative or detective work in the Borges–Bioy orbit. Their use is less consistently catalogued than Bustos Domecq and Suárez Lynch; they should not be treated as major independent Bioy personae without edition-specific evidence.
- **ABC:** initials used in correspondence, criticism, and later titles such as Daniel Martino's *ABC de Adolfo Bioy Casares*.
- **Bioy** and, among intimates, **Adolfito:** ordinary shortened forms. Silvina and Adolfo together were commonly called **“los Bioy.”**

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and inherited social position

Bioy was the only child of **Adolfo Bioy Domecq** (1882–1962) and **Marta Ignacia Casares Lynch**. Both lines belonged to Argentina's landowning elite. This background supplied houses, travel, tutors, leisure, a multilingual library, and the rural estate that became a writing refuge; it also meant that Bioy's adult literary life did not depend on a salaried career in the usual sense.

His father was a lawyer, rancher, memoirist, and public figure. He supported José Félix Uriburu's 1930 military overthrow of Hipólito Yrigoyen and served the resulting government in senior posts, including the foreign-affairs sphere; he later presided over the Sociedad Rural Argentina and served in diplomatic or international functions after the 1955 overthrow of Juan Perón. He wrote *Antes del 900*, a memoir of the old Buenos Aires elite. The son's liberal anti-Peronism grew in a household already connected to conservative landowning politics, although his later constitutional liberalism should not simply be equated with every office or allegiance of his father.

Bioy's maternal grandfather, **Vicente Casares**, founded **La Martona**, a pioneering large-scale Argentine dairy enterprise, and possessed extensive holdings in Cañuelas and Tandil. This is the business connection behind the celebrated Borges–Bioy advertising commission for La Martona's curdled milk. Bioy himself did not run the dairy company. His mother's interest in cinema and visual effects was formative. Bioy later connected the three-panel Venetian mirror in her dressing room—capable of multiplying a person into apparently autonomous images—with the image-machine of *La invención de Morel*.

The paternal Bioy family had been established around Pardo in the nineteenth century. Local histories associate the family with the estate later called **Rincón Viejo**. A frequently repeated genealogical claim, attributed to genealogist Narciso Binayán Carmona, makes Bioy a descendant of the sixteenth-century conquistador Domingo Martínez de Irala and therefore of remote Guaraní as well as Spanish ancestry. This is a **genealogical claim**, not one independently verified here from parish or civil records.

### Marriage and household

Bioy met **Silvina Ocampo**, Victoria Ocampo's younger sister, in 1934. Silvina was approximately eleven years older. They married in Las Flores in 1940; a surviving wedding photograph identifies **Jorge Luis Borges, Enrique L. Drago Mitre, and Óscar Pardo** as witnesses. An Argentine culture ministry account describes an eccentric attempted honeymoon by house trailer that reached Rosario and Córdoba before failing. They then based themselves in Buenos Aires and used the country estates seasonally.

The marriage was literary, domestic, and sexually nonexclusive in practice, although the surviving evidence is much fuller for Bioy's affairs than for Ocampo's. They worked in adjoining literary worlds, read and corrected one another, shared the friendship with Borges, and jointly wrote the detective novel *Los que aman, odian* (1946). They did not divorce or publicly separate. Accounts by houseworker Jovita Iglesias, Mariana Enríquez's biography of Ocampo, letters, and Bioy's diaries describe recurrent jealousies, accommodations, and parallel attachments. These sources are intimate testimony and literature, not a complete objective ledger of the marriage.

### Children and descendants

- **Marta Bioy Ocampo** was born on 8 July 1954. Her biological mother was **María Teresa von der Lahr**, with whom Bioy had a relationship; Silvina accepted, adopted, and raised Marta as the couple's daughter. The birthplace is **unresolved**: later intimate reporting places it in Pau, France; an Instituto Cervantes biographical notice gives Buenos Aires; genealogical summaries sometimes give New York. No digitized birth record was located to settle the conflict. Marta married and had three children commonly identified in estate and funeral reporting as **Florencio, Victoria, and Lucila**. She died on 4 January 1994 in Buenos Aires after two automobiles collided and one mounted the sidewalk where she was walking.
- **Fabián Bioy Demaría**, born in 1963, was Bioy's biological son by **Sara Josefina “Fina” Demaría Madero**, who was then part of the Ayerza family household. He was raised under another paternal identity. Bioy established a closer relationship with him in the 1990s and formally recognized him in 1998. Fabián died in Paris on 11 February 2006, aged 42, without descendants. His death materially changed the succession to Bioy's and Ocampo's copyrights.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1914–1931

Bioy was born in Recoleta and grew up in an affluent, book-filled, multilingual household. He learned English and French, spent time with servants, tutors, relatives, and country workers, and moved between urban interiors and the plains around Pardo. In his later *Autocronología* he remembered powerful childhood dreams and imagined presences; these retrospective anecdotes cannot be externally verified, but they matter because he made them part of his own origin story as a writer of unstable realities.

His father recited Argentine verse, including *Martín Fierro*, Estanislao del Campo's *Fausto*, and poems by José Mármol and Bartolomé Mitre. At about nine, Bioy and friends Enrique Drago Mitre and the Menditeguy brothers produced the ephemeral magazine *El batitú*. He read Buffon and *Pinocchio*, followed by Gaston Leroux and Arthur Conan Doyle. These two lines—natural or technical explanation and detective plotting—would later structure his “reasoned fantasies.”

Family travel enlarged his geographic and literary frame. A 1924 European trip is documented in the Cervantes chronology. In 1928 the family traveled through Europe and, according to the Instituto Cervantes biography, Egypt and the Near East. During the same period he drafted “Vanidad o una aventura terrorífica,” an early fantastic-detective story.

At fifteen he published *Prólogo* (1929), signed “Adolfo V. Bioy.” His father paid for 300 copies. The episode combines privilege and serious adolescent ambition: private means secured publication, but Bioy later rejected the book and judged most of his pre-1940 writing an apprenticeship. His early reading expanded rapidly to Dante, Joyce's *Ulysses*, Apollinaire, Cocteau, Ramón Gómez de la Serna, classical literature, and the European avant-gardes.

## Education and Formation

Bioy attended several private schools and completed secondary study at the **Instituto Libre de Segunda Enseñanza** in Buenos Aires, an institution linked to the University of Buenos Aires and organized as an alternative to rigid official schooling. He subsequently enrolled in law and then in Philosophy and Letters at the University of Buenos Aires. He completed neither degree.

The sequence is clearer than some short biographies suggest:

1. He entered law, found it uncongenial, and abandoned it.
2. In 1933 he enrolled in Philosophy and Letters.
3. In 1934, with encouragement from Borges and Silvina Ocampo, he left university to write independently.

There was no single academic master. His “teachers” were the family library, private language formation, reading, conversation, and ruthless mutual editing. Early philosophical reading included Nietzsche, Lessing, Goethe, Hegel, Kant, Marcus Aurelius, Epictetus, Berkeley, Hume, Bertrand Russell, and Schopenhauer. Literary formation included the Russians, Stevenson, De Quincey, Wells, Chesterton, Kipling, Shaw, Conrad, Kafka, Proust, Faulkner, and the Argentine writers Cervantes had made available through the national tradition—especially Quevedo, Cervantes, Lugones, Macedonio Fernández, and Arturo Cancela.

The decisive formative relationship began in 1932 at **Villa Ocampo**, Victoria Ocampo's house in San Isidro. Bioy, aged seventeen, met the thirty-two-year-old Borges. Bioy later described an initially unequal relation in which the younger writer learned compression, structural discipline, and distrust of decorative solemnity. Their conversations soon became reciprocal. They shared enthusiasm for Samuel Johnson, De Quincey, Stevenson, detective fiction, translation, idealism, time, and formal literary games.

## Early Career, 1929–1939

### Apprenticeship books

After *Prólogo*, Bioy issued *17 disparos contra lo porvenir* (1933) under Martín Sacastrú, *Caos* (1934), *La nueva tormenta o la vida múltiple de Juan Ruteno* (1935), *La estatua casera* (1936), and *Luis Greve, muerto* (1937). These books show Symbolist, Surrealist, fantastic, and philosophical experimentation. Bioy later tried to exclude them from his canonical oeuvre. The repudiation helped create a neat “birth of the real writer” in 1940, but the surviving books document the less elegant process by which his mature methods emerged.

*Caos* won a short-story prize from *Revista Americana* in 1934. In 1935 he attempted to administer **Rincón Viejo**. The venture failed as a practical career; he abandoned estate management and returned to writing. This was his most substantial direct encounter with business administration.

### *Destiempo* and the collaborative workshop

In 1936 Borges and Bioy founded the short-lived magazine *Destiempo*. The nominal director, Ernesto Pissavini, was the caretaker of the building—a characteristic Borges–Bioy joke at the boundary of publication and invented persona. The circle included Manuel Peyrou, Xul Solar, Carlos Mastronardi, Silvina Ocampo, and figures around Victoria Ocampo's *Sur*.

In 1935 or 1937—sources differ because the commission, composition, and surviving brochure are dated inconsistently—Borges and Bioy wrote an unsigned promotional booklet for **La Martona** on *leche cuajada*. Martino's bibliography dates *Leche cuajada* to 1935; the Cervantes chronology assigns the collaboration to 1937. The factual core is firm: the maternal family company commissioned the text, and the pair later treated the exercise as the moment when a comic “third writer” emerged from their joint composition.

Bioy began *La invención de Morel* at Pardo in 1937. Draft evidence reported by Daniel Martino shows earlier titles and names: *La invención de Gopar* through 1939 and *La invención de Guerin* in a July 1940 publishing contract. The eventual name “Morel” was associated by Borges with H. G. Wells's Dr. Moreau, but the draft sequence shows a more complicated evolution than a single direct allusion.

## Chronological Life History

### 1940–1949: mature debut, marriage, war-era collaboration

**1940** was the hinge year. Bioy married Silvina Ocampo; Losada published *La invención de Morel*, with a cover by Borges's sister Norah and a prologue by Borges; and Borges, Silvina, and Bioy published the first *Antología de la literatura fantástica*. Bioy wrote the anthology's original theoretical prologue. The anthology presented the fantastic as an international tradition of controlled invention rather than a minor deviation from realism. It also involved selection, translation, and reframing: the collaborators were cultural editors, not merely compilers.

*La invención de Morel* is the diary of a fugitive on an island whose apparent inhabitants are recordings produced by a machine that captures an entire week of life while killing the originals. Its central relations—image and body, repetition and time, love and inaccessible presence—condensed Bioy's interests in cinema, mirrors, immortality, scientific explanation, and the detective revelation. Borges called its plot perfect. The **first edition is 1940**; sources giving 1941 generally conflate publication with the **1941 First Municipal Prize**.

The collaborators followed with *Antología poética argentina* (1941). Borges and Bioy then produced *Seis problemas para don Isidro Parodi* (1942) as H. Bustos Domecq: a detective confined in a prison cell solves cases brought to him by verbose, self-incriminating visitors. In 1943 Bioy completed “El perjurio de la nieve,” reportedly in a single sleepless night; it appeared as a separate Emecé booklet in 1944 and later entered *La trama celeste*.

In 1945 Bioy published *Plan de evasión*, an island-prison novel organized around experiments in perception, and became an adviser at **Emecé**. With Borges he launched the detective series **El Séptimo Círculo**, which introduced Spanish-language readers to translated crime fiction and helped give the genre institutional prestige in Argentina. This editorial project was among their most consequential professional achievements.

The year 1946 produced three different kinds of collaboration: Silvina and Bioy's *Los que aman, odian*; Borges and Bioy's privately printed *Dos fantasías memorables* as Bustos Domecq; and *Un modelo para la muerte* as Suárez Lynch. The latter small editions, issued by the invented imprint Oportet & Haereses, extended the joke of fictive authors, critics, and institutions. Borges and Bioy also contributed a “Museo” miscellany to *Los Anales de Buenos Aires* under collective names; a posthumous volume later gathered this material.

Bioy and Borges wrote “La fiesta del Monstruo,” a ferocious anti-Peronist rewriting of Echeverría's *El matadero* and Ascasubi's political verse. Scholarly sources usually date composition to **1947**, although some accounts move it toward 1945. It circulated privately and was first printed in the Montevideo weekly *Marcha* on 30 September 1955, after Perón's overthrow. The story puts the narration in the voice of a Peronist demonstrator involved in the murder of a Jewish student. Its literary architecture and political caricature are documented; whether it offers historical description of Peronist crowds or elite propaganda is an interpretive dispute, not a factual one.

Bioy's *La trama celeste* appeared in 1948, gathering stories of alternate worlds, doubled identities, aviation, failed communication, and time. He began *El sueño de los héroes* in 1949. In Paris that year he met the Mexican writers **Octavio Paz and Elena Garro**. His long clandestine relationship with Garro would continue through visits and correspondence.

### 1950–1959: fiction, film, family, and a changing style

Bioy's mother died in 1952. The first major film adaptation, *El crimen de Oribe*, directed by Leopoldo Torres Ríos and Leopoldo Torre Nilsson from “El perjurio de la nieve,” was released in 1950; a few literary chronologies date the adaptation to 1953, probably reflecting circulation or a mistaken secondary date.

*El sueño de los héroes* appeared in 1954. Set in Buenos Aires around Carnival, it moved away from the austere islands of the earlier novels toward colloquial speech, neighborhood life, masculinity, memory, destiny, and social ritual. Bioy acknowledged Arturo Cancela's *Tres relatos porteños* as an influence on his later, plainer Argentine idiom. That year Marta was born and entered the Bioy–Ocampo household.

With Borges he wrote the screenplays *Los orilleros* and *El paraíso de los creyentes* (published 1955), compiled *Cuentos breves y extraordinarios*, and continued detective anthologies. Catalogues disagree between 1953 and 1955 for the first edition of *Cuentos breves y extraordinarios*; 1955 is the standard book date. Bioy published *Historia prodigiosa* in Mexico in 1956. He traveled to New York in 1956, where the Garro correspondence indicates another encounter.

He took up photography around the end of the decade. Retrospective exhibitions give slightly different active ranges—approximately 1958/59 to 1971/74—but the negatives and prints establish sustained practice. His subjects included Silvina, Borges's circle, Julio Cortázar, Manuel Mujica Láinez, and Alejandra Pizarnik. The camera was both private notebook and another version of his fiction's central technology: the arrest and survival of appearances.

In 1959 he published *Guirnalda con amores*, a hybrid of stories, maxims, fragments, and observations, and Argentine television adapted “En memoria de Paulina.”

### 1960–1969: travel, anthology-making, satire, and political violence in fiction

In 1960 Borges and Bioy published *Libro del cielo y del infierno* and edited work by the gauchesque poet Hilario Ascasubi. Bioy attended the PEN congress in Rio de Janeiro and traveled through São Paulo and the newly inaugurated Brasília; his notes appeared decades later as *Unos días en el Brasil*.

His father died in 1962, the year Bioy published *El lado de la sombra*. The collection received a National Prize in 1963. In 1965 the trio issued an enlarged *Antología de la literatura fantástica*, adding texts and a postscript. By this period translations of *Morel* had established an international readership.

*El gran serafín* and Borges–Bioy's *Crónicas de Bustos Domecq* appeared in 1967. Bioy also made an extended solo European journey in a rented Peugeot through France, Britain, Switzerland, Germany, Austria, Italy, and Andorra. Letters to Silvina, later edited as *En viaje*, show affection, dependence, observation, and the marital logistics of separation. A French television version of *Morel* was broadcast the same year.

In Mar del Plata in 1968 he began *Diario de la guerra del cerdo*, a novel about the organized hunting of old people by the young. He also published the essays *La otra aventura* and the play “Siete soñadores” in *Sur*. *Diario de la guerra del cerdo* appeared in 1969, as did the selection *Adversos milagros*. Its social terror has been read through Argentina's recurrent political violence, but the novel is not a transcript of one party program. Bioy, Borges, and director **Hugo Santiago** co-wrote *Invasión* (1969), an austere film about defenders of the imaginary city Aquilea. Initially little seen, it became a cult film.

### 1970–1979: national recognition, aging, dictatorship, and grief

Bioy received the National Prize for *El gran serafín* in 1970 and published *Memoria sobre la pampa y los gauchos*. He spent several months in Pau, France. The following year he published *La cueva de vidrio* and, as Javier Miranda, *Breve diccionario del argentino exquisito*, a satirical lexicon of fashionable language and pretension. He later revised the dictionary under his own name.

The paired selections *Historias de amor* and *Historias fantásticas* appeared in 1972. *Dormir al sol* (1973), presented as a report by a Buenos Aires clockmaker whose wife's identity may have been exchanged with that of a dog, was one of Bioy's own favorites. He and Borges collaborated again with Hugo Santiago on *Les autres* (1974).

International film and literary attention increased: Emidio Greco's Italian *L'invenzione di Morel* appeared in 1974; Leopoldo Torre Nilsson filmed *La guerra del cerdo* in 1975. Bioy received the **Gran Premio de Honor of the Sociedad Argentina de Escritores** in 1975.

The military dictatorship that seized power in March 1976 placed Bioy's liberal anti-Peronism in a new political context. He did **not** attend Jorge Rafael Videla's famous May 1976 lunch with writers; the attendees were Borges, Ernesto Sábato, Leonardo Castellani, and Horacio Ratti. On 21 May 1976, however, Bioy witnessed men in civilian clothes and soldiers pursue and shoot a man near San José and Hipólito Yrigoyen streets. His diary called it a **fusilamiento** and recorded fear that the killers would notice him as a witness. This is direct diary testimony to an apparent extrajudicial killing, not proof that he knew the victim or perpetrators.

His chronology calls 1976–77 “very sad years.” He published *El héroe de las mujeres* in 1978; Borges and Bioy issued *Nuevos cuentos de Bustos Domecq* in 1977. Italian television adapted Don Isidro Parodi in 1979. He continued dining and working with Borges while recording their conversations privately.

### 1980–1989: late fiction, illness, democracy, and international recognition

Around 1980 Bioy drafted “Un viaje inesperado” and “El camino de Indias” and began the unfinished novel *Irse*. Four chapters were reconstructed and published by Daniel Martino in 2001. Bioy received the French **Légion d'honneur** in 1981. He developed *La aventura de un fotógrafo en La Plata* from 1982 and published it in 1985. The novel's photographer enters an opaque family and coercive urban system; later readers have interpreted its disappearances and bureaucratic menace as an oblique treatment of the dictatorship. The political reading is plausible and influential, but the novel does not supply a one-to-one historical key.

In 1980 Bioy and Borges signed a public petition in *Clarín* demanding information and lists concerning Argentina's disappeared. In a diary entry on the restoration of democracy in December 1983, Bioy described the outgoing regime as authoritarian, unscrupulous, and criminal and expressed hope in Raúl Alfonsín. He later summarized his conduct during the dictatorship with the self-critical sentence: “Durante la dictadura no fui el héroe que me hubiera gustado ser.” The record therefore contains both public protest and his admission of limited courage; it supports neither a myth of heroic resistance nor an allegation of regime collaboration.

The 1980s brought awards from the Konex Foundation, the Esteban Echeverría institution, Mondello, and Italian cultural bodies. *Historias desaforadas* appeared in 1986; Buenos Aires named him an illustrious citizen and PEN made him an honorary member. Borges died in Geneva on 14 June 1986, ending fifty-four years of friendship and collaboration.

Bioy's health deteriorated. A widely repeated chronology assigns a **double hip fracture to a fall in 1992**. An editorial annotation to the diaries refers to a hip fracture in **1987**. These may have been separate injuries, but the accessible sources do not conclusively reconcile them. He received honorary degrees in Italy in 1988 and continued publishing selections and occasional pieces.

### 1990–1999: Cervantes Prize, bereavement, memoir, recognition of Fabián

The Spanish Ministry announced the **Miguel de Cervantes Prize for 1990** on 15 November 1990. Bioy delivered the acceptance address in Alcalá de Henares on 23 April 1991. Thus sources variously call him the 1990 or 1991 laureate; the award year is 1990 and the ceremony year 1991. He also received the Alfonso Reyes International Prize, commonly dated to 1990–91.

He published *Una muñeca rusa* and the limited travel diary *Unos días en el Brasil* in 1991 and received Argentina's Premio de Consagración Nacional and Italy's Grinzane Cavour in 1992. *Un campeón desparejo* appeared in 1993, and the University of Grenoble awarded him an honorary doctorate.

Silvina Ocampo suffered progressive Alzheimer's disease and was bedridden during her final years. She died on **14 December 1993**; one contemporary report gave 15 December, but Argentine institutional records and standard bibliographies use the 14th. Three weeks later Marta died in the street accident. Bioy's memoirs, prepared with **Marcelo Pichon-Rivière and Cristina Castro Cranwell**, appeared in 1994. They are an authorized autobiographical construction produced through conversation and editing, not an unmediated manuscript autobiography.

In his last years Bioy traveled when health allowed, received honorary doctorates from Argentine universities, and published *En viaje* (edited letters), *De jardines ajenos*, *Una magia modesta*, and *De un mundo a otro*. He re-established contact with Fabián and recognized him legally in 1998, traveling to Paris in connection with the relationship. His secretary **Amelia Francisco**, nurse **Lidia Benítez**, former household worker **Jovita Iglesias**, friend Cristina Castro Cranwell, son, and grandchildren formed different parts of his late-life support network.

Bioy experienced recurrent cardiac and respiratory trouble. Press reports state that he was hospitalized three times in the last two months of life. He continued revising diary materials with Daniel Martino and working on the selection *De las cosas maravillosas*, published in 1999.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Institutions and professional roles

- **Sur:** contributor and participant in Victoria Ocampo's network, although not an employee in the conventional sense.
- **Destiempo:** co-founder with Borges, 1936; short-lived experimental magazine.
- **Emecé:** literary adviser from 1945; co-director with Borges of **El Séptimo Círculo**, the influential detective-fiction series.
- **Losada, Emecé, Sur, Tusquets and other publishers:** successive publishers and rights partners rather than companies he controlled.
- **SADE, PEN, Instituto Ítalo-Latino Americano, Cervantes and universities:** prize, honorary, and literary relationships.
- **Photography:** no patented technology or commercial studio. His surviving photographs are an artistic/private archive.
- **Film projects:** screenplays and adaptations, especially with Borges and Hugo Santiago; no evidence he owned a production company.

### Property, inherited wealth, and estate litigation

Bioy's economic base came from landowning family wealth, property, and later royalties. Exact lifetime net worth is not reconstructable from public records. He briefly managed Rincón Viejo and failed; La Martona belonged to the Casares business legacy but was not his own operating enterprise.

Posthumous succession reporting based on the court file provides rare figures, all of which are **provisional historical valuations**, not a final net-worth statement:

- The combined succession was reported in 2007 as exceeding **ARS 8.5 million**, when the exchange rate was approximately ARS 3.15 per US dollar.
- The adjoining fifth- and sixth-floor properties at **Posadas 1650**, totaling about 697 square metres plus terrace, were sold in December 2000 for approximately **US$2.075 million**.
- Bioy's copyrights were provisionally valued in December 2005 at more than **ARS 1.5 million**; a half share in Ocampo's rights was listed at about **ARS 258,000**.
- Rincón Viejo received a provisional 2007 valuation above **ARS 7.5 million**. Bioy had transferred the property to Marta. Fabián's succession sought compensation, while Marta's branch, associated with grandson Florencio Basavilbaso Bioy, retained the land.
- Bioy left nurse **Lidia Benítez** one fifth of his estate. Litigation over conversion of a judicial dollar deposit ended with a reported payment of **US$307,706** in 2013.
- Fabián's death without children transferred his economic-rights position to his mother, Fina Demaría; Marta's children retained interests through her branch. Moral rights and economic rights did not always follow the same path.

This was civil inheritance litigation, not a criminal fraud or corruption case. No reliable evidence located in the consulted sources supports money laundering, tax crime, secret corporate structures, or financial fraud by Bioy.

The couple's library was long immobilized by succession issues. A Princeton interest or offer was reported around 2000, but the collection remained in Argentina. In 2017 the Biblioteca Nacional Mariano Moreno acquired and received the library through a purchase-and-donation arrangement. Contemporary announcements described roughly **17,000 volumes in 330 boxes**; later cataloguing reports gave **15,860 volumes**. The figures describe an initial estimate and a catalogued count, not necessarily missing books.

Late in life Bioy appeared in an American Express advertisement. An obituary reported that he accepted because the fee was “a very interesting figure.” It is a documented commercial endorsement, not evidence of financial distress by itself.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Politics

Bioy generally identified with constitutional liberalism and was a persistent opponent of Peronism. His father's participation in the Uriburu regime and the family's rural-elite position form relevant background, but Bioy's own record must be separately assessed.

- **Anti-Peronism:** clearest in the collective “La fiesta del Monstruo,” private conversation, diaries, and later dictionary satire. The story also contains an antisemitic murder committed by its Peronist narrators; scholars debate whether its use of grotesque popular speech exposes political violence or reproduces elite contempt for the working-class crowd.
- **Military dictatorship, 1976–83:** he witnessed and recorded an apparent street execution, later signed a petition for the disappeared, and condemned the regime in his diary. He held no known dictatorship office. He also acknowledged that he had not acted as courageously as he wished.
- **Democratic transition:** his 1983 notes welcomed Alfonsín while retaining hostility to Peronism. A 1987 diary reflection reportedly compared liberalism and social democracy with concern for the social costs of rigid economic doctrine.

No source consulted documents party membership, elective office, formal ideological organization, imprisonment, exile, or participation in political violence.

### Religion and philosophy

No sustained evidence establishes Bioy as a practicing Catholic, atheist activist, occultist, or member of a religious organization. His funeral included a brief religious office, which proves a burial form rather than inner belief. *Libro del cielo y del infierno* is a literary anthology, not a creed.

His durable philosophical posture was skeptical and epistemological. Berkeley and Hume supplied problems of perception; Schopenhauer, Russell, William James, J. W. Dunne, relativity, and fourth-dimensional speculation supplied models of time and reality. Daniel Martino describes a reason-seeking skepticism: the ordinary world's order is inadequate, but disciplined inquiry remains preferable to surrender to chaos.

### Artistic development

1. **1929–37, experimental apprenticeship:** Symbolist, Surrealist, philosophical, and avant-garde influences; ornate and unstable forms; later repudiated by the author.
2. **1940–48, “reasoned fantasy”:** compact plots, island laboratories, machines, perceptions, and final explanations. The detective structure controls metaphysical speculation.
3. **1954 onward, Buenos Aires and irony:** greater use of colloquial dialogue, neighborhood social types, erotic comedy, aging, and costumbrismo without abandoning fantasy.
4. **Late period:** shorter novels, fragments, memoir, diaries, commonplace books, and revisions of earlier work. Critics disagree whether this represents formal liberation, documentary richness, or declining fictional concentration.

Cinema was not an incidental influence. Bioy's fiction thinks through framing, projection, replay, spectatorship, and the survival of images. Photography gave him a practical analogue. The famous machine in *Morel* is fictional—neither an invention he built nor a patent—but it has been repeatedly read as anticipating immersive recording, avatars, virtual presence, and digital afterlives.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Jorge Luis Borges

The Borges relationship lasted from 1932 until Borges's death in 1986. It combined mentorship, friendship, editorial partnership, daily conversation, and joint authorship. Their practice was oral and iterative: one proposed, the other contradicted, both rewrote until the sentence no longer belonged to either. The collective pseudonyms made this loss of individual ownership visible.

Their projects included advertising, anthologies, detective stories, parodies, editions of Quevedo and gauchesque poetry, film scripts, and El Séptimo Círculo. Bioy's private dinner records preserve Borges's jokes, prejudices, literary judgments, and political opinions. The posthumous *Borges* (2006) is therefore both an unparalleled primary record and a disputed act of publication.

Calling Bioy merely Borges's “secretary” or “sidekick” misstates documented co-authorship; calling them equal in every phase erases the early age and authority difference. Their own “third writer” metaphor is the most accurate description of the collaborative texts, while each retained a distinct solo oeuvre.

### Silvina Ocampo

Ocampo was spouse, reader, co-anthologist, co-author, and a major writer independently of Bioy. Their one joint novel, *Los que aman, odian*, turns a provincial hotel, poisoning, erotic rivalry, and unreliable narration into a detective comedy. Their household was also an archive and salon. Silvina's late illness made Bioy and domestic carers responsible for an increasingly medicalized household.

The erotic asymmetry of the surviving archive is important: Bioy catalogued affairs and impressions in diaries, while Ocampo left more poems, stories, and indirect testimony. Claims about her relations with women, including Alejandra Pizarnik or Bioy's mother Marta Casares, range from suggestive correspondence and biographical interpretation to rumor. They should not be converted into verified lists of partners.

### Elena Garro and Octavio Paz

Bioy met Garro and Paz in Paris in 1949. Garro and Bioy maintained an intermittent romance and correspondence for roughly two decades. The **Princeton University Library** catalogue documents **91 letters, 13 telegrams, and 3 postcards** from Bioy to Garro; a 2025 edition published a selection of 36 letters and nine poems from the archive. Meetings are documented in Paris, New York, and later contacts; the relationship was not merely invented from a single memoir.

Garro remained married to or entangled with Paz during much of the affair, and Bioy remained married to Ocampo. Helena Paz Garro later cast the liaison as a tragic family legend, then criticized Bioy for failing to protect her mother. Her claim that Garro became pregnant by Bioy and aborted after Octavio Paz threatened to claim the child is discussed under disputed narratives below because no independent medical, civil, or contemporaneous documentary confirmation was located.

### Other writers, artists, and collaborators

- **Victoria Ocampo:** hostess and institutional patron through Villa Ocampo and *Sur*; sister-in-law; facilitator of the Borges meeting. Their relationship included affection and tensions over manners, loyalties, and family authority.
- **José Bianco, Juan Rodolfo Wilcock, Manuel Peyrou, Carlos Mastronardi, Xul Solar:** friends in the *Sur* and Buenos Aires literary network. Bioy's later diary selection *Wilcock* records both admiration and corrosive private observation.
- **Hugo Santiago:** director and co-writer of *Invasión* and *Les autres*.
- **Daniel Martino:** scholar, editor, and organizer of the late and posthumous papers. His editorial decisions shaped modern access to the diaries and bibliography.
- **Amelia Francisco:** secretary for more than thirty years, transcribing, correcting, and returning drafts through repeated cycles.
- **Jovita Iglesias:** household worker for approximately fifty years and principal witness in *Los Bioy*, written with Silvia Renée Arias.
- **Julio Cortázar, Octavio Paz, Italo Calvino, and later Roberto Bolaño:** writers who publicly valued *Morel* or Bioy's fiction, helping move his reputation beyond the “Borges collaborator” frame.

### Lovers and intimate relationships

Bioy's diaries and later biographies document numerous affairs. Names supported by recurring biographical evidence include **María Teresa von der Lahr**, **Fina Demaría**, **Elena Garro**, and **Silvia Angélica García Victorica**, a niece in the Ocampo family orbit. Some journalistic accounts describe simultaneous relationships and assign further names or initials. Because Bioy often coded people and because later reconstructions sometimes identify initials speculatively, this dossier does not turn every diary entry into a verified partnership. The established facts are that he had repeated extramarital relationships, fathered two children with two women outside the marriage, and preserved a major correspondence with Garro.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, or Financial Questions

### 1. Class privilege and private publication

**Documented fact:** family money financed Bioy's youthful publication, education, travel, and writing time. His father paid for 300 copies of *Prólogo*. He inherited access to urban and rural property. This materially distinguishes his career from writers dependent on employment.

**Qualification:** privilege explains opportunity, not the authorship or reception of the mature books. His attempt to run Rincón Viejo failed, and his recognized professional work included editing, translation, anthologies, and rights income.

### 2. Adultery, secrecy, and children outside marriage

**Documented fact:** extensive affairs, Marta's and Fabián's parentage, and late recognition of Fabián. **Interpretive dispute:** whether the marriage was an agreed open arrangement, a tolerated asymmetry, or repeated betrayal. Sources from different household members describe different emotional realities. No criminal charge followed.

### 3. Anti-Peronist writing and representations of popular politics

**Documented fact:** Bioy and Borges wrote and privately circulated “La fiesta del Monstruo”; it was published after Perón's overthrow. **Controversy:** the text has been read as an exposure of fascistic mass violence, an elite class caricature, an antisemitism critique, and a work whose own grotesque language risks reproducing what it condemns. These positions concern interpretation. The story is not reliable by itself as a factual report of one specific rally.

### 4. Conduct during the 1976–83 dictatorship

**Documented fact:** Bioy witnessed an apparent execution in May 1976, signed a 1980 petition on the disappeared, condemned the regime privately, and did not attend Videla's famous literary lunch. **Unresolved moral-historical question:** whether he could or should have acted more publicly. Bioy's own diary admits insufficiency. No evidence located ties him to intelligence services, disappearances, detention centers, or state office.

### 5. Publication of private conversations in *Borges*

**Documented fact:** Bioy kept contemporaneous records of decades of dinners and conversations; Daniel Martino edited the 1,600-plus-page *Borges* in 2006. **Controversy:** admirers called it a major documentary and literary event; critics viewed publication of unguarded remarks as disloyal or as reputational injury. María Kodama and some Borges defenders challenged its authority or ethics. The diary is a primary source for what Bioy wrote, not a stenographic recording guaranteeing Borges's exact words. Its editorial selection and posthumous publication require source criticism.

### 6. Succession dispute

**Documented civil dispute:** competing heirs and legatees litigated property, copyright, dollar deposits, and the effects of Fabián's death. The published figures came from the succession file. There is no consulted evidence of a criminal conviction, fraudulent will, or corrupt judicial finding.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### The “perfect novel”

1. **Story:** *La invención de Morel* is a flawless or “perfect” novel.
2. **Appearance:** Borges's 1940 prologue called its plot perfect; Octavio Paz and later writers circulated similar formulations.
3. **Circulators:** Borges, publishers, critics, Cortázar, Calvino, Bolaño, and generations of readers.
4. **Evidence:** it is a value judgment grounded in the book's unusually tight causal design, not a falsifiable fact. Critics have also identified artificial exposition and the machine as a “necessary defect,” a phrase Bioy himself used.
5. **Effect:** the formulation canonized the book, helped erase the earlier oeuvre, and sometimes reduced Bioy's career to a single masterpiece.

### Faustine as Louise Brooks

1. **Story:** the silent-film actress Louise Brooks, especially her image and bobbed hair, inspired Faustine.
2. **Appearance:** repeated in author profiles, agency material, and film-literary commentary.
3. **Circulators:** Bioy commentators, publishers, cinephiles.
4. **Evidence:** the association is compatible with Bioy's cinephilia and visual archive, but the consulted sources do not produce a dated composition note saying that Brooks alone generated the character. Drafts show the character was first named Justine and may have contained other private allusions.
5. **Effect:** it strengthened the interpretation of *Morel* as a novel of cinematic spectatorship and immortal screen images.

### *Last Year at Marienbad* as an adaptation of *Morel*

1. **Story:** Alain Resnais and Alain Robbe-Grillet's *L'Année dernière à Marienbad* (1961) secretly adapted or copied *The Invention of Morel*.
2. **Appearance:** film criticism noticed the enclosed setting, repeating gestures, unstable time, inaccessible woman, and mechanical quality of recurrence soon after the film's release.
3. **Circulators:** critics, publishers, Bioy readers, and some author biographies.
4. **Evidence for:** Robbe-Grillet had read and reviewed Bioy's novel; thematic and structural parallels are strong. **Evidence against direct adaptation:** Resnais said he had not known the novel, the film has no recording machine or equivalent plot revelation, and Robbe-Grillet's screenplay developed through his own nouveau-roman concerns. The safest description is **probable influence on Robbe-Grillet and disputed indirect relation**, not authorized adaptation.
5. **Effect:** the claim brought Bioy into histories of modernist cinema and expanded *Morel*'s aura as a hidden source text.

### Bioy as merely “Borges's accomplice”

1. **Story:** Bioy was a charming minor writer, secretary, or echo attached to a genius.
2. **Appearance:** reviews and obituaries shaped by the global disparity between Borges's fame and Bioy's.
3. **Circulators:** international journalism, simplified literary histories, and at times Bioy's own self-effacing anecdotes.
4. **Evidence for:** Borges was older, initially mentored him, wrote the canonical *Morel* prologue, and shared many projects. **Evidence against:** independent manuscripts, prizes, stylistic development, international influence, and the genuinely collective nature of Bustos Domecq.
5. **Effect:** it delayed serious reception of Bioy's solo work while making the collaboration central to his market identity. Later scholarship has emphasized difference: Bioy's erotic comedy, visual technology, colloquial Buenos Aires, and sustained novel form.

### The Bustos Domecq “third writer”

1. **Story:** when Borges and Bioy wrote together, a third author appeared who resembled neither man.
2. **Appearance:** their recollections of the La Martona brochure and later interviews.
3. **Circulators:** Borges, Bioy, critics of collaboration.
4. **Evidence:** manuscripts and stylistic analysis establish collaborative rewriting, but the “third writer” is a metaphor and self-created mythology, not a concealed human ghostwriter.
5. **Effect:** it authorized comic, baroque, populist, and vulgar voices each writer might have avoided under his own name and created a durable fictional genealogy around Parodi, Bustos Domecq, Suárez Lynch, and invented publishers.

### The Garro pregnancy and forced abortion

1. **Story:** Elena Garro became pregnant by Bioy; Octavio Paz threatened to take the child and Garro underwent an abortion with a physician named Lievain.
2. **Appearance:** Helena Paz Garro's memoir testimony and later interviews/articles, circulated especially from the 2000s and 2010s.
3. **Circulator:** principally Helena Paz, repeating what she said her mother told her and dramatizing Paz's alleged words.
4. **Evidence for:** the underlying Bioy–Garro affair and long correspondence are documented. **Evidence against or absent:** no medical record, contemporaneous letter explicitly confirming the pregnancy, civil record, or independent witness was located in the consulted material. It remains a **named family allegation**, not established fact.
5. **Effect:** it transformed the affair into a tragic legend involving two canonical male writers and intensified hostile portraits of both men's private conduct.

### Silvina Ocampo's alleged affairs and the “triangle” household

1. **Story:** Ocampo had affairs with women including Alejandra Pizarnik, García Victorica, or Bioy's mother, producing shifting triangles within the family.
2. **Appearance:** biographies, memoirs, letters interpreted erotically, and literary journalism.
3. **Circulators:** household witnesses, biographers, later critics, and rumor in Buenos Aires literary society.
4. **Evidence:** some intense correspondence and testimony exist; identification of every relationship as sexual is disputed. The alleged Ocampo–Marta Casares relation is especially speculative.
5. **Effect:** it displaced the old image of a merely patient wife with a counter-myth of a mutually unconventional household, but can also turn Ocampo's independent life into gossip organized around Bioy.

### The machine that predicted digital immortality

1. **Story:** Bioy foresaw virtual reality, holograms, avatars, surveillance, or uploaded consciousness.
2. **Appearance:** late twentieth- and twenty-first-century technology criticism and adaptations of *Morel*.
3. **Circulators:** science-fiction readers, artists, technologists, publishers.
4. **Evidence:** the novel imagines total sensory recording and replay that destroys the recorded body. It does not describe digital computation, interactive AI, or a workable engineering system.
5. **Effect:** it keeps Bioy current in debates over simulations and digital afterlives while sometimes converting a metaphysical love story into technological prophecy.

## Works by Adolfo Bioy Casares

### Authorship key

- **Solo:** authored and published by Bioy alone.
- **Collaborative:** jointly written or selected; names of collaborators supplied.
- **Pseudonymous:** genuine Bioy authorship under another name.
- **Edited/compiled:** Bioy selected, introduced, or translated material by others.
- **Posthumous edited:** assembled after death from diaries, papers, correspondence, or previously unpublished manuscripts; contents may be Bioy's, but the book as a unit is editorial.
- **Repudiated apprenticeship:** genuine early authorship later excluded by Bioy from his preferred oeuvre.

- Scholarly studies of “La fiesta del Monstruo” in *América: Cahiers du CRICCAL*, on anti-Peronism, rewriting, crowd speech, antisemitic violence, and elite representation.
- Teresa López-Pellisa, studies of dystopian virtuality and simulacra in *La invención de Morel*.
- Mariano García, “Bioy y sus precursores: la tradición de la ciencia ficción en la narrativa de Adolfo Bioy Casares” (2017).
- Contemporary and retrospective reviews by Borges, Eduardo González Lanuza, Carlos Mastronardi, Enrique Pezzoni, Alfred Mac Adam, Jorgelina Loubet, and others listed in Martino's critical bibliography.

These studies disagree over periodization, the weight of Borges's influence, whether the later colloquial works represent advance or dilution, and whether Bioy's autobiographical simplicity is candor, performance, or concealment.

### Archives

- **Biblioteca Nacional Mariano Moreno, Buenos Aires:** the Bioy–Ocampo library—first editions, annotated books, dedications, photographs, and manuscripts—acquired in 2017 and opened progressively to research. Initial publicity estimated 17,000 books; cataloguing reports 15,860.
- **Princeton University Library:** Adolfo Bioy Casares letters to Elena Garro, 1952–69 (the catalogue title begins in 1952 although the relationship began earlier): 91 letters, 13 telegrams, 3 postcards, in Spanish, French, and English.
- **Published diary archive:** *Descanso de caminantes*, *Borges*, and *Wilcock*, all editorial selections rather than the entire raw diary corpus.
- **Photographic archive:** negatives and prints shown in the 2014 Buenos Aires exhibition *El lado de la luz: Bioy fotógrafo* and related cataloguing.
- **Unfinished and unpublished material:** *Irse* survives only in four reconstructed chapters; archival references also mention incomplete projects including *Catón siglo XX*. Their unfinished state should not be concealed by later editorial packaging.

## Death and Final Disposition

Bioy was hospitalized repeatedly for cardiac and respiratory problems in early 1999. He died on 8 March in Buenos Aires. The immediate reporting varies in medical phrasing—heart condition, respiratory complications, or combined cardiopulmonary deterioration—and no consulted public source reproduces a full death certificate or autopsy. There was no documented violent or suspicious cause.

At his request there was no public wake. Family, friends, secretary Amelia Francisco, son Fabián, and grandchildren attended a brief religious service at Recoleta; he was buried in the family vault. Contemporary reports stressed that he had worked and joked until near the end. Silvina had preceded him by a little over five years, Marta by five years, and Borges by nearly thirteen.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Bioy's posthumous standing has developed along several partly conflicting lines:

- **Independent canonical novelist:** *La invención de Morel*, *El sueño de los héroes*, *Diario de la guerra del cerdo*, and *Dormir al sol* sustain a reputation distinct from Borges. Translations and adaptations continue.
- **Collaborative author:** the full Borges–Bioy corpus changed theories of authorship. Bustos Domecq is studied as a collective style with a fictive biography, not merely a pair of signatures.
- **Diarist and witness:** *Borges* turned Bioy into the principal chronicler of Borges's private speech and of a Buenos Aires literary world. Its value and ethics remain contested.
- **Technological precursor:** *Morel* has been reread through cinema, holography, simulation, virtual reality, avatars, and digital immortality, even though its machine is metaphysical fiction rather than engineering forecast.
- **Photographer:** the 2014 exhibition expanded the archive beyond literature and showed that visual capture was a practice as well as a theme.
- **Political re-reading:** scholarship situates “La fiesta del Monstruo,” *Diario de la guerra del cerdo*, and *La aventura de un fotógrafo en La Plata* within anti-Peronism, class conflict, antisemitic violence, dictatorship, and disappearance without reducing the novels to political allegory.
- **Intimate revision:** publication of diaries, the Garro letters, *Los Bioy*, and biographies of Ocampo dismantled the image of a frictionless gentlemanly marriage. The result is better documentation mixed with rumor; the two must remain distinguished.
- **Archive as shared national collection:** the recovery and cataloguing of the Bioy–Ocampo library keep both writers' marginalia, reading histories, and exchanges with Borges available in Argentina rather than dispersed entirely through sale.

His influence is visible in Latin American speculative fiction, in writers including Cortázar and later Bolaño, in film criticism around *Marienbad*, in adaptations from Italy to Argentina, and in contemporary art about recording and replay. The most durable symbolic pair is Bioy–Borges; the most productive correction is to place **Silvina Ocampo as a third independent writer and collaborator**, not merely the wife beside two men.

## Atlas Connections

### Jorge Luis Borges

- **[T3] [T5] Documented fact:** Bioy met Borges at Victoria Ocampo’s house in San Isidro in 1932. Their friendship continued until Borges’s death in 1986 and encompassed correspondence, mutual criticism, editorial work, screenwriting, anthologies, and extensive joint authorship under their own and collective names. Bioy also wrote published articles about Borges—among them “El jardín de los senderos que se bifurcan” and “Desagravio a Borges” (1942)—and recorded decades of their conversations in the diary selection published posthumously as *Borges* (2006). [Cervantes chronology](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/portales/adolfo_bioy_casares/autor_cronologia/); [Cervantes bibliography](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/portales/adolfo_bioy_casares/su_obra_bibliografia/)

### Silvina Ocampo

- **[T3] [T5] Documented fact:** Bioy met Ocampo in 1934, married her at Las Flores in 1940, and remained with her until her death in 1993. Their documented literary crossings include the jointly written novel *Los que aman, odian* (1946) and, with Borges, the jointly selected and translated *Antología de la literatura fantástica* (1940; enlarged 1965) and *Antología poética argentina* (1941). [Cervantes chronology](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/portales/adolfo_bioy_casares/autor_cronologia/); [Cervantes bibliography](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/portales/adolfo_bioy_casares/su_obra_bibliografia/)

### Victoria Ocampo

- **[T5] Documented fact:** Victoria Ocampo, Bioy’s future sister-in-law, hosted the 1932 gathering at Villa Ocampo in San Isidro where Bioy met Borges. Bioy subsequently belonged to her familial and *Sur* milieu; photographs document Bioy, Victoria, and Borges together at Mar del Plata around 1940 and at a 1962 anniversary gathering for *Sur* in Buenos Aires. [Cervantes chronology](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/portales/adolfo_bioy_casares/autor_cronologia/); [photographic record, c. 1940](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/portales/adolfo_bioy_casares/imagenes_album/imagen/imagenes_album_09_adolfo_bioy_casares_victoria_ocampo_y_borges_1940/); [photographic record, 1962](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/portales/adolfo_bioy_casares/imagenes_album/imagen/imagenes_album_16_adolfo_bioy_casares_aniversario_sur_borges_pezzoni_lanuza_torre_1962/)

### Xul Solar

- **[T3] [T5] Documented fact:** Bioy’s chronology records that he met Xul Solar in 1936. That year Xul contributed “Visión sobr el trilíneo” to *Destiempo*, the magazine edited by Bioy and Borges, establishing a direct editorial as well as personal crossing. [Cervantes chronology](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/portales/adolfo_bioy_casares/autor_cronologia/); [study of the Borges–Bioy collaboration](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/obra-visor/la-colaboracion-literaria-de-jorge-luis-borges-y-adolfo-bioy-casares/)

### Julio Cortázar

- **[T3] [T5] Documented fact:** Bioy personally photographed Cortázar during his sustained photographic activity, placing them in direct contact; the surviving portrait was exhibited in Buenos Aires in 2014. Their written discourse was reciprocal: Bioy discussed Cortázar in published interviews, while Cortázar invoked Bioy in “Diario de un cuento” and publicly commented on affinities between their fiction. The evidence supports meeting and literary engagement, though not a comparably intimate friendship to Bioy’s relationship with Borges. [Buenos Aires municipal exhibition record](https://buenosaires.gob.ar/gcaba_historico/noticias/se-inauguro-la-muestra-inedita-de-fotografias-de-adolfo-bioy-casares); [Cervantes interview](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/obra-visor/la-literatura-la-fotografia-el-cine-y-la-eternidad--entrevista-a-adolfo-bioy-casares/html/df1f3510-b5c1-45db-bc9d-dd02a1bdda04_2.html)

### H. G. Wells

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Bioy wrote “Elogio de Wells,” published in the September 1946 memorial-oriented issue of *Los Anales de Buenos Aires*, shortly after Wells’s death on 13 August 1946. The essay is a direct instance of the later Bioy writing about the earlier Wells, whose fantastic fiction also figured prominently in Bioy’s literary formation. [Issue catalogue and contents](https://ahira.com.ar/ejemplares/los-anales-de-buenos-aires-no-9/)
