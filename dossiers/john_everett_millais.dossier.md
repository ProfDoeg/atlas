# Sir John Everett Millais: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier reconstructs the documented life and career of Sir John Everett Millais from birth to death and traces his posthumous reputation through 2026.

Evidence labels used here:

- **Documented fact**: supported by contemporary records, correspondence, institutional catalogues, official appointments, or multiple scholarly sources.
- **Reported fact**: asserted by a generally reliable secondary source but not independently confirmed here from the underlying document.
- **Disputed claim**: competing interpretations exist.
- **Allegation or theory**: proposed explanation lacking conclusive contemporary evidence.
- **Rumor or myth**: a story circulating in biography, journalism, drama, or popular culture without adequate documentary confirmation.

Millais’s son John Guille Millais’s *The Life and Letters of Sir John Everett Millais* (1899) remains indispensable because it prints letters, reminiscences, and a detailed chronology. It is also a filial Victorian biography, prepared close to the family, and may protect family reputations or omit private material. Its evidence should therefore be distinguished from its interpretations.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal family

**Documented fact:** Millais’s father was John William Millais, associated with an established Jersey family described in early biographies as of Norman descent. The family had lived in Jersey for generations. John William was remembered as a capable amateur artist and musician, an officer in the Jersey militia, and a man prominent in island society.

A near-contemporary *Dictionary of National Biography* entry reports that John William Millais helped arrest Edward Oxford after Oxford fired at Queen Victoria in 1840. This is a **reported fact** in the biographical record; it is incidental to the artist’s life and was not independently reconstructed here from Oxford’s criminal papers.

John William Millais died in 1870.

### Maternal family

Millais’s mother was Emily Mary Millais, née Evamy (the spelling “Evamey” also appears). She was the daughter of John Evamy, from a prosperous saddlery family. Before marrying John William Millais she had been married to Enoch Hodgkinson and had two sons.

Emily Mary was the decisive organizer of her youngest son’s artistic education. Early biographies credit her with recognizing his talent, educating him at home for part of his childhood, soliciting professional opinions, and moving or maintaining the family in London so that he could obtain formal instruction.

### Siblings and half-siblings

John Everett was described as the youngest son. His full brother William Henry Millais became a painter and illustrator and published on natural history, including *The Game Birds and Wild Fowl of Great Britain and Ireland*. William accompanied John Everett on the consequential 1853 Scottish expedition with John and Effie Ruskin.

The two sons from Emily Mary’s first marriage were Millais’s maternal half-brothers. Their names and life histories are not consistently supplied in the principal public biographical summaries consulted, and no firm reconstruction is offered here.

### Marriage

Millais married Euphemia Chalmers Gray—universally known as Effie—at Kinnoull parish church near Perth on 3 July 1855. She was born in Perth on 7 May 1828, the daughter of George Gray, a Writer to the Signet, and Sophia Margaret Jameson.

Effie had married John Ruskin in 1848. That marriage was annulled in 1854 for non-consummation. Her marriage to Millais took place after the annulment.

Effie became central to Millais’s professional organization. Later scholarship describes her as his studio manager: she handled social relations, exhibitions, patrons, household presentation, and aspects of the commercial circulation of his work. She also modeled for him, notably in *The Order of Release, 1746*, *Peace Concluded*, and portraits.

### Children

John and Effie had eight children:

1. **Everett Millais** (born 1856), eldest son and eventual 2nd Baronet.
2. **George Gray Millais** (born 1857; died 1878), whose early death was a major family bereavement.
3. **Euphemia “Effie” Gray Millais** (born 1858).
4. **Mary Hunt Millais** (1860–1944).
5. **Alice Sophia Caroline “Carrie” Millais** (born 1862).
6. **Geoffroy William Millais** (born 1863).
7. **John Guille Millais** (1865–1931), artist, naturalist, traveler, horticultural writer, and author of the principal family biography of his father.
8. **Sophia Margaret “Tottie” Millais** (born 1868).

All eight children appeared in at least one work by their father. His daughters were particularly important as models for his later child and “fancy” pictures.

Effie died on 23 December 1897, about sixteen months after her husband.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1829–1838

Millais was born in Southampton while his Jersey family was temporarily living in England. Soon afterward the family returned to Jersey, where he spent much of his early childhood. The household’s means and social position permitted intensive cultivation of his talent.

He showed an early interest in drawing and natural history. The story that he drew his maternal grandfather at eight is repeated in early biographical sources and is consistent with surviving accounts of his precocity.

In 1835 the family moved to Dinan in Brittany. The boy reportedly sketched French soldiers and officers. The family returned to Jersey in 1837, where he received lessons from a drawing teacher named Bissel or Bissell.

Early narratives sometimes present the young Millais as uninterested in ordinary schooling and educated chiefly by his mother. This is substantially a family tradition preserved by his son and early biographers, although his extraordinary technical progress is independently demonstrated by surviving juvenilia and his later competition record.

In 1838 Emily Mary brought him to London. Sir Martin Archer Shee, then President of the Royal Academy, reportedly examined the boy’s drawings and strongly encouraged a professional artistic career.

---

## Education and Formation, 1838–1848

### Sass’s Academy

In 1838 Millais entered Henry Sass’s private drawing school in London. Sass’s academy prepared students for admission to the Royal Academy Schools through drawing from casts and the antique.

### Society of Arts recognition

In 1839, aged about ten, Millais won a silver medal from the Society of Arts. The award reinforced the family’s commitment to his career.

### Royal Academy Schools

In 1840, aged eleven, Millais entered the Royal Academy Schools. He is regularly described as the youngest student ever admitted; some institutional accounts state that he qualified at ten and formally entered at eleven. The difference is one of wording rather than a substantive contradiction.

His Academy formation emphasized drawing from antique sculpture, anatomy, composition, and the conventions of European history painting. He won:

- a silver medal for drawing from the antique in 1843;
- prizes during his subsequent course;
- the Academy gold medal in 1847 for *The Tribe of Benjamin Seizing the Daughters of Shiloh*.

### Teachers, peers, and influences

His most consequential fellow students were William Holman Hunt and Dante Gabriel Rossetti. Hunt and Millais developed an especially close professional friendship. Rossetti supplied literary, medievalist, and anti-academic energy that complemented Millais’s exceptional technical discipline.

Formative sources included:

- early Italian painting before and around Raphael;
- Northern European naturalism;
- Shakespeare;
- John Keats;
- Alfred Tennyson;
- the Bible and Christian typology;
- British historical painting;
- close outdoor observation;
- later, portrait traditions associated with Reynolds, Gainsborough, Velázquez, and continental painters.

Millais’s relationship with the Royal Academy was never a simple rejection. He was one of its most highly rewarded pupils, rebelled against its dominant formulas, then advanced through its institutional ranks and ultimately became its president.

## Early Career and Formation of the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood, 1846–1850

### Academic debut

In 1846, aged sixteen, Millais exhibited *Pizarro Seizing the Inca of Peru* at the Royal Academy. In 1847 he exhibited *Elgiva Seized by the Soldiers of Odo* and won the gold medal with *The Tribe of Benjamin Seizing the Daughters of Shiloh*.

These early works belong to conventional academic history painting and show that he had mastered the institution’s methods before repudiating some of them.

### Founding the Brotherhood

**Documented fact:** In September 1848, Millais, Hunt, and Rossetti formed the nucleus of the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood. Meetings occurred at the Millais family home at 83 Gower Street, London, and in the artists’ studios.

The seven usually recognized original members were:

- John Everett Millais;
- William Holman Hunt;
- Dante Gabriel Rossetti;
- William Michael Rossetti;
- Thomas Woolner;
- Frederic George Stephens;
- James Collinson.

Their principles were never reduced to one binding manifesto. Shared commitments included direct study from nature, intense color, sharp detail, serious literary and religious subjects, and opposition to mechanical imitation of post-Raphaelite academic convention. The initials “PRB” were initially used without public explanation.

### *Isabella*

Millais’s *Isabella* or *Lorenzo and Isabella* (1848–49), derived from Keats’s poem and ultimately Boccaccio, was his first major painting executed under the new principles. It was exhibited at the Royal Academy in 1849. Its compressed space, sharp profiles, symbolic detail, and deliberately awkward social tension announced the Brotherhood’s break with conventional composition.

### *The Germ*

The Brotherhood’s periodical, *The Germ*, appeared in four issues in 1850. Millais contributed visual material rather than functioning as its principal author. Commercially the journal failed, though it later became a primary documentary source for the group’s ideas.

## Chronological Life History

## 1850–1852: Public controversy and technical culmination

### *Christ in the House of His Parents*

Millais exhibited *Christ in the House of His Parents* at the Royal Academy in 1850. It showed the Holy Family as an ordinary carpenter’s household, with workshop dirt, labor-worn figures, and typological details anticipating the Crucifixion.

The response was exceptionally hostile. Charles Dickens, writing in *Household Words*, attacked the physical appearance of the Virgin and Christ and treated the painting’s working-class realism as grotesque and degrading. The criticism is a **documented fact**; Dickens’s language also supplies evidence of the class, bodily, and racialized expectations governing religious art in 1850.

The painting was sometimes described by opponents as irreverent or blasphemous. No criminal or ecclesiastical proceeding against Millais resulted.

### Ruskin’s defense

In 1851 John Ruskin published letters to *The Times* defending the young Pre-Raphaelites’ sincerity and close observation. Ruskin initially stated that he did not personally know them. His intervention materially improved their public standing and began a relationship of patronage and friendship.

### Paintings of 1851

Important works included:

- *Mariana*;
- *The Woodman’s Daughter*;
- *The Return of the Dove to the Ark*.

These works combined literary subjects, saturated color, symbolic objects, and detailed natural settings.

### *Ophelia*

Millais painted the landscape portion of *Ophelia* beside the Hogsmill River near Ewell, Surrey, in 1851. Elizabeth Siddal modeled for Ophelia in a bathtub in the London studio during the winter of 1851–52.

**Documented core:** Siddal lay clothed in heated bathwater while Millais painted. The lamps used to warm the bath went out, and she became ill. Her father demanded that Millais pay medical expenses.

**Uncertain detail:** Popular accounts variously call her illness a severe cold or pneumonia. The precise clinical diagnosis is not securely established.

Exhibited at the Royal Academy in 1852, *Ophelia* did not immediately receive the nearly universal admiration it later acquired. It subsequently became the best-known individual image associated with British Pre-Raphaelitism.

### *A Huguenot*

*A Huguenot, on St Bartholomew’s Day, Refusing to Shield Himself from Danger by Wearing the Roman Catholic Badge* was exhibited in 1852 and became a major popular and commercial success. It established a lucrative formula: emotionally legible historical romance combined with technical brilliance.

---

## 1852–1855: Effie Gray, Scotland, annulment, and marriage

### *The Order of Release*

Effie Ruskin modeled for *The Order of Release, 1746*, completed in 1852–53. The painting depicts a Jacobite prisoner being released through the intervention of his wife. Its popularity at the 1853 Academy was reportedly so great that crowd control was necessary.

### Royal Academy associate

Millais was elected an Associate of the Royal Academy in 1853. The election shows that his institutional rehabilitation was already underway even before the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood ceased functioning as a coherent body.

### Scottish expedition of 1853

In the summer of 1853, Millais, his brother William, John Ruskin, Effie Ruskin, and at points Ruskin’s friend Henry Acland stayed in the Trossachs around Brig o’ Turk and Glenfinlas.

John Ruskin’s father had commissioned Millais to paint Ruskin’s portrait. Millais painted the landscape outdoors in difficult wet conditions and completed the figure later in London. He also made drawings and small paintings of Effie, including *Effie with Foxgloves in Her Hair*.

**Documented fact:** Millais and Effie developed an intense attachment during the expedition.

**Historical caution:** Later accounts often call this an “affair.” Their surviving correspondence establishes emotional love, but public evidence does not conclusively establish a sexual relationship before the annulment. Representing adultery as established fact would exceed the evidence.

### Annulment of the Ruskin marriage

Effie left Ruskin in April 1854. Proceedings investigated the non-consummation of their marriage, and she underwent a medical examination establishing virginity. The marriage was annulled in 1854 on grounds of incurable impotence in relation to her.

Millais was not a defendant in the ecclesiastical case and was not charged with adultery.

After the separation, Millais and Effie communicated under conditions of family supervision and social sensitivity. He completed Ruskin’s portrait despite the personal rupture. Ruskin eventually gave the picture to Henry Acland in 1871.

### Marriage to Effie

Millais and Effie married on 3 July 1855 at Kinnoull. Because she was a divorced or annulled woman in Victorian society, her status remained socially difficult. She was for many years unable to appear at court, a restriction that became particularly awkward after Millais received a baronetcy. Near the end of his life, intervention associated with Princess Louise helped secure her reception by Queen Victoria.

---

## 1855–1863: Marriage, family, transition, and illustration

The couple’s early married life was closely connected with Perthshire and Effie’s family home, Bowerswell. Millais increasingly developed interests in fishing, shooting, Scottish scenery, and natural history. The family also maintained a London career and household.

### Major paintings

Important works of the transitional years include:

- *The Rescue* (1855);
- *Autumn Leaves* (1855–56);
- *The Blind Girl* (1856);
- *Peace Concluded* (1856);
- *The Vale of Rest* (1858–59);
- *Apple Blossoms* or *Spring* (1856–59);
- *Sir Isumbras at the Ford* (1857);
- *The Black Brunswicker* (1860).

These works show a progressive loosening of the painstaking early Pre-Raphaelite handling. Millais retained strong color, observation, and emotional narrative but adopted broader brushwork and increasingly accessible subjects.

Ruskin praised several 1856 works but attacked *Sir Isumbras at the Ford* and other later developments. Their relationship did not end at one clean moment, but the intimate alliance of the early 1850s was over.

### Illustration

From the late 1850s through the 1860s, Millais became one of Britain’s leading illustrators. He designed images for wood engraving, often executed by the Dalziel Brothers or Joseph Swain, for:

- *Once a Week*;
- *The Cornhill Magazine*;
- *Good Words*;
- *The Illustrated London News*;
- Moxon’s illustrated edition of Tennyson;
- novels by Anthony Trollope;
- religious and gift books.

His Trollope work was especially substantial. Contemporary and later estimates credit him with approximately eighty-seven Trollope designs. Important collaborations included:

- *Framley Parsonage* (serial 1860–61; book 1861);
- *Orley Farm* (parts 1861–62; book 1862), forty illustrations;
- *The Small House at Allington* (serial 1862–64; book 1864);
- *Rachel Ray* (illustrated edition, 1864);
- *Phineas Finn* (serial 1867–69; book 1869);
- *Kept in the Dark* (1882 serial/frontispiece reuse).

Trollope praised Millais for studying the text and promoting the author’s intended characterization rather than merely decorating it.

### Royal Academician

Millais was elected a full Royal Academician in 1863.

---

## 1863–1878: Establishment career, family growth, and prosperity

From 1861 to 1877 the family lived at 7 Cromwell Place, Kensington. Millais worked at high speed and maintained a large household. Effie organized social connections and commissions.

### Subject painting and “fancy pictures”

Works included:

- *My First Sermon* (1863);
- *My Second Sermon* (1864);
- *The Romans Leaving Britain* (1865);
- *Jephthah* (1867);
- *Rosalind and Celia* (1868);
- *A Flood* (1870);
- *The Boyhood of Raleigh* (1870);
- *Chill October* (1870);
- *Hearts Are Trumps* (1872);
- *The North-West Passage* (1874);
- *Princes in the Tower* (1878);
- *Cherry Ripe* (1879).

His children and other young models supplied subjects for paintings marketed around childhood, sentiment, beauty, and domestic feeling. These pictures were popular with collectors and publishers of reproductions.

### Portraiture

Portraiture became an increasingly important source of income and status. Millais painted politicians, aristocrats, clergy, writers, physicians, actors, and family members. Sitters included:

- Thomas Carlyle;
- Alfred Tennyson;
- John Henry Newman;
- Benjamin Disraeli;
- William Ewart Gladstone;
- John Bright;
- the Marquess of Salisbury;
- Lord Rosebery;
- the Duke of Argyll;
- the Duke of Westminster;
- Sir James Paget;
- Sir Henry Irving;
- John Hare;
- Louise Jopling.

He made a posthumous drawing of Charles Dickens in 1870.

### Travel

Millais’s continental travel was less extensive than that of many contemporaries. Documented locations included:

- Jersey and Southampton in childhood;
- Dinan, Brittany, 1835–37;
- London from 1838;
- frequent travel between London and Perthshire after marriage;
- the Scottish Highlands and Trossachs;
- Holland in 1875;
- periodic professional and recreational visits elsewhere in Britain.

There is no evidence that he experienced exile, imprisonment, or forced migration.

### Death of George Millais

The death of his second son George in 1878 was one of the principal personal losses of his mature life. Sources differ between 1876 and 1878 in some modern summaries; the near-contemporary biographical tradition gives 1878 and is preferred here.

---

## 1879–1889: Palace Gate, honors, portrait dominance, and *Bubbles*

### Palace Gate

Millais commissioned a substantial house and studio at 2 Palace Gate, Kensington, designed by Philip Charles Hardwick. He moved there around 1878–79 and remained until death. Its scale and furnishing became material symbols of his success.

### Scottish properties and seasonal life

In 1881 he leased Murthly near Little Dunkeld, Perthshire, with access to fishing and shooting. Scottish seasonal residences supplied subjects for late landscapes, including:

- *Murthly Moss*;
- *Murthly Water*;
- *Dew-Drenched Furze*;
- *Lingering Autumn*.

The Murthly lease ended in 1891. He then took Newmill, which burned in January 1892. No evidence located indicates fraud, arson, insurance misconduct, or litigation arising from the fire.

### Self-portrait and exhibitions

In 1880 he painted a self-portrait for the Uffizi Gallery in Florence. A smaller exhibition was held by the Fine Art Society in 1881; a major retrospective appeared at the Grosvenor Gallery in 1886.

He served as president of the Royal Birmingham Society of Artists in 1881–82.

### Baronetcy and foreign honors

On 16 July 1885, at Gladstone’s suggestion, Queen Victoria created him a baronet. He is widely described as the first British artist to receive a hereditary title specifically for artistic distinction.

Other honors included:

- honorary DCL from Oxford, 9 June 1880;
- an honorary degree from Durham;
- associate status in the Institut de France;
- honorary membership in the Royal Scottish and Royal Hibernian Academies;
- affiliations with academies in Vienna, Belgium, Antwerp, Rome, and Madrid;
- Officer of Belgium’s Order of Leopold;
- Order of Saints Maurice and Lazarus;
- Prussian *Pour le Mérite*.

### *A Child’s World* / *Bubbles*

In 1886 Millais painted his grandson William Milbourne James blowing soap bubbles. The work was originally known as *A Child’s World*. It was acquired by Thomas J. Barratt, the advertising manager of A. & F. Pears, and adapted into an advertisement by adding Pears branding and a bar of soap.

The transaction became one of the central controversies of Millais’s reputation.

**Documented fact:** Millais painted a private or exhibition picture, not an original commissioned soap advertisement.

**Documented fact:** Copyright ownership allowed commercial reproduction and alteration. Millais was consulted about the altered form.

**Disputed claim:** His son later represented him as attempting unsuccessfully to prevent the advertisement; other evidence indicates that, after initial reservations, Millais accepted or approved the adaptation.

**Documented fact:** Critics treated the advertisement as a debasement of fine art. Marie Corelli later accused him of degrading or “prostituting” his talent to sell soap. Millais responded that he had sold the copyright and lacked control over its use; Corelli removed or modified the attack in a later edition.

Modern scholarship treats *Bubbles* as an important case in art copyright, mass reproduction, advertising, celebrity culture, and the unstable boundary between elite and commercial imagery.

The broader Pears advertising archive later included racist and imperial “civilizing mission” advertisements. No evidence located shows that Millais designed those later advertisements or controlled the company’s campaigns. His documented connection is the commercial reuse of *A Child’s World*. It is therefore inaccurate to attribute Pears’s entire imperial advertising program personally to him.

---

## 1889–1896: Late landscapes, institutional authority, illness, and death

Millais participated in the institutional life surrounding the National Portrait Gallery and served ex officio as a trustee when President of the Royal Academy.

By the early 1890s his health declined. After influenza, he developed a swelling in his throat. The condition proved cancerous. Contemporary accounts describe increasing hoarseness, respiratory difficulty, depression, and periods when he was nevertheless able to work.

Late works included:

- *St Stephen*;
- *A Disciple*;
- *Speak! Speak!*;
- *The Forerunner*;
- portraits of John Hare and Sir Richard Quain;
- atmospheric Scottish landscapes.

*Speak! Speak!* was acquired under the Chantrey Bequest.

When Royal Academy president Frederic Leighton became ill, Millais presided at the Academy banquet in 1895. Following Leighton’s death on 25 January 1896, Millais was unanimously elected President of the Royal Academy in February.

By May 1896 his breathing had deteriorated sufficiently to require a tracheotomy. The operation relieved obstruction but did not cure the cancer.

Millais died at Palace Gate on 13 August 1896. He was sixty-seven. He had held the Academy presidency for only a few months.

He was buried in the crypt of St Paul’s Cathedral. The Prince of Wales chaired a memorial committee, and a public statue was commissioned.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Royal Academy

Millais’s longest institutional affiliation:

- student, 1840;
- exhibitor from 1846;
- Associate, 1853;
- Royal Academician, 1863;
- President, 1896.

His trajectory from youthful dissident to president was central to both his success and accusations that he abandoned the Brotherhood’s ideals.

### Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood

The Brotherhood was an informal artistic and intellectual association, not an incorporated business. It published *The Germ* but did not develop a durable joint financial structure.

### Publishers and engravers

Millais worked within Victorian reproductive publishing networks involving:

- Smith, Elder & Co.;
- Chapman & Hall;
- *The Cornhill Magazine*;
- *Once a Week*;
- *Good Words*;
- the *Illustrated London News*;
- Edward Moxon;
- the Dalziel Brothers;
- Joseph Swain;
- print dealers and publishers including Agnew’s.

He often sold paintings and copyrights separately. Reproductive rights could greatly widen an image’s audience but could also deprive the artist of later control, as the *Bubbles* dispute demonstrated.

### A. & F. Pears

Millais did not own, manage, or invest in Pears. His financial relationship concerned the sale and reproduction of *A Child’s World*. The exact total personally received by Millais from the chain of painting and copyright transactions is inconsistently described in popular accounts. Claims that Pears spent £2,200 to acquire the painting and tens of thousands on reproduction concern the advertiser’s expenditures and should not automatically be treated as Millais’s income.

### Wealth and income

By the later nineteenth century Millais was among Britain’s best-paid painters. One frequently repeated estimate places his peak annual income around £30,000. This is a **reported estimate**, not an audited annual figure. His wealth came primarily from painting sales, portrait commissions, illustration, and reproduction rights.

Material evidence of prosperity includes:

- a large family household;
- 2 Palace Gate and its purpose-built studio;
- Scottish leases;
- servants, travel, fishing and shooting;
- the ability to support an extensive professional and social life.

No evidence was located of bankruptcy, insolvency, fraudulent companies, secret offshore structures, bribery, money laundering, patent holdings, or speculative financial schemes.

### Technologies and production

Millais was not an inventor or patent holder. He worked extensively with the technologies and markets of:

- wood engraving;
- steel and reproductive engraving;
- periodical illustration;
- chromolithographic and mass color reproduction;
- photography as a source or competitor in portrait culture;
- modern advertising.

His career illustrates the transformation of unique paintings into mass-market images.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Politics

Millais did not develop a sustained public career as a party politician. His portraits brought him into contact with politicians across party lines, including Gladstone, Disraeli, Salisbury, Rosebery, and John Bright.

Gladstone’s role in recommending the baronetcy was a political connection and patronage relationship, but it does not establish that Millais was a Liberal activist. His public identity was principally professional and institutional.

Some paintings participated in contemporary imperial and national narratives. *The North-West Passage* presents patriotic frustration at Britain’s failure to complete the passage; *The Boyhood of Raleigh* contributed to a heroic culture surrounding exploration and empire. These are documented ideological functions of the images, but they do not by themselves establish a systematic political program personally articulated by Millais.

### Religion

The early Pre-Raphaelite work used Christian typology intensely. *Christ in the House of His Parents*, *The Return of the Dove to the Ark*, and later biblical subjects demonstrate sustained engagement with scripture.

The Brotherhood contained differing religious commitments, and its medieval and Christian interests cannot be reduced to one ecclesiastical position. Millais did not become chiefly known as a theologian, religious controversialist, or sectarian organizer.

### Artistic development

A useful periodization is:

1. **Academic formation, 1840–48:** polished history painting within Royal Academy conventions.
2. **Pre-Raphaelite phase, 1848–54:** luminous color, detailed naturalism, literary and religious symbolism, often painted from direct observation.
3. **Transition, 1855–60:** broader handling, atmospheric subjects, psychological narrative.
4. **Illustration and mature genre painting, 1860s:** rapid, expressive black-and-white design and accessible narrative.
5. **Portrait and “grand manner” phase, 1870s–90s:** high-status portraiture, broad handling, references to British eighteenth-century painting and continental realism.
6. **Late landscape:** large, often unpeopled Scottish scenes with subdued mood and increasingly free paint handling.

The old account described the later work as simple decline or commercial betrayal. Later scholarship has argued that the changes reflected deliberate experimentation with Aestheticism, portrait tradition, modern realism, reproduction, and painterly surface.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### William Holman Hunt

Close friend, fellow Royal Academy student, and co-founder of the Brotherhood. Hunt and Millais shared early technical and reformist ambitions. Their careers later diverged: Hunt retained a more explicitly religious Pre-Raphaelite identity, while Millais moved toward portraiture and the Academy establishment.

### Dante Gabriel Rossetti

Co-founder and intellectual catalyst. Rossetti supplied poetry, medievalism, and an anti-academic imagination. Millais supplied exceptional formal training and technique. Their association weakened as the original Brotherhood dissolved.

### John Ruskin

Initially defender, patron, friend, and sitter. Ruskin’s public support was crucial during the 1851 critical crisis. The relationship fractured through Millais’s love for Effie and subsequent marriage to her, as well as Ruskin’s objections to Millais’s stylistic development.

### Effie Gray Millais

Spouse, model, household organizer, intermediary with patrons, and manager of his public career. Reducing her to a romantic prize in a “triangle” obscures her active professional role.

### Elizabeth Siddal

Model for *Ophelia*. Their relationship was professional; no credible evidence suggests a romance between them. The bathtub illness became a durable anecdote in Pre-Raphaelite lore.

### Anthony Trollope

Authorial collaborator. Millais’s illustrations became an important visual interpretation of Trollope’s fictional society. Trollope explicitly praised his fidelity to character and text.

### Dalziel Brothers and Joseph Swain

Engravers who converted Millais’s designs into printable wood engravings. Their technical labor was essential to the mass circulation of his illustrations.

### John Leech, Wilkie Collins, William Allingham, Thomas Combe, Coventry Patmore, George du Maurier, and Henry Acland

Members of overlapping artistic, literary, patronage, and friendship networks. Combe and Patmore were especially important in early Pre-Raphaelite support; Acland participated in the Scottish circle and later received the Ruskin portrait.

### Patrons and dealers

Millais’s patrons ranged from private collectors and northern industrial wealth to aristocrats and public institutions. Dealers and reproduction publishers transformed his pictures into widely circulated commodities.

## Successes and Failures

### Significant successes

- Admission to the Royal Academy Schools at eleven.
- Society of Arts silver medal and Royal Academy prizes.
- Co-founding the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood.
- Creation of *Ophelia*, *Isabella*, *The Blind Girl*, *Autumn Leaves*, and other canonical works.
- Popular breakthrough with *A Huguenot* and *The Order of Release*.
- Highly successful illustration career.
- Election as ARA and RA.
- Financial success as a portrait painter.
- Baronetcy, honorary degrees, and foreign academy honors.
- Election as President of the Royal Academy.
- Durable influence on painting, illustration, photography, cinema, fashion, and advertising.

### Failures or reversals

- Commercial failure of *The Germ* after four issues.
- Severe critical hostility to the first public Pre-Raphaelite works.
- Breakdown of the Brotherhood as an active association.
- Collapse of the intimate friendship with Ruskin.
- Long-standing criticism of his later art as sentimental and commercial.
- Loss of control over the reproduction and advertising context of *Bubbles*.
- Destruction of Newmill by fire.
- Death of his son George.
- Progressive throat cancer shortly before achieving the Academy presidency.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### The Ruskin–Effie–Millais relationship

**Documented facts:**

- Ruskin and Effie’s marriage was not consummated.
- Effie initiated annulment proceedings.
- Medical and ecclesiastical evidence supported annulment.
- Millais and Effie became emotionally attached during the 1853 Scottish visit.
- They married in July 1855.

**Unresolved question:** Whether Millais and Effie engaged in adultery before the annulment. Surviving evidence does not establish it.

**Later distortion:** Many dramatizations condense the events into a secret sexual affair followed by divorce. The legal event was an annulment, and the sexual claim remains unproven.

### Explanation for Ruskin’s non-consummation

Effie wrote that Ruskin said he had imagined women differently and was “disgusted” by her person. Ruskin offered his own more guarded explanation, referring to circumstances in her person that checked passion.

Proposed causes have included:

- pubic hair;
- menstruation;
- body odor;
- psychological inhibition;
- reluctance to have children;
- incompatibility;
- a medical or sexual dysfunction.

None is conclusively established. The pubic-hair explanation was prominently advanced by biographer Mary Lutyens in the twentieth century, not documented in those explicit terms by the participants.

### Was Millais a “sellout”?

This is a critical judgment, not a factual charge.

Evidence cited by critics:

- abandonment of minute early technique;
- increasing production of profitable portraits;
- sentimental child pictures;
- Royal Academy advancement;
- aristocratic honors;
- the commercial use of *Bubbles*.

Counterevidence and competing interpretation:

- the transition began before his highest commercial success;
- he continued formal experimentation;
- illustration represented serious interpretive work;
- late portraits and landscapes have been reassessed as innovative;
- sale of copyright limited his control over commercial reproduction.

### *Bubbles* and commercial exploitation

**Documented fact:** Pears used the picture in advertising.

**Disputed:** Whether Millais resisted, acquiesced, or welcomed the final use. The record supports initial concern followed by acceptance more strongly than the simple legend that the advertisement was imposed entirely against his will.

**No evidence found:** fraud, breach-of-contract litigation, or criminal misconduct by Millais.

### Class, race, and religious imagery

Dickens’s 1850 attack on *Christ in the House of His Parents* included contemptuous descriptions of the figures’ bodies and social appearance. Modern analysis often identifies class prejudice and racialized assumptions in this response.

Later Pears advertisements used racial hierarchies and imperial “cleanliness” symbolism. Millais’s image entered Pears’s larger commercial system, but attributing unrelated racist advertisements to him personally would be unsupported.

### Imperial subjects

*The Boyhood of Raleigh* and *The North-West Passage* have been reinterpreted within histories of British imperial culture. These readings are grounded in subject matter and reception. No evidence located shows Millais participating in colonial administration, political violence, military operations, or imperial lobbying.

### Criminal, legal, or intelligence allegations

The adversarial search located no credible evidence that Millais was:

- arrested or imprisoned;
- criminally charged or convicted;
- investigated for fraud or corruption;
- involved in money laundering;
- an intelligence operative;
- implicated in political violence;
- accused in a substantiated sexual-abuse proceeding;
- party to a major lawsuit arising from his professional conduct.

The principal legal episode surrounding his life was the annulment of Effie and Ruskin’s marriage, to which Millais was personally relevant but not the respondent.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### “Ruskin was horrified because Effie had pubic hair”

- **Story:** Ruskin supposedly knew only idealized hairless classical nudes and was shocked by Effie’s pubic hair on their wedding night.
- **Appearance and circulation:** Most influentially associated with Mary Lutyens’s twentieth-century biographies; repeated in journalism, popular histories, stage works, and films.
- **Evidence for:** Effie’s contemporary statement that Ruskin was “disgusted” by her person and had imagined women differently.
- **Evidence against:** Neither Effie nor Ruskin explicitly identified pubic hair in the known contemporary statements. Menstruation, odor, psychological conflict, reproductive anxiety, and other explanations remain possible.
- **Label:** **Rumor or myth built on a documented but unspecified marital statement.**
- **Reputational effect:** It has made the Ruskin–Effie–Millais triangle one of the most familiar stories of Victorian sexual culture, often overshadowing Effie’s management of Millais’s career and Millais’s art.

### “Millais stole his patron’s wife”

- **Story:** Millais betrayed Ruskin, seduced Effie during the Scottish holiday, and took her from him.
- **Evidence:** Millais was Ruskin’s friend and protégé; he and Effie fell in love while she was married.
- **Qualification:** Effie independently pursued annulment of a six-year unconsummated marriage. Premarital intercourse between her and Millais has not been established.
- **Label:** **Polemic based on documented emotional attachment but often extending beyond the evidence.**
- **Effect:** It produced the durable “love triangle” narrative central to later fiction and film.

### “Elizabeth Siddal nearly died because Millais forgot her in a freezing bath”

- **Story:** Millais became so absorbed in painting *Ophelia* that he let the bath go cold, causing Siddal to contract pneumonia and nearly die.
- **Evidence:** The heating lamps failed; Siddal became ill; her father pursued Millais for medical costs.
- **Uncertain elements:** Deliberate neglect, “near death,” and a precise diagnosis of pneumonia are embellishments not uniformly supported.
- **Label:** **Documented accident with later dramatic amplification.**

### “The PRB was a coherent secret conspiracy against the Academy”

- **Story:** Seven revolutionaries formed a clandestine anti-Academy conspiracy with a fixed doctrine.
- **Evidence:** The initials were initially secret, meetings occurred, and members deliberately challenged prevailing art.
- **Against:** They differed in religion, politics, temperament, and artistic method; Millais remained deeply shaped by Academy training and soon advanced within it.
- **Label:** **Partly documented group secrecy, subsequently simplified into movement mythology.**

### “Millais simply abandoned Pre-Raphaelitism for money”

- **Story:** Marriage and a growing household forced him to discard serious art for lucrative sentimental pictures and portraits.
- **Evidence:** His family expenses grew; his later subjects were commercially successful; contemporaries attacked his changed style.
- **Against:** Technical transition was gradual; later work engaged Aestheticism, portrait history, continental realism, modern reproduction, and ambitious landscape.
- **Label:** **Hostile critical narrative, not a provable single motive.**

### “Millais painted a Pears soap advertisement”

- **Story:** Millais directly painted *Bubbles* as a commercial commission.
- **Evidence against:** The original painting was *A Child’s World*; Pears subsequently acquired reproduction rights and added branding.
- **Label:** **False simplification.**
- **Effect:** It became the primary symbol used to criticize his commercialism.

### Child-prodigy hagiography

- **Story:** Millais could draw with adult mastery almost from infancy and swept every available Academy prize.
- **Evidence:** His exceptionally early admission, juvenile works, medal record, and teenage Academy exhibits substantiate extraordinary ability.
- **Qualification:** Specific domestic anecdotes come largely through family remembrance.
- **Label:** **Substantially documented achievement surrounded by filial hagiographic detail.**

## Death and Funeral

By 1894–95 Millais’s throat disease was publicly evident through hoarseness. His respiratory condition worsened in spring 1896. A tracheotomy was performed in May.

He died on 13 August 1896 at Palace Gate. Contemporary obituary reporting identified a prolonged throat disorder; subsequent biographies specify cancer.

His burial in St Paul’s Cathedral placed him among nationally commemorated British artists. The funeral and memorialization emphasized his presidency, baronetcy, technical achievement, and public popularity.

The presidency passed after a tenure too short for him to implement a substantial administrative program.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Immediate commemoration

The Royal Academy devoted its 1898 winter exhibition to his work. His son’s two-volume biography appeared in 1899, establishing the basic documentary narrative for subsequent scholarship.

A memorial statue by Thomas Brock was installed near the National Gallery and later moved to the grounds of Tate Britain. Its movements mirror the institutional repositioning of Millais from national academic master to a central figure in histories of British modernity.

### Early twentieth-century decline

Changing taste damaged his reputation. Modernist criticism tended to value the early Pre-Raphaelite works while dismissing the later portraits, child pictures, and narratives as commercial Victorian sentimentality. *Bubbles* became shorthand for this alleged decline.

### Later reassessment

From the later twentieth century onward, scholars and museums reassessed:

- his illustration as a major independent achievement;
- Effie’s role in the business and social organization of his career;
- his later portraiture in relation to Velázquez, British eighteenth-century art, Manet, Whistler, Carolus-Duran, and Sargent;
- his late Scottish landscapes as formally ambitious and psychologically charged;
- his work’s relation to photography, mass reproduction, advertising, and commodity culture;
- the gendered labor of his models and family.

The 1967 Royal Academy/Walker exhibition, the 1999 portrait exhibition, and the 2007–08 Tate-led retrospective were major stages in this recovery.

### Continuing cultural afterlife

*Ophelia* has become one of the most reproduced paintings in Western visual culture. It has influenced:

- fashion photography;
- cinema;
- music-video imagery;
- theatrical staging;
- feminist reinterpretations of Shakespeare’s Ophelia;
- environmental and botanical discussion;
- contemporary photographic restagings.

Its fame has also intensified scrutiny of Elizabeth Siddal’s physical labor and illness as a model.

*Bubbles* remains important to histories of advertising and intellectual property. The picture’s afterlife exceeds its original subject and has helped define the modern branded image.

The Ruskin–Effie–Millais story remains the subject of recurrent biography and drama. Popular treatments frequently center male rivalry; recent scholarship gives greater attention to Effie’s decisions, correspondence, family strategy, and professional management.
