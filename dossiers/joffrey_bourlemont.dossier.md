# Joffrey de Bourlémont: Research Dossier

## Scope, identity, and evidentiary method

This dossier concerns the thirteenth-century Lorraine nobleman most commonly identified in modern French scholarship as **Geoffroy, Joffroy, Joffroi, or Joffre de Bourlémont**, lord of Bourlémont and seneschal or governor of the Kingdom of Navarre in 1255–1257.

The spelling **“Joffrey de Bourlémont”** is a modernized variant rather than the form most often found in surviving records. The 2024 documentary study by María Raquel García Arancón notes that Navarrese documents call him **Joffre**, while French records generally use **Geoffroy** or **Joffroy**. Latinized or scribal variants include **Goifridus de Bolleinmont**, and medieval vernacular forms include **Jofroi de Borlainmont**.

This is an unusually fragmentary medieval biography. No narrative life, personal archive, portrait securely made from life, birth record, educational record, or medical evidence survives. Reconstruction depends primarily on:

- feudal acknowledgments and homage rolls;
- charters recording gifts, purchases, debts, and guarantees;
- Navarrese royal and administrative documents;
- records of litigation and arbitration;
- Jean de Joinville’s retrospective testimony;
- archival inventories from Lorraine;
- architectural and local historical studies;
- later genealogical and legendary traditions.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** directly supported by a contemporary act, administrative entry, seal, or contemporary/near-contemporary testimony.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a modern specialist who cites documentary evidence not independently reproduced in full here.
- **Scholarly inference:** a reasoned reconstruction from gaps or circumstantial evidence.
- **Disputed claim:** a claim for which sources conflict or whose interpretation is contested.
- **Rumor or myth:** a late, legendary, genealogical, or promotional story without adequate contemporary support.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal line

**Reported fact:** Geoffroy’s father was **Pierre II de Bourlémont**, who died around 1227. The family descended from the lords of **Brixey**, associated with Brixey-aux-Chanoines. A member of that family had been entrusted by the bishops of Toul with the fortress of Bourlémont, and the branch established there adopted the territorial name Bourlémont by the late twelfth century.

The family’s earlier reconstruction is not completely uniform:

- Modern specialist accounts identify Pierre II’s father as **Simon de Brixey**, the first securely recognizable lord to use the Bourlémont territorial designation.
- Simon’s wife is variously identified in modern genealogies as **Agnès de Broyes**, **Agnès de Commercy**, or by similarly reconstructed territorial names.
- Because these details depend on later genealogical synthesis rather than a continuous series of contemporary filiations, the precise identity of Geoffroy’s paternal grandmother should be treated as **disputed or incompletely established**.

The official château history reports that the original fortress was built by the bishops of Toul in the early twelfth century and entrusted to the Brixey family. The present site’s major round towers are associated with Geoffroy’s thirteenth-century rebuilding program: [Château de Bourlémont history](https://www.chateaudebourlemont.com/decouvrir-le-chateau/).

### Maternal line and the Joinville connection

**Documented/reported fact:** Geoffroy’s mother was **Félicité de Joinville**, approximately 1185–1240, daughter of:

- **Geoffroy IV de Joinville**, seneschal of Champagne; and
- **Helvide de Dampierre**.

Félicité married Pierre II around 1200. After Pierre’s death in approximately 1227, she exercised control over Bourlémont and rendered homage for the lordship and castle.

Through Félicité, Geoffroy was a first cousin of **Jean de Joinville**, later the celebrated seneschal of Champagne and biographer of Louis IX. Their relationship was both familial and practical: Jean remembered advice Geoffroy gave him before the Seventh Crusade, and after Geoffroy’s death Jean assisted or witnessed acts involving Geoffroy’s widow and heirs.

García Arancón also traces a kinship through Helvide de Dampierre to the extended family of **Margaret of Provence**, queen of Louis IX. This helped place Geoffroy within the broader aristocratic network surrounding Louis IX, the counts of Champagne, and the Navarrese crown.

### Siblings

The best-attested sibling in the genealogical literature is a sister:

- **Béatrice or Élisabeth de Bourlémont**, reportedly married to **Hugues IV de La Fauche**.

The name varies across secondary genealogies, and some databases split or conflate women attached to the La Fauche marriage. The relationship between the Bourlémont and La Fauche houses is independently credible because the lord of La Fauche held property or rights under Geoffroy and is described in specialist work as his relative and vassal.

### Marriage

Geoffroy married **Sibille or Sibylle de Saulxures**, lady of Saulxures-les-Vannes.

Her approximate birth around 1215 is a reconstruction. Her death in 1275 is better supported; García Arancón reports that she selected burial with the Franciscans of Neufchâteau. One genealogical source gives 11 April 1275, while some local summaries give March 1275. The exact day should therefore be regarded as **probable but not conclusively established here**.

Their marriage brought Geoffroy into the property and kinship network of Saulxures-les-Vannes. In 1256 Sibille formally approved before the bishop of Toul the pledge of movables made by her husband as security for his large royal loan.

### Children

García Arancón, following Poull’s charter catalogue, identifies four sons:

1. **Geoffroy de Bourlémont**, lord of Rorthey, who died around 1269.
2. **Pierre III de Bourlémont**, also called **Perrin**, who inherited the lordship and died in 1309 or 1310.
3. **Jean de Bourlémont**, chantre and archdeacon of Toul.
4. **Thomas de Bourlémont**, canon and *primicier* of Verdun.

The *primicier* was a cathedral dignitary below the archdeacon, associated with oversight and instruction of deacons.

Some online genealogies list only Pierre, or Pierre and Geoffroy. These abbreviated trees are incomplete rather than evidence against the four-son reconstruction.

Later members of the family included another Thomas de Bourlémont, bishop of Toul from 1333 to 1350. Modern summaries sometimes create confusion between this later bishop and Geoffroy’s son Thomas. The 2024 study describes the bishop as a **grandson or later descendant**, not the same man as Geoffroy’s son.

## Childhood and Early Life

No direct account of Geoffroy’s childhood survives.

He was probably born around 1205 into a family occupying a politically complex frontier between the Kingdom of France and the Holy Roman Empire. Bourlémont lay in the upper Meuse region, in what is now the Vosges department. Authority there was divided among or layered beneath:

- the bishop of Toul;
- the count of Champagne, who also became king of Navarre;
- the duke of Lorraine;
- the count of Bar;
- numerous lesser lords holding fragmented jurisdictions and sub-fiefs.

This was not a single, territorially exclusive system of allegiance. Geoffroy could be a vassal of several lords for different properties, sometimes ranking those allegiances by ligesse.

His father died when Geoffroy was perhaps in his early twenties. Félicité thereafter appears in the Champagne feudal record as rendering homage for Bourlémont, suggesting that Geoffroy’s transition to full independent control of the family lordship was gradual.

No evidence has been located concerning:

- literacy or languages;
- schooling;
- fosterage;
- formal knightly training;
- the date or circumstances of his knighting;
- specific childhood residences;
- relationships with siblings during youth.

His later ability to function as a high officer in Navarre implies aristocratic administrative and military formation, but the form and location of that training remain unknown.

## Education and Formation

No school, tutor, chaplain, or formal mentor is documented.

His probable formative influences were institutional rather than scholastic:

- the episcopal lordship of Toul;
- the Champagne court;
- the Joinville family’s tradition of holding the seneschalship of Champagne;
- frontier aristocratic practice in Lorraine;
- feudal litigation, arbitration, and property administration;
- crusading culture.

The likely importance of the Joinville connection is visible in Geoffroy’s later advice to Jean de Joinville and in Jean’s participation in the affairs of Geoffroy’s heirs.

During Geoffroy’s Navarrese governorship, **Creste de Sézanne**, a royal chamberlain and financial officer familiar with Navarre, may have instructed him in local laws, language, customs, and administrative practice. García Arancón presents this as a strong historical inference rather than a formally recorded teacher-student relationship.

## Early Career and Lordship

### Accession and feudal position

By 1237 Geoffroy was acting as lord of Bourlémont. A local study records that in that year he gave Hugues, lord of Fische, the fief of Pagney-derrière-Barine: [study of the seigneurie and Bruley](https://www.etudes-touloises.fr/archives/30/art2.pdf).

In June 1239 he formally recognized the bishop of Toul as his liege lord for lands and obligations inherited from his father and ancestors. An archival inventory describes his duty of residence or castle service at Brixey and confirms the long-standing episcopal relationship: [Meurthe-et-Moselle archival inventory](https://archives.meurthe-et-moselle.fr/sites/default/files/Contenu/Guide/Inventaire_3_F.pdf).

He was simultaneously connected through homage or tenure to:

- the bishop of Toul;
- the count of Champagne;
- the duke of Lorraine;
- the count of Bar.

Under the count of Champagne he held, among other rights:

- Bourlémont and its ban or seigneurial jurisdiction;
- Frebécourt;
- Rorthey;
- Noncourt near Neufchâteau;
- superior rights over what the lord of La Fauche held at Orquevaux.

Under the count of Bar, he held the castle and lordship of Domrémy.

### Crusading vow and the Barons’ Crusade

**Documented fact:** In April 1238 Geoffroy had “taken the cross.” The information survives through episcopal approval of one of his donations to Mureau.

**Unresolved question:** He may have joined the expedition led by Theobald I of Navarre and Champagne to Palestine in 1239–1240. The argument in favor is:

- his known crusading vow;
- the absence of preserved acts by him between June 1239 and May 1242;
- his political association with Theobald.

The evidence against treating participation as established is substantial:

- he is absent from the two narrative chronicles listing participants;
- he is not included in later specialist lists of the expedition’s knights;
- no surviving charter places him in the Levant.

Accordingly, his participation in the Barons’ Crusade is **possible but unproved**.

### Relations with Lorraine

In 1249 Geoffroy guaranteed or supported Duke Matthias II of Lorraine in arrangements for the marriage of the duke’s son Frederick to Margaret, daughter of Theobald I.

In 1252 he again served as a guarantor, this time for Catherine, dowager duchess of Lorraine, in an agreement involving the counts of Bar and Luxembourg.

These appearances show that he was more than a purely local castle lord. He operated within the diplomatic network of Lorraine, Bar, Champagne, and Luxembourg.

### Financial pressures and castle reconstruction

In 1250 Theobald I, count of Champagne and king of Navarre, advanced Geoffroy **1,000 livres of Provins** for work on the fortifications of Bourlémont.

The modern architectural study cited by García Arancón associates the major thirteenth-century reconstruction with Geoffroy. The château’s official history similarly attributes the large round towers to him.

The surviving evidence supports:

- a major expenditure on the castle;
- external financing from his overlord;
- Geoffroy’s direct responsibility for the works.

It does not establish every architectural feature attributed to him in later tourist descriptions.

## Chronological Life History

### 1200s–1220s: birth and inheritance environment

- Born around 1205, probably within the Bourlémont/Brixey family’s Lorraine lands.
- Father: Pierre II de Bourlémont.
- Mother: Félicité de Joinville.
- Pierre died around 1227.
- Félicité thereafter appears as the person rendering homage for Bourlémont.

### 1237–1238: independent lord and crusading commitment

- By 1237 Geoffroy was issuing or confirming feudal grants.
- In April 1238 he took the cross.
- He donated property at Pargny to the Premonstratensian abbey of Mureau, with episcopal approval.

### 1239–1242: uncertain eastern expedition

- June 1239: recognized episcopal overlordship and obligations to the bishop of Toul.
- No known acts survive between June 1239 and May 1242.
- Possible participation in Theobald I’s Palestinian expedition remains unconfirmed.

### 1246–1248: mortgages and transfers of revenue

In 1246 Geoffroy pledged or mortgaged revenues from Domrémy to Mureau for **50 livres of Provins**.

In 1248 the bishop of Toul and count of Bar approved transfers of other revenues by Geoffroy. The records show active use of rents as security and indicate persistent need for liquid funds.

### 1249–1252: Lorraine diplomacy

- 1249: guarantor in a Lorraine dynastic marriage arrangement.
- 1250: received 1,000 livres from Theobald I for the Bourlémont fortifications.
- February 1251: gave Mureau a yearly cartload of wine from Charmes-la-Côte.
- 1252: guarantor for Catherine, dowager duchess of Lorraine.

### 1255: transition to Navarrese government

On 23 July 1255 Geoffroy and Pierre de Villouxel renewed feudal recognition concerning the house of Montbras under the bishop of Toul.

In November 1255 Theobald II returned to Navarre from France. Louis IX had supported Theobald’s marriage to his daughter Isabella and his effort to strengthen royal control in Navarre.

At Estella in early December, Geoffroy sat in the royal council and witnessed the individual homages of Navarrese magnates.

On **19 December 1255**, Theobald appointed him seneschal or governor for two years, effective from Christmas Day.

Terms included:

- annual salary of **300 livres tournois**, plus expenses;
- a royal loan of **2,000 livres tournois**;
- 500 livres assigned in Navarre;
- repayment through deduction of two years’ salary and an annual charge of 100 livres on Bourlémont revenues.

The scale of the loan confirms that Geoffroy entered office with significant financial needs, likely connected to castle expenditure and lordship management.

### 1256: first year as seneschal

On **10 February 1256**, at Larrasoaña after Mass, Bishop Pedro Jiménez de Gazólaz asked Theobald II whether he would observe the Estella settlement between crown and church. After the king agreed, Geoffroy was asked to swear that he would observe and enforce it among barons, merinos, and other bailiffs. Geoffroy first asked whether the king commanded the oath and then swore with his hands on the book.

This scene is preserved in ecclesiastical history based on cathedral records: [“Los obispos de Pamplona del siglo XIII”](https://www.culturanavarra.es/uploads/files/pv118133/RPVIANAnro-0066-pagina0041.pdf).

On **22 February**, at Tiebas, Geoffroy and the bishop participated in the designation of authorities for the borough of San Cernin in Pamplona. The assembly included urban representatives, knights, and clerics.

On **17 March**, at Tudela, he witnessed or supervised the appointment of representatives in litigation between the Order of Saint John and the council of Sangüesa.

On **30 May**, at a general court at Tafalla, residents of San Cernin complained about structures raised by residents of San Nicolás. Geoffroy ordered construction halted while deferring a final decision until the king could respond.

This illustrates a recurrent feature of his administration: interim restraint, consultation, and procedural delay where jurisdiction was disputed.

Sometime late in 1256 he may have made a short journey back to France. A charter records him approving a donation by the lords of Pargny to Mureau during a gap in the Navarrese documentation. The trip is **probable, not certain**.

In the same year Sibille approved the pledge of the couple’s movables securing Geoffroy’s royal loan.

### 1257: disputes, pasture enforcement, and treaty with Aragón

On **18 January 1257**, at Olite, Geoffroy ordered the merino of Tudela to conduct a second division of disputed woodland between Villafranca and Cadreita.

He also acted in favor of the hospital of Roncesvalles against inhabitants of Eugui who repeatedly occupied the hospital’s mountain pastures and allegedly mistreated royal enforcement agents. The surviving text is damaged and undated, but its internal chronology places the relevant interventions in approximately 1256–1257.

On **13 November 1257**, James I of Aragón and Geoffroy, acting for Navarre, concluded a two-year truce valid until Saint Martin’s Day 1259. The wider political background was contentious: Navarrese magnates accused Theobald II of breaking earlier oaths and alliances with Aragón, while the king pursued accommodation with Castile. The evidence concerns accusations against the king’s policy, not a personal accusation of fraud or treason against Geoffroy.

His contract expired on **25 December 1257**.

### 1258: departure from office and return to France

Geoffroy did not receive a renewal. Clement de Launay is first documented as seneschal on 1 May 1258.

Possible explanations include:

- Geoffroy’s wish to return to his family and properties;
- the difficulty of the post;
- dissatisfaction by Theobald with his cautious, consultative style;
- the availability of Launay, who already possessed relevant experience.

No document states formally that Geoffroy was dismissed for misconduct. The theory that the king found him too conciliatory is a **modern scholarly inference**.

By late 1258 Geoffroy was again documented in France.

He and Anseau de Traînel were appointed arbitrators or trusted intermediaries in financial disputes involving the counts of Champagne, Burgundy, and Bar. A July 1259 charter identifies Geoffroy as one of the persons to whom a guaranteed sum of 500 marks could be entrusted: [TELMA charter 118088](https://telma-chartes.irht.cnrs.fr/en/118088).

### 1259: the Tiebas identification error

Some modern castle lists identify Geoffroy as castellan of Tiebas in 1259.

García Arancón’s re-examination of the Navarrese accounts concludes that this is a **misreading**. The castellan was the treasurer Creste de Sézanne. Geoffroy should not be included among the documented castellans of Tiebas for that year.

### 1261–1265: Gerbonvaux and Bermont

In April 1261 Geoffroy and Sibille purchased the house or estate of **Gilbonval/Gerbonvaux** from the Premonstratensian abbey of Saint-Nicolas de Septfontaines for **70 livres**.

They transformed or developed it into a **Maison-Dieu**, hospital, and charitable religious foundation serving:

- a master;
- religious brothers;
- sick persons;
- poor travelers;
- later, prebendaries.

In 1263 Geoffroy acquired the chapel or hermitage of **Bermont** from the Benedictine abbey of Bourgueil. Bermont was geographically distant from Bourgueil and expensive for the abbey to maintain. The chapel was dedicated to the Virgin and associated from the eleventh century with Saint Theobald of Provins.

In 1263 or 1264 Geoffroy borrowed **300 livres of Provins** from the provost of Chaumont-en-Bassigny, with the king acting as guarantor. The loan was repayable over three years. García Arancón associates this new debt with the Gerbonvaux foundation and its endowment.

In 1265 Geoffroy and Sibille endowed the hospital with rents and property at:

- Martigny-les-Gerbonvaux;
- Charmes-la-Côte;
- Saulxures-les-Vannes.

The foundation was subsequently known as the hospital of Saint-Éloi of Gerbonvaux.

In September and December 1265 Geoffroy rendered liege homage to Duke Frederick III of Lorraine. In September Theobald II authorized Geoffroy’s son Geoffroy to render Lorraine homage for the castle of Rorthey.

Geoffroy also acted in September 1265 as one of three arbitrators in a conflict involving Jean I de Choiseul and the count of Bar.

### 1266: final documented activities

An archival inventory preserves notice of a **1266 charter by Geoffroy and Sibille donating the greater and lesser tithes of Bruley to the church or abbey of Mureau**: [Premonstratensian archive inventory](https://archives.meurthe-et-moselle.fr/sites/default/files/Contenu/Guide/Inventaire_Serie_H_.pdf).

The Navarrese accounts for 1266 contain a payment of 120 livres tournois owed to Geoffroy for maravedís he had advanced “on the journey of Sibilia.” García Arancón argues that “Sibilia” almost certainly refers not to his wife but to **Seville**, probably in connection with a diplomatic mission by Clement de Launay to Alfonso X.

The entry indicates that Geoffroy remained in financial dealings with the Navarrese crown after leaving office.

### 1266–1268: death

Geoffroy died after the last securely dated acts of 1266 and before an act of September 1268. Many summaries give simply **1268**, while older genealogical trees sometimes offer September 1268 as if it were an exact death date. The documentary evidence establishes a range rather than a precise day.

No source located documents:

- illness;
- accident;
- violent death;
- execution;
- imprisonment;
- battlefield death;
- cause of death.

He may have been buried at Gerbonvaux, later used as a family burial place, but this is **probable rather than proved**.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

The modern categories “company,” “investment,” and “technology” are only partly applicable. Geoffroy’s material interests were feudal and seigneurial.

### Bourlémont lordship

The Bourlémont complex included:

- castle and fortifications;
- the village and ban of Bourlémont;
- Frebécourt;
- lands and rights at Greux and Domrémy;
- Rorthey;
- Noncourt;
- portions of Charmes-la-Côte;
- rights over subordinate lords and knights.

His revenues came from land, rents, wine, tithes, mills or productive rights, dependent tenants, and feudal dues.

### Fortification project

**Documented core:** a 1,000-livre loan in 1250 was intended for the castle.

**Architectural attribution:** major round towers and enlargement of the château are attributed to Geoffroy by the château’s institutional history and later architectural studies.

**Unverified embellishment:** some tourist sources claim that he rebuilt the castle specifically “after returning from the Holy Land.” Since his crusading journey is not proved, that causal narrative is likewise unproved.

### Rorthey

Rorthey formed part of the family’s defensive and seigneurial network. Geoffroy’s son Geoffroy became lord there and rendered homage for it to Lorraine in 1265.

Later descriptions claim that the father rebuilt Rorthey into a complete forward defensive work. The general association is plausible, but detailed claims about every wall, tower, gate, and machicolation require archaeological rather than merely promotional support.

### Gerbonvaux hospital

This was Geoffroy and Sibille’s largest institutional project. It required:

- purchase of the core estate for 70 livres;
- acquisition of Bermont;
- transfer of rents and tithes;
- further borrowing;
- coordination with Premonstratensian and Benedictine institutions;
- gifts from dependent knights.

It outlived them and accumulated additional property, protections, and market rights under their descendants.

### Debt structure

Known major obligations or credit relationships included:

- 50 livres of Provins secured against Domrémy revenues in 1246;
- 1,000 livres of Provins from Theobald I in 1250;
- 2,000 livres tournois from Theobald II in 1255;
- 300 livres of Provins from the provost of Chaumont-en-Bassigny in 1263/1264.

These were very large sums for a regional lord. They demonstrate sustained liquidity problems or unusually high capital expenditures, but they do not by themselves prove insolvency.

The available records attribute the borrowing principally to:

- castle reconstruction;
- charitable and religious foundation;
- administration of a dispersed lordship.

No evidence was found of commercial companies, banking partnerships, maritime investment, patents, inventions, or manufacturing enterprises.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Political orientation

Geoffroy’s political position was defined by layered allegiance rather than modern national loyalty.

He maintained obligations to Lorraine, Bar, Toul, and Champagne while accepting the governorship of distant Navarre. His strongest upward political connection became the Champagne-Navarre dynasty.

As seneschal he participated in Theobald II’s effort to:

- replace a pro-Aragonese governing faction;
- strengthen royal authority;
- settle or manage conflict with the bishop of Pamplona;
- align Navarre more closely with Castile and the Capetian sphere;
- maintain negotiated peace with Aragón.

There is no surviving manifesto or political treatise by Geoffroy. His governing outlook must be inferred from acts. Those acts suggest consultation, procedural caution, and preference for interim settlements in difficult cases.

### Religion

Documented religious activities include:

- taking the cross in 1238;
- repeated gifts to the Premonstratensian abbey of Mureau;
- purchase of Bermont;
- foundation and endowment of Gerbonvaux;
- support for Roncesvalles’ pasture rights;
- placement of two sons in cathedral careers.

These actions fit aristocratic religious practice of the period: patronage, commemoration, care for travelers and the sick, cultivation of monastic alliances, and provision for family burial.

No evidence establishes a distinctive theology, heresy, mystical experience, personal devotional writing, or monastic vocation.

### The advice to Jean de Joinville

Jean de Joinville remembered Geoffroy warning him, before Joinville departed on crusade, that a knight who returned while leaving poor Christian companions in Muslim captivity would be dishonored.

The wording survives in Joinville’s later account and was summarized in nineteenth-century scholarship: [“Joinville, saint Louis et le XIIIe siècle”](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Joinville%2C_saint_Louis_et_le_XIIIe_si%C3%A8cle).

This is the closest surviving evidence for Geoffroy’s personal moral language. It shows an aristocratic code that extended obligations of solidarity beyond wealthy knights to ordinary crusaders. Because Joinville wrote decades later, the exact wording is retrospective, though the relationship and substance are credible.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Theobald I of Champagne and Navarre

Patron, overlord, creditor, and possible crusading commander.

- Geoffroy owed feudal service to him as count of Champagne.
- Theobald financed the rebuilding of Bourlémont in 1250.
- Geoffroy may, but is not proved to have, joined his 1239–1240 crusade.

### Theobald II of Navarre

Principal political patron.

- Appointed Geoffroy seneschal in December 1255.
- Lent him 2,000 livres tournois.
- Used him in government, diplomacy, and dispute resolution.
- Did not renew his two-year contract.

### Jean de Joinville

First cousin, friend or trusted kinsman, seneschal of Champagne, and later chronicler.

- Received Geoffroy’s pre-crusade advice.
- Sealed a 1269 act by Sibille and her sons executing Geoffroy’s testamentary donation.
- Attended confirmation of the Gerbonvaux gifts.

### Sibille de Saulxures

Wife, co-landholder, co-founder, and financial participant.

- Approved the pledge securing Geoffroy’s loan.
- Jointly purchased Gerbonvaux.
- Jointly endowed the hospital.
- Survived him until 1275.

### Bishops of Toul

Liege lords and ecclesiastical supervisors.

- Held superior rights over much of the family’s property.
- Approved or witnessed transfers and financial pledges.
- Received Geoffroy’s homage.

### Duke Matthias II and Duke Frederick III of Lorraine

Feudal and political associates.

- Geoffroy acted as guarantor in Lorraine affairs.
- Later rendered liege homage to Frederick III.
- His son rendered homage for Rorthey.

### Counts of Bar

Overlords for certain holdings, particularly Domrémy, and participants in regional arbitration and guarantees.

### Bishop Pedro Jiménez de Gazólaz of Pamplona

A principal collaborator during Geoffroy’s governorship.

- Received Geoffroy’s oath to respect the Estella settlement.
- Shared jurisdictional and appointive actions with him.
- The settlement they implemented was later annulled by Pope Alexander IV.

### Clement de Launay

Colleague, witness, and successor.

- Appeared in Geoffroy’s Navarrese act network.
- Succeeded him as seneschal in 1258.
- Later proved a longer-serving and more forceful royal administrator.

### Creste de Sézanne

Royal chamberlain and financial administrator.

- Likely assisted Geoffroy in navigating Navarrese law and administration.
- Was later misidentified in secondary literature through an account-entry error concerning the castle of Tiebas.

### Anseau de Traînel

Co-arbitrator and financial intermediary in disputes involving Burgundy, Bar, and Champagne after Geoffroy returned to France.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

### Results of the dedicated adversarial search

Searches were conducted in English, French, and Spanish combining the subject’s name variants with terms equivalent to controversy, scandal, allegation, lawsuit, fraud, corruption, accusation, conspiracy, and criticism, including searches directed toward material published or indexed in 2024–2026.

No evidence was found that Geoffroy was:

- criminally prosecuted;
- formally accused of fraud;
- convicted of corruption;
- excommunicated personally;
- accused of homicide or political violence;
- imprisoned;
- investigated for money laundering or analogous financial concealment;
- involved in a documented sexual scandal;
- dismissed following a formal finding of misconduct.

For a thirteenth-century lord, absence from surviving evidence is not proof that abuses never occurred. It means no responsibly attributable accusation was located.

### Heavy indebtedness

**Documented fact:** Geoffroy repeatedly borrowed or pledged rents.

**Interpretive question:** Was he financially incompetent or merely undertaking capital-intensive projects?

The evidence supports high expenditure on fortification and Gerbonvaux. It does not establish bankruptcy, deception, or unlawful diversion. The 2024 study describes a deterioration in his finances associated with expensive projects.

### “Donation of a man” to Mureau

The Meurthe-et-Moselle archival inventory summarizes a charter in which Geoffroy declared that he had given the abbey of Mureau **“un homme, à toujours mais,”** effectively transferring a dependent man at Housselmont in perpetuity.

This is **documented seigneurial practice**, not a modern criminal allegation. It is nevertheless important evidence of the coercive social and property order from which his wealth and patronage derived. Medieval dependent persons and their obligations could be treated as transferable components of lordship.

### Pamplona jurisdiction and papal annulment

In 1255 the king and bishop reached a settlement dividing jurisdiction and revenues in Pamplona, including ecclesiastically sensitive revenues. Geoffroy helped implement it in 1256.

Pope Alexander IV annulled the arrangement on **9 September 1257**, on grounds that it violated privileges and improperly converted ecclesiastical revenues into temporal income for a lay king. A published documentary study summarizes the underlying act and papal intervention: [documents on Navarrese political institutions](https://www.boe.es/biblioteca_juridica/anuarios_derecho/abrir_pdf.php?id=ANU-H-1929-10046200522_ANUARIO_DE_HISTORIA_DEL_DERECHO_ESPA%C3%91OL_Documentos_para_el_estudio_de_las_instituciones_pol%C3%ADticas_de_Navarra_durante_las_dinast%C3%ADas_de_Champagne_y_de_Francia).

Evidentiary classification:

- Geoffroy’s role in implementation: **documented fact**.
- Papal annulment: **documented fact**.
- A finding of personal corruption against Geoffroy: **not documented**.

### Navarrese complaints of royal perjury

Navarrese nobles accused Theobald II of perjury for breaking oaths and earlier alliances with Aragón. The king sought papal release from those obligations. Geoffroy was part of the replacement government and negotiated the 1257 truce with James I.

The political accusations are described in [“Relaciones navarro-aragonesas a mediados del siglo XIII”](https://ibdigital.uib.es/greenstone/sites/localsite/collect/mayurqa/index/assoc/Mayurqa_/1989v22n/1p269.dir/Mayurqa_1989v22n1p269.pdf).

Classification:

- accusation against Theobald’s policy: **documented contemporary accusation**;
- Geoffroy’s participation in the new alignment: **documented fact**;
- personal perjury by Geoffroy: **not established**.

### Possible dissatisfaction and non-renewal

García Arancón suggests that Geoffroy’s cautious and consensual administration may have seemed insufficiently decisive to the king. His replacement by Clement de Launay after exactly two contractual years could reflect dissatisfaction.

Classification: **scholarly inference**. There is no surviving dismissal order, formal accusation, or adverse judgment.

### Later litigation over Geoffroy’s donations

Archival collections contain later lawsuits among Mureau, the cathedral chapter of Toul, parish clergy, communities, and Geoffroy’s descendants regarding tithes and property derived partly from his grants.

These proceedings concern later interpretation or fulfillment of rights. They do not establish that Geoffroy fraudulently conveyed property. In some cases later heirs were ordered to honor inherited obligations.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### Myth 1: Geoffroy certainly went on crusade and rebuilt Bourlémont after returning

**Story:** Geoffroy accompanied Theobald I to the Holy Land and reconstructed his château after his return, using architectural ideas acquired overseas.

**Appearance and circulation:** Modern château histories, heritage descriptions, Wikipedia-derived summaries, and tourism pages repeat versions of this story.

**Evidence for:**

- Geoffroy took the cross in April 1238.
- His documentary record has a gap during Theobald’s 1239–1240 expedition.
- He later undertook major castle works.

**Evidence against or limiting it:**

- His name is absent from the expedition chronicles and participant lists.
- No surviving act places him in Palestine.
- The architectural program is financed in 1250, a decade later.
- No evidence establishes that its design derived from Levantine experience.

**Assessment:** **Plausible but unverified crusade participation; legendary or promotional embellishment regarding architectural inspiration.**

**Reputational effect:** The story converts a financially documented building campaign into a romantic crusader narrative and is now one of the most repeated claims about him.

### Myth 2: exact birth in 1210 and exact death in 1268

**Story:** Geoffroy was born in 1210 and died in 1268.

**Circulation:** Genealogical trees and heritage sites.

**Evidence:** These are convenient rounded dates. Modern documentary scholarship favors birth around 1205 and death between 1266 and September 1268.

**Assessment:** **Approximate genealogical convention, not an exact documented pair of dates.**

### Myth 3: seneschal or castellan of Tiebas in 1259

**Story:** Geoffroy was still a Navarrese officeholder or castellan of Tiebas in 1259.

**Origin:** A mistaken reading of a Navarrese financial account, repeated by later castle histories.

**Evidence against:** The account concerns Creste de Sézanne; Clement de Launay had already succeeded Geoffroy as seneschal.

**Assessment:** **Disproved identification error.**

### Myth 4: his governorship began in 1244

**Story:** Geoffroy acted from 1244 as associate or lieutenant of Sancho Fernández de Monteagudo.

**Circulation:** Attributed by García Arancón to an unsupported statement in Béatrice Leroy’s 2013 work.

**Evidence against:** The documentary sequence places his entry into Navarrese royal government in late 1255. García Arancón states that no evidence supports the 1244 claim.

**Assessment:** **Unsupported scholarly claim.**

### Myth 5: Pica de Bourlémont and Saint Francis of Assisi

A much later tradition identifies **Pica**, mother of Francis of Assisi, as a French noblewoman of the Bourlémont family. Online genealogies sometimes make her a daughter of an invented or poorly substantiated Pierre de Bourlémont and thereby a member of Geoffroy’s extended paternal family.

**What the story says:** Pica, sometimes called Joanna or Giovanna Pica de Bourlémont, belonged to the noble Bourlémont house, met or married Pietro di Bernardone through his French commercial activity, and became the mother of Francis.

**When it appears:** The Bourlémont identification is not found in the earliest lives of Francis. A modern Italian review traces it to the seventeenth-century Franciscan scholar **Claude Frassen**, who claimed that a marriage contract was still in the Bourlémont archive in 1703. Nineteenth-century authors repeated the story.

**Who circulated it:** Franciscan writers, genealogists, devotional literature, heritage sites, and user-created family trees.

**Evidence for:**

- Francis’s mother was traditionally called Pica.
- His father had commercial connections with France.
- A late scholarly tradition explicitly called her a Bourlémont.

**Evidence against:**

- no contemporary marriage contract has been produced;
- no securely traced medieval Bourlémont genealogy identifies Pica;
- Bourlémont is in Lorraine, not Provence as the late tradition says;
- the claim emerges centuries after Francis and Geoffroy;
- early Franciscan sources do not supply the Bourlémont lineage.

A useful modern presentation of the controversy is [“The Figure of Monna Pica, Mother of Francis: facts and controversies”](https://www.umbriametu.it/museums/museo-civico-palazzo-dei-consoli-gubbio/saint-francis-and-the-wolf-of-gubbio/the-figure-of-monna-pica-mother-of-francis-facts-and-controversies/?lang=en).

**Assessment:** **Late genealogical legend; no documented family connection to Geoffroy.**

**Reputational effect:** The tradition places the Bourlémont name inside Franciscan sacred history, but it contributes no reliable evidence to Geoffroy’s biography.

### Myth 6: association with Joan of Arc

Geoffroy died roughly a century and a half before Joan of Arc was born. Nevertheless, his name frequently appears in Joan-related heritage literature because:

- his descendants continued to hold rights around Domrémy;
- he acquired Bermont, where Joan later prayed according to local tradition;
- Bourlémont controlled significant land in the area;
- the château’s history is marketed as part of the “country of Joan of Arc.”

There is no personal connection between Geoffroy and Joan. Any wording implying that he protected, patronized, or influenced her is chronologically impossible.

A separate later legend concerns a **“fairy” lady of Bourlémont**, associated with the fairy tree near Domrémy. That woman belongs to a later generation, generally tied to a Pierre de Bourlémont and his wife Béatrice. She is not Geoffroy’s wife Sibille.

## Works by the Person

Geoffroy left no known literary, theological, historical, or technical work. His surviving “works” are juridical and administrative acts, many composed by clerks and authenticated under his authority rather than personally written.

The following categories are attributable to him:

- homage declarations to the bishop of Toul and other lords;
- grants or confirmations to the abbey of Mureau;
- mortgage or pledge of Domrémy revenues;
- guarantees in Lorraine dynastic agreements;
- the 1255 seneschalship contract;
- administrative orders and judgments in Navarre;
- participation in the 1257 truce with Aragón;

- purchase of Gerbonvaux;
- purchase of Bermont;
- foundation and endowment charters for Gerbonvaux;

## Death and Immediate Aftermath

Geoffroy died between 1266 and September 1268.

His widow Sibille and their sons carried out his testamentary intentions. In 1269:

- Sibille, Perrin/Pierre, and Geoffroy made a donation to Mureau in execution of his testament;
- Jean de Joinville sealed the act;
- the sons confirmed their father’s 1265 gifts to Gerbonvaux.

His son Geoffroy of Rorthey died around the same time, approximately 1269. Pierre III therefore became the principal secular heir.

Sibille survived until 1275 and chose burial among the Franciscans of Neufchâteau.

No securely documented tomb effigy, epitaph, exhumation, or remains of Geoffroy have been identified in the consulted sources.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Dynastic legacy

Pierre III continued the house. The male line holding the main lordship survived until Pierre de Bourlémont’s death in 1412. Through female inheritance the château passed to the Anglure family and later to other aristocratic houses.

Later marriages renewed links between the Bourlémont and Joinville families.

### Institutional legacy

Gerbonvaux was Geoffroy and Sibille’s most durable institutional creation. It continued as a hospital and religious property, received later gifts, and became involved in subsequent litigation and protection arrangements.

Bermont later acquired significance in the devotional geography of Joan of Arc. Although Geoffroy had no connection to Joan personally, his purchase helped transfer the chapel into the lordship network that existed in her region.

### Architectural legacy

The enlarged thirteenth-century form of Bourlémont, particularly its monumental round towers, remains associated with Geoffroy. The strength of the evidence differs by feature:

- his financing of castle works: documented;
- major rebuilding under him: strongly reported;
- precise construction sequence: dependent on architectural study;
- rebuilding inspired by personal crusading experience: unverified.

### Administrative reputation

The 2024 reassessment portrays his Navarrese administration as:

- legally attentive;
- consultative;
- careful with inherited disputes;
- reliant on local elites and experienced officers;
- perhaps too cautious for a king seeking rapid consolidation.

This is an interpretation of his acts, not a contemporary character judgment.

### Historiographical obscurity

Geoffroy’s reputation is fragmented among several fields:

- Lorraine castle history;
- Navarrese institutional history;
- Joinville studies;
- crusade history;
- Joan of Arc regional heritage;
- noble genealogy.

Short biographies often combine these fields without distinguishing documentation from tradition. The result has been several persistent errors: 1210 as an exact birth date, definite crusading service, continued Navarrese office in 1259, and overconfident architectural narratives.

**Nature:** Father and son, if the atlas entry “Pierre Bourlémont” designates Pierre III/Perrin; alternatively the roster name may refer to another member of the frequently repeated Bourlémont line.

**Nature claimed:** A supposed earlier member of the Bourlémont house, mother of Francis of Assisi, and therefore a possible remote relative of Geoffroy.

**Dates and places claimed:** Late twelfth century; Provence, France, and Assisi.

**Evidentiary label:** **Rumor or myth.** The Bourlémont identification appears centuries after Pica’s lifetime. No reliable documentary genealogy connects her to Geoffroy’s Lorraine family.

**Nature claimed:** Remote family connection through Francis’s mother, Pica “de Bourlémont.”

**Evidentiary label:** **Allegation or theory derived from a late genealogical legend.** No documented meeting was possible—Francis died in 1226, when Geoffroy was young—and the asserted kinship is unproved.
