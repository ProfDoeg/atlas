# Franz Pforr: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

Franz Pforr’s documented life lasted only twenty-four years. The principal evidence consists of his surviving paintings and drawings; letters to Johann David Passavant and others; manuscripts preserved in Frankfurt and Lübeck; correspondence and recollections of Friedrich Overbeck, Peter von Cornelius, Ludwig Vogel, and their circle; nineteenth-century biographies and memorial publications; and modern museum and art-historical research.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by an archival record, surviving work, contemporary correspondence, or institutional catalogue.
- **Reported fact:** stated in reputable secondary literature but not independently confirmed here from the underlying document.
- **Interpretation:** an art-historical or biographical reading rather than an independently demonstrable event.
- **Disputed attribution:** scholars or cataloguers disagree about authorship, identity, date, or subject.
- **Theory:** a proposed reconstruction resting on circumstantial or interpretive evidence.
- **Myth or retrospective image:** a story or representation important to Pforr’s reputation but not necessarily literal biography.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Franz Pforr.
- **Alternative forms:** François Pforr occasionally appears in French-language cataloguing; no stable pseudonym is documented.
- **Brotherhood identity:** member and co-founder of the **Lukasbund**, whose members called themselves *Lukasbrüder* (“Brothers of Saint Luke”).
- **Later movement name:** classified posthumously as a principal early **Nazarene** (*Nazarener*). The collective nickname arose in Rome from the artists’ appearance and quasi-monastic manner of life; it was not Pforr’s personal pseudonym.
- **Professions:** painter, draughtsman, illustrator, and graphic artist.
- **Religion:** born and raised Protestant/Evangelical. No reliable evidence was found that Pforr converted to Roman Catholicism before his death.
- **Birth:** modern authority records give **5 April 1788**, Frankfurt am Main, then a free imperial city in the Holy Roman Empire.
- **Birth-date discrepancy:** the nineteenth-century *Allgemeine Deutsche Biographie* gives **7 April 1788**. Modern German authority records and museum literature generally prefer 5 April. Whether 7 April reflects baptism, registration, or a simple biographical error has not been established from the sources consulted.
- **Death:** **16 June 1812**, Albano, now Albano Laziale, in the Alban Hills southeast of Rome.
- **Age at death:** twenty-four.
- **Cause:** a pulmonary illness generally identified as tuberculosis or consumption. Contemporary descriptions use the language of chest or lung disease rather than a modern laboratory diagnosis.
- **Marital status:** unmarried.
- **Known children:** none.
- **Principal residences:** Frankfurt am Main; Kassel, 1801–1805; Vienna, 1805–1810; Rome, 1810–1812; Albano, final weeks of 1812.

The most authoritative concise modern biography is Ekkehard Mai’s entry in the *Neue Deutsche Biographie*. The Frankfurt University Library’s archive inventory supplies documentary confirmation of the surviving correspondence and posthumous papers.

## Family and Ancestry

### Father

**Johann Georg Pforr** (1745–1798) was a painter, draughtsman, engraver, porcelain painter, and specialist in horses, livestock, hunting, and landscape subjects. Born in Ulfen in Hesse, he trained in Kassel and worked at the Kassel porcelain manufactory before moving to Frankfurt in 1781. His mature reputation rested particularly on horse painting.

**Documented or consistently reported connection:** Franz received his first drawing instruction from his father. The child’s early concentration on horses, riders, soldiers, and animal anatomy clearly arose in this household environment, though his mature artistic program moved away from his father’s Dutch-influenced animal painting toward historical and religious subjects.

### Mother and the Tischbein family

His mother was **Johanna Christiane Pforr, née Tischbein**, who died in 1800. She was a daughter of **Conrad or Konrad Tischbein** (1712–1778), placing Franz within the extensive Tischbein dynasty of Hessian artists.

The modern *Neue Deutsche Biographie* identifies his maternal uncle and guardian as **Johann Heinrich Tischbein the Younger** (1742–1808), animal and landscape painter, engraver, academy professor, and inspector of the Kassel gallery. Museum metadata also associates Pforr with more than one Tischbein relative, reflecting the density and sometimes confusing nomenclature of the family.

Care is required not to conflate Pforr’s Kassel guardian with **Johann Heinrich Wilhelm Tischbein** (1751–1829), the artist popularly called “Goethe Tischbein.” They were separate members of the dynasty.

### Sibling

Franz had an older or approximately older brother, **Heinrich Pforr**, reportedly sixteen when he died in **1801**. The sequence of deaths left Franz without his immediate nuclear family:

- father, 1798;
- mother, 1800;
- brother, 1801.

The cumulative effect is frequently treated as formative, but no clinical or sufficiently detailed contemporary testimony survives to reconstruct his psychological response. What is demonstrable is that the orphaned boy entered his maternal uncle’s household in Kassel in 1801.

### Marriage and descendants

Pforr never married and left no known descendants. His writings and images contain an elaborate imaginary domestic future involving the idealized figure “Maria,” but it must not be mistaken for an actual wife or fiancée.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1788–1801

Pforr grew up in Frankfurt in an artisan-artist household. His father’s profession gave him early access to studio practice, animal studies, engravings, and the commercial world of painting.

A remarkable survival is a **1794 sketchbook comprising fifty-six leaves**, attributed to Pforr at approximately six years of age and later preserved in the Schlosser collection. This and the Städel Museum’s group of twelve juvenile drawings document precocious activity rather than merely retrospective family legend.

The juvenile subjects include:

- church attendance;
- fairs or circus performers;
- musicians;
- a man standing on two horses;
- fanciful cats emerging from a hole and cats in costume;
- *The Magic Flute*;
- horse-drawn traffic;
- processions;
- students duelling;
- animals holding an assembly;
- donkey herds and riders;
- armed horsemen;
- soldiers and messengers.

These sheets combine close observation of horses and military figures with comic invention and theatrical storytelling. They anticipate Pforr’s later preference for narrative sequences, historical costume, and sharply articulated contours.

The deaths of his father, mother, and brother between 1798 and 1801 ended his Frankfurt childhood. In 1801 his uncle took him to Kassel.

## Education and Formation

### Kassel, 1801–1805

In Kassel, Pforr lived under the guardianship of Johann Heinrich Tischbein the Younger. Tischbein trained him in drawing and painting and encouraged his candidacy for the Vienna Academy.

Kassel offered access to:

- an established art academy;
- the landgrave’s picture gallery;
- Tischbein family workshops and professional networks;
- collections of old-master painting and prints;
- systematic study of animals, landscape, and historical composition.

Pforr initially expected to become a battle painter, a logical continuation of his father’s equestrian specialty. A retrospective report sent to his guardian records that during his first Vienna year he devoted particular attention to horse anatomy for that purpose.

After Tischbein’s death in 1808, Pforr’s guardianship and financial arrangements involved others in the Frankfurt–Kassel network. The exact size of his inheritance or stipend has not been established. Sources describe support from a committee or guardians who financed his Vienna study, not independent wealth.

### Vienna Academy, 1805–1810

Pforr entered the **Vienna Academy of Fine Arts** in autumn 1805. The institution was led by the prominent classicist **Heinrich Friedrich Füger** and sculptor **Franz Anton Zauner**. Pforr received instruction from Füger and professors including **Franz Caucig** and probably Hubert Maurer.

His initial curriculum included:

- drawing from antique casts;
- anatomical study;
- figure drawing;
- copying;
- historical composition;
- horse anatomy and preparation for battle painting.

The curriculum’s emphasis on antique models, idealized form, technical sequence, and professorial authority increasingly frustrated him.

### Friedrich Overbeck

Pforr met **Johann Friedrich Overbeck** at the academy in the summer of **1806**. Their surviving reports indicate intense collaboration: Overbeck visited Pforr daily; they drew together, devised compositions, read, discussed art, and criticized one another’s work.

The relationship was not uninterrupted harmony. Pforr’s retrospective account describes discouragement and envy when he compared his own difficulty drawing a head with Overbeck’s cleaner and more assured studies after antique statues. He withdrew for a time and associated with companions he himself later judged questionable. The two reconciled in spring 1807, aided by living in the same building.

Pforr then shared lodgings with the Kassel student **Wolff Rinald** and the Frankfurt painter **Carl Jung**, while Overbeck occupied separate rooms in the same house.

This episode is significant because later memorial literature transformed the pair into nearly flawless “heart-brothers.” Their own accounts instead show a demanding friendship involving rivalry, insecurity, reconciliation, mutual discipline, and unusual emotional intensity.

### Intellectual and artistic sources

Pforr and Overbeck sought alternatives to the academy in:

- Albrecht Dürer and early German printmaking;
- late-medieval and early Renaissance German painting;
- early Italian painters before and including Raphael;
- the Bible and Christian legend;
- medieval history and chivalric literature;
- Goethe’s *Götz von Berlichingen*;
- Schiller’s historical ballads, especially “Der Graf von Habsburg”;
- Shakespeare, including *Macbeth*;
- Cervantes’s *Don Quixote*;
- the idea of the medieval guild as a moral and artistic brotherhood.

Pforr gravitated toward what he called or understood as an “old German” manner: precise outline, flat and lucid arrangements, minute costume, civic architecture, medieval narrative, moral earnestness, and Dürer as exemplary national master. Overbeck leaned more toward Raphael and Italian sacred art. Their friendship images made that difference part of a complementary German–Italian program.

## Early Career and the Formation of the Lukasbund

### Informal opposition, 1807–1809

From 1807 onward, Pforr and Overbeck developed a private program of collective composition. Fellow students in their circle included:

- Ludwig Vogel;
- Joseph Wintergerst;
- Joseph Sutter;
- Johann Konrad Hottinger;
- Franz Hottinger in some accounts;
- Johann Nepomuk Höfel and others on the circle’s margins.

They assigned subjects, brought completed designs to meetings, and subjected them to mutual criticism. The aim was not merely stylistic reform but ethical reformation of the artist through friendship, piety, disciplined labor, and communal judgment.

### Founding date

The group traced its association to **10 July 1808** and formally constituted or reaffirmed the **Lukasbund on 10 July 1809**, the anniversary of the first compact. Different summaries therefore give either 1808 or 1809 as the “foundation.” The best reconstruction is:

- **10 July 1808:** initial compact or informal association;
- **10 July 1809:** formalized brotherhood, with rules, emblem, and Saint Luke identity.

The emblem devised by Overbeck showed Saint Luke and incorporated initials or identifying signs of the brothers.

### Conflict with the Academy

The standard older narrative says the academy expelled Pforr and his associates for rebelling against Füger’s classicism. Recent scholarship provides a more differentiated picture:

- **Documented fact:** they sharply rejected the academy’s prevailing methods and organized an alternative association.
- **Documented/reported fact:** the academy closed during the 1809 war and occupation crisis.
- **Reported fact:** when it reopened, foreign students including Pforr and Overbeck were not readmitted or were excluded.
- **Reported fact:** Pforr was also denied access to copy originals in the imperial gallery because his manner and conduct did not conform to academy expectations.
- **Interpretation:** later Nazarene histories dramatized the administrative exclusion as a heroic expulsion and “birth of Romantic painting.”

The rupture was genuine, but “expelled solely because of artistic rebellion” is too simple.

## Chronological Life History

### 1808–1809: Historical and friendship imagery

Pforr produced or began several defining compositions:

- **Allegorie der Freundschaft** (*Allegory of Friendship*), 1808;
- designs based on German imperial and knightly history;
- *Der Graf von Habsburg und der Priester*, approximately 1809–1810;
- *Der heilige Georg, den Drachen tötend*, approximately 1809–1811;
- the large procession *Der Einzug des Königs Rudolf von Habsburg in Basel 1273*, begun in Vienna;
- drawings after Goethe’s *Götz von Berlichingen*.

His medieval Frankfurt in the Rudolf painting was imaginative rather than archaeologically exact. Gothic civic architecture, heraldry, ceremonial order, and dense costume produced an idealized historical community.

### 1810: Departure from Vienna

Pforr, Overbeck, Ludwig Vogel, and Johann Konrad Hottinger left Vienna for Italy in 1810. The journey represented both practical migration and programmatic withdrawal from the academy.

Their destination was Rome, where they sought direct study of early Italian art and a life organized around artistic fraternity. They established themselves in the disused Franciscan monastery of **Sant’Isidoro a Capo le Case**, near the Pincian Hill.

### Rome and Sant’Isidoro, 1810–1812

At Sant’Isidoro the group pursued a deliberately simple communal life. Accounts emphasize:

- shared study and criticism;
- copying in churches, palaces, and the Vatican;
- religious reading;
- rejection of conventional metropolitan social ambition;
- plain or archaizing clothing;
- long hair;
- a quasi-monastic organization of artistic work.

The Italians’ later label **“I Nazareni”** referred to their appearance, especially long hair and perceived biblical style. Its application to the entire art movement became standard only through later reception.

Pforr completed *The Entry of King Rudolf of Habsburg into Basel in 1273* in Rome in 1810. Its procession-like construction, sharply bounded forms, historical costume, and imagined German civic order are central to his mature style.

He also made direct studies after early Italian art. A surviving sheet inscribed **“Capella Fiesole in Vaticano”** records study of Fra Angelico’s frescoes in the Niccoline Chapel. Other Roman work included landscapes, costume studies, figures from live models, religious compositions, and the continuing friendship project with Overbeck.

### The friendship portraits

Overbeck painted the celebrated **Portrait of the Painter Franz Pforr** around 1810. The Nationalgalerie describes it as an ideal image of the friend at a moment of completed happiness. Its Gothic interior, window view, cat, lily-like or devotional symbolism, book, and domestic setting turn portraiture into an imagined moral biography.

Pforr’s small 1810 **Self-Portrait** at the Städel was once attributed to Overbeck because an inscription on its reverse reportedly read that Pforr had been painted by Overbeck in Rome. Discovery of a preparatory drawing inscribed **“Pforr ipse fec.”**, together with stylistic evidence, led to reattribution to Pforr himself. This is a notable attribution history, not evidence of fraud.

### *Sulamith und Maria*

Pforr and Overbeck planned reciprocal friendship paintings involving two imaginary women:

- **Sulamith**, associated with Overbeck, Italy, Raphael, southern color, and the Song of Songs;
- **Maria**, associated with Pforr, Germany, Dürer, northern domesticity, and an Annunciation-like ideal.

Pforr completed **Sulamith und Maria** in 1811 as a small double image resembling a late-medieval house altar. He gave the associated manuscript tale to Overbeck on **24 September 1811**.

In the narrative the painter-companions appear as **Johannes** and **Albrecht**:

- Johannes represents Johann Friedrich Overbeck;
- Albrecht represents Pforr through allusion to Albrecht Dürer.

The twin sisters Sulamith and Maria become their ideal brides. The story ends by supplying the artists with an ordered, pious domestic future they did not possess in life.

Overbeck’s corresponding painting was conceived for Pforr but remained unfinished at Pforr’s death. It evolved over many years into the work now called **Italia und Germania**.

### Naples and Sorrento, 1811

Pforr travelled to **Naples and Sorrento** in 1811. The exact itinerary and daily dates were not established from the accessible sources. He made landscape studies and encountered southern Italian scenery, but the journey was followed by a serious recurrence of pulmonary illness in October 1811.

### Final illness, 1811–1812

Pforr had reportedly shown symptoms of chest disease before Italy. By late 1811 his condition had deteriorated.

A surviving letter to Johann David Passavant dated **11 February 1812**, written in Rome, belongs to the last phase of his correspondence. In spring 1812 friends arranged for him to leave Rome for the higher air of Albano. Older biographies report that he undertook an **ass’s-milk cure**, a period treatment for consumption.

He was moved to Albano in May. The move did not arrest the disease.

## Death

Pforr died in Albano on **16 June 1812**. Peter von Cornelius wrote to Johann David Passavant from Ariccia on **17–18 June 1812**, immediately after the death; a later copy survives in the Frankfurt archive. Overbeck wrote Passavant from Rome on **21 October 1812** concerning Pforr.

No evidence was found for accident, violence, poisoning, addiction, self-harm, or criminal involvement. Tuberculosis or consumption remains the consistently reported cause.

The location of Pforr’s original grave and the survival or later fate of any marker were not securely established in the consulted materials.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

Pforr had no known company, commercial partnership, patent, industrial project, real-estate enterprise, investment portfolio, or formal public office.

Relevant institutional relationships were:

- his father’s Frankfurt workshop;
- Johann Heinrich Tischbein the Younger’s Kassel household and studio;
- Kassel artistic and gallery institutions;
- Vienna Academy of Fine Arts;
- imperial picture-gallery access in Vienna;
- Lukasbund;
- the Sant’Isidoro community in Rome;
- the Frankfurt Kunstverein, posthumously;
- the Städel Museum and Frankfurt City Library, as later repositories;
- Museum Georg Schäfer, later home of *Sulamith und Maria*;
- Swiss collections holding materials inherited by Ludwig Vogel.

### Finances

Pforr appears to have depended on guardianship funds, family support, or educational assistance rather than substantial earned income. No reliable inventory of his personal estate or exact income was found.

His surviving production is dominated by studies and unfinished or privately exchanged works, not a large commercial output. Friendship gifts were central to his practice. His early death prevented the patronage, fresco commissions, and institutional careers later available to other Nazarenes.

His market prices are consequently part of his posthumous history. For example, a drawing sold by Lempertz in 2010 for €4,800 including premium against an estimate of €2,000–3,000. Such modern sales say nothing about Pforr’s lifetime wealth.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Political-historical orientation

Pforr was not a documented political officeholder or activist. His historical imagery nevertheless emerged amid:

- Napoleonic warfare and occupation;
- dissolution of the Holy Roman Empire;
- intensified German cultural nationalism;
- Romantic interest in medieval civic and imperial history.

His Rudolf of Habsburg subjects, Dürer cult, Gothic architecture, and *Götz von Berlichingen* illustrations participated in a search for an “old German” cultural past. This was cultural-historical nationalism rather than a documented party program.

### Religion

The Lukasbund treated Christianity as the ethical ground of art. Members understood artistic truth as requiring sincerity, humility, brotherhood, and devotional seriousness.

Pforr remained formally Protestant as far as the record shows. Later narratives sometimes allow the Catholic identity of the mature Nazarene movement—especially Overbeck’s 1813 conversion—to flow backward over him. Evidence does not support identifying Pforr as a Catholic convert.

### Artistic doctrine

Pforr’s surviving letters and works oppose:

- mechanical academic imitation;
- excessive finish detached from spiritual purpose;
- fashionable virtuosity;
- compositional practices he regarded as artificial or corrupting.

He favored:

- firm completed contours;
- legibility of narrative;
- close study of Dürer and early masters;
- historical costume;
- collective criticism;
- moral purpose;
- concord between artistic life and artistic subject.

A July 1811 letter to Passavant reportedly called one insufficiently resolved mode of finish a **“Verderb der Kunst”** (“corruption of art”) and insisted on working out the contours. The phrase concerns artistic method, not political or financial corruption.

### Gender and domestic ideals

*Sulamith und Maria* fuses bride, saint, Madonna, homemaker, and artistic allegory. Modern scholarship has examined how it reconciles erotic attraction with chastity and defines women as moralizing or civilizing partners for male artists.

These images document Pforr’s idealized conception of marriage. They do not document actual relationships with the fictional women.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Johann Friedrich Overbeck

**Documented fact.** Pforr’s closest artistic friend, collaborator, sometime rival, Lukasbund co-founder, Roman housemate or near-housemate, portraitist, correspondent, and intended recipient of *Sulamith und Maria*.

They called one another brothers or “heart-brothers.” Their relationship included:

- shared drawing from 1806;
- temporary estrangement and reconciliation;
- formulation of the Lukasbund;
- complementary German and Italian artistic identities;
- reciprocal friendship paintings;
- Overbeck’s long posthumous commemoration of Pforr.

### Johann David Passavant

**Documented fact.** Frankfurt childhood friend and correspondent. Pforr’s enthusiasm helped sustain Passavant’s artistic interests while Passavant worked in commerce and banking. Passavant received letters from Vienna and Rome and was deeply affected by Pforr’s death. Pforr’s papers were initially incorporated into Passavant’s estate before being reorganized as a distinct archival collection.

### Ludwig Vogel

**Documented fact.** Swiss painter, Lukasbund member, travelling companion to Rome, and inheritor or custodian of working materials. In 2005 Heinrich Thommen identified more than 150 sheets in Vogel’s estate as material associated with Pforr’s lost “Costümsammlung.” Their long concealment resulted partly from Vogel’s failure to record authorship and provenance systematically.

### Peter von Cornelius

**Documented fact.** A major Nazarene associate in Rome and member of the enlarged circle. His letter from Ariccia on 17–18 June 1812 is an immediate documentary witness to Pforr’s death.

### Joseph Wintergerst and Joseph Sutter

**Documented fact.** Founding Lukasbund colleagues. Some works connected with the early circle have generated joint-authorship or attribution proposals, including a modern proposal that Pforr and Wintergerst collaborated on particular sheets.

### Johann Konrad and Franz Hottinger

**Documented/reported fact.** Members of the student and Lukasbund circle. Johann Konrad Hottinger travelled from Vienna to Rome with Pforr, Overbeck, and Vogel.

Modern research has investigated possible real-life connections between Pforr and Overbeck’s imagined brides and **Regula and Lisette Hottinger**. This remains a reconstructed theory requiring cautious separation from the fictional narrative.

### Academy teachers

- Heinrich Friedrich Füger: academy director and representative of the classicism the brothers opposed.
- Franz Caucig: Pforr’s direct teacher in drawing and painting.
- Hubert Maurer: associated with the academy curriculum.
- Franz Anton Zauner: academy leadership.

They were teachers and institutional authorities, not personal “enemies” in any substantiated sense.

### Patrons

No dominant private patron comparable to those of later Nazarene careers is documented. Guardians and family networks funded his formation. Later collectors, curators, and artists created his posthumous institutional presence.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

### Dedicated adversarial search result

Searches were conducted in English and German combining Pforr’s name with controversy, scandal, allegations, accusations, lawsuits, fraud, forgery, conspiracy, criticism, and provenance, including material indexed or published in 2024–2026.

**Finding:** no credible evidence was found that Pforr was accused, investigated, charged, sued, convicted, or credibly connected to fraud, corruption, money laundering, political violence, sexual abuse, intelligence activity, or other criminal conduct.

The relevant contested matters concern art history and attribution:

### Academy exclusion

- **Documented core:** conflict with academic methods, autonomous organization, interrupted enrollment, and non-readmission or exclusion.
- **Traditional narrative:** heroic expulsion for defying classicism.
- **Qualification:** war closure, foreign-student status, and administrative circumstances complicate the simple martyrdom narrative.

### Self-portrait attribution

- **Earlier attribution:** the small Städel portrait was assigned to Overbeck, encouraged by a reverse inscription.
- **Current attribution:** Pforr, supported by a preparatory drawing marked “Pforr ipse fec.” and stylistic analysis.
- **Evidentiary label:** disputed attribution subsequently revised; no evidence of intentional forgery.

### “Figurengruppe” drawing

A drawing sold in 2010 had previously been catalogued as Peter Cornelius and was later accepted as Pforr by Colin Bailey and Christian Jensen. Bailey interpreted the central figure as Martin Luther; Jensen allowed that it might instead be Rudolf of Habsburg.

- **Evidentiary label:** expert-supported reattribution; subject remains disputed.

### Pforr materials in Vogel’s estate

More than 150 sheets associated with Pforr’s costume collection survived in Ludwig Vogel’s estate. Vogel did not systematically identify the makers of all inherited or collected sheets, causing works by different hands to be attributed incorrectly over time.

- **Evidentiary label:** documented provenance and cataloguing problem.
- There is no evidence that Vogel’s omissions constituted deliberate fraud.

### Modern collection provenance

The Museum Georg Schäfer, which houses *Sulamith und Maria*, has faced broader provenance and restitution questions concerning works in the Schäfer collection. The museum reports systematic provenance research and acknowledged problematic or unresolved histories for parts of its graphic holdings.

No consulted source specifically identifies *Sulamith und Maria* as Nazi-looted art. The collection-wide controversy must therefore not be converted into an allegation about Pforr’s painting without object-specific evidence.

### Sexuality and the Overbeck relationship

Heinrich Thommen has proposed that *Sulamith und Maria* veiled both Overbeck’s unhappy attachment and Pforr’s own unfulfilled inclination toward Overbeck.

- **Evidentiary label:** modern interpretive theory.
- **Supporting material:** exceptionally intense friendship language, reciprocal identities, imaginary brides, and friendship paintings.
- **Limitation:** no documentary evidence establishing a consummated sexual relationship was found. Nineteenth-century male friendship language cannot by itself prove sexual conduct or identity.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### The flawless “heart-brothers”

**Story:** Pforr and Overbeck formed an almost providential union without rivalry.

**Appearance and circulation:** Nazarene memorial literature, later biographies, friendship portraits, and art-historical narratives.

**Evidence:** their intimacy and collaboration are thoroughly documented. Pforr’s own account, however, records jealousy, withdrawal, feelings of inferiority, and reconciliation.

**Effect:** the idealized friendship became a foundation myth of the Nazarene movement and encouraged interpretation of every paired female or national figure as an emblem of perfect male fraternity.

### The heroic academy expulsion

**Story:** reactionary professors expelled spiritually awakened students, thereby inaugurating German Romantic painting.

**Evidence for:** real opposition to academy doctrine, separate meetings, exclusion, and denial of copying access.

**Evidence against a simple version:** the academy’s wartime closure and restrictions on foreign students formed part of the circumstances.

**Effect:** transformed an institutional dispute into the movement’s exodus narrative.

### “The Nazarenes” as a self-chosen order name

**Story:** Pforr and his companions founded themselves as “Nazarenes.”

**Correction:** their chosen name was Lukasbund or Lukasbrüder. “Nazarene” was an externally applied Roman nickname related to appearance and perceived biblical affect; it later became the standard art-historical term.

### Pforr’s imaginary marriage

**Story:** Maria was Pforr’s actual beloved or promised bride.

**Evidence:** Pforr’s tale treats her as Albrecht’s ideal bride; the painting gives her a highly particular domestic form.

**Against literal identification:** the narrative is explicitly fictional and allegorical, and Pforr died unmarried. Attempts to identify Maria and Sulamith with Regula and Lisette Hottinger remain scholarly reconstructions.

**Effect:** gave his unfinished life a retrospective domestic completion and reinforced the pathos of his early death.

### Pforr as Catholic martyr-artist

**Story:** because the Nazarene movement became closely Catholic, Pforr is sometimes assimilated into a Catholic conversion narrative.

**Evidence:** he pursued Christian art and quasi-monastic fraternity.

**Against:** reliable biographical authorities list him as Evangelical; no conversion record has been established.

### The doomed young genius

**Story:** Pforr’s self-portrait visibly foretells his death, and his small oeuvre represents a perfect Romantic fragment.

**Appearance:** museum interpretation and later Romantic reception.

**Evidence:** he was already suffering from a serious pulmonary illness, and the portrait’s physical severity may reflect it.

**Limitation:** reading foreknowledge of death from facial appearance is retrospective.

**Effect:** his youth, illness, monastic setting, intense friendship, and unfinished projects made him a prototype of the artist whose early death guarantees symbolic purity.

### The “lost” oeuvre

**Story:** Pforr left only a handful of pictures and approximately one hundred drawings.

**Revision:** discovery and reconstruction of more than 150 working sheets in the Vogel estate substantially enlarged the known body of material.

**Effect:** moved scholarship away from treating him solely as the author of several iconic panels and toward understanding his systematic research into costume, narrative, and historical detail.

## Works by Franz Pforr

Because no fully accessible modern catalogue raisonné was located, the following combines principal independent works, major cycles, texts, and documented groups. The Städel’s online catalogue currently lists seventy-six works or entries; Thommen’s research attributes or associates more than 150 additional working sheets with Pforr’s costume collection.

### Paintings and major pictorial works

- **Selbstbildnis** (*Self-Portrait*), 1810, oil on canvas mounted on board, Städel Museum, Frankfurt. Formerly attributed to Overbeck; now catalogued as Pforr.
- **Der Einzug des Königs Rudolf von Habsburg in Basel 1273**, begun approximately 1808 and completed 1810, oil painting, Städel Museum.
- **Der Graf von Habsburg und der Priester**, approximately 1809–1810, oil on canvas, Städel Museum; derived from Schiller’s ballad.
- **Der heilige Georg, den Drachen tötend**, approximately 1809–1811, Städel Museum. The painting was reportedly rediscovered in 1923 after having dropped from view.
- **Sulamith und Maria**, 1811, oil on wood, approximately 34.5 × 32 cm, Museum Georg Schäfer, Schweinfurt; conceived as a friendship gift and paired in idea with Overbeck’s eventual *Italia und Germania*.
- **Nächtliche Heimkehr**, monogrammed and dated 1809; a work brought to modern auction after having been treated as missing. Its attribution and relation to Lukasbund task-setting have been discussed in auction scholarship.
- Historical, religious, and allegorical compositions surviving in incomplete or study form, including subjects involving Saint Michael, Otto the Great, Albert I, and John of Swabia.

### Drawings and cycles

- **Allegorie der Freundschaft**, 1808, surviving in versions or related sheets.
- **Raphael und Dürer vor dem Thron der Kunst** (*Raphael and Dürer before the Throne of Art*), allegorical composition.
- **Götz von Berlichingen** illustration cycle, approximately 1810:
  - title page;
  - Brother Martin and Götz;
  - Adelheid and Weislingen;
  - Götz, Selbitz, and Georg in the Spessart;
  - inn and peasant wedding;
  - emperor and Nuremberg merchants;
  - wounded Selbitz;
  - siege of Jaxthausen;
  - Götz before the council at Heilbronn;
  - Sickingen before Heilbronn;
  - Götz and the peasant leaders;
  - dying Weislingen and Maria;
  - Götz’s death;

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Pforr received little public recognition during his lifetime. His small painted output, unfinished projects, and death before the major Nazarene fresco commissions initially made him a remembered founder rather than a publicly successful artist.

### Immediate memorialization

Overbeck’s grief and long preservation of the friendship imagery were decisive. The portrait of Pforr, the unfinished reciprocal painting that became *Italia und Germania*, and correspondence within the circle turned Pforr into the lost founding brother.

Passavant, Vogel, Cornelius, and the Frankfurt network preserved papers and drawings. The Frankfurt Kunstverein’s 1832 and 1834 portfolios made his compositions available to a larger audience.

### Nineteenth-century reputation

Pforr was incorporated into histories of the Nazarene renewal as:

- the Dürer-oriented, “German” counterpart to Overbeck;
- an originator of Christian-Romantic brotherhood;
- a painter of medieval civic and imperial history;
- an artist whose early death preserved the founding movement’s ideals from later compromise.

This reputation often privileged biography and symbolic friendship over formal analysis.

### Twentieth-century reassessment

Lehr’s 1924 monograph established Pforr as “master of the Lukasbund.” The rediscovery of *Saint George* in 1923 strengthened his visible painted corpus.

Later scholarship examined:

- the material and visual construction of friendship;
- gender and invented brides;
- German nationalism and medievalism;
- academy politics;
- Protestant and Catholic dimensions of the movement;
- attribution problems;
- the relationship between private fantasy and public artistic doctrine.

### Twenty-first-century reassessment

Thommen’s discovery of extensive Pforr material in the Vogel estate significantly enlarged the reconstructable oeuvre. Research now presents him less as the author of four isolated masterpieces and more as a systematic draughtsman who accumulated historical costume, figure, and compositional resources.

The modern reception also recognizes tension within his ideals:

- collective discipline versus individual jealousy;
- religious humility versus artistic ambition;
- imagined marriage versus intense male friendship;
- archaic form versus a distinctly modern self-conscious artistic movement;
- national historical imagery versus transnational formation in Vienna and Rome.

### Influence

Pforr’s direct teaching influence was limited by his death. His conceptual influence operated through Overbeck and the Nazarene movement:

- revival of early German and Italian models;
- artist brotherhoods modeled on medieval guilds;
- religious history painting;
- German–Italian allegory;
- the Romantic *Freundschaftsbild*;
- later Pre-Raphaelite interest in pre-academic sincerity and medieval form.

A direct documentary chain from Pforr personally to every later movement cannot be demonstrated; much of the influence was mediated by Overbeck, reproductions, exhibitions, and the collective reputation of the Nazarenes.

In 1965–1966 **Georg Baselitz** invoked Pforr in *Mann im Mond – Franz Pforr*, part of the *Helden* or “Hero” paintings. This is a modern symbolic reuse rather than stylistic discipleship: Pforr became a figure through whom a postwar German painter could approach the compromised category of the German artistic hero.

- **1794:** six-year-old Pforr produces the surviving fifty-six-leaf sketchbook.
- **1798:** father Johann Georg Pforr dies.
- **1800:** mother Johanna Christiane Pforr dies.
- **1801:** brother Heinrich dies; Franz enters the Kassel household of Johann Heinrich Tischbein the Younger.
- **1801–1805:** artistic education in Kassel under Tischbein; preparation for academy study.
- **Summer 1805:** returns temporarily to Frankfurt after the change in his Kassel circumstances.
- **Autumn 1805:** enters the Vienna Academy.
- **1805–1806:** studies horse anatomy with the initial intention of becoming a battle painter.
- **Summer 1806:** meets Friedrich Overbeck.
- **1806–1807:** daily collaborative drawing; subsequent jealousy and temporary estrangement.

- **1810:** settlement at Sant’Isidoro; completion of *Entry of King Rudolf*.
- **1810:** Pforr’s self-portrait and Overbeck’s ideal portrait of Pforr.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are included. Mere contemporaneity, shared geography, or indirect thematic resemblance has been excluded.

### Johann Friedrich Overbeck

**Evidentiary label: documented fact.**

Pforr met Overbeck at the Vienna Academy in summer 1806. They drew together, became intimate friends and artistic rivals, jointly developed an anti-academic reform program, founded the Lukasbund, migrated to Rome in 1810, and lived in the Sant’Isidoro community. They devised reciprocal friendship paintings: Pforr’s *Sulamith und Maria* and Overbeck’s work later called *Italia und Germania*. Overbeck painted Pforr’s best-known portrait and preserved his memory until 1869.

### Peter von Cornelius

**Evidentiary label: documented fact.**

Cornelius joined the enlarged Nazarene circle in Rome. His letter written at Ariccia on 17–18 June 1812, one to two days after Pforr’s death, survives in copy in the Frankfurt Pforr archive and is an immediate source for the final episode.

### Johann Wolfgang von Goethe

**Evidentiary label: documented artistic and textual connection; no securely documented personal meeting found.**

Pforr created a substantial illustration cycle for Goethe’s *Götz von Berlichingen*. The accompanying address to Goethe asks him to accept the drawings as evidence of a heart moved by the work. This supports direct engagement with—and apparently intended presentation or communication to—Goethe, but the sources consulted do not establish an in-person meeting.

### Friedrich Schiller

**Evidentiary label: documented artistic influence; no personal meeting.**

Pforr based *Der Graf von Habsburg und der Priester* on Schiller’s ballad “Der Graf von Habsburg.” The Rudolf subjects also participated in the historical-poetic culture Schiller helped shape. Schiller died in 1805, when Pforr was seventeen, and no meeting or correspondence was found.

### Albrecht Dürer

Dürer is not a separate person in the supplied Atlas roster, so he is not counted as an Atlas connection. He was nevertheless Pforr’s most important acknowledged historical artistic model.

### Johann Heinrich Wilhelm Tischbein

**Evidentiary label: reported extended-family connection requiring genealogical caution.**

Städel authority metadata associates Pforr with Johann Heinrich Wilhelm Tischbein as an uncle within the larger Tischbein artistic dynasty. Pforr’s actual Kassel guardian and teacher was Johann Heinrich Tischbein the Younger (1742–1808), a different person. No evidence was found that Johann Heinrich Wilhelm Tischbein personally taught Pforr.

### Napoleon Bonaparte

**No qualifying personal connection found.**

Napoleonic warfare affected the Vienna Academy’s closure and the conditions under which the Lukas brothers left Austria. That is historical context, not evidence of meeting, correspondence, collaboration, patronage, rivalry, or acknowledged personal influence.

### Johann Heinrich Pestalozzi

**No qualifying direct connection found.**

Passavant’s archive mentions a letter about Passavant’s own encounter with Pestalozzi. No source consulted documents Pforr meeting or corresponding with Pestalozzi.

### Other roster members

No additional supported crossings with the supplied Atlas roster were identified. Given Pforr’s short life and restricted network, absence of further connections is itself a finding.
