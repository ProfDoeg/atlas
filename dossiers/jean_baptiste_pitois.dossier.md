# Jean-Baptiste Pitois (Paul Christian) — Research Dossier

## Research Scope and Evidence Notes

This dossier reconstructs the life and work of **Jean-Baptiste Pitois (1811–1877)**, better known as **Paul Christian**, from French national-library records, nineteenth-century bibliographic sources, digitized primary editions, biographical notices, scholarship on the occult tarot, scholarship on French Algeria, and later family and occultist traditions.

The surviving record is uneven. Pitois was a prolific journalist, translator, compiler, political publicist, Catholic writer, colonial chronicler, and occult author, but no modern full-scale archival biography appears to exist. Several frequently repeated biographical episodes derive from posthumous family tradition rather than independently verified contemporary documents. Conversely, parts of his political career in 1848 and his publishing career are considerably better documented than most short occult biographies suggest.

Evidence is therefore marked implicitly or explicitly as one of the following:

- **Documented / strongly supported:** supported by contemporary publication records, national-library catalogues, known historical records, or multiple independent scholarly sources.
- **Reported / probable:** supported by serious secondary research but lacking a primary document located in this research.
- **Disputed / unresolved:** sources conflict or documentary confirmation is incomplete.
- **Self-claim:** stated by Pitois himself but not independently corroborated.
- **Legend / posthumous family tradition:** first securely encountered in late testimony or occultist biography.
- **Demonstrably false literary construction:** a narrative attributed by Pitois to an earlier historical source that can be checked and does not contain what he claimed.

The name **Paul Christian** became more than a one-off pseudonym. Modern scholarship notes that acquaintances apparently used it socially and that his son was later known as **Paul Christian fils**. Nineteenth-century and modern catalogues nevertheless vary in how they parse his civil and literary names.

### Name problems

The BnF authority file treats **P. Christian** as a pseudonym of a person catalogued as “Pitois, Christian, Jean-Baptiste,” while later tarot historians caution that many catalogues misleadingly make “Christian” look like part of his civil surname or given-name sequence. Ross Nichols, drawing on French biographical material and claiming reference to the Remiremont civil register, gives the birth name simply as **Jean Baptiste Pitois**.

The safest reconstruction is therefore:

> **Jean Baptiste Pitois** was the civil name; **Paul Christian** was the durable literary and social name.

“Pierre Christian,” encountered in some catalogues, should not be treated as a securely established legal name.

## Family and Ancestry

### Maternal family

The strongest accessible genealogical reconstruction encountered in this research is a Geneanet tree that cites Lyon civil-registration material. Because this is a user-compiled genealogical database rather than the civil act itself, its details should be treated as **provisionally sourced, not independently verified primary evidence**.

It gives:

- **Mother:** Marie Anne Pitois, born 1790.
- **Father:** not named.
- **Maternal grandfather:** Jean Blaise Pitois (1770–1811).
- **Maternal grandmother:** Marguerite Léonard (1771–1833).
- Several maternal uncles are listed, including Jean Charles Pitois and Raison François Pitois.

Modern occult-tarot scholarship likewise states that Pitois appears to have been born outside marriage. Paul Chacornac later speculated that “Christian” might have been his father’s surname, but the evidence located here does not establish that. It is better treated as a biographical conjecture.

### Publishing relatives

Several biographical sources describe Pitois as the **nephew of the bookseller-publisher Pitois-Levrault**. An older French literary bibliography also identifies him in this way. This connection is significant because it placed him near the book trade and helps explain the ease with which he moved among compilation, editing, translation, journalism, and publishing.

### Half-siblings

The Geneanet reconstruction gives children born to his mother and Pierre Symphorien Bertrand, including:

- Jean Charles Symphorien Bertrand, born 1822
- Léonie Marguerite Marie Thérèse Bertrand, born 1827

If correct, these were Pitois’s maternal half-siblings. No substantial evidence about his relationship with them was located.

### Marriage: unresolved conflict

The marital record is unusually contradictory.

**Version 1 — Maitron:**  
The French labor-history dictionary *Le Maitron* reports that Pitois married in **1853** an Italian woman said to be related to **Walter Scott**. The wording is striking but the underlying marriage document was not located in this research.

**Version 2 — Lyon civil-register indexing via Geneanet:**  
A genealogical record citing “Mariages de Lyon 02” gives a marriage on **26 June 1877** in Lyon’s 2nd arrondissement to **Louise Marie Tortillet (1825–1877)**.

This date is only sixteen days before Pitois’s reported death on 12 July 1877.

The two versions could reflect:
- two different marriages;
- a late civil regularization of an earlier union;
- an error in the Maitron notice;
- an error or misidentification in the genealogical tree.

No basis was found for silently choosing among these possibilities.

### Children

The same genealogical reconstruction attributes three children to Pitois and Louise Marie Tortillet:

- **Marie Joséphine Pitois**, born 1851
- **Paul Christian Marie Bernard Pitois**, born 1856
- **Emma Christian Pitois**, born 1859

The son is particularly important to Pitois’s posthumous reputation. Tarot historians identify **Paul Marie-Bernard Pitois**, often called **Paul Christian fils**, as the probable source or transmitter of a posthumous biographical serial about his father in the journal *La Lumière* in 1893–1894.

The younger Paul Christian published at least one occult-themed book, *La Reine Zinzarah: Comment on devient sorcier* (1894).

No reliable evidence of Pitois’s household finances, inheritance arrangements, property holdings, or probate estate was located.

## Childhood and Early Life

### 1811: birth in Remiremont

Jean Baptiste Pitois was born at **Remiremont**, in the Vosges, on **15 May 1811**.

Modern tarot historians describe his background as modest and provincial. His maternal grandfather is reported to have been connected with forest administration or custodianship. The presence of a bookselling/publishing uncle in the wider family later offered a route into print culture.

### Catholic upbringing

His family appears to have intended him for a religious or clerical career.

Ross Nichols and later scholars state that he studied first under local ecclesiastical supervision, including a convent or church school at **Harcourt**, and later at the **Clercs de la Chapelle Royale** in Paris.

The exact institutional sequence is imperfectly documented in accessible sources, but the broad point is consistent across the better biographies: Pitois received a substantial Catholic formation and initially tested a religious vocation.

---

## Education and Formation

### 1828–1829: La Trappe

In **1828**, at about seventeen, Pitois entered a **Trappist monastery** and took the religious name:

> **Marie-Bernard**

He remained only about a year and left without taking permanent vows.

This episode matters for several reasons:

1. The name **Marie-Bernard** remained important to his self-presentation.
2. He later used **Dom Marie-Bernard** as the supposed author of an enormous Catholic historical series.
3. His son’s name, **Paul Christian Marie Bernard Pitois**, appears to preserve the same religious name.
4. His later occultism did not represent a simple abandonment of Catholic culture. He continued to publish explicitly Catholic devotional and apologetic works.

### Strasbourg

After leaving La Trappe, Pitois is reported to have gone to **Strasbourg**, where an uncle held an academic post, described in later biography as rector of the academy. There he completed or continued his studies.

No university degree, thesis, or formal professional qualification has been located.

### Formative intellectual streams

By the time his mature writing emerged, several streams were already visible:

- Catholic theology and devotional literature
- German Romantic and fantastic literature
- French historical compilation
- antiquarian and bibliographical work
- astrology
- popular prophetic literature
- the emerging nineteenth-century occult revival
- Napoleonic folklore
- colonial and military history
- revolutionary politics

His later career should not be reduced to occultism. For much of his working life he was a professional man of letters whose occult books were part of a much larger publishing output.

## Early Career

## The disputed Caribbean and Spanish adventures

A posthumous biographical tradition says that during the obscure years between his religious education and his secure appearance in Parisian journalism, Pitois traveled to the Caribbean and became involved in anti-slavery agitation, then went to Spain and became involved in partisan conflict.

Two variants survive:

- modern tarot historians, following the *La Lumière* family tradition, give **Martinique**;
- Ross Nichols gives **Dominica**.

Nichols’s version is geographically suspect if read literally with his surrounding claim of a French colonial setting, because Dominica had long been British. The discrepancy may represent a transmission error, confused memory, or conflation.

No contemporary travel record, military record, passport, newspaper report, or colonial document corroborating the episode was located in this research.

The story is therefore preserved below under **Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives** rather than treated as established biography.

### By 1836: Paris and journalism

By **1836**, Pitois was securely associated with Parisian literary journalism.

Modern tarot historians reconstruct:

- **1836–1838:** work on the *Revue Germanique*
- **1838:** editorial work for *L’Éclair*
- **1839–1840:** association with the *Gazette des salons*

The journals placed him in the world of translation, foreign literature, criticism, popular history, and literary networking.

### Charles Nodier and *Paris historique*

Pitois became associated with **Charles Nodier**, novelist, bibliophile, Romantic writer, and librarian of the Bibliothèque de l’Arsenal.

The lavish *Paris historique, promenade dans les rues de Paris* appeared in 1838–1839 under Nodier’s name with illustrations by Auguste Régnier and Champin and a historical résumé by P. Christian.

Later occult biography sometimes inflated this association into a more intimate intellectual apprenticeship or extensive co-authorship. The publication itself allows a narrower documented statement: Pitois contributed the historical résumé and moved in Nodier’s literary orbit.

### 1839: Ministry of Public Instruction

In **1839**, Pitois obtained a position connected with the library or bibliographical administration of the **Ministry of Public Instruction**.

The BnF authority record describes him as attached to the ministry’s library. Later biographies say he worked on inventories or cataloguing of duplicate books and volumes originating in confiscated monastic libraries.

This employment became part of his occult origin story. Pitois later represented the handling of old books as the occasion on which he encountered forgotten astrological and magical works, including Auger Ferrier’s sixteenth-century *Des jugemens astronomiques sur les nativitez*.

The bibliographical employment is well supported. The dramatic “discovery” narrative is harder to test but is plausible as a description of how an antiquarian reader might encounter astrological literature.

## Chronological Life History

## 1838–1843: translator, editor, compiler, and literary professional

Pitois published at extraordinary speed.

His work in these years included:

- historical writing about Paris;
- Catholic and moral tales;
- translations and adaptations of Christoph von Schmid;
- editions of Montaigne, Machiavelli, Helvétius, Boccaccio, Young, Ossian, Washington Irving, and E. T. A. Hoffmann;
- prefaces and critical essays;
- collections of tales.

The BnF currently links roughly **100 bibliographic records** to his authority identity, including about **60 records as author**, **31 as translator**, and **7 as prefacer**. These counts include reprints and later editions, but they demonstrate the scale of his print career.

His editorial practice was characteristic of nineteenth-century commercial literary culture: “translation” could include extensive adaptation, rewriting, annotation, compilation, and introductory essays.

### Hoffmann and fantastic literature

Pitois’s translations or editions of **E. T. A. Hoffmann** are relevant to his later occult imagination. Hoffmann provided a literary model in which the fantastic, uncanny, supernatural, psychological, and ironic could coexist without requiring documentary literalism.

### Ossian, antiquity, and invented tradition

His work on **Ossian** similarly placed him close to one of Europe’s most influential cases of literary antiquity, disputed authenticity, national myth, and imaginative reconstruction.

Nothing proves that this directly caused his later pseudo-historical occult method, but it formed part of the literary environment in which purported ancient texts, reconstructed traditions, and romantic antiquarianism carried enormous authority.

## 1843–1847: Bugeaud, Algeria, Morocco, and colonial writing

### Secretary to Marshal Bugeaud

Pitois served as a secretary or attached literary functionary to **Marshal Thomas-Robert Bugeaud**, governor-general of Algeria and one of the central figures of the French conquest.

The BnF authority file calls him Bugeaud’s secretary. Later scholarship places his direct service in approximately **1843–1844**.

Bonnie Effros, in a modern academic history of French military archaeology in North Africa, identifies Pitois as a journalist and Ministry of Public Instruction bibliographer who served as Bugeaud’s personal secretary in 1843–1844.

### 1844 expedition

He accompanied French operations in North Africa in a noncombatant or reporting capacity.

Posthumous tradition adds a much more dramatic adventure: he supposedly fell into quicksand, faced decapitation by a Kabyle fighter, attempted to defend himself with a pistol he thought unloaded, discovered it was loaded, and was rescued by a French detachment; the same tradition says he suffered two bullet wounds.

The existence of North African travel and employment is well supported. The cinematic details come from later biographical tradition and were not independently corroborated in this research.

### Colonial publications

His North African experience generated or supported a series of books, including:

- *Souvenir du maréchal Bugeaud, de l’Algérie et du Maroc* (1845)
- *L’Afrique française, l’empire de Maroc et les déserts de Sahara* (1846)
- *Histoire de nos colonies françaises de l’Algérie et du Maroc* (1846)
- *La Nouvelle France. Souvenirs de l’Algérie et du Maroc* (1846)
- *L’Algérie de la jeunesse* (1847)

### Not simply colonial celebration

Some later occult scholarship describes these works collectively as books celebrating the “glories” of French colonialism. That description is incomplete.

Effros shows that Pitois could also be sharply critical of French “pacification” methods and compared some French behavior to that of the Huns and Vandals. In *L’Afrique française*, he also reproduced or transmitted testimony concerning the **Dahra cave killings/asphyxiations of 1845**, one of the most notorious episodes of the conquest.

This does not make Pitois an anti-colonial activist in the modern sense. He remained a French colonial chronicler, had served Bugeaud, and wrote within the assumptions of his time. But his published record includes criticism of the methods used in conquest.

The 1845 Dahra episode occurred after the main period usually assigned to his direct service with Bugeaud, so it should not be confused with an event he personally witnessed or participated in.

## 1846–1850: pirates, revolution, Jacobin politics, arrest, and pardon

### *Histoire des pirates et corsaires de l’Océan*

From roughly **1846 to 1850**, editions or volumes of his large history of pirates and corsairs appeared. This is another example of the breadth of his interests beyond occultism.

### 1848: Club central des Jacobins

Pitois entered the revolutionary politics of **1848** in a documented public role.

A surviving BnF-catalogued pamphlet records a speech delivered:

> at the opening session of the **Club central des Jacobins**, **29 March 1848**

The title page identifies **“le citoyen Pitois, Christian”** as provisional president.

The club’s invocation of the Jacobin name placed it explicitly within the symbolic inheritance of the French Revolution. This is not a later occultist legend; it is part of his printed political record.

### *Journal des Jacobins*

Pitois was editor-in-chief of a short-lived paper:

> *Journal des Jacobins : sentinelle des droits de l’homme*

A rare-book description based on Eugène Hatin’s nineteenth-century press bibliography reports only one issue or delivery and identifies Pitois-Christian as editor-in-chief and president of the Club central des Jacobins.

The extreme brevity of the newspaper’s run should be distinguished from the significance of his political position.

### June Days and arrest

The *Maitron* biographical dictionary reports:

- an executive order for his arrest dated **23 June 1848**;
- the arrest itself carried out on **31 July 1848**;
- papers seized at his home were regarded as incriminating;
- he was **“transporté”**;
- he was later pardoned;
- he returned in **December 1849**.

Some secondary notices connect the arrest to participation in or implication in the **June Days**.

No court judgment or criminal conviction was located in this research. The record supports **arrest, executive action, transportation, and pardon**; it does not justify upgrading those facts into a specific criminal conviction without further archival evidence.

The destination and conditions of the reported transportation were not securely recovered from the sources consulted here.

### Political significance

This episode makes Pitois’s later strong imperial and Bonapartist associations more historically interesting. His political trajectory cannot be described as lifelong ideological consistency:

- 1848: public Jacobin/radical-democratic activism;
- 1848–1849: arrest, transportation, pardon;
- 1850 onward: a visibly altered political tone;
- 1850s–1860s: increasingly favorable literary and prophetic treatment of Napoleon III and the imperial household.

---

## 1850–1853: post-revolutionary writing and reorientation

### *Dieu et France. Agonie de la Révolution* (1850)

Soon after his return, Pitois published:

> *Dieu et France. Agonie de la Révolution*

The title itself announces a marked shift in rhetoric from his Jacobin-club presidency two years earlier.

This book belongs to the political afterlife of 1848 and should be read alongside, not instead of, the earlier revolutionary evidence.

### *Moniteur du Soir*

Modern scholarship places him on the staff or in political editorial work at the **Moniteur du Soir** around **1850–1852**. Ross Nichols gives approximately 1851–1852.

### *Histoire de la Terreur* (1853)

In 1853 he published *Histoire de la Terreur*, returning to the French revolutionary past in historical form.

The book should not be treated as transparent autobiography of his 1848 politics, but it belongs to a period when he was repositioning himself after direct revolutionary involvement.

### Reported marriage in 1853

*Maitron* places his marriage to an Italian woman in 1853. As noted above, this is unresolved against the later Lyon marriage record.

## 1850s: encounter with Éliphas Lévi and movement into occultism

### Éliphas Lévi

Pitois’s mature occultism developed in the same Parisian milieu as **Alphonse-Louis Constant**, better known as **Éliphas Lévi**.

Modern tarot historians treat Lévi as Pitois’s most important immediate occult influence. Chacornac’s later biography of Lévi went further, portraying Christian as neighbor, friend, or pupil who benefited from Lévi’s conversation or teaching.

What can be said securely is:

- Christian knew Lévi’s work;
- his occult writings came after Lévi’s major formulations;
- he borrowed or adapted important parts of the contemporary occult synthesis;
- he nevertheless constructed a distinctive system focused much more heavily on astrology, numerology, prophecy, and redesigned tarot imagery than on Lévi’s Kabbalistic structure.

### Catholicism and occultism

Christian did not simply replace Catholicism with occultism.

During and after his occult turn he continued producing highly Catholic books. His career therefore contains overlapping identities:

- former Trappist novice;
- Catholic devotional author;
- sometimes anticlerical or sarcastic critic of church institutions;
- anti-Masonic polemicist;
- astrologer;
- occult historian;
- Bonapartist prophet.

The mixture is historically more accurate than forcing him into a single ideological category.

## 1854–1856: *Carmen sibyllinum* and imperial prophecy

Christian dated a prophetic composition **3 April 1854**.

Later published as:

> *“Carmen sybillinum”. 3 avril 1854*

the text was represented as having foretold:
- victory for the French-allied cause in the Crimean War;
- the birth of an imperial heir.

Christian sent a “double ode” or prophetic communication toward the imperial household and received a brief acknowledgment through an official associated with the Empress.

The critical chronological problem is publication.

The prophecy was not printed until **1856**, after the events it was said to predict had occurred. The surviving publication therefore documents the **claim to prior prophecy**, but it does not provide the same evidentiary force as a securely dated public pre-event publication.

Christian dramatized the prophecy through an astrological or visionary state, helping to construct his identity as an inspired interpreter of imperial destiny.

This became one of the clearest examples of his self-created occult mythology.

---

## 1855–1857: the Dom Marie-Bernard authorship fiction

One of the most revealing episodes in Christian’s publishing career was the large Catholic series:

> *Les Héros du christianisme*

It appeared in multiple volumes under the supposed authorship of **Dom Marie-Bernard**, with P. Christian presented as editor, introducer, or annotator.

Christian supplied the supposed monk with a biography.

The name had obvious personal significance: **Marie-Bernard** had been Christian’s own Trappist religious name.

At the end of the project, he disclosed that **he himself had written the entire work** and that the elderly ecclesiastical author was fictional.

This episode is important because it is not an accusation reconstructed by hostile later critics. It is a **documented literary deception eventually acknowledged by Christian himself**.

It demonstrates that Christian was willing to:
- construct an authoritative fictitious author;
- manufacture biographical provenance;
- publish under a persona with religious credibility;
- later reveal the fiction.

That established practice is relevant when assessing his later invented ancient authorities, although each case still requires separate evidence.

## 1859–1860: sacramental Catholic fiction

Christian published a sequence of books organized around the sacraments:

- *Les Coeurs brisés, ou le Sacrement de pénitence*
- *L’Écho des Catacombes, ou le Sacrement de confirmation*
- *La Prison mamertine, ou le Sacrement de baptême*
- *L’Ange de Wehrda, ou le Sacrement de mariage*
- *La Crosse et le glaive, ou le Sacrement de l’ordre*
- *La Forêt vierge, ou le Sacrement de l’extrême-onction*
- *Une page de ma vie, ou le Sacrement de l’Eucharistie*

He also published *Les Fleurs du ciel*.

The series is evidence against a simplistic narrative in which occult study caused a clean break with Catholic religiosity.

## 1863: *L’Homme rouge des Tuileries*

### Publication

In **1863**, Christian published his first major public occult synthesis:

> *L’Homme rouge des Tuileries*

The original title page advertises the book as illustrated with **22 Kabbalistic figures** and gives Christian’s own Paris publishing address at **22 rue d’Angoulême, Champs-Élysées**.

The book blends:
- astrology;
- prophecy;
- Napoleonic folklore;
- numerology;
- historical anecdote;
- occult fiction;
- a radical redesign of tarot symbolism.

### The Red Man legend

The “Red Man of the Tuileries” was not created from nothing by Christian. Napoleonic and royalist folklore had already circulated stories and publications about a mysterious little red man or supernatural adviser connected with the Tuileries and Napoleon.

Christian’s innovation was to absorb that folklore into an elaborate occult literary framework centered on an aged Benedictine-like figure, **Dom Bonaventure Guyon**, who becomes an esoteric adviser and interpreter of destiny.

### The 78 “symbolic houses”

Christian reorganized the tarot’s 78-card structure into **78 symbolic houses or keys** without treating it merely as a gaming deck.

For the four suits he used names corresponding roughly to:

- **Sceptres**
- **Cups**
- **Glaives**
- **Sicles**

His court-card terminology also changed.

Most influentially, he supplied detailed new titles and iconographic instructions for the twenty-two trump figures. Among them:

1. The Magus
2. The Gate of the Sanctuary
3. Isis-Urania
4. The Cubic Stone
5. Master of the Mysteries
6. The Two Roads
7. Chariot of Osiris
8. Themis
9. The Veiled Lamp
10. The Sphinx
11. The Tamed or Muzzled Lion
12. The Sacrifice
13. The Reaper
14. The Two Urns / Genius of the Sun
15. Typhon
16. The Lightning-Struck or Beheaded Tower
17. The Star of the Magi
18. Twilight
19. Blazing Light
20. Awakening / Genius of the Dead
0. The Crocodile
21. The Crown of the Magi

Christian placed the **Crocodile / Fool as 0** between the twentieth and twenty-first numbered mysteries in parts of his system.

### Abstract meanings

He attached abstract meanings such as:

- Will
- Knowledge
- Action
- Realization
- Inspiration
- Test
- Victory
- Equilibrium
- Wisdom
- Fortune
- Strength
- Sacrifice
- Transformation
- Initiative
- Fate
- Ruin
- Hope
- Deception
- Happiness
- Renewal
- Expiation
- Reward

The exact terminology varies in translation and later retelling.

### `Lames` and `arcanes`

Christian helped make **lames** (“blades” or “cards”) and **arcanes** standard words in French occult-tarot writing.

A widespread modern claim says he coined the exact expressions **“Major Arcana”** and **“Minor Arcana.”** The detailed historical analysis by Decker, Depaulis, and Dummett corrects this.

Christian used **arcana/arcanes**, but the paired qualifiers **major/minor** were not his standard terminology. Those expressions become visible later, notably in writers such as **Ely Star** and **Papus** in the late 1880s.

Thus:

- **Supported:** Christian was pivotal in occult use of *arcana/arcanes* and *lames*.
- **Overstatement/myth:** he personally coined the fully formed modern pair “Major Arcana / Minor Arcana.”

### Reception

Sources disagree on the book’s immediate commercial reception.

- Ross Nichols says it had considerable vogue.
- Decker, Depaulis, and Dummett characterize it as too eccentric to achieve much immediate success and say it became more sought after later.

Without sales records, the safest conclusion is that the book’s **long-term influence is much clearer than its first-edition commercial performance**.

The same period produced:

- *L’Esprit du château de Xhénemont*
- *Histoires héroïques des Français, racontées à S. A. Napoléon-Eugène, prince impérial*

The latter makes his alignment with the imperial household explicit in its intended audience and framing.

## 1865–1869: Catholic journalism, pseudonyms, and astrology

### *Le Moniteur catholique*

In **1865–1866**, Christian was editor-in-chief or manager of:

> *Le Moniteur catholique : journal du jeudi et du dimanche*

Again, an overtly Catholic periodical project overlapped with his occult research.

### Charles Moreau

In **1866**, *Fulla l’Égyptienne* appeared under the name **Charles Moreau**, which the BnF authority record attributes to Pitois/Christian.

### Lussides

An **1868** astrological article or pamphlet, *Horoscope de Napoléon Ier*, was signed **Lussides**.

Chacornac attributed this name to Christian, and modern tarot historians consider the attribution strong because of extensive reuse of language and material from *L’Homme rouge*.

It should therefore be listed as **strongly attributed**, while still distinguishing the printed pseudonym from his usual name.

### *Messager du Centre*

Modern scholarship places Christian as editor of the **Messager du Centre** in **1869**.

## 1870: *Histoire de la magie*

### Full title and scale

Christian’s most famous work appeared in **1870**:

> *Histoire de la magie, du monde surnaturel et de la fatalité à travers les temps et les peuples*

The BnF and Wellcome records describe a large illustrated volume of roughly 666 pages plus preliminary matter and plates.

It is organized into seven broad books:

1. **Les portes du monde surnaturel**
2. **Les Mystères des Pyramides**
3. **Les Oracles antiques, les Sibylles et les Sorts**
4. **La Magie depuis l’ère chrétienne jusqu’à la fin du moyen âge**
5. **Curiosités des sciences surnaturelles**
6. **Théorie générale de l’Horoscope**
7. **Clefs générales de l’Astrologie**

The work combines:
- historical compilation;
- legendary history;
- occult doctrine;
- astrology;
- demonology;
- ancient religion;
- Sibylline and oracular traditions;
- grimoires;
- witchcraft;
- tarot-derived images;
- numerology;
- fictional documentation.

### The fictional Egyptian initiation

This is Christian’s most consequential historical fabrication.

In Book II, he invokes **Iamblichus**, the late antique Neoplatonist, and claims that Iamblichus’s *On the Mysteries* supplies or supports the principal tests of Egyptian initiation.

Christian even points to a specific early modern edition, giving the appearance of exact scholarship.

He then narrates an elaborate initiation in or beneath the Sphinx and Great Pyramid. The candidate descends through dangerous spaces, encounters symbolic tests, and eventually reaches a hall associated with **78 stages or symbolic structures** and **22 sacred images**.

The twenty-two images correspond to the same redesigned tarot trumps Christian had published in *L’Homme rouge*.

### What the source actually says

Decker, Depaulis, and Dummett compared the claim with Iamblichus and found that the supposed passage is not there.

The elaborate Egyptian initiation is therefore not a lost or obscure Iamblichean report. It is Christian’s literary construction.

Parts of its structure appear to have been adapted from **Crata Repoa**, an eighteenth-century German Masonic initiation fiction that purported to reveal ancient Egyptian mysteries. Specific ordeal motifs, including temptation scenes, have close precedents there.

### Why the fiction mattered

Christian’s method gave a modern tarot redesign the appearance of independent ancient confirmation.

The logic worked backward:

1. Christian had already developed a 78-part occult structure and 22 redesigned trump images in 1863.
2. In 1870, he placed homologous images inside an allegedly ancient Egyptian initiation.
3. He invoked Iamblichus as authority.
4. He largely avoided foregrounding the word **Tarot** in the Egyptian-initiation passage.
5. A reader could therefore infer that the tarot preserved a genuinely ancient Egyptian initiatory system.

This was historically powerful even though the source claim was false.

It reinforced the broader **Egyptian-origin myth of the tarot**, a myth already developed in eighteenth-century France by writers such as Court de Gébelin, but Christian gave it a new dramatic architecture.

### Court de Gébelin and Cagliostro: a second fabricated historical scene

Elsewhere Christian presents supposed memoir material of **Antoine Court de Gébelin** describing a Masonic-occult encounter in **May 1785**, with Court de Gébelin presiding over or participating in the interrogation of **Cagliostro**.

The historical problem is decisive:

- the relevant Philalethes convention did take place in Paris in 1785;
- Cagliostro did not attend in the manner Christian describes;
- Court de Gébelin had **died on 12 May 1784**, about a year before the alleged 1785 scene.

The supposed memoir testimony is therefore impossible as described.

This is not merely an unresolved legend. The chronology falsifies the scene.

### Attitude toward cartomancy

An important nuance: Christian’s interest in tarot was not simply a handbook approach to ordinary card fortune-telling.

His 1870 book could be dismissive of common cartomancy. He transformed tarot structure into an esoteric cosmology and astrological mechanism rather than treating it primarily as a salon divination game.

### Astrology

The later books of *Histoire de la magie* elaborate Christian’s highly personal system of:
- horoscope construction;
- planetary correspondences;
- zodiacal relations;
- number symbolism;
- name analysis;
- “fatidic” or destiny circles;
- correlations with the 78 arcana.

Later historians judge much of the system idiosyncratic and internally arbitrary. Its historical significance lies less in scientific or mathematical coherence than in how it fused tarot, astrology, Egyptian symbolism, and occult initiation.

## 1870–1872: collapse of the Empire and last works

The **Franco-Prussian War**, defeat of Napoleon III, and collapse of the Second Empire in 1870 destroyed the political order Christian had increasingly celebrated.

In **1872**, he published:

> *Histoire de la guerre avec la Prusse et des deux sièges de Paris, 1870–1871*

in two volumes.

This appears to have been his last major published historical work.

## 1871: unpublished occult teaching and declining health

Ross Nichols and later tarot historians report surviving or once-accessible letters from **February to June 1871** addressed to a pupil named **Jacques Charrot**.

The letters concern an “onomantic” or name-based astrology and include horoscopic material involving figures such as Éliphas Lévi and the occult cartomancer Edmond.

They also reportedly contain:
- complaints about Christian’s health;
- plans or hopes to establish a **“Faculty of Occult Sciences.”**

No evidence was found that the proposed faculty became a functioning institution.

The letters are significant because they show Christian still developing a pedagogical occult project after the publication of *Histoire de la magie*.

## Political / Religious / Intellectual / Artistic Development

## Catholic formation and persistence

Christian’s religious trajectory contains continuity beneath apparent contradiction.

- Catholic childhood education
- Trappist novitiate
- use of the religious name Marie-Bernard
- Catholic historical writing
- large devotional output
- editorial work on *Le Moniteur catholique*
- continued references to Christian moral and sacramental structures

His later writing could nevertheless attack clerical institutions, mock dogmatism, and criticize Masonic as well as ecclesiastical claims.

“Occultist” and “Catholic” were therefore overlapping rather than mutually exclusive categories in his life.

## Revolutionary politics

The documented 1848 Jacobin presidency prevents any account of Christian as uniformly conservative.

His speech to the Club central des Jacobins and his newspaper editorship place him directly in radical republican political organization during the revolutionary year.

## Post-1848 reorientation

After arrest, transportation, pardon, and return:
- *Dieu et France. Agonie de la Révolution* signaled a changed political register.
- His later books became increasingly compatible with the imperial order.
- He cultivated Napoleonic symbolism and prophecy.
- He addressed work to the Prince Imperial.
- He represented Napoleon III’s dynasty as astrologically significant.

Whether this was ideological conversion, adaptation to political reality, personal Bonapartism, or a mixture cannot be established from the available record.

## Occult synthesis

Christian’s mature occultism combined:

- Lévi-era French occultism
- Renaissance astrology
- Egyptianizing Freemasonry and pseudo-Masonry
- Court de Gébelin’s Egyptian tarot myth
- tarot iconography
- number symbolism
- prophetic folklore
- Catholic imagery
- Napoleonic legend
- antiquarian scholarship
- literary invention

His originality lies less in discovering an ancient tradition than in **constructing a persuasive modern synthesis and giving it the appearance of antiquity**.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Charles Nodier

**Relationship:** literary acquaintance/collaborator and possible mentor.

**Documentary basis:** Christian contributed to *Paris historique* in a project headed by Nodier.

**Later amplification:** occult biographies sometimes present a more intimate master-pupil relationship than the publication record alone establishes.

### Pitois-Levrault family

**Relationship:** publishing relatives.

**Importance:** likely provided early familiarity with bookselling, printing, and literary commerce.

### Comte de Salvandy

Ross Nichols associates Christian’s Ministry of Public Instruction appointment with the administration of **Narcisse-Achille de Salvandy**.

The appointment context is plausible and consistent with the period, though no personnel file was located.

### Marshal Thomas-Robert Bugeaud

**Relationship:** employer/patron during North African service.

Christian’s later colonial writing shows that service did not prevent criticism of conquest practices.

### Éliphas Lévi

**Relationship:** major occult influence; probably personal acquaintance.

Chacornac’s tradition presents Christian as neighbor/friend/pupil. Modern scholarship accepts strong intellectual influence but is more cautious about reconstructing an extensive formal discipleship.

### Napoleon III and Empress Eugénie

**Relationship:** distant patronage/imperial audience rather than demonstrated friendship.

Christian sent prophetic material to the imperial household and received acknowledgment. His later literary work openly cultivated imperial symbolism.

No evidence of an intimate advisory role at court was located.

### Napoléon-Eugène, Prince Imperial

Recipient or intended audience of Christian’s *Histoires héroïques des Français*.

### Edmond Billaudot

A nineteenth-century French cartomancer/occult practitioner whose later **Grand Tarot Belline** tradition reflects Christian’s nomenclature and iconography.

The degree of direct personal contact is less secure than the intellectual/iconographic relationship.

### Jacques Charrot

Described in later biography as Christian’s pupil and recipient of his 1871 occult letters.

### Honoré de Balzac

Christian referred to Balzac and represented himself as having had conversation or acquaintance with him.

No independent evidence establishing a significant Balzac-Christian relationship was located. It should remain a **self-claim**.

### Paul Christian fils

Christian’s son was likely instrumental in constructing or preserving his father’s adventurous posthumous biography.

The younger man is important not only as family but as an early **reputation manager and myth transmitter**.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

No evidence was located linking Pitois to modern categories such as corporate fraud, money laundering, bribery, embezzlement, or organized financial crime.

His controversies are instead political, historiographical, literary, and occult.

## 1. 1848 arrest, transportation, and pardon

**Status:** documented in serious biographical reference; not equivalent to a located criminal conviction.

- Arrest order dated 23 June 1848.
- Arrest reportedly executed 31 July.
- Papers seized at his home were considered compromising.
- He was “transported.”
- He was pardoned and returned in December 1849.

Further archival work would be needed to establish:
- exact legal authority;
- destination;
- detention conditions;
- formal accusations;
- tribunal proceedings, if any;
- whether “participation in the June Days” was proved, inferred, or administratively alleged.

## 2. Colonial entanglement

**Status:** documented service and publications.

Christian served Bugeaud during the French conquest of Algeria and wrote extensively about Algeria and Morocco.

This placed him within the machinery of a violent colonial expansion. At the same time, his writings included criticism of French methods and descriptions of atrocities.

No evidence was found that Christian personally ordered or carried out colonial violence.

## 3. Dom Marie-Bernard fictitious authorship

**Status:** documented literary deception, later admitted by Christian.

Christian:
- created a fictitious monk-author;
- supplied the persona with a life history;
- presented himself as editor;
- eventually disclosed that he had written the work himself.

Whether contemporaries regarded this primarily as fraud, literary play, marketing, or pseudonymous authorship would require reception research beyond the sources located.

## 4. False attribution to Iamblichus

**Status:** demonstrably false source claim.

Christian presented his detailed Egyptian-initiation narrative as if grounded in Iamblichus.

Direct comparison shows that Iamblichus does not contain the narrative.

The source misrepresentation is therefore factual. Claims about Christian’s motive—deliberate deception, joke, esoteric literary method, or commercial fabrication—are interpretive.

Decker, Depaulis, and Dummett use unusually severe language and regard the fabrication as deliberate.

## 5. Fabricated Court de Gébelin memoir scene

**Status:** demonstrably chronologically impossible.

Christian’s supposed 1785 scene involving Court de Gébelin fails because Court de Gébelin died in 1784.

Again, motive can be debated; the chronology cannot.

## 6. Retrospective prophecy

**Status:** documented post-event publication of a claimed pre-event prophecy.

The *Carmen sibyllinum* was dated 1854 but published after the predicted outcomes.

This prevents the printed edition alone from proving successful pre-event prediction.

## 7. Bibliographic self-multiplication

Christian’s use of multiple pseudonyms and invented authorial personae complicates the bibliography.

Not every occult work later attributed to him can be accepted automatically. In particular, the early occult identities **Hortensius Flamel** and **Frédéric de la Grange** remain attributions rather than secure signatures.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## Myth 1 — The Caribbean abolitionist and Spanish partisan

### 1. What the story says

After his religious studies, the young Pitois supposedly traveled to a Caribbean colony, agitated against slavery or participated in a native/anti-slavery rising, and then went to Spain, where he became involved in partisan warfare.

### 2. When and where it appears

The story appears in posthumous biographical material associated with *La Lumière* in **1893–1894**, apparently derived from or attributed to **Paul Christian fils**.

Ross Nichols later retold it.

### 3. Who circulated it

- Christian’s son or family circle
- *La Lumière*
- later occult biographers
- Ross Nichols

### 4. Evidence for or against

- No contemporary documentary corroboration was found.
- Sources disagree between **Martinique** and **Dominica**.
- The Dominica version contains an apparent colonial-geography problem in some retellings.
- Modern tarot historians explicitly treat the episode cautiously and doubt the military-adventure component.

### 5. Effect on reputation

The story created a romantic prehistory for Christian:
- youthful rebel;
- abolitionist;
- traveler;
- fighter;
- man of action before becoming scholar and magician.

It contributes strongly to the “adventurer-magus” image.

---

## Myth 2 — Quicksand, Kabyle captor, pistol, rescue, and bullet wounds

### 1. What the story says

During North African travel, Christian supposedly:
- became trapped in quicksand;
- was threatened with decapitation by a Kabyle;
- reached for a pistol he believed unloaded;
- found it unexpectedly usable;
- was rescued by French soldiers;
- later bore two bullet wounds.

### 2. When and where it appears

The narrative is transmitted through the posthumous biographical tradition, reportedly drawing on diary material.

### 3. Who circulated it

- the Christian family/posthumous biographical circle;
- later occult biographers.

### 4. Evidence for or against

His presence in North Africa and service with Bugeaud are well supported.

The specific action sequence has not been independently confirmed in military reports, correspondence, or contemporary newspapers in the sources consulted.

### 5. Effect on reputation

It transformed a secretary/reporter into a near-picaresque colonial adventurer and reinforced the later image of a magician whose authority came from dangerous lived experience.

---

## Myth 3 — Dom Marie-Bernard, the nonexistent monk-author

### 1. What the story says

A venerable monk, Dom Marie-Bernard, supposedly authored a major series on Christian heroes; P. Christian merely edited or presented the work.

### 2. When and where it appears

In the publication apparatus of *Les Héros du christianisme* (1855–1857).

### 3. Who circulated it

Christian himself and the publishers of the work.

### 4. Evidence for or against

Christian eventually disclosed that he had written the entire series and that the supposed monk was fictional.

### 5. Effect on reputation

The episode demonstrates a proven willingness to create documentary-seeming authorial identities.

It has become important to modern historians assessing the evidentiary status of his later occult “sources.”

---

## Myth 4 — The 1854 double imperial prophecy

### 1. What the story says

Christian claimed that on 3 April 1854 he foresaw both:
- allied/French victory in the Crimean conflict;
- the continuation of the Bonaparte dynasty through an imperial heir.

### 2. When and where it appears

The prophecy was published in **1856** as *Carmen sibyllinum*, while carrying the earlier date of 3 April 1854.

### 3. Who circulated it

Christian himself, with an imperial acknowledgment forming part of the story’s authority.

### 4. Evidence for or against

There is evidence that material was addressed to the imperial household and acknowledged.

The decisive limitation is that the surviving printed publication came after the events.

### 5. Effect on reputation

It strengthened Christian’s persona as:
- astrologer;
- prophet;
- reader of dynastic destiny;
- occult intellectual with access to imperial attention.

---

## Myth 5 — The Red Man of the Tuileries

### 1. What the story says

A mysterious red-clad supernatural or semi-supernatural figure appeared around the Tuileries and Napoleon, warning, guiding, or predicting political destiny.

Christian transformed the figure into a learned occult adviser.

### 2. When and where it appears

The legend predates Christian in early nineteenth-century Napoleonic folklore and pamphlet literature.

Christian’s major version is *L’Homme rouge des Tuileries* (1863).

### 3. Who circulated it

- earlier Napoleonic popular literature;
- theatrical and pamphlet culture;
- Christian;
- later occultists.

### 4. Evidence for or against

Evidence exists for the **folklore**, not for a literal supernatural adviser.

Christian’s narrative belongs to literary-occult elaboration.

### 5. Effect on reputation

It fused:
- Napoleon;
- prophecy;
- Catholic-monastic imagery;
- astrology;
- tarot;
- French political legend.

This gave Christian a national rather than merely antiquarian occult mythology.

---

## Myth 6 — The Egyptian tarot initiation described by Iamblichus

### 1. What the story says

Ancient Egyptian initiates supposedly passed through a sequence of ordeals beneath or around the Sphinx and Great Pyramid and encountered twenty-two sacred symbolic images corresponding to the tarot trumps.

Christian presented Iamblichus as an ancient authority for the initiation.

### 2. When and where it appears

The mature form appears in Book II of *Histoire de la magie* (1870).

Its imagery is built from material already used in *L’Homme rouge des Tuileries* (1863).

### 3. Who circulated it

- Paul Christian
- later French occultists
- tarot authors
- designers of Egyptianizing tarot packs
- popular occult histories

### 4. Evidence for or against

Against:
- the claimed Iamblichus passage does not exist;
- the tarot structure is recognizably Christian’s own nineteenth-century construction;
- parts of the ordeal sequence resemble *Crata Repoa*, an eighteenth-century pseudo-Egyptian Masonic initiation fiction.

For:
- there is evidence that ancient Egyptian religion used complex temple ritual and initiation-like priestly structures in broad terms, but not Christian’s specific 22-tarot-image narrative.

The precise Christian story is therefore literary invention, not recovered ancient documentation.

### 5. Effect on reputation

This may be Christian’s single most influential myth.

It:
- supplied a dramatic “ancient” origin scene for the occult tarot;
- gave later initiatory occultism a ready-made ritual architecture;
- encouraged Egyptianizing tarot imagery;
- helped detach the tarot’s esoteric history from the actual documented history of European playing cards.

---

## Myth 7 — Court de Gébelin interrogates Cagliostro in 1785

### 1. What the story says

Christian claimed to possess or transmit memoir material in which Court de Gébelin participated in a 1785 occult/Masonic encounter involving Cagliostro.

### 2. When and where it appears

*Histoire de la magie* (1870).

### 3. Who circulated it

Christian; later readers who accepted his historical apparatus.

### 4. Evidence for or against

Decisive evidence against:
- Court de Gébelin died **12 May 1784**.
- The alleged meeting is placed in **1785**.
- The relevant Masonic convention was real, but Christian populated it with people who were not there in the stated manner.

### 5. Effect on reputation

For believers, the episode connected Christian to a secret eighteenth-century chain of occult transmission.

For modern historians, it is one of the clearest demonstrations that his historical notes cannot be accepted without external checking.

---

## Myth 8 — Edmond, Napoleon III, and the future Grand Tarot Belline

### 1. What the story says

The cartomancer **Edmond Billaudot** was supposedly summoned to Napoleon III and used occult divination in an imperial context.

A later card associated with the deck now called the **Grand Tarot Belline** helps transmit the story.

### 2. When and where it appears

In the later tradition surrounding Edmond/Belline decks and in twentieth-century occult discussion.

### 3. Who circulated it

Edmond/Belline tradition and later tarot writers.

### 4. Evidence for or against

The deck’s dependence on Christian’s nomenclature and system is visible.

The claimed imperial consultation is not securely documented in the sources used here.

### 5. Effect on reputation

It placed Christian’s symbolic system in an imagined chain running from occult author to professional diviner to the imperial court and then to a commercially enduring tarot deck.

---

## Myth 9 — “Paul Christian coined Major Arcana and Minor Arcana”

### 1. What the story says

Many modern summaries credit Christian with inventing the exact tarot terms “Major Arcana” and “Minor Arcana.”

### 2. When and where it appears

The claim is common in twentieth- and twenty-first-century popular tarot history.

### 3. Who circulated it

Popular tarot authors, websites, encyclopedic summaries, and derivative histories.

### 4. Evidence for or against

Historical tarot scholarship finds that Christian used and popularized **arcana/arcanes**, but not the modern paired terminology in its mature form.

“Major” and “minor” emerge in later occultists, including **Ely Star** and **Papus**, in the 1880s.

### 5. Effect on reputation

The myth overstates one part of Christian’s genuine influence.

A more accurate claim is stronger historically: he helped make **arcana** and **lames** basic occult-tarot vocabulary and supplied influential new trump names and images.

---

## Myth 10 — Balzac as personal occult interlocutor

### 1. What the story says

Christian presents himself as having known or conversed with **Honoré de Balzac**.

### 2. When and where it appears

In Christian’s own literary-occult recollections.

### 3. Who circulated it

Christian and later biographers repeating him.

### 4. Evidence for or against

No independent documentary confirmation was located in this research.

### 5. Effect on reputation

The association situates Christian inside the prestigious literary-occult Paris of Balzac, Nodier, and Romantic supernaturalism.

Until corroborated, it should remain a self-claim rather than a documented friendship.

## Posthumous Editions and Translations of Christian’s Occult Works

### *L’Homme rouge des Tuileries*
- Reprinted photographically and in occult facsimile editions, including a notable 1937 reprint.
- Portions of its tarot imagery entered later tarot manuals and decks independently of full reprints.

### *Histoire de la magie*
- Reprinted repeatedly in French.
- Its occult sections circulated in excerpted, adapted, and translated form.

### *The History and Practice of Magic*
- English-language form of Christian’s major occult history.
- First English publication is generally catalogued around **1952**, though some later organizational biographies give **1954**.
- Translation associated with **James Kirkup** for Books I–V and **Julian Shaw** for Books VI–VII.
- Edited/revised with biographical and supplementary material by **Ross Nichols**.
- Later editions appeared through Citadel and other occult publishers.

Because Nichols added editorial framing and supplementary matter, the English book should not be treated as a simple untouched authorial equivalent of the 1870 French edition.

## Books and Major Works About Jean-Baptiste Pitois / Paul Christian

No full modern archival biography dedicated exclusively to Pitois was located. The most substantial discussions occur inside broader histories of occult tarot, Éliphas Lévi, French occultism, and colonial history.

### Ronald Decker, Thierry Depaulis, Michael Dummett
**A Wicked Pack of Cards: The Origins of the Occult Tarot**. Duckworth, 1996.

This is the most important modern critical source located for Pitois.

It provides:
- detailed biographical reconstruction;
- analysis of his aliases and literary career;
- reconstruction of *L’Homme rouge*;
- comparison between Christian’s Egyptian-initiation claim and Iamblichus;
- identification of likely *Crata Repoa* borrowing;
- analysis of the false Court de Gébelin/Cagliostro scene;
- careful history of his terminology and influence on tarot.

Its authors are openly hostile to Christian’s historical fabrications and sometimes use polemical descriptors such as “charlatan.” Their factual demonstrations can be separated from their judgments.

### Ronald Decker and Michael Dummett
**A History of the Occult Tarot, 1870–1970**. Duckworth, 2002.

Continues the history from the period in which Christian’s *Histoire de la magie* appeared.

Useful for:

- Papus;
- Ely Star;
- tarot terminology;
- Egyptianizing traditions;
- deck lineages.

### Paul Chacornac
**Éliphas Lévi, rénovateur de l’occultisme en France (1810–1875)**. Paris, 1926.

A major early occultist biography of Lévi containing biographical information about Christian.

**Eliphas Lévi and the French Occult Revival**. First published in the 1970s; later editions reissued.

Broader study of the French occult revival with Christian in Lévi’s intellectual network.

## Death / Later Life / Present Status

### Declining health

The late correspondence described by Ross Nichols indicates health problems by 1871.

No precise diagnosis was located.

No reliable evidence was found for:
- addiction;
- psychiatric hospitalization;
- suicide attempt;
- major late-life accident;
- specific terminal illness.

### 1877 death

The strongest reconstructed date is:

> **12 July 1877, Lyon**

The Geneanet entry gives **Lyon 2e arrondissement** and age 66.

This agrees with the standard 1811–1877 identity used by the University of Pennsylvania Online Books Page and modern occult scholarship.

### BnF conflict

The BnF authority record currently gives:

> **7 December 1872**

This date is difficult to reconcile with the stronger 1877 biographical tradition.

Because national-library authority files can perpetuate inherited cataloguing errors, and because multiple independent later sources support 1877, the dossier uses **12 July 1877** as the best-supported death date while preserving the BnF disagreement explicitly.

### Cause of death

No cause of death was securely established in the consulted sources.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

## 1. From prolific man of letters to “occultist”

During his lifetime Christian published across an unusually broad range:
- journalism;
- Catholic devotion;
- translation;
- political polemic;
- colonial history;
- revolutionary history;
- pirate history;
- fantastic literature;
- bibliography;
- astrology;
- occultism.

Posthumous memory dramatically narrowed this career.

Today he is overwhelmingly remembered for:
- *L’Homme rouge des Tuileries*;
- *Histoire de la magie*;
- tarot;
- astrology;
- the invented Egyptian initiation.

His mainstream historical, devotional, and colonial books became comparatively obscure.

## 2. Tarot vocabulary

Christian’s role in establishing **arcana/arcanes** and **lames** as occult-tarot vocabulary was durable.

Later writers normalized the language to the point that its nineteenth-century origin became invisible.

## 3. Tarot iconography

Christian did more than rename cards.

He supplied:
- elaborate visual specifications;
- Egyptian deities and symbols;
- a Crocodile Fool;
- Isis-Urania;
- Osiris;
- Typhon;
- the Sphinx;
- the Crown of the Magi;
- new allegorical titles and meanings.

Later occult authors and deck designers repeatedly drew on this repertoire.

## 4. Grand Tarot Belline

The deck tradition now known as the **Grand Tarot Belline**, associated with Edmond Billaudot and later Belline, visibly inherits Christian’s nomenclature and symbolic architecture.

This is one of the clearest material afterlives of his 1863 system.

## 5. Ely Star

**Ely Star**, especially in *Les Mystères de l’Horoscope* (1888), borrowed heavily from the astrological-occult framework developed by Christian.

The later terminology of major/minor arcana appears in this post-Christian phase.

## 6. Papus

**Papus (Gérard Encausse)** incorporated Christian into the canon of modern French occult predecessors.

By the late 1880s, Papus and his contemporaries were creating a synthetic tarot vocabulary in which Lévi’s Kabbalistic scheme and Christian’s arcana/lame language could coexist.

Christian therefore became one of the intellectual ancestors of the fin-de-siècle French occult revival despite not belonging to its later formal organizations.

## 7. Egyptianizing tarot

Christian’s fictional initiation was exceptionally fertile because it could be converted into visual design and ritual sequence.

Later Egyptianizing tarot writers and artists inherited an atmosphere in which:
- tarot trumps were ancient mysteries;
- the mysteries belonged to Egypt;
- initiation took place through staged symbolic chambers;
- each trump corresponded to an initiatory ordeal or doctrinal secret.

Christian did not originate every element of this myth, but he gave the myth one of its most elaborate nineteenth-century narrative forms.

## 8. Historical criticism

Twentieth-century historical work on playing cards and occult tarot substantially changed Christian’s reputation.

Writers such as Michael Dummett, Thierry Depaulis, and Ronald Decker:
- recognized his real innovations;
- reconstructed his borrowings;
- separated eighteenth-century tarot mythology from documented card history;
- demonstrated that the Iamblichus attribution was false;
- exposed the impossible Court de Gébelin chronology.

The result is a double legacy:

**Historically false antiquity:**  
Christian invented or misattributed major parts of the ancient story he told.

**Historically real modern influence:**  
The inventions themselves became important components of Western occultism.

## 9. Colonial historiographical afterlife

Modern scholarship on French Algeria has recovered a different Christian:
- Bugeaud’s secretary;
- historian of conquest;
- transmitter of military testimony;
- critic of some French pacification practices.

This non-occult Pitois remains much less known than “Paul Christian the magician.”

## 10. Political rediscovery

The *Maitron* record restores still another identity:
- revolutionary political activist;
- Jacobin club president;
- editor of a radical paper;
- detainee/transportee of 1848;
- pardoned returnee who later moved toward imperial cultural politics.

This makes his life a particularly compressed example of the ideological mobility of nineteenth-century France.

Associated with *Gazette des salons*.

*Le Livre rouge* under Hortensius Flamel later attributed to Christian; attribution remains disputed.

Publishes *L’Algérie de la jeunesse*.

**29 March** — delivers opening speech as provisional president of the **Club central des Jacobins**.

**December** — returns after pardon, according to *Maitron*.

Publishes sequence of Catholic sacramental fiction/devotional books.

### 1863
Publishes **L’Homme rouge des Tuileries** with 22 redesigned tarot-derived figures.

Edits/manages *Le Moniteur catholique*.

### 1866
Publishes *Fulla l’Égyptienne* under **Charles Moreau**.

### 1868
*Horoscope de Napoléon Ier* appears under **Lussides**, strongly attributed to Christian.

### 1869
Associated with editorship of *Messager du Centre*.

### 1870
Publishes **Histoire de la magie, du monde surnaturel et de la fatalité à travers les temps et les peuples**.  
The Franco-Prussian War and fall of Napoleon III end the imperial political order Christian had celebrated.

### 1871
Letters to Jacques Charrot reportedly discuss occult teaching, onomantic astrology, ill health, and a projected Faculty of Occult Sciences.

### 1872
Publishes *Histoire de la guerre avec la Prusse et des deux sièges de Paris, 1870–1871*.

### 1877
**26 June** — Geneanet record citing Lyon marriage registers gives marriage to Louise Marie Tortillet; primary act not independently checked here.  
**12 July** — strongest evidence for death in Lyon, age 66.

### 1893–1894
*La Lumière* publishes a biographical series about Christian, attributed in later indexing to **Paul Christian fils**; source for several adventure legends.

### 1894
Paul Christian fils publishes *La Reine Zinzarah: Comment on devient sorcier*.

### 1937
Notable photographic/facsimile reissue of *L’Homme rouge des Tuileries*.

### c. 1952
First major English edition of Christian’s occult history appears as *The History and Practice of Magic*, translated by James Kirkup and Julian Shaw and edited by Ross Nichols. Some later sources give 1954 for Nichols’s edition/history.

### 1984
Thierry Depaulis’s BnF exhibition catalogue *Tarot, jeu et magie* places Christian within a rigorous history of tarot and emphasizes his terminology.

### 1996
Decker, Depaulis, and Dummett publish *A Wicked Pack of Cards*, offering the most substantial modern critical reconstruction of Christian and demonstrating the invented nature of his Iamblichus-based Egyptian initiation.

### 2002
Decker and Dummett’s *A History of the Occult Tarot, 1870–1970* traces the later traditions that developed from the period opened by Christian’s 1870 book.

## Major Unresolved Questions for Further Archival Research

1. **Birth act:** obtain the Remiremont civil birth registration to establish the exact legal name, parentage, and status at birth.
2. **Father:** determine whether any document supports Chacornac’s speculation that “Christian” derived from his father.
3. **Education:** identify the exact Harcourt institution and documentary record of his studies.
4. **La Trappe:** locate monastery records for the 1828 novitiate and the name Marie-Bernard.
5. **Caribbean/Spain:** test the *La Lumière* adventure story against passports, colonial records, military files, and newspapers.
6. **Ministry employment:** retrieve his personnel dossier from the Ministry of Public Instruction.
7. **Bugeaud service:** identify exact appointment dates, pay, duties, military movement, correspondence, and injury records.
8. **1848 case:** recover the executive arrest dossier, seized-paper inventory, transportation destination, detention file, and pardon instrument.
9. **Marriage:** reconcile the 1853 *Maitron* claim with the 26 June 1877 Lyon record.
10. **Children:** verify all three children through civil birth acts and establish their household history.
11. **Lévi relationship:** recover correspondence or diaries establishing the frequency and character of personal contact.
12. **Carmen sibyllinum:** locate any securely dated 1854 manuscript, dépôt, palace receipt, or sealed pre-publication copy capable of testing the prophecy’s date independently.
13. **Hortensius Flamel / Frédéric de la Grange:** perform typographical, publisher-network, textual, and archival authorship analysis.
14. **Jacques Charrot letters:** locate the present archive or provenance of the 1871 correspondence.
15. **Finances:** reconstruct author payments, government salary, direct-publishing income, property, and estate.
16. **Death:** inspect the Lyon death act for address, occupation, witnesses, spouse status, and possible cause-of-death clues.
17. **La Lumière biography:** identify the complete 1893–1894 serial, establish its author from editorial records, and compare it with family papers.
18. **Sales/reception:** reconstruct first-edition print runs and reviews of *L’Homme rouge* and *Histoire de la magie* to resolve conflicting claims about immediate popularity.
