## Works by Michael Faraday

### Working manuscripts and documentary projects

- **1812–1813:** *Notes of Humphry Davy’s Lectures*. A carefully written and illustrated manuscript volume made from lectures Faraday attended, then bound and presented to Davy; it is Faraday’s work as auditor, draftsman, and bookbinder, not authorship of Davy’s lectures. [Jeffreys; RI Papers]
- **1813–1815:** *Continental Tour Journal*. Faraday’s manuscript record of his journey through Europe as Davy’s assistant. It remained an archival travel diary rather than a book planned for publication and appeared posthumously as *“Curiosity Perfectly Satisfyed”: Faraday’s Travels in Europe 1813–1815*. [RI Publications]
- **1816–1818:** *City Philosophical Society essays*. A project of self-education through papers read to an artisan essay-circle; identifiable pieces include *Some Observations on the Means of Obtaining Knowledge* (1817) and *Observations on the Inertia of the Mind* (1818), but the surviving corpus is not a uniformly published “work.” [Jenkins]
- **1819:** *Journal of a Tour through Wales*. A manuscript journey-record, subsequently published from Faraday’s papers; its status is diary rather than retrospective travel book. [Jeffreys]
- **1820–1862:** *Experimental Notes*, commonly called *Faraday’s Diary*. Eleven manuscript laboratory notebooks recording experiments, plans, failures, diagrams, and conclusions; the seven-volume printed *Diary* of 1932–1936 is Thomas Martin’s posthumous manifestation of this working archive, not a book Faraday composed for publication. [RI Papers; AIP]
- **1822:** *Chemical Notes, Hints, Suggestions and Objects of Pursuit*. A manuscript research agenda: part notebook, part list of possible experiments, and therefore more accurately a project than a finished treatise. It was first published in a scholarly edition in 1991. [RI Publications]
- **1811–1867:** *Correspondence*. Approximately 4,900 extant letters constitute a dispersed epistolary body of work rather than one authored book; Frank A. J. L. James’s six-volume edition includes letters both by and to Faraday, so the edition is larger than Faraday’s own textual production. [RI Correspondence]

### Essays, protocols, reports, and books

- **1816:** *Analysis of the Native Caustic Lime of Tuscany*. Faraday’s first published scientific paper, an analytical-chemistry report. [RSC]
- **1817:** *Some Observations on the Means of Obtaining Knowledge*. An essay first read to the City Philosophical Society and then printed separately; it is both a public address and a manifesto for disciplined self-education. [Jenkins; Jeffreys]
- **1821:** *Historical Sketch of Electro-Magnetism*. A two-part, initially anonymous survey in the *Annals of Philosophy*; its anonymity and its mixture of history, review, and intervention make it resist classification as a conventional research paper. [Jeffreys]
- **1827:** *Chemical Manipulation: Being Instructions to Students in Chemistry*. Faraday’s only independently conceived book rather than a collection of papers or lecture transcripts. It is chiefly a protocol: an extensive manual for performing chemical experiments accurately and safely, revised by Faraday in 1830 and 1842. [RI Publications]
- **1831:** *On the Alleged Decline of Science in England*. Faraday edited this pamphlet from Charles Babbage’s work; it belongs to his editorial production, not to his authorship, and catalogues sometimes obscure that distinction. [Jeffreys]
- **1831–1856:** *Experimental Researches in Electricity*, Series I–XXX. A continuing research project issued as separately communicated papers rather than conceived as a single book; Series I–XIV were later gathered in volume I, XV–XVIII in volume II, and XIX–XXIX in volume III, while the thirtieth series fell outside the three-volume collection. [LOC; Cambridge; Jeffreys]
- **1833:** *On the Practical Prevention of Dry Rot in Timber*. The printed substance of a Royal Institution lecture, combining experimental report, technical advice, and practical protocol. [CiNii; Jeffreys]
- **1839:** *Experimental Researches in Electricity*, volume I. A collected manifestation of Series I–XIV, reprinted from papers of 1831–1838; Faraday explicitly presented it as a faithful record of investigations written piecemeal rather than a retrospectively unified treatise. [LOC]
- **1844:** *Experimental Researches in Electricity*, volume II. A collected manifestation containing Series XV–XVIII and related electrical papers; it overlaps with already published articles and is therefore not a wholly new work. [LOC]
- **1854:** *Observations on Mental Education*. A separately printed Royal Institution lecture delivered before Prince Albert; it acts partly as a manifesto for experimental habits of judgment rather than merely describing education. [Google Books; Jeffreys]
- **1855:** *Experimental Researches in Electricity*, volume III. A collected manifestation containing Series XIX–XXIX and related papers on magnetism, light, and lines of force. The later Series XXX is absent. [LOC; Cambridge]
- **1857:** *Some Thoughts on the Conservation of Force*. A Royal Institution discourse and speculative scientific essay, later reprinted in Edward L. Youmans’s *The Correlation and Conservation of Forces* (1865); 1865 is the date of that manifestation, not the original work. [Jeffreys; Smithsonian]
- **1859:** *Experimental Researches in Chemistry and Physics*. Faraday’s authorized collection of earlier papers and lectures outside the principal electrical series. It is a curated body of previously made texts, not a newly written continuous treatise. [RI Publications; RSC]
- **1860:** *A Course of Six Lectures on the Various Forces of Matter, and Their Relations to Each Other*. William Crookes’s edited report of Faraday’s 1859 juvenile lectures; the performances were Faraday’s, but the continuous printed wording is mediated by a reporter-editor and should not be treated as an authorial manuscript. [RI Publications; BHL]
- **1861:** *A Course of Six Lectures on the Chemical History of a Candle*. Crookes’s edited report of Faraday’s 1860 Christmas lectures, with a lecture on platinum added in some manifestations. It is a post-performance reconstruction, not a book manuscript written by Faraday. [RI Publications; Jeffreys]
- **1873:** *On the Various Forces in Nature*. A posthumous manifestation of the lectures on forces, again mediated by Crookes; it is not an additional lecture project merely because its title and publication date differ. [RI Publications]
- **1896:** *The Liquefaction of Gases*. A posthumous collection of Faraday’s papers from 1823–1845; it names a real body of experimental writing but was not assembled by Faraday as a lifetime book. [RI Publications]

### Christmas lecture-series obras

- **1827:** *Chemistry*. Faraday’s first Royal Institution juvenile or Christmas lecture series: a performed, demonstration-based obra whose surviving notes and reports do not amount to a verbatim authored text. [Jeffreys; RI History]
- **1829:** *Electricity*. A juvenile lecture series made from speech, apparatus, and live experiment; its work-status lies in the performances, not in a definitive book. [Jeffreys]
- **1832:** *Chemistry*. A lecture series distinct from the 1827 series despite the shared generic title. [Jeffreys]
- **1835:** *Electricity*. A second series under that title, not an edition or reprint of the 1829 performances. [Jeffreys]
- **1837:** *Chemistry*. Another independently delivered juvenile course whose title alone does not preserve its changing demonstrations. [Jeffreys]
- **1841:** *The Rudiments of Chemistry*. A performed course combining instruction with experiment. [Jeffreys]
- **1843:** *First Principles of Electricity*. A performed experimental course for young audiences. [Jeffreys]
- **1845:** *The Rudiments of Chemistry*. A new lecture series sharing the 1841 title; it should not be silently collapsed into the earlier course. [Jeffreys]
- **1848:** *The Chemical History of a Candle*. The first lecture-series version of this obra; the famous printed book records the substantially later 1860 series, not this performance cycle. [Jeffreys]
- **1851:** *Attractive Forces*. A demonstration-led lecture series, sometimes catalogued under the near-variant *Attraction*. [Jeffreys]
- **1852:** *Chemistry*. A juvenile course with a generic contemporary title; no more specific title should be invented. [Jeffreys]
- **1853:** *Voltaic Electricity*. A performed course on chemical electricity. [Jeffreys]
- **1854:** *The Chemistry of Combustion*. A lecture series whose experiments and performance are integral to the work. [Jeffreys]
- **1855:** *The Distinctive Properties of the Common Metals*. A juvenile experimental series. [Jeffreys]
- **1856:** *Attractive Forces*. A new course, also reported as *Attraction*; the title varies in later lists. [Jeffreys]
- **1857:** *Static Electricity*. A juvenile experimental course. [Jeffreys]
- **1858:** *The Metallic Properties*. A lecture series also listed as *Metalline Properties*; the title is not stable across catalogues. [Jeffreys]
- **1859:** *The Various Forces of Matter and Their Relations to Each Other*. A six-lecture performance later transformed by Crookes into the 1860 book; lecture and transcription are related manifestations, not identical objects. [Jeffreys; RI Archive]
- **1860–1861:** *The Chemical History of a Candle*. Faraday’s final Christmas course and the performance underlying Crookes’s 1861 book; sources variously label it 1860 or 1860/61 because it crossed the calendar year. [Jeffreys; RI Archive]

### Institutional projects

- **1825 onward:** *Royal Institution Friday Evening Discourses*. Faraday shaped and sustained this public-science format and delivered many individual discourses within it. The institution cautions that the series evolved gradually and has no uncontested founding date, so it is better classified as a continuing institutional project than as Faraday’s sole creation. [RI History]
- **1825 onward:** *Royal Institution Christmas Lectures*. Faraday helped establish the demonstration-rich lecture form and subsequently delivered nineteen series, but the Royal Institution’s Managers initiated the programme and John Millington delivered the first course; calling Faraday its sole inventor overstates the attribution. [RI History; RI Archive]

### Apparatus, specimens, and material creations

- **1812:** *Seven-coin voltaic pile*. Faraday’s first recorded experiment: a battery assembled from halfpennies, zinc discs, and salt-water-moistened paper. The original is not identified as surviving. [Faraday biography]
- **1816:** *Miner’s safety-lamp experiments*. Experimental lamp work undertaken with Humphry Davy; authorship is collaborative and subordinate to Davy’s lamp project, so it should not be assigned to Faraday alone. [RSC]
- **1819–1824:** *Experimental alloy steels and miniature knife*. Faraday and James Stodart produced alloy specimens, including platinum-bearing steels; the surviving miniature knife is a crafted experimental object, while credit for the metallurgical project is shared. [SMG; RSC]
- **1821:** *Electromagnetic-rotation apparatus*. The experimental arrangement that produced continuous motion from electricity—the motor principle. The discovery apparatus and the surviving museum object are not identical. [RI Motor; RI Rotation]
- **1822:** *Mercury-bath electric motor*. The only surviving original example of Faraday’s rotation apparatus, made in the Royal Institution basement from glass, wood, copper, iron, wax, and mercury. [RI Motor]
- **1823–1845:** *Sealed tubes of liquefied gases*. Experimental preparations in which Faraday condensed gases into liquids; individual tubes and substances form a material series rather than one fixed-titled work. [RSC; SMG]
- **1824–1830:** *Optical-glass specimens*. Experimental heavy glasses, including borosilicate-of-lead specimens, made during Faraday’s prolonged glassmaking project. The project was judged unsuccessful for its original optical-manufacturing purpose, yet one glass later enabled his magneto-optical experiments. [SMG; RSC]
- **1825:** *Benzene specimens*. Faraday isolated and characterized “bicarburet of hydrogen,” now benzene, from compressed illuminating-gas residues; surviving sealed samples are preparations or manifestations of a substance, not an invention of the molecule itself. [RSC; SMG]
- **August 1831:** *Ring-coil apparatus*. An iron induction ring wound with separate coils, made by Faraday to demonstrate electromagnetic induction; it is retrospectively called the first transformer, although Faraday did not make it as a commercial transformer. [RI Ring]
- **October 1831:** *Magnet-and-coil generator*. A hand-operated experimental apparatus producing current by moving a magnet through a wire coil; it is a generator prototype rather than a practical power machine. [RI Generator]
- **1831:** *Homopolar disc generator*. Faraday’s rotating copper-disc arrangement produced continuous direct current. Surviving museum displays and later working models must not automatically be mistaken for Faraday’s original apparatus. [RI Laboratory]
- **1836:** *Metal-gauze electrical chamber*. A twelve-foot conducting enclosure constructed for electrostatic experiments—the project later generalized as the “Faraday cage.” It was a temporary experimental room rather than a product or patent. [UCL; RI Laboratory]
- **1837:** *Specific-inductive-capacity apparatus*. Equipment devised to compare dielectric materials; the Science Museum Group object is explicitly a replica, so the extant object is not Faraday’s handiwork even though the experimental design is his. [SMG]
- **1845:** *Magneto-optical apparatus*. An electromagnet-and-glass arrangement with which Faraday demonstrated magnetic rotation of polarized light. It is both instrument and staged experimental argument. [RI Laboratory]
- **1845–1850:** *Diamagnetic apparatus, including the giant electromagnet*. A changing ensemble used to investigate magnetism in matter; because components were reused and rearranged, it resists treatment as one stable instrument. [RI Laboratory; RSC]
- **1851:** *Earth inductor*. An apparatus for generating current by rotating a conductor through the Earth’s magnetic field; the Science Museum Group catalogue identifies its displayed example as a replica. [SMG]
- **circa 1851–1855:** *Magnetic lines-of-force patterns*. Material diagrams produced with iron filings to make magnetic form visible; surviving examples include patterns Faraday gave to a friend. They are simultaneously experimental traces, images, and crafted objects. [SMG]
- **1857:** *Colloidal-gold preparations*. Faraday’s ruby-red gold fluids and related specimens are deliberately produced material creations now understood as nanocrystalline colloids; they are preparations rather than conventional titled works. [RSC]
- **1820–1862:** *Experimental apparatus and specimens*. Beyond the individually named survivals above, Faraday made or modified numerous coils, magnets, tubes, cells, glass pieces, samples, and demonstration devices. Neither Jeffreys’s bibliography nor the surviving museum collections constitute a catalogue raisonné of every transient setup, so claiming an item-by-item complete material oeuvre would be false. [RI Papers; RI Laboratory; SMG]
