# Francisco de Miranda: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

This dossier reconstructs the life of Sebastián Francisco de Miranda Rodríguez Espinoza (1750–1816) from his personal archive, edited diaries and correspondence, Spanish archival descriptions, contemporary records, and later scholarship.

Labels used for contested material:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a contemporary document, Miranda’s diary or correspondence, an official record, or convergent archival evidence.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a generally reliable secondary source but not independently verified here against the underlying document.
- **Allegation:** an accusation made by an identified person or authority.
- **Disputed interpretation:** the evidence is accepted, but its meaning or responsibility remains contested.
- **Unverified claim:** repeated in secondary literature without adequate documentary confirmation.
- **Rumor or myth:** a culturally significant story unsupported or contradicted by the available evidence.

Miranda’s organized personal papers, later called the **Colombeia**, cover 1771–1810 and include diaries, correspondence, political projects, military papers, printed matter, and collected documents. UNESCO describes it as a closed archive—one not enlarged after Miranda organized it—and as a first-person record of the American, French, and Spanish-American revolutionary worlds. It entered UNESCO’s regional Memory of the World register in 2002 and international register in 2007.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full baptismal name:** Sebastián Francisco de Miranda Rodríguez Espinoza.

**Usual name:** Francisco de Miranda.

**Other forms:** Francisco Miranda; François de Miranda in French records; General Miranda; Generalísimo Francisco de Miranda; “the Precursor” or *El Precursor*; “Precursor of Spanish-American independence”; “General of Colombia.”

**Self-applied political-geographical terms:** Miranda increasingly used **Colombia** for the Spanish-American world he hoped to emancipate and unite. His archive’s later name, **Colombeia**, was formed from this usage.

**Birth:** 28 March 1750, Caracas, Province of Venezuela, Spanish Empire.

**Baptism:** Commonly dated 5 April 1750 in Caracas. The parish record should be treated as the controlling source for the full baptismal formula.

**Death:** Approximately 1:05 a.m., 14 July 1816, Royal Hospital of the Arsenal of La Carraca, near San Fernando and Cádiz, Spain.

**Recorded civil or legal status at death:** The burial certificate called him a *particular de causa pendiente y reo de Estado*—a private individual with a pending case and prisoner of state—and incorrectly or formally described him as unmarried and about sixty years old. He was sixty-six.

**Burial:** Cemetery serving La Carraca. The exact grave is unidentified. Later descriptions often call it a common grave, although the surviving certificate says that the body was buried in the district cemetery. His remains have never been securely identified.

**Occupations and roles:**

- Merchant’s son and privately educated Creole
- Officer in the Spanish Army
- Military administrator and intelligence gatherer
- Traveler, diarist, collector, bibliophile, and political publicist
- General in the French Revolutionary Army
- Diplomatic lobbyist in Britain, France, Russia, and the United States
- Organizer of Spanish-American revolutionary networks
- Commander of the 1806 expedition to Venezuela
- Deputy to the Venezuelan Congress
- Generalissimo and commander in chief of the First Venezuelan Republic

**Languages:** Spanish; fluent or highly competent French and English; reading knowledge of Latin, Italian, and other European languages is reported. His papers show persistent study of languages and extensive multilingual reading.

**Nationality:** Born a subject of the Spanish Crown in colonial Venezuela. Later a revolutionary advocate of an independent “Colombia.” Modern descriptions identify him as Venezuelan, although Venezuela did not exist as a sovereign state at his birth.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

Miranda’s father, **Sebastián de Miranda Ravelo**, was born in the Canary Islands and migrated to Caracas. He became a prosperous merchant, owned property, and held militia office. His mother, **Francisca Antonia Rodríguez de Espinoza**, belonged to a Caracas family.

The family was economically comfortable but socially vulnerable within Caracas’s hierarchy. Established Creole elites—later commonly called *mantuanos*—challenged Sebastián de Miranda’s standing, treating his Canarian and mercantile origin as inferior. Opposition sharpened when he received militia rank and the right to wear the corresponding uniform.

In 1769 Sebastián produced notarized genealogical evidence intended to establish the family’s **limpieza de sangre**, meaning the absence, under the racial-religious categories of the Spanish ancien régime, of Jewish, Muslim, African, or other ancestry deemed disqualifying. This was a legal strategy within a discriminatory status system, not modern genetic evidence. The episode became formative for Francisco: it exposed the conflict between wealth, ancestry, office, and recognition in colonial Caracas.

King Charles III ultimately upheld Sebastián’s rights and honors. The victory did not erase the family’s social humiliation and may have contributed to Francisco’s distrust of Caracas oligarchies.

### Siblings

Sources identify a large Miranda-Rodríguez family, but lists differ in completeness because several children died young. A securely relevant sister was **Micaela Antonia de Miranda**. Her daughter **Josefa María Rodríguez y Miranda** married the Venezuelan diplomat **Luis López Méndez**, creating a later family connection between Miranda and the 1810 Caracas mission in London.

A definitive sibling register should be drawn from Caracas parish and probate records rather than abbreviated biographies.

### Partner and children

Miranda established a household in London with **Sarah Andrews**, an Englishwoman usually described as his housekeeper, companion, or wife. Claims that they underwent a formal marriage ceremony are repeated but not consistently documented. Miranda’s death certificate recorded him as single, which may reflect Spanish non-recognition, lack of documentation, or the absence of a legal marriage.

They had two acknowledged sons:

1. **Leandro Miranda Andrews**, born in London in 1803; died in Paris in 1886. He later served in diplomatic or public capacities connected with Venezuela and helped preserve his father’s memory.
2. **Francisco Miranda Andrews**, born in London in 1806; died in 1831 at Cerinza, in present-day Colombia, while serving in the military.

Miranda’s travel diaries record numerous sexual and romantic encounters. Historian Mónica Bolufer has cautioned against reducing him to the later stereotype of a “Latin American Casanova”: his writings also record sustained intellectual friendships with women, admiration for learned women, and the gendered habits of an eighteenth-century elite male traveler. The diaries nevertheless show behavior that could be acquisitive, boastful, and unequal by modern standards.

No securely documented additional surviving children have been established.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1750–1770

Miranda grew up in a prosperous merchant household in Caracas. The family’s resources gave him access to books, tutors, clothing, and social ambition unavailable to most colonial subjects. At the same time, the dispute over his father’s ancestry and militia rank demonstrated that money did not automatically confer full acceptance among established Creole families.

Caracas was then a provincial capital shaped by the Catholic Church, transatlantic commerce, plantation slavery, free mixed-race populations, militia organization, and Bourbon administrative reform. Miranda’s later political language cannot simply be projected backward into his childhood; no childhood declaration of independence survives.

He attended classes associated with the **University of Caracas**, commonly identified with the Royal and Pontifical University of Caracas, and studied Latin grammar, religion, rhetoric, and elements of philosophy. Some biographical accounts overstate his degree status. The evidence supports study but not completion of a university degree.

His formative experiences included:

- The father’s commercial and militia world
- Colonial distinctions of birth and legal “quality”
- Catholic schooling
- Classical history and literature
- The Bourbon monarchy’s military culture
- Exposure to Atlantic trade and information
- Family resources sufficient to finance migration to Spain

On 25 January 1771 he sailed from La Guaira aboard the Swedish vessel *Prins Frederik* or a similarly rendered name. His surviving navigation diary begins the documentary record later incorporated into the *Colombeia*. He reached Cádiz in early March and continued to Madrid.

## Education and Intellectual Formation

### Formal and private study

In Madrid, Miranda pursued mathematics, geography, fortification, military science, drawing, French, and English through private instruction and self-education. Buying books became a lifelong practice. His library eventually contained classical authors, histories, political theory, military works, travel writing, banned Enlightenment literature, and works in several languages.

His education was neither a single university course nor a simple conversion to “the Enlightenment.” It developed through:

- Spanish military service
- Reading prohibited or restricted authors
- Observing British and American institutions
- Conversations with soldiers, ministers, aristocrats, scholars, artists, and clergy
- Museums, libraries, academies, theaters, factories, fortifications, and archaeological sites
- Direct experience of the American and French revolutions
- Repeated confrontation with imperial surveillance and political imprisonment

### Influences

Important intellectual reference points in his papers include classical republican history, the British constitutional tradition, natural-rights language, the American revolutionary settlement, French political theory, and the example—partly idealized—of the Inca state.

Miranda admired institutions selectively rather than adopting one ideological system. He could advocate representative government while also proposing:

- A strong executive
- A hereditary ruler titled **Inca**
- A bicameral legislature
- Moral and educational qualifications for citizenship
- A centralized continental union
- British strategic protection or partnership

His projects thus combined republican, monarchical, aristocratic, and imperial elements.

## Early Military Career in Spain and North Africa, 1771–1780

### Purchase of commission

In 1772 Miranda obtained a captaincy in the **Regiment of the Princess**, apparently through the purchase system customary in the Spanish military. This required substantial family financing. Purchase secured entry but not effortless advancement.

### Melilla and Morocco

Miranda served in North Africa and took part in the defense of **Melilla** during the Moroccan siege of 1774–1775. He later participated in the unsuccessful Spanish expedition against **Algiers** in 1775.

These campaigns gave him practical experience in:

- Fortification and siege conditions
- Supply failure
- Amphibious operations
- Relations between regular officers and commanders
- The dangers of poorly planned expeditions

### Disciplinary conflicts and Inquisition interest

Miranda quarreled with superiors and acquired a reputation for independence, pride, and insubordination. Military proceedings concerned aspects of his conduct, accounts, and obedience. Separately, the Spanish Inquisition became interested in his possession of prohibited books and possibly irreligious or heterodox ideas.

These were genuine official investigations, not retrospective inventions. Their precise charges varied over time and should not be collapsed into a single criminal conviction. Miranda was neither convicted of heresy nor executed by the Inquisition.

---

## Caribbean and North American War, 1780–1783

### Deployment to Cuba

Spain entered the Anglo-Spanish phase of the American Revolutionary War against Britain. Miranda went to the Caribbean under **Juan Manuel de Cagigal y Monserrat**, governor and military commander in Cuba.

### Pensacola, 1781

Miranda participated in the Spanish operations culminating in the capture of **Pensacola** on 9 May 1781. The victory, led overall by Bernardo de Gálvez, weakened British power in West Florida. Miranda received temporary promotion to lieutenant colonel.

His role was significant but later patriotic accounts sometimes inflate it into command of the battle. He served as an officer within a much larger Spanish force.

### Bahamas, intelligence, and Jamaica

In 1782 Spanish forces under Cagigal captured the Bahamas. Miranda was used in negotiations and intelligence-related work, including contact with British-held Jamaica.

### Contraband accusation

Spanish archival records preserve a case concerning alleged **contraband brought by Miranda from Jamaica to Cuba in 1781**, together with related inquiries into Cagigal’s conduct and access given to the British officer John Campbell.

Evidentiary status:

- **Documented fact:** Spanish authorities investigated the importation and Miranda’s conduct.
- **Allegation:** Miranda had engaged in illegal commerce or misuse of military channels.
- **Unresolved in the available record:** Whether his activity was private enrichment, intelligence work under authorization, ordinary wartime procurement, or a mixture.
- **No established final conviction:** The surviving descriptions do not justify calling him a convicted smuggler.

The Supreme Council of the Inquisition ordered his return to Spain in February 1782. Procedural defects, jurisdictional conflict, and Cagigal’s protection delayed enforcement.

### Flight to the United States

Facing return and prosecution, Miranda left Cuba with assistance from Cagigal and the American merchant or agent **James Seagrove**. He landed at New Bern, North Carolina, in June or July 1783; sources disagree between 10 June and 10 July, and the diary chronology favors June for his initial landing.

His departure was unauthorized from the Spanish government’s perspective. From this point he lived for years as a fugitive or politically suspect Spanish officer.

---

## United States, 1783–1784

Miranda traveled through North Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia, Virginia, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, New York, Connecticut, Massachusetts, and other parts of the newly independent United States.

He examined:

- State constitutions
- Legislative practice
- Military installations
- Prisons and poor relief
- Colleges and libraries
- Commerce, agriculture, and slavery
- Indigenous-settler relations
- Religious pluralism
- The social position of women
- The weaknesses of the Confederation

He met **George Washington** at Mount Vernon and discussed the war and Spanish America. He met or corresponded with **Alexander Hamilton**, **Thomas Jefferson**, Henry Knox, Samuel Adams, Governor George Clinton, and other political and military figures. Hamilton’s surviving letter of 23 November 1784 and later recollections document substantive discussion of Spanish-American emancipation.

Miranda’s own 1792 memorandum recalled that in New York in 1784 he formed a project for the independence of the whole Spanish-American continent with British cooperation. This is retrospective self-testimony but is partly corroborated by Hamilton’s acknowledgement of repeated conversations.

He observed slavery while accepting hospitality from slaveholders. His later emancipation plans attacked Spanish imperial rule but did not produce a complete or consistently egalitarian program for abolishing slavery and racial hierarchy. The social content of his projected revolution remained less developed than its constitutional and geopolitical design.

In December 1784 he sailed from Boston for London.

---

## European and Mediterranean Grand Tour, 1785–1789

Miranda’s travels were unusually extensive and minutely documented. Major movements included:

- Britain
- The Dutch Republic and Austrian Netherlands
- Prussia and other German states
- Austria and Hungary
- Italy
- Greece and the eastern Mediterranean
- Constantinople
- The Russian Empire
- Poland
- Sweden and Norway
- Denmark
- German states again
- Switzerland
- France

He visited battlefields, arsenals, hospitals, prisons, farms, factories, universities, observatories, art collections, theaters, libraries, ruins, and courts. His diaries mix measurements and institutional observations with gossip, aesthetic judgments, food, health, sex, and social encounters.

### Britain

In London he attempted to regularize his position through the Spanish ambassador while cultivating British political contacts. Spanish diplomacy alternated between negotiation, surveillance, and efforts to secure his return.

### Italy, Greece, and the Ottoman Empire

Miranda studied Roman antiquities, Renaissance art, military architecture, and Catholic institutions. In Greece he visited sites associated with classical history. At Constantinople he observed Ottoman administration and society, obtained travel documents, and prepared to enter Russia.

### Russia, 1786–1787

Miranda reached the Russian Empire through the Black Sea region and Crimea and entered the circle around **Prince Grigory Potemkin**. In Kyiv in 1787 he met **Empress Catherine II** and members of her court.

Documented features of the relationship include:

- Multiple court encounters
- Conversation about Spanish America and the Inquisition
- Russian financial assistance or credit
- Protection against Spanish diplomatic pressure
- Permission or encouragement to use a Russian military uniform
- Correspondence with Catherine, Potemkin, Alexander Bezborodko, and Russian diplomats
- An invitation or suggestion that he remain in Russia

The Spanish minister reported Miranda’s extraordinary favor at court. Miranda later thanked Catherine and Potemkin for protection.

Claims that Miranda became Catherine’s lover are **rumor or romantic legend**. No reliable documentary evidence establishes a sexual relationship. Catherine’s patronage was political, personal, and courtly, but its precise emotional character cannot be recovered.

### Scandinavia and central Europe

Miranda continued through Sweden, Norway, Denmark, and German territories. He met courtiers, military officers, scholars, and artists and recorded local institutions.

A meeting or substantive conversation with **Johann Wolfgang von Goethe** is often connected to the origin of Miranda’s tricolor. The two men were in Weimar in the relevant period, and Miranda’s writings later connected color theory to a learned German conversation. The precise wording often quoted in flag literature is not securely preserved as a contemporary verbatim dialogue.

### France, 1789

Miranda reached France during the early Revolution. He observed its opening transformations but initially avoided permanent commitment. By late 1789 he returned to London, where his Spanish-American project became his central occupation.

---

## London Political Projects, 1790–1792

Miranda opened negotiations with Prime Minister **William Pitt the Younger** and other British ministers. He proposed that Britain support the independence of Spanish America in return for commercial access and strategic partnership.

His projected “Colombia” generally encompassed Spanish America—at times also Portuguese America—from the Mississippi or northern Spanish territories to Cape Horn. Plans varied, but recurrent institutional features included:

- A continental federation or empire
- A hereditary executive called the **Inca**
- Two legislative chambers
- Provincial administration
- Civil liberties
- Commercial opening
- British naval support
- Protection against European reconquest

The proposals served several purposes at once: constitutional design, propaganda, bargaining with Britain, and self-positioning as leader of a continental movement.

Britain listened when war with Spain seemed likely and withdrew when diplomacy changed. Miranda received intermittent attention and possibly financial support, but never the sustained expeditionary commitment he sought. This cycle—strategic interest followed by abandonment—recurred throughout his London career.

---

## French Revolutionary Career, 1792–1798

### Entry into French service

In 1792 revolutionary France needed experienced officers. Miranda joined the French army and became a general under **Charles-François Dumouriez**.

He took part in the campaign associated with **Valmy** and the occupation of Belgium. His exact tactical role at Valmy was subordinate to the principal commanders Dumouriez and Kellermann, but his service in the campaign is documented.

### Antwerp and command

Miranda helped command operations in the Low Countries and gained prominence. He was promoted and entrusted with major responsibilities.

### Siege of Maastricht and Neerwinden

In early 1793 he unsuccessfully besieged **Maastricht**. At **Neerwinden** on 18 March 1793, French forces were defeated. Dumouriez subsequently defected to the Austrians after attempting to turn against the Convention.

Miranda was accused of responsibility for military failure and of complicity with Dumouriez.

Evidentiary status:

- **Documented fact:** The Maastricht operation failed; the army retreated after Neerwinden.
- **Documented fact:** Miranda was prosecuted before the Revolutionary Tribunal.
- **Allegation:** Treason, negligence, or complicity with Dumouriez.
- **Court finding:** Acquitted in May 1793 after a defense that distinguished his conduct from Dumouriez’s.
- **Disputed interpretation:** His military competence at Maastricht and Neerwinden remains debated. Inadequate forces, confused orders, and Dumouriez’s political maneuvering complicate attribution.

### Imprisonment during the Terror

Miranda was arrested again in July 1793 and imprisoned, including at La Force. He survived the Terror while many associates and fellow prisoners were executed. Released after the fall of Robespierre, he remained politically active in Paris.

### Directory-era politics and deportation orders

Miranda associated with constitutional republicans, émigrés, diplomats, and political salon networks. Authorities alternately valued and suspected him. His name appeared in disputes surrounding royalists and moderate republicans, and deportation orders were issued.

He met **Napoleon Bonaparte** in revolutionary Paris. Later accounts attribute to Napoleon the description of Miranda as a “Don Quixote” with revolutionary fire; the wording is reported through memoir traditions and should not be treated as a stenographic quotation.

Miranda left France and returned to London in January 1798.

## Revolutionary Networks and the Masonry Question

Miranda’s London home became a meeting place for Spanish-American visitors and exiles. He circulated plans, maps, books, letters, and political intelligence.

### Gran Reunión Americana and Lautaro traditions

Later nationalist and Masonic histories credited him with founding:

- The Gran Reunión Americana
- The Logia Lautaro
- The Caballeros Racionales
- A transatlantic Masonic command structure directing independence movements

The evidence requires separation:

- **Documented fact:** Miranda organized political meetings and cultivated Spanish-American revolutionary contacts.
- **Reported fact:** A secret or semi-secret association around him has often been called the Gran Reunión Americana.
- **Unverified or disputed claim:** That it was a regular Masonic lodge with documented initiation records, rituals, and institutional descent.
- **Contrary evidence:** Scholars William Spence Robertson, Frederic Seal-Coon, and José Antonio Ferrer Benimeli found no proof in Miranda’s papers that he was initiated as a Freemason or founded the later Lautaro lodges.
- **Historical consequence:** The Masonic story helped later movements construct a genealogy linking Miranda, O’Higgins, San Martín, Bolívar, and the independence revolutions.

Secret political societies sometimes borrowed Masonic vocabulary and forms without being recognized Masonic lodges. Treating every “lodge” as Freemasonry produces false institutional certainty.

## The 1806 Expedition

### Preparation in the United States

After renewed disappointment in Britain, Miranda traveled to the United States in 1805. He sought support from officials and private citizens. President **Thomas Jefferson** and Secretary of State James Madison received him but avoided official responsibility.

Miranda worked with **Colonel William S. Smith**, former U.S. official and son-in-law of John Adams, and merchant **Samuel Ogden**. They helped procure the ship *Leander*, arms, recruits, and supplies.

The expedition’s finances were private, improvised, and politically ambiguous. Miranda implied high-level sympathy to recruits and supporters, while the U.S. government maintained formal neutrality.

### Neutrality prosecutions

Smith and Ogden were prosecuted for violating U.S. neutrality law.

Evidentiary status:

- **Documented fact:** The expedition was organized from U.S. territory; Smith and Ogden were tried.
- **Court result:** They were acquitted.
- **Disputed issue:** How much Jefferson’s administration knew or tacitly encouraged. Meetings and awareness are documented; formal authorization is not.
- **Political interpretation:** The administration benefited from plausible deniability.

### Ocumare failure

Miranda’s small force sailed through Haiti and the Caribbean. Two support vessels, *Bacchus* and *Bee*, were captured near Ocumare in April 1806. Spanish authorities executed several captured expedition members and imprisoned others.

Miranda escaped aboard the *Leander*. The loss exposed inadequate intelligence, weak local coordination, and dependence on foreign recruits.

### Jacmel and the tricolor

At Jacmel, Haiti, on 12 March 1806, Miranda raised or formally inaugurated the yellow-blue-red tricolor associated with his expedition. It was later flown on Venezuelan soil at La Vela de Coro.

### Coro landing

With British Caribbean assistance, Miranda landed at **La Vela de Coro on 3 August 1806**, occupied the town briefly, issued proclamations, and attempted to stimulate insurrection.

The expected popular rising did not occur. Local elites and much of the population withdrew or remained hostile; royal authorities had prepared resistance; Miranda lacked cavalry, supplies, and a local organization. He evacuated.

### Assessment

**Successes:**

- Created an enduring flag and political symbolism
- Issued a concrete emancipation proclamation
- Demonstrated that an expedition could reach the Venezuelan coast
- Internationalized the independence project

**Failures:**

- Failed to establish a durable bridgehead
- Lost men to capture and execution
- Misjudged local readiness
- Relied heavily on foreign sailors and adventurers
- Accumulated debts and damaged relations with supporters

The expedition was not a mass Venezuelan revolution. It was an externally organized attempt that lacked sufficient domestic coordination.

## London Household, Property, and Political Work, 1807–1810

Miranda returned to London and lived at **27 Grafton Street**, now associated with 58 Grafton Way. His house contained his family, library, archive, maps, artworks, and political papers.

His finances were unstable. Sources of support included:

- Family wealth and remittances earlier in life
- Military pay
- Russian gifts or credit
- French pay and claims
- British governmental or private subsidies
- Loans and advances from merchants and sympathizers
- Credit secured by personal connections
- Funds raised for expeditions

He was not a modern business magnate and had no durable company structure. His political projects repeatedly created debt.

His most valuable personal assets were probably:

- The *Colombeia*
- A large multilingual library
- Maps and prints
- Furniture and household effects
- Claims for unpaid compensation
- Political correspondence whose strategic value exceeded its market value

Sarah Andrews and their sons depended on the London household. Miranda’s departure in 1810 left the family financially exposed.

---

## Return to Venezuela, 1810

On 19 April 1810 Caracas elites removed the captain general and formed a Supreme Junta nominally defending the rights of the captive Ferdinand VII. The Junta sent **Simón Bolívar, Luis López Méndez, and Andrés Bello** to London.

They arrived in July 1810 and met Miranda. The British government would not formally recognize Venezuelan independence but permitted unofficial contacts. Miranda’s house and library became an operational center. Bello used the library extensively and studied Greek there.

Bolívar and his colleagues urged Miranda to return. Despite distrust among some Caracas leaders, he sailed for Venezuela and arrived in December 1810, nearly forty years after leaving.

---

## First Venezuelan Republic, 1811–1812

### Political position

Miranda joined the **Patriotic Society**, where radicals pressed for an unambiguous declaration of independence. He was elected to the Congress as a representative of El Pao.

On **5 July 1811**, Congress declared independence. Miranda signed the declaration.

He served with Lino de Clemente and José de Sata y Bussy on the committee responsible for national symbols. Congress adopted a form of his yellow-blue-red tricolor.

### Constitution and ideology

Miranda considered the 1811 federal constitution too weak for wartime conditions. He favored stronger central authority. His reservations anticipated a genuine military problem but also reflected his lifelong preference for concentrated executive power.

### Valencia campaign

Royalist resistance at Valencia produced fighting in 1811. Miranda received military command and eventually helped suppress the uprising. The campaign exposed poor discipline, factionalism, shortages, and racial and regional divisions inside the republic.

### Generalissimo

As the crisis deepened, Miranda was named **Generalissimo** and dictator or supreme commander with extraordinary powers. His authority remained constrained by fragmented institutions and suspicious civilian leaders.

### Earthquake of 26 March 1812

A destructive earthquake struck Caracas, La Guaira, Barquisimeto, and other republican centers on Holy Thursday. Royalist clergy portrayed it as divine punishment for rebellion. The physical disaster damaged logistics, morale, and political legitimacy.

The royalists also suffered in places, so the earthquake was not evidence of divine targeting. Its propagandistic interpretation nevertheless materially aided the royalist cause.

### Military collapse

Royalist commander **Domingo de Monteverde** advanced from the west. The republic faced:

- Loss of territory
- Mutiny and desertion
- Food and ammunition shortages
- Social hostility to Creole leadership
- Racial and class divisions
- Disputes between federal and central authorities
- The fall of Puerto Cabello after prisoners revolted in the fortress commanded by Bolívar

The loss of Puerto Cabello was a major blow. Bolívar acknowledged the disaster in correspondence.

### Capitulation

Miranda negotiated with Monteverde and signed the **Capitulation of San Mateo**, commonly dated 25 July 1812, though some documents and narratives use 24 or 26 July for stages of the negotiation.

Miranda believed continued resistance was militarily hopeless and sought guarantees for persons and property. Opponents believed he surrendered without adequate consultation, concealed terms, or intended to escape with public funds.

Evidentiary distinctions:

- **Documented fact:** Miranda negotiated and signed the capitulation as commander.
- **Documented fact:** The republic was militarily disintegrating.
- **Disputed interpretation:** Whether surrender was necessary, premature, or personally motivated.
- **Allegation by Monteverde:** Miranda attempted to carry off treasury money and church silver collected after the earthquake.
- **Unresolved:** Some funds were prepared for evacuation; this does not by itself establish personal theft. No impartial judicial finding adjudicated the allegation.
- **Documented fact:** Monteverde did not honor the capitulation’s protections in practice, arresting and persecuting republicans.

---

## Arrest at La Guaira and the Bolívar Controversy

After the capitulation Miranda went to La Guaira intending to leave, probably for Curaçao or another Caribbean port. His baggage and papers were being prepared for embarkation.

During the night of 30–31 July 1812, republican officers and civilians arrested him while he slept. Participants included **Simón Bolívar**, Manuel María de las Casas, Miguel Peña, and others, though lists vary.

Their apparent intention has competing versions:

1. To try Miranda themselves for treason.
2. To prevent him leaving with funds or papers.
3. To continue resistance despite the capitulation.
4. To hand him to Monteverde in exchange for favor or safe conduct.

The port commander Casas then allowed royalist forces to take control. Miranda passed into Spanish custody.

Evidentiary assessment:

- **Documented fact:** Bolívar participated in Miranda’s arrest.
- **Documented fact:** Miranda subsequently fell into royalist hands.
- **Disputed interpretation:** Whether Bolívar knowingly “delivered” him to Spain.
- **Reported fact:** Monteverde later gave Bolívar a passport and referred approvingly to his service against Miranda.
- **Insufficient evidence for a bargain:** No conclusive document proves that Bolívar arrested Miranda under a prior agreement exchanging him for a passport.
- **Later propaganda:** Miranda’s arrest became a foundational accusation against Bolívar; Bolivarian narratives conversely portrayed Miranda as cowardly or treasonous.
- **Later reassessment:** In 1826 Bolívar reportedly called Miranda “the most illustrious Colombian,” showing at least partial revision of his earlier denunciations.

The simplest documented sequence is that Bolívar helped arrest Miranda as a supposed traitor, Casas lost or surrendered the port, and royalists seized the prisoner. Intent beyond that remains contested.

---

## Imprisonment, 1812–1816

Miranda was imprisoned successively in:

- The vaults of La Guaira
- San Carlos fortress, Venezuela
- El Morro in Puerto Rico
- Cádiz
- The prison of the Four Towers at the Arsenal of La Carraca

He was transported in chains and treated as a state prisoner. Spanish authorities proceeded slowly; his case remained unresolved at death.

His property and papers were vulnerable to seizure. The major archive survived because portions had been left or transferred through trusted hands, notably the British merchant **Peter Turnbull**. Miranda continued writing petitions and arranging plans for escape.

Friends in Britain raised money and attempted to assist him. Correspondence indicates that an escape from La Carraca was under preparation. His servant **Pedro José Morán** remained an important witness to the final period.

Prison conditions damaged his health. Sources report a stroke or apoplectic attack shortly before death. There is no reliable evidence that he was murdered or poisoned.

---

## Death

Miranda died in the Royal Hospital at La Carraca at approximately 1:05 a.m. on 14 July 1816.

The surviving burial entry states that:

- He was a prisoner of state with a pending case.
- He was the son of Sebastián.
- He was a native of Caracas, Venezuela.
- He received extreme unction.
- He did not make a final will there.
- He was buried in the district cemetery.

Morán later complained that priests or officials prevented the funeral rites he wished to arrange and that bedding, clothing, and effects were removed and burned. This may have been a disease-control or prison procedure, but the motive is not established.

### Cause of death

**Best-supported cause:** Natural death following apoplexy or stroke, aggravated by imprisonment and declining health.

**Rumor:** Poisoning or deliberate killing.

**Evidence:** No reliable medical or forensic evidence supports murder. The existence of escape preparations helped later rumor, but temporal coincidence is not proof.

### Remains

The exact grave was lost. Later efforts to identify remains associated with La Carraca have not produced a universally accepted identification. The tomb dedicated to Miranda in Venezuela’s National Pantheon is therefore cenotaphic.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Colonial subject to continental separatist

Miranda did not begin life with a fully formed independence ideology. His transformation appears cumulative:

1. Colonial status conflict in Caracas
2. Frustration inside the Spanish military
3. Prosecution and flight
4. Observation of the independent United States
5. British strategic thinking
6. Enlightenment and classical reading
7. The French Revolution
8. Decades of Spanish-American organizing

### Continental “Colombia”

Miranda’s Colombia was broader than the later Republic of Colombia or Gran Colombia. It was a projected political identity for the Spanish-American continent, sometimes extending to Brazil.

He was among the earliest sustained promoters of a unified Spanish-American political project. The concept was both emancipatory and state-building: independence without effective central institutions, in his view, invited reconquest and civil war.

### Government

Miranda’s constitutional designs included liberal rights but were not consistently democratic by modern standards. He feared faction, clerical control, military disorder, and poorly educated electorates. His ideal government combined representation with hierarchy.

### Indigenous symbolism

The title **Inca** and indigenous imagery in projected institutions and flags offered an American legitimacy distinct from Spain. This did not amount to a documented program transferring power to contemporary Indigenous communities. It often functioned as elite revolutionary symbolism.

### Religion

Raised Catholic, Miranda became anticlerical toward the Inquisition and politically suspicious of clerical power. He criticized superstition, monastic institutions, and intolerance. He nevertheless received Catholic last rites. Claims that he was an atheist are too categorical; the evidence fits heterodox Enlightenment religiosity, skepticism, or private belief better than a securely documented creed.

### Slavery and race

Miranda opposed colonial subordination and sometimes used universal-rights language. His record on slavery was incomplete and strategically cautious. He sought support from Haiti and recruited people of varied backgrounds, but he did not leave a comprehensive abolition program comparable to later decrees of the independence wars.

His own family’s reliance on *limpieza de sangre* documents shows participation in the status system of the period, even as his later continental politics challenged other inherited hierarchies.

### Gender and sexuality

His diaries openly recorded sexual encounters, physical judgments, flirtations, and relationships. These records are unusually candid but cannot always distinguish mutual relationships from commercial sex or unequal encounters. They document both cosmopolitan curiosity and elite masculine entitlement.

He also exchanged ideas with educated women and praised women’s intellectual accomplishment. The two dimensions should not be reduced to either libertine legend or sanitized friendship.

## Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

Miranda founded no durable commercial corporation and held no known patent. His significant “projects” were military, archival, constitutional, and propagandistic.

### The Colombeia

A deliberately organized personal archive containing:

- Travel diaries
- Correspondence
- Military reports
- Political plans
- Proclamations
- Diplomatic memoranda
- Newspaper clippings
- Printed pamphlets
- Maps and copied documents

Its organization was a form of self-documentation and self-created historical memory. Miranda expected his papers to serve a future continental history.

### Library

His Grafton Street library was both private collection and political institution. It supported:

- His own comparative studies
- Constitutional drafting
- Instruction of younger Americans
- Diplomatic preparation
- Andrés Bello’s studies
- Production of propaganda and translations

### “Colombia” constitutional project

Not one fixed constitution but a series of evolving proposals. Features included a continental union, Inca executive, bicameral legislature, civic education, and provincial government.

### The *Leander* expedition

A privately financed transnational venture involving:

- Ship charter and purchase arrangements
- Arms procurement
- Recruitment in the United States
- Haitian staging
- British Caribbean assistance
- Promises of rank, land, pay, or prize-related compensation

The venture generated criminal proceedings against associates, unpaid claims, and political controversy.

### Flags and emblems

Miranda designed or sponsored several flags and military emblems before the definitive yellow-blue-red arrangement. The 1806 tricolor became the principal ancestor of the national flags of Venezuela, Colombia, and Ecuador.

## Controversies, Allegations, and Unresolved Questions

### Status and ancestry dispute

**Documented fact:** Caracas elites challenged his father’s rank and social eligibility.

**Historical significance:** Later hagiography sometimes depicts the Mirandas simply as oppressed commoners; they were wealthy but status-contested. Hostile accounts sometimes depict Sebastián as socially fraudulent; the Crown upheld his claims.

### Prohibited books and Inquisition investigation

**Documented fact:** Miranda possessed restricted works and attracted inquisitorial attention.

**Not established:** A final conviction for heresy.

### Military insubordination

**Documented fact:** He had conflicts with superiors and disciplinary proceedings.

**Disputed interpretation:** Independent judgment versus chronic insubordination and self-promotion.

### Cuban contraband

**Documented fact:** Official investigation.

**Allegation:** Smuggling goods from Jamaica.

**Not established:** Final criminal conviction or purely private motive.

### French treason charges

**Documented fact:** Formal prosecution.

**Court finding:** Acquittal.

**Continuing dispute:** Military responsibility for defeat, distinct from legal treason.

### Political opportunism

Miranda served the Spanish monarchy, sought Russian protection, bargained with Britain, and became a French revolutionary general.

**Documented pattern:** Repeated changes of patron and allegiance.

**Competing interpretations:**

- A consistent pursuit of Spanish-American emancipation through available great powers.
- Personal ambition and opportunistic self-reinvention.
- Both motives operating together.

The archive supports persistent continental ambition but also deliberate cultivation of rank, celebrity, and patronage.

### British intelligence connections

Miranda exchanged strategic intelligence with British ministers and sought British military intervention.

**Documented fact:** Repeated confidential negotiations and provision of information.

**Terminological caution:** Calling him a formal salaried “British spy” requires proof of an institutional intelligence appointment. He functioned as an informant, lobbyist, planner, and occasionally subsidized political client, but surviving evidence does not reduce the relationship to a simple agent contract.

### U.S. neutrality and the 1806 expedition

**Documented fact:** Prepared on American soil; associates prosecuted.

**Unresolved:** Degree of Jefferson administration complicity.

### Expeditionary representations

Some recruits believed they were entering an officially supported or well-financed operation. Miranda’s representations and the expedition’s weak preparation caused later grievances.

**Documented failure:** Pay, supply, and command problems.

**Not established:** A fraudulent scheme for personal enrichment.

### Capitulation and treasury funds

**Allegation:** Theft or intended theft of public and church valuables.

**Evidence:** Monteverde made the accusation; funds and valuables were associated with the evacuation.

**Unresolved:** Ownership, intended use, and whether Miranda sought personal gain.

### Responsibility for the fall of the First Republic

Competing assignments of responsibility include:

- Miranda’s centralized command and capitulation
- Bolívar’s loss of Puerto Cabello
- Federal fragmentation
- Civilian political indecision
- Social exclusion by the Creole republic
- Monteverde’s military advance
- Royalist clerical propaganda
- The earthquake
- Desertion and material shortages

No single-cause account adequately explains the collapse.

### Bolívar’s role in the arrest

**Documented fact:** Participant in the arrest.

**Disputed:** Whether arrest equaled intentional delivery to Spain.

**Unproven:** A prior bargain exchanging Miranda for Bolívar’s passport.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The lover of Catherine the Great”

**Story:** Miranda became Catherine II’s lover; she protected him from Spain.

**Appearance and circulation:** Romantic biographies, popular histories, flag legends, and nationalist storytelling.

**Evidence for:** Documented court favor, personal conversation, financial protection, and invitation to remain in Russia.

**Evidence against:** No reliable contemporary proof of sexual relations.

**Status:** **Rumor or romantic myth.**

**Reputational effect:** Turned diplomatic patronage into an adventure narrative and reinforced the image of Miranda as an irresistible cosmopolitan.

### Catherine’s hair, eyes, and lips created the tricolor

**Story:** Yellow represented Catherine’s blond hair, blue her eyes, red her lips.

**Evidence:** No secure contemporary statement by Miranda supports it. Miranda’s own color discussions point more plausibly toward primary-color or rainbow theories, possibly connected with Goethe.

**Status:** **Myth.**

**Effect:** Personalized the national flag through romance and linked Venezuelan symbolism to the Russian court.

### Goethe dictated the colors

**Story:** Goethe explained to Miranda that yellow was the warmest color, blue serenity or distance, and red their synthesis; Miranda then designed the flag.

**Evidence:** A meeting or intellectual contact is plausible and often reported, but the polished dialogue circulated later and is not securely established as verbatim.

**Status:** **Reported tradition with uncertain wording.**

### Miranda as a fully initiated Freemason

**Story:** He was initiated in Virginia or Europe and founded a supreme Masonic lodge directing liberation.

**Evidence for:** Contacts with Freemasons; political societies using lodge-like secrecy; later testimony and institutional tradition.

**Evidence against:** No initiation record or claim in Miranda’s exhaustive archive; specialist scholars distinguish the political societies from regular Masonry.

**Status:** **Unverified claim; institutional myth.**

**Effect:** Supplied independence movements with a secret lineage and made Miranda the ritual “grandfather” of continental revolution.

### Founder of the Lautaro Lodge

**Story:** Miranda founded the London mother lodge from which San Martín’s and O’Higgins’s organizations descended.

**Evidence:** Later histories and fraternal traditions.

**Counterevidence:** The Cádiz Caballeros Racionales and later Lautaro organizations have separate, incompletely documented origins. A direct institutional chain is not demonstrated.

**Status:** **Disputed theory.**

### “Participant in three great revolutions”

**Story:** Miranda was a leading participant in the American, French, and Spanish-American revolutions.

**Evidence:** He served Spain in the war connected to American independence, became a French general, and led Venezuelan independence efforts.

**Qualification:** In North America he fought as a Spanish officer in an imperial war allied to the United States, not as an officer of Washington’s Continental Army.

**Status:** **Broadly factual, often rhetorically simplified.**

### His name on the Arc de Triomphe

**Story:** Miranda’s name is inscribed among French commanders on the Arc de Triomphe.

**Evidence:** The inscription “MIRANDA” appears among the names. It refers to Francisco de Miranda.

**Status:** **Documented fact.**

### “First universal American”

Later writers call him *El Primer Venezolano Universal* or the first universal Latin American.

**Status:** **Honorific interpretation**, not a contemporary office.

### Murder or poisoning at La Carraca

**Story:** Spanish authorities killed Miranda as he was preparing to escape.

**Evidence for:** Escape plans, political motive, harsh imprisonment.

**Evidence against:** Death and prison records describe natural illness; contemporary evidence supports apoplexy.

**Status:** **Rumor.**

### Bolívar sold Miranda to Spain

**Story:** Bolívar deliberately traded Miranda to Monteverde for a passport.

**Evidence:** Bolívar helped arrest him; Monteverde later permitted Bolívar to leave and praised his conduct.

**Counterevidence:** No conclusive prior agreement; Casas controlled the port and royalist seizure followed a changing military situation.

**Status:** **Disputed allegation, not an established bargain.**

### Miranda as sole creator of Latin American independence

**Story:** All major independence movements descended organizationally from his plans.

**Evidence:** His influence was extensive; his writings and contacts preceded later movements.

**Counterevidence:** Revolutions arose from multiple local crises, institutions, wars, and actors. Some alleged personal links are unsupported.

**Status:** **Hagiographic exaggeration.**

### Miranda as coward or traitor in 1812

**Story:** He surrendered for personal survival and intended to flee with the treasury.

**Circulation:** Bolívar’s early denunciations, royalist accusations, republican polemic.

**Evidence:** He capitulated and prepared to leave.

**Counterevidence:** The military situation was catastrophic, and capitulation was a recognizable command decision intended to secure protections.

**Status:** **Political accusation and disputed interpretation.**

### The Michelena prison image as eyewitness portrait

Arturo Michelena’s **Miranda en La Carraca** (1896) became the canonical visual representation of Miranda in prison.

**Evidence:** Painted eighty years after Miranda’s death; not eyewitness documentation.

**Status:** **Posthumous artistic reconstruction.**

**Effect:** Fixed the public image of Miranda as an isolated, contemplative martyr surrounded by books and chains.

## Works by Francisco de Miranda

Miranda was not primarily a conventional book author. Much of his work survives as manuscripts, diaries, memoranda, proclamations, correspondence, constitutional plans, and documents later edited from the *Colombeia*. Titles vary across editions.

### Contemporary and lifetime publications

- **Proclamations and orders associated with the 1806 expedition** (1806). Spanish-language revolutionary proclamations issued aboard the *Leander* and after the Coro landing. Genuine authorship or authorization; some texts were drafted with collaborators.
- **Letters and political communications published in British, French, American, Caribbean, and Spanish-American newspapers** (1790s–1812). Individual attribution must be checked against each printing.
- **Venezuelan political and military proclamations, addresses, and orders** (1811–1812). Genuine official documents, sometimes collectively drafted or issued through secretaries.
- **Contributions to independence propaganda linked to José de Antepara’s publications**, including material incorporated into *South American Emancipation* (1810). Miranda supplied documents and direction; Antepara was the named compiler or author.

### Manuscript works and documentary series

- **Diario de navegación del Puerto de La Guaira al de Cádiz** (1771). Earliest known personal travel journal.
- **Travel diaries, 1771–1800.** Spain, North Africa, Caribbean, United States, Britain, continental Europe, Ottoman lands, Russia, and Scandinavia.
- **United States diary, 1783–1784.** Later published separately in English and Spanish editions.
- **European travel diaries, 1785–1789.**
- **French Revolutionary military papers, 1792–1798.**
- **Correspondence with Alexander Hamilton, Catherine II, Potemkin, Vorontsov, British ministers, French officials, Spanish Americans, and others.**
- **Memoranda to William Pitt and British ministers concerning Spanish-American emancipation** (principally 1790–1808).
- **Constitutional projects for Colombia**, various dates and versions.
- **Plans for government headed by an Inca**, late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries.
- **Military plans and expedition papers for Venezuela** (1805–1806).
- **Prison petitions and final correspondence** (1812–1816).

### Principal posthumous editions

- **Archivo del General Miranda**, 24 volumes, Caracas and Havana, 1929–1950. First large documentary edition; posthumous and editorially arranged.
- **Colombeia**, second major documentary edition initiated by Venezuelan decree in 1976. Posthumous scholarly or state-sponsored edition with revised organization and annotation.
- **Diario de viajes**, numerous selections and editions. Posthumous selections from the archive.
- **Diarios: una selección, 1771–1800**, selected and introduced by Juan Carlos Chirinos, 2006. Modern abridgment.
- **The Diary of Francisco de Miranda: Tour of the United States, 1783–1784**, English-language scholarly edition. Translation and edited primary text.
- **América espera**, edited by José Luis Salcedo-Bastardo. Thematic posthumous compilation of writings and political documents.
- **Textos sobre la independencia**, various editors and dates. Modern selections rather than lifetime books.
- **Correspondencia**, various national and thematic editions. Editorial collections, not books designed by Miranda as such.

### Attribution cautions

- *Colombeia* is Miranda’s archive, not a single authored book.
- Later “complete works” often mix diary entries, incoming letters, copied documents, and official papers.
- Proclamations issued in his name may have been drafted by secretaries or collaborators.
- Polished maxims circulated online under Miranda’s name frequently lack manuscript citations.
- “A country begins with a name and a flag…” is widely attributed to Miranda but should not be quoted as authentic without a specific archival reference.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Immediate aftermath

Miranda died while Spanish-American independence remained unresolved. News of his death reached Britain and the Americas through correspondence and periodicals. Early notices remembered him as the originator or leader of the first sustained independence project.

His sons, associates, editors, and later Venezuelan governments preserved or recovered parts of his documentary legacy.

### Bolívar and the “Precursor”

Miranda’s reputation was long filtered through Bolívar’s greater military success. The title **Precursor** honored chronological priority while also placing him before, rather than within, the supposedly decisive liberation. Modern scholarship increasingly treats him as an autonomous transatlantic actor rather than merely Bolívar’s unsuccessful predecessor.

### National symbols

His clearest continuing institutional legacy is the yellow-blue-red tricolor. Variants form the national flags of:

- Venezuela
- Colombia
- Ecuador

The flags’ common ancestry provides a visible remnant of the larger Colombian union Miranda imagined.

### France

His French generalship is commemorated by:

- His name on the Arc de Triomphe
- Portraiture and memorials
- Association with Valmy and the revolutionary army

French commemoration often minimizes his later Spanish-American program, while Latin American commemoration sometimes minimizes his complicated French politics.

### Venezuela

Venezuelan states, municipalities, avenues, plazas, military institutions, schools, universities, and the satellite **VENESAT-1 Simón Bolívar’s** surrounding national pantheon of heroes invoke Miranda. The state of Miranda bears his name.

Different governments have emphasized different Mirandas:

- Cosmopolitan republican
- Military Generalissimo
- Anti-imperialist precursor
- Advocate of continental unity
- Victim of oligarchic betrayal
- Founder of national symbolism

### Archive and memory

The *Colombeia* may be his most unusual legacy. Few eighteenth-century revolutionaries preserved such a systematic personal record of travel, politics, sexuality, reading, military service, and diplomacy. Its organization was also an act of self-memorialization: Miranda constructed documentary evidence for the future historical role he expected to occupy.

### Historical reinterpretation

Recent research has shifted attention toward:

- Atlantic rather than narrowly Venezuelan history
- Gender and sexuality in the diaries
- Material culture and collecting
- Information networks and political lobbying
- Race, slavery, and social hierarchy
- The difference between political secrecy and Freemasonry
- The instability of his finances and patrons
- The agency of Sarah Andrews and his household
- The 1812 capitulation as a systemic collapse rather than a one-man betrayal
- His archive as both evidence and curated self-presentation

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted evidence are included. Mere contemporaneity has been excluded.

### Alexander Hamilton — documented fact

Miranda met Hamilton in New York in 1784 and discussed the liberation of Spanish America. Hamilton wrote to him on 23 November 1784. Miranda’s 1792 retrospective memorandum recalled that their conversations helped crystallize a project for the independence of the continent. Hamilton later acknowledged frequent discussion of the subject.

### George Washington — documented fact

Miranda visited Washington at Mount Vernon in 1783. His diary records the meeting and observations of Washington, the household, and the recent war. The encounter connected Miranda directly to the military leadership of the American Revolution.

### Thomas Jefferson — documented fact

Miranda met Jefferson during his United States travels and again dealt with him as president in Washington in late 1805. Their discussions concerned Spanish America and the planned expedition. Jefferson did not give formal authorization; the degree of tacit encouragement remains disputed.

### Benjamin Franklin — reported fact

Miranda moved in Philadelphia circles connected with Franklin and the American revolutionary elite. Some biographies state that they met. The sources consulted here did not expose a sufficiently precise diary entry or correspondence to classify a personal meeting as fully documented. The connection is therefore **reported fact**, not certain.

### Johann Wolfgang von Goethe — reported fact

Miranda visited Weimar and is reported to have conversed with Goethe. Later flag traditions connect their discussion of color theory with Miranda’s tricolor. Contact is plausible and widely reported; the elaborate quoted dialogue is not securely verified.

### Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart — no documented personal connection found

Miranda attended musical and theatrical performances during his European travels, but the consulted evidence did not establish a meeting, correspondence, or collaboration with Mozart. Shared presence in the European musical world is insufficient.

### Napoleon Bonaparte — documented or strongly reported fact

Miranda and Napoleon encountered one another in revolutionary Paris. Later recollections attribute remarks by Napoleon about Miranda’s revolutionary character. The meeting is accepted; the famous phrasing is a reported recollection rather than a verbatim contemporary record.

### Simón Bolívar — documented fact

They met in London in July 1810, collaborated in the independence project, returned to Venezuela, and served in the First Republic. Bolívar commanded Puerto Cabello under Miranda and participated in Miranda’s arrest at La Guaira on 31 July 1812. Their final relationship is central to the disputed “betrayal” narrative.

### Bernardo O’Higgins Riquelme — reported fact

O’Higgins is traditionally described as having encountered Miranda in London and absorbed his continental ideas. The ideological association is strong, but details of the meetings and later claims of Masonic initiation depend heavily on retrospective testimony. Personal contact is best labeled **reported fact**; a formal lodge relationship is unverified.

### José de San Martín — no documented personal meeting found

Later Masonic and nationalist histories link San Martín to Miranda through the Lautaro or Caballeros Racionales societies. Specialist research has questioned whether they ever met. San Martín’s London stay occurred too late for many popular versions. The safest finding is indirect ideological or network affinity, not a documented personal connection.

### Manuel Belgrano — no documented personal connection found

Belgrano belonged to the wider Spanish-American reform and independence generation, but the consulted sources did not establish a meeting or correspondence with Miranda.

### Carlos María de Alvear — reported fact

Alvear is included in later accounts of the London and Cádiz revolutionary networks associated with the Caballeros Racionales. A direct personal relationship with Miranda is reported in secondary traditions but was not securely established in the consulted primary material.

### Antonio José de Sucre — no documented personal connection found

Sucre was a young officer during Miranda’s final Venezuelan period, but the sources consulted did not establish a meeting, correspondence, or command relationship between them.

### Simón Rodríguez — no documented personal connection found

Both were Caracas-born intellectual figures connected to Bolívar’s world, but no direct relationship was verified.

### Alexander von Humboldt — reported fact

Miranda and Humboldt belonged to overlapping European and Spanish-American intellectual networks. Some biographical accounts place them in contact, but the consulted sources did not establish a precise meeting or correspondence strongly enough for “documented fact.”

### Marie-Joseph Paul Yves Roch Gilbert du Motier, Marquis de Lafayette — reported fact

Miranda moved in overlapping American and French revolutionary circles with Lafayette. Later Masonic stories claim Lafayette initiated him, but specialist research finds no supporting lodge evidence. Personal acquaintance is reported; Masonic initiation is **unverified**.

### Louis XVI — documented institutional connection, no personal meeting established

Miranda served in the French army only after the monarchy’s fall. He experienced revolutionary France during Louis XVI’s reign and revolution, but no direct personal encounter was established.

### Catherine II is not on the supplied atlas roster

Her major documented connection has nevertheless been treated extensively elsewhere in this dossier.

### Christopher Columbus — documented intellectual-symbolic connection, not personal

Miranda derived **Colombia** from Columbus’s name as a designation for emancipated Spanish America. This was a documented symbolic and linguistic influence, not a relationship between contemporaries.

### Atahualpa — documented symbolic influence, not personal

Miranda’s proposal for a continental executive titled **Inca** drew on the political memory of the Inca world. No singular personal doctrine traceable specifically to Atahualpa has been established; the connection is symbolic.

### Lautaro — documented symbolic influence, disputed institutional connection

Later revolutionary organizations used Lautaro’s name. Miranda’s personal responsibility for naming or founding the Lautaro lodges is disputed. The connection belongs chiefly to posthumous organizational mythology.

### Adam Smith — documented intellectual influence is plausible but not individually verified

Miranda read extensively in British political economy, but the consulted sources did not expose a specific annotated citation or explicit acknowledgment sufficient to classify Smith as a documented personal influence.

### Jean-Jacques Rousseau — reported intellectual influence
