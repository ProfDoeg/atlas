# Margarita / Marina / Pelagia: Research Dossier

## Scope and Identity Note

The heading **“Margarita / Marina / Pelagia” does not identify one securely documented historical individual**. It points to an entangled group of late-antique female saints whose names, feast days, geographical associations, and narratives were repeatedly combined:

1. **Pelagia the Penitent**, also called Pelagia of Antioch, Pelagia the Actress, Pelagia the Harlot, Pelagia the Courtesan, Margarita (“Pearl”), and—in some Latin and later sources—Marina. According to the *Life of Saint Pelagia* attributed to Jacob/James the Deacon, she was an Antiochene actress or courtesan who converted, relinquished her property, and lived near Jerusalem as a male-presenting ascetic called Pelagius.

2. **Pelagia the Virgin Martyr of Antioch**, a young Christian remembered by Ambrose of Milan and John Chrysostom as having leapt from a building when soldiers came to seize her. Her cult was observed on 8 October, the same day eventually assigned to Pelagia the Penitent.

3. **Marina/Margaret of Antioch**, a legendary virgin martyr ordinarily placed in Antioch of Pisidia, known as Marina in Greek and Eastern traditions and Margaret or Margarita in Latin Western traditions. Her later legend includes imprisonment, torture, a demonic dragon, miraculous survival, and execution.

4. **Marina/Marinos the Monk**, a separate cross-dressing monastic saint whose story was sometimes drawn into the same complex.

5. **Pelagia of Tarsus**, another largely legendary martyr whose narrative acquired material related to the Antiochene Pelagias and other virgin-martyr legends.

This dossier centers on **Pelagia the Penitent—the woman called Margarita in her principal Life—while documenting the Marina/Margaret/Pelagia conflations that make a conventional single life history impossible**.

The strongest historical conclusion is therefore limited: a famous Antiochene performer or courtesan may have converted to ascetic Christianity in the late fourth century. Nearly every biographical detail beyond that proposition comes from hagiography rather than independently corroborated records.

### Name etymologies and their role in the conflation

- **Pelagia** derives from Greek *pelagos*, “sea,” and may mean “of the sea.”
- **Marina** derives from Latin *mare*, “sea,” and similarly means “marine” or “of the sea.”
- **Margarita** derives from Greek *margaritēs* and Latin *margarita*, “pearl.”

The semantic association of sea and pearl made Pelagia, Marina, and Margarita especially susceptible to textual reinterpretation. In the *Life of Pelagia*, Margarita is explained as the performer’s professional nickname, associated with her beauty or abundant pearls. Marina is not simply a literal translation of Margarita; its presence reflects the wider collision of several saintly identities.

## Sources and Evidentiary Problems

### The principal Life

The main source is the *Vita Sanctae Pelagiae*, attributed to a deacon named Jacob or James. He identifies himself as the deacon of Bishop Nonnus and presents the events as having occurred “in our days.” A Latin version was made by a translator identifying himself as **Eustochius**. The work survives in Greek, Syriac, Latin, Armenian, Georgian, Arabic, Slavonic, and other traditions, with variant recensions.

The work is catalogued in the Greek hagiographic tradition as **BHG 1478–1478d**, with related recensions. Its broad circulation demonstrates the legend’s popularity but does not prove the historicity of its episodes.

Historical problems include:

- no year is stated;
- the archbishop of Antioch is repeatedly left unnamed;
- Nonnus’s episcopal see is not consistently or securely identified;
- Jacob cannot be independently verified as an eyewitness;
- the narrative follows recognizable conversion-romance and desert-hagiography conventions;
- speech, dreams, demonic dialogue, private correspondence, and internal thoughts are narrated with literary completeness;
- divergent recensions alter names, locations, chronology, and institutional details.

The work remains evidence for late-antique Christian ideals, attitudes toward actresses and sex work, baptismal practice, property renunciation, asceticism, and gendered sanctity even where it cannot be treated as a documentary biography.

### Possible independent historical kernel

John Chrysostom, in *Homily 67 on Matthew*, refers to a notorious actress or prostitute who had been exceptionally wealthy but converted and became more fervent than many lifelong Christians. He does not name her Pelagia in the surviving passage.

Some scholars identify this unnamed convert with the woman behind the *Life of Pelagia*. If correct, she belonged in late-fourth-century Antioch, during Chrysostom’s preaching career there. The identification is plausible but unproved.

### Pelagia the Virgin

Ambrose of Milan and John Chrysostom knew a different Antiochene Pelagia: a young virgin who died by jumping from a roof or upper part of a house when officials or soldiers came to seize her. This testimony is earlier than the surviving developed Life of the penitent courtesan.

The common city and feast day encouraged later conflation. The martyr may be a historical figure; the relationship between her and Pelagia the Penitent is unresolved.

### Proposed chronology

Three principal chronological constructions appear:

1. **Late fourth century:** Pelagia the Penitent is the unnamed converted actress mentioned by Chrysostom. Nonnus would then have to be a different or otherwise unknown bishop from the better-documented fifth-century Nonnus of Edessa.

2. **Mid-fifth century:** Nonnus is identified with the bishop of Edessa active around 449–451 and again later in the fifth century. Pelagia’s conversion and death are consequently placed around the 450s, often approximately 457.

3. **Primarily literary figure:** Pelagia the Penitent is a hagiographic composite developed from the virgin Pelagia, Chrysostom’s unnamed converted actress, biblical penitent-woman traditions, ascetic folklore, and literary romance.

No surviving external record conclusively decides among these possibilities.

## Family and Ancestry

No dependable information survives concerning Pelagia the Penitent’s:

- parents;
- siblings;
- ancestry;
- childhood household;
- marital status;
- partners;
- children;
- legal guardians;
- ethnic identity;
- precise civic status.

Later devotional accounts sometimes call her wealthy by birth or a member of Antioch’s prosperous social class. The principal Life instead emphasizes the wealth generated or displayed through her profession. Statements that she was “born into immense wealth” are later expansions and should not be treated as independently documented.

Her slaves and household servants imply substantial resources and a managerial household. The Life gives no genealogy and does not explain whether she had inherited property, accumulated earnings, received gifts from patrons, or controlled property through an intermediary.

## Childhood and Early Life

Nothing is securely known about Pelagia’s childhood.

The Life introduces her as an already famous adult performer or courtesan. It does not supply:

- an age;
- a birth narrative;
- parents’ names;
- childhood religion;
- training history;
- an explanation of how she entered performance or prostitution;
- details of coercion, enslavement, manumission, or voluntary employment.

Later retellings sometimes confuse her childhood with that of Marina/Margaret, allegedly the daughter of a pagan priest, or with Pelagia of Tarsus, allegedly a noblewoman. These details do not belong to the earliest Life of Pelagia the Penitent.

## Education and Formation

No formal education is documented.

As a prominent performer and leader of an entourage, the Pelagia of the Life would have required some combination of:

- dance or theatrical training;
- music and performance skills;
- dress and cosmetic expertise;
- social fluency;
- household and financial management;
- familiarity with elite patrons and public spectacle.

These are reasonable contextual inferences, not facts individually stated in surviving administrative records.

Her Christian formation, according to the Life, was sudden and concentrated:

- she heard Nonnus preach;
- she wrote requesting baptism;
- she received direct instruction and examination;
- the deaconess Romana became her baptismal sponsor;
- she spent approximately a week under Romana’s supervision;
- she confronted temptation personified as the Devil;
- she relinquished her property;
- she departed for Jerusalem;
- she adopted severe solitary asceticism.

Nonnus is therefore presented as preacher, baptizer, spiritual director, and facilitator of her renunciation. Romana is presented as sponsor, guardian, and immediate female ecclesiastical supervisor.

## Antioch: Social and Institutional Setting

Late-antique Antioch was one of the Roman Empire’s largest cities and a center of imperial administration, commerce, entertainment, and Christianity. Its theatrical and circus cultures were frequent targets of clerical criticism. Performers could enjoy visibility and elite patronage while occupying ambiguous or degraded legal and moral categories.

Christian rhetoric regularly grouped actresses, dancers, mimes, and prostitutes. Consequently, the Life’s description of Pelagia as an actress and “harlot” does not by itself establish the exact form of her employment. Possibilities include:

- professional stage performer;
- mime or dancer;
- leader of a performance troupe;
- elite courtesan;
- sex worker;
- a performer stereotyped as sexually available by clerical authors;
- some combination of these roles.

The text’s terminology and moral agenda prevent a more exact occupational classification.

## Early Career and Public Persona

### “Foremost actress”

The Life calls Margarita the foremost or leading actress of Antioch. Some recensions describe her as leader of the city’s dancers or theatrical women.

This is a hagiographic claim without corroborating payroll, guild, tax, court, or theatrical records. It nevertheless locates the character at the top of an entertainment hierarchy rather than among anonymous street prostitution.

### Margarita, “the Pearl”

Her professional or popular name was **Margarita**. Two explanations recur:

- she wore large quantities of pearls;
- she was regarded as the “pearl” of Antioch because of her beauty or eminence.

The first is explicit in many retellings; the second is an interpretive expansion. The name may also perform a literary function: the “pearl” buried in vice is recovered for Christianity.

### Entourage and spectacle

The Life stages her first appearance before the bishops as a public procession. She passes the Basilica of Saint Julian:

- riding a donkey;
- surrounded by servants, attendants, young men, or a theatrical entourage;
- perfumed;
- bareheaded;
- clothed in gold, pearls, and precious stones;
- wearing garments described as revealing the shape of her body.

Most bishops avert their eyes. Nonnus continues looking and later asks why they were disturbed. He uses the spectacle as an indictment of their own negligence: she spends more effort adorning herself for mortal admirers than clerics spend preparing their souls for God.

This highly constructed scene establishes the Life’s governing reversal: public erotic display becomes the stimulus for episcopal self-accusation and ultimately for conversion.

## Chronological Life History

## 1. The episcopal gathering at Antioch

According to the Life, the archbishop of Antioch summoned eight neighboring bishops for an unspecified matter. They lodged or met at the Basilica of the martyr Julian.

Among them was **Nonnus**, formerly an Egyptian or Pachomian monk and now a bishop. The Life does not consistently state his see. Later tradition commonly calls him bishop of Heliopolis or Edessa, but the historical Nonnus of Edessa does not fit comfortably into every proposed chronology.

Jacob, the narrator, identifies himself as Nonnus’s deacon.

**Evidentiary label:** hagiographic narrative; no surviving council act corroborates this gathering.

## 2. Margarita passes the basilica

The bishops see Margarita pass with her entourage. Her dress, cosmetics, jewelry, perfume, and bodily display are described at length. The bishops avert their faces. Nonnus watches and weeps.

After she passes, Nonnus asks whether they took pleasure in her beauty. When they do not answer, he contrasts her elaborate preparation for human admirers with their inadequate preparation for God.

**Evidentiary label:** literary scene within the Life.

## 3. Nonnus’s dream

That night Nonnus dreams that a filthy black dove circles him during worship. At the dismissal he catches it and plunges it into water; it emerges clean and white and flies upward.

The dream foreshadows baptism. Its sequence—pollution, immersion, whitening, ascent—is a transparent theological construction.

**Evidentiary label:** hagiographic dream, not independently verifiable.

## 4. The Sunday sermon

On the following Sunday, Nonnus is asked to preach. Pelagia enters the church, reportedly for the first time. His sermon concerns judgment, punishment, paradise, and divine mercy. She weeps continuously.

Some later retellings say she entered by chance; others imply that she had heard about Nonnus or was providentially drawn to the church.

**Evidentiary label:** hagiographic narrative.

## 5. Letter requesting baptism

Pelagia sends two servants to locate Nonnus. She then sends wax tablets or a written message addressing him as a disciple of Christ. She calls herself a sinner and servant of the Devil but appeals to Christ’s reception of sinners.

Nonnus initially refuses a private meeting, warning that he is a sinner and a bishop and requiring ecclesiastical witnesses. She appears before the assembled bishops and throws herself at his feet. She demands baptism and declares that if he refuses her, her future sins will be charged to him.

**Evidentiary label:** hagiographic correspondence; the original tablets do not survive.

## 6. Examination, confession, and baptism

The unnamed archbishop authorizes the chief deaconess, **Romana**, to receive Pelagia. Nonnus questions her. She identifies Pelagia as her original name and Margarita as the name by which the public knew her.

Romana serves as baptismal sponsor. Pelagia is baptized, anointed, and clothed in the white baptismal garment. She receives the Eucharist.

The narrative contains details useful to historians of late-antique baptism and the female diaconate. Those details may reflect actual ecclesiastical practice even if the individual episode was literary.

**Evidentiary label:** hagiographic narrative containing plausible contemporary ritual information.

## 7. Temptation after baptism

The Devil appears or speaks to Pelagia, protesting that he had enriched and adorned her and asking why she has rejected him. Pelagia makes the sign of the cross and drives him away. The encounter is repeated.

This is a personification of post-baptismal temptation common in conversion literature.

**Evidentiary label:** theological legend.

## 8. Property inventory and renunciation

On the third day after baptism, Pelagia summons the steward or manager of her household and orders a complete inventory of:

- gold;
- silver;
- jewels;
- clothing;
- household goods;
- other possessions.

She transfers the property to Nonnus. He entrusts it to the church’s steward for distribution to widows, orphans, and the poor and reportedly warns that none of it should be diverted into ordinary church funds because it represents morally compromised wealth.

Pelagia also frees her male and female slaves, giving them gold ornaments or other resources before dismissal.

The account is important but cannot be financially quantified. No deeds, inventories, manumission records, or church accounts survive. The text gives no reliable amount and no property addresses.

**Evidentiary label:** hagiographic claim; no external financial documentation.

## 9. Departure from Antioch

Pelagia remains with Romana for approximately eight days. She then removes her baptismal dress and dons a rough tunic and mantle associated with Nonnus. She secretly leaves Antioch at night.

The Life does not describe:

- her precise route;
- companions;
- travel expenses;
- whether she traveled by land or sea;
- the time required;
- stops between Antioch and Jerusalem.

A journey from Antioch to Jerusalem would have been several hundred miles through Roman Syria and Palestine.

**Evidentiary label:** hagiographic narrative.

## 10. Life as Pelagius on the Mount of Olives

Years later, Jacob asks Nonnus’s permission to visit Jerusalem. Nonnus tells him to seek a eunuch monk named **Pelagius**, renowned for ascetic discipline, without revealing that the monk is Pelagia.

Jacob finds Pelagius enclosed in a cell on the Mount of Olives, communicating through a small window. The ascetic’s former beauty has disappeared; the body is emaciated, darkened, and roughened by fasting and exposure. Jacob reports that neither he nor Jerusalem generally recognized Pelagius as a woman.

The designation “eunuch” could mean that others perceived Pelagius as a castrated man, an asexual religious figure, or a person outside ordinary reproductive masculinity. The Life does not provide Pelagia/Pelagius’s own explanation of identity.

Pelagius blesses Jacob and advises prayer. Jacob visits other holy places and returns several days later.

**Evidentiary label:** hagiographic narrative presented as eyewitness testimony.

## 11. Death and discovery

On Jacob’s return, Pelagius does not answer. Looking through the window, he sees that the ascetic has died. He informs monks and clergy in Jerusalem.

When the body is prepared for burial, those present discover that Pelagius was anatomically female. The news spreads. Crowds of monks and religious communities—reported as coming from Jerusalem, Jericho, the Jordan region, and elsewhere—attend the burial.

The Life presents the discovery as confirmation that a woman had surpassed conventional assumptions about feminine weakness.

No grave inscription, contemporary burial record, or securely identified relic deposit corroborates the account.

**Evidentiary label:** hagiographic narrative.

## Death, Date, and Burial

Pelagia’s historical date of death is unknown.

The frequently repeated date **457** depends on identifying Nonnus with the fifth-century bishop of Edessa and synchronizing the Life with later chronographic traditions. The identification is contested. Other popular accounts place Pelagia in the third or fourth century, but these commonly confuse her with Pelagia the Virgin or Pelagia of Tarsus.

The Life says Pelagia died peacefully in her cell after severe asceticism. Later summaries sometimes state that she died from fasting or “excessive austerities.” This is a reasonable reading of her depicted physical condition but is not a clinical cause of death.

Her burial is traditionally placed on the **Mount of Olives**. A tomb or cave associated with Pelagia has been venerated there, but sacred-site attribution is not equivalent to archaeological identification of an individual.

## Family, Partners, and Household Network

### Sexual or romantic relationships

The Life supplies no named lover, patron, spouse, or partner. Its repeated designation of Pelagia as a courtesan implies customers or patrons, but none can be identified.

### Household staff and slaves

Pelagia controls an organized household that includes:

- at least two servants used to follow Nonnus;
- a steward with knowledge of her property;
- male and female slaves;
- a broader public entourage.

The Life does not preserve their names. Their disappearance from the narrative after manumission reflects its concentration on Pelagia’s renunciation rather than their subsequent lives.

### Children

No children are mentioned. Claims that she was childless or had never married cannot be verified.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Nonnus

**Nature:** preacher, bishop, baptizer, spiritual guide, and manager of her renunciation.

**Documentary status:** central character in the Life; exact historical identity unresolved.

Tradition identifies him variously with:

- a former Pachomian monk;
- a bishop of Heliopolis;
- Nonnus of Edessa;
- an otherwise unidentified Syrian bishop.

Chronological and institutional inconsistencies prevent secure identification.

### Romana

**Nature:** chief deaconess, baptismal sponsor, guardian, and instructor.

**Documentary status:** hagiographic character; historically plausible ecclesiastical office.

Romana receives Pelagia into the women’s ecclesiastical sphere, remains with her during the immediate post-baptismal period, and attempts to prevent her premature departure.

### Jacob/James the Deacon

**Nature:** narrator, purported eyewitness, deacon of Nonnus, and visitor to Pelagius’s cell.

**Documentary status:** attributed author; independent identity unverified.

### The unnamed archbishop of Antioch

**Nature:** convener of the bishops and senior authority who assigns Romana.

**Documentary status:** deliberately or conventionally anonymous in most recensions. A recension identifying him with Flavian of Antioch would place the story in the late fourth century, but this is not universally accepted.

### Antiochene patrons and spectators

The Life presupposes a public whose money, attention, and desire sustain Margarita’s career. No patron is named. Later descriptions of “all Antioch” admiring her belong to rhetorical amplification.

### Jerusalem ascetic communities

Pelagius becomes known as a holy eunuch or monk. Monks from Jerusalem and neighboring regions reportedly gather for the burial. The Life supplies no securely identifiable monastery.

## Employment, Wealth, and Financial Relationships

### Profession

Pelagia is described by overlapping moral and occupational terms:

- actress;
- dancer;
- leader of a theatrical group;
- courtesan;
- prostitute;
- “harlot.”

Late-antique clerical sources often treated the theatrical profession itself as sexually disreputable. It is therefore impossible to determine whether prostitution was a separately documented occupation or a moral characterization of performance work.

### Wealth

The Life depicts extraordinary movable wealth:

- pearls;
- gold;
- silver;
- precious stones;
- luxury garments;
- perfume and cosmetics;
- servants and slaves;
- a steward-managed household.

No value is stated. Modern claims assigning precise fortunes are unsupported.

### Sources of wealth

Possible sources within the story’s social setting include:

- performance fees;
- gifts from wealthy admirers;
- sexual patronage;
- control of a troupe;
- inherited or purchased household property.

The Life does not separate them.

### Renunciation and distribution

Her property was allegedly transferred to Nonnus and distributed to widows, orphans, and poor persons. This was a financial and institutional rupture: resources accumulated through urban spectacle passed into episcopally supervised charity.

No evidence survives for:

- the total amount;
- tax consequences;
- ownership disputes;
- creditor claims;
- challenges by relatives or patrons;
- the legal mechanism of transfer;
- the eventual fate of individual assets.

### Slavery and manumission

Pelagia’s ownership of enslaved people is treated in the Life as part of her wealth. She reportedly freed them and provided material gifts. This is presented as an act of renunciation, but the text gives no information about how the enslaved household members had been acquired or treated before conversion.

## Religious, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

### Before conversion

The Life calls Pelagia a servant or instrument of the Devil and emphasizes luxury, erotic display, and public acclaim. It does not provide a serious description of her prior beliefs. She may have participated in conventional urban paganism, been religiously unaffiliated, or already possessed some knowledge of Christianity.

Claims that she was a committed pagan are not independently established.

### Conversion theology

Her conversion is structured around:

- fear of judgment;
- confidence that Christ receives sinners;
- baptism as cleansing;
- destruction of the former social identity;
- redistribution of wealth;
- withdrawal from the city;
- ascetic transformation of the body.

### Gender and asceticism

The Life presents Pelagia’s movement into the identity Pelagius as part of ascetic perfection. Major modern interpretations include:

- **disguise interpretation:** she dressed as a man to enter a male monastic environment safely;
- **eunuch interpretation:** “Pelagius” represented an ascetic identity beyond ordinary sexuality;
- **spiritual masculinization:** the author’s culture coded advanced spiritual authority as masculine;
- **gender-crossing interpretation:** Pelagius’s sustained male social existence deserves analysis as more than temporary disguise;
- **apophatic interpretation:** modern categories such as “transgender,” “cross-dresser,” or “woman disguised as a monk” each illuminate and obscure different aspects of the text.

The source records no first-person statement by Pelagia/Pelagius about gender identity. Assertions that Pelagius was definitively a transgender man, or definitively only a disguised woman, exceed what the text can establish.

### Ascetic body

The transformation from jeweled, perfumed visibility to emaciated enclosure is deliberately total. The body once curated for spectators becomes inaccessible except through a cell window. Beauty is not merely rejected but rewritten as bodily austerity and spiritual authority.

---

## Political Activity

No political office, factional activity, court connection, or explicit relationship with imperial officials can be securely attributed to Pelagia the Penitent.

Her story indirectly concerns political and institutional structures:

- urban entertainment under Roman imperial government;
- bishops’ expanding charitable authority;
- Christian clerical regulation of sexuality and spectacle;
- slavery and household property;
- monastic settlement in Palestine.

Accounts placing her under Diocletian or Numerian ordinarily belong to the virgin-martyr Pelagias, not securely to the penitent actress.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, or Financial Questions

No documentary evidence survives of a lawsuit, criminal prosecution, state investigation, fraud case, or financial scandal involving Pelagia the Penitent.

The significant contested questions are historiographic.

### Was Pelagia a prostitute?

**Claim:** Pelagia was Antioch’s most prominent prostitute or courtesan.

**Evidence for:** the Life explicitly labels her in sexualized terms; Chrysostom mentions an unnamed notorious prostitute or actress who converted.

**Evidence against or limiting:** Christian polemic often treated actresses as prostitutes or moral corrupters regardless of their specific sexual commerce. No independent client record, legal charge, or civic registration survives.

**Classification:** hagiographic claim; possible historical fact, exact occupation unresolved.

### Was she responsible for corrupting others?

The Life presents her wealth and beauty as instruments of temptation. This is theological characterization, not a documented criminal accusation.

**Classification:** religious accusation within hagiography.

### Wealth acquired through exploitation or vice

Nonnus reportedly treats her property as tainted and orders it distributed directly to the poor rather than retained for ordinary ecclesiastical use.

This establishes the narrator’s moral interpretation, not a legal finding about proceeds of crime. No amount, victim, transaction, or civil claim is documented.

**Classification:** hagiographic moral claim; underlying finances unknown.

### Ownership of enslaved people

The Life explicitly places enslaved household members under her authority. Within Roman law this would not have been criminal. From a documentary standpoint, their ownership and later manumission are narrative claims without surviving legal instruments.

**Classification:** hagiographic claim consistent with the period’s social institutions.

### Authenticity of the Life

The Life’s attribution to an eyewitness deacon is unverified. Its missing dates and unnamed officials may indicate:

- loss of historical information;
- deliberate universalization;
- fictional construction;
- adaptation of oral tradition;
- pseudonymous authorship.

There is no evidence of a modern-style fraudulent intent. Pseudonymous or rhetorically shaped saints’ Lives belonged to different literary conventions.

**Classification:** disputed authorship and historicity, not established fraud.

### Suicide controversy surrounding the other Pelagia

Pelagia the Virgin’s leap from a building generated a theological problem because Christianity ordinarily condemned suicide. Chrysostom defended her action as inspired courage and escape from anticipated sexual violence, not self-destruction from despair. Later interpreters sometimes suggest she hoped to escape and accepted the risk of death.

This episode belongs primarily to Pelagia the Virgin, but its absorption into the larger Pelagia tradition contributed to later identity confusion.

**Classification:** documented late-antique theological controversy concerning a possibly separate saint.

### Adversarial pass: modern allegations and scandals, 2024–2026

Searches combining the names with *controversy*, *scandal*, *allegation*, *fraud*, *lawsuit*, *accusation*, *conspiracy*, and *criticism* produced no credible newly reported criminal, financial, or institutional allegations concerning the ancient subject.

Recent material instead continues four older disputes:

1. whether Pelagia the Penitent was historical;
2. whether her story developed from Pelagia the Virgin;
3. whether Pelagia/Marina represents a Christianized Aphrodite;
4. whether Pelagius should be interpreted through transgender history.

Recent devotional pages also repeat mutually incompatible dates—284, the late fourth century, approximately 457, or unspecified Diocletianic dates—without resolving the identity conflations.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## The Aphrodite theory

### What the theory says

Hermann Usener argued in *Legenden der Pelagia* that the Pelagia complex represented a Christian transformation of **Aphrodite Pelagia**, the maritime Aphrodite. The associated names appeared to reinforce the theory:

- Pelagia: “of the sea”;
- Marina: “of the sea”;
- Margarita: “pearl”;
- courtesan and erotic beauty;
- gender ambiguity;
- maritime religious symbolism.

### When and by whom

Usener developed the argument in the nineteenth century as part of comparative research into the transformation of pagan cults and myths into Christian legends.

### Evidence cited for it

- the epithet Aphrodite Pelagia;
- Venus Marina;
- the sea/pearl relationship among the names;
- erotic and gender-transformative features;
- shared calendrical or cultic patterns.

### Evidence against it

Hippolyte Delehaye and later scholars argued that:

- attestations of Aphrodite Pelagia and Venus Marina were too sparse;
- similar names do not establish institutional continuity;
- cross-dressing ascetic stories had broader literary precedents;
- the Pelagia Life contains specifically Christian baptismal, penitential, and monastic structures;
- no direct evidence shows a pagan shrine becoming Pelagia’s cult site.

### Assessment

**Classification:** influential scholarly theory, not established historical fact; generally regarded as insufficiently demonstrated.

### Effect on reputation

The theory made Pelagia an important case in debates over pagan survivals in Christian sainthood. It also encouraged the treatment of multiple Pelagias, Marinas, and Margarets as variants of a single archetype.

---

## The dragon legend

The famous episode in which Margaret/Marina is swallowed by a dragon and escapes when a cross causes the monster to burst belongs to **Margaret/Marina of Antioch**, not the principal Life of Pelagia the Penitent.

Some medieval versions acknowledge the episode’s implausibility even while repeating it. The dragon became Margaret’s dominant iconographic attribute and helped make her one of medieval Europe’s most recognizable saints.

**Classification:** medieval miracle legend arising in a conflated neighboring tradition.

---

## Patronage of childbirth

Margaret/Marina became a patron invoked during pregnancy and difficult labor. The association was reinforced by the dragon episode: her emergence intact from the dragon’s body supplied a symbolic analogy to safe childbirth.

Pelagia the Penitent’s Life contains no childbirth episode. Application of this patronage to the combined Margarita/Marina/Pelagia identity is a product of conflation.

**Classification:** documented cultic tradition; symbolic rather than biographical.

---

## The three Pelagias on 8 October

Liturgical traditions commemorated several Pelagias on or around 8 October:

- Pelagia the Virgin of Antioch;
- Pelagia the Penitent;
- Pelagia of Tarsus.

Shared commemoration facilitated narrative exchange. A compiler encountering the same name, city, and feast could interpret separate notices as phases or versions of one life.

**Classification:** documented liturgical circumstance and probable mechanism of conflation.

---

## The converted actress mentioned by Chrysostom

### Story

Chrysostom says that an actress or prostitute who had amassed great wealth converted and attained remarkable Christian zeal.

### Proposed identification

Many scholars regard her as a possible historical basis for Pelagia the Penitent.

### Limitations

Chrysostom does not name her Pelagia, Margarita, or Marina and gives no Mount of Olives sequel.

**Classification:** documented report of an unnamed convert; identification with Pelagia is a plausible but unproved theory.

---

## Nonnus the poet and Nonnus the bishop

Some scholarship has explored whether the Bishop Nonnus of Pelagia’s Life could be related to or confused with **Nonnus of Panopolis**, author of the *Dionysiaca* and a poetic paraphrase of John’s Gospel.

Shared name and approximate period prompted speculation, but no evidence establishes that Pelagia’s bishop was the poet.

**Classification:** scholarly speculation; unproved.

---

## Pelagia as Mary Magdalene

Pelagia’s story belongs to the wider Christian “penitent harlot” tradition associated in Western imagination with Mary Magdalene. Elements include:

- beauty and sexual notoriety;
- public repentance;
- renunciation;
- bodily mortification;
- withdrawal from society.

The canonical New Testament never calls Mary Magdalene a prostitute. Pelagia’s story was not simply copied from the canonical Magdalene narrative, though later preaching, art, and compilations allowed their images to reinforce one another.

**Classification:** documented typological and legendary association, not personal identity.

---

## Pelagius and modern gender narratives

Modern writers have variously presented Pelagius as:

- a woman disguised as a man;
- a female saint who transcended gender;
- a “transvestite saint”;
- a transgender man;
- a eunuch-like monastic figure;
- an example of spiritual masculinity imposed by male hagiographers.

The Life states that Pelagia lived publicly and successfully as a male ascetic and was believed to be a eunuch until death. It does not record an explicit statement of enduring internal gender identity.

Twentieth-century “transvestite saint” scholarship sometimes imported medicalized ideas of disguise and pathology. Recent transgender historiography argues for greater caution about assuming that the male identity was merely deceptive or insignificant.

**Classification:** modern interpretive dispute grounded in a documented narrative of gender crossing.

---

## Relics and Sacred Geography

Relics and shrines attributed to the Pelagia, Margaret, and Marina traditions appear in multiple places.

Claims associated with Margaret/Marina include:

- relics formerly in Constantinople;
- transfer to Italy, including Montefiascone;
- an arm relic at Vatopedi Monastery on Mount Athos;
- numerous Western churches dedicated to Saint Margaret.

Pelagia the Penitent is associated with a cave or tomb on the Mount of Olives.

Because several saints shared the names and because relic histories were repeatedly rewritten, no scientific or continuous documentary chain securely links extant relics to a historically verified Pelagia.

**Classification:** documented cult and relic-attribution traditions; biological identity unverified.

## Artistic and Cultural Representation

### Iconography of Pelagia the Penitent

Typical scenes include:

- Margarita riding past the bishops;
- Nonnus watching while other bishops avert their eyes;
- Pelagia kneeling before Nonnus;
- baptism by Nonnus with Romana present;
- distribution of jewelry and wealth;
- departure in male monastic clothing;
- Pelagius enclosed on the Mount of Olives;
- discovery of the body’s sex after death.

### Iconography of Margaret/Marina

Typical attributes include:

- a dragon;
- a cross;
- torture implements;
- a palm of martyrdom;
- emergence from the dragon;
- execution by beheading.

The dragon image often causes cataloguers and viewers to treat any Margarita/Marina as the better-known Western Margaret, further obscuring Pelagia.

### Medieval literature

Pelagia entered:

- Greek and Syriac menologia;
- Latin *Vitae Patrum* collections;
- Jacobus de Voragine’s *Legenda aurea* tradition;
- vernacular saints’ lives;
- medieval Castilian narratives;
- sermons and penitential exempla.

Her story was frequently paired with those of Thaïs, Mary of Egypt, and Mary Magdalene as evidence that extreme sexual sin could be reversed by extreme penance.

---

## Successes and Failures Within the Narrative

Because the recoverable material is hagiographic, these are narrative outcomes rather than independently measurable career judgments.

### Significant successes

- achieved fame and wealth in Antiochene public entertainment;
- controlled a substantial household and entourage;
- obtained baptism despite Nonnus’s initial caution;
- freed her slaves and disposed of her property;
- successfully disappeared from her former public environment;
- acquired a reputation for holiness as Pelagius;
- became the center of a durable translingual cult.

### Failures or losses

- the Life depicts her former career as spiritual captivity;
- almost all biographical particulars were lost or never recorded;
- her property and public identity were deliberately abandoned;
- her historicity became inseparable from literary motifs;
- later cult transmission repeatedly confused her with other saints.

---

## Illnesses, Injuries, Addictions, and Psychological Crises

No diagnosed illness, addiction, accident, or injury is documented.

The Life records:

- intense weeping after Nonnus’s sermon;
- urgent self-accusation;
- abrupt abandonment of her former life;
- severe fasting and bodily mortification;
- extreme emaciation by the end of her life.

These can be described as religious conduct within the narrative. Retrospective psychiatric diagnosis would be unsupported.

Her cause of death is not medically known. “Excessive asceticism” is a later explanatory inference.

---

## Works by or Attributed to Pelagia

No book, treatise, sermon, poem, diary, or independently surviving letter can be securely attributed to Pelagia.

### Letter to Nonnus

The Life quotes or paraphrases a letter sent on wax tablets requesting baptism.

- **Original date:** unknown.
- **Language:** presumably Greek in the narrative setting.
- **Survival:** only within the Life.
- **Authenticity:** unverified; probably literary composition or reconstruction by the hagiographer.

### Sayings to Jacob

Pelagius gives Jacob a blessing and brief spiritual counsel.

- **Survival:** only within the Life.
- **Authorship:** attributed speech, not independently transmitted.

### Property instructions

Orders to the steward concerning the inventory, slaves, and transfer of wealth survive only as narrated speech.

**Bibliographical conclusion:** Pelagia left no securely authentic corpus. Material published “under her name” consists of liturgical texts, later lives, reconstructed prayers, and attributed speech composed or transmitted by others.

## Primary and Early Works Concerning Pelagia

### Jacob/James the Deacon

- *Βίος καὶ μετάνοια τῆς ὁσίας Πελαγίας* / *Life and Repentance of Saint Pelagia*.
- Common Latin title: *Vita Sanctae Pelagiae* or *Vita S. Pelagiae*.
- Catalogued in several recensions under BHG 1478–1478d and related entries.
- Date: probably fifth or sixth century; exact date disputed.
- Claimed authorship: Jacob, deacon of Nonnus.
- Latin translator: Eustochius.
- Status: principal hagiographic source; purported eyewitness narrative, not independently verified.

### John Chrysostom

- *Homily on Saint Pelagia*.
- Late fourth century.
- Concerns Pelagia the Virgin Martyr, not unambiguously Pelagia the Penitent.
- Status: early evidence for an Antiochene virgin-martyr cult.

- *Homily 67 on Matthew*.
- Contains the story of an unnamed wealthy actress or prostitute who converted.
- Status: possible independent historical kernel for Pelagia the Penitent.

### Ambrose of Milan

- *De virginibus*, Book III.
- Fourth century.
- Refers to the young Antiochene virgin who chose a fatal leap when threatened.
- Status: early evidence for Pelagia the Virgin.

### Theophanes the Confessor

- *Chronographia*.
- Includes notices used by later scholars to identify Nonnus and date the Pelagia story.
- Status: later chronographic evidence; identification remains disputed.

### Jacobus de Voragine

- *Legenda aurea* / *Golden Legend*.
- Thirteenth century.
- Disseminated Pelagia and Margaret material in Latin and vernacular Europe.
- Status: medieval compilation, not an independent witness to late-antique events.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Late antiquity

Pelagia became a model of:

- baptismal transformation;
- repentance after sexual notoriety;
- renunciation of wealth;
- female ascetic achievement;
- victory over spectatorship and bodily display.

Her Life circulated especially effectively in Syriac and Greek Christian environments.

### Byzantine world

She entered calendars, menologia, sermons, hymnography, and ascetic collections. The male monastic identity of Pelagius made the Life particularly useful for discussions of whether holiness transcended embodied sex.

### Latin Middle Ages

Latin translations and compilations spread the story westward. Pelagia was grouped with converted courtesans such as Thaïs and Mary of Egypt. Meanwhile, Margarita became increasingly associated with Margaret/Marina and the dragon legend.

### Early-modern and modern devotional reputation

Pelagia remained a symbol of dramatic repentance. Modern devotional accounts often:

- harmonize incompatible chronologies;
- call her both actress and prostitute without qualification;
- give 457 as a firm death date;
- identify Nonnus confidently as bishop of Edessa;
- conflate her with the virgin martyr;
- repeat invented ages or lengths of ascetic life.

### Feminist interpretation

Pelagia has been read both as:

- a story that permits a woman spiritual authority only after destroying feminine beauty and entering male identity;
- a narrative in which a woman escapes economic and sexual objectification, controls her property, frees dependents, and acquires autonomous religious authority.

These interpretations concern the text’s social work; neither proves the underlying biography.

### Transgender and queer interpretation

Pelagius is now discussed as a premodern gender-crossing saint. The key dispute is methodological: whether a modern category can illuminate a person represented as living socially as a man without erasing the ascetic and theological terms of the source.

### Historical reputation

Most critical scholars do not treat every scene as factual. Positions range from acceptance of a historical converted performer behind the Life to treatment of Pelagia as a composite literary saint. The Life itself remains historically important regardless of the answer.

## Atlas Connections

The following connections satisfy the atlas requirement. They are textual, cultic, or legendary connections unless otherwise stated; no personal meeting can be demonstrated.

### Saint Thecla of Iconium

**Nature:** documented literary and ascetic precedent. The *Acts of Paul and Thecla* portrays Thecla asking to cut her hair and dress as a man for missionary activity. Scholarship on gender-crossing saints treats this tradition as an important predecessor to Pelagia/Pelagius and Marina/Marinos.

**Dates and places:** Thecla’s apocryphal Acts were circulating by the second century; Pelagia’s Life probably took shape in the fifth or sixth century in the Syrian or eastern Mediterranean Christian world.

**Evidentiary label:** documented textual influence or shared hagiographic tradition; no personal contact.

### Mary Magdalene

**Nature:** typological association in the tradition of the repentant sexual sinner. Pelagia was repeatedly grouped with Mary Magdalene in preaching, art, and scholarship. The association was strengthened by the later Western identification of Mary Magdalene as a prostitute, although the canonical Gospels do not call her one.

**Dates and places:** especially medieval and early-modern Christian Europe, with roots in late-antique preaching on repentant women.

**Evidentiary label:** documented reception history; no historical meeting.

### Jacobus de Voragine

**Nature:** compiler and transmitter. His thirteenth-century *Legenda aurea* helped circulate stories of Pelagia and Margaret/Marina across Latin Europe.

**Dates and places:** thirteenth-century Italy and the wider Latin manuscript world.

**Evidentiary label:** documented literary transmission; posthumous connection.

### Joan of Arc

**Nature:** visionary and cultic connection through Saint Margaret. Joan testified that Saint Margaret was among the heavenly voices guiding her. Because Western Margaret was the Latin counterpart of Eastern Marina and had become entangled with Pelagia traditions, this belongs to the subject’s wider name-and-cult complex, not securely to Pelagia the Penitent.

**Dates and places:** Joan’s reported visions in fifteenth-century France; testimony preserved in her trial records.

**Evidentiary label:** documented claim by Joan concerning a visionary Saint Margaret; identity with Pelagia the Penitent unsupported.

### Mary of Egypt

**Nature:** shared “penitent harlot” and desert-ascetic tradition. Pelagia and Mary of Egypt were frequently compared in hagiography and modern scholarship.

**Dates and places:** late-antique and Byzantine eastern Mediterranean; later dissemination throughout Christian Europe.

**Evidentiary label:** documented literary and cultic association; no historical meeting.

### Jesus of Nazareth

**Nature:** explicit religious and literary influence. In the Life, Pelagia appeals to Christ as the one who came to save sinners. Her conversion is modeled on Gospel traditions of Christ receiving socially stigmatized women and sinners.

**Dates and places:** literary dependence in the late-antique Life; no claim of personal contact.

**Evidentiary label:** documented textual and religious influence.

### The Queen of Sheba and King Solomon

**Nature:** proposed literary-symbolic background. Some scholars have compared Pelagia’s jeweled public procession before Nonnus with traditions concerning the richly adorned Queen of Sheba appearing before Solomon. The parallel is interpretive and not explicit in the Life.

**Dates and places:** proposed influence in late-antique literary construction.

**Evidentiary label:** scholarly theory; no personal or directly documented connection.

No reliable source consulted establishes a meeting, correspondence, family tie, patronage relationship, or institutional collaboration between the historical person—if one existed—and any atlas-roster figure.
