## Works by Henry Clay Frick

### Industrial systems and organizations

- **1871–1900:** *H. C. Frick & Company*. A coke-manufacturing enterprise begun as a partnership and expanded by Frick into an integrated complex of coal lands, mines, rail connections, and thousands of coke ovens. This is an institutional and industrial obra rather than a discrete object, and its authorship was collective: capital, engineering, and workers’ labor were indispensable to what Frick organized. ([Frick Collection](https://www.frick.org/about/history/henry_clay_frick); [Warren](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt5vkgd7))

- **1879–1889:** *South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club*. Frick helped organize this private resort corporation and served among its founding members. The club’s alteration and maintenance of the South Fork Dam culminated in the 1889 Johnstown Flood, but responsibility for particular modifications—and Frick’s individual role in them—cannot safely be separated from the club’s collective agency; the lethal infrastructure resists classification as his “work.” ([National Park Service](https://home.nps.gov/jofl/learn/historyculture/henry-clay-frick.htm))

- **1889–1892:** *Reorganization and consolidation of Carnegie’s steel companies*. As chairman, Frick reorganized Carnegie Brothers and helped consolidate its mills, furnaces, coke supply, transportation, and sales into the Carnegie Steel Company. It is an organizational project, jointly made with Carnegie’s partners, managers, engineers, and workforce, rather than a sole-authored corporation. ([National Park Service](https://home.nps.gov/jofl/learn/historyculture/henry-clay-frick.htm); [Warren](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt5vkgd7))

- **1892:** *The Homestead lockout*. Frick’s preparations against the Amalgamated Association—including fencing the mill, employing Pinkerton guards, and refusing union terms—made an industrial-security protocol that produced violent conflict. It should not be aestheticized as an obra: it was a coercive labor action, collectively executed and contested, in which people were killed and wounded. ([Frick Collection](https://www.frick.org/about/history/henry_clay_frick); [PBS, American Experience](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/americanexperience/features/goldman-henry-clay-frick-1849-1919/))

- **1894–1901:** *Union Railroad and the integrated Carnegie industrial system*. Frick promoted a railroad network connecting formerly separated mines, coke works, furnaces, mills, river ports, and trunk railroads. This was infrastructural system-making, not the personal design of a railway; its boundaries and authorship merge into the larger Carnegie organization and subsequently United States Steel. ([Warren](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt5vkgd7); [Henry Clay Frick Papers—Diaries finding aid](https://www.frick.org/sites/default/files/FindingAids/HenryClayFrickDiaries.html))

### Houses, interiors, and estate buildings

- **1882–1883; 1890–1892:** *Clayton*. Frick and his wife purchased an existing Italianate house, first remodeled it and later commissioned Frederick J. Osterling to transform it into a twenty-three-room château-style residence. It is therefore a patron’s evolving domestic project, not a building designed by Frick alone. ([The Frick Pittsburgh](https://www.thefrickpittsburgh.org/clayton); [Clayton preservation history](https://www.thefrickpittsburgh.org/Story-Preserving-an-Era-Celebrating-Thirty-Years-of-Clayton))

- **1897:** *The Playhouse at Clayton*. A children’s playhouse commissioned by Frick from Alden & Harlow as part of improvements to the estate. Its program was shaped for Childs and Helen Frick, making it a family commission as much as the patron’s obra. ([The Frick Pittsburgh](https://www.thefrickpittsburgh.org/Story-The-Playhouse))

- **1904–1906:** *Eagle Rock*. A 104-room summer estate at Prides Crossing, Massachusetts, furnished by Cottier & Company with advice from Joseph Duveen and enlarged conceptually through imported historic interiors. This was a collaborative architectural-decorative Gesamtkunstwerk assembled by patronage; Helen Frick removed a wing in 1938 and demolished the remainder in 1969. ([The Frick Pittsburgh, *Fashioning Aristocracy at Eagle Rock*](https://www.thefrickpittsburgh.org/Files/Admin/FINAL-VMR-DIDACTICS-AND-LABELS_formatted-for-web.pdf?d=202441793457))

- **1912–1914:** *One East 70th Street*. Frick commissioned Thomas Hastings of Carrère & Hastings to design this New York residence, while White, Allom & Company and Elsie de Wolfe furnished substantial portions of its interior. The house was simultaneously a dwelling, a setting constructed around Frick’s collection, and the material premise of his projected public museum. ([One East 70th Street Papers finding aid](https://www.frick.org/sites/default/files/FindingAids/OneEast70thStreetPapers.html); [Frick Collection, “Building the House”](https://www.frick.org/about/history/building_house))

- **1915:** *The Fragonard Room*. A room at One East 70th Street remodeled to house Fragonard’s *Progress of Love* panels and complementary decorative arts acquired through Joseph Duveen. Neither paintings nor interior were authored by Frick, but their deliberately unified installation is a patron-made Gesamtkunstwerk within the larger house. ([One East 70th Street Papers finding aid](https://www.frick.org/sites/default/files/FindingAids/OneEast70thStreetPapers.html))

- **1916:** *Projected sculpture gallery*. Frick commissioned Thomas Hastings to prepare a gallery for land beside his residence, but it was never built. Its surviving architectural drawings make it a project—an intention with material documentation—not a completed building; Hastings later adapted the designs for the first Frick Art Reference Library building. ([Helen Clay Frick Records finding aid](https://www.frick.org/sites/default/files/FindingAids/FARLHelenClayFrickCorrespondence.html))

### Commercial architecture and monuments

- **1901–1903:** *Henry Clay Frick Monument*. A family funerary monument at Homewood Cemetery commissioned from D. H. Burnham & Company. It belongs to Frick’s architectural patronage, while the architectural design remains Burnham’s work. ([Pittsburgh History & Landmarks Foundation](https://phlf.org/education-department/architectural-history/articles/burnham-company-in-pittsburgh/))

- **1901–1902:** *Henry Clay Frick Building*. A Pittsburgh office tower commissioned by Frick from D. H. Burnham & Company as a speculative building and headquarters. It is a developer’s obra whose architectural authorship belongs to Burnham’s firm. ([Pittsburgh History & Landmarks Foundation](https://phlf.org/education-department/architectural-history/articles/burnham-company-in-pittsburgh/))

- **1905:** *Frick Building Annex*. A commercial annex, now called the Allegheny Building, commissioned from D. H. Burnham & Company. Like Frick’s other office projects, it is best classified as real-estate development rather than personal architectural composition. ([Pittsburgh History & Landmarks Foundation](https://phlf.org/education-department/architectural-history/articles/burnham-company-in-pittsburgh/))

- **1909–1910:** *Highland Building*. A thirteen-story speculative office building commissioned from D. H. Burnham & Company in Pittsburgh’s East Liberty district. The documented clientage makes it part of Frick’s development project, but not a design authored by him. ([Pittsburgh History & Landmarks Foundation](https://phlf.org/education-department/architectural-history/articles/burnham-company-in-pittsburgh/))

- **1914–1916:** *William Penn Hotel*. A major Pittsburgh hotel commissioned and developed by Frick, with the original building designed by Janssen & Abbott. Later additions do not belong to Frick’s lifetime project, so the present hotel is not identical with the thing he commissioned. ([Historic Hotels of America](https://www.historichotels.org/us/hotels-resorts/omni-william-penn-hotel-pittsburgh/history); [Skyscraper Center](https://www.skyscrapercenter.com/building/omni-william-penn-hotel/23156))

- **1915–1917:** *Union Arcade*. A block-sized speculative retail and office building, later the Union Trust Building, commissioned from Frederick J. Osterling. Pierre Liesch claimed an important role in its initial form; historians have questioned the distribution of design credit, although Osterling remained architect of record. ([Pennsylvania Heritage](https://paheritage.wpengine.com/article/frederick-osterling-tale-two-buildings/); [Pittsburgh historic nomination](https://www.pittsburghpa.gov/files/assets/city/v/1/dcp/documents/historic-nominations/7195_the_bradberry_historic_nomination.pdf))

### Collection and institutional project

- **1881–1919:** *The art collection of Henry Clay Frick*. An intentionally assembled body of paintings, sculpture, furniture, porcelain, enamels, clocks, and other decorative arts—not artworks created by Frick, but a collector’s obra produced through selection, purchase, arrangement, dealers, advisers, and rooms designed around objects. Its limits are historically unstable: fewer than half of the approximately 1,800 objects now owned by The Frick Collection came from Frick’s original bequest, and the archive records proposed and rejected purchases as well as acquisitions. ([Art Collecting Files of Henry Clay Frick finding aid](https://www.frick.org/sites/default/files/FindingAids/ArtCollectingFiles.html); [Frick Collection history](https://www.frick.org/about/history))

- **1915; realized 1931–1935:** *The Frick Collection as a public museum*. Frick’s final will made his residence, furnishings, and collection the basis of a public gallery and supplied an endowment and governing trustees: a legal protocol with manifesto-like institutional force. The museum that opened in 1935 was nevertheless a posthumous manifestation substantially designed and organized by its trustees, Frederick Mortimer Clapp, John Russell Pope, and Frick’s children, so it cannot be treated as Frick’s sole completed work. ([Frick Collection construction-records finding aid](https://www.frick.org/sites/default/files/FindingAids/TFCConstructionRecords.html); [Frick Collection history](https://www.frick.org/about/history))

### Documentary traces rather than discrete works

- **1897–1919:** *Office diaries*. Chronological records of Frick’s activities survive in the family papers, but they were maintained substantially by secretaries and continued beyond his death. They resist inclusion as authored literary works: they are administrative records generated around Frick rather than a diary securely written by him. ([Henry Clay Frick Papers—Diaries finding aid](https://www.frick.org/sites/default/files/FindingAids/HenryClayFrickDiaries.html))

- **1882–1919:** *Correspondence, memoranda, contracts, vouchers, and instructions*. Frick produced or authorized a large documentary body concerning business, collecting, politics, philanthropy, real estate, buildings, and family affairs. The archival descriptions identify records rather than a fixed canon of titled texts, and much correspondence is incoming or secretarial; treating every document as a separately authored obra would manufacture a completeness the evidence does not support. ([Henry Clay Frick Papers—Correspondence finding aid](https://www.frick.org/sites/default/files/pdf/archives/HCFCorrespondence.pdf); [Henry Clay Frick Papers—Subject Files finding aid](https://www.frick.org/sites/default/files/FindingAids/HenryClayFrickSubjectFiles.html); [One East 70th Street Papers finding aid](https://www.frick.org/sites/default/files/FindingAids/OneEast70thStreetPapers.html))
