## Works by Prince Hall

### Political petitions and civic manifestos

- **13 January 1777:** *Petition for Freedom to the Massachusetts Council and House of Representatives*. An abolitionist manifesto-petition made collectively with seven other Black men; Hall signed it, but the surviving manuscript does not establish that he alone composed it. ([signed manuscript and editorial history](https://ushistory.education/historical-documents/prince-hall-petition-for-freedom/))

- **4 January 1787:** *Petition by the African Lodge to the General Court of Massachusetts*. A collective petition and projected plan for voluntary settlement in Africa, including proposed civil and religious institutions; Hall’s leadership is clear, but sole authorship should not be assumed. ([document and provenance](https://hamilton.gilderlehrman.org/supporting-document/petition-african-lodge-freemasons-1787))

- **17 October 1787:** *Petition for the Education of Colored People*. A collective petition-manifesto arguing that taxpaying Black residents were entitled to publicly supported education; Hall participated with other petitioners, and the precise division of authorship is unknown. ([documentary account](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED418183.pdf))

- **27 February 1788:** *Petition of Prince Hall to the Massachusetts General Court*. An antikidnapping petition acting on behalf of Boston’s Black community after free Black sailors and residents had been seized for sale into slavery; contemporary newspapers reprinted it under Hall’s signature. ([scholarly account](https://open.upress.virginia.edu/read/black-cosmopolitans/section/bdffa97e-6ac8-41b9-ac32-6d9d352b9bc1), [contemporary printed manifestation](https://www.gilderlehrman.org/sites/default/files/GLC/documents/2023-07/09728_OS.pdf))

- **4 October 1796:** *Petition to the Selectmen of Boston for a School for Black Children*. A collective civic protocol requesting a schoolhouse and teacher; the surviving copy ends with Hall’s name, although it speaks for “a number” of Black families rather than presenting itself as an individual composition. ([scholarly discussion and archival citation](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/history-of-education-quarterly/article/an-african-school-for-african-americans-black-demands-for-education-in-antebellum-boston/B0F15F19EB0C27E76643A7209F9D53D9))

### Masonic charges: spoken works with printed manifestations

- **25 June 1792:** *A Charge Delivered to the Brethren of the African Lodge, at the Hall of Brother William Smith, in Charlestown*. A public Masonic oration—simultaneously sermon, political argument, and ethical charge—delivered by Hall and printed at the lodge’s request; it is not merely a “book,” though the pamphlet is its principal surviving manifestation. ([bibliographical record](https://onlinebooks.library.upenn.edu/webbin/book//lookupname?key=Hall%2C+Prince%2C+1748-1807), [scholarly classification](https://academic.oup.com/book/7614/chapter-abstract/152629609))

- **24 June 1797:** *A Charge, Delivered to the African Lodge, at Menotomy*. Hall’s second surviving printed charge is a spoken lodge address that also functions as an abolitionist and Black-political manifesto; the eighteen-page pamphlet says it was published at the members’ request. ([Library of Congress copy and description](https://www.loc.gov/item/87880347/), [scholarly classification](https://academic.oup.com/book/7614/chapter-abstract/152629609))

### Masonic correspondence, warrants, and operative texts

- **2 March and 30 June 1784:** *Letters to William Moody seeking a warrant for African Lodge*. Two institutional letters whose purpose was performative rather than literary: they petitioned English Masonry to turn the provisionally operating African Lodge into a regularly chartered lodge. The correspondence belongs jointly to Hall’s agency and the lodge’s corporate project. ([documented dates](https://mwphgldc.com/history), [account of the first letter](https://mwphglva.org/ph-history/))

- **16 February 1792:** *Masonic certificate*. An operative credential entered in Hall’s letter book and issued under the titles of the lodge’s “Grand Officers”; Hall’s role as presiding officer is evident, but the certificate is a corporate instrument rather than securely his sole composition. ([Upton’s documentary edition and summary](https://www.wikitree.com/wiki/Space%3APrince_Hall_and_African_Lodge))

- **22 March 1797:** *Letter and license to Peter Mantore and the Philadelphia brethren*. A letter that acts as a warrant-like instrument, authorizing Black Masons in Philadelphia to assemble under African Lodge No. 459; its legal Masonic authority was—and remains—debatable because Hall’s English warrant did not expressly empower him to create subordinate lodges. ([textual and historical analysis](https://www.wikitree.com/wiki/Space%3APrince_Hall_and_African_Lodge))

- **15 June 1802:** *Letter to Grand Secretary William White*. An institutional report and complaint recording members admitted and deceased, remittances sent, and years of unanswered correspondence with the Grand Lodge of England. It survives through Hall’s manuscript letter book and is better understood as an administrative act than as a titled literary work. ([documentary account](https://thephylaxis.org/walkes/draffen.php))

- **16 August 1806:** *Memorandum of a letter to William White*. This entry resists attribution: later accounts sometimes call it Hall’s letter, but William H. Upton’s examination of the manuscript describes it as an unsigned memorandum of a letter sent by Nero Prince. It therefore cannot safely be included as Hall’s individual composition, even though it occurs in the book bearing his name. ([Upton manuscript description](https://verity59.org/uploads/3/4/7/7/34770163/1900_arsquatuorcorona13.pdf))

- **c. 1792–1806:** *Prince Hall’s Letter Book* (manuscript label: *Prince Hall’s Sermons, 1787*). A roughly three-hundred-page working archive in Hall’s hand containing correspondence, certificates, accounts, copied texts, and lodge records. It is an obra as compilation and administrative instrument, but not every item in it is by Hall; even its cover-title is misleading, so it resists classification as either an authored book or a coherent “work.” ([Upton’s documentary description](https://verity59.org/uploads/3/4/7/7/34770163/1900_arsquatuorcorona13.pdf), [bibliographical record](https://books.google.com/books?vid=UOM%3A39015051343575))

### Institutions and sustained projects

- **1775–1787:** *African Lodge No. 1 / African Lodge No. 459*. A collective institutional obra initiated by Hall and fourteen other Black Masons: first a restricted lodge without a full warrant, then a chartered lodge after the English warrant of 1784 reached Boston in 1787. It should not be reduced to Hall’s individual “creation,” but his organizing, petitions, and long service as master made it his central sustained project. ([Library of Congress historical overview](https://guides.loc.gov/prince-hall-freemasonry), [charter history](https://mwphglva.org/ph-history/))

- **1797:** *African Lodge in Philadelphia*. A daughter-lodge project authorized through Hall’s letter to Peter Mantore and subsequently led by figures including Absalom Jones; whether Hall possessed lawful power to establish it was disputed, making it both an institution and a contested performative creation. ([documentary reconstruction](https://www.wikitree.com/wiki/Space%3APrince_Hall_and_African_Lodge))

- **1797:** *African Lodge in Providence*. A lodge-building project associated with Hall’s extension of Black Masonry beyond Boston. The documentation is thinner than for Philadelphia, and later accounts differ over the exact day and institutional form, so it should not be treated as a securely titled individual work. ([Library of Congress overview](https://guides.loc.gov/prince-hall-freemasonry), [historical account](https://mwphglin.org/prince-hall-bio/))

- **after 1796–c. 1806:** *School for Black children in Hall’s home*. A practical educational project undertaken after public authorities rejected petitions for adequate schooling; Hall supplied domestic space while teachers, pupils, families, and later the African Society collectively made the school. Sources disagree about precisely when teaching began and when it moved from his house, so the project has no safely fixed single date. ([scholarly history of the school campaign](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/history-of-education-quarterly/article/an-african-school-for-african-americans-black-demands-for-education-in-antebellum-boston/B0F15F19EB0C27E76643A7209F9D53D9), [institutional account](https://mwphglin.org/prince-hall-bio/))
