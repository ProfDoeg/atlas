## Works by Carl Friedrich Gauss

Gauss's œuvre does not sit comfortably in a single mold. A handful of books are genuine treatises, finished and published because Gauss judged them "ripe." Around them sits a larger body of short papers and memoirs, often returning to the same problem (the fundamental theorem of algebra, the law of quadratic reciprocity) across decades. Beneath all of it lies the part of his work he never finished for publication at all: a private mathematical diary kept for eighteen years, and a much larger Nachlass of manuscripts, letters, and working notes that his heirs, Göttingen colleagues, and later editors spent the rest of the nineteenth century sorting, transcribing, and printing. Any list of "the books Gauss wrote" misses most of what he actually produced.

### Major treatises published in his lifetime

- **1801:** *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae*. Gauss's first and most consequential book, written largely by 1798 but delayed in press until 1801. It gathers congruences, quadratic forms, quadratic reciprocity, and the theory of cyclotomy (the division of the circle, which yields the construction of regular polygons including the 17-gon) into a single systematic treatment with rigorous proofs, effectively founding modern number theory as a discipline rather than a miscellany of isolated results. Its Latin and its difficulty limited readers at first; its influence grew for decades through the generation of mathematicians, Dirichlet and Dedekind among them, who learned number theory from it.
- **1809:** *Theoria motus corporum coelestium in sectionibus conicis solem ambientium*. A two-part treatise on determining and refining planetary and cometary orbits from observational data, written up after Gauss's 1801 calculation of Ceres's orbit had already made him famous. It lays out methods of orbit computation from a short observed arc and develops the statistical machinery, including an early rigorous treatment of least squares, needed to reconcile imperfect observations.
- **1823, with supplements in 1826 and 1828:** *Theoria combinationis observationum erroribus minimis obnoxiae*, and its sequels. A theoretical account of how to combine imperfect measurements to minimize error, integrating least squares with probability theory. Gauss had used equivalent methods since at least 1801; Adrien-Marie Legendre had published the method first, in 1805, and the overlap became a lasting priority dispute rather than a plagiarism case.
- **1827:** *Disquisitiones generales circa superficies curvas*. Gauss's principal work in differential geometry, grown directly out of his practical geodetic survey work for the Kingdom of Hanover. It introduces what is now called Gaussian curvature and proves the *theorema egregium* ("remarkable theorem"): that this curvature can be determined entirely from measurements taken within a surface, without reference to the surrounding space the surface sits in. The result reoriented how geometers thought about surfaces as objects with an intrinsic geometry of their own.
- **1841:** *Dioptrische Untersuchungen*. A late treatise on optical systems, working out the behavior of lenses and compound optical instruments through idealized "cardinal points" (focal and principal points). The paraxial approximation it develops is still taught as Gaussian optics.

### Memoirs, shorter papers, and recurring problems

Gauss returned repeatedly to certain problems across his career rather than treating them as closed by a single publication:

- **The fundamental theorem of algebra** occupied him four times: his 1799 Helmstedt doctoral dissertation offered a first proof (with real gaps by later standards), and he produced further treatments in 1815, 1816, and 1849, each tightening the argument.
- **Quadratic reciprocity**, the central result of the *Disquisitiones*, received additional independent proofs in later papers, including *Theorematis arithmetici demonstratio nova* (1808).
- **Biquadratic residues** were treated in two formal commentationes (1828, 1831/32), extending reciprocity-type results to fourth powers and introducing what are now called Gaussian integers along the way.
- **Terrestrial magnetism** produced a sequence of papers rather than one book: *Intensitas vis magneticae terrestris ad mensuram absolutam revocata* (1833) established a method for expressing magnetic force in mechanical units; *Allgemeine Theorie des Erdmagnetismus* (1839), printed in the *Resultate aus den Beobachtungen des magnetischen Vereins*, applied potential theory and spherical harmonics to separate the internal and external sources of Earth's field.
- **Hypergeometric series** and **elliptic/modular functions** appear across several papers and a great deal of unpublished calculation (see below); the 1813 *Disquisitiones generales circa seriem infinitam* is the main printed statement.
- A late **actuarial report** (1851) on the Göttingen University widows' fund is a different genre again: an applied expert opinion rather than a research memoir, using mortality and interest tables, and an early serious contribution to actuarial mathematics.

### Instruments, surveys, and institutional reports

Not everything Gauss produced was a text meant to be read. The **heliotrope** (1821), a mirror device for signaling across long survey distances, is a piece of instrumentation, documented through correspondence and reports rather than a treatise of its own. The Hanoverian triangulation he directed from 1821 to 1825, and the wider land survey that continued to 1845, produced roughly 2,600 calculated triangulation points: a body of technical, administrative work whose intellectual residue is the *Disquisitiones generales circa superficies curvas* rather than a publication about the survey itself. He also wrote some ninety-four reviews for the *Göttingische Gelehrte Anzeigen* between 1808 and 1850, a genre of short scholarly notice distinct from original research.

### The diary, the correspondence, and the Nachlass

Gauss's most famous working habit shaped his bibliography as much as any book did. His motto, *pauca sed matura* ("few, but ripe"), meant he frequently withheld a result for years or decades, sometimes until someone else published it first.

- The ***Notizen-Journal*** (mathematical diary) runs from 29 March 1796, the day he proved the constructibility of the regular 17-gon, to 9 July 1814. Its entries are terse, often a line or two, and record discoveries, many of which he never wrote up for publication: work toward non-Euclidean geometry, elliptic and modular functions, numerical methods resembling later fast-Fourier-transform ideas, and more. The diary itself was not printed in his lifetime; it surfaced among his papers decades after his death and was first published only in 1898.
- The **Nachlass** more broadly, the manuscripts, loose calculations, and working papers left at his death, was mined by nineteenth- and twentieth-century editors for material on non-Euclidean geometry, complex analysis, and number theory that Gauss had investigated privately but never published. Distinguishing a dated notebook entry (private awareness) from a finished, publishable theory matters here: editors sometimes converted a suggestive fragment into a sweeping posthumous priority claim that the surviving manuscript does not fully support.
- His **correspondence**, with Bessel, Olbers, Schumacher, Gerling, Sophie Germain, Humboldt, and many others, functioned as a parallel channel of scientific communication, sometimes disclosing results (or claiming priority for them) that never appeared in print at all.
- The collected ***Werke***, assembled by the Göttingen Academy over the following decades, run to twelve volumes and remain the only place most of this material, printed papers, diary, Nachlass excerpts, and selected correspondence together, can be found.
