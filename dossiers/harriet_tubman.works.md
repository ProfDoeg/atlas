## Works by Harriet Tubman

### Journeys as emancipatory actions

- **1849:** *Self-emancipation journey from Maryland to Pennsylvania*. An untitled, clandestine journey by which Tubman made herself legally and geographically free; calling it merely “travel” obscures its character as a self-creating political act. The precise route and date are not recoverable, and accounts of an earlier departure with her brothers differ. ([Humez, pp. 32–37](https://www.ayanetwork.com/aya/research/AramintaWHB%20Story/%5BJean_M._Humez%5D_Harriet_Tubman_The_Life_and_the_L.pdf))

- **1850–1860:** *Underground Railroad rescue journeys*. A sustained project of secret route-making, logistical planning, and collective emancipation rather than a conventional “work”: Tubman repeatedly returned to Maryland and guided relatives and friends north. The familiar total of thirteen missions and about seventy people is a scholarly reconstruction, not Tubman’s own catalogue; surviving evidence does not permit every journey to be dated or separated with certainty. ([Humez, pp. 349–352](https://www.ayanetwork.com/aya/research/AramintaWHB%20Story/%5BJean_M._Humez%5D_Harriet_Tubman_The_Life_and_the_L.pdf); [National Park Service, *Historic Resource Study*, pp. 8–9](https://npshistory.com/publications/hatu/hrs.pdf))

- **December 1850:** *Rescue of Kessiah Bowley and her children*. A planned extraction from an auction in Baltimore, made with Kessiah’s husband John Bowley and other collaborators; the resulting “thing” was a family’s escape, not a surviving object or authored text. ([Humez, “Rescue of the Bowley Family,” pp. 190–193](https://www.ayanetwork.com/aya/research/AramintaWHB%20Story/%5BJean_M._Humez%5D_Harriet_Tubman_The_Life_and_the_L.pdf))

- **December 1854:** *Christmas rescue of Tubman’s brothers and companions*. A clandestine journey planned through encoded and oral messages, with free Black farmer Jacob Jackson among its collaborators. Sources disagree over some identities and numbers, so it resists treatment as a completely documented itinerary. ([Humez, pp. 44–46, 291](https://www.ayanetwork.com/aya/research/AramintaWHB%20Story/%5BJean_M._Humez%5D_Harriet_Tubman_The_Life_and_the_L.pdf); [National Park Service, *Historic Resource Study*, p. 13](https://npshistory.com/publications/hatu/hrs.pdf))

- **1857:** *Rescue of Ben and Rit Ross*. Tubman devised and conducted the removal of her parents from Maryland, using a makeshift vehicle and then rail travel; contemporary retellings differ in incidental details. It is an obra of care and movement whose material apparatus did not survive. ([Humez, pp. 48–50, 291](https://www.ayanetwork.com/aya/research/AramintaWHB%20Story/%5BJean_M._Humez%5D_Harriet_Tubman_The_Life_and_the_L.pdf))

- **April 1860:** *Rescue of Charles Nalle*. A collective public intervention in Troy, New York, in which Tubman helped free Nalle from federal custody under the Fugitive Slave Law. Authorship belongs to a crowd rather than to Tubman alone, and the “work” is the successful political action itself. ([Humez, pp. 53–55](https://www.ayanetwork.com/aya/research/AramintaWHB%20Story/%5BJean_M._Humez%5D_Harriet_Tubman_The_Life_and_the_L.pdf))

### Military and intelligence operations

- **1862–1865:** *South Carolina intelligence network*. A collaborative operational project in which Tubman recruited and directed Black scouts, gathered geographical and military intelligence, and supported Union expeditions. Its reports and maps were normally embodied in military paperwork made by others, so the project survives more securely as documented practice than as a bounded object bearing her name. ([Humez, pp. 55–67](https://www.ayanetwork.com/aya/research/AramintaWHB%20Story/%5BJean_M._Humez%5D_Harriet_Tubman_The_Life_and_the_L.pdf); [National Park Service, *Historic Resource Study*, p. 8](https://npshistory.com/publications/hatu/hrs.pdf))

- **June 2, 1863:** *Combahee River Raid*. Tubman supplied intelligence, guided Union gunboats through mined waters, and helped direct scouts during an operation that destroyed Confederate resources and enabled hundreds of enslaved people to become free. It was a collective military action under Colonel James Montgomery, not a solely authored work; contemporary and later sources differ on the number liberated and on the appropriate description of Tubman’s command. ([Humez, pp. 67–73](https://www.ayanetwork.com/aya/research/AramintaWHB%20Story/%5BJean_M._Humez%5D_Harriet_Tubman_The_Life_and_the_L.pdf))

### Oral texts and collaborative books

- **1850s–early 1900s:** *Life stories, sayings, songs, and public performances*. An evolving oral body of work made and remade in abolitionist meetings, interviews, suffrage appearances, domestic storytelling, and religious performance. No complete, unmediated text survives: Jean M. Humez identifies “core stories” inside records made by listeners and stresses that every surviving version bears the intervention of an editor, reporter, or amanuensis. ([Humez, pp. 137–275](https://www.ayanetwork.com/aya/research/AramintaWHB%20Story/%5BJean_M._Humez%5D_Harriet_Tubman_The_Life_and_the_L.pdf))

- **1855; published 1856:** *Canadian Testimony of Harriet Tubman*. An orally delivered antislavery testimony recorded and printed by Benjamin Drew in *The Refugee*; it is Tubman’s text in subject and performance but Drew’s mediated transcription. Humez’s heading dates the represented testimony to 1855, while its source citation gives the printed text as 1856. ([Humez, pp. 279, 437](https://www.ayanetwork.com/aya/research/AramintaWHB%20Story/%5BJean_M._Humez%5D_Harriet_Tubman_The_Life_and_the_L.pdf))

- **1868–1869:** *Scenes in the Life of Harriet Tubman*. A collaborative fundraising manifestation made from Tubman’s interviews and selected stories, Sarah Hopkins Bradford’s composition, and testimonial documents supplied by Tubman’s associates. Bradford is the named author, but Humez argues that Tubman substantially shaped the story selection and public self-presentation; the book therefore should not be listed either as Tubman’s sole-authored autobiography or as wholly someone else’s work. ([Humez, pp. 95–98, 145–149](https://www.ayanetwork.com/aya/research/AramintaWHB%20Story/%5BJean_M._Humez%5D_Harriet_Tubman_The_Life_and_the_L.pdf); [1869 text](https://www.ibiblio.org/pub/docs/books/gutenberg/5/7/8/2/57821/57821-h/57821-h.htm))

- **1886; revised 1901:** *Harriet, the Moses of Her People*. Bradford’s extensive rewriting of *Scenes*, undertaken at Tubman’s request as a fundraising instrument for her proposed home for elderly and disabled Black people; a further 1901 manifestation added material. Its status remains hybrid: Bradford controlled the prose and arrangement, while Tubman supplied stories, authorization, purpose, and part of the book’s performative function. ([Humez, pp. 106–112, 146–149](https://www.ayanetwork.com/aya/research/AramintaWHB%20Story/%5BJean_M._Humez%5D_Harriet_Tubman_The_Life_and_the_Life_Stories.pdf); [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/north-carolina-scholarship-online/book/18538))

### Dictated correspondence

- **August 4, 1860:** *Fund-raising Letter to Wendell Phillips*. A dictated request for the remaining money needed for an impending rescue mission. The surviving language passed through an unidentified amanuensis, so verbal authorship cannot be assigned to Tubman word for word. ([Humez, pp. 282, 437](https://www.ayanetwork.com/aya/research/AramintaWHB%20Story/%5BJean_M._Humez%5D_Harriet_Tubman_The_Life_and_the_Life_Stories.pdf))

- **June 30, 1863:** *Letter to Franklin B. Sanborn about the Combahee River Raid*. A dictated report, fundraising appeal, and claim for Black participation in the raid. Humez warns that its unusually formal diction probably reflects its scribe, making it a jointly produced letter rather than an exact transcript of Tubman’s speech. ([Humez, pp. 142–143, 282–284, 437](https://www.ayanetwork.com/aya/research/AramintaWHB%20Story/%5BJean_M._Humez%5D_Harriet_Tubman_The_Life_and_the_Life_Stories.pdf))

- **April 9, 1894:** *Letter to Ednah Dow Cheney*. A fundraising and progress letter dictated through Jane Kellogg concerning Tubman’s proposed home for aged Black women. Because Kellogg signed as writer “for Harriet Tubman,” it is best classified as collaborative dictated correspondence. ([Humez, pp. 109, 285, 437](https://www.ayanetwork.com/aya/research/AramintaWHB%20Story/%5BJean_M._Humez%5D_Harriet_Tubman_The_Life_and_the_Life_Stories.pdf))

- **May 29, 1896:** *Letter to Mary Wright*. A dictated letter acknowledging donations and explaining the incorporation and financing of Tubman’s asylum project. The amanuensis is not identified in Humez’s transcription, and its precise wording therefore remains mediated. ([Humez, pp. 285–286, 437](https://www.ayanetwork.com/aya/research/AramintaWHB%20Story/%5BJean_M._Humez%5D_Harriet_Tubman_The_Life_and_the_Life_Stories.pdf))

- **1850s–1900s:** *Lost or unidentified dictated correspondence*. Humez estimates that Tubman produced dozens and possibly hundreds of dictated letters and messages, including inscriptions in gift books, but states that only a small portion survived or has been located. This is a body of work that resists enumeration: its existence is documented, but most individual texts have neither recoverable titles nor dates. ([Humez, pp. 141–143](https://www.ayanetwork.com/aya/research/AramintaWHB%20Story/%5BJean_M._Humez%5D_Harriet_Tubman_The_Life_and_the_Life_Stories.pdf))

### Testimony and legal texts

- **June 5, 1863:** *Testimony in United States v. Private John E. Webster*. Tubman’s sworn courtroom answers concerning abuses in the Beaufort contraband camp, preserved in a Union Army court-martial record. It is a co-produced legal text—her oral testimony shaped by questions and recorded by a military clerk—not an independently composed manuscript. ([National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/publications/prologue/2000/fall/harriet-tubman-beaufort-testimony); [Humez, p. 437](https://www.ayanetwork.com/aya/research/AramintaWHB%20Story/%5BJean_M._Humez%5D_Harriet_Tubman_The_Life_and_the_Life_Stories.pdf))

- **1890–1898:** *Pension declarations and affidavits*. A performative legal dossier comprising Tubman’s July 14, 1890 widow’s-pension declaration and affidavits dated May 28, 1892, November 10, 1894, and January 1, 1898. These documents act by asserting identity, service, marriage, and entitlement; because clerks and attorneys supplied their written form and Tubman sometimes signed by mark, they are collaborative legal manifestations rather than conventional authored prose. ([Humez, pp. 284–285, 437](https://www.ayanetwork.com/aya/research/AramintaWHB%20Story/%5BJean_M._Humez%5D_Harriet_Tubman_The_Life_and_the_Life_Stories.pdf))

- **June 11, 1903:** *Deed to the African Methodist Episcopal Zion Church*. A legal instrument transferring the Home for the Aged property to the church on terms intended to make Tubman’s charitable project operational. Its language was made through legal convention and professional mediation, but Tubman’s execution of it performed the transfer and secured the institution’s purpose. ([Humez, p. 437](https://www.ayanetwork.com/aya/research/AramintaWHB%20Story/%5BJean_M._Humez%5D_Harriet_Tubman_The_Life_and_the_Life_Stories.pdf); [National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/hart/learn/historyculture/home-for-the-aged.htm))

### Places, households, and institutions

- **1859–1913:** *South Street farm and caregiving household*. Tubman purchased a seven-acre Auburn-area farm in 1859 and made it into an extended-family household, farm, workplace, and informal refuge for people needing food, shelter, or nursing. The wooden house burned in 1880 and the surviving brick residence was built in 1883, so the present site is a changing collective environment rather than a single structure designed or built solely by Tubman. ([National Park Service, *Historic Resource Study*, pp. 8–9](https://npshistory.com/publications/hatu/hrs.pdf); [Humez, pp. 91–94](https://www.ayanetwork.com/aya/research/AramintaWHB%20Story/%5BJean_M._Humez%5D_Harriet_Tubman_The_Life_and_the_Life_Stories.pdf))

- **By 1888–1908:** *Informal asylum and self-help community*. Tubman organized her household’s farming, brickmaking, gardening, livestock raising, and caregiving into an interdependent community. It resists the category of a discrete institution because it existed first as daily practice within her home and only later developed legal organization and a separate site. ([Humez, pp. 93–94](https://www.ayanetwork.com/aya/research/AramintaWHB%20Story/%5BJean_M._Humez%5D_Harriet_Tubman_The_Life_and_the_Life_Stories.pdf))

- **c. 1884–1908:** *John Brown Hall / Harriet Tubman Home for Aged and Indigent Negroes*. Tubman conceived the project, purchased the adjacent twenty-five-acre property in 1896, organized fundraising and incorporation, and transferred it to the A.M.E. Zion Church in 1903; the church opened the operating home in 1908. It is her institutional obra and a collective realization, not a building she personally designed, and its name and physical form changed during development. ([Humez, pp. 92–95, 105–112](https://www.ayanetwork.com/aya/research/AramintaWHB%20Story/%5BJean_M._Humez%5D_Harriet_Tubman_The_Life_and_the_Life_Stories.pdf); [National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/hart/learn/historyculture/home-for-the-aged.htm))
