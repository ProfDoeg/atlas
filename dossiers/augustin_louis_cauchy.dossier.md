# Augustin-Louis Cauchy — Research Dossier

**Subject:** Augustin-Louis Cauchy (1789–1857)  
**Primary fields:** mathematics, mathematical physics, civil engineering  
**Political orientation:** French Legitimist / Bourbon royalist  
**Religion:** Roman Catholic; active lay Catholic organizer  
**Prepared:** 19 August 2026

---

## Research Note and Evidence Conventions

This dossier is organized as a documentary reconstruction rather than a continuous interpretive essay. The principal modern biographical framework used is Bruno Belhoste's *Augustin-Louis Cauchy: A Biography* (1991), checked against digitized works, archival notices, contemporary or near-contemporary material, institutional histories, specialist studies in the history of mathematics, and modern scholarship on disputed episodes.

Where the evidence differs in quality, the following distinctions are used:

- **Documented:** supported by contemporary records, Cauchy's writings or correspondence, institutional records, or convergent scholarly reconstruction.
- **Later testimony:** reported by a witness or family member after the event, sometimes through a later biographer.
- **Historiographical interpretation:** a conclusion proposed by later historians rather than a directly documented fact.
- **Disputed:** serious scholarly disagreement exists.
- **Legend / hostile anecdote / reputation story:** a story with historical importance whose literal truth is uncertain, exaggerated, or unsupported.
- **Unverified:** encountered in secondary retellings but not established from stronger material consulted here.

For a mathematician who published hundreds of papers, "complete bibliography" has practical limits. Cauchy's *Œuvres complètes* ultimately occupied 27 large volumes and contains roughly 789 mathematical papers according to the standard biographical count. The "Works by Cauchy" section therefore gives the principal books, serial publications, landmark memoirs, major research clusters, and the complete-works apparatus through which the remainder can be located rather than reproducing 789 individual article titles.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name:** Augustin-Louis Cauchy.

**Common forms:** Augustin Louis Cauchy; A.-L. Cauchy; Augustin-Louis, baron Cauchy; Baron Cauchy.

**Title:** Cauchy was given the title **baron** by the exiled Bourbon court of Charles X in connection with his service as tutor to the Duke of Bordeaux. The title is routinely attached to his name in later nineteenth-century publications. Its use arose from the exiled Legitimist court rather than from the July Monarchy that governed France at the time.

**Pseudonyms / noms de plume:** No significant pseudonym has been identified in the sources consulted.

**Birth:** 21 August 1789, Paris, Kingdom of France.

**Baptism:** Belhoste records that he was baptized at the church of Saint-Roch in Paris. His given names were connected in family memory with the month of August and his father Louis-François.

**Death:** 23 May 1857, Sceaux, near Paris, France, at about 4:00 a.m. according to the account later published from his daughter.

**Age at death:** 67.

**Nationality / political setting over his lifetime:** French. He was born during the opening phase of the French Revolution and lived under the constitutional monarchy, First Republic, Directory, Consulate, First Empire, Bourbon Restoration, July Monarchy, Second Republic, and Second Empire. His employment history was repeatedly affected by regime change.

**Principal occupations:**
- civil engineer in the Corps des Ponts et Chaussées;
- professor of analysis at the École Polytechnique;
- member of the Académie des sciences;
- substitute lecturer at the Collège de France and other institutions;
- professor of theoretical physics at Turin;
- private scientific tutor to Henri d'Artois, Duke of Bordeaux;
- professor of mathematical astronomy at the Faculté des sciences de Paris in his final period;
- mathematical physicist and extraordinarily prolific independent researcher.

**Principal places associated with his life:** Paris; Arcueil; Cherbourg; Sceaux; Fribourg/Switzerland; Turin; Prague and other locations of the exiled Bourbon court in Central Europe.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

Cauchy's father was **Louis-François Cauchy (1760–1848)**. At the time of Augustin-Louis's birth he was a senior administrative official—described in Belhoste's account as a *principal commis* in the police administration. He survived the revolutionary period and subsequently held administrative positions under several successive French regimes. His political adaptability as a civil servant contrasts with the son's later refusal to take loyalty oaths to regimes he considered illegitimate.

Louis-François was deeply involved in his eldest son's education. The household was sufficiently connected to the French scientific elite that **Pierre-Simon Laplace** and **Joseph-Louis Lagrange** visited the family. Lagrange took particular notice of the boy's mathematical gifts and advised his father not to accelerate him immediately into mathematics, but first to give him a strong classical and linguistic education. This advice appears to have shaped Cauchy's early schooling.

Cauchy's mother was **Marie-Madeleine Desestre** (secondary genealogical sources commonly give 1767–1839). The surviving biographical tradition repeatedly shows Cauchy writing to her from Cherbourg about work, health, religion, and his social difficulties. His 1810 correspondence with her is one of the important windows into his already intense Catholic religiosity.

### Siblings

Standard biographical and genealogical accounts identify at least two younger brothers:

- **Alexandre-Laurent Cauchy (1792–1857)**, who pursued a legal/judicial career and reached high judicial office.
- **Eugène-François Cauchy (1802–1877)**, a publicist and writer who also wrote on mathematical or technical subjects.

The Cauchy family therefore belonged to the educated administrative bourgeoisie rather than to hereditary aristocracy. Augustin-Louis's later title of baron was acquired through Bourbon dynastic patronage, not inherited at birth.

### Revolutionary displacement of the family

During the Terror, when Augustin-Louis was a small child, Louis-François feared remaining in Paris and moved the family to **Arcueil**, south of the city. A family letter preserved through the biographical tradition describes food shortages severe enough that the household sometimes had no more than half a pound of bread, supplemented with rationed crackers and rice. The family subsequently returned to Paris.

This childhood displacement is one of the rare direct points at which the violence and scarcity of the Revolution entered Cauchy's personal life. Later Legitimist convictions cannot simply be derived from this single childhood experience, but the family memory of revolutionary insecurity formed part of his early environment.

### Marriage: Aloïse de Bure

At twenty-eight Cauchy was still living with his parents. His father helped arrange his marriage to **Aloïse de Bure**, approximately five years younger than Cauchy and the only daughter of bookseller **Marie-Jacques de Bure**. Belhoste places the de Bures within an established bourgeois world of Parisian bookselling and publishing, connected with other book-trade families such as the Didots and Saugrains.

The marriage is generally dated **4 April 1818** and is reported to have taken place at **Saint-Sulpice** in Paris.

The marriage also tied Cauchy's household directly to the Paris book trade. De Bure family firms published important Cauchy volumes. This was a family and publishing relationship rather than a modern corporate "business interest" in the sense of an equity investment.

### Children

The couple had two daughters:

- **Marie-Françoise Alicia Cauchy**, born 1819.
- **Marie-Mathilde Cauchy**, born 1823.

Later genealogical sources identify marriages of Alicia into the d'Escalopier family and Mathilde into the Saint-Pol family. Those later family connections are less central to Cauchy's scientific biography and were not independently reconstructed here from civil records.

### Household and residences

Belhoste's reconstruction places the married couple for a period in the **Hôtel de Bure, 7 rue Serpente, Paris**, a de Bure family property and a location closely connected to the family's bookselling activity.

From about **1822**, Cauchy habitually spent summers at **Maison Trudon in Sceaux**, a property belonging to Aloïse's family. Sceaux eventually became the place of his death.

When Cauchy left France after the July Revolution in 1830, he initially went abroad without his wife and daughters. The family separation lasted for years; Aloïse and the children eventually joined him in Prague in 1834.

### Wealth and property

No reliable quantitative reconstruction of Cauchy's net worth, investments, inheritance, or estate was located in the sources consulted. His income came principally from state or university posts, engineering status, teaching, Academy-related positions, publications, and later royal tutoring. His refusal to swear political oaths demonstrably cost him salaried appointments. In 1839, for example, his oath refusal prevented him from taking up the Bureau des Longitudes position to which he had been elected and therefore prevented him from receiving its salary.

The de Bure family brought property and book-trade connections into the marriage, but the evidence consulted does not justify treating Cauchy as proprietor of a large publishing enterprise or assigning him a specific fortune.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1789–1802

Cauchy was born in Paris on **21 August 1789**, only weeks after the storming of the Bastille. His infancy therefore coincided almost exactly with the transformation of the French political order.

Around age four, amid the Terror, his father removed the family from Paris to **Arcueil**. The family's material hardship there is unusually well documented through Louis-François's correspondence. They later returned to Paris, where the father personally supervised Augustin-Louis's education.

The household's scientific contacts were exceptional. **Laplace** and **Lagrange** were visitors. Lagrange's recommendation that the gifted child be trained first in languages and classics rather than prematurely specialized in mathematics was followed: Cauchy received a substantial classical education before his technical training.

The later image of Cauchy as a mathematical prodigy is therefore partly accurate but can be misleading if it suggests an isolated child calculating outside institutions. His talent developed inside a highly educated, scientifically connected family environment.

## Education and Formation, 1802–1810

### École Centrale du Panthéon

In **1802**, Cauchy entered the **École Centrale du Panthéon**. He spent roughly two years concentrated on classical languages and humanities. The curriculum fulfilled Lagrange's earlier recommendation.

### Preparation in mathematics

From **1804**, Cauchy moved more decisively into mathematics. He sat the entrance examination for the **École Polytechnique** in **1805**. **Jean-Baptiste Biot** examined him, and Cauchy placed second.

### École Polytechnique, 1805–1807

At the École Polytechnique his instructors included:

- **Sylvestre-François Lacroix**;
- **Gaspard de Prony**;
- **Jean Nicolas Pierre Hachette**;
- **André-Marie Ampère**, who served as his analysis tutor.

The institution was still closely linked to the Revolutionary and Napoleonic project of creating technically trained state servants. Cauchy entered it as a future engineer, not initially as a professional pure mathematician.

### École des Ponts et Chaussées, 1807 onward

After graduating from the École Polytechnique in **1807**, Cauchy entered the **École des Ponts et Chaussées**. His practical training included work on the **Ourcq Canal** under engineer **Pierre-Simon Girard**.

The engineering formation was consequential for his later mathematics. His lifelong work in elasticity, mechanics, wave propagation, fluids, stress, and mathematical physics emerged from a culture in which analysis and engineering were intertwined.

### Formative intellectual influences

The major identifiable formative figures were:

- **Lagrange**, for analytic mathematics and the early educational advice given to his father;
- **Laplace**, whose *Mécanique céleste* Cauchy carried to Cherbourg;
- **Ampère**, his analysis tutor;
- **Lacroix, de Prony, and Hachette**, as institutional teachers;
- **Legendre and Malus**, who encouraged his first research in geometry;
- the École Polytechnique culture linking analysis, mechanics, geometry, and engineering.

Cauchy's Catholic devotional life also formed early. By the time he was a young engineer at Cherbourg, his letters show that colleagues were already reacting to the intensity of his religious practice.

## Early Engineering Career, 1810–1815

### Appointment and move to Cherbourg

On **18 January 1810**, Cauchy was appointed an *aspirant-ingénieur*. In February he was assigned to the great works at **Cherbourg**, and he left for Normandy in March.

Napoleon's government was developing the port on an enormous scale, connected strategically to naval rivalry with Britain. Cauchy's job was therefore part of imperial military infrastructure, even though he was employed as a civil engineer rather than a combatant.

He carried with him:
- Laplace's *Mécanique céleste*;
- Lagrange's *Théorie des fonctions analytiques*;
- Virgil;
- *The Imitation of Christ*.

The combination is revealing: advanced mechanics, analytic mathematics, Latin literature, and Catholic devotion.

### Work routine and religious tensions

Cauchy's correspondence from Cherbourg describes exceptionally long working days, beginning as early as four in the morning. At the same time he pursued mathematical research after engineering duties.

A surviving letter to his mother from 1810 reports that others accused his piety of making him proud or self-important. The episode is important because it shows that the later reputation for combining mathematics with overt religious commitment did not arise only after he became famous; the tension was already present among his young engineering colleagues.

### First mathematical papers

In **1811**, Cauchy produced important work on convex polyhedra, including what is now associated with **Cauchy's rigidity theorem**. Encouraged by **Legendre** and **Étienne-Louis Malus**, he continued with work on polygons and polyhedra in 1812.

These papers established him as more than an engineer with a private interest in mathematics.

### Illness and return to Paris, 1812

In **September 1812**, Cauchy left Cherbourg and returned to Paris because of illness.

**Documented fact:** he was ill enough to leave the post and take extended leave.

**Later interpretation:** some modern biographical accounts characterize the episode as psychological and possibly a severe depression rather than a purely physical disease.

**Limit of evidence:** no modern clinical diagnosis can responsibly be assigned from the surviving documentation. "Depression" should therefore be treated as a historical interpretation of the episode, not a securely diagnosed illness.

### Transition toward an academic career

Back in Paris, Cauchy worked on **symmetric functions** and submitted a memoir in November 1812, later published in the *Journal de l'École Polytechnique*.

His desire to remain close to the mathematical center in Paris was now clear. He sought an associate teaching post at the Ponts et Chaussées but was unsuccessful. Girard nevertheless supported his staying in Paris to work on the Ourcq Canal rather than returning to Cherbourg.

Cauchy then repeatedly sought scientific posts:

- a position at the **Bureau des Longitudes** went instead to Legendre;
- a geometry seat at the Institute went to **Louis Poinsot**;
- another vacancy in 1814 went to Ampère;
- in one 1814 Institute election Cauchy reportedly received **zero of 53 votes**.

The period is useful evidence against a retrospective story of effortless ascent. By 1815 he was already producing important mathematics, but he experienced repeated institutional rejection.

### Definite integrals and complex analysis

In **1814**, Cauchy developed a memoir on definite integrals that became one of the roots of his later complex-function theory. The exact publication history is complicated because some memoirs circulated, were presented, or were printed separately before appearing in Academy volumes.

### 1815: engineering-to-academic transition

In **1815**, Cauchy lost a competition for a mechanics chair at the École Polytechnique to **Jacques Philippe Marie Binet**, but he was then appointed assistant professor of analysis and became responsible for second-year instruction.

His engineering identity never completely disappeared—his publications continued to list him as an engineer of Ponts et Chaussées—but mathematics became his dominant profession.

## Rise at the École Polytechnique and Académie des Sciences, 1815–1821

### Fermat's polygonal-number theorem

Cauchy's proof of the polygonal-number theorem associated with **Fermat** brought major attention. The result concerned Fermat's claim that every positive integer could be represented as a sum of a bounded number of polygonal numbers of a given order.

### Academy Grand Prix, 1816

In **1816**, Cauchy won the **Grand Prix of the Académie des sciences** for work on waves. His treatment of wave propagation and fluid mechanics belongs to the same research trajectory that later historians have identified as containing important precursors of Lagrangian fluid mechanics.

### Politically conditioned Academy appointment

Cauchy's entry into the **Académie des sciences** in 1816 is one of the most politically sensitive episodes of his career.

After the Bourbon Restoration, **Lazare Carnot** and **Gaspard Monge** fell from favor and were removed. Cauchy filled one of the resulting places.

The relevant distinctions are:

- **Documented:** the vacancies resulted from a political purge/reorganization under the restored monarchy.
- **Documented:** Cauchy benefited institutionally from that reorganization.
- **Also documented:** he had already produced mathematics of Academy-level quality and had just won a major prize.
- **Not established:** that his scientific achievements were fraudulent or that he personally engineered the removals.
- **Historical controversy:** republican and liberal contemporaries could reasonably view the appointment as politically tainted because the route by which the seat became available was political.

His later opponents never forgot the circumstances.

### Collège de France substitution, 1817

When **Biot** left Paris in 1817 for work connected with an expedition to the Shetland Islands, Cauchy substituted for him at the **Collège de France**.

There he lectured on integration methods and convergence. The period is important in the reconstruction of how Cauchy's foundational ideas developed before the publication of the *Cours d'analyse*.

### The creation of a rigorous teaching program

At the École Polytechnique, Cauchy increasingly tried to rebuild analysis around explicit definitions and proofs rather than relying primarily on formal manipulations.

This generated institutional tension. Engineering students and administrators did not necessarily want an analysis course transformed into a foundational research seminar. Cauchy's tendency to expand the theoretical content of the course, his difficulty keeping within prescribed limits, and his teaching style became recurring problems.

---

## Marriage, Household, and the De Bure Publishing Connection, 1818 onward

Cauchy married **Aloïse de Bure** in 1818. The de Bures were not incidental to his publishing career. Important Cauchy books appeared through **De Bure frères**, whose shop was at rue Serpente.

This family connection helped give Cauchy an unusually direct route between lecture notes, research, and book publication.

The household's two daughters were born in 1819 and 1823. Cauchy's surviving life was therefore not that of an isolated celibate savant, despite later iconography that often presents him almost entirely as a professor and Catholic intellectual.

The family's regular connection to **Sceaux**, especially Maison Trudon, created a second domestic center outside central Paris.

## The Foundational Analysis Program, 1821–1830

### *Cours d'analyse* (1821)

Cauchy's **1821** *Cours d'analyse de l'École royale polytechnique. Ire partie: Analyse algébrique* became one of the canonical texts in the history of analysis.

Its importance lies in the systematic use of definitions of:
- variable quantity;
- limit;
- infinitesimal;
- continuity;
- convergence;
- series.

Cauchy sought proofs rather than appeals to geometric intuition alone.

The standard twentieth-century story often portrays this book as the direct birth of modern epsilon-delta analysis. That is too simple. Cauchy certainly made analysis more rigorous, but he also reasoned with infinitesimals in ways that do not map neatly onto later Weierstrassian foundations. Modern historians disagree over whether he should be read mainly as a precursor of epsilon-delta formalism or as a mathematician operating in a still genuinely infinitesimal tradition.

### *Résumé des leçons ... sur le calcul infinitésimal* (1823)

In **1823**, Cauchy published the *Résumé des leçons données à l'École royale polytechnique, sur le calcul infinitésimal*, commonly shortened in later references to the *Calcul infinitésimal*.

It presented differential and integral calculus in a deliberately structured lecture form. It was written for Polytechnique students and converted Cauchy's evolving classroom program into a printed reference.

### Elasticity and continuum mechanics

During the 1820s Cauchy created some of his most influential work in continuum mechanics.

His contributions include:
- formulation of stress in a continuum;
- what later became the **Cauchy stress tensor**;
- equations of motion for deformable bodies;
- mathematical treatment of elasticity;
- development of methods that later became basic to continuum mechanics.

Clifford Truesdell and later historians of mechanics have treated Cauchy's work as foundational for the mathematical theory of continua, although the modern tensor notation and conceptual framework were developed later.

### Complex integration and residues

Cauchy's research on definite and singular integrals matured into what became the central architecture of complex analysis:

- contour-like integration methods;
- treatment of singularities;
- the **calculus of residues**;
- results now organized as the **Cauchy integral theorem** and **Cauchy integral formula**.

The modern textbook presentation is more unified than Cauchy's actual publication sequence. Ideas were developed across memoirs, lectures, exercises, and revised arguments over many years.

### *Leçons sur les applications du calcul infinitésimal à la géométrie* (1826)

Cauchy applied infinitesimal analysis to geometry, including curvature and related questions. Modern scholarship has revisited these works because his infinitesimals often behave more like mathematical quantities than like merely abbreviated sequences of ordinary real numbers.

### *Exercices de mathématiques* (1826–1830)

Cauchy's productivity exceeded the normal capacity of Academy memoir series. Beginning in **1826**, he issued **Exercices de mathématiques**, effectively a personal serial vehicle for publishing new work. It appeared yearly through the revolutionary break of 1830.

This publication practice is a significant part of his working method: Cauchy did not simply wait for Academy print cycles but created a channel capable of absorbing a continuous research output.

### *Leçons sur le calcul différentiel* (1829)

Published by De Bure frères in **1829**, this treatise developed differential calculus and included work relevant to functions of a complex variable.

### Permutations, substitutions, and group theory

Cauchy was an early major contributor to the theory of permutations. His work preceded the modern abstract concept of a group but developed results that became central to permutation-group theory, including the theorem now called **Cauchy's theorem** in finite-group theory.

The chronology of exactly when particular group-theoretic ideas should be credited to Cauchy has itself been the subject of historical scholarship.

### Determinants, matrices, and spectral theory

Cauchy made important contributions to:
- determinants;
- characteristic equations;
- diagonalization-related procedures;
- real roots/eigenvalue questions for symmetric forms.

Later linear algebra absorbed these ideas into a vocabulary that did not yet exist in his lifetime.

### Wave theory, optics, and dispersion

Cauchy worked intensively on:
- waves;
- polarization;
- elasticity-based theories of light;
- dispersion.

Some parts of his physical models were later superseded, especially with the decline of mechanical ether theories. Nevertheless the mathematics developed in those investigations had a longer life than the physical ontology that motivated it.

Historical priority is not always simple: scholarship on Cauchy's dispersion theory has shown that **Fresnel** anticipated portions of what later became associated with Cauchy's formula.

### Errors and correction

Cauchy's reputation for rigor should not be confused with infallibility. **Dirichlet's 1829 work on Fourier-series convergence explicitly corrected errors in an earlier Cauchy attempt.** The history of rigor includes both Cauchy's demand for proof and the discovery that some of his own arguments required stronger hypotheses.

## Teaching Style and Institutional Friction

Cauchy could be extraordinarily effective in creating new mathematics while being a difficult classroom teacher.

Two types of evidence need to be separated:

1. **Institutional evidence:** Polytechnique administrators repeatedly objected to the volume and abstraction of his analysis courses and attempted to keep the curriculum aligned with engineering needs.
2. **Later student testimony:** Luigi Federico Menabrea, who heard Cauchy in Turin, remembered lectures that jumped abruptly between ideas and formulas, and said that only one of a class of roughly thirty persisted to the end.

The later phrase "notoriously bad lecturer" condenses these materials but should not be treated as a measurable universal fact. Cauchy's lectures were intellectually generative enough to become major books, yet several witnesses found his oral exposition hard to follow.

## Scientific Networks, Collaborators, Rivals, and Difficult Relationships Before 1830

### Legendre

**Adrien-Marie Legendre** encouraged Cauchy's early work and later intervened in the Abel manuscript affair, pressing for a report. Their relationship illustrates both mentorship and the Academy's referee culture.

### Poisson

**Siméon-Denis Poisson** was a central mathematical-physics peer and, at times, institutional rival. He later opposed Cauchy's return to some offices, including the Bureau des Longitudes contest.

### Arago

**François Arago**, politically very different from Cauchy, was often an opponent, but their relationship cannot be reduced to permanent hostility. In 1839 Arago supported Cauchy's Bureau des Longitudes candidacy despite political differences. Under Napoleon III, both men were eventually exempted from the political oath required of professors.

### Poncelet

**Jean-Victor Poncelet** experienced Cauchy as a harsh referee of his work on projective geometry. Poncelet later described an unsatisfactory personal encounter in which he felt that Cauchy dismissed him abruptly.

The dispute is relevant to Cauchy's reputation as an uncompromising and sometimes abrasive Academy judge. It is not evidence of fraud.

### Abel

The case of **Niels Henrik Abel** became one of the most damaging stories attached to Cauchy's reputation and requires separate treatment below.

### Galois

The case of **Évariste Galois** became an even larger legend in popular mathematical biography. Modern archival reconstruction substantially changes the usual story; see the myths and controversies section.

## Religion, Catholic Networks, and Intellectual Development

### Early Catholic commitment

Cauchy's Catholicism was intense before his scientific fame. His 1810 letter from Cherbourg already shows that colleagues regarded his devotional behavior as conspicuous.

He was associated with **Marian/Catholic congregational networks** during the Restoration and later became a conspicuous Catholic lay activist.

### Religion inside scientific institutional life

Cauchy did not consistently separate religious judgment from Academy activity. In an 1824 scientific report he attacked what he understood as an irreligious claim about Newton and the soul. Contemporary journalists mocked the spectacle of an academician behaving like a missionary.

This is documented as a feature of his public scientific persona. It also helps explain why colleagues who favored a more secular conception of the Academy reacted strongly against him.

### Jesuit education

After returning to France in 1838, Cauchy became an active defender of Catholic and especially Jesuit educational activity. His allies in this milieu included figures such as:

- **Xavier de Ravignan**, Jesuit preacher;
- **Charles de Montalembert**;
- **Pierre-Antoine Berryer**;
- **Vatisménil** and other Catholic/Legitimist public figures.

These campaigns affected his academic prospects. The 1843 Collège de France election became entangled with political and religious hostility.

### Society of St. Vincent de Paul

Cauchy was active in the **Société de Saint-Vincent-de-Paul**, the Catholic charitable network founded in the 1830s. The Catholic Encyclopedia and later institutional histories treat his charitable work as an important part of his public life.

### Institut catholique / Centre du Luxembourg

He participated in or helped establish a Catholic intellectual center commonly referred to in the sources as the **Institut catholique** or **Centre du Luxembourg**, where he was involved in the scientific section.

### Crime-prevention project

In **1843**, Cauchy prepared an *opuscule* concerning the prevention of crime and sent it to **Alexis de Tocqueville**. This is a minor but revealing example of his attempt to apply organized Catholic-social thinking beyond mathematics.

### Irish Famine relief

During the **Great Irish Famine**, Cauchy participated in French Catholic relief organization. Scholarship on French aid identifies him among the figures involved in the **Comité de secours pour l'Irlande** and related appeals.

This is a documented transnational humanitarian activity, not merely a later Catholic hagiographic claim.

### Sunday observance

In the 1850s he was involved in an organization supporting **Sunday observance**, seeking limits on commercial work on Sunday. This reflects the social-Catholic politics of his final years.

### Œuvre des Écoles d'Orient

On **4 April 1856**, Cauchy was among the founders of the **Œuvre des Écoles d'Orient**, an organization created to support Christian schools in the Ottoman East, particularly French-language schools in Lebanon. The modern organization, now the **Œuvre d'Orient**, lists Cauchy among its founders together with Charles Lenormant, Alfred de Falloux, Montalembert, and Ravignan.

The BnF's historical presentation likewise identifies Cauchy and Charles Lenormant among the founding professors.

This institution survived Cauchy and became part of the longer history of French Catholic engagement in the Eastern Mediterranean.

## Political Development: Royalism and Legitimism

Cauchy's politics were not incidental private preferences. They repeatedly determined where he could live and work.

### Restoration allegiance

Under the restored Bourbons he rose rapidly in the Academy and state educational system. His Catholic and monarchist convictions fit the Restoration political order better than they fit the liberal or republican cultures of many scientific colleagues.

### Legitimism

After 1830 he remained loyal to the deposed senior Bourbon line rather than recognizing **Louis-Philippe**. He became a **Legitimist**, believing in the dynastic legitimacy of Charles X and, eventually, the Duke of Bordeaux as Bourbon claimant.

### Political oath as practical test

The decisive expression of this allegiance was his refusal to swear loyalty oaths to regimes he considered illegitimate.

This cost him:
- Paris teaching posts after 1830;
- formal assumption of the Bureau des Longitudes position after his election in 1839;
- various opportunities for normal state employment.

His Academy membership survived because that institution did not require the same oath.

### Not a revolutionary conspirator

No evidence was found that Cauchy participated in armed royalist conspiracy, political violence, intelligence activity, or clandestine insurrection. His political action was primarily institutional, educational, dynastic, religious, and symbolic.

## July Revolution and Voluntary Exile, 1830–1833

### Revolution of 1830

The **July Revolution** overthrew Charles X in 1830 and installed Louis-Philippe. For Cauchy this was both a political rupture and a personal crisis.

Charles X abdicated on **2 August 1830**. About a month later, Cauchy left France. Belhoste emphasizes that this departure initiated roughly eight years of voluntary exile.

### Switzerland

Cauchy initially went to Switzerland, spending time around **Fribourg**. He became involved in a proposed **Académie Helvétique**, a Catholic-oriented educational/scientific project. The project collapsed amid political difficulties.

### Loss of French positions

Because Cauchy would not return and swear allegiance to the July Monarchy, he lost state teaching positions in Paris. His Académie des sciences membership remained.

### Turin

In **1831**, he moved to **Turin**, in the Kingdom of Sardinia-Piedmont. The king offered him a chair of **theoretical physics**, created or arranged for him.

He taught there beginning in 1832.

### Turin teaching and Menabrea

Future statesman and engineer **Luigi Federico Menabrea** attended Cauchy's Turin lectures. His later recollection was unflattering: he described abrupt transitions, little connective explanation, and a class that dwindled drastically.

The testimony is later and rhetorically vivid, but it reinforces earlier evidence that Cauchy's research imagination could outrun his pedagogical pacing.

### Scientific activity in exile

Cauchy continued research in Turin and interacted with Italian scientific circles. He was elected to foreign learned societies during this phase.

The exile was therefore not an intellectual disappearance, but it did break the institutional rhythm of his Paris career.

## Prague and the Exiled Bourbon Court, 1833–1838

### Recruitment as tutor

A letter of **22 June 1833** from **Baron de Damas**, tutor to the Duke of Bordeaux, invited Cauchy into the educational household of the exiled Bourbons. By the summer of 1833 he left Turin for the court in Central Europe.

His pupil was **Henri d'Artois, Duke of Bordeaux** (1820–1883), grandson of Charles X and the Legitimist heir later known as the **comte de Chambord** and, to Legitimists, "Henri V."

### Geographic movements

The Bourbon exile court moved among Central European residences, with **Prague** the main place associated with Cauchy's tutorship and **Teplice/Toeplitz** also appearing in the correspondence and court itinerary.

Cauchy's wife and daughters joined him in Prague in **1834**, ending the prolonged family separation that had begun in 1830.

### Teaching the prince

Cauchy took the task seriously but the evidence suggests a poor pedagogical match. The duke showed little interest in mathematics and science. Later accounts describe Cauchy becoming frustrated during examinations and the royal household trying to moderate his intensity.

The episode generated one of the durable comic legends about Cauchy: after he mentioned having once dealt with a sewer-repair problem during engineering work, the young prince reportedly joked that Cauchy had "begun his career in the sewers of Paris." The story appears in later biographical retellings as an example of the pupil teasing his solemn tutor. It should not be mistaken for a literal account of Cauchy's professional origins: his engineering career began in elite state engineering institutions and major public works.

### Meeting with Bolzano, 1834

While in Prague, Cauchy met **Bernard Bolzano** in **1834**, apparently at Bolzano's request.

This meeting later became central to a historiographical controversy over whether Cauchy's concepts of continuity and related foundational ideas might have been borrowed from Bolzano.

The chronology is critical: Cauchy's foundational definitions were already in print in 1821 and had antecedents in his lectures before that. The 1834 meeting therefore cannot explain the original appearance of the 1821 definitions. More subtle questions about later influence remain distinct from the strong plagiarism claim.

### Scientific productivity

Cauchy's research output declined during his years as royal tutor compared with the explosive publication pace of the 1820s. Teaching and court obligations occupied significant time.

### Baronial title

Charles X bestowed the title **baron** on Cauchy for his service. Thereafter he was frequently styled "Baron Cauchy."

### End of tutorship

The tutoring role ended when the Duke of Bordeaux approached adulthood in **1838**. Cauchy returned to France that autumn.

## Return to Paris Under the July Monarchy, 1838–1848

### Academy membership resumed in practice

Cauchy returned to Paris in 1838 and resumed active participation in the Académie des sciences. Since Academy membership did not require the oath that had blocked other appointments, this gave him an institutional foothold.

### Bureau des Longitudes election, 1839

After de Prony's death, a place opened at the **Bureau des Longitudes**.

Cauchy was supported by **Biot** and, notably, **Arago**, despite their political differences. **Poisson** opposed him.

Cauchy was elected in **1839**, but the government would not validate his service without the required loyalty oath. He refused the oath. The practical result was anomalous: he had won the institutional election but could not formally exercise the office, attend as a normal member, or draw its salary.

A modern Bureau des Longitudes historical project specifically treats this "election ... not validated," preserving the event as an institutional case study.

### Catholic educational activism

Unable or unwilling to re-enter ordinary state teaching under the oath regime, Cauchy devoted considerable energy to Catholic educational projects and the defense of Jesuit schools.

These activities deepened hostility with portions of the academic establishment and made him a visible participant in the French struggle over control of education.

### Collège de France contest, 1843

When **Lacroix** died, Cauchy sought the mathematics chair at the **Collège de France**.

Other candidates included:
- **Joseph Liouville**;
- **Guglielmo Libri**.

Libri was selected.

Contemporary and later accounts agree that politics and institutional factionalism mattered heavily. Cauchy's Catholic/Jesuit activism hurt him. Liouville, who also lost, expressed humiliation at the outcome.

It is important not to retroactively transfer Libri's later crimes to Cauchy. **Libri** later fled France when facing prosecution for theft of valuable books from libraries. That scandal affected the later chair succession, but there is no evidence that Cauchy participated in Libri's thefts.

### Mathematical work, 1838–1848

Cauchy's output was lower than in his pre-exile peak but still enormous by ordinary standards. Important areas included:

- differential equations;
- mathematical physics;
- mathematical astronomy;
- complex-variable theory;
- operational/symbolic calculus.

His **four-volume *Exercices d'analyse et de physique mathématique*** appeared from **1840 to 1847**.

In **1843**, his memoir on symbolic equations in infinitesimal and finite-difference calculus became part of the later genealogy of operational calculus.

### 1848 election mechanics

Cauchy was also drawn into the technical problem of the first mass elections under universal male suffrage in the Second Republic. Archival scholarship on the Academy's role in the **23 April 1848** elections identifies him as a commissioner/rapporteur studying proposed methods for the *dépouillement* and *recensement*—the counting and tabulation—of votes.

This is a distinctive intersection of mathematics and politics: he was not simply expressing a partisan opinion, but acting as a scientific expert on the mechanics of large-scale vote counting.

## Revolution of 1848 and Final Academic Phase, 1848–1857

### February Revolution

The fall of Louis-Philippe in **February 1848** removed the oath barrier.

Belhoste notes that Cauchy did not initially regard the revolution with simple hostility: as a Legitimist, he could hope that the collapse of the Orléans monarchy might reopen the dynastic question in favor of his former pupil, the comte de Chambord. That did not occur.

### Appointment in mathematical astronomy

A vacancy/reorganization at the Faculté des sciences de Paris enabled Cauchy to return to a salaried university chair in **mathematical astronomy**. Secondary chronologies usually place the formal reinstatement/appointment on **1 March 1849**, following arrangements made after the 1848 revolution.

He held the chair through the final period of his life.

### Second Empire and oath exemption

After Louis-Napoléon Bonaparte established the Second Empire, political oaths again became an issue.

Cauchy again would not swear allegiance. Instead of removing him, the government made an exception. **Cauchy and the republican Arago**, political opposites, were both exempted from the oath requirement.

The paired exemption became a striking example of scientific prestige overriding normal political conformity.

### Liouville dispute over the Collège de France

After Libri fled France, the Collège de France succession reopened. Cauchy and **Liouville** again became entangled in the contest around **1850**.

Liouville ultimately obtained the chair. Subsequent efforts by Cauchy's supporters to reverse or challenge the outcome produced severe personal hostility between the two mathematicians.

This was an academic-political feud, not a criminal case.

### Renewed complex analysis

In the later 1840s and 1850s Cauchy returned intensively to complex function theory. He continued to refine the theory of analytic/monogenic functions, integral formulas, series, and convergence.

### The 1853 "sum theorem"

One of the major technical/historiographical episodes concerns Cauchy's theorem on sums of series of continuous functions.

An earlier form of Cauchy's claim was too strong if interpreted with merely pointwise convergence. By **1853**, Cauchy introduced a strengthened condition involving behavior for infinitely large indices and infinitesimal quantities. Later historians have debated whether this should be understood as essentially uniform convergence, as an infinitesimal criterion, or through some other conceptual reconstruction.

This episode is central to the modern dispute over whether Cauchy should be read through later Weierstrassian language.

### Duhamel–Poncelet priority dispute

In the final years Cauchy entered a bitter priority dispute with **Jean-Marie Duhamel** over results concerning inelastic impacts/shocks.

Cauchy claimed priority from work dated to 1832. Duhamel challenged the claim. **Poncelet** then pointed to his own 1826 work.

The standard historical account concludes that Cauchy's priority claim was not sustainable. Cauchy did not concede the matter, and Valson's near-contemporary biography says the dispute saddened his final period.

**Status:** documented scientific priority controversy.  
**Not supported:** fraud, plagiarism conviction, or legal misconduct.

## Illness, Health, Psychological Crises, and Death

### 1812 illness

As noted above, Cauchy's departure from Cherbourg in 1812 was caused by illness. Later historians have interpreted the episode as possibly psychological or depressive. The documentation is not adequate for a modern diagnosis.

### Later life

No reliable evidence was found of addiction, chronic intoxication, self-harm, or major accidental injury.

His extraordinary work habits and sustained publication output are repeatedly noted, but retrospective psychiatric labels should be avoided.

### Final illness, 1857

In April and May 1857 Cauchy was still producing scientific work, including material connected with astronomy. His physician advised him to leave Paris for the healthier surroundings of Sceaux.

The final disease is commonly described in secondary sources as a **bronchial or respiratory condition**. The precise modern diagnosis is uncertain.

A daughter later described his final hours in a strongly Catholic devotional register: he remained mentally alert until shortly before death, invoked Jesus, Mary, and Joseph, and died at about **4 a.m. on 23 May 1857**.

The devotional framing belongs to family testimony and was later incorporated into Catholic and hagiographic portrayals of Cauchy.

He died at **Sceaux**, where the local historical record associates his death with the house/site later incorporated into the grounds of the Lycée Marie-Curie.

## Major Mathematical and Scientific Contributions

The eponymous vocabulary attached to Cauchy is enormous. Not every item was named by Cauchy himself; many are later commemorative labels.

### Analysis

- Cauchy sequence
- Cauchy criterion
- Cauchy condensation test
- Cauchy product
- Cauchy estimates
- Cauchy remainder
- Cauchy principal value
- rigorous treatment of limits and convergence
- systematic use and analysis of infinitesimals

### Complex analysis

- Cauchy integral theorem
- Cauchy integral formula
- Cauchy's residue methods
- Cauchy estimates
- Cauchy–Riemann equations, jointly named retrospectively with Riemann
- development of analytic/monogenic function theory

### Differential equations

- Cauchy problem / initial-value problem
- Cauchy–Kovalevskaya theorem, a later joint eponym connecting his work with Sofia Kovalevskaya
- methods for ordinary and partial differential equations

### Algebra and group theory

- Cauchy's theorem for finite groups
- permutation/substitution theory
- determinants
- symmetric functions
- characteristic equations and spectral questions

### Geometry

- Cauchy rigidity theorem for convex polyhedra
- differential geometry and curvature
- work leading into what is now called Cauchy–Crofton integral geometry

### Probability

Cauchy contributed to probability and distributions; the modern **Cauchy distribution** bears his name.

### Mechanics and physics

- Cauchy stress tensor
- Cauchy momentum equation
- elasticity
- continuum mechanics
- wave propagation
- hydrodynamics
- optics, polarization, and dispersion

### Fluid mechanics and "Cauchy invariants"

Modern work by Uriel Frisch and Barbara Villone has recovered Cauchy's **1815 Lagrangian formulation of the three-dimensional incompressible Euler equations**, including invariants related to vorticity dynamics. These results were largely forgotten, rediscovered, and reattributed over the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

This is a good example of a contribution whose modern importance is partly **posthumous rediscovery**, not continuous fame from Cauchy's own lifetime.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, and Financial Questions

### 1. Academy appointment after the Restoration purge

**Claim:** Cauchy obtained his Academy place through political favoritism.

**Documented core:** Carnot and Monge were removed for political reasons after the Bourbon Restoration, creating vacancies; Cauchy entered the Academy in that context.

**Countervailing fact:** Cauchy was already scientifically distinguished and had won the Academy's Grand Prix.

**Best characterization:** a scientifically defensible appointment made possible by an explicitly political purge. There is no evidence that Cauchy bribed officials, falsified qualifications, or personally orchestrated the purge.

### 2. Religious pressure inside scientific evaluation

Cauchy sometimes introduced religious criticism into scientific reports and used his academic prestige in Catholic educational struggles.

**Documented:** yes.

**Interpretation:** contemporaries differed sharply over whether this was principled integration of faith and public life or inappropriate clerical intrusion into science.

### 3. Poncelet referee dispute

Poncelet believed Cauchy treated him unfairly or dismissively in connection with projective geometry.

**Documented source base:** Poncelet's later recollection and the institutional record of criticism.

**No evidence:** legal wrongdoing or secret suppression for financial gain.

### 4. Abel's 1826 Paris memoir

Abel submitted his great Paris memoir on elliptic functions to the Académie des sciences in late **1826**. Cauchy and Legendre were involved in the refereeing process, with Cauchy bearing responsibility for the report.

**Documented:**
- the memoir was submitted;
- Cauchy failed to report promptly;
- Abel died on 6 April 1829 before the matter had been satisfactorily resolved;
- Legendre pressed for action;
- a report was finally made in June 1829;
- the manuscript later disappeared from ordinary Academy custody and was eventually rediscovered in Florence in 1952.

**Historical judgment:** Belhoste and others sharply criticize Cauchy's handling as negligent and superficial.

**Not established:** that Cauchy deliberately stole Abel's work, intended to destroy it, or suppressed it because he feared competition.

The scandal is therefore best classified as **documented serious refereeing delay/negligence**, followed by later institutional loss of the manuscript, not proven malicious theft.

### 5. Galois manuscripts

The popular story says that Galois submitted revolutionary work to Cauchy and Cauchy simply lost, ignored, or suppressed it.

Modern archival scholarship substantially revises this.

**Documented:**
- on **25 May and 1 June 1829**, Galois submitted two articles through Cauchy;
- Poinsot and Cauchy were appointed referees;
- those manuscripts are now lost;
- René Taton found evidence that Galois **very probably withdrew them in January 1830**;
- evidence indicates that Cauchy planned to present Galois's results and likely encouraged him to recast the work for the Academy's Grand Prize;
- Galois submitted a later memoir to **Fourier**, not Cauchy; that later manuscript disappeared after Fourier's death.

**Best-supported conclusion:** the famous story of Cauchy personally "losing Galois's masterpiece" conflates separate submissions and is not supported by the strongest archival reconstruction.

### 6. Bolzano plagiarism accusation

A twentieth-century historiographical controversy asked whether Cauchy borrowed foundational ideas from **Bolzano** without acknowledgment.

**Basis of claim:** similarities between aspects of their approaches to continuity and analysis; Cauchy met Bolzano in Prague in 1834.

**Evidence against strong plagiarism claim:**
- Cauchy's central definitions were published in 1821;
- evidence from his course development places related ideas before 1821;
- the documented personal meeting occurred only in 1834.

Historians including **Hans Freudenthal** argued against the plagiarism hypothesis. Later scholarship has continued to debate influence and parallel development but the strong claim that the 1821 Cauchy definitions came from the 1834 meeting is chronologically impossible.

### 7. Liouville institutional conflict

Cauchy and Liouville became bitter rivals over academic appointments, especially after Libri's departure.

**Documented:** institutional contest and personal hostility.

**Not a criminal matter.**

### 8. Duhamel/Poncelet priority dispute

Cauchy claimed priority for a result on inelastic shocks; Duhamel challenged him and Poncelet produced earlier work.

**Historical consensus in standard accounts:** Cauchy's priority claim failed.

**Cauchy's response:** he did not concede.

**Status:** scientific priority dispute; no criminal or civil fraud finding.

### 9. Libri book theft

Because Libri competed with Cauchy for a chair, his later theft scandal often appears in Cauchy biographies.

**Critical distinction:** the accusation and prosecution concerned **Guglielmo Libri**, who fled France. There is no evidence that Cauchy participated in the theft or sale of stolen books.

### 10. Financial impropriety, corruption, laundering, criminality

No credible evidence was found of:
- embezzlement;
- bribery;
- financial fraud;
- money laundering;
- criminal prosecution;
- imprisonment;
- intelligence service;
- political assassination or violence;
- abuse of office for personal financial extraction.

The relevant "bad" material in Cauchy's record concerns political patronage, institutional combat, refereeing failures, abrasive relationships, religious-political activism, and disputed scientific priority—not criminal finance.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### Myth 1: "Cauchy lost or suppressed Galois's great paper"

**Story:** Galois handed his revolutionary theory to Cauchy; Cauchy lost it, forgot it, or suppressed it, delaying group theory.

**Appearance/circulation:** twentieth-century popular biographies of Galois and mathematicians' anecdotal lore, reinforced by romantic narratives of the doomed young genius ignored by the establishment.

**Evidence:** Academy records show the 1829 submissions and the appointment of Cauchy and Poinsot as referees. René Taton's archival research indicates Galois probably withdrew those manuscripts in January 1830. Cauchy appears to have taken the work seriously enough to plan an Academy presentation and probably suggested a revised Grand Prize submission.

**Crucial conflation:** a later manuscript submitted for the Grand Prize was entrusted to Fourier and disappeared after Fourier's death. That loss cannot be assigned to Cauchy.

**Effect on reputation:** the story helped transform Cauchy into the bureaucratic villain of the Galois legend.

**Best classification:** **popular myth built around a real but misrepresented referee episode.**

### Myth 2: "Cauchy stole or intentionally buried Abel's masterpiece"

**Story:** Cauchy was jealous of Abel and deliberately buried or stole the Paris memoir.

**Evidence for wrongdoing:** Cauchy's delay was real and serious. By Abel's death he had still not produced an adequate report despite pressure.

**Evidence against stronger story:** no documentary evidence establishes deliberate theft or a plan to destroy Abel's work. The physical manuscript had a complicated later history and was ultimately found in the Biblioteca Moreniana in Florence in 1952.

**Effect on reputation:** combined with the Galois story, it produced an image of Cauchy as a senior mathematician who habitually sabotaged younger geniuses.

**Best classification:** **documented negligence expanded into an unproven malicious-suppression legend.**

### Myth 3: "Cauchy began his career in the sewers"

**Story:** the Duke of Bordeaux reportedly mocked Cauchy by saying his tutor had begun professionally in the sewers of Paris.

**Origin:** later accounts of the Prague tutoring period say Cauchy mentioned a minor sewer-repair engineering task and the young prince exaggerated it for comic effect.

**Historical reality:** Cauchy's professional formation was at the École Polytechnique and Ponts et Chaussées; his important first assignments included the Ourcq Canal and Cherbourg harbor.

**Effect:** a court anecdote that humanized—or ridiculed—the solemn tutor.

**Classification:** **hostile/comic anecdote based on a small kernel of engineering reality.**

### Myth 4: "Cauchy invented modern epsilon-delta rigor exactly as later formalized"

**Story:** Cauchy single-handedly replaced infinitesimals with modern epsilon-delta analysis.

**Evidence:** he unquestionably imposed new standards of definition and proof and made limits, continuity, convergence, and infinitesimals central objects of analysis.

**Problem:** Cauchy continued to use infinitesimals in ways that modern scholars have argued are not merely disguised epsilon-delta arguments. The later Weierstrassian reconstruction can project nineteenth-century developments backward.

**Effect:** made Cauchy a clean origin point for a teleological history of modern analysis.

**Classification:** **partly true canonical simplification; historiographically disputed in its strongest form.**

### Myth 5: "Cauchy was simply an enemy of all liberal scientists"

His politics were strongly anti-liberal and Legitimist, but personal/institutional behavior was more complicated.

**Counterexample:** Arago, a major political opponent, supported Cauchy for the Bureau des Longitudes in 1839. Later both were exempted from the imperial oath.

**Classification:** **overgeneralization from real political division.**

### Myth 6: "Cauchy was insane"

Abel's private correspondence contains a famous hostile formulation describing Cauchy as "mad" while simultaneously calling him the mathematician who best understood how mathematics should be done.

The remark is evidence of Abel's frustration and Cauchy's contemporary reputation, not medical evidence of psychosis or another diagnosis.

**Classification:** **rhetorical contemporary insult, not a clinical fact.**

### Myth 7: Cauchy as Catholic saint-scientist

Catholic biographies, especially the older Catholic Encyclopedia tradition, construct Cauchy as a model Catholic savant whose scientific and devotional lives formed a single exemplary whole.

**Evidence supporting the core:** he was genuinely devout, publicly defended Catholic education, participated in charity, and co-founded the Œuvre des Écoles d'Orient.

**Hagiographic element:** the presentation often minimizes his abrasiveness, political patronage, scientific disputes, and failures.

**Effect:** created a parallel memory of Cauchy distinct from the secular mathematical story—less the difficult Academy referee, more the scientist-apostle.

**Classification:** **hagiographic interpretation built on substantial documented religious activity.**

### Myth 8: Cauchy as uniquely reactionary beneficiary of politics

The counter-myth presents all of his institutional success as a product of Bourbon reaction.

**Evidence:** his 1816 Academy appointment was indeed facilitated by politically created vacancies.

**Against total reduction:** he had already won the Academy Grand Prix, solved difficult problems, and produced work recognized by the same scientific community. Later political fidelity also damaged rather than helped his career for long periods.

**Classification:** **polemic based on a genuine episode but inadequate as a total explanation of his career.**

## Works by Augustin-Louis Cauchy

### General bibliographical scale

Cauchy's research output is usually counted at approximately **789 mathematical papers**, not including every administrative report, letter, or unpublished teaching document. The definitive posthumous **Œuvres complètes d'Augustin Cauchy**, published under the scientific direction of the Académie des sciences, eventually occupied **27 large volumes** in two series. Bibliographic sources give the long publication span as **1882–1974**; some catalogs describe earlier partial series endpoints because individual runs were issued over decades.

The complete works are therefore the indispensable bibliography for anyone seeking article-level completeness.

### Major books and serial publications during Cauchy's lifetime

#### 1821 — *Cours d'analyse de l'École royale polytechnique. Ire partie: Analyse algébrique*

Paris: Imprimerie Royale / De Bure frères.

Foundational textbook of algebraic analysis. Major subjects include limits, infinitesimals, continuity, convergence of series, power series, and related foundations.

**Authorship:** genuine, lifetime publication.

#### 1823 — *Résumé des leçons données à l'École royale polytechnique, sur le calcul infinitésimal. Tome premier*

Paris. The title page identifies Cauchy as engineer of Ponts et Chaussées, professor of analysis at the École Polytechnique, member of the Académie des sciences, and chevalier of the Légion d'honneur.

Frequently cited in shortened form as *Calcul infinitésimal*.

**Authorship:** genuine, lifetime publication.

#### 1825 — *Mémoire sur les intégrales définies, prises entre des limites imaginaires*

Paris: De Bure frères.

Part of the development of complex integration. The underlying research had roots in earlier Academy work; publication dates can differ depending on separate-print versus Academy-volume citation.

**Authorship:** genuine.

#### 1826 — *Leçons sur les applications du calcul infinitésimal à la géométrie*

A major geometric application of infinitesimal calculus.

**Authorship:** genuine.

#### 1826–1830 — *Exercices de mathématiques*

Issued annually in multiple volumes/years. This functioned almost like Cauchy's private research periodical and contains many original papers rather than simply textbook exercises.

**Authorship:** genuine; serial lifetime publication.

#### 1829 — *Leçons sur le calcul différentiel*

Paris: De Bure frères, rue Serpente no. 7.

A major differential-calculus treatise, 289 pages in the original bibliographic record consulted.

**Authorship:** genuine.

#### 1831 — *Sur la mécanique céleste et sur un nouveau calcul qui s'applique à un grand nombre de questions diverses*

A work connecting celestial mechanics with new calculational methods.

**Authorship:** genuine.

#### 1840–1847 — *Exercices d'analyse et de physique mathématique*

Paris: Bachelier, four volumes.

Contains important later work in analysis, differential equations, mathematical physics, and related areas.

**Authorship:** genuine; lifetime publication.

#### 1844 — *Leçons de calcul différentiel et de calcul intégral*

Bibliographic records associate this publication with Cauchy's methods and with Abbé Moigno in the transmission/redaction of lecture material. Editions and attributions should be checked title-page by title-page because later compilations sometimes combine Cauchy's methods with another editor's rédaction.

**Authorship status:** Cauchy-based teaching material; editorial/redaction role requires bibliographic qualification by edition.

### Landmark memoirs and research clusters

The following are not an exhaustive list of 789 papers but are among the works or clusters most important for reconstructing his intellectual development.

#### 1811–1812 — Polyhedra and rigidity

Memoirs on convex polyhedra, Euler-type relations, and rigidity.

**Legacy:** Cauchy's rigidity theorem.

#### 1812 / 1815 publication — Symmetric functions

Memoir on symmetric functions submitted in 1812 and published in the *Journal de l'École Polytechnique*.

#### 1814 onward — Definite integrals

Early memoirs on definite integrals, later forming a foundation for complex integration.

#### 1815–1816 — Waves and fluid mechanics

Prize-winning wave research and Lagrangian hydrodynamic methods.

**Modern recovery:** "Cauchy invariants" in three-dimensional incompressible Euler flow.

#### 1815 — Polygonal numbers

Proof of Fermat's polygonal-number claim.

#### 1820s — Permutations/substitutions

Series of works central to the emergence of permutation-group theory.

#### 1820s — Determinants and algebraic equations

Important work on determinants and spectral/characteristic-equation problems.

#### 1822–1823 onward — Elasticity and stress

Papers establishing mathematical continuum mechanics and stress theory.

#### 1820s–1830s — Singular integrals and residues

A long sequence leading to the residue calculus and Cauchy integral theory.

#### 1826 — *Sur un nouveau genre de calcul analogue au calcul infinitésimal*

Introduces/develops the calculus of residues in Cauchy's evolving terminology.

#### 1829 onward — Complex functions

The 1829 *Leçons* and subsequent memoirs contributed to defining and studying functions of a complex variable.

#### 1830s — Optics, polarization, dispersion

Papers seeking mathematical models for light in an elastic ether, including dispersion relations.

#### 1843 — *Mémoire sur l'emploi des équations symboliques dans le calcul infinitésimal et dans le calcul aux différences finies*

Published in *Comptes rendus de l'Académie des sciences*, vol. XVII, pp. 449–458.

Often cited in histories of operational calculus.

#### 1840s–1850s — Differential equations

Large body of work on existence methods, analytic solutions, and initial-value problems.

#### 1853 — revised work on the "sum theorem"

Cauchy's later return to the convergence of series of continuous functions is central to the historiography of uniform convergence and infinitesimals.

### Posthumous collections and editions

#### *Œuvres complètes d'Augustin Cauchy*

Paris: Gauthier-Villars, under the scientific direction of the Académie des sciences, **1882–1974**, 27 volumes in two series.

**Status:** posthumous scholarly collection of genuine Cauchy publications and papers; not a single lifetime-authored book.

Digital copies are distributed across Mathdoc, Internet Archive, Gallica, Google Books, HathiTrust, and other institutional repositories.

#### *Nouveaux exercices de mathématiques* (1895)

Posthumous publication under Cauchy's name drawing on his mathematical material.

**Status:** posthumous; edition history must be distinguished from works Cauchy personally brought to press.

### Correspondence and archival papers

Cauchy's correspondence survives in dispersed form rather than one universally cited complete critical edition. Important letters appear in:
- Valson's 1868 biography;
- family archives used by Belhoste;
- Académie and institutional records;
- Czech archival holdings related to the Prague years;
- local and national archival collections.

A complete documentary edition of all Cauchy correspondence was not located in the sources consulted.

The Norwegian Academy's Abel Prize historical material and modern research into the rediscovered Paris manuscript are central sources for separating Cauchy's actual refereeing delay from later suppression legends.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Nineteenth-century reputation

By his death, Cauchy was recognized as one of the dominant mathematicians of the century. His work was simultaneously admired for technical power and remembered through stories of political intransigence, Catholic activism, poor interpersonal relations, and difficult refereeing.

### The "rigor" canon

Twentieth-century textbooks made him one of the founders of rigorous analysis. Terms such as Cauchy sequence and Cauchy criterion made his name part of the everyday language of mathematical education.

This reputation is secure in broad outline but contested in detail. Modern historians emphasize that Cauchy's own foundations were not simply identical to the later real-number constructions and quantifier patterns of Weierstrass.

### Complex analysis

Cauchy's position is even more central in complex analysis. The integral theorem, integral formula, residue methods, estimates, and analytic-function framework form a large portion of the subject's standard architecture.

### Mechanics

Continuum mechanics retains Cauchy's name through the stress tensor, stress principle, momentum equations, and related concepts. Historical work has strengthened rather than weakened recognition of his role.

### Algebra and group theory

His work on substitutions occupies a major place in the prehistory and early history of group theory, though Galois and later abstraction transformed the subject.

### Rediscovered fluid mechanics

The modern recovery of Cauchy's 1815 Lagrangian hydrodynamic formulation shows how even a famous mathematician can have important results disappear from disciplinary memory.

### Religious memory

Catholic sources preserved a second Cauchy: charitable organizer, defender of Catholic education, and co-founder of the Œuvre des Écoles d'Orient. This memory was sometimes hagiographic but was grounded in real organizational activity.

### Political memory

Secular and republican histories often remembered him as a Legitimist and beneficiary of Restoration politics. That is also grounded in fact, but his later career shows the cost of the same convictions: exile, lost salaries, blocked appointments, and recurring institutional isolation.

### Character mythology

A durable composite image emerged:
- mathematical genius;
- fanatically productive worker;
- bad lecturer;
- rigid Catholic;
- Bourbon loyalist;
- harsh referee;
- man who mishandled Abel and Galois.

The first five have varying degrees of documentation; the last requires major qualification, especially for Galois.

### Historiographical reinterpretation

Recent work has complicated several textbook stories:

- Cauchy's infinitesimals may not be reducible to proto-Weierstrassian shorthand.
- His 1853 convergence work can be read in more than one foundational framework.
- His Galois role was less culpable than popular legend claimed.
- His Abel role remains legitimately open to criticism because the delay is documented.
- His mechanics and fluid work was broader and more forward-reaching than older pure-mathematics biographies often emphasized.
- His political and religious commitments were not external decorations; they materially structured his career, networks, migrations, and access to institutions.

July Monarchy falls. Oath barrier disappears.

Serves in Academy work on methods of counting/tabulating votes for the new mass electorate.

Académie-directed *Œuvres complètes* issued over many decades in 27 volumes.

Cauchy's foundational place in analysis, complex analysis, group theory, differential equations, and continuum mechanics remains canonical, while historians revise the textbook narratives concerning infinitesimals, Bolzano, Abel, Galois, and forgotten mechanics.

## Atlas Connections

### Joseph-Louis Lagrange

- **[T5] Reported fact:** Lagrange visited the Cauchy family in Paris around 1800, examined or heard about the young Augustin-Louis’s mathematical work, and advised Louis-François Cauchy to give his son a strong classical education before advanced mathematics. This advice shaped Cauchy’s schooling at the École Centrale du Panthéon. ([MacTutor biography](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Cauchy/))

### Pierre-Simon Laplace

- **[T5] Reported fact:** Laplace was personally connected with Cauchy’s father and visited the family home during Augustin-Louis’s childhood. Cauchy consequently encountered Laplace before beginning his professional career; in 1810 he took Laplace’s *Mécanique céleste* with him to Cherbourg. ([MacTutor biography](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Cauchy/))

### Évariste Galois

- **[T3] Documented fact:** Galois submitted two algebraic papers through Cauchy to the Académie des sciences in Paris on **25 May and 1 June 1829**. Cauchy presented them to the Academy and was assigned referee responsibilities. Archival reconstruction indicates that Cauchy recognized their importance and intended to report on them; Galois probably withdrew and revised the papers in January 1830 for the Grand Prize competition. The later competition manuscript was entrusted to Fourier, so its disappearance cannot be attributed to Cauchy. ([EMS edition of Galois’s writings](https://ems.press/content/book-chapter-files/20742), [Académie des sciences retrospective](https://www.academie-sciences.fr/pdf/eloges/galois_tits.pdf))

### Siméon-Denis Poisson

- **[T3] Documented fact:** Cauchy and Poisson repeatedly engaged through Académie des sciences procedures. Most concretely, they were appointed together to examine one of Galois’s papers on **1 June 1829**. Poisson later opposed Cauchy’s 1839 candidacy for the Bureau des Longitudes, although Cauchy was elected and then prevented from serving by his refusal of the loyalty oath. ([EMS edition of Galois’s writings](https://ems.press/content/book-chapter-files/20742), [MacTutor biography](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Cauchy/))

### Bernard Bolzano

- **[T5] Documented fact:** Cauchy and Bolzano had one personal meeting in **Prague in 1834**, reportedly at Bolzano’s request, while Cauchy was attached to the exiled Bourbon court. This meeting later became evidence in an influence or plagiarism controversy, but Cauchy’s principal definitions of continuity and limits had appeared in 1821, thirteen years before the meeting. ([MacTutor biography](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Cauchy/), [study of the personal contacts](https://www.persee.fr/doc/rhs_0048-7996_1962_num_15_2_4418), [continuity and priority study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.13259))

### Alexis de Tocqueville

- **[T3] Documented fact:** In **1843**, Cauchy sent Tocqueville an opuscule concerning the prevention of crime. This was a one-sided written approach connecting Cauchy’s Catholic social activism with Tocqueville’s work on crime and penal reform; the evidence supports transmission of the text, not a friendship or personal meeting.

No other roster crossing was included where the available evidence established only contemporaneity, shared geography, later mathematical influence, or a common intellectual tradition rather than one of the specified connection types.
