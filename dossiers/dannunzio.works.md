## Works by Gabriele d'Annunzio

### Poetry and lyric cycles

- **1879:** *All’Augusto Sovrano d’Italia Umberto I di Savoia nel 14 marzo del 1879. Suo giorno natalizio. Augurii e voti*. An occasional ode written with Vittorio Garbaglia; because authorship is shared, it is not wholly d’Annunzio’s obra.
- **1879; revised 1880:** *Primo vere*. His first poetry collection, issued under the name “Floro”; the substantially revised second edition makes the title a changing textual object rather than a single fixed text.
- **1880:** *In memoriam*. A small memorial collection, signed “Floro Bruzio,” written after his grandmother’s death.
- **1882; revised 1896:** *Canto novo*. A lyric collection whose later authorized form differs markedly from its first edition.
- **1883–1884:** *Intermezzo di rime*. A poetry collection first issued as *Intermezzo* and then expanded; its publication history resists treatment as one immutable text.
- **1886:** *Isaotta Guttadàuro ed altre poesie*. A composite poetry volume subsequently dismantled and reorganized into *L’Isotteo* and *La Chimera*.
- **1888:** *La Chimera*. A lyric collection separated from the earlier *Isaotta Guttadàuro* material and revised again for later collected editions.
- **1890:** *L’Isotteo*. A reconstituted poetry collection derived substantially from *Isaotta Guttadàuro ed altre poesie*.
- **1887–1891; published 1892:** *Elegie romane*. A cycle of elegies associated with Rome and modeled formally on the classical elegiac tradition.
- **1891–1892; published 1893:** *Poema paradisiaco*. A lyric collection whose quieter, intimate register distinguishes it from d’Annunzio’s heroic and civic poetry.
- **1891–1893; published 1893:** *Odi navali*. A cycle of naval and nationalist odes, published in the same volume as *Poema paradisiaco* but conceptually a separate obra.
- **1896; published posthumously in 1946:** *Sonnets cisalpins*. Twelve French-language sonnets preserved through d’Annunzio’s correspondence with Georges Hérelle; their posthumous assembly complicates their status as a finished collection.
- **1899–1933:** *Laudi del cielo, del mare, della terra e degli eroi*. A projected seven-book poetic Gesamtkunstwerk of landscape, myth, politics, and self-mythology. Only five books materialized, so the intended whole is also an unfinished project.
- **1903:** *Maia: Laus vitae*. The first book of the *Laudi*, constructed as a long autobiographical and mythic poem.
- **1903:** *Elettra*. The second book of the *Laudi*, comprising civic, commemorative, and heroic poems.
- **1903:** *Alcyone*. The third book of the *Laudi*, a seasonal lyric sequence; although often treated as an independent collection, it remains part of the larger unfinished cycle.
- **1911–1912:** *Canzoni delle gesta d’oltremare*. Newspaper poems celebrating the Libyan war, collected with alterations as *Merope*.
- **1912:** *Merope*. The fourth book of the *Laudi*, made from the colonial-war *Canzoni delle gesta d’oltremare*.
- **1914–1918; collected posthumously in 1948:** *Asterope: Canti della guerra latina*. The fifth book of the *Laudi*, assembling First World War poems. D’Annunzio shaped much of the material, but the definitive book-form arrangement is posthumous and therefore not an entirely author-supervised obra.

### Stories, novellas, and novels

- **1880:** *Cincinnato*. D’Annunzio’s first published short story, later absorbed into *Terra vergine* rather than maintained as an autonomous book.
- **1882:** *Terra vergine*. A collection of Abruzzese stories; later revisions and redistribution of its contents make the collection historically unstable.
- **1884:** *Il libro delle vergini*. A collection of three extended stories or short novellas.
- **1886:** *San Pantaleone*. A story collection, much of whose contents were later rewritten or reassigned to *Le novelle della Pescara*.
- **1889:** *Il piacere*. A novel and the first component of the retrospectively organized “Romanzi della Rosa.”
- **1889–1894:** *L’invincibile*. The working and serial form of the project that eventually became *Trionfo della morte*; it is best treated as a superseded novel-project, not simply an alternative title for a stable finished text.
- **1891; book publication 1892:** *Giovanni Episcopo*. A short novel cast substantially as its protagonist’s confession.
- **1892:** *L’innocente*. A novel and the second “Romanzo della Rosa.”
- **1894:** *Trionfo della morte*. A novel developed from the unfinished and serialized *L’invincibile* and subsequently designated the third “Romanzo della Rosa.”
- **1895:** *Le vergini delle rocce*. A novel intended as the first of three “Romanzi del Giglio.” The two projected sequels, *La grazia* and *L’annunciazione*, were never completed, so the trilogy exists primarily as a project.
- **1900:** *Il fuoco*. A novel intended as the first of three “Romanzi del Melagrano.” The projected continuations were not completed.
- **1902:** *Le novelle della Pescara*. An authorial recombination and revision of earlier Abruzzese stories, not merely a new edition of *Terra vergine* or *San Pantaleone*.
- **1910:** *Forse che sì forse che no*. D’Annunzio’s final completed novel, organized around aviation, speed, erotic obsession, and modern technology.
- **1913; book publication 1916:** *La Leda senza cigno*. A novella surrounded in its book form by the autobiographical *Licenza*; the hybrid publication refuses a clean division between fiction, memoir, and commentary.

### Plays, mysteries, and texts for musical theatre

- **1896–1897:** *Sogno d’un mattino di primavera*. A one-act “dream” for the theatre, written for Eleonora Duse.
- **1897–1898:** *Sogno d’un tramonto d’autunno*. A “tragic poem” for performance and companion to the earlier *Sogno*.
- **1896; published 1898:** *La città morta*. A tragedy first written with Duse in view, although Sarah Bernhardt gave the first performance in French.
- **1898:** *La Gioconda*. A modern tragedy centered on the destructive claims of art, erotic attachment, and the artist’s studio.
- **1899:** *La gloria*. A political tragedy.
- **1901; published 1902:** *Francesca da Rimini*. A verse tragedy and the first realized part of a projected Malatesta trilogy.
- **1903–1904:** *La figlia di Iorio*. A “pastoral tragedy” drawing on Abruzzese ritual and oral culture; d’Annunzio later recast it as a libretto for Alberto Franchetti.
- **1905:** *La fiaccola sotto il moggio*. A tragedy and the second component of d’Annunzio’s Abruzzese dramatic cycle.
- **1906:** *Più che l’amore*. A “modern tragedy,” issued with a prefatory speech and additional framing pieces after its hostile theatrical reception.
- **1908:** *La nave*. A verse tragedy conceived as mass civic spectacle; its later operatic and film forms are adaptations rather than identical manifestations.
- **1909:** *Fedra*. A verse tragedy that reworks the classical Phaedra myth.
- **1911:** *Le Martyre de saint Sébastien*. A French-language mystery play made for Ida Rubinstein, with incidental music by Claude Debussy, choreography, dance, décor, and ritualized staging. It is properly a collaborative Gesamtkunstwerk, not merely a spoken play or a Debussy composition.
- **1911–1915:** *La crociata degli innocenti*. A mystery play in four acts; it also generated a distinct cinema project, so the dramatic text and proposed film should not be collapsed into one object.
- **1912–1913:** *Parisina*. A lyric tragedy written as the libretto for Pietro Mascagni’s opera and conceived as another, but not sequentially completed, component of the Malatesta project.
- **1913:** *La Pisanelle, ou Le jeu de la rose et de la mort*. A French-language comedy with music by Ildebrando Pizzetti, created as a theatrical-musical spectacle.
- **1913:** *Le Chèvrefeuille*. The French theatrical manifestation of the drama later published in Italian as *Il ferro*; the relationship is adaptation and rewriting, not simple translation.
- **1914:** *Il ferro*. A drama in three acts and the Italian reworking of *Le Chèvrefeuille*.

### Prose experiments, biography, memoir, and self-archive

- **1888:** *L’Armata d’Italia*. A polemical assemblage of newspaper articles advocating Italian naval expansion; it is journalism deliberately remade as a political book.
- **1906:** *Prose scelte*. An authorial anthology rather than a wholly new composition; its made object is the selection and arrangement of existing prose.
- **1910; published 1913:** *La vita di Cola di Rienzo*. A literary biography intended to inaugurate a series, *Vite di uomini illustri e di uomini oscuri*; the larger series remained an unfinished project.
- **1912:** *Contemplazione della morte*. A memorial and meditative prose work prompted principally by the deaths of Giovanni Pascoli and Adolphe Bermond.
- **1911–1914; collected 1924 and 1928:** *Le faville del maglio*. Autobiographical prose first manifested as newspaper “sparks” and later remade into books; neither the serial corpus nor its later arrangements are reducible to a single fixed text.
- **1924:** *Il venturiero senza ventura e altri studii del vivere inimitabile*. The first book-form gathering of *Faville del maglio*, mixing memoir, fiction, criticism, and self-mythography.
- **1928:** *Il compagno dagli occhi senza cigli e altri studii del vivere inimitabile*. The second major *Faville del maglio* volume, again a constructed self-archive rather than straightforward autobiography.
- **1916; published 1921:** *Notturno*. A fragmentary memorial prose work drafted while d’Annunzio’s injured eyes were bandaged, partly on narrow paper strips and with assistance from his daughter. Its material procedure is inseparable from the obra.
- **1919; published posthumously in 1939:** *Solus ad solam*. A private diary addressed to Giuseppina Mancini; publication as a book was posthumous, so d’Annunzio made the diary but not its public book-form manifestation.
- **1932:** *Carmen votivum*. An erotic memorial or private votive chronicle whose classification oscillates between diary, prose poem, and self-document.
- **1935:** *Cento e cento e cento e cento pagine del Libro segreto di Gabriele d’Annunzio tentato di morire*. A deliberately unstable mixture of diary, aphorism, memoir, fiction, and self-commentary, published under the framing persona “Angelo Cocles.”
- **1935:** *Aux bons chevaliers de France et d’Italie*. A political address in French urging reconciliation between Fascist Italy and France.
- **1936:** *Le dit du sourd et muet qui fut miraculé en l’an de grâce 1266*. An archaizing French prose narrative issued under the name “Guerri de Dampnes.”
- **1936:** *Teneo te, Africa*. A six-volume propagandistic assemblage concerning the Ethiopian war; its objecthood lies partly in its monumental, deluxe publication form.

### Manifestos, speeches, commands, and protocols

- **1915:** *Per la più grande Italia: Orazioni e messaggi*. A collection of interventionist speeches and messages whose language was intended to mobilize political action: text functioning as manifesto.
- **5 May 1915:** *Orazione per la Sagra dei Mille*. The Quarto intervention speech; contemporary print dating sometimes gives 6 May, while the speech itself was delivered on 5 May.
- **1915–1918:** *War speeches, proclamations, trench messages, and aerial leaflets*. A dispersed body of performative texts later redistributed among several collections. It resists listing as one work because the corpus comprises speeches, broadsides, manuscript messages, and ephemeral print rather than a single authorial book.
- **1918:** *La beffa di Buccari*. A documentary-literary account of the naval raid, published with related poems, messages, and facsimiles; it turns a military action into a mixed-media commemorative object.
- **1918:** *La canzone del Quarnaro*. A poem and performance-text attached to the Buccari action, later incorporated into the war-poetry corpus.
- **1918:** *La riscossa*. A collection of wartime orations and appeals delivered or circulated between late 1917 and 1918.
- **1918:** *Leaflets for the Flight over Vienna*. Propaganda texts dropped from aircraft on 9 August. They are manifesto-actions and ephemeral printed objects rather than a conventional literary book.
- **1919:** *Lettera ai Dalmati*. A public political letter concerning the Adriatic territorial settlement.
- **1919:** *Contro uno e contro tutti*. A collection of polemical Fiume-period writings and pronouncements.
- **1920:** *Italia e vita*. A Fiume-period collection of speeches and political texts.
- **1920:** *Carta del Carnaro: Disegno di un nuovo ordinamento dello Stato libero di Fiume*. A constitutional protocol and political-aesthetic manifesto. Alceste De Ambris drafted its substantive syndicalist framework; d’Annunzio extensively recast its language and promulgated it, so sole authorship would be misleading.
- **1919–1920:** *Fiume proclamations, mottos, ceremonies, and command performances*. A body of speeches, ritual formulae, public letters, staged assemblies, salutes, and political ceremonies. It resists being called a discrete “work”: its obra was a temporary political theatre distributed across language, bodies, architecture, music, and collective action.
- **1923:** *Il nuovo patto marino*. A speech and proposed organizational compact for Italian maritime workers, functioning simultaneously as manifesto and protocol.
- **1923:** *Per l’Italia degli Italiani*. A large retrospective collection of political speeches and messages; as a book it remakes previously performed language into a documentary monument.

### Cinema and moving-image projects

- **1909:** *Unrealized films promised to Luca Comerio*. A contractual project under which d’Annunzio was to supply film subjects; the promised works were not delivered. Its incompletion is the principal surviving fact of the project.
- **1911:** *La nave*. A silent-film adaptation of the play: d’Annunzio supplied the underlying subject, while Arrigo Frusta wrote the scenario. It should not be attributed to d’Annunzio as sole filmmaker.
- **1911–1915:** *La crociata degli innocenti* film project. A cinematic subject developed from the mystery play but never realized in the intended form; it survives as a project rather than a completed film.
- **1914:** *Cabiria*. D’Annunzio supplied the title, character names, intertitles, and publicity prose, but did not originate or direct the film’s complete scenario. Giovanni Pastrone’s production deliberately advertised broader d’Annunzian authorship than the evidence supports.
- **1914:** *Del cinematografo considerato come strumento di liberazione e come arte di trasfigurazione*. An essay-manifesto presenting cinema as a new transformative art, written in connection with *Cabiria*.
- **1916:** *Il fuoco* film project. One of several unrealized or only partially developed screen adaptations associated with d’Annunzio’s French and Italian film negotiations; it is safer to classify it as a project than as a completed screenplay.
- **1917:** *La guerra e il sogno di Momi*. A film associated with d’Annunzio through its propagandistic and literary framing, but not a securely documented sole-authored screenplay; attribution should remain qualified.
- **1918:** *La riscossa delle maschere*. A film project or scenario documented among d’Annunzio’s cinema ventures but not securely equivalent to a completed authorial film.
- **1919:** *Fiume o morte!*. A documentary manifestation of the Fiume enterprise rather than a film authored by d’Annunzio; his political theatre is its subject and organizing presence, not straightforward cinematic authorship.

### Song texts and collaborative musical objects

- **1892; first musical publication 1904:** *’A vucchella*. A Neapolitan-language song text written by d’Annunzio and set by Francesco Paolo Tosti. The words and Tosti’s music form a collaborative song, not a book and not d’Annunzio’s musical composition.
- **1880s–1910s:** *Romanze for voice and piano with Francesco Paolo Tosti*. A substantial body of d’Annunzio poems set by Tosti, including texts sometimes first made as poems and only later manifested as songs. Authorship is divided: d’Annunzio made the words, Tosti the musical works.
- **1907–1908:** *Quattro canzoni d’Amaranta*. Four linked song texts written for Tosti’s settings. The cycle is a collaborative musical-literary object, although d’Annunzio did not compose its music.
- **1909:** *Fedra*. The tragedy also became a musical object through incidental music by Ildebrando Pizzetti; the staged result is collaborative and distinct from the printed play.
- **1911:** *Le Martyre de saint Sébastien*. As performed, this was d’Annunzio’s French text joined to Debussy’s score and Ida Rubinstein’s embodied performance; it therefore belongs equally to theatre, music, dance, and ritual.
- **1913:** *Parisina*. An opera by Pietro Mascagni for which d’Annunzio made the libretto; the opera is collaborative even though the text also circulated independently.
- **1913:** *La Pisanelle*. A stage work with music by Ildebrando Pizzetti; its musical manifestation must be distinguished from d’Annunzio’s French dramatic text.
- **1914:** *Cabiria* intertitles and musical-performance design*. D’Annunzio made the verbal apparatus and helped authorize the work’s elevated “vision” identity; Ildebrando Pizzetti and Manlio Mazza made the music, and Pastrone made the film.
- **1917–1927:** *Raccolta nazionale delle musiche italiane*. A monumental editorial project directed by d’Annunzio with Gian Francesco Malipiero, Ildebrando Pizzetti, Francesco Balilla Pratella, and Carlo Perinello. D’Annunzio’s role was programmatic and editorial, not that of composer.

### Naming, advertising, and verbal design

- **1917:** *La Rinascente*. The name d’Annunzio proposed for Senatore Borletti’s Milan department-store company. It is a commercial naming obra: one made word functioning as corporate identity, not a literary text.
- **1920s:** *Aurum*. A brand name devised for Amedeo Pomilio’s orange-based liqueur, formed through Latin associations with gold and orange.
- **1922:** *SAIWA*. The company name traditionally credited to d’Annunzio for the Società Accomandita Industria Wafer e Affini; it is verbal-industrial design, though popular accounts sometimes overstate the precision of the attribution.
- **1920s–1930s:** *Advertising slogans, product names, dedications, and endorsements*. A dispersed commercial corpus made for foods, drinks, perfumes, medicines, and manufacturers. It resists treatment as a finite work because many items survive in letters, labels, advertisements, or company traditions rather than in an authorially delimited collection.
- **Various dates:** *Velivolo, fusoliera, tramezzino, scudetto, vigili del fuoco,* and other attributed coinages or revivals. These are linguistic creations or acts of resemanticization, but several popular attributions are disputed: d’Annunzio often popularized or repurposed existing words rather than inventing them ex nihilo.

### Architecture, interiors, monuments, and total environments

- **1910s:** *La Capponcina as a staged dwelling*. D’Annunzio treated his Florentine residence, furnishings, animals, costumes, and hospitality as an aesthetic environment. Because the villa pre-existed him and its material arrangement was collaborative and largely dispersed, it resists designation as a wholly authored architectural work.
- **1921–1938:** *Il Vittoriale degli Italiani*. A monumental house, garden, archive, theatre-project, reliquary, mausoleum, and autobiographical landscape developed by d’Annunzio with architect Gian Carlo Maroni. It is his clearest Gesamtkunstwerk, although neither sole authorship nor completion at his death can be claimed.
- **1921–1938:** *La Prioria*. The villa at the center of the Vittoriale, transformed room by room into a symbolic, textual, and memorial interior. Maroni and numerous artisans executed much of it, while d’Annunzio directed its iconographic and ritual program.
- **1923–1925:** *The ship Puglia installation*. The forward portion of the warship was placed in the Vittoriale grounds as memorial sculpture, architectural fragment, and theatrical viewpoint. D’Annunzio conceived its symbolic relocation; engineers, naval personnel, and Maroni made it materially possible.
- **1923 onward:** *MAS 96 at the Vittoriale*. The surviving assault craft from the Buccari raid was incorporated into d’Annunzio’s self-monument. It is an appropriated historical object made into a new autobiographical installation, not an object he originally manufactured.
- **1920s:** *SVA 10 and the Flight-over-Vienna display*. The aircraft was converted from military vehicle into relic and exhibition object within the Vittoriale’s memorial system.
- **1920s–1938:** *Schifamondo*. A projected new residence within the Vittoriale, designed with Maroni but unfinished when d’Annunzio died. Its identity lies substantially in being an incomplete architectural project.
- **1920s–1938:** *The Vittoriale open-air theatre*. Conceived by d’Annunzio and Maroni as a performance space modeled on ancient theatres but completed only long after d’Annunzio’s death. The realized building is therefore a posthumous manifestation of his project, not a finished work he saw.
- **1920s–1938:** *The Vittoriale mausoleum project*. D’Annunzio planned his own monumental burial setting, but the present mausoleum was designed and completed by Maroni after his death. It belongs to d’Annunzio’s self-monumental project while resisting attribution as his completed architecture.

### Correspondence, notebooks, and works that resist work-status

- **Throughout his life:** *Taccuini*. Thousands of notebook observations, sketches, verbal studies, travel records, and compositional materials. They form a workshop archive and body of making rather than a single finished obra; published editions are editorial constructions.
- **Throughout his life:** *Letters*. An enormous correspondence with family, lovers, artists, publishers, soldiers, and political figures. D’Annunzio made the individual letters, but later titled epistolaries are posthumous scholarly corpora rather than books he composed as such.
- **Various dates:** *Motti*. A dispersed body of mottoes for military units, personal seals, rooms, objects, businesses, and political campaigns. Each is a small performative text, but no stable authorial boundary turns the whole corpus into one work.
- **Various dates:** *Signatures, pseudonyms, seals, emblems, and personal personae*. Names such as “Floro,” “Ariel,” and “Angelo Cocles” helped manufacture distinct authorial identities. These are creations and manifestations of self-fashioning, but they resist being called works in the ordinary bibliographical sense.
- **1880s–1938:** *The public figure “Gabriele d’Annunzio.”* Through journalism, clothing, residences, love affairs, military actions, ceremonies, photographs, publicity, and controlled legend, d’Annunzio made his own life into an aesthetic-political spectacle. This is not a single text or securely bounded work; its resistance to work-status is precisely what made it central to his practice.
