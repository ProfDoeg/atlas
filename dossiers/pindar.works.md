## Works by Pindar of Thebes

### Olympian victory songs

- **476 BCE:** *Olympian 1: For Hieron of Syracuse*. An epinician song celebrating Hieron’s Olympic horse-race victory; its central myth concerns Pelops.
- **476 BCE:** *Olympian 2: For Theron of Acragas*. An epinician for a four-horse-chariot victory, notable for its account of judgment after death and the Isles of the Blessed.
- **476 BCE:** *Olympian 3: For Theron of Acragas*. A second song for the same chariot victory, apparently performed during the Theoxenia at Acragas rather than simply duplicating *Olympian 2*.
- **452 BCE, probably:** *Olympian 4: For Psaumis of Camarina*. An epinician for an equestrian victory; manuscripts and ancient notices leave both the precise contest and its relationship to *Olympian 5* uncertain.
- **460 or 456 BCE, disputed:** *Olympian 5: For Psaumis of Camarina*. A processional-style victory song conventionally assigned to Pindar but suspected as non-Pindaric by some editors; it celebrates a mule-car victory.
- **468 BCE:** *Olympian 6: For Hagesias of Syracuse*. An epinician for victory in the mule-car race, built around the myth of the seer Iamus.
- **464 BCE:** *Olympian 7: For Diagoras of Rhodes*. A boxing-victory song whose myths connect Rhodes with Helios and Tlepolemus.
- **460 BCE:** *Olympian 8: For Alcimedon of Aegina*. An epinician for a boy’s wrestling victory, associating the victor’s Aeginetan family with Aeacus and Troy.
- **468 BCE:** *Olympian 9: For Epharmostus of Opus*. A wrestling-victory song; older chronologies sometimes place it in 466 BCE, but the Olympic victory is generally dated to 468.
- **476 BCE; performance possibly later:** *Olympian 10: For Hagesidamus of Western Locri*. An epinician for a boy’s boxing victory, apparently the fuller song promised by the shorter *Olympian 11*; the date of composition or performance is consequently debated.
- **476 BCE:** *Olympian 11: For Hagesidamus of Western Locri*. A short victory song for the same boy’s boxing success celebrated in *Olympian 10*, probably composed for the immediate celebration.
- **466 BCE:** *Olympian 12: For Ergoteles of Himera*. An epinician for victory in the long footrace, addressed prominently to Fortune.
- **464 BCE:** *Olympian 13: For Xenophon of Corinth*. A victory song celebrating success in both the stadion race and pentathlon, with the Corinthian myth of Bellerophon and Pegasus.
- **488 BCE, uncertain:** *Olympian 14: For Asopichus of Orchomenus*. A monostrophic song for a boy’s footrace victory, addressed to the Graces; its date is conjectural.

### Pythian victory songs

- **470 BCE:** *Pythian 1: For Hieron of Aetna*. An epinician for a chariot victory, joining praise of Hieron’s new city with the myth of Typhon and contemporary Greek warfare.
- **c. 475 BCE, uncertain:** *Pythian 2: For Hieron of Syracuse*. A chariot-victory song conventionally classed as Pythian, although its precise contest, date, and original performance context remain disputed.
- **c. 474 BCE, uncertain:** *Pythian 3: For Hieron of Syracuse*. Although attached to Hieron’s earlier horse-race victory, this consolatory song responds to his illness and therefore resists classification as an ordinary epinician.
- **462 BCE:** *Pythian 4: For Arcesilas of Cyrene*. A chariot-victory song and Pindar’s longest surviving ode, incorporating an extensive Argonautic narrative and an appeal concerning the exile Damophilus.
- **462 BCE:** *Pythian 5: For Arcesilas of Cyrene*. A second, probably processional, song for Arcesilas’s chariot victory, prominently honoring the driver Carrhotus and Cyrene’s founder Battus.
- **490 BCE:** *Pythian 6: For Xenocrates of Acragas*. A monostrophic chariot-victory song addressed chiefly through Xenocrates’s son Thrasybulus and the exemplary story of Antilochus and Nestor.
- **486 BCE:** *Pythian 7: For Megacles of Athens*. A short epinician for a chariot victory, shadowed by the Alcmaeonid patron’s ostracism.
- **446 BCE:** *Pythian 8: For Aristomenes of Aegina*. A wrestling-victory song and the latest securely dated surviving epinician by Pindar.
- **474 BCE:** *Pythian 9: For Telesicrates of Cyrene*. An epinician for victory in the race in armor, organized around Apollo’s courtship of the nymph Cyrene.
- **498 BCE:** *Pythian 10: For Hippocles of Thessaly*. A song for a boy’s double-stadion victory and Pindar’s earliest securely dated surviving work.
- **474 BCE:** *Pythian 11: For Thrasydaeus of Thebes*. A boy’s footrace epinician containing the story of Orestes; an alternative ancient chronology placed the victory in 454 BCE, but modern editors generally prefer 474.
- **490 BCE:** *Pythian 12: For Midas of Acragas*. A monostrophic song for victory in the aulos-playing contest, linking the instrument’s music to Athena, Perseus, and Medusa.

### Nemean and appended local songs

- **c. 476 BCE, uncertain:** *Nemean 1: For Chromius of Aetna*. A chariot-victory song whose actual victory was probably at the local games of Sicyon rather than Nemea; its traditional book-position is therefore an editorial classification, not a reliable description of its occasion.
- **c. 485 BCE, uncertain:** *Nemean 2: For Timodemus of Acharnae*. A short monostrophic epinician for a pancration victory.
- **c. 475 BCE, uncertain:** *Nemean 3: For Aristocleides of Aegina*. A pancration-victory song centered on the Aeacids and Achilles; proposed dates vary.
- **c. 473 BCE, uncertain:** *Nemean 4: For Timasarchus of Aegina*. A monostrophic song for a boy’s wrestling victory, also functioning as praise of the victor’s family.
- **c. 483 BCE, highly disputed:** *Nemean 5: For Pytheas of Aegina*. An epinician for a youth’s pancration victory; nearly every plausible odd-numbered year in the early fifth century has been proposed for its date.
- **c. 465 BCE, uncertain:** *Nemean 6: For Alcimidas of Aegina*. A song for a boy’s wrestling victory, presenting athletic excellence as an intermittent inheritance within the Bassid family.
- **c. 485 BCE, uncertain:** *Nemean 7: For Sogenes of Aegina*. An epinician probably celebrating a boy’s pentathlon victory; its treatment of Neoptolemus appears to answer or revise an earlier Pindaric account.
- **c. 459 BCE, uncertain:** *Nemean 8: For Deinias of Aegina*. A victory song probably for a footrace, although the precise event and date are not securely recoverable.
- **c. 474 BCE, uncertain:** *Nemean 9: For Chromius of Aetna*. A monostrophic chariot-victory song for the Sicyonian games, not a Nemean victory; Alexandrian editors appended it to the Nemean book because it did not belong to one of the four Panhellenic sequences.
- **c. 444 BCE, uncertain:** *Nemean 10: For Theaeus of Argos*. A song celebrating wrestling successes at Argive and other games rather than a single Nemean victory; its date is conjectural.
- **c. 446 BCE, uncertain:** *Nemean 11: For Aristagoras of Tenedos*. A song for Aristagoras’s installation as prytanis, not an athletic epinician; its attachment to the Nemean book is an ancient editorial expedient.

### Isthmian victory songs

- **c. 458 BCE, uncertain:** *Isthmian 1: For Herodotus of Thebes*. A chariot-victory song, apparently composed while Pindar was also working on *Paean 4*.
- **c. 470 BCE, uncertain:** *Isthmian 2: For Xenocrates of Acragas*. A chariot-victory song addressed after Xenocrates’s death to his son Thrasybulus; the date and whether Pindar himself performed or merely “sent” it remain debated.
- **c. 474–473 BCE, uncertain:** *Isthmian 3: For Melissus of Thebes*. A very short celebration of a recent equestrian victory, transmitted beside *Isthmian 4*; whether it is an independent ode, a supplement, or part of a composite performance is disputed.
- **c. 474–473 BCE, uncertain:** *Isthmian 4: For Melissus of Thebes*. A pancration epinician featuring Heracles and Antaeus; its textual and performative relationship to *Isthmian 3* is unresolved.
- **c. 478 BCE, uncertain:** *Isthmian 5: For Phylacidas of Aegina*. A pancration-victory song that invokes the recent battle of Salamis, providing only an approximate chronological anchor.
- **480 BCE, probably:** *Isthmian 6: For Phylacidas of Aegina*. Another pancration epinician for the same victor, celebrating the athletic record of his family through Heracles and Telamon.
- **c. 454 BCE, uncertain:** *Isthmian 7: For Strepsiades of Thebes*. A pancration-victory song that also commemorates the victor’s namesake, a kinsman killed in battle.
- **478 BCE:** *Isthmian 8: For Cleandrus of Aegina*. A monostrophic pancration-victory song whose myth concerns the divine competition to marry Thetis.
- **Date unknown:** *Isthmian 9*. Only eight opening verses survive because the end of the ancient Isthmian book was physically lost; neither the victor nor the complete occasion can now be recovered, so the fragment resists treatment as a fully identifiable work.

### Fragmentary sacred and civic songs

- **Fifth century BCE; individual dates mostly unknown:** *Hymns*. The Alexandrian edition assigned one papyrus roll to hymns for gods; only quotations and fragments survive, including remains associated by editors with a *Hymn to Zeus*. The roll is an ancient collected manifestation rather than a single composition, and the surviving evidence cannot securely recover every constituent work.
- **Fifth century BCE; individual dates mostly unknown:** *Paeans*. One Alexandrian book contained choral cult songs, principally for Apollo; modern critical editions arrange the recoverable remains as numbered paeans and related fragments. Several assignments are disputed because the boundary between paean and processional song was already unstable in antiquity.
- **Fifth century BCE; individual dates unknown:** *Dithyrambs*. Two Alexandrian books contained choral songs associated especially with Dionysus; substantial fragments include songs for Thebes and Athens, but none is complete and some ancient or modern genre assignments remain uncertain.
- **Fifth century BCE; individual dates mostly unknown:** *Processionals (Prosodia)*. Two Alexandrian books contained songs made to accompany ritual approaches or processions. Their fragmentary remains resist clean separation from paeans because the same composition could be classified by cult, destination, or mode of performance.
- **Fifth century BCE; individual dates unknown:** *Maiden-songs (Partheneia)*. Three Alexandrian books contained songs performed by choruses of girls; papyrus pieces preserve parts of at least several compositions, but titles, sequence, occasions, and limits are often unrecoverable.
- **Fifth century BCE; individual dates unknown:** *Dance-songs (Hyporchemata)*. Two Alexandrian books contained choral songs associated with expressive dancing. Only scattered fragments survive, and “hyporchema” is an ancient performance-category rather than evidence that the remains formed one unified obra.
- **Fifth century BCE; individual dates unknown:** *Encomia*. One Alexandrian book collected songs praising living people, including banquet or convivial compositions. The fragments include material addressed to figures such as Alexander son of Amyntas and Xenophon of Corinth, but their original titles and complete forms are generally lost.
- **Fifth century BCE; individual dates unknown:** *Dirges (Threnoi)*. One Alexandrian book contained funerary or consolatory songs. Surviving quotations preserve portions of several otherwise vanished compositions, including passages on death and the afterlife, but normally not enough to determine their titles, patrons, or limits.

### Unclassifiable, doubtful, and falsely attributed remains

- **Fifth century BCE; date otherwise unknown:** *Song for the Theoxenia of the Dioscuri*. A fragmentary cult song usually represented by fragment 140b in the Snell–Maehler/Race numbering; its original Alexandrian genre-book is uncertain.
- **Fifth century BCE; date otherwise unknown:** *Nomos basileus (“Law/Custom, king of all”)*. A famous fragment conventionally numbered 169a, surviving through quotation rather than as an independent manuscript. “Nomos basileus” is its opening designation, not a securely transmitted authorial title, and the original composition’s genre and extent are unknown.
- **Fifth century BCE; individual dates unknown:** *Fragments of uncertain genre*. Standard critical editions preserve numerous quotations, papyrus scraps, and isolated words as fragments 140–310 and supplements, but these witnesses cannot reliably be converted into a corresponding number of distinct works; doing so would invent titles and boundaries that the evidence does not supply.
- **Dates unknown:** *Doubtful and spurious fragments*. Critical editions print a separate body of verses transmitted under Pindar’s name but judged doubtful or non-Pindaric. These resist inclusion as things Pindar certainly made: their presence records ancient attribution and textual transmission, not established authorship.

### Ancient collected manifestation

- **Late third to early second century BCE:** *The seventeen-book Alexandrian edition of Pindar*. Aristophanes of Byzantium and other Alexandrian scholars organized Pindar’s songs into one book of hymns, one of paeans, two of dithyrambs, two of processionals, three of maiden-songs, two of dance-songs, one of encomia, one of dirges, and four of victory songs. This is not itself a work made by Pindar but a later editorial manifestation of his body of work; only the four epinician books survive nearly complete.
