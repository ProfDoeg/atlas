# Muhammad Baha al-Din Naqshband Bukhari: Research Dossier

## Scope, Method, and Evidentiary Limits

Muhammad Bahāʾ al-Dīn Naqshband lived in fourteenth-century Transoxiana. The surviving record is unusually rich for a medieval Sufi but structurally difficult:

- Most information comes from disciples, successors, or later members of the tradition.
- The earliest substantial biography, Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn ibn Mubārak al-Bukhārī’s Persian *Anīs al-ṭālibīn wa-ʿuddat al-sālikīn*, was completed in 785 AH/1383 CE, approximately six years before Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s death. It is therefore nearly contemporary but is a hagiography organized around teachings, spiritual states, and miracles rather than a continuous documentary biography.
- Another early source, *Manāqib-i Amīr Kulāl*, preserves important—and sometimes unflattering—material about his apprenticeship.
- Muḥammad Pārsā, one of his principal disciples, collected sayings in *Risāla-yi qudsiyya*.
- Later authorities—including ʿAbd al-Raḥmān Jāmī’s *Nafaḥāt al-uns* and Fakhr al-Dīn ʿAlī Ṣafī’s *Rashaḥāt ʿayn al-ḥayāt*—systematized traditions after the order had acquired an institutional identity.
- There are no known administrative dossiers, personal diaries, autograph correspondence, court proceedings, financial accounts, or securely authenticated contemporary portraits.
- The sources are collections of episodes rather than connected annals. Exact dates for most events cannot be established.

The labels used below are:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a near-contemporary text, manuscript evidence, inscription, or strong agreement among independent historical sources.
- **Reported fact:** found in early or established sources but not independently verifiable.
- **Scholarly identification or inference:** a modern historical reconstruction from incomplete evidence.
- **Hagiographic tradition:** a devotional narrative intended to demonstrate sanctity.
- **Disputed claim:** a claim for which significant contrary evidence or chronological difficulty exists.
- **Legend or myth:** a supernatural, symbolic, genealogical, or etiological story important to the subject’s reception but not historically demonstrable.
- **No evidence found:** the requested category was searched, but no responsible source supports a claim.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Most common scholarly name:** Khwāja Muḥammad Bahāʾ al-Dīn Naqshband Bukhārī.

**Persian/Arabic forms:**

- خواجه محمد بهاءالدین نقشبند بخاری
- بهاء الدين نقشبند
- Muḥammad ibn Muḥammad al-Bukhārī
- Bahāʾ al-Dīn Muḥammad Naqshband
- Bahāuddin/Bahauddin Naqshband
- Bahouddin Naqshband, a common modern Uzbek transliteration
- Bahâeddin Nakşibend, the standard Turkish form

**Expanded patronymic in some later sources:** Bahāʾ al-Dīn Muḥammad ibn Burhān al-Dīn Muḥammad al-Bukhārī. The earliest scholarly summaries more conservatively give Muḥammad ibn Muḥammad.

**Titles and sobriquets:**

- **Khwāja**, “master,” the conventional title of the Central Asian Khwājagān.
- **Bahāʾ al-Dīn**, “Splendor/Glory of the Religion.”
- **Naqshband**, literally something like “pattern-maker,” “image-imprinter,” or “designer.”
- **Shāh-i Naqshband / Şâh-ı Nakşibend**, especially common in Turkish and later devotional usage.
- **Khwāja-yi buzurg**, “the great master.”
- **Khwāja-yi Khwājagān**, “master of the masters.”
- **Khwāja Balā-gardān**, “the averter of calamity,” a Bukharan devotional title.
- **al-Bukhārī**, indicating association with Bukhara.
- **al-Uwaysī**, describing a spiritual initiation received without physical contact from a deceased master.

**Birth:** Muḥarram 718 AH, corresponding to March 1318 CE. Some later accounts specify 14 Muḥarram, but this precision is not equally secure across the early sources.

**Birthplace:** Qaṣr-i Hinduvān, a village approximately one farsakh from Bukhara in Transoxiana, now in the Bukhara region of Uzbekistan.

**Death:** 3 Rabīʿ I 791 AH, generally converted as 2 March 1389 CE. Occasional references to 1388, 1390, or 1391 reflect calendar conversion or copying errors; 791/1389 is the established scholarly dating.

**Place of death and burial:** His native village, by then called Qaṣr-i ʿĀrifān, “Castle of the Gnostics.” The settlement later acquired the colloquial name Bāvaddīn/Bahouddin.

**Religious identity:** Sunni Muslim Sufi, generally situated within the Hanafi legal culture of Transoxiana. He became the eponym—not necessarily the institutional founder in the modern organizational sense—of the Naqshbandiyya.

**Languages:** His surviving sayings are transmitted principally in Persian. He lived among a largely Persian/Tajik-speaking urban and village milieu but also trained with Turkic Yasawī-associated masters. No securely identifiable autograph survives.

## Names and the Problem of “Naqshband”

The origin of the name *Naqshband* is unresolved.

### Spiritual-imprinting explanation

**Later hagiographic interpretation:** *naqsh* means “imprint” and *band* suggests fastening or fixing. The name was explained as his ability to imprint the divine name Allāh permanently upon the disciple’s heart through silent remembrance.

This explanation became nearly universal in later Naqshbandi literature, but the earliest Naqshbandi texts do not explain when or why he acquired the sobriquet.

### Occupational explanation

**Disputed claim:** He or his family may have worked as textile weavers, embroiderers, or pattern-makers. Modern biographies sometimes present this as fact and describe the family as artisans.

Evidence for an actual occupation in embroidery or brocade manufacture is late and uncertain. It may be an inference from the sobriquet itself.

### Place-name explanation

Some Persian-language accounts propose that Naqshband was the name of a village. This is weakly attested and is not the dominant scholarly explanation.

### Historical conclusion

The sobriquet was indisputably attached to him and subsequently to the order. Its original literal occasion is unknown. The theological interpretation is historically important but cannot be demonstrated as the name’s original meaning.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

The father is generally named **Muḥammad**, sometimes expanded to **Burhān al-Dīn Muḥammad** in later sources. Little reliable information survives about either parent.

No dependable name for his mother has been established in the major early-source summaries.

### Paternal grandfather

His paternal grandfather was reportedly devoted to the Khwājagān master Bābā Muḥammad Sammāsī. The grandfather is an important actor in the childhood narratives:

- he brought the newborn or infant Bahāʾ al-Dīn into Sammāsī’s spiritual orbit;
- he later consulted Sammāsī concerning Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s marriage.

These are **reported facts from hagiographic sources**, not independently documented family records.

### Prophetic descent

**Later genealogical claim:** Bahāʾ al-Dīn was a sayyid descended from the Prophet Muhammad, usually through Imam Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq; other genealogies connect him with al-Ḥasan al-ʿAskarī.

**Evidence against treating this as established fact:**

- The earliest sources do not identify Bahāʾ al-Dīn as a prophetic descendant.
- They do explicitly describe his teacher Amīr Kulāl as a Ḥusaynī sayyid, demonstrating that such ancestry would probably have been mentioned had it been part of Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s known identity.
- The traditional spiritual chain includes Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq. Later writers may have converted this initiatic relationship into biological genealogy.

**Assessment:** A historically influential but unverified—and probably secondary—genealogical tradition.

### Marriage

An early hagiographic account says that, when Bahāʾ al-Dīn was of marriageable age, his grandfather sent him to Bābā Sammāsī so that the proposed bride could receive the master’s approval.

The spouse’s securely established name, family, and dates have not been recovered. Details sometimes supplied by modern devotional genealogies should not be treated as documentary fact without manuscript support.

### Children

The sources agree that he had at least one daughter. Later traditions commonly say that he had two or three daughters and no surviving son.

One daughter married his disciple and eventual principal successor **Khwāja ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn ʿAṭṭār**. Some later genealogical sources name her Sayyida Zahrā, but this name is not equally secure in the earliest materials.

Later families—especially lines associated with the Hazrat Ishaan tradition—claimed biological descent through this daughter and ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn ʿAṭṭār. These descent claims are historically significant but require case-by-case genealogical verification.

No dependable evidence establishes biological sons, siblings, or a large household.

## Childhood and Early Life

### Birth and spiritual adoption

According to Jāmī and related traditions, Bābā Muḥammad Sammāsī passed through Qaṣr-i Hinduvān before Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s birth and announced that the “scent” of a future saint was coming from the village.

Three days after the child’s birth, Sammāsī allegedly accepted him as a spiritual son and assigned his eventual formation to his foremost disciple, Amīr Kulāl.

**Classification:** Hagiographic tradition. It establishes retrospective predestination: the saint was recognized before or immediately after birth. It cannot be independently verified.

### Childhood education

No detailed curriculum survives. As a major Sunni religious teacher in fourteenth-century Bukhara, Bahāʾ al-Dīn must have acquired familiarity with:

- the Qurʾan;
- ritual and legal norms;
- Arabic religious vocabulary;
- Persian devotional and Sufi discourse;
- the manners and practices of the Khwājagān.

There is, however, no reliable list of madrasas, jurists, Qurʾan teachers, degrees, or formal licenses. Later claims portraying him as a comprehensively credentialed jurist go beyond the available evidence.

### Historical setting

His childhood unfolded during the decline and fragmentation of the Chaghatay Khanate. Transoxiana experienced political instability, shifting military power, and competition among khans, emirs, and regional elites. Bukhara nevertheless remained a major center of commerce, scholarship, crafts, and Islamic religious life.

This setting matters because one phase of his apprenticeship appears to have placed him inside a ruler’s entourage.

## Education and Formation

## Bābā Muḥammad Sammāsī

Sammāsī was the master whom later tradition credited with identifying Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s spiritual potential and entrusting him to Amīr Kulāl.

Bahāʾ al-Dīn may have encountered Sammāsī again when his marriage was being arranged. The chronology is uncertain because the source narratives are episodic.

## Amīr Kulāl

Amīr Kulāl was Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s principal physically present teacher in the Khwājagān lineage.

The sources credit him with giving Bahāʾ al-Dīn:

- companionship with a master (*nisbat-i ṣuḥbat*);
- instruction in the etiquette of the Sufi path;
- initiation into remembrance of God (*talqīn-i dhikr*);
- prolonged practical discipline.

The *Manāqib-i Amīr Kulāl* depicts Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s apprenticeship as difficult rather than automatically triumphant. He was rebuked for complacency or insufficient development and ordered to perform menial duties, including carrying water for his master’s ablutions.

Later accounts expand this discipline into years of service to the sick, injured animals, roads, and travelers. These stories embody the Sufi ideal of breaking pride through service; their literal chronology is not verifiable.

## The silent-dhikr conflict

Amīr Kulāl’s circle practiced vocal remembrance. Bahāʾ al-Dīn came to prefer exclusively silent remembrance after a visionary encounter with the deceased ʿAbd al-Khāliq Ghijduwānī.

Other disciples reportedly resented Bahāʾ al-Dīn for leaving the circle when vocal dhikr began. At the construction of a mosque in Sūkhārī, Amīr Kulāl rebuked them and publicly affirmed Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s exceptional standing.

**Assessment:** The episode probably preserves a genuine ritual differentiation within the master’s circle, although its dialogue and visionary explanation are hagiographic.

## Uwaysī initiation from ʿAbd al-Khāliq Ghijduwānī

Bahāʾ al-Dīn reportedly wandered among the graves of earlier masters near Bukhara and encountered their spiritual presences. The deceased Ghijduwānī instructed him:

- to follow the sharīʿa rigorously;
- to choose strict observance over dispensations;
- to adhere to silent dhikr.

Because Ghijduwānī died about a century before Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s birth, this was explicitly understood as an **Uwaysī**, nonphysical transmission.

**Classification:** Hagiographic and doctrinal claim, not a physical teacher-student meeting.

Its historical function was substantial. It authorized Bahāʾ al-Dīn to emphasize silent remembrance even where the living teacher’s circle used vocal dhikr, while preserving continuity with the older Khwājagān.

## Mawlānā ʿĀrif Dīgarānī

After or during his association with Amīr Kulāl, Bahāʾ al-Dīn spent time with Mawlānā ʿĀrif Dīgarānī, another disciple of Kulāl. They reportedly sought practitioners of silent dhikr together.

The exact length and sequence of this association are unknown.

## Qutham Shaykh

Bahāʾ al-Dīn had a comparatively brief association with **Qutham Shaykh**, described as a Turkic master connected with the Yasawī tradition.

## Khalīl Atā

His most politically significant apprenticeship was with **Khalīl Atā**, another Turkic or Yasawī-associated master. The sources say Bahāʾ al-Dīn served him for twelve years.

Later scholarship has tried to identify Khalīl Atā with a Chaghatay ruler variously called Qadan, Qazan, Ghazan, or Khalīl Sultan. The chronology and exact name have been debated.

Masatomo Kawamoto argues from hagiography, estimated ages, chronicles, and coinage that Khalīl/Qadan was Qazan Sultan, son of Yasaur, who ruled in practical terms during the final phase of the Chaghatay khanate and was killed in 747 AH/1346 CE.

This political service ended when the ruler was defeated in rebellion.

## Chronological Life History

### 1318: Birth near Bukhara

Bahāʾ al-Dīn was born in Qaṣr-i Hinduvān during Muḥarram 718/March 1318.

The predestination story—that Sammāsī detected his spiritual “scent” before birth—is a later hagiographic frame.

### Childhood to adolescence

He grew up under the religious influence of a family already attached to the Khwājagān. No dependable evidence fixes the dates or locations of his elementary education.

### Marriageable age

His grandfather reportedly submitted the choice of spouse to Sammāsī. This suggests that Bahāʾ al-Dīn remained embedded in a household and kinship network rather than living permanently as a celibate renunciant.

### Approximately the 1330s: Training under Amīr Kulāl

Bahāʾ al-Dīn entered sustained companionship with Amīr Kulāl. His formation included instruction, ritual initiation, obedience, rebuke, and manual service.

The early sources do not supply a clean beginning or ending date.

### Visionary turn toward silent remembrance

While visiting tombs associated with the Khwājagān, he reportedly received instruction from the spiritual presence of Ghijduwānī. Silent dhikr became central to his practice.

The resulting tension with some of Amīr Kulāl’s disciples suggests that this was perceived as a distinctive ritual position, even though silent remembrance had antecedents in the Khwājagān.

### Association with Dīgarānī and the “Turkish shaykhs”

He subsequently associated with ʿĀrif Dīgarānī, Qutham Shaykh, and Khalīl Atā. This gave his formation both Persianate-Khwājagān and Turkic-Yasawī elements.

### Approximately 1330s–1346: Service under Khalīl Atā/Qazan

The twelve-year figure is reported in hagiographic material. If approximately correct, the association began when Bahāʾ al-Dīn was about eighteen.

During the latter part of this period, Khalīl reportedly became ruler of Transoxiana, and Bahāʾ al-Dīn served within his governing entourage.

The fall and death of the ruler ended this phase. Later devotional literature portrays the collapse as a lesson in the impermanence of political power.

### Return to the Bukhara region

After his extended apprenticeship, Bahāʾ al-Dīn returned to Qaṣr-i Hinduvān and began training disciples.

His ordinary economic life remains obscure. Traditions associate him with agricultural labor, simple food, and refusal to accumulate possessions. No land deed, waqf deed, tax record, workshop account, or household inventory attributable to him is presently known.

### Teaching community

His circle drew heavily from Persian/Tajik-speaking urban and learned communities. It included:

- Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn ibn Mubārak al-Bukhārī;
- Khwāja Muḥammad Pārsā;
- ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn ʿAṭṭār;
- Yaʿqūb Charkhī;
- ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn Ghijduwānī;
- other Bukharan scholars and aspirants.

The community did not initially resemble a centralized international order with standardized branches. “Naqshbandiyya” became the retrospective and then general name for the line.

### Journeys outside Bukhara

A major scholarly summary says that, after settling into his teaching role, Bahāʾ al-Dīn left the Bukhara region only three times:

1. a pilgrimage to Mecca;
2. a second pilgrimage to Mecca, during which he stopped in Herat for three days to visit Zayn al-Dīn Abū Bakr Ṭayyābādī;
3. a visit to Herat at the invitation of the Kart ruler Muʿizz al-Dīn Ḥusayn.

The precise dates and routes are not securely known.

### First pilgrimage

Little documentary detail survives. Later sources describe spiritual encounters and exemplary poverty, but a reliable itinerary cannot be reconstructed.

### Second pilgrimage and Herat meeting

During the second pilgrimage he reportedly stayed three days in Herat with the prominent Sufi **Zayn al-Dīn Abū Bakr Ṭayyābādī**, who died in the same Islamic year as Bahāʾ al-Dīn.

Some accounts place discussion of succession during a pilgrimage and suggest that Muḥammad Pārsā was designated as principal khalīfa. The wording and finality of this designation are disputed by the subsequent emergence of ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn ʿAṭṭār.

### Visit to Muʿizz al-Dīn Ḥusayn in Herat

The Kart ruler invited Bahāʾ al-Dīn to Herat. Bahāʾ al-Dīn reportedly explained his path at court while refusing luxury and displaying detachment.

This is the clearest report of direct engagement with a ruler after the Khalīl Atā period. It does not prove an organized political program.

### Final years

He remained based at Qaṣr-i Hinduvān, received disciples, and became an increasingly prominent sacred figure in the Bukhara region.

Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn Bukhārī completed *Anīs al-ṭālibīn* in 1383, while Bahāʾ al-Dīn was still alive. Its existence demonstrates that his sanctity, teachings, and miracle stories were already being collected before his death.

### 1389: Death

Bahāʾ al-Dīn died on 3 Rabīʿ I 791/2 March 1389 and was buried in his home village.

No dependable medical cause of death is recorded. No specific terminal illness, accident, injury, poisoning, or violent death is established.

## Religious and Intellectual Development

## Continuity and innovation

Bahāʾ al-Dīn did not invent his path from nothing. He inherited the Central Asian **Khwājagān**, especially the principles associated with ʿAbd al-Khāliq Ghijduwānī.

His historical importance lies in the consolidation and emphatic transmission of particular features:

- silent rather than vocal dhikr;
- constant inward recollection amid ordinary social activity;
- close companionship with a living guide;
- strict conformity to Islamic law;
- opposition to spiritual display;
- disciplined attention to breath, time, number, and the heart;
- avoidance of institutional and sartorial theatricality.

## The eleven principles

Tradition attributes eight principles to Ghijduwānī:

1. **Hūsh dar dam** — awareness in breathing.
2. **Nazar bar qadam** — watching one’s step.
3. **Safar dar watan** — journeying within the homeland/self.
4. **Khalwat dar anjuman** — solitude in the crowd.
5. **Yād kard** — recollection.
6. **Bāz gasht** — returning.
7. **Nigāh dāsht** — guarding attention.
8. **Yād dāsht** — sustained awareness.

Three additional principles are conventionally associated with Bahāʾ al-Dīn:

9. **Wuquf-i zamānī** — awareness of one’s state in time.
10. **Wuquf-i ʿadadī** — numerical awareness during remembrance.
11. **Wuquf-i qalbī** — awareness or concentration of the heart.

The precise standardization of this eleven-part list may owe something to later systematization. It nevertheless accurately represents the practices transmitted under his authority.

## Silent dhikr

Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s most consequential ritual preference was **dhikr-i khafī**, remembrance conducted silently or inwardly.

The practice was not entirely new. Ghijduwānī and earlier Khwājagān supplied precedents. Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s consistent insistence gave it defining force in the lineage descending from him.

## Sharīʿa and strict observance

The visionary instruction attributed to Ghijduwānī demanded *ʿazīma*—rigorous observance—rather than reliance on permissible relaxations.

Later Naqshbandi movements frequently combined Sufism with legalist revival. It would be anachronistic, however, to attribute every later Naqshbandi political or juridical program to Bahāʾ al-Dīn personally.

## Rejection of conspicuous Sufi institutions

He reportedly opposed or avoided:

- musical audition and dance (*samāʿ*);
- prolonged solitary retreat (*khalwa*);
- distinctive Sufi clothing;
- permanent residence in a khānaqāh;
- ostentatious miracle-working;
- reliance on a prestigious chain without actual discipline.

His preferred ideal was later summarized by the celebrated Persian maxim **“dil ba yār, dast ba kār”**—“heart with the Friend, hand at work.” The phrase accurately captures Naqshbandi ethos, but its status as a verbatim saying coined by Bahāʾ al-Dīn is uncertain.

## Malāmatī affinities

His concealment of spiritual states, avoidance of outward distinction, and suspicion of public displays resemble the earlier Malāmatī tradition of Nishapur.

Muḥammad Pārsā explicitly compared the Khwājagān with the Malāmatiyya. Modern scholarship therefore treats Bahāʾ al-Dīn as a diffuse heir to Malāmatī ethics, not necessarily as a formally initiated member of a Malāmatī institution.

## Attitude toward miracles

Hagiographies attribute many supernatural acts to him, yet sayings attributed to Bahāʾ al-Dīn deprecate miracles as a criterion of sainthood. This tension is characteristic of Sufi hagiography: the saint’s refusal to claim miracles becomes part of the proof of sanctity.

## Doctrine and Ibn ʿArabī

Later Naqshbandi branches developed differing positions on *waḥdat al-wujūd*, associated with Ibn ʿArabī, and *waḥdat al-shuhūd*. The surviving evidence does not justify placing Bahāʾ al-Dīn himself securely within those later doctrinal controversies. His transmitted teaching is practical, devotional, and disciplinary more often than formally metaphysical.

## Employment, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Profession

No securely documented profession survives.

Possibilities found in later literature include:

- artisan, weaver, embroiderer, or textile pattern-maker;
- farmer or cultivator;
- religious teacher and Sufi guide;
- court servant during Khalīl Atā’s rule.

Only the last two have substantial textual grounding, and the court role remains contested in its precise character.

### Sufi organization

During his lifetime, his community was a master-disciple network within the Khwājagān. There is no evidence that he registered or endowed a formally constituted “Naqshbandi order” under that name.

The order’s institutional spread was principally the achievement of successors and later renovators.

### Property and wealth

The available evidence depicts voluntary simplicity, manual labor, and refusal of court luxury.

No verifiable information was found concerning:

- monetary wealth;
- investments;
- commercial partnerships;
- inherited estates;
- urban property;
- endowed lands personally established by him;
- debts or loans;
- taxation disputes;
- ownership of a workshop;
- charitable foundations created through a surviving deed.

Later wealth associated with shrines, khānaqāhs, land endowments, and Naqshbandi families must not be projected backward onto his personal finances.

### Technologies, inventions, and patents

None. Breath regulation and attention practices may be described metaphorically as spiritual technologies, but they were neither patented inventions nor creations ex nihilo.

## Political Activity

### Service to Khalīl Atā/Qazan

This is the most serious contested episode in his life.

The *Manāqib-i Amīr Kulāl* says that Bahāʾ al-Dīn served Khalīl Atā when the latter became ruler. A passage interpreted by Hamid Algar and Masatomo Kawamoto associates Bahāʾ al-Dīn with the role of **jallād**, “executioner,” or with the ruler’s execution service.

Kawamoto argues that Khalīl Atā was Qazan Sultan and that Bahāʾ al-Dīn belonged to the ruler’s close associates, who could have been required to carry out executions.

**Evidentiary classification:** Report in an early hagiographic source, strengthened by scholarly historical identification, but lacking independent court or chronicle confirmation of particular executions.

**What cannot responsibly be claimed:**

- No named victim has been identified.
- No specific execution carried out personally by Bahāʾ al-Dīn is documented.
- No court appointment, salary, warrant, or administrative record survives.
- “Executioner” may denote a formal or entourage role rather than a complete occupational biography.
- Later claims that he entered government specifically to impose religious reform are retrospective interpretations.

The report is nevertheless too early and specific to dismiss merely because it conflicts with later saintly imagery.

### Relationship with rulers

The reported Herat audience with Muʿizz al-Dīn Ḥusayn shows that rulers sought his religious authority. The narrative emphasizes his independence rather than patronage.

Later Naqshbandis became deeply involved in imperial and regional politics. Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s own record provides precedents for proximity to power but not proof of a comprehensive political ideology.

### Wars, rebellion, or organized violence

No evidence establishes that he:

- commanded troops;
- led a rebellion;
- held territorial office;
- organized political assassinations;
- participated in a sectarian war;
- imposed a legal code;
- maintained an armed following.

## Succession

Succession after Bahāʾ al-Dīn was not entirely simple.

### Muḥammad Pārsā

Some sources say Bahāʾ al-Dīn designated Pārsā during a pilgrimage and confirmed him on his deathbed. Pārsā became the principal intellectual expositor of the master’s sayings.

### ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn ʿAṭṭār

ʿAṭṭār, already linked to Bahāʾ al-Dīn by marriage to his daughter, emerged as the principal practical successor.

### Interpretation

This may reflect complementary roles rather than a clean disputed election:

- Pārsā as scholar, compiler, and doctrinal transmitter;
- ʿAṭṭār as familial and initiatic leader.

Later lineages used different succession pathways, so narratives could be shaped by institutional genealogy.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, and Financial Questions

## Service as an executioner

**Claim:** Bahāʾ al-Dīn served Khalīl Atā/Qazan Sultan as a *jallād*.

**Source basis:** *Manāqib-i Amīr Kulāl*, discussed by Hamid Algar and studied historically by Masatomo Kawamoto.

**Status:** Serious reported claim, not a court-proven fact. The role may have involved executions, but no individual act can be reconstructed.

**Reception:** The episode is frequently omitted from devotional biographies. Some scholarly treatments regard it as an early example of Naqshbandi access to political power; Algar cautions that the evidence does not establish an intention to influence the ruler toward sharīʿa government.

## Genealogical inflation

**Claim:** He was a biological descendant of Muhammad through Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq or al-Ḥasan al-ʿAskarī.

**Status:** Later genealogical claim; absent from the earliest sources and probably influenced by confusion between spiritual and biological chains.

**Effect:** The claim enhanced hereditary charisma for later families tracing descent through Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s daughter.

## Founder-title problem

He is routinely called “founder of the Naqshbandiyya.”

**Qualification:** The practices, lineage, and eight foundational principles existed earlier under the name Khwājagān. He was the eponymous consolidator whose emphasis on silent dhikr became decisive. Describing him as sole inventor obscures Ghijduwānī and earlier masters.

## Authorship inflation

Poems, prayers, litanies, and compilations have circulated under his name.

**Status:** Apart from the possibly authentic *Awrād-i Bahāʾiyya*, no book is securely established as his composition. The earliest biographical tradition even says that he discouraged disciples from writing down his sayings.

## Buddhist-influence theory

Aziz Ahmad suggested a Mahāyāna Buddhist influence on the early Naqshbandiyya.

**Status:** Scholarly hypothesis rejected by Hamid Algar for lack of evidence. Similarities in contemplative technique do not document transmission.

## Modern polemics

Modern anti-Sufi critics characterize shrine visitation, saintly intercession, Uwaysī transmission, and specialized litanies as unlawful innovations or exaggerations. These are theological judgments about Sufism and later Naqshbandi practice, not documentary evidence of fraud by Bahāʾ al-Dīn.

Conversely, modern devotional organizations sometimes treat every later teaching or “Golden Chain” claim as directly traceable to him. Historical continuity varies substantially among branches.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

## The prenatal “scent of a saint”

**Story:** Sammāsī passed through Qaṣr-i Hinduvān and announced that he smelled the spiritual fragrance of a future saint. Later he said the scent had grown stronger after Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s birth.

**Appearance and circulation:** Preserved in Naqshbandi hagiographic traditions and repeated by Jāmī and later authors.

**Evidence:** No independent evidence; the motif is a conventional sign of predestined sainthood.

**Reputational effect:** It places Bahāʾ al-Dīn under authorized Khwājagān guidance from birth and eliminates the appearance of a self-appointed founder.

## Adoption at three days old

**Story:** Sammāsī accepted the infant as a spiritual son three days after birth and ordered Amīr Kulāl to train him.

**Status:** Hagiographic tradition. The precise age serves symbolic and legitimating purposes.

## Meetings with the dead

**Story:** At the graves of earlier masters, Bahāʾ al-Dīn saw their spiritual forms and received initiation from Ghijduwānī.

**Status:** Uwaysī religious claim. It is neither presented by believers nor assessable by historians as an ordinary physical meeting.

**Effect:** It resolved the problem of Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s emphasis on silent dhikr despite the vocal practice found in Amīr Kulāl’s circle.

## Khwāja Balā-gardān

**Story:** Sammāsī or divine favor gave Bahāʾ al-Dīn power to avert calamity from Bukhara and its people.

**Status:** Devotional belief and local saint-cult title.

**Effect:** He became a patron saint of Bukhara, and the tomb became a site for petitions, protection, healing, and blessing.

## Imprinting the divine name

**Story:** Constant silent dhikr enabled him to stamp the name Allāh upon the heart, explaining “Naqshband.”

**Status:** Later etiological and mystical interpretation; absent from the earliest explanations.

**Effect:** It turned an obscure sobriquet into a concise image of the order’s contemplative method.

## Miracles involving animals, illness, clairvoyance, and distant knowledge

The hagiographic corpus credits him with:

- recognizing hidden spiritual states;
- knowing disciples’ thoughts;
- healing or relieving suffering;
- communicating with animals;
- receiving instruction from deceased saints;
- protecting travelers and communities;
- foretelling the spiritual rank of disciples.

These stories were already being collected during his lifetime in *Anīs al-ṭālibīn*. Their early date makes them evidence for contemporary reputation, not proof that the supernatural events occurred.

## Service to roads and animals

**Story:** He spent years tending sick people, wounded animals, and neglected roads, placing service above spiritual prestige.

**Status:** Hagiographic moral narrative, possibly preserving actual charitable labor.

**Effect:** It supports the Naqshbandi ethic of humility, work, and unobtrusive service.

## “Neither of the East nor the West”

Later traditions universalize him as the axial saint of his age. Such cosmic titles express devotional hierarchy rather than a historically measurable office.

## The “Golden Chain”

Modern Naqshbandi orders present a continuous chain from Muhammad through Abu Bakr, Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq, Abū Yazīd al-Bisṭāmī, Yūsuf Hamadānī, Ghijduwānī, Sammāsī, Kulāl, and Bahāʾ al-Dīn.

**Historical qualification:**

- Some links represent ordinary teacher-disciple relationships.
- Others span chronological gaps and are explicitly Uwaysī or symbolic.
- Lists were standardized retrospectively.
- Their importance is theological and institutional, not equivalent to modern documentary prosopography.

## Death and Burial

Bahāʾ al-Dīn died in his home village on 3 Rabīʿ I 791/2 March 1389.

The available sources do not establish:

- a cause of death;
- a named terminal disease;
- prolonged incapacity;
- poisoning;
- execution;
- assassination;
- accidental death.

He was buried locally rather than in a major urban monumental tomb. The burial place subsequently reshaped the identity of the settlement:

1. Qaṣr-i Hinduvān;
2. Qaṣr-i ʿĀrifān;
3. Bāvaddīn/Bahouddin in colloquial and modern usage.

## Shrine and Posthumous Cult

### Early pilgrimage

The grave became a major pilgrimage destination. Bahāʾ al-Dīn came to function as Bukhara’s protecting saint, and visiting his tomb became part of the region’s sacred geography.

### Monumental development

The surviving complex accumulated over centuries.

Important phases include:

- construction of a raised tomb or *dakhma* with carved marble enclosure;
- the Shaybanid ruler ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz Khan’s major works in 1544–1545;
- construction of a large khānaqāh;
- mosques, gates, a pond, water pavilion, minaret, madrasa spaces, and royal burials;
- later patronage by the mother of Abū al-Fayż Khan and by officials including Ḥakīm Qushbegi.

The irony is notable but not necessarily contradictory: Bahāʾ al-Dīn reportedly disliked residence in a khānaqāh, while later patrons constructed a monumental khānaqāh at his tomb.

### Soviet period

Religious visitation was restricted or prohibited. Accounts report that parts of the shrine were closed, secularized, used as a museum, or used for storage—including fertilizer storage.

Local devotion did not disappear. The site’s treatment became emblematic of Soviet pressure on Central Asian Islamic institutions.

### Post-Soviet restoration

The Uzbek government supported major restoration and commemoration, notably around the 675th anniversary of his birth in 1993. Additional work occurred in the early twenty-first century.

The restoration had several functions:

- conservation of architecture;
- revival of pilgrimage;
- presentation of an indigenous, socially disciplined Islamic heritage;
- state promotion of Bahāʾ al-Dīn as a national and civilizational figure;
- differentiation of recognized local Sufism from movements classified by the state as extremist.

The architectural complex appears on Uzbekistan’s UNESCO-related heritage documentation and remains one of the country’s most important pilgrimage sites.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

## Formation of the Naqshbandiyya

Before Bahāʾ al-Dīn, the lineage was commonly called the **Khwājagān**, “the Masters.” The name Naqshbandiyya became attached to the path after him, although Jāmī could still describe it as the path of the Khwājagān more than a century later.

His centrality appears to derive from:

- consolidation of silent dhikr;
- an influential community of learned disciples;
- disciplined integration of contemplation with social life;
- the work of Pārsā, ʿAṭṭār, and later transmitters;
- the growth of his shrine and saintly reputation.

## Geographic expansion

Within several generations, the order spread beyond Persian-speaking Transoxiana into Turkic Central Asia. Later branches expanded through:

- Afghanistan;
- Iran;
- the Ottoman lands;
- the Balkans;
- the Caucasus;
- Kurdistan;
- India;
- China and Eastern Turkestan;
- Southeast Asia;
- Africa;
- Europe and North America.

This expansion was not directed personally by Bahāʾ al-Dīn. It resulted from later networks.

## Major later renovators

Two later figures were especially important:

- **Aḥmad Sirhindī** (d. 1624), associated with the Mujaddidī branch in South Asia;
- **Mawlānā Khālid al-Baghdādī** (d. 1827), associated with the Khālidī expansion through the Ottoman domains.

They were regarded as renovators or branch founders, not replacements for Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s eponymous position.

## Political legacy

Later Naqshbandis engaged deeply with rulers, bureaucracies, reform movements, anticolonial struggles, and state formation.

Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s reported court service and Herat audience supplied precedents for engagement with power, but later political behavior varied radically and cannot be reduced to his biography.

## Intellectual legacy

His influence lies less in a personal written corpus than in transmitted practice:

- silent remembrance;
- watchfulness over breath and heart;
- companionship;
- inward solitude amid public life;
- strict religious observance;
- concealment of spiritual claims;
- integration of work and contemplation.

## National and global commemoration

In modern Uzbekistan he is represented as:

- a major native religious thinker;
- one of the “Seven Pirs” of Bukhara;
- a symbol of cultural heritage;
- a focus of pilgrimage tourism;
- an exemplar of disciplined, socially integrated spirituality.

Global Naqshbandi groups deploy his name in differing doctrinal, genealogical, and organizational claims. There is no single contemporary authority accepted by all branches.

## Works by or Attributed to Bahāʾ al-Dīn

## Securely authored works

No substantial book is securely known to have been written by Bahāʾ al-Dīn.

The early tradition states that he discouraged the recording of his words. His intellectual corpus is therefore principally oral and disciple-mediated.

## Possibly authentic

### *Awrād-i Bahāʾiyya* / *Awrād-i Bahāʾ al-Dīn*

A litany transmitted under his name.

- **Original language:** Arabic prayers and devotional formulas, transmitted in Persianate manuscript settings.
- **Date:** Fourteenth century if authentic; earliest securely identified recension requires further manuscript study.
- **Attribution:** Possible but unproven.
- **Problem:** It is not mentioned by the earliest Naqshbandi texts and never became the universal foundation of ordinary Naqshbandi practice.
- **Publication:** Printed repeatedly, including in Aḥmad Żiyāʾ al-Dīn Gümüşhānevī’s *Majmūʿat al-aḥzāb*, Istanbul, 1311 AH/1893–1894, vol. II, with commentary by Muḥammad Saʿīd Khādimī.
- Modern Uzbek manuscript scholarship identifies copies catalogued in collections of Arabic manuscripts.

## Disciple-recorded sayings

### *Risāla-yi qudsiyya* / *Kalimāt-i Bahāʾ al-Dīn Naqshband*

- **Compiler/author:** Khwāja Muḥammad Pārsā.
- **Nature:** Sayings attributed to Bahāʾ al-Dīn, arranged with Pārsā’s explanation.
- **Authorship status:** Not a book written by Bahāʾ al-Dīn; a disciple’s edited record.
- **Notable edition:** Edited by Aḥmad Ṭāhirī ʿIrāqī, Tehran, 1354 SH/1975.

### Sayings in *Anīs al-ṭālibīn wa-ʿuddat al-sālikīn*

- **Author:** Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn ibn Mubārak al-Bukhārī.
- **Completed:** 785 AH/1383.
- **Nature:** Biography, teachings, states, and miracles.
- **Authorship status:** Work about him by a disciple, not his own composition.
- **Recensions:** A longer and an abridged recension.
- **Translations:** Anonymous Urdu and a Turkish translation by Süleyman İzzî, both published in 1328 AH/1910 under forms of the title *Maqāmāt-i Muḥammad Bahāʾ al-Dīn Naqshband*.

### Sayings in later compilations

His statements also occur in:

- Jāmī, *Nafaḥāt al-uns*;
- Fakhr al-Dīn ʿAlī Ṣafī, *Rashaḥāt ʿayn al-ḥayāt*;
- Abū al-Ḥasan Muḥammad Bāqir, *Maqāmāt-i Shāh-i Naqshband*;
- later Naqshbandi manuals and chain histories.

These are posthumous transmissions of varying proximity.

## Poetry and quatrains

Persian quatrains circulate under his name.

**Attribution:** Disputed. Modern researchers have argued for his activity as a *rubāʿī*-poet, but the absence of a securely dated autograph collection and the fluid transmission of Sufi verse make individual attribution uncertain.

There is no securely established personal *Dīwān*.

## Letters, diaries, legal opinions, or correspondence

No securely authenticated corpus is known.

## Major Primary and Near-Primary Sources

1. **Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn ibn Mubārak al-Bukhārī, *Anīs al-ṭālibīn wa-ʿuddat al-sālikīn***, completed 785/1383. Earliest substantial life and teaching collection; Persian; hagiographic; survives in numerous manuscripts and two recensions.

2. **Mawlānā Shihāb al-Dīn, *Manāqib-i Amīr Kulāl***, fourteenth-century hagiography. Important for Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s apprenticeship and the report of service connected with Khalīl Atā’s executions.

3. **Khwāja Muḥammad Pārsā, *Risāla-yi qudsiyya***, late fourteenth or early fifteenth century. Disciple’s collection and exposition of Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s sayings.

4. **ʿAbd al-Raḥmān Jāmī, *Nafaḥāt al-uns min ḥaḍarāt al-quds***, completed in the fifteenth century. Influential later biographical synthesis.

5. **Fakhr al-Dīn ʿAlī Ṣafī, *Rashaḥāt ʿayn al-ḥayāt***, early sixteenth century. Major Naqshbandi hagiographical compilation.

6. **Abū al-Ḥasan Muḥammad Bāqir ibn Muḥammad ʿAlī, *Maqāmāt-i Shāh-i Naqshband***, known through later manuscripts and a Bukhara lithograph of 1327 AH/1909.

7. **Muḥammad al-Rakhāwī, *al-Anwār al-qudsiyya fī manāqib sādāt al-Naqshbandiyya***, Cairo, 1344 AH/1925. Late synthetic hagiography.

8. **ʿAbd al-Majīd al-Khānī, *al-Ḥadāʾiq al-wardiyya fī ḥaqāʾiq ajillāʾ al-Naqshbandiyya***, Cairo, 1308 AH/1890. Late lineage and saintly compilation.

## Atlas Connections

Only source-supported intersections with the supplied atlas roster are listed. The roster contains hundreds of people separated from Bahāʾ al-Dīn by geography and centuries; absence of a connection is therefore the principal finding.

### Muhammad ibn Abdullah

**Nature:** The Naqshbandi initiatic chain is retrospectively traced to the Prophet Muhammad. Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s strict adherence to the Qurʾan, sunna, and sharīʿa was central to his transmitted teaching.

**Dates and place:** Muhammad died in 632 CE, almost seven centuries before Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s birth. No physical meeting was possible.

**Label:** **Documented religious influence; traditional initiatic-chain claim.** The historical influence of Muhammad on Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s Islamic practice is self-evident; the exact esoteric chain is a later institutional formulation.

### Abu Bakr as-Siddiq

**Nature:** The standard Naqshbandi “Golden Chain” traces its transmission from Muhammad through Abu Bakr. Silent remembrance is sometimes connected in later tradition with Muhammad and Abu Bakr’s concealment in the cave during the Hijra.

**Dates and place:** Abu Bakr died in 634 CE. No physical meeting was possible.

**Label:** **Traditional lineage claim, retrospectively documented in Naqshbandi literature.** It is not a direct biographical relationship.

### Abu Yazid Tayfur ibn Isa al-Bistami

**Nature:** Abū Yazīd al-Bisṭāmī appears as an early link in the retrospectively standardized Naqshbandi chain, between Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq and later Central Asian masters.

**Dates and place:** Abū Yazīd died in the ninth century, centuries before Bahāʾ al-Dīn.

**Label:** **Traditional initiatic connection; no direct meeting.** Some links in this portion of the chain are understood spiritually rather than as documented face-to-face teaching.

### Abu al-Qasim al-Junayd ibn Muhammad al-Baghdadi

**Nature:** Junayd’s sober Sufism forms part of the broader Sunni Sufi inheritance with which Naqshbandi discipline was later compared.

**Label:** **Reported intellectual affinity, not a documented personal or direct initiatic connection.** No source consulted establishes a distinct Bahāʾ al-Dīn–Junayd transmission beyond the general Sufi tradition.

### Abu al-Qasim Abd al-Karim ibn Hawazin al-Qushayri

**Nature:** Qushayrī’s writings belong to the broader textual environment of sober Sunni Sufism.

**Label:** **No specific documented connection found.** General compatibility is not evidence of direct influence.

### Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani

**Nature:** Later composite and parallel Sufi chains sometimes place Qādirī and Naqshbandī inheritances together.

**Label:** **No documented personal connection.** ʿAbd al-Qādir died more than a century before Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s birth and is not part of the defining Khwājagān succession by which Bahāʾ al-Dīn was trained.

### Ibn Arabi

**Nature:** Later Naqshbandi authors engaged extensively with Ibn ʿArabī’s metaphysics.

**Nature:** Both became central figures in Persianate Sufism, and later communities sometimes compare their methods.

**Label:** **No documented connection found.** Rumi died in 1273, before Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s birth; no consulted source establishes a specific acknowledged textual influence.

### Farid al-Din Attar

**Nature:** Persian Sufi literary predecessor.

**Label:** **No documented direct connection found.** The surname of Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s disciple ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn ʿAṭṭār does not establish a relationship with the poet Farīd al-Dīn ʿAṭṭār.

**Nature:** Ibn Baṭṭūṭa’s *Riḥla* is relevant to scholarly attempts to reconstruct the Chaghatay political world and identify Khalīl Atā/Qazan.

**Label:** **Contextual source only; no documented meeting.** Shared chronology and Central Asian travel do not prove personal contact.

### Genghis Khan

**Nature:** Bahāʾ al-Dīn lived in a Chaghatay polity produced by the division of the Mongol Empire after Chinggis Khan.

**Label:** **Documented structural historical context, not a personal connection.** Chinggis Khan died approximately ninety years before Bahāʾ al-Dīn’s birth.

**Nature:** Modern esoteric literature sometimes associates Gurdjieff with Central Asian Sufi or Naqshbandi environments.

**Label:** **No documented connection to Bahāʾ al-Dīn personally.** Any relationship is through much later claims about Central Asian esoteric traditions.

### René Guénon

**Nature:** Guénon’s conversion to Islam and interest in Sufism led later traditionalists to discuss major Sufi orders, including the Naqshbandiyya.

**Label:** **No personal connection; later reception only.**
