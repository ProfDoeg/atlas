# Ahmad al-Qalqashandi: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

Aḥmad al-Qalqashandī is unusually difficult to reconstruct as a private person. Contemporary biographers supplied only brief notices; most surviving evidence concerns his education, offices, writings, patrons, and scholarly contacts. No reliable source located for this dossier documents his mother, siblings, wife or wives, household property, personal wealth, illnesses, or cause of death.

This dossier distinguishes:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a contemporary text, authorial statement, dated colophon, manuscript, or correspondence.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a contemporary or near-contemporary chronicler but not independently documented.
- **Later tradition:** repeated in later biographical literature without surviving contemporary corroboration.
- **Disputed:** conflicting sources or modern reassessment.
- **Unsupported/legend:** a popular claim for which the available evidence is inadequate.

Dates are given in both Hijrī and Common Era forms where they can be established. Conversions of medieval Islamic dates can vary by a day.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

**Most securely reconstructed name:** Abū al-ʿAbbās Shihāb al-Dīn Aḥmad ibn ʿAbd Allāh ibn Aḥmad al-Fazārī al-Qalqashandī al-Shāfiʿī.

**Conventional name in reference works:** Abū al-ʿAbbās Shihāb al-Dīn Aḥmad ibn ʿAlī ibn Aḥmad al-Fazārī al-Qalqashandī al-Shāfiʿī.

**Arabic:** أبو العباس شهاب الدين أحمد بن عبد الله بن أحمد الفزاري القلقشندي الشافعي; conventionally أحمد بن علي بن أحمد.

**Other forms and spellings:**

- Aḥmad al-Qalqashandī
- al-Qalqashandī / Qalqashandi / Qalqashandī
- al-Qarqashandī, reflecting an attested interchange of *lām* and *rāʾ*
- al-Miṣrī, “the Egyptian”
- al-Qāhirī, “the Cairene,” in some later catalogues
- al-Fazārī, from his claimed tribal affiliation
- al-Shāfiʿī, identifying his legal school
- Shihāb al-Dīn, honorific *laqab*
- Abū al-ʿAbbās, *kunya*
- “Ibn Abī Ghudda” or “Ibn Abī Ghidda” appears in some biographical traditions, but the better-documented bearer of this designation was his son Muḥammad; careless cataloguing can conflate father and son.

**Occupations:** Shāfiʿī jurist; teacher; hadith transmitter; chancery secretary; *kātib al-darj* (“scribe of the scroll”); deputy judge; notarial-court official; genealogist; historian; geographer; poet; prose stylist; compiler and encyclopedist.

**Birth:** 756 AH, conventionally 1355 CE. Some catalogues give 1355/56 because the Hijrī year overlaps two Common Era years. A modern Arabic notice incorrectly gives 765 AH; this appears to be a transposition and conflicts with the contemporary tradition and his age at death.

**Birthplace:** Qalqashanda or Qarqashanda, a village in Qalyūbiyya in the southern Nile Delta, north of Cairo.

**Death:** in Cairo, in Jumādā II 821 AH. Al-Maqrīzī specifies the night of Saturday, 10 Jumādā II, conventionally converted as 15 July 1418.

**Date problem:** Frédéric Bauden notes that 10 Jumādā II fell on a Friday; because the Islamic “night of Saturday” began after Friday sunset, al-Maqrīzī may technically have meant the night belonging to 11 Jumādā II. The conventional date remains 15 July 1418, with a possible one-day calendrical ambiguity.

**Age at death:** reported as sixty-five lunar years, consistent approximately with birth in 756 AH and death in 821 AH.

**Burial:** no securely documented burial place was found.

## Name and Paternal-Line Dispute

The standard name “Aḥmad ibn ʿAlī ibn Aḥmad” derives principally from Ibn Ḥajar al-ʿAsqalānī and was defended by al-Sakhāwī, who accused al-Maqrīzī and al-ʿAynī of error for calling the father ʿAbd Allāh.

Modern manuscript evidence favors **ʿAbd Allāh**:

1. Al-Maqrīzī, a contemporary who knew and corresponded with al-Qalqashandī, calls him Aḥmad ibn ʿAbd Allāh.
2. A presentation copy, possibly authorial, of *Maʾāthir al-ināfa* names him Aḥmad ibn ʿAbd Allāh al-Qalqashandī.
3. A presentation copy, probably an autograph, of *Ḍawʾ al-ṣubḥ* gives Aḥmad ibn ʿAbd Allāh ibn Aḥmad al-Fazārī al-Qalqashandī.
4. Al-Sakhāwī’s own biography of al-Qalqashandī’s son identifies the son as Muḥammad ibn Aḥmad ibn ʿAbd Allāh, contradicting al-Sakhāwī’s correction elsewhere.

**Assessment:** “Aḥmad ibn ʿAbd Allāh ibn Aḥmad” is the strongest reconstruction. “Aḥmad ibn ʿAlī ibn Aḥmad” remains the conventional catalogue form and must be retained as an important variant.

## Family and Ancestry

### Claimed ancestry

Biographical tradition calls him al-Fazārī and connects him to Fazāra, a branch of Dhubyān within Ghaṭafān and Qays ʿAylān. Some accounts specify descent through Badr ibn Fazāra.

**Evidentiary status:** documented as a medieval genealogical claim and social identification, not independently verifiable biological ancestry. His interest in Arab tribal genealogy and his production of genealogical manuals were consonant with this identity, but do not independently prove it.

One modern secondary text instead connects his family to Bakr ibn Wāʾil or Banū Badr. This conflicts with the more consistent Fazāra/Ghaṭafān tradition and may represent confusion among tribal names.

### Parents and siblings

- **Father:** probably ʿAbd Allāh ibn Aḥmad; conventionally but probably incorrectly called ʿAlī ibn Aḥmad.
- **Mother:** unknown.
- **Siblings:** none securely documented.

### Marriage and children

No wife or partner is identified in the consulted sources.

One son is securely documented:

**Najm al-Dīn Abū al-Faḍl Muḥammad ibn Aḥmad ibn ʿAbd Allāh al-Qalqashandī**, known as **Ibn Abī Ghudda**:

- born 796 AH/1394;
- served as a deputy judge, administrator of charitable-endowment properties, and secretary to several amirs;
- copied his father’s *Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā* at least twice—an eleven-volume copy for Nāṣir al-Dīn Muḥammad ibn Muḥammad Ibn al-Bārizī and a seven-volume copy for Badr al-Dīn Muḥammad ibn Muḥammad Ibn Muzhir;
- wrote or compiled works of his own;
- drowned during the Nile flood of 876 AH/1471.

His son’s manuscript activity was crucial to the transmission and early institutional reception of al-Qalqashandī’s encyclopedia.

No other children have been securely identified.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1355–c. 1370s

Al-Qalqashandī was born in Qalqashanda in 756 AH/1355. Later biographies describe the village as part of Qalyūbiyya, close to Cairo but within the southern Delta.

Reports that he memorized the Qurʾan and acquired elementary learning in his birthplace are plausible components of a conventional scholarly upbringing, but the surviving contemporary notices do not provide a detailed childhood narrative.

At a young age he moved to Alexandria. The precise year is unknown. Alexandria was both a port and a major center of Shāfiʿī legal and hadith study. There he studied religious and literary disciplines and eventually entered the service of its governor.

No evidence was located for foreign travel, pilgrimage, exile, military campaigning, enslavement, or imprisonment. His securely traceable geographical world is Qalqashanda, Alexandria, and Cairo.

## Education and Formation

### Subjects

Sources attribute to him study or mastery of:

- Qurʾanic and religious learning;
- Shāfiʿī jurisprudence;
- hadith and hadith transmission;
- Arabic grammar and lexicography;
- literature, poetry, rhetoric, and epistolography;
- history and geography;
- genealogy;
- legal formularies;
- arithmetic, practical geometry, and aspects of the natural sciences;
- philosophy, according to later reference literature.

The breadth of *Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā* should not automatically be read as evidence that he personally conducted original research in every subject it contains. Much of the work is an organized compilation from earlier authorities, documents, and bureaucratic practice.

### Ibn al-Mulaqqin

His best-documented teacher was the Shāfiʿī jurist and hadith scholar **Sirāj al-Dīn Abū Ḥafṣ ʿUmar ibn ʿAlī Ibn al-Mulaqqin**.

An authorization reproduced in *Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā* records that Ibn al-Mulaqqin licensed him in 778 AH, when al-Qalqashandī was approximately twenty-one or twenty-two, to:

- issue Shāfiʿī legal opinions;
- teach specified legal and hadith works;
- transmit hadith.

The authorization praises his sustained pursuit of learning and association with senior teachers. Its ceremonious language is partly formulaic, but the license itself is strong evidence of formal scholarly qualification.

Other teachers are mentioned inconsistently in later studies. The available sources establish an Alexandrian and Cairene scholarly formation but do not permit a complete teacher list.

### Teaching and legal activity

Later biographies say he taught, transmitted hadith, and issued legal opinions from approximately 778 AH onward. A common reconstruction gives about thirteen years between his authorization and entry into the chancery in 791 AH.

He memorized or closely mastered works in jurisprudence, grammar, and literature. Al-Maqrīzī remembered him as learned, a poet, and capable of discussing practical geometry.

---

## Early Career in Alexandria

A neglected notice by al-Maqrīzī records that al-Qalqashandī served **Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn Khalīl ibn ʿAlī ibn ʿArrām**, governor of Alexandria.

Ibn ʿArrām held the governorship at several intervals:

- before 766–767 AH/1365;
- 768–769/1367;
- 770–772/1369–71;
- 774–775/1373;
- 777–779/1376–78;
- again in 782/1380, when he was executed.

Because al-Qalqashandī was born in 756/1355, Bauden considers service during Ibn ʿArrām’s penultimate or final tenure—approximately 1376–80—the most reasonable reconstruction.

Al-Qalqashandī later mentioned the issue of gold coins in Alexandria during this period, perhaps reflecting direct observation while in Ibn ʿArrām’s administration.

His precise duties, title, and remuneration in Alexandria are unknown.

---

## Move to Cairo and Entry into the Mamluk Chancery

Al-Qalqashandī settled in Cairo by approximately 791 AH/1388–89. Through access to **Badr al-Dīn Muḥammad ibn Faḍl Allāh al-ʿUmarī**, chief secretary and a member of the celebrated al-ʿUmarī chancery family, he obtained a place in the sultanic **Dīwān al-Inshāʾ**.

He states in *Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā* that he was established in the chancery around 791 AH. His position was **kātib al-darj**, a scribe responsible for composing or copying documents on the rolled-paper format associated with the chancery.

The chancery handled:

- correspondence issued in the sultan’s name;
- communications with governors and officials;
- diplomatic correspondence with Muslim and non-Muslim rulers;
- decrees, appointments, grants, oaths, truces, safe-conducts, and petitions;
- the documentary conventions of titulature, paper, scripts, seals, spacing, and protocol.

### Salary dispute

Al-Maqrīzī says al-Qalqashandī initially worked **bi-ghayr maʿlūm**, without an assigned salary.

Al-Qalqashandī’s own autobiographical postscript to *al-Kawākib al-durriyya* depicts his entry into chancery employment as a solution to economic anxiety. He writes that he was torn between scholarly study and earning a living: reliance on scholarship for income seemed dishonorable, but neglecting income threatened hunger. Once established in Badr al-Dīn’s bureau, he says, he stopped seeking other earnings.

**Assessment:** the two sources can be reconciled if he began provisionally or without a fixed stipend and later received remunerated employment. The amount of any salary is unknown.

## Chronological Life History

### 1355–c. 1376: Delta upbringing and education

Born in Qalqashanda; moved young to Alexandria; studied Shāfiʿī law, hadith, Arabic, and literature.

### 778 AH/1376–77: Formal scholarly authorization

Received Ibn al-Mulaqqin’s authorization in law, teaching, and hadith transmission at about age twenty-one.

### c. 1376–80: Alexandrian government service

Probably served Governor Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn ibn ʿArrām during one of his last tenures. This is his earliest identified administrative employment.

### c. 1378–88: Teaching, legal work, and writing

The record is thin. Later accounts describe teaching, legal opinions, and composition. No exact institutional affiliation is securely documented.

### 791 AH/1388–89: Chancery appointment in Cairo

Joined the Mamluk sultanic chancery under Badr al-Dīn al-ʿUmarī during the reign of Sultan al-Ẓāhir Barqūq.

To mark or assist his entry, he composed *al-Kawākib al-durriyya fī al-manāqib al-Badriyya*, a *maqāma* praising Badr al-Dīn while presenting the intellectual qualifications of the ideal secretary.

### 1392: Sword-and-pen composition

When Sultan Barqūq appointed the *dawādār* Zayn al-Dīn Abū al-Ẓāhirī to an important office, al-Qalqashandī composed *Ḥilyat al-faḍl wa-zīnat al-karam fī al-mufākhara bayna al-sayf wa-l-qalam*, a literary disputation between sword and pen. He later incorporated it into *Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā*.

### 1390s–early 1400s: Chancery work and transition to judicial-notarial service

Traditional biographies often say he remained in the chancery until his death. Al-Maqrīzī’s chronologically arranged notice instead says that after chancery service he spent years as deputy to Chief Judge **Jalāl al-Dīn ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Bulqīnī** at one of the stations or offices of professional witnesses/notaries.

**Modern assessment:** he probably left regular chancery employment and entered deputy judicial/notarial service before his death. Whether he retained informal chancery ties is unknown.

### c. 1392–1412: Compilation of *Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā*

The work developed from his shorter chancery *maqāma* and from a suggestion—apparently by a patron—that he expand its principles into a comprehensive manual.

Later accounts call it a twenty-year undertaking. Its materials came from:

- his administrative experience;
- official documents and archival copies;
- earlier chancery manuals;
- historical, geographical, genealogical, legal, medical, and literary books;
- documents copied into bureaucratic or private collections.

A modern index identifies approximately 540 written sources. Especially important were:

- Ibn Faḍl Allāh al-ʿUmarī’s *al-Taʿrīf bi-l-muṣṭalaḥ al-sharīf* and *Masālik al-abṣār*;
- Ibn Nāẓir al-Jaysh’s *Tathqīf al-taʿrīf*;
- Abū al-Fidāʾ’s *Taqwīm al-buldān*;
- al-Samʿānī’s *al-Ansāb*;
- Ibn Sīnā’s *al-Qānūn fī al-ṭibb*;
- Ibn al-Durayhim’s material on codes and decipherment.

### 812 AH/1409: Genealogical manual

Completed *Nihāyat al-arab fī maʿrifat ansāb/qabāʾil al-ʿArab* and dedicated it to Amir Jamāl al-Dīn Yūsuf al-Qurashī.

### 28 Shawwāl 814/12 February 1412: Completion of *Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā*

The authorial completion notice gives Friday, 28 Shawwāl 814. The modern fourteen-volume form is a printing arrangement; medieval copies could occupy four, seven, or eleven manuscript volumes. Ibn Ḥajar’s report of four volumes may therefore describe a physical copy rather than the work’s modern extent, though Bauden considers it doubtful that Ibn Ḥajar had inspected a copy.

### 817 AH/1414: Early manuscript circulation

A seven-volume copy was made while al-Qalqashandī was alive, dated Muḥarram 817/March–April 1414. Incomplete portions survive in the Topkapı Palace Library, MS Ahmet III 2930.

The production of deluxe or commissioned copies for senior secretaries indicates that the work acquired institutional prestige during its author’s final years.

### 819 AH/1416: Later genealogy

A colophon dates *Qalāʾid al-jumān fī al-taʿrīf bi-qabāʾil ʿArab al-zamān* to 13 Rajab 819/6 September 1416. It supplements or reorganizes the earlier genealogical work and was presented to Abū al-Maḥāsin Muḥammad al-Juhanī al-Muʾayyadī.

### 819 AH/1416–17: Work on the caliphate

He prepared *Maʾāthir al-ināfa fī maʿālim al-khilāfa*, dedicated to the Cairo Abbasid caliph al-Muʿtaḍid bi-llāh. The work discusses the legal theory, ceremonies, titles, succession, symbols, and documentary practice of the caliphate.

### 814–821 AH/1412–18: Correspondence with al-Maqrīzī

Historian **Taqī al-Dīn al-Maqrīzī** wrote to al-Qalqashandī asking him to explain the chancery’s technical use of words derived from the root *r-s-m*, particularly *marsūm*, “decree.” Al-Qalqashandī replied with historical and linguistic hypotheses.

The letters establish:

- a personal scholarly relationship;
- al-Maqrīzī’s respect for al-Qalqashandī’s chancery expertise;
- al-Qalqashandī’s willingness to acknowledge uncertainty;
- their shared interest in language, documents, and administrative history.

Al-Maqrīzī also reports that al-Qalqashandī visited him frequently and took dictation from him.

### 20–29 Rabīʿ II 821/27 May–5 June 1418: Final dated work

He completed a two-volume abridgment of *Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā*, *Ḍawʾ al-ṣubḥ al-musfir wa-jany al-dawḥ al-muthmir*, at the request of Kamāl al-Dīn Muḥammad ibn Muḥammad Ibn al-Bārizī, son of the chief secretary Nāṣir al-Dīn Ibn al-Bārizī. Only the first volume is known to survive.

### Jumādā II 821/July 1418: Death

He died in Cairo only weeks after completing the abridgment. No illness, accident, or cause of death is recorded in the consulted sources.

## Institutions, Projects, Technology, and Financial Interests

### Dīwān al-Inshāʾ

His central institutional association was the Cairo sultanic chancery. He was a middle-ranking secretary, not the chief secretary or equivalent of a modern foreign minister.

His encyclopedia documents the chancery’s:

- office hierarchy;
- documentary genres;
- diplomatic titulature;
- layout and paper conventions;
- scripts and writing implements;
- procedures for appointments, grants, petitions, treaties, truces, oaths, and safe-conducts;
- postal routes, relays, signaling, and carrier pigeons.

### Judicial and notarial administration

After chancery employment, he reportedly acted for several years as a deputy judge in an office of court witnesses or professional notaries under Jalāl al-Dīn al-Bulqīnī.

Some later biographies also associate him with administration of charitable endowments. The clearest evidence for direct *waqf* administration concerns his son, so claims concerning the father should be treated cautiously.

### Communications technology

*Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā* describes:

- paper formats and their relationship to official rank;
- reed pens, inks, and scripts;
- courier and postal systems;
- carrier-pigeon networks and towers;
- beacon or fire signaling;
- coded writing and decipherment;
- calendrical conversion;
- practical measurement.

These are descriptions and compilations, not patented inventions. No patent system, proprietary company, investment enterprise, or independently owned technology is associated with him.

### Personal finances and property

His *maqāma* explicitly describes anxiety about subsistence before his appointment. Beyond this, no salary figure, estate inventory, real property, commercial venture, debt, investment, or probate record has been identified.

## Intellectual, Religious, and Literary Development

### Shāfiʿī legal identity

His formal qualification and legal writings place him firmly in the Shāfiʿī scholarly tradition. *Maʾāthir al-ināfa* reflects Sunni juristic theories of the caliphate and the ceremonial legitimacy of the Cairo Abbasids under Mamluk protection.

No evidence was located of conversion, sectarian change, heresy proceedings, Sufi initiation, or a personal theological crisis.

### The ideal secretary

Al-Qalqashandī presented chancery writing as an intellectual profession requiring encyclopedic preparation. A secretary should know:

- Arabic language, grammar, rhetoric, and literature;
- religious law and history;
- geography and routes;
- dynasties, offices, ranks, and titles;
- diplomatic precedent;
- document forms and material technologies.

His *al-Kawākib al-durriyya* is partly a defense of the profession and partly a strategic self-presentation to a patron. Modern readings differ over whether its primary object is professional theory or advancement of his own career; both functions can coexist.

### Knowledge and political authority

His encyclopedia organizes knowledge for state use. It preserves administrative classifications and the official hierarchies of a Mamluk chancery, including differentiated forms of address for rulers, officials, religious communities, and foreign powers.

It should therefore be read as both:

1. an archive-like preservation of otherwise lost material; and
2. a prescriptive representation of how the Mamluk state and its scribes wanted hierarchy and authority expressed.

It is not a neutral transcript of all medieval Egyptian social experience.

### Compilation and authorship

Much of *Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā* consists of quotation, excerpt, adaptation, classification, and documentary reproduction. This was a recognized mode of medieval encyclopedic authorship. He often named sources, but modern textual work is still required to distinguish:

- his original prose;
- inherited chancery formulas;
- documents copied from archives or collections;
- excerpts from earlier authors;
- later scribal interventions.

## Cryptography and Cryptanalysis

The fifth section of *Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā* includes an important treatment of secret writing. It describes or transmits:

- substitution ciphers;
- transposition procedures;
- multiple signs for a plaintext letter;
- changes of cipher alphabet;
- numerical and symbolic encipherment;
- decipherment through letter frequencies;
- attention to Arabic letter combinations and linguistic probabilities.

The principal identified source was **Tāj al-Dīn ʿAlī ibn Muḥammad Ibn al-Durayhim al-Mawṣilī** (died c. 1359–62), whose cryptological work al-Qalqashandī excerpted or summarized.

Al-Qalqashandī’s importance is largely **transmissive and systematizing**: he preserved a sophisticated Arabic cryptological tradition within a widely copied chancery encyclopedia. He should not be credited without qualification as inventor of frequency analysis, which is documented centuries earlier in al-Kindī.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Ibn al-Mulaqqin

**Documented:** teacher and licensor in Shāfiʿī jurisprudence, hadith, and teaching, 778 AH.

### Badr al-Dīn Muḥammad ibn Faḍl Allāh al-ʿUmarī

**Documented:** patron and superior who facilitated or presided over al-Qalqashandī’s entry into the chancery around 791 AH. Recipient and subject of *al-Kawākib al-durriyya*.

### Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn Khalīl ibn ʿAlī ibn ʿArrām

**Reported by al-Maqrīzī:** governor of Alexandria whom al-Qalqashandī served, probably c. 1376–80.

### Sultan al-Ẓāhir Barqūq

**Institutional relationship:** al-Qalqashandī entered the sultanic chancery during Barqūq’s reign. No evidence establishes personal intimacy or a private meeting.

### Jalāl al-Dīn ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Bulqīnī

**Reported fact:** chief judge under whom al-Qalqashandī served for years as deputy in a notarial or witnesses’ office.

### Taqī al-Dīn al-Maqrīzī

**Documented:** correspondent, scholarly acquaintance, source of personal observations, and probably the best-informed contemporary biographer. Al-Qalqashandī frequently visited him and took dictation. Their surviving exchange concerned chancery terminology.

### Nāṣir al-Dīn and Kamāl al-Dīn Ibn al-Bārizī

**Documented:** senior chancery family. Kamāl al-Dīn commissioned the final abridgment of *Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā*; al-Qalqashandī’s son made a copy of the complete work for Nāṣir al-Dīn.

### Badr al-Dīn Ibn Muzhir

**Documented through the son’s statement:** senior secretary for whose library the son copied *Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā*.

### Cairo Abbasid caliph al-Muʿtaḍid bi-llāh

**Documented dedication:** recipient of *Maʾāthir al-ināfa*. A personal audience is not established.

### Sultan al-Muʾayyad Shaykh

**Literary-political association:** subject of the attributed *Naẓm sīrat al-Muʾayyad*. The extent of any direct relationship is unclear.

### Rivals, enemies, and political factions

No personal enemy, literary feud, prosecution, or factional rivalry is securely documented. Al-Qalqashandī discusses competitive “testing” letters among secretaries, but no surviving evidence makes him a party to a personal campaign of this kind.

## Controversies, Allegations, and Contested Questions

### No documented criminal or judicial scandal

The dedicated adversarial search located no credible evidence that al-Qalqashandī was accused, investigated, charged, or convicted of fraud, corruption, theft, violence, espionage, money laundering, or another crime. No lawsuit involving him was located.

This absence should not be transformed into a claim that every aspect of his official service is known. Mamluk chancery employment necessarily involved politically sensitive and sometimes secret state correspondence, but that is an institutional function, not proof of misconduct or intelligence activity in the modern sense.

### Was he unpaid?

- **Al-Maqrīzī:** says he served as *kātib al-darj* without an assigned salary.
- **Al-Qalqashandī:** represents the post as ending his search for subsistence.

**Status:** unresolved; possibly unpaid at first and salaried later.

### Did he remain in the chancery until death?

- **Traditional version:** he served continuously from 791/1388–89 until 821/1418.
- **Al-Maqrīzī’s version:** chancery service was followed by years as a deputy judge.
- **Modern reassessment:** the second chronology is more likely because al-Maqrīzī knew him personally.

**Status:** disputed, with the later deputy-judgeship chronology better supported.

### Father’s name

As detailed above, the traditional “ibn ʿAlī” conflicts with contemporary manuscript and family evidence supporting “ibn ʿAbd Allāh.”

**Status:** traditional error probably corrected by modern manuscript research.

### Attribution confusion between father and son

Two similarly named works have been conflated:

- the father’s *Qalāʾid al-jumān fī al-taʿrīf bi-qabāʾil ʿArab al-zamān*, on tribes;
- the son’s *Qalāʾid al-jumān fī mukātabāt ahl al-zamān*, on correspondence.

An edition of the tribal *Qalāʾid* was at one point attributed to “ʿAlī ibn Aḥmad al-Qalqashandī,” adding further confusion.

Bauden also argues that a manuscript *Nihāyat al-arab fī maʿrifat ansāb al-ʿArab* produced under the son’s name is essentially a plagiarized copy of his father’s genealogical book.

**Status:** documented bibliographical confusion; the plagiarism judgment concerns the son’s reuse of the father, not misconduct by Aḥmad himself.

### *Sabāʾik al-dhahab*

Some modern Arabic reference texts list *Sabāʾik al-dhahab fī maʿrifat qabāʾil al-ʿArab* as al-Qalqashandī’s work. It is better identified as a later continuation or derivative work by Muḥammad Amīn al-Suwaydī, carried down to 1814.

**Status:** later misattribution.

### *Kunh al-murād fī sharḥ Bānat Suʿād*

A commentary on Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr’s *Bānat Suʿād* is attributed to al-Qalqashandī in some lists.

**Status:** disputed attribution; the TDV encyclopedia considers his authorship doubtful.

### Accuracy and dependence on earlier sources

Modern scholarship values *Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā* but cautions that it:

- incorporates large amounts of earlier writing;
- sometimes describes past offices and protocols rather than contemporary practice;
- preserves ideal prescriptions that surviving documents do not always confirm;
- contains information on distant regions obtained indirectly;
- must be checked against manuscripts and actual documents.

A 2019 review of a modern English selection criticized its editors—not al-Qalqashandī—for relying on the early printed edition without consulting manuscripts, using outdated identifications, and insufficiently comparing his work with contemporary chancery manuals.

### Social and confessional hierarchy

The encyclopedia records differentiated and sometimes derogatory or triumphalist formulas concerning non-Muslim rulers, Christian and Jewish dignitaries, protected communities, warfare, and state supremacy.

**Status:** documented content. It reflects medieval chancery ideology, inherited legal categories, quoted documents, and authorial framing in varying proportions. Each passage requires source-level analysis before being treated as a personal statement by al-Qalqashandī.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Propaganda

### “The first cryptologist” or author of the first cryptology book

**Story:** al-Qalqashandī invented cryptanalysis or wrote the first work combining cryptography and codebreaking.

**Origin and circulation:** strengthened in twentieth-century histories of cryptography, particularly when the earlier works on which his chapter depended were considered lost.

**Evidence against:** his section explicitly relies substantially on Ibn al-Durayhim. Rediscovery and study of al-Kindī’s ninth-century cryptanalytic treatise established a substantially earlier written tradition of frequency analysis.

**Evidence for a narrower claim:** *Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā* is one of the most important surviving medieval compilations that combines cipher construction, decipherment, linguistic analysis, and chancery context.

**Reputational effect:** the inflated claim made him a symbolic “father” of cryptology; the corrected account emphasizes preservation and systematization rather than first invention.

### “Fourteen-volume medieval encyclopedia”

**Story:** al-Qalqashandī wrote the work as fourteen volumes.

**Correction:** fourteen is the arrangement of the standard modern Cairo edition. Medieval copies were organized in different physical numbers of volumes, including seven and eleven; Ibn Ḥajar reported four.

**Reputational effect:** the phrase accurately conveys scale but can misleadingly project the printed edition’s binding structure backward.

### “The Dawn of the Blind”

The title *Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā* is often rendered literally as “Dawn of the Blind.” The Arabic *aʿshā* more specifically suggests one who sees poorly at night or suffers night blindness. Translations such as “Daybreak for the night-blind” better preserve the wordplay: the encyclopedia supplies illumination to a secretary unable to navigate the craft.

### “Secret archive of the Mamluk state”

Popular accounts sometimes imply that he reproduced the contents of a centralized, modern-style state archive to which he had unrestricted access.

**Evidence:** chancery work gave him access to administrative materials, precedents, and document collections, and he preserved hundreds of documents.

**Qualification:** the institutional history of Mamluk record preservation was unlike a modern national archive. Some materials may have come from exemplars, personal notebooks, earlier manuals, and private collections. “The Mamluk secret archive” is an oversimplification.

### Idealized polymath

Later notices call him a mathematician, natural scientist, geographer, historian, jurist, and cryptographer. These descriptions reflect the scope of his compilation.

**Qualification:** secure expertise exists in law, Arabic letters, chancery practice, genealogy, and documentary organization. His inclusion of medicine, zoology, mineralogy, mathematics, or astronomy does not by itself prove original specialization in all those fields.

### The geometry anecdote

Al-Maqrīzī reports that al-Qalqashandī explained how to measure the height of a palm, minaret, or mountain using a wooden stick and proportional geometry.

**Status:** contemporaneously reported anecdote, credible but known through one witness. It contributed to his later image as a mathematician or practical scientist.

### “Great talker and babbler”

Al-Maqrīzī called him much given to talking or prattling after recording his learning and frequent visits.

**Status:** contemporaneous personal characterization, not a diagnosis or formal condemnation. It is the rare unflattering personal detail preserved about him.

## Works by Ahmad al-Qalqashandi

### Secure or substantially secure works

1. **نهاية الأرب في معرفة أنساب/قبائل العرب — *Nihāyat al-arab fī maʿrifat ansāb/qabāʾil al-ʿArab***  
   Completed 812 AH/1409; dedicated to Jamāl al-Dīn Yūsuf al-Qurashī. An alphabetically organized guide to Arab tribes, preceded by material on genealogy, early Arab history, and *ayyām al-ʿArab*. Printed in Baghdad in 1332 AH; edited by Ibrāhīm al-Abyārī, Cairo, 1959; later Beirut editions. Not to be confused with the anonymous Persian-Arab historical work bearing a similar title.

2. **الكواكب الدرية في المناقب البدرية — *al-Kawākib al-durriyya fī al-manāqib al-Badriyya***  
   Composed around 791 AH/1388–89 for Badr al-Dīn ibn Faḍl Allāh al-ʿUmarī. A *maqāma* praising its patron and defending the knowledge and status of chancery secretaries. Incorporated into *Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā*, volume XIV of the standard edition.

3. **حلية الفضل وزينة الكرم في المفاخرة بين السيف والقلم — *Ḥilyat al-faḍl wa-zīnat al-karam fī al-mufākhara bayna al-sayf wa-l-qalam***  
   Written in connection with an appointment made by Sultan Barqūq in 1392. Literary contest between sword and pen. Survives separately and in *Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā*.

4. **صبح الأعشى في كتابة الإنشا — *Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā fī kitābat al-inshāʾ***  
   Author’s preferred title, completed 28 Shawwāl 814/12 February 1412. More commonly printed as **صبح الأعشى في صناعة الإنشا — *Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā fī ṣināʿat al-inshāʾ***.  
   A vast encyclopedia of chancery writing, political geography, administrative history, offices, titles, diplomatic protocol, documents, genealogy, scripts, paper, postal systems, codes, chronology, and the intellectual formation of secretaries. Standard Cairo edition, 1913–22, fourteen volumes; repeatedly reprinted. A 1484 complete manuscript underlies part of the editorial tradition.

5. **قلائد الجمان في التعريف بقبائل عرب الزمان — *Qalāʾid al-jumān fī al-taʿrīf bi-qabāʾil ʿArab al-zamān***  
   Completed 13 Rajab 819/6 September 1416; presented to Abū al-Maḥāsin Muḥammad al-Juhanī al-Muʾayyadī. Supplement or companion to *Nihāyat al-arab*. Edited by Ibrāhīm al-Abyārī, though the printed attribution has generated confusion.

6. **مآثر الإنافة في معالم الخلافة — *Maʾāthir al-ināfa fī maʿālim al-khilāfa***  
   Completed or substantially composed in 819 AH/1416–17 and dedicated to the Cairo Abbasid caliph al-Muʿtaḍid bi-llāh. Treats the meaning and conditions of the caliphate, succession, allegiance, regalia, ceremonies, titles, and correspondence. Edited by ʿAbd al-Sattār Aḥmad Farrāj in three volumes, Kuwait, 1964; later Beirut printing.

7. **ضوء الصبح المسفر وجنى الدوح المثمر — *Ḍawʾ al-ṣubḥ al-musfir wa-jany al-dawḥ al-muthmir***  
   Two-volume abridgment of *Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā*, completed during 20–29 Rabīʿ II 821/27 May–5 June 1418 for Kamāl al-Dīn Ibn al-Bārizī. Only the first volume is known to survive. Cairo edition, 1906; later reprint and edition.

8. **قصيدة في مدح النبي — *Qaṣīda fī madḥ al-nabī***  
   Poem in praise of the Prophet. Printed in Alexandria in 1288 AH/1871–72. Precise date of composition unknown.

9. **نظم سيرة المؤيد — *Naẓm sīrat al-Muʾayyad***  
   Verse treatment of Sultan al-Muʾayyad Shaykh. Listed by major bibliographical authorities, but its manuscript state, completeness, and date require further investigation.

10. **Commentary on *al-Jāmiʿ al-mukhtaṣar***  
    Commentary on Kamāl al-Dīn al-Mudlijī’s Shāfiʿī legal work. Some sources call it *al-Ghuyūth al-hawāmiʿ fī sharḥ Jāmiʿ al-mukhtaṣarāt*. Publication and manuscript status are not fully resolved.

11. **Commentary on *al-Ḥāwī al-ṣaghīr***  
    Commentary on ʿAbd al-Ghaffār al-Qazwīnī’s Shāfiʿī legal compendium. Manuscript and title details need specialist verification.

12. **Letters, licenses, commendations, poems, and chancery compositions**  
    Numerous specimens are embedded in *Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā*. Individual authorship must be checked because the encyclopedia also quotes earlier writers and anonymous state documents.

### Disputed, confused, or wrongly assigned works

- **كنه المراد في شرح بانت سعاد — *Kunh al-murād fī sharḥ Bānat Suʿād***: attribution doubtful.
- **سبائك الذهب في معرفة قبائل العرب — *Sabāʾik al-dhahab fī maʿrifat qabāʾil al-ʿArab***: later work by Muḥammad Amīn al-Suwaydī, not securely al-Qalqashandī’s.
- **قلائد الجمان في مكاتبات أهل الزمان — *Qalāʾid al-jumān fī mukātabāt ahl al-zamān***: by his son Muḥammad, not Aḥmad; distinct from Aḥmad’s tribal *Qalāʾid*.
- A *Nihāyat al-arab* circulating under the son’s authorship appears to reproduce the father’s work extensively.

## Structure and Documentary Importance of *Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā*

The standard work consists of a preface, ten major *maqālāt*, and a conclusion. Its approximately 6,000–6,500 printed pages treat:

- the virtues and deficiencies of scribes;
- meaning and history of *inshāʾ*;
- qualifications of the secretary;
- Arabic language, rhetoric, literature, law, and history;
- peoples, tribes, dynasties, religions, animals, minerals, perfumes, astronomy, calendars, and natural phenomena;
- political geography, roads, Egypt, Syria, the Islamic world, Byzantium, and Europe;
- offices, ranks, honorifics, and forms of address;
- scripts, pens, inks, paper, spacing, and documentary layout;
- letters between rulers and officials;
- appointments, investitures, grants, fiefs, oaths, truces, treaties, and safe-conducts;
- secret writing and decipherment;
- literary letters, questions and answers, licenses, certificates, and panegyrics;
- postal routes, couriers, carrier pigeons, beacons, and transport.

Its principal historical value lies in preserving copies or models of documents that would otherwise be lost. Its limitations are those of a prescriptive manual and compilation: chronology, provenance, and correspondence with actual practice must be checked case by case.

## Death and Immediate Afterlife

Al-Qalqashandī died in Cairo in July 1418, shortly after finishing *Ḍawʾ al-ṣubḥ*. No cause is recorded.

Al-Maqrīzī’s exact-date notice is the strongest testimony because he knew him. Ibn Ḥajar, al-ʿAynī, and al-Ṣayrafī give only the month; Ibn Taghrī Birdī and al-Sakhāwī appear dependent on al-Maqrīzī for greater precision.

His son’s copying activity and the commissioning of copies by senior chancery officials demonstrate immediate posthumous circulation. The work remained useful to secretaries, historians, geographers, jurists, and genealogists even as administrative practice changed.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Administrative and diplomatic history

*Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā* is one of the principal sources for Mamluk state terminology, offices, diplomacy, documentary form, and political geography. Its preservation of texts often compensates for the loss of original chancery documents.

### History of information organization

Its nested classifications, numerous headings, and systematic ordering make it a major example of late-medieval Arabic encyclopedism. The modern printed text occupies roughly fourteen volumes and thousands of pages.

### Cryptological history

The cipher chapter transmitted techniques associated with Ibn al-Durayhim and earlier Arabic cryptanalysis. Al-Qalqashandī’s modern fame in computing and cryptography often exceeds his medieval reputation, sometimes at the cost of attributing inherited methods to him as inventions.

### Genealogy

His tribal dictionaries influenced later genealogical writing. Al-Suyūṭī abridged the tribal *Qalāʾid*, and al-Suwaydī extended the tradition in *Sabāʾik al-dhahab*.

### Geography and interregional history

Researchers use him for Egypt, Syria, Nubia, Ethiopia, the Maghreb, India, Byzantium, Europe, postal roads, and relations between Muslim and Christian powers. For distant regions, his evidence must be separated into observation, official intelligence, inherited geography, and book-based compilation.

### Documentary criticism

Recent scholarship increasingly compares his prescriptions with surviving deeds and manuscripts. This has corrected assumptions about paper dimensions, archival organization, titles, authorship, and even his father’s name and professional chronology.

### Reputation as a person

The posthumous image is dominated by the vastness of his encyclopedia. The surviving evidence supports a more specific portrait:

- a Delta-born Shāfiʿī scholar;
- educated and initially employed in Alexandria;
- economically concerned with securing a viable profession;
- admitted to the Cairo chancery through patronage;
- later active in judicial-notarial work;
- exceptionally skilled in organizing inherited knowledge and documentary precedent;
- socially connected to al-Maqrīzī and leading secretarial families;
- remembered by that friend as learned, technically curious, and excessively talkative.

## Atlas Connections

The 896-person roster was checked for supported personal, textual, or institutional connections. Only one strong connection was found.

### Tāj al-Dīn ʿAlī ibn Muḥammad Ibn al-Durayhim al-Mawṣilī

**Evidentiary label: documented textual influence.**

Al-Qalqashandī’s section on codes and decipherment draws substantially on cryptological material by Ibn al-Durayhim. Ibn al-Durayhim died before al-Qalqashandī reached adulthood, so no meeting or teacher–student relationship is documented. The connection is transmission through texts: al-Qalqashandī preserved and reorganized Ibn al-Durayhim’s cipher systems and decipherment procedures in *Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā*.

### Other roster names

No documented meeting, correspondence, collaboration, patronage, rivalry, family tie, or explicitly acknowledged direct influence was established with the other listed atlas figures.

In particular:

- **Abū ʿAbd Allāh Muḥammad Ibn Baṭṭūṭa:** both are important sources for fourteenth-century geography, but no direct encounter or demonstrated textual dependence was found.
- **Abū Yūsuf Yaʿqūb ibn Isḥāq al-Kindī:** al-Kindī is essential to correcting modern “first cryptanalyst” claims, but the consulted evidence does not establish that al-Qalqashandī directly used or knew al-Kindī’s cryptological treatise.
- **Aḥmad al-Majrīṭī / Maslama al-Majrīṭī:** no supported personal or textual connection was found.
- Shared treatment of ancient, biblical, Islamic, or political figures in an encyclopedia is not by itself an atlas connection.

Absence of additional connections is therefore the documentary finding.
