## Works by Hermann Keyserling

Keyserling's bibliography resists the standard "book with a date" treatment. His signature form was the philosophical travel diary, a genre he effectively made his own: not reportage and not a conventional treatise, but a first-person journey transmuted into metaphysical speculation, where a place becomes an occasion for reflection on consciousness and self-transformation rather than an object of description. His School of Wisdom, meanwhile, produced less a shelf of authored books than an ongoing publishing operation — yearbooks and periodicals built from the voices of many contributors under his editorship. Both facts matter more than any list of titles.

### The travel diaries

**Das Reisetagebuch eines Philosophen / The Travel Diary of a Philosopher (1919)** is Keyserling's major and best-known work, built from his roughly year-long round-the-world journey of 1911–1912 through the Mediterranean and Suez, Ceylon, India, China, Japan, and North America. Delayed into print by the war, it became a Weimar-era bestseller, reportedly reaching some 50,000 copies — unusual for a two-volume work of philosophy (Wikipedia, English and German editions: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Graf_Keyserling and https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Graf_Keyserling). Keyserling's own motto for the book, "the shortest path to oneself leads around the world," states the genre's logic plainly: geography is instrumental to self-realization, not the subject. The book is frequently mistaken for travel journalism; it is better read as philosophical literature in which countries and peoples become occasions for speculation on culture, race, and religion — a method open to real intercultural insight and, by the same token, to sweeping essentialist typology. The diary includes his meeting with Rabindranath Tagore in Calcutta (before Tagore's 1913 Nobel Prize) and with the sinologist Richard Wilhelm in Qingdao, both relationships that later fed directly into the School of Wisdom.

**Südamerikanische Meditationen / South American Meditations (1932)** grew out of his 1929 South American tour — Argentina, Uruguay, Brazil, and an honorary doctorate from the University of La Paz — and is the work of direct relevance to the atlas. It is not a travel narrative in the mode of the 1919 diary but a set of philosophical meditations organized around a concept Keyserling coined for the continent: **Gana**, an irrational, "telluric" impulse that binds a person to raw, bodily nature, which he opposed to an "irruption of spirit" capable of transcending that earthboundness (German Wikipedia: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Graf_Keyserling). The book is the clearest instance of his recurring move — turning a journey and the people met on it into symbolic material for a metaphysical scheme — and it is inseparable from his fraught relationship with Victoria Ocampo, who had invited him to Argentina and who later published her own counter-account of the visit. Read against Ocampo's record, South American Meditations is as much a document of Keyserling's projections onto the continent as it is a philosophy of it.

**America Set Free (1929)**, published in German as *Amerika. Der Aufgang einer neuen Welt* (1930), is the equivalent diary-essay for his North American lecture tours, reading the United States as a new, pragmatic, history-unburdened human type.

### The School of Wisdom and its publications

Keyserling founded the **Schule der Weisheit** (School of Wisdom) in Darmstadt in 1920, with its legal/financial body the Gesellschaft für Freie Philosophie. It was not a university but a recurring lecture forum and publishing venture, deliberately "polyphonic": speakers from different disciplines addressing a shared question rather than one doctrine being taught. Its two running serials were:

- ***Der Leuchter* (1919 onward)** — a yearbook disseminating the School's outlook, with contributions from the wide roster of speakers Keyserling assembled (Jung, Richard Wilhelm, Leo Baeck, Hans Driesch, Leopold Ziegler, Nikolai Berdyaev, among others). One volume, *Mensch und Erde* (1927), appeared as *Der Leuchter* no. 8 — an edited collection, not a Keyserling monograph.
- ***Der Weg zur Vollendung* (1920–c.1942)** — the School's own periodical of "communications," running for decades even as the institution's public conferences were curtailed under National Socialist censorship after 1933 (German Wikipedia, as above).

As editor rather than sole author, Keyserling also produced ***Schöpferische Erkenntnis* (1922)**, subtitled an introduction to the School of Wisdom, and ***Das Ehebuch* (1925)**, a multi-author volume on marriage gathering essays from two dozen contemporaries, later translated as *The Book of Marriage*. These are jewels of a different kind than his own authored books: Keyserling's real product in Darmstadt was often the forum itself, with the printed yearbook as its residue.

### Other philosophical writings

Keyserling's purely philosophical monographs run from *Das Gefüge der Welt* (1906), written in Paris and engaging Kant and Bergson, through *Philosophie als Kunst* (1920) — a title that states his guiding conviction that philosophy is a mode of creative self-formation rather than a system of demonstrated propositions — to **Das Spektrum Europas / Europe (1928)**, his comparative typology of European national characters, and the late, unfinished ***Das Buch vom Ursprung* (published posthumously, 1947)**, which he considered his principal philosophical statement. His memoir cycle *Reise durch die Zeit* (begun 1937, published in three volumes 1948–1963) extends the travel-diary habit of mind to time itself, treating his own life as the terrain being journeyed through.
