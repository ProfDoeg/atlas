# Mircea Eliade: Research Dossier

**Prepared:** 24 August 2026  
**Scope:** Documentary life history, intellectual and political record, Eranos/Ascona network, works, controversies, myths, and legacy. Dates follow the Gregorian calendar unless otherwise stated.

Eliade did not normally publish under a stable pseudonym. His name itself was inherited from his father’s change of surname from **Ieremia** to **Eliade**, reportedly in admiration of the nineteenth-century writer Ion Heliade Rădulescu. Fictional narrators such as Allan in *Maitreyi* are not pseudonymous documentary selves, though their autobiographical basis is substantial.

## Family and Ancestry

- **Father:** Gheorghe Eliade, born Gheorghe Ieremia, an officer in the Romanian army, Orthodox and nationalist in outlook. His postings caused the family to move between Bucharest, Râmnicu Sărat and Tecuci.
- **Mother:** Jeana (Ioana) Eliade, née Vasilescu, from a Moldavian family. Eliade’s autobiographical recollections associate her with domestic religiosity and storytelling.
- **Siblings:** Brother **Nicu/Nicolae** and sister **Corina**. Corina later became the mother of literary scholar and semiotician **Sorin Alexandrescu**, an important editor and interpreter of Eliade.
- **First wife:** **Nina Mareș** (often Nina Eliade), married in 1934. She had a daughter, **Adalgiza (“Giza”)**, from an earlier marriage; Eliade treated/adopted Giza as his daughter. Nina died of uterine cancer in Lisbon on 20 November 1944. Her illness, wartime isolation and death produced a prolonged crisis recorded in Eliade’s journals and memoirs.
- **Second wife:** **Christinel Cotescu** (1909–1998), a Romanian exile from a boyar family and sister-in-law of conductor Ionel Perlea. They married in Paris in 1950 (some summaries give 1948; the archival chronology favors 1950). She became his principal household partner, translator, secretary and guardian of papers.
- **Children:** No documented biological children. Adalgiza/Giza was his stepdaughter/adopted daughter.

Claims that Eliade descended from a distinguished old “Eliade” line should be treated cautiously: the surname was his father’s adopted name, not proof of direct descent from Heliade Rădulescu.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1907–1924

Eliade was born in Bucharest during the reign of Carol I. His childhood included periods at **Râmnicu Sărat** and **Tecuci** before the family settled in Bucharest in 1914, eventually at 1 Melodiei Street near Piața Rosetti. He attended primary school on Mântuleasa Street—the urban geography later mythologized in *Pe strada Mântuleasa*—and the **Spiru Haret High School**.

The First World War supplied early memories of German air raids, occupation, displaced family life and Romanian patriotic mobilization. His later autobiography makes childhood a laboratory of hierophany: most famous is the “golden room,” a drawing room suffused with green-gold light, remembered as an eruption of another mode of being. The experience is autobiographical testimony, not independently verifiable; it became a foundational self-myth used in his fiction, memoir and theory of the sacred.

As an adolescent he collected insects, read chemistry and natural history, practiced scouting, mountaineering and sailing, and wrote popular-scientific pieces. His first printed article, on the enemy of the silkworm, appeared in 1921; “How I Found the Philosopher’s Stone” followed. He read Balzac, Papini, Frazer, Hasdeu, Iorga, Unamuno, Gide and occult/theosophical literature. He cultivated will through reduced sleep, physical tests and intense schedules—practices he later narrated as a campaign against biological limitation and weak eyesight. The heroic tone is part documentation, part retrospective literary construction.

He drafted the autobiographical **Romanul adolescentului miop** (*Novel/Diary of a Short-Sighted Adolescent*) and its university sequel **Gaudeamus** in the 1920s. Neither appeared as a book during his lifetime; their posthumous publication demonstrates the unusually early formation of his themes: self-making, encyclopedic ambition, sexuality, knowledge and escape from historical time.

## Education and Formation, 1925–1932

### University of Bucharest

From 1925 Eliade studied philosophy and letters at the **University of Bucharest**. His licensing thesis concerned Renaissance philosophy from Marsilio Ficino to Giordano Bruno, with special attention to Tommaso Campanella. The decisive teacher was the logician, metaphysician and newspaper editor **Nae Ionescu**. Around Ionescu gathered the “young generation” later associated with *trăirism*—an existential philosophy of lived experience—and, for several members, authoritarian politics.

Eliade wrote for *Cuvântul*, traveled to Italy in 1927 and 1928, interviewed or corresponded with Giovanni Papini, and met the Tibetologist **Giuseppe Tucci**. Tucci became a long-term scholarly contact: both sought large morphological comparisons across Asian religions; both later participated in Eranos; surviving correspondence, partly published in Romania, records exchange of books, scholarly information and institutional contacts. They should not be collapsed into a master-pupil relationship: Eliade’s formal Indian training was with Dasgupta, while Tucci was a colleague and gatekeeper in European Orientalist networks.

### India, 1928–1931

With a stipend from the Maharaja of Kassimbazar, Eliade sailed to British India in late 1928. In **Calcutta** he studied Sanskrit and Indian philosophy under **Surendranath Dasgupta**, author of *A History of Indian Philosophy*, and for a period lived in the Dasgupta household at 3 Bakul Bagan Road. He attended the University of Calcutta, traveled in Bengal and northern India, visited temples and ashrams, and spent months in **Rishikesh** at Swami Shivananda’s ashram studying yoga practice.

His relationship with Dasgupta’s daughter **Maitreyi Devi** is one of the best-documented disputes between lived event and literary myth. Eliade’s 1933 novel *Maitreyi* presented an intimate and ultimately sexual love affair between “Allan” and the young Bengali woman. Devi said there had been affection but denied the consummated affair and objected that Eliade had exposed her under a transparent fictional disguise without consent. After learning of the novel decades later, she answered with *Na Hanyate* (1974; English *It Does Not Die*, 1976/1994) and confronted Eliade in Chicago in 1973. The evidence establishes emotional attachment and expulsion from the household; the sexual details rest chiefly on Eliade’s novel and later statements and remain disputed. The novel made the story famous in Romania and France; Devi’s counter-narrative transformed it into a case about Orientalism, gender, privacy and unequal control of publication.

Eliade returned to Romania at the end of 1931, completed compulsory military service, and prepared a doctorate on yoga. His Indian experience furnished scholarly authority, literary capital and the vocabulary—initiation, freedom, techniques of the body, escape from conditioned existence—that structured his career.

## Early Career and Bucharest Public Life, 1932–1937

Eliade received his doctorate from the University of Bucharest in 1933 for **Yoga: Essai sur les origines de la mystique indienne**, derived from *Yoga. Psihologia meditației indiene*. He served as an unpaid/honorary assistant to Nae Ionescu and lectured on metaphysics and religious history. He did not secure a stable conventional chair; journalism, royalties, lectures and teaching supplied income.

He became a leading member of **Criterion**, a heterogeneous Bucharest forum that included Petru Comarnescu, Mihail Sebastian, Emil Cioran, Constantin Noica, Eugène Ionesco, Arșavir and Haig Acterian. Criterion staged public symposia on Lenin, Freud, Gandhi, Chaplin, Mussolini and modern culture. Far-right students disrupted meetings and deployed antisemitic abuse. In 1933 Eliade signed a protest against Nazi racial policy; in 1934 he challenged Nae Ionescu’s theological antisemitism in the notorious preface to Sebastian’s *De două mii de ani*. These facts document that Eliade’s movement toward the Legion was neither automatic nor ideologically uniform.

His literary breakthrough was **Maitreyi** (1933), winner of a prize and a major Romanian success. Other work—*Isabel și apele diavolului*, *Întoarcerea din rai*, *Lumina ce se stinge*, *Huliganii*, *Șantier*, *Solilocvii*, *Oceanografie*—made him a visible novelist and essayist. His early prose repeatedly joined erotic experiment, ascetic self-fashioning and the “new man.”

Mihail Sebastian introduced him to Nina Mareș; the marriage coexisted with documented or claimed relationships with other women, including actress **Sorana Țopa**. Eliade’s private journals and fiction frequently converted relationships into literary material, complicating efforts to separate confession from invention.

## Chronological Life History

### 1937–1938: Legionary commitment, election work and detention

By 1937 Eliade publicly supported the **Legionary Movement/Iron Guard**, Corneliu Zelea Codreanu’s ultranationalist, Orthodox-mystical, antisemitic and violent movement. In articles for *Buna Vestire*, *Sfarmă-Piatră* and related venues he praised the Legion as a “Christian revolution,” celebrated Codreanu and the dead Legionaries Ion Moța and Vasile Marin, attacked parliamentary democracy, and imagined national regeneration through sacrifice and a religiously renewed elite. A party list and campaign evidence associate him with **Totul pentru Țară** (Everything for the Country), the Legion’s electoral party, in Prahova during the December 1937 election.

The documentary minimum is therefore stronger than “youthful sympathy”: public advocacy, party identification and campaign activity are established. Uncertainty remains over formal initiation, rank and duration. Eliade later minimized or omitted much of this record; defenders have emphasized the spiritual rather than racial language of his support, while critics note that the Legion’s antisemitism and political violence were public and constitutive.

After King Carol II imposed royal dictatorship and suppressed the Guard, Eliade was arrested on **14 July 1938** and held first at Siguranța headquarters and then in the **Miercurea-Ciuc** camp, where Nae Ionescu and other Legionary-associated intellectuals were detained. Accounts say release was offered in exchange for a declaration dissociating himself from the Guard; Eliade refused or delayed, then was released in November because of illness (often described as hemoptysis/tuberculosis risk). The precise wording and whether he ultimately signed any declaration are not securely established in the readily accessible record. He recuperated at Moroeni.

### 1939–1945: War, diplomacy, Portugal and bereavement

In April 1940, through the nationalist government’s diplomatic network, Eliade became cultural press attaché at the Romanian Legation in **London**. When Britain broke relations with Axis-aligned Romania in February 1941, he was transferred to **Lisbon**, serving under ministers including the Legion-linked Victor Cădere and later continuing under Marshal Ion Antonescu’s regime.

Portugal was neutral but authoritarian. Eliade admired elements of **António de Oliveira Salazar’s Estado Novo**, wrote the propagandistic/interpretive *Salazar și revoluția în Portugalia* (1942), and in *Românii, latinii Orientului* (1943) presented Romania to a Portuguese public. He conducted cultural diplomacy, monitored press, promoted Romanian interests, lectured and maintained contacts in Spain and Portugal. This was official employment by a government allied to Nazi Germany; it is not by itself proof that he was a German intelligence officer.

In Lisbon he kept a Portuguese journal that records hope for an Axis defeat of Soviet Russia, Romanian nationalism, despair after Stalingrad, work plans, erotic episodes and Nina’s terminal illness. Nina died there in November 1944. Eliade experienced suicidal thoughts and severe grief, while continuing work on *Prolegomena to the History of Religions* and fiction. Romania changed sides in August 1944; the postwar communist takeover made return increasingly dangerous for a former Legionary sympathizer and Antonescu-era diplomat.

In 1945 Eliade left Portugal for **Paris**, accompanied for part of the transition by Giza. He never again lived in Romania.

### 1945–1956: Parisian exile and reconstruction of a career

Postwar Paris was financially precarious. **Georges Dumézil** was crucial: he arranged lectures at the École pratique des hautes études and wrote a preface for the 1949 *Traité d’histoire des religions*. Eliade taught without a secure full professorship, translated/recast Romanian research into French and worked long hours. Contacts included Dumézil, **Henry Corbin**, Louis Massignon, Jean Daniélou, Georges Bataille, Paul Ricoeur, René Guénon’s circle at a distance, Emil Cioran, Eugène Ionesco, Vintilă Horia and other Romanian exiles.

Major works established the postwar Eliade: *Techniques du Yoga* (1948), *Traité d’histoire des religions* and *Le Mythe de l’éternel retour* (1949), *Le Chamanisme* (1951), *Images et symboles* (1952), *Yoga: Immortalité et liberté* and *Forgerons et alchimistes* (1956). He reframed himself as a comparative historian of religions, rarely discussing his 1930s politics in public.

He married Christinel Cotescu and traveled repeatedly in Europe. Royalties and lectures gradually improved the household’s position, but early correspondence records shortages and dependence on friends, publishers and fellowships. Ananda Coomaraswamy explored an American teaching position for him before dying in 1947.

### Eranos, Fröbe-Kapteyn, Ascona, Jung and Tucci

**Olga Fröbe-Kapteyn** founded the Eranos meetings at Casa Gabriella, Moscia near **Ascona**, on Lake Maggiore, in 1933. Henry Corbin probably recommended Eliade; Fröbe-Kapteyn’s direct invitation is dated **5 October 1949**. Eliade first attended in 1950 and returned repeatedly through the 1960s.

Eranos mattered materially and intellectually. At Ascona he met or renewed contact with **C. G. Jung, Fröbe-Kapteyn, Corbin, Gershom Scholem, Karl Kerényi, Adolf Portmann, Paul Radin, Gilles Quispel, Jean Gebser** and **Giuseppe Tucci**. Lectures appeared in the *Eranos-Jahrbuch*. The meetings created a transdisciplinary vocabulary of archetype, symbol, myth, Gnosis and spiritual anthropology, outside stricter disciplinary boundaries.

Fröbe-Kapteyn was organizer, hostess, editor, patron and network broker, not simply Jung’s assistant. Her letters connected Eliade to invitations and the **Bollingen Foundation**, whose fellowship support aided his research. Eliade’s diary later called Eranos one of modern Western culture’s most creative experiences and noted that Ascona was where he met **Joachim Wach**, whose invitation opened Chicago. This was accurate network memory, though it also formed Eliade’s self-myth of providential Ascona.

Eliade admired Jung but insisted on differences: Jung interpreted archetypes psychologically, whereas Eliade treated religious symbols as historically manifested structures irreducible to individual psychology. Letters from 1953–55 show Eliade apologizing to Jung amid tensions, demonstrating dependence and professional attachment rather than frictionless discipleship. Tucci, a comparativist of Tibet and India and president of Italy’s ISMEO, shared the Eranos platform and corresponded with Eliade; their relationship joined Romanian, Italian and Swiss Orientalist circuits.

### 1956–1985: University of Chicago

The German-American historian of religion **Joachim Wach** invited Eliade to give the 1956 Haskell Lectures at the University of Chicago. Wach died before the transition was completed, but Eliade came as visiting professor in autumn 1956 and settled permanently in Chicago in 1957. He succeeded to the intellectual space Wach had built and became professor in the Divinity School and a member of the Committee on Social Thought. In 1964 he was named **Sewell L. Avery Distinguished Service Professor**; after retirement he held emeritus status.

With **Joseph Kitagawa** and Charles Long, he helped make Chicago a global center for the history of religions. He co-founded the journal **History of Religions** (1961), trained or influenced Jonathan Z. Smith, Wendy Doniger, Charles Long, Lawrence Sullivan, Ioan Petru Culianu and many others, and supervised an international flow of students. “Chicago School” names a real institutional formation but can overstate doctrinal unity: Smith and Doniger substantially revised or criticized Eliade’s assumptions.

His American years included lectures worldwide, annual European travel, continuing summers or meetings in France and Switzerland, and visits to Japan and elsewhere. Major syntheses included *The Sacred and the Profane*, *Myths, Dreams and Mysteries*, *Birth and Rebirth*, *Myth and Reality*, *The Quest*, *Occultism, Witchcraft and Cultural Fashions*, and the three-volume *Histoire des croyances et des idées religieuses*. His fiction continued in Romanian exile editions: *Noaptea de Sânziene*, *La țigănci*, *Pe strada Mântuleasa*, *Tinerețe fără de tinerețe*, *Nouăsprezece trandafiri* and other fantastic narratives.

He became editor in chief of Macmillan’s **sixteen-volume Encyclopedia of Religion**, coordinating hundreds of contributors. It appeared posthumously in 1987. The University of Chicago archive holds manuscripts, correspondence, photographs, awards, audiovisual material, address books, personal artifacts and Christinel’s papers.

### Final illness and death

Eliade suffered progressive arthritis and declining health in later years. In December 1985 a fire damaged his office and library at Meadville Lombard Theological School; he interpreted it as an omen, a characteristic fusion of event and symbolic reading. On 14 April 1986 he suffered a major stroke while reading Emil Cioran’s *Exercises d’admiration*, lost speech, and entered Bernard Mitchell Hospital. He died on 22 April.

The memorial at Rockefeller Chapel drew a large academic audience; Saul Bellow read Eliade’s account of the childhood luminous room. Ioan Petru Culianu called the death a *mahāparinirvāṇa*, an explicitly hagiographic Buddhist analogy. No evidence supports poisoning, assassination or supernatural causation.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects and Financial Interests

Eliade founded no significant commercial company and held no patents. His material economy was that of a journalist-author, diplomat and academic:

- Romanian newspaper and publishing royalties in the 1930s; *Maitreyi* was a major commercial success.
- State salary as cultural/press attaché in London and Lisbon.
- Precarious postwar teaching, translation, magazine work and assistance from colleagues.
- Bollingen and other fellowship/patronage support connected partly through Eranos.
- University of Chicago salary, distinguished chair, lecture fees and international book royalties.
- Editorial authority rather than ownership in *History of Religions* and Macmillan’s *Encyclopedia of Religion*.

No documented evidence located establishes major investments, secret corporate holdings, money laundering or unusual property accumulation. The Eliades maintained a Chicago home and a scholar’s library; archival objects and rights passed under Christinel’s stewardship and institutional arrangements. Sales figures exceeding 100,000 are reported for *The Myth of the Eternal Return* across editions, but a reliable accounting of lifetime wealth or estate value is not publicly established.

## Political, Religious and Intellectual Development

### Intellectual phases

1. **Adolescent encyclopedism and self-fashioning:** natural science, occultism, Italian vitalism, Renaissance philosophy, exercises of will.
2. **Trăirism and Indian formation:** Nae Ionescu’s existential Orthodox metaphysics plus Dasgupta’s Sanskritic scholarship and lived yoga.
3. **National regeneration:** critique of liberal modernity; growing Orthodox nationalism; explicit Legionary advocacy in 1937–38.
4. **Comparative morphology in exile:** sacred/profane, hierophany, center, axis mundi, archetype, initiation, eternal return, “archaic ontology,” terror of history.
5. **Late global humanism:** a projected “new humanism” in which study of religious worlds would provincialize modern Western historicism, alongside continued interest in occult revivals and literature as camouflage for myth.

### Core concepts

- **Hierophany:** manifestation of the sacred in an object, place, event or person.
- **Sacred and profane:** qualitatively different modes of space, time and existence, not merely church versus secular institution.
- **Myth of eternal return:** rituals reactualize primordial acts; “archaic” people resist linear history by returning to beginnings.
- **Coincidentia oppositorum:** union or conjunction of opposites in symbol and mystical experience.
- **Center/axis mundi:** sacred centers organize inhabited worlds; mountains, temples, trees and houses can join cosmic levels.
- **Initiation:** symbolic death and rebirth as a widespread structure of transformation.
- **Terror of history:** suffering experienced as brute, irreversible event when no transhistorical meaning absorbs it.

### Method and criticism

Eliade described his work as history and phenomenology of religion, assembling comparisons across continents to disclose recurring structures. His strengths were synthetic scale, attention to practitioners’ worlds and recovery of myth/ritual as serious intellectual forms. Recurrent criticisms are also documentary features of his reception:

- selection of examples to fit universal patterns;
- weak attention to chronology, economics, institutions, gender and colonial power;
- treating “archaic man” and “the sacred” as transhistorical essences;
- reliance on outdated ethnography or secondary reports;
- crypto-theological normativity under the name of history;
- possible continuities between anti-modern, sacrificial ideas of the 1930s and postwar scholarship.

Defenders such as Mac Linscott Ricketts and Bryan Rennie argue against reducing the scholarship to fascism and stress development, empirical breadth and hermeneutic charity. Critics including Adriana Berger, Daniel Dubuisson, Alexandra Laignel-Lavastine, Florin Țurcanu and Steven Wasserstrom emphasize concealment, ideological continuity or methodological politics. Later scholarship generally treats “politics irrelevant to scholarship” and “all scholarship coded fascism” as positions requiring claim-by-claim testing.

## Controversies, Allegations and Evidentiary Status

### Iron Guard and antisemitism — documented core

**Established:** Eliade published pro-Legionary articles; praised Codreanu, Moța and Marin; identified the Guard with Christian-national renewal; worked in the 1937 election environment of Everything for the Country; was arrested in the 1938 anti-Legionary repression. Mihail Sebastian’s diary reports antisemitic statements and political estrangement. Eliade served Antonescu-era Romanian diplomacy and wrote admiringly about Salazar.

**Disputed:** Whether he was a formally initiated Legionary, how deeply he knew of particular acts of violence, whether his antisemitism was racial, theological, political or opportunistic, and how directly the concepts of his mature religious studies encode Legionary ideology. Membership evidence is strong but not equivalent to a surviving initiation card; ideological continuity is an interpretive thesis, not a court finding.

**Postwar handling:** Eliade seldom offered a public, explicit reckoning. Autobiographical narration omitted or softened central political facts. This documentary silence helped create later accusations of concealment. It does not erase the primary articles; nor does it prove every later scholarly concept was secretly political.

### Intelligence claims

Romanian communist/Securitate narratives described Eliade as a British intelligence or Gestapo agent. He was a cultural press attaché in wartime capitals and necessarily handled political information; his Legionary and Axis-aligned state connections made him a plausible target for surveillance. **No accessible documentary evidence located establishes that he was a recruited officer or paid agent of the Gestapo or SIS.** These should remain allegations produced in an adversarial security environment, not facts. He was also the target of communist attempts to contact, compromise or recruit Romanian exiles.

### Maitreyi Devi and autobiographical appropriation

The relationship existed; Eliade’s novel was transparently based on it; Devi denied the sexual story and objected to publication. There was no located lawsuit or judicial finding. The controversy turns on conflicting testimony and literary appropriation, not a proven criminal offense.

### Sexual conduct and drugs

Eliade’s journals and autobiographical fiction describe multiple erotic relationships and experiments, sometimes while partnered. *Șantier* includes opium/intoxication material connected to India. The record supports literary and some autobiographical experimentation; it does **not** establish a clinically documented addiction. Claims extrapolated from fictional narrators—especially involving minors—cannot be treated as admissions without corroboration.

### Academic criticisms

Eliade was not found guilty of research fraud, fabrication or plagiarism by a court or formal university inquiry located for this dossier. Criticisms concern method, selective citation, universalization, ideological subtext and failure to disclose politics. These are serious scholarly disputes, not formal fraud adjudications.

### Political violence, criminal cases and finances

No evidence located shows Eliade personally ordered or committed Legionary violence. His public support aided a movement that did commit assassinations, pogroms and political terror; responsibility for advocacy should be distinguished from direct operational participation. His 1938 confinement was administrative political detention under royal dictatorship, not conviction after a criminal trial. No substantiated record of corruption, money laundering or financial crime was located.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda and Disputed Narratives

### “The apolitical scholar of universal myth”

- **Story:** Eliade transcended politics after a brief, insignificant youthful episode.
- **Appearance/circulation:** His postwar autobiographical self-presentation, institutional tributes and early discipleship.
- **Evidence:** Contradicted by numerous 1937–38 articles and campaign evidence; supported only in the narrower sense that his postwar institutional work was not party organizing.
- **Effect:** Enabled rapid international canonization; later archival recovery produced a reputational reversal.

### “The secret fascist code in every mature book”

- **Story:** Sacred/profane, sacrifice, archaic ontology and terror of history are encrypted Legionary doctrine.
- **Circulation:** Strong versions in polemical and some academic criticism from the 1980s onward.
- **Evidence:** Real thematic continuities—anti-historicism, regeneration, sacrifice—exist. Yet sources also include India, phenomenology, Durkheim, van der Leeuw, Jung, Guénon and broad ethnography; no single-key decoding explains all works.
- **Effect:** Reoriented Eliade studies from method alone toward biography and political genealogy.

### “Gestapo/British double agent”

- **Story:** Eliade secretly worked for German or British intelligence.
- **Circulation:** Communist Romanian press and Securitate files; later repetition online.
- **Evidence:** Diplomatic office and intelligence-adjacent contacts, but no located recruitment/payroll/operational file proving agency.
- **Effect:** Intensified the hostile image of exile and made ordinary diplomatic work appear covert.

### The golden room and destiny of a savant

- **Story:** Childhood entry into a green-gold room revealed sacred reality and prefigured his vocation.
- **Appearance:** Eliade’s memoirs and fiction; read at his funeral by Saul Bellow.
- **Evidence:** Only autobiographical memory; symbolically consistent across his oeuvre.
- **Effect:** Central hagiographic scene linking life to theory.

### “Eranos as modern mystery school”

- **Story:** Ascona’s gathering was a twentieth-century initiatory center or hidden spiritual academy.
- **Circulation:** Fröbe-Kapteyn’s own comparisons, participants’ memoirs and later esoteric histories.
- **Evidence:** Eranos was a real annual learned conference with ritualized hospitality, Jungian atmosphere and patronage; it was not a secret order with initiatory membership.
- **Effect:** Enhanced the aura of Eliade, Jung, Corbin, Tucci and Scholem as a council of sages while obscuring mundane funding, editing and academic networking.

### Death as *mahāparinirvāṇa*

- **Story:** Eliade’s death resembled the Buddha’s final passing; the office fire was a portent.
- **Circulation:** Culianu’s commemorative language and Eliade’s own symbolic reading of the fire.
- **Evidence:** Metaphor and personal interpretation only; medically, stroke caused terminal decline.
- **Effect:** Completed a hagiographic life pattern of hierophanies, omens and passage beyond history.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

At death Eliade was arguably the world’s most internationally visible historian of religions. His vocabulary entered religious studies, anthropology, architecture, literary criticism, Jungian psychology and popular spirituality. The *Encyclopedia of Religion* consolidated institutional authority; translations made him a major Romanian writer abroad.

From the late 1980s, recovery of his Legionary journalism and Sebastian’s diary changed the field. Debate now operates on at least four levels: factual reconstruction of political activity; degree of antisemitism; continuity between politics and method; institutional responsibility for naming chairs and teaching a compromised canon. The University of Chicago held a 2006 conference explicitly reconsidering Wach and Eliade in social and political context.

In post-communist Romania he was rapidly republished, elected to the Academy and incorporated into the national canon. Streets, schools, stamps and cultural programs turned him into a symbol of world-recognized Romanian culture. Parallel publication of political texts and the Portuguese journal prevented a simple restoration.

Methodologically, religious studies moved away from his universal “archaic man,” yet his questions persist. Jonathan Z. Smith’s work is unimaginable without sustained correction of Eliade; Wendy Doniger inherited the comparative ambition while adding philology, gender and difference. Outside the academy, “sacred space,” “axis mundi” and “eternal return” remain widely used, sometimes detached from their sources.

His fiction has undergone a separate revival through translations and films. The Maitreyi controversy now centers the agency of Devi and the ethics of transforming intimate cross-cultural encounters into marketable myth. Eranos scholarship likewise relocates Eliade from solitary genius to a patronage-and-correspondence network organized by Fröbe-Kapteyn at Ascona.

## Research Limitations

The corpus is multilingual and enormous. Newspaper articles, correspondence and diaries exist in variant, abridged and posthumously edited forms. Exact legal adoption papers for Giza, naturalization date, complete income/estate accounts, full diplomatic personnel files, full Securitate files and a complete item-by-item bibliography were not accessible in this web-based research. Where the record did not sustain precision, this dossier identifies uncertainty rather than filling gaps. The stopping point was reached after the major phases, controversies and requested Eranos–Tucci–Fröbe-Kapteyn network had primary/archival or specialist support; further searches were increasingly duplicative or pointed to closed books/archives.
