## Works by Margarita / Marina / Pelagia

### Performances and fashioned appearances

- **Late fourth century, if historical:** *Stage performances in Antioch*. The surviving *Life* calls Pelagia the leader of a mime or actors’ troupe, but preserves no titles, scripts, roles, choreography, or securely attributable individual performance; even her identification with the unnamed converted performer mentioned by John Chrysostom remains conjectural. These therefore resist enumeration as discrete works. ([Brock and Harvey, pp. 40–42](https://answersforchrist.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Brock_Harvey_1987_Holy_Women.pdf))

- **Late fourth century, if historical:** *Public procession and self-fashioned persona of Margarita*. Clothing, cosmetics, perfume, jewelry, attendants, and choreographed movement produced a spectacular urban manifestation rather than a text; the account nevertheless belongs to a literary, probably embellished saint’s life, so neither its precise form nor Pelagia’s authorship of the spectacle can be verified. ([Brock and Harvey, pp. 41–42](https://answersforchrist.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Brock_Harvey_1987_Holy_Women.pdf))

### Messages

- **Late fourth century in the narrative; text composed or transmitted in the fifth century:** *Plea to Bishop Nonnos*. An untitled petition written on a wax tablet requesting an audience and instruction: the only substantial written composition attributed to Pelagia. Its wording survives solely as dialogue embedded in the pseudonymous *Life of Pelagia*, a literary elaboration whose earliest extant witness is Syriac but whose source was Greek; consequently the letter cannot be authenticated as her verbatim work. ([Brock and Harvey, pp. 40, 48–49](https://answersforchrist.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Brock_Harvey_1987_Holy_Women.pdf))

- **Late fourth century in the narrative; text composed or transmitted in the fifth century:** *Notice of arrival at the shrine of Saint Julian*. A second, apparently brief note announcing that Pelagia had arrived to meet Nonnos and the assembled bishops. No independent text or wording survives, so it is better classified as a reported communicative act than as an extant work. ([Brock and Harvey, p. 49](https://answersforchrist.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Brock_Harvey_1987_Holy_Women.pdf))

### Social and ascetic projects

- **Late fourth century in the narrative:** *Dissolution of the household*. Pelagia ordered a complete inventory, transferred her fortune for distribution to the poor, legally freed her servants, and gave each a parting gift. This was a coordinated social project rather than a text or object; the inventory itself was made by her steward, not by Pelagia, and its contents do not survive independently. ([Brock and Harvey, pp. 56–57](https://answersforchrist.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Brock_Harvey_1987_Holy_Women.pdf))

- **Late fourth century in the narrative:** *Pelagios, eunuch monk and recluse*. By adopting male monastic dress, a masculine name and voice, fasting, keeping vigil, singing the hours, and inhabiting a cell on the Mount of Olives, Pelagia fashioned an ascetic life in which body, clothing, dwelling, ritual, and social identity cannot readily be separated—a biographical Gesamtkunstwerk, if the category “work” is allowed at all. The *Life* says this project lasted approximately three years and generated a reputation for miracles, but names none individually; scholars regard the narrative as romantic legend constructed around, at most, a possible historical nucleus. ([Brock and Harvey, pp. 40–41, 58–61](https://answersforchrist.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Brock_Harvey_1987_Holy_Women.pdf))

### Attributed oeuvre: limits

- **No secure date:** *No securely recoverable oeuvre*. Margarita, Marina, Pelagia, and the monk Pelagios are overlapping names or manifestations within a multilingual hagiographic tradition, while other saints called Pelagia, Marina, and Margaret were repeatedly confused with one another. The standard critical dossier distinguishes textual recensions in Greek, Latin, Syriac, Arabic, Armenian, Georgian, and Slavonic; these are works about the figure, not works demonstrably made by her, and the evidence does not support expanding the attributed oeuvre beyond the reported performances, two messages, and life-projects above. ([*Pélagie la Pénitente*, vol. 1 description](https://www.persee.fr/authority/236029); [*Encyclopedia of Ancient Christianity*, “Pelagia”](https://www.thetbs.org/study-materials/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Encyclopedia-of-Ancient-Christianity-by-Angelo-Di-Berardino-Thomas-C.-Oden-Joel-C.-Elowsky-James-Hoover-z-lib.org_.pdf))
