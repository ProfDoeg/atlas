# Martín Miguel de Güemes: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier reconstructs the life of Martín Miguel de Güemes from documentary compilations, surviving correspondence, government and military records, recent academic social history, official heritage material, and later historiography.

Evidentiary labels used throughout:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a contemporary document, official record, archival transcription, or multiple independent contemporary accounts.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a generally reliable later historian or institution, but the underlying primary record was not directly inspected here.
- **Disputed claim:** competing versions exist or the surviving evidence does not establish the claim conclusively.
- **Allegation:** an accusation made by an identifiable contemporary or later writer without a judicial finding.
- **Rumor or myth:** a story circulating without adequate documentary support.
- **Propaganda or commemorative tradition:** a claim shaped principally by political, patriotic, regional, or memorial purposes.

Güemes died in 1821. Searches for lawsuits, fraud, corruption, money laundering, modern criminal investigations, and recent scandal therefore produced no applicable modern proceedings. The historically relevant adverse material instead concerns accusations of despotism, arbitrary government, military privilege, forced levies, confiscations, tolerated plunder, sexual misconduct, factional repression, and responsibility for an unsuccessful provincial war.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full baptismal name:** Martín Miguel Juan de Mata Güemes.
- **Expanded genealogical form frequently used:** Martín Miguel Juan de Mata Güemes Montero Goyechea y la Corte.
- **Usual name:** Martín Miguel de Güemes.
- **Titles and offices:**
  - Cadet and officer of the Regimiento Fijo de Infantería de Buenos Aires.
  - Commander of cavalry, provincial militias, and the northern vanguard.
  - Governor-intendant of Salta, 1815–1821.
  - Colonel major and subsequently general.
  - General-in-chief of the Ejército de Observación sobre el Perú, appointed in 1820.
- **Later honorifics:** “General Güemes,” “Héroe Gaucho,” “Héroe Nacional,” “Padre de los Pobres,” “Mártir de la Emancipación,” and “Numen Tutelar de la Gendarmería Nacional.”
- **Birth:** 8 February 1785, city of Salta, then Intendancy of Salta del Tucumán, Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata.
- **Baptism:** 9 February 1785 in Salta’s principal church; the recorded officiant was Gabriel Gómez Recio and the godparents José González de Prada and María Ignacia Cornejo.
- **Death:** 17 June 1821, aged thirty-six, at Cañada de la Horqueta near Salta.
- **Cause:** complications of a gunshot wound received during the royalist surprise occupation of Salta on the night of 6–7 June 1821. Hemophilia is a later hypothesis, not an established diagnosis.
- **Original burial:** chapel of El Chamical.
- **Present burial:** since 1918, the Panteón de las Glorias del Norte in Salta Cathedral.
- **Nationality:** “Argentine” is appropriate retrospectively. During his life he was born a subject of the Spanish monarchy and later served the revolutionary Provinces of the Río de la Plata.
- **Religion:** baptized and married as a Roman Catholic. No evidence has been found of heterodox religious affiliation.
- **Principal geographical sphere:** Salta, Jujuy, the Quebrada de Humahuaca, Tarija, the Puna, Lípez, the upper Río de la Plata/Upper Peru frontier, Tucumán, Santiago del Estero, and Buenos Aires.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Gabriel de Güemes Montero**, Güemes’s father, was a Spanish royal treasury official, usually identified as a native of Santander or the northern Spanish region historically associated with the Güemes family. He became principal minister-treasurer of the Royal Treasury in Salta. His position placed the household securely within the colonial administrative elite.

**María Magdalena Goyechea y la Corte**, his mother, belonged to an established Jujuy-Salta family descended from Spanish settlers, officeholders, encomienda holders, and landowners. She managed family affairs after her husband’s death and supported Martín’s revolutionary career. Later partisan tradition called her “La Tesorera,” reflecting both her husband’s office and her own family role.

The combination is important: Güemes was not born a peasant gaucho. He emerged from a prosperous, interconnected colonial family with access to official employment, literacy, property, and elite networks. His later command depended, however, on a mass rural following drawn from social strata substantially below his own.

### Siblings

The marriage reportedly produced nine children. Lists vary slightly in ordering and baptismal names, but the following are consistently identified:

1. **Juan de Dios Tomás Manuel, usually Juan Manuel Güemes** — born in Jujuy in 1783; lawyer, cabildo officer, political collaborator, and supporter of Martín’s government.
2. **Martín Miguel Juan de Mata** — born 1785.
3. **María Magdalena Dámasa, “Macacha” Güemes** — born 12 December 1787; political organizer, intermediary, confidante, and one of Martín’s most important collaborators.
4. **Francisca Josefa Güemes** — later married Sebastián Fructuoso de Figueroa.
5. **Gabriel José Mariano León Güemes** — executed at Cuzco in 1820 after being accused of complicity in an anti-royalist movement.
6. **José Francisco Güemes** — served in the independence forces and later entered provincial politics.
7. **Juan Clímaco Benjamín Güemes** — militia officer; killed in Salta in 1822 during continuing factional violence.
8. **Manuel Antonio Isaac Güemes.**
9. **Napoleón Quintín José Güemes** — the youngest brother.

Some genealogical summaries reorder Francisca and Macacha or differ over the full names of the younger brothers. The baptismal and family records assembled in *Güemes documentado* are preferable to abbreviated online pedigrees.

After Gabriel de Güemes Montero’s death, María Magdalena Goyechea reportedly married Colonel José Francisco de Tineo y Castellanos and had a further son, Cupertino Tineo, who died in childhood.

### Macacha Güemes

Macacha was neither merely a ceremonial “patriot lady” nor demonstrably the secret co-governor sometimes imagined in popular biography.

- **Documented fact:** she supported the revolutionary movement, corresponded on political matters, and petitioned the Buenos Aires government in 1811 concerning the punishment of her husband, Román Tejada.
- **Reported fact:** contemporaries, including José María Paz, remembered her as exceptionally politically active.
- **Strongly reported but incompletely documented claim:** she mediated between Güemes and José Rondeau during the crisis ending in the Pact of Los Cerrillos in March 1816.
- **Later hagiographic expansion:** descriptions of her as the sole intelligence chief or author of virtually every political negotiation go beyond the surviving record.

### Marriage

Güemes married **María Margarita del Carmen Puch de la Vega Velarde**, generally called **Carmen Puch**, a member of another wealthy Salta family.

Her parents were the landowner **Domingo Puch Izuleta** and **Dorotea Velarde**. The Puch family supplied money, animals, property, shelter, and political connections to the independence effort.

The commonly cited wedding date is **10 July 1815** in the Salta Cathedral record; some biographies give **15 July**. The archival marriage-information file reportedly shows that ecclesiastical dispensation was required because Güemes and Carmen were related within prohibited degrees of consanguinity. Macacha is traditionally credited with arranging or facilitating the match.

Carmen was eighteen; Güemes was thirty. Correspondence surviving from the marriage shows affection alongside the prolonged separations imposed by military operations.

### Children

The couple had three sons:

- **Martín del Milagro or Martín María Güemes Puch** — reported dates vary between 15 September 1816 and dates in 1817. He became a landowner, militia officer, legislator, and governor of Salta in the 1850s.
- **Luis Güemes Puch** — born 21 June 1819 according to family reconstructions.
- **Ignacio Güemes Puch** — died in infancy.

The children were still very young when both parents died. Martín and Luis were raised principally through the Puch family network and spent part of their youth in Peru.

### Carmen’s death

Carmen died on **3 April 1822**, less than ten months after her husband.

- **Documented fact:** date of death and proximity to Güemes’s death.
- **Later family and literary tradition:** on learning of his death, she cut her hair, withdrew into a darkened room, refused ordinary life, and “allowed herself to die.”
- **Unresolved medical question:** no adequate clinical record establishes starvation, depression, tuberculosis, infection, or another cause. “She died of grief” belongs to romantic and hagiographic tradition, not demonstrable medical history.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1785–1799

Güemes grew up in Salta in a house associated with the Royal Treasury administration. The building later became the Museo Güemes. His upbringing combined urban elite society with close exposure to the surrounding rural and frontier economy.

Salta was a commercial and administrative junction linking Buenos Aires and Tucumán with Jujuy, Tarija, Potosí, and the Andean mining economy. Its wealth depended heavily on mule breeding, livestock, transport, and trade with Upper Peru. Güemes therefore grew up amid the routes, horse culture, estate relations, and frontier geography that later defined his military system.

### Education

No evidence establishes attendance at a university or advanced college. His education appears to have been local and practical:

- reading, writing, accounting, and administrative routines suitable for a treasury household;
- Catholic religious instruction;
- horsemanship and familiarity with rural labor and transport;
- military training after entering the regiment.

A handwriting examination reproduced in *Güemes documentado* supports the family tradition that he worked as a young clerk or copyist in the treasury office under his father. Claims that he had little formal education should not be confused with illiteracy: his surviving correspondence demonstrates functional literacy, political vocabulary, and sustained administrative work, although secretaries almost certainly drafted or copied some official communications.

No securely identifiable private tutor or formative intellectual mentor has emerged from the consulted record.

---

## Entry into the Royal Army, 1799–1805

On **13 February 1799**, shortly after turning fourteen, Güemes entered as a cadet in the third battalion company of the **Regimiento Fijo de Infantería de Buenos Aires**, whose Salta detachment had existed since the late colonial period.

This was normal elite military formation rather than an early commitment to independence. He initially served the Spanish crown. His training included infantry drill, discipline, paperwork, arms, logistics, and regional garrison duties.

Around 1805 he was transferred or called to Buenos Aires, placing him in the viceregal capital before the British invasions.

---

## Buenos Aires and the British Invasions, 1806–1808

Güemes served in Buenos Aires during the British invasions of the Río de la Plata. He participated in the reconquest and defense under commanders including Santiago de Liniers and Juan Martín de Pueyrredón.

### The capture of the *Justina*

A famous story says that on **12 August 1806**, Güemes led mounted men into the shallow Río de la Plata and captured the British vessel *Justina*, which had grounded or become immobilized by low water.

- **Substantial historical core:** the *Justina* was captured during the reconquest, cavalry or mounted forces participated from the shore, and Güemes was present in the operations.
- **Disputed elaboration:** modern retellings frequently call it the only or one of the only cases in history in which cavalry captured a warship. The vessel’s exact classification, the degree of resistance, Güemes’s personal command, and the uniqueness claim vary among accounts.
- **Commemorative effect:** the episode became an ideal prefiguration of the “gaucho general,” although Güemes was then a royal army officer and not yet the leader of gaucho militias.

Claims that he served as Liniers’s personal aide are repeated by some biographers but remain less securely documented.

In 1807 he took part in the second defense of Buenos Aires. The experience brought contact with future revolutionary officers and demonstrated the effectiveness of locally raised forces against a professional invading army.

In April 1808, citing ill health and his father’s death, he obtained leave and returned to Salta.

---

## Return to Salta and Final Colonial Service, 1808–1810

On **13 January 1809**, the Supreme Central Junta of Seville reportedly confirmed or granted his promotion to sub-lieutenant of the Buenos Aires infantry regiment. In July the governor-intendant secured his attachment to the Salta garrison, where he served with the grade of lieutenant.

Thus, on the eve of the May Revolution, Güemes remained an officer commissioned within the Spanish imperial system.

---

## Revolution and the First Upper Peru Campaign, 1810–1811

After the Buenos Aires revolution of May 1810, Güemes adhered to the revolutionary government and joined the northern expedition. Diego José de Pueyrredón and other regional figures helped organize local forces and intelligence.

He operated through Humahuaca, Tarija, Lípez, and the approaches to Upper Peru, disrupting communications and recruiting mounted auxiliaries. His command drew on Salta, Jujuy, Orán, and Tarija men familiar with the terrain.

### Cotagaita and Suipacha

He took part in the campaign around Cotagaita and the revolutionary victory at **Suipacha on 7 November 1810**. Patriotic historiography often describes his role as “decisive.” The more cautious conclusion is:

- **Documented fact:** he commanded an advanced mounted detachment involved in reconnaissance, skirmishing, and exploitation of the victory.
- **Historiographical dispute:** formal battle reports did not give him the prominence later Salta historians believed he deserved. This omission contributed to an early quarrel with senior command.
- **Unresolved detail:** it is difficult to isolate a single “decisive” actor in the surviving record.

Güemes was removed or separated from the army after Suipacha, during the ascendancy of Juan José Castelli and Antonio González Balcarce. Accounts attribute this to disputes over credit, discipline, jurisdiction, or personal hostility. On **23 June 1811**, the Junta ordered his reinstatement.

After the patriot defeat at Huaqui in June 1811, he helped protect the retreat and assisted Juan Martín de Pueyrredón in removing the Potosí treasury funds through a collapsing military front. Pueyrredón’s report acknowledged Güemes’s offer of men and personal support.

---

## Tarija, Belgrano, and Confinement, 1811–1813

In January 1812 Güemes participated in the unsuccessful operations near Suipacha/Nazareno. Pueyrredón then sent him to Tarija, where he suppressed or forestalled a royalist rising and collected men, horses, weapons, and artillery. Later accounts credit him with returning with approximately 300 men, 500 firearms, and two cannon, but the exact totals should be treated as reported rather than audited figures.

When Manuel Belgrano assumed command, Güemes was employed in recruiting and logistical work around Santiago del Estero. During this period his relations with Belgrano deteriorated sharply.

### The Juana Inguanzo affair

Belgrano sent Güemes to Buenos Aires in late 1812 under what was nominally an official commission. A confidential communication accused Güemes of “scandalous conduct” with **Juana Inguanzo**, wife of Lieutenant Sebastián Mella, including their lodging in the same house.

- **Documented fact:** the accusation appears in military correspondence and was connected with Güemes’s removal to Buenos Aires.
- **Not a judicial finding:** no consulted record establishes a criminal prosecution or conviction.
- **Nature of accusation:** adultery or an openly improper relationship, not the later allegation of rape attached to a different death legend.
- **Güemes’s response:** in January 1813 he asked to be informed of the reason for his confinement; officials reportedly replied that the record before them contained no adequate formal cause and requested clarification from Belgrano.
- **Historical significance:** this is the strongest documented personal scandal of Güemes’s life and explains why the later Belgrano–Güemes friendship should not be projected backward without qualification.

He remained away from the northern front during the battles of Tucumán and Salta. No evidence supports later attempts to insert him into those victories.

---

## Rehabilitation under San Martín, 1813–1814

In December 1813 Güemes petitioned to return to the northern army. José de San Martín, about to replace Belgrano, supported the request. Güemes accompanied or joined San Martín’s command in early 1814.

San Martín recognized that a conventional advance through Upper Peru had repeatedly failed. He placed Güemes in charge of the northern advance or vanguard and relied on mobile local forces to contain royalist incursions while developing the continental strategy through Cuyo and Chile.

This was a pivotal professional relationship:

- San Martín supplied institutional rehabilitation and strategic authority.
- Güemes supplied local intelligence, mounted warfare, regional leadership, and a defensive system that could operate with limited central funding.
- They corresponded thereafter, although distance meant that much of the relationship was mediated through letters, Belgrano, Pueyrredón, and other officers.

During 1814 Güemes’s forces harassed Joaquín de la Pezuela’s army, attacked detachments and communications, removed livestock and supplies, and made occupation of Salta’s countryside costly. The Cabildo initially praised the rural fighters but soon complained about disorder, requisitions, and the permanent military jurisdiction claimed by militiamen.

The expression **“Guerra Gaucha”** is later, made canonical by Leopoldo Lugones. Contemporary actors used terms including gauchos, militia, partidas, vanguard forces, or montoneras.

---

## Puesto del Marqués and the Break with Rondeau, 1815

On **14 April 1815**, Güemes and allied forces defeated a royalist detachment at **Puesto del Marqués**. The victory yielded arms, prisoners, and supplies. Command responsibility became contested between Güemes and senior officers of José Rondeau’s Army of the North.

Güemes withdrew to Jujuy and appropriated or removed weapons belonging to the main army. From his perspective, local forces needed the weapons to defend the province; Rondeau viewed the act as insubordination and a threat to centralized command.

The dispute illustrates three overlapping conflicts:

1. regular army versus provincial militia;
2. Buenos Aires-appointed command versus locally based authority;
3. rival claims to weapons, taxes, livestock, and manpower in an exhausted war zone.

---

## Election as Governor of Salta, 1815

The collapse of Supreme Director Carlos María de Alvear’s authority in April 1815 produced governmental uncertainty. On **6 May 1815**, Salta’s cabildo chose Güemes governor-intendant.

- **Documented fact:** election by the cabildo in an open political setting shaped by military pressure and the absence of effective central authority.
- **Supporters’ interpretation:** the first governor chosen by Salta without appointment from Buenos Aires, embodying provincial autonomy.
- **Opponents’ interpretation:** an irregular election secured by armed influence, facilitated by his brother Juan Manuel’s position in the cabildo.
- **Balanced conclusion:** it was both a locally grounded political election and an exercise conducted under the unmistakable presence of Güemes’s armed following.

The jurisdiction nominally included Salta, Jujuy, Orán, Tarija, and extensive rural districts. Actual control varied with royalist occupation, local resistance, and Jujuy’s increasingly assertive autonomy.

His marriage to Carmen Puch followed in July, consolidating a major family and property alliance.

---

## Governmental System and Social Coalition, 1815–1821

### Military organization

Güemes maintained a layered military structure:

- gaucho cavalry squadrons organized by district;
- local commanders and estate-linked militia officers;
- regular or semi-regular line units;
- the **Infernales**, formally organized as a distinctive cavalry formation in 1815–1816;
- intelligence, messengers, scouts, women couriers, and rural supply networks;
- forces and allied commanders in Jujuy, the Puna, Tarija, Cinti, and adjacent Upper Peru territories.

Important commanders included Manuel Eduardo Arias, José Ignacio Gorriti, Bonifacio Ruiz de los Llanos, Luis Burela, Apolinario Saravia, Francisco Pérez de Uriondo, Eustaquio Méndez, Juan José Feliciano Fernández Campero, and later the French officer Jorge Enrique Vidt.

Güemes did not simply invent rural resistance. He integrated pre-existing local militias, insurgent bands, estate networks, Indigenous and mestizo communities, Afro-descendant soldiers, small producers, laborers, and experienced frontier commanders. Relationships with those commanders were sometimes cooperative and sometimes tense.

### Fuero gaucho

Güemes extended military jurisdiction—the **fuero militar**—to permanently mobilized militiamen.

Effects included:

- shielding gauchos from some ordinary civil process while in service;
- giving military commanders jurisdiction over disputes;
- protecting men absent from farms and estates because of war;
- weakening landlords’ and civil judges’ ability to enforce debts, labor obligations, rents, and punishment.

Later tradition describes a “Fuero Gaucho” as if Güemes enacted a comprehensive social-welfare statute. Modern archival research suggests a more specific institution: permanent military jurisdiction with major unintended or negotiated social consequences.

### Rents and rural obligations

Militiamen increasingly refused rents and services while mobilized.

- **Documented social process:** nonpayment became widespread and persisted after Güemes’s death.
- **Disputed attribution:** Güemes did not clearly issue a universal decree abolishing rents.
- **Political reality:** he often declined to compel payment because doing so risked losing the men on whom defense depended.
- **Result:** rural soldiers converted military service into bargaining power against landowners.

### Finance and requisitions

The province was devastated by repeated invasions and cut off from much Upper Peru trade. Güemes financed defense through:

- extraordinary “patriotic contributions”;
- forced or negotiated loans;
- seizures and requisitions of cattle, horses, food, clothing, and metal;
- customs and provincial revenue;
- family and elite supporters’ resources;
- the uncompensated labor and property of rural inhabitants.

Accounts were incomplete and wartime administration improvised. No documentary basis was found for personal embezzlement or a concealed business structure. The major financial controversy concerns coercive public extraction, not proven private enrichment.

### Property

Güemes came from a property-owning family and married into the wealthy Puch family. In 1817 he reportedly purchased the estate known as **El Carmen**, approximately 360 hectares southwest of Salta, naming it for his wife. It served as residence, military encampment, and training ground.

He was therefore not economically identical with his poor followers. His coalition joined sections of the old elite to mobilized rural society against royalists and against other elite factions. Modern social historians reject both the image of a classless patriotic unity and the simpler image of an elite manipulator exercising total control over passive gauchos.

---

## Crisis with Rondeau and the Pact of Los Cerrillos, 1815–1816

After the patriot disaster at Sipe Sipe in November 1815, Rondeau retreated toward Salta and attempted to depose or subordinate Güemes. He declared him a traitor or disobedient officer and moved against him.

Güemes’s forces denied supplies to Rondeau’s army and surrounded or constrained it. The conflict approached civil war while royalist forces remained the larger threat.

On **22 March 1816**, the parties concluded the **Pact of Los Cerrillos**:

- Rondeau recognized Güemes as governor and commander of the local forces.
- Güemes recognized the national army’s larger military authority.
- Cooperation against the royalists resumed.

Macacha’s mediation is strongly embedded in contemporary memory and later accounts, but the precise extent of her negotiating role is not fully demonstrated by the surviving papers.

The pact allowed delegates from the north to support the Congress of Tucumán and restored enough coordination for defense.

---

## Relations with Belgrano, 1816–1820

Belgrano returned to command the Army of the North in 1816. The earlier disciplinary conflict gave way to an unusually warm military correspondence.

The surviving compilation records:

- at least 126 letters from Belgrano to Güemes;
- approximately fourteen letters from Güemes to Belgrano;
- additional reports, enclosures, and official communications.

The imbalance reflects archival survival and chain of command, not necessarily one-sided interest. Güemes continued to regard Belgrano as his senior commander. They coordinated intelligence, reinforcements, promotions, supplies, enemy movements, and political news.

Belgrano defended Güemes to central authorities and transmitted San Martín and Pueyrredón communications. Their friendship was therefore real but developed after serious earlier hostility.

Belgrano’s worsening health and the dispersal of the Army of the North left Güemes increasingly isolated. Belgrano died on 20 June 1820.

---

## The Great Royalist Invasion, 1816–1817

In late 1816 José de la Serna led a large, experienced royalist army south. Pedro Antonio Olañeta commanded part of the advance. Royalists entered Humahuaca and Jujuy and pushed into Salta in early 1817.

Güemes avoided a conventional decisive battle. His system relied on:

- evacuation of useful supplies;
- destruction or removal of forage and livestock;
- ambushes;
- attacks on isolated detachments;
- interception of couriers;
- persistent harassment;
- denial of stable occupation outside fortified positions.

Local commanders operated semi-independently. Manuel Eduardo Arias’s March 1817 attack in the Humahuaca area was one notable success. The royalists occupied Salta but could not establish secure supply or political control of the countryside.

De la Serna withdrew from Salta in May 1817, harassed on the return north. News of San Martín’s success in Chile compounded the strategic pressure.

This campaign established Güemes’s continental significance: his defense prevented the royalists from advancing securely toward Tucumán while San Martín crossed the Andes.

---

## Later Invasions and Regional Warfare, 1817–1820

Royalist forces repeatedly returned:

- Olañeta occupied or threatened Jujuy in 1818.
- Further expeditions in 1819 were checked by the same combination of intelligence, resource denial, and mobile attack.
- In May 1820 Olañeta and José de Canterac again occupied Jujuy and entered Salta but withdrew in June.

The phrase “six invasions defeated” is common in commemorative literature. Counting varies according to whether separate advances, occupations, or reconnaissance operations are treated as distinct invasions. The underlying fact is repeated royalist penetration without permanent conquest.

### Internal command conflicts

Güemes’s authority over the broad frontier was never absolute. He faced:

- rival ambitions among militia commanders;
- Jujuy resentment of Salta’s fiscal and political control;
- disputes with Manuel Eduardo Arias and other officers;
- desertion and indiscipline;
- conflict between locally rooted forces and officers of the former Army of the North;
- resistance from clergy, merchants, landlords, and cabildo factions.

Modern scholarship emphasizes negotiation: gauchos followed Güemes because he protected interests and distributed recognition, jurisdiction, and material opportunity—not merely because of personal charisma.

### José Antonio Moldes

In February 1817 Güemes ordered Colonel **José Antonio Moldes** arrested and subjected to a military inquiry for allegedly undermining the government, corrupting military obedience, and attempting to form a faction.

- **Documented fact:** arrest, military proceeding, and political charges.
- **Moldes’s defense:** his meetings concerned representative government, legitimacy, and the law of nations.
- **No neutral adjudication:** the proceeding served to remove a powerful dissident from provincial politics.
- **Historical interpretation:** an example of how military justice could operate as political exclusion under Güemes.

---

## Relations with San Martín and the Peruvian Strategy, 1817–1821

Güemes and San Martín did not operate as equal commanders in one physical headquarters after 1814, but their work remained strategically connected.

San Martín required the north to remain defended while he crossed the Andes, liberated Chile, and prepared the expedition to Peru. Güemes sought supplies and authorization for a complementary advance from Salta and Upper Peru.

In 1820, following the collapse of central authority after Cepeda and the mutiny at Arequito, San Martín named Güemes **general-in-chief of the Ejército de Observación sobre el Perú**. Güemes received notification in July and accepted in August.

He planned to assemble roughly 3,500–4,000 men for a northward offensive coordinated with San Martín’s landing in Peru. Correspondence went to San Martín, Bernardo O’Higgins, provincial governors, former Army of the North officers, and regional commanders.

The plan failed to become operational because:

- Buenos Aires central authority had collapsed;
- provinces withheld money, weapons, or troops;
- former national units dispersed;
- Salta was financially exhausted;
- Tucumán and Salta entered armed conflict;
- royalist pressure continued;
- Güemes was killed before the campaign could mature.

## Political and Ideological Development

Güemes left no systematic political treatise. His ideas must be reconstructed from proclamations, correspondence, governmental practice, and alliances.

### Independence and “America”

His letters use a political geography broader than present-day Argentina. He treated Salta, Upper Peru, Chile, and Peru as theaters of a common American independence war.

### Provincial autonomy

His 1815 election asserted Salta’s ability to select its own authority without waiting for Buenos Aires. This has led to his later classification as a federalist.

That label requires caution:

- he defended provincial authority against central interference;
- he also accepted military subordination to Belgrano and strategic cooperation with Pueyrredón and San Martín;
- he sought coordinated interprovincial action rather than isolation;
- mature Argentine federal and unitary parties did not yet exist in their later form.

### Social politics

Güemes protected the military privileges and practical claims of rural soldiers because they were indispensable to defense. Whether this amounted to a conscious program of social revolution is disputed.

Modern social history shows that mobilized rural people pursued their own agenda. Güemes sometimes enabled it, sometimes tried to discipline it, and sometimes used the threat of popular violence against elite adversaries.

### Religion and the clergy

His administration operated within Catholic political culture and used religious ceremonies and language. Nevertheless, relations with parts of the clergy became hostile. Clerical opponents described his government as despotic, objected to seizures and military privilege, and issued censures following attacks on property. This was a political and jurisdictional conflict within Catholic society, not evidence of anticlerical ideology.

---

## War with Tucumán and Military Failure, 1820–1821

After the collapse of the central government, Governor **Bernabé Aráoz** established the Republic of Tucumán and controlled arms and resources Güemes wanted for the projected Upper Peru campaign.

Güemes accused Aráoz of withholding necessary military assistance. The struggle also involved Santiago del Estero, where Juan Felipe Ibarra sought independence from Tucumán.

Güemes launched or supported military operations against Tucumán. On **3 April 1821**, his forces were defeated at **Rincón de Marlopa**.

This was a serious failure:

- it consumed scarce provincial resources;
- it took Güemes and leading units away from Salta;
- it intensified merchant and landowner opposition;
- it weakened his political position immediately before a renewed royalist operation;
- it blurred the boundary between the continental independence war and interprovincial civil war.

Supportive accounts present the campaign as necessary to secure arms for San Martín’s plan. Critics treat it as an aggressive war against a neighboring province. Both motives—strategic need and provincial power politics—are supported by the context.

---

## The Revolution of the Commerce, May 1821

While Güemes was occupied with Tucumán, opponents in Salta moved against him. The faction later called **Patria Nueva** included merchants, landowners, cabildo figures, political rivals, and people exhausted by contributions, disrupted trade, military privilege, and rural nonpayment.

On **24 May 1821**, the Salta cabildo declared Güemes removed and chose **Saturnino Saravia** in his place.

The movement became known as the **Revolución del Comercio** because commercial interests played a central role. Its participants wanted relief from:

- extraordinary levies;
- restrictions on trade with royalist Upper Peru;
- requisitions;
- militia disorder;
- Güemes’s dominance of provincial government.

Güemes returned with loyal forces on 29 May. Opposing troops defected or refused to fight. He recovered the city.

### Authorized plunder

After recovering power, Güemes allowed his gauchos to plunder the shops or houses of identified opponents.

- **Documented or strongly corroborated fact:** both Bernardo Frías and modern archival scholarship accept that he authorized punitive plunder.
- **Victims’ description:** arbitrary robbery by a tyrant’s armed plebe.
- **Participants’ social meaning:** compensation for unpaid military service and punishment of men deemed enemies of the independence cause.
- **Responsibility:** whatever the structural causes, authorization makes Güemes politically responsible for the episode.
- **Unknowns:** complete victim lists, total property loss, and degree of discipline cannot be reconstructed.

## Wounding, Final Days, and Death

### Royalist entry into Salta

Pedro Antonio Olañeta exploited Salta’s divisions. Colonel **José María Valdés**, called **“el Barbarucho,”** led a force through little-used mountain routes and entered Salta by surprise during the night of **6–7 June 1821**.

The force is often said to have numbered approximately 400 men, although later narratives sometimes enlarge the figure. Local knowledge was essential.

### Mariano Benítez and the alleged paid betrayal

A long-standing account says **Mariano Benítez**, a domestic opponent of Güemes, guided Valdés and was paid 5,000 pesos raised by Salta merchants.

- **Documented context:** Benítez was associated with the anti-Güemes faction and Valdés had local assistance.
- **Reported claim:** notes attributed to José Manuel García state that Benítez pursued Valdés to Tupiza, offered guidance, and received 5,000 pesos supplied by “the commerce.”
- **Evidentiary limitation:** the surviving report is retrospective and does not by itself prove a formal merchant subscription or payment ledger.
- **Conclusion:** local collaboration is highly probable; the exact 5,000-peso transaction remains a reported allegation rather than a fully verified financial fact.

### The shooting

The standard account says Güemes was in the city—often placed at or near Macacha’s house—when royalist troops closed the exits. He escaped on horseback, passed or jumped enemy lines, and was shot while crossing the Tagarete del Tineo, near present-day Avenida Belgrano.

The bullet entered the buttock or hip region and caused severe bleeding. He reached loyal forces outside the city and was moved to the Cañada de la Horqueta.

### Final command

During approximately ten days of decline, Güemes continued to receive officers. Royalist representatives reportedly offered medical attention or terms if he would order an end to resistance.

The famous deathbed story says he rejected the offer and required his officers to swear that they would continue fighting. He transferred command to **Jorge Enrique Vidt**.

- **Documented core:** Vidt succeeded to operational command and resistance continued.
- **Commemorative shaping:** exact speeches and dramatic dialogue were preserved through later accounts and should not be treated as verbatim transcripts.

Güemes died on **17 June 1821**.

His forces continued operations. The royalists subsequently withdrew, and an armistice ended the immediate campaign. Describing Güemes as “the only Argentine general killed in action during the independence war on present Argentine soil” is an official commemorative formula; it depends on later national classifications and on whether death ten days after wounding counts as “in action,” but accurately conveys the unusual circumstances.

## Health, Injuries, and Physical Characteristics

No reliable evidence establishes chronic addiction, psychiatric illness, or major psychological crisis.

Known or reported health matters include:

- ill health cited in his 1808 request to return to Salta;
- the fatal gunshot wound in June 1821;
- substantial blood loss and prolonged decline;
- a later hypothesis of hemophilia.

### Hemophilia hypothesis

- **What the story says:** Güemes bled to death because he had hemophilia.
- **Evidence offered:** the ten-day course and inability to stop bleeding.
- **Evidence against or limiting it:** no contemporary diagnosis, no known characteristic family pattern among relevant male descendants, and many possible infectious or vascular complications of an untreated gunshot wound.
- **Status:** disputed retrospective medical hypothesis.

Some descriptions mention a noticeable stammer or speech impediment. This is found principally in later biographical tradition and cannot be clinically reconstructed.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Manuel Belgrano

Initially a disciplinary superior who removed Güemes following the Juana Inguanzo scandal; later his closest senior military correspondent and advocate. Their surviving exchange documents a transition from hostility to trust.

### José de San Martín

Strategic patron, rehabilitator, and collaborator. San Martín placed Güemes in command of the northern vanguard in 1814 and made him commander of the Ejército de Observación in 1820.

### Juan Martín de Pueyrredón

Commander during the 1811 retreat and later Supreme Director. He corresponded with Güemes and supported the northern defense while balancing complaints from Güemes’s enemies.

### José Rondeau

Senior commander turned rival in 1815–1816. Their conflict nearly became civil war before the Pact of Los Cerrillos.

### Macacha Güemes

Sibling, confidante, family intermediary, and political collaborator. Her precise institutional authority is uncertain, but her importance is supported well beyond later folklore.

### Carmen Puch and Domingo Puch

Carmen was spouse and correspondent; her father and brothers formed a major financial, logistical, and kinship support network.

### Manuel Eduardo Arias

One of the most capable local commanders, particularly in Humahuaca and Zenta. Their relationship eventually deteriorated amid disputes over authority.

### Juan José Feliciano Fernández Campero, Marquis of Yavi

Frontier commander and ally. Surviving correspondence records extensive coordination in 1815–1816.

### José Ignacio and José Francisco Gorriti

Jujuy-Salta political and military allies, though relations between Jujuy and Güemes’s Salta government were not uniformly harmonious.

### Pedro Antonio Olañeta and José María Valdés

Principal late royalist enemies. Olañeta coordinated repeated invasions; Valdés led the surprise operation in which Güemes was mortally wounded.

### Bernabé Aráoz

Former revolutionary associate and later provincial rival. Their conflict culminated in Güemes’s failed Tucumán campaign.

### José Antonio Moldes

Powerful Salta political rival arrested and prosecuted through military procedure under Güemes.

### José Gervasio Artigas

Correspondent. Artigas wrote to Güemes explaining his conflict with Buenos Aires. The exchange contributes to—but does not alone prove—later descriptions of Güemes as an Artiguist federal leader.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

No companies, patents, inventions, or modern corporate interests apply.

Relevant institutions and projects were:

- Spanish Royal Treasury at Salta;
- Regimiento Fijo de Infantería de Buenos Aires;
- Army of the North/Army Auxiliary of Peru;
- Salta provincial government and cabildo;
- district gaucho squadrons;
- the Infernales;
- Ejército de Observación sobre el Perú;
- the continental military coordination associated with San Martín’s Peruvian expedition;
- the El Carmen estate and Puch-family properties used for residence, supply, and military purposes.

His principal “technology” was organizational rather than mechanical: a distributed frontier-defense system integrating horse mobility, local intelligence, supply denial, ambush, messenger networks, and negotiated rural military privilege.

## Controversies, Allegations, Political Violence, and Financial Questions

### 1. Adultery or improper relationship with Juana Inguanzo

- **Status:** documented contemporary accusation; no known conviction.
- **Source context:** Belgrano’s disciplinary communication in 1812.
- **Likely consequence:** transfer or confinement in Buenos Aires.
- **Unresolved:** duration and exact nature of the relationship.

### 2. Despotism and irregular accession

- **Opponents’ claim:** Güemes was a tyrant imposed by armed gauchos.
- **Evidence:** armed influence was present; he governed under emergency conditions and suppressed rivals.
- **Counter-evidence:** his authority originated in a cabildo election, enjoyed substantial popular and elite support, and was repeatedly defended by provincial forces.
- **Assessment:** “tyrant” is a partisan label; militarized and coercive government is a documented description.

### 3. Fuero militar and abuses by militiamen

- **Documented fact:** permanent military jurisdiction generated conflict with ordinary courts.
- **Contemporary allegations:** theft, assault, rent refusal, interference with judges, and impunity.
- **Modern finding:** the fuero protected soldiers but also redistributed legal power toward rural mobilized men.
- **Unresolved:** individual offenses require case-by-case judicial files; collective accusations cannot automatically be assigned to every gaucho or to Güemes personally.

### 4. Forced contributions and confiscations

- **Documented fact:** extraordinary levies, requisitions, and coerced assistance financed the war.
- **Opponents’ claim:** arbitrary expropriation for a personal dictatorship.
- **Evidence against personal-enrichment theory:** no demonstrated secret fortune, company structure, or embezzlement scheme.
- **Assessment:** coercive wartime extraction is established; personal corruption is not.

### 5. Political use of military justice

The Moldes proceeding shows military law being used to incapacitate a dissident. Whether Moldes actually conspired remains disputed; the process was not independent by modern standards.

### 6. Punitive plunder in May 1821

- **Status:** documented or strongly corroborated.
- **Responsibility:** Güemes authorized his followers to loot named opponents after defeating the Revolution of the Commerce.
- **Interpretations:** elite accounts saw barbarism and robbery; social history identifies redistribution, military compensation, and class retaliation.
- **Legal outcome:** no later judicial adjudication of Güemes’s responsibility.

### 7. War against Tucumán

- **Documented fact:** Güemes initiated or sustained military operations and was defeated.
- **Justification:** obtain arms and protect the continental campaign.
- **Criticism:** aggression driven by provincial rivalry, worsening the northern military crisis.
- **Assessment:** strategic and factional motives coexisted.

### 8. Alleged sexual assault and shooting by a jealous subordinate

A modern rumor claims Güemes was found with—or sexually assaulting—the wife of a subordinate, who shot him.

- **Status:** rumor or hostile myth.
- **Evidence:** no identified contemporary military report, witness deposition, medical record, or credible archival document was found.
- **Probable formation:** conflation of the documented 1812 Juana Inguanzo scandal with uncertainty over Güemes’s location immediately before Valdés’s ambush.
- **Circulation:** oral tradition, internet discussion, and recent journalistic references to a persistent northern story.
- **Reputational use:** undermines the patriotic death narrative by replacing royalist combat with private sexual misconduct.
- **Conclusion:** not established and substantially weaker than the documented royalist-surprise account.

### 9. “The merchants paid for his murder”

- **Status:** allegation with circumstantial and retrospective support.
- **Established:** merchant opposition, the May coup, local collaboration with Valdés, and benefit to anti-Güemes factions.
- **Unestablished:** a complete conspiracy agreement, payment record, and collective culpability of “the commerce.”
- **Appropriate conclusion:** some domestic enemies probably aided the royalist entry; the scope and financing remain unresolved.

### 10. Recent adversarial search, 2024–2026

Searches combining Güemes’s name with controversy, scandal, accusations, fraud, corruption, conspiracy, crime, criticism, and lawsuit found:

- continuing debate over hagiography and the quality of public historical commemoration;
- repetition of the jealous-subordinate/sexual encounter story;
- academic work on political exclusion, military justice, and coercive social order;
- no modern criminal, financial, or judicial discovery altering the documentary record;
- no credible evidence of fraud, money laundering, or hidden financial enterprises.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “A gaucho from birth”

Güemes is frequently represented as socially indistinguishable from poor rural gauchos.

- **Evidence against:** elite family, treasury household, officer-cadet career, property, and advantageous marriage.
- **Historical basis:** mastery of rural horsemanship, regional speech and networks, and genuine political leadership of gaucho forces.
- **Effect:** made him a symbol of democratic provincial identity and concealed the negotiated, cross-class nature of his coalition.

### “Father of the poor”

- **Origin:** popular and later historiographical representations of his protection of rural soldiers.
- **Basis:** military jurisdiction, resistance to rent enforcement, distribution of recognition and protection, and reliance on poor troops.
- **Limitation:** he remained a member of the landowning elite and did not enact a documented general program abolishing class hierarchy.
- **Effect:** central to populist, federalist, and regional memory.

### The cavalry capture of a warship

- **Story:** Güemes captured the British *Justina* by charging it on horseback.
- **Core:** mounted forces participated in taking the stranded vessel.
- **Mythic enlargement:** unique cavalry capture of a fully operational warship personally led by Güemes.
- **Effect:** supplies a dramatic youthful prophecy of later mounted warfare.

### “The creator of guerrilla war”

- **Status:** commemorative exaggeration.
- **Evidence:** irregular frontier warfare, montoneras, Indigenous resistance, and rural militias predated him.
- **Achievement:** he coordinated these methods into an unusually durable regional defense.

### Six or seven invasions defeated

The count varies because “invasion” is defined differently. Repeated royalist occupations and withdrawals are documented; a fixed sacred number is a commemorative simplification.

### The deathbed oath

- **Story:** refusing a royalist doctor, Güemes summoned his officers beneath a tree and made them swear never to negotiate.
- **Core:** he rejected terms, transferred command to Vidt, and resistance continued.
- **Uncertainty:** exact wording and staging.
- **Effect:** transformed death from medical decline into a final political act.

### Hemophilia

A retrospective medical theory, not a documented diagnosis. Its repetition supplies an explanation for prolonged bleeding but exceeds the evidence.

### Carmen Puch dying of love

A romantic narrative built upon the documented sequence of deaths. It shaped Carmen as the ideal loyal widow but cannot substitute for a medical cause.

### Deliberate national erasure

A recurring regional claim says Buenos Aires or the Mitre school intentionally erased Güemes from national history.

- **Historical basis:** nineteenth-century national histories often privileged regular armies, Buenos Aires institutions, Belgrano, and San Martín; Güemes’s regional cult developed unevenly.
- **Overstatement:** he was not literally absent from all national histories, and formal national commemoration expanded steadily from the late nineteenth century.
- **Effect:** the “forgotten hero” narrative became a powerful engine of Salta identity and successful campaigns for national recognition.

### Hagiography versus hostile provincial mythology

Salta writers often elevated Güemes as a unifying hero. Jujuy traditions sometimes depicted Salta’s government as tyrannical toward Jujuy autonomy. Merchant and clerical writings described him as a despot; socialist and nationalist writers later recast his opponents as an oligarchic “gente decente.”

Each tradition preserves real conflicts but tends to reduce a fragmented wartime society to heroes and traitors.

## Works by Martín Miguel de Güemes

Güemes wrote no known book or formal treatise. His corpus consists of letters, reports, orders, proclamations, government communications, and administrative papers, often preserved through copies.

### Authentic contemporary writings

- Military correspondence from the Upper Peru campaigns, 1810–1812.
- Petitions concerning his removal and confinement, 1812–1813.
- Reports to San Martín from the northern vanguard, 1814.
- Communications concerning Puesto del Marqués and relations with Rondeau, 1815.
- Government orders, military instructions, and proclamations, 1815–1821.
- Correspondence with Manuel Belgrano, especially 1816–1820.
- Correspondence with Juan Martín de Pueyrredón.
- Correspondence with José Rondeau.
- Letters to Carmen Puch and other relatives.
- Correspondence with Juan José Feliciano Fernández Campero.
- Letters to Manuel Eduardo Arias and other subordinate commanders.
- Letter to José Miguel Carrera, 4 September 1820.
- Letter to Facundo Quiroga, 20 November 1820.
- Letter to Francisco Ramírez, 22 December 1820.
- Letter to Bernardo O’Higgins, 16 August 1820.
- Communications concerning acceptance of command of the Ejército de Observación.
- Final-period letters from Sauces and the Tucumán campaign, including letters to Carmen and Domingo Puch dated 26 March 1821.

### Major documentary editions

- **Luis Güemes, editor, *Güemes documentado***, 12 volumes, Buenos Aires, Plus Ultra, 1979–1982. Posthumous documentary compilation, not a work authored as a book by Güemes. It includes family papers, official records, correspondence, military reports, hostile documents, and later testimony.
- **Martín Miguel de Güemes, *Obras completas***, Aguilar edition. Posthumous collected writings; one bibliographical account gives 522 letters and documents.
- **Susana Martorell de Laconi, *Ideario de Martín Güemes***. Modern thematic selection and interpretation of correspondence, reports, and proclamations; not an original Güemes-authored volume.

### Attribution cautions

- Many official letters were drafted, copied, or registered by secretaries; signature and responsibility do not always prove autograph composition.
- Some documents survive only in later copies.
- Exact deathbed speeches are attributed through recollection, not stenographic transcription.
- Aphorisms circulated under Güemes’s name should be checked against *Güemes documentado* before being treated as authentic.

- Official commemorative publications are useful for dates and memorial history but regularly use heroic formulas as historical conclusions.
- Recent social history offers the strongest corrective by treating gauchos, tenants, commanders, women, clergy, merchants, and cabildos as actors with independent interests.

## Death, Burial, and Immediate Aftermath

Güemes was initially buried at El Chamical. His death disrupted the planned advance toward Upper Peru but did not instantly dissolve the militia movement.

Vidt and other commanders continued resistance. Royalist forces withdrew after negotiations and military pressure. Political struggle in Salta persisted:

- Güemes’s followers remained mobilized.
- Macacha, their mother, and other supporters faced imprisonment.
- In September 1821 a military-popular rising, later called the “Revolution of the Women,” helped overthrow Governor José Antonio Fernández Cornejo amid plunder and factional conflict.
- Juan Clímaco Güemes was killed during later violence in 1822.
- Carmen Puch died in April 1822.

The post-Güemes settlement restored portions of elite authority, but rural nonpayment and political mobilization could not simply be reversed.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Nineteenth century

Early national historiography emphasized regular armies and central institutions. Güemes remained better remembered in Salta and Jujuy, where memories divided sharply between patriotic devotion and recollections of coercion.

A major public rehabilitation began with an 1885 Salta commemoration organized by historian Ángel Justiniano Carranza. Provincial historians, descendants, veterans’ memories, and nationalist writers enlarged the documentary and ceremonial record.

### Nationalization of the hero

- 1905: Lugones’s *La guerra gaucha* popularized the conflict nationally.
- 1909: decision to erect a major monument in Salta.
- 1910: foundation stone placed.
- 1918: remains transferred to Salta Cathedral’s Panteón de las Glorias del Norte.
- 1920: President Hipólito Yrigoyen created a centennial homage commission.
- 1931: Víctor Garino’s monument inaugurated under the de facto presidency of José Félix Uriburu—a political irony noted in official heritage history.
- 1946: the Salta cathedral burial site was protected as a national historic sepulcher.
- 1956 onward: the **Guardia bajo las Estrellas** became an annual vigil.
- 1971: Torre Nilsson’s film gave the story a durable mass-media form.
- 1999: Law 25,172 declared 17 June the National Day of Latin American Liberty.
- 2006: Law 26,125 declared Güemes a Hero of the Argentine Nation.
- 2016: Law 27,258 made 17 June a national holiday.
- Twenty-first century: the federal state, Salta government, Army, Gendarmería, gaucho associations, schools, and museums institutionalized annual ceremonies.

### Military legacy

His operational legacy lies in:

- decentralized cavalry;
- intimate use of terrain;
- intelligence from civilians and local networks;
- resource denial;
- avoiding strategically unnecessary pitched battles;
- political protection of fighters’ households;
- sustaining resistance despite limited central finance.

It would be anachronistic to describe this as a fully modern doctrine, but later Argentine military institutions have adopted Güemes as a model of frontier and irregular defense.

### Social and political legacy

Competing interpretations portray him as:

- provincial autonomist;
- early federalist;
- popular caudillo;
- elite officer who negotiated with rural insurgency;
- architect of a socially radical wartime coalition;
- military governor who damaged civil law and property;
- continental strategist supporting San Martín;
- regional leader pulled into destructive civil war.

These interpretations are not mutually exclusive. They emphasize different documented aspects of the same coalition and crisis.

### Cult and symbolic associations

Güemes is associated with:

- red-and-black Infernales uniforms;
- the poncho and mounted gaucho;
- the Cerro San Bernardo monument;
- the Guardia bajo las Estrellas;
- “the northern shield” of San Martín’s continental campaign;
- provincial dignity against Buenos Aires centralism;
- popular military citizenship;
- the sacrifice of Salta and Jujuy in the independence war.

The cult merges the historical governor with the anonymous gauchos, women, Indigenous people, Afro-descendant soldiers, tenants, laborers, and small producers whose contributions were long subsumed under his name.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted record are listed. Mere contemporaneity, shared nationality, or appearance in later films is excluded.

### Manuel Belgrano — documented fact

Belgrano was Güemes’s commander, early disciplinary antagonist, later strategic collaborator, advocate, and extensive correspondent. In 1812 he caused Güemes to be sent away following the Juana Inguanzo accusation. From 1816 until Belgrano’s final illness, they coordinated the northern defense through a large surviving correspondence, principally from Tucumán to Salta.

### José de San Martín — documented fact

They worked together in the Army of the North in 1814. San Martín rehabilitated Güemes, placed him over the northern vanguard, and incorporated the Salta defense into his continental strategy. In 1820 he appointed Güemes general-in-chief of the Ejército de Observación sobre el Perú. Their relationship is documented by official correspondence and surviving letters.

### Juan Martín de Pueyrredón y O’Doggan — documented fact

Pueyrredón employed and commended Güemes during the retreat after Huaqui in 1811. As Supreme Director he corresponded with him, supplied limited support, received reports through Belgrano, and dealt with complaints concerning Güemes’s authority.

### Carlos María de Alvear — reported political connection, not a documented personal relationship

Alvear’s fall as Supreme Director in April 1815 created the political vacuum in which Salta selected Güemes as governor. No consulted source proves an important personal meeting, collaboration, or correspondence. The connection is institutional and causal, not interpersonal.

### Bernardo O’Higgins Riquelme — documented correspondence

Güemes wrote to O’Higgins on 16 August 1820 concerning the force he was preparing in coordination with San Martín’s expedition to Peru. This is a documented continental military-political connection; no personal meeting was found.

### Juana Azurduy de Padilla — reported and partly documented military connection

After Manuel Ascencio Padilla’s death, Azurduy’s surviving force and cause became connected with Güemes’s northern command. Institutional sources report that Güemes gave her a military uniform and incorporated or supported her within the frontier resistance. The consulted record does not establish a sustained personal correspondence or the frequency of face-to-face meetings. Her appearance alongside Güemes in the 1971 film is artistic memory, not evidence of a particular meeting.

### María Remedios del Valle — no verified direct connection found

Both served within the broad revolutionary military world of the Army of the North, but no consulted source documented a meeting, correspondence, command relationship, or acknowledged personal influence between her and Güemes. Shared institutional space alone is insufficient.

### Domingo Faustino Sarmiento — no lifetime connection found

Sarmiento belonged to a younger generation and did not have a documented relationship with Güemes before 1821. Later intellectual treatments of caudillismo do not constitute a personal connection.

### Simón Bolívar, Antonio José de Sucre, Simón Rodríguez, Manuela Sáenz, José Antonio de Sucre, and Francisco de Miranda — no direct documented connection found

Güemes’s projected campaign formed part of the wider liberation of South America, but no consulted source established a personal meeting or direct correspondence with these roster members. Strategic convergence is not recorded here as interpersonal contact.
