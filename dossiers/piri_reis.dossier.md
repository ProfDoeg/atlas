# Muhiddin Piri: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

Muhiddin Piri—better known as Pîrî Reis—lived in the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries. No continuous contemporary biography, personal archive, household register, or securely identified portrait survives. His life must be reconstructed from:

- statements in his own maps and *Kitâb-ı Bahriyye*;
- Ottoman chronicles, fiscal and administrative records;
- Portuguese chronicles and reports concerning the Indian Ocean;
- surviving manuscripts;
- later Ottoman geographical literature;
- modern historical and cartographic scholarship.

Dates before approximately 1510 are particularly uncertain. Modern biographies frequently turn conjectures into facts; this dossier retains the uncertainty.

Evidentiary labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a contemporary object, text, archival record, or mutually reinforcing primary evidence.
- **Reported fact:** found in a generally credible historical source but not independently established here from the original record.
- **Probable reconstruction:** a conclusion accepted by substantial scholarship but dependent on incomplete evidence.
- **Disputed claim:** supported and challenged by identifiable scholarly interpretations.
- **Allegation:** an accusation made by a contemporary or later interested party.
- **Rumor or myth:** a story whose evidentiary basis is absent or inadequate, but whose circulation is historically significant.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name:** Commonly reconstructed as Pîrî Muhyiddin ibn Hacı Mehmed; also Muhyiddin Piri or Ahmed Muhyiddin Piri.

**Ottoman forms:** Pîrî Reʾîs, Pîrî Reis, Piri Reis.

**Name and titles:**

- **Pîrî/Piri:** personal name or shortened form associated with Muhyiddin Piri.
- **Reis/Reʾîs:** captain, shipmaster, or naval commander; not a hereditary surname.
- **Pîrî Kethüdâ:** an administrative designation appearing in connection with him, interpreted as evidence that he served at the Gallipoli naval establishment.
- **Hint kaptanı:** commander of the Ottoman Indian Ocean or Suez fleet late in his career.
- Modern descriptions such as “admiral,” “cartographer,” “geographer,” “navigator,” “hydrographer,” and “corsair” are broadly descriptive rather than exact equivalents of a single lifelong rank.

**Birth:** Usually placed between approximately 1465 and 1470.

**Birthplace:** Gallipoli—Ottoman Gelibolu—on the Dardanelles is the dominant scholarly identification. Some later accounts say Karaman or infer Karamanid ancestry, but surviving evidence does not securely establish a Karaman birthplace.

**Death:** Probably late 1553, conventionally in Cairo, by execution on an order issued within the Ottoman governing structure of Egypt.

**Religious and political affiliation:** A Muslim subject and naval servant of the Ottoman Empire. His own writing employs the confessional vocabulary of Ottoman maritime warfare.

**Primary professions:** Mariner, corsair or raider, naval captain, fleet commander, pilot, hydrographer, cartographic compiler, author, and chartmaker.

The most reliable concise modern Turkish biographical synthesis is İdris Bostan’s entry in the *Türkiye Diyanet Vakfı İslâm Ansiklopedisi*. It emphasizes both the strength of the documentation for Piri’s mature career and the uncertainty surrounding his origins.

## Family and Ancestry

### Father

**Reported fact:** Piri’s father is named in later biographical literature as Hacı Mehmed. “Hacı” indicates that he had performed the pilgrimage to Mecca, but little else can be securely reconstructed about him.

No reliable evidence establishes his father’s occupation, wealth, exact residence, or role in Piri’s upbringing.

### Kemal Reis

**Documented or strongly reported relationship:** Piri identifies the celebrated Ottoman seaman Kemal Reis as his close elder relative. Most scholarship calls Kemal his paternal uncle, although Ottoman kinship terminology and later transmission leave limited room for uncertainty.

Kemal Reis was the decisive patron and practical instructor in Piri’s early life. He had participated in Ottoman naval warfare and independent maritime raiding before Sultan Bayezid II formally drew him into imperial service around 1495–96.

### Claimed Karamanid ancestry

**Disputed claim:** Some modern Turkish accounts describe the family as originating in Karaman and migrating to Gallipoli after the Ottoman conquest or population transfers. This may reflect traditions surrounding Gallipoli’s settlement by maritime families. It is not supported by a securely identified contemporary genealogical document.

### Spouses, partners, children, and siblings

No spouse, partner, child, or sibling can presently be identified from reliable published evidence. Claims that he founded a continuing family line are unsupported unless accompanied by new archival documentation.

His mature career and possession of ships or movable wealth do not establish whether he maintained a household in Gallipoli, Cairo, Suez, or elsewhere.

## Childhood and Early Life

Piri probably grew up in Gallipoli, the principal Ottoman naval base on the Dardanelles. The town exposed him to shipbuilding, Mediterranean pilots, portolan charts, sailors speaking several languages, naval administration, and the movement of military and commercial vessels between Istanbul and the Aegean.

No contemporary source supplies:

- an exact birth date;
- a childhood address;
- information about his mother;
- a formal account of his literacy training;
- evidence of attendance at a medrese;
- the date of his first voyage.

### Entry into seafaring

**Probable reconstruction:** Piri went to sea under Kemal Reis while still young, perhaps in his teens or early twenties. His later competence in sailing directions and coastal topography implies prolonged practical observation rather than exclusively book-based study.

Piri’s own retrospective account says that he and Kemal sailed widely in the Mediterranean and fought enemies of their faith. This is firsthand testimony to the general character of his apprenticeship, although it is not a dated logbook.

---

## Education and Formation

No named schoolteacher, religious scholar, or cartographic master is securely documented. His education appears to have combined several forms of training.

### Practical seamanship

Under Kemal Reis, Piri learned:

- coastal navigation;
- recognition of capes, islands, channels, reefs, shoals, and anchorages;
- winds and seasonal sailing conditions;
- piloting by landmarks;
- fleet operations;
- amphibious warfare;
- intelligence gathering from captives and foreign sailors.

### Cartographic formation

Piri worked within the Mediterranean portolan-chart tradition. The 1513 world map demonstrates access to multiple cartographic cultures, including:

- Italian or Catalan-style portolan charts;
- Portuguese charts of Africa and the Atlantic;
- Spanish information about the Americas;
- older world maps or *mappae mundi*;
- Islamic geographical and nautical traditions.

The surviving map’s own inscription says it was compiled from approximately twenty source maps, including maps associated with Alexander’s age, Arabic maps, Portuguese charts, and a chart connected with Christopher Columbus. “Alexander’s age” should not be read literally as proof that Piri possessed a fourth-century-BCE nautical chart: early modern Islamic writers often used “Alexander” as a chronological or legendary marker for ancient geographical knowledge.

### Literary collaboration

The expanded 1526 recension of *Kitâb-ı Bahriyye* contains a long verse introduction. Scholarship attributes substantial responsibility for versifying or shaping this material to the poet and compiler Seyyid Muradî. Piri supplied the geographical and autobiographical substance; Muradî appears to have helped recast it into a courtly presentation work.

## Early Career: Mediterranean Corsair and Ottoman Captain

### Service with Kemal Reis

From approximately the 1480s or early 1490s, Piri accompanied Kemal Reis on Mediterranean expeditions. Their exact itinerary is difficult to reconstruct because later biographies combine Piri’s autobiographical passages, Kemal’s career, and general Ottoman naval movements.

Activities attributed to their group include operations around:

- the Aegean islands;
- the western Mediterranean;
- the coasts of Italy and Sicily;
- Sardinia and Corsica;
- North Africa;
- Iberian waters;
- the Balearic Islands.

It is not safe to assume that Piri participated personally in every voyage attributed to Kemal Reis.

### Muslims and Jews expelled from Iberia

**Reported fact, with uncertain individual participation:** Kemal Reis is traditionally credited with helping transport Muslims and Jews escaping or expelled from Spain after the fall of Granada and during the expulsions of the 1490s. Piri probably served with him during parts of this period, but surviving evidence does not identify individual refugee voyages commanded by Piri.

The episode later became part of a Turkish national narrative contrasting Ottoman refuge with Iberian persecution. The underlying expulsions and Ottoman maritime assistance are historical; the scope of Piri’s personal involvement remains uncertain.

### Incorporation into the imperial navy, 1495–96

Sultan Bayezid II summoned Kemal Reis into formal state service around 901 AH/1495–96. Piri was probably with him. This marked the transition from semi-independent or frontier corsairing to service within the Ottoman fleet.

### Ottoman–Venetian War, 1499–1503

Piri is generally reported as participating under Kemal Reis in the naval war against Venice. Operations associated with the fleet included:

- the Battle of Zonchio or Sapienza in 1499;
- operations near Modon, Coron, and Lepanto;
- the Battle of Modon in 1500;
- attacks upon Venetian maritime possessions.

The degree to which Piri held independent command during individual engagements is not consistently documented.

### Death of Kemal Reis, approximately 1510–11

Kemal Reis died when ships in his fleet were lost in a storm, usually dated to 1510 or 1511. Piri’s life thereafter entered a more literary and cartographic phase, although he remained a naval officer.

Later accounts say he returned to Gallipoli and organized the geographical knowledge accumulated during his voyages.

## Chronological Life History

## 1511–1513: Compilation of the First World Map

Piri compiled his first world map at Gallipoli, completing it in Muharram 919 AH, corresponding to 1513.

Only its western portion survives. It includes:

- the Atlantic Ocean;
- western Europe and northwest Africa;
- parts of West Africa;
- Atlantic islands;
- the Caribbean;
- the eastern coast of Central and South America;
- a large southward-curving landmass.

The parchment fragment measures roughly 86 by 61–64 centimeters, depending on the cataloguing convention. It is often called “gazelle skin,” but the species identification is not consistently documented in conservation literature; “parchment” is safer.

The map contains Ottoman Turkish inscriptions, ships, rulers, animals, mountains, and narrative episodes. Its legends identify Piri as its maker and explain his compilation from numerous earlier sources.

### Columbus material

One inscription states that the western coasts and islands were taken from a map of “Colombo.” Another recounts information supplied by a Spanish captive who had supposedly sailed with Columbus and later entered Kemal Reis’s possession.

**Documented fact:** The inscription exists and claims a Columbian source.

**Unresolved question:** No source map belonging to Columbus has survived that can be directly compared with Piri’s exemplar.

Paul Kahle argued that the map preserved information from Columbus’s third voyage. Gregory McIntosh’s later reconstruction instead associates its Columbian geography mainly with the second voyage. Both positions accept substantial Columbian influence but differ over the specific voyage and intermediate chart.

## 1516–17: Ottoman Conquest of Egypt

Piri served as a ship captain in the fleet accompanying Sultan Selim I’s campaign against the Mamluk Sultanate. The fleet was commanded by Cafer Bey.

He reached Cairo by way of the Nile and, according to the standard reconstruction, presented the 1513 world map to Selim in 1517. What Selim did with it is unknown. There is no evidence that it guided a major Ottoman Atlantic expedition.

The map subsequently disappeared from the historical record until its twentieth-century rediscovery.

## 1521: First Recension of *Kitâb-ı Bahriyye*

Piri completed the first version of his Mediterranean sailing guide in 927–28 AH/1521–22. It contained roughly 130 chapters.

Unlike a decorative court atlas alone, the work was designed as a practical pilot book. It recorded:

- harbors and anchorages;
- coastal silhouettes and landmarks;
- shoals, reefs, and rocks;
- prevailing winds;
- water sources;
- distances;
- passages between islands;
- towns, fortifications, ruins, and local products;
- occasional historical and legendary narratives.

The first recension is valued as the version closest to Piri’s own prose. The work combined personal observation, reports from other sailors, earlier charts, and accumulated Mediterranean pilotage.

## 1521: Belgrade campaign

Piri reportedly served with the Danube fleet during Sultan Süleyman I’s campaign against Belgrade. Details of his precise command are sparse.

## 1522: Siege of Rhodes

Piri participated in the Ottoman expedition against the Knights Hospitaller on Rhodes. His knowledge of Aegean waters would have been directly relevant to the campaign.

## 1524: Voyage with Grand Vizier Ibrahim Pasha

Piri piloted or commanded the vessel carrying Grand Vizier Pargalı/Makbul Ibrahim Pasha toward Egypt, where Ibrahim was to restore order after the revolt of Hain Ahmed Pasha.

Bad weather caused Ibrahim to continue overland after Rhodes. During the voyage he examined Piri’s earlier *Kitâb-ı Bahriyye* material and encouraged the production of an enlarged court version.

This contact was important patronage. It connected a working pilot book to the highest level of the Ottoman court.

## 1525–26: Expanded *Kitâb-ı Bahriyye*

Piri returned to Gallipoli and prepared a greatly expanded version, completed in 932 AH/1525–26 and presented to Süleyman.

The second recension had approximately 210 chapters and a long metrical introduction of about 972 couplets, followed by the detailed prose coastal guide. Seyyid Muradî is generally credited with literary assistance in the verse sections.

The introduction surveys:

- the shape and extent of the earth and seas;
- navigation and winds;
- the compass and charts;
- Portuguese voyages around Africa;
- Spanish voyages to the Americas;
- the Indian Ocean;
- the strategic importance of Hormuz.

Many surviving illuminated manuscripts are later copies. Variations in chapter sequence, number, decoration, and supplementary maps mean that no single late manuscript can automatically be treated as Piri’s autograph.

## 1528: Second World Map

Piri produced and presented a second world map to Sultan Süleyman, conventionally dated 1528.

Only a fragment survives, showing:

- Greenland;
- Newfoundland and Labrador;
- part of North America;
- Florida;
- Cuba and Hispaniola;
- portions of Central America.

Its geography incorporates information from post-Columbian voyages and is generally more technically developed than the 1513 map. The fragment is held at the Topkapı Palace Museum Library, Hazine 1824.

The missing portions may have shown a much larger part of the world, but their extent and fate are unknown.

## 1530s: Naval administration and Mediterranean service

The title **Pîrî Kethüdâ** has been interpreted to mean that Piri held an office connected with the Gallipoli arsenal or naval establishment.

He later served in the reorganized Ottoman fleet associated with Hayreddin Barbarossa. An Ottoman record places Piri among the commanders assembled at Avlonya on 22 August 1537 for the Corfu campaign; he received a robe of honor.

It is unclear whether he participated in the 1538 Battle of Preveza. His association with Barbarossa’s wider naval establishment is documented more securely than his personal presence in every famous engagement.

## 1540s: Appointment to the Suez/Indian Ocean command

Piri eventually became commander of the Ottoman fleet based at Suez, often called the captaincy of Egypt or India. Dating differs among accounts, with approximately 1547–48 commonly proposed.

He was by then elderly—probably in his late seventies or eighties if the conventional birth range is correct. The appointment nevertheless placed him at the center of Ottoman-Portuguese rivalry in the Red Sea, Arabian Sea, and Persian Gulf.

The command’s strategic responsibilities included:

- protecting the Red Sea approaches and pilgrimage routes;
- supplying Ottoman positions in Yemen and southern Arabia;
- contesting Portuguese control of maritime trade;
- cooperating with the governors of Egypt and Basra;
- maintaining a fleet whose hulls, rowers, artillery, and supplies were divided between distant bases.

## 1548: Capture of Aden

**Reported fact:** Piri led Ottoman forces that recovered Aden from a local ruler who had challenged Ottoman authority. The operation restored a strategically important entrance to the Red Sea.

Sources differ over the precise sequence and degree of resistance.

## 1549–51: Preparations against Portuguese positions

Ottoman planning focused on removing Portuguese power from Muscat and Hormuz. Piri assembled ships, weapons, soldiers, and rowers at Suez. Coordination was inherently difficult because Basra lay beyond the Portuguese-monitored Strait of Hormuz and ships could not easily return to Suez against seasonal winds.

## 1552: Expedition to Muscat and Hormuz

Piri departed Suez on 23 April 1552 with approximately thirty vessels; reported totals vary because sources distinguish galleys, galliots, transports, and auxiliary craft differently.

### Muscat

The Ottoman fleet attacked Portuguese-held Muscat. The fort or settlement fell after resistance, and the position was plundered or destroyed. Portuguese personnel were killed or captured.

### Hormuz

Piri then proceeded to Hormuz, the island fortress controlling the entrance to the Persian Gulf. He besieged it but failed to capture the Portuguese fort.

Factors reported in Ottoman and Portuguese sources include:

- the fortress’s strength;
- effective Portuguese resistance;
- shortage of Ottoman supplies;
- expectation of reinforcements from Portuguese India;
- inadequate coordination with Ottoman authorities at Basra;
- uncertainty over whether Piri possessed orders to besiege Hormuz immediately or first unite with other forces.

He lifted the siege after roughly three weeks.

### Qeshm raid and booty

Piri attacked Qeshm, a wealthy commercial dependency and supply center of Hormuz. A contemporary account quoted in *Encyclopaedia Iranica* describes the seizure of a very large quantity of gold, silver, cash, and merchandise.

This booty became central to later accusations that Piri had placed private enrichment above the imperial objective.

### Arrival at Basra

Piri brought most of the fleet to Basra, where relations with Governor Kubad Pasha were hostile. Piri expected manpower and logistical assistance for a return voyage. Kubad either could not or would not provide the requested rowers and support.

Portuguese naval pressure and the seasonal wind regime complicated any attempt to return the entire galley fleet to Suez.

## 1552–53: Departure from Basra

Piri left most of the fleet at Basra and sailed back toward Egypt with only three galleys laden with booty. One vessel was lost or abandoned during the passage, while two reached Suez.

The decision proved fatal to him politically.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

Piri lived before modern companies and patent systems. No corporation, commercial partnership, patent, or formal investment portfolio is known.

### Ottoman naval institutions

His career intersected with:

- the Gallipoli naval arsenal;
- the central Ottoman fleet;
- the Danube flotilla;
- the Suez fleet;
- Ottoman administrations in Egypt, Yemen, Aden, and Basra.

### Ships and private economic interests

Early Ottoman corsair captains often equipped or shared ownership in vessels, received prize shares, and combined state service with maritime raiding. Kemal Reis certainly operated within this world. Piri probably benefited from prize-taking, but no ledger establishes his personal shipholdings or ordinary income.

### Cartographic technology

Piri did not claim to have invented a new projection, navigational instrument, or surveying method. His achievement was synthetic:

- reconciling charts of different scales and conventions;
- incorporating recent Atlantic discoveries;
- combining images and written sailing directions;
- converting accumulated pilot knowledge into an Ottoman-language maritime encyclopedia.

No patent or exclusive legal right attached to his work.

### Wealth and confiscation

Piri was said to be wealthy at the end of his life. The extent and source of that wealth are uncertain.

**Documented context:** The Qeshm expedition produced valuable booty.

**Allegation:** Hostile accounts associated the booty with personal avarice, bribery, or illicit arrangements.

**Probable consequence:** His property was confiscated after execution, as was common in Ottoman capital cases involving high officials. Exact inventories have not been located in the sources consulted.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Ottoman imperial service

Piri’s career moved from frontier corsairing into increasingly formal imperial service. His maps and texts served several overlapping purposes:

- navigation;
- military intelligence;
- imperial geography;
- courtly display;
- demonstration that Ottoman readers could appropriate Iberian discoveries.

### Religious language

Piri described conflict against Christian maritime powers in conventional Islamic terms. This does not by itself reveal private theology, Sufi affiliation, or sectarian commitments. No reliable evidence associates him with a particular religious order.

### Intellectual method

His work was cumulative rather than doctrinal. He valued:

- eyewitness observation;
- interrogation of experienced sailors;
- comparison of charts;
- preservation of practical local knowledge;
- incorporation of foreign discoveries without suppressing their origin.

This was not modern scientific source criticism. Legends, wonders, monsters, saints’ tales, and hearsay appear beside accurate sailing directions because early sixteenth-century geography did not sharply separate those categories.

### Artistic dimensions

Surviving copies of *Kitâb-ı Bahriyye* are celebrated for painted maps, coastal profiles, city views, compass roses, and decorative ships. Much of the illumination in later manuscripts was produced by copyists and court artists rather than by Piri personally. The geographical conception is his; the hand of every illustration is not.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Kemal Reis

**Documented/strongly reported:** Uncle, mentor, commander, and patron. Kemal supplied Piri with practical training, access to prizes and captives, and entry into imperial naval service.

### Sultan Bayezid II

**Reported fact:** Bayezid summoned Kemal Reis into state service in 1495–96; Piri probably entered formal service through the same patronage.

### Sultan Selim I

**Reported fact:** Piri served during the Egyptian conquest and presented Selim with the 1513 world map in Cairo in 1517.

### Sultan Süleyman I

**Documented through the presentation tradition:** Recipient of the expanded *Kitâb-ı Bahriyye* and the second world map. The final execution occurred under his reign, although the precise chain of authorization is disputed.

### Pargalı Ibrahim Pasha

**Strongly reported:** Grand vizier who examined Piri’s book during the 1524 Egypt voyage and encouraged its expanded presentation version.

### Seyyid Muradî

**Scholarly attribution:** Literary collaborator or redactor of the metrical portions of the expanded *Kitâb-ı Bahriyye*. The prose navigational substance remains associated with Piri.

### Hayreddin Barbarossa

**Documented institutional association:** Piri served in the reorganized fleet associated with Barbarossa and was present at the Avlonya assembly in 1537. Evidence does not establish a close personal friendship.

### Kubad Pasha

**Documented political conflict:** Governor of Basra during the Hormuz expedition. His refusal or failure to support Piri’s proposed return became part of the crisis leading to Piri’s execution. Ottoman historians have often treated Kubad’s accusations as politically interested.

### Semiz Ali Pasha

**Reported role:** Governor of Egypt associated in several accounts with Piri’s prosecution and execution. Some narratives say Piri failed to give him an expected share of booty or bribe; this motive is an allegation, not an established fact.

### Portuguese commanders

Piri’s operational opponents included Portuguese officers at Muscat and Hormuz and the Estado da Índia administration. Portuguese governor Afonso de Noronha organized a large relief fleet; his nephew Antão de Noronha commanded forces sent toward Hormuz.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, and Financial Questions

## Corsairing, captivity, and violence

**Documented historical activity:** Piri openly participated in Mediterranean raiding and religiously framed naval warfare. Such operations included seizure of ships, enslavement or captivity, destruction of settlements, and division of booty.

Modern celebratory accounts often substitute “explorer” for “corsair,” obscuring this aspect of his career. Conversely, applying modern piracy law without regard to Ottoman letters of authority and contemporary maritime warfare is anachronistic. His activity crossed the fluid boundary between private raiding and state naval service.

No surviving court proceeding accuses Piri of piracy under Ottoman law.

## Violence at Muscat and Qeshm

**Documented general fact:** The 1552 campaign involved assault, killing, capture, looting, and destruction.

**Unresolved details:** Ottoman and Portuguese narratives differ about troop numbers, casualties, surrender terms, and the treatment of captives. Both bodies of sources were shaped by imperial rivalry.

## Failure at Hormuz

The failure to capture Hormuz was the most consequential defeat of Piri’s career.

Competing explanations include:

1. **Military explanation:** The Portuguese fortress was too strong, Ottoman supplies were inadequate, and a relief force was expected.
2. **Operational explanation:** Piri attacked before coordinating with Basra and lacked the resources to sustain the siege.
3. **Allegation of bribery:** Some accounts claim the Portuguese paid him to withdraw.
4. **Allegation of avarice:** Critics said he preferred looting Qeshm and preserving booty to pursuing the strategic objective.
5. **Defensive reconstruction:** Later historians argue that withdrawing from an untenable siege and leaving ships safely at Basra was militarily rational.

No extant judicial dossier settles these alternatives.

## Abandonment of the fleet

**Documented fact:** Piri left the bulk of his fleet at Basra and returned with only a few ships.

**Official accusation:** This was treated as abandonment of command and failure to execute orders.

**Defense:** Seasonal winds, lack of rowers, damaged ships, and Portuguese naval interception may have made a mass return impossible. The fleet itself was not simply discarded: it remained an Ottoman force at Basra and later came under Murad Reis and Seydi Ali Reis.

## Booty and alleged corruption

Piri returned with treasure from Qeshm.

- **Documented:** Rich booty was taken.
- **Allegation:** He retained an improper share.
- **Allegation:** He bribed or attempted to bribe officials.
- **Counter-allegation:** Governor Kubad Pasha and the Egyptian administration denounced him because of disputes over division of the booty.
- **Unresolved:** No surviving audited account fixes the lawful imperial share, Piri’s entitlement, or what he actually delivered.

## Trial and execution

Piri was executed in Cairo, probably in late 1553. Some catalogues give 1554 because of chronological conversion or later narrative traditions.

The procedure is obscure. He may have been examined before the provincial council of Egypt rather than receiving a modern-style public trial. Accusations probably included:

- raising the siege without authorization;
- failure to take Hormuz;
- leaving the main fleet at Basra;
- returning with treasure in only three vessels;
- disobedience or dereliction of command.

Turkish historian Cengiz Orhonlu and later researchers noted that sixteenth-century writers criticized Piri’s military conduct but did not associate the execution with his cartographic work.

### Was Süleyman personally responsible?

**Documented political fact:** The execution occurred under Süleyman’s sovereign authority.

**Unresolved:** The surviving record does not reveal how fully the sultan reviewed the evidence or whether the decisive initiative came from Egypt, Basra, or central officials.

### “Executed for drawing the true world”

**Myth:** Piri was killed because his map revealed forbidden geographical knowledge.

No contemporary source connects his execution to either world map. The chronology alone weighs heavily against the story: the first map had been presented approximately forty years earlier and the second about twenty-five years earlier. His prosecution followed directly upon the Hormuz campaign.

## Dedicated Adversarial Research Pass

Searches were conducted using Piri’s names together with terms equivalent to controversy, scandal, accusations, fraud, corruption, bribery, conspiracy, criticism, crime, and lawsuit in English and Turkish, including material circulating in 2024–26.

Findings:

- No modern lawsuit or financial fraud can apply to a sixteenth-century individual.
- The substantial historical controversies concern the Hormuz failure, seized booty, alleged bribery, abandonment of the Basra fleet, and the fairness of the execution.
- Modern negative controversy is concentrated less on Piri personally than on exaggerated claims made about his map.
- Recent online discussions continue to allege that the map proves knowledge of ice-free Antarctica, aerial surveying, extraterrestrial intervention, Bimini, or a suppressed Ice Age civilization.
- No new primary documentation published in the most recent two years was found that alters the established reconstruction of Piri’s life or death.
- Recent social-media allegations were not used as factual sources; their persistence is noted as evidence of continuing mythmaking.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## The Antarctica claim

### Story

The southern landmass on the 1513 map is said to depict the coast of Antarctica without its ice sheet, supposedly proving that an advanced prehistoric civilization mapped the continent thousands of years ago.

### Emergence and circulation

Retired engineer Arlington Mallery promoted an Antarctic interpretation in the mid-twentieth century. Charles Hapgood developed it in *Maps of the Ancient Sea Kings* (1966), connecting the map with a lost civilization and a proposed shifting of Earth’s crust. Erich von Däniken and later Graham Hancock carried versions into ancient-astronaut and lost-civilization literature.

### Evidence cited for it

Proponents point to:

- the apparent coastline south of Brazil;
- claimed resemblance to Queen Maud Land;
- supposed longitudinal accuracy;
- Piri’s reference to ancient maps.

### Evidence against it

Gregory McIntosh and other historians of cartography identify the land as a distorted continuation of South America, shaped by the map’s source charts, limited parchment space, and contemporary conventions. The map does not label it Antarctica. Its inscriptions associate southern regions with warm climates and familiar early-modern marvels, not a polar ice sheet.

Piri’s “ancient” sources do not demonstrate prehistoric global surveying. The map contains numerous ordinary sixteenth-century distortions and is not geometrically compatible with modern aerial or satellite mapping.

**Assessment:** Rumor or modern pseudo-historical theory; unsupported by the artifact’s inscriptions, cartographic context, or geology.

### Reputational effect

The claim made Piri globally famous outside specialist history. It simultaneously brought attention to a genuine cartographic masterpiece and displaced his real achievement with a story about hidden technology.

## Extraterrestrial or aerial survey

**Rumor:** The map could only have been produced from the air or from space.

There is no supporting evidence. Its mixed scales, duplicated features, displaced islands, and distorted coasts are characteristic of compilation from early modern charts, not aerial observation.

## Bimini Road and Ice Age Bahamas

Some recent alternative-history discussions claim that the map depicts the Bimini Road or the exposed Grand Bahama Bank during lower Ice Age sea levels.

No accepted cartographic analysis identifies Bimini Road on the map. The claimed match depends on selecting ambiguous island shapes and relocating them outside the map’s historical coordinate context.

**Assessment:** Unverified modern claim.

## Perfect longitude

**Myth:** Piri mapped longitude with accuracy impossible before the marine chronometer.

The map does not preserve a uniformly accurate modern longitude grid. Apparent local matches result from selective comparison, transformations, and the comparatively good coastal data in some Portuguese and Spanish sources.

## A direct surviving map by Columbus

Piri explicitly says he used a chart of Columbus. This is not simply a later myth.

**Documented:** Piri’s inscription.

**Disputed:** Whether his immediate source was an autograph Columbus chart, a copy, a derivative, or a composite chart.

Kahle called the surviving fragment effectively a witness to a lost Columbus map. McIntosh argues that the Columbian component can be isolated but that earlier reconstructions assigned it to the wrong voyage.

## The captive who sailed three times with Columbus

Piri records information attributed to a Spanish captive of Kemal Reis who said that he had voyaged with Columbus.

**Documented:** The story appears on Piri’s map.

**Unverified:** The captive’s identity, truthfulness, number of voyages, and possession of the source map.

The episode is valuable evidence for how Atlantic information could travel through capture, oral testimony, and chart exchange, but it cannot be independently confirmed.

## Saint Brendan’s island

The map includes a narrative derived from the medieval legend of Saint Brendan, in which monks or voyagers mistake a great fish or whale for an island and light a fire upon it.

Piri probably copied this from a European source chart rather than inventing it. The legend demonstrates the coexistence of accurate recent discovery, medieval Christian folklore, and Ottoman compilation.

## Alexander the Great’s maps

Piri’s source statement mentions charts “from the time of Alexander.”

**Later literal reading:** He possessed authentic maps made for Alexander the Great.

**Historical reading:** “Alexander” functioned as an emblem of ancient geography and universal kingship; the wording cannot authenticate the date of the underlying charts.

## Discovery by Atatürk

Popular retellings sometimes say Mustafa Kemal Atatürk personally discovered the map.

**Documented:** The fragment was found during cataloguing work at Topkapı Palace in 1929, associated with museum director Halil Edhem and scholar Adolf Deissmann. Paul Kahle recognized and publicized its cartographic importance. Atatürk took a strong interest and supported study and facsimile publication.

**Assessment:** Atatürk was an influential patron of its modern reception, not the physical discoverer.

## Works by Muhiddin Piri

## 1. First World Map

**Original title:** No formal title survives; conventionally *Pîrî Reis’in 1513 Dünya Haritası* or the Piri Reis World Map.

**Date:** Muharram 919 AH/1513.

**Language:** Ottoman Turkish inscriptions.

**Medium:** Painted portolan-style chart on parchment.

**Survival:** Only the western fragment is known, Topkapı Palace Museum Library, Revan 1633 mükerrer.

**Authorship:** Genuine; signed or identified in its inscription by Piri.

**Sources claimed by Piri:** Approximately twenty maps and world charts, including Portuguese and Arabic material and a chart of Columbus.

## 2. *Kitâb-ı Bahriyye*, first recension

**Title:** كتاب بحرية — *Kitâb-ı Bahriyye* (“Book of Maritime Matters,” commonly “Book of Navigation” or “Book of the Sea”).

**Date:** 927–28 AH/1521–22.

**Form:** Approximately 130 chapters, prose sailing directions with charts.

**Authorship:** Substantially genuine and regarded as closer to Piri’s own direct composition than the expanded court recension.

**Survival:** Multiple manuscript witnesses and derivative copies; relationships among manuscripts remain an area of textual scholarship.

## 3. *Kitâb-ı Bahriyye*, expanded recension

**Date:** 932 AH/1525–26.

**Form:** Approximately 210 chapters; a long verse introduction followed by prose descriptions and charts.

**Dedication:** Sultan Süleyman I.

**Authorship:** Piri supplied the maritime and geographical work. Seyyid Muradî is credited with composing or recasting substantial verse portions. It should therefore not be described uncritically as every word being Piri’s unaided composition.

### Major printed editions and translations

- *Bahriye*, edited by Mehmed Şerafeddin, early twentieth-century partial publication.
- *Kitabı Bahriye*, edited by Fevzi Kurdoğlu, Haydar Alpagut, and Fehmi Pekol, Istanbul, 1935.
- *Kitab-ı Bahriye*, four-volume facsimile, transliteration, modern Turkish, and English edition, edited by Ertuğrul Zekâi Ökte with Vahit Çabuk, Tülay Duran, and Robert Bragner, 1988.
- *Le Kitab-ı Bahriye de Piri Reis*, French selections and studies in several modern editions.
- Regional sections have appeared separately in Italian, Greek, Croatian, Arabic, English, and other languages.

Because the work exists in substantially varying manuscripts, many modern publications are editions, translations, selections, or reproductions rather than independent authored works.

## 4. Second World Map

**Date:** 935 AH/1528, sometimes catalogued as 1528–29.

**Medium:** Illustrated parchment chart.

**Survival:** Northwest fragment, Topkapı Palace Museum Library, Hazine 1824.

**Authorship:** Accepted as genuine.

## Works incorrectly or loosely attributed

- Modern compilations titled *The Maps of Piri Reis*, *Piri Reis Map*, or similar are usually facsimile or editorial publications, not separate works authored by him.
- Modern reconstructions of the missing portions of either world map are not Piri’s works.
- Later decorated copies of *Kitâb-ı Bahriyye* reproduce his text and geographic design but often contain illustrations and additions by unidentified copyists.

## Books and Major Works About the Person

### Foundational cartographic scholarship

- Paul Kahle, “A Lost Map of Columbus,” *Geographical Review* 23 (1933), 621–638. Early major study connecting Piri’s chart to Columbian geography.
- Afet İnan, *The Oldest Map of America, Drawn by Piri Reis*, Ankara, 1954. Important to the map’s Turkish republican reception; some formulations have been superseded.
- Svat Soucek, *Piri Reis and Turkish Mapmaking after Columbus*, London, 1992. Major study of Piri within Ottoman and Mediterranean cartography.
- Gregory C. McIntosh, *The Piri Reis Map of 1513*, University of Georgia Press, 2000. The most extensive English monograph devoted to the map; revises Kahle’s Columbian-source argument and rejects Antarctica theories.
- J. B. Harley and David Woodward, eds., *The History of Cartography*, vol. 2, book 1, especially Svat Soucek’s chapter on Islamic charting in the Mediterranean. Major contextual reference.

### Ottoman naval and biographical studies

- İdris Bostan, “Pîrî Reis,” *Türkiye Diyanet Vakfı İslâm Ansiklopedisi*.
- Fikret Sarıcaoğlu, “Kitâb-ı Bahriyye,” *Türkiye Diyanet Vakfı İslâm Ansiklopedisi*.
- Cengiz Orhonlu, “Hint Kaptanlığı ve Pîrî Reis,” *Belleten* 34/134 (1970), 235–254. Fundamental use of Ottoman documentation concerning the final command.
- Salih Özbaran, studies of Ottoman-Portuguese conflict in the Persian Gulf and Indian Ocean.
- Dejanirah Couto, studies of Piri’s 1552 Hormuz campaign and the Basra question using Ottoman and Portuguese documentation.
- Giancarlo Casale, *The Ottoman Age of Exploration*, Oxford University Press, 2010. Places Piri within sixteenth-century Ottoman expansion into the Indian Ocean.

### Studies of *Kitâb-ı Bahriyye*

- Alessandro Bausani, studies of Italy in Piri’s *Kitâb-ı Bahriyye*.
- Robert Mantran, study of the French Mediterranean coast in the work.
- Thomas D. Goodrich, studies of Topkapı maps and supplementary maps in *Kitâb-ı Bahriyye*.
- İdris Bostan, study of imperial-arsenal plans appearing in the work.
- Ayşe Afetinan and later Turkish scholars produced commemorative and cartographic studies, particularly around the 400th anniversary of Piri’s death and UNESCO observances.

### Alternative-history literature

These works are important to reputation, not reliable biographies:

- Charles H. Hapgood, *Maps of the Ancient Sea Kings* (1966).
- Erich von Däniken, *Chariots of the Gods?* (1968).
- Graham Hancock, *Fingerprints of the Gods* (1995).

They transformed Piri’s map into evidence for lost civilizations or extraordinary ancient technology. Their conclusions are rejected in specialist cartographic scholarship.

## Films, Documentaries, Interviews, and Archival Material

### Archival objects

- **Topkapı Palace Museum Library, Revan 1633 mükerrer:** surviving fragment of the 1513 world map.
- **Topkapı Palace Museum Library, Hazine 1824:** surviving fragment of the 1528 world map.
- **Süleymaniye Library, Ayasofya 2612:** important *Kitâb-ı Bahriyye* manuscript.
- **Walters Art Museum, W.658:** richly illustrated seventeenth-century manuscript derived from the expanded recension.
- Further manuscripts are held in Istanbul, Paris, Oxford, London, Dresden, Bologna, Vienna, Baltimore, and other collections.

### Documentary and broadcast treatment

Numerous Turkish television documentaries have been produced under titles equivalent to *Piri Reis*, *Dünya Haritasını Çizen Denizci*, and *Piri Reis ve Haritası*. Production details vary, and many are commemorative rather than source-critical.

The 1513 map has also appeared in programs about:

- ancient astronauts;
- lost civilizations;
- unexplained artifacts;
- Antarctica;
- Columbus and early American cartography.

Such appearances often repeat Hapgood-derived claims without examining the Ottoman inscriptions.

### UNESCO

The 1513 world map was entered in UNESCO’s Memory of the World Register in 2017. The nomination emphasizes its importance as an early cartographic record of Columbian voyages and as a synthesis of Islamic and European geographical knowledge.

### Accessibility

The original map is preserved at Topkapı and is not continuously displayed. Modern access is primarily through facsimiles, exhibition reproductions, digitized manuscripts, and scholarly editions.

## Death

Piri reached Egypt after returning from Basra. He was arrested or subjected to proceedings arising from the failed Hormuz campaign and his departure from the fleet.

He was beheaded in Cairo, probably near the end of 1553. Statements that he was ninety or older depend on accepting the earliest proposed birth dates.

### Cause of death

**Documented general fact:** Judicial or administrative execution by beheading.

### Immediate causes

**Strong reconstruction:** Failure to capture Hormuz, unauthorized withdrawal, departure from the principal fleet, and politically damaging accusations concerning the booty.

### Place

Cairo is overwhelmingly reported. A precise execution site and burial place are not securely identified.

### Burial

No authenticated tomb is known. Modern memorials should not be mistaken for a documented grave.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Sixteenth and seventeenth centuries

Piri’s reputation initially rested more on *Kitâb-ı Bahriyye* than on his world maps. The pilot book was copied, enlarged, decorated, excerpted, and used as a repository of Mediterranean knowledge.

Later Ottoman cartographers and geographers worked in a broader tradition to which Piri contributed, although direct dependence must be demonstrated manuscript by manuscript.

### Rediscovery in 1929

The 1513 fragment was identified during reorganization and cataloguing at Topkapı Palace. German theologian and orientalist Adolf Deissmann drew attention to it, and Paul Kahle recognized its importance for Columbian cartography.

Atatürk encouraged its reproduction and study. In the early Turkish Republic, Piri became a symbol of Ottoman scientific achievement compatible with the state’s promotion of Turkish contributions to world civilization.

### National commemoration

His name has been given to:

- Piri Reis University in Istanbul;
- Turkish naval and research vessels;
- schools, streets, museums, exhibitions, and research centers;
- Turkey’s first domestically built Type 214TN submarine, **TCG Piri Reis**;
- geographical and cartographic commemorations.

UNESCO declared 2013 a commemorative year associated with the five-hundredth anniversary of the 1513 map, greatly increasing international attention.

### Scholarly legacy

His enduring importance lies in four connected areas:

1. documentation of practical Mediterranean navigation;
2. Ottoman reception of Portuguese and Spanish oceanic discoveries;
3. evidence for circulation of Columbian cartography;
4. the integration of maps, sailing instructions, ethnographic notices, history, and legend in an Ottoman maritime text.

### Popular-culture legacy

Outside academic history, Piri is frequently presented as:

- a genius centuries ahead of his time;
- the keeper of a lost ancient archive;
- proof of pre-Columbian global civilization;
- a victim killed for forbidden knowledge.

These images have little relationship to the documented reason for his execution, but they have made his name far more recognizable internationally than that of most Ottoman captains.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are included. Mere contemporaneity has been excluded.

### Christopher Columbus

**Evidentiary label: Documented textual and cartographic connection; no personal meeting documented.**

Piri’s 1513 map states that parts of its American geography were taken from a chart of Columbus. Its longest inscription recounts Columbus’s westward voyage and information from a Spanish captive said to have sailed with him. Piri and Columbus are not known to have met. The connection operated through a lost chart or derivative and oral testimony, probably acquired in the western Mediterranean before Kemal Reis’s death.

### San Brendan de Clonfert / Saint Brendan

**Evidentiary label: Documented legendary-textual connection; not a historical personal connection.**

A Saint Brendan-type story appears among the legends on the 1513 map: seafarers land upon what proves to be a gigantic fish or whale. Piri probably copied the story from a medieval European map. Its presence shows how Christian maritime folklore entered an Ottoman cartographic compilation.

### Alexander the Great

**Evidentiary label: Documented reference; legendary or conventional source claim rather than personal connection.**

Piri’s source note refers to maps dating from the time of Alexander. No evidence demonstrates possession of an authentic Alexandrian-period chart. Alexander functioned in Islamic and European geographical writing as a marker of antiquity, exploration, and universal kingship.

### Leonardo da Vinci

**Evidentiary label: Disputed theory; no documented relationship.**

Occasional modern comparisons have been made between Piri’s world map and the world map sometimes attributed to Leonardo. No correspondence, meeting, shared workshop, or demonstrable transmission between Piri and Leonardo has been established.

### Fernão de Magalhães

**Evidentiary label: No documented personal connection.**

Claims that Piri accurately mapped southern South America before Magellan’s voyage are central to alternative-history arguments. Piri’s 1513 southern coastline is better understood as a distorted or conjectural extension derived from earlier sources. No contact with Magellan is documented.

### Remaining atlas roster

No further supported personal meeting, correspondence, kinship, patronage, teacher-student relationship, collaboration, or rivalry with a listed atlas subject was found. Absence is especially important here: visual resemblance between Piri’s maps and another person’s geography, or presence in the same broad century, is not evidence of contact.
