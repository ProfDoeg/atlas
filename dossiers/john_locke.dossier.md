# John Locke: Research Dossier

## Scope and identification

This dossier concerns **John Locke (1632–1704), the English philosopher, physician, political writer, colonial administrator, and government official**, not the twentieth-century American novelist or other namesakes.

Dates before Britain adopted the Gregorian calendar are given in the English Old Style unless otherwise indicated. Locke’s birth is often rendered **29 August 1632**; some modern sources convert it to **8 September 1632 New Style**. His death was **28 October 1704 Old Style**.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** John Locke.
- **Alternative spellings:** None regularly used. Seventeenth-century records sometimes preserve ordinary orthographic variation.
- **Anonymous or pseudonymous publication:** Locke published the *Two Treatises of Government*, *Epistola de Tolerantia*, subsequent toleration letters, and several religious and political writings anonymously or under descriptions such as “By the Author of the Essay.”
- **Titles and offices:** MA, MB; Fellow of the Royal Society; secretary to the Lords Proprietors of Carolina; secretary and treasurer to the Council of Trade and Plantations; Commissioner of Appeals; Commissioner of Trade and Plantations.
- **Retrospective labels:** “Father of liberalism,” “founder of British empiricism,” and “Enlightenment philosopher.” These are historiographical labels, not titles used consistently by Locke.
- **Born:** 29 August 1632, Wrington, Somerset, England.
- **Died:** 28 October 1704, Oates/Otes Manor, High Laver, Essex, England, aged seventy-two.
- **Buried:** Churchyard of All Saints’ Church, High Laver.
- **Nationality and political status:** English subject under Charles I, the Commonwealth and Protectorate, Charles II, James II, and William III and Mary II.
- **Principal professions:** Academic tutor, physician and medical researcher, political secretary, colonial and commercial administrator, economic adviser, philosopher, theologian, educational writer.
- **Marriage and children:** Locke never married and had no documented children.

The [National Portrait Gallery](https://www.npg.org.uk/collections/search/personExtended/mp02773/john-locke?tab=biography) records his Oxford degrees, Royal Society fellowship, French residence, Dutch exile, later residence at Oates, and principal publications.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

Locke’s father, also **John Locke (1606–1661)**, was a Somerset attorney, small landowner, clerk to local justices, and steward or legal agent to the prominent Popham family. During the English Civil War he served as a captain in a Parliamentary cavalry unit associated with Alexander Popham. This connection later helped secure the younger Locke’s admission to Westminster School.

His mother was **Agnes Keene Locke**, usually dated approximately **1597–1654**, the daughter of a local family associated with Wrington. Locke was born in the home of a maternal relative there, but the household ordinarily lived at **Pensford**, particularly at the family property at **Belluton**.

### Sibling

Locke had a younger brother, **Thomas Locke**, born in the 1630s. Thomas died young, probably in the early 1660s. The surviving documentation is much poorer than for John. No continuing family line through either brother has been securely demonstrated.

### Social and ancestral background

The Lockes were provincial Puritan or Protestant gentry rather than aristocrats. The father possessed property and professional standing but not a great hereditary fortune. Later genealogical attempts to attach the philosopher to more distinguished Locke or Lok mercantile families are not securely established.

### Heirs

Because Locke had no spouse or descendants, his cousin **Peter King**, later 1st Baron King and Lord Chancellor, became his principal literary heir. Locke’s will left King his manuscript papers and part of his library. The papers remained with King’s descendants, the Earls of Lovelace, until most were acquired by the Bodleian Library in 1947. This provenance explains the name **Lovelace Collection**.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1632–1647

Locke grew up near Pensford during the political and religious crisis that culminated in the Civil Wars. His father’s Parliamentarian service placed the family on the anti-royalist side, although the surviving evidence does not support portraying Locke’s household as revolutionary in the later republican sense.

The war brought military activity into Somerset. Locke’s childhood therefore unfolded amid armed conflict, religious controversy, contested royal power, and experiments in parliamentary government. These conditions later formed part of the background to his concern with political legitimacy, religious uniformity, resistance, and the limits of authority.

Little direct testimony survives about his earliest schooling. He probably received instruction locally before Alexander Popham’s patronage enabled him to enter Westminster.

## Education and Formation

### Westminster School, 1647–1652

Locke entered **Westminster School** in 1647. Its headmaster, **Richard Busby**, imposed a demanding classical curriculum centered on Latin, Greek, rhetoric, logic, and public disputation. Locke became a King’s Scholar.

The school trained a notable group later associated with Restoration intellectual life. The Royal Society’s historical material identifies Locke, Robert Hooke, and Christopher Wren among Busby’s pupils, although attendance at the same institution is not by itself evidence of a close school friendship.

The execution of Charles I occurred in January 1649 while Locke was at Westminster. He proceeded to Oxford through a Westminster studentship.

### Christ Church, Oxford, 1652–1667

Locke entered **Christ Church, Oxford**, in 1652.

- BA: 1656.
- MA: 1658.
- Lecturer in Greek: 1660–1661.
- Tutor: from 1661.
- Lecturer in rhetoric: 1662–1663.
- Censor in moral philosophy: 1663–1664.
- Bachelor of Medicine and license to practice: 1674/1675.

The official curriculum remained dominated by Aristotle and scholastic disputation. Locke later found much of it unproductive. His intellectual interests shifted toward René Descartes, Francis Bacon’s experimental program, medicine, chemistry, anatomy, and the Oxford experimental circle around **Robert Boyle**, **Thomas Willis**, **Richard Lower**, **John Wilkins**, and others.

### Teachers and formative influences

- **Richard Busby:** classical and rhetorical formation at Westminster.
- **Edward Pococke:** Oxford orientalist and theologian whom Locke later praised for learning and personal character.
- **Robert Boyle:** experimental natural philosophy and corpuscular explanations of matter.
- **Thomas Sydenham:** clinical observation and suspicion of elaborate medical systems.
- **René Descartes:** a major stimulus in epistemology and philosophy of mind, though Locke rejected innate ideas and important Cartesian doctrines.
- **Francis Bacon:** empirical and natural-historical method.
- **Pierre Gassendi and French Gassendists:** empiricist and anti-scholastic currents encountered through books and French intellectual contacts.
- **Richard Hooker:** natural law, political society, and consent.
- **Robert Filmer:** an opponent rather than mentor; Locke’s first *Treatise* dismantled Filmer’s patriarchal defense of monarchy.
- **Anthony Ashley Cooper:** patron, employer, political associate, and formative connection to Restoration government and Whig opposition.

### Medicine and science

Locke studied chemistry and medicine without completing the doctorate normally required for a major medical career. He collaborated with Boyle and maintained detailed meteorological and medical records. With Sydenham he emphasized observation of diseases as recurring clinical entities and distrusted theory unsupported by experience.

In 1666 Locke met **Anthony Ashley Cooper**, later 1st Earl of Shaftesbury, when Ashley came to Oxford seeking medical treatment. Locke subsequently joined his household. He participated in the diagnosis and management of Ashley’s liver disorder and a dangerous cyst or abscess. A silver drainage tube was inserted surgically in 1668. Locke did not single-handedly perform the operation, as popular retellings sometimes imply, but his medical judgment and continuing supervision helped establish the trust between them.

Locke was elected a **Fellow of the Royal Society in 1668**. Its catalogue describes his chemical and medical work and later public offices.

## Early Writings and Ideological Development

### Authoritarian beginnings

Locke’s earliest political-religious manuscripts do not express his mature doctrine of toleration. The two works now called the **Two Tracts on Government**, written around 1660–1662, defended substantial power in the civil magistrate to regulate “indifferent” features of worship for public order.

This is a documented development, not an unbroken lifelong liberal position. By the **Essay Concerning Toleration** of 1667 and especially the *Epistola de Tolerantia* of the 1680s, he had moved toward sharply limiting coercion in religion.

### Natural law lectures

As censor in moral philosophy, Locke composed the Latin **Essays on the Law of Nature** around 1663–1664. They treated natural law as divinely grounded and accessible to reason. The manuscripts were not published until 1954.

### Origins of the *Essay*

Locke later recalled that a discussion with several friends, probably in 1671, became stalled by disagreement about morality and revealed religion. He proposed first investigating the powers and limits of human understanding. Surviving drafts—commonly called Draft A and Draft B—document the beginning of the project that became *An Essay concerning Human Understanding*.

## Chronological Life History

## 1665: Diplomatic mission to Brandenburg

Locke served as secretary to **Sir Walter Vane’s diplomatic mission** to the Elector of Brandenburg at Cleves. The mission concerned Anglo-Dutch and German political relations. Locke observed different religious communities living under one government, an experience sometimes connected to his developing interest in toleration.

He was offered further diplomatic employment but returned to Oxford.

## 1666–1675: Shaftesbury’s household and Restoration government

In 1667 Locke moved to **Exeter House, London**, the residence of Anthony Ashley Cooper. His duties extended far beyond medicine:

- Household physician and confidential adviser.
- Supervisor of the education and marital arrangements of Ashley’s son.
- Adviser on the upbringing of Ashley’s grandson, the future third Earl of Shaftesbury.
- Political secretary and researcher.
- Colonial and commercial administrator.
- Collector of intelligence, statistical information, and economic data.

Locke helped arrange the marriage of Ashley’s son to Lady Dorothy Manners. When the couple produced a child, Locke participated in medical care and educational planning.

### Government and colonial offices

Locke’s offices included:

- **Secretary to the Lords Proprietors of Carolina**, approximately 1669–1675.
- **Registrar to the Commissioners of Excise**, approximately 1670–1675.
- **Secretary of Presentations** while Ashley was Lord Chancellor in 1672.
- **Secretary and treasurer to the Council of Trade and Foreign Plantations**, 1673–1675.

These posts gave him direct knowledge of trade, money, population, colonial administration, land grants, religious policy, and enslavement.

### Carolina

Locke copied, revised, and helped administer the **Fundamental Constitutions of Carolina**, a scheme associated principally with Shaftesbury and the proprietors. The document created hereditary ranks and contained the notorious provision that “every freeman of Carolina shall have absolute power and authority over his negro slaves.”

**Documented fact:** Locke worked as the proprietors’ secretary; manuscripts in his hand survive; he participated in revisions and colonial administration.

**Disputed authorship:** Older accounts frequently call him the constitution’s principal author. More recent manuscript scholarship argues that the plan was collaborative, that important hierarchical and slaveholding provisions preceded some of Locke’s work, and that Shaftesbury and the other proprietors exercised controlling authority. This reduces but does not eliminate Locke’s institutional participation. He transmitted and worked upon a constitution protecting hereditary slavery.

### Slave-trade and colonial investments

Locke held stock in the **Royal African Company**, which possessed a crown-backed role in the English trade in enslaved Africans, and an interest in the **Bahama Adventurers**.

**Documented fact:** His name appears among Royal African Company shareholders.

**Financial interpretation disputed:** Some descriptions call this a deliberate personal investment. Historian Holly Brewer argues that Locke received shares as compensation connected to administrative service and sold them after a relatively short period. Either route made him a temporary proprietor of an enterprise trafficking enslaved Africans. The available evidence does not establish that he personally organized a voyage or purchased an enslaved person.

### Shaftesbury’s fall

Ashley became Earl of Shaftesbury and Lord Chancellor, then lost office in 1673. The Council of Trade was dissolved in 1675. Locke’s government employment contracted, and his chronic respiratory illness encouraged travel to a warmer climate.

## France, 1675–1679

Locke left London in November 1675 and began a detailed journal. His route included Calais, Paris, Lyon, Orange, Avignon, Nîmes, and Montpellier. He arrived in Montpellier on Christmas Day.

### Montpellier and southern France

Locke lived chiefly in **Montpellier** through early 1677, with excursions to Celleneuve, Marseille, Toulon, Aigues-Mortes, and other towns. His interests included:

- Medical practices and the medical faculty.
- Weather and respiratory health.
- French Protestant life.
- Prices, wages, taxation, poverty, agriculture, and land use.
- Botany and natural history.
- Philosophical problems of space, identity, knowledge, and matter.

Contacts included physicians **Charles Barbeyrac** and **Pierre Magnol** and the Cartesian **Sylvain Régis/Leroy**.

### Paris and second journey

In 1677 Locke traveled by Toulouse and Bordeaux to Paris. He became tutor and medical attendant to **Caleb Banks**, son of Sir John Banks. He participated in the learned circle of **Henri Justel**, visited the Paris Observatory, and met or corresponded with astronomers and instrument makers including **Ole Rømer**, **Jean Picard**, **Jean-Dominique Cassini**, and **Adrien Auzout**.

He became close to **Nicolas Toinard/Thoynard**, a Catholic antiquarian and biblical chronologist. This friendship generated extensive correspondence.

In 1678 Locke traveled again through western and southern France. A projected Italian journey did not occur. He returned to England in May 1679. His journals combine travel narrative, medical case notes, reading records, political observation, shorthand, and commonplace-book entries. A scholarly overview of the journeys is provided in the [review of John Lough’s edition](https://www.persee.fr/doc/rhs_0048-7996_1954_num_7_2_3418).

## Political crisis, 1679–1683

Locke returned during the **Exclusion Crisis**, when Shaftesbury and allies sought to prevent the Catholic Duke of York, later James II, from succeeding Charles II. Locke again moved within Shaftesbury’s circle.

Most scholars date substantial composition of the *Two Treatises of Government* to this crisis rather than to the aftermath of 1688. The first treatise answered Filmer’s *Patriarcha*. The second defended natural equality, government by consent, fiduciary political power, property acquired through labor, and a right of resistance when rulers violated their trust.

Shaftesbury was arrested for treason in 1681 but the grand jury refused to indict him. He fled to the Netherlands in 1682 and died there in January 1683.

### Rye House Plot question

The Rye House Plot of 1683 allegedly involved plans by different opposition groups to assassinate Charles II and James or organize an uprising.

**Documented fact:** Locke knew several opposition activists; his patron’s circle engaged in resistance planning; Locke left England for the Netherlands in 1683; the government regarded him as politically suspect.

**Scholarly allegation/theory:** Richard Ashcraft argued that Locke was deeply involved in revolutionary Whig organization and that coded correspondence and meetings tied him to conspirators.

**Unresolved question:** No surviving document proves that Locke joined an assassination plan or knew of the Rye House scheme as such. Other historians distinguish a broader revolutionary network from the specific regicide plot. Mark Goldie’s reassessment emphasizes the ambiguous, circumstantial nature of the evidence. The question cannot be resolved by either depicting Locke as an apolitical refugee or declaring him a proven would-be assassin.

## Dutch exile, 1683–1689

Locke lived in the Dutch Republic, principally in **Amsterdam, Rotterdam, Utrecht, and The Hague**, sometimes moving or using discretion about his name and address because English agents sought political exiles.

He joined networks of English Whigs, Huguenot refugees, Dutch Remonstrants, physicians, theologians, and publishers. Important contacts included:

- **Philipp van Limborch**, Remonstrant theologian and intimate friend.
- **Jean Le Clerc**, Genevan-born scholar and editor.
- **Benjamin Furly**, Quaker merchant and host of an international intellectual circle.
- **Peter Molyneux** and, through correspondence, William Molyneux.
- English political exiles associated with resistance to James II.

Locke completed or extensively revised the *Essay*. He prepared an abridgment in French for Le Clerc’s *Bibliothèque universelle et historique*. He wrote the Latin *Epistola de Tolerantia*, addressed to Limborch.

### Oxford expulsion, 1684

Charles II’s secretary of state, the Earl of Sunderland, instructed John Fell, dean of Christ Church and bishop of Oxford, to remove Locke. Fell replied that Locke had conducted himself quietly and that he lacked evidence of treasonable behavior. The king nevertheless ordered Locke’s expulsion from his studentship.

The surviving [Sunderland–Fell correspondence](https://slaverylawpower.org/chapters/glorious-revolutions/lord-sunderland-correspondence-appointment-john-locke-1684/) is direct documentary evidence that this was political punishment imposed by royal command rather than an ordinary academic disciplinary finding.

The English government later sought Locke’s extradition or return. Dutch protection and political circumstances prevented a prosecution.

### Glorious Revolution

Locke’s associates supported **William of Orange’s intervention** against James II in 1688. Locke returned to England in February 1689 in the company or convoy associated with Princess Mary.

The claim that the *Two Treatises* was written simply to justify the completed Glorious Revolution follows Locke’s published preface but conflicts with manuscript and contextual evidence dating much of it to the Exclusion Crisis. It was nevertheless revised and published in the revolutionary settlement.

## Return and major publications, 1689–1692

Within a short period Locke published:

- *Epistola de Tolerantia* in Latin, 1689.
- William Popple’s English translation, *A Letter Concerning Toleration*, 1689.
- *Two Treatises of Government*, dated 1690 but probably issued late in 1689.
- *An Essay concerning Human Understanding*, dated 1690 but probably issued in December 1689.

Only the *Essay* openly carried Locke’s identity. The political and toleration works were anonymous.

Locke accepted appointment as **Commissioner of Appeals**, receiving a salary commonly reported as £200 annually. He declined diplomatic office, citing health.

## Oates, government service, and final intellectual period, 1691–1704

From late 1690 or 1691 Locke made his principal home at **Oates Manor, High Laver**, in the household of **Sir Francis Masham** and **Damaris Cudworth Masham**. He continued to travel to London for government business when health allowed.

Damaris Masham was a philosopher and writer, not merely Locke’s hostess. Their relationship was an intimate intellectual friendship extending from the 1680s until his death. Claims that they were lovers have circulated, but no decisive evidence establishes a sexual relationship. Both surviving correspondence and household arrangements support deep affection, philosophical collaboration, and trust.

### William Molyneux

Irish scientist and politician **William Molyneux** became one of Locke’s most important correspondents. Molyneux posed the problem later named after him: whether a person born blind, taught to distinguish a cube and sphere by touch, could distinguish them by sight immediately after gaining vision. Locke inserted the problem into later editions of the *Essay*. They met in England in 1698 shortly before Molyneux’s death.

### Isaac Newton

Locke formed a documented friendship and correspondence with **Isaac Newton**. They discussed the *Principia*, biblical criticism, Christology, prophecy, chemistry, money, and Locke’s writings. Newton sent Locke his manuscript on two supposed textual corruptions in the New Testament. Their exchanges also included a temporary rupture: during Newton’s psychological crisis in 1693, Newton wrote a suspicious letter accusing Locke of attempting to entangle him with women and other improprieties, then apologized after recovering. Locke answered without retaliation.

The [Oxford Handbook of Newton](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/34749/chapter-abstract/296599467) characterizes this as a friendship based on natural philosophy, chemistry, and theology. An original catalogue record survives for [Newton’s letter of 19 September 1698](https://newtonproject.ox.ac.uk/catalogue/record/MINT01768).

### Economic writings and the currency controversy

The government debated reducing interest rates, recoining clipped silver, and raising the nominal value of money. Locke opposed arbitrary revaluation. His principal arguments were:

- Money serves by socially accepted value but silver possesses a market quantity not altered by legislative naming.
- Lowering the legal interest rate below market conditions would not reliably create cheap credit.
- Debased or underweight coin damaged trust and contracts.
- Recoinage should restore full-weight silver rather than change nominal values.

These views appeared in *Some Considerations of the Consequences of the Lowering of Interest, and Raising the Value of Money* (1691) and *Further Considerations concerning Raising the Value of Money* (1695). Locke advised figures involved in the Great Recoinage, including Newton after Newton became Warden of the Mint.

### Board of Trade, 1696–1700

Locke became a salaried **Commissioner of Trade and Plantations** in 1696. His duties concerned colonies, commerce, currency, poor relief, and administrative reporting. Ill health increasingly prevented attendance; he resigned in 1700.

His 1697 memorandum on poor relief proposed:

- Parish registers and closer supervision.
- Compulsory labor for able-bodied adults receiving relief.
- “Working schools” where poor children would work and receive basic instruction and food.
- Restrictions and punishment directed at begging and vagrancy.

The document is preserved in government records and manuscripts. It is described in the [Cambridge edition](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/locke-political-essays/an-essay-on-the-poor-law-1697/505701602B83244456FA5B9EBBE66BAD).

**Documented fact:** These coercive proposals were Locke’s administrative project.

**Contextual qualification:** They were not enacted in full and belonged to a wider seventeenth-century poor-law discourse.

**Modern controversy:** The plan sits uneasily with generalized portrayals of Locke as an advocate of unrestricted individual liberty. His political liberty was compatible with extensive discipline of dependent poor people.

### Public controversies

Locke conducted several prolonged printed disputes:

- **Jonas Proast:** whether limited coercion could legitimately encourage religious truth. Locke answered Proast in three letters on toleration.
- **Edward Stillingfleet, bishop of Worcester:** whether the *Essay* encouraged skepticism, Socinianism, or denial of immaterial substance. Locke issued three extensive replies.
- **John Edwards:** whether *The Reasonableness of Christianity* reduced or distorted Christian doctrine. Locke published two vindications.
- **John Norris and Nicolas Malebranche:** Locke criticized “seeing all things in God” and aspects of Malebranche’s theory of ideas.

## Intellectual Development

## Epistemology

The *Essay* rejected innate speculative and moral principles. Locke argued that the mind receives materials from:

- **Sensation:** external experience.
- **Reflection:** awareness of the mind’s operations.

Simple ideas are received passively; complex ideas are formed by combination, comparison, and abstraction. Knowledge is the perception of agreement or disagreement among ideas. Locke distinguished intuitive, demonstrative, and sensitive knowledge and emphasized the limits of certainty.

The metaphor of the mind as “white paper” became the later “blank slate” formula. Locke did not claim that human beings are born without capacities, temperaments, appetites, or faculties; he denied innate propositions and fully formed ideas.

## Personal identity

Locke defined personal identity through continuity of consciousness rather than identity of substance. The “prince and the cobbler” thought experiment separated personhood from continuity of body. This generated enduring debates about memory, responsibility, resurrection, dementia, and duplication.

## Language and classification

Book III of the *Essay* treated general terms, abstraction, nominal and real essences, and linguistic abuse. Human classifications of species depend substantially upon nominal essences constructed for communication, while the full internal constitutions of substances remain imperfectly known.

## Natural philosophy

Locke adopted much of Boyle’s corpuscularian framework but remained cautious about claiming knowledge of ultimate matter. He distinguished primary qualities—solidity, extension, figure, motion, number—from secondary qualities such as color and taste, understood as powers to produce sensations.

## Ethics and theology

Locke grounded moral obligation in divine law and believed demonstrative moral knowledge might be possible, although he never completed the systematic moral treatise implied in the *Essay*. He defended a rational Christianity centered on Jesus as Messiah and obedience to divine law.

His precise Christology remains disputed. Contemporary opponents accused him of **Socinianism**, especially denial or minimization of the Trinity and Christ’s full divinity. Locke rejected the label, avoided a complete public confession on the contested points, and emphasized scriptural rather than creedal formulations.

## Political theory

The mature political theory included:

- Natural freedom and equality.
- Natural rights to life, liberty, and estate.
- Government formed by consent.
- Majority rule after political society is created.
- Legislative supremacy subject to natural law.
- Taxation requiring consent.
- Separation of legislative and executive functions.
- A right of resistance when rulers dissolve government or violate their trust.

Locke did not advocate modern universal democracy. Political participation was embedded in a property-owning, patriarchal, confessional, and imperial society. The texts do not establish equal suffrage regardless of sex, wealth, dependency, or colonial status.

## Property

Locke argued that labor could establish private rights in resources initially held in common, subject initially to leaving “enough, and as good” for others and avoiding spoilage. Money, accepted by consent, allowed durable accumulation and substantial inequality.

Interpretations divide over whether this theory:

1. Restrains appropriation through natural-law duties;
2. Rationalizes capitalist accumulation;
3. Defends agrarian “improvement” and enclosure;
4. Supplies a justification for English colonial appropriation.

Barbara Arneil argues that Locke’s references to America and uncultivated “waste” gave an ethical and economic rationale for taking Indigenous land. The [Oxford study *John Locke and America*](https://academic.oup.com/book/5585/chapter-abstract/148560829) presents the colonial defense as integral to the *Two Treatises*. Other scholars argue that Locke recognized Indigenous political communities and rights and that the property chapter cannot simply be reduced to a charter for conquest. His administrative connection to Carolina makes the colonial context documentary rather than hypothetical.

## Education

*Some Thoughts concerning Education* developed from advice to Edward and Mary Clarke concerning their son. Locke emphasized:

- Formation of character and self-command.
- Habit rather than rote moral instruction.
- Physical hardiness.
- Learning through interest and activity.
- Practical subjects and modern languages.
- Distrust of corporal punishment except in limited circumstances.
- Education suited to a gentleman capable of managing affairs.

Its immediate subject was elite domestic education, not a public system. Gender interpretations conflict. Some scholars see anti-patriarchal implications in Locke’s theories of reason and contract; others emphasize negative descriptions of women and the predominantly male, gentlemanly program.

## Religious toleration

Locke separated the civil interests protected by government—life, liberty, bodily security, and property—from salvation, which civil force could not produce. Churches were voluntary associations.

The toleration was not universal:

- **Atheists:** explicitly excluded because Locke believed oaths and promises could not bind them.
- **Catholics:** not always named directly, but groups owing political allegiance to a foreign prince or teaching that faith need not be kept with heretics were excluded; in context this targeted Roman Catholicism.
- **Religions demanding political domination:** excluded.
- Jews, Muslims, and pagans were in principle entitled to civil protection if politically peaceable, though Locke still wrote within a Christian missionary and providential framework.

The exclusion of atheists appears in the [primary text of the *Letter*](https://press-pubs.uchicago.edu/founders/print_documents/amendI_assemblys7.html).

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Anthony Ashley Cooper, 1st Earl of Shaftesbury

Patron, patient, employer, political leader, and gateway to government. Locke’s fortunes rose and fell with Shaftesbury’s. Their relationship connected philosophical writing to practical questions of succession, colonial government, commerce, and resistance.

### Damaris Cudworth Masham

Philosopher, correspondent, confidante, and host. Locke lived in the Masham household from 1690/1691 until death. Project Vox documents him as her lodger and intellectual companion from December 1690.

### Robert Boyle

Friend and scientific collaborator. Boyle’s experimental and corpuscular philosophy shaped Locke’s account of natural knowledge. Locke edited or assisted with some of Boyle’s materials and maintained medical and chemical interests within Boyle’s network.

### Thomas Sydenham

Mentor and collaborator in medicine. Locke worked on clinical observations with him and drafted or contributed material associated with Sydenham’s medical writings. Precise authorship of particular medical manuscripts remains debated.

### Isaac Newton

Friend and correspondent on science, theology, biblical criticism, and money. Their relationship is directly documented by letters.

### Philipp van Limborch

Dutch Remonstrant theologian, close friend, and recipient of the *Epistola de Tolerantia*. Their correspondence is central evidence for Locke’s religious thought and Dutch exile.

### Jean Le Clerc

Editor and Republic-of-Letters intermediary. Published Locke’s French *Essay* abridgment and helped disseminate his work on the Continent.

### William Molyneux

Irish correspondent, advocate of Locke’s philosophy, and originator of Molyneux’s problem. Molyneux also encouraged Locke to write about Irish political and economic questions.

### Edward and Mary Clarke

Friends and recipients of Locke’s educational advice, which became *Some Thoughts concerning Education*.

### Peter King

Cousin, legal and political protégé, executor, heir to papers, and later Lord Chancellor.

### Opponents

- Robert Filmer, posthumous theoretical opponent.
- Jonas Proast, toleration controversy.
- Edward Stillingfleet, epistemological and theological controversy.
- John Edwards, controversy over Christianity.
- James II’s and Charles II’s ministers, who treated Locke as a suspect opposition activist.

### Wealth and property

Locke inherited family property in Somerset and accumulated income from land, public appointments, investments, lending, and publication. He kept close accounts and offered financial advice to friends. He was comfortable by professional standards but not a magnate comparable to Shaftesbury.

His government salaries and patronage relationships were significant. Periods of political exclusion and exile threatened both income and security. At Oates he lived as a paying or contributing member of the Masham household rather than as owner of the estate.

The surviving record supports financial prudence and diversification, not accusations of fraud, embezzlement, or unexplained enrichment.

### Technologies, inventions, and patents

Locke held no major documented patent and is not securely credited with a mechanical invention. His technical activities included medical instruments and procedures, chemical experimentation, weather observation, systems for indexing commonplace books, monetary analysis, and administrative data collection.

His published **commonplace-book indexing method** was a practical information technology: subjects were keyed through letter-and-vowel combinations, allowing entries to be distributed efficiently through a notebook.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Contested Conduct

## Slavery

### Political theory

The opening of the *First Treatise* calls slavery “so vile and miserable an estate of man” that it is difficult to imagine an English gentleman defending it. In the *Second Treatise*, Locke permits a narrow condition of enslavement: a person who has forfeited life through unjust aggression may be subjected by a victor in a just war.

This theory does not fit hereditary racial chattel slavery, since children had not committed the parents’ aggression and captives in commercial systems were not individually tried as unjust aggressors.

### Institutional conduct

**Documented fact:** Locke held Royal African Company stock, served the Carolina proprietors, and handled versions of a constitution expressly protecting masters’ “absolute power” over enslaved people.

**Disputed mitigation:** Some scholars emphasize his subordinate secretarial role, compensation in stock rather than voluntary purchase, sale of the shares, and later natural-rights theory. These facts affect the characterization of intention but do not erase material participation.

**Disputed severity:** Calling Locke the sole “author of Carolinian slavery” overstates the manuscript evidence and the proprietors’ collective authority. Calling him wholly uninvolved understates his years of administrative labor and manuscript participation.

## Indigenous peoples and colonial dispossession

Locke repeatedly used “America” and Indigenous hunting or land use as examples in the property chapter. His agricultural “improvement” standard could treat intensively used Indigenous territories as economically underused or “waste.”

**Documented fact:** Locke possessed colonial information through government service and wrote theory using American examples.

**Scholarly interpretation:** Arneil and others argue that the property theory justified English settlement and Indigenous dispossession. Critics of this interpretation contend that Locke’s natural law still forbade conquest, recognized communities, and required just acquisition.

**Unresolved issue:** There is no known visit by Locke to America and no sustained account by him of a named Indigenous polity. The relationship between abstract theory and specific colonial seizure must therefore be reconstructed through administrative context, textual language, and implementation rather than direct field experience.

## Catholics, atheists, and limits of toleration

**Documented fact:** Locke excluded atheists from toleration and excluded religious adherents whose doctrines or foreign allegiance threatened civil government—a category directed particularly toward Catholics.

**Later-discovered manuscript:** A manuscript titled “Reasons for Tolerateing Papists Equally with Others,” attributed to Locke and published by scholars only in the twenty-first century, indicates that he considered a more qualified legal toleration for Catholics where political danger could be controlled. It does not convert his published position into modern universal religious liberty.

## Revolutionary conspiracy

**Government allegation:** Locke was “factious and undutiful” and associated with Shaftesbury.

**Documented sanction:** Expulsion from Christ Church occurred by royal command.

**Scholarly allegation:** Participation in organized revolutionary opposition is strongly plausible and defended by Richard Ashcraft.

**Unproven allegation:** Direct involvement in the Rye House assassination design has not been established by a confession, trial, indictment, or unequivocal letter. Locke was never convicted.

## Poor relief and child labor

**Documented fact:** Locke proposed compulsory labor and working schools for poor children.

**Interpretive controversy:** Admirers sometimes isolate his educational and liberty doctrines from this memorandum; critics treat the memorandum as evidence that Lockean freedom depended on class and economic independence. Both texts belong to the record.

## Gender and household authority

Locke attacked Filmer’s derivation of political monarchy from paternal power and distinguished paternal from political authority. His theory recognized mothers as sharing parental authority more fully than Filmer did.

He nevertheless assumed a gendered household and allowed the husband a deciding role in ordinary marital conflicts. His major educational treatise was designed chiefly for gentry children and centered on producing a male gentleman. Scholarship therefore divides between identifying resources for later feminism and emphasizing persistent patriarchal assumptions.

## Theology and alleged Socinianism

**Contemporaneous accusation:** John Edwards, Stillingfleet, and other opponents accused Locke of Socinian or heterodox theology.

**Documented ambiguity:** Locke minimized creedal tests, centered Christianity on belief in Jesus as Messiah, and corresponded with figures interested in anti-Trinitarian questions.

**No formal finding:** He was not convicted of heresy, and the label “Socinian” was also polemical. His exact private view of the Trinity remains debated.

## Medical authorship and the Ashley operation

Popular accounts sometimes say Locke “saved Shaftesbury’s life” by personally performing surgery. The documentation supports an important role in diagnosis, planning, and care, but specialist surgeons performed the operation. The heroic-surgeon version is an exaggeration of a real medical achievement.

## Fraud, corruption, criminality, and lawsuits

No reliable evidence located in the dedicated adversarial search establishes that Locke committed financial fraud, embezzlement, money laundering, or ordinary criminal offenses. “Money laundering” is anachronistic in this context.

His most serious exposure to criminal law arose from alleged treasonable political association. He was neither tried nor convicted. The absence of prosecution is not proof that he undertook no clandestine political activity; it does mean assassination or treason should not be stated as adjudicated fact.

The search for 2024–2026 controversy yielded modern reinterpretations of slavery, colonialism, gender, and property, not a newly authenticated criminal allegation concerning Locke.

## Health, Illness, and Death

Locke suffered for much of adulthood from chronic respiratory disease described as asthma, chest weakness, or consumption. Retrospective diagnosis is uncertain; tuberculosis, chronic bronchitis, emphysema, or overlapping pulmonary illness have been proposed.

His health shaped:

- The move to France and Montpellier.
- Avoidance of strenuous diplomatic appointments.
- Residence in the cleaner rural air at Oates.
- Increasing absence from Board of Trade meetings.
- Resignation in 1700.

He also recorded fevers and managed his health through diet, air, exercise, and medical observation. No addiction is documented. There is no firm evidence of a major psychiatric illness in Locke himself.

By 1704 breathing was increasingly difficult. He remained mentally engaged and corresponded with friends. He died at Oates on **28 October 1704**, reportedly while Damaris Masham was reading or attending him. His documented cause of death is the culmination of chronic respiratory decline; a precise modern pathological diagnosis is unavailable.

He was buried at High Laver. His epitaph, substantially composed by himself in Latin, characterizes him as a person content with a modest estate and devoted to truth. The [National Portrait Gallery](https://www.npg.org.uk/collections/search/person/mp02773/john-locke) preserves the record of numerous lifetime and posthumous portraits.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Father of liberalism”

- **Story:** Locke created modern liberalism.
- **Origin and circulation:** Especially common in nineteenth- and twentieth-century political histories and modern textbooks.
- **Evidence:** His consent theory, rights language, toleration arguments, and resistance doctrine became central to later liberal traditions.
- **Against literal acceptance:** “Liberalism” as an organized ideology postdates him; his thought remained Christian, property-qualified, colonial, and compatible with coercive poor laws.
- **Effect:** The label made Locke a symbolic ancestor claimed by constitutional liberals, libertarians, social democrats, and their critics.

### The completely blank slate

- **Story:** Locke thought infants have absolutely no inborn traits and environment mechanically creates everything.
- **Evidence:** He denied innate ideas and principles and compared the mind’s initial ideational state to white paper.
- **Against:** He repeatedly acknowledged faculties, appetites, temperaments, capacities, and differences in disposition.
- **Effect:** “Tabula rasa” became a simplified symbol in education, psychology, nature–nurture debates, and political propaganda.

### Author of “life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness”

- **Story:** Thomas Jefferson simply copied Locke’s formula “life, liberty, and property.”
- **Evidence:** Jefferson explicitly named Locke among authorities underlying the Declaration; Lockean natural-rights and resistance arguments were pervasive in the revolutionary world.
- **Against:** Locke did not habitually use the exact textbook triad “life, liberty, and property.” The Declaration drew on a wider legal, classical, Scottish, religious, and revolutionary vocabulary.
- **Effect:** It anchored Locke in American civic mythology.

### Sole author of the Fundamental Constitutions

- **Story:** Locke personally designed Carolina’s feudal and slave constitution.
- **Evidence:** Versions survive in his hand, and he worked for the proprietors.
- **Against:** Manuscript layers and institutional authorship show a collaborative project controlled by Shaftesbury and other proprietors; some provisions predated Locke’s revisions.
- **Effect:** The story has alternately been used to condemn Locke as architect of racial slavery or dismissed to preserve an uncomplicated liberal image. Neither extreme captures the evidence.

### Secret atheist or secular philosopher

- **Story:** Locke disguised secular or atheist doctrines in conventional Christian language.
- **Evidence cited by proponents:** Epistemic restraint, criticism of enthusiasm, heterodox associates, and cautious publication.
- **Against:** Extensive theological manuscripts, biblical studies, natural-law writings, and correspondence show sustained theistic and Christian commitments.
- **Assessment:** Theory, not established fact. Heterodox Christianity is better supported than atheism.

### Secret Socinian

- **Story:** Locke covertly denied the Trinity and Christ’s divinity.
- **Evidence:** Anti-creedal arguments, scriptural minimalism, and association with heterodox networks.
- **Against:** Locke rejected the accusation and did not leave an unequivocal private declaration matching every Socinian doctrine.
- **Assessment:** Contemporaneous allegation and continuing scholarly dispute.

### Heroic surgeon

- **Story:** Locke personally carried out the pioneering operation that saved Shaftesbury.
- **Evidence:** He was medically involved and the operation’s success strengthened their bond.
- **Against:** Surgeons, not Locke alone, performed the procedure.
- **Assessment:** Hagiographic amplification.

### Purely reluctant exile

- **Story:** An innocent scholar fled only because of arbitrary persecution.
- **Evidence:** The Crown had no public proof and expelled him without an academic finding.
- **Against:** Locke belonged to an active opposition network and likely supported revolutionary resistance.
- **Assessment:** The persecution was real; complete political innocence is doubtful; complicity in regicide remains unproven.

### *Lost*

The fictional character **John Locke** in the television series *Lost* was deliberately named after the philosopher. The character’s themes—identity, experience, faith, freedom, and destiny—generated popular symbolic associations. This is a posthumous cultural reference, not biographical evidence.

- ***Fundamental Constitutions of Carolina*** — institutional and collaborative document. Locke copied and revised versions and participated administratively; sole authorship should not be assigned to him.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Eighteenth century

Locke rapidly became a central authority in British and European epistemology, education, religious toleration, and political argument. His readers included:

- George Berkeley and David Hume, who developed and criticized empiricist themes.
- Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, whose *Nouveaux essais sur l’entendement humain* answered the *Essay* chapter by chapter.
- Voltaire, who helped present Locke to French readers.
- Jean-Jacques Rousseau, especially in education and political theory, though Rousseau’s conclusions differed.
- American colonial clergy, lawyers, pamphleteers, and revolutionaries.
- French Enlightenment writers and Encyclopedists.

The *Essay* was attacked by rationalists and theologians but became a standard reference point. The political writings circulated more unevenly before becoming emblematic of resistance and natural rights.

### American reception

Thomas Jefferson explicitly identified Locke among the foundational writers of public right. Natural equality, consent, dissolution of government, and resistance supplied language and argumentative structures used in the American revolutionary tradition.

Influence was not simple copying. Colonial constitutionalism, common law, republicanism, Protestant covenant thought, classical sources, and other natural-law writers also shaped the founders.

### French Revolution and conservative reaction

By the 1790s conservative polemic sometimes grouped Locke with Voltaire and Rousseau as an intellectual ancestor of revolution, secularization, and social disorder. Conversely, reformers claimed him as a defender of constitutional government and conscience.

### Nineteenth and twentieth centuries

Locke became canonical in histories of:

- British empiricism.
- Liberalism.
- Constitutionalism.
- Natural rights.
- Educational psychology.
- Religious toleration.
- Theories of personal identity.

Marxist and socialist interpretations scrutinized property, wage labor, class, and accumulation. C. B. Macpherson’s “possessive individualism” became particularly influential and controversial.

Analytic philosophy renewed attention to personal identity, substance, perception, language, and primary and secondary qualities.

### Twenty-first-century reassessment

Recent scholarship places greater emphasis on:

- Colonial government as part of Locke’s career, not a footnote.
- Royal African Company shareholding.
- Carolina manuscript authorship and institutional responsibility.
- Indigenous land and “improvement.”
- Poor-law coercion.
- Gender and household hierarchy.
- The theological basis of equality and rights.
- Locke’s clandestine revolutionary networks.
- Manuscript evidence that complicates simplified labels.

These reinterpretations have not produced consensus that Locke’s entire philosophy either reduces to empire or remains untouched by it. They have made a compartmentalized biography—philosopher detached from administrator—untenable.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by documentary or explicit intellectual evidence are listed. Mere contemporaneity is excluded.

### Aristotle — documented fact

Aristotelian logic, ethics, and natural philosophy formed part of Locke’s Oxford curriculum. Locke reacted against scholastic Aristotelian teaching while continuing to use inherited logical and metaphysical vocabulary.

### Francis Bacon — documented intellectual influence

Locke’s experimental orientation and suspicion of premature systems belong to the Baconian tradition cultivated by Boyle and the Royal Society. The influence is intellectual rather than a personal meeting; Bacon died before Locke’s birth.

### René Descartes — documented intellectual influence and opposition

Locke read Descartes during his Oxford formation. Cartesian philosophy helped redirect him away from scholasticism, but the *Essay* opposed innate ideas and other Cartesian doctrines. They could not have met: Descartes died in 1650 while Locke was still a schoolboy.

### Robert Hooke — documented institutional and correspondence connection

Both were Fellows in the early Royal Society and part of its experimental culture. The Royal Society’s correspondence database records a Locke communication associated with Hooke and an eclipse, read to the Society on 12 December 1678. This supports institutional contact, though not a close friendship comparable to Locke’s relationships with Boyle or Newton.

### Sir Isaac Newton — documented fact

Locke and Newton were friends and correspondents from the late 1680s onward. They exchanged work on theology, biblical textual criticism, natural philosophy, chemistry, and money. Newton sent Locke his study of two New Testament corruptions; Locke discussed publication through Jean Le Clerc. Locke also advised on monetary questions relevant to Newton’s Mint work.

### Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz — documented intellectual controversy; no direct friendship established

Leibniz composed *Nouveaux essais sur l’entendement humain* as a systematic response to Locke’s *Essay*. Locke and Leibniz did not maintain a substantial direct correspondence comparable to Locke and Newton. Leibniz’s response was withheld after Locke’s death and published in 1765.

### Jean-Jacques Rousseau — documented intellectual reception

Rousseau read and reacted within an educational and social-contract tradition strongly shaped by Locke. *Émile* differs sharply from *Some Thoughts concerning Education*, but the connection is an acknowledged history of ideas, not personal contact.

### Thomas Jefferson — documented intellectual reception

Jefferson identified Locke among the “elementary books of public right” supporting the Declaration of Independence and elsewhere grouped Bacon, Locke, and Newton as foundational figures. There was no personal contact because Jefferson was born in 1743.

### Benjamin Franklin — reported intellectual reception

Lockean ideas circulated in the colonial American print and scientific worlds in which Franklin operated. Franklin and Locke never met. The precise extent of direct textual dependence varies by subject and should not be inferred solely from shared Enlightenment vocabulary.

### Adam Smith — documented intellectual inheritance, indirect

Smith inherited and revised natural-law, property, money, and stages-of-society debates to which Locke was central. No personal connection was possible; Smith was born in 1723. Particular similarities require textual demonstration rather than assuming comprehensive Lockean discipleship.

### Immanuel Kant — documented intellectual reception, indirect

Kant treated Locke as a major empiricist predecessor while criticizing empiricism and recasting questions about experience and the limits of reason. No personal connection was possible.

### Mary Wollstonecraft Shelley — no supported direct connection

Mary Shelley appears in the atlas roster, but no significant documentary connection to Locke was located beyond membership in later intellectual traditions influenced by empiricism and rights discourse. Her mother, Mary Wollstonecraft—who is not separately listed in the roster—engaged Locke’s educational legacy more directly.

### John Dewey — documented intellectual reception, indirect

Dewey’s educational philosophy developed in a later empirical and anti-innatist tradition in which Locke was a canonical predecessor. No direct connection existed.

### John Langshaw Austin, John Rogers Searle, William James, and Paul-Michel Foucault — no specific atlas connection recorded

These later philosophers worked on language, mind, identity, knowledge, or power in fields where Locke remained part of the historical canon. The sources consulted did not establish a sufficiently specific acknowledged connection for a stronger entry. Absence of a listed connection is not a claim that comparative scholarship does not exist.
