## Works by Merlin

### Prophetic and juridical works

- **Legendary fifth century; no fixed date:** *Prophetiae Merlini* (*Prophecies of Merlin*). An extended prophetic performance interpreting the red and white dragons and forecasting Britain’s political future; it is a spoken oracle rather than a book made by Merlin, although Geoffrey presents himself as its translator and circulated it separately before incorporating it into *De gestis Britonum*. Modern scholarship treats both the text and its attribution to Merlin as Geoffrey’s composition, drawing on earlier Welsh prophetic traditions and the fatherless Ambrosius episode ([Reeve and Wright, eds.](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.7722/j.ctt14brrjp); [Faletra, “Riddling Words”](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004410398/BP000016.xml); [text, Book VII](https://www.roman-britain.co.uk/classical-references/historia-regum-britanniae/)).

- **Legendary fifth century; no fixed date:** Prophecy of Vortigern’s death. A short oral oracle predicting the king’s destruction, distinct in the narrative from the long *Prophetiae* but not transmitted as an independently titled work; its status is therefore an utterance or manifestation of prophetic knowledge rather than a stable text ([*De gestis Britonum*, Book VIII](https://www.roman-britain.co.uk/classical-references/historia-regum-britanniae/)).

- **Legendary fifth century; no fixed date:** Interpretation of Uther’s dragon-shaped comet. An oral prophetic reading announcing Aurelius Ambrosius’s death, Uther’s accession, and the future rule of Uther’s descendants; Uther’s two golden dragon objects are responses to Merlin’s interpretation, not objects made by Merlin himself ([*De gestis Britonum*, VIII.15–17](https://www.roman-britain.co.uk/classical-references/historia-regum-britanniae/)).

- **Before the legendary battle of Arfderydd, traditionally 573:** Laws for the people of Dyfed. The *Vita Merlini* introduces Merlin as a king who “gave laws” to the people of Dyfed. Their provisions, medium, extent, and survival are unstated, so these resist identification as a recoverable text; at most they constitute a lost or purely fictional protocol and body of legislation ([Cambridge, *Vita Merlini*, lines 19–22](https://www.asnc.cam.ac.uk/research/latin-arthur/texts/text-one/)).

- **Before the legendary battle of Arfderydd, traditionally 573:** Prophetic songs for the rulers of Dyfed. The *Vita Merlini* says generally that Merlin sang future events to the leaders, but supplies neither titles nor boundaries for the songs. This is an attributed body of oral work, not a securely enumerable collection ([Cambridge, *Vita Merlini*, lines 19–22](https://www.asnc.cam.ac.uk/research/latin-arthur/texts/text-one/)).

- **After the legendary battle of Arfderydd, traditionally 573:** Prophecy sung in the woodland observatory. This long, untitled political oracle extends from British civil conflict through Saxon, Danish, and Norman rule and recalls the earlier prophecy to Vortigern; it is a performed song which Merlin intended scribes to record, though the poem does not establish the resulting tablets as a completed, independently circulating book ([Cambridge, *Vita Merlini*, lines 555–82 and following](https://www.asnc.cam.ac.uk/research/latin-arthur/texts/text-one/); [Flood, “Prophecy as History”](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11061-018-9564-x)).

- **After the legendary battle of Arfderydd, traditionally 573:** Short predictions and diagnostic utterances at Rhydderch’s court. These include the exposure of Ganieda’s adultery, the apparently contradictory threefold death of a disguised boy, the treasure beneath a gatekeeper, and a purchaser’s imminent drowning. They are separate oral manifestations rather than titled works, and the narrative explicitly stages doubt about their truth before later vindicating them ([Cambridge, *Vita Merlini*, lines 299–397 and 490–34](https://www.asnc.cam.ac.uk/research/latin-arthur/texts/text-one/)).

### Poetry and lament

- **At the legendary battle of Arfderydd, traditionally 573:** Lament for Peredur’s three brothers. An untitled extemporaneous lament uttered on the battlefield; it is a bounded verbal composition within the *Vita Merlini*, but no independent text or traditional title survives ([Cambridge, *Vita Merlini*, lines 38–55](https://www.asnc.cam.ac.uk/research/latin-arthur/texts/text-one/)).

### Monumental work

- **Legendary fifth century; no fixed date:** *The Giant’s Dance*, re-erected in Britain and later identified with Stonehenge. Merlin did not originate or quarry its stones: Geoffrey says giants had brought them from Africa and erected them in Ireland. Merlin devised the machinery for dismantling them, directed their transport, and reconstructed the arrangement as a funerary monument on Salisbury Plain, making this a relocated and reconfigured obra rather than an entirely new building ([*De gestis Britonum*, VIII.10–12](https://www.roman-britain.co.uk/classical-references/historia-regum-britanniae/); [Tatlock, “Geoffrey of Monmouth and Stonehenge”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/pmla/article/abs/geoffrey-of-monmouth-and-stonehenge/A64966B1A61AFAA1E8B7C0A1E76E61F5)).

### Designed project executed by another maker

- **After the legendary battle of Arfderydd, traditionally 573:** Woodland compound and astronomical hall. Merlin specifies a group of winter houses and a remote observatory with seventy doors and seventy windows, together with seventy scribes to record his song; Ganieda, not Merlin, actually has the buildings constructed. It is therefore Merlin’s architectural and intellectual project but Ganieda’s executed obra, combining residence, observatory, archive, and performance space ([Cambridge, *Vita Merlini*, lines 550–70](https://www.asnc.cam.ac.uk/research/latin-arthur/texts/text-one/); [Lopatin and McMullen, “Merlin’s Woodland House”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/arthurian-literature-xxxviii/merlins-woodland-house-irish-cosmology-in-the-vita-merlini/BC63F16E58C71A47DA3D06598D1FDB61); [Otter, “Merlin’s Open Mind”](https://press.ici-berlin.org/doi/10.37050/ci-23/otter_merlins-open-mind.html)).

### Magical manifestations

- **Legendary fifth century; no fixed date:** Three manufactured likenesses at Tintagel. Using medicinal or magical arts, Merlin gives Uther the appearance of Gorlois, Ulfin that of Jordan, and himself that of Bricel. These temporary embodied disguises are manifestations rather than durable objects, and Geoffrey makes them instrumental to Uther’s deception of Igraine and Arthur’s conception ([*De gestis Britonum*, VIII.19–20](https://www.roman-britain.co.uk/classical-references/historia-regum-britanniae/)).
