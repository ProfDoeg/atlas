# Salomon Oppenheim jr.: Research Dossier

## Scope, identification, and evidentiary method

This dossier concerns **Salomon Hertz Oppenheim junior** (1772–1828), the Bonn-born merchant-banker who established the enterprise later known as **Sal. Oppenheim jr. & Cie.**

He must not be confused with:

- Samuel Oppenheimer (1630–1703), the Viennese court financier.
- Joseph Süß Oppenheimer (1698–1738), the Württemberg court financier.
- Samuel Oppenheim (1857–1928), the Austrian astronomer.
- J. Robert Oppenheimer (1904–1967), the American physicist.
- Later members of the Cologne Oppenheim banking family who carried the titles *von Oppenheim* or *Freiherr von Oppenheim*.

The evidence base is uneven. Oppenheim left business and administrative records, but no known autobiography, diary, memoir, or published body of writing. Much of the reconstructable biography comes from municipal and banking records subsequently used by historians, especially Gabriele Teichmann. Statements designated “family tradition” are not treated as equivalent to independently dated contemporary records.

### Evidentiary labels

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary records or a scholarly account explicitly grounded in identifiable records.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a generally reliable historical source, but the underlying document was not directly available for examination here.
- **Contemporaneous allegation:** an accusation made during Oppenheim’s lifetime, not necessarily substantiated.
- **Family tradition:** a claim preserved in institutional or family history whose surviving contemporary basis is incomplete.
- **Later claim / unverified:** a story found in later secondary or popular accounts without adequate supporting documentation.
- **Rumor or myth:** a narrative whose evidentiary basis is absent, demonstrably confused, or primarily conspiratorial.

### Death-date discrepancy

Two dates circulate:

- **8 November 1828** appears in Wikimedia-derived authority records, the Commons record, and several popular biographies.
- **9 November 1828** is given by the *Neue Deutsche Biographie*, a scholarly reference work.

The discrepancy has not been resolved from an independently inspected Mainz civil or Jewish death register. The strongest prudent formulation is therefore: **died in Mainz on 8 or 9 November 1828**. No reliable source consulted establishes the hour of death, cause of death, medical history, or burial place.

### Title problem

Some database displays attach “Freiherr von” to his name. There is no evidence that Salomon himself was ennobled. His sons Simon and Abraham received noble or baronial rank decades after his death. Salomon’s documented personal honor was **Royal Prussian Senior Court Agent**, not a hereditary barony. Calling him “Salomon Freiherr von Oppenheim” is therefore anachronistic.

## Family and Ancestry

## Paternal ancestry

The Cologne banking family’s documented ancestry reaches back to Jewish silk merchants in sixteenth-century Frankfurt and Worms. The exact earliest sequence varies between genealogical summaries.

The better-supported near ancestry is:

- **Paternal grandfather:** Salomon Hertz Oppenheim, born 1694, died 1757 or 1758.
- **Paternal grandmother:** Brendele Abraham Halevy, approximately 1720–1780.
- Around **1740**, the grandfather moved from Frankfurt am Main to Bonn. He worked as a Jewish merchant in the residence of the prince-elector of Cologne but, according to modern banking historians, remained a relatively small trader rather than the founder of a major financial house.

The family surname referred ultimately to the Rhineland town of Oppenheim. This does not establish descent from every other family using the name.

## Parents

- **Father:** Herz Salomon Oppenheim, also rendered Hertz Salomon or Naftali Herz Salomon Oppenheim, **1750–1832**, a merchant in Bonn.
- **Mother:** Helene or Hanna Oppenheim, née Seligmann, **1752–1793**.
- The *Neue Deutsche Biographie* identifies Helene as a daughter of Elijahr Salman. Other biographical summaries describe her as the sister of banker **Aron Elias Seligmann**, later Baron von Eichthal.

Salomon was reportedly the **eldest son** and one of **ten children**. Teichmann’s account says he had nine siblings, none of whom made a comparable independent commercial departure. A complete verified sibling list was not located.

## “Court Jew” ancestry: qualification

Later accounts often describe Salomon as the descendant of an “illustrious” dynasty of *Hofjuden* who had advised and financed the prince-archbishops of Cologne for generations. The narrower documentary picture is more restrained:

- His father traded primarily with the electoral court.
- His grandfather had moved to the court city and operated as a protected Jewish merchant.
- Modern bank historians characterize both as comparatively modest traders.
- Salomon did not inherit a large banking house or sufficient capital to explain his later rise.

Thus, **court-connected Jewish commerce is documented**; a long-established, wealthy banking dynasty before Salomon is an amplified later formulation.

## Marriage

In **1792**, Salomon married:

- **Deigen Levi**, later known as **Therese Stein**
- Born **9 July 1775**, Dülmen
- Died **25 March 1842**, Cologne
- Daughter of the Dülmen merchant **Levi Isaac**
- Her father is described in Teichmann’s account as a prosperous merchant who held the honorary designation of Dutch consul-general.

The change from Deigen Levi to Therese Stein belongs to the period in which Jews in French-administered territories were required to adopt fixed civil names. Broad claims that “all European states” simultaneously imposed such changes are imprecise.

### Dowry and business role

Therese reportedly brought a **10,000-taler dowry**, a large capital contribution. She was not merely a socially advantageous spouse:

- She participated in the family business.
- In **1821**, Salomon gave her authority to sign for the firm.
- After his death, she continued the bank with Simon and Abraham.
- Sources differ over whether she should be described as having “run the bank alone” or jointly with her sons. The documented continuity of her authority is clear; the precise daily allocation of management is less so.

## Children

Sources consistently report **twelve children**. Teichmann’s reconstruction names the following twelve:

1. **Helene Oppenheim** — born before the family’s final move to Cologne; later married **Benoît/Benedict Fould**, connecting the family to the Paris banking house.
2. **Charlotte Oppenheim** — born in the Bonn period; later married into the Ratisbonne family according to genealogical summaries.
3. **Isaac Oppenheim** — born in the Bonn period and died in childhood; buried in Bonn.
4. **Bertha Oppenheim**, commonly called **Betty**, born 1802 — married Heinrich David Hertz of Hamburg; an ancestor of physicists Heinrich Hertz and Gustav Hertz.
5. **Simon Oppenheim**, 1803–1880 — banker; entered the family firm; later Austrian baron and Prussian nobleman.
6. **Abraham Oppenheim**, 1804–1878 — banker and patron; later a Prussian baron.
7. **Eva Oppenheim**, born 1805.
8. **Euphrosine Oppenheim**, born 1806.
9. **David Oppenheim**, later **Dagobert Oppenheim**, 1809–1889 — jurist, newspaper proprietor, railway and steamship executive, and Cologne municipal politician; converted to Protestantism in 1839.
10. **Caroline Oppenheim**, born 1811.
11. **Arnold Oppenheim**, 1813–1884 — later associated with Gebrüder Oppenheim & Co. in Amsterdam.
12. **Alexander Oppenheim**, 1814–1903 — likewise associated with the Amsterdam house.

Dates and later marriages for several daughters require verification from civil and synagogue registers. Some online genealogies report thirteen children; the best narrative reconstruction consulted gives twelve and explicitly accounts for Isaac, who died young.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1772–1789

Salomon was born in Bonn’s **Judengasse**, later called Tempelstraße. Approximately 300 Jews lived in Bonn at the time. The street’s gates were locked by authorities at night, embodying the legally segregated status of Jewish residents under the electoral regime.

His childhood combined:

- Jewish communal and religious formation.
- Exposure to his father’s court-oriented trading.
- Familiarity with bills, commodities, protected commerce, and the legal disabilities imposed on Jewish merchants.
- The intellectual atmosphere of the Jewish Enlightenment, or *Haskalah*.

Teichmann places Moses Mendelssohn’s program of linguistic, educational, and civil integration in the formative background of the generation. No source consulted proves that Salomon personally studied Mendelssohn, corresponded with him, or formally joined a Haskalah circle. This should be treated as historical context, not a documented teacher-student relationship.

## Education

No school, university, formal apprenticeship contract, curriculum, or named academic teacher has been securely identified.

His demonstrated capabilities imply practical commercial training in:

- Bookkeeping.
- Currency conversion.
- Bill discounting.
- Commodity and commission trading.
- Correspondence across multiple markets.
- Assessment of credit and counterparty risk.

His father’s business and his older relative Samuel Wolff are the most plausible training environments. Samuel Wolff later became his financial backer and silent partner. Calling Wolff a “mentor” is reasonable as an interpretation, but the exact training relationship is not documented.

## Early Career in Bonn, 1789–1798

## Claimed founding in 1789

Family and bank tradition dates the beginning of Oppenheim’s independent business to **1789**, when he was seventeen. The *Neue Deutsche Biographie* carefully labels this “according to family tradition.”

The enterprise was a **commission and exchange house**, not a modern deposit bank. It dealt in combinations of:

- Goods on commission.
- Forwarding and transport.
- Currency exchange.
- Bills of exchange.
- Credit.
- Securities administration.

A dated record from **7 May 1793**, in which Oppenheim appears as creditor on a 300-taler bill involving a Bonn couple named Schön, is cited as the earliest surviving direct evidence of his banking activity.

## Samuel Wolff and the founding-capital discrepancy

Salomon recruited **Samuel Wolff**, a cousin of his father and an experienced banker and silver dealer, as a silent partner.

Two incompatible figures circulate:

- Teichmann’s 2025 account says Wolff provided **40,000 talers in cash** and entrusted Oppenheim with a substantial securities portfolio.
- Later summaries of the bank’s history give **90,000 talers**.

The discrepancy may reflect different dates, aggregation of cash and administered securities, or repetition of an institutional tradition. Without the partnership accounts, neither figure should be silently preferred. The 40,000-taler figure comes from the more recent detailed scholarly narrative.

Therese’s reported 10,000-taler dowry supplied an additional major capital base.

## Political rupture

French troops occupied the left bank of the Rhine in **1794**. The electoral court fled Bonn shortly before the conquest. Since court demand had sustained much of Bonn’s economy and Salomon’s father had traded heavily with the court, its departure undermined the city’s commercial prospects.

Bonn tax lists repeatedly marked Salomon as **“absent” from 1794 onward**, indicating travel or business beyond Bonn. The exact destinations and lengths of these journeys are not preserved in the sources consulted.

## Chronological Life History

## 1798–1801: Commercial move to Cologne

In **1798**, Oppenheim transferred his business to Cologne. His move followed the French administration’s introduction of civil and religious toleration into a city that had excluded permanent Jewish settlement since 1424.

The move occurred in stages:

- **1798:** business transferred to Cologne.
- Salomon initially went without his young household.
- Therese remained in Bonn with Helene, Charlotte, and Isaac.
- Isaac died and was buried in Bonn.
- Approximately **1801:** Therese and the surviving children joined Salomon in Cologne.

Sources that date “the move” to either 1798 or 1801 are often describing different stages rather than genuinely contradictory events.

The family’s first known Cologne residence was **Am Hof 16**, close to the Alter Markt, Heumarkt, and Rhine port. The location served simultaneously as home, counting house, and access point to commodity and shipping markets.

## 1801–1804: Reconstitution of Jewish communal life

A Jewish congregation formally emerged in Cologne in October 1801. Oppenheim was among its founders and served as a leading communal representative or *Vorsteher*.

The congregation initially occupied former buildings of the secularized St. Clarissa convent in Glockengasse. In **1804**, with substantial financial help from Oppenheim, the community acquired the property. It installed:

- A prayer hall for approximately 120 people.
- A ritual bath.
- Schoolrooms.
- Housing for communal employees.
- Facilities for a teacher and cantor.

His role combined religious-community leadership, property negotiation, and finance. No evidence indicates that he held rabbinical office or produced theological writings.

## 1802–1808: Expansion of family and firm

At Am Hof 16, five children were born in quick succession:

- Bertha, 1802.
- Simon, 1803.
- Abraham, 1804.
- Eva, 1805.
- Euphrosine, 1806.

The household also included a maid and three men recorded as *Commis*, meaning commercial clerks or assistants.

The firm dealt in:

- Grain.
- Cotton.
- Tobacco.
- Wine.
- Oil and other agricultural goods.
- Forwarding and commission shipments.
- Bills of exchange.
- Foreign currencies.
- Arbitrage between markets.

Commission on forwarding transactions could reach roughly one-third of the commodity’s value. Merchandise remained important throughout Salomon’s lifetime; the house did not abandon commodity trade completely until around 1830, after his death.

Its business network expanded from Cologne and the surrounding Rhineland to Hamburg, Berlin, Amsterdam, Paris, and London.

## 1804–1808: Jewish tax administration

Under the French authorities, Oppenheim received the lucrative right or responsibility to collect outstanding Jewish communal obligations commonly described as **“Judenschulden”** or, in abbreviated accounts, a “Jews’ tax.”

These obligations were remnants of protection payments imposed in the former electoral regime. Although revolutionary France had proclaimed equality, inherited fiscal claims and later discriminatory regulations continued to operate.

The position gave Oppenheim regular access to liquid funds. The available accounts do not demonstrate that these receipts became his personal property, that he embezzled them, or that his fellow Jews formally accused him of abuse. They do show that state-authorized tax collection materially strengthened his liquidity and placed him in a powerful intermediary position between government and community.

## 1808: Property, public status, and discriminatory regulation

In **January 1808**, Oppenheim purchased the property at **Große Budengasse 8** from former mayor Jacob von Hilgers for **17,000 francs**. He also paid **2,568 francs** for part of its furnishings.

The house:

- Had been built in 1780 for banker Jakob Pelzer.
- Possessed a Louis XVI-style façade.
- Ranked twelfth in a contemporary assessment of Cologne’s most imposing houses.
- Contained the family home, offices, probable warehouses, courtyard, garden, side buildings, and rear buildings.
- Served as the bank’s location from 1808 until its destruction in 1945, although the nineteenth-century structure was later rebuilt.

Salomon, Therese, and seven children moved there in summer 1808. David/Dagobert, Caroline, Arnold, and Alexander were subsequently born there.

The French administration classified him in 1808 as **“Banquier et Négociant Notable”**—notable banker and merchant—and listed him first among recommended Jewish merchants. Cologne’s mayor described him to the prefect as a citizen recommendable in every respect.

### Petition against Napoleon’s restrictive decree

On **17 March 1808**, Napoleon promulgated the so-called *décret infâme*, which imposed special restrictions on Jewish credit, trade, occupational licensing, settlement, and military substitution.

In **April 1808**, Oppenheim petitioned for exemption. Cologne’s mayor supported his request, but it was initially unsuccessful. This episode documents both his vulnerability to anti-Jewish law and his ability to mobilize municipal support.

Later in 1808, French law required Jews to register fixed civil names. The Oppenheim family received permission to retain its existing surname.

## 1810: Position among Cologne bankers

By 1810:

- The bank reportedly possessed **250,000 talers**, or approximately one million francs, in equity.
- It ranked second among Cologne banking houses, behind Abraham Schaaffhausen.
- Salomon was the highest-taxed member of Cologne’s Jewish community.

His wealth did not derive solely from banking. It reflected the interaction of:

- Commission commerce.
- Forwarding.
- Foreign-exchange transactions.
- Bills.
- Arbitrage.
- Tax administration.
- Therese’s dowry.
- Samuel Wolff’s capital.
- A smaller degree of real-estate investment.

The crisis of 1810 affected parts of the European banking network. Salomon’s daughter Helene’s marriage to Benoît Fould later helped bind the Cologne house to Paris and Atlantic commercial routes through Bordeaux, Bayonne, and Spain.

## 1814: Philipp Gompertz and Rothschild criticism

By **1814**, Salomon’s relative **Philipp Gompertz** had joined Samuel Wolff within the “& Cie.” partnership structure.

That year, the Frankfurt Rothschild house sent an adverse warning about Oppenheim to an Amsterdam correspondent. It instructed the recipient to check his transactions carefully, describing him as excessively profit-seeking and alleging that he did not always remain on the straight path.

This is important evidence of rivalry but requires strict labeling:

- **Documented fact:** Rothschild regarded Oppenheim as a competitor and circulated the warning.
- **Contemporaneous allegation:** Oppenheim was excessively grasping and commercially irregular.
- **Not established:** No specific fraudulent transaction, victim, prosecution, judgment, or regulatory finding accompanied the accusation in the material consulted.
- Cologne municipal and French administrative assessments from 1808 had, by contrast, described him as highly reputable.

## 1815–1816: Transfer to Prussian rule and formal firm name

After Napoleon’s defeat, Cologne and much of the Rhineland passed to Prussia in **1815**. Oppenheim adapted from the French to the Prussian regime without an apparent loss of official trust.

In **September 1816**, the business first formally used the enduring style:

**Sal. Oppenheim jr. & Cie.**

The suffix “junior” distinguished Salomon from older family namesakes and became part of the permanent brand.

## 1818: French indemnity transfer

In 1818, Oppenheim and the Berlin house **Mendelssohn & Co.** received a Prussian government commission to transfer part of the indemnity France owed after the Napoleonic Wars.

The figures require distinction:

- Some institutional histories give the total indemnity operation as **265 million francs**.
- A surviving description of the Oppenheim-Mendelssohn contract assigns their consortium **52.5 million francs**.
- The November 1818 contract reportedly required the bankers to collect available money in Paris and deliver the same coin types within fourteen days to the General Liquidation Commission in Aachen.
- Compensation was **¾ percent**, covering collection, transport risk or guarantee, and commission.

The 52.5-million-franc sum is best understood as the portion handled by Oppenheim and Mendelssohn, not necessarily the entire French obligation.

The transaction required:

- Paris correspondents.
- Coin and exchange expertise.
- Secure physical transfer.
- A high level of sovereign trust.
- Large guarantees and dependable counterparties.

It established Oppenheim as a banker capable of state-scale international finance.

## 1818: Transport insurance

Oppenheim participated in establishing or financing the **Rheinschifffahrts-Assekuranz-Gesellschaft**, a Rhine-shipping insurance enterprise and predecessor in the institutional lineage later associated with Agrippina.

This investment marked a shift from transactions in existing commodities and public finance toward organized corporate enterprise.

## 1820–1821: Industrial and family succession

Around 1820, Oppenheim increasingly entered *Gründungsgeschäft*: the financing and organization of new companies.

In **1821**:

- Simon and Abraham began working in the family bank.
- Therese received signatory authority.
- The household firm began a deliberate transition to the second generation.

Some accounts say Simon entered first and Abraham followed slightly later; others place both in the firm in 1821. The difference probably concerns formal versus practical entry.

## 1822: Honor and Chamber of Commerce

In recognition of the indemnity operation and other services, King Frederick William III granted Oppenheim the title:

**Königlich Preußischer Oberhofagent**

In the same year, he was elected to the **Cologne Chamber of Commerce**, reportedly unanimously, becoming its first Jewish member. This was an important public office at a time when Jewish civil status in Prussia remained insecure and the French discriminatory decree of 1808 had not yet been fully eliminated.

His appointment did not mean that legal or social equality had been completed. It demonstrates that commercial standing could produce selective institutional admission despite continuing confessional barriers.

## 1825: Steam navigation

On **3 October 1825**, Oppenheim helped establish the **Preußisch-Rheinische Dampfschifffahrtsgesellschaft**, later part of Köln-Düsseldorfer Deutsche Rheinschiffahrt.

The bank reportedly held **25 percent** of the new company.

This was among his most consequential investments:

- It applied steam technology to regular Rhine transport.
- It integrated capital, shipping, insurance, and regional commerce.
- It anticipated the family bank’s later role in railways, mining, metallurgy, insurance, and joint-stock banking.

Oppenheim was a financier and co-initiator, not the inventor of the steam engine or steamship.

## 1826–1828: Delegation and succession

Accounts disagree on whether Simon and Abraham received general power of attorney in **1826** or **1827**. The more common German scholarly date is 1827; some English summaries give 1826.

By **1828**, Abraham was a partner. The delegation suggests planned succession rather than an enterprise left unexpectedly without preparation.

## Death in Mainz

Oppenheim died in Mainz on **8 or 9 November 1828**, aged fifty-six.

Unresolved questions include:

- Why he was in Mainz.
- Whether he was traveling for business, family, health, or another purpose.
- Whether death was sudden.
- Cause of death.
- Attending physician.
- Exact burial site.
- Whether his remains were later transferred.

No reliable documentation of chronic illness, addiction, psychological crisis, accident, imprisonment, or violent death was located.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

## Sal. Oppenheim jr. & Cie.

### Legal and organizational character

The firm originated as a private merchant partnership. It was not initially a modern incorporated or deposit bank. Household and business occupied the same property, and capital came from family, marriage, and silent partners.

Known participants included:

- Salomon Oppenheim — managing merchant-banker.
- Samuel Wolff — senior relative, capital provider, securities client, and silent partner.
- Philipp Gompertz — relative and partner by 1814.
- Therese Oppenheim — spouse, adviser, and authorized signatory by 1821.
- Simon Oppenheim — son and successor.
- Abraham Oppenheim — son and successor.

### Business lines

- Commodity commission trade.
- Forwarding and logistics.
- Bills of exchange.
- Currency exchange.
- Arbitrage.
- Credit.
- Securities administration.
- State financial transfers.
- Shipping insurance.
- Corporate formation and equity participation.
- Limited real-estate investment.

### Geographical network

The firm’s documented or reported commercial reach included:

- Bonn.
- Cologne.
- Aachen.
- Berlin.
- Hamburg.
- Amsterdam.
- Paris.
- London.
- Through the Fould relationship: Bordeaux, Bayonne, Spain, and Atlantic trade.

No evidence establishes that Salomon personally traveled to every city in which his firm had correspondents.

## Public-infrastructure finance

Oppenheim’s firm participated in financing Cologne’s:

- Free port.
- Northern safety harbor, designed to protect ships from winter ice.

## Rheinschifffahrts-Assekuranz

- Established or reorganized with Oppenheim’s participation in 1818.
- Insured river-borne goods.
- A predecessor within the later Agrippina insurance lineage.

## Preußisch-Rheinische Dampfschifffahrtsgesellschaft

- Founded 1825.
- Oppenheim was a co-initiator and substantial investor.
- Reported family-bank stake: 25 percent.
- Later associated with the Köln-Düsseldorfer Rhine-navigation enterprise.

## Technologies and patents

No patent, invention, engineering design, or technical publication by Salomon Oppenheim has been located. His role in technology was financial and organizational: he mobilized capital for steam navigation.

## Wealth, Property, and Estate

## Sources of wealth

His wealth came from a blended merchant-bank model rather than a single speculative windfall:

- Capital and securities supplied by Samuel Wolff.
- Therese’s dowry.
- High-margin commission and forwarding business.
- Currency and bill transactions.
- Arbitrage.
- State-authorized collection of inherited Jewish fiscal obligations.
- Government financial commissions.
- Corporate and insurance investments.

## Known property

### Am Hof 16, Cologne

- Rented residence and place of business after the family came to Cologne.
- Near principal commercial markets and the Rhine harbor.

### Große Budengasse 8

- Purchased January 1808 for 17,000 francs.
- Furnishings purchased for another 2,568 francs.
- Combined town palace, family home, offices, and probable storage facilities.
- Remained associated with the bank until 1945, though later substantially rebuilt.

### Klettenberg estate

Later estate accounts include a **Klettenberg property**, used by the family as a summer retreat, valued at approximately **15,686 talers**.

## 1810 bank capital

Bank equity was reportedly **250,000 talers**, equivalent in the contemporary source to one million francs.

## Estate at death

A later reconstruction reports:

- More than **170,000 talers** in the estate’s stated financial assets.
- Painting collection valued at **3,846 talers and five silver groschen**.
- Klettenberg estate valued at **15,686 talers**.
- Große Budengasse residence valued at **16,000 talers**.
- Household furnishings valued at nearly as much as the house.
- Each of ten surviving children credited with a bank deposit of **23,261 talers and 24 silver groschen**.

These figures may represent different portions of the inventory and cannot simply be added without checking the underlying probate accounts. The report of ten beneficiary children is compatible with twelve births if Isaac and one other child predeceased distribution, but the second death has not been securely identified.

## Attributed wealth maxim

A statement attributed to Oppenheim says, in substance, that the first hundred thousand Reichstalers cost sweat and sleepless nights, but after that money multiplied almost by itself if one worked honestly and intelligently.

Its historical status is **family or institutional tradition**. The surviving source cited in the modern account was not independently inspected, and no contemporary diary or letter by Oppenheim containing it was found.

## Religious, Political, and Intellectual Development

## Judaism

Oppenheim remained Jewish throughout his life. No conversion is reported.

His religious and communal record includes:

- Growing up in Bonn’s Jewish quarter.
- Helping reconstruct organized Jewish life in Cologne.
- Serving as a communal leader.
- Financing acquisition of the Glockengasse property.
- Supporting a prayer hall, mikveh, schoolrooms, and communal personnel.
- Petitioning against discriminatory Napoleonic restrictions.

He combined Jewish communal leadership with integration into the wider commercial and Prussian civic elite.

## Emancipation and integration

His life crossed three political systems:

1. The confessional and estate-based order of the Electorate of Cologne.
2. Revolutionary and Napoleonic French rule, which first granted equality and then reimposed discriminatory restrictions.
3. Prussian rule, under which incomplete Jewish emancipation coexisted with selective public recognition.

No political manifesto or systematic ideological statement by Oppenheim survives. His acts indicate practical support for:

- Civil inclusion.
- Jewish communal autonomy and institutions.
- Participation in non-Jewish commercial bodies.
- Cooperation with successive governments.

## Political activity

His politics were primarily institutional rather than partisan:

- Petitioned French authorities in 1808.
- Served as intermediary in fiscal administration.
- Undertook a major Prussian state-finance commission.
- Accepted a Prussian court title.
- Joined the Cologne Chamber of Commerce.

His son Dagobert later became involved in the oppositional *Rheinische Zeitung*, but this cannot be projected backward as Salomon’s political position.

## Important Relationships and Networks

## Therese Stein / Deigen Levi

Spouse, capital contributor, adviser, authorized signatory, and successor. Her role is essential to the bank’s formation and continuity.

## Samuel Wolff

Relative of Salomon’s father; banker and silver dealer; initial capital provider, silent partner, and likely formative commercial influence.

## Philipp Gompertz

Relative and partner by 1814. Details of his capital share and division of labor were not located.

## Simon and Abraham Oppenheim

Sons trained for succession. They entered the firm in the early 1820s, received general authority in 1826 or 1827, and continued the bank after Salomon’s death.

## Mendelssohn & Co.

Berlin banking partner in the 1818 French indemnity transfer. This was an institutional collaboration; the precise individual Mendelssohn partners negotiating directly with Oppenheim require archival confirmation.

## Abraham Schaaffhausen

Leading Cologne banker and principal local benchmark. Schaaffhausen’s house ranked ahead of Oppenheim in 1810. The relationship combined proximity, competition, and participation in Cologne finance; no personal feud was documented.

## Rothschild house

Competitor and later family ally:

- The Frankfurt firm regarded Oppenheim as a significant competitor by 1814.
- It circulated an adverse commercial warning.
- After Salomon’s death, Abraham Oppenheim married Charlotte Beyfus, a granddaughter of Mayer Amschel Rothschild, in 1834.

## Fould family

Salomon’s daughter Helene married Benoît/Benedict Fould. This linked the Oppenheims to a Paris banking network and reportedly helped the Fould house during the 1810 crisis while giving Oppenheim closer access to French and Atlantic markets.

## Cologne municipal authorities

Mayor Maximilian von Wittgenstein supported Oppenheim’s 1808 request for relief from Napoleon’s discriminatory decree and described him favorably to the prefect.

## Cologne Jewish community

Oppenheim was a communal founder, financial patron, administrative leader, and collector of inherited fiscal obligations. This created both patronage and power, although no documented communal revolt against him was located.

## Ludwig van Beethoven

Several popular biographies state that the two Bonn contemporaries met and became lifelong friends. No letter, diary entry, account book, dedication, meeting record, or modern specialist Beethoven source was found to support the claim.

Current evidentiary status: **later unverified claim**. Shared Bonn origin and near contemporaneity do not prove acquaintance.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

## Collection of Jewish fiscal obligations

**Documented fact:** French authorities empowered Oppenheim to collect outstanding Jewish communal debts or protection obligations inherited from the electoral system.

**Financial significance:** This gave him access to recurring liquid funds and enhanced his position as an intermediary.

**Unresolved questions:**

- Exact contractual terms.
- Whether he advanced funds before collection.
- Commission rate.
- Security arrangements.
- Whether collections were coercive beyond the underlying legal obligation.
- How fellow Jews evaluated his role.

**Not established:** Embezzlement, fraud, illegal conversion of taxes, or conviction.

## 1808 suspicion of Jewish credit activity

Napoleon’s decree arose from generalized allegations of Jewish usury and fraudulent commerce. Oppenheim was subject to its restrictions and petitioned for exemption.

This was **collective discriminatory regulation**, not a finding that he personally committed usury or fraud.

Municipal evidence cited in the Cologne archival project points the other way: the mayor supported him and official assessments called him highly reputable.

## Rothschild warning of 1814

**Documented fact:** The Rothschild house warned an Amsterdam correspondent to scrutinize Oppenheim’s transactions.

**Contemporaneous allegation:** It called him excessively profit-seeking and suggested that he did not invariably remain on the proper commercial path.

**Evidence for the allegation:** The existence of the rival’s letter.

**Evidence against treating it as established misconduct:**

- No specified transaction.
- No identified injured counterparty.
- No suit or criminal proceeding.
- No adjudication.
- Contradictory official character assessments.
- Clear competitive motive.

**Status:** significant hostile business testimony, unresolved as to underlying conduct.

## Government patronage and regime adaptation

Oppenheim profited under both French and Prussian governments:

- French tax-collection authority.
- Prussian indemnity commission.
- Prussian court title.

This is documented patronage and political-economic adaptation. No evidence was found of bribery, corruption, intelligence work, clandestine political financing, or illegal state capture.

## Wealth accumulation

His rapid rise from limited inherited means to major wealth generated suspicion. Known capital sources and revenue streams substantially explain the growth, although complete ledgers are not publicly reconstructed.

No bankruptcy, insolvency, criminal charge, civil judgment, or official investigation involving Salomon personally was located.

## Later Sal. Oppenheim scandals: separation from the founder

The bank bearing his name experienced severe failures in 2008–2010 and later criminal and civil proceedings concerning Arcandor exposure, Oppenheim-Esch property transactions, breach of trust, investment advice, and former managing partners.

These events occurred approximately 180 years after Salomon’s death. They belong to the corporate name’s later history and to his descendants or unrelated managers, not to his personal biography.

Accordingly:

- They are relevant to the posthumous reputation of the name.
- They are not evidence that Salomon participated in fraud or that the founding business was criminal.
- Modern search results that collapse “Salomon Oppenheim” the man into “Sal. Oppenheim” the twentieth- and twenty-first-century bank create a major identification hazard.

## Dedicated recent adversarial pass

Searches combining his name with controversy, scandal, allegations, lawsuits, fraud, accusations, conspiracy, criticism, usury, money laundering, Freemasonry, Illuminati, and recent dates produced:

- The documented 1814 Rothschild criticism.
- The documented tax-collection role.
- The 1808 discriminatory context of accusations against Jewish lenders.
- Numerous results about the later bank’s 2009 collapse and subsequent trials.
- Generic antisemitic or conspiratorial texts that insert “Oppenheim” into lists of Jewish banking families without primary evidence.
- No newly reported 2024–2026 archival discovery establishing personal criminality, fraud, corruption, espionage, political violence, or secret-society membership.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## Myth 1: The seventeen-year-old solitary founder

### Story

In 1789, an impoverished seventeen-year-old single-handedly founded a great bank.

### Appearance and circulation

Repeated in anniversary publications, journalistic histories, bank publicity, and encyclopedic summaries.

### Evidence

- Family tradition dates his independent trading to 1789.
- A surviving bill documents financial activity by 1793.
- His age is correct.

### Qualification

He was not financially solitary. Samuel Wolff supplied major capital and securities; Therese later brought a major dowry. The precise legal form of the 1789 enterprise is not established from a surviving charter in the sources consulted.

### Reputation effect

The story made him an entrepreneurial prodigy and supplied the bank with a founding legend tied symbolically to the year of the French Revolution.

## Myth 2: An ancient wealthy court-banking dynasty

### Story

Salomon inherited the resources and expertise of generations of powerful court financiers.

### Evidence for

His father and grandfather operated in a court city and traded with the electoral court.

### Evidence against the stronger version

Modern bank historians characterize the earlier generations as modest or unsuccessful traders. Salomon depended on outside family capital and marriage wealth.

### Reputation effect

The story naturalized the family’s later wealth by projecting greatness backward.

## Myth 3: Lifelong friendship with Beethoven

### Story

Oppenheim met Ludwig van Beethoven in Bonn and remained his lifelong friend.

### Circulation

Popular English-language and derivative biographies.

### Evidence

They were near contemporaries in Bonn.

### Evidence absent

No correspondence, dedication, financial account, meeting report, or specialist Beethoven documentation was found.

### Status

**Unverified later claim.**

## Myth 4: “Freiherr von Oppenheim”

### Story

Salomon himself was Baron von Oppenheim.

### Origin

Likely backward contamination from his sons’ later ennoblement and database headings for the baronial family.

### Evidence against

His documented title was *Königlich Preußischer Oberhofagent*. Simon and Abraham were ennobled decades after 1828.

### Status

**Anachronistic misidentification.**

## Myth 5: Secret Jewish, Rothschild, Illuminati, or Masonic control

### Story

Conspiracy literature links Oppenheim to an occult or coordinated Jewish banking network manipulating governments, revolutions, or world finance.

### Circulation

Modern antisemitic tracts, Illuminati compilations, copied internet lists, and generalized banking-family conspiracy narratives.

### Evidence

- Oppenheim had documented commercial and later family connections to major Jewish banking families.
- He handled a Prussian state-finance operation.
- He became wealthy and held a court title.

These facts do not prove a secret society or centralized conspiracy.

### Evidence absent

No lodge register, Illuminati membership document, covert political directive, shared command structure, secret treaty, intelligence file, or authenticated conspiratorial correspondence was found.

### Status

**Rumor or myth.**

### Reputation effect

The firm’s long survival, family marriages, and government business made the name available for generalized banking conspiracies. Later scandals involving the bank have sometimes been retroactively attached to the founder.

## Myth 6: Founder implicated in the modern bank collapse

### Story

Search snippets and popular retellings sometimes speak of “Salomon Oppenheim’s fraud” or “Salomon Oppenheim on trial.”

### Explanation

This normally refers to the institution, later descendants, or twenty-first-century managers.

### Status

**Identity conflation.** Salomon died in 1828.

## Myth 7: Relationship to J. Robert Oppenheimer

### Story

Occasional name-based speculation treats the banker and physicist as members of the same traceable family.

### Evidence

No genealogical connection was established in the sources consulted. Oppenheim is a geographic surname borne by unrelated families.

### Status

**Unverified and unsupported.**

## Successes and Failures

## Documented successes

- Established an independent commercial operation while still a teenager, at least according to well-established family tradition.
- Survived the economic destruction of Bonn’s court-centered market.
- Entered Cologne immediately after Jewish settlement became legally possible.
- Became the city’s second-largest banker by 1810.
- Built an international correspondence network.
- Helped reconstruct Cologne’s Jewish community and institutions.
- Secured high-level French and Prussian confidence.
- Executed part of the transfer of France’s war indemnity.
- Became the first Jewish member of Cologne’s Chamber of Commerce.
- Shifted the family enterprise toward insurance, corporate formation, and steam transportation.
- Prepared succession before his death.

## Failures, limitations, or unresolved risks

- His 1808 petition did not immediately defeat Napoleon’s discriminatory decree.
- The firm remained reliant on concentrated family and partner capital.
- His role as collector of Jewish obligations placed him in an inherently conflicted position.
- Rothschild circulated doubts about his commercial behavior.
- He died at fifty-six before the corporate-finance strategy could fully develop.
- No comprehensive personal archive has yet yielded a detailed account of his inner life, education, health, or final journey.

## Death, Succession, and Immediate Aftermath

Oppenheim’s death did not dissolve the enterprise.

- Therese continued the business.
- Simon and Abraham assumed increasingly formal control.
- The commodity-trading component was phased out by approximately 1830.
- The bank expanded into railways, insurance, mining, metallurgy, steam navigation, and joint-stock banks.
- In 1834 Abraham married Charlotte Beyfus, granddaughter of Mayer Amschel Rothschild.
- Simon and Abraham were later ennobled.
- Other sons founded or joined an Amsterdam bank.
- Dagobert became associated with liberal journalism, railways, steam navigation, and Cologne civic life.

The successful transition contributed to the retrospective portrayal of Salomon as the founder not only of a firm but of a durable financial dynasty.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

## Banking legacy

Salomon’s principal historical significance lies in the transition from eighteenth-century merchant and court-connected finance to nineteenth-century corporate and industrial finance.

He began with:

- Goods.
- Bills.
- Currencies.
- Forwarding.
- Tax administration.

He ended with:

- State-scale international transfers.
- Insurance.
- Port finance.
- Joint-stock organization.
- Steamship investment.
- Planned intergenerational management.

His sons greatly enlarged this model. Consequently, later achievements are sometimes retroactively credited entirely to Salomon. Railways, heavy industry, large joint-stock banks, Colonia insurance, and many major later enterprises were predominantly projects of his successors.

## Jewish history of Cologne

His legacy includes:

- Early legal resettlement after centuries of exclusion.
- Founding leadership in the modern Jewish congregation.
- Financing of the first modern communal property at Glockengasse.
- Entry into the Chamber of Commerce.
- Demonstration of Jewish civic and commercial prominence amid continuing discrimination.

## Family descendants

Through his children, the family became connected to:

- Fould bankers in Paris.
- Rothschild descendants.
- Hertz scientists.
- Major German industrial and political networks.
- Later cultural patronage, journalism, diplomacy, archaeology, horse breeding, and philanthropy.

## Institutional survival and collapse

The firm remained under family ownership for approximately 220 years. It survived:

- Nineteenth-century industrialization.
- German unification.
- Financial crises.
- The First World War and inflation.
- Nazi persecution and forced removal of the Oppenheim name.
- The Second World War.
- Postwar reconstruction.

It lost its independence during the financial crisis and Arcandor collapse, passing to Deutsche Bank in 2009–2010.

This ending transformed the founder’s public image. Anniversary accounts had represented him as the originator of prudence, continuity, and personal liability; post-collapse journalism contrasted that mythology with the later bank’s risk concentration and governance failures.

## Memory in Cologne

The Oppenheim name remains attached to:

- Oppenheimstraße in Cologne.
- Foundations.
- Former bank properties.
- Jewish and municipal history.
- Cultural and artistic collections.
- Family philanthropy.

The street name was removed under Nazi rule in 1938 and restored in 1950, making the name itself part of the history of persecution and restitution.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are included. Mere shared geography, name similarity, or contemporaneity has been excluded.

### Mayer Amschel Rothschild

- **Nature:** Rival banking network during Salomon’s lifetime; posthumous family connection.
- **Date and place:** In 1814, the Frankfurt Rothschild house circulated a warning about Salomon to an Amsterdam correspondent. In 1834, six years after Salomon’s death, his son Abraham married Charlotte Beyfus, a granddaughter of Mayer Amschel Rothschild.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Documented fact** for the Rothschild-house warning and marriage; **not established** that Mayer Amschel personally authored the warning, met Salomon, or corresponded directly with him.
- **Qualification:** The marriage created a family tie between their descendants after both founders’ deaths. It was not a lifetime kinship between Salomon and Mayer Amschel.

### Karl Marx

- **Nature claimed:** Later indirect institutional association through Salomon’s son Dagobert.
- **Date and place:** Cologne, 1841–1843.
- **Evidence:** Dagobert Oppenheim, Salomon’s son, helped found and publish the *Rheinische Zeitung*, which later employed Marx as editor.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Documented fact as an indirect posthumous family/institutional link.**
- **Qualification:** Salomon died in 1828. He did not meet Marx through the newspaper and cannot be described as Marx’s patron, collaborator, or political associate.

### J. Robert Oppenheimer

- **Nature:** None established.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Absence of documented connection.**
- The shared surname does not establish kinship. No reliable genealogy consulted connects the Cologne banking family to J. Robert Oppenheimer.

### Joseph Süß Oppenheimer and Samuel Oppenheimer

- **Nature:** No genealogical or personal connection established.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Absence of documented connection.**
- They belong to different periods and family histories. Similar surnames and shared association with Jewish court finance have encouraged conflation.

### Adam Smith, Alexander Baring, Sir Francis Baring, Paul Moritz Warburg, Max Moritz Warburg, David Sassoon, and other atlas financiers

- No meeting, correspondence, partnership, rivalry, family tie, or explicitly acknowledged influence involving Salomon personally was documented in the sources consulted.
- His 1818 indemnity work existed within a broader European financial system in which major London and continental banks participated, but common participation in a market is insufficient to establish a personal Atlas connection.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Absence of documented connection.**

### Atlas finding

Despite the very large roster, only the Rothschild connection is directly meaningful for Salomon’s own commercial network, and even there the personal role of Mayer Amschel is not proven. The Marx link is posthumous and mediated through Dagobert. No other roster connection should be forced from shared era, city, profession, religion, or surname.
