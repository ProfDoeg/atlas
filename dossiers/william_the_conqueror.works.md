## Works by William the Conqueror

### Ducal and royal acts

- **1035–1087:** *The ducal and royal acta*. This is William’s documentary œuvre: grants, confirmations, writs, diplomas, judgments and mandates issued in his name, rather than texts physically written by him. Marie Fauroux catalogued the pre-Conquest Norman acts, while David Bates’s *Acta of William I* critically edits the English-reign corpus and distinguishes authentic, doubtful, forged and lost instruments; treating every later copy as a separate work would mistake manifestations for acts. [1][2]
- **c.1067–1077:** *Willelmes cyninges asetnysse*. An Old English royal ordinance prescribing different modes of proof when a Frenchman accused an Englishman, or conversely; it is a protocol of judicial procedure, issued under William rather than personally composed by him. [3]
- **c.1070–1076:** *Episcopales leges*. A royal ordinance directing that ecclesiastical causes be heard according to canon law outside the hundred court. Its traditional description as a complete “separation of church and state” overstates what this short administrative protocol accomplished. [3]
- **Attributed to William I; compiled later:** *Articuli Willelmi* or *Leges Willelmi*. A small Latin collection of provisions presented as William’s legislation. Scholars accept that it may preserve Conquest-period material but classify the surviving assemblage as a private compilation, not an intact code authored by William. [3][4]
- **12th century, attributed retrospectively:** *Leis Willelme*. An Anglo-Norman legal compilation claiming to represent William’s law; the text is an imposture or later treatise, not a surviving work by the king, although some of its material may descend from earlier customs. Its resistance to inclusion is essential: it belongs to William’s attributed afterlife, not securely to his œuvre. [4]
- **12th century, attributed retrospectively:** *Leges Edwardi Confessoris*. A treatise purporting to record Edward the Confessor’s laws as confirmed for William. It was composed after William’s death and is catalogued as an imposture; it is therefore not his creation despite making his authority part of its textual machinery. [4]

### Surveys and administrative projects

- **1085–1086:** *The Domesday survey*. A kingdom-wide fiscal and tenurial inquiry ordered by William at Christmas 1085. It was a project and protocol—commissioners gathered sworn local testimony according to prescribed questions—whose surviving manifestations include Great Domesday, Little Domesday and several regional working records; William initiated and received the survey but did not author their entries. [5]
- **1086:** *Great Domesday*. The principal, unfinished fair-copy manifestation of the survey, covering most of England but omitting the intended account of Essex, Norfolk and Suffolk. Calling it simply William’s “book” conceals its production by royal officials and its incomplete state. [5]
- **1086:** *Little Domesday*. The fuller surviving account of Essex, Norfolk and Suffolk. It is probably material awaiting reduction into the format of Great Domesday, although the precise relationship between the two volumes remains debated. [5]
- **1086:** *Exon Domesday*. A working compilation preserving material for south-western counties, including information omitted during the making of Great Domesday. It is a documentary manifestation of William’s survey rather than a separately commissioned literary work. [5]
- **1086:** *Inquisitio Eliensis*. A regional record of the survey’s findings for lands associated with Ely. Like the other satellite texts, it survives because of local institutional transmission and should not be mistaken for William’s personal composition. [5]
- **1086:** *Inquisitio comitatus Cantabrigiensis*. A record of Cambridgeshire survey material retaining details and procedural traces suppressed in Great Domesday. It is evidence of the project’s making rather than a finished royal book. [5]

### Monastic and ecclesiastical foundations

- **from 1063; consecrated 1077:** *Abbaye Saint-Étienne de Caen (Abbaye-aux-Hommes)*. William’s Benedictine foundation and burial church, created partly in connection with the papal reconciliation of his marriage to Matilda. The surviving complex is not wholly his building: construction and alteration continued under successors. [6][7]
- **c.1071–1087:** *Battle Abbey*. A Benedictine foundation endowed by William as a memorial to his victory at Hastings and placed on the battlefield. William died before the abbey church was completed, so the foundation is securely his project while much of its architectural manifestation belongs to later builders. [8][9]

### Castles and fortified landscapes

- **from c.1060:** *Caen Castle*. A ducal castle established by William on the rocky spur above Caen. Its later walls, halls and gates represent centuries of rebuilding, so “William’s castle” properly names the foundation and initial enclosure rather than every surviving stone. [6][10]
- **September–October 1066:** *Pevensey Castle*. William’s invading army adapted the Roman fort as an expeditionary stronghold immediately after landing. The attribution concerns a rapidly made earth-and-timber occupation; the prominent masonry remains are principally later manifestations. [11]
- **September–October 1066:** *Hastings Castle*. A prefabricated or rapidly erected timber fortification established during the invasion and represented in the Bayeux Tapestry. Subsequent stone rebuilding makes the surviving ruin an altered descendant of the original work. [11]
- **1066–1067:** *The first royal castle in London*. William ordered a fortress within the south-east corner of the Roman walls, the nucleus of the Tower of London. The earliest enclosure and the later White Tower must be distinguished. [12]
- **c.1075–c.1100:** *The White Tower*. The monumental stone keep commissioned by William and traditionally associated with Bishop Gundulf of Rochester’s supervision. Building continued after William’s death, making it an unfinished royal project at his death rather than a wholly completed personal creation. [12]
- **1066:** *Dover Castle’s Conquest-period fortification*. William strengthened Dover after its submission, but little can be assigned securely to his works and the dominant stone castle is Henry II’s. This is therefore an attested fortification project whose physical boundaries remain uncertain. [13]
- **1067:** *Winchester Castle*. A royal castle founded within Winchester shortly after the Conquest. Almost nothing of its first timber phase survives, and the later great hall is not William’s work. [14]
- **1068:** *Exeter Castle (Rougemont Castle)*. A royal fortress established after Exeter’s surrender, imposed partly through the demolition of urban property. Later masonry survives, but the extent attributable to William’s campaign is uncertain. [15]
- **1068:** *Warwick Castle*. A motte-and-bailey foundation made during William’s advance through the Midlands. The motte preserves part of that first intervention; the famous stone buildings are later works. [16]
- **1068:** *Nottingham Castle*. A royal earth-and-timber castle established during William’s northern campaign. Its medieval successors and the present ducal mansion are not manifestations William could have seen. [17]
- **1068:** *Lincoln Castle*. A royal castle planted within the Roman enclosure, with houses cleared to provide its site. Its earthworks may preserve the Conquest-period plan, while the surviving stone structures are substantially later. [18]
- **1068:** *Huntingdon Castle*. A royal motte-and-bailey established during the same campaign. The attribution is to the strategic foundation and earthwork, not to a surviving building. [19]
- **1068:** *Cambridge Castle*. A royal castle established as William returned south from York, probably executed locally by Sheriff Picot. It resists simple personal authorship: William ordered the work, while subordinates and displaced townspeople materially made it. [20]
- **1068:** *The first York Castle*. A royal motte-and-bailey erected at the confluence of the Ouse and Foss, on the site later occupied by Clifford’s Tower. It was destroyed during the 1069 rising and rebuilt, so the visible stone tower is not William’s. [21]
- **1069:** *The second York Castle*. A second royal stronghold erected across the Ouse at Baile Hill. It formed, with the first castle, a paired apparatus for controlling the city rather than an independent monumental building. [21]
- **1069–1070:** *Stafford Castle*. A royal fortification associated with suppression of resistance in the Midlands; it was apparently abandoned or destroyed before the Domesday survey. Its attribution and exact site-history are less secure than those of the castles explicitly described in contemporary narrative sources. [22]
- **1070:** *Chester Castle*. A royal castle established following William’s north-western campaign, probably first in earth and timber. Later rebuilding has obscured the extent of the Conqueror’s work. [23]
- **1066–1087:** *The Conquest castle network*. As an obra this was a distributed military, residential and administrative system rather than a single building: royal foundations worked alongside castles raised by William’s tenants. “William built castles everywhere” is a contemporary description of policy, but it does not make every eleventh-century Norman castle his personal commission; many local attributions remain traditional or disputed. [2][11]

### Landscape and jurisdiction

- **traditionally c.1079:** *The New Forest*. A legal and environmental project that placed an inhabited Hampshire landscape under royal forest law for hunting. William did not create its woods or design a park from empty land; what he made was a jurisdiction, and even the conventional date 1079 lacks firm contemporary documentary support. [24][25]

### Royal image-making

- **1066–1087:** *William I’s English coinage*. An official series manufactured by many named moneyers and mints in successive types bearing William’s royal name and image. It is better classified as a regulated serial manifestation of kingship than as personally designed sculpture; individual dies and coins are workshop products, and the standard British Museum catalogue distinguishes the types. [26][27]
- **after 1066:** *William I’s English great seal*. A double-sided instrument and royal image presenting William as enthroned king on one face and mounted duke on the other. The seal authenticated acts made in his name, but its engraver is unknown and surviving wax impressions are manifestations from a lost matrix. [1][28]
- **c.1070s, attribution rejected or unproved:** *Bayeux Tapestry*. William is its central ruler and beneficiary, but no reliable evidence shows that he commissioned or designed it; patronage is more often associated, cautiously, with his half-brother Odo of Bayeux and an English workshop. It should therefore not be counted as William’s work, though it is one of the principal creations of his regime. [29]
