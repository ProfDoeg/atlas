# Taj al-Din Ali ibn Muhammad Ibn al-Durayhim al-Mawsili: Research Dossier

## Scope, identity, and evidentiary method

This dossier concerns the fourteenth-century Mosuli scholar, merchant, Mamluk official, cryptologist, mathematician, poet, religious writer, and manuscript compiler commonly called Ibn al-Durayhim.

The most important biographical witness is Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn Khalīl ibn Aybak al-Ṣafadī (d. 764/1363), who knew Ibn al-Durayhim personally, questioned him about his birth and education, met him repeatedly in Damascus, corresponded with him in verse, and recorded some information from Ibn al-Durayhim’s own handwriting. Al-Ṣafadī’s notices in *Aʿyān al-ʿaṣr* and *al-Wāfī bi-l-wafayāt* therefore carry more evidentiary weight than substantially later dictionaries.

Other medieval accounts—including those transmitted by Ibn Ḥajar al-ʿAsqalānī, Ibn Qāḍī Shuhba, and later al-Shawkānī—preserve additional details but sometimes disagree over his death year, offices, and the titles or attribution of particular works.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a contemporary witness, dated manuscript, colophon, or closely contemporary administrative/biographical record.
- **Reported fact:** stated in later biographical literature but not independently confirmed here.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** conclusion drawn by modern researchers from manuscripts and historical notices.
- **Disputed attribution/date:** incompatible catalogues, manuscripts, or biographers exist.
- **Allegation or hostile claim:** an accusation recorded without adequate proof.
- **Legend or reception narrative:** a historically influential story whose literal accuracy is uncertain.

## Basic Identifying Information

### Full name

Al-Ṣafadī gives the extended genealogy as:

**ʿAlī ibn Muḥammad ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz ibn Futūḥ ibn Ibrāhīm ibn Abī Bakr ibn Abī al-Qāsim ibn Saʿīd ibn Muḥammad ibn Hishām ibn ʿUmar.**

Common scholarly form:

**Tāj al-Dīn Abū al-Ḥasan ʿAlī ibn Muḥammad ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz al-Thaʿlabī al-Mawṣilī al-Shāfiʿī, known as Ibn al-Durayhim.**

### Alternative names and forms

- تاج الدين علي بن محمد بن عبد العزيز ابن الدريهم الموصلي
- Tāj al-Dīn ʿAlī ibn Muḥammad Ibn al-Durayhim al-Mawṣilī
- Ibn al-Durayhim
- Ibn al-Durayhim al-Mawṣilī
- Ibnü’d-Düreyhim in Turkish scholarship
- Al-Mawṣilī: “of Mosul”
- Al-Dimashqī: “of Damascus,” used in some later descriptions
- Al-Miṣrī: “the Egyptian,” in some manuscript and printed-book catalogues
- Al-Thaʿlabī or al-Taghlibī: lineage/tribal attribution, with variation in modern transliteration
- Al-Shāfiʿī: follower and jurist of the Shāfiʿī legal school
- Ibn Abī al-Khayr: an alternative byname in some later biographical dictionaries
- Abū al-Ḥasan: teknonym
- Tāj al-Dīn: honorific, “Crown of the Religion”

Some sources style him **al-ṣadr al-raʾīs**, “the eminent chief,” alongside such conventional learned honorifics as *al-fāḍil*, *al-ʿālim*, *al-naḥrīr*, and *al-mutafannin*.

### Origin of “Ibn al-Durayhim”

**Documented report from al-Ṣafadī:** *al-Durayhim* was a nickname attached to the ancestor Saʿīd. Al-Ṣafadī says Saʿīd once pronounced or used the diminutive *durayhim*, “little dirham,” and the expression remained his nickname. The family’s descendants consequently became known as “Ibn al-Durayhim.”

### Birth

- **Place:** Mosul, on the Tigris, then a major city of the Jazīra.
- **Islamic date:** the night of Thursday at mid-Shaʿbān 712 AH.
- **Gregorian equivalent:** December 1312; conversion of a medieval lunar date to an exact Gregorian day varies slightly between tables.
- **Evidence:** al-Ṣafadī says that he asked Ibn al-Durayhim directly and received this date and place from him.

### Death

The best-supported account is:

- **Place:** Qūṣ in Upper Egypt, north of Luxor, while travelling toward the Red Sea route to Abyssinia.
- **Islamic date:** Ṣafar 762 AH.
- **Gregorian equivalent:** approximately December 1360–January 1361.
- **Circumstance:** death during an unwanted diplomatic mission from Sultan al-Nāṣir Ḥasan to the ruler of Christian Ethiopia/Abyssinia.
- **Age:** approximately forty-eight lunar years, not fifty-four.

Competing dates include:

- **762 AH / 1360–61:** supported by the contemporary al-Ṣafadī tradition and accepted by the TDV *İslâm Ansiklopedisi*.
- **766 AH / 1364–65:** given by al-Shawkānī and repeated in some later works.
- **776 AH:** found in at least one modern secondary publication; almost certainly a typographical or transmission error because it conflicts with the reported mission, contemporary notice, and purported age.
- **1359 or 1362:** loose conversions or database harmonizations rather than securely documented alternative death events.

There is no evidence that he survived into a later period under another identity.

## Family and Ancestry

### Ancestral line

The extended paternal sequence recorded by al-Ṣafadī is:

ʿAlī → Muḥammad → ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz → Futūḥ → Ibrāhīm → Abū Bakr → Abū al-Qāsim → Saʿīd → Muḥammad → Hishām → ʿUmar.

The attribution al-Thaʿlabī/al-Taghlibī probably identifies an Arab tribal or lineage affiliation, but the exact genealogy behind the nisba is not reconstructed in the surviving notices.

### Father

His father was **Muḥammad**, also associated in his son’s honorific genealogy with titles such as Muwaffaq al-Dīn and Najm al-Dīn in some formulations.

**Reported fact:** Muḥammad died while ʿAlī was still young and left him a very large estate.

### Inheritance dispute

According to Ibn Ḥajar’s account as used by modern biographers:

- The inheritance was placed under the guardianship or control of Mosul’s *shaykh al-balad* or other senior local custodians.
- Other people took control of the property while Ibn al-Durayhim was a minor.
- Upon reaching legal discretion or adolescence, he recovered only part of it.
- He used the recovered portion as trading capital.

This is the earliest identifiable financial conflict in his life, although no original legal pleading or judgment has been located. The description should therefore be treated as a **reported fact**, not proof of a formally adjudicated embezzlement.

### Other relatives

The available notices do not securely identify:

- his mother;
- siblings;
- a wife or wives;
- concubines or partners;
- children;
- direct descendants.

Later references to another “Ibn al-Durayhim” must not automatically be treated as relatives. The byname existed in more than one family and period.

## Childhood and Early Life in Mosul, 1312–c. 1332

Ibn al-Durayhim grew up in Mosul after losing his father. His youth combined two conditions repeatedly emphasized in later biography:

1. descent from a prosperous and cultivated household; and
2. loss of control over much of the family fortune.

No source establishes that he grew up in destitution. The better reconstruction is that his resources were seriously reduced but not eliminated: enough capital eventually remained for long-distance commerce.

Mosul’s location connected Iraq, northern Syria, Anatolia, Iran, and the Mamluk territories. This environment helps explain—but does not by itself prove—his later interest in multiple scripts, commercial movement, secret correspondence, calculation, and languages.

## Education and Formation

### Qurʾānic studies

Ibn al-Durayhim told al-Ṣafadī that he read the Qurʾān in its recognized variant readings with:

- **Shams al-Dīn Abū Bakr ibn al-ʿAlam Sanjar al-Mawṣilī**, a Mosuli specialist in recitation.

His later works on the opening letters of Qurʾānic suras, individual suras, divine names, and numerological correspondences grew out of this formation.

### Shāfiʿī jurisprudence

He studied Shāfiʿī law with:

- **Zayn al-Dīn ʿAlī Ibn Shaykh al-ʿUwayna**, a Mosuli scholar of jurisprudence, theology, Arabic, and mathematics;
- **Sharaf al-Dīn ʿAbd Allāh ibn Yūnus**, under whom he studied *al-Ḥāwī al-ṣaghīr* or material from the commentary of the teacher’s father, Kamāl al-Dīn.

He memorized a work called *al-Hādī*. Its exact bibliographical identity is not resolved by the abbreviated notice.

### Arabic language and grammar

He reported memorizing:

- *al-Mulḥa*;
- Ibn Muʿṭī’s *Alfiyya*;
- Ibn Mālik’s *Alfiyya*.

He studied Ibn Mālik’s *al-Tashīl* with Zayn al-Dīn Ibn Shaykh al-ʿUwayna, who reportedly completed a commentary on that work.

### Mathematics

Ibn al-Durayhim said that he read “much of mathematics” with Zayn al-Dīn Ibn Shaykh al-ʿUwayna. His surviving reputation and book titles indicate work in:

- arithmetic;
- computational rules;
- numerical correspondences;
- inheritance calculations;
- magic squares or *awfāq*;
- letter-number relationships;
- calendars and astronomical reckoning.

### Hadith study in Egypt

In Egypt he studied or heard:

- *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* with **ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn Ibn al-Turkumānī**, **Shams al-Dīn al-Iṣfahānī**, and **Nūr al-Dīn al-Hamadhānī**;
- *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim*;
- *Sunan Abī Dāwūd*;
- part of al-Tirmidhī’s collection.

### Abū Ḥayyān al-Andalusī

The famous grammarian **Athīr al-Dīn Abū Ḥayyān al-Andalusī** authorized Ibn al-Durayhim to transmit material and heard him read some of Abū Ḥayyān’s writings.

This is a documented teacher-student connection, though the sources do not indicate prolonged exclusive discipleship.

### Formative intellectual traditions

His work combines several traditions:

- Shāfiʿī law and Sunni hadith learning;
- Arabic grammar, morphology, and lexicography;
- arithmetic and letter-number calculation;
- *ʿilm al-ḥurūf*, the science of letters;
- *awfāq*, including numerical and letter squares;
- riddles and poetic enigmas;
- dream, omen, physiognomic, and prognostic literature;
- practical cryptography and cryptanalysis;
- Mamluk chancery and court practice;
- zoological and pharmacological compilation.

Modern categories such as “scientist,” “occultist,” “bureaucrat,” and “literary scholar” divide fields that his own intellectual setting treated as partially overlapping.

## Early Commercial Career and First Journeys

### From Mosul to Damascus and Cairo

After recovering part of his inheritance, Ibn al-Durayhim travelled first to Damascus and then to Cairo.

Al-Ṣafadī dates his first arrival in Egypt as a merchant to either:

- 732 AH / 1332; or
- 733 AH / 1333.

He subsequently returned north and travelled between Syria and Egypt on several occasions.

### Merchant to rulers

Sources characterize him as a merchant who sold goods to rulers and high officials. The precise commodities are not identified.

His combination of capital, literacy, calculation, calligraphy, and access to elite households drew him into Mamluk court society. He accumulated another substantial fortune in Cairo.

No surviving source permits reconstruction of:

- a formal partnership;
- warehouse holdings;
- real estate;
- ships or caravans owned;
- profit rates;
- tax arrangements;
- the exact merchandise supplied to the court.

### The 200,000-dirham receivable

**Reported fact with specific financial detail:** the Mamluk *Dīwān al-Khāṣṣ*, the bureau concerned with the sultan’s private revenues and expenditures, owed him more than **200,000 dirhams** for merchandise he had supplied.

He repeatedly tried to recover the debt and failed.

The episode was not a modern lawsuit in a surviving court docket. It was a merchant’s claim against a politically controlled treasury. Later accounts treat the unpaid receivable as a major cause of his subsequent political vulnerability, forced movements, and financial decline.

His earlier wealth is said eventually to have disappeared. How much was lost through the unpaid debt, confiscation, ordinary commercial reverses, or court politics cannot be separated precisely.

## Chronological Life History

### 1312: Birth in Mosul

Born at mid-Shaʿbān 712 in Mosul. The date derives from his own answer to al-Ṣafadī.

### Childhood and adolescence

His father died, leaving a large estate. Guardians or local power-holders controlled it; Ibn al-Durayhim recovered only a portion. He pursued Qurʾān, law, grammar, mathematics, and associated sciences in Mosul.

### 1332–33: First commercial arrival in Cairo

He reached Egypt as a merchant, then returned north. His movements thereafter repeatedly joined Mosul or northern Syria, Damascus, and Cairo.

### 1330s–1340s: Court access and government service

In Cairo he became associated with Mamluk emirs and the sultans’ inner governmental apparatus. Sources describe service in the *Dīwān al-Khāṣṣ* and close relations with leading emirs.

He was especially close to Sultan **al-Malik al-Kāmil Shaʿbān**, who ruled briefly in 746–747/1345–46, and to members of the *khāṣṣakiyya*, the sultan’s intimate military-administrative circle.

His cryptographic skill would have been directly relevant to chancery correspondence, but no surviving appointment instrument says that “cryptanalyst” was his formal office. Modern descriptions should distinguish demonstrated expertise from an undocumented job title.

### 1346–47: Political reversal

After al-Kāmil Shaʿbān’s fall, Ibn al-Durayhim remained exposed to changing court factions.

Al-Ṣafadī says that opponents incited Sultan **al-Muẓaffar Ḥājjī** against him. The sultan ordered him out to Syria shortly before al-Muẓaffar’s own death.

- **Event:** forced removal or banishment from Egypt.
- **Date:** 748 AH / 1347–48.
- **Status:** documented by the contemporary biographical account.
- **Underlying accusation:** not specified.
- **Finding:** surviving evidence establishes political expulsion, not a proved criminal offense.

Ibn al-Durayhim arrived in Damascus after Ramaḍān 748. Al-Ṣafadī met him there repeatedly.

### 1348–49: Damascus house raid and confiscation

A letter from the chamberlain or *dawādār* al-Ḥājj Bahādur, associated with the powerful emir Yūsuf al-Dīn Baybughā Arūs, instructed the Damascus authorities to remove Ibn al-Durayhim.

The order went to **Sayf al-Dīn Qarābughā**, *dawādār* of the deputy of Syria.

The authorities:

- raided Ibn al-Durayhim’s house;
- seized his books;
- expelled him from Damascus;
- sent or forced him toward Aleppo.

Al-Ṣafadī dates this to one of the two Jumādā months of 749 AH, approximately 1348.

This is the best-documented coercive episode in his life. The source does not report a judicial hearing, indictment, conviction, or stated criminal charge.

The two *dawādārs* involved reportedly died within four months. Later retellings can give this sequence a providential or ominous tone, but al-Ṣafadī does not demonstrate supernatural causation.

### 1349–50: Aleppo, Damascus, and the abandoned pilgrimage

After the expulsion he went to Aleppo.

He returned to Damascus in Ramaḍān 750 AH intending to undertake the pilgrimage to Mecca. The pilgrimage “was not decreed” or did not become possible, and he returned to Aleppo.

Why he could not proceed is not recorded. Financial difficulty, official restriction, illness, security, and ordinary logistical failure remain possibilities, not facts.

### 1350s: Repeated movement and public offices

He subsequently moved among Aleppo, Damascus, and Cairo.

Later biographical notices place him in several offices:

- teacher or lecturer at the Umayyad Mosque in Damascus;
- an official associated with the mosque’s bureau or accounts;
- an official in the *Dīwān al-Asrā*, the bureau of captives/prisoners;
- service in other Mamluk *dīwāns*.

The exact sequence, tenure, salary, and functions are incompletely documented.

A secondary claim that he travelled “as an ambassador between Damascus and Cairo” is plausible only in the general sense of carrying business or official communications; no specific bilateral diplomatic commission has been established.

### 1354: *Kitāb Manāfiʿ al-ḥayawān*

A manuscript now in the Royal Library of El Escorial, Arabic MS 898, identifies Ibn al-Durayhim as its compiler and is dated 755 AH / 1354.

Modern codicological study describes it as:

- 154 folios;
- approximately 185 × 265 mm;
- elegant naskh script;
- thirteen lines per page;
- ninety-one surviving illustrations;
- a later Escorial binding;
- damaged or missing opening material.

The work reorganizes zoological, medical, magical, and pharmacological traditions concerning animals. It draws substantially on the Ibn Bakhtīshūʿ animal-book tradition and ultimately on Aristotelian and other inherited zoological materials.

Whether Ibn al-Durayhim personally painted every miniature is not firmly proved by the available evidence. The colophon associates him with compiling the work and some modern descriptions understand it to credit him with its images; art-historical scholarship treats the codex as a production requiring careful distinction among compiler, copyist, designer, and painters.

The manuscript is important because relatively few illustrated Mamluk codices provide both a date and a named compiler.

### 1359–60: Return to Egypt

He returned to Egypt in late 759 or early 760 AH and remained there approximately two years or more.

### 1360: Embassy to Abyssinia

Sultan **al-Nāṣir Ḥasan** sent him as an envoy to the ruler of Abyssinia/Ethiopia.

The surviving account says that Ibn al-Durayhim undertook the mission unwillingly or “without an open heart.”

The precise object of the embassy is not preserved in the sources consulted. No surviving Ethiopian counterpart document has been identified here. It should therefore not be tied to a particular ecclesiastical, commercial, military, or prisoner-exchange negotiation without further evidence.

### 1360–61: Death at Qūṣ

He travelled south through Upper Egypt but died at Qūṣ in Ṣafar 762 before reaching Abyssinia.

No source supplies:

- a diagnosed illness;
- evidence of poisoning or violence;
- a fatal accident;
- a burial place;
- an autopsy or medical description.

The cause of death is unknown.

## Professions, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Professions and roles

- merchant and court supplier;
- scholar and teacher;
- Shāfiʿī jurist;
- hadith student and transmitter;
- grammarian;
- mathematician and calculator;
- cryptographer and cryptanalyst;
- poet and riddle composer;
- letter-science and magic-square specialist;
- manuscript compiler;
- government and *dīwān* official;
- diplomatic envoy.

### Institutional settings

- scholarly circles of Mosul;
- Cairene hadith and grammar circles;
- the Mamluk *Dīwān al-Khāṣṣ*;
- the courts of al-Kāmil Shaʿbān, al-Muẓaffar Ḥājjī, and al-Nāṣir Ḥasan;
- the Umayyad Mosque in Damascus;
- the mosque’s associated bureau or accounts;
- the *Dīwān al-Asrā*;
- the Mamluk diplomatic service.

### Cryptographic technology

In *Miftāḥ al-kunūz fī īḍāḥ al-marmūz*, Ibn al-Durayhim organizes encipherment into eight broad types:

1. reversal;
2. systematic letter substitution;
3. addition or omission of letters;
4. use of devices, including a chessboard and perforated tablet;
5. replacement of letters by *abjad* numerical values;
6. concealment of letters through words;
7. assignment of letters to categories of objects;
8. invented graphic signs.

His cryptanalytic procedure includes:

- identifying the language and script;
- knowing grammar and morphology;
- counting the number of signs;
- measuring their frequency;
- locating word divisions where possible;
- comparing ciphertext frequencies with ordinary language;
- using word lengths;
- testing likely words and grammatical particles;
- examining permissible and impermissible letter combinations;
- verifying proposed readings against context.

The surviving treatise gives two worked examples in which he reconstructs encrypted Arabic verse step by step.

### Languages and scripts

The treatise discusses the sizes or features of approximately fifteen alphabets or writing systems, including:

- Arabic;
- Hebrew;
- Syriac;
- Persian;
- Turkish;
- Armenian;
- Greek;
- “Roman” scripts;
- Coptic;
- Indian;
- Mongol.

Knowledge of an alphabet’s inventory does not establish spoken fluency. Claims that Ibn al-Durayhim “knew fifteen languages” should therefore be qualified: his treatise demonstrates comparative scribal knowledge, not necessarily conversational competence.

### Wealth and property

Documentable financial points are limited:

- he inherited a large paternal fortune;
- much of it was controlled or lost during his minority;
- he recovered capital and entered long-distance trade;
- he became wealthy in Cairo;
- the state allegedly owed him more than 200,000 dirhams;
- the debt was not successfully recovered;
- his books were confiscated in the Damascus raid;
- his great commercial wealth was later said to have dissipated.

No reliable inventory of land, houses, slaves, shops, endowments, movable goods, or heirs survives in the consulted material.

### Patents and inventions

Patents did not exist in his institutional world in their modern form.

No surviving document grants him a monopoly over a cipher or device. His identifiable innovations or syntheses lie in:

- systematic classification of cipher types;
- coordinated use of linguistic and frequency analysis;
- detailed worked cryptanalysis;
- possible early tableau-like or multiple-substitution arrangements;
- pedagogical verse for teaching cryptology.

Claims that he “invented the Vigenère cipher” are too strong. His work contains structures compared by historians to later tabular and polyalphabetic devices, but direct transmission to Johannes Trithemius, Leon Battista Alberti, Blaise de Vigenère, or later European cryptographers has not been demonstrated.

## Religious, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

### Sunni-Shāfiʿī formation

His formal legal identity was Shāfiʿī, supported by his teachers and curriculum. His study of the canonical hadith collections places him firmly within mainstream Sunni learned institutions.

### Science of letters and *awfāq*

His intense work on letters operated across several registers:

- grammatical structure;
- ciphers;
- Qurʾānic interpretation;
- numerical correspondences;
- the divine name;
- magic squares;
- prognostication;
- literary riddles.

It is misleading either to reduce all of this to “modern cryptography” or to dismiss it all as “occultism.” The same expertise in alphabets, positional transformation, numerical values, and combinatorial manipulation supported both cryptanalytic and esoteric applications.

### Qurʾānic and devotional writing

Numerous titles concern:

- Sūrat al-Fātiḥa;
- Sūrat al-Wāqiʿa;
- Sūrat Nūḥ;
- Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ;
- the disconnected letters at the beginnings of suras;
- the “Greatest Name” of God;
- phrases of praise;
- the nature of the spirit;
- names of prophets and of Muḥammad.

These works indicate a sustained movement from conventional exegesis into letter-symbolic and numerological interpretation.

### Anti-dhimmi employment polemic

*Nahj/Manhaj al-ṣawāb fī qubḥ istiktāb ahl al-kitāb* argues against employing Jews and Christians as government scribes.

The work belongs to a Mamluk polemical literature that treated administrative authority over Muslims as religiously and politically sensitive. It draws on:

- Qurʾānic passages;
- hadith;
- legal arguments about the *dhimma*;
- historical anecdotes;
- claims that non-Muslim officials were disloyal or harmful;
- complaints about control of fiscal and scribal knowledge.

Modern scholarship identifies its central thesis as the exclusion of non-Muslims from positions of governmental authority. The text contains broad hostile claims about Jews and Christians and treats religious difference as a criterion for employment.

These propositions are documented features of the work, not evidence that every accusation it repeats against named or unnamed non-Muslim officials was true.

Authorship has sometimes been catalogued under “ʿAlī ibn Abī al-Fatḥ” or “Nūr al-Dīn ʿAlī Ibn Abī al-Fatḥ,” creating confusion. Manuscript comparison and recent theses generally attribute it to Ibn al-Durayhim, but cataloguing variation should remain visible.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Teachers

- Abū Bakr ibn al-ʿAlam Sanjar al-Mawṣilī — Qurʾānic readings.
- Zayn al-Dīn ʿAlī Ibn Shaykh al-ʿUwayna — Shāfiʿī law, grammar, and mathematics.
- Sharaf al-Dīn ʿAbd Allāh ibn Yūnus — Shāfiʿī jurisprudence.
- ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn Ibn al-Turkumānī — hadith in Egypt.
- Shams al-Dīn al-Iṣfahānī — hadith.
- Nūr al-Dīn al-Hamadhānī — hadith.
- Abū Ḥayyān al-Andalusī — Arabic works and scholarly authorization.

### Al-Ṣafadī

**Documented relationship:** contemporary scholar, correspondent, admiring evaluator, and personal witness.

Al-Ṣafadī met Ibn al-Durayhim repeatedly in Damascus and exchanged riddles, enigmas, and verse with him. He praised Ibn al-Durayhim above Ibn Dunaynīr in the arts of ciphers and letter manipulation.

His praise is valuable eyewitness testimony but is not neutral measurement. It belongs to a learned culture of literary admiration and competitive comparison.

### Mamluk patrons and political associates

- **Al-Malik al-Kāmil Shaʿbān:** principal royal patron or close court connection.
- **Al-Muẓaffar Ḥājjī:** ruler who expelled him to Syria after being incited against him.
- **Al-Nāṣir Ḥasan:** later employed him and sent him as envoy to Abyssinia.
- Members of the **khāṣṣakiyya:** court intimates and benefactors.
- **Yūsuf al-Dīn Baybughā Arūs:** powerful emir within the political network behind his second expulsion.
- **Al-Ḥājj Bahādur:** *dawādār* whose letter initiated the Damascus action.
- **Sayf al-Dīn Qarābughā:** Syrian deputy’s *dawādār* who carried out the order.

### Intellectual predecessors

- Al-Kindī — early systematic frequency analysis and cryptanalysis.
- Ibn Dunaynīr — author of an earlier broad cryptological work used in the later tradition.
- ʿAfīf al-Dīn ʿAlī ibn ʿAdlān al-Mawṣilī — practical cryptanalysis and analysis of unspaced cryptograms.
- Ibn Bakhtīshūʿ tradition — source tradition for the animal book.
- Aristotle and Arabic Aristotelian zoology — ultimate source material in animal literature.

### Later transmitter

- **Aḥmad al-Qalqashandī:** quoted or closely reproduced Ibn al-Durayhim’s cryptological material in *Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā*, preserving knowledge of it for later readers.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### Loss of his inheritance

- **Status:** reported fact.
- **Claim:** guardians or other local figures appropriated much of his paternal estate.
- **Evidence:** later biographical report ultimately associated with Ibn Ḥajar’s notice.
- **Unresolved:** identities, legal process, amount, and whether the loss constituted unlawful seizure under contemporary law.

### Unpaid state debt

- **Status:** detailed reported fact.
- **Claim:** the *Dīwān al-Khāṣṣ* owed him more than 200,000 dirhams for merchandise.
- **Evidence:** medieval biographical tradition.
- **Unresolved:** account ledgers, commodity list, whether any partial payment occurred, and whether rival officials disputed the debt.

### Expulsion by al-Muẓaffar Ḥājjī

- **Status:** documented political action.
- **Claim:** unnamed persons incited the sultan against him.
- **Evidence:** al-Ṣafadī.
- **Unresolved:** the accusations and identities of the informants.
- **Finding:** no conviction or proven offense is preserved.

### Raid, book confiscation, and expulsion from Damascus

- **Status:** documented coercive administrative action.
- **Date:** 749 AH / 1348–49.
- **Evidence:** al-Ṣafadī.
- **Finding:** his house was raided, books seized, and he was expelled.
- **Unresolved:** whether manuscripts were destroyed, entered official libraries, sold, or later restored.

This confiscation is a plausible reason for the disappearance of some of his approximately eighty works, but no surviving shelf mark proves that a specific confiscated volume is identical with a manuscript now in a particular collection.

### Cryptography and intelligence activity

- **Documented fact:** he mastered ciphers, served court circles, worked in government bureaus, and explained that rulers valued secret writing.
- **Reasonable inference:** his expertise had intelligence and diplomatic utility.
- **Unverified claim:** that he headed a formal Mamluk intelligence or codebreaking office.
- **No evidence located:** espionage trial, treason charge, clandestine foreign allegiance, or criminal misuse of cryptography.

### Polemics against Jews and Christians

- **Documented fact:** *Manhaj al-ṣawāb* advocates excluding Jews and Christians from government scribal employment.
- **Documented content:** the work circulates collective allegations of hostility or disloyalty and treats non-Muslim administrative power as dangerous.
- **Evidentiary limitation:** the polemic is evidence of Ibn al-Durayhim’s argument and Mamluk political-religious discourse, not independent proof of its generalized accusations.
- **Modern controversy:** current catalogues and online editions sometimes present the book devotionally or apologetically, while modern religious-studies work analyzes it as an exclusionary anti-dhimmi text.

### Attribution of *Manhaj al-ṣawāb*

- **Status:** disputed catalogue attribution, increasingly resolved in favor of Ibn al-Durayhim.
- One manuscript or catalogue tradition names ʿAlī Ibn al-Durayhim.
- Another associates it with ʿAlī ibn Abī al-Fatḥ.
- Ibn al-Durayhim’s own titulature includes a chain containing “Ibn Abī al-Fatḥ,” which may explain part of the confusion.
- Recent doctoral and master’s editions attribute it to him.

### Adversarial-search result

Dedicated Arabic and English searches combining his name with allegations, scandal, fraud, conspiracy, criticism, lawsuits, and related terms—including material indexed in 2024–26—found no modern criminal allegation, lawsuit, corruption investigation, sexual scandal, or credible conspiracy claim concerning the historical person.

The negative and contested material actually supported by sources consists of:

- inheritance dispossession;
- a major disputed government debt;
- court factional hostility;
- banishment;
- a house raid and confiscation;
- uncertain book attributions;
- competing death dates;
- exclusionary polemics against Jews and Christians;
- exaggerated modern claims about his priority in cryptographic history.

Searches also returned unrelated modern people bearing *al-Durayhim*; these were excluded.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### “Father of cryptography”

**Story:** Ibn al-Durayhim was the father or inventor of cryptography.

**Origin and circulation:** the claim grew from David Kahn’s use of al-Qalqashandī and from later Arab cryptological scholarship before al-Kindī’s cryptographic treatise became widely known.

**Evidence against literal priority:** al-Kindī’s ninth-century treatise and works by Ibn Dunaynīr and Ibn ʿAdlān precede him. Ibn al-Durayhim himself belongs to an existing Arabic cryptological tradition.

**Historical significance:** the label stimulated the search for *Miftāḥ al-kunūz* and helped recover the depth of medieval Arabic cryptology.

**Classification:** reception narrative; false if it means first cryptographer, defensible only as shorthand for an exceptionally important systematic practitioner.

### “Mythical or nonexistent person”

Muḥammad Ḥassān al-Ṭayyān reports that skeptics responding to claims about the lost cipher book suggested Ibn al-Durayhim was a legendary or fictitious figure.

Evidence decisively contradicts that claim:

- al-Ṣafadī personally knew him;
- multiple medieval biographers recorded him;
- an illustrated animal manuscript has a dated colophon naming him;
- several manuscript traditions preserve his works.

**Classification:** documented modern skeptical claim, refuted by manuscript and contemporary evidence.

### “Inventor of the Vigenère table”

**Story:** Ibn al-Durayhim invented the tableau later associated with Trithemius or Vigenère.

**Evidence:** his work describes multiple substitution arrangements and has been compared to a tableau or polyalphabetic system.

**Against:** the surviving descriptions do not establish identity with the later European systems or a transmission chain.

**Classification:** overstated priority claim based on a real technical resemblance.

### “His cipher book was lost twice”

This modern narrative combines distinct episodes:

1. in 749/1348–49, authorities raided his Damascus house and confiscated his books;
2. his large *Īḍāḥ al-mubham* subsequently disappeared;
3. *Miftāḥ al-kunūz*, itself an abridgment/commentarial reconstruction of earlier material, was long considered lost by modern historians;
4. researchers located its manuscript in Istanbul’s Süleymaniye Library in 1981 and published it in 1987.

There is no proof that the Süleymaniye manuscript was among the volumes confiscated in 749. “Lost twice” is an effective narrative description, not a continuous documented provenance.

### Deaths of the officials who expelled him

Al-Ṣafadī notes that the two *dawādārs* involved in expelling him died approximately four months later.

Later audiences may interpret the sequence as divine retribution. No contemporary causal evidence supports that conclusion.

**Classification:** chronological report capable of hagiographic reinterpretation; supernatural causation unverified.

### Ability to produce poems from constrained letters

Al-Ṣafadī describes a performance in which Ibn al-Durayhim received a concealed subject or set of letters, broke the letters down according to letter-science procedures, and produced a poetic answer containing no letters outside the supplied set.

The eyewitness frames this as an extraordinary demonstration of intellect and verbal composition. It need not imply a miracle: constrained writing, anagrammatic manipulation, memorization, and rapid poetic improvisation offer ordinary mechanisms.

**Classification:** eyewitness-reported feat; exact performance conditions cannot now be tested.

### Deciphering ancient Egyptian inscriptions before Champollion

Some modern popular writing associates Ibn al-Durayhim’s poem on scripts “written on the *barābī*” with Arab decipherment of Egyptian hieroglyphs.

His interest in symbols on ancient monuments is real. No surviving demonstration shows that he correctly deciphered the Egyptian language in the modern philological sense.

**Classification:** modern extrapolation from genuine interest in ancient scripts; not established decipherment.

## Works by Ibn al-Durayhim

Approximately eighty titles were reportedly listed by al-Ṣafadī, many from Ibn al-Durayhim’s own written record. Most are now lost, unidentified, or known only through titles. The following is the fullest reasonably secure consolidated list available from the consulted catalogues and studies.

### Cryptography, cryptanalysis, ciphers, riddles, and letter sciences

1. **مفتاح الكنوز في إيضاح المرموز — *Miftāḥ al-kunūz fī īḍāḥ al-marmūz***  
   “Key to the Treasures in the Clarification of Ciphers.” Survives; manuscript found in the Süleymaniye Library and critically published in 1987 in *ʿIlm al-taʿmiya wa-istikhrāj al-muʿammā ʿinda al-ʿArab*, vol. I, pp. 321–365.

2. **إيضاح المبهم في حل المترجم — *Īḍāḥ al-mubham fī ḥall al-mutarjam***  
   Large earlier work on decipherment. Ibn al-Durayhim says time destroyed or carried it away. Lost.

3. **مختصر المبهم في حل المترجم — *Mukhtaṣar al-mubham fī ḥall al-mutarjam***  
   Abridgment of the preceding work. Survival uncertain.

4. **نظم قواعد فن المترجم وضوابطه — *Naẓm qawāʿid fann al-mutarjam wa-ḍawābiṭihi***  
   Didactic versification of cryptological rules; related to or possibly represented by his cipher poem.

5. **قصيدة في حل رموز المكاتبات وفهم أقلام المتقدمين**  
   Poem on solving the symbols of correspondence and understanding ancient scripts. Survives in manuscript/edited form.

6. **قصيدة في حل رموز الأقلام المكتوبة على البرابي**  
   Poem on solving scripts written on ancient monuments or temples. Possibly an alternative description or related recension of the preceding poem; exact identity requires manuscript comparison.

7. **غاية الإعجاز في الأحاجي والألغاز — *Ghāyat al-iʿjāz fī al-aḥājī wa-l-alghāz***  
   On riddles and enigmas. Survival uncertain.

8. **اقتـناع/إقناع الحذاق في أنواع الأوفاق — *Iqtināʿ/ Iqnāʿ al-ḥudhāq fī anwāʿ al-awfāq***  
   On types of magic or numerical squares. Title varies in catalogues.

9. **سبر الصرف في سر الحرف — *Sabr/Sayr al-ṣarf fī sirr al-ḥarf***  
   On morphology and the secret of the letter. Known by title.

10. **بوادر الحلوم في نوادر العلوم — *Bawādir al-ḥulūm fī nawādir al-ʿulūm***  
    Miscellaneous rare sciences.

11. **تناءي المناظر في المرائي والمناظر — *Tanāʾī al-manāẓir fī al-marāʾī wa-l-manāẓir***  
    Exact subject uncertain; probably optical, reflective, or combinatorial curiosities.

12. **تصاريف الدهر في تعاريف الزجر — *Taṣārīf al-dahr fī taʿārīf al-zajr***  
    On prognostication or omens.

13. **سلّم الحراسة في علم الفراسة — *Sullam al-ḥirāsa fī ʿilm al-firāsa***  
    On physiognomy.

14. **غاية المغنم في الاسم الأعظم — *Ghāyat al-maghnam fī al-ism al-aʿẓam***  
    On the Greatest Name of God. Survives in Süleymaniye Library, Hacı Mahmud Efendi MS 627.

### Mathematics, calculation, games, and numerical correspondences

15. **بسط الفوائد في حساب/شرح حساب القواعد — *Basṭ al-fawāʾid fī ḥisāb/sharḥ ḥisāb al-qawāʿid***  
    Arithmetic and computational rules.

16. **شرح الأسعردية في الحساب — *Sharḥ al-Asʿardiyya fī al-ḥisāb***  
    Commentary on an arithmetic work associated with al-Asʿardī.

17. **إيقاظ المصيب فيما في الشطرنج من المناصيب — *Īqāẓ al-muṣīb fīmā fī al-shaṭranj min al-manāṣīb***  
    On chess arrangements or problems. The title is corrupted in some catalogues.

18. **المناسبات العددية في الأسماء المحمدية — *al-Munāsabāt al-ʿadadiyya fī al-asmāʾ al-Muḥammadiyya***  
    Numerical correspondences in names of Muḥammad.

19. **مناسبة الحساب في أسماء الأنبياء المذكورين في الكتاب**  
    Numerical calculation involving names of prophets in scripture.

20. Works on inheritance recalculation or *munāsakhāt* are reported in general descriptions, but a secure title was not recovered from the sources consulted.

### Qurʾān, theology, devotion, and hadith

21. **النسمات الفائحة لما في آيات الفاتحة — *al-Nasamāt al-fāʾiḥa li-mā fī āyāt al-Fātiḥa***  
    On verses of the Fātiḥa.

22. **الآثار الرائعة في أسرار الواقعة — *al-Āthār al-rāʾiʿa fī asrār al-Wāqiʿa***

23. **كنز الدرر في حروف أوائل السور — *Kanz al-durar fī ḥurūf awāʾil al-suwar***  
    On the disconnected letters at the beginnings of Qurʾānic suras.

24. **إشراق النفس في الحمدلات الخمس — *Ishrāq al-nafs fī al-ḥamdalāt al-khams***  
    On five formulas or passages of praise.

25. **إضاءة بوح على “ويسألونك عن الروح” — *Iḍāʾat būḥ ʿalā “wa-yasʾalūnaka ʿan al-rūḥ”***  
    On Qurʾān 17:85 and the spirit; title orthography is uncertain.

26. **منام النصوح في الكلام على سورة نوح — *Manām al-naṣūḥ fī al-kalām ʿalā Sūrat Nūḥ***  
    On Sūrat Nūḥ.

27. **الافتحاص على سورة الإخلاص — *al-Iftiḥāṣ ʿalā Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ***  
    On Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ; vocalization/title uncertain.

28. **نفع الجدوى في الجمع بين أحاديث العدوى — *Nafʿ al-jadwā fī al-jamʿ bayna aḥādīth al-ʿadwā***  
    Reconciliation of hadith reports about contagion.

29. **قصيدة في مدح الرسول — *Qaṣīda fī madḥ al-Rasūl***  
    Thirty-verse praise poem accompanied by lexical and grammatical analysis. A manuscript is catalogued in the Ẓāhiriyya collection, no. 3342.

30. **ذات القوافي — *Dhāt al-qawāfī***  
    Elaborate prophetic praise poem in which verses support multiple rhyming possibilities. It has received modern literary study.

31. **الرد على تقي الدين الأخنائي — *al-Radd ʿalā Taqī al-Dīn al-Akhnāʾī***  
    Polemical response to Taqī al-Dīn al-Akhnāʾī. Survival/status uncertain.

### Government, interreligious polemic, and society

32. **منهج/نهج الصواب في قبح استكتاب أهل الكتاب — *Manhaj/Nahj al-ṣawāb fī qubḥ istiktāb ahl al-kitāb***  
    Against employment of Jews and Christians as state scribes. Survives in more than one manuscript; modern editions appeared in 2002 and 2005, and it was the subject of later master’s and doctoral research. Attribution variants exist.

33. **الإنصاف بالدليل في أوصاف النيل — *al-Inṣāf bi-l-dalīl fī awṣāf al-Nīl***  
    On characteristics of the Nile. Known through bibliographical notice.

34. **رسالة التراضي بين الأمير والقاضي — *Risālat al-tarāḍī bayna al-amīr wa-l-qāḍī***  
    “Reconciliation between the emir and the judge.” Survival uncertain.

### Zoology, medicine, and natural lore

35. **كتاب منافع الحيوان — *Kitāb Manāfiʿ al-ḥayawān***  
    Compiled in Damascus in 755/1354. Survives as El Escorial Arabic MS 898. Modern Spanish translation by Carmen Ruiz Bravo-Villasante, 1980; facsimile editions followed.

### General or uncertain titles

36. **منهج الصواب — *Manhaj al-ṣawāb***  
    Shortened citation of no. 32 rather than necessarily a separate work.

37. Other works on the secrets of worship, Biblical alteration, Qurʾānic letters, calendars, astronomy, inheritance, and unusual sciences are reported, but their complete titles and manuscript identities were not recoverable from the web-accessible sources consulted.

### Authorship cautions

- The approximately eighty-work total derives chiefly from al-Ṣafadī’s list, reportedly copied from Ibn al-Durayhim’s writing.
- A title in a medieval biography proves that a work was claimed, not that it was completed.
- Closely similar titles may be alternate names, abridgments, or separate recensions.
- Modern online book pages often reproduce corrupted OCR titles.
- *Maqāṣid al-fuṣūl al-mutarjama ʿan ḥall al-tarjama* belongs to **Ibn Dunaynīr**, not Ibn al-Durayhim; popular summaries sometimes incorrectly assign it to Ibn al-Durayhim.
- *Miftāḥ al-kunūz* is Ibn al-Durayhim’s securely attributed cryptological work.

## Death and Immediate Aftermath

Ibn al-Durayhim died at Qūṣ before completing the Ethiopian embassy. His death ended a career marked by commercial wealth, elite access, political expulsion, book confiscation, recovery into institutional employment, and a final involuntary diplomatic assignment.

No contemporary source consulted records:

- funeral arrangements;
- burial site;
- estate distribution;
- surviving dependents;
- official replacement;
- recovery of the 200,000-dirham debt;
- return of his confiscated library.

The absence of an identified family archive and the dispersal or destruction of his books contributed to the fragmentary survival of his work.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Medieval transmission

Al-Ṣafadī remembered him as an intellectual prodigy, especially in letters, magic squares, cryptanalysis, riddles, arithmetic, and rapid composition.

Al-Qalqashandī transmitted cryptological material derived from him in *Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā*. Because al-Qalqashandī’s encyclopedia remained accessible while Ibn al-Durayhim’s own treatise disappeared from scholarly view, later readers often knew his techniques indirectly.

### Bibliographical survival

Ottoman and later Arabic bibliographers preserved titles even when the physical books vanished. This produced a reputation based partly on a “ghost library” of approximately eighty named works.

### Rediscovery of Arabic cryptology

Modern historians initially relied on al-Qalqashandī. David Kahn’s history helped publicize the conclusion that systematic cryptography and cryptanalysis had deep Arabic roots.

The discovery and publication of manuscripts by al-Kindī, Ibn ʿAdlān, Ibn Dunaynīr, and Ibn al-Durayhim changed the chronology:

- Ibn al-Durayhim was no longer treated as the earliest Arab cryptologist;
- his place became that of a major late synthesizer and practitioner;
- his work demonstrated institutional continuity from Abbasid and Ayyubid scholarship into Mamluk bureaucratic culture.

### Technical significance

His principal contributions include:

- an extensive classification of cipher systems;
- integration of frequency analysis with morphology and syntax;
- attention to scripts and alphabet sizes;
- detailed demonstration of solving substitution ciphers;
- use of likely-word reasoning and letter compatibility;
- pedagogical conversion of cryptology into verse;
- evidence for practical court and chancery interest in cryptography.

### Art-historical legacy

The El Escorial animal manuscript links his name to one of the important surviving illustrated Mamluk bestiaries. It preserves evidence of:

- fourteenth-century animal imagery;
- medical and pharmacological lore;
- textual transmission from the Ibn Bakhtīshūʿ tradition;
- collaboration between scholarship, copying, and artistic production.

### Religious and political legacy

His Qurʾānic, letter-science, and devotional works represent a learned world in which linguistic analysis, numerology, esoteric interpretation, and orthodox study could coexist.

*Manhaj al-ṣawāb* also preserves an exclusionary strand of Mamluk political thought directed against Jewish and Christian participation in government. Its modern republication gives that polemic a new afterlife distinct from its original administrative context.

### Limits of the legacy record

No continuous “school of Ibn al-Durayhim” has been demonstrated. Later authors used or preserved his material, but named direct pupils are largely absent.

His modern fame is divided among three largely separate scholarly fields:

- history of cryptography;
- Islamic manuscript painting and zoological literature;
- Mamluk religious-political polemic.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are included. Mere coexistence in the same century or residence in the same city has been excluded.

### Afif al-Din Ali ibn Adlan al-Mawsili

- **Nature:** intellectual predecessor in Arabic cryptanalysis.
- **Dates and places:** Ibn ʿAdlān, 1187–1268, also associated with Mosul and Ayyubid court culture; Ibn al-Durayhim worked in Mosul, Syria, and Egypt in the fourteenth century.
- **Evidence:** modern manuscript comparison finds that Ibn al-Durayhim’s *Miftāḥ al-kunūz* relies on or develops techniques present in Ibn ʿAdlān’s cryptological treatise, particularly practical decipherment traditions.
- **Label:** **documented textual/intellectual influence in modern scholarship**; no personal meeting was possible.

### Abu Yusuf Ya'qub ibn Ishaq al-Kindi

- **Nature:** foundational predecessor in frequency-based cryptanalysis.
- **Dates:** al-Kindī died centuries before Ibn al-Durayhim.
- **Evidence:** Ibn al-Durayhim’s frequency counts, linguistic analysis, and treatment of letter combinations belong to the tradition first securely documented in al-Kindī’s cryptological treatise. Modern scholarship describes this as intellectual transmission through intermediate Arabic works.
- **Label:** **documented tradition-level influence; direct manuscript dependence not fully reconstructed**.

### Al-Khalil ibn Ahmad al-Farahidi

- **Nature:** remote intellectual predecessor in Arabic lexicography, phonology, prosody, combinatorial letter analysis, and a reportedly lost work on cryptic messages.
- **Evidence:** later scholarship argues that the linguistic sciences founded or systematized by al-Khalīl supplied essential tools for Arabic cryptanalysis.
- **Label:** **scholarly reconstruction of indirect influence**, not a documented citation by Ibn al-Durayhim.

### Ahmad al-Qalqashandi

- **Nature:** posthumous transmitter and adapter.
- **Dates and place:** al-Qalqashandī, 1355–1418, worked in the Mamluk chancery after Ibn al-Durayhim’s death.
- **Evidence:** the cryptological chapter of *Ṣubḥ al-aʿshā* explicitly draws on Ibn al-Durayhim and reproduces his examples and techniques.
- **Label:** **documented textual transmission**.
- **Personal contact:** none established; al-Qalqashandī was a child when Ibn al-Durayhim died.

### Abu Abdallah Muhammad ibn Battuta

- Both men travelled within Mamluk Egypt and Syria during overlapping decades and had access to court society.
- No source consulted documents a meeting, correspondence, shared mission, or acknowledged influence.
- **Finding:** **no documented connection**.

### Ibn Arabi

- Ibn al-Durayhim’s letter sciences can be compared with wider traditions in which Ibn ʿArabī was influential, but no source consulted documents direct textual dependence, discipleship, or explicit citation.
- **Finding:** **no securely documented atlas connection**.
