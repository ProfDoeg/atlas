# Elsa Lindenberg: Research Dossier

## Scope, Evidence, and Research Status

Elsa Lindenberg’s public biography remains fragmentary. Her surviving private papers were guarded for decades by her friend and executor Ida Korsvold. As of 2026, researcher Ilse Trees Ghekiere has obtained access to the collection and is arranging its transfer to the Norwegian National Archives, but her full study is not expected before 2028. Consequently, basic facts that would normally be recoverable—exact birth date and place, parents, siblings, legal marriages, addresses, citizenship history, and estate—remain unpublished or uncertain.

This dossier distinguishes:

- **Documented fact:** supported by institutional records, archival catalogues, contemporaneous production records, or direct testimony.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a generally credible secondary source but not independently verified here from the underlying document.
- **Interpretation:** a scholar’s reconstruction or inference.
- **Allegation or disputed claim:** contested or dependent on testimony whose documentary basis is unavailable.
- **Rumor or myth:** a story that circulated but lacks adequate supporting evidence.
- **Unknown:** no reliable public evidence located.

The most substantial published treatment is Courtenay Young’s *On Elsa Lindenberg and Reich*, which synthesizes Reich’s diaries and correspondence, interviews conducted by Myron Sharaf with Lindenberg, Ilse Ollendorff’s memoir, dance histories, and recollections by Lindenberg’s students. It is valuable but partly interpretive and should not be mistaken for a definitive archival biography.

## Family and Ancestry

No reliable public record located in this research identifies Lindenberg’s:

- parents;
- siblings;
- grandparents or ancestry;
- family religion;
- childhood household;
- socioeconomic background;
- exact birthplace;
- birth name, if different from Lindenberg;
- children or descendants.

A 1934 episode reported in Young’s reconstruction says Lindenberg visited “family and friends” in Berlin while Reich was traveling, demonstrating that members of her family or close pre-exile network remained there. Their identities are not supplied.

The absence of family information is a documentary gap, not evidence that she was estranged from her relatives or lacked surviving family.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1906–1918

Lindenberg was born in Germany in 1906. No exact date or locality has been established in the accessible sources.

She was approximately eight when the First World War began and twelve at the German Revolution of 1918–1919. No reliable account was found of her childhood during these upheavals, her family’s political affiliations, material circumstances, health, religion, or early exposure to dance.

Claims that she was specifically born in Berlin should be treated as **unverified** unless a birth or residency record emerges. The sources establish Berlin as the place of her education and early career, not necessarily her birthplace.

## Education and Formation

### Helen-Lange-Schule, Berlin

**Reported fact:** In 1919, at approximately thirteen, Lindenberg began dance training as a scholarship student at the Helen-Lange-Schule in Berlin. This information derives from Lilian Karina and Marion Kant’s dance-historical research and is reproduced by Courtenay Young.

The precise institutional status of the course—school curriculum, extracurricular program, or separate professional training—has not been clarified.

### Hertha Feist and Rudolf Laban

**Reported fact:** In 1925 Lindenberg enrolled at the Berlin school of Hertha Feist, an important practitioner of Rudolf von Laban’s movement system. She completed her diploma around 1927.

Some reference works describe her directly as “educated by Rudolf von Laban.” The more precise surviving account places her at Feist’s Laban-oriented school. It remains unclear whether she also took classes personally from Laban, attended his workshops, or was trained through Feist’s authorized curriculum.

Her formation combined:

- dance technique;
- Laban’s movement analysis and spatial principles;
- free or expression dance;
- “körperbetonte Bewegungslehre,” a body-centered approach to movement;
- group or dance-choir methods later visible in her Norwegian choreography.

### Elsa Gindler

**Documented through multiple independent secondary accounts:** Lindenberg studied with the German somatic educator Elsa Gindler.

The exact beginning and duration of the relationship remain uncertain. Young explicitly notes that no source establishes when Lindenberg first entered Gindler’s classes. The contact apparently began before the Second World War and resumed afterward during Lindenberg’s visits from Norway to Germany.

Gindler called her practice *Arbeit am Menschen*—“work on the human being”—rather than giving it a proprietary method name. It emphasized:

- attention to bodily sensation;
- observation rather than imposed form;
- breathing;
- economical and responsive movement;
- recognition and regulation of unnecessary muscular tension;
- experimentation with ordinary actions;
- integration of physical, emotional, and environmental awareness.

In Norway, Lindenberg’s transmission of this work became known as *spenningsregulering*, literally “tension regulation.”

### Additional formation

No conventional university education, academic degree, medical qualification, physiotherapy license, or formal psychotherapy credential has been located. “Dance therapist” and “movement therapist” describe her work and historical function; they should not be read as proof of a modern regulated clinical license.

## Early Career in Berlin, 1927–1933

### Städtische Oper / Deutsches Opernhaus

From 1927 until 1933 Lindenberg danced as a group or ensemble dancer at Berlin’s municipal opera, identified in later sources as the Städtische Oper or Deutsches Opernhaus.

Her individual roles, repertoire, salary, contractual rank, and touring history have not yet been reconstructed. The description “group dancer” suggests ensemble rather than principal status.

The period placed her within Weimar Berlin’s intersection of:

- institutional ballet;
- Ausdruckstanz and free dance;
- body-culture and reform-gymnastics movements;
- left-wing theatrical and political organizations;
- sexual-reform activism.

### Political activity

**Reported fact:** Lindenberg was politically left-wing and participated in anti-Nazi activity with fellow dancers Ruth Abramowitsch and Ilse Larede.

Her precise party membership is unclear. Some sources describe her as a communist activist or member of the same communist cell as Wilhelm Reich. A reliable membership card or party register was not located. “Communist-associated” is therefore more secure than a categorical claim of formal Communist Party membership.

She lived in or was associated with Berlin’s Künstlerkolonie in Wilmersdorf, a residential complex known for left-wing writers, performers, and political activists. Nazi police raided the complex soon after the Reichstag fire.

### Meeting Wilhelm Reich

The date is disputed within a narrow range:

- Some sources place the meeting in **1931**, possibly during political organizing.
- Most biographical accounts date the beginning of their serious relationship to **May 1932**.
- The German Wilhelm Reich Society describes her as both a dancer and political activist when the relationship began.

Wilhelm Reich was then married to the psychoanalyst Annie Pink Reich and had two daughters, Eva and Lore. His marriage was already under severe strain, but the relationship with Lindenberg became a decisive factor in its final dissolution.

**Documented through correspondence and later testimony:** Annie wrote bitterly to Lindenberg that the new couple’s happiness would be built on her tears. The wording is transmitted through biographical sources; the original letter was not directly inspected here.

Lindenberg subsequently had a relationship with Reich’s daughters during travel and family visits. Eva Reich later recalled an idyllic 1934 camping trip, including dancing with Elsa, open-air exercise, bathing nude, and unusual tenderness between Reich and Lindenberg.

## Dismissal, Nazi Persecution, and Flight, 1933

### Removal from the opera

**Reported fact, grounded in dance-historical documentation:** On 21 April 1933 Lindenberg and Ruth Abramowitsch were formally removed from the opera ballet under the Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service. Lindenberg was accused of *staatsschädigendes Verhalten*—conduct damaging to the state.

The dismissal appears political rather than racial. Later sources describe Lindenberg as “Aryan” under Nazi classifications. No evidence located suggests that the Nazi action was based on Jewish ancestry.

### Arrest danger

Sources report:

- police interest in her political activity;
- a search of her residence or the Künstlerkolonie;
- an arrest warrant or imminent arrest;
- flight from Germany.

The detailed claim that an arrest warrant had already been issued is repeated in later accounts, but the warrant itself was not located. It should be treated as a **reported fact awaiting archival confirmation**.

Reich, who was Jewish, communist-associated, psychoanalytic, and publicly active in sexual reform, was in more immediate danger. A Nazi newspaper attacked his pamphlet *Der sexuelle Kampf der Jugend* on 2 March 1933. He left Berlin for Vienna shortly afterward. The chronology of whether Lindenberg accompanied him immediately or joined him after completing affairs in Berlin is not uniform across the sources.

## Scandinavian Exile, 1933–1935

### Denmark

After Germany and Vienna, Lindenberg and Reich lived in Copenhagen. Reich attempted to continue psychoanalytic teaching and sexual-political work. Lindenberg continued dance and movement work.

Reich’s advocacy of contraception, abortion access, sexual education, and adolescent sexual autonomy produced political and professional resistance. His *Massenpsychologie des Faschismus* was also rejected by communist officials as counterrevolutionary. Danish authorities declined to renew his residence permit.

There is no evidence that Lindenberg herself was charged with an offense in Denmark.

### Sweden: Malmö

The pair next lived in Malmö. Swedish police placed Reich under surveillance, reportedly suspecting that the regular arrival of patients at his room indicated a brothel and that Lindenberg might be a prostitute.

This was a **police suspicion, not a substantiated finding**. No prostitution charge was brought against Lindenberg or Reich. Police searched Reich’s room without a warrant, according to David Boadella’s reconstruction, but no criminal charges resulted. His permit was nevertheless not renewed.

While living in Malmö, Lindenberg reportedly commuted to Copenhagen several days a week to continue dance work.

### Illegal or precarious residence

After the Swedish permit expired in June 1934, the couple spent part of the summer in Denmark under precarious or illegal conditions; Reich reportedly used an alias.

They then drove across Europe with Reich’s daughters, ending at the International Psychoanalytic Congress in Lucerne. Lindenberg’s exact itinerary included Denmark, Germany or Central European transit routes, and Switzerland. The journey combined family travel with Reich’s unsuccessful attempt to preserve his position in the International Psychoanalytic Association.

### Oslo arrival: 1934 or 1935

Sources disagree:

- Reich and Lindenberg appear to have settled in Oslo in **October 1934**.
- Norwegian reference works often say Lindenberg “came to Norway in 1935.”
- Young reports that she returned from a visit to Berlin to Oslo in March 1935.

The strongest reconstruction is that the couple established an Oslo base in late 1934, while Lindenberg’s continuous Norwegian residence or documented professional career began in 1935.

## Oslo Career and Relationship with Reich, 1934–1939

### Household and status

Lindenberg and Reich lived together openly. Friends and colleagues referred to her as his second wife, and Reich later called her his “wife” or “companion-wife.” They never legally married.

Calling her “Elsa Reich” or describing a legal marriage is inaccurate unless used as a quotation from a source.

Reich was invited to Norway by psychologist Harald K. Schjelderup. The couple became part of a network including psychoanalysts, physicians, writers, artists, dancers, and left-wing intellectuals.

### Dance teaching and therapeutic synthesis

Lindenberg developed movement studies combining:

- Laban-based dance;
- Gindler’s bodily attention;
- her own theatrical experience;
- Reich’s concepts of character structure and chronic muscular “armor.”

Gerda Geddes, then a young Norwegian dancer and later a prominent teacher of tai chi in Britain, recalled attending Lindenberg’s classes and seminars. Geddes described the work as movement rather than conventional dance: ballet training transformed into an avant-garde, Reichian approach.

Lindenberg attracted dancers, psychologists, and people seeking therapeutic bodywork. The precise boundary between artistic teaching, movement education, and psychotherapy was fluid.

### Possible influence on Reich

Three evidentiary levels must be separated.

**Documented fact:** Reich’s transition from verbally centered character analysis toward direct attention to breathing, posture, muscular tension, and vegetative expression occurred during his relationship with Lindenberg.

**Documented contextual fact:** Lindenberg already possessed Labanian and Gindler-influenced movement knowledge. Ilse Ollendorff later wrote that Lindenberg developed movement studies using Reich’s theory of muscular armor.

**Interpretation:** Courtenay Young, David Boadella, Ulfried Geuter, Michael Heller, Judyth Weaver, and later body-psychotherapy historians argue that Lindenberg’s embodied expertise influenced Reich’s development of character-analytic vegetotherapy.

**Unresolved question:** No located document contains Reich’s explicit acknowledgment that Lindenberg originated a particular clinical technique. It is therefore too strong to claim as settled fact that she “invented vegetotherapy.” The historically supportable conclusion is mutual influence occurring at the relevant moment, with the direction and magnitude still debated.

No evidence establishes that Reich personally studied with or met Elsa Gindler. Claims of a direct Reich–Gindler teacher relationship are unsupported. Lindenberg was the probable conduit.

### Arbeiderteatret and labor movement choreography

Later accounts place Lindenberg at Oslo’s Arbeiderteatret from approximately 1935 to 1939. She choreographed a “red revue” or dance-choir work with an amateur labor-movement group.

Dance historian Valdemar Hansteen judged from surviving photographs that this work resembled Laban’s group dance choirs. According to his account, labor-organization leaders did not approve its continuation. The reasons—artistic, ideological, organizational, or financial—remain unstated.

### Nationaltheatret productions

Institutional production records establish:

- **26 December 1937:** choreography for *H. C. Andersens eventyr*, adapted by Vera Smirnowa and directed by Gerda Ring at Nationaltheatret.
- **8 August 1938:** dance preparation or choreography for *Faust* at Nationaltheatret.
- **18 April 1939:** dancer and choreographer in *Forfengelighetens komedie*.
- **6 January 1946:** choreography for *Stor ståhei – Bryllup – Jubileum – Frieri* at Studioteatret.

### Tri-balletten

In 1938 Lindenberg joined Signe Hofgaard and Gerd Kjølaas in establishing Tri-balletten, generally described as Norway’s first independent professional group based on free dance.

The group performed in connection with the *Vi Kan* exhibition and received a favorable reception. Contemporary dance-history material reports that insufficient finances prevented further performances.

This was one of Lindenberg’s clearest institutional contributions to Norwegian modern dance. It was also a significant failure of sustainability: artistic recognition did not produce a viable operating structure.

### Dartington Hall, 1936

Lindenberg taught ballet at the summer school at Dartington Hall, Totnes, Devon, in 1936. Dartington was then a prominent experimental arts and education center.

Reich joined her there after traveling through Europe. During or around this visit he may have begun a sexual relationship with Norwegian textile designer Gerd Bergersen.

The Bergersen affair is a **reported and partly disputed claim**. It rests largely on Bergersen’s taped recollections used by Colin Wilson. Young considers some sexual involvement plausible but questions details because contemporary writings by Reich do not mention her.

### Conflict within the relationship

Reich’s surviving diary and letters portray an intense, unstable partnership. He recorded:

- love for Lindenberg’s “realness”;
- desire to have a child with her;
- resentment that she did not sufficiently support his work;
- accusations that she was jealous of coworkers;
- recurring declarations that the relationship was over;
- renewed attachment.

Lindenberg later recalled:

- Reich’s jealous rages;
- his affairs;
- demands that she subordinate her independent professional life;
- an abrupt termination after affectionate letters;
- continuing love mixed with criticism.

These accounts are personal testimony, not court-tested findings. They support a relationship marked by affection, infidelity, possessiveness, professional tension, and conflicting expectations of independence.

No evidence was found of a police report, civil suit, criminal charge, or judicial finding concerning abuse within the relationship.

---

## Separation from Reich, 1939–1940

Reich obtained an American visa and sailed from Norway on 19 August 1939. Their romantic relationship had broken down shortly before or during his departure.

Competing versions survive:

- Reich later maintained that Lindenberg could have gone with him but made a mistaken interpretation of independence.
- Lindenberg believed he abruptly ended the relationship after sending passionate letters.
- Reich periodically invited her to come to America so that they could see whether reconciliation was possible.
- Lindenberg did not want to arrive merely as an assistant or uncertain former partner.
- The German invasion of Norway in April 1940 then made emigration much harder.

The evidence does not support a single-cause explanation. Personal conflict, Reich’s affairs and jealousy, Lindenberg’s professional independence, unequal expectations, finances, visa problems, and the war all contributed.

### Finances after separation

**Reported fact from Ilse Ollendorff and Sharaf:** There was no separation settlement. Reich believed a woman able to earn her own living was not entitled to alimony and compared alimony to prostitution.

After the German invasion, Lindenberg lost employment, went underground, and became destitute. She asked Reich for help; he reportedly sent **US$25**.

No bank record or correspondence proving the transfer was inspected here, but the amount is specific and appears in published testimony. There is no evidence of jointly owned businesses, investments, real property, or a formal financial partnership.

---

## Occupied Norway and Refuge in Sweden, 1940–1945

Germany invaded Norway on 9 April 1940. Lindenberg’s political record and earlier removal from the Berlin opera placed her at risk, despite her non-Jewish classification.

### Underground existence

Sharaf’s account, based partly on Lindenberg’s testimony, says:

- she lost her job;
- she went underground;
- she was never arrested;
- she crossed or fled to Sweden on several occasions;
- she endured severe financial and emotional hardship.

### Marriage of convenience

Ida Korsvold reported that Gestapo investigations forced Lindenberg to contract a marriage of convenience with a Norwegian.

This is a **reported fact based on close-associate testimony**, but the Norwegian spouse has not been identified and no marriage certificate was found. It is unknown whether the purpose was citizenship, legal residence, protection from deportation, concealment, or some combination.

This wartime marriage should not be confused with her non-legal union with Reich.

### Wanted status and Sweden

Korsvold further reported that by 1944 Lindenberg was listed as a wanted person or enemy of the German state and fled to Sweden, remaining in hiding until the end of the war.

The underlying Gestapo list has not been located in the consulted material. The statement is plausible in context but awaits archival verification.

No evidence was found that Lindenberg was imprisoned, tortured, formally tried, or convicted. Claims that she “escaped the Gestapo” describe a genuine danger but should not be expanded into an undocumented arrest narrative.

---

## Return to Norway and Postwar Career, 1945–1963

Lindenberg returned to Oslo in 1945.

### Postwar treatment as a German

Ida Korsvold’s account says Lindenberg was persecuted or subjected to adverse treatment because she was German. The precise form is not specified. It could refer to administrative, social, employment, or security-related discrimination.

Later accounts state that the Norwegian government rehabilitated her and granted her a state pension. No decision date, pension category, or administrative file was located.

### Theatre work

Lindenberg resumed professional activity and choreographed *Stor ståhei – Bryllup – Jubileum – Frieri* at Studioteatret, premiered 6 January 1946.

Her later theatrical credits remain poorly catalogued. Norwegian reference sources say she worked at several Oslo theatres, but only four productions were found in Sceneweb and Nationaltheatret’s searchable records.

### Return to Gindler

Lindenberg traveled regularly to Germany after the war to continue studying with Elsa Gindler. Gindler died in 1961.

This postwar renewal is important: Lindenberg’s mature Norwegian practice was not merely a static survival of her 1930s training. It was informed by repeated contact with Gindler’s evolving work.

### Dance therapy

Sharaf reports that for several years after the war Lindenberg taught a form of dance therapy influenced by Reich’s psychiatric concepts. Over time, she increasingly emphasized the Gindler lineage.

No surviving treatment manual, patient register, clinical protocol, or outcome study has been identified. Statements about therapeutic effectiveness are therefore historical descriptions, not validated medical conclusions.

## Spenningsregulering and Later Teaching, 1964–1981

Current research at the Oslo National Academy of the Arts dates Lindenberg’s formal establishment of *spenningsregulering* to **1964**.

The practice emphasized:

- sensation and attentiveness;
- recognizing habitual tension;
- allowing rather than forcing movement;
- experimenting with posture, weight, breath, balance, and environment;
- discovering spontaneous movement;
- integration of bodily and psychological experience.

Lindenberg conducted groups and educational courses rather than publishing a standardized curriculum. Her oral and embodied mode of transmission is one reason her contribution became difficult to document.

### Students and professional following

Named students or participants include:

- Gerda Geddes;
- Berit Heir Bunkan;
- Tove Lund;
- Gro Z. M. Torgersbråten;
- Ingunn Rimestad;
- Lise Ferner;
- Bibbi Winberg;
- Steffi Lund;
- other Norwegian dancers, physiotherapists, and psychologists.

Gro Torgersbråten began studying the work as a teenager. Lindenberg authorized her in 1981 to lead groups.

### Conflict with Aadel Bülow-Hansen

Berit Heir Bunkan recalled that physiotherapist Aadel Bülow-Hansen discouraged students from attending Lindenberg’s classes because Lindenberg had lived unmarried with Reich. Bunkan and Tove Lund attended secretly.

This is **first-person reported fact**. It illustrates how sexual morality and professional rivalry affected Lindenberg’s reception. It does not prove a broader institutional ban.

### Territorial confrontation with Miriam Goldberg

Bunkan also recalled arranging a meeting between Lindenberg and Israeli body therapist Miriam Goldberg. Lindenberg reportedly objected strongly to Goldberg teaching Charlotte Selver-derived work in Norway, declaring Scandinavia her domain and warning that Selver’s approach should not be reduced to short courses.

This episode is **reported fact from Bunkan’s recollection**. It portrays Lindenberg as protective and territorial about the lineage she transmitted. No independent account from Goldberg was located.

## Accident, Disability, Illness, and Final Years

At an unspecified date, Lindenberg was involved in a car accident and injured or developed persistent trouble with her hip.

The injury forced her to stop or greatly reduce dancing. She led groups while seated in a chair. Bunkan remembered that Lindenberg’s strong presence caused students to anticipate her needs, adjusting pillows or windows without being asked.

Bunkan also recalled a class in which the older, heavier, gray-haired Lindenberg unexpectedly rose from her chair and danced with extraordinary softness and elegance. This story became a form of oral hagiography among students: the disabled older teacher suddenly revealed the dancer still embodied within her. It is an eyewitness recollection, though shaped by memory and reverence.

By 1990 Lindenberg was ill and hospitalized at Lovisenberg Hospital in Oslo. Journalist Anne Britta Staal and several Norwegian therapists visited her during a series of interviews that summer. When speaking about “Willy,” she reportedly became animated and discussed the couple’s sexual openness. She denied that disagreement over therapy was the reason she had not accompanied Reich to America, but the surviving summary does not record what she said the actual reason was.

She died on 6 November 1990. Her precise illness and medical cause of death are not publicly established.

## Political, Philosophical, and Artistic Development

### Left politics and anti-fascism

Lindenberg emerged from the left-wing cultural world of late Weimar Berlin. Her politics were expressed through association, activism, and theatrical practice rather than a known body of political writing.

Her reported activities included:

- participation in a communist or communist-associated cell;
- association with the Künstlerkolonie;
- opposition to Nazism;
- activity alongside Ruth Abramowitsch and Ilse Larede;
- labor-movement choreography in Oslo.

No evidence was found that she later renounced, formally revised, or publicly codified her political commitments.

### Free-body culture and sexual reform

Her life with Reich placed her in the radical sexual-reform movement. In her final interview she reportedly remembered a household in which sexuality and bodies were treated without conventional shame.

This does not establish that she accepted every element of Reich’s sex-political program. Reich’s own diary says she criticized Sexpol colleagues as neurotic or bourgeois and eventually stayed away from meetings.

### Artistic development

Lindenberg’s trajectory moved through three overlapping stages:

1. institutional opera dance;
2. Laban-derived free dance and group choreography;
3. somatic and therapeutic movement education.

The sequence was not a simple abandonment of performance. She retained theatrical and expressive dance while progressively concentrating on the conditions underlying movement: tension, sensation, breath, coordination, affect, and interpersonal presence.

### Relationship to psychoanalysis

Lindenberg was adjacent to Norwegian psychoanalytic and character-analytic circles but was not a documented psychoanalyst.

Her contribution lay in embodied practice. Reich supplied a psychological language of character and muscular armor; Lindenberg brought dance, movement observation, and Gindler’s work. Later historians see the encounter as an important bridge between modern dance, somatics, and body psychotherapy.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Wilhelm Reich

Partner, approximately 1932–1939; political associate; intellectual collaborator by proximity and probable mutual influence.

Their relationship combined:

- exile;
- political danger;
- artistic and therapeutic exchange;
- love and attempted family formation;
- affairs and jealousy;
- financial inequality;
- conflict over independence and professional commitment.

No legal marriage occurred and no children are known.

### Elsa Gindler

Teacher and major formative influence. Lindenberg transmitted Gindler’s work to Norway and continued studying with her after the war.

### Hertha Feist and Rudolf Laban

Feist was Lindenberg’s direct professional teacher in a Laban-oriented school. Laban was the originating figure of the movement system central to Lindenberg’s education and Norwegian free-dance work.

### Annie Reich

Wilhelm Reich’s first wife and an injured party in the relationship’s beginning. Annie’s reported letter to Lindenberg documents direct contact and personal conflict. No later relationship between the women was established.

### Eva and Lore Reich

Lindenberg traveled with and spent time with Reich’s daughters. Eva later recalled dancing and camping with her. Lindenberg was not their legal stepmother.

### Signe Hofgaard and Gerd Kjølaas

Professional collaborators and cofounders of Tri-balletten in 1938.

### Gerda Geddes

Student or participant in Lindenberg’s Oslo movement work; later a significant tai chi teacher.

### Ida Korsvold

Close friend, Mensendieck physiotherapist, collaborator, heir, and administrator of Lindenberg’s estate. Korsvold protected the archive for decades and initially denied researchers access. At age 102 she reportedly allowed Ilse Ghekiere to begin examining it.

### Gro Z. M. Torgersbråten

Student and authorized transmitter of *spenningsregulering*. Lindenberg authorized her to lead groups in 1981.

### Berit Heir Bunkan and Tove Lund

Physiotherapists and students who attended Lindenberg’s work despite Bülow-Hansen’s disapproval.

### Aadel Bülow-Hansen

Parallel or rival figure in Norwegian physiotherapy and body psychotherapy. According to Bunkan, she opposed her students’ participation in Lindenberg’s classes on moral grounds.

### Miriam Goldberg and Charlotte Selver

Goldberg was confronted by Lindenberg over teaching work associated with Selver. Selver, like Lindenberg, was a Gindler student, but no direct Lindenberg–Selver collaboration was established in the consulted sources.

### Ruth Abramowitsch and Ilse Larede

Fellow dancers and reported anti-Nazi associates in Berlin.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### Nazi accusation of “state-damaging behavior”

- **Status:** Reported administrative accusation.
- **Evidence:** Later dance-historical documentation places her dismissal on 21 April 1933 under Nazi civil-service legislation.
- **Finding:** The accusation reflects Nazi political repression; it is not evidence of an ordinary criminal offense.

### Communist activity

- **Status:** Reported fact, with formal party status unresolved.
- **Evidence:** Multiple sources identify her as left-wing, politically active, and associated with communist resistance networks.
- **Finding:** No membership record or party disciplinary file was located.

### Swedish prostitution suspicion

- **Status:** Police suspicion; unsubstantiated.
- **Story:** Regular visitors to Reich’s Malmö lodging caused police to suspect that he operated a brothel and Lindenberg was a prostitute.
- **Evidence:** Surveillance and questioning are reported; no charge followed.
- **Finding:** The allegation was never established and appears to have resulted from misinterpretation of Reich’s clinical appointments.

### Relationship with a married man

- **Status:** Documented fact.
- **Evidence:** Reich was married to Annie when his relationship with Lindenberg began. Annie’s marriage ended afterward.
- **Finding:** This caused personal conflict but was not a crime.

### Reich’s jealousy, affairs, and rages

- **Status:** Reported fact based on Lindenberg’s later testimony and Reich’s diaries.
- **Evidence:** Sharaf’s interviews and Reich’s writings independently show jealousy, infidelity, recurrent rupture, and emotional volatility.
- **Finding:** No legal proceeding or contemporaneous complaint was found.

### Influence on vegetotherapy

- **Status:** Scholarly interpretation.
- **Claim:** Lindenberg’s Gindler/Laban training was central to Reich’s shift toward body psychotherapy.
- **Evidence for:** Chronological convergence; her expertise; testimony about mutual exchange; later scholarly reconstruction.
- **Evidence against or limiting:** No explicit surviving acknowledgment by Reich assigning her authorship of a technique.
- **Finding:** Influence is probable; claims of sole or primary invention exceed the evidence.

### Wartime marriage of convenience

- **Status:** Reported fact from Ida Korsvold.
- **Evidence:** Reproduced by Karina and Kant and Young.
- **Unresolved:** Spouse, date, place, legal duration, and exact protective purpose.

### Postwar persecution and rehabilitation

- **Status:** Reported fact.
- **Evidence:** Korsvold’s account says Lindenberg was persecuted as a German, later rehabilitated, and granted a state pension.
- **Unresolved:** No administrative file or pension decision was located.

### Professional territoriality

- **Status:** Reported fact from Bunkan.
- **Event:** Lindenberg challenged Miriam Goldberg’s right to teach Selver-influenced bodywork in Scandinavia.
- **Interpretation:** Evidence of lineage protection, rivalry, or professional territoriality; not evidence of fraud or legal misconduct.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Wilhelm Reich’s second wife”

**Story:** Lindenberg was Reich’s second wife.

**Circulation:** Reich himself called her his wife; colleagues used the description; later books, films, and reference works repeated it.

**Evidence:** They lived in a socially marital union.

**Contrary evidence:** No legal marriage occurred.

**Effect:** The designation recognized the relationship’s seriousness but also reduced Lindenberg to a biographical appendage and obscured her independent work.

### “The woman who invented body psychotherapy”

**Story:** Without Lindenberg there would have been no Reichian vegetotherapy or body psychotherapy.

**Circulation:** Body-psychotherapy writers, including Courtenay Young and commentators on his historical work.

**Evidence:** Her embodied expertise coincided with Reich’s decisive methodological transition.

**Against:** Direct technical authorship is undocumented; Reich’s earlier clinical and theoretical trajectory also contributed.

**Effect:** The narrative counteracts her erasure but risks replacing neglect with an unprovable founding myth.

### Direct Reich–Gindler transmission

**Story:** Reich learned bodywork from Elsa Gindler.

**Evidence:** Lindenberg, Reich’s partner, studied with Gindler; other psychoanalysts also attended Gindler’s work.

**Against:** Young’s investigation found no evidence that Reich ever met Gindler.

**Finding:** Lindenberg is the probable indirect connection; a direct teacher-student bond is unsupported.

### Prostitution in Malmö

**Story:** Lindenberg was acting as a prostitute while Reich operated a brothel.

**Circulation:** Swedish police suspicion generated by visitors to Reich’s room.

**Evidence:** Surveillance occurred.

**Against:** Visitors were patients or students; no charge was filed.

**Effect:** The suspicion contributed to the couple’s precarious status and later exile narrative.

### The abandoned lover

**Story:** Reich abruptly abandoned Lindenberg for America.

**Evidence:** Lindenberg remembered a sudden break after passionate correspondence.

**Competing version:** Reich claimed she could have accompanied him and later attempted to help her secure a visa.

**Finding:** Neither simplified version accounts for the prolonged ambivalence, professional disagreements, affairs, immigration barriers, and war.

### The “swan” episode

**Story:** An older, injured Lindenberg rose unexpectedly from her chair and transformed from an ordinary gray-haired woman into a beautiful, elegant dancer.

**Source:** Berit Heir Bunkan’s firsthand recollection.

**Status:** Reported eyewitness memory with hagiographic qualities.

**Effect:** The story became an emblem of embodied knowledge surviving age and disability.

### Hidden or erased woman

**Story:** Lindenberg was deliberately written out of body-psychotherapy and Norwegian dance history.

**Evidence:** Her work was transmitted orally; she published little or nothing; biographies often discuss her only as Reich’s partner; her archive was inaccessible.

**Qualification:** Some erasure arose from structural patterns and archive inaccessibility rather than a demonstrable coordinated suppression.

**Effect:** Current research explicitly seeks to reconstruct her as an autonomous dancer, activist, and somatic teacher.

## Works by Elsa Lindenberg

### Authored publications

No verified book, article, manifesto, technical paper, diary, memoir, patent, or independently published text authored by Lindenberg was found.

This is consistent with the Gindler tradition’s emphasis on oral and embodied teaching. It should not be assumed that she wrote nothing: her private archive reportedly contains extensive papers, and Reich’s archive includes correspondence with and about her.

### Choreographic and performance works

1. **Unidentified “red revue” / labor-movement dance work**, Oslo, approximately 1935–1939  
   Choreographer. Exact title, premiere date, and complete program not established.

2. **H. C. Andersens eventyr**, Nationaltheatret, Oslo, premiered 26 December 1937  
   Choreography. Adapted by Vera Smirnowa; directed by Gerda Ring; music by Joseph Kowner.

3. **Tri-balletten program at the Vi Kan exhibition**, 1938  
   Co-choreographer and performer with Signe Hofgaard and Gerd Kjølaas. Individual dance titles remain unidentified.

4. **Faust**, Nationaltheatret, Oslo, premiered 8 August 1938  
   Credited with dance preparation; Sceneweb renders the role more generally as choreography.

5. **Forfengelighetens komedie**, Nationaltheatret, Oslo, premiered 18 April 1939  
   Dancer and choreographer; credited in association with Tri-balletten.

6. **Stor ståhei – Bryllup – Jubileum – Frieri**, Studioteatret, Oslo, premiered 6 January 1946  
   Choreography.

### Teaching work

- Dance and movement classes in Copenhagen and Oslo, 1930s.
- Early dance-movement therapy in Oslo after the war.
- Training courses in the Gindler tradition.
- *Spenningsregulering*, formally established under that Norwegian designation around 1964.
- Authorization of Gro Torgersbråten to lead groups, 1981.

### Interviews and correspondence

- Multiple interviews with Myron Sharaf between 1957 and 1978, used in *Fury on Earth*.
- Unpublished interview tapes preserved or held by Haakon Sandøy.
- Final known hospital interview, early summer 1990, associated with Anne Britta Staal.
- Correspondence with Wilhelm Reich, catalogued in the Wilhelm Reich Archives.
- Other papers inherited by Ida Korsvold and now being researched for archival transfer.

These are archival or attributed materials, not authored publications issued by Lindenberg.

## Death and Estate

Lindenberg was hospitalized at Lovisenberg Hospital in 1990 and was described as ill, pale, and elderly during one of her last interviews. No diagnosis has been published.

She died on 6 November 1990, aged approximately eighty-four.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Lindenberg’s legacy divides into four partially overlapping fields.

### Norwegian modern dance

She is recognized as:

- a pioneer of Laban-derived free dance in Norway;
- a cofounder of Tri-balletten;
- a choreographer at leading Oslo theatres;
- an influence on several generations of Norwegian dancers.

### Somatics and movement education

Her transmission of Gindler’s work established a durable Norwegian lineage of *spenningsregulering*. Gro Torgersbråten and other students carried elements of the work forward.

### Dance therapy and body psychotherapy

Lindenberg is increasingly treated as an early bridge between:

- modern dance;
- sensory and somatic education;
- character analysis;
- movement therapy;
- body psychotherapy.

The degree of her influence on Reich remains the central historiographical dispute.

### Reputation through Reich

For decades she appeared mainly as:

- Reich’s lover;
- “second wife”;
- the woman who remained in Norway;
- a witness interviewed by Reich’s biographers.

This framing preserved her name while obscuring her independent career. Current scholarship seeks to correct the imbalance through her own archive, without assuming that every aspect of her work can be reconstructed from Reich’s papers.

### Current reinterpretation

Ghekiere’s 2025–2028 research is the first sustained project based on the private archive. It interprets Lindenberg through “somatic performative biography,” combining archival work with embodied reconstruction.

Because that work is ongoing, major revisions to Lindenberg’s biography are likely. In particular, the archive may clarify:

- her birth and family;
- exact political affiliations;
- Nazi and Norwegian police documentation;
- wartime marriage;
- correspondence with Reich;
- choreography;
- teaching notes;
- finances;
- final years.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are included. Shared geography or broad intellectual resemblance alone is excluded.

### Elsa Gindler — documented fact

Teacher and formative influence. Lindenberg studied Gindler’s *Arbeit am Menschen* in Berlin before the war and returned regularly after 1945. She transmitted this work to Norway as *spenningsregulering* and began a formal teaching lineage under that name around 1964.

### Rudolf Laban — documented educational lineage; direct personal contact not fully established

Lindenberg trained at Hertha Feist’s Berlin Laban school from 1925 and received a diploma around 1927. Norwegian sources consequently identify her as educated by Laban and a pioneer of his free-dance approach. Whether she studied personally with Laban, rather than principally through Feist, remains unresolved.

### Wilhelm Reich — documented fact

Romantic partner, political associate, and probable intellectual collaborator, approximately 1932–1939, in Berlin, Denmark, Sweden, and Oslo. Their shared work connected movement awareness with character analysis and muscular tension. They were never legally married, despite calling one another husband and wife.

### Erich Fromm — no direct connection established

Fromm reviewed one of Reich’s books during the period in which Reich met Lindenberg, and both Fromm and Lindenberg are sometimes placed within wider Gindler or psychoanalytic networks. No meeting, correspondence, collaboration, or acknowledged influence between Fromm and Lindenberg was located.

### Moshé Feldenkrais — no direct connection established

Feldenkrais belonged to the broader historical lineage descending through Heinrich Jacoby and Elsa Gindler. No documentary evidence of a personal Lindenberg–Feldenkrais relationship was found.

### Other roster figures

No adequately documented personal crossing was found with Mary Wigman, Dore Hoyer, Hanya Holm, Katja Wulff, Suzanne Perrottet, Émile Jaques-Dalcroze, Isadora Duncan, Loïe Fuller, Martha Graham, or the other atlas figures. Their inclusion in the same history of modern dance or somatics is insufficient to establish a connection.
