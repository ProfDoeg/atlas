# Felipe Guamán Poma de Ayala: Research Dossier

## Scope and Evidentiary Note

Felipe Guamán Poma de Ayala left one securely attributed major work, several drawings in another chronicler’s manuscript, a royal petition, signatures, and traces in colonial legal records. No parish register, will, probate inventory, marriage record, or securely identified death record is presently known. Much of his genealogy and early life comes from his own retrospective narrative, written to establish his authority before King Philip III. Consequently:

- **Documented fact** means supported by an extant manuscript or colonial record.
- **Reported fact** means stated by Guamán Poma or a near-contemporary but not independently confirmed.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** identifies an inference based on manuscript, linguistic, or contextual evidence.
- **Disputed claim** identifies a materially contested interpretation.
- **Myth or self-created genealogy** identifies a story important to his authority or reputation but unsupported—or contradicted—by documentary evidence.

Modern statements giving exact dates such as “1534–1615” should not be treated as established vital records. The defensible formulation is that he was probably born in the middle decades of the sixteenth century and was alive on 14 February 1615; revisions to his manuscript may extend into 1616.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Principal name:** Felipe Guamán Poma de Ayala.

**Forms in the sources and scholarship:**

- Don Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala
- Phelipe Guaman Poma
- Felipe Huamán Poma de Ayala
- Felipe Waman Puma de Ayala
- Waman Puma
- Felipe Lázaro or simply Lázaro—an identity assigned to him by adversaries in the Chupas litigation
- Felipe Guamán Ayala or Felipe Huaman Ayala—modern attempts to regularize the name
- “El autor,” “el dicho autor,” “indio ladino,” “cronista indio,” and “príncipe”—descriptive or self-ascribed titles rather than separate names

In Quechua, *waman/huamán* means a hawk or falcon and *puma* means puma. Modern spellings vary because early colonial orthography was not standardized.

**Status and occupations:** Indigenous Andean writer, draftsman, interpreter, legal assistant, witness, survey assistant, litigant, Catholic moralist, political memorialist, and probably occasional scribe or notarial aide.

**Ethnic and regional identification:** Associated with Lucanas, Sondondo, Huamanga, the mitmaq population identified with the Yarovilca/Yaro tradition, and the wider Quechua-speaking Andes.

**Probable birthplace:** Guamán Poma called himself a native of the Sondondo area in Lucanas, in present-day Ayacucho, Peru. San Cristóbal de Suntuntu/Suntunto is often proposed more specifically, but no birth record establishes it.

**Birth:** Unknown. Proposals range from approximately 1530–1555, with 1534 frequently repeated. A newer reconstruction places his birth nearer the middle of the century or around 1550–1560. His assertion late in life that he was about eighty is not chronologically reliable. Galen Brokaw and other scholars emphasize that an exact year cannot be recovered from the evidence ([Brokaw, “Texto y contexto”](https://www.scielo.org.pe/scielo.php?pid=S2071-50722020000100057&script=sci_arttext)).

**Death:** Unknown. He was alive on 14 February 1615. The manuscript underwent possible revision in 1615–1616, so “1616 or shortly afterward” is a reasonable terminus, not a documented death date. “Lima, 1615” is a conventional biographical claim without a known death record.

**Languages:** Quechua and colonial Spanish; he incorporated smaller amounts of Aymara and Latin ecclesiastical material. His Spanish reflects oral usage, incomplete formal schooling, Quechua influence, legal formulas, and deliberate experimentation.

**Known portrait:** No independent likeness is known. His manuscript includes self-representations, notably the aged, poorly clothed author walking toward Lima.

## Family and Ancestry

### Claimed paternal line

Guamán Poma named his father **Martín Guaman Mallqui de Ayala**. In the *Nueva corónica*, he elevated him as:

- a principal lord of Lucanas and neighboring territories;
- a descendant of the Yarovilca rulers;
- “second person” or representative of the Inca;
- an ambassador of Huáscar;
- a loyal supporter of the Spanish Crown in the civil wars following the conquest.

He further narrated that his father met Francisco Pizarro at Tumbes as Huáscar’s ambassador and voluntarily transferred allegiance to the Spanish king.

**Evidentiary status:** The father may have been a real local figure, but the titles, continental authority, and Tumbes embassy are **self-reported dynastic claims**. Brokaw regards the Tumbes encounter as a fictive construction used to argue that Andean sovereignty had been peacefully entrusted to the Crown and that no “just war” had occurred ([Brokaw](https://www.scielo.org.pe/scielo.php?pid=S2071-50722020000100057&script=sci_arttext)).

A 2025 study argues that the more plausible social background was descent from mitmaq families relocated from Huánuco to Ayacucho during Inca rule, rather than membership in the supreme Inca or Yarovilca aristocracy ([González Díaz](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0717-88322025000100209&script=sci_arttext)).

### Claimed maternal line

He called his mother **Juana Curi Ocllo**, also rendered Cusi Ocllo, Curi Ocllo Coya, or Juana Chuquitanta. He represented her as a daughter of Túpac Inca Yupanqui and therefore a sister or close relative of Huayna Cápac.

**Evidentiary status:** **Self-reported and unverified.** The chronology and genealogical elevation appear designed to join his family to the royal Inca line.

### Half-brother and the Ayala name

The chronicle describes **Martín de Ayala**, a mestizo priest or hermit, as Guamán Poma’s older half-brother and early Christian instructor. Martín’s father was reportedly the Spanish captain **Luis Ávalos de Ayala**, while his mother was Juana Curi Ocllo. The family’s adoption of “Ayala” was connected in Guamán Poma’s narrative to Ávalos de Ayala and to his father’s claimed rescue of that captain at the battle of Huarina in 1547.

Earlier scholars attempted to reconstruct a liaison between Curi Ocllo and Ávalos de Ayala. The order and legal character of these relationships cannot be securely established. The narrative may combine family memory, patronage, baptismal ties, and status-building.

### Other relatives

Guamán Poma referred collectively to sons, daughters, nephews, and other relatives when describing his return to a ruined household after decades of service. The passage says he found “his sons and daughters” impoverished and serving others.

**Evidentiary status:** **Self-reported.** No names of a wife, partner, or children have been securely identified in an independent record. It is possible that “sons and daughters” included descendants, dependants, or younger members of an extended household.

The Chupas litigation connects him to members of the **Tingo family**, with whom he asserted common land and hereditary interests. The precise genealogical relationships remain disputed.

## Childhood and Early Life

No contemporary document describes Guamán Poma’s childhood.

He portrayed himself as an Andean nobleman formed inside Christianity rather than as a convert detached from his own society. A recent reconstruction proposes that the child initially called **Felipe Lázaro** may have lived as a dependent or foundling servant in the Huamanga household of **Doña Leonor de Ayala** and her husband, **Pedro Díez de Solier**, a prominent encomendero and corregidor. This would explain access to Spanish domestic culture, literacy, and the Ayala name, but it remains a **scholarly reconstruction**, not a settled biography ([“Guaman Poma, a special case?”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/americas/article/guaman-poma-a-special-case/606DA5305D57E63E325CD0A27DAC551F)).

His own account credits the priest Martín de Ayala with teaching him Christianity. Modern textual research suggests additional contact with the Franciscan environment of **Luis Jerónimo de Oré**, catechists, doctrinal manuals, and clerical households.

## Education and Formation

Guamán Poma did not attend a documented college or university. The first institutional colegios for sons of Indigenous elites in Peru postdated most or all of his education; the Lima college opened in 1618.

His learning was probably cumulative and practical:

1. **Domestic Christian instruction**, reportedly from Martín de Ayala.
2. **Catechetical literacy:** prayers, sermons, confessional manuals, saints’ lives, biblical narrative, and doctrinal texts.
3. **Legal literacy:** petitions, witness statements, land titles, administrative formulas, and notarial conventions.
4. **Translation work:** movement between Quechua-speaking communities and Spanish officials.
5. **Artistic apprenticeship:** copying or adapting prints and manuscript illustrations.
6. **Oral Andean knowledge:** genealogies, songs, narratives, calendars, place-based memory, and testimony attributed to elders and quipucamayoc.

Soledad González Díaz characterizes his literacy as elementary and unsystematic but exceptionally productive. She identifies approximately eighteen printed or manuscript sources reflected in his text and pictures, including the Bible, *Speculum humanae salvationis*, saints’ lives, Sebastián Brant’s *Ship of Fools*, Hartmann Schedel’s *Liber chronicarum*, Éloy d’Amerval’s *Livre de la deablerie*, works of Fray Luis de Granada, early Peruvian chronicles, catechisms, and legal or administrative literature ([González Díaz](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0717-88322025000100209&script=sci_arttext)).

The irregular grammar of the *Nueva corónica* is therefore not evidence of an absence of learning. It records a multilingual writer appropriating several documentary systems without scholastic training in Latin grammar or rhetoric.

## Chronological Life History

### Mid-sixteenth century: birth and formation

Guamán Poma was born somewhere in or near the Sondondo valley of Lucanas. No exact date is recoverable. His family likely belonged to the Indigenous provincial population shaped by Inca resettlement and early Spanish rule.

He grew up during demographic collapse, coercive evangelization, the reorganization of communities, and the consolidation of Spanish administration. These processes later became central subjects of his chronicle.

### Approximately 1568–1571: Cristóbal de Albornoz and the Taki Onqoy campaign

The first known public-service episode associated with him was assistance to the ecclesiastical visitor **Cristóbal de Albornoz** in Soras and Lucanas. Albornoz investigated and suppressed the Taki Onqoy movement and other practices classified as “idolatry.”

Guamán Poma apparently worked as a Quechua-speaking assistant or interpreter. His name appears in Albornoz-related testimonial material, though the precise length and coercive content of his service are not fully reconstructable.

This episode gave him experience with:

- ecclesiastical visitation;
- interrogation and testimony;
- the classification of huacas and ritual objects;
- bilingual mediation;
- Catholic reform doctrine;
- the violent institutional conditions of conversion.

**Contested significance:** This was participation in a colonial campaign that destroyed sacred objects and punished Indigenous ritual specialists. Guamán Poma later remained a committed Catholic opponent of “idolatry,” yet criticized clerics who could not distinguish ordinary stones from huacas and recorded testimony about extirpators’ abuses. The evidence supports neither a simple portrait of him as an anticolonial resister nor one of uncomplicated collaboration ([“Y dice que adora piedras”](https://www.scielo.org.pe/scielo.php?pid=S2071-50722020000100233&script=sci_arttext)).

### 1570s–1580s: interpreter and mobile colonial intermediary

He later claimed thirty years of royal service. This cannot be verified year by year, but his legal knowledge and geographical range support prolonged employment or informal work as interpreter, assistant, messenger, survey aide, witness, and scribe.

He probably traveled through areas including Lucanas, Soras, Andamarca, Huamanga, Cuzco, Jauja, and the central-southern Andean road system. Claims that he systematically traversed the whole viceroyalty should be treated cautiously: some geographical descriptions may derive from written or oral informants rather than personal visits.

### Late 1580s–1590s: Martín de Murúa’s workshop

Material analysis of the two Murúa manuscripts establishes that Guamán Poma supplied drawings to the Mercedarian friar **Martín de Murúa**, author of histories of the Incas.

His contribution included watercolor images, and probably information about Andean genealogy, clothing, ritual, and political organization. The exact direction of influence remains debated:

- Adorno and Ivan Boserup argue that Guamán Poma’s participation in the Galvin Murúa was late and limited.
- Juan Ossio has defended the possibility that an earlier Guamán Poma project influenced Murúa.
- Physical examination nevertheless establishes direct artistic contact and later corrections or interventions.

The relationship deteriorated. In the *Nueva corónica*, Murúa appears as a negative example of clerical abuse. Whether the hostility arose from personal conflict, intellectual rivalry, witnessed misconduct, or all three is unresolved.

### 1594: land-survey work

A surviving administrative record—preserved in an eighteenth-century copy—shows Guamán Poma conducting or assisting with a land measurement under the visitor **Gabriel Solano de Figueroa**. This supports his portrayal as an intermediary familiar with territorial boundaries, legal formulas, and colonial inspection.

### 20 November 1595: Quinua document

A document concerning Quinua bears the signature of Amador de Valdepeña and ratification by “don Phelipe Guaman Poma.” It is the earliest archival item to have brought independent confirmation of the chronicler to modern scholarship. It supports his employment as interpreter or validating witness ([Brokaw](https://www.scielo.org.pe/scielo.php?pid=S2071-50722020000100057&script=sci_arttext)).

### 1590s–1600: the Chupas litigation

Guamán Poma and members of the Tingo family sought control of lands at Santa Catalina de Chupas/Chiara, near Huamanga. Their opponents were Chachapoya settlers whose families had occupied the area.

The records now called the **Expediente Prado Tello** and **Compulsa de Ayacucho** contain petitions, testimony, sales, boundary lists, and decisions. Guamán Poma appeared in shifting capacities: interpreter, witness, representative, claimant, and accused party.

He initially achieved some favorable results. In 1597 he submitted a boundary list that later reappeared almost verbatim in the *Nueva corónica*. The named landmarks demonstrate how his personal land case entered the book’s general indictment of dispossession.

His opponents maintained that he:

- was not a hereditary member of the family whose rights he asserted;
- was a poor commoner or servant;
- had appropriated the name Guamán Poma;
- was really an unrelated man called **Lázaro**;
- falsified or misrepresented noble status.

The court ultimately accepted enough of the opposing case to defeat his claim.

### 1600: judgment, flogging, and exile

The adverse judgment reportedly sentenced him to:

- **two hundred lashes**, and
- **two years’ banishment from Huamanga**.

The sentence is documented in the litigation record. Whether every lash was physically administered is not independently known, but the punishment was formally imposed.

This was the clearest material failure of his life: loss of land, public degradation, and exclusion from a city where he had built professional connections. His later hostility to corrupt corregidores, false local chiefs, opportunistic interpreters, and legal representatives was directly shaped by the case.

### Approximately 1600–1608: displacement and writing

He later wrote that enemies had entered his house, fields, and pastures and reduced his family to dependency. He portrayed himself returning old, gray, thin, barefoot, and unrecognized by relatives.

The literal claim that he was then eighty and had served exactly thirty years is doubtful. The passage functions as autobiography, biblical lament, and a representation of the dispossessed Indigenous litigant.

A second expulsion from Lucanas around 1606 or 1608 is frequently reported, but the documentation is less secure than for the 1600 sentence. It should be treated as **reported or reconstructed**, not certain.

### After 1608: composition of the surviving manuscript

Guamán Poma claimed to have worked on his book for twenty or thirty years. Codicological and internal evidence indicates that the surviving, substantially unified manuscript was chiefly produced after 1608, though it may incorporate older notes, drawings, narratives, or drafts.

Its three broad movements are:

1. universal and Andean history, from Creation through the Incas;
2. conquest and civil war;
3. *Buen gobierno*: an extensive account of colonial society, abuses, cities, administration, reform, roads, and the author’s journey.

It eventually reached approximately 1,189 numbered pages and nearly four hundred drawings. The precise drawing count differs according to whether the title page and particular graphic units are included.

### 1613–1615: dedication and revision

An introductory letter within the manuscript is dated 1613. The text contains evidence of additions through 1615 and possibly 1616.

The addressee was **King Philip III of Spain**. Guamán Poma intended the work to be a royal memorial, history, visual archive, and program of reform. He imagined a direct dialogue in which the king asks questions and the Indigenous author answers.

### Journey to Lima

Near the end, “Camina el autor” recounts his road to Lima. He presents himself walking through an inverted colonial world, hearing complaints, observing abuses, and carrying his manuscript toward the administrative center.

The episode is grounded in real geography but is also a literary pilgrimage. Its exact itinerary and dates cannot be independently reconstructed.

### 14 February 1615: letter to the king

A separately preserved letter in the Archivo General de Indias, signed by Guamán Poma and dated **14 February 1615**, informed Philip III that the work existed and asked the king to order the viceroy to send it to Spain.

This is the last precisely dated, securely documented event in his life.

### 1615–1616 or later: disappearance from the record

No surviving response from Philip III has been identified. There is no proof that Guamán Poma personally placed the manuscript aboard a ship, that the king read it, or that it reached the royal court during the author’s lifetime.

He disappears from presently known documentation. His place, date, and cause of death are unknown.

## Employment, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

No evidence identifies a formal salaried career, company, investment portfolio, business partnership, patent, or invention in the modern sense.

His economic and professional world consisted of:

- interpreter’s work for ecclesiastical and civil officials;
- legal assistance and document production;
- land inspection and boundary measurement;
- artistic work for Martín de Murúa;
- representation of Indigenous parties;
- agricultural land, fields, pastures, houses, and hereditary rights claimed at Chupas;
- the long, resource-intensive production of his manuscript.

His litigation suggests that land was his principal material asset and basis for status. No reliable valuation survives. After losing the case, he represented himself as impoverished. There is no evidence that the *Nueva corónica* earned him money or patronage.

His “technologies” were documentary: alphabetic writing, bilingual translation, pen-and-ink drawing, watercolor, maps, genealogical diagrams, calendars, legal formularies, and the visual logic of Andean spatial ordering.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Catholicism

Guamán Poma was an emphatic Catholic, not a clandestine advocate for restoring pre-Christian religion. He accepted Creation, Scripture, the sacraments, Christian monarchy, sin, judgment, heaven, and hell. He demanded better evangelization and proposed Indigenous clergy.

His Catholicism did not amount to approval of Spanish colonial practice. He repeatedly argued that colonists, priests, corregidores, encomenderos, and local intermediaries behaved as bad Christians.

### Political thought

His political program combined incompatible-looking elements:

- loyalty to Philip III;
- recognition of universal Christian monarchy;
- insistence that “the Indies belong to the Indians” as Spain belongs to Spaniards;
- hereditary Indigenous local government;
- opposition to encomienda and corrupt corregimiento;
- racial and corporate separation of social groups;
- Indigenous self-rule under the Crown;
- regulation of tribute and labor;
- protection of communal lands;
- punishment of abusive officials;
- social discipline, hierarchy, and Catholic morality.

He was not proposing a modern egalitarian state. His ideal order was hierarchical, patriarchal, corporatist, and ethnically bounded. He defended Indigenous commoners against abuse while repeatedly presenting himself as their rightful superior.

### Attitude toward Inca rule

He celebrated Andean antiquity and administrative achievement but did not simply glorify the Incas. His text could be anti-Inca in its account of idolatry and imperial usurpation while remaining pro-Andean in asserting prior civilization and natural ownership.

### Artistic method

His images combine:

- European devotional prints;
- legal and heraldic layouts;
- late-medieval visual conventions;
- Andean hierarchy, duality, direction, dress, and spatial order;
- theatrical scenes of testimony and punishment;
- recurring left/right, upper/lower, male/female, and center/periphery relations.

The drawings do not merely illustrate the prose. They organize arguments, expose contradictions, and often make violence visible more directly than the text.

### Historical method

He blended oral testimony, local memory, biblical chronology, European chronicles, legal documents, personal experience, and imaginative reconstruction. Consequently, the work is not a neutral transcript of pre-Hispanic history. It is evidence for both Andean memory and an individual early-colonial author’s political project.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Martín de Ayala

Reported half-brother, priest or hermit, and early religious instructor. His independent biography is poorly documented.

### Cristóbal de Albornoz

Employer or supervising ecclesiastical visitor around 1568–1571. Albornoz offered training in inquiry and documentation but also implicated Guamán Poma in coercive extirpation.

### Martín de Murúa

Collaborator, probable patron, artistic employer, intellectual interlocutor, and later target of severe criticism. Their overlapping manuscripts provide the strongest material evidence for Guamán Poma’s artistic work before the *Nueva corónica*.

### Gabriel Solano de Figueroa

Official associated with Guamán Poma’s 1594 land-measurement activity.

### Amador de Valdepeña

Signatory in the 1595 Quinua proceeding ratified by Guamán Poma.

### Pedro de Rivera

Lieutenant corregidor whom Guamán Poma blamed for disregarding favorable orders and harming him in the Chupas case.

### Chachapoya litigants

Collective legal adversaries in the Chupas dispute. Their longer alliance with Spanish authorities and local administrative experience strengthened their position.

### Named informants

At the end of his discussion of earlier chronicles, he named Diego Zatuni, Juan Martínez Malma, Felipe Chuqui Taypi, Cristóbal Uari Callo, Diego Mullo, and Pedro Ninarua. No substantial independent information about them has been found.

### Intellectual influences

Major documented or textually demonstrated influences included the Bible, Catholic liturgy and catechesis, Luis Jerónimo de Oré’s milieu, Bartolomé de las Casas’s arguments, Fray Luis de Granada, Spanish chronicles of Peru, saints’ lives, *Speculum humanae salvationis*, Brant, Schedel, and d’Amerval.

## Controversies, Allegations, Legal Proceedings, and Adverse Material

### Land appropriation and imposture allegations

**Status: documented accusations followed by an adverse colonial judgment.**

His Chachapoya opponents accused him of inventing hereditary ties and noble identity to obtain Chupas lands. The court rejected his claim and sentenced him to flogging and exile.

What the judgment establishes is that colonial authorities ruled against him. It does not by itself establish the ultimate historical truth of every genealogical allegation. His own manuscript’s inflated titles, however, give independent reason to treat his noble pedigree cautiously.

### Participation in extirpation campaigns

**Status: documented or strongly supported employment; precise acts unresolved.**

He aided Albornoz’s investigation of Taki Onqoy-era religion. Such campaigns involved interrogation, punishment, destruction of sacred objects, and forced religious discipline. No known record assigns a particular beating, execution, or confiscation personally to Guamán Poma. His participation should be recorded without enlarging it into undocumented personal crimes.

### Hostility toward mestizos and social mixture

**Status: documented textual position.**

The *Nueva corónica* contains severe denunciations of mestizos, mulattos, unauthorized social mobility, interracial sexual relationships, and people who crossed prescribed corporate boundaries. He associated mixture with disorder, exploitation, and demographic danger to Indigenous communities.

The stance was partly connected to his attempt to preserve Indigenous land and jurisdiction from outsiders, but it also expressed racialized, patriarchal, and status-conscious prejudices. His having a reported mestizo half-brother does not erase the text’s hostility.

### Representations of women, sexuality, and Black people

**Status: documented textual and visual material; interpretation debated.**

He condemned sexual misconduct by priests, Spaniards, Indigenous officials, and women. His ideal system strictly controlled women’s movement, sexuality, marriage, dress, and labor. Certain images reproduce misogynistic and racial stereotypes; others depict enslaved Black men and women as victims of brutal Spanish masters and insist upon their Christian humanity.

The work therefore contains both denunciation of racial violence and a hierarchical vocabulary inherited from colonial society.

### Violence and punishment

**Status: documented proposals and historical descriptions.**

He reproduced or imagined severe Inca punishments and endorsed strong moral discipline. Not every punishment he attributed to Inca government can be confirmed archaeologically or from independent texts. Some may represent early-colonial legal concepts projected backward.

### Accuracy of historical claims

**Status: disputed by comparison with other sources.**

His chronology of universal and Andean history, royal genealogies, apostolic evangelization, and conquest contains contradictions and anachronisms. These do not make the work a wholesale fraud. They reveal its character as political history, memory, theological argument, and status claim rather than modern empirical historiography.

### No modern criminal or financial allegations

The dedicated adversarial searches—including combinations of his name with controversy, scandal, fraud, conspiracy, corruption, accusations, and criticism, as well as searches limited to 2024–2026—found no credible modern allegation of money laundering, commercial fraud, intelligence activity, or comparable conduct. The relevant contested matters remain the Chupas case, religious-extirpation work, genealogy, ideological prejudices, relations with Murúa, and the modern authorship forgery controversy.

## Myths, Legends, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### The royal Inca–Yarovilca prince

**Story:** Guamán Poma was a high prince descended paternally from Yarovilca rulers and maternally from Túpac Inca Yupanqui.

**Appearance and circulation:** The *Nueva corónica* itself; repeated by nationalist, literary, and popular biographies.

**Evidence:** His manuscript supplies the pedigree. Legal opponents called him an unrelated commoner named Lázaro. Recent scholarship places him among provincial mitmaq descendants without accepting royal rank.

**Effect:** The claim gave him authority to address Philip III as an Andean counterpart and continues to underwrite descriptions of him as an “Inca prince.”

### His father’s peaceful meeting with Pizarro

**Story:** Martín Guaman Mallqui met Pizarro at Tumbes as Huáscar’s ambassador; both parties knelt, embraced, and peacefully joined sovereignty.

**Evidence:** The narrative is absent from better-supported conquest accounts and conflicts with chronology and circumstances. Brokaw treats it as fictive.

**Function:** It allowed Guamán Poma to deny that Spain acquired legitimate rights through a just war. If sovereignty had been peacefully entrusted to the monarch, conquistadors could not claim land and labor as rewards for conquest.

### Saint Bartholomew evangelized the Andes

**Story:** The apostle Bartholomew reached the Andes before the Spaniards, leaving traces remembered in Andean tradition.

**Evidence:** No historical or archaeological evidence establishes an apostolic mission to Peru. Similar stories circulated in colonial efforts to incorporate American peoples into biblical history.

**Function:** The legend established that Andeans had ancient access to Christian truth and weakened Spanish claims that conquest was necessary for evangelization.

### The author was eighty years old

**Story:** Guamán Poma described himself as approximately eighty, gray, thin, barefoot, and exhausted after thirty years of service.

**Evidence:** Literal acceptance creates serious chronological problems, particularly if his work with Albornoz began in the late 1560s. Scholars variously interpret the age as exaggeration, symbolic old age, scribal imprecision, or evidence for an earlier birth.

**Effect:** Modern biographies turned the figure into exact birth dates, especially 1534. The underlying evidence does not support that precision.

### The king received and read the book

**Story:** Guamán Poma sent the manuscript directly to Philip III, who received or ignored it.

**Evidence:** The 1615 petition asked the king to order its transmission. No receipt, royal reading note, or response survives. The manuscript’s route to Europe is unknown.

**Effect:** The image of a monumental letter unanswered for three centuries has become central to his symbolic reputation.

### The Miccinelli/Blas Valera authorship narrative

**Story:** Manuscripts publicized from a private Neapolitan collection claimed that Jesuit **Blas Valera**, officially recorded as dead in 1597, secretly survived, returned to Peru, composed the *Nueva corónica*, and employed Guamán Poma as a public front; Gonzalo Ruiz supposedly wrote or illustrated it. Associated claims included syllabic khipus and a poisoned Spanish attack on the Incas.

**Appearance:** Publicized principally from the late 1980s and 1990s by Clara Miccinelli and Laura Laurencich-Minelli; debated at conferences and in publications.

**Evidence offered for it:** Purported seventeenth-century papers, signatures, drawings, coded texts, and khipu specimens.

**Evidence against it:**

- The Copenhagen manuscript is materially and paleographically coherent as an autograph work associated with Guamán Poma.
- Legal documents independently establish his historical existence and writing.
- Ivan Boserup showed that a key “Contract” drawing was traced from a retouched image in the 1936 Paris facsimile.
- Another “Chaves” drawing is probably derived from the same modern reproduction.
- Davide Domenici demonstrated that most of the purported syllabic-khipu material depends upon Raimondo de Sangro’s 1750 *Lettera apologetica* and is therefore later than it claims.
- Erika Valdivieso identified passages dependent upon Dante editions printed in 1921 and 1960.

**Current evidentiary status:** The specific claim that Valera secretly authored the *Nueva corónica* is a **discredited allegation associated with modern forgeries**. A limited older component among the Naples materials has been discussed, but it does not validate the authorship story. The controversy remains historically important because it redirected attention to Indigenous authorship, manuscript authentication, and the appeal of sensational secret histories ([Boserup](https://tidsskrift.dk/fundogforskning/article/view/118877); [Domenici](https://tidsskrift.dk/fundogforskning/article/view/118875)).

## Works by Felipe Guamán Poma de Ayala

### Securely attributed major work

#### *El primer nueva corónica y buen gobierno*

- **Date:** Substantially completed 1615; revised possibly into 1616.
- **Language:** Colonial Spanish and Quechua, with Aymara and Latin elements.
- **Format:** Autograph illustrated manuscript, Royal Danish Library, Copenhagen, GKS 2232 4º.
- **Extent:** Approximately 1,189 numbered pages; nearly four hundred drawings.
- **Original publication:** Unpublished in his lifetime.
- **Contents:** Creation and biblical history; Andean ages; Incas and coyas; law and administration; ritual calendar; conquest; civil wars; colonial officials; clergy; Indigenous society; women; Black people and slavery; labor and mining; cities; roads; reform proposals; dialogue with the king; journey to Lima.
- **Digital original:** Complete facsimile, transcription, commentary, and indexes are maintained by the Royal Danish Library ([Guaman Poma Website](https://poma.kb.dk/)).

### Letter to Philip III

#### Carta de Felipe Guamán Poma de Ayala al rey, 14 February 1615

- **Repository:** Archivo General de Indias, Seville, Lima 145.
- **Nature:** Signed petition announcing the book and requesting royal instructions for its transmission.
- **Authorship:** Secure.
- **Publication status:** Archival letter later reproduced and transcribed in scholarly editions.

### Legal writings, signatures, and administrative documents

These are not independent literary works but form part of his documentary corpus:

- 1594 land-measurement declaration or report preserved through a later copy;
- 1595 Quinua ratification bearing his name;
- petitions, statements, boundary lists, and appearances preserved in the Prado Tello/Chupas dossier and Compulsa de Ayacucho;
- the 1597 boundary list later incorporated into the *Nueva corónica*.

Authorship of every hand and formula must be assessed document by document; some items were produced by professional scribes and merely signed, ratified, or spoken by him.

### Drawings in Martín de Murúa’s manuscripts

A group of watercolor and ink illustrations in the **Galvin Murúa**, *Historia del origen y genealogía real de los reyes ingas del Piru*, has been attributed to Guamán Poma through stylistic and codicological analysis. These are genuine artistic contributions, but they occur within Murúa’s authored project.

### Principal editions and translations

- **1936:** Paul Rivet, ed., *Nueva corónica y buen gobierno (Codex péruvien illustré)*, Institut d’Ethnologie, Paris—first complete facsimile publication.
- **1944:** Luis Bustíos Gálvez, Lima edition.
- **1969:** Selected or modernized editions circulated in Peru.
- **1978:** *Letter to a King: A Peruvian Chief’s Account of Life under the Incas and under Spanish Rule*, edited and translated by Christopher Dilke—abridged English presentation.
- **1980:** John V. Murra and Rolena Adorno, with Quechua analysis by Jorge L. Urioste, critical transcription, Mexico City, Siglo XXI, three volumes.

## Manuscript Transmission and Rediscovery

The route from Peru to Denmark is unresolved. Proposed scenarios place the manuscript in a Spanish collection before acquisition through diplomatic or private-library channels in the seventeenth century. Associations with the Count-Duke of Olivares, Danish envoys, Laurids Ulfeldt, and the library of King Frederick III have been proposed but not decisively proven.

By the eighteenth century the manuscript was in the Danish Royal Library. German scholar **Richard Pietschmann**, director of the Göttingen University Library, recognized its significance there in 1908 and announced it to the scholarly world.

Paul Rivet’s Paris facsimile appeared in 1936. The Royal Danish Library placed the complete manuscript online in 2001, making it one of the institution’s early comprehensive manuscript digitization projects ([Royal Danish Library collection page](https://www.kb.dk/en/find-materials/collections/manuscript-collection/chronicle-guaman-poma)).

## Death and Final Status

No death certificate, burial entry, will, estate inventory, or contemporary obituary is known.

The firm endpoint is:

- **14 February 1615:** signed letter to Philip III.
- **1615–1616:** probable final revision phase of the manuscript.
- **Afterward:** no securely identified record.

Claims that he died in Lima in 1615 should be labeled **traditional but unverified**. Cause of death, final residence, health history, and burial place are unknown. His descriptions of age, poverty, exhaustion, and frailty are literary self-testimony, not medical documentation.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Seventeenth to nineteenth centuries

The manuscript apparently received little or no public circulation. There is no demonstrated seventeenth-century readership comparable to that of Garcilaso de la Vega’s printed works. Its obscurity explains its negligible direct effect on colonial policy.

### Twentieth-century rediscovery

After 1908 and especially after the 1936 facsimile, Guamán Poma became a foundational source for:

- Inca and colonial administration;
- Andean religion and calendrics;
- dress, labor, music, punishment, mining, and urban life;
- Indigenous visual history;
- colonial law and evangelization;
- multilingual writing;
- the study of conquest from an Indigenous-authored perspective.

Earlier evaluations sometimes dismissed him as confused, resentful, uneducated, or unreliable. Later scholarship treated the irregularities as evidence of cultural translation, deliberate appropriation, legal strategy, and alternative historiography.

### National and Indigenous symbolism

His drawings have become ubiquitous in Peruvian textbooks, museums, political graphics, Indigenous-rights publications, and accounts of colonial violence. This mass reuse often detaches the images from his Catholic orthodoxy, hierarchical politics, anti-mestizo rhetoric, and personal land claims.

He has variously been represented as:

- the first great Indigenous chronicler of colonial Peru;
- a precursor of anticolonial or decolonial thought;
- a loyal Catholic reformer;
- an Indigenous noble petitioning the monarch;
- a dispossessed commoner manufacturing nobility;
- an artist whose visual archive preserves voices absent from official records.

None of those descriptions alone encompasses the surviving evidence.

### Scholarly influence

His work transformed ethnohistory by making it impossible to describe early colonial Peru solely through Spanish administrative or clerical texts. Its images now shape how the Inca and colonial worlds are visually imagined, although scholars caution that they are seventeenth-century arguments rather than transparent photographs of the past.

The digital manuscript has enabled page-level comparison of handwriting, revisions, drawings, sources, and Murúa connections. It also provided material evidence decisive in rejecting the Miccinelli authorship story.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted evidence are included. Mere contemporaneity has been excluded.

### Blas Valera

- **Nature:** Intellectual source and subject of a later authorship controversy.
- Guamán Poma referred to or used historical material associated with Valera, although the route of transmission may have involved other chronicles.
- From the late twentieth century, the Miccinelli papers alleged that Valera was the secret author of the *Nueva corónica*. Physical and textual evidence has discredited that allegation.
- **Label:** Documented intellectual/textual relationship; later allegation disproven as an authorship claim.

### Atahualpa

- **Nature:** Historical subject in Guamán Poma’s account of the Inca civil war, Cajamarca, captivity, and execution.
- There could have been no personal meeting if the accepted mid-century birth range is correct. Guamán Poma reconstructed Atahualpa from oral tradition and written sources.
- **Label:** Documented literary-historical treatment; no personal connection.

### Francisco Pizarro González

- **Nature:** Central actor in Guamán Poma’s conquest narrative and his family’s self-created political genealogy.
- The chronicler claimed that his father met Pizarro at Tumbes as Huáscar’s ambassador. Independent evidence does not support the episode.
- **Label:** Documented literary treatment; paternal meeting is myth or political fiction.

### El Inca Garcilaso de la Vega

- **Nature:** Near-contemporary Andean chronicler with overlapping subjects.
- Their works offer contrasting treatments of Inca history and Spanish rule, but no meeting, correspondence, collaboration, or secure direct borrowing between the two men was found.
- The Miccinelli materials attempted to connect both authors to a concealed Valera network; those papers do not provide reliable evidence.
- **Label:** Documented shared intellectual field; no documented personal connection.

### Christopher Columbus

- **Nature:** Figure in Guamán Poma’s universal and conquest history and in visual/textual material adapted from European sources.
- No personal link was possible.
- **Label:** Documented literary treatment only.

### Jesus of Nazareth

- **Nature:** Central religious and moral authority throughout the *Nueva corónica*.
- Guamán Poma used Christ’s teaching, passion, charity, judgment, and the obligations of Christian rulers to condemn colonial misconduct.
- **Label:** Documented intellectual and religious influence, not a historical meeting.

### Abraham, Noah son of Lamech, Moses, David ben Jesse, King Solomon, Paul the Apostle, and Aurelius Augustinus

- **Nature:** Biblical or Christian authorities incorporated into the chronicle’s universal history and moral argument.
- Noah was particularly important to Guamán Poma’s claim that Indigenous Americans belonged within biblical genealogy.
- **Label:** Documented textual/religious influence; legendary or scriptural rather than personal connections.

### Roster members for whom no supported crossing was found

No documented meeting, correspondence, family tie, patronage, collaboration, or acknowledged direct influence was found with the remaining roster members. In particular, the presence of later Latin American political or literary figures on the roster does not demonstrate that they directly studied or invoked Guamán Poma. Absence of evidence for a personal connection is the finding.
