# Thomas Jefferson: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

This dossier reconstructs Thomas Jefferson’s life from surviving correspondence, public records, plantation and financial records, family testimony, archaeological research, documentary editions, and later scholarship.

Evidentiary labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary records, surviving manuscripts, official proceedings, physical evidence, or multiple independent records.
- **Reported fact:** preserved in a later recollection or credible secondary account but not independently established in every detail.
- **Allegation:** a claim made by an identified person or publication without conclusive proof at the time.
- **Disputed interpretation:** the underlying evidence is accepted but its meaning remains contested.
- **Rumor or myth:** a circulating story for which reliable evidence is absent or inadequate.

Dates before Britain’s 1752 calendar reform are not involved. Jefferson’s birthday is nevertheless sometimes given as April 2, 1743, “Old Style,” because colonial Virginians continued to record dates in that manner; April 13 is its Gregorian equivalent and the date conventionally observed.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name:** Thomas Jefferson  
**Common titles:** Third president of the United States; second vice president; first secretary of state; minister plenipotentiary to France; governor of Virginia; delegate to the Continental and Confederation Congresses; “Father of the University of Virginia.”  
**Political designation:** Republican or Democratic-Republican, though “Democratic-Republican” became common mainly among later historians.  
**Nicknames and epithets:** “Sage of Monticello”; “Man of the People” in partisan commemoration; “Long Tom,” occasionally applied because of his height; “Apostle of Liberty” in nineteenth-century political memory. None was a consistent personal pseudonym.  
**Birth:** April 13, 1743, Gregorian calendar; April 2 under the contemporary Old Style convention.  
**Birthplace:** Shadwell plantation, Goochland County—later Albemarle County—Colony of Virginia.  
**Death:** July 4, 1826, approximately 12:50 p.m.  
**Place of death:** Monticello, Albemarle County, Virginia.  
**Burial:** Jefferson family cemetery at Monticello.  
**Principal residences:** Shadwell; Williamsburg; Monticello; Richmond; Annapolis; Philadelphia; New York; Paris; Washington, D.C.; and periodic residence at Poplar Forest in Bedford County, Virginia.  
**Height and appearance:** Contemporary descriptions place him near 6 feet 2½ inches, unusually tall for the period, with a slender or later stooped build, sandy or reddish hair, light eyes, and a freckled complexion. Portraits by Mather Brown, Rembrandt Peale, Gilbert Stuart, and others differ noticeably.

Jefferson designed his own epitaph:

> Here was buried Thomas Jefferson, Author of the Declaration of American Independence, of the Statute of Virginia for Religious Freedom, and Father of the University of Virginia.

He instructed that “not a word more” be added. He omitted the presidency and every other political office.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal line

**Documented fact:** Jefferson’s father was **Peter Jefferson** (1708–1757), a Virginia planter, surveyor, cartographer, justice, and county officeholder. Peter helped survey the Fairfax boundary and collaborated with Joshua Fry on the influential 1751 map of Virginia.

Jefferson wrote that family tradition placed an earlier ancestor near Mount Snowdon in Wales, but he admitted that he possessed no definite genealogy beyond his grandfather. Modern researchers have not conclusively established the claimed Welsh origin. It remains **family tradition, not demonstrated ancestry**.

His paternal grandfather, also named Thomas Jefferson, lived at Osborne’s in Chesterfield County. Peter Jefferson accumulated land and enslaved laborers and entered the regional gentry through property, officeholding, and marriage.

### Maternal line

Jefferson’s mother was **Jane Randolph Jefferson** (1720–1776), daughter of Isham Randolph and Jane Rogers Randolph. The Randolphs were among colonial Virginia’s most prominent planter families, connecting Jefferson to an extensive network of officeholders and landowners.

Jane’s surviving will distributed enslaved people among her children. Jefferson was her executor.

### Siblings

Peter and Jane Jefferson had ten children; two died in childhood. Those reaching later childhood or adulthood included:

- Jane Jefferson (1740–1765)
- Mary Jefferson Bolling (1741–1804)
- Thomas Jefferson (1743–1826)
- Elizabeth Jefferson (1744–1774)
- Martha Jefferson Carr (1746–1811)
- Peter Field Jefferson (1748; died in infancy)
- An unnamed son (1750; died in infancy)
- Lucy Jefferson Lewis (1752–1810)
- Anna Scott Jefferson Marks (1755–1828)
- Randolph Jefferson (1755–1815)

Thomas was the eldest surviving son. At Peter Jefferson’s death he inherited approximately 2,750 acres, including Shadwell and the mountain on which he subsequently built Monticello, as well as enslaved people.

### Marriage

On January 1, 1772, Jefferson married **Martha Wayles Skelton** (October 19, 1748–September 6, 1782), a wealthy widow and accomplished keyboard player. She was the daughter of lawyer, planter, and slave trader **John Wayles** and Martha Eppes Wayles.

Martha had previously married Bathurst Skelton. Both Skelton and their son John died before her marriage to Jefferson. No securely identified portrait of Martha survives.

Jefferson later called the marriage ten years of “unchequered happiness.” Martha endured repeated pregnancies and prolonged ill health. According to family recollection, Jefferson promised her during her final illness that he would not remarry. He never did.

### Children with Martha Wayles Jefferson

Six children were recorded:

1. **Martha “Patsy” Jefferson Randolph**, September 27, 1772–October 10, 1836; married Thomas Mann Randolph Jr.
2. **Jane Randolph Jefferson**, April 3, 1774–September 1775.
3. **Unnamed son**, May 28–June 14, 1777.
4. **Mary/Maria “Polly” Jefferson Eppes**, August 1, 1778–April 17, 1804; married John Wayles Eppes.
5. **Lucy Elizabeth Jefferson**, November 3, 1780–April 15, 1781.
6. **Lucy Elizabeth Jefferson**, May 8, 1782–October 13, 1784.

Only Martha and Maria survived childhood. Maria died after childbirth in 1804. Martha Randolph survived Jefferson and became the central white-family manager at Monticello during his retirement.

### The Wayles–Hemings family relationship

**Documented fact and strong genealogical conclusion:** John Wayles fathered children with **Elizabeth “Betty” Hemings**, an enslaved woman of mixed African and European ancestry. The evidence includes testimony from her grandson Madison Hemings and the family structure recorded in plantation documents. Their children included Sally Hemings. Consequently, Martha Wayles Jefferson and Sally Hemings were half-sisters.

When John Wayles died in 1773, Jefferson and his wife inherited substantial land, large debts, and more than 100 enslaved people, including members of the Hemings family.

### Children attributed to Jefferson and Sally Hemings

Sally Hemings’s recorded children were:

1. Harriet Hemings, 1795–1797
2. Beverly Hemings, born 1798; disappeared from the documentary record after leaving Monticello in 1822
3. An unnamed daughter, 1799–1800
4. Harriet Hemings, born 1801; left Monticello in 1822
5. Madison Hemings, 1805–1877
6. Eston Hemings, 1808–1856; later used the surname Jefferson

The Thomas Jefferson Foundation, National Genealogical Society specialists, and the prevailing modern scholarly consensus conclude that Thomas Jefferson fathered all six documented children. The evidence and remaining limits are treated separately below.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1743–1760

Jefferson was born within Virginia’s slaveholding planter elite. Shadwell stood near the Rivanna River and the frontier edge of intensive British settlement. His childhood combined plantation management, classical education, surveying culture, and proximity to Indigenous lands being incorporated into colonial property systems.

At about five he entered an English-language school. At nine he studied Latin, Greek, and French with the Reverend William Douglas. After Peter Jefferson died in 1757, fourteen-year-old Thomas inherited substantial property but remained under guardianship.

From 1758 to 1760 he boarded with the Reverend **James Maury**, a well-trained classical scholar and Anglican minister near Gordonsville. Maury strengthened his Latin and Greek and exposed him to geography, science, literature, and the intellectual networks of Virginia’s clergy.

Jefferson later remembered his father chiefly in connection with land, physical strength, surveying, and the family’s origins. He wrote comparatively little about his mother. Claims that he was emotionally estranged from her are **biographical inference**, not firmly documented fact.

---

## Education and Formation, 1760–1767

Jefferson entered the College of William & Mary in Williamsburg in 1760. He initially participated in the social life of a young Virginia gentleman but later described adopting an intensive course of study, sometimes claiming fifteen hours per day.

### William Small

Scottish professor **William Small** taught mathematics, natural philosophy, ethics, rhetoric, and belles-lettres. Jefferson later said Small “probably fixed the destinies of my life” by giving him his first expansive view of science and intellectual systems.

### George Wythe

Small introduced Jefferson to **George Wythe**, lawyer, classical scholar, legislator, and later signer of the Declaration. Wythe became Jefferson’s legal teacher and lifelong mentor. Jefferson read law under him for approximately five years and inherited books from him.

### Francis Fauquier’s circle

Small and Wythe brought Jefferson to the table of Lieutenant Governor **Francis Fauquier**. Their discussions linked metropolitan Enlightenment thought with colonial government, political economy, science, music, and law.

### Reading and intellectual influences

Jefferson studied or collected:

- Classical writers including Cicero, Tacitus, Livy, Horace, Virgil, Homer, Aristotle, and Epicurus.
- English common-law authorities, especially Edward Coke.
- John Locke, Francis Bacon, and Isaac Newton, whom he later called the three greatest men in history.
- Montesquieu, Algernon Sidney, Henry St John Bolingbroke, Lord Kames, Francis Hutcheson, David Hume, Cesare Beccaria, Joseph Priestley, and other Enlightenment and Scottish moral-philosophy writers.
- Palladio’s architectural writings.
- Adam Smith’s moral philosophy and, after 1776, political economy.
- Religious texts, including a 1764 George Sale translation of the Qur’an purchased in 1765.

Jefferson was admitted to practice before Virginia’s General Court in 1767.

---

## Early Career: Lawyer, Planter, and Burgess, 1767–1774

Jefferson practiced law throughout central and western Virginia. His surviving legal commonplace books show extensive engagement with property, inheritance, religion, enslavement, and colonial constitutional law. He handled hundreds of matters but abandoned ordinary practice as revolutionary conflict closed the courts.

In 1768 he was elected to the House of Burgesses. That year he began clearing and building on Monticello—Italian for “little mountain.” Construction, demolition, enlargement, and rebuilding continued for more than four decades.

The Shadwell house burned in 1770, destroying books, papers, and possessions. Jefferson moved into the partially completed South Pavilion at Monticello.

His 1770 representation of **Samuel Howell**, a man of mixed ancestry seeking freedom under Virginia law, is often cited as an early antislavery legal action. Jefferson argued from natural right but lost. One isolated case does not establish a sustained emancipation practice.

In 1773 the Wayles inheritance enlarged Jefferson’s estate while adding heavy British mercantile debt. His property system thereafter rested on multiple plantations and the labor of a large enslaved population.

---

## Revolution and Independence, 1774–1776

### *A Summary View*

In 1774 Jefferson drafted instructions for Virginia delegates that were published as *A Summary View of the Rights of British America*. It rejected Parliament’s authority over the colonies, treated the colonies as constitutionally linked to Britain only through the crown, and accused George III of obstructing colonial efforts to restrict the Atlantic slave trade.

### Continental Congress

Jefferson joined the Second Continental Congress in June 1775. He drafted *A Declaration by the Representatives of the United Colonies of North America, Setting Forth the Causes and Necessity of Their Taking Up Arms*, revised by John Dickinson.

In June 1776 a committee consisting of Jefferson, John Adams, Benjamin Franklin, Roger Sherman, and Robert R. Livingston was appointed to prepare a declaration of independence. Adams later recalled that he urged Jefferson to draft it because Jefferson was a Virginian, was popular, and wrote better.

Jefferson’s “original Rough draught” drew upon colonial grievances and a broad natural-rights tradition. Congress revised it extensively. It replaced “sacred & undeniable” with “self-evident,” compressed the text, and deleted a long denunciation of George III for sustaining the slave trade. The deleted passage also criticized enslaved people whom Britain encouraged to seek freedom and fight the colonists, making it simultaneously an attack on slavery, the crown, and British wartime policy.

Congress adopted the Declaration on July 4, 1776.

---

## Virginia Legislator and Legal Reformer, 1776–1779

Jefferson returned to Virginia and worked with George Wythe and Edmund Pendleton on a comprehensive revision of state law.

His projects included:

- Abolishing entail, weakening hereditary concentration of landed wealth.
- Facilitating the eventual abolition of primogeniture.
- A bill for establishing religious freedom.
- A proposed system of publicly supported elementary and advanced education.
- Revision of criminal law, substituting graded punishments for many capital offenses.
- Naturalization and citizenship measures.
- Proposals concerning emancipation and expatriation that were drafted or discussed but not enacted.

The education scheme would have admitted selected boys to higher levels through competitive advancement, while girls would receive only elementary education. It was not adopted in Jefferson’s lifetime.

His criminal-law bill retained death or severe punishment for some offenses and was defeated. Later portrayals of it as straightforward penal humanitarianism omit its punitive provisions.

The **Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom**, drafted in 1777 and introduced in 1779, declared that civil government had no jurisdiction over opinion and that no person should be compelled to support religious worship. James Madison secured its enactment in 1786.

---

## Governor of Virginia, 1779–1781

Jefferson served two one-year terms as governor during the Revolutionary War. Virginia confronted British raids, logistical disorganization, inflation, militia shortages, and divided authority.

In January 1781 Benedict Arnold’s forces attacked Richmond. Jefferson and the government evacuated. In June, cavalry under Banastre Tarleton approached Charlottesville; Jefferson left Monticello shortly before their arrival.

### Legislative inquiry

**Documented fact:** Political critics accused Jefferson of indecision and abandoning the government. The Virginia legislature investigated his conduct. Jefferson appeared to answer allegations, and the House adopted a resolution expressing appreciation for his administration; no formal finding of misconduct was entered.

**Disputed interpretation:** Defenders emphasize impossible wartime conditions and the weakness of the governorship. Critics cite delayed decisions, poor military preparation, and his departure from Monticello. “He fled in cowardice” and “he was completely exonerated of every failure” are rival simplifications.

Jefferson declined immediate reelection and described the episode as deeply wounding.

---

## Widower, Author, and Confederation Statesman, 1781–1784

At the request of François Barbé-Marbois, Jefferson prepared answers about Virginia’s geography, population, government, economy, Indigenous peoples, natural history, and culture. These became *Notes on the State of Virginia*, the only full-length book he published.

Martha Jefferson died on September 6, 1782, after childbirth and prolonged illness. Family accounts describe Jefferson as collapsing emotionally and spending weeks walking and riding with his daughter Martha. Claims of a diagnosable psychiatric disorder cannot be established retrospectively, but **documented and reported evidence supports an acute grief crisis**.

He returned to Congress in 1783. He helped devise a decimal currency system and drafted a plan for governing western territory. His original territorial proposal would have prohibited slavery after 1800; Congress did not adopt that clause. The later Northwest Ordinance of 1787 barred slavery north of the Ohio River through a different legislative process.

---

## France and European Travel, 1784–1789

Jefferson sailed for Europe in July 1784 with his daughter Martha and the enslaved servant James Hemings. He joined Benjamin Franklin and John Adams in negotiating commercial agreements. In 1785 he succeeded Franklin as minister to France.

### Residences and official work

He lived principally at the Hôtel de Langeac on the Champs-Élysées in Paris. His responsibilities included trade, debt, consular affairs, the Barbary states, American shipping, and observation of French politics.

James Hemings was trained in French cuisine. Jefferson paid him wages while in France but continued to treat him legally as enslaved. In 1793 Jefferson promised freedom once Hemings trained a successor; James was formally emancipated in 1796.

### European journeys

In 1786 Jefferson traveled in England with John Adams, visiting gardens, estates, industrial sites, and places associated with English history.

From February to June 1787 he traveled approximately 1,200 miles through southern France and northern Italy. His route included Burgundy, Lyon, Nîmes, Aix-en-Provence, Marseille, Toulon, Nice, Turin, Milan, Pavia, Genoa, the Mediterranean and Atlantic coasts, Bordeaux, and western France. He studied architecture, agriculture, canals, ports, rice production, wine, olive cultivation, and commercial conditions.

He later toured parts of the Netherlands and German states, including the Rhine region, while conducting financial and diplomatic business.

### Maria Cosway

In Paris Jefferson developed an emotionally intense attachment to **Maria Cosway**, an Anglo-Italian artist married to Richard Cosway. His celebrated “Head and Heart” letter of October 1786 dramatized the conflict between reason and romantic attachment.

The correspondence documents intimacy and longing but does not conclusively establish a sexual affair. Claims that Cosway was his “mistress” are **unproven**.

### French Revolution

Jefferson attended the opening of the Estates-General at Versailles in May 1789. He advised Lafayette and other reformers and helped draft or review constitutional proposals, including a declaration of rights. He was not a formal author of France’s final Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, though he participated in its surrounding political network.

He sympathized with the early Revolution and underestimated the violence and institutional breakdown to come.

## Sally Hemings in France and the Paternity Question

Sally Hemings traveled to London and Paris in 1787 while accompanying Jefferson’s daughter Maria. She was about fourteen. Under French law and practice, she may have had a viable claim to freedom, although enforcement and diplomatic immunity introduced uncertainty.

Her son Madison Hemings stated in 1873 that Sally became pregnant by Jefferson in France, intended to remain there, and returned only after Jefferson promised extraordinary privileges for her children and freedom when they reached twenty-one.

### Findings

- **Documented fact:** Sally lived in Jefferson’s household in Paris.
- **Reported fact:** Madison Hemings’s account states that the sexual relationship began in France and that an agreement preceded her return.
- **Documented fact:** Every recorded conception of Sally’s children occurred during a period when Jefferson was at Monticello; none is known to have occurred during his documented absences.
- **Scientific finding:** The 1998 Y-chromosome study found a match between descendants of Eston Hemings and the Jefferson male line and excluded the tested Carr line. It could not identify Thomas Jefferson individually because other Jefferson-line males shared the chromosome.
- **Documented circumstantial evidence:** Jefferson gave freedom, or allowed unopposed departure, to all four of Sally’s children who lived to adulthood. Their treatment closely tracks Madison’s description of the reported agreement.
- **Historical conclusion:** The Thomas Jefferson Foundation’s 2000 research committee found a strong likelihood that Jefferson fathered all six children. Most specialists accept this.
- **Minority position:** A dissenting committee member and the later Thomas Jefferson Heritage Society proposed Jefferson’s younger brother Randolph or another male-line relative. No direct evidence places an alternative Jefferson male at Monticello during all the relevant conception periods.
- **Unresolved question:** The precise character, frequency, and subjective meaning of the relationship cannot be recovered. Sally left no known writing.
- **Legal and power context:** Sally was enslaved by Jefferson. She could not enter a legally equal relationship with him, and he controlled her residence, labor, legal status, and the status of her children. Whether she experienced affection, negotiation, coercion, or some combination cannot be conclusively determined.
- **Terminological dispute:** Historians variously call the relationship sexual, coerced, concubinage, or rape. “Rape” expresses the impossibility of equal consent under chattel slavery; no surviving testimony describes a particular initiating assault. Calling it a conventional “romance” is likewise unsupported.

## Secretary of State and Party Formation, 1790–1793

Jefferson returned to the United States in late 1789. George Washington appointed him the first secretary of state. He took office in March 1790.

### Hamilton conflict

Jefferson opposed Treasury Secretary **Alexander Hamilton** over:

- Federal assumption of state debts.
- The national bank.
- Broad versus narrow constitutional construction.
- Commercial and manufacturing policy.
- Relations with Britain and revolutionary France.
- The social implications of funded debt and centralized finance.

Jefferson hosted the dinner associated with the 1790 compromise under which Hamilton’s assumption program advanced and the permanent national capital was located on the Potomac.

Jefferson and James Madison helped organize opposition through correspondence, congressional coordination, and newspapers. Their coalition developed into the Republican Party.

### Philip Freneau and partisan journalism

Jefferson arranged a State Department translating clerkship for poet and editor **Philip Freneau**, who published the anti-administration *National Gazette*. Jefferson denied controlling the paper, but the appointment and political purpose are documented. It was an early instance of government patronage intersecting with partisan media.

### Citizen Genêt

Jefferson initially sympathized with French minister Edmond-Charles Genêt but ultimately joined the cabinet in requesting his recall after Genêt commissioned privateers and appealed over Washington’s government.

Jefferson resigned effective December 31, 1793.

---

## Retirement, Opposition Politics, and the Election of 1796

From Monticello, Jefferson remained politically active through Madison, James Monroe, and other Republican correspondents.

He opposed the Jay Treaty and believed the Washington administration increasingly favored monarchy and Britain. His private 1796 letter to Philip Mazzei referred to “an Anglican monarchical, and aristocratical party” in the United States. Publication in Europe and America damaged his relations with Washington.

In 1796 Jefferson narrowly lost the presidency to John Adams and, under the original constitutional system, became vice president.

---

## Vice Presidency, 1797–1801

Jefferson presided over the Senate and compiled *A Manual of Parliamentary Practice for the Use of the Senate of the United States*. He privately opposed the Adams administration.

### Kentucky Resolutions

In 1798 Jefferson secretly drafted the Kentucky Resolutions against the Alien and Sedition Acts. They argued that the federal government possessed delegated powers and that states could declare unauthorized federal acts void. Madison’s Virginia Resolutions used more restrained language.

Jefferson’s authorship remained secret during his lifetime. Later nullification and secession advocates appropriated the resolutions. Whether Jefferson meant unilateral state nullification with binding legal effect remains disputed; his original draft used stronger language than the version enacted by Kentucky.

### James Callender

Jefferson gave money to journalist **James Thomson Callender**, whose writings attacked Hamilton, Washington, and Adams. A surviving 1799 letter and payment record document the relationship.

- **Documented fact:** Jefferson subsidized Callender and praised or encouraged some of his anti-Federalist work.
- **Disputed interpretation:** Jefferson described the money as charitable assistance to a persecuted writer; opponents characterized it as covert payment for defamatory attacks.
- Callender was prosecuted under the Sedition Act and imprisoned.
- After Jefferson became president, Callender expected a postmastership and further support. Jefferson refused.
- Callender then attacked Jefferson and in September 1802 publicly alleged that Jefferson had fathered children with Sally Hemings.

Callender’s motives were retaliatory and partisan, but that does not by itself make the underlying Hemings claim false.

### Election of 1800

Jefferson and Aaron Burr each received 73 electoral votes. The tie sent the election to the House of Representatives, which selected Jefferson on the thirty-sixth ballot in February 1801. Hamilton opposed Burr and encouraged Federalists not to block Jefferson. The crisis contributed to adoption of the Twelfth Amendment.

## Presidency, First Term, 1801–1805

Jefferson took office on March 4, 1801, calling the peaceful party transfer evidence of republican government’s durability.

### Administrative program

His administration:

- Reduced internal taxes and military expenditure.
- Cut parts of the diplomatic and administrative establishment.
- Retained the Bank of the United States despite his earlier constitutional objection.
- Used customs revenue and Treasury Secretary Albert Gallatin’s fiscal program to reduce national debt before the Embargo and later war reversed the trend.
- Pardoned people convicted under the Sedition Act.
- Allowed the Sedition Act to expire.
- Repealed the Judiciary Act of 1801 while becoming embroiled in conflict with Federalist judges.

### Executive power and *Marbury v. Madison*

Jefferson withheld some last-minute judicial commissions signed under Adams. William Marbury sued Secretary of State James Madison. Chief Justice John Marshall’s 1803 decision denied Marbury’s commission on jurisdictional grounds while establishing a canonical statement of judicial review. Jefferson rejected broad judicial supremacy.

### Barbary War

Tripoli demanded tribute and declared war. Jefferson sent naval forces without an advance congressional declaration, then obtained legislative support. Operations included the destruction of the captured frigate *Philadelphia* and William Eaton’s overland expedition to Derna. A 1805 treaty ended the immediate conflict but involved ransom for American captives.

### Louisiana Purchase

Napoleon’s government offered the Louisiana territory after France’s plans for a revived American empire collapsed amid the Haitian Revolution, disease, and renewed war with Britain. American negotiators Robert Livingston and James Monroe agreed to purchase it for $15 million in 1803.

Jefferson privately worried that the Constitution did not expressly authorize territorial acquisition. He considered an amendment but ultimately accepted statutory and treaty mechanisms without one. The purchase approximately doubled the claimed area of the United States, though much of the land remained occupied and governed by Indigenous nations that had not consented to the transfer.

### Lewis and Clark

Jefferson secured funding for exploration before formal announcement of the purchase. Meriwether Lewis and William Clark’s Corps of Discovery traveled from 1804 to 1806, gathering geographic, diplomatic, linguistic, botanical, and zoological information and asserting American claims across Indigenous territories.

### Indigenous policy

Jefferson publicly spoke of peace, trade, agriculture, and incorporation. His private February 27, 1803 letter to William Henry Harrison set out a coercive land-acquisition strategy:

- Government trading houses would encourage influential Indigenous people to incur debt.
- Debt would make land cessions more likely.
- American settlement would surround Indigenous communities.
- Tribes would ultimately incorporate or move west of the Mississippi.
- Resistance could be answered by seizure of tribal country and forced removal.

This is **documented policy planning**, not a later allegation. Jefferson did not implement the mass-removal statute associated with Andrew Jackson, but his administration accelerated land cessions and articulated important precedents for removal.

### Haiti

Jefferson’s policy toward revolutionary Saint-Domingue/Haiti shifted with strategic circumstances. As secretary of state and president he feared slave revolt, French power, and the effects of Black independence on the American slave states. His administration withheld recognition from independent Haiti and supported commercial isolation, though trade continued intermittently. This aided slaveholding interests and France’s effort to isolate the new republic.

### End of the Atlantic slave trade

Jefferson’s 1806 annual message urged Congress to act at the first constitutionally permitted date. Congress prohibited the importation of enslaved people effective January 1, 1808. Domestic slavery and interstate sale remained legal and expanded.

## Presidency, Second Term, 1805–1809

### Aaron Burr and executive privilege

After killing Hamilton in 1804, former vice president Aaron Burr became involved in ambiguous western schemes. Federal authorities arrested him for treason.

Jefferson publicly treated Burr as guilty before trial. Chief Justice Marshall subpoenaed presidential documents and sought Jefferson’s personal attendance. Jefferson supplied selected materials but rejected an unlimited judicial power to compel a sitting president. Burr was acquitted in 1807 because the prosecution failed to prove the constitutional requirement of an overt act of treason.

- **Documented fact:** Burr was formally charged and tried.
- **Court finding:** Acquitted.
- **Controversy:** Jefferson’s prejudgment, control of prosecution evidence, and resistance to the subpoena are important early episodes in the history of executive privilege.

### Chesapeake–Leopard affair and Embargo

In June 1807 the British warship *Leopard* fired on the American frigate *Chesapeake*, killing three Americans and removing alleged deserters. Rather than request war, Jefferson pursued economic coercion.

The Embargo Act of December 1807 prohibited American overseas trade. Enforcement required extensive federal intervention, searches, seizures, and coastal patrols.

Exports fell from roughly $108 million to approximately $22 million. Smuggling proliferated; New England commerce suffered severely; agricultural exporters also lost markets. Congress replaced the embargo shortly before Jefferson left office.

- **Documented policy failure:** The embargo did not compel Britain or France to respect American neutrality.
- **Disputed interpretation:** It postponed war and preserved ships, but imposed high domestic costs and contradicted Jefferson’s limited-government rhetoric.

### Slave-trade controversy in Indiana Territory

William Henry Harrison and territorial petitioners sought temporary suspension of the Northwest Ordinance’s slavery prohibition. Jefferson’s administration transmitted petitions but did not secure suspension. Characterizing Jefferson as either personally imposing or decisively preventing territorial slavery overstates the executive role.

## Plantation, Businesses, Technology, and Financial Interests

### Landholdings

Jefferson held or controlled properties including:

- Monticello and surrounding quarter farms in Albemarle County.
- Shadwell.
- Tufton and Lego.
- Elk Hill in Goochland County.
- Poplar Forest in Bedford County.
- Lands inherited or acquired elsewhere, including unproductive or disputed western holdings.

His plantations shifted from tobacco toward wheat and mixed agriculture. Flooding, exhausted soil, transport costs, poor markets, managerial problems, and debt undermined profitability.

### Enslaved labor

Jefferson enslaved more than 600 people over his lifetime through inheritance, birth, purchase, and marriage. The number present at any one time was lower. Enslaved people worked as farmers, household servants, cooks, seamstresses, blacksmiths, coopers, carpenters, joiners, wagoners, gardeners, weavers, nail-makers, and mill workers.

Jefferson’s farms operated through hierarchical management: overseers, enslaved foremen, quarter farms, labor gangs, production quotas, and written reports. His meticulous recordkeeping treated human beings as labor units, capital, collateral, inheritances, and family members under his legal ownership.

### Nailery and child labor

Beginning in the 1790s, Jefferson operated a nail-making shop staffed largely by enslaved boys. Production was measured and incentives were sometimes offered.

Overseer Gabriel Lilly’s whipping of boys for truancy is documented in correspondence. Jefferson objected to excessive severity at times but retained Lilly and continued a system in which physical coercion was available to managers.

In 1803 nailer Cary violently attacked Brown Colbert with a hammer. Jefferson directed that Cary be sold out of state as an example. This separated him from his community and exposed him to sale within the interstate slave system.

Claims that Jefferson personally whipped enslaved workers are **not established**. Claims that Monticello functioned without systematic violence are contradicted by overseer correspondence, punishments, sales, and the legal structure of slavery.

### Manumissions

Jefferson freed remarkably few of the people he enslaved.

- Robert Hemings obtained freedom through a purchase arrangement.
- James Hemings was freed in 1796 after training another cook.
- Jefferson’s will freed Burwell Colbert, John Hemings, Madison Hemings, and Eston Hemings and requested legislative permission for them to remain in Virginia.
- Beverly and Harriet Hemings were allowed to leave in 1822 without formal pursuit and passed into white society.
- Sally Hemings was not formally freed in Jefferson’s will; Martha Randolph apparently gave her “time,” an informal freedom that allowed her to live with her sons in Charlottesville.

Approximately 130 people from Jefferson’s estate were offered for sale after his death, separating some families.

### Mills and manufacturing

Jefferson invested heavily in flour mills, canals, blacksmithing, weaving, and plantation manufacturing. A catastrophic 1810 Rivanna flood destroyed mill facilities representing a claimed investment of about $30,000.

### Inventions and designs

Jefferson received no known patent. He opposed monopolies but supported limited patent protection and served on the early federal patent board.

Designs and technical adaptations associated with him include:

- Moldboard plow experiments using mathematical curves.
- Great Clock and calendar systems at Monticello.
- Revolving bookstands and chairs.
- Dumbwaiters, alcove beds, folding doors, and household mechanisms.
- Improved or adapted copying devices.
- Cipher systems, including the wheel cipher later associated with the “Jefferson disk.”
- Architectural drawings, surveying instruments, and meteorological systems.

He did not invent the polygraph copying machine. John Isaac Hawkins invented it and Charles Willson Peale manufactured it; Jefferson promoted it and proposed improvements.

### Architecture

Jefferson was a self-trained architect influenced by Palladio, classical ruins, French neoclassicism, and British country-house design. Principal projects included:

- Monticello.
- The Virginia State Capitol, modeled partly on the Maison Carrée at Nîmes.
- The University of Virginia’s Academical Village and Rotunda.
- Poplar Forest.
- Plans or advice for private houses, courthouses, the national capital, and public buildings.

Enslaved and free artisans, including John Hemings, James Dinsmore, John Neilson, and numerous unnamed workers, translated his drawings into physical structures.

### Library

Jefferson assembled one of North America’s largest private libraries. After British forces burned the congressional library in 1814, he sold Congress 6,487 volumes for $23,950 in 1815. This became the nucleus of the rebuilt Library of Congress. He immediately resumed collecting books.

### Debt

Jefferson died owing more than $107,000.

Contributing factors included:

- Wayles inheritance debts.
- Dependence on volatile agricultural markets.
- Lifestyle and imported luxury goods.
- Continuous building.
- Spending beyond diplomatic and presidential salaries.
- Poor plantation returns.
- The 1810 flood.
- War of 1812 trade disruption.
- Falling land and commodity values after the Panic of 1819.
- A $20,000 loan Jefferson guaranteed for Wilson Cary Nicholas, who died insolvent.
- Inability or unwillingness to sell land and household assets early enough to stabilize the estate.

Attempts to obtain legislative permission for a lottery provided little relief. After his death, furniture, land, livestock, and enslaved people were sold. The final principal on some associated obligations was not cleared until decades later.

## Intellectual, Religious, and Ideological Development

### Republican political thought

Jefferson’s central ideas included:

- Natural rights and popular sovereignty.
- Periodic political renewal.
- Religious liberty.
- Broad access to education.
- Suspicion of standing armies, funded debt, monopolies, and concentrated finance.
- Preference for an agrarian republic of independent landholders.
- Federalism and narrow construction of enumerated powers.
- Support for territorial expansion.

His practice repeatedly departed from his theory: the Louisiana Purchase stretched constitutional construction; embargo enforcement enlarged federal power; territorial growth depended on Indigenous dispossession; and plantation independence rested on enslaved labor.

### Religion

Jefferson was baptized and raised Anglican and remained legally connected to the Episcopal tradition. His mature theology rejected the Trinity, original sin, miracles, Christ’s divinity, and bodily resurrection. He admired Jesus as a moral teacher.

He prepared two scriptural compilations:

- *The Philosophy of Jesus of Nazareth* (1804), now lost except through documentation and later reconstruction.
- *The Life and Morals of Jesus of Nazareth* (c. 1819–1820), made by cutting passages from Greek, Latin, French, and English New Testaments and removing miracles and supernatural claims.

Opponents called him atheist or infidel during the elections of 1796 and 1800. Those descriptions were **partisan accusations**, not accurate statements of his professed belief. “Deist” captures elements of his thought but not all; he described himself in different contexts as a Christian, Epicurean, Unitarian, and believer in providence.

### Race and slavery

In *Notes on the State of Virginia*, Jefferson condemned slavery’s corrupting effects and expressed fear of divine judgment. He also speculated that Black people were inferior in intellect and aesthetic capacity, while acknowledging inadequate evidence and allowing that the conclusion might be wrong.

He favored schemes combining emancipation with expatriation or colonization, arguing that racial prejudice and memories of injury made coexistence dangerous. He did not adopt an effective emancipation program for his own plantations.

He opposed some expansions of slavery, supported ending foreign importation, and occasionally aided individual manumissions. He simultaneously bought, sold, mortgaged, punished, and profited from enslaved people throughout his adult life.

### Sex and gender

Jefferson supported women’s elementary education principally for domestic and maternal roles, not equal political participation. His correspondence with daughters and granddaughters combined affection, intellectual instruction, discipline, and conventional expectations of female dependency.

## Retirement and University of Virginia, 1809–1826

Jefferson returned permanently to Monticello in March 1809, while regularly visiting Poplar Forest. His household included Martha Randolph, grandchildren, visiting political figures, workers, and a large enslaved community.

### Political elder

He advised presidents James Madison and James Monroe, supported the War of 1812 after diplomacy failed, and continued writing about constitutional law, education, religion, agriculture, and international affairs.

### Reconciliation with John Adams

At Benjamin Rush’s urging, Jefferson and John Adams resumed correspondence in 1812 after years of political estrangement. Their letters discussed revolution, religion, government, aging, books, family, and historical reputation. Both died on July 4, 1826, the Declaration’s fiftieth anniversary.

### University of Virginia

Jefferson promoted a secular public university as the culmination of his educational program. The Virginia legislature chartered the University of Virginia in 1819.

He:

- Selected the site near Charlottesville.
- Designed the Academical Village, pavilions, student rooms, lawns, and Rotunda.
- Helped recruit professors.
- Designed curricula and library classifications.
- Served as rector.
- Sought independence from denominational control.

The university opened in 1825. Construction depended substantially on enslaved laborers hired from owners, enslaved people owned by contractors and the university, and free artisans. Early student disorder and violence disappointed Jefferson.

### Missouri crisis

During the 1819–1820 debate over slavery in Missouri, Jefferson called the controversy a “fire bell in the night.” He feared sectional dissolution. He criticized federal restriction while continuing to describe slavery as an injustice. His response prioritized constitutional and sectional concerns rather than an immediate antislavery program.

### Lafayette’s visit

The Marquis de Lafayette visited Monticello in November 1824 during his celebrated American tour. Jefferson, Madison, and Lafayette reunited, and Lafayette visited the University of Virginia.

## Health, Illness, and Death

Jefferson was generally physically active and rode horseback into old age. Documented health problems included:

- Recurrent headaches in youth and middle age.
- Dysentery or chronic bowel disorders.
- A serious right-wrist injury in Paris in 1786, possibly from a fall while trying to impress Maria Cosway, though the romantic detail is partly retrospective.
- Rheumatism and painful joints.
- A fractured left wrist.
- Increasing deafness.
- Urinary obstruction, probably involving an enlarged prostate or possible cancer.
- Leg swelling and fatigue.
- A severe infection or boil after visiting Warm Springs in 1818.
- Chronic diarrhea and probable kidney or urinary infection during his final illness.

No reliable evidence establishes alcoholism or drug addiction. He consumed and collected wine extensively but is not documented as habitually intoxicated. Retrospective psychiatric diagnoses are speculative.

By June 1826 he was bedridden. Physician Robley Dunglison treated him with the limited therapies then available. He became largely stuporous after July 2.

The exact cause of death remains uncertain. The strongest medical reconstruction combines dehydration and exhaustion from severe diarrhea, urinary infection and kidney dysfunction, uremia or toxemia, and terminal pneumonia; prostate disease or cancer may have contributed.

He died on July 4, 1826, aged eighty-three.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### George Washington

Washington appointed Jefferson secretary of state. Their early relationship involved mutual respect, but conflict over Hamiltonian policies, Jay’s Treaty, partisan organization, and the Mazzei letter produced a lasting rupture.

### Alexander Hamilton

Colleague, ideological rival, and architect of the opposing Federalist political economy. Their conflict structured the first American party system. Hamilton nevertheless opposed Aaron Burr during the 1800 deadlock, indirectly assisting Jefferson’s election.

### James Madison

Jefferson’s closest political collaborator. Their partnership encompassed religious freedom, constitutional debate, party organization, opposition to the Alien and Sedition Acts, foreign policy, education, and retirement correspondence.

### John Adams and Abigail Adams

John Adams was revolutionary collaborator, diplomatic colleague, electoral rival, and late-life correspondent. Abigail initially maintained a warm relationship with Jefferson, then denounced his political conduct and perceived betrayal before participating indirectly in the later reconciliation.

### Benjamin Franklin

Diplomatic colleague in France and fellow member of the Declaration drafting committee. Jefferson succeeded Franklin as minister to France and cultivated Franklin’s scientific and republican image.

### Marquis de Lafayette

Military ally, friend, and French reformer. Jefferson advised Lafayette during the opening phase of the French Revolution and reviewed constitutional and rights proposals.

### George Wythe and William Small

Wythe was Jefferson’s legal mentor and lifelong friend. Small was his principal scientific and intellectual teacher.

### James Monroe

Political associate, neighbor, diplomat, and successor in the presidency. Monroe negotiated the Louisiana Purchase with Livingston and consulted Jefferson throughout his career.

### John Marshall

Distant cousin and political-constitutional adversary. Marshall’s jurisprudence strengthened the federal judiciary in ways Jefferson regarded as dangerous.

### Aaron Burr

Running mate, vice president, rival in the 1800 electoral tie, and defendant in the treason prosecution Jefferson energetically supported.

### Meriwether Lewis

Jefferson’s former secretary, neighbor, and chosen leader of the western expedition. Jefferson later wrote a biographical memoir after Lewis’s death.

### Alexander von Humboldt

The Prussian naturalist visited Washington and Monticello in 1804 and shared geographic and scientific information from Spanish America. They continued corresponding.

### Enslaved and free artisans

James Hemings, John Hemings, Robert Hemings, Burwell Colbert, Isaac Granger Jefferson, Wormley Hughes, and many others were essential to Jefferson’s domestic, agricultural, architectural, and political life. The archive records them unevenly, usually through his ownership and accounting system.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Abuses

### Enslavement and family separation

**Documented fact:** Jefferson owned, bought, sold, mortgaged, hired out, disciplined, and transferred human beings. Sales helped service debt. Estate sales after his death dispersed families.

### Sally Hemings

**Original allegation:** Publicly printed by James Callender in 1802, though rumors apparently circulated earlier.

**Current evidentiary judgment:** Strongly supported historical conclusion that Jefferson fathered her children. DNA supports Jefferson-line paternity of Eston but does not individually identify Thomas; documentary chronology, naming, treatment, and oral history supply the individualized case.

### Elizabeth Walker

Callender and others accused Jefferson of attempting to seduce **Elizabeth Moore Walker**, wife of his friend John Walker, while John was absent in 1768.

In a private 1805 letter Jefferson admitted that, while young and unmarried, he had “offered love to a handsome lady.” This is a **documented admission of an attempted advance**. The precise extent of the conduct beyond the offer remains uncertain. John Walker’s later account was more accusatory.

### Callender financing

**Documented fact:** Jefferson gave Callender money while Callender was attacking Federalists.  
**Unresolved characterization:** Charity, political patronage, covert press subsidy, or a mixture. There is no documentary proof that Jefferson commissioned every specific libel.

### Maria Cosway

**Documented fact:** Emotionally intimate correspondence.  
**Unverified claim:** A consummated extramarital affair. No conclusive evidence establishes one.

### Governor’s wartime conduct

**Documented fact:** Investigation following British invasions; Jefferson answered charges; legislature thanked him.  
**Disputed interpretation:** Administrative failure versus scapegoating under an institutionally weak governorship.

### Racial theories

**Documented fact:** Jefferson published claims of possible Black inferiority in *Notes on Virginia* and advocated colonization. Later selective quotations have sometimes detached these statements from his stated uncertainty or antislavery passages, but contextualization does not erase the published hierarchy.

### Indigenous dispossession

**Documented fact:** Jefferson privately endorsed debt-driven land cessions and contemplated removal west of the Mississippi. His administration concluded numerous treaties transferring land to the United States. Public language of benevolence coexisted with strategic pressure.

### Haiti

**Documented fact:** His administration refused recognition and participated in Haiti’s isolation.  
**Historical debate:** The relative weight of racism, slave-state security, French diplomacy, commerce, and geopolitical strategy.

### Burr prosecution

**Documented fact:** Jefferson announced Burr’s guilt before trial, resisted an unrestricted subpoena, and supported prosecution.  
**Court finding:** Burr acquitted.  
**Constitutional controversy:** Executive privilege and presidential interference with criminal adjudication.

### Embargo enforcement

**Documented fact:** Severe economic contraction, widespread smuggling, and coercive federal enforcement.  
**Contested assessment:** A failed experiment in peaceful coercion versus a temporary alternative to war.

### Louisiana’s constitutional status

Jefferson’s decision to use the treaty power despite doubts about constitutional authority is a **documented inconsistency**, not evidence of criminal conduct.

### No substantiated fraud or money-laundering case

No reliable evidence shows Jefferson was indicted or convicted of fraud, corruption, money laundering, or ordinary crime. His hidden political authorship, patronage of partisan journalism, plantation accounting, debt practices, and executive conduct generated ethical and constitutional controversies, but should not be relabeled as criminal convictions.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Jefferson had children who visibly resembled him running around Monticello”

The story appears in nineteenth-century recollections and family traditions. Some observers reportedly remarked on resemblance.

- **Evidence for:** Hemings descendants’ oral history; later reported resemblance; DNA and chronology supporting Jefferson-line paternity.
- **Evidence against literal versions:** No reliable record establishes that Benjamin Franklin or another named founder confronted Jefferson after seeing the children.
- **Status:** Core paternity conclusion strongly supported; colorful confrontation stories are **rumor or myth**.

### “The DNA test proved Thomas Jefferson’s paternity”

- **Story:** Genetics conclusively identified Thomas Jefferson.
- **Evidence:** The test matched Eston Hemings’s male-line descendants to the Jefferson Y chromosome and excluded the tested Carr line.
- **Limitation:** It could not distinguish Thomas from other Jefferson-line males.
- **Status:** **Misleading simplification**. The conclusion depends on DNA plus documentary, chronological, and oral evidence.

### “Randolph Jefferson was the father”

Advanced most prominently after the DNA findings by defenders of Jefferson.

- **Evidence offered:** Randolph shared the Y chromosome and sometimes visited Monticello.
- **Evidence against:** No direct evidence places him there at every relevant conception; surviving chronology repeatedly places Thomas there.
- **Status:** **Alternative theory lacking affirmative evidence sufficient to displace the prevailing conclusion**.

### “Jefferson fathered children with several enslaved women”

Stories extend paternity beyond Sally Hemings.

- **Evidence:** Jefferson-line traditions exist in more than one family, but documentation and genetic evidence are incomplete or inconsistent.
- **Status:** **Unverified claims** unless supported in an individual case. Sally Hemings’s children should not be used automatically to validate every additional attribution.

### “Jefferson freed all his slaves”

- **Origin and circulation:** Hagiographic schoolbook and commemorative traditions.
- **Evidence:** He freed or enabled the departure of only a small Hemings-family group. Most remained enslaved and many were sold.
- **Status:** **False myth**.

### “Jefferson never freed people because Virginia law made it impossible”

Virginia law complicated residence for emancipated people but did not make manumission impossible. Jefferson himself executed manumissions.

- **Status:** **Apologetic myth based on a real legal obstacle but contradicted by his own actions**.

### “Jefferson was an atheist”

Federalist clergy and newspapers circulated the charge in 1800.

- **Evidence:** He rejected orthodox Christian doctrines and edited miracles out of the Gospels.
- **Against:** He repeatedly professed belief in God, providence, moral accountability, and Jesus’s ethical teaching.
- **Status:** **Partisan propaganda and theological labeling**, not a precise description.

### “Jefferson wrote the Declaration alone”

Jefferson drafted the principal text; Adams and Franklin reviewed it; Congress debated and revised it.

- **Status:** **Heroic simplification**.

### “Jefferson inserted a secret code into the Declaration”

Numerological, Masonic, Illuminati, and cryptographic theories periodically claim hidden structures.

- **Evidence:** Jefferson used ciphers in diplomacy and admired scientific systems, but no credible manuscript evidence establishes a secret code in the Declaration.
- **Status:** **Conspiracy narrative**.

### “He invented the swivel chair, dumbwaiter, polygraph, macaroni machine, or French fries”

Jefferson used, adapted, popularized, commissioned, or documented versions of these objects and foods.

- The polygraph was invented by John Isaac Hawkins.
- Rotating chairs predated Jefferson, though he owned and modified one.
- Dumbwaiters and pasta-making machines predated him.
- Fried potatoes were already European.
- **Status:** **Inventor folklore created by conflating adoption and modification with invention**.

### “He brought ice cream, tomatoes, or macaroni to America”

These existed in North America before Jefferson. His recipes, household service, gardens, and elite social prominence helped popularize some foods.

- **Status:** **Culinary legend with a limited popularization basis**.

### “The Declaration’s equality clause was intended to include every person exactly as understood today”

Jefferson presented natural equality as a general political principle, but the revolutionary political community excluded enslaved people, most women, Indigenous nations, and many propertyless men.

- **Status:** The universal language is documentary fact; its intended legal and social scope remains a major **interpretive dispute**.

### July 4 deaths of Jefferson and Adams

Both died on the Declaration’s fiftieth anniversary. The coincidence immediately acquired providential and national symbolism.

Stories that Adams’s exact final words were “Thomas Jefferson survives” are based on family reports and vary in wording. Jefferson had died hours earlier, unbeknownst to Adams.

- **Status:** Simultaneous-date deaths are **documented fact**; exact last words are **reported fact with variant versions**.

## Works by Thomas Jefferson

Jefferson published one conventional book but produced an enormous documentary corpus. Dates below distinguish original composition or publication from later editorial collection.

### Books and separately published works

- *A Summary View of the Rights of British America* (1774). Published anonymously from instructions Jefferson prepared for Virginia delegates.
- *Notes on the State of Virginia* (written mainly 1781–1782; privately printed in Paris, 1785; authorized English edition, London, 1787). His only full-length authored book.
- *A Manual of Parliamentary Practice for the Use of the Senate of the United States* (1801). Jefferson called it primarily a compilation with his arrangement and explanatory observations.
- *The Philosophy of Jesus of Nazareth* (1804). Private compilation; original volume lost.
- *The Life and Morals of Jesus of Nazareth* (c. 1819–1820). Private multilingual Gospel compilation; first published by Congress in 1904, commonly called the “Jefferson Bible.”
- *Memoir, Correspondence, and Miscellanies from the Papers of Thomas Jefferson* (1829; edited posthumously by Thomas Jefferson Randolph). Not a lifetime publication and editorially selective.
- *Autobiography* or *Notes on Early Career* (written 1821; published posthumously). Fragmentary, ending with events of 1790.

### Major public documents and legislative texts

- *Declaration of the Causes and Necessity of Taking Up Arms* (1775 draft; revised with John Dickinson).
- Virginia Constitution drafts (1776).
- Declaration of Independence, original rough draft and adopted text (1776; principal drafter, extensively revised by committee and Congress).
- Bill for Establishing Religious Freedom (drafted 1777; introduced 1779; enacted with revisions in 1786).
- Bills for revising Virginia law (1776–1779), including inheritance, education, crime, naturalization, and religious measures.
- Proposed ordinance for western territories (1784).
- Report on a Money Unit (1784).
- Report on Weights and Measures (1790).
- Opinion on the Constitutionality of a National Bank (1791).
- Report on the Privileges and Restrictions on the Commerce of the United States (1793).
- Kentucky Resolutions draft (1798; secret authorship).
- First through eighth annual presidential messages (1801–1808).
- First and second inaugural addresses (1801, 1805).
- Numerous state papers, diplomatic memoranda, proclamations, messages, and executive opinions.

### Journals, notebooks, and technical writings

- Legal Commonplace Book.
- Literary Commonplace Book.
- Farm Book.
- Garden Book.
- Memorandum Books and financial accounts.
- Weather records.
- European travel journals, including the 1787 journey through France and Italy.
- Architectural drawings for Monticello, the Virginia Capitol, Poplar Forest, the University of Virginia, and other projects.
- Agricultural notes and moldboard-plow calculations.
- Indigenous-language vocabularies; much of the collection was reportedly lost or destroyed during transport.
- Library catalogs, especially the classified catalog behind the 1815 Library of Congress purchase.
- Cipher designs and diplomatic code materials.
- “Anas,” memoranda recording political conversations and recollections.

### Correspondence

Jefferson wrote approximately 19,000 known letters and received many thousands more. Major correspondences include those with John and Abigail Adams, James Madison, George Washington, James Monroe, Benjamin Franklin, Lafayette, Maria Cosway, Joseph Priestley, Benjamin Rush, William Short, John Taylor, Pierre Samuel du Pont de Nemours, Alexander von Humboldt, and members of the Jefferson–Randolph family.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Immediate commemoration

Jefferson’s death on July 4, 1826, made him part of a providential national story linking the Declaration, its fiftieth anniversary, and John Adams’s death the same day.

His heirs could not preserve Monticello. It passed through sales before Commodore Uriah P. Levy acquired and preserved it. The Levy family’s role was later minimized in some commemorative narratives. The Thomas Jefferson Memorial Foundation purchased Monticello in 1923.

### Democratic and sectional appropriation

Nineteenth-century Democrats claimed Jefferson as founder of their political tradition. Advocates of states’ rights and nullification used the Kentucky Resolutions. Antislavery writers invoked the Declaration against slavery while condemning Jefferson’s conduct.

Abraham Lincoln repeatedly used Jefferson’s equality language as an “abstract truth” applicable beyond its immediate eighteenth-century enforcement, while rejecting proslavery attempts to narrow it permanently.

### Memorial culture

Jefferson appears on:

- The two-dollar bill.
- The nickel.
- Mount Rushmore.
- The Jefferson Memorial in Washington.
- State and local memorials, universities, counties, towns, streets, and schools.

The Jefferson Memorial, dedicated in 1943, institutionalized him as a prophet of liberty during wartime democracy.

### Hemings reinterpretation

For much of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, leading biographers dismissed Hemings-family testimony while accepting exculpatory Jefferson-family traditions. Fawn Brodie reopened the question; Annette Gordon-Reed exposed evidentiary inconsistencies; the 1998 DNA study and 2000 Monticello report shifted the scholarly consensus.

Monticello subsequently incorporated slavery, the Hemings family, Mulberry Row, archaeological evidence, and descendant testimony more fully into interpretation.

### Twenty-first-century conflict

Jefferson remains central to disputes over:

- Whether universal principles can be separated from their author’s exclusions.
- The terminology applied to Jefferson and Hemings.
- The balance between presidential, intellectual, architectural, and plantation history.
- Removal or contextualization of statues and institutional names.
- Descendant inclusion at Monticello.
- Whether critical interpretation constitutes historical correction or political denigration.

In 2024–2026, public controversy intensified around Monticello and national semiquincentennial interpretation. Critics alleged that slavery-centered interpretation displaced Jefferson’s achievements; former staff and defenders of the institution described political pressure and hostile media campaigns. Annette Gordon-Reed resigned from the Thomas Jefferson Foundation board in January 2024. These are **documented contemporary disputes over interpretation**, not discoveries changing the underlying eighteenth-century evidence.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted record are included. Mere contemporaneity has been excluded.

- **Adam Smith — documented intellectual influence.** Jefferson read Smith and later treated *The Wealth of Nations* as an important work of political economy. There is no evidence that they met.

- **Alexander Hamilton — documented colleague and rival, 1790–1793, New York and Philadelphia.** They served together in Washington’s cabinet, negotiated around the assumption/capital compromise, and became the principal intellectual and partisan antagonists of the early federal government.

- **Alexander von Humboldt — documented meeting and correspondence.** Humboldt met President Jefferson in Washington in 1804, visited Monticello, shared information about Spanish America, and corresponded with him afterward.

- **Aristotle — documented intellectual influence.** In his 1825 explanation of the Declaration’s sources, Jefferson placed Aristotle among the writers whose ideas formed the era’s “harmonising sentiments.” No personal connection was possible.

- **Benjamin Franklin — documented collaborator, Philadelphia and Paris.** Fellow Declaration committee member in 1776; diplomatic colleague in Europe; Jefferson succeeded him as minister to France in 1785.

- **Francis Bacon — documented acknowledged influence.** Jefferson named Bacon with Locke and Newton as one of the three greatest figures in physical and moral science.

- **George Washington — documented military-political associate and later estranged colleague.** Shared revolutionary leadership; Washington appointed Jefferson secretary of state in 1789. Relations deteriorated during the 1790s over party conflict and foreign policy.

- **Isaac Newton — documented acknowledged influence.** Jefferson named Newton among his three greatest intellectual heroes and owned visual representations of him.

- **John Locke — documented acknowledged influence.** Jefferson repeatedly identified Locke as a major authority. Locke’s natural-rights, toleration, and government-by-consent arguments formed part of the intellectual background of the Declaration and Jefferson’s religious-liberty thought.

- **Louis XVI — documented diplomatic and ceremonial connection, France, 1785–1789.** Jefferson served as accredited American minister at the French court. A passport for Jefferson’s departure in 1789 was issued in the king’s name and signed by Louis XVI. This was an official relationship, not a close friendship.

- **Marie-Joseph Paul Yves Roch Gilbert du Motier (Marquis de Lafayette) — documented friendship and political collaboration.** They knew each other from the American Revolution, worked within the Paris reform network in 1789, corresponded for decades, and reunited at Monticello in November 1824.

- **Napoleon Bonaparte — documented indirect political relationship, not a meeting.** Napoleon’s government sold Louisiana to the Jefferson administration in 1803 through diplomatic intermediaries. Jefferson’s policy toward France was profoundly shaped by Napoleon’s ambitions, but no documented face-to-face meeting occurred.

- **Abraham Lincoln — documented posthumous influence.** Lincoln repeatedly invoked Jefferson’s Declaration language, especially natural equality, as a standard extending beyond the founders’ immediate practice. Jefferson died before Lincoln’s political career.

No reliable connection was established in the consulted sources with the many other roster members. Shared nationality, institutional commemoration, intellectual resemblance, or occupation alone was not treated as a connection.

## Compact Chronology

- **1743, April 13:** Born at Shadwell, Virginia.
- **1752:** Begins classical schooling under William Douglas.
- **1757:** Father Peter Jefferson dies; Thomas inherits land and enslaved people.
- **1758–1760:** Studies with James Maury.
- **1760–1762:** Attends William & Mary; studies under William Small.
- **1762–1767:** Reads law with George Wythe.
- **1767:** Admitted to the Virginia bar.
- **1768:** Elected to House of Burgesses; begins Monticello project.
- **1770:** Shadwell house burns.
- **1772, January 1:** Marries Martha Wayles Skelton.
- **1773:** John Wayles dies; Jefferson family inherits land, debt, and more than 100 enslaved people.
- **1774:** Publishes *A Summary View of the Rights of British America*.
- **1775:** Joins Second Continental Congress.
- **1776, June–July:** Drafts Declaration of Independence.
- **1776–1779:** Revises Virginia laws with Wythe and Pendleton.
- **1779–1781:** Governor of Virginia.
- **1781:** British invasion; legislative controversy over his governorship; begins *Notes on Virginia*.
- **1782, September 6:** Martha Jefferson dies.
- **1783–1784:** Serves in Confederation Congress.
- **1784:** Sails to Europe.
- **1785:** Succeeds Franklin as minister to France; privately prints *Notes on Virginia*.
- **1786:** Tours England with John Adams; intimate correspondence with Maria Cosway.
- **1787:** Tours southern France and northern Italy; Sally Hemings arrives in Paris.
- **1789:** Observes opening of French Revolution; returns to United States.
- **1790:** Becomes first secretary of state.
- **1790–1793:** Cabinet struggle with Hamilton; formation of Republican opposition.
- **1793, December 31:** Resigns.
- **1796:** Loses presidential election to Adams; elected vice president.
- **1797–1801:** Vice president and president of the Senate.
- **1798:** Secretly drafts Kentucky Resolutions.
- **1800–1801:** Electoral tie with Burr; House elects Jefferson on thirty-sixth ballot.
- **1801, March 4:** Inaugurated president.
- **1802:** Callender publicly prints Hemings allegation.
- **1803:** Louisiana Purchase; *Marbury v. Madison*; private Harrison letter describes debt-and-cession Indigenous policy.
- **1804:** Lewis and Clark expedition begins; Humboldt visits; first Gospel compilation.
- **1805:** Second inauguration; admits attempted advance toward Elizabeth Walker.
- **1806:** Urges end of international slave importation; Burr conspiracy unfolds.
- **1807:** Burr acquitted; Chesapeake–Leopard affair; Embargo enacted.
- **1808:** Importation of enslaved people prohibited; embargo crisis.
- **1809:** Leaves presidency and returns to Monticello.
- **1810:** Rivanna flood destroys major mill investment.
- **1812:** Resumes correspondence with John Adams.
- **1815:** Sells 6,487-volume library to Congress.
- **1817:** Guarantees Wilson Cary Nicholas’s $20,000 loan.
- **1818:** Severe illness after Warm Springs visit.
- **1819:** University of Virginia chartered; financial panic worsens Jefferson’s debts.
- **1819–1820:** Missouri crisis; prepares *Life and Morals of Jesus of Nazareth*.
- **1821:** Writes fragmentary autobiography.
- **1822:** Beverly and Harriet Hemings leave Monticello.
- **1824:** Lafayette visits.
- **1825:** University of Virginia opens.
- **1826, March:** Executes will freeing selected Hemings-family men.
- **1826, July 4:** Dies at Monticello.
- **1827:** Major estate auction includes approximately 130 enslaved people.
- **1829:** Grandson publishes first major collection of Jefferson’s papers.
- **1943:** Jefferson Memorial dedicated.
- **1998:** Y-chromosome study links Eston Hemings’s descendants to the Jefferson male line.
- **2000:** Monticello research committee concludes that the best evidence strongly supports Jefferson’s paternity.
- **2008:** Annette Gordon-Reed’s *The Hemingses of Monticello* substantially reshapes family scholarship.
- **2024–2026:** Renewed institutional and political disputes over interpreting slavery and Jefferson’s public legacy.
