## Works by George Orwell

### Novels and book-length fiction

- **1934:** *Burmese Days*. A novel of British imperial society, first published in the United States on 25 October 1934 and in Britain on 24 June 1935; the different publication dates belong to one obra, not two works. [CW 2; Fenwick]
- **1935:** *A Clergyman’s Daughter*. A novel published on 11 March 1935; Orwell later resisted its republication without extensive alteration, so its status within his own preferred canon was uneasy. [CW 3; Fenwick]
- **1936:** *Keep the Aspidistra Flying*. A novel published on 20 April 1936; Orwell likewise later objected to reprinting it unrevised. [CW 4; Fenwick]
- **1939:** *Coming Up for Air*. A novel published on 12 June 1939. [CW 7; Fenwick]
- **1943–1945:** *Animal Farm: A Fairy Story*. A satirical animal fable completed during the war and published on 17 August 1945 after several publishers rejected or delayed it; “A Fairy Story” is part of Orwell’s subtitle. [CW 8; Fenwick]
- **1946–1949:** *Nineteen Eighty-Four*. A dystopian novel published on 8 June 1949. Unlike Orwell’s other novels, part of its working manuscript survives, making the manuscript and published book distinguishable manifestations of the same obra. [CW 9; Davison, *Facsimile*]

### Documentary and autobiographical books

- **1933:** *Down and Out in Paris and London*. A documentary-autobiographical book published on 9 January 1933, assembled from lived experience, reportage, and material first drafted under other titles; it resists a clean division between memoir and constructed documentary narrative. [CW 1; Fenwick]
- **1937:** *The Road to Wigan Pier*. A two-part documentary and political book, issued first to the Left Book Club in February 1937 and to the general public on 8 March. Its reportage and autobiographical-socialist argument form one deliberately divided obra. [CW 5; Fenwick]
- **1938:** *Homage to Catalonia*. A documentary memoir and political testimony concerning the Spanish Civil War, published on 25 April 1938; later editions restored passages displaced in the first edition, so no single early manifestation completely represents Orwell’s intended arrangement. [CW 6; Fenwick]

### Essay books, pamphlets, and manifestos

- **1940:** *Inside the Whale and Other Essays*. Orwell’s first author-arranged essay collection, containing “Inside the Whale,” “Charles Dickens,” and “Boys’ Weeklies.” It is a made book rather than merely a later editorial gathering. [CW 12; Orwell Society]
- **1941:** *The Lion and the Unicorn: Socialism and the English Genius*. A political essay issued as the first Searchlight Book on 19 February 1941. Its programme for a specifically English socialist revolution makes it function partly as manifesto rather than detached description. [CW 12; Fenwick]
- **1941:** *Betrayal of the Left*. A collaborative political pamphlet edited by Victor Gollancz, containing Orwell’s contribution alongside writings by Gollancz, John Strachey, and others; it is therefore a shared composite object, not a wholly Orwell-authored book. [CW 12; Davison bibliography]
- **1942:** *Victory or Vested Interest?* A Labour Book Service symposium containing Orwell’s “Culture and Democracy,” itself assembled from “Fascism and Democracy” and “Patriots and Revolutionaries.” The pamphlet is collaborative; only the specified contribution is Orwell’s obra. [CW 13; Davison bibliography]
- **1944/1947:** *The English People*. A book-length national portrait commissioned for the “Britain in Pictures” series and written principally in 1944, but delayed by wartime paper restrictions until 1947. The writing date and the date of its first physical manifestation therefore differ. [CW 16; Orwell Society]
- **1945–1946:** *James Burnham and the Managerial Revolution*. A political pamphlet published in July 1946; it is a separate publication of Orwell’s revised essay “Second Thoughts on James Burnham,” not an independently composed second text. [CW 18; Davison bibliography]
- **1946:** *Critical Essays*. An author-arranged collection published on 14 February 1946. Its American manifestation, *Dickens, Dali and Others: Studies in Popular Culture*, appeared under another title and with a different publishing history, but is not a new Orwell obra. [CW 18; Orwell Society]
- **1946:** *British Cookery*. An essay with recipes commissioned by the British Council. The Council declined to publish it at the time, so it survives as a completed protocol-like manuscript whose intended public manifestation did not occur during Orwell’s life. [CW 18; British Council]
- **1950:** *Shooting an Elephant and Other Essays*. A collection issued on 5 October 1950, after Orwell’s death. Because the final selection and publication were handled posthumously, it should not unreservedly be treated as an author-made collection even though its component texts are his. [Davison bibliography]

### Edited and composite works

- **1917–1921:** *The Election Times*. A sequence of five handwritten school-newspaper issues made collectively by Orwell and other Eton pupils; Orwell acted as business manager. Only issue 4 survives intact, contributions were normally unsigned, and Davison and Gillian Fenwick disagree over several attributions, so the object resists division into a secure list of individually authored texts. [CW 10, item 29; Fenwick]
- **1917–1921:** *College Days / The Colleger*. A school periodical that Orwell helped edit and to which he contributed stories, verse, and nonfiction. Anonymous publication and collaborative copying make some authorship uncertain. [CW 10, item 37; Fenwick]
- **1941–1942:** *Searchlight Books*. A projected series of political books jointly edited by Orwell and T. R. Fyvel. It is best classified as an editorial project: several volumes appeared, but the larger intervention imagined by its editors was curtailed by wartime conditions. [CW 12–13; Fenwick]
- **1941–1943:** *Voice*. Six BBC poetry-programme booklets and their associated broadcasts, assembled with Mulk Raj Anand, William Empson, Una Marson, Stephen Spender, and other contributors. This is a multimedia editorial-broadcast project rather than an Orwell-authored text; the fifth booklet has not been recovered. [CW 13–15; West, *War Broadcasts*]
- **1941–1943:** BBC Eastern Service Indian Section programming. Orwell produced talks, news commentaries, dramatic adaptations, literary programmes, introductions, and printed programme material for this service. The result is a collaborative broadcast œuvre—part editorial project, part performance, part institutional propaganda—and some scripts were written by others or merely presented by Orwell. [CW 13–15; West, *War Broadcasts*; West, *War Commentaries*]
- **1943:** *Talking to India: A Selection of English Language Broadcasts to India*. An anthology edited and introduced by Orwell from broadcasts by E. M. Forster, Ritchie Calder, Cedric Dover, Hsiao Ch’ien, and others; the selection and framing are Orwell’s, while the constituent talks are collaborative. [CW 15; Fenwick]
- **1948:** *British Pamphleteers, Volume I: From the Sixteenth Century to the French Revolution*. A historical anthology co-edited with Reginald Reynolds and introduced by Orwell. It was intended as the first part of a larger editorial project; Orwell’s death left his projected modern volume unrealized in its planned form. [CW 19–20; Orwell Society]

### Drama and radio adaptations

- **1918:** *Free Will*. A one-act dramatic sketch published unsigned in *The Election Times* and reprinted unsigned in *College Days* in 1920. Fenwick accepts Orwell’s authorship, while the circumstances of anonymous collaborative production prevent absolute attribution. [CW 10; Fenwick]
- **1916–1918:** Untitled incomplete play. A surviving 26-folio dramatic manuscript from Orwell’s school years; because it is incomplete and has no secure title, it is better recorded as a project than supplied with an invented work-name. [CW 10]
- **1943:** *Crainquebille*. Orwell’s radio adaptation of Anatole France’s work, broadcast on 11 August. It is a transformed broadcast script, not an original Orwell plot. [CW 15; West, *War Broadcasts*]
- **1943:** *The Fox*. Orwell’s radio adaptation of Ignazio Silone’s story, broadcast on 9 September. Its authorship is necessarily divided between Silone’s narrative and Orwell’s adaptation. [CW 15; West, *War Broadcasts*]
- **1943:** *A Slip Under the Microscope*. Orwell’s radio adaptation of H. G. Wells’s story, broadcast on 9 September. It is a performable radio text and not another Orwell short story. [CW 15; West, *War Broadcasts*]
- **1943:** *The Emperor’s New Clothes*. Orwell’s radio adaptation of Hans Christian Andersen’s tale, broadcast on 18 November. It is an adapted script whose manifestation was a collaborative BBC performance. [CW 15; West, *War Broadcasts*]

### Poetry

- **1914:** *Awake! Young Men of England*. A patriotic poem and Orwell’s earliest known print publication. [CW 10; Venables]
- **1916:** *Kitchener*. A commemorative poem published under Orwell’s birth name, Eric Blair. [CW 10; Venables]
- **1918:** *Our Minds Are Married, but We Are Too Young*. A youthful poem. [CW 10; Venables]
- **1918:** *The Pagan*. A youthful poem. [CW 10; Venables]
- **1918–1919:** *Suggested by a Toothpaste Advertisement*. A comic or satirical school-period poem; its exact date is not fixed. [CW 10; Venables]
- **c. 1919:** *Then up Waddled Wog*. A surviving piece of juvenile verse; its historical title contains language now recognized as offensive. [CW 10; Venables]
- **1920:** *The Wounded Cricketer (Not Walt Whitman)*. A school poem published unsigned; its placement in *The Election Times* is disputed, although it appeared in *College Days*. [CW 10; Fenwick; Venables]
- **1921:** *Friendship and Love*. A poem addressed to Jacintha Buddicom and preserved outside Orwell’s lifetime publications. [CW 10; Venables]
- **1922–1927:** Untitled poem from Burma. A poem surviving without a fixed authorial title; “Poem from Burma” is a descriptive catalogue label, not necessarily a title supplied by Orwell. [CW 10; Venables]
- **1924:** *The Lesser Evil*. A poem. [CW 10; Venables]
- **1925:** *Romance*. A poem. [CW 10; Venables]
- **1929:** *Ballade*. A poem from Orwell’s Paris period. [CW 10; Venables]
- **1933:** *A Dressed Man and a Naked Man*. A poem published under the name Eric Blair in *The Adelphi*. [CW 10; Venables]
- **1933:** *Sometimes in the Middle Autumn Days*. A poem. [CW 10; Venables]
- **1933:** *Summer-like for an Instant*. A poem. [CW 10; Venables]
- **1934:** *On a Ruined Farm near the His Master’s Voice Gramophone Factory*. A poem published in *The Adelphi*; the grammatically awkward commercial name belongs to the historical title and should not be silently corrected. [CW 10; Venables]
- **1935:** *A Happy Vicar I Might Have Been*. A poem. [CW 10; Venables]
- **1935:** *A Little Poem*. A poem. [CW 10; Venables]
- **before 1936:** Untitled ironic poem about prostitution. The descriptive phrase commonly used for it is not a secure authorial title, and its date is only bounded rather than known. [CW 10; Venables]
- **1943:** *As One Non-Combatant to Another*. A wartime poem. [CW 15; Venables]

### Short fiction and unfinished fictional projects

- **1918:** *The Adventure of the Lost Meat-card*. A juvenile short story attributed with reasonable confidence to Orwell despite unsigned publication in the collaboratively produced *Election Times*. [CW 10, item 29]
- **1918:** *A Peep into the Future*. A juvenile short story attributed with reasonable confidence to Orwell. [CW 10, item 29]
- **1918:** *The Slack-bob*. A juvenile short story reprinted in *College Days*; its earlier placement in *The Election Times* is less secure. [CW 10, item 29]
- **1919:** *The White Man’s Burden*. A short story published unsigned in *College Days* and judged probably, rather than certainly, to be Orwell’s. [CW 10; Fenwick]
- **1920:** *The Cricket Enthusiast*. A short story published unsigned in *College Days*. [CW 10; Fenwick]
- **c. 1928–1929:** Two untitled novels written in Paris. Orwell reported writing and subsequently destroying two novel manuscripts; neither survives, and no invented titles or exact dates should be assigned to these abandoned projects. [CW 10; Davison bibliography]
- **c. 1928–1929:** Unpublished Paris stories. Orwell wrote several stories during his Paris years and destroyed their manuscripts. Since their number, titles, and boundaries are not securely recoverable, this is evidence of creative activity rather than a safely enumerable set of works. [CW 10; Davison bibliography]
- **1949:** *A Smoking-Room Story*. An unfinished story preserved in a notebook. Its fragmentary state is constitutive: it is a fictional project, not a completed short story. [CW 20]

### Journalism, essays, reviews, and recurring prose forms

- **1928–1950:** Shorter prose corpus. Orwell made hundreds of separately titled essays, articles, reviews, polemics, reports, prefaces, obituaries, letters to editors, and occasional pieces. Peter Davison’s *Complete Works*, volumes 10–20, is the controlling scholarly enumeration; this corpus is treated here as an obra because reproducing its hundreds of item-level bibliographical records would confuse the made texts with their many later editions and reprints. [CW 10–20; Fenwick]
- **1929:** *Ayant toujours trait au Quartier Montparnasse* / articles concerning the Quartier Montparnasse. A reported series of French-language newspaper articles whose texts have not survived. The series’ existence is recorded, but its exact contents and titles cannot now be reconstructed. [CW 10; Davison bibliography]
- **1931:** *The Spike*. Literary reportage drawn from Orwell’s experience in casual wards and first published in *The Adelphi*; parts were subsequently remade within *Down and Out in Paris and London*. [CW 10]
- **1931:** *Hop-Picking*. Reportage based on seasonal labour, later reused in altered form in *A Clergyman’s Daughter*. It therefore exists both as a discrete article and as material absorbed into another obra. [CW 10]
- **1936:** *Shooting an Elephant*. A colonial memoir-essay first published in *New Writing*. Its retrospective narrator and shaped episode make it resist a simple classification as either factual report or short story. [CW 10]
- **1936:** *A Hanging*. A colonial memoir-essay first published under Orwell’s name in *The Adelphi*, developed from an earlier text signed Eric A. Blair; its precise relation to a single witnessed execution has been debated. [CW 10; Fenwick]
- **1940–1941:** Theatre-review column for *Time and Tide*. A recurring critical form rather than one book: Orwell reviewed theatrical productions from May 1940 to August 1941. [CW 12]
- **1941–1942:** “London Letter” series for *Partisan Review*. A sequence of wartime political-cultural reports addressed to an American readership. The repeated letter-form makes the sequence a serial documentary project as well as a set of individual essays. [CW 12–14]
- **1943–1947:** *As I Please*. A serial column written for *Tribune*, conventionally counted as eighty numbered installments. Each installment is a composite of observations rather than a chapter in a continuously planned book, but together they form a recognizable journalistic obra. [CW 15–19]
- **1945:** Continental war reporting. Reports from liberated France and Germany for *The Observer* and the *Manchester Evening News* form a field-reportage project rather than a book; their publication across two newspapers produced overlapping but nonidentical manifestations. [CW 17]
- **1946:** *Politics and the English Language*. An essay that describes linguistic corruption while also prescribing rules for resisting it; because its closing rules instruct future practice, part of the text functions as a protocol. [CW 18]
- **1946:** *Why I Write*. An autobiographical-critical essay published in *Gangrel*, framing Orwell’s body of writing as a political and aesthetic project. [CW 18]
- **1946:** *The Prevention of Literature*. A polemical essay developed from a PEN discussion; its defence of intellectual liberty makes it function partly as manifesto. [CW 18]
- **1947:** *Toward European Unity*. A political essay proposing a federation of democratic socialist states; it is programmatic enough to approach a manifesto, though presented as argument rather than organizational declaration. [CW 19]
- **1949:** “Notes on the Blue Pencil.” A memorandum on prospective writers and sympathizers prepared for Celia Kirwan at the Information Research Department. Often called “Orwell’s list,” it is neither a literary work nor a public denunciation: it resists work-status and is better classified as a private, politically consequential document whose purpose remains disputed. [CW 20; *The Lost Orwell*]

### Broadcast talks, commentaries, and performed texts

- **1941–1943:** BBC talks and literary broadcasts. Orwell wrote and delivered—or arranged for others to deliver—talks on literature, politics, wartime Britain, and culture. These survive unevenly as scripts, transcripts, programme notes, and printed adaptations, so “the broadcast” and “the text” are not always the same manifestation. [CW 13–15; West, *War Broadcasts*]
- **1941–1943:** BBC war commentaries. Orwell prepared a numbered sequence of news analyses for the Eastern Service, many read by Indian broadcasters and some by Orwell himself. They are institutional and collaborative propaganda texts, and the surviving written scripts cannot be equated without qualification with the performed broadcasts. [CW 13–15; West, *War Commentaries*]
- **1941:** *The Frontiers of Art and Propaganda*. A radio talk first broadcast by the BBC Overseas Service and subsequently printed in *The Listener*; broadcast and printed versions are manifestations of one argument, not separate works. [CW 12]
- **1941:** *The Meaning of a Poem*. A radio talk subsequently printed in *The Listener*. Its first material form was performed speech. [CW 12]
- **1943:** *English Poetry since 1900*. A BBC broadcast on modern English poetry. It is a scripted critical performance rather than a conventional print essay in origin. [CW 15]

### Diaries, notebooks, letters, and private documentary objects

- **1931–1932:** Hop-picking and tramping notebooks. Working records of poverty, labour, lodging houses, and the road, later transformed into published prose. They are source-objects and compositional tools, not merely preliminary editions of *Down and Out in Paris and London*. [CW 10]
- **1936:** Wigan Pier diary. A field diary kept during Orwell’s journey through industrial northern England and used in making *The Road to Wigan Pier*. Published selections do not necessarily reproduce every feature of the original documentary object. [CW 10]
- **1938–1939:** Domestic diary. A record of daily life, animals, gardening, and household economy at Wallington and in Morocco; it is both private documentation and a sustained observational text. [CW 11]
- **1939–1942:** Wartime diaries. Several diary sequences record the approach and early years of the Second World War. Editorial labels such as “War-time Diary A,” “B,” and “C” identify surviving or selected sequences rather than titles necessarily supplied by Orwell. [CW 12–14]
- **1946–1949:** Jura diary and notebooks. Records of life and work on Jura, including domestic observations and material associated with the making of *Nineteen Eighty-Four*. These are working and private documents, not a single finished literary work. [CW 18–20]
- **c. 1911–1950:** Correspondence. Orwell’s surviving letters constitute a dispersed epistolary corpus involving family, friends, publishers, editors, political organizations, and readers. It is an obra only in the broad sense of a documentary body of writing; no complete author-made “book of letters” existed, and survival is accidental and incomplete. [CW 10–20; *The Lost Orwell*]

### Surviving and lost material evidence

- **1946–1948:** Extant manuscript of *Nineteen Eighty-Four*. A partial working manuscript with revisions, deletions, and alternative formulations, later reproduced in facsimile. It is a material stage in the novel’s creation, not a separately authored book or a complete manuscript of the published text. [Davison, *Facsimile*; CW 9]
- **before 1950:** Destroyed manuscripts and discarded drafts. Orwell routinely destroyed drafts, including the two reported Paris novels and much preparatory fiction; this lost material cannot responsibly be converted into a titled list. Its resistance to catalogue status is itself evidence about his practice of making and unmaking texts. [CW 10–20; Davison bibliography]
