# Nabucodonosor II: Research Dossier

## Scope, identity, and evidentiary method

This dossier concerns **Nabucodonosor II**, better known in English as **Nebuchadnezzar II**, king of Babylon from 605 to 562 BCE. He is not to be confused with the earlier Babylonian king Nebuchadnezzar I, who reigned in the twelfth century BCE.

The reconstruction rests on four source classes that must be kept distinct:

1. **Contemporary Babylonian evidence:** chronicles, royal inscriptions, administrative tablets, ration lists, bricks, cylinders, and archaeological remains.
2. **Near-contemporary Judean literature:** especially 2 Kings, Jeremiah, and Ezekiel. These preserve historical information but also interpret events theologically.
3. **Later traditions:** Daniel, Berossus as quoted by Josephus, Josephus’s own works, rabbinic literature, and Greek and Roman descriptions of Babylon.
4. **Modern historical and archaeological interpretation.**

The surviving Babylonian Chronicle becomes fragmentary after Nebuchadnezzar’s eleventh regnal year. Consequently, the period after about 594 BCE is much less securely documented than his accession and early campaigns. Royal inscriptions are abundant but overwhelmingly commemorate religious building: they are not neutral biographies and say little about defeats, internal opposition, family conflict, or the destruction of foreign cities.

Dates use BCE throughout. Babylonian regnal years began in Nisannu, in the spring. Differences between accession-year and non-accession-year systems explain some apparent one-year disagreements among Babylonian and biblical sources.

His royal inscriptions routinely identify him as the “foremost heir” or eldest son of Nabopolassar. They present kingship as conferred by Marduk and portray the king’s central duties as maintaining temples, providing offerings, securing Babylon, administering justice, and protecting the population.

No authentic portrait has been identified with absolute certainty except the small royal figure on the so-called Tower of Babel stele, whose associated inscription identifies Nebuchadnezzar. Unlike Assyrian kings, he left no extensive narrative relief cycle of military conquest.

## Names, Titles, and Scribal Forms

The Akkadian royal name appears in several orthographic forms because the divine name Nabu could be written syllabically or with a logogram. Biblical Hebrew preserves two principal spellings, conventionally rendered **Nebuchadnezzar** and **Nebuchadrezzar**. Jeremiah frequently uses a form closer to the Akkadian consonantal structure.

“Nabucco” is not an ancient nickname. It is the Italian title and protagonist’s name in Giuseppe Verdi’s 1842 opera, derived through European biblical and theatrical tradition.

The numeral “II” is a modern historiographical designation distinguishing him from Nebuchadnezzar I. He would not have called himself Nebuchadnezzar “the Second.”

“Chaldean king” is conventional but potentially misleading. Nabopolassar’s exact ethnic and tribal origins remain disputed. Contemporary royal inscriptions emphasize Babylonian kingship and do not define Nebuchadnezzar by a clearly documented Chaldean genealogy.

## Family and Ancestry

### Father: Nabopolassar

**Documented fact:** Nebuchadnezzar was the eldest son and crown prince of Nabopolassar. Nabopolassar seized the Babylonian throne in 626 BCE, rebelled against Assyrian domination, allied with Cyaxares’s Medes, and participated in the destruction of the Assyrian imperial centers, including Nineveh in 612.

Nabopolassar’s ancestry is not securely known. Later classifications of the dynasty as “Chaldean” do not establish a precise lineage. Suggestions that the family came from the Chaldean tribal south, from Uruk, or from an Assyrian-connected official household remain reconstructions rather than demonstrated genealogy.

### Mother

No securely identified contemporary text names Nebuchadnezzar’s mother. Assertions about her identity should therefore be treated as speculation.

### Siblings

A man named **Nabu-shum-lishir** appears in chronicle material and is commonly reconstructed as a younger son of Nabopolassar and thus Nebuchadnezzar’s brother. A damaged entry associated with 602 BCE has prompted a theory that he rebelled or attempted to seize power. The text does not preserve enough information to prove this; the proposed fraternal revolt is an **historical hypothesis**, not an established event.

Other proposed siblings or half-siblings are inadequately documented.

### Wife or wives

The frequently repeated identification of Nebuchadnezzar’s queen as **Amytis of Media**, daughter of Astyages or sometimes daughter of Cyaxares, rests on later Greek-language tradition associated with Berossus and subsequent authors.

- **Later reported tradition:** A Median princess married Nebuchadnezzar to consolidate the Babylonian-Median alliance.
- **Legendary elaboration:** Nebuchadnezzar constructed the Hanging Gardens because Amytis missed the wooded mountains of her homeland.
- **Evidentiary problem:** No known contemporary Babylonian document securely names Amytis as his wife. Greek sources disagree about the princess’s parentage and sometimes attach similarly named women to Persian rulers.
- **Assessment:** A dynastic marriage with Media is historically plausible, but Amytis’s identity, genealogy, and association with the gardens are unverified.

Nebuchadnezzar may have had multiple wives or palace women, as was normal in ancient Near Eastern royal courts, but their identities and status are not recoverable.

### Children

Administrative documents and later succession evidence indicate several sons and daughters. Proposed children include:

- **Amel-Marduk** (*Amēl-Marduk*, earlier possibly Nabu-shuma-ukin): securely identified as Nebuchadnezzar’s son and successor.
- **Eanna-sharra-usur:** identified in administrative documentation as a royal son.
- **Marduk-shuma-usur:** identified as a royal son.
- **Marduk-nadin-ahi:** probably a royal son.
- **Mushezib-Marduk:** probably a royal son.
- **Marduk-nadin-shumi:** listed in modern prosopographical reconstructions as another son, though individual identifications require caution.
- **Kashshaya** (*Kaššaya*): a well-attested royal woman, generally regarded as Nebuchadnezzar’s daughter.
- **Innina-etirat:** proposed as a daughter in some reconstructions, less securely.
- An unnamed daughter is traditionally identified as the wife of **Neriglissar**, making the latter Nebuchadnezzar’s son-in-law and Labashi-Marduk potentially his grandson. Whether this woman was Kashshaya is disputed.

The precise number of children is unknown. Terms such as “son of the king” in administrative tablets are strong evidence but can be complicated by damaged texts, recurring names, and questions of whether all individuals belong to the same reign.

### Dynastic aftermath

Amel-Marduk succeeded in 562 but was killed in 560, reportedly through a conspiracy led by Neriglissar. Neriglissar ruled until 556 and was followed briefly by Labashi-Marduk, who was overthrown and killed. Nabonidus then took the throne.

The rapid collapse of the family’s direct control suggests severe factional conflict after Nebuchadnezzar’s death. The evidence does not permit a confident reconstruction of the factions during Nebuchadnezzar’s own lifetime.

## Childhood and Early Life

Nothing securely documents Nebuchadnezzar’s childhood, exact birthplace, childhood residences, tutors, or formal education.

As Nabopolassar’s eldest son, he was probably trained in:

- palace administration;
- military command;
- Babylonian cultic protocol;
- Akkadian scribal and royal literary traditions;
- management of relations with temples and urban elites;
- the ideological history of earlier Babylonian kingship.

That training is an inference from his position, not a surviving curriculum.

Royal inscriptions present him as wise, learned, pious, and attentive to divine commands. These are conventional royal attributes, though the scale and technical specificity of his building program indicate access to sophisticated scribal, engineering, surveying, hydraulic, and administrative institutions.

The suggestion that he was born about 642 BCE is a modern estimate derived from his holding senior command by 605. It is not an anciently attested date. He may have been younger or older.

## Education, Formation, and Formative Influences

No named teacher or mentor survives. The strongest reconstructable influences were institutional:

### Nabopolassar and the war against Assyria

Nebuchadnezzar matured while his father transformed Babylonia from an Assyrian dependency into the center of a new empire. The long war taught the dynasty the importance of:

- fortified capitals;
- control of the Euphrates corridor;
- alliances with Median power;
- management of formerly Assyrian territories;
- the political value of Babylonian religious restoration.

### Babylonian royal and scribal tradition

His inscriptions deliberately situate him among earlier restorers of temples. Foundation deposits were placed in buildings for future kings to find. Restoration required locating older foundations and preserving or invoking inscriptions of predecessors.

### Marduk and Nabu

Marduk, patron god of Babylon, dominates Nebuchadnezzar’s public ideology. Nabu, patron of writing and wisdom and chief god of Borsippa’s Ezida temple, was embedded in his personal name. His inscriptions repeatedly style him the provider for **Esagil** in Babylon and **Ezida** in Borsippa.

### Imperial inheritance from Assyria

Although Babylonian propaganda positioned the dynasty against Assyria, its imperial administration, mass deportations, military campaigning, tribute systems, and monumental urbanism continued many Assyrian practices. This is a structural influence rather than a documented personal admiration.

## Early Career as Crown Prince

### Possible participation in Nabopolassar’s campaigns

Nebuchadnezzar was crown prince by 605 BCE and probably had military and administrative responsibilities earlier. Exactly when he began commanding troops is unknown.

Later Berossian tradition, preserved by Josephus, says that Nabopolassar entrusted the young prince with forces against a rebel governor and Egyptian power in Syria. The contemporary Chronicle independently confirms that Nebuchadnezzar commanded the army in 605, but not every detail of Josephus’s later narrative.

### 607–606 BCE

Nabopolassar campaigned west of the Euphrates as the remaining Assyrian regime and its Egyptian allies struggled to retain Harran and the upper Euphrates. Nebuchadnezzar’s whereabouts are not explicitly preserved for every operation.

### Carchemish, 605 BCE

**Documented fact:** In Nabopolassar’s twenty-first year, the crown prince led the Babylonian army against Pharaoh Necho II’s Egyptian forces at **Carchemish**. The Babylonian Chronicle states that he crossed the Euphrates, defeated the Egyptian army, and pursued the survivors toward Hamath. Jeremiah 46:2 independently remembers the battle.

Carchemish eliminated Egypt’s principal army in northern Syria and transferred the strategic initiative in Syria-Palestine to Babylon.

While Nebuchadnezzar was campaigning, Nabopolassar died on 8 Abu. The Chronicle says the crown prince returned rapidly to Babylon and took the throne on 1 Ululu—usually correlated with August and September 605, although conversions vary slightly with calendrical tables.

## Chronological Life History

## 605 BCE: Accession and consolidation

After Carchemish and the pursuit to Hamath, Nebuchadnezzar received news of his father’s death. He hurried back to Babylon, leaving the army and captives to follow, and formally assumed kingship.

Later tradition dramatized this as an exceptionally rapid desert crossing. The Chronicle establishes haste but does not confirm every colorful detail in Josephus.

He then returned west. The Levantine territories formerly contested by Assyria and Egypt were compelled to recognize Babylonian overlordship and deliver tribute.

A biblical tradition in Daniel 1 associates an early Jerusalem deportation with the third year of Jehoiakim. This chronology is difficult to reconcile literally with the contemporary Chronicle and other biblical regnal systems. Some scholars interpret it as a different year-counting convention; others regard it as part of Daniel’s later literary setting. There is no contemporary Babylonian record securely proving a Jerusalem capture in 605.

## 604–603 BCE: Western campaign and destruction of Ashkelon

The Chronicle records another march through Hatti, the Babylonian term for Syria and the Levant. Local kings submitted and brought tribute.

In Kislev of his first full regnal year, Nebuchadnezzar attacked **Ashkelon**, captured its king, plundered the city, and turned it into a ruin mound. Archaeological destruction evidence at Ashkelon is consistent with a violent Babylonian conquest around this period.

This campaign demonstrated the treatment awaiting western cities that relied upon Egyptian protection or withheld submission.

## 603–602 BCE: Continued Levantine campaigning

The Chronicle is damaged but records continued operations in Hatti. Individual cities cannot always be securely identified.

A fragment involving Nabu-shum-lishir has been interpreted as evidence of conflict within the royal family. Because the passage is incomplete, the alleged rebellion remains unresolved.

## 601 BCE: Battle with Egypt and strategic setback

Nebuchadnezzar marched against Egypt. The Chronicle says the Babylonian and Egyptian armies fought with heavy losses on both sides. Nebuchadnezzar returned to Babylon and spent the following year rebuilding his chariotry and cavalry.

This was not recorded as a Babylonian victory. It was at minimum a failed invasion and probably a strategic setback. It weakened the appearance of Babylonian invincibility and encouraged rebellion among western vassals, including Judah.

## 600 BCE: Military reconstruction

Nebuchadnezzar remained in Babylonia and refitted his forces. The episode reveals the material limits of his military system: horses, chariots, equipment, trained personnel, and remounts had to be replaced after the Egyptian battle.

## 599–598 BCE: Western operations and Arabian raids

The Chronicle records campaigning in Hatti and action against Arab groups, including the seizure of their property and divine images. The exact communities and geography are uncertain.

Judah’s King Jehoiakim had ceased submission to Babylon, apparently calculating that Egypt could protect him.

## 598–597 BCE: First capture of Jerusalem

Nebuchadnezzar marched west and besieged Jerusalem. Jehoiakim died during the crisis, under circumstances that are unclear. His son **Jehoiachin** succeeded him.

The Babylonian Chronicle records that on 2 Addaru in Nebuchadnezzar’s seventh year—conventionally 16 March 597—he captured the “city of Judah,” took its king, appointed a king of his own choice, and received heavy tribute.

The replacement ruler was Jehoiachin’s uncle Mattaniah, renamed **Zedekiah**. Jehoiachin, members of the royal family, officials, soldiers, craftsmen, and other inhabitants were deported to Babylonia. Biblical sources give differing totals and categories for the deportees, reflecting different counting schemes or stages.

Administrative ration tablets from Babylon later list **Ya’u-kīnu, king of the land of Judah**, usually identified with Jehoiachin, together with his sons. These texts provide independent evidence that the deported Judean king and his household were maintained at the Babylonian court.

Nebuchadnezzar did not abolish Judah in 597. He established a subordinate government and removed strategically important elites.

## 596 BCE: Elamite alarm

The Chronicle records a threat or military mobilization involving Elam. Nebuchadnezzar assembled his forces; the Elamite king withdrew. No major battle is recorded.

## 595–594 BCE: Rebellion in Babylonia

**Documented fact, details unclear:** A rebellion broke out in Akkad, meaning Babylonia. The Chronicle says Nebuchadnezzar brought his army against the insurgents, killed many, and defeated his enemy.

The rebel leader, grievances, scale, and relationship to the royal family are unknown. This is the clearest contemporary evidence of violent internal opposition to his rule. Attempts to identify the rebellion with a named prince or ideological faction are speculative.

He campaigned again in Hatti after suppressing the revolt.

## After 594 BCE: Documentary gap

The main chronicle covering Nebuchadnezzar’s early reign ends. Royal inscriptions continue, but because they focus on construction and piety, political narrative becomes much thinner.

This asymmetry matters: the silence about later defeats, domestic problems, or prolonged warfare is not evidence that none occurred.

## 594–589 BCE: Western diplomatic crisis

Jeremiah 27–29 depicts emissaries from Edom, Moab, Ammon, Tyre, and Sidon gathering in Jerusalem and discussing resistance to Babylon. Jeremiah opposed revolt and presented submission to Nebuchadnezzar as divinely required.

Whether every detail reflects a single conference is debated, but the texts preserve a credible environment of anti-Babylonian coordination.

Zedekiah visited or sent a delegation to Babylon. The purpose and exact date are uncertain.

## 589–587/586 BCE: Zedekiah’s revolt and the final siege of Jerusalem

Zedekiah rebelled, probably with expectations of Egyptian assistance under Pharaoh Apries. Nebuchadnezzar’s army invaded Judah and besieged Jerusalem.

An Egyptian force moved north, temporarily causing the Babylonians to lift or adjust the siege. Jeremiah warned that the Egyptians would withdraw and Babylon would return. The Egyptian intervention failed to save Jerusalem.

The city fell in either **587 or 586 BCE**, depending on the synchronization of Judean and Babylonian calendars. Current scholarship often favors 587, but 586 remains common.

Nebuchadnezzar himself was apparently at **Riblah**, in the Orontes region, functioning as imperial headquarters. Zedekiah fled but was captured near Jericho and taken to Riblah. According to 2 Kings and Jeremiah:

- his sons were killed before him;
- Judean nobles were executed;
- Zedekiah was blinded;
- he was bound and transported to Babylon.

These events are reported by Judean literary sources; no surviving Babylonian royal inscription narrates them.

Nebuzaradan, commander of the guard, burned the temple, royal palace, and major houses, dismantled fortifications, removed valuable temple metalwork, and deported additional inhabitants. Archaeological destruction layers in Jerusalem and other Judean sites are broadly consistent with Babylonian conquest, although assigning individual remains requires site-specific caution.

The province was placed under **Gedaliah son of Ahikam** at Mizpah. Gedaliah was assassinated by Ishmael son of Nethaniah. A group of Judeans then fled to Egypt despite Jeremiah’s opposition.

### Political violence and deportation

By ancient imperial standards, the destruction of Jerusalem, execution of elites, blinding of Zedekiah, deportations, enslavement, and confiscation were recognizable instruments of statecraft. They are **documented or strongly reported acts of imperial violence**, not later criminal charges. No ancient court adjudicated them as crimes, and modern legal categories cannot simply be applied retroactively.

The events created the central historical rupture remembered as the Babylonian Exile.

## Tyre: prolonged pressure or siege

Ezekiel depicts Nebuchadnezzar’s army laboring against Tyre until soldiers’ heads were rubbed bare and shoulders chafed, yet obtaining insufficient spoil. Josephus, relying on Phoenician material and Menander, is traditionally read as saying that the siege lasted thirteen years, commonly dated about 585–572/573.

Recent textual reexamination has challenged the certainty of “thirteen years.” Some Josephus manuscripts support “three years and ten months,” and his chronological framework is inconsistent. Archaeology has not revealed an unequivocal destruction layer or Babylonian siegeworks corresponding to a complete destruction of insular Tyre.

What can be said:

- **Strongly supported:** Tyre came under prolonged Babylonian military pressure.
- **Supported by later testimony and indirect cuneiform evidence:** Tyre ultimately entered a subordinate relationship with Babylon.
- **Disputed:** exactly thirteen years of uninterrupted siege.
- **Not established:** Nebuchadnezzar stormed and destroyed the island city.
- **Contrary evidence:** Ezekiel 29 itself implies that the army received little material reward from its labor.

Later accounts of Nebuchadnezzar constructing a causeway like Alexander the Great appear to import details from Alexander’s better-documented siege and should be treated as legend or retrospective harmonization.

## Phoenician administration

A palace inscription lists rulers or contingents associated with Tyre, Gaza, Sidon, Arwad, and Ashdod. Administrative texts indicate Babylonian military personnel stationed in the west. The structure was one of vassal kings, tribute, garrisons, deportations, and direct intervention where rebellion occurred.

## Egypt in the thirty-seventh year, 568/567 BCE

A fragmentary tablet in the British Museum reports a campaign involving Egypt in Nebuchadnezzar’s thirty-seventh year. It may mention Pharaoh **Amasis II**, but the tablet is damaged.

The campaign’s route, battles, territorial results, and outcome are unknown. Claims that Nebuchadnezzar conquered Egypt are not supported. Claims that no late campaign occurred also go beyond the evidence: the fragment is genuine evidence for military action directed toward or involving Egypt.

## Final years

The final decade of his reign is obscure. Dated administrative and building texts show that government continued, while the monumental program remained active. No contemporary source records retirement, deposition, exile, captivity, or prolonged absence from Babylon.

He died after forty-three years of rule in early October 562 BCE. The earliest dated tablets of Amel-Marduk establish the transition. Berossus, preserved in Josephus, says that Nebuchadnezzar fell ill and died. No diagnosis is possible.

## Government, Institutions, and Imperial Structure

Nebuchadnezzar ruled through a palace-centered monarchy interacting with:

- major temples and their landholdings;
- governors and urban officials;
- military commanders;
- scribal bureaux;
- merchant and entrepreneurial families;
- deported labor populations;
- vassal kings in the Levant;
- palace dependents and foreign royal hostages.

The empire was not a modern territorial state with uniform administration. Babylonia itself contained powerful cities and temples with established privileges. The western territories were managed through varying combinations of tribute, local kings, deportation, garrisons, and direct punishment.

### Officials known from Judean sources

Jeremiah names Babylonian officers including Nergal-sharezer, Samgar-nebo, Sarsekim, Nebushazban, and Nebuzaradan. Some names or offices correspond plausibly to Babylonian nomenclature. The biblical lists are valuable but textually difficult, and older translations sometimes divide personal names incorrectly.

### Justice ideology

One inscription calls Nebuchadnezzar a king concerned with justice and claims that he prevented the strong from oppressing the weak. This belongs to ancient Mesopotamian royal ideology. It documents how the monarchy presented itself, not an independent assessment of actual social conditions.

## Cities, Buildings, Technologies, and Projects

Nebuchadnezzar’s best-documented achievement was the transformation of Babylon and the restoration of cult centers across the kingdom.

### Babylon

Projects associated with his reign include:

- completion and enlargement of the **inner and outer city walls**;
- construction or rebuilding of the **Ishtar Gate**;
- monumentalization of the **Processional Way**;
- rebuilding of **Esagil**, Marduk’s principal temple;
- work on **Etemenanki**, the great ziggurat associated in later reception with the Tower of Babel;
- the Southern Palace and other palace complexes;
- river embankments, quays, canals, and hydraulic defenses;
- temples dedicated to numerous Babylonian deities;
- a defensive wall or system north of Babylon sometimes connected with the later Greek “Median Wall.”

His inscriptions stress cedar imported from Lebanon, baked brick, bitumen, bronze, precious metals, colored glazed brick, and skilled labor.

### Ishtar Gate and Processional Way

The monumental gate carried glazed representations of bulls and *mušḫuššu* dragons, associated with Adad and Marduk. Lions decorated the Processional Way and evoked Ishtar. Excavation and recent architectural study show multiple construction levels rather than a single instantaneous project.

### Esagil and Etemenanki

Esagil was the ritual center of Babylonian kingship. Etemenanki—“House of the Foundation of Heaven and Earth”—was the adjacent temple tower. Nebuchadnezzar’s inscriptions describe rebuilding and completing the tower with labor and materials assembled from across his dominions.

The identification of Etemenanki as the historical stimulus for the biblical Tower of Babel is plausible as reception history, but Genesis does not name Nebuchadnezzar or describe his project.

### Borsippa

At Borsippa, he restored **Ezida**, the temple of Nabu, and worked on its ziggurat, E-ur-me-imin-anki. Later travelers sometimes misidentified Borsippa’s ruins as the Tower of Babel.

### Other cult centers

His building inscriptions are attested from or refer to work at Ur, Uruk, Sippar, Larsa, Marad, Dilbat, Kish, and other Babylonian cities. At Uruk he restored structures connected with the Eanna complex, the great sanctuary of Ishtar/Inanna. Bricks and foundation cylinders carried his name throughout the country.

### Labor and resources

The works required taxation, agricultural surplus, temple contributions, military booty, tribute, deported workers, corvée, craftsmen, and long-distance trade.

It is not possible to calculate Nebuchadnezzar’s personal net worth. Palace, temple, and crown resources were institutionally interwoven and cannot be translated responsibly into a modern fortune.

Temples controlled extensive land, livestock, workshops, credit, and trade. Private business families also flourished. Imperial construction therefore depended on a mixed economy of palace, temple, dependent labor, hired labor, entrepreneurial contractors, and obligatory service.

### Forced labor

Royal inscriptions celebrate the mobilization of peoples but do not describe working conditions. Deportees and subject populations were unquestionably incorporated into Babylonian agriculture, construction, palace service, and military systems. Not every deportee was a chattel slave: surviving archives show some foreign communities leasing land, paying taxes, forming households, and conducting business. Status ranged from royal prisoner and dependent worker to integrated landholder.

## Wealth, Property, and Financial Relationships

No complete royal budget survives.

Nebuchadnezzar’s resources derived from:

- the agricultural surplus of irrigated Babylonia;
- land controlled by palace and temples;
- tribute from subordinate rulers;
- war booty;
- deported labor;
- customs and long-distance exchange;
- taxes and service obligations;
- control of canals and transport routes.

The conquest of Jerusalem involved seizure of palace and temple wealth. The 597 Chronicle explicitly records heavy tribute. Biblical sources describe the later removal and breaking-up of bronze temple furnishings.

The king was a patron of Esagil, Ezida, and other temples, but patronage also bound these institutions to the monarchy. Gifts of land, commodities, metal, animals, and building resources had political as well as devotional functions.

There is no reliable evidence for private “investments,” corporations, bank accounts, or a separately calculable royal estate in the modern sense. Nor are there documented patents: technical knowledge was embodied in scribal, craft, architectural, and hydraulic practice rather than modern intellectual-property law.

## Religious and Ideological Development

Nebuchadnezzar’s authentic inscriptions present no conversion away from Babylonian religion.

His ideological program emphasized:

- Marduk’s selection of the king;
- Nabu’s protection and wisdom;
- the ruler as pious shepherd;
- maintenance of offerings and festivals;
- restoration of ancient foundations;
- military expansion as divinely authorized protection;
- Babylon as the cosmic and political center of the world.

Royal violence and temple patronage were not contradictory within this ideology. Defeating foreign kings supplied tribute, labor, and sacred objects while demonstrating divine favor.

The Book of Jeremiah sometimes calls Nebuchadnezzar the servant or instrument of Israel’s God. This is Judean theological interpretation, not evidence of the king’s own beliefs.

Daniel portrays him as acknowledging the supremacy of the Jewish God after miraculous confrontations. These narratives are not corroborated by his inscriptions and are usually dated in their received form centuries after his reign.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Nabopolassar

Father, predecessor, and foundational political influence. Nebuchadnezzar completed several projects begun by him and inherited his war against Egypt and the Assyrian remnant.

### Necho II

Principal military opponent at Carchemish and in the inconclusive or damaging battle of 601. Their rivalry determined control of Syria-Palestine.

### Cyaxares and the Medes

The Medes were Nabopolassar’s major allies against Assyria. Direct personal meetings between Nebuchadnezzar and Cyaxares are not documented. The alleged marriage alliance through Amytis is plausible but later reported.

### Jehoiakim

King of Judah who became a Babylonian vassal and later rebelled. He died before or during the 597 crisis. His exact fate is disputed.

### Jehoiachin

Captured king of Judah, deported with his family in 597. Babylonian ration tablets document his continued existence as a royal dependent. He was released from confinement by Amel-Marduk after Nebuchadnezzar’s death.

### Zedekiah

Installed by Nebuchadnezzar and later rebelled. Captured, subjected to execution of his sons, blinded, and deported.

### Jeremiah

No documented personal meeting. Jeremiah interpreted Nebuchadnezzar as the divinely permitted conqueror and urged submission. The relationship is therefore one of historical intersection and theological representation, not proven face-to-face association.

### Ezekiel son of Buzi

A deported Judean priest and prophet living in Babylonia. No meeting is documented. Ezekiel’s oracles discuss Nebuchadnezzar’s campaigns against Jerusalem, Tyre, and Egypt.

### Ithobaal III of Tyre

Later Phoenician/Josephan chronology places him opposite Nebuchadnezzar during the Tyrian conflict. Details are poorly preserved.

### Pharaoh Apries

Egyptian intervention encouraged or supported resistance during the final Judean revolt. A direct meeting is not recorded.

### Amasis II

Probably the Egyptian ruler named in the damaged text concerning Nebuchadnezzar’s thirty-seventh-year campaign.

### Amel-Marduk

Son and successor. Later rabbinic stories claim that Nebuchadnezzar imprisoned him, but contemporary proof is absent.

### Neriglissar

High official, probable son-in-law, later king after assassinating Amel-Marduk. He may already have been a powerful court and military figure under Nebuchadnezzar.

## Wars, Repression, and Other Contested or Negative Material

Modern categories such as fraud, money laundering, lawsuits, and criminal prosecution have no direct application to the available sixth-century-BCE record. The adversarial searches specified in the request produced no ancient indictment, court proceeding, or credible modern discovery of financial fraud by Nebuchadnezzar. The significant negative record instead concerns imperial warfare, deportation, destruction, executions, propaganda, and disputed reconstructions.

### Destruction of Ashkelon

**Documented fact:** The Babylonian Chronicle states that he captured, plundered, and reduced Ashkelon to ruins.

### Destruction of Jerusalem and its temple

**Strongly documented historical event:** Judean textual evidence, broader archaeology, subsequent deportee documentation, and the political sequence establish the Babylonian destruction. Exact casualty and deportee totals remain uncertain.

### Execution and mutilation at Riblah

**Reported fact from multiple related Judean texts:** Zedekiah’s sons and officials were killed; Zedekiah was blinded and taken away. No Babylonian parallel survives, but the account is consistent with Neo-Babylonian and wider ancient Near Eastern punitive practice.

### Mass deportation

**Documented imperial policy:** Deportations followed both Jerusalem captures. Biblical figures differ because they may count distinct groups, adult males, households, or different episodes.

### Internal rebellion of 595/594

**Documented fact with unknown participants:** The Chronicle says Nebuchadnezzar killed many rebels. The absence of names prevents assessment of competing political claims.

### Alleged execution of Jehoiakim

Biblical passages generated different traditions:

- 2 Kings says Jehoiakim “slept with his fathers,” without describing burial.
- Jeremiah predicts disgraceful treatment or lack of proper burial.
- Chronicles says Nebuchadnezzar bound him for transport.
- Josephus supplies a violent death narrative.

The manner and location of Jehoiakim’s death are **unresolved**.

### Alleged imprisonment of Amel-Marduk

Later rabbinic tradition says officials proclaimed Amel-Marduk king while Nebuchadnezzar was absent, after which the returning king imprisoned him together with Jehoiachin.

No contemporary source confirms this. It may be an etiological story explaining why Amel-Marduk later favored Jehoiachin. Status: **late tradition, unverified**.

### Supposed conquest of Egypt

The year-37 fragment establishes a campaign but not conquest. Later assertions that Nebuchadnezzar decisively subjugated Egypt are **unproved**.

### Tyre as complete victory or complete failure

The evidence permits neither simple conclusion. Tyre apparently endured prolonged pressure and ultimately acknowledged Babylonian power, but a destructive storming of the island is not documented. Status: **campaign documented indirectly; duration and outcome disputed**.

### Royal inscriptions as propaganda

Nebuchadnezzar’s inscriptions are authentic primary evidence but highly selective. They present him as:

- chosen by the gods;
- unfailingly pious;
- just;
- protective;
- a universal provider;
- architect of security and abundance.

They largely suppress:

- the costly 601 battle;
- the domestic rebellion;
- suffering imposed on conquered populations;
- court conflict;
- unsuccessful or inconclusive campaigns.

Calling these inscriptions propaganda does not make their building information false. It identifies their institutional purpose and selection of material.

## Health, Illness, and Death

### Historically documented health

No contemporary medical text or administrative record diagnoses Nebuchadnezzar with mental illness, neurological disease, addiction, or chronic physical disorder.

Berossus’s later account, preserved by Josephus, says he fell sick and died after forty-three years. Nothing more medically specific can be recovered.

### The madness of Daniel 4

Daniel describes Nebuchadnezzar being driven from human society, eating grass like cattle, growing hair like eagles’ feathers and nails like birds’ claws, and recovering after “seven times.”

Status: **religious-literary narrative, not independently documented biography**.

Medical diagnoses such as boanthropy, clinical lycanthropy, porphyria, bipolar disorder, dementia, or general psychosis are retrospective speculation. The narrative’s symbolic structure—royal pride, divine judgment, abasement, acknowledgment, restoration—is more securely demonstrable than any diagnosis.

### Nabonidus parallel

The Aramaic **Prayer of Nabonidus** from Qumran tells of King Nabonidus suffering a long affliction at Tayma and receiving interpretation or healing through a Jewish diviner. Nabonidus’s prolonged residence at Tayma is independently historical.

Many scholars therefore argue that a tradition originally connected with Nabonidus influenced Daniel’s Nebuchadnezzar story. The direction and exact literary relationship remain debated, but the parallel weakens claims that Daniel 4 is independent medical documentation of Nebuchadnezzar.

### Death

Nebuchadnezzar died in early October 562 BCE. His burial place is not securely identified. No verified tomb, body, or funerary assemblage survives.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## The Hanging Gardens

### Story

Nebuchadnezzar supposedly built an elevated garden of terraces, trees, and hydraulic machinery for his Median wife Amytis, who longed for her mountainous homeland.

### Appearance and circulation

The attribution ultimately depends chiefly on Berossus, a Babylonian priest writing in Greek approximately three centuries later, preserved only through later quotations. Other Greek and Roman descriptions—Diodorus, Strabo, Quintus Curtius Rufus, and the Seven Wonders tradition—are late and interdependent.

### Evidence for

- Berossus was a Babylonian scholar with access to native traditions.
- Nebuchadnezzar demonstrably constructed enormous palaces and hydraulic works.
- Greek descriptions place a major garden in Babylon.
- Some architectural remains have been proposed as garden substructures.

### Evidence against or complicating

- No surviving Nebuchadnezzar inscription names the Hanging Gardens or Amytis.
- Excavated features proposed as the gardens have alternative interpretations.
- Greek accounts disagree over builders and details.
- Stephanie Dalley has argued that descriptions better match Sennacherib’s documented garden and waterworks at Nineveh.
- The phrase “Hanging Gardens” may itself reflect later misunderstanding of terraced or raised construction.

### Assessment

The gardens’ construction by Nebuchadnezzar is a **famous but unverified attribution**. The possibilities remain: a real but unidentified Babylon garden; literary amplification of ordinary royal gardens; confusion with Nineveh; or a composite wonder created through later transmission.

### Reputation

The story transformed Nebuchadnezzar from conqueror-builder into a romantic monarch performing an immense act for a homesick queen. It remains one of the most influential narratives attached to him despite its weak contemporary documentation.

## The Tower of Babel

Nebuchadnezzar restored Etemenanki, Babylon’s great ziggurat. Later readers frequently identify its remains with the Tower of Babel.

- **Documented:** his work on Etemenanki.
- **Plausible cultural association:** Babylonian temple towers contributed to later imagination of Babel.
- **Not documented:** that Nebuchadnezzar built the tower described in Genesis or was the ruler of that narrative.
- **Later confusion:** Borsippa’s ziggurat was also identified by travelers as Babel.

## The fiery furnace

Daniel 3 says Nebuchadnezzar erected a golden image and ordered Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego cast into a furnace for refusing worship.

No contemporary inscription or administrative record confirms the episode or the three men. The story belongs to Jewish court-tale and martyr traditions. Its themes—idolatry, imperial coercion, miraculous preservation—were more important to its transmission than administrative specificity.

Status: **scriptural narrative; historically unverified**.

## Dreams and Daniel’s interpretation

Daniel 2 presents the king’s dream of a composite statue symbolizing successive empires. Daniel 4 presents the dream of the great tree and the king’s abasement.

There is no contemporary confirmation of Daniel as a named court official under Nebuchadnezzar. Dream interpretation was, however, a genuine Mesopotamian scholarly practice.

Status: **literary tradition using historically appropriate court motifs, without independent confirmation of the episodes**.

## Conversion to worship of Israel’s God

Daniel depicts Nebuchadnezzar successively praising Daniel’s God and finally acknowledging divine sovereignty. Verdi’s opera goes farther, dramatizing a conversion and promise to rebuild the Jerusalem temple.

Nebuchadnezzar’s own inscriptions remain thoroughly devoted to Marduk, Nabu, and the Babylonian pantheon. Status: **theological and dramatic tradition, contradicted as literal biography by the king’s surviving public ideology**.

## Becoming an animal or werewolf-like figure

Medieval and modern art converted Daniel’s account into a striking image of the king crawling naked, hairy, clawed, and animal-like. William Blake’s color print *Nebuchadnezzar* is the best-known example.

Daniel says he lived like an animal; it does not say he physically transformed into one. Later visual and occult associations intensified the metamorphosis.

Status: **artistic elaboration of scriptural myth**.

## Semiramis

Some Seven Wonders traditions attributed Babylonian monuments or gardens to Semiramis, the legendary Assyrian queen. She was not Nebuchadnezzar’s queen and lived, insofar as she reflects the historical Shammuramat, centuries earlier.

The merging of Semiramis, Amytis, and Babylonian building traditions illustrates the instability of classical memory.

## Saddam Hussein as reincarnation or “son of Nebuchadnezzar”

Saddam Hussein’s government reconstructed portions of Babylon, stamped modern bricks in imitation of ancient royal practice, staged the Babylon International Festival, and promoted visual parallels between Saddam and Mesopotamian monarchs.

Documented slogans and imagery associated Saddam with Nebuchadnezzar. UNESCO heritage documentation records that incompatible modern bricks and construction were placed on or near ancient foundations.

The stronger internet claim that Saddam literally declared himself Nebuchadnezzar’s reincarnation is often repeated without adequate primary documentation. What is securely documented is **political identification and symbolic succession**, not a verified metaphysical claim.

This modern appropriation amplified Nebuchadnezzar as an Iraqi national and military archetype while physically complicating the conservation of Babylon.

## Nabucco and nationalist mythology

Verdi’s *Nabucco* premiered at La Scala in 1842. Temistocle Solera’s libretto reshaped biblical materials:

- Nabucco is called an Assyrian king;
- invented daughters Fenena and Abigaille drive the dynastic plot;
- madness and recovery structure the drama;
- the king converts and promises restoration;
- “Va, pensiero” gives the exiled Hebrews a collective patriotic voice.

Later tradition cast the chorus as an immediate anthem of the Italian Risorgimento. Its nationalist reception is real, although accounts of instant revolutionary adoption have themselves been romanticized.

## Works by or Attributed to Nebuchadnezzar

Nebuchadnezzar did not author books in the modern sense. His surviving “works” are inscriptions issued in his royal name and produced by palace scribes. They are institutional compositions expressing royal authority. Personal dictation cannot normally be demonstrated.

### Authentic contemporary inscriptional corpus

Major groups include:

1. **East India House Inscription / Stone Tablet Inscription**  
   Akkadian royal building text describing work at Esagil, Ezida, Babylon’s walls, gates, Processional Way, and palaces. Now British Museum BM 129397. Composition belongs to the reign, 604–562 BCE.

2. **Wadi Brisa inscriptions**  
   Monumental rock inscriptions in Lebanon, written in archaizing and contemporary scripts. They describe royal piety, temple restoration, cedar procurement, kingship, and the taming or cultivation of the landscape.

3. **Nahr el-Kalb inscription and relief**  
   Badly weathered royal monument in Lebanon, associated with western campaigning and cedar acquisition.

4. **Tower of Babel stele**  
   Fragmentary stele bearing an image of the king and a plan or image of Etemenanki. Authentic Neo-Babylonian royal monument.

5. **Babylon cylinders**  
   Numerous foundation cylinders concerning palaces, walls, temples, embankments, gates, and urban works.

6. **Ishtar Gate and Processional Way inscriptions**  
   Building inscriptions embedded in or associated with the gate and roadway.

7. **Etemenanki inscriptions**  
   Texts describing work on the ziggurat and its cosmic-religious significance.

8. **Esagil inscriptions**  
   Records of rebuilding and furnishing Marduk’s sanctuary.

9. **Ezida and Borsippa inscriptions**  
   Texts recording construction for Nabu and restoration of Borsippa’s sacred complex.

10. **Uruk inscriptions**  
    Building records concerning the Eanna complex and other cult buildings.

11. **Sippar inscriptions**  
    Temple and construction documents connected with Ebabbar and the cult of Shamash.

12. **Ur, Larsa, Marad, Dilbat, and Kish texts**  
    Bricks, cylinders, and foundation records attesting restoration across Babylonia.

13. **Standard stamped-brick inscriptions**  
    Usually short formulas identifying Nebuchadnezzar as king of Babylon, provider for Esagil and Ezida, and eldest son of Nabopolassar.

### Administrative documents issued under his reign

These are not personally authored but are indispensable products of his government:

- Babylonian Chronicle ABC 5/BM 21946;
- year-37 Egypt campaign fragment BM 33041;
- ration tablets naming Jehoiachin and his sons;
- palace provision lists;
- temple administrative records;
- dated contracts, receipts, loans, leases, and tax documents;
- military and labor allocations.

### Biblical royal speech attributed to him

Daniel 4 presents a proclamation in Nebuchadnezzar’s first-person voice. It is not accepted as an authentic Babylonian state document. It is an attributed literary speech within the Book of Daniel.

### Later sayings and deathbed material

Berossus and later writers preserve speeches or statements attributed to the king. Because they survive through authors centuries later, they should be treated as **later literary testimony**, not verbatim transcripts.

## Death and Succession

Nebuchadnezzar died in early October 562 BCE after a reign of approximately forty-three years. Berossus says illness preceded death, but no contemporary clinical account survives.

Amel-Marduk succeeded him. Within two years, Amel-Marduk was killed, and Neriglissar became king. After Neriglissar’s death, the young Labashi-Marduk reigned only briefly before being removed. Nabonidus, who was not a direct continuation of Nebuchadnezzar’s male line, then took power.

The instability after 562 contrasts with the relative durability of Nebuchadnezzar’s own reign. It suggests that his long personal rule had not produced an uncontested succession structure.

No securely identified tomb of Nebuchadnezzar survives. Later stories identifying particular Babylonian ruins as his tomb are unsupported.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### In Babylonia

Nebuchadnezzar’s monumental inscriptions remained embedded in Babylon’s physical fabric. Later rulers encountered and sometimes restored his buildings. His name became inseparable from the city’s greatest late period.

### In Jewish history

He became the principal human agent of:

- the destruction of the First Temple;
- the end of the Davidic monarchy in Judah;
- forced displacement to Babylonia;
- the theological and literary transformations associated with exile.

Jeremiah could simultaneously portray him as a devastating conqueror and as an instrument of divine judgment. Daniel transformed him into a paradigmatic proud monarch humbled by God.

### In Christianity

Christian readings inherited Daniel’s images of the golden statue, fiery furnace, dream-tree, and animalized king. Nebuchadnezzar became a figure for pride, tyranny, punishment, repentance, and the submission of earthly empire to divine authority.

### In Islam

The Qur’an does not name Nebuchadnezzar. Later Muslim historians and storytellers discuss **Bukht Naṣṣar**, often in connection with Jerusalem’s destruction, the Israelites, prophecy, and divine punishment. Narratives vary widely and commonly blend biblical, Iranian, and local traditions.

### In European culture

Nebuchadnezzar became:

- a model of the overreaching monarch;
- a subject of madness imagery;
- an emblem of oriental splendor;
- a destroyer of sacred Jerusalem;
- an architectural patron associated with a Wonder of the World.

Verdi’s *Nabucco* recast the Babylonian captivity into nineteenth-century European nationalist theater.

### In Iraqi nationalism

Twentieth-century Iraqi governments used Babylonian antiquity to construct national continuity. Saddam Hussein’s regime made the association particularly explicit through reconstruction, festivals, monumental imagery, and stamped bricks. This was modern political reception, not evidence about the ancient king’s intentions.

### In archaeology and museums

Excavations by Robert Koldewey’s German expedition fundamentally changed knowledge of Nebuchadnezzar’s Babylon. The removal and reconstruction of the Ishtar Gate in Berlin made his building program globally visible while also generating modern disputes over excavation-era partage, cultural property, and the appropriate location of monumental antiquities.

### Historical reassessment

Modern scholarship has moved away from biographies based primarily on Daniel and classical wonder literature. The contemporary picture is both narrower and firmer:

- an exceptionally successful dynastic monarch;
- commander at Carchemish;
- organizer of repeated Levantine campaigns;
- destroyer of Ashkelon and Jerusalem;
- suppressor of domestic rebellion;
- ruler of a deportation-based imperial system;
- patron of the greatest building phase of Neo-Babylonian Babylon;
- a man whose private personality, childhood, intimate relationships, health, and motives are mostly unrecoverable.

## Atlas Connections

Only documented or source-supported connections to names in the supplied atlas roster are included. Mere contemporaneity is excluded.

### Ezekiel son of Buzi

**Evidentiary label: documented historical intersection; no documented meeting.**

Ezekiel was among the Judean deportee population in Babylonia and dated prophetic activity from the exile of Jehoiachin. His oracles explicitly discuss Nebuchadnezzar’s operations against Jerusalem, Tyre, and Egypt. Ezekiel 29 describes the Babylonian army’s unrewarded labor against Tyre. There is no evidence that Ezekiel and Nebuchadnezzar met.

### Flavius Josephus

**Evidentiary label: documented posthumous literary connection.**

Josephus lived more than six centuries after Nebuchadnezzar. In *Antiquities of the Jews* and *Against Apion*, he narrated the king’s actions and preserved excerpts attributed to Berossus and Menander. His works are crucial to the later traditions of the Tyrian siege, Nebuchadnezzar’s buildings, death, and succession. This is not a lifetime encounter.

### William Blake

**Evidentiary label: documented artistic reception.**

Blake created the color print *Nebuchadnezzar*, first produced in 1795, representing the king in the animalized madness described by Daniel 4. Blake had no historical connection to the ancient ruler; the connection is posthumous artistic interpretation.

### Saddam Hussein al-Tikriti

**Evidentiary label: documented political and architectural appropriation; literal reincarnation claim unverified.**

During the 1980s and 1990s, Saddam’s government reconstructed parts of Babylon, stamped bricks with modern royal-style inscriptions, and cultivated parallels between Saddam and Nebuchadnezzar. The documented relationship is symbolic appropriation and intervention at the ancient king’s capital. Claims that Saddam formally declared himself Nebuchadnezzar reincarnated are insufficiently sourced.

### Alexander the Great

**Evidentiary label: documented posthumous geographical and imperial connection.**

Alexander entered Babylon in 331 BCE, more than two centuries after Nebuchadnezzar. He occupied and planned work in the monumental city substantially shaped by Nebuchadnezzar. Later literary descriptions of Babylon and its gardens were influenced by Alexander-era observers. Alexander’s siege of Tyre also supplied details that later writers sometimes projected backward onto Nebuchadnezzar’s Tyrian campaign. No personal connection was possible.

### Cyrus the Great

**Evidentiary label: documented dynastic-geopolitical succession, not a personal relationship.**

Cyrus conquered Babylon in 539 BCE, twenty-three years after Nebuchadnezzar’s death. His conquest terminated the Neo-Babylonian Empire built under Nabopolassar and Nebuchadnezzar. The Cyrus Cylinder’s political theology partly responds to the failures attributed to Nabonidus rather than directly attacking Nebuchadnezzar. There was no meeting.

### David ben Jesse

**Evidentiary label: posthumous dynastic and biblical connection.**

Nebuchadnezzar destroyed the independent kingdom ruled by David’s dynasty and removed Zedekiah. David lived centuries earlier. The connection is institutional and literary, not personal.

### King Solomon

**Evidentiary label: posthumous architectural and biblical connection.**

The Jerusalem temple traditionally attributed to Solomon was destroyed by Nebuchadnezzar’s forces in 587/586 BCE. Solomon lived centuries earlier and could not have met him.

### Abraham

**Evidentiary label: later religious-geographical association only.**

Abraham was traditionally connected with Ur and Mesopotamia, while Nebuchadnezzar restored temples and monuments at Ur. No source supports a personal or genealogical connection; their association exists within later biblical geography and historical memory.

### Zoroaster

**Evidentiary label: theory or later chronological association; no documented connection.**

Various ancient and modern chronologies have placed Zoroaster across a very wide span, including periods near the Neo-Babylonian age. No contemporary evidence documents a meeting, correspondence, influence, or shared institution with Nebuchadnezzar.

### Herodotus of Halicarnassus

**Evidentiary label: documented posthumous literary-geographical connection.**

Herodotus described Babylon approximately a century after Nebuchadnezzar. He does not provide a secure personal biography of the king and attributes major works to figures such as Nitocris and Semiramis. His description contributed to later confusion over the builders of Babylon.

### Ctesias of Cnidus

**Evidentiary label: documented posthumous literary connection.**

Ctesias wrote Persian and Assyrian history before Berossus and helped circulate Semiramis-centered traditions about Babylon. His lost work survives in fragments and later summaries. It is an important source for the mythology against which Nebuchadnezzar’s building reputation developed, not evidence of a meeting.

### Strabo of Amaseia

**Evidentiary label: documented posthumous literary connection.**

Strabo described Babylon and the Hanging Gardens centuries after Nebuchadnezzar. His account is part of the classical reception of the city and may depend on earlier Alexander-era literature.

### Plinio el Viejo

**Evidentiary label: documented posthumous literary connection.**

Pliny discussed Babylon and Mesopotamian wonders within Roman encyclopedic tradition. He lived centuries later and transmitted classical images rather than firsthand knowledge of Nebuchadnezzar.

### Nabucodonosor II

The subject appears in the supplied roster under his Spanish name. This is self-identity, not a separate atlas connection.

### No supported lifetime connection

No source consulted supports a personal encounter, correspondence, family relationship, or acknowledged influence between Nebuchadnezzar and the other listed roster figures. In particular, contemporaneity or a shared association with Babylon, Jerusalem, kingship, exile, mysticism, architecture, or empire was not treated as a connection without direct documentary support.
