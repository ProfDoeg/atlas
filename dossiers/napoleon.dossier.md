# Napoleon Bonaparte: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

This dossier reconstructs Napoleon Bonaparte’s life from birth through death and posthumous reception. It distinguishes:

- **Documented fact:** supported by official records, surviving correspondence, legislation, archival documents, or multiple closely contemporary accounts.
- **Reported fact:** found in credible scholarship or testimony but not independently established by a surviving primary record consulted here.
- **Allegation or theory:** a substantive claim whose evidence remains disputed or incomplete.
- **Rumor or myth:** folklore, propaganda, retrospective anecdote, forgery, or a story contradicted by stronger evidence.

Napoleon governed in an age before modern international criminal law, corporate disclosure, standardized public accounts, or forensic medicine. Terms such as “war crime,” “dictatorship,” and “genocide” therefore require attention to whether they describe contemporary law, modern legal analogies, or political argument.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name at birth:** Napoleone di Buonaparte, with spelling variants including Napoleone Buonaparte and Napoleone Bonaparte.

**Usual French name:** Napoléon Bonaparte.

**Regnal name:** Napoléon Ier, Napoleon I.

**Corsican form:** Napulione Buonaparte.

**Titles and offices:**

- Second lieutenant, later lieutenant and captain of artillery
- Lieutenant-colonel in the Corsican National Guard
- Brigadier general, 1793
- General of division, 1795
- Commander of the Army of the Interior, 1795–1796
- Commander-in-chief of the Army of Italy, 1796–1797
- Commander-in-chief of the Army of the Orient, 1798–1799
- First Consul of the French Republic, 10 November 1799–18 May 1804
- First Consul for life, from 1802
- Emperor of the French, 18 May 1804–6 April 1814 and 20 March–22 June 1815
- King of Italy, 1805–1814
- Protector of the Confederation of the Rhine, 1806–1813
- Mediator of the Swiss Confederation, 1803–1813
- Sovereign of Elba, 1814–1815

**Nicknames and political labels:**

- *Le Petit Caporal* (“the Little Corporal”), reportedly popularized after the Italian campaign
- “Boney,” common in British caricature
- “The Corsican,” “the Corsican Ogre,” “the Usurper,” and “Buonaparte” in hostile propaganda
- “The Man of Destiny,” a later expression associated with the Napoleonic legend
- “General Vendémiaire,” after his suppression of the Paris royalist rising
- “Father of the Civil Code,” a commemorative description, not an exclusive claim of authorship

**Born:** 15 August 1769.

**Birthplace:** Ajaccio, Corsica. Genoa had transferred its rights over Corsica to France in 1768; French forces defeated Pasquale Paoli’s resistance at Ponte Nuovo in May 1769. The timing later supported polemical claims that Napoleon was “not really French,” although he was legally born under French rule.

**Baptized:** 21 July 1771 in Ajaccio.

**Died:** 5 May 1821, approximately 5:49 p.m., at Longwood House, Saint Helena.

**Age at death:** 51.

**Principal cause of death:** The autopsy evidence most strongly supports advanced ulcerating gastric carcinoma, probably accompanied by gastrointestinal bleeding. Arsenic poisoning remains a famous but poorly supported theory.

**Burials:**

- 9 May 1821: buried in the Valley of the Willows, Saint Helena.
- 1840: remains exhumed during the *retour des cendres*, transported to France aboard *Belle-Poule*, and ceremonially received in Paris.
- 1861: placed in the completed sarcophagus beneath the dome of Les Invalides.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal and maternal background

Napoleon belonged to a provincial Corsican family of Italian cultural origin. The Bonapartes claimed descent from Tuscan noble families associated with Sarzana and Florence. The precise continuity and social importance of that ancestry have sometimes been inflated by dynastic literature, but the family’s Corsican noble status was officially recognized in 1771.

His father, **Carlo Maria di Buonaparte** or **Charles-Marie Bonaparte** (1746–1785), studied law, served in Corsican political and judicial institutions, and initially supported Pasquale Paoli against France. After the French conquest, Charles accommodated himself to the new administration. He represented the Ajaccio nobility in the Corsican Estates and used recognized noble status to obtain educational assistance for his sons.

His mother, **Maria-Letizia Ramolino**, commonly Letizia Bonaparte or Madame Mère (1750–1836), came from another Corsican family of Italian ancestry. She married Charles in 1764, while still an adolescent. Later family mythology emphasized her austerity, endurance, financial caution, and dominating maternal presence.

Charles and Letizia had thirteen recorded pregnancies or children, depending on the counting system; eight children survived infancy:

1. **Joseph Bonaparte** (1768–1844), later king of Naples and Spain.
2. **Napoleon Bonaparte** (1769–1821).
3. **Lucien Bonaparte** (1775–1840), politician and a participant in the coup of Brumaire, later estranged from Napoleon.
4. **Elisa Bonaparte** (1777–1820), later ruler of Lucca and Piombino and grand duchess of Tuscany.
5. **Louis Bonaparte** (1778–1846), king of Holland and father of Napoleon III.
6. **Pauline Bonaparte** (1780–1825), later princess Borghese.
7. **Caroline Bonaparte** (1782–1839), wife of Joachim Murat and queen of Naples.
8. **Jérôme Bonaparte** (1784–1860), king of Westphalia.

### Father’s death and family responsibility

Charles died at Montpellier on 24 February 1785, probably of stomach cancer. Napoleon was fifteen. Joseph was legally the senior son, but Napoleon increasingly assumed practical responsibility for family finances, education, patronage, and advancement. The later resemblance between Charles’s fatal illness and Napoleon’s gastric disease influenced Napoleon’s own request for an autopsy.

### Marriages

#### Désirée Clary

Before his first marriage, Napoleon became engaged informally or semi-formally to **Désirée Clary**, daughter of a wealthy Marseille merchant. Her sister Julie married Joseph Bonaparte. Napoleon abandoned the match as his political prospects changed. Désirée later married Jean-Baptiste Bernadotte and became queen of Sweden.

#### Joséphine de Beauharnais

On 9 March 1796 Napoleon married **Marie-Josèphe-Rose Tascher de La Pagerie** (1763–1814), widow of the guillotined Alexandre de Beauharnais. Napoleon called her Joséphine, the name by which she became known.

She brought social connections in Directorial Paris and two children:

- **Eugène de Beauharnais**, whom Napoleon employed as aide, viceroy of Italy, and dynastic ally.
- **Hortense de Beauharnais**, who married Napoleon’s brother Louis and became mother of Napoleon III.

The marriage was politically and emotionally consequential but turbulent. Surviving letters document Napoleon’s intense attachment during the Italian campaign. Contemporary testimony supports affairs by both spouses, though particular stories vary in reliability. Their inability to produce a child became a dynastic problem. Napoleon adopted or promoted Joséphine’s children but sought a biological heir.

Their civil marriage was dissolved in December 1809. A church tribunal subsequently annulled the religious marriage on disputed canonical grounds. Joséphine retained the title of empress, residences including Malmaison, and a large settlement. They continued corresponding. She died in 1814.

#### Marie Louise of Austria

Napoleon married **Archduchess Marie Louise of Austria** (1791–1847), daughter of Emperor Francis I of Austria, by proxy in Vienna on 11 March 1810 and ceremonially in France in April. The marriage joined Napoleon to the Habsburg dynasty.

Their son, **Napoléon François Joseph Charles Bonaparte** (1811–1832), received the title King of Rome. Bonapartists later called him Napoleon II, though he never exercised independent rule. After 1814 he lived at the Austrian court as Duke of Reichstadt. Marie Louise did not join Napoleon on Elba or Saint Helena and formed a relationship with Adam Albert von Neipperg while Napoleon was alive.

### Biological and alleged children

**Documented and acknowledged:**

- **Napoleon II**, by Marie Louise.
- **Charles Léon Denuelle** (1806–1881), son of Éléonore Denuelle de La Plaigne. Napoleon acknowledged or materially recognized him; the 1821 testament included a bequest.

**Widely accepted, though not publicly acknowledged in the same manner:**

- **Alexandre Colonna-Walewski** (1810–1868), son of the Polish countess Maria Walewska. Historical testimony and modern Y-chromosome comparison strongly support Napoleon’s paternity.

**Claimed but uncertain:** Various writers have attributed additional children to Napoleon through mistresses or short relationships. The documentation is insufficient to treat most as established.

### Mistresses and intimate relationships

Women credibly identified as mistresses or sexual partners include Pauline Fourès in Egypt, Éléonore Denuelle, Maria Walewska, actress Marguerite-Joséphine Weimer (“Mademoiselle George”), and possibly several court women. The frequency and details are difficult to reconstruct because memoirists embellished imperial sexual gossip. Claims of a Saint Helena affair with Albine de Montholon remain disputed.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1769–1778

Napoleon grew up in an Italian- and Corsican-speaking environment. French was an acquired language; his spelling and accent remained distinctive. In 1774 he attended a school associated with Beguine lay sisters in Ajaccio. Around 1776 he studied with Abbé Recco, whom he remembered with a 20,000-franc bequest in his will.

Corsica’s defeat, Paoli’s exile, his father’s accommodation with France, and the family’s precarious social ambitions shaped his early political imagination. Surviving youthful writings show attachment to Corsican distinctiveness and admiration for Paoli, not an inevitable childhood project of becoming ruler of France.

---

## Education and Formation, 1779–1785

Charles left Corsica with Joseph and Napoleon in December 1778. The brothers entered the Collège d’Autun on 1 January 1779 to improve their French. Napoleon transferred to the royal military school at Brienne in May.

At Brienne he studied mathematics, geography, history, French, Latin, fortification, drawing, fencing, and related subjects. Mathematics and geography suited him better than polite accomplishments. Later stories of extreme isolation, constant bullying, and an epic snowball battle contain elements derived from school testimony but were magnified by biographers and film.

Important teachers or educational figures included:

- **Abbé Recco**, early instruction in Ajaccio.
- **Abbé Chardon**, associated with his French instruction at Autun.
- **Father Dupuy**, a Brienne teacher whom Napoleon later consulted about a projected history of Corsica.
- **Louis Leguille** and other military-school instructors.
- **Pierre-Simon Laplace**, sometimes loosely called one of Napoleon’s teachers; Laplace examined military-school candidates, but a sustained teacher-student relationship is not established.

A September 1784 letter to Charles requested James Boswell’s *Account of Corsica* and other histories of the island. It is direct evidence of Napoleon’s Corsican intellectual preoccupation.

He entered the École Militaire in Paris in October 1784. His father’s death forced financial economy, and Napoleon completed the nominally two-year program in approximately one year. On 1 September 1785 he was examined for the artillery; later that month he received a commission as second lieutenant in the La Fère artillery regiment.

### Formative reading and ideas

His notebooks and later recollections show engagement with:

- Plutarch and classical biography
- Julius Caesar and Alexander the Great
- Polybius and ancient military history
- Jean-Jacques Rousseau
- Montesquieu
- Voltaire
- Raynal
- Mably
- English and Corsican history
- Enlightenment political theory
- Mathematics, geography, artillery science, and fortification

His earliest politics combined Corsican patriotism, Enlightenment historical speculation, republican vocabulary, and personal ambition. His later imperial system did not emerge fully formed from his education.

## Early Military Career and the Corsican Crisis, 1785–1793

Napoleon joined his regiment at Valence in late 1785. He alternated between garrison duty and long leaves in Corsica, where family disputes and revolutionary politics consumed him. He lived or served at Valence, Auxonne, Paris, and several Corsican locations.

He wrote essays on Corsica, monarchy, happiness, and political authority. He began a history of Corsica and sent material to Paoli. Paoli, returned from exile after the Revolution, did not embrace him as an intellectual heir.

During the Revolution Napoleon supported the new political order and joined Jacobin circles. In Corsica he became lieutenant-colonel second-in-command of a volunteer National Guard battalion after a contested election in 1792. Electoral pressure, armed intimidation, and factional maneuvering surrounded Corsican politics; accounts differ over Napoleon’s personal responsibility for particular irregularities.

The failed French expedition against Sardinia and La Maddalena in early 1793 deepened conflict between the Bonapartes and Paoli. Lucien denounced Paoli to the Convention; Paoli’s supporters retaliated. The Bonaparte family fled Corsica in June 1793, abandoning or losing property and settling as refugees in southern France.

Napoleon’s transformation from Corsican nationalist to French revolutionary officer was therefore gradual and conflict-driven, not a simple patriotic conversion.

## Chronological Life History

## Toulon and Revolutionary Advancement, 1793–1795

### Siege of Toulon

Royalist forces surrendered Toulon and its naval facilities to British and allied forces in 1793. Napoleon, serving as an artillery officer, proposed concentrating fire against positions controlling the harbor. After the French assault in December forced the allied evacuation, he received promotion to brigadier general.

His role was important but later legend sometimes presented Toulon as a one-man victory. The operation also depended on commanders including Jacques Dugommier, infantry assaults, engineers, and political representatives.

### Robespierre connection and brief detention

Napoleon developed a working relationship with Augustin Robespierre, Maximilien Robespierre’s brother, and with representative Antoine Christophe Saliceti. After the fall of the Robespierres in July 1794, he was arrested in August because of those associations. He was released after approximately two weeks; investigators found insufficient grounds for continued detention.

This was an investigation and detention, not a trial or conviction.

### Vendémiaire

In 1795 Napoleon was removed from an active command after refusing or delaying an infantry posting. The royalist rising of 13 Vendémiaire Year IV—5 October 1795—revived his fortunes. Serving under Paul Barras, he organized troops and artillery that defeated insurgents in Paris. Joachim Murat helped obtain cannon.

The phrase “a whiff of grapeshot,” often used for the episode, comes from Thomas Carlyle rather than a documented Napoleonic utterance. The repression involved military force and substantial casualties. It secured the Convention and brought Napoleon command of the Army of the Interior.

---

## The First Italian Campaign, 1796–1797

Napoleon married Joséphine on 9 March 1796 and departed almost immediately to command the Army of Italy.

His army was poorly supplied but strategically positioned against separate Piedmontese and Austrian forces. Major operations included:

- Montenotte, Millesimo, and Dego, April 1796
- Defeat of Piedmont and Armistice of Cherasco
- Crossing of the Po and victory at Lodi, May
- Entry into Milan
- Siege of Mantua
- Castiglione, August
- Arcole, November
- Rivoli, January 1797
- Advance toward Vienna
- Treaty of Campo Formio, October 1797

He displayed rapid concentration, aggressive maneuver, political improvisation, and an ability to motivate troops. He also benefited from capable subordinates, divided enemies, and French numerical or operational advantages at key moments.

### Political and financial dimensions

The Army of Italy financed itself through requisitions, indemnities, forced contributions, confiscation, and removal of artworks. Funds and valuables went to the French state, the army, officers, contractors, and private networks. Accounting was incomplete and corruption widespread. Napoleon acquired personal wealth and patronage resources, but precise separation between state, army, political, and private funds is often impossible.

French forces reorganized Italian territories into dependent republics. Napoleon negotiated treaties with limited supervision from the Directory. He sent artworks and manuscripts to France, forming part of the Louvre’s expanding collections. Supporters described this as legal treaty acquisition or revolutionary liberation; opponents described it as organized plunder.

His bulletins constructed a public image of astonishing victory. They sometimes exaggerated enemy casualties, minimized French losses, or converted reverses into triumphs. The joke “to lie like a bulletin” reflected contemporary awareness of their propaganda function.

---

## Paris, Diplomacy, and the Institute, 1797–1798

Returning to Paris, Napoleon was celebrated as the Republic’s leading general. He was elected to the Institut de France in December 1797, in the class associated with mechanics. The appointment reinforced his identity as a patron of science.

He considered invasion of Britain impractical without maritime superiority and supported an expedition to Egypt, intended to threaten British imperial routes, enhance French power in the Mediterranean, and create personal opportunity.

---

## Egypt and Syria, 1798–1799

### Invasion and scientific expedition

Napoleon departed Toulon in May 1798 with a large fleet, approximately 35,000–40,000 soldiers, and more than 150 scholars, engineers, artists, physicians, and technicians. Malta was seized in June. French forces landed near Alexandria in July.

At the Battle of the Pyramids, Napoleon defeated Mamluk forces and entered Cairo. Admiral Nelson destroyed much of the French fleet at Aboukir Bay on 1–2 August, isolating the expedition.

Napoleon founded the Institut d’Égypte. The accompanying scholars surveyed antiquities, natural history, engineering, geography, and society. Their work eventually produced the monumental *Description de l’Égypte*. Pierre-François-Xavier Bouchard’s unit discovered the Rosetta Stone in 1799; Britain acquired it after the French capitulation in 1801.

### Occupation and resistance

Napoleon issued Arabic proclamations claiming friendship with Islam and hostility to Mamluk oppression. He participated in or sponsored Muslim festivals and sought cooperation from religious notables. These were documented political acts. The later story that he sincerely converted to Islam is unsupported.

The occupation imposed taxes, requisitions, surveillance, and military government. Cairo revolted in October 1798. French forces bombarded districts, entered al-Azhar Mosque, executed participants, and conducted reprisals. Egyptian chronicler Abd al-Rahman al-Jabarti recorded killing, looting, rape, and religious violation. French and Egyptian accounts differ in emphasis but not on the violent suppression.

### Syrian campaign and Jaffa

In 1799 Napoleon marched through Sinai into Ottoman Syria, taking al-Arish, Gaza, and Jaffa. After Jaffa’s capture, French troops engaged in looting and violence against inhabitants.

Napoleon ordered the execution of a large body of Ottoman prisoners. Estimates range roughly from 1,500 to more than 3,000; still higher numbers appear in hostile literature. Some prisoners had allegedly previously been released on parole and taken up arms again. Napoleon’s stated rationale was inability to feed, guard, or release them during an advancing campaign. Those considerations explain the decision but do not make the killings disputed: the mass execution itself is documented.

The siege of Acre failed. Ottoman resistance, British naval assistance under Sidney Smith, plague, inadequate siege equipment, and French losses forced retreat.

### The plague controversy

Napoleon visited plague patients at Jaffa, an episode later monumentalized by Antoine-Jean Gros. The painting functioned as propaganda, presenting him as fearless and quasi-sacred.

**Allegation:** British and émigré accounts charged that Napoleon ordered sick French soldiers poisoned with opium during the retreat.

**Evidence:** French medical testimony confirms discussion of euthanasia or poisoning in the face of capture. Physician René-Nicolas Desgenettes stated that he rejected such a proposal. Whether poison was actually administered, to how many men, and under whose precise orders remains unresolved. Claims that Napoleon personally poisoned the patients are not established.

### Departure from Egypt

Napoleon left Egypt secretly in August 1799, transferring command to Jean-Baptiste Kléber. Critics called this abandonment of a stranded army; defenders note that he believed France faced political crisis and that command transfer was a conventional prerogative. The expedition ultimately capitulated in 1801.

Militarily and politically, the Egyptian project failed to displace Britain or retain Egypt. Scientifically and culturally, it accelerated European Egyptology while also exemplifying the connection between scholarly collection and colonial conquest.

## Coup of Brumaire and the Consulate, 1799–1804

Napoleon returned to France in October 1799. Emmanuel-Joseph Sieyès, Talleyrand, Lucien Bonaparte, Roger Ducos, military officers, and legislators participated in the conspiracy against the Directory.

On 18–19 Brumaire Year VIII—9–10 November 1799—the conspirators moved the legislative councils to Saint-Cloud under a claimed security emergency. Napoleon’s personal appearance before the councils went badly; Lucien, as president of the Council of Five Hundred, and armed troops proved decisive. Legislators were dispersed, and a smaller group approved a provisional consulate.

This was a **documented coup d’état**, later ratified through controlled constitutional procedures and plebiscites.

The Constitution of Year VIII created three consuls, but Napoleon as First Consul held predominant executive authority. The regime progressively reduced electoral choice, legislative independence, press freedom, and political pluralism.

### Administrative reconstruction

Measures associated with the Consulate included:

- Prefects appointed to administer departments
- Centralized tax assessment and collection
- Bank of France, founded in 1800 as a private institution with public functions
- Stabilization of the franc through the *franc germinal*
- Reorganization of the judiciary
- Council of State
- Lycées and a state-directed educational hierarchy
- Legion of Honour
- Support for infrastructure, roads, canals, ports, and cadastral work
- Peace with Austria through Lunéville, 1801
- Temporary peace with Britain through Amiens, 1802
- Concordat with Pope Pius VII, signed 1801 and promulgated with Organic Articles in 1802
- Codification of civil and criminal law

Napoleon did not personally invent every institution. Many reforms extended Revolutionary, monarchical, ministerial, or specialist projects. His role was to select, drive, revise, and politically impose them.

### Civil Code

The Civil Code was promulgated in 1804. Principal jurists included Jean-Étienne-Marie Portalis, François Denis Tronchet, Jacques de Maleville, and Félix-Julien-Jean Bigot de Préameneu. Napoleon attended many Council of State discussions and intervened actively.

The Code consolidated equality of male citizens before civil law, property rights, secular civil status, contractual freedom, and abolition of feudal privilege. It also reinforced paternal and marital authority. Married women had severely restricted legal capacity; adultery rules treated husbands and wives unequally; divorce was narrowed and later abolished under the Restoration. Workers were subjected to restrictive labor documentation and combinations remained prohibited.

### Concordat and religion

Napoleon was baptized Catholic and used Catholic ceremony, but his personal beliefs are difficult to define. He spoke in Catholic, deist, skeptical, providential, and pragmatic registers depending on context.

The Concordat recognized Catholicism as the religion of the great majority of French citizens but did not restore it as the exclusive established church. Bishops depended on state nomination; clergy received state salaries; church property confiscated during the Revolution was not returned.

The Organic Articles extended state control beyond what Rome had agreed. Napoleon later occupied papal territory and held Pope Pius VII under coercive detention. His religious policy was institutional and political more consistently than devotional.

### Political repression and surveillance

The regime censored newspapers, theater, and publishing; used police ministries under Fouché and Savary; maintained informant networks; detained or deported opponents; manipulated elections and plebiscite totals; and restricted open political organization.

After the “infernal machine” assassination attempt of December 1800, the government deported Jacobins despite evidence that royalists had organized the attack. This is a documented case in which political repression was directed beyond the perpetrators.

## Colonial Policy, Slavery, and Haiti

The National Convention had abolished slavery in French colonies in 1794, although implementation varied.

In 1802 Napoleon’s government sought restoration of French colonial authority and plantation production:

- The law of 20 May 1802 maintained slavery where abolition had not taken effect, notably Martinique and the Indian Ocean colonies.
- A consular order of 16 July 1802 restored the former slave regime in Guadeloupe after military repression.
- French policy in Saint-Domingue attempted to disarm autonomous Black leadership and reassert metropolitan control. Evidence from instructions and subsequent conduct supports the conclusion that restoration of slavery was an intended or probable outcome, despite tactical assurances to the contrary.
- The Leclerc expedition captured Toussaint Louverture through deception; he was deported to France and died imprisoned at Fort de Joux.
- Under Donatien de Rochambeau, French forces used mass drowning, execution, torture, and dogs in a racialized war. Haitian forces also committed atrocities. The French expedition was defeated, and Haiti declared independence on 1 January 1804.

**Documented fact:** Napoleon’s government preserved and restored slavery and the slave trade in French colonial possessions, accompanied by mass coercion and racial discrimination.

**Disputed characterization:** Claude Ribbe and others have called the colonial campaign a genocide and alleged that ship holds were used as sulfur-dioxide “gas chambers,” sometimes drawing comparisons with Nazi extermination.

**Assessment:** Historians studying the campaign document extreme atrocities and exterminatory rhetoric. Philippe Girard concluded that French actions only partly fit modern genocide criteria. The specific “gas chamber” and direct Nazi-lineage claims have not obtained broad scholarly acceptance. Rejecting those particular claims does not negate the documented restoration of slavery or atrocities.

During the Hundred Days in 1815 Napoleon decreed an end to the French slave trade. The measure was politically significant but came after his earlier restoration policy and during a brief return to power.

## From Life Consul to Emperor, 1802–1804

A 1802 plebiscite made Napoleon First Consul for life. Official results overwhelmingly favored the change, but the regime controlled administration and tabulation.

Royalist conspiracies continued. In March 1804 French agents seized **Louis Antoine, Duke of Enghien**, from Baden, outside French territory. A military commission tried him rapidly at Vincennes and sentenced him to death. He was executed during the night of 20–21 March.

**Documented fact:** Napoleon approved the seizure, trial machinery, and execution.

**Disputed issues:** Whether he personally issued a direct final shooting order, whether Enghien had any role in the current conspiracy, and how much ministers such as Talleyrand encouraged the decision. No convincing evidence connects Enghien to the plot for which he was effectively punished. The abduction violated Baden’s sovereignty, and the trial lacked normal safeguards.

On 18 May 1804 a senate decree proclaimed Napoleon Emperor of the French. A plebiscite endorsed hereditary empire. Pope Pius VII attended the coronation at Notre-Dame on 2 December. Napoleon crowned himself and then Joséphine, though interpretations that he snatched the crown from the pope dramatize a ceremony whose procedure was negotiated in advance.

---

## Empire and the War of the Third Coalition, 1804–1805

War with Britain had resumed in 1803. Napoleon assembled the Army of England near Boulogne but lacked naval control. The Franco-Spanish fleet’s defeat at Trafalgar on 21 October 1805 ended any immediate invasion prospect.

Napoleon redirected the Grande Armée eastward:

- Austrian forces capitulated at Ulm, October 1805.
- French forces occupied Vienna.
- Napoleon defeated Russian and Austrian armies at Austerlitz on 2 December.
- The Treaty of Pressburg weakened Austria.
- The Holy Roman Empire dissolved in 1806.

Austerlitz became central to Napoleon’s military legend. Later narratives sometimes overstate the battle’s predetermined perfection; nevertheless, his deception, concentration of force, and exploitation of Allied movement were decisive.

---

## Prussia, Poland, and Tilsit, 1806–1807

Prussia entered war in 1806. French forces defeated Prussian armies at Jena and Auerstedt. Napoleon entered Berlin and issued the Berlin Decree inaugurating the Continental System, intended to exclude British trade from European ports.

The winter Polish campaign produced the indecisive and bloody Battle of Eylau in February 1807. Victory over Russia at Friedland in June led to the Treaties of Tilsit.

Napoleon and Tsar Alexander I met on a raft in the Niemen River. They divided spheres of influence, while Prussia lost territory. Napoleon created the Duchy of Warsaw, stimulating Polish hopes without restoring a fully independent Poland.

Maria Walewska became Napoleon’s mistress during the Polish period. Later patriotic narratives described Polish elites pressuring her into the relationship for national advantage. Her degree of agency cannot be reconstructed confidently from romanticized memoir traditions.

---

## Iberia and the “Spanish Ulcer,” 1807–1814

French troops entered Portugal in 1807 under treaty arrangements with Spain. Napoleon exploited conflict inside the Spanish royal family, summoned Charles IV and Ferdinand VII to Bayonne, induced abdications, and installed Joseph Bonaparte as king.

The resulting uprising and war transformed the Peninsula:

- Madrid uprising and French repression, 2–3 May 1808
- Spanish victory at Bailén, July 1808
- British intervention under Arthur Wellesley
- Napoleon’s personal campaign in Spain, late 1808
- Prolonged guerrilla warfare
- Sieges, reprisals, executions, famine, requisition, and atrocities by French, imperial-allied, Spanish, Portuguese, and British forces
- French withdrawal and restoration of Ferdinand VII

Napoleon’s imposition of Joseph lacked durable legitimacy. Some Spanish *afrancesados* supported administrative and constitutional reform, but occupation and violence drove mass resistance. Napoleon later called Spain an ulcer that destroyed him; the exact wording and context vary among Saint Helena recollections.

---

## Austria and Dynastic Empire, 1809–1811

Austria renewed war in 1809. Napoleon suffered a major reverse at Aspern-Essling, where Marshal Jean Lannes was mortally wounded. He recovered and won at Wagram in July.

The Treaty of Schönbrunn imposed further Austrian losses. Napoleon divorced Joséphine and married Marie Louise in 1810. Their son was born on 20 March 1811.

At its maximum extent, Napoleon’s system included territories directly annexed to France, dependent kingdoms, allied states, military occupations, and relatives placed on thrones. His brothers and marshals did not always obey him. Louis resisted Continental System policies damaging Holland; Napoleon annexed Holland in 1810. Lucien remained estranged over marriage and political independence. Murat eventually defected in an effort to preserve Naples.

---

## Russia, 1812

Relations with Russia deteriorated over the Continental System, Poland, commercial policy, and competing spheres of influence. Napoleon crossed the Niemen in June 1812 with a multinational army commonly estimated at more than 400,000 in the initial invasion group and larger if all supporting formations are counted.

Major events:

- Advance through Lithuania and Belarus
- Severe losses from heat, disease, desertion, hunger, and logistics before the main battle
- Battle of Smolensk
- Battle of Borodino, 7 September: immense casualties without destruction of the Russian army
- Occupation of Moscow
- Moscow fire, whose precise mixture of Russian policy, disorder, and accidental causes remains disputed
- Failed peace overtures
- Retreat beginning in October
- Battles at Maloyaroslavets, Vyazma, Krasnoi, and the Berezina
- Cold, starvation, disease, combat, and collapse of supply
- Napoleon’s departure from the army in December after learning of the Malet coup attempt in Paris

The invasion destroyed the Grande Armée as an effective multinational force. Exact losses depend on who is included, but deaths, captures, disappearances, and desertions reached several hundred thousand. The popular image that winter alone defeated Napoleon is a myth: logistics, disease, strategic overextension, Russian resistance, scorched resources, battle losses, and delayed retreat all contributed.

---

## Collapse, 1813–1814

Napoleon raised new armies in 1813 but could not fully replace experienced cavalry, officers, and horses.

- Victories at Lützen and Bautzen
- Armistice, followed by Austria joining the coalition
- French victory at Dresden
- Defeat at Leipzig, 16–19 October 1813
- Retreat across the Rhine
- Allied invasion of France in 1814

During the 1814 campaign Napoleon won several tactical victories, notably in the Six Days’ Campaign, but could not compensate for Allied numerical superiority, political exhaustion, and the fall of Paris.

The Senate declared him deposed. Marshals refused continued resistance. Napoleon abdicated conditionally on 4 April and unconditionally on 6 April. Under the Treaty of Fontainebleau he retained the title of emperor and sovereignty over Elba, received a guard and promised pension, and was separated from Marie Louise and his son.

At Fontainebleau he attempted suicide with poison carried since the Russian campaign, according to testimony from Caulaincourt and others. He survived. Details differ, but the episode is generally accepted as documented through close witnesses.

---

## Elba, 1814–1815

Napoleon arrived on Elba in May 1814. He resided principally at the Palazzina dei Mulini in Portoferraio and at San Martino.

As sovereign he reorganized administration, roads, mines, agriculture, military forces, and court ceremony. The island’s revenues were inadequate, and the Bourbon government did not reliably pay the pension promised at Fontainebleau.

He received visits from his mother, Pauline, officials, agents, and Maria Walewska with their son. Joséphine had died before she could visit.

Learning of Bourbon unpopularity and fearing removal to a more remote location, Napoleon escaped Elba on 26 February 1815.

---

## The Hundred Days and Waterloo, 1815

Napoleon landed near Cannes on 1 March. Troops sent to arrest him joined him. Louis XVIII fled, and Napoleon entered Paris on 20 March.

He attempted to present a more liberal regime through the Additional Act drafted with Benjamin Constant. Former opponents doubted the conversion, while European powers declared him outside the peace settlement and mobilized.

Napoleon moved into Belgium to defeat Wellington’s and Blücher’s armies before they combined:

- Ligny, 16 June: Napoleon defeated the Prussians but did not destroy them.
- Quatre Bras: Ney failed to secure a decisive separation.
- Waterloo, 18 June: Wellington’s army resisted French attacks until Prussian forces arrived in strength.

The defeat resulted from combined Allied action, delayed operations, command problems, French tactical failures, Prussian recovery, and the strategic imbalance of the wider coalition. Claims that hemorrhoids, rain, one subordinate, or one tactical decision alone caused defeat are reductive.

Napoleon abdicated for the second time on 22 June. He considered escape to the United States but British naval control prevented it. At Rochefort he surrendered to Captain Frederick Maitland aboard HMS *Bellerophon*, hoping for asylum in Britain.

Britain and its allies instead classified him as a political prisoner and exiled him to Saint Helena. Later legal scholarship has questioned the status and legality of indefinite detention after hostilities, but Napoleon was not given a criminal trial.

---

## Saint Helena, 1815–1821

Napoleon reached Saint Helena in October 1815. He briefly stayed at the Briars with the Balcombe family, then moved to Longwood House.

His household included:

- Henri-Gatien Bertrand and family
- Charles-Tristan de Montholon and family
- Gaspard Gourgaud
- Emmanuel de Las Cases and his son
- Louis Marchand
- Louis-Étienne Saint-Denis, called “Ali”
- Other servants and attendants

The relationship with British governor Sir Hudson Lowe was antagonistic. Disputes concerned Napoleon’s title, surveillance, visitors, expenses, correspondence, movement, and living conditions. Longwood was damp and exposed, but later descriptions sometimes exaggerated it into a dungeon. Napoleon had a household, servants, horses, books, gardens, and limited freedom within prescribed boundaries; he was nevertheless under involuntary and closely monitored detention.

He dictated accounts of his campaigns and political ideas, read extensively, gardened, received visitors, quarreled with companions, and constructed a posthumous interpretation of his rule. Las Cases’s *Mémorial de Sainte-Hélène* became the most influential vehicle of the liberal and national Napoleonic legend, but it is a mediated work shaped by Las Cases, memory, political strategy, and publication after exile.

### Final illness

Napoleon’s health declined markedly from 1817–1821. Reported symptoms included abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, weakness, weight changes, constipation, and eventually gastrointestinal bleeding. Physicians disagreed over diagnosis and treatment.

He dictated and signed his will on 15 April 1821, followed by codicils. He distributed money, objects, and claims on his private domain among relatives, companions, servants, officers, and others. Some large bequests depended on assets or claims that were never fully realized.

He died on 5 May 1821. The autopsy the following day found extensive disease of the stomach. Contemporary reports differ in terminology and detail but strongly converge on a large ulcerating lesion consistent with gastric cancer.

## Employment, Institutions, Technologies, and Projects

Napoleon was a professional artillery officer, general, head of government, legislator, dynastic sovereign, military planner, patron of science, and propagandist. He was not a conventional entrepreneur or inventor and held no known patents.

### Military system

He inherited and adapted Revolutionary military institutions:

- Corps capable of semi-independent movement
- Mass conscription
- Concentrated artillery
- Staff coordination through Berthier
- Rapid operational movement
- Living from requisitioned territory
- Advancement based partly on performance
- Imperial Guard as reserve, political symbol, and patronage institution

He did not single-handedly invent the corps system or modern warfare. His distinction lay in integrating existing developments with personal command speed, intelligence analysis, political authority, and mass mobilization.

### Scientific and technical patronage

Projects and institutions associated with his government included:

- Institut d’Égypte
- *Description de l’Égypte*
- École Polytechnique and engineering education
- Bureau des Longitudes and scientific administration
- Decimal and metric reforms, though implementation was inconsistent
- Bridges, roads, canals, ports, military arsenals, and urban projects
- Arc de Triomphe, Vendôme Column, Rue de Rivoli, and other commemorative construction
- Cadastral survey
- Rewards for inventions and industrial substitution during the Continental System
- Continued development of semaphore telegraph networks
- Support for vaccination, medical institutions, and statistical administration

The Rosetta Stone and Egyptological publications became major intellectual consequences of an otherwise failed occupation.

### Legal and administrative projects

The Napoleonic codification program included:

- Civil Code, 1804
- Code of Civil Procedure, 1806
- Commercial Code, 1807
- Code of Criminal Procedure, 1808
- Penal Code, 1810

These were collective juristic productions enacted under Napoleon’s authority, not books personally authored by him.

## Wealth, Finances, Property, and Financial Networks

Napoleon entered the army from a family with noble status but limited cash. His rise transformed the Bonapartes into one of Europe’s richest ruling families.

### Sources of state and dynastic wealth

- Crown civil list
- Imperial domains
- Conquered and annexed property
- War indemnities and forced contributions
- Confiscated estates
- Tribute and occupation expenses
- Distribution of titles, estates, and *dotations*
- The *domaine extraordinaire*, funded substantially by conquered territories
- Italian and German revenues
- Artworks, manuscripts, horses, and luxury property transferred under treaty or coercion

The imperial civil list was fixed in the tens of millions of francs annually; published figures commonly cite 25 million francs, but civil-list revenue, palace expenditure, military treasure, extraordinary domain, and Napoleon’s private purse were not cleanly separated by modern accounting standards.

### Patronage and family finance

Napoleon allocated kingdoms, principalities, estates, pensions, marriage settlements, and offices to siblings, marshals, ministers, and servants. He also monitored family expenditure and complained about extravagance.

Joséphine’s debts and spending repeatedly caused conflict. Her divorce settlement preserved a costly establishment. Marie Louise received dynastic property and later the duchies of Parma, Piacenza, and Guastalla through the postwar settlement.

### Corruption and contracting

Military supply under the Directory and Empire involved contractors, bankers, requisition agents, and officials whose accounts were frequently opaque. Napoleon prosecuted or pressured some financiers while also using extralegal demands and politically dependent credit networks.

There is no established judicial finding that Napoleon personally committed a modern offense of fraud or money laundering. There is, however, documented systemic appropriation of occupied territories and commingling of state, military, dynastic, and personal power.

### Testamentary estate

Napoleon’s will invoked funds and claims totaling very large sums, including a claim of 200 million francs against the French state connected to savings from his civil list. Whether that amount represented recoverable private property was contested. Many legacies were delayed, reduced, or administered through later Bonapartist arrangements.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Corsican phase

Youthful Napoleon identified intensely with Corsica and Paoli. His writings criticized French rule and imagined historical regeneration under a Corsican political community.

### Revolutionary phase

By 1793–1794 he was a French republican officer associated with Jacobin political language. *Le Souper de Beaucaire* defended the Revolutionary government against federalist resistance. His loyalty was also professional and strategic: France offered the career Corsica could not.

### Consular authoritarianism

After Brumaire he claimed to preserve the Revolution’s practical gains while ending faction, parliamentary instability, and ideological experimentation. His preferred political formula combined:

- Equality before law
- Careers theoretically open to talent
- Secure property
- Religious settlement
- centralized administration
- plebiscitary legitimacy
- executive supremacy
- restricted press and political participation

### Imperial monarchy

The Empire added hereditary rule, nobility, court hierarchy, dynastic marriages, ceremonial sacrality, and territorial domination. Napoleon argued that hereditary authority stabilized revolutionary society. Critics regarded it as military despotism and personal usurpation.

### Nationality and empire

Napoleonic government spread uniform administration, legal equality for men, secularization, and abolition of some feudal structures. It also imposed conscription, taxation, censorship, economic controls, and foreign rule. Local reception varied sharply. National movements sometimes drew on French reforms while mobilizing against French occupation.

### Religion

Napoleon treated Catholicism, Protestantism, Judaism, and—in Egypt—Islam as institutions to be organized by the state. His recorded comments show fascination with religion’s social and political power. Claims that he secretly converted to Islam, died an orthodox unbeliever, or underwent a simple Catholic reconversion are all too categorical. He received Catholic ministrations near death, but the character of his personal faith remains unresolved.

### Art and propaganda

Napoleon used:

- Official bulletins
- Medals and coins
- Monumental architecture
- Court portraiture
- Jacques-Louis David’s paintings
- Antoine-Jean Gros’s campaign imagery
- Theater censorship
- Controlled newspapers
- Ritual entries, coronations, and victory celebrations
- Selective publication and commemoration

He understood imagery as government. Later Saint Helena dictation extended this project into posthumous self-defense.

## Controversies, Accusations, Violence, and Unresolved Questions

## 1. Coup d’état and plebiscitary manipulation

**Documented fact:** Napoleon came to power through the military-supported coup of Brumaire and progressively concentrated power.

**Documented fact:** Official plebiscites reported overwhelming support.

**Historical finding:** Voting and tabulation lacked modern independence; officials inflated or managed results. Exact genuine support cannot be reconstructed, though Napoleon plainly possessed substantial popularity at several stages.

## 2. Censorship and police government

**Documented fact:** The number of Paris newspapers was sharply reduced; publication, theater, and printing were licensed or supervised; political suspects were watched, detained, or deported.

There was no single totalitarian apparatus in the twentieth-century sense, but “authoritarian police state” is a defensible analytical description when carefully defined.

## 3. Duke of Enghien

**Documented fact:** Abduction from Baden, summary military proceeding, and execution.

**Unproven allegation at the time:** Enghien’s involvement in the active assassination conspiracy.

**Unresolved:** Exact balance of initiative among Napoleon, Talleyrand, Savary, and other officials. Ultimate political responsibility rested with Napoleon.

## 4. Jaffa prisoners

**Documented fact:** Napoleon ordered mass execution of surrendered Ottoman prisoners in March 1799.

**Disputed:** Exact number, prisoner status, and whether all had violated prior parole.

**Not disputed:** The killings occurred under his authority.

## 5. Jaffa plague poisoning

**Allegation:** Napoleon ordered opium administered to incapacitated plague victims.

**Evidence for:** Testimony that he or staff raised the proposal; British and émigré accounts; later recollections.

**Evidence against or limiting:** Desgenettes said he refused; direct proof of implementation is absent; later propaganda amplified the accusation.

**Status:** Unresolved allegation, not established mass poisoning.

## 6. Egypt and Syria

**Documented fact:** Occupation forces imposed heavy extraction and violently suppressed resistance. Egyptian chronicles report killings, looting, rape, executions, mosque violations, and collective punishment.

**Context:** Insurgent forces also killed French personnel and local minorities. This does not erase French command responsibility for French actions.

## 7. Slavery and colonial racial policy

**Documented fact:** Napoleon’s government maintained or restored slavery, revived the slave trade, sent military expeditions to restore colonial control, and imposed racial restrictions.

**Documented fact:** The Saint-Domingue and Guadeloupe campaigns involved mass atrocities.

**Disputed legal-historical label:** “Genocide.” Some scholarship sees partial conformity to modern genocide criteria; most specialists do not accept claims of a centrally designed extermination program equivalent to twentieth-century genocide.

## 8. War casualties and responsibility

Estimates of deaths associated with the Revolutionary and Napoleonic wars vary from several million upward depending on dates, geography, civilian mortality, and attribution.

It is not methodologically sound to assign every death solely to Napoleon: the wars involved coalitions, pre-existing Revolutionary conflicts, state interests, insurgencies, and independent decisions by many governments. It is equally unsound to absolve him: he initiated or escalated several campaigns, most conspicuously Iberian intervention and the Russian invasion, and repeatedly chose war to sustain an expansive imperial system.

## 9. Looting of art and cultural property

**Documented fact:** French armies transferred artworks, manuscripts, antiquities, and scientific collections from occupied states to France under armistices, treaties, requisitions, and coercion.

After 1815, Allied powers recovered many objects; others remained in French collections. Whether described as lawful treaty cession or plunder depends partly on the coercive validity attributed to the treaties.

## 10. Women and the Civil Code

**Documented fact:** Napoleonic civil law subordinated married women to husbands, restricted property and legal independence, treated adultery unequally, and strengthened paternal power.

Claims that Napoleon alone invented these provisions overstate personal authorship; claims that the Code was simply emancipatory omit its gender hierarchy.

## 11. Jews and the “Infamous Decree”

Napoleon extended civic integration in some conquered territories and organized Jewish consistories. The Assembly of Jewish Notables and Great Sanhedrin sought declarations reconciling Jewish religious practice with French citizenship.

The decree of 17 March 1808—called the *décret infâme*—restricted Jewish lending and, in some regions, commercial and conscription rights. It introduced a discriminatory exception to general civil equality. Its provisions were temporary, but its existence complicates portrayals of Napoleon as either straightforward emancipator or straightforward persecutor.

The alleged 1799 proclamation inviting Jews to restore a state in Palestine is regarded as a forgery or unsupported text circulated in later messianic and political contexts. Napoleon never reached Jerusalem.

## 12. Spain and dynastic coercion

**Documented fact:** Napoleon coerced Spanish Bourbon abdications and placed his brother on the throne.

Reformist statutes did not overcome the legitimacy crisis created by foreign occupation. Reprisals, counter-reprisals, executions, and guerrilla warfare caused extensive civilian suffering.

## 13. Treatment of Pope Pius VII

**Documented fact:** Napoleon annexed papal territories and had Pius VII removed and detained after the pope excommunicated those responsible. Later negotiations occurred under coercion.

## 14. Financial corruption

**Documented fact:** The regime extracted occupied Europe and maintained opaque military and extraordinary funds.

**Not established:** A judicially proven personal scheme equivalent to modern fraud, embezzlement, or money laundering.

Napoleon alternately attacked corrupt contractors, compelled unofficial payments, and benefited from a system without clear boundaries between sovereign and private resources.

## 15. Suicide attempt

**Reported fact with strong contemporary support:** Napoleon attempted suicide by poison at Fontainebleau in April 1814.

Details about dosage, vomiting, physician intervention, and the poison’s age vary.

## 16. Legality of Saint Helena confinement

Napoleon surrendered to Britain but was not tried. The Allies treated him as a special international danger. Modern scholarship questions whether he could legally be held indefinitely as a prisoner of war after peace, but no court adjudicated his status.

## 17. Recent controversy and adversarial search findings, 2024–2026

Recent publications and public controversies continue to concentrate on:

- restoration of slavery and the legality of the 1802 Guadeloupe order;
- violence in Haiti, Guadeloupe, Egypt, Syria, Spain, and occupied Europe;
- the distinction between documented colonial atrocity and disputed genocide claims;
- the *décret infâme* and the limits of Jewish emancipation;
- authoritarian invocations of phrases falsely or uncertainly attributed to Napoleon;
- cinematic fabrication in Ridley Scott’s *Napoleon*;
- continued polarization between heroic, national, colonial, and anti-imperial memories.

The searches produced no credible new lawsuit, criminal indictment, financial investigation, or newly authenticated evidence against Napoleon himself in 2024–2026. Modern legal reporting using “Napoleon” frequently concerned unrelated persons or metaphorical “Napoleons.” Absence of such proceedings is unsurprising for a person who died in 1821.

## Health, Injuries, Habits, and Psychological Crises

Reported medical conditions include:

- Possible scabies contracted at Toulon, though diagnosis is retrospective
- Recurrent digestive discomfort
- Urinary complaints
- Episodes of fever and infectious disease exposure
- Hemorrhoids, whose role at Waterloo is often exaggerated
- Injuries or bruising from falls and battle exposure, none conclusively disabling
- Increasing obesity or altered body composition in later imperial years
- Severe gastric illness at Saint Helena
- Probable gastric cancer

Napoleon was not documented as addicted to alcohol or opiates. He drank wine diluted with water and used tobacco snuff. He reportedly slept in short blocks during campaigns but could also sleep for long periods when circumstances allowed.

Descriptions of mania, narcissistic personality disorder, epilepsy, pathological short-man compensation, or other modern psychiatric diagnoses are retrospective speculation. His attempted suicide in 1814 is evidence of an acute crisis after political and dynastic collapse, not by itself proof of a chronic psychiatric disorder.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## “Napoleon was unusually short”

**Story:** Napoleon was a tiny man whose conquests compensated for short stature.

**Origins and circulation:** British caricature, especially James Gillray’s “Little Boney,” emphasized a diminutive, tantrum-prone figure. Confusion between French and British measurement systems reinforced the story.

**Evidence:** Recorded French measurements place him around 1.68–1.69 meters, approximately average or slightly above average for a French male of his period. His elite guards were selected for height, making him appear smaller.

**Status:** Myth derived from caricature and measurement confusion.

**Effect:** Produced the modern phrase “Napoleon complex,” a psychological stereotype unsupported as a diagnosis of Napoleon.

## The snowball battle at Brienne

**Story:** The schoolboy Napoleon organized classmates with military genius during an elaborate snowball fight.

**Evidence:** Later recollections by former pupils support some form of organized snow fighting. Biographers and Abel Gance transformed it into prophecy of future command.

**Status:** Reported childhood episode enlarged into hagiographic foreshadowing.

## “Whiff of grapeshot”

**Story:** Napoleon described his destruction of the Vendémiaire rising as a “whiff of grapeshot.”

**Evidence:** The famous formulation comes from Thomas Carlyle’s later history.

**Status:** Literary phrase, not an authenticated quotation.

## Crossing the Alps on a rearing horse

**Story:** Napoleon crossed the Great St Bernard dramatically on a rearing charger.

**Evidence:** Jacques-Louis David’s portrait is an idealized political image. Napoleon crossed on a mule or sure-footed mount, dressed for travel. The army faced difficult logistics, but the iconic moment is invented.

**Status:** State-sponsored visual mythology.

## Personal heroism at Arcole

**Story:** Napoleon carried a flag across the bridge under fire and personally turned the battle.

**Evidence:** He did expose himself near the fighting and entered dangerous marshy ground. The precise bridge-centered heroic scene was reconstructed and amplified through official art and memoir.

**Status:** Based on a real hazardous episode, converted into a simplified hero image.

## Firing cannon at the pyramids

**Story:** Napoleon’s artillery struck or blew apart a pyramid.

**Evidence:** No such event occurred. Ridley Scott’s 2023 film knowingly used the image.

**Status:** Modern cinematic invention.

## “Forty centuries look down upon you”

**Story:** Before the Battle of the Pyramids, Napoleon told his troops that forty centuries looked down on them.

**Evidence:** The remark appears through later recollection rather than a securely contemporary transcript. Its precise wording is uncertain.

**Status:** Plausible tradition, not securely authenticated verbatim.

## Conversion to Islam

**Story:** Napoleon adopted Islam or intended to do so in Egypt.

**Evidence:** He used Islamic references, attended celebrations, praised Muhammad in some contexts, and cultivated clerics. Private and later comments show instrumental religious politics and Enlightenment distance from dogmatic conversion.

**Status:** Unsupported as literal conversion; documented as political accommodation.

## Jewish proclamation from Jerusalem

**Story:** Napoleon issued a proclamation from Jerusalem inviting Jews to reclaim Palestine.

**Evidence:** The army never reached Jerusalem; no authenticated original has been established. Research traces versions to later messianic or Frankist circulation.

**Status:** Forgery or later propaganda.

## Poisoning plague victims at Jaffa

**Story:** Napoleon personally ordered all plague patients murdered.

**Evidence:** A proposal involving opium is supported by testimony; implementation remains unproved. Numbers and circumstances expanded in British propaganda.

**Status:** Unresolved allegation surrounded by hostile myth.

## Arsenic murder at Saint Helena

**Story:** Hudson Lowe, Montholon, or another conspirator slowly poisoned Napoleon with arsenic.

**Origins:** Elevated arsenic in hair samples, motive speculation, wallpaper chemistry, and popular murder narratives.

**Evidence against:** Arsenic was present in hair from periods before Saint Helena and in hair associated with relatives; environmental arsenic was common. Autopsy reports describe advanced gastric cancer. Modern medical studies find cancer the best explanation.

**Status:** Conspiracy theory; not supported by the balance of forensic evidence.

## Substitution of the body

**Story:** Napoleon’s corpse was secretly replaced before the 1840 exhumation.

**Evidence:** Based on discrepancies among coffin and burial descriptions. No credible physical or documentary evidence establishes substitution.

**Status:** Conspiracy narrative.

## Napoleon as sole author of modern Europe

**Story:** Napoleon personally created the Civil Code, meritocracy, secular government, modern education, nationalism, and administrative rationality.

**Evidence:** His regime decisively promulgated or extended many institutions, but most drew on Revolutionary, monarchical, juristic, and local precedents and collective work.

**Status:** Heroic simplification.

## Napoleon as uniquely responsible for every Napoleonic War death

**Story:** All European warfare from 1799–1815 was the unilateral product of one man.

**Evidence:** Napoleon initiated major aggressions and maintained an expansionist empire; other states also formed coalitions, pursued territorial interests, broke agreements, and continued conflicts originating before his seizure of power.

**Status:** Polemical over-attribution. It should not be replaced by the opposite apologetic myth that he fought only defensive wars.

## Prophecy and destiny

Napoleon and his propagandists cultivated providence, fortune, the star of destiny, Caesar, Charlemagne, and Alexander as symbolic associations. After his fall, admirers recast him as Prometheus, martyr, lawgiver, and champion of nationality. Enemies depicted him as Antichrist, ogre, racial outsider, criminal, and insatiable conqueror.

These incompatible images became part of the historical Napoleonic phenomenon independently of their factual accuracy.

## Works by Napoleon Bonaparte

The boundary between “authored,” “dictated,” “signed,” and “issued under authority” is essential. Napoleon produced letters, notes, essays, proclamations, military orders, constitutional interventions, dictated memoirs, and literary attempts. He did not personally draft every decree or code bearing his name.

### Youthful and literary writings

- **Various school notebooks and historical essays**, 1780s. Genuine manuscripts or copies survive; generally published posthumously.
- **“Sur l’amour de la patrie” and related Corsican or historical reflections**, 1780s. Genuine youthful material, posthumously collected.
- **Projected history of Corsica**, late 1780s–early 1790s. Unfinished; surviving fragments and correspondence.
- **“Lettre à Matteo Buttafoco”**, 1791. Political pamphlet attacking Buttafoco and defending Corsican patriotic positions.
- **“Discours sur le bonheur” / Lyon Academy prize essay**, 1791. Genuine, unsuccessful competition essay.
- **Le Souper de Beaucaire**, 1793. Political dialogue defending the Republican government in the federalist crisis; published with official assistance.
- **Clisson et Eugénie**, written 1795. Unfinished romantic novella, reconstructed from scattered manuscripts and published posthumously.
- **“Dialogue sur l’amour” and related fragments.** Attribution varies by manuscript and edition.

### Correspondence

- **Correspondance de Napoléon Ier publiée par ordre de l’Empereur Napoléon III**, 32 volumes, 1858–1869. Major posthumous state edition; selective, edited, and incomplete.
- **Correspondance générale de Napoléon Bonaparte**, Fondation Napoléon/Fayard, 15 volumes, 2004–2018. Modern critical edition containing approximately 40,500 letters, with doubtful and false items treated critically.
- **Letters to Joséphine**, numerous editions. Genuine letters, though older popular editions may silently modernize, omit, or misdate material.
- **Political, diplomatic, military, and administrative correspondence**, dispersed through French and international archives.

Secretaries drafted many documents from oral instruction; Napoleon revised or signed them. “Authorship” may therefore mean personal handwriting, dictation, approved draft, or sovereign issuance.

### Proclamations, bulletins, and official political texts

- Proclamations to the Army of Italy, 1796–1797.
- Egyptian proclamations in French and Arabic, 1798–1799. Translation and propagandistic adaptation complicate wording.
- Bulletins of the Army of Italy.
- Bulletins of the Army of the Orient.
- *Bulletins de la Grande Armée*, especially 1805–1814.
- Speeches to legislative bodies, the Council of State, troops, and public delegations.
- Imperial decrees, orders, senatus-consulta, and proclamations issued under his name. Many were drafted by ministers or councils.
- Abdication instruments of April 1814 and June 1815.
- The Additional Act of 1815: primarily drafted by Benjamin Constant under Napoleon’s authority, not solely authored by Napoleon.

### Dictated memoirs and Saint Helena works

- **Mémoires pour servir à l’histoire de France sous Napoléon**, published 1822–1825. Campaign narratives dictated on Saint Helena to Bertrand, Gourgaud, Montholon, Marchand, Saint-Denis, and others, then edited and published posthumously.
- **Campaign of Italy memoirs.**
- **Egypt and Syria memoirs.**
- **Memoirs of Elba and the Hundred Days.**
- **Précis des guerres de César**, dictated commentary connecting Napoleon’s strategy with Caesar’s campaigns.
- **Notes sur l’art de la guerre and military commentaries**, assembled in later editions.
- **Commentaires de Napoléon Ier**, posthumous editorial collections drawn from dictated or written material.
- **Political and historical notes dictated at Longwood.**
- **Testament**, 15 April 1821, and seven codicils. Genuine legal and autobiographical documents preserved in archival copies and originals.

### Works published under or associated with his name but not solely authored

- **Code civil des Français / Code Napoléon**, 1804: collective juristic and legislative work strongly directed and promoted by Napoleon.
- **Other imperial codes:** collective government products.
- **Mémorial de Sainte-Hélène**, Emmanuel de Las Cases, 1823: not authored by Napoleon. It records conversations and dictations selected and shaped by Las Cases.
- **Maximes de guerre de Napoléon:** posthumous selection and arrangement from correspondence and memoirs.
- **Collections of “Napoleon quotations”:** highly unreliable unless traced to a dated document. Many famous sayings are paraphrases, translations, or later inventions.

### Artworks, inventions, patents, and recordings

No authenticated body of professional visual art, musical composition, film, sound recording, invention, or patent by Napoleon is known. His surviving maps, sketches, diagrams, marginalia, and dictated plans are documentary rather than an independent artistic or patent corpus.

## Death, Autopsy, and Competing Medical Accounts

The autopsy was conducted on 6 May 1821 by Francesco Antommarchi in the presence of British physicians. Reports described:

- a large ulcerated or cancerous lesion of the stomach;
- extensive destruction or alteration of the gastric lining;
- adhesion near the liver;
- a near-perforation or perforation sealed by adjoining tissue;
- enlarged local lymphatic structures in some accounts;
- blood or dark material in the stomach.

Terminology differed because microscopic pathology was not yet available. Modern retrospective studies conclude that advanced gastric carcinoma is the most coherent diagnosis.

### Arsenic findings

Hair attributed to Napoleon showed elevated arsenic. This initially encouraged poisoning theories. Later comparisons found arsenic in hair from earlier periods and in samples associated with his family. Arsenic exposure was common through pigments, medicines, cosmetics, wine treatments, and environmental sources.

The arsenic data do not establish homicidal poisoning. Neither wallpaper vapor nor deliberate dosing explains the autopsy as well as gastric cancer.

### Last words

Versions include “France, armée, tête d’armée, Joséphine” and variants. They were recorded by attendants under emotionally and medically difficult circumstances. The general cluster is plausible; no exact formula should be treated as a stenographic certainty.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Saint Helena legend

Through Las Cases and other companions, Napoleon was recast as:

- defender of revolutionary equality;
- victim of monarchies;
- prophet of nationality;
- liberal ruler prevented from completing reform;
- romantic exile and martyr.

This selectively deemphasized censorship, dynastic expansion, colonial policy, and military coercion. It proved politically powerful under the Bourbon Restoration.

### Return of the ashes

King Louis-Philippe’s government arranged the return of Napoleon’s remains in 1840. The ceremony attempted to appropriate Bonapartist memory for the July Monarchy. It instead strengthened popular fascination.

### Bonapartism

Bonapartism combined strong executive authority, military prestige, popular plebiscite, administrative centralization, national grandeur, social mobility, and direct appeal over representative institutions. Napoleon III adapted this inheritance to nineteenth-century mass politics.

### Legal and administrative influence

Napoleonic codes or derivatives influenced Belgium, the Netherlands, Italy, parts of Germany, Poland, Switzerland, Spain, Latin America, Louisiana, Quebec’s legal development indirectly, and other civil-law systems.

The Empire also consolidated:

- prefectural administration;
- centralized education;
- state statistical practice;
- secular civil registration;
- merit-based but politically controlled honors;
- modern conscription and military administration.

Institutional survival varies and should not be confused with unchanged direct descent.

### Military influence

Napoleon became a central case study for Clausewitz, Jomini, staff colleges, operational historians, and modern commanders. His career illustrated both concentration and maneuver and the dangers of unlimited political objectives, coalition warfare, logistical overreach, and strategic exhaustion.

### National and colonial memories

- **France:** alternating admiration, republican unease, military cult, legal commemoration, and debate over dictatorship and slavery.
- **Britain:** caricatured enemy, later romanticized worthy opponent.
- **Germany and Italy:** conqueror and reformer whose domination helped stimulate national reaction.
- **Poland:** associated with hopes for restored statehood, tempered by his instrumental treatment of Polish aspirations.
- **Spain and Portugal:** occupier whose invasion produced resistance, constitutional change, devastation, and imperial repercussions.
- **Russia:** invader defeated by army, society, geography, logistics, and climate.
- **Egypt:** remembered ambivalently as invader and as catalyst of European Egyptology and contested narratives of modernization.
- **Haiti and Guadeloupe:** associated with the attempted destruction of emancipation, restoration of slavery, and racial war.
- **Jewish history:** both civic integration and coercive state reorganization, including discriminatory measures.

### Historical reinterpretation

Major interpretive traditions include:

- **Heroic:** military genius, lawgiver, social modernizer.
- **Liberal-national:** carrier of revolutionary equality and national awakening.
- **Republican-critical:** destroyer of the Republic and architect of personal dictatorship.
- **Conservative:** restorer of authority, religion, hierarchy, and social order.
- **Marxist/social:** stabilizer of bourgeois property after revolutionary upheaval.
- **International:** imperial aggressor whose domination elicited coalition and national resistance.
- **Colonial and postcolonial:** restorer of slavery, racial ruler, and invader whose scientific projects were inseparable from conquest.
- **Cultural:** expert manufacturer of celebrity, visual sovereignty, and self-authored memory.

No single interpretation accounts for the coexistence of civil equality for men, authoritarian rule, administrative modernization, dynastic hierarchy, scientific patronage, mass warfare, religious settlement, colonial slavery, and enduring myth.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by documentary or reputable historical evidence are included. Shared chronology alone is excluded.

### Empress Joséphine

**Evidentiary label:** Documented fact.

Napoleon met Marie-Josèphe-Rose de Beauharnais in Directorial Paris in 1795, married her on 9 March 1796, crowned her empress on 2 December 1804, and divorced her dynastically in December 1809. Their correspondence documents intense attachment, conflict, infidelity, financial disputes, political partnership, and continued affection after divorce.

### Jean-Jacques Rousseau

**Evidentiary label:** Documented intellectual influence.

Napoleon read Rousseau closely in youth. His Corsican writings, political vocabulary, and historical reflections show Rousseau’s influence. He later reassessed Rousseau critically and reportedly remarked that the French Revolution might not have occurred without him. The connection was intellectual; Rousseau died before Napoleon’s public career.

### Gaius Julius Caesar

**Evidentiary label:** Documented intellectual and symbolic influence.

Napoleon studied Caesar’s campaigns, invoked Caesar in political and military comparison, and dictated the *Précis des guerres de César* at Saint Helena. Imperial propaganda and hostile critics both compared the two men.

### Alexander the Great

**Evidentiary label:** Documented intellectual and symbolic influence.

Alexander served as an acknowledged model of conquest, eastern empire, battlefield command, and heroic biography. Napoleon discussed Alexander in reading notes and Saint Helena conversations. Later art and historiography reinforced the comparison.

### Charlemagne

**Evidentiary label:** Documented dynastic-symbolic connection.

Napoleon consciously adopted Carolingian symbolism, visited Aachen, invoked Charlemagne in imperial legitimacy, and presented the French Empire as a continental succession distinct from the Bourbon monarchy.

### Johann Wolfgang von Goethe

**Evidentiary label:** Documented fact.

Napoleon met Goethe at Erfurt on 2 October 1808 in the presence of Daru and Talleyrand. They discussed literature, particularly *The Sorrows of Young Werther* and French drama. Napoleon decorated Goethe with the Legion of Honour. Goethe remained fascinated by Napoleon after the Empire’s fall.

### Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel

**Evidentiary label:** Reported fact/documented observation, not a personal meeting.

Hegel saw Napoleon in Jena in October 1806 and described him in correspondence as the “world-soul” on horseback. No personal conversation between them is established. The connection is a documented act of observation and philosophical interpretation.

### Jean-Baptiste Joseph Fourier

**Evidentiary label:** Documented institutional and professional connection.

Fourier joined Napoleon’s Egyptian expedition, served in the Institut d’Égypte, undertook administrative and scientific work, and later became prefect of Isère under the Consulate and Empire. He wrote the historical preface to the *Description de l’Égypte*.

### Pierre-Simon Laplace

**Evidentiary label:** Documented professional and political connection.

Laplace participated in the scientific institutions within Napoleon’s orbit. Napoleon appointed him minister of the interior after Brumaire in November 1799 but replaced him after approximately six weeks with Lucien Bonaparte. Napoleon later made him a senator and count. The famous exchange in which Laplace allegedly told Napoleon that he had no need of the “hypothesis” of God exists in varying retrospective versions and should not be treated as verbatim certainty.

### Joseph-Louis Lagrange

**Evidentiary label:** Documented institutional connection.

Lagrange was a leading mathematician of the Institut and received honors under Napoleon, including senatorial office and imperial titles. Napoleon publicly valued his scientific standing. Claims of close personal intimacy would overstate the record.

### Siméon Denis Poisson

**Evidentiary label:** Documented institutional connection.

Poisson rose within the French mathematical and educational institutions that Napoleon patronized and reorganized, including the École Polytechnique. A significant personal relationship with Napoleon is not established.

### Alexander von Humboldt

**Evidentiary label:** Reported institutional and political intersection.

Humboldt lived and worked extensively in Paris during Napoleon’s rule and interacted with leading imperial scientists. Napoleon’s government viewed his Prussian connections with suspicion, and Napoleon reportedly ordered or contemplated his removal from Paris. Evidence for a substantial personal relationship is weak; the connection is principally through imperial scientific and police networks.

### Thomas Jefferson

**Evidentiary label:** Documented diplomatic connection; no personal meeting established.

As president of the United States, Jefferson’s administration negotiated the Louisiana Purchase with Napoleon’s government in 1803 through Robert Livingston, James Monroe, Talleyrand, and François Barbé-Marbois. Napoleon authorized the sale. Direct personal correspondence or a meeting between Napoleon and Jefferson is not established in the consulted sources.

### Benjamin Franklin

**Evidentiary label:** Documented intellectual-symbolic connection only.

Napoleon admired aspects of Franklin’s scientific and republican reputation and inherited a French political culture in which Franklin was prominent. Franklin died in 1790; no meeting is documented.

### George Washington

**Evidentiary label:** Documented ceremonial connection; no meeting.

After Washington’s death in December 1799, First Consul Bonaparte ordered French military mourning observances and praised him publicly. They never met.

### Marie-Joseph Paul Yves Roch Gilbert du Motier, Marquis de Lafayette

**Evidentiary label:** Documented political connection.

Lafayette opposed Napoleon’s concentration of power and later participated in political action surrounding the second abdication in June 1815. Their relationship was political rather than intimate. Napoleon alternately courted, distrusted, and criticized him.

### Louis XVI

**Evidentiary label:** Institutional connection, personal meeting unestablished.

Napoleon received royal educational support as a king’s scholar under institutions of Louis XVI’s monarchy and became an officer in the royal army. His father had an audience with Louis XVI. No securely documented personal meeting between Napoleon and Louis XVI has been established.

### Francisco de Miranda

**Evidentiary label:** Reported political and social connection.

Miranda moved in Revolutionary and Directorial French circles and encountered leading officials and officers. Some accounts report meetings or social contact with Bonaparte, but the scope and substance are less securely documented than their shared institutional environment. No durable collaboration resulted.

### Simón Bolívar

**Evidentiary label:** Documented observation and political influence; no personal meeting established.

Bolívar was in Paris during the imperial period and witnessed or closely observed Napoleonic public ceremony, commonly associated with the 1804 coronation and/or the 1805 Italian coronation in later recollection. Napoleon’s rise and self-coronation influenced Bolívar both positively and negatively as a model of power. A direct meeting is not established.

### José de San Martín

**Evidentiary label:** Documented indirect military connection.

San Martín served in Spanish forces fighting Napoleonic France during the Peninsular War, including at Bailén. No personal meeting with Napoleon is documented. The connection is adversarial and institutional.

### Manuel Belgrano

**Evidentiary label:** Documented indirect political connection.

Napoleon’s invasion of Spain and the captivity of Ferdinand VII created the imperial crisis that helped precipitate the Río de la Plata revolutions in which Belgrano became a leader. No personal relationship or correspondence is established.

### Bernardo O’Higgins

**Evidentiary label:** Documented indirect political connection.

The Napoleonic occupation of Spain contributed to the crisis of imperial legitimacy underlying Chilean independence. No meeting or correspondence with Napoleon is documented.

### Antonio José de Sucre

**Evidentiary label:** Documented indirect historical connection only.

Sucre’s independence career arose from the Spanish imperial crisis intensified by Napoleon’s intervention. No personal contact is documented.

### Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart

**Evidentiary label:** No direct connection established.

Mozart died in 1791 before Napoleon’s rise to power. Although music by Mozart circulated in Napoleonic Europe, the consulted sources do not support a meaningful personal or explicitly acknowledged intellectual connection.

### Ludwig van Beethoven

Beethoven is not included in the supplied atlas roster, but he is often invoked in Napoleonic mythology. He originally associated the *Eroica* Symphony with Bonaparte and withdrew the dedication after Napoleon assumed the imperial title. This note is included only to clarify a conspicuous non-roster relationship and is not counted as an Atlas entry.

### Abraham Lincoln, Karl Marx, Friedrich Nietzsche, Victor Hugo, Alexandre Dumas, Benito Mussolini, and later atlas figures

**Evidentiary label:** Posthumous influence where documented; no lifetime crossing.

Napoleon died before these figures’ mature activity or, in some cases, before their birth. Several studied, represented, criticized, or appropriated him:

- **Alexandre Dumas** was the son of General Thomas-Alexandre Dumas, whose relationship with Napoleon became antagonistic; Alexandre later wrote extensively about the Napoleonic era.
- **Victor Hugo** transformed from a royalist childhood stance to an important interpreter of the Napoleonic legend.
- **Karl Marx** used Bonapartism as a political category in analyzing Napoleon III.
- **Friedrich Nietzsche** repeatedly considered Napoleon as a type of exceptional European power.
- **Benito Mussolini** appropriated Roman and Napoleonic imagery.
- **Abraham Lincoln** lived during the continuing global Napoleonic legend but had no personal connection.

These are posthumous reception links, not biographical meetings.

### Roster audit conclusion

The clearest direct atlas crossings are Joséphine, Goethe, Fourier, Laplace, Lagrange, Lafayette, and numerous political or intellectual relationships mediated by institutions and texts. Many roster members were ancient, medieval, geographically remote, or born after Napoleon’s death. No link has been inferred merely from shared geography or fame.
