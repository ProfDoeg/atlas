## Works by Felix Klein

Klein is a hard case for the question "what is a text?" His most famous production, the Erlangen Program, is not a treatise or a book in any ordinary sense but a programmatic accession document, written for a professorial appointment and only loosely connected to the inaugural lecture he actually delivered. Several of his most influential "books" are not things he sat down and wrote for the press at all, but lecture courses taken down, organized, and published by students or colleagues, sometimes decades after the lectures were given and, in a few cases, after Klein's own death. And a large share of his documented output is editorial and institutional rather than authorial: a journal he ran rather than wrote, an encyclopedia he organized rather than composed, a reform movement he led through memoranda and committee work rather than treatises.

### The Erlangen Program: a manifesto, not a treatise

In October 1872, as a condition of his appointment to a full professorship at Erlangen at age twenty-three, Klein submitted *Vergleichende Betrachtungen über neuere geometrische Forschungen* ("A Comparative Review of Recent Researches in Geometry"), the document later known as the Erlangen Program. Its claim is organizational rather than a single proof: that a geometry can be characterized by a group of transformations together with the properties that remain unchanged (invariant) under that group, so that Euclidean, affine, projective, and the various non-Euclidean geometries can all be placed within one classificatory scheme instead of being treated as separate, unrelated subjects. Popular accounts often say Klein delivered this as his inaugural lecture; the archival record shows otherwise, the written Program and the spoken inaugural address of 7 December 1872 (which was about teaching) were two different occasions. The Program's influence came less from presenting new theorems than from supplying later geometers and physicists with a vocabulary, "the geometry belonging to this group," that proved durable well into the twentieth century.

### Research monographs

- **1882:** *Über Riemann's Theorie der algebraischen Functionen und ihrer Integrale*. An exposition of Riemann's function theory recast in Klein's own geometric and physically-motivated terms, connecting potential theory and conformal mapping to the study of algebraic curves. Klein himself later regarded his work in function theory, not the Erlangen Program, as the high point of his mathematical research.
- **1884:** *Vorlesungen über das Ikosaeder und die Auflösung der Gleichungen vom fünften Grade* ("Lectures on the Icosahedron and the Solution of Equations of the Fifth Degree"). A genuine monograph, not merely transcribed lectures, developing the connection between the symmetry group of the icosahedron, invariant theory, and a complete method for solving the general quintic equation, building on and surpassing earlier partial approaches by Hermite and Kronecker.

### Large collaborative treatises

Several of Klein's most substantial books were produced jointly, over years, with former students who supplied much of the sustained writing while the research program and organizing ideas were Klein's:

- **1890–1892:** *Vorlesungen über die Theorie der elliptischen Modulfunktionen*, two volumes, with Robert Fricke.
- **1897–1912:** *Vorlesungen über die Theorie der automorphen Funktionen*, two volumes, again with Fricke, extending the automorphic-function program that had grown out of Klein's competitive correspondence with Henri Poincaré in the early 1880s.
- **1897–1910:** *Über die Theorie des Kreisels* ("On the Theory of the Top"), four volumes, with the physicist Arnold Sommerfeld, applying Klein's geometric and group-theoretic methods to the mechanics of the spinning gyroscope, an example of his later turn toward applied mathematics and mathematical physics.

In each case, calling the result simply "a Klein book" understates the co-author's share of the actual writing, and calling it "a Fricke book" or "a Sommerfeld book" would understate whose research program and whose name the public associated it with.

### Lecture-derived publications during his lifetime

Much of Klein's output reached print only because a student, editor, or stenographer worked up his spoken lectures into a text, with Klein's supervision varying from close to minimal:

- **1894:** *The Evanston Colloquium: Lectures on Mathematics*, Klein's twelve lectures at Northwestern University (28 August–9 September 1893), edited by Alexander Ziwet. In substance a survey of contemporary research (Clebsch, Lie, algebraic curves, non-Euclidean geometry) aimed at an American audience; in practice, also an effective recruiting pitch for graduate study at Göttingen.
- **1895:** *Vorträge über ausgewählte Fragen der Elementargeometrie*, worked out from Klein's lectures by F. Tägert and translated into English as *Famous Problems of Elementary Geometry*.
- **1900s onward:** *Elementarmathematik vom höheren Standpunkte aus* ("Elementary Mathematics from an Advanced Standpoint"), grown out of Klein's university courses for future secondary-school teachers. It is organized around his diagnosis of the "double discontinuity" in teacher training, that students experience university mathematics as disconnected from the school mathematics they learned, and then are expected to teach school mathematics as if their university training were irrelevant, and argues for reconnecting elementary arithmetic, algebra, analysis, and geometry to modern concepts rather than teaching either in isolation. Its publication history runs across several volumes and editions, some finished only after Klein's death, so individual printings need to be checked for how much is Klein's own text and how much is later editorial completion.

### Editorial and institutional projects

Klein's name attaches to several large projects that he organized, recruited for, and steered, but did not write in the ordinary authorial sense:

- ***Mathematische Annalen***. Founded by Alfred Clebsch and Carl Neumann, the journal passed increasingly under Klein's direction after Clebsch's death in 1872. Klein used it to promote complex analysis, algebraic geometry, invariant theory, and group theory, and to build an international alternative to Berlin's Crelle's Journal. Editorial decisions were made by a small team, not by Klein alone; his contribution here is institutional judgment and recruitment, not authored text.
- ***Encyklopädie der mathematischen Wissenschaften mit Einschluss ihrer Anwendungen*** (1898 into the 1930s). A systematic multi-author survey of the whole of mathematics and its applications, for which Klein was a principal initiator, organizer, and editor rather than a contributor of original articles throughout. The project outlived him; a planned final volume on the history, philosophy, and didactics of mathematics was never finished, disrupted by the First World War.
- ***Abhandlungen über den mathematischen Unterricht in Deutschland*** (1909–1916, five volumes) and related reform publications, commissioned multi-author series produced under the German mathematics-education reform movement that Klein led, and which fed into his 1908–1920 presidency of the International Commission on Mathematical Instruction.
- Editorial work on **Gauss's scientific diary**. In 1901 Klein was involved in publishing and annotating the *Notizen-Journal* that Carl Friedrich Gauss had kept privately between 1796 and 1814, a historical-editorial contribution to another mathematician's Nachlass rather than an original work of Klein's own, but a telling instance of Klein's broader role as an organizer and custodian of mathematics's own documentary record.

### Research papers late in his career

Klein did not stop producing original research after his 1882 health collapse redirected his energies toward institutions and education. In 1918, in retirement, he published three papers engaging directly with Einstein's general relativity and with David Hilbert's work on the foundations of physics, including studies of conservation laws for energy and momentum in general-relativistic terms, work that intersected with the emergence of Emmy Noether's theorems at Göttingen.

### Collected works, autobiography, and posthumous lecture volumes

- **1921–1923:** *Gesammelte mathematische Abhandlungen*, three volumes, prepared with collaborators while Klein was alive and organized by subject rather than by chronology, a deliberate authorial act of self-collection rather than a purely posthumous compilation.
- **1923:** *Göttinger Professoren (Lebensbilder von eigener Hand): Felix Klein*, a first-person autobiographical essay, a genuine memoir but, like all autobiography, retrospective and selective.
- **1926–1933, posthumous:** Several further lecture courses were edited into print only after Klein's 1925 death, including *Vorlesungen über höhere Geometrie* (edited by Wilhelm Blaschke), the two-part *Vorlesungen über die Entwicklung der Mathematik im 19. Jahrhundert* ("Lectures on the Development of Nineteenth-Century Mathematics," edited by Richard Courant with Otto Neugebauer and then Stefan Cohn-Vossen), *Vorlesungen über nichteuklidische Geometrie* (edited by Walther Rosemann), and *Vorlesungen über die hypergeometrische Funktion*. The nineteenth-century-mathematics lectures in particular, delivered at his home during the First World War when illness had confined him, treat mathematics as a network of people, schools, and institutions rather than a sequence of isolated theorems, which is also how Klein had spent the second half of his career practicing it.
