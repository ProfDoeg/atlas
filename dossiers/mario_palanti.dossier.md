# Mario Palanti: Research Dossier

## Scope, method, and evidentiary conventions

This dossier concerns the Italian architect, designer, painter, sculptor, architectural theorist, inventor, and sometime property investor Mario Palanti, principally active in Italy, Argentina, and Uruguay.

The surviving record is asymmetrical. Palanti’s buildings, drawings, publications, patents, institutional petitions, and attempts to obtain political patronage are comparatively well documented. His childhood, intimate life, health, and final years are much less fully documented. The principal modern reconstruction is Virginia Bonicatto’s doctoral dissertation, *Mario Palanti. La búsqueda de una nueva arquitectura*, which draws on Palanti’s family papers, the Archivio Centrale dello Stato, the Getty Research Institute, professional records in Argentina, and contemporary architectural periodicals. Claims traceable only to Palanti’s retrospective curriculum vitae are identified as such because he repeatedly used autobiographical publications for professional promotion.

Evidentiary labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by an archival record, contemporary publication, official register, surviving object, or reliably catalogued primary document.
- **Reported fact:** stated in serious secondary scholarship but not independently verified here against the underlying record.
- **Self-reported claim:** made by Palanti in a curriculum vitae, publication, letter, or promotional text.
- **Allegation or claim:** an accusation made by an identified person or institution without an established adjudication.
- **Disputed interpretation:** a scholarly or popular interpretation for which the evidence is contested.
- **Rumor or myth:** a narrative circulated without adequate contemporary documentary support.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Mario Palanti.
- **Expanded form sometimes found in the book trade:** Mario Vittorio Palanti. The middle name “Vittorio” appears in some cataloguing and commercial descriptions but is not consistently used in the principal archival and scholarly records; it should therefore be treated as **reported, not securely established here**.
- **Professional styles and titles:** Architetto; Cavaliere and later Commendatore dell’Ordine della Corona d’Italia; Commendatore dell’Ordine di San Gregorio Magno; Accademico di San Luca; member of the Pontificia Insigne Accademia dei Virtuosi al Pantheon; officer in the Italian military engineers.
- **Birth:** 20 September 1885, Milan, Kingdom of Italy.
- **Death:** Milan, Italy, in September 1978.
  - Most biographical authorities and catalogues give **4 September 1978**.
  - Bonicatto’s documentary chronology gives **6 September 1978**.
  - Some older catalogues and the Getty finding aid incorrectly give **1979**.
  - Until a civil death certificate or cemetery register is produced, 4 September 1978 is the best-supported conventional date, with 6 September retained as a documentary discrepancy.
- **Burial:** Civico Mausoleo Palanti, Cimitero Monumentale di Milano.
- **Nationality:** Italian. Palanti spent long periods in Argentina but apparently did not acquire Argentine citizenship.
- **Principal occupations:** architect, architectural draughtsman, urban and monumental-project designer, painter, sculptor, writer, editor, inventor of prefabricated and dry-construction systems, and property investor.
- **Principal regions of activity:** Milan, Rome, Trieste, Stresa, Buenos Aires, Montevideo, Rosario, Mar del Plata, Paysandú, and other locations in Argentina and Uruguay.
- **Best-known buildings:** Palacio/Passage Barolo in Buenos Aires; Palacio Salvo in Montevideo; Hotel Castelar in Buenos Aires; Palacio Chrysler, later Palacio Alcorta, in Buenos Aires; Roccatagliata buildings in Buenos Aires.
- **Associated technologies and trade names:** Maison Universelle Palanti, Casa Universale Palanti, Palanthome, Sistema Palanti, and Palandomus.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and origins

**Documented or strongly reported facts:**

Mario Palanti was born into a comparatively modest Milanese family with roots in the Cremonese commune of Casalbuttano ed Uniti.

His father was **Giovanni Palanti**, described as a carpenter. Giovanni died in Milan on 8 June 1920.

His mother was **Virginia De Gaspari** or **De Gasperi**, described as a dressmaker. The surname varies in published accounts and catalogues. She died in Milan on 7 April 1906.

The craft occupations of his parents offer a plausible material background for Mario’s later attention to ornament, interiors, construction components, and total design, but no surviving source consulted here records him explicitly attributing his career to either parent.

### Siblings and extended artistic family

Mario’s elder brother was **Giuseppe Palanti** (1881–1946), a painter, theatre and costume designer, illustrator, poster artist, and teacher at the Brera Academy. Giuseppe painted a portrait of Mario in 1924 and, at Mario’s request, painted a portrait of Pope Pius XI for the Apostolic Nunciature in Buenos Aires.

Giuseppe married Ada Romussi. Their children included the architect **Giancarlo Palanti** and the painter Maria Virginia Palanti. Mario was therefore the uncle of Giancarlo Palanti, later an important modern architect in Italy and Brazil.

The painters **Riccardo Palanti** and Paolo Palanti belonged to another branch of the family; Riccardo is described as a cousin of Mario and Giuseppe.

Mario and Giuseppe’s relationship deteriorated badly. Bonicatto’s chronology records a serious dispute in 1938 and another in 1942. When Giuseppe died in 1946, Mario reportedly refused permission for him to be buried in the Palanti family mausoleum. The precise substance of the quarrel remains insufficiently reconstructed in the accessible record.

### Marriages, separations, and children

#### María Elena Castagnino

Palanti married **María Elena Castagnino** in Rosario, Argentina, on 31 July 1933. She belonged to a socially prominent Rosario family of Genoese origin.

The marriage rapidly broke down. In early 1934 the couple were staying in Mar del Plata. Archival notes cited by Bonicatto state that Palanti abruptly left, took money and a horse called *Campeón*, and sailed for Italy. The horse was intended as a gift to Benito Mussolini. Mussolini’s secretariat rejected it after learning that it belonged to Palanti’s wife and that the couple were in conflict.

This is a **documented marital dispute and unsuccessful political-gift episode**, not evidence of theft established by a court. No judicial disposition of the money or horse has been found in the sources consulted.

The ultimate legal status of the marriage to Castagnino is unclear in the accessible sources.

#### Nahir Marazzi Castiglione

On 7 January 1943, aged fifty-seven, Palanti married **Nahir Marazzi Castiglione**, then aged forty, in Milan.

After their Milan residence was affected by Allied bombing in August 1943, they moved to Stresa Borromeo. They later returned to Milan and travelled together to Argentina in 1948.

Nahir returned to Italy without Palanti in 1952. The couple legally separated by mutual agreement in 1957. In 1966 and again in 1969, an impoverished Palanti sought financial support from her. He requested an annual sum of 4.2 million lire for food and living expenses and accused her of leaving the marital home without gratitude. These were **Palanti’s claims in a private financial and marital conflict**; no court judgment establishing wrongdoing by Nahir was found.

No reliably documented children of either marriage were identified. If Palanti had children, they do not appear in the principal reconstruction consulted here.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1885–1904

Little verifiable information survives about Palanti’s childhood, schooling before Brera, friendships, or early residences beyond Milan.

His family’s limited means are repeatedly noted in modern biographies. Palanti nevertheless entered the rich artistic and architectural environment of turn-of-the-century Milan, where the Brera Academy, the Politecnico, international exhibitions, Gothic-revival scholarship, Lombard medievalism, and debates over a modern national architecture converged.

The later prominence of his elder brother Giuseppe at Brera probably strengthened Mario’s access to artistic circles, although the exact role Giuseppe played in Mario’s admission or education is not documented.

## Education and Formation, approximately 1904–1910

### Brera and the Milanese architectural milieu

Palanti began formal artistic study around 1904–1905. He studied painting and architectural drawing at the **Accademia di Belle Arti di Brera**. A 1909 Brera credential identified in scholarship was a diploma qualifying him as a professor of architectural drawing.

He also attended or was associated with the architecture school of the **Politecnico di Milano**. Popular biographies often say that he graduated as an architect from the Politecnico in 1909. The more careful academic record emphasizes the 1909 Brera diploma, while book dealers have supplied inconsistent graduation dates, including an implausible 1904. The exact degree title and institution should therefore be handled cautiously.

His teachers included:

- **Gaetano Moretti**, architect and professor, who became Palanti’s most important identifiable mentor and selected or employed him in connection with the Italian pavilion in Buenos Aires.
- **Lodovico Pogliaghi or Cesare Tallone-era Brera circles**, relevant to the institution’s decorative culture, although a direct instructional relationship was not verified here.
- **Giuseppe Mentessi**, identified in scholarship as one of his painting teachers.
- **Ettore Ferrari**, identified as a teacher in the Brera context.
- **Giulio Carotti**, professor of architectural history at Brera and author of the introduction to Palanti’s 1917 monograph.

### Intellectual and stylistic formation

Palanti’s education belonged to the tradition shaped by **Camillo Boito**: historical styles were not merely to be copied but studied for their constructive logic and adapted to contemporary needs. The writings and designs of **Eugène Viollet-le-Duc**, especially his structural interpretation of Gothic architecture, were formative reference points.

Palanti developed a personal repertory combining:

- Lombard Romanesque and Gothic forms;
- Renaissance and Mannerist composition;
- Art Nouveau and Symbolist ornament;
- reinforced-concrete construction;
- American skyscraper scale;
- European commercial galleries;
- monumental sculpture;
- explicitly Catholic, national, Masonic, Fascist, and later Peronist or universal-peace symbolism, depending on patron and period.

This combination was never a stable “style.” His practice moved between conventional façades for conservative clients, intensely ornamental rental buildings, advanced concrete towers, propagandistic monuments, and later simplified or quasi-rationalist schemes.

### Early competitions and distinctions

Before leaving Italy, Palanti produced student projects for churches, convents, portals, exchanges, and monuments. These included a votive church under the motto *Justus ut palma florebit* and an alpine convent.

He received the **Premio della Fondazione Clericetti**, according to Buenos Aires municipal research.

Palanti sent work to the 1910 Brussels International Exhibition and received a gold-medal diploma. Some biographies date the honor earlier; the best documentary chronology places it in 1910.

## Migration to Argentina and Early Career, 1910–1916

### Italian Pavilion and arrival

Palanti arrived in Argentina on **1 May 1910**, according to his 1958 curriculum vitae. Some summaries give 1909, probably conflating preparations for the centennial exhibition with his physical arrival.

He travelled as part of an official Italian mission for the centenary of the May Revolution. Palanti’s retrospective list named Senator and engineer Luigi Luiggi, Senator Silvestri, Gaetano Moretti, Count De Visart, Piccinini, Ragnoli, and Ravà among the delegation. Palanti and his compatriot **Francisco/Francesco Gianotti** supervised construction and decoration of the Italian Pavilion, designed under Moretti’s authority.

Palanti claimed that the pavilion was completed in three months and received a grand-prize diploma from the Argentine government. The pavilion and the exhibition introduced him to Italian immigrant patrons and the rapidly expanding Buenos Aires construction market.

### Prins and Ranzenhofer

After the exhibition, Palanti remained in Buenos Aires because of professional opportunities. From approximately 1910 to 1912 he worked in the office of **Arturo Prins and Oskar Ranzenhofer**. He later claimed that he directed a studio handling more than sixty buildings, though that managerial description is **self-reported**.

Within this office he collaborated on the neo-Gothic building for the University of Buenos Aires Faculty of Law on Las Heras, later used by the Faculty of Engineering. He encountered **Francisco Gianotti** and **Alejandro Virasoro** in this professional environment.

In 1911 he entered the competition for the Faculty of Medicine in Rosario, using the motto *Pro Humanitate*, and received a mention. He also designed an unexecuted scheme to relocate Buenos Aires’s Pirámide de Mayo.

### Independent office

By 1912 Palanti had opened an independent office at **Avenida de Mayo 695**, Buenos Aires.

Projects and buildings attributed to his first independent phase include:

- a church for Tigre;
- a projected National Museum of Natural History;
- a petit hôtel at Rodríguez Peña 1650;
- the David Spinetto vault door in Recoleta Cemetery;
- Francesco/F. Cassotana house, Tucumán 1146;
- Costaguta petit hôtel, Uruguay 640–646;
- rental building at Avenida Rivadavia 1916, associated with Giuseppe Peretti;
- Bernardo Vila house, Avenida Rivadavia 2625;
- Cine Presidente Roca, Avenida Rivadavia 3736;
- A. Grimoldi house, Avenida Corrientes 3400;
- Ginocchio hotel or rental building at Pueyrredón and Bartolomé Mitre;
- Alberto Grimoldi chalet, Avenida Rivadavia 5050;
- Villa Vasena project in San Isidro;
- projected Mercado de Abasto;
- bank-branch designs for the Nuevo Banco Italiano;
- a projected Buenos Aires cathedral;
- houses, villas, kiosks, commercial buildings, mausoleums, and medievalizing fantasies.

Several street numbers and owner names vary among later lists. Some projects survive only in Palanti’s own illustrated books and may never have advanced beyond drawings.

### First individual exhibition

On **3 November 1916**, Palanti opened what was advertised as the first personal exhibition of architecture in Argentina, at the Palacio de Bellas Artes under the national fine-arts authorities. It included buildings, monumental fantasies, drawings, decorative studies, and sculpture.

The exhibition helped establish the self-created image that followed him for the rest of his career: Palanti as an architect-artist of total works, equally capable of technical construction and visionary monuments.

## First World War Service, 1916–1919

Following his exhibition, Palanti returned to Italy and joined the military engineers. Sources variously call the formation the *Arma del Genio* and place him at Trieste.

He became a **sottotenente**, or second lieutenant, in 1917 and served at Trieste in 1918. His 1958 curriculum described him as decorated in the field; the exact decoration has not been independently identified. He was later promoted in the reserve and by 1937 described as a captain.

In July 1918 he presented Prime Minister **Vittorio Emanuele Orlando** with a vast memorial project, *Mausoleo glorificando gli eroi nostri di terra, del mare, del cielo*. It was exhibited in Trieste in January 1919. This project established themes that would recur in the Mole Littoria, Palazzo del Littorio, universal-peace monuments, and later 600-metre towers: collective sacrifice, heroic ascent, national rebirth, and architecture at an enormous scale.

On **11 February 1919**, before returning to Argentina, Palanti was initiated as an apprentice in the **Fratelli Bandiera Masonic lodge in Milan**. This is a documented lodge affiliation. It does not establish later claims that he belonged to a medieval “Fede Santa” order or that his patrons were members of the same lodge.

## Return to the Río de la Plata and Professional Ascendancy, 1919–1923

Palanti returned to Argentina in 1919 or early 1920. His own curriculum said 1920 after demobilization; Bonicatto’s chronology places the return in 1919. He encountered a postwar economic contraction but soon obtained major commissions.

### Buildings and clients, 1919–1924

Constructed works and commissions from this period included:

- Juan B. Uboldi house, Avenida Rivadavia 2460;
- luxury apartment house for Lorenzo Raggio, Libertad and Viamonte;
- Ganduglia/Canduglia petit hôtel, Arenales 2275;
- Merlo residence or *palazzina*;
- Merlo and Peluffo building, Arenales and Junín;
- Carmelo Novello petit hôtel, Avenida Montes de Oca 141;
- headquarters of the Unión de la Boca mutual-aid society, Olavarría and Palos;
- Banco Francés e Italiano para la América del Sur, Perón and San Martín;
- Emilio Valsecchi commercial palace;
- Aranda y Zavaleta factory;
- rental and commercial buildings on Corrientes and Rivadavia;
- Azaretto-Spato petit hôtel, Viamonte 1871;
- buildings for Andrés Roccatagliata at Santa Fe and Callao;
- Nicolás Bruzzone house, Libertad 145;
- the Apostolic Nunciature at Riobamba and Arenales;
- numerous projected churches, schools, hotels, theatres, mausoleums, and cheap-housing schemes.

His clientele was disproportionately drawn from prosperous Italian or Italian-Argentine industrial, commercial, and property-owning circles. This immigrant network gave him commissions on a scale that he had not secured in Italy.

## Palacio Barolo, 1919–1923

### Commission and construction

Textile industrialist and landowner **Luigi/Luis Barolo** commissioned Palanti to design a rental and office skyscraper on Avenida de Mayo. Work began around 1919–1920. The tower was inaugurated on 7 July 1923.

The structure, generally described as approximately 100 metres high with twenty-two principal levels, was among the first Buenos Aires buildings of its height and was constructed with a reinforced-concrete frame. It contained offices, commercial space, a public passage, multiple elevators, elaborate metalwork, lighting fixtures, and a lighthouse.

Palanti designed not merely the massing but many interior details: elevator cages, handles, lamps, ornament, and circulation elements. The building combined the European passage or commercial gallery with the American skyscraper.

The reported construction cost was approximately **4.5 million Argentine pesos**, with roughly 650,000 kilograms of steel, 3.5 million bricks, and 70,000 barrels of Portland cement. These figures come from later municipal accounts and should be understood as reported project statistics.

### Ownership and finance

Barolo intended to occupy only part of the building and rent the remainder. The project was therefore a private income-producing real-estate operation as well as a monument.

Palanti himself later owned income property at Corrientes 1463–1469 and at Victoria and Sarandí. The documentary chronology also records his investment in five Berlin rental houses in 1922 on the advice of his brother Giuseppe.

In 1938 the Pasaje Barolo was auctioned. The sources consulted do not fully reconstruct the financial causes, ownership changes, mortgage structure, or Palanti’s personal financial exposure.

### Contemporary reception

The periodical *El Arquitecto* predicted in 1920 that the Barolo would make an epoch on Avenida de Mayo. Other architects reacted negatively.

**Víctor Julio Jaeschke**, writing ironically in *Revista de Arquitectura*, treated the building as aesthetically harmful to the city. **Alberto Prebisch**, in *Martín Fierro*, and **Alejandro Christophersen**, in the journal of the Sociedad Central de Arquitectos, criticized Palanti’s work and professional posture. Their attacks combined aesthetic objections with hostility toward the socially successful immigrant “parvenu.”

The building became a visible fault line between academic classicism, emerging modernism, immigrant patronage, speculative real estate, and Palanti’s personalized eclecticism.

## Palacio Salvo, 1922–1928

### Competition and patrons

In March 1922 Palanti entered the competition for a major building at 18 de Julio, Andes, and Plaza Independencia in Montevideo. He was announced as the winner in August.

The patrons were the textile-industrialist brothers **Ángel, José, and Lorenzo Salvo**. They had acquired the site of the famous Confitería La Giralda and initially contemplated a grand hotel.

Construction began in 1923 and continued through 1928. Palanti worked with engineer **Lorenzo Gori Salvo**. The Getty collection preserves correspondence, postcards, photographs, approximately 180 blueprints, interior studies, structural details, elevator plans, and marble, joinery, lighting, and ventilation designs.

### Design and use

The building combined shops, passageways, offices, residential or hotel spaces, entertainment facilities, and a tower. Its final height is variously reported at approximately 95–105 metres depending on the measuring method and the now-altered crown.

It was more programmatically complex and more prominent in the Montevideo skyline than the Barolo. Palanti’s drawings document extraordinary attention to marble cladding, wood panelling, elevator doors, stairs, ventilation, shopfronts, and at least twenty-eight lighting-fixture types.

A large sign attached to the construction around 1927 read “El Batllismo lucha por la felicidad,” placing the private skyscraper within the political symbolism of Uruguayan Batllismo. This does not establish Palanti as a Batllista; it documents the building’s use in that political environment.

### Reception and structural rumors

Like the Barolo, the Salvo attracted predictions that its unusual form was structurally unsafe. Palanti later referred to malicious talk about static deficiency and cited the building’s survival as refutation. No evidence was found of a formal engineering finding that it was unsound.

The Salvo was declared a National Historic Monument of Uruguay in 1996.

## Professional Organizations, Publishing, and Institutional Patronage

Palanti participated with De Pretis, Gianotti, and Locati in the editorial council of *Arquitectura y Arte Decorativo*, an illustrated architectural periodical published at least through 1926.

His books functioned simultaneously as:

- portfolios;
- architectural manifestos;
- promotional catalogues;
- political offerings;
- records of constructed work;
- vehicles for monumental projects;
- instruments for attracting clients and official patronage.

In 1921 he was named an ordinary academician of Brera, according to the documentary chronology. In 1923 he became a member of the Accademia di San Luca by merit.

He established scholarships and prizes:

- a scholarship for architecture students at the Reale Scuola di Architettura in Rome, founded around 1921;
- the **Fondazione Palanti**, funded in 1921–1922 with a reported 371,000 lire;
- a further donation of 91,000 lire connected with architectural study and the Holy See;
- a prize named for Pope Pius XI, intended for children of Italians born in Argentina and Uruguay;
- an annual architectural prize named for Benito Mussolini in 1923;
- a triennial prize at the Reale Accademia d’Italia, for which he pledged a reported 120,000 lire in 1931.

A royal decree of 12 May 1927 authorized the Mario Palanti Foundation at the Royal School of Architecture in Rome to accept donations and approved its revised statute.

The donations demonstrate real wealth during his peak career. Their exact funding sources, investment management, and later financial condition are incompletely documented.

## Construction Systems, Companies, Patents, and Technologies

### Maison Universelle Palanti and Casa Universale Palanti

By 1921 Palanti was patenting or promoting prefabricated dry-construction systems under the names **Maison Universelle Palanti** and **Casa Universale Palanti**.

### Palanthome

In 1923 he established **Palanthome. La Casa Argentina S.A.**, with a factory at Zepita and Vélez Sarsfield in Barracas, Buenos Aires.

The system used prefabricated components for rapid and inexpensive house construction. Palanti exhibited a small Palanthome house at the 1923 Exposición Rural Argentina. In his later curriculum, he claimed that a three-room house with services was erected from bare ground in five days, received a grand-prize diploma, remained inhabited for more than twenty years, and was eventually demolished for urban redevelopment. The exhibition and demonstration house are documented; the five-day completion and later history remain partly **self-reported**.

Palanthome patents or protections were pursued in Argentina, France, Uruguay, Britain, Canada, and the United States. The exact claims, grant numbers, legal owners, and commercial returns require patent-office research not available from the records consulted.

### Sistema Palanti and Palandomus

In 1925 Palanti patented in Argentina a “universal system of reinforced-concrete houses,” called the Sistema Palanti.

By the late 1930s the principal name was **Palandomus**. Palanti described it as a dry masonry or prefabricated system based on specially formed interlocking blocks. A later version was called the “laterizio ermafrodita,” or hermaphrodite brick, because its complementary projections and recesses allowed components to join without conventional wet masonry.

Documented activity included:

- patents and correspondence in 1939;
- a sixteen-page Palandomus booklet from 1938;
- a demonstration tower at the Milan trade fair in 1938;
- patenting of a dry-construction block in Milan;
- material tests at the Politecnico di Milano in October 1939;
- experimental firing of Palandomus bricks by S.A. Palli e Figli at Voghera around 1946–1947;
- designs for workers’ housing, apartment blocks, rural dwellings, hospitals, schools, offices, port structures, and high-rise buildings;
- submission of the system to the planners of EUR 1942;
- approval by the Argentine Ministry of Public Works in 1952 for potential use in housing under the Second Five-Year Plan.

Palanti retroactively presented the Resta automobile building and other earlier works as examples of the system. The evidence for full-scale commercial use remained limited. Its history is principally one of persistent experimentation, patent promotion, prototypes, and projected mass housing rather than widespread adoption.

## Other Major Built Works

### Roccatagliata buildings

Palanti designed buildings for Andrés Roccatagliata at the intersection of Avenida Santa Fe and Avenida Callao around 1920–1921. Their bow windows, sculptural surfaces, and conspicuous corner treatment exemplify his transition from historicist residential design toward a more vertically expressive metropolitan language.

### Hotel Castelar

The Hotel Excelsior, later **Hotel Castelar**, at Avenida de Mayo 1142/1148, was designed and constructed around 1927–1928. Palanti’s curriculum described it as having 300 rooms and 300 bathrooms. It became especially associated with Federico García Lorca’s stay in Buenos Aires, although that was not a personal connection between Lorca and Palanti.

### Palacio Chrysler / Palacio Automotor / Palacio Alcorta

The automobile agency and workshop for **Resta Hermanos**, later known as the Palacio Chrysler and today the Palacio Alcorta, was built around 1927 at Avenida Figueroa Alcorta 3351.

It incorporated an automobile test track on or near the roof, prompting contemporary publicity as South America’s first “palace-autodrome.” Its program was compared with Fiat’s Lingotto factory in Turin. It was a conspicuous example of Palanti combining commercial spectacle, circulation technology, concrete construction, and metropolitan imagery.

### Paysandú commercial building

In 1927 Palanti designed the London-París store at 18 de Julio and the relevant adjoining streets in Paysandú, Uruguay.

### Apostolic Nunciature

Palanti designed and directed the Apostolic Nunciature in Buenos Aires at Riobamba and Arenales beginning in 1923. He reportedly provided the design and supervision without fee. The commission expressed his active search for papal patronage and his Catholic monumental interests.

## Religious, Masonic, Political, and Intellectual Development

### Catholicism and papal patronage

Palanti repeatedly designed votive temples, mausoleums, convents, schools, and universal-peace monuments. He cultivated relations with Catholic institutions, created a prize named for Pope Pius XI, donated to architectural study associated with the Holy See, and designed the Buenos Aires nunciature.

His 1945 *Temple of Universal Peace* and postwar *Mole Universale* for the Holy See continued this line. His Catholic symbolism coexisted with documented Masonic initiation and Fascist membership; the sources do not indicate that Palanti perceived these affiliations as incompatible.

### Freemasonry

His initiation in the Fratelli Bandiera lodge on 11 February 1919 is a **documented fact**.

The duration and degree of his Masonic participation are unknown. No adequate documentation was found showing that Luigi Barolo joined the same lodge, that Palanti belonged to the supposed medieval Fede Santa, or that a Masonic organization commissioned the Barolo.

### Fascism

Palanti’s engagement with Fascism was sustained and documentary, not merely an attribution based on architectural style.

- In 1923 he enrolled in the **Partito Nazionale Fascista all’Estero** in Buenos Aires.
- He founded an architectural prize bearing Mussolini’s name.
- On 5 October 1925 he enrolled in the PNF in Italy.
- His Mole Littoria writings described Fascism as the great historical event of the century, a renewal of spirit, reconstruction of the fatherland, and replacement or succession of civilization.
- He repeatedly sought audiences with Mussolini.
- He presented books and projects to Mussolini and tried to secure government commissions.
- In a 10 December 1936 letter to Mussolini’s private secretariat, he described himself as a “Fascista di fede” and lamented that he was unable to contribute his art and experience to Mussolini’s new Italy.
- In 1940 he again complained that the Fascist government had failed to give him adequate consideration.

His Fascist commitment was accompanied by professional frustration. Despite his loyalty, he did not gain stable access to the favored architectural networks of the regime.

### Peronist-era projects

After returning to Argentina in 1948, Palanti proposed:

- Palandomus housing for Ciudad Evita;
- a monument to Juan Perón and Eva Perón;
- a Monumento al Descamisado;
- an arch of victory on Avenida 9 de Julio;
- a monument to José de San Martín.

These projects show another attempt to align monumental and social-housing work with the ruling political order. They do not, by themselves, establish formal Peronist party membership or a personal relationship with Perón or Eva Perón.

### Universal-peace ideology

After the Second World War, Palanti increasingly described his monumental projects in universalist and pacific terms. He designed the *Temple of Universal Peace*, the 600-metre *Mole Vittoria*, the *Mole Universale* for the Holy See, a Volta monument, and commemorative medals.

In the early 1970s he sent universal-peace medals and cards to foreign embassies, the Vatican, scientific institutions, and international bodies. By 1975 he had received acknowledgment letters from the embassies of Canada, Japan, the United States, China, Germany, Austria, and France, and from the International Court of Justice. Such acknowledgment letters establish receipt, not endorsement of his architectural program.

## The Mole Littoria / L’Eternale, 1923–1932

### Conception

Palanti developed an immense skyscraper for central Rome, initially called **L’Eternale** and later **Mole Littoria**. The project evolved through several versions between approximately 1923 and 1932.

The largest version was described as:

- 330–350 metres high;
- eighty-eight storeys;
- approximately 70,000 square metres;
- 4,500 rooms;
- 100 large halls;
- faced in white Carrara marble;
- containing parliament or state offices, meeting rooms, hotels, baths, libraries, sports facilities, gardens, telecommunications stations, an astronomical observatory, clock, and lighthouse.

Proposed sites shifted among areas near Palazzo Chigi, Via del Corso and Via di Ripetta, Castro Pretorio, and Villa Borghese.

### Presentation to Mussolini

Mussolini wrote an encouraging dedication to Palanti in 1923, praising his knowledge of Latin or Italian building daring. Palanti presented the developed project at Palazzo Chigi in September 1924. Mussolini visited and wrote the Fascist salute “Per la Mole Littoria, alalà!”

Palanti repeatedly revised the tower downward—from roughly 350 to 330, then 190 or 130, and finally approximately 90 or 80 metres—to answer objections concerning cost, scale, and Rome’s skyline. He exhibited a revised project at the Salone della Vittoria in June 1926.

### Failure

The project was never built. Its extreme scale, uncertain site, immense cost, and disruptive relation to Rome’s historic skyline counted against it. Its ornamental and expressionist language also became increasingly remote from the sober monumental classicism and rationalism favored by influential Fascist architects.

The municipal building commission reportedly warned that the tower would create serious aesthetic harm to the panorama of Rome.

Palanti’s failure was therefore not caused by opposition to Fascism. He was an avowed Fascist attempting to win support from a Fascist government but lacked the stylistic alignment, political network, and institutional authority required to execute the proposal.

### Controversy with Ottavio Dinale

A Lombardy archival file documents a dispute between Palanti and Fascist journalist and intermediary **Ottavio Dinale** over Dinale’s contested contribution to the promotion or realization of *L’Eternale* between 1924 and 1927. The file includes correspondence, cost explanations, demands, notifications, and acts of citation.

This establishes a **documented professional and legal controversy**. The accessible catalogue does not disclose the complete pleadings, financial amount, or judicial outcome; it would be unsafe to assign liability without consulting the file.

## Return to Italy and Failed Fascist Competitions, 1924–1940

Palanti crossed the Atlantic repeatedly during the 1920s and early 1930s. He registered in the professional register of engineers and architects of Milan province on 23 October 1928.

He returned more permanently to Italy in 1931 aboard the *Conte Verde*, though he continued travelling to Argentina.

### Palazzo del Littorio competition, 1934

In 1934 Palanti entered the competition for the Palazzo del Littorio in Rome. His “ship” or “prow” design used giant political symbols, eagles, the letter M for Mussolini, a memorial hall, and formulas associated with Fascist martyr commemoration.

The design reached a later stage of the competition but was not selected.

Architect and critic **Giuseppe Pagano** described Palanti’s work in language translated as “shark-like and South American snobbery.” *Il Popolo di Roma* mocked the proposal as a “carnival cake” that could not be made digestible even to a South American. These were **documented contemporary critical attacks**, mixing aesthetic hostility with condescension toward Palanti’s South American career.

### Conflict with Marcello Piacentini

After losing further competitions, Palanti accused **Marcello Piacentini** and his circle of a “prepotente invasione piacentiniana” and of commercializing architecture under the pretext of creating a Fascist rationalism. One archival reference records Palanti accusing Piacentini of earlier anti-Fascism.

These were **Palanti’s professional and political accusations**, not established findings. Piacentini’s influence on state commissions was real, but that does not prove that Palanti’s losses resulted from improper conduct.

### Other unsuccessful competitions

Palanti entered or published proposals for:

- the Rome Auditorium, 1934–1935, with eight alternative acoustic schemes;
- the Torre Littoria in Milan, 1935;
- another Casa or Palazzo Littorio competition in Rome, 1937;
- Monumento alla Vittoria in Milan;
- Fascist exhibition and EUR-related projects.

His Auditorium proposals attempted a more restrained language. He said that internal rationalism should be reflected externally and treated acoustic performance as a primary design problem. The schemes nevertheless failed.

### Journey aboard the Graf Zeppelin

In June 1935 Palanti returned to Argentina via Rio de Janeiro aboard the airship **Graf Zeppelin**. He returned to Italy aboard the *Neptunia* in October. The journey illustrates both his transatlantic mobility and his attraction to conspicuous modern technology.

## Second World War and Stresa, 1940–1947

Palanti’s residence at **Via Mussolini 4, Milan**, was affected during an Allied air raid in August 1943. He and Nahir moved to Stresa Borromeo.

At Stresa he produced extensive drawings rather than major executed buildings. Projects included:

- Palandomus grouped, terraced, chain, block, and high-rise housing;
- metropolitan “hive” buildings;
- hospitals and schools;
- mausoleums;
- hotels;
- a golf course;
- a railway station and railway reorganization;
- a nautical grandstand;
- an auditorium;
- a bank;
- casino-theatre-cinema-hotel complexes;
- a power station;
- a convent;
- a covered swimming pool;
- a lyric theatre;
- a Fountain of the Heroes of Labor;
- the 600-metre *Mole Vittoria*.

His 1958 curriculum claimed participation in municipal commissions for the reconstruction and urban development of bomb-damaged Milan. The precise commission memberships and results remain **self-reported** in the consulted sources.

## Second Argentine Period, 1948–1955

Palanti and Nahir travelled to Argentina in 1948. He hoped to recover his earlier position but found a transformed professional and political environment.

He designed housing for **La Fabril Los Plátanos** and continued promoting Palandomus. He also generated monumental proposals aligned with Peronist public symbolism.

### Lack of professional authorization

A decisive obstacle was his failure to obtain Argentine recognition of his foreign architectural qualification.

On 17 September 1950, Civil Court No. 2 in Buenos Aires requested information from the Consejo Profesional de Arquitectura y Urbanismo concerning his professional status. The council reported that Palanti was not registered in any capacity and therefore was not authorized to practice as an architect under its rules.

In June 1952 the council discussed a construction sign bearing his name at Confitería París, Libertad and Charcas. It instructed him to remove the sign or regularize his status.

In 1953 the council again asked him to remove professional signage. Palanti attempted unsuccessfully to validate his title. In his later account, he said the University of Buenos Aires lost his first application, delayed the second, and that a government decree then suspended validation of foreign professional qualifications. These procedural claims are **Palanti’s account** and should be checked against university files.

There is no evidence in the consulted record that Palanti was criminally prosecuted for unauthorized practice. The matter appears to have been a regulatory and professional-registration dispute.

### Departure

Nahir returned to Italy in 1952. After Perón’s overthrow and with his qualification still unrecognized, Palanti left Argentina permanently in November 1955.

## Final Italian Period, 1955–1978

### Mole Universale and Aurelio Marcato

In 1956 Palanti began work on a **Mole Universale** for the Holy See in Rome. He met **Aurelio Marcato**, who offered to locate financing in exchange for a percentage. Palanti financed Marcato’s efforts.

On 25 September 1965 Palanti formally alleged that Marcato had defrauded him “through hypnosis.” He described himself in 1966 as impoverished by a series of misfortunes and a major fraud.

This must be classified carefully:

- Palanti’s complaint or denunciation is a **documented allegation**.
- No investigation result, charge, trial, judgment, or conviction of Marcato was found.
- The hypnosis component is Palanti’s assertion and is not independently established.
- The record could reflect fraud, manipulation of an elderly client, a deteriorating personal relationship, an unusual subjective interpretation, or another unresolved circumstance.

### Financial decline

By 1966 Palanti was asking his separated wife Nahir for 4.2 million lire annually to meet basic needs. In 1969 he renewed his claim for support.

The available evidence suggests that the architect who had once funded foundations, prizes, foreign patents, real estate, and monumental publications ended his career in financial difficulty. The exact loss of his Argentine property, Berlin investments, foundation capital, patent income, and Italian assets has not been reconstructed.

### Late projects and retirement

Late projects included:

- a circular stadium for Rome’s Foro Italico;
- the 400-metre *Torre Babele Utilitaria* in Buenos Aires;
- the Mole Universale for the Holy See;
- a 600-metre Mole Alessandro Volta in Milan;
- a monumental labor antenna;
- tourism and infrastructure schemes;
- universal-peace monuments and medals.

Around 1971 he informed the Accademia di San Luca that he had abandoned professional practice in 1960.

In 1974 he transferred the costly family mausoleum to the City of Milan. He also left funds intended for architecture projects commemorating universal peace.

## Death and Burial

Palanti died in Milan in September 1978. As noted above, 4 September is the standard date; Bonicatto’s chronology gives 6 September.

No reliable source consulted states a cause of death. No documented final illness, accident, addiction, psychiatric diagnosis, or hospitalization was identified.

He was entombed in the mausoleum he had designed at Milan’s Monumental Cemetery.

## Civico Mausoleo Palanti

Palanti acquired the mausoleum site in May 1924 and designed the structure initially as a tomb for his parents and family. It was built from approximately 1924 to 1928 and inaugurated in 1929, Year VII of the Fascist era.

The building combines funerary, familial, monumental, and civic symbolism. Palanti transferred it to the City of Milan in 1974 because of the cost of maintenance and restoration. The city subsequently used it as a secondary famedio for distinguished Milanese. It formally became the **Civico Mausoleo Palanti** in 1981.

Its later occupants include notable Milanese from fields beyond Palanti’s family. Mario himself is buried there; his brother Giuseppe was excluded following their quarrel.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Gaetano Moretti

Teacher, mentor, and professional sponsor. Moretti’s involvement in the Italian Pavilion led directly to Palanti’s relocation to Buenos Aires. **Documented fact.**

### Francisco/Francesco Gianotti

Compatriot, colleague, and collaborator in construction and decoration of the Italian Pavilion. Both later worked in or around the Prins–Ranzenhofer milieu and served on the editorial council of *Arquitectura y Arte Decorativo*. **Documented fact.**

### Arturo Prins and Oskar Ranzenhofer

Employers or office principals during Palanti’s first Buenos Aires years. Palanti collaborated in their office on the neo-Gothic Faculty of Law. **Documented fact.**

### Luigi/Luis Barolo

Industrialist and patron of the Palacio Barolo. Their documented relationship was architectural and commercial. Stories that they belonged to the same secret lodge and jointly planned to obtain Dante’s remains are later, disputed narratives. **Documented patronage; disputed esoteric partnership.**

### Ángel, José, and Lorenzo Salvo

Uruguayan industrialists and patrons of Palacio Salvo. **Documented fact.**

### Lorenzo Gori Salvo

Engineer associated with construction of Palacio Salvo and later Palandomus correspondence. **Documented fact.**

### Giuseppe Palanti

Brother, artistic collaborator, adviser, and later family adversary. Giuseppe painted Mario’s portrait and was involved in Mario’s Berlin property investment. The relationship ended in severe conflict. **Documented or strongly reported fact.**

### Benito Mussolini

Political leader from whom Palanti repeatedly sought patronage. Mussolini viewed the Mole Littoria model or exhibition, supplied laudatory inscriptions, and rejected Palanti’s attempted horse gift. Palanti’s repeated petitions did not produce a major commission. **Documented fact.**

### Ottavio Dinale

Fascist intermediary involved in promoting *L’Eternale* and later in a legal and financial dispute with Palanti; also mediated attempted presentation of Palanti’s Palazzo Littorio book. **Documented fact.**

### Marcello Piacentini

Powerful architect and juror whom Palanti accused of monopolistic and professionally improper influence. **Documented rivalry and accusation; wrongdoing not established.**

### Giuseppe Pagano

Architect and critic who attacked Palanti’s Fascist competition entries as aesthetically and culturally alien. **Documented critical rivalry.**

### María Elena Castagnino

First known wife; the marriage ended in a bitter separation within months. **Documented fact, with unresolved property allegations.**

### Nahir Marazzi Castiglione

Second wife, wartime companion, Argentine travelling partner, later separated spouse, and object of Palanti’s support claims. **Documented fact.**

### Aurelio Marcato

Would-be financial promoter of the Mole Universale, later accused by Palanti of hypnosis-assisted fraud. **Documented relationship and allegation; no adjudication found.**

No complete probate file, corporate filing set, property register, banking record, or tax history was found. Assertions about his exact wealth should therefore be avoided. The pattern is clear: substantial professional income and philanthropy in the 1920s, ambitious but poorly monetized inventions and publications thereafter, declining commissions, and reported hardship in old age.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### Fascist affiliation and political opportunism

**Documented fact:** Palanti formally joined Fascist organizations, described himself as a Fascist of faith, praised Mussolini’s regime, created Fascist monuments, and persistently pursued state patronage.

**Interpretive question:** Some scholars treat him as a sincere Fascist; others emphasize his repeated adaptation to Catholic, Fascist, Peronist, and universalist patrons. The two explanations are not mutually exclusive. The documentary evidence establishes commitment and self-presentation but does not disclose his private beliefs at every stage.

### Presentation copy to Adolf Hitler

A surviving copy of *L’Eternale, mole littoria* in the former Hitler library and Third Reich Collection bears an inscription catalogued as written by Palanti to Adolf Hitler in Milan in 1932, expressing reverent admiration. A Hitler postcard accompanied the volume.

This is a **documented surviving object and inscription**. It proves that Palanti prepared or presented a laudatory copy for Hitler. It does not prove a personal meeting, correspondence, political office under Nazism, or involvement in Nazi crimes.

### Dispute with Ottavio Dinale

A Milan archival file contains legal notices, demands, correspondence, cost statements, and competing claims over Dinale’s contribution to *L’Eternale*. **Documented controversy; outcome unresolved in the accessible catalogue.**

### Horse intended for Mussolini

Palanti brought or sent a horse obtained from his estranged wife as a gift to Mussolini. Mussolini’s secretariat rejected it after learning of the marital dispute. **Documented episode.** The available evidence does not establish criminal appropriation.

### Attacks on competition juries

Palanti accused Piacentini and his circle of dominating architectural practice and commercializing Fascist rationalism. **Documented accusation, not a finding of corruption.**

### Professional licensing in Argentina

The Buenos Aires professional council established that Palanti was not registered and asked him to remove or regularize signs identifying him as architect. **Documented regulatory finding.** No criminal conviction or established fraud was found.

### Alleged Marcato fraud and hypnosis

Palanti accused Aurelio Marcato of defrauding him by hypnosis. **Documented allegation by Palanti; no corroborating judgment found.**

### Family conflicts

His break with Giuseppe and refusal to allow Giuseppe’s burial in the family mausoleum are reported in archival scholarship. His disputes with both wives involved property, abandonment, or support accusations. No finding of criminal conduct was identified.

### Adversarial-search result, including 2024–2026 material

Dedicated searches combining Palanti’s name with controversy, scandal, allegations, lawsuits, fraud, accusations, conspiracy, and criticism found:

- the Dinale litigation file;
- the Marcato fraud/hypnosis allegation;
- professional-registration disputes in Argentina;
- marital and family conflicts;
- sustained Fascist activity and the Hitler presentation copy;
- extensive contemporary aesthetic criticism;
- modern disputes over the Dante, Masonic, ashes, and “bridge of light” legends.

No credible source found evidence of criminal conviction, corruption adjudication, money laundering, intelligence-service activity, political violence, war crimes, or formal postwar prosecution.

Recent material has concentrated on reassessing the Barolo’s supposed Dante program. A 2025 academic study argues that the elaborate Danteum interpretation was constructed from 1993 onward and then repeated as fact. This is the most important recent contested issue concerning Palanti’s reputation.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### The Palacio Barolo as a complete architectural Divine Comedy

**The story:** The Barolo is said to reproduce Dante’s *Divine Comedy* exactly: the entrance is Hell, intermediate floors are Purgatory, the tower is Paradise, and the lighthouse is the Empyrean. Its height of 100 metres supposedly corresponds to the poem’s one hundred cantos; twenty-two floors correspond to its verse structure; nine entrance vaults represent the circles of Hell; golden-ratio measures, astronomical alignments, and the Southern Cross complete the program.

**Appearance and circulation:** The fully elaborated interpretation is associated particularly with architect **Carlos Hilger**, who published it in 1993. It was subsequently amplified in tourism, journalism, heritage promotion, popular books, websites, and Sebastián Schindel’s 2012 documentary *El rascacielos latino*.

**Evidence for it:**

- Palanti demonstrably employed intense symbolic ornament.
- He had documented Masonic affiliation.
- Dante and Italian national culture were prominent within the immigrant milieu.
- Certain formal numbers and divisions can be correlated with the poem.
- Later sources claim a Palanti sculpture called *Ascensión* supplies additional evidence of Dantean interest.
- Astronomical studies have explored alignments between the building, Dante’s cosmology, and the Southern Cross.

**Evidence against or limiting it:**

- No surviving Palanti building specification, contemporary commission document, or 1923 explanation has been shown to set out the now-familiar complete Dante program.
- Palanti published his own work extensively and had strong incentives to publicize a major symbolic program, yet the elaborate reading appears absent from his contemporary descriptions.
- Some numerical correspondences depend on selective counting or later alterations.
- Mariano Pérez Carrasco’s 2025 study argues that Hilger framed an interpretation as a discovery and that subsequent writers converted it into historical fact.

**Assessment:** **Disputed interpretation, with a substantial later mythic superstructure.** Dantean or broader Italian-symbolist influence remains plausible; the precise totalizing code is not established.

**Reputational effect:** The narrative transformed the Barolo from an eccentric early skyscraper into one of Buenos Aires’s leading esoteric and literary attractions, greatly increasing public interest in Palanti.

### The plan to rescue Dante’s ashes

**The story:** Palanti and Barolo supposedly feared another European war and planned to move Dante Alighieri’s remains from Ravenna to Buenos Aires, placing them in the Barolo beneath a special receptacle or statue.

**Appearance and circulation:** The story appears in post-1993 interpretations, heritage literature, tours, journalism, and *El rascacielos latino*.

**Evidence:** No contemporary contract, letter, diplomatic approach to Ravenna, Church document, Italian-government record, or Palanti text substantiating the plan was identified.

**Assessment:** **Rumor or myth.** It may crystallize real immigrant reverence for Dante and later knowledge of European war, but it is not documented as a historical project.

**Reputational effect:** It is central to the palace’s modern aura of secrecy, exile, cultural rescue, and initiatory purpose.

### Palanti, Barolo, and Dante in the same lodge

**The story:** Palanti and Barolo belonged to a secret Masonic lineage called the Fede Santa, supposedly also associated with Dante.

**Evidence for it:** Palanti was genuinely initiated into a named Milanese lodge in 1919.

**Evidence against it:** The documented lodge was Fratelli Bandiera, not Fede Santa. No adequate record establishes Barolo’s membership in the same lodge, much less institutional continuity with Dante.

**Assessment:** Palanti’s Masonry is **documented fact**; the common Fede Santa lineage is **myth or unverified esoteric genealogy**.

### Twin lighthouses forming a bridge of light

**The story:** Palanti placed lighthouses on the Barolo and Salvo so their beams would meet over the Río de la Plata, welcoming ships or spiritually joining Buenos Aires and Montevideo.

**Evidence:** Both towers were conceived with luminous crowns or lighthouse imagery. Palanti repeatedly used towers and beacons.

**Problems:** The buildings are roughly 200 kilometres apart; direct intervisibility is prevented by distance, atmospheric conditions, and Earth’s curvature. Specialists cited in later reporting regard the literal intersecting-beams story as a tourism-era interpretation, and there is uncertainty over whether the Salvo’s proposed lighthouse was ever installed and operational in the required form.

**Assessment:** The symbolic pairing of the towers is plausible; a functioning beam-to-beam optical link is **myth**.

### Predictions that the Barolo and Salvo would collapse

**The story:** Critics allegedly said the towers would fall because of their eccentric forms or defective structures.

**Evidence:** Palanti himself referred in his 1958 curriculum to a “legend” that the Barolo would collapse and to malicious claims of structural weakness concerning the Salvo.

**Assessment:** **Contemporary or retrospective reported rumor.** The buildings’ survival refutes the predicted outcome, but the original speakers and engineering claims are not fully identified.

### Palanti as a suppressed genius

**The story:** Palanti’s unbuilt towers were too visionary for conservative European institutions, and professional cliques deliberately excluded him.

**Evidence:** He did anticipate mixed-use high-rise programs, prefabrication, integrated circulation, and monumental urban landmarks. He also faced hostile juries and influential rivals.

**Counter-evidence:** Many proposals were financially implausible, radically oversized, poorly related to their sites, and stylistically remote from the directions favored even within Fascist architecture. His own antagonistic lobbying damaged his access to patrons.

**Assessment:** **Self-created professional mythology with a foundation in real exclusion and real innovation, but not a sufficient explanation of every failure.**

### The “twin” Barolo and Salvo

They are routinely called twins. They share architect, tower-passage typology, metropolitan ambition, and family resemblance. Their dimensions, sites, programs, plans, and ornament differ substantially.

**Assessment:** “Twin” is a useful popular metaphor, not a literal architectural description.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Palanti’s reputation passed through several phases.

### Contemporary prominence and criticism

In the 1910s and 1920s he was a conspicuous representative of Italian immigrant architectural ambition in the Río de la Plata. His towers, publications, and personal exhibitions made him unusually visible.

At the same time, academic architects and emerging modernists attacked his work as excessive, impure, theatrical, socially pretentious, or damaging to urban coherence.

### Marginalization by modernist histories

From the 1930s through much of the postwar period, architectural history privileged rationalism and canonical modernism. Palanti’s eclectic ornament, propaganda monuments, and unsuccessful megastructures appeared outdated or embarrassing. His most experimental technical work, including Palandomus, had limited commercial success.

His nephew Giancarlo Palanti achieved a different form of modernist recognition, particularly in Brazil, while Mario became associated chiefly with two eccentric skyscrapers.

### Recovery

Heritage scholarship, CEDODAL research, Italian-Argentine cultural projects, restoration campaigns, and architectural tourism revived interest from the late twentieth century onward.

The Barolo became an Argentine National Historic Monument in 1997. The Salvo had been declared a Uruguayan National Historic Monument in 1996. Both are now central urban icons.

### Esoteric popular culture

The post-1993 Dante interpretation and the 2012 documentary produced a second public career for Palanti: visionary initiate, Masonic architect, guardian of Dante, and builder of a trans-Río de la Plata cosmological pair.

This reputation has been commercially and culturally powerful. Recent scholarship has begun separating Palanti’s documented symbolic practice from later interpretive accretions.

### Architectural significance

Palanti’s principal historical importance lies in the combination of:

- Italian academic and medievalist training;
- immigrant patronage;
- the commercial gallery;
- reinforced-concrete high-rise construction;
- total design;
- mixed-use metropolitan programs;
- architectural self-publication;
- prefabricated housing experiments;
- monuments designed to obtain access to political power.

His built skyscrapers were genuine technical and typological achievements. His unbuilt work documents how an architect could move among liberal immigrant capitalism, Catholic internationalism, Fascism, Peronist monumentalism, industrialized housing, and universal-peace rhetoric without abandoning a persistent belief in the architect as an individual creator of civilizational symbols.

## Atlas Connections

Only relationships supported by the sources are included. Mere residence in the same city or chronological overlap has been excluded.

### Alejandro Christophersen — documented professional criticism

Christophersen publicly criticized Palanti and the kind of architecture represented by the Palacio Barolo through the professional press of the Sociedad Central de Arquitectos in the early 1920s. He defended an academically sanctioned architectural culture against Palanti’s immigrant eclecticism. The connection was that of senior professional critic and criticized rival, not collaboration.

### Benito Mussolini — documented political patronage sought and limited personal contact

Palanti presented the Mole Littoria project to Mussolini at Palazzo Chigi in Rome in 1924. Mussolini viewed the work and inscribed an encouraging Fascist salute. Palanti repeatedly sent him books, requested audiences, founded a prize bearing his name, attempted to give him a horse, and described himself in 1936 as a Fascist of faith. Mussolini’s government never entrusted him with the monumental commission he sought.

### Dante Alighieri — disputed posthumous artistic influence

The connection is posthumous and interpretive. Palanti worked in an Italian cultural environment in which Dante was a national symbol, and later scholarship and tourism read the Palacio Barolo as a complete architectural translation of the *Divine Comedy*. The fully elaborated Danteum program and plan to house Dante’s remains are not documented in contemporary commission records. Label: **documented cultural context; disputed specific influence; ashes narrative is myth**.

### Francisco Gianotti — documented collaboration and shared professional network

Gianotti and Palanti worked on construction and decoration of the Italian Pavilion for the Buenos Aires Centennial Exhibition around 1910. They were also connected through the Prins–Ranzenhofer professional environment and later served on the editorial council of *Arquitectura y Arte Decorativo*.

### Gabriele d’Annunzio — reported symbolic and rhetorical influence

No personal meeting has been documented. The Fascist cry “Alalà,” originating in d’Annunzio’s nationalist vocabulary and later adopted by Fascism, appeared in Mussolini’s dedication to Palanti’s Mole Littoria. Palanti’s monumental language also shared the period’s D’Annunzian rhetoric of heroic sacrifice, Latin rebirth, and grand spectacle. This is a **reported intellectual-symbolic connection**, not a documented personal relationship.

### Adolf Hitler — documented presentation-copy connection; no meeting established

A 1926 copy of *L’Eternale* in the former Hitler library bears a 1932 Milan inscription by Palanti expressing reverent admiration for Hitler. Hitler is catalogued as former owner. This establishes a presentation-copy relationship. No documented meeting, correspondence, employment, or organizational tie was located.

### Juan Domingo Perón — documented project addressed to his regime; no personal relationship established

During Palanti’s 1948–1955 Argentine period he designed a monument honoring Perón and Eva Perón, a Monumento al Descamisado, and housing schemes associated with the Peronist Second Five-Year Plan. The Ministry of Public Works reportedly approved Palandomus for potential housing use. No personal meeting or commission directly from Perón was established.

### Eva Duarte de Perón — documented commemorative project; no personal relationship established

Palanti prepared a monumental scheme honoring Perón and Eva and worked on Ciudad Evita-related housing ideas. This was an architectural-political association, not evidence of a personal meeting.

### José de San Martín — documented posthumous commemorative subject

Palanti prepared a Buenos Aires monument to San Martín around 1950. The connection is solely that of architect to posthumous commemorative subject.

### Federico García Lorca — no documented personal connection

Lorca later stayed at the Hotel Castelar, a Palanti building. No source consulted documents a meeting, correspondence, collaboration, or acknowledged influence between the two men. Under the atlas rules, use of the same building at different times is not treated as a personal connection.

### Filippo Tommaso Marinetti — no supported direct connection found

Palanti’s language sometimes intersected with Futurist themes of dynamism and technology, but no meeting, correspondence, collaboration, or explicit acknowledgment of Marinetti was established.

### Francesco Tamburini, Vittorio Meano, Virginio Colombo, Francisco Salamone, and Juan Antonio Buschiazzo — no supported direct connection found

These architects belong to overlapping Italian-Argentine architectural history, but the sources consulted did not establish a personal or institutional crossing sufficient for inclusion as a connection.

### Absence finding

Among the remainder of the supplied atlas roster, no documented meeting, correspondence, collaboration, rivalry, family tie, teacher-student relationship, patronage relationship, shared organization, or explicitly acknowledged influence was located. This does not prove that no incidental contact ever occurred; it means that no connection meeting the stated evidentiary threshold emerged from the sources consulted.
