# Igor Stravinsky: Research Dossier

**Subject:** Igor Fyodorovich Stravinsky (Игорь Фёдорович Стравинский; Igor Fedorovich Stravinsky; French publications often *Igor Strawinsky*)  
**Life:** 17 June 1882 (5 June Old Style) – 6 April 1971  
**Scope note:** This dossier distinguishes documented events, Stravinsky’s changing recollections, later testimony, scholarly inference, and legend. Dates of compositions sometimes indicate the span of composition rather than publication or première. The work catalogue is extensive but consolidates revisions, arrangements, and minor occasional pieces; the definitive work-and-edition authority is Helmut Kirchmeyer’s continuously updated **K Catalog**.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full baptismal/civil name:** Igor Fyodorovich Stravinsky (Игорь Фёдорович Стравинский). His patronymic is also transliterated *Fedorovich*; the surname appears as *Strawinsky* in German and in many early editions.
- **Born:** 17 June 1882 New Style / 5 June Old Style, at Oranienbaum, a Baltic resort west of Saint Petersburg in the Russian Empire (renamed Lomonosov in 1948). He was baptized in the Orthodox Church.
- **Died:** 6 April 1971, 5:20 a.m., at 920 Fifth Avenue, New York City, aged 88. Contemporary and later accounts identify recurrent pulmonary edema in the setting of severe cardiovascular/arterial illness; “heart failure” is the broader formulation.
- **Buried:** 15 April 1971, Orthodox section of San Michele cemetery island, Venice, near Sergei Diaghilev. A funeral service was held at Santi Giovanni e Paolo.
- **Citizenships:** Russian imperial subject; French citizen from 1934; naturalized United States citizen in 1945. Exile after 1914 became permanent after revolution and civil war.
- **Professions:** composer, pianist, conductor, orchestrator, arranger, lecturer, recording artist, author/interview subject. He held no long-term conservatory post. Performance and recording were important income streams as well as vehicles for controlling interpretation.
- **Principal residences:** Oranienbaum and Saint Petersburg; family estate at Ustyluh (then Volhynia, Russian Empire; now Ukraine); Clarens and Morges, Switzerland (1914–20); Brittany/Paris and Anglet–Biarritz/Nice, France (1920–39); Cambridge, Massachusetts (1939–40); West Hollywood, California (1940–69); New York City (1969–71), with repeated European touring and summer stays in Switzerland and France.
- **Public identity:** Russian-born, French-naturalized, later Russian-American. He resisted a single national or stylistic label while repeatedly remaking his public image: Russian modernist, cosmopolitan neoclassicist, Orthodox traditionalist, anti-Romantic formalist, and late serialist.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and background

His father, **Fyodor Ignatievich Stravinsky** (1843–1902), was a celebrated bass of the Mariinsky Theatre, particularly associated with Russian opera. The paternal family came from the Polish–Lithuanian borderlands; family tradition claimed descent from the Polish noble Sulima-Strawiński line. The exact genealogical connection is less secure than the family’s use of the Sulima arms and identity. Igor later emphasized noble and Polish antecedents when useful, while his artistic self-presentation stressed Russianness.

His mother, **Anna Kirillovna Kholodovskaya** (1854–1939), was a capable pianist and singer and regularly accompanied her husband. Her maternal family had Ukrainian/Cossack connections. The household was affluent, musically saturated, socially connected to the imperial theatre, and equipped with a major music library. It was emotionally formal. Stravinsky later described a childhood short on parental warmth and portrayed himself as isolated, testimony that is subjective but consistent across his memoiristic accounts.

Igor was the third of four sons. His brothers were **Roman** (1874–97), **Yury** (1878–1941, an architect), and **Gury** (1884–1917, an actor who died during wartime service). The father’s theatrical colleagues and the family library gave Igor unusually direct exposure to opera and orchestral repertory. He remembered seeing Tchaikovsky at the Mariinsky shortly before Tchaikovsky’s death, a formative encounter preserved chiefly through later recollection.

### First marriage and children

On 23 January 1906 Stravinsky married his first cousin **Yekaterina Gavrilovna Nosenko** (usually Catherine or Katya, 1880–1939). Because the Russian Orthodox Church prohibited first-cousin marriage, the couple obtained clerical permission and married near Saint Petersburg. Catherine was educated, musical, and an essential domestic and practical collaborator; she copied music and managed family life through repeated illness, exile, and Igor’s absences.

Their children were:

1. **Fyodor (Théodore) Stravinsky** (1907–89), painter and designer.
2. **Ludmila Stravinsky** (1908–38), who married the poet Yury Mandelstam; she died of tuberculosis.
3. **Sviatoslav Soulima Stravinsky** (1910–94), pianist and composer.
4. **Milena (Mika) Stravinsky** (1914–2013), later Milena Marion.

The family endured recurrent tuberculosis. Catherine spent long periods in sanatoria. Ludmila died on 30 November 1938; Catherine died on 2 March 1939. Anna Stravinsky died in June 1939. Igor himself was treated in a sanatorium and recovered.

### Vera de Bosset / Sudeikina

Stravinsky met **Vera Bosse, known as Vera de Bosset or Vera Sudeikina** (officially 1889–1982, though Robert Craft recorded her private claim to have been born four years earlier), a dancer, artist, and wife of stage designer Serge Sudeikin, in Paris in 1921. Their affair began that summer. Vera left Sudeikin; Stravinsky did not leave Catherine. For roughly seventeen years he maintained two households and divided his travel and domestic life between Catherine and Vera. Catherine knew of the relationship. Evidence for her attitude comes largely through later family and Craft accounts; descriptions of “acceptance” should not be mistaken for freely documented consent.

Vera followed Stravinsky to the United States in early 1940. They married at Bedford, Massachusetts, on 9 March 1940, one year after Catherine’s death. Vera managed the household, social calendar, art collection, and aspects of his estate. The marriage lasted until his death.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1882–1901

Stravinsky spent childhood winters in Saint Petersburg and summers at country properties. He began piano lessons around age nine with Alexandra Snetkova, studied harmony and counterpoint intermittently, improvised, read scores, and attended Mariinsky performances. He was not a conservatory prodigy. His parents intended a respectable legal career.

He attended the Second Saint Petersburg Gymnasium from 1892. Surviving accounts portray an uneven and unhappy pupil, drawn more to music than formal schooling. Friendship with **Vladimir Rimsky-Korsakov**, son of Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov, became decisive: it gave Stravinsky access to the senior composer.

In 1901 he entered the law faculty of Saint Petersburg University. University disruption during the 1905 Revolution and his increasing commitment to music meant that he completed course work but apparently did not take the final state examination or establish a legal career. Statements that he “graduated in law” simplify an ambiguous record.

## Education and Formation, 1901–09

Stravinsky studied harmony with **Fyodor Akimenko** and then **Vasily Kalafati**. In summer 1902 he showed music to **Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov** at Heidelberg. Rimsky advised against entering the conservatory and instead supervised him privately, regularly from about 1905 until Rimsky’s death in 1908. Training emphasized form, counterpoint, orchestration, revision, and practical craft. Stravinsky also learned from Glazunov, Tchaikovsky, Borodin, Mussorgsky, French orchestration, and the symbolist/modernist arts surrounding the *Mir iskusstva* circle.

Early works—*Scherzo* and *The Storm Cloud* (1902), the large Piano Sonata in F-sharp minor (1903–04), *Symphony in E-flat* (1905–07), *The Faun and the Shepherdess* (1906–07), *Scherzo fantastique* (1907–08), and *Feu d’artifice* (1908)—show rapid technical assimilation rather than a single revolutionary break.

At a 1909 Saint Petersburg concert, impresario **Sergei Diaghilev** heard *Scherzo fantastique* and *Feu d’artifice*. He first employed Stravinsky to orchestrate Chopin pieces for *Les Sylphides*, then commissioned a full ballet after more established composers were unavailable or unsuitable. This patronage transformed Stravinsky’s career.

## Chronological Life History

### Ballets Russes breakthrough, 1909–14

For **The Firebird (*L’Oiseau de feu*)**, Stravinsky worked rapidly in 1909–10 with choreographer Michel Fokine, designer Alexander Golovin, and Diaghilev. Its Paris première on 25 June 1910 made the twenty-eight-year-old composer internationally famous. The score fused Rimskian color, Russian folklore, octatonic technique, and a newly recognizable rhythmic profile.

During rehearsals he met leading Parisian artists and musicians, including Debussy, Ravel, and members of Diaghilev’s network. **Petrushka** followed, premièred 13 June 1911 with Vaslav Nijinsky in the title role, Fokine’s choreography, Alexandre Benois’s scenario/design, and Pierre Monteux conducting. Its layered rhythms, “Petrushka chord,” and street-fair materials extended his reputation.

Stravinsky and ethnographic artist **Nicholas Roerich** developed the pagan-sacrifice scenario for **The Rite of Spring (*Le Sacre du printemps*)**. Nijinsky choreographed; Monteux conducted. The 29 May 1913 première at the Théâtre des Champs-Élysées produced loud disputes, laughter, shouting, and factional conflict. The performance continued. Contemporary reports disagree on timing, intensity, and whether music, choreography, social antagonism, or organized cliques caused the disturbance. Police intervention and physical scuffles are reported in some accounts, but the canonical “riot” was amplified after the fact. The concert première in 1914 was a major success.

Stravinsky’s later story that *Rite* began as a sudden autonomous “vision” conflicts with earlier accounts and with documented collaboration with Roerich. His claim to have borrowed only the opening Lithuanian melody also conflicts with later source studies identifying multiple Russian and Lithuanian folk materials. These are not findings of plagiarism in a legal sense; they show selective self-mythologizing about compositional originality.

After the 1913 première Stravinsky contracted typhoid fever. Catherine was also treated for tuberculosis. The family spent increasing time in Switzerland. A final prewar visit to Ustyluh and Kyiv in summer 1914 preceded the closure of borders.

### Swiss exile and wartime reinvention, 1914–20

World War I stranded the family in Switzerland; the Russian Revolution eliminated property income, publishing access, and the practical possibility of return. They lived principally at Clarens and Morges. Financial constraint favored smaller ensembles and portable theatrical forms.

Important collaborators included Swiss conductor **Ernest Ansermet**, writer **Charles-Ferdinand Ramuz**, artist **René Auberjonois**, and philanthropist **Werner Reinhart**. Works included *Three Pieces for String Quartet*, *Pribaoutki*, *Renard*, *Les Noces* (begun 1914, repeatedly rescored), *Ragtime*, *Piano-Rag-Music*, and **L’Histoire du soldat** (1918). The latter’s touring plan collapsed during the influenza epidemic after its Lausanne première, a practical failure but a durable artistic success.

The period intensified his extraction and recomposition of Russian folk texts and tunes. It also forced professional entrepreneurship: benefit concerts, private patronage, piano performance, editions, and contracts replaced imperial and Ballets Russes structures. In 1917 he met **Pablo Picasso** in Italy; Picasso drew the well-known portrait that customs officials reportedly suspected was a coded military plan—a story plausible in outline and repeated in memoirs, though details vary.

### France, neoclassicism, and the double household, 1920–39

In 1920 Diaghilev staged **Pulcinella**, using music then attributed wholly to Pergolesi but now known to include works by several eighteenth-century composers. Picasso designed and Léonide Massine choreographed. Stravinsky called the encounter a discovery of the past; it became the emblematic beginning of his neoclassical phase, although earlier works already anticipated it.

**Coco Chanel** offered the Stravinsky family use of her house at Garches and anonymously or privately supported the 1920 revival of *Rite*. A sexual affair between Chanel and Stravinsky is **disputed**. Chanel later told Paul Morand that it occurred; Stravinsky never confirmed it, Vera denied it, and no decisive contemporary correspondence establishes it. Chris Greenhalgh’s 2002 novel and Jan Kounen’s 2009 film converted the allegation into popular narrative.

Stravinsky signed contracts with **Pleyel** in 1921 to prepare piano-roll versions for the Pleyela. This supplied advances/income and a mechanical means of fixing tempo, rhythm, and voicing. He also developed an active career as pianist and conductor, often writing works he could perform: *Concerto for Piano and Wind Instruments*, *Sonata*, *Serenade in A*, *Capriccio*, and the *Concerto for Two Pianos*. His son Soulima later became his duo partner.

The opera buffa **Mavra** (1922), dedicated to Pushkin, was poorly received at its première. The wind **Octet** (1923) announced a spare, objectified idiom. Stravinsky returned formally to Russian Orthodoxy around 1925–26 after decades of distance. Religious works followed: *Pater Noster*, *Symphony of Psalms*, *Credo*, *Ave Maria*, and later the *Mass*. His practice combined genuine devotion with intellectual order, hierarchy, and ritual; Jacques Maritain and Catholic/Orthodox interlocutors mattered, though he did not convert to Catholicism.

Major theatrical collaborations included **Oedipus Rex** (1927; Jean Cocteau, Latin translation by Jean Daniélou), **Apollon musagète** (1928; George Balanchine), **Le Baiser de la fée** (1928; after Tchaikovsky), **Jeu de cartes** (1937; Balanchine), and **Perséphone** (1934; André Gide). His relationship with Cocteau mixed collaboration and suspicion. Gide and Stravinsky clashed over text and staging. With Balanchine, the partnership became one of the century’s most consequential composer–choreographer relationships.

Stravinsky lived principally in France, at Anglet/Biarritz and Nice as well as Paris, while touring Europe and the Americas. From 1921 he maintained the long affair with Vera alongside his family life. He applied unsuccessfully for election to the Institut de France in 1936; financial and critical frustrations, family deaths, and worsening European politics contributed to departure.

He became a French citizen in 1934. In 1935 the family published the French memoir **Chroniques de ma vie**, prepared with Walter Nouvel; it is indispensable but self-fashioning and sometimes factually unreliable. The Harvard invitation for the Charles Eliot Norton lectures offered both income and escape in 1939.

### Politics in interwar Europe

Stravinsky was anti-Bolshevik, monarchically and hierarchically inclined, hostile to liberal internationalism, and admiring of authoritarian order. He praised **Benito Mussolini** after meeting him in 1930 and made pro-Fascist statements. His private correspondence contains anti-Jewish remarks. In 1933 he worried about association with Jewish conductors Klemperer and Walter; in 1934 he told his publisher that his opposition to communism and Judaism was well known; when seeking relief from Nazi restrictions he invoked opposition to “Jewish cultural Bolshevism.” These are documented statements, not merely hostile rumor.

Qualification is necessary. Stravinsky never joined a fascist party, held fascist office, or participated in political violence. He worked extensively with Jewish musicians and patrons before and after 1933, emigrated to the United States rather than collaborate in occupied France, and his music itself was attacked and exhibited by Nazis as “degenerate.” Robert Craft argued that the surviving remarks were concentrated before the mid-1930s and that Stravinsky changed in America. Richard Taruskin and later scholars interpret them as part of a coherent right-wing, antisemitic politics. The documentary record establishes prejudice and pro-Fascist admiration; the characterization of its depth, duration, and relation to the music remains debated.

### American migration and Hollywood, 1939–48

Stravinsky sailed for the United States in September 1939 and delivered the Norton lectures at Harvard in French in 1939–40. Published as **Poétique musicale** (1942), the text has complex authorship: Alexis Roland-Manuel shaped and wrote much of the polished French from conversations and notes, and Pierre Souvtchinsky contributed substantially, including a chapter. It should be catalogued as collaborative/ghosted discourse issued under Stravinsky’s name, not as untouched sole prose.

After marrying Vera, Stravinsky settled in West Hollywood, first at 1260 North Wetherly Drive. Southern California’s émigré community included Schoenberg, Thomas Mann, Alma Mahler-Werfel, Otto Klemperer, Aldous Huxley, and many film artists. Proximity did not imply intimacy: his relationship with **Arnold Schoenberg** was famously cool and competitive. Stravinsky cultivated Anglo-American patrons and institutions while maintaining French and Russian networks.

He explored film commissions but found Hollywood’s demand for flexible, subordinate cues uncongenial. Material contemplated for *The Song of Bernadette*, *Jane Eyre*, and other projects was redirected into concert pieces including *Symphony in Three Movements*. The exact film-project genealogy varies by source.

On 15 January 1944, after conducting his reharmonized arrangement of **The Star-Spangled Banner** in Boston, police warned him that Massachusetts law restricted alteration of the anthem. He was not arrested and no prosecution followed. The often reproduced “mug shot” is in fact a 1940 immigration/alien-registration photograph. The myth combines a real police warning with the unrelated image.

American works included *Symphony in C* (completed 1940), *Danses concertantes*, *Circus Polka* (for Balanchine and Ringling Bros. elephants), *Ode*, *Scherzo à la russe*, *Ebony Concerto* for Woody Herman, *Babel*, *Orpheus*, and the **Mass**. Jazz was an intermittent resource, filtered through notation and European modernism rather than improvisational participation.

Stravinsky became a U.S. citizen on 28 December 1945. Copyright law shaped his revisions. Early Russian works lacked effective U.S. protection; new arrangements and editions of *Firebird*, *Petrushka*, and *Rite* could generate protected performance and rental income. Artistic revision and business necessity overlapped.

### Robert Craft, *The Rake’s Progress*, and serial turn, 1948–60

Young American conductor **Robert Craft** entered the Stravinsky household in 1948. He became assistant, conductor, amanuensis, translator, travel organizer, interlocutor, recording collaborator, and eventually literary executor. Craft introduced Stravinsky more deeply to Webern, Schoenberg, and postwar serial music. Their closeness generated claims that Craft controlled or even composed late Stravinsky. No credible evidence establishes secret musical authorship by Craft; his editorial and linguistic role in the books and his influence on repertory and technique were, however, extensive.

The opera **The Rake’s Progress**, with libretto by W. H. Auden and Chester Kallman after Hogarth, premièred in Venice in 1951. It is the culmination of neoclassicism. Stravinsky suffered severe pneumonia that year. Soon afterward, with Schoenberg dead and the neoclassical project seemingly exhausted, he cautiously adopted serial procedures: *Cantata* (transitional), *Septet*, *Three Songs from William Shakespeare*, *In Memoriam Dylan Thomas*, *Canticum Sacrum*, **Agon**, *Threni*, and *Movements*.

The change was once portrayed as Craft’s capture of an aging composer or capitulation to fashion. Sketches demonstrate Stravinsky’s own sustained technical work. Craft was catalyst, teacher, and advocate; the compositions are Stravinsky’s.

Beginning in the late 1940s and especially under producer Goddard Lieberson, Stravinsky recorded a near-canonical survey of his music for Columbia. Craft often rehearsed or conducted difficult late scores. These recordings preserve the composer’s preferences but are not mechanically definitive: his conducting technique, changing tempi, revised opinions, studio editing, and the participation of Craft complicate the idea of absolute authority.

### International elder statesman, 1960–66

Stravinsky’s late career was exceptionally mobile: festivals, honorary ceremonies, conducting, recording, and residencies across Europe, North America, Israel, South America, South Africa, Australia, and Asia. He and Craft published a series of dialogue/memoir books beginning with **Conversations with Igor Stravinsky** (1959). They contain invaluable testimony but were heavily shaped by Craft and sometimes revise the past polemically.

President John F. Kennedy hosted a White House dinner in Stravinsky’s honor on 18 January 1962. In September–October 1962, aged eighty, Stravinsky returned to the Soviet Union for the first time in nearly half a century, visiting Moscow and Leningrad and conducting concerts. He met Soviet musicians including Shostakovich and Khachaturian and was received by Nikita Khrushchev. The visit served Soviet cultural diplomacy and Stravinsky’s own reconnection with Russia; it did not signal endorsement of Soviet rule.

Late works included *A Sermon, a Narrative and a Prayer*, *The Flood* (for CBS television), *Anthem*, *Abraham and Isaac* (Hebrew), *Elegy for J.F.K.*, *Variations: Aldous Huxley in memoriam*, *Introitus: T. S. Eliot in memoriam*, *Requiem Canticles*, and the final arrangement of Bach’s preludes and fugues.

### Final decline, 1967–71

From 1967 Stravinsky suffered arterial strokes and repeated hospitalization. His last public conducting appearance was in Toronto in 1967 (*Pulcinella*). Increasing weakness, tremor, memory problems, and restricted mobility reduced work. The Stravinskys left Hollywood for New York in 1969, partly to be near medical care and Craft.

He spent part of 1970 at Évian on Lake Geneva and briefly improved. On 18 March 1971 he entered Lenox Hill Hospital with pulmonary edema. Discharged after about ten days, he moved into a newly prepared apartment at 920 Fifth Avenue. Edema returned on 4 April. Vera chose care at home; he stopped taking food and drink and died early on 6 April.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

- **Ballets Russes / Diaghilev:** commission system and international platform from 1909 until Diaghilev’s death in 1929. It produced fame but also disputes over control, revisions, fees, and ownership.
- **Publishers:** early relations included P. Jurgenson and Édition Russe de Musique; later Chester, Schott, and especially Boosey & Hawkes. Publisher competition, territorial copyright, rentals, and revised editions materially shaped the catalogue.
- **Pleyel/Pleyela:** 1921 contracts for mechanical piano rolls. The technology let Stravinsky encode performance information and monetize repertory; it was not literal sound recording.
- **Recording companies:** early acoustic/electrical sessions and a long Columbia Masterworks relationship. The “Stravinsky Conducts Stravinsky” project became both revenue source and curated legacy.
- **Performance enterprise:** From the 1920s he toured as pianist and conductor, collecting fees and promoting his own editions. Works for piano, duo piano, and chamber orchestra were adapted to the economics of touring.
- **Property loss:** Revolution severed access to Ustyluh and Russian assets. Exile accounts consistently report real financial strain, though elite patrons and rapidly growing international fees prevented destitution.
- **Patrons:** Diaghilev, Princess Edmond de Polignac (commissioner of the Piano Concerto), Werner Reinhart, Winnaretta Singer/Polignac’s circle, Coco Chanel, Mildred and Robert Woods Bliss (*Dumbarton Oaks*), Lincoln Kirstein/Balanchine institutions, and American orchestras.
- **Copyright strategy:** Stravinsky revised and reorchestrated works for artistic reasons and to secure new copyright, especially where pre-Revolutionary publication left U.S. rights weak. Posthumous litigation concerned British statutory reversions, publisher shares, descendants, Vera’s heirs, Craft, Chester, and Schott.
- **Archive:** At the end of his life the vast archive—thousands of music pages and roughly 17,000 letters/documents—was reportedly offered for about $3.5 million. Major holdings later went to the Paul Sacher Foundation; additional Stravinsky/Craft material is at the Bodleian, Rice, Library of Congress, and other repositories.

No evidence found supports involvement in patents, industrial inventions, money laundering, corruption, intelligence work, imprisonment, or criminal conviction.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Artistic phases

1. **Apprenticeship (c. 1898–1908):** Russian nationalist and late-Romantic craft under Rimsky-Korsakov.
2. **Russian/Ballets Russes modernism (1909–19):** folklore, ritual, block construction, irregular accent, ostinato, brilliant orchestration.
3. **Neoclassicism (c. 1920–51):** historical forms and styles transformed through rhythmic displacement, harmonic estrangement, and formal clarity. The label conceals wide variety.
4. **Serial period (c. 1952–68):** gradual adoption of rows, rotation, and Webernian compression without abandoning his characteristic rhythm and sonority.

The clean three-period scheme is useful but retrospective. Russian materials recur late; neoclassical procedures begin early; serial thinking grows gradually.

### Aesthetics and authorship

Stravinsky attacked Romantic self-expression and presented composition as ordering, constraint, and craft. The famous proposition that music is “powerless to express anything at all” came from a polemical context and was repeatedly qualified. His prose persona was collaborative: Nouvel helped shape the autobiography; Roland-Manuel and Souvtchinsky the Harvard lectures; Craft the late books. They express Stravinsky’s positions but cannot always be treated as verbatim solitary authorship.

### Religion

Raised Russian Orthodox, Stravinsky became distant from practice and returned around 1925–26. He prayed, attended services, consulted clergy, and composed liturgical or sacred works in Church Slavonic, Latin, and Hebrew. His library and correspondence show movement “between Orthodoxy and Catholicism,” but no documented conversion from Orthodoxy.

### Politics

His settled positions were anti-communism, distrust of egalitarian mass politics, admiration for discipline/hierarchy, Russian émigré conservatism, and—during the interwar period—documented admiration for Mussolini and antisemitic language. In America he performed patriotic gestures, accepted citizenship, participated in Cold War cultural diplomacy, and condemned Soviet control of art. He was not a systematic political theorist or officeholder.

## Controversies, Allegations, Legal Proceedings, and Financial Questions

### Documented

- **Adultery/double household:** Affair with Vera from 1921 while married to Catherine; documented by correspondence and multiple witnesses.
- **Antisemitic statements and pro-Fascist admiration:** Documented in correspondence and interviews. Disagreement concerns interpretation and duration, not the existence of key statements.
- **Nazi restriction:** His music was labeled “degenerate”; he attempted to protect German performances partly by advertising anti-communist and anti-Jewish credentials. This juxtaposition is documented.
- **Copyright-oriented revisions:** Commercial protection was one motive among artistic motives.
- **Craft v. Kobler (S.D.N.Y., 1987):** A federal court found extensive infringement—72 established instances plus disputed additional quotations—in Paul Kobler’s Stravinsky biography from copyrighted Stravinsky/Craft material. The injunction was a copyright ruling, not a judicial finding that Kobler’s biography was factually false.
- **Estate of Stravinsky litigation (New York, 2003):** litigation parsed British reversionary copyrights and royalty shares among descendants/publishers and interests derived through Vera and Craft. The appellate decision records eight-ninths/one-ninth ownership arguments and a separate 20% royalty interest.

### Disputed or unproved

- **Coco Chanel affair:** supported primarily by Chanel’s later recollection; denied by Vera/Craft and unconfirmed by contemporary evidence.
- **Craft as secret composer:** allegation/hostile mythology. Craft’s influence and editorial labor are documented; secret authorship of Stravinsky’s scores is not.
- **Plagiarism:** *Rite* and other Russian-period works use transformed folk sources that Stravinsky later minimized. This supports borrowing and self-mythologizing, not a demonstrated legal theft judgment.
- **Deliberately engineered 1913 riot:** Diaghilev welcomed publicity and audience factions existed, but evidence does not prove he scripted the entire disturbance.

### No substantiated record located

No imprisonment, indictment, criminal conviction, fraud judgment, corruption proceeding, money-laundering allegation, intelligence affiliation, addiction, or political-violence participation was located in the consulted sources. Heavy smoking and recurrent illness are documented; labeling nicotine dependence an “addiction” would be a modern clinical inference rather than a recorded diagnosis.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The Rite of Spring caused a riot”

1. **Story:** New music alone drove an outraged bourgeois audience into a violent riot requiring mass police intervention.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** Contemporary reports described uproar and disputes, but the compact “riot” formula became standard especially in 1920s revivals and later modernist histories, films, program notes, and textbooks.
3. **Evidence:** Noise, laughter, shouting, factional argument, difficulty hearing, and some scuffling are well attested; the show continued and later performances were calmer. Witnesses disagree on onset and cause. Choreography, social faction, expectation, and publicity mattered alongside music.
4. **Assessment:** **Documented disturbance; scale and singular musical causation mythologized.**
5. **Effect:** It made Stravinsky the archetypal revolutionary and gave modernism an origin legend.

### The Boston arrest and “mug shot”

1. **Story:** Boston police arrested and booked Stravinsky for illegally reharmonizing the national anthem.
2. **Origin/circulation:** A real 1944 police warning fused with a frontal/profile photograph widely reposted online.
3. **Evidence:** Police questioned/warned him under a Massachusetts anthem statute; there was no arrest or charge. The image is a 1940 immigration record.
4. **Assessment:** **False as an arrest story; true underlying warning.**
5. **Effect:** Reinforced his image as a modernist outlaw and generated a durable internet meme.

### “A vision created *The Rite*”

1. **Story:** While finishing *Firebird*, Stravinsky suddenly saw elders watching a girl dance herself to death.
2. **Origin:** Most influentially his 1935–36 autobiography; it conflicts with a 1920 account privileging music and with the documented Roerich collaboration.
3. **Evidence:** Scenario development, ethnographic reading, and folk-source research were collaborative and gradual.
4. **Assessment:** **Possible remembered image, unreliable as a complete genesis.**
5. **Effect:** Centered solitary genius and reduced Roerich’s contribution.

### Pure originality and denial of folk sources

Stravinsky acknowledged the opening Lithuanian tune in *Rite* but minimized other borrowings. Source scholarship by Lawrence Morton, Richard Taruskin, and others identifies numerous folk-derived materials. The transformation is radical, but the denial helped manufacture an autonomous cosmopolitan modernist after the Russian period.

### Chanel romance

Later Chanel testimony, biographical repetition, Greenhalgh’s novel, and the 2009 film built a full erotic drama around documented patronage and co-residence at Garches. No conclusive contemporary proof survives. **Unresolved allegation with large cultural afterlife.**

### Stravinsky as perpetual revolutionary

Critical legend treats every stylistic turn as rebellion. Contemporary critics also accused him of reaction, pastiche, coldness, and belated submission to serial fashion. The “chameleon” and “kleptomaniac” hostile myths invert the heroic story. Both respond to the documented practice of appropriating, transforming, and publicly redefining past styles.

### The customs “spy map” Picasso portrait

According to repeated memoir accounts, Italian border officials treated Picasso’s cubist drawing of Stravinsky as a possible coded military plan. A confiscation or extended detention is not securely documented in official records. **Plausible anecdote, details unstable.** It symbolizes wartime suspicion of avant-garde art.

### Books, lectures, interviews, correspondence, and attributed prose

- **Chroniques de ma vie** (Paris, 1935; English *Chronicle of My Life*, 1936). Issued as autobiography; prepared in French with Walter Nouvel. Genuine authorized memoir but collaboratively written and factually selective.
- **Poétique musicale sous forme de six leçons** (Harvard lectures 1939–40; published 1942; English *Poetics of Music in the Form of Six Lessons*, 1947). Under Stravinsky’s name; substantial drafting by Alexis Roland-Manuel and contribution by Pierre Souvtchinsky. Complex collaborative/ghostwritten authorship.
- With Robert Craft: **Conversations with Igor Stravinsky** (1959); **Memories and Commentaries** (1960); **Expositions and Developments** (1962); **Dialogues and a Diary** (1963); **Themes and Episodes** (1966); **Retrospectives and Conclusions** (1969). Authorized dialogue/memoir books, heavily edited and shaped by Craft; not stenographic transcripts.
- **Igor Stravinsky: An Autobiography** (later English editions of *Chroniques*).
- **Selected Correspondence**, ed. Robert Craft, 3 vols. (1982, 1984, 1985): posthumous edited primary documents.
- **Stravinsky: Selected Correspondence**, further editions/selections: posthumous editorial products, not newly authored books.
- Articles, program notes, tributes, interviews, broadcasts, and speeches appeared across *The Times*, *The New York Times*, French/Russian periodicals, recording booklets, and institutional publications. Attribution must be checked case by case because secretarial translation and editorial rewriting were routine.

### Recordings

Stravinsky recorded as pianist, conductor, speaker, and supervisor from the acoustic era through stereo. Key bodies include early piano rolls for Pleyel; 1920s–30s composer-conducted recordings; RCA and American wartime sessions; and Columbia’s systematic composer-led cycle, later reissued as **Igor Stravinsky: The Recorded Legacy** and **Stravinsky Conducts Stravinsky** collections. Discographies should distinguish sessions conducted by Craft “under Stravinsky’s supervision” from those actually conducted by Stravinsky.

### Hostile and sympathetic traditions

The critical record ranges from Theodor W. Adorno’s hostile contrast of Stravinsky’s objectivism with Schoenbergian historical progress, through Pierre Boulez’s criticism of neoclassicism, to Craft’s intimate defense, Taruskin’s ideological/source critique, and later scholarship separating musical analysis from composer-controlled autobiography. These are interpretive traditions, not interchangeable factual sources.

## Awards, Honors, Offices, and Appointments

- French citizenship (1934) and U.S. naturalization (1945) were decisive civic statuses rather than artistic awards.
- Charles Eliot Norton Professor/lecturer at Harvard (1939–40 lectures); a temporary distinguished appointment, not a permanent faculty career.
- Royal Philharmonic Society Gold Medal (1954).
- Sonning Award, Denmark (1959).
- Wihuri Sibelius Prize (1963).
- Erasmus Prize (1959, shared context in standard award listings; exact institutional formulation should be checked against the award archive).
- Multiple Grammy recognitions, including performances/recordings of his music and posthumous Lifetime Achievement recognition (1987); category-level discographies vary by reissue.
- Numerous honorary doctorates and memberships from American and European universities/academies; White House dinner in his honor (1962).
- His 1936 candidacy for the Institut de France failed—an important dismissal/rejection in his French career.

Because honors lists often conflate composition awards, recording awards, honorary memberships, and awards to performers, the above is deliberately conservative.

## Illness, Psychological Strain, and Cause of Death

- Recurrent respiratory illness and tuberculosis risk were lifelong. Catherine and Ludmila died of tuberculosis; Stravinsky underwent sanatorium treatment in 1938–39.
- Typhoid fever followed the 1913 *Rite* première season.
- Severe pneumonia in 1951 was life-threatening.
- Heavy cigarette smoking is repeatedly documented; he temporarily stopped during serious pulmonary episodes.
- From 1967 he experienced arterial strokes, declining mobility, and repeated hospitalization. Sources also describe tremor, fatigue, and memory decline; no firm retrospective psychiatric diagnosis is warranted.
- Final illness: pulmonary edema, hospitalization beginning 18 March 1971, recurrence at home, death on 6 April. “Heart failure” and “pulmonary edema” are compatible levels of description rather than necessarily competing causes.
- No documented alcoholism, narcotic addiction, suicide attempt, psychiatric hospitalization, or major accidental injury was located.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Stravinsky’s death left three intertwined legacies: compositions, composer-supervised recordings, and a controlled autobiographical/archive apparatus. Craft and Vera initially exercised strong influence over documents and quotation; subsequent archive access enabled revisionist scholarship.

His rhythmic language, block construction, instrumentation, recomposition of historical material, and serial practice influenced composers across modernism, film, jazz, rock, minimalism, and concert music. Specific inheritors range from Messiaen and Boulez to Copland, Bernstein, Carter, Reich, Zappa, and many film composers, though “influence” differs from direct borrowing.

*The Rite of Spring* became a repertory staple and a cultural shorthand for modernist rupture; *Firebird*, *Petrushka*, *Pulcinella*, *Symphony of Psalms*, *The Rake’s Progress*, and *Agon* anchor different institutions—ballet, orchestra, opera, choral music, and academic analysis. Balanchine’s stagings helped establish Stravinsky as central to American ballet.

Reinterpretation since the 1980s has emphasized: Russian folk and ethnographic sources that Stravinsky minimized; the labor of collaborators; copyright and commercial strategy; his interwar authoritarian sympathies and antisemitic remarks; collaborative authorship of the prose books; and the independent sophistication of late serial works. These findings complicate rather than erase the catalogue.

The archive itself became contested property. Litigation over quotation and reversionary copyright demonstrated how control of documents, editions, and royalties affected biography. Major institutional acquisition has since broadened scholarly access.
