# Marie Sophie Taglioni: Research Dossier

## Scope and Evidentiary Note

Marie Taglioni’s surviving documentation is unusually rich for a nineteenth-century dancer but unevenly published. The principal archive is the **Fonds Taglioni** at the Bibliothèque nationale de France’s Bibliothèque-musée de l’Opéra. It contains legal documents, passports, contracts, correspondence, contemporary reviews, family papers, Filippo Taglioni’s diaries, and several manuscript or typescript versions of Marie’s autobiography. The New York Public Library holds six microfilm reels of most of this collection. [NYPL archival description](https://archives.nypl.org/dan/19720)

Several basic dates and relationships remain disputed even in authoritative reference works. This dossier therefore distinguishes:

- **Documented fact:** supported by archival, official, or contemporaneous evidence.
- **Reported fact:** repeated in serious secondary literature but not independently confirmed here from the underlying document.
- **Disputed:** materially inconsistent accounts exist.
- **Allegation or theory:** proposed without conclusive documentary proof.
- **Rumor or myth:** a culturally consequential story whose historical truth is doubtful or unverified.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal family

Marie belonged to a trans-European Italian theatrical dynasty.

- **Father — Filippo Taglioni** (also Philippe Taglioni, 1777–1871): dancer, ballet master, teacher, and extraordinarily prolific choreographer. His employment carried the family among Stockholm, Vienna, Kassel, Paris, Stuttgart, Munich, Berlin, London, Warsaw, Milan, and St Petersburg. He became Marie’s principal teacher, choreographic author, artistic manager, and contractual partner.
- **Paternal grandfather — Carlo Taglioni:** dancer and choreographer. Older reference works sometimes confuse Carlo with his son Salvatore or describe Salvatore as Marie’s grandfather.
- **Uncle — Salvatore Taglioni** (1789–1868 in some authorities; other dates occur): dancer and ballet master strongly associated with Naples.
- The extended Taglioni family included dancers and choreographers such as Louise Taglioni and Marie’s brother and niece.

Filippo’s notebooks record engagements, compensation, roles, and choreography from 1817 onward. They remain in the BnF’s Fonds Taglioni and constitute an important corrective to retrospective family mythology. [Treccani family entry](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/taglioni_%28Dizionario-Biografico%29/)

### Maternal family

- **Mother — Hedvig Sophia Karsten**, usually called **Sophie Karsten** (1783–1862), a Swedish dancer.
- **Maternal grandfather — Christoffer Christian Karsten** (1756–1827), prominent Swedish opera singer.
- **Maternal grandmother — Sophie Stebnowska**, a Polish-born singer and actress.

Marie’s maternal ancestry therefore connected her not merely to Sweden but to the Polish-Swedish operatic stage.

### Siblings

- **Paul Taglioni** (1808–1884): dancer, ballet master, and choreographer. Marie and Paul appeared together during their early careers. Paul later worked extensively in Berlin.
- **Gustave Taglioni:** mentioned in some biographical accounts as another brother, born during the family’s Austrian years; he did not attain Paul’s theatrical importance.

### Niece and recurrent identity confusion

Paul’s daughter **Marie Taglioni the Younger** (1833–1891) was also a dancer. She later married Prince Joseph Windisch-Grätz. Catalogues and popular histories sometimes attribute events or honors belonging to the niece to the elder Marie. In particular, Johann Strauss II’s **“Marie Taglioni Polka,” Op. 173**, honored the younger dancer, not the creator of the Sylph.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1804–1821

Marie was born in Stockholm while Filippo was employed in Sweden. Her earliest years followed the unstable geography of a theatrical family dependent on short contracts. Accounts place the family successively in Vienna, Kassel, and Paris; the precise sequence and duration vary.

During the Napoleonic wars Sophie Karsten and the children lived for a substantial period in Paris while Filippo continued traveling for employment. Marie began formal ballet training there under **Jean-François Coulon**, an important Paris Opéra teacher who had also taught Filippo.

Retrospective biographies describe her as an unpromising student: thin, stooped, long-limbed, awkward, inattentive, and sometimes mocked as a “little hunchback” or “ugly duckling.” Some writers infer spinal curvature from these descriptions. No medical diagnosis has been located, and the language comes largely from later biographical tradition rather than a clinical record.

A repeated story says that Paris ballet master **Jean Aumer** advised Sophie that Marie would be better suited to dressmaking. This is a **reported anecdote**, not an independently verified judgment from an identified contemporary document.

### Filippo’s intensive training regime

When Filippo obtained a Vienna appointment and prepared Marie for a stage debut, he found her technique inadequate. He imposed approximately five or six months of intensive daily training.

Later accounts describe:

- two hours of difficult leg exercises in the morning;
- two hours devoted to adagio, balance, and poses in the afternoon;
- additional practice bringing the total as high as six hours;
- positions held for counts of one hundred;
- repeated jumps, pointe exercises, and work intended to produce silent landings;
- exhaustion severe enough that Marie sometimes collapsed or required assistance undressing;
- bleeding or injured toes;
- Sophie Karsten weeping while Filippo continued the lessons.

The broad fact of extreme training is well established in dance biography, including Marie’s own retrospective material. The most dramatic physical details have been transmitted through later writers and should be treated as **reported facts**, not as a complete contemporaneous record. A modern gender-history reading describes the regime as brutal discipline and bodily exploitation, while older biographies presented the same conduct as severe but productive artistic formation. [Molly Engelhardt’s study](https://www.ncgsjournal.com/issue63/engelhardt.html)

## Education and Formation

### Teachers and mentors

- **Jean-François Coulon:** initial professional training in Paris.
- **Sophie Karsten:** maternal instruction and supervision are mentioned in some accounts.
- **Filippo Taglioni:** decisive technical and artistic teacher, choreographer, manager, and controlling professional influence.
- **Jean Aumer:** not a sustained teacher but part of the Paris institutional setting and, according to tradition, an early skeptic.
- Later in her career, repeated work with **Jules Perrot** expanded her repertory and placed her beside other major ballerinas.

### Technical formation

Filippo constructed a style around both Marie’s strengths and her unconventional proportions:

- exceptional balance;
- large, apparently effortless elevation;
- quiet descents through the toe and then the heel;
- prolonged balances on pointe;
- restrained pirouettes and avoidance of overt muscular display;
- softened arms and upper torso;
- clear, unmannered mime;
- an appearance of vertical elongation and weightlessness.

Marie later described attempting to remain straight without rigidity and to land toe-first so that the heel reached the ground gradually and silently. The surviving memoir texts are important evidence, although their textual history and authorship require care: multiple manuscript and typescript versions exist, and at least one scholar has described the authorship of a frequently cited *Mes souvenirs* text as uncertain. [Italian biographical discussion](https://www.enciclopediadelledonne.it/edd.nsf/biografie/maria-taglioni) [Interview with editor Bruno Ligore](https://dansercanalhistorique.fr/?q=content%2Fpublication-des-souvenirs-de-marie-taglioni-linterview)

### Artistic and intellectual context

Her artistic image emerged from European Romanticism:

- supernatural women, spirits, dreams, unattainable ideals, moonlit forests, and divided worlds;
- German and French Romantic literature;
- operatic works derived from Goethe and supernatural legend;
- the increasing theatrical importance of gas lighting, gauze, wire effects, painted panoramas, and illusionistic scenery;
- a change from eighteenth-century courtly ornament and earthy bravura toward an apparently incorporeal female ideal.

This was not an articulated political philosophy by Taglioni. No substantial political writings or public ideological program are known. Her significance lies in embodied theatrical aesthetics and institutional practice.

## Early Career, 1822–1826

### Vienna debut

On **10 June 1822**, at Vienna’s Kärntnertortheater, Marie appeared in a ballet created by Filippo for her:

*La Réception d’une jeune nymphe à la cour de Terpsichore*  
(*The Reception of a Young Nymph at the Court of Terpsichore*)

The debut was successful and established the professional partnership between father and daughter.

### German and Austrian engagements

During the following years Marie performed in Vienna, Munich, and Stuttgart, generally in ballets mounted or adapted by Filippo. Paul sometimes danced with her. These provincial and court-theatre years allowed Filippo to refine her repertory before taking her to Paris.

The exact itinerary differs among biographies, partly because the family repeatedly crossed borders for short contracts. The Fonds Taglioni contains reviews beginning in 1826 and later contracts arranged by city and date.

## Chronological Life History

## Paris and the Construction of “La Taglioni,” 1827–1831

### Paris Opéra debut

Marie made her Paris Opéra debut in July 1827, most often dated **23 July**, in a pas de deux inserted into *Le Sicilien, ou L’Amour peintre*. Some modern accounts describe six trial appearances before the Opéra granted her a regular engagement.

A story that rival dancers might sabotage her debut, causing her rehearsal to be conducted privately with the orchestra, is repeated in popular biographies but was not confirmed here from a primary document.

Her immediate repertory included:

- *Le Sicilien*;
- *Le Carnaval de Venise*;
- *La Belle au bois dormant*;
- the Tyrolienne in Rossini’s *Guillaume Tell*;
- *Manon Lescaut*, including the pas of Niuka;
- Zoloé in Auber and Scribe’s *Le Dieu et la Bayadère* (1830).

### Contract and earnings

Under Paris Opéra director **Louis-Désiré Véron**, Marie and Filippo obtained an exceptional contract. A secondary study citing André Levinson gives Marie’s salary as **30,000 francs annually**, with three months’ leave, control over her roles, and a privilege for works choreographed by Filippo or eventually herself. The figure should be treated as a **reported contractual amount** pending inspection of the original contract in the Fonds Taglioni. [Contract discussion](https://thesis.unipd.it/retrieve/9aee7cfd-86de-4eec-8325-7d60e1372334/Zanta_Silvia.pdf)

### *Robert le Diable*

On 21 November 1831 Meyerbeer’s *Robert le Diable* premiered at the Paris Opéra. Filippo choreographed its celebrated **Ballet of the Nuns**, and Marie danced the abbess Helena, rising from a tomb in a moonlit cloister. The sequence’s erotic-supernatural imagery joined the sacred, forbidden, and spectral, anticipating *La Sylphide*.

Véron’s management integrated composers, librettists, dancers, scenery, lighting, and publicity into the commercial form of Parisian grand opera. Marie’s stage identity was therefore not an individual invention alone but the product of a network involving Filippo, Véron, Meyerbeer, Eugène Scribe, Adolphe Nourrit, designers, musicians, lithographers, and critics. [Paris Opéra history](https://www.operadeparis.fr/en/about/history/louis-desire-veron-1798-1867)

## *La Sylphide* and International Celebrity, 1832–1834

### Premiere and collaborators

Filippo Taglioni’s *La Sylphide* premiered at the Paris Opéra on **12 or 14 March 1832**; modern Paris Opéra material gives 14 March, while some reference traditions give 12 March.

Principal contributors were:

- choreography: Filippo Taglioni;
- title role: Marie Taglioni;
- libretto: tenor **Adolphe Nourrit**;
- music: **Jean-Madeleine Schneitzhoeffer**;
- scenery: associated with Pierre-Luc-Charles Cicéri;
- costume design: usually credited to **Eugène Lami**.

The narrative follows the Scottish farmer James, who abandons his fiancée Effie for an unattainable sylph and destroys the supernatural woman when he attempts to possess her.

### Pointe work

Marie was **not securely the first person or first woman to rise onto the tips of the toes**. Antoine Pitrot, Geneviève Gosselin, Amalia Brugnoli, and other dancers preceded her in forms of toe dancing. What *La Sylphide* did was give pointe work a sustained expressive and dramatic purpose: it made the ballerina seem weightless and ontologically different from the humans around her.

Her shoes lacked the rigid box and shank of a modern pointe shoe. They were soft satin slippers reinforced through darning or stitching. She therefore could not perform prolonged modern pointe sequences in the contemporary manner.

The Paris Opéra has sometimes described *La Sylphide* as the first ballet danced entirely on pointe. More recent dance historians qualify that institutional shorthand: Taglioni rose only intermittently, and pointe technique evolved incrementally. [Paris Opéra nineteenth-century history](https://www.operadeparis.fr/apropos/histoire/lopera-au-19e-siecle) [Le Monde on pointe history](https://www.lemonde.fr/series-d-ete/article/2024/07/26/les-pointes-prolongement-du-corps-du-danseur_6258804_3451060.html)

### Costume and visual image

The Sylph’s white muslin or tulle dress—with fitted bodice, exposed shoulders and neck, and a bell-shaped skirt ending around mid-calf—became the prototype of the **Romantic tutu**. Wings, floral head ornaments, white stockings, and pale slippers completed the image.

Taglioni did not single-handedly “invent the tutu.” Rather, Lami’s costume and her performance helped canonize a garment emerging from wider changes in women’s dress and theatrical technique.

### Reception and celebrity economy

The performance established what contemporaries called “La Taglioni”:

- critics likened her to Ariel, air, vapor, flame, and poetry;
- lithographs by **Alfred Edward Chalon** and others circulated her roles;
- poems, songs, statuettes, prints, dolls, hairstyles, fabrics, and keepsakes traded on her name;
- white dresses, pale complexions, and Sylph-inspired fashion spread among admirers;
- her surname became shorthand for a style of dance and a class of ethereal femininity.

A cholera epidemic soon disrupted Parisian performances, after which she toured Berlin and London.

## Marriage, Separation, Partners, and Children

### Gilbert de Voisins

Marie married **Jean-Pierre-Victor-Alfred Gilbert de Voisins** (1800–1863), generally called Count Gilbert de Voisins or Auguste Gilbert de Voisins in some English accounts.

The marriage chronology is confused:

- **September 1832 in London** appears in some sources;
- **14 July 1834**, a Protestant ceremony in London, appears in another substantial biographical account;
- a French civil ceremony reportedly followed in Paris on **28 August 1834**, delayed because the groom’s father opposed the marriage;
- Treccani treats 1832 as the marriage year;
- separation is variously dated 1835 or 1836;
- divorce is reported in 1844.

The inconsistencies may reflect multiple ceremonies rather than one erroneous date. The archival collection includes family legal papers that may resolve the sequence.

The marriage was brief in practice. Marie and the count lived largely separate lives.

### Eugène Desmares

Marie’s relationship with **Victor-Eugène Desmares**, a writer, librettist, or devoted admirer, is a major but incompletely documented part of her private life.

Reported episodes include:

- Desmares defending her honor in a duel;
- meeting or beginning an affair around a masked ball in 1836;
- Desmares contributing the scenario for *La Fille du Danube*;
- his death after illness or in a hunting accident, usually placed around 1839.

Accounts disagree over whether Desmares fathered Marie’s first or second child. No conclusive evidence reviewed here establishes biological paternity.

### Children

Marie had two legally recognized children:

1. **Hedda-Marie-Eugénie-Ernestine Gilbert de Voisins**, also called Marie, Eugénie, Edwige, or “Nini,” born **30 March 1836**. She married Russian Prince **Alexander Troubetzkoy/Trubetskoy** and had children.
2. **Georges-Philippe-Marie Gilbert de Voisins**, called Georges or Youri, born **5 October 1843**. He later married **Sozonga Ralli**, connected to the wealthy Greek-Levantine Ralli mercantile family.

Gilbert de Voisins recognized both children legally. Their biological paternity remains **unresolved**. Published suggestions identify Desmares or Prince Alexander Trubetskoy, but the claims are mutually inconsistent and are not supported here by genetic or conclusive documentary evidence. They should not be presented as established fact.

## Parisian Competition and Uneven Success, 1833–1837

Marie continued creating roles for Filippo:

- **Zulma** in *La Révolte au sérail* (1833);
- title or principal roles in *Nathalie, ou La Laitière suisse*;
- *La Fille du Danube* (1836), with music by Adolphe Adam and a scenario associated with Desmares;
- *Brézilia, ou La Tribu des femmes* (1835).

Not every work succeeded. Italian biographical scholarship records:

- **failure or weak reception** for *Brézilia* in 1835;
- **critical dissent** concerning her performance as Fleur-des-Champs in *La Fille du Danube* in 1836.

These reverses complicate later accounts that portray an uninterrupted triumph.

### Fanny Elssler and rival publics

**Fanny Elssler** became Marie’s chief symbolic rival at the Paris Opéra. Their contrast was repeatedly stylized:

- Taglioni: aerial, chaste, Christian or spiritual, northern, white;
- Elssler: terrestrial, sensual, fiery, southern or Spanish, colorful.

Supporters divided into “Taglionists” and “Elsslerists,” and critics shifted allegiance. Théophile Gautier, initially an admirer of Taglioni, increasingly championed Elssler.

This rivalry was real as a matter of repertory, publicity, critical discourse, and audience partisanship. Claims of sustained personal hatred or organized private warfare are harder to substantiate. Much of the antagonism was manufactured or amplified by managers, newspapers, critics, and fan groups.

## Russia and Continuing European Tours, 1837–1842

Marie and Filippo accepted a major contract with the Russian Imperial Theatres. The engagement is often described as a five-year arrangement covering **1837–1842**, although Marie’s first contract may have been followed by renewals.

She made her St Petersburg debut in *La Sylphide* at the **Imperial Bolshoi Kamenny Theatre** on **6 September 1837**.

### Russian repertory

Productions included revivals and new works:

- *La Sylphide*;
- *La Fille du Danube*;
- *Miranda* (1838);
- *La Gitana* (1838);
- *L’Ombre* (1839);
- *Aglaé, ou L’Élève de l’Amour* (1841);
- *Gerta, reine des Elfrides* (1842).

In *La Gitana*, Marie danced the Spanish cachucha, showing that her repertory was not confined to passive or incorporeal spirits. *L’Ombre* exploited the image of a heroine pursuing or attempting to grasp her own shadow.

Russian imperial patronage included valuable jewelry. Secondary accounts report gifts from Tsar Nicholas I, including diamond and emerald brooches and earrings and a diamond-and-turquoise floral ornament.

### Touring within the Russian contract

The engagement allowed or required travel during off-seasons. Documented or strongly reported appearances included:

- London, repeatedly between 1839 and 1845;
- Vienna, 1839–40;
- Warsaw, 1840;
- Stockholm, 1841;
- Milan, beginning in 1841;
- Trieste, Vicenza, Bologna, and Rome during the first half of the 1840s.

Filippo’s Russian contract and the bureaucratic machinery behind it are supported by material in the Russian State Historical Archive. [Russian archival study](https://russianmusicology.com/index.php/RM/article/download/1282/1353)

### Russian farewell

Marie’s last Russian season ended in 1842. Her farewell was associated with intense “balletomania,” imperial gifts, souvenirs, and the famous shoe-eating legend discussed below.

---

## Italy, London, and Retirement, 1841–1847

Marie appeared at Milan’s La Scala, with a major debut there on **20 May 1841**, and subsequently toured other Italian cities. Italian reception framed her simultaneously as an international Parisian celebrity and as an artist belonging to an Italian family.

### *Pas de Quatre*

In July 1845 at Her Majesty’s Theatre, London, impresario **Benjamin Lumley** and choreographer **Jules Perrot** assembled four leading ballerinas:

- Marie Taglioni;
- Carlotta Grisi;
- Fanny Cerrito;
- Lucile Grahn.

Cesare Pugni composed the music. The ordering of entrances and bows required diplomatic negotiation because precedence signified professional rank. Taglioni, the senior figure, received the place of honor. Queen Victoria attended the third performance, although the frequently repeated claim that the work itself was created “by command of the queen” is less securely documented.

Fanny Elssler was not a participant; accounts that she refused to appear alongside the other four circulate, but the specific motive requires caution.

### Final performances

Taglioni appeared in Perrot’s *Le Jugement de Pâris*, or the associated *Pas des Déesses*, with Cerrito and Grahn. Her last documented public performance occurred in London on **21 August 1847**, when she danced Venus.

A nineteenth-century DNB account claimed that the sensational arrival of singer Jenny Lind displaced ballet at Her Majesty’s Theatre and that Taglioni retired rather than tolerate a rival. It attributes to her the remark, “La danse est comme la Turquie, bien malade” (“Dance, like Turkey, is very ill”). This is a **reported anecdote from a late secondary source**, not a secure explanation of retirement. Age, repertory change, finances, family circumstances, and shifting audience fashion all provide less theatrical explanations. [Original DNB entry](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionary_of_National_Biography%2C_1885-1900/Taglioni%2C_Marie)

## Property, Wealth, and Financial Relationships

### Peak wealth

Taglioni earned exceptional salaries and accumulated jewelry, art, and property. A later *Times* account described her collection as a museum of jewelry and artworks. After the Russian period she invested in or occupied substantial Italian properties:

- a residence at **Blevio on Lake Como**;
- Venetian property, including a residence associated in accounts with the **Ca’ d’Oro**;
- a second Venetian palace is mentioned in some biographies.

Exact title records, purchase prices, and ownership structures were not established from the accessible sources. “Ca’ d’Oro” may refer to residence or occupation rather than simple sole ownership; the distinction should be preserved.

### Financial collapse

The common narrative is that:

- Filippo made unwise investments or speculated with family funds;
- Marie’s capital was substantially depleted;
- market disruption around the Franco-Prussian War and Paris Commune worsened her position;
- loss of her Paris Opéra post in 1870 eliminated regular income;
- she relocated to London and taught for money.

The BnF history committee states that she died “in destitution.” Other sources say “poor” or “bankrupt.” These descriptions establish severe financial contraction but do not supply a probate inventory, creditor schedule, or exact net worth. “Destitute” should therefore be understood as a reported condition, not a quantified insolvency judgment. [BnF history committee](https://comitehistoire.bnf.fr/dictionnaire-fonds/taglioni)

No evidence found in the dedicated adversarial search supports fraud, embezzlement, money laundering, criminal business activity, or formal bankruptcy proceedings by Marie herself.

---

## Retirement in Italy, 1847–1858

After leaving the stage, Marie lived for years near Lake Como and in Venice. Her daughter’s marriage connected the family to Prince Alexander Troubetzkoy and to properties near Blevio.

She remained socially prominent and retained connections to theatrical Europe, but documentation for this interval is less continuous than for her performing years. Her father died in 1871, not in 1852 as stated by one modern popular page.

---

## Paris Opéra Administrator, Teacher, and Choreographer, 1858/59–1870

Marie returned to Paris when the Paris Opéra’s dance organization was being reformed. She became **inspectrice des classes et du service de la danse**, variously dated from 1858 or 1859 to 1870.

Her functions included:

- supervising advanced dance classes;
- advising on training and professional discipline;
- participating with Lucien Petipa, Louis Mérante, and others in the jury for the first annual corps-de-ballet competition on **13 April 1860**;
- mentoring selected dancers;
- contributing to a more regularized system of evaluation.

Her administrative role is significant because women were rarely entrusted with comparable choreographic or institutional authority in major European theatres.

### Emma Livry

Taglioni’s most famous pupil was **Emma Livry** (1842–1863), widely regarded as the last outstanding French ballerina of the Romantic generation. Livry’s success in *La Sylphide* was understood as a revival of Taglioni’s own tradition.

### *Le Papillon*

Taglioni choreographed *Le Papillon*, premiered at the Paris Opéra on **26 November 1860**.

Credits:

- choreography: Marie Taglioni;
- scenario/libretto: Jules-Henri Vernoy de Saint-Georges;
- music: Jacques Offenbach;
- principal role: Emma Livry as Farfalla;
- Louis Mérante as Prince Djalma;
- Louise Marquet as Hamza;
- scenery by a large Paris Opéra team including Despléchin, Nolau, Rubé, Cambon, Martin, and Thierry;
- costumes by Alfred Albert.

The ballet succeeded, and account documents for performances through March 1861 survive in the Fonds Taglioni. [Rome Opera historical catalogue](https://archiviostorico.operaroma.it/balletto/le-papillon/) [NYPL archival description](https://archives.nypl.org/dan/19720)

### Emma Livry’s fire

On **15 November 1862**, while rehearsing the opera *La Muette de Portici*, Livry’s gauze costume caught fire from a gaslight. Fireproofing treatments existed, but dancers often resisted them because they discolored and stiffened fabric; theatre administrations commonly used liability waivers instead.

Taglioni was present or arrived immediately. A repeated account says she applied theatrical grease or makeup remover to Livry’s burns in the mistaken belief that it would soothe them. Whether this materially worsened the injuries is not established by the accessible medical evidence. Livry survived for eight months and died on **26 July 1863**.

This was an industrial theatre accident involving flammable costume, open gaslight, and inadequate safety practice. There is no evidence that Taglioni caused the ignition. Her use of grease, if accurately reported, was an improvised and medically mistaken response, not intentional harm.

### *Zara*

Taglioni’s projected second ballet, *Zara*, had:

- choreography by Taglioni;
- scenario by Charles Nuitter;
- music by Ernest Boulanger;
- scenery by Édouard Despléchin;
- costumes by Alfred Albert.

Rehearsals began on 28 October 1862 with Livry in the title role. After Livry’s accident, Amalia Ferraris and then Mlle Vernon were considered or rehearsed. The project was finally abandoned in March 1863. Sets were reused for *Don Juan* and later *La Source*. [BnF production record](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark%3A/12148/cb409167985)

This failure was not simply a consequence of Livry’s eventual death: replacement-casting problems also prevented completion.

## London Teaching and Final Years, 1870–1884

The Franco-Prussian War closed or disrupted Parisian theatrical operations, ending Taglioni’s Opéra appointment. She then lived in London, generally dated **1871–1880**, teaching:

- ballet to a limited number of pupils;
- ballroom and social dancing;
- posture, movement, and deportment;
- the ceremonial curtsey required for presentation at court.

She reportedly lived at **14 Connaught Square** in 1875–76. The diaries of amateur pupil **Margaret Rolfe**, covering lessons between 1876 and 1880, are an important record of this period, although they were not available in full through the sources consulted.

These lessons placed the former international star in a financially dependent but still prestigious role. Her pupils included upper-class girls and women who valued instruction from a living emblem of courtly grace.

Around **1880**—some sources say 1882—she moved to Marseille to live with her son Georges and his family.

## Death and Burial

Marie died in Marseille, most probably on **22 April 1884**, one day before her eightieth birthday.

Competing death dates include:

- 22 April: modern French and Italian authority-file tradition;
- 23 April: several encyclopedic accounts;
- 24 April: the original DNB and some later art catalogues.

No specific terminal illness or medically documented cause of death was found in the consulted material. Reports simply describe old age and poverty.

She was initially buried in Marseille and later transferred to the Gilbert de Voisins family vault in **Division 94 of Père-Lachaise Cemetery**, Paris.

### The “wrong grave” at Montmartre

A grave in Montmartre Cemetery is routinely identified by visitors as Marie Taglioni’s. Local grave research indicates that it is actually the tomb of her mother, Sophie, bearing an inscription from Marie to her “beloved mother.” Dancers nevertheless leave worn pointe shoes there in tribute to Marie.

Thus:

- **Documented/reported fact:** Marie’s remains are in the Gilbert de Voisins family tomb at Père-Lachaise.
- **Mistaken popular identification:** the Montmartre grave is Marie’s.
- **Modern ritual fact:** dancers deposit shoes at the Montmartre tomb regardless of the mistaken identification.
- **Historical significance:** the geographical error has created a functioning memorial site, making the “wrong grave” part of Taglioni’s posthumous cult. [Montmartre grave investigation](https://www.landrucimetieres.fr/spip/spip.php?article84=&commenter=oui&debut_article_rubrique_date=80&id_forum=1916)

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Political activity

No evidence was found of party membership, public office, electioneering, revolutionary activity, imprisonment, exile imposed by a government, or political prosecution.

Her career was nevertheless structurally political:

- she depended on Bourbon, July Monarchy, Habsburg, British royal, Russian imperial, and Second Empire institutions;
- her Russian position relied on imperial patronage under Nicholas I;
- the Franco-Prussian War materially disrupted her employment and investments;
- images of chaste supernatural femininity circulated within bourgeois and aristocratic gender culture.

These conditions should not be mistaken for a documented political program of her own.

### Religion

No firm evidence of distinctive religious commitment, conversion, devotional movement, or theological writing was found. Her marriage involved a Protestant London ceremony and a later French civil proceeding, but that sequence appears to have been driven at least partly by family and legal complications.

Her roles exploited Christian and supernatural iconography, notably the resurrected nuns in *Robert le Diable*. Stage imagery is not evidence of her personal belief.

### Artistic development

Her artistic trajectory moved through four phases:

1. **Technical reconstruction:** Filippo remade an initially weak student through extreme physical training.
2. **Parisian synthesis:** pointe, elevation, quiet landings, restrained mime, white costume, and supernatural narrative formed a new style.
3. **International expansion:** she broadened the style through national and character dances, imperial touring, and celebrity commerce.
4. **Transmission:** as inspector, teacher, and choreographer she attempted to institutionalize and pass on the Romantic style, principally through Emma Livry.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Filippo Taglioni — father, teacher, manager, choreographer

The central relationship of her life. Their partnership produced her style and most of her signature roles. It also involved control, harsh bodily discipline, contract negotiation, and possibly damaging financial management. Marie copied portions of Filippo’s diary, evidence of continued family archival work.

### Sophie Karsten — mother

Dancer, first teacher, emotional support, and witness to Filippo’s severe training. Marie’s dedication on Sophie’s Montmartre tomb later generated the mistaken belief that Marie herself was buried there.

### Paul Taglioni — brother and colleague

Dancer and choreographer; performed with Marie early and extended the Taglioni dynasty through his daughter.

### Louis-Désiré Véron — director and commercial patron

Recognized and monetized the Taglioni phenomenon at the Paris Opéra; negotiated exceptional terms and placed her in major productions.

### Adolphe Nourrit, Jean-Madeleine Schneitzhoeffer, Eugène Lami, Cicéri

The librettist, composer, designer, and scenographic network that made *La Sylphide* possible.

### Giacomo Meyerbeer and Eugène Scribe

Operatic collaborators in the Paris environment, especially *Robert le Diable* and *Le Dieu et la Bayadère*.

### Jules Perrot

Dancer and choreographer who partnered Marie and later placed her at the center of *Pas de Quatre* and *Le Jugement de Pâris*.

### Fanny Elssler

Professional rival and symbolic antithesis. The rivalry was magnified by press, critics, claques, repertory, and gendered national stereotypes.

### Carlotta Grisi, Fanny Cerrito, Lucile Grahn

Co-stars in *Pas de Quatre*. The collaboration was simultaneously a celebration and a carefully negotiated hierarchy among rival celebrity ballerinas.

### Eugène Desmares

Intimate companion or lover, defender, and contributor to *La Fille du Danube*. Details of the relationship and paternity claims remain unresolved.

### Emma Livry

Pupil, protégée, and intended carrier of Taglioni’s tradition. Taglioni choreographed *Le Papillon* and planned *Zara* for her. Livry’s fatal burning ended that project.

### Lucien Petipa and Louis Mérante

Colleagues in the Paris Opéra’s dance examinations and professional reorganization.

### Théophile Gautier

Critic who helped define Taglioni’s literary reputation, later shifted allegiance toward Elssler, and continued assessing Taglioni in the 1840s.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### 1. Was Filippo’s training abusive?

**Documented core:** exceptionally severe, exhausting training imposed by a father who also controlled Marie’s repertory and livelihood.

**Interpretive dispute:** older accounts describe heroic discipline; recent gender scholarship calls it brutal bodily control and exploitation. The evidence supports coercive severity but does not allow a modern clinical or legal finding about abuse.

### 2. Did Marie “invent” pointe?

**Disputed claim:** she is often called the first ballerina to dance en pointe or inventor of the pointe shoe.

**Evidence against literal priority:** toe dancing predated her; Geneviève Gosselin, Amalia Brugnoli, and Antoine Pitrot are among the predecessors. Taglioni’s shoes were soft and lacked the modern box.

**Strongest formulation:** she made pointe an integrated aesthetic and dramatic language and thereby established it as a defining ballerina technique.

### 3. Did she invent the tutu?

**Disputed/overstated:** *La Sylphide* popularized the white calf-length Romantic dress, but costume design was collaborative and evolved from contemporary fashion. Eugène Lami, dressmakers, theatre workshops, and earlier stage costumes share the history.

### 4. Sexual respectability and the paternity of her children

**Documented:** Gilbert de Voisins legally recognized both children; the couple separated.

**Reported:** Marie had a relationship with Eugène Desmares.

**Unresolved:** the biological paternity of Hedda and Georges.

**Rumor:** later accounts name Desmares or Alexander Trubetskoy without conclusive evidence. The stories reflect nineteenth-century scrutiny of ballerinas’ sexuality and aristocratic patronage as much as recoverable family fact.

### 5. Nepotism and insider advantage

Marie was born into a theatrical dynasty and benefited from her father’s choreography, teaching, contracts, and professional connections. This is a **documented structural advantage**. It does not negate the equally documented physical labor and technical distinction on which her career depended.

### 6. Rivalry with Fanny Elssler

The rivalry is documented in criticism, programming, and fan affiliation. Stories of personal enmity should be separated from publicity-driven opposition. Managers and critics profited from casting dancers as moral and erotic opposites.

### 7. Emma Livry’s treatment after the fire

Taglioni reportedly applied theatrical grease to Livry’s burns. That response was medically erroneous. There is no evidence of malicious intent, formal accusation, criminal investigation, or legal liability against Taglioni.

### 8. Financial mismanagement

Marie’s fortune diminished through investments generally attributed to Filippo, compounded by the upheavals of 1870. No detailed accounting was found. Claims that Filippo simply “stole” her fortune are unsupported; “bad speculation or investment” is the strongest defensible wording.

### 9. Adversarial-search findings

Dedicated searches combining Taglioni’s name with controversy, scandal, accusation, lawsuit, fraud, conspiracy, and criticism—including material published in 2024–26—found no credible evidence of:

- criminal charges or convictions;
- fraud or corruption;
- civil litigation of public consequence;
- political violence;
- intelligence relationships;
- money laundering;
- substance addiction;
- deliberate professional sabotage;
- sexual coercion committed by Taglioni.

The significant negative or contested material concerns paternal training, privilege and nepotism, uncertain paternity, aesthetic mythology, financial collapse, professional rivalry, and her mistaken first aid after Livry’s accident.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### The eaten shoes

**Story:** After Taglioni’s 1842 Russian farewell, balletomanes purchased her used slippers for 200 rubles, cooked them with sauce, and ate them.

**Earliest trace identified:** London’s *Morning Post*, 3 January 1855—thirteen years after the supposed event—reporting a lecture by conservative travel writer **Edward Tracy Turnerelli**. Turnerelli claimed personal participation. His lecture portrayed Russians as extravagantly enthralled by foreigners.

**Evidence for:** a named claimant presented the story publicly; extreme souvenir collecting around Taglioni is independently consistent with Russian balletomania.

**Evidence against or limiting confidence:** the account is late, sensationalist, politically colored, and lacks a corroborating 1842 Russian document. Later versions disagree over whether 35 men participated and whether the price was 200 or 1,000 rubles.

**Label:** **unverified contemporary-adjacent anecdote, subsequently mythologized**.

**Reputational effect:** it became the most durable symbol of the “cult of the ballerina,” literalizing consumption of celebrity. [ABC investigation](https://www.abc.net.au/listen/classic/features/can-you-really-eat-a-ballet-slipper/9771926)

### The first dancer on pointe

**Story:** Taglioni invented pointe dancing in *La Sylphide*.

**Origin and circulation:** nineteenth- and twentieth-century ballet histories seeking a single founding moment; dance schools, encyclopedias, product histories, and publicity repeated it.

**Evidence:** she unquestionably made pointe central to a historically transformative role.

**Contrary evidence:** earlier practitioners are documented, and the technique evolved gradually.

**Label:** **founder myth built around a genuine transformation but false as strict priority**.

### The effortless natural genius

**Story:** Taglioni floated naturally and danced without visible exertion.

**Evidence:** contemporary spectators repeatedly described silence, buoyancy, suspension, and apparent ease.

**Corrective evidence:** her style resulted from grueling daily training, exceptional strength, careful mechanics, and continued rehearsal.

**Label:** **self-conscious performance effect converted by criticism into mythology**.

### The sexless or wholly chaste Sylph

**Story:** Taglioni displaced sensual ballet with pure spirituality.

**Evidence:** critics contrasted her restrained style with Elssler’s sensual character dancing.

**Complication:** *La Sylphide* is a narrative of erotic temptation, betrayal, possession, and death; *Robert le Diable* eroticized resurrected nuns. Taglioni’s public image sublimated sexuality rather than eliminating it.

**Label:** **critical ideology, not a literal description of repertory or private life**.

### Retirement because of Jenny Lind

**Story:** singer Jenny Lind’s arrival displaced Taglioni, who refused to endure a rival and retired.

**Evidence:** a late nineteenth-century DNB article gives the story and a witty remark about dance being “very ill.”

**Against:** Taglioni was already in her forties after a long international career; no decisive primary document establishes Lind as the sole cause.

**Label:** **reported theatrical anecdote**.

### The grave at Montmartre

**Story:** Marie is buried under the shoe-covered grave in Montmartre Cemetery.

**Evidence against:** its inscription identifies it as Marie’s memorial to her mother; Marie was later transferred to Père-Lachaise.

**Label:** **mistaken identification that became an authentic memorial ritual**.

### The Strauss polka

**Story:** Johann Strauss II’s *Marie Taglioni Polka*, Op. 173, honored the elder Marie.

**Evidence against:** it honored her niece, Marie Taglioni the Younger.

**Label:** **identity conflation**.

Completed and performed. Ballet-pantomime in two acts and four tableaux; music by Jacques Offenbach; libretto by Saint-Georges; created for Emma Livry.

Uncompleted and unperformed. Two-act, three-tableau ballet; scenario by Charles Nuitter; music by Ernest Boulanger. Rehearsed with several projected ballerinas and abandoned.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Romantic ballet

Taglioni became the exemplary Romantic ballerina. Her legacy includes:

- the female supernatural protagonist;
- the white act or *ballet blanc*;
- expressive pointe work;
- verticality and apparent defiance of gravity;
- softened arms and torso;
- the Romantic tutu;
- celebrity ballerina culture;
- touring on an international scale;
- the elevation of the ballerina over the male dancer as ballet’s principal star.

### Loss and reconstruction

Filippo’s original choreography for *La Sylphide* was not preserved in modern notation. August Bournonville’s surviving Danish *La Sylphide* of 1836 uses different choreography and music. Modern productions labeled “the original *La Sylphide*” usually reconstruct an idea of Romantic style rather than reproduce Taglioni’s exact movement.

### Critical reinterpretation

Successive histories have presented her as:

- divinely gifted natural genius;
- disciplined technical revolutionary;
- passive instrument of her father;
- collaborator who actively shaped her own technique and image;
- chaste corrective to sexualized dancing;
- participant in a celebrity economy that eroticized the inaccessible woman;
- pioneer of female choreographic authority;
- beneficiary of family privilege;
- working artist whose apparent effortlessness concealed severe labor.

Recent scholarship emphasizes that the Taglioni phenomenon cannot be reduced either to Filippo’s authorship or to Marie’s body alone. It emerged from family enterprise, institutional patronage, costume, print culture, technology, contracts, critics, and Marie’s sustained physical and interpretive work.

### Pointe lineage

Her strict priority is mythical, but her historical centrality is not. Before Taglioni, pointe was experimental and episodic; after her, it became progressively indispensable to the ballerina’s training and stage identity.

### Memorial cult

Two shoe narratives frame her posthumous image:

- Russian admirers allegedly ate her slippers.
- Modern dancers leave their own shoes at a grave that belongs to her mother.

Both turn footwear into relics. The first consumes the relic; the second offers relics to a misidentified shrine. Neither story is a reliable substitute for biography, but both show how completely Taglioni’s reputation became attached to pointe shoes.

### Anna Matveyevna Pavlova

**Evidentiary label: documented historical influence; no personal meeting possible.**

Pavlova was born in 1881, three years before Taglioni died, and there is no evidence they met. Dance historians nevertheless place Pavlova in Taglioni’s technical and symbolic lineage. Taglioni used soft slippers whose toes were reinforced; Pavlova later strengthened the sole and toe area of her shoes while pursuing a similarly ethereal stage effect.
