# Constantine XI Palaiologos: Research Dossier

**Subject:** Constantine XI Dragases Palaiologos, last reigning emperor of the Romans at Constantinople  
**Scope:** Life, reign, documentary record, fall of Constantinople, contested death traditions, posthumous myth, and reception  
**Evidence cutoff:** 26 August 2026  
**Method:** Contemporary evidence is separated from later reconstruction and folklore. Dates follow the Common Era; Byzantine and Venetian dating problems are noted where material.

### The regnal number

Constantine did not call himself “Constantine XI.” Byzantines distinguished emperors through dynasty, patronymic, or epithet. Edward Gibbon numbered him XIII because he counted additional co-emperors; other enumerations produce XII, XIV, or even XVIII depending on whether Constantine Laskaris, Constans II (whose name was Constantine), western claimants, and junior co-emperors are counted. **XI** became the standard modern convention during the nineteenth century.

## Source Base and Evidentiary Problems

No continuous contemporary biography of Constantine survives. His adult life must be assembled from diplomatic records, Venetian deliberations and dispatches, charters, coinage, and narratives whose interests differ sharply.

### Principal near-contemporary witnesses

- **George Sphrantzes, *Chronikon* or *Chronicon Minus*.** Courtier, diplomat, Constantine’s close companion, and godfather/client within his household network. He is the indispensable source for family affairs, journeys, embassies, marriages, and chronology. He fought in the siege but, by his explicit statement, was inspecting another sector when Constantine died. His authentic chronicle says only that “my late master and emperor, Lord Constantine, was killed.” It is loyal, terse, and selective.
- **Pseudo-Sphrantzes, *Chronicon Maius*.** A much fuller sixteenth-century compilation by Makarios Melissenos/Melissourgos, formerly mistaken for Sphrantzes. It incorporates Leonard of Chios and other sources, adds documents and dramatic scenes, and contains the famous extended last speech. It is valuable for reception and literary history, not an eyewitness transcript.
- **Doukas, *Historia Turco-Byzantina*.** A Greek administrator connected with Genoese Lesbos who had access to Ottoman and Christian information. Especially important for Constantine’s Orhan policy, Mehmed’s preparations, church union, and the conquest. His theology interprets catastrophe as punishment for sin.
- **Laonikos Chalkokondyles, *Apodeixis Historion*.** Athenian/Moreot Greek historian writing in a classicizing register; valuable for the Morea, Ottoman strategy, and competing death reports.
- **Michael Kritovoulos of Imbros, *History of Mehmed the Conqueror*.** A Greek Christian who entered Mehmed’s service. His Thucydidean history is close in time but deliberately presents Mehmed as a legitimate world ruler. It supplies an influential heroic death scene and elegy for Constantine.
- **Nicolò Barbaro, *Giornale dell’assedio di Costantinopoli*.** Venetian surgeon and eyewitness; an unusually detailed siege diary, strongly pro-Venetian and hostile to Genoese conduct.
- **Leonard of Chios, letter to Pope Nicholas V, 16 August 1453.** Latin archbishop of Mytilene, eyewitness and captive who escaped. His short version of Constantine’s final address is the closest source for a last speech, though it remains a rhetorical report written after the event.
- **Cardinal Isidore of Kiev, letters of 6 July 1453 and afterward.** Unionist papal legate and siege survivor. Important for the Constantine-son-of-Helena symmetry and the Latin response.
- **Jacopo Tedaldi, Giacomo Languschi, Ubertino Pusculo, Benvenuto, Niccolò Tignosi, Filippo da Rimini, Angelo Giovanni Lomellino, and Venetian state material.** These preserve differing reports of the fighting, death, casualties, and aftermath.
- **Tursun Beg, Aşıkpaşazade, Neşri, Ibn Kemal, and later Ottoman chroniclers.** Muslim/Ottoman accounts range from near-contemporary administrative history to later dynastic narrative. Tursun Beg’s unheroic version has Constantine flee toward the sea and die in combat with a marine.
- **The Nestor-Iskander tradition and other Slavic accounts.** Textually complicated. Some material may derive from an eyewitness, but surviving recensions were reworked and contain demonstrably legendary scenes, an imaginary empress, prophecy, and heroic single combat.

Modern reconstruction changed decisively when the *Chronicon Maius* was exposed as a sixteenth-century work. Any narrative citing “Sphrantzes” for Constantine’s elaborate final speech, last communion, farewell tour, or exact last words must specify whether it means the authentic *Minus* or pseudo-Sphrantzes.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

- **Father: Manuel II Palaiologos** (1350–1425), emperor 1391–1425, son of John V Palaiologos and Helena Kantakouzene. Manuel was a soldier, diplomat, theologian, and author who traveled through Italy, France, and England seeking aid. His long experience taught his sons both the necessity and unreliability of western intervention.
- **Mother: Helena Dragaš** (c. 1372–23 March 1450), daughter of the Serbian regional ruler Konstantin Dejanović/Dragaš and a granddaughter through her paternal line of Serbian aristocratic houses formed after Stefan Dušan’s empire. After Manuel’s death she became the nun **Hypomone**. She served as dynastic arbiter after John VIII’s death and secured Constantine’s succession against Demetrios. Later Orthodox devotion venerates her as Saint Hypomone.

Constantine’s maternal grandfather Konstantin Dragaš died fighting as an Ottoman vassal at Rovine in 1395. The last emperor’s family was therefore neither ethnically nor politically reducible to a simple Greek-versus-Turk opposition: it joined Roman imperial, Serbian, Kantakouzenos, and Palaiologan lines and had lived for generations within systems of marriage diplomacy and Ottoman vassalage.

### Siblings

Manuel II and Helena had at least ten children, several dying young. The adult sons who structure Constantine’s career were:

- **John VIII Palaiologos** (1392–1448), emperor and Constantine’s principal patron; childless.
- **Theodore II Palaiologos** (c. 1396–1448), despot in the Morea, alternately collaborator and succession rival.
- **Andronikos Palaiologos** (1400–c. 1428/29), despot of Thessaloniki, who transferred that city to Venice while under Ottoman pressure and later became a monk.
- **Constantine.** He is commonly called the fourth surviving son, though sometimes eighth child in the whole birth order.
- **Demetrios Palaiologos** (1407–1470), anti-unionist claimant who attacked Constantinople with Ottoman support in 1442; later co-despot of the Morea and Ottoman pensioner.
- **Thomas Palaiologos** (1409–1465), Constantine’s generally cooperative co-ruler in the Morea, later refugee in Rome; ancestor of later western Palaiologan claims.

At least two daughters—often given as Maria and an unnamed daughter—died young; lists vary because infancy records are incomplete.

### Marriages and attempts at a third marriage

1. **Maddalena/Creusa Tocco, renamed Theodora** (married 1 July 1428). She was a niece of Carlo I Tocco, ruler in Epirus and the Ionian sphere; Glarentza and other lands formed part of the settlement/dowry after Tocco’s naval defeat. She died in November 1429 and was first buried at Glarentza, later translated to Mystras. Later accounts often say childbirth or miscarriage; the contemporary evidence securely establishes the death, not every obstetric detail.
2. **Caterina Gattilusio** (married on Lesbos, July/August 1441). Daughter of Dorino I Gattilusio, Genoese lord of Lesbos. During Constantine’s attempt to reach Constantinople against Demetrios in 1442, the couple was trapped by an Ottoman blockade on Lemnos. Caterina became ill and died there in August 1442; she was buried at Myrina. Later sources connect the death with pregnancy or miscarriage, but the precise cause is uncertain.
3. **Third-marriage diplomacy, 1449–1451.** Constantine instructed Sphrantzes to investigate a Georgian princess and a Trapezuntine princess. A separate proposal concerned **Mara Branković**, Serbian daughter of Đurađ Branković and widow of Sultan Murad II. Mara declined, reportedly because she had vowed not to remarry. Negotiations were unfinished when the siege began.

### Children and supposed descendants

No contemporary household or diplomatic source records a surviving child. Sphrantzes, who arranged Constantine’s marriages and searched for a third wife, would almost certainly have known. The emperor died without legitimate or documented illegitimate issue. Stories of an empress, daughters raped or killed by Mehmed, and a son escaping to Galata begin in confused western reports after the conquest and expand in later chronicles; they are treated below as reception history.

## Childhood, Education, and Formation

Constantine grew up in the reduced but still ceremonially elaborate court of Constantinople. Nothing like a curriculum, named school, or corpus of school exercises survives. Assertions that he received a complete classical education are plausible for an imperial prince but undocumented. Sphrantzes says that his own uncle had been Constantine’s tutor and that Sphrantzes and his cousins were the prince’s companions and attendants. The tutor’s precise identity and program are not securely recoverable.

His demonstrated formation was practical:

- court protocol, Orthodox liturgy, and imperial ideology;
- riding, hunting, weapons, and command;
- estate and appanage administration;
- diplomacy with Ottomans, Venetians, Genoese, Latin lords, Serbian rulers, and the papacy;
- the politics of dynastic marriage and hostage claimants.

Later encomia describe him as courageous, athletic, and skilled in horsemanship and hunting. These traits accord with his career but the childhood anecdotes are retrospective praise. No evidence makes him an original philosopher, theologian, author, or patron on the scale of Manuel II or John VIII. He lived in the intellectual orbit of Mystras, where **George Gemistos Plethon** advocated a centralized Hellenic state and offered reform memoranda to the despots, but there is no proof that Constantine adopted Plethon’s non-Christian religious speculations or his entire constitutional program.

## Early Career, 1422–1427

### The siege of 1422 and first regency

The Ottoman siege of Constantinople under Murad II in 1422 was Constantine’s first major military-political experience. Direct detail about his actions is thin, but John VIII subsequently trusted him. When John traveled west in November 1423 to seek support, Constantine—still about nineteen—was left as regent beside the incapacitated Manuel II. He helped conclude the peace of February 1424 with Murad II. The settlement preserved the capital at the cost of tribute and territorial concessions.

After Manuel’s death on 21 July 1425, John VIII gave Constantine an appanage extending along the Thracian/Black Sea approaches, usually described through **Mesembria, Derkos, and Selymbria**. Its fragmentation and proximity to both Constantinople and Ottoman territory made it more a strategic command than an independent principality.

### Transfer toward the Morea

The Morea contained the empire’s largest remaining territorial base but was divided among Palaiologan princes, Latin lords, Venetian ports, and Albanian and Greek local communities. Ottoman raids had broken the Hexamilion across the Isthmus in 1423. Carlo I Tocco’s pressure in western Greece drew John VIII and Constantine south in late 1427. Constantine reached Mystras with the imperial expedition on 26 December.

## Chronological Life History

### 1427–1432: Tocco settlement, Patras, and the near-unification of the Peloponnese

John VIII’s forces defeated Carlo I Tocco’s fleet near the **Echinades**. The peace transferred Glarentza and other holdings to Constantine and was sealed by his marriage to Tocco’s niece Theodora. Constantine received the title and territorial position of Despot of the Morea on or about 1 May 1428. Theodore II did not retire as first intended; Thomas was also installed. The supposedly unitary despotate consequently became a negotiated three-prince condominium.

Constantine’s first base was connected with Glarentza and Chlemoutsi; his holdings also included Aigio, Kalamata, Messenia, and Laconian properties. His principal early objective was **Patras**, a wealthy port ruled by its Latin archbishop. A joint attempt failed. On 1 March 1429 Constantine and Sphrantzes began a second campaign; formal siege operations followed around 20 March. Constantine’s horse was shot beneath him in fighting and Sphrantzes was captured while helping save him. The lower city yielded, while the citadel held out.

During the campaign, Catalan forces seized Glarentza. Constantine bought it back for **6,000 Venetian ducats**, then had its defenses demolished so it could not again serve pirates or an invader. The destruction sacrificed an important commercial city and, according to the later anonymous Greek *Capture of the City*, included sacred buildings and began the chain of divine punishments ending in 1453. In July 1430 the Patras citadel surrendered, ending roughly two centuries of Latin political control there; Sphrantzes later became governor.

Theodora’s death in November 1429 left Constantine widowed. In 1432 Thomas’s marriage to Catherine Zaccaria extinguished the remaining Principality of Achaea. With the exception of Venetian ports such as Modon, Coron, Nauplion, and Argos, the Peloponnese was again under Roman/Palaiologan rule.

### 1432–1440: territorial rearrangement and second regency

In March 1432 Constantine exchanged territories with Thomas: Constantine took **Kalavryta**, closer to Mystras, while Thomas received Elis. Succession tension grew because John VIII had no children. John favored Constantine; Theodore feared displacement and hurried to Constantinople in 1435 when he thought Constantine might be made co-emperor.

When John VIII departed in November 1437 for the Council of Ferrara-Florence, Constantine again governed Constantinople. The regency lasted until John’s return in February 1440. Constantine did not attend the council, but the regency placed him inside the state apparatus while John and a large ecclesiastical delegation negotiated union with Rome. He thus inherited the political consequences of the **Union of Florence (6 July 1439)** without having personally signed its decree.

### 1440–1443: Gattilusio marriage, Demetrios’s attack, and Lemnos

Sphrantzes negotiated Constantine’s marriage to Caterina Gattilusio in late 1440–1441. Constantine married on Lesbos and returned toward the Morea, leaving Caterina temporarily with her family. He explored exchanging his Moreot lands for Demetrios’s Black Sea appanage, which would place him nearer the capital.

Demetrios instead allied with Murad II and presented himself as the anti-unionist alternative to John VIII. In April 1442 Ottoman-supported forces attacked Constantinople. John summoned Constantine. Traveling with Caterina, Constantine was blockaded on Lemnos; she died there in August. Constantine reached the capital only in November, after the attack had failed. Demetrios was briefly imprisoned. In 1443 Constantine relinquished Selymbria to Theodore, while Theodore left the Morea; Constantine took **Mystras** and ruled with Thomas.

### 1443–1446: Hexamilion and the last Roman offensive

Constantine and Thomas rebuilt the **Hexamilion wall**, completing it by March 1444. The project required money and labor that local landowners and Venetian colonies resisted supplying. Some Moreot magnates fled to Venetian territory to evade levies; resistance was compelled militarily, then Constantine used land grants and privileges to rebuild elite loyalty. This is one of the few documented windows onto the coercive fiscal basis of his rule. He also sponsored local athletic competitions, a small sign of civic/classical display at his court.

The crusade of Varna created an opening. In summer 1444 Constantine advanced beyond the Isthmus into central Greece. He took Thebes and forced **Nerio II Acciaioli**, duke of Athens and an Ottoman tributary, to recognize his suzerainty and redirect tribute. He pushed into Boeotia and possibly toward the Pindus/Thessaly sphere; local Albanian leaders made submissions. This was the last significant territorial offensive conducted in the name of the Roman Empire.

The enterprise depended on a Christian victory in the north. Murad II destroyed the crusading army at **Varna on 10 November 1444**. Constantine nevertheless retained his advances temporarily and declined Ottoman demands to withdraw. Murad entered Greece in 1446 with a large army and artillery. Constantine and Thomas concentrated perhaps 20,000 defenders—figures are uncertain—behind the Hexamilion. Ottoman bombardment began at the end of November; by **10 December 1446** the line was breached. Large numbers were killed or enslaved, and the countryside was devastated. Constantine and Thomas escaped to Mystras. They accepted Ottoman overlordship, tribute, and a prohibition on rebuilding the wall.

The Hexamilion episode supports two different assessments found in modern scholarship. It demonstrates administrative energy, mobilization, and a strategic effort to make the Morea defensible; it also demonstrates coercive financing, overextension after Varna, and a willingness to stake a fragile population on a fixed defense that Ottoman artillery could defeat.

### 1448–1449: succession

Theodore II died in June 1448. John VIII died childless on **31 October 1448**. Their mother Helena Dragaš acted as regent in Constantinople. Demetrios, backed by anti-unionist sentiment, maneuvered for the throne; Thomas accepted Constantine. Helena asserted Constantine’s succession, and envoys **Alexios Philanthropenos Laskaris** and **Manuel Palaiologos Iagros** traveled to Mystras. Murad II’s assent was sought, an index of Byzantine dependency.

Constantine was proclaimed and ceremonially invested at Mystras on **6 January 1449**. The ceremony may have occurred in the palace rather than a church; no patriarch crowned him. Scholars explain the irregularity through haste, precedent for acclamation outside the capital, and the danger that a coronation by a unionist or anti-unionist cleric would trigger conflict. It does not invalidate his contemporaneous recognition as emperor. He sailed on a Catalan vessel and entered Constantinople on **12 March 1449**. Demetrios publicly acknowledged him.

### 1449–1451: imperial government, court, and diplomacy

Constantine inherited a state consisting of Constantinople and nearby places, the Princes’ Islands, and a dynastically separate Morea. The capital’s inhabited neighborhoods were broken by gardens, ruins, and empty areas; its population is usually estimated near 40,000–50,000, but no precise census survives. The imperial household occupied the Blachernae palace rather than the old Great Palace.

His court combined:

- wealthy grandees such as **Loukas Notaras**, grand duke and chief minister;
- career officials and diplomats such as Sphrantzes and Iagros;
- Latin, Genoese, Venetian, and other foreign intermediaries whose commercial and diplomatic connections could substitute for the empire’s lost institutional power.

Constantine mediated an early conflict between Demetrios and Thomas in the Morea (1449–1450). The recently discovered Aigialeia portrait has been connected by Greek archaeological authorities to his brothers’ patronage of the Taxiarches monastery after that settlement.

The treasury was nearly empty. Constantine raised duties on Venetian imports. Venice protested that its treaties exempted or limited such taxation, and the emperor retreated or compromised. The episode exhibits the central financial trap: foreign merchants commanded indispensable shipping and liquidity while imperial sovereignty required revenue from the same trade.

Constantine renewed treaties and sent embassies to Venice, Genoa, Ragusa, the papacy, Aragon-Naples, Hungary, Serbia, Georgia, and Trebizond. A **1451 chrysobull for Ragusa/Dubrovnik**, surviving with gold seal and imperial subscription, confirms commercial privileges and is among the clearest material records of his chancery. The emperor’s diplomacy was personal and concessionary: he could offer titles, islands, ports, trade rights, or dynastic marriage more readily than cash or armies.

### 1451–1452: Mehmed II, Orhan, and strategic crisis

Murad II died on 3 February 1451. Constantine exchanged envoys and renewed peace with the nineteen-year-old **Mehmed II**. The apparent settlement was soon damaged by the question of **Orhan Çelebi**, an Ottoman dynastic claimant maintained in Constantinople with funds owed by the sultan.

Late in 1451 Constantine’s envoys demanded a larger payment—reported as doubling an annual 20,000 *hyperpyra*—and implied that Orhan might otherwise be released. Byzantine governments had repeatedly used rival Ottoman princes this way. Doukas portrays grand vizier Çandarlı Halil as furiously warning the envoys that they had misjudged the new sultan. The threat was real statecraft, not invented modern propaganda. Its causal weight remains disputed: it gave Mehmed a grievance and exposed Byzantine weakness, but his desire and preparations to control or conquer Constantinople did not originate solely in this incident.

Mehmed began **Rumeli Hisarı** on the European shore of the Bosporus in spring 1452, opposite Anadolu Hisarı. The fortress controlled Black Sea access. Constantine protested; Mehmed ignored or abused the embassy, and Byzantine capacity to stop construction was nonexistent. Constantine briefly closed the city gates and detained Ottoman subjects, then released them. In November 1452 the guns of the fortress sank a Venetian grain ship captained by Antonio Rizzo after it refused inspection/toll; survivors were executed, with sources differing on the manner.

Constantine ordered repairs to the Theodosian walls under Manuel Iagros and intensified appeals:

- Venice was asked for men, weapons, powder, and ships;
- Alfonso V of Aragon-Naples was offered Lemnos for decisive assistance;
- John Hunyadi was offered Selymbria or Mesembria;
- Genoese Chios was offered compensation;
- Thomas and Demetrios were asked for aid, while Ottoman operations in the Morea prevented it.

The results were fragmented private or colonial initiatives rather than a European coalition.

### Church union and internal division

Pope Nicholas V tied major papal action to implementation of the Union of Florence. Cardinal **Isidore of Kiev** reached Constantinople on 26 October 1452 with Leonard of Chios and about 200 Neapolitan archers. On **12 December 1452** a liturgy in Hagia Sophia solemnly proclaimed union and commemorated the pope. Constantine participated.

The anti-unionist party, intellectually led by the monk **Gennadios Scholarios**, rejected the settlement. Gennadios posted a denunciation from his monastic retirement. Many clergy and citizens avoided the unionist service. The famous statement attributed to Loukas Notaras—preferring a Turkish turban to a Latin cardinal’s hat—comes through Doukas and may condense a genuine position into a hostile epigram. Constantine did not resolve the theological conflict; he imposed the diplomatic form needed for western aid while the city remained ecclesiastically divided.

There is no evidence that Constantine privately repudiated Florence before death. There is also no secure evidence of a formal personal conversion to Latin Catholicism. A final Eucharist shared by Greeks and Latins is described most fully in later narrative. Confessional claims that he died straightforwardly “Catholic,” “Orthodox,” “Uniate,” or repentant of union are posthumous constructions from incomplete evidence.

## The Siege and Fall, April–May 1453

### Preparations and forces

Mehmed isolated the Morea through Turahan’s forces and assembled artillery, transport, ships, and troops at Adrianople. The famous founder **Urban/Orban**, often said to have first offered a great cannon to Constantine and defected because he could not be paid, is attested through limited and rhetorically shaped sources. Modern military studies accept extensive Ottoman artillery but dispute the popular story that one super-cannon single-handedly ended medieval fortification.

The Genoese commander **Giovanni Giustiniani Longo** arrived in late January 1453 with perhaps 700 trained men and was promised Lemnos if the city survived. Constantine gave him command of the critical land-wall defense. Venetian residents, after internal deliberation, remained to fight under bailo Girolamo Minotto. The defenders included Greeks/Romans, Genoese, Venetians, Cretans, Catalans under Pere Julià, papal troops, and several hundred men loyal to Orhan.

Sphrantzes’s secret count recorded **4,773 Roman/Greek fighting men and about 200 imperial foreigners**; independent Italian, Genoese, Venetian, papal, and other bodies bring common modern estimates to roughly **7,000–8,000** defenders. Ottoman figures in sources range absurdly high; modern reconstructions often propose approximately **60,000–80,000** regular and irregular combatants, with substantial uncertainty. Constantine kept the small count secret to prevent panic.

The chain across the Golden Horn was raised on 2 April. Mehmed arrived before the land walls on 5 April; bombardment and formal siege operations began on **6 April**. Constantine established command near the weak Mesoteichion, around the St Romanos valley, alongside Giustiniani.

### Course of the siege

- **6–17 April:** artillery batteries opened breaches that defenders repaired at night with timber, earth, rubble, and cloth-filled barriers. Constantine coordinated assignments along the full land and sea circuit.
- **18 April:** a major night assault was repelled.
- **20 April:** four Christian relief/supply ships fought through the Ottoman fleet into the Golden Horn, a visible Ottoman failure that cost admiral Baltaoğlu his command.
- **22 April:** Mehmed moved vessels overland behind Galata into the Golden Horn. The maneuver stretched the defense and neutralized some advantage of the chain, though it did not by itself decide the siege.
- **Late April–May:** repeated assaults, mining and countermining, bombardment, a siege tower, naval pressure, and repair work exhausted both sides. Johannes Grant, probably German or Scottish, directed successful countermines. Defenders destroyed the Ottoman tower.
- **23 May:** a small Venetian reconnaissance vessel returned despite the chance to escape and reported that no relief fleet was near.
- **Late May:** a lunar eclipse, the failed procession of the Hodegetria icon amid violent weather, and strange light around Hagia Sophia were read as omens in Christian accounts. Meteorological events are fact; the prophetic meanings belong to contemporary religious interpretation.
- **26–27 May:** Mehmed’s council debated continuing. Çandarlı Halil reportedly favored negotiation; Zaganos and the war party favored a final assault. Preparations accelerated.

### Surrender negotiations

Kritovoulos and related traditions report that Mehmed offered to spare the population and allow Constantine to rule in the Morea if the city surrendered. Constantine offered higher tribute but refused to alienate the city. The compact reply often translated as “To surrender the city is not for me alone … we have all resolved to die of our own free will” is plausible in substance but survives through authored narrative, not an official transcript.

### 28–29 May

On 28 May both sides prepared ritually and militarily. A service in Hagia Sophia brought some unionists and anti-unionists together. The famous sequence in which Constantine asks forgiveness of everyone, receives communion, bids farewell at Blachernae, and rides the walls comes principally through Leonard and expanded later sources; the broad pattern is credible, the intimate details cannot all be verified.

The Ottoman assault began before dawn on 29 May in successive waves. Irregular troops attacked first, then Anatolian forces, then janissaries. Fighting centered at breaches in the St Romanos/Mesoteichion sector. Giustiniani was badly wounded—sources disagree whether in chest, arm, or leg and whether by bolt, shot, or splinter—and withdrew through an inner gate. His Genoese followed, damaging morale and exposing the position. Christian and Ottoman accounts dispute whether his departure was necessary evacuation, collapse, or desertion.

The **Kerkoporta** story, in which an accidentally open postern admitted fifty Ottomans who raised a flag, comes from Doukas and became a dominant legend. Topographical and source criticism makes it unlikely to have been the principal cause. The decisive penetration occurred amid breaches and the withdrawal at the central land-wall position.

Constantine disappeared in the fighting. The authentic Sphrantzes does not place himself beside him. No uncontested eyewitness saw the emperor’s last blow.

## Death / Later Life / Present Status: Competing Accounts

### Points of broad agreement

Most early Greek, Latin, Venetian, and Ottoman narratives agree that Constantine died during the capture on 29 May. He did not emerge as a claimant, captive, exile, or pensioner afterward. Flight legends exist, but no subsequent documentary trace supports survival. Death in the battle is therefore the strongest conclusion.

### Principal versions

| Source/tradition | What it reports | Evidentiary status |
|---|---|---|
| Authentic Sphrantzes | Constantine was killed; Sphrantzes was elsewhere under orders | Near-contemporary insider; reliable for death, silent on manner |
| Leonard of Chios | Emperor pleaded not to be left alive, then was killed in the press after Giustiniani’s withdrawal | Eyewitness to siege, not demonstrably to the precise death |
| Nicolò Barbaro | Records the emperor’s death in the catastrophe but no secure heroic tableau | Eyewitness to siege; Venetian bias |
| Kritovoulos | Constantine, seeing the city taken, cried in despair that he still lived, then charged with companions and died fighting | Early non-eyewitness, literary/Thucydidean shaping, pro-Mehmed context |
| Doukas/Christian reports | Death amid the breach; body later identified through imperial insignia, purple footwear, or eagles | Identification details conflict and may rationalize uncertainty |
| Tursun Beg | Constantine and companions fled toward the sea; he struck a lost Ottoman marine and was killed/decapitated in return | Near-contemporary Ottoman version; hostile language and anti-heroic framing |
| Ibn Kemal | Flight toward Yedikule/Golden Gate; an Ottoman warrior killed and beheaded him without knowing his identity | Later Ottoman elaboration |
| Niccolò Tignosi and Filippo da Rimini/Languschi | Captured or beheaded; head presented to Mehmed and recognized | Early western reports, partly derivative and rhetorically embellished |
| Nestor-Iskander recensions | Prayer in Hagia Sophia, farewell to imaginary empress, heroic ride/single combat, death at Golden Gate, head preserved or stolen | Composite Slavic tradition with possible early material and demonstrable legend |
| Escape accounts | Constantine escaped in disguise to Galata, the islands, or farther east/west | Minority rumor; contradicted by disappearance and no later documentation |

Reports that Mehmed displayed the head, stuffed the body, sent the head through Islamic lands, or allowed burial cannot be reconciled. Donald Nicol’s source study concludes that a body may have been found and a head identified, but neither fact nor the place of burial can be proved. Claimed tombs in Istanbul—especially sites associated with Vefa, Gül Mosque, or unnamed dervish graves—are late identifications without archaeological confirmation.

No medical cause can be specified beyond fatal battle trauma. No reliable source documents chronic illness, addiction, psychiatric disease, or a preexisting disabling injury. The earlier loss of a horse under him at Patras nearly killed him but produced no recorded permanent impairment.

## Offices, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Offices and appointments

- Regent of Constantinople, 1423–1424.
- Appanage-holder/governor in the Mesembria–Derkos–Selymbria region from 1425, with later exchanges.
- Despot of the Morea, 1428–1449, in condominium with Theodore II and Thomas.
- Regent again, 1437–1440.
- Emperor and autocrat of the Romans, 1449–1453.

### Territorial and infrastructure projects

- Acquisition and subsequent demolition of Glarentza’s defensive works.
- Siege and incorporation of Patras.
- Redistribution of Moreot estates and privileges.
- Rebuilding of the Hexamilion in 1443–1444; destroyed by Murad II in 1446.
- Repair and reinforcement of Constantinople’s land walls, especially in 1452–1453.
- Organization of emergency barriers, countermines, naval boom, artillery positions, and sector commands during the siege.

### Treasury, property, and commerce

Constantine personally controlled dynastic appanage revenues, urban dues, land taxes, and imperial domains, but no modern balance sheet or credible personal net-worth figure is possible. The distinction between emperor’s household assets and state revenue was not modern or absolute. Relevant facts include:

- payment of 6,000 Venetian ducats to recover Glarentza from Catalans;
- coercive fiscal/labor demands for the Hexamilion;
- duties imposed on Venetian imports and later negotiated under treaty pressure;
- inability or unwillingness to fund expensive specialists and large mercenary bodies;
- pledged territories—Lemnos, Selymbria, Mesembria—as diplomatic currency;
- continued striking of debased silver **stavrata, half-stavrata, and one-eighth stavrata** at Constantinople.

Coins of Constantine were not securely recognized until the twentieth century; a 1991 hoard greatly expanded the corpus. The obverse bears Christ; the reverse identifies Constantine as despot/emperor of the Romans by God’s grace. Their rough execution and small surviving population testify to emergency-scale production, though the romantic claim that a particular piece was literally struck during the final assault cannot be proved.

No evidence links him to private companies, inventions, patents, banking structures, money laundering, or modern-style investments. Commercial arrangements were sovereign privileges and tax concessions, not corporate holdings.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Political orientation

Constantine’s consistent objective was preservation and territorial recovery of the Roman polity through whichever instruments remained: Ottoman treaties and tributary status, Latin marriages, western church union, hostage politics, land grants, and direct war. He did not articulate a surviving political theory. His Moreot campaigns show a more aggressive program than his final defensive reign; the reversal followed Varna and the 1446 devastation.

### Roman and Hellenic identities

His official title remained emperor of the **Romans** (*Rhomaioi*), not king of a modern Greek nation. Mystras intellectuals, especially Plethon, could speak of Hellenes and a renewed Spartan/Hellenic center. Constantine’s rule existed inside that revival, but documents do not show him renouncing Roman identity or adopting Plethon’s paganizing program. Later Greek nationalism recast him as both last Roman emperor and first/eternal king of the Greek nation.

### Religious practice

Sphrantzes reports Constantine’s fasting, prayer, charity to the poor, and payments for priestly prayers. These are observations of a loyal courtier but correspond to late Byzantine rulership. His decisive religious policy was pragmatic support for Florence. He sought military aid through ecclesiastical union while avoiding, until late 1452, the domestic explosion a full proclamation might cause. The policy failed to produce timely mass intervention and deepened public division, but union alone did not cause the Ottoman conquest.

### Patronage and material image

Constantine’s short reign and poverty left no building program comparable to earlier emperors. His material representation survives in:

- silver coins;
- imperial seals and the Ragusan chrysobull;
- a manuscript portrait in a Biblioteca Estense copy of Zonaras, schematic in character;
- the mid-fifteenth-century fresco uncovered at the Old Monastery of Taxiarches in Aigialeia and announced in December 2024. Greek Ministry of Culture officials identify the individualized ruler as Constantine and suggest an artist who knew him at Mystras painted from observation. The dynastic eagles, imperial costume, date, and Palaiologan patronage strongly support the identification, but “painted from life” remains an archaeological/art-historical inference rather than a signed statement by the painter.

## Controversies, Coercion, and Strategic Failures

No documentary basis has been found for personal fraud, embezzlement, sexual scandal, money laundering, addiction, or criminal prosecution. Relevant controversies belong to war, fiscal coercion, diplomacy, and religious legitimacy.

### Destruction of Glarentza

**Documented core:** Constantine demolished defenses/parts of Glarentza after paying to recover it, preventing reuse by pirates or enemies.  
**Controversy:** The act destroyed economic and ecclesiastical fabric. The anonymous post-conquest poem *The Capture of the City* treated destruction of churches there as sacrilege that caused Constantine’s later disasters.  
**Assessment:** Strategic demolition is historical; divine punishment is contemporary/post-conquest interpretation.

### Hexamilion finance and landowner resistance

**Documented core:** Rebuilding required extraordinary resources; some magnates fled into Venetian territory and others resisted before being compelled.  
**Implication:** Constantine’s celebrated defensive project rested partly on forced extraction from a population already vulnerable to raids. Later grants and privileges were political repair.

### Expansion after Varna

**Documented core:** Constantine continued his central Greek program after the crusade’s defeat, rejected Ottoman demands, and suffered catastrophic invasion in 1446.  
**Dispute:** Nicol emphasizes energy and adverse circumstances; Philippides stresses limited military achievement and diplomatic failure. Counterfactual claims that restraint would have preserved the Morea longer cannot be proved.

### The Orhan threat

**Documented core:** Constantine sought increased Ottoman payment and threatened, explicitly or implicitly, to release a rival claimant.  
**Dispute:** Traditional narratives call this a disastrous provocation. Other analysis notes that Mehmed already had structural and ideological reasons to conquer the city; the threat was a pretext, not a sufficient cause.

### Union of Florence

**Documented core:** Constantine implemented union in December 1452 despite organized opposition, to unlock western aid.  
**Competing claims:** Anti-unionists represented the policy as betrayal of Orthodoxy and cause of divine abandonment; unionist and western sources blamed Greek resistance for the failure of rescue. Neither theological causal claim is historical proof. Militarily, the aid arriving with Isidore was real but small.

### Treatment of surrender

Constantine rejected reported terms that could have saved his life and perhaps spared some inhabitants under the laws/customs of negotiated capitulation. The decision exposed civilians to sack and enslavement if the defense failed. The sources also represent the decision as collective and note that Ottoman promises were not risk-free. The documentary record cannot recover each inhabitant’s consent or determine the counterfactual outcome.

### Military command and Giustiniani

Assigning the central defense to a foreign professional was rational and politically sensitive. Greek, Venetian, and Genoese sources later allocated blame for the collapse. Giustiniani’s withdrawal after wounding was decisive in sequence; calling it treason goes beyond what can be proved about the severity of the wound or command conditions.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. The last Constantine, son of Helena

**Story:** The city founded by Constantine the Great, son of Helena, was fated to fall under another Constantine, son of Helena.  
**Appearance/circulation:** Barbaro calls it an ancient prophecy; Isidore’s letter of 6 July 1453, Kritovoulos, Gennadios Scholarios, short chronicles, and laments repeat the symmetry.  
**Evidence:** The names are factual. No pre-1453 securely dated oracle predicting this precise pair has been established; conquest narratives retrospectively organize coincidence as providence.  
**Effect:** It converted a military defeat into a closed sacred chronology, “Constantine to Constantine,” and made the last emperor structurally inseparable from the founder.

### 2. The final speech

**Story:** Constantine summons Greeks and Latins, appeals to faith, homeland, emperor, family, and the “seven-hilled city,” asks forgiveness, and commands voluntary death.  
**Appearance:** A short rhetorical address is reported by Leonard of Chios in August 1453. The canonical long speech is in the sixteenth-century *Chronicon Maius* of pseudo-Sphrantzes, derived partly from Leonard and classical models.  
**Evidence:** A commander almost certainly addressed officers before the assault. No stenographic record exists; the long version is not authentic Sphrantzes.  
**Effect:** It became the emperor’s political testament in histories, sermons, schoolbooks, videos, and nationalist commemoration.

### 3. Casting off the imperial insignia

**Story:** Constantine removed purple insignia so he could die as an ordinary soldier.  
**Evidence:** Early sources variously identify a body by purple shoes/eagles, say he was unrecognized, or offer no body. Removing insignia elegantly explains both anonymity and later heroic memory but is not securely witnessed.  
**Effect:** It produces the image of office dissolving into communal defense.

### 4. “The city has fallen and I am still alive”

**Story:** Constantine’s final cry before charging.  
**Source:** Kritovoulos’s literary history, not an eyewitness transcript.  
**Evidence:** Possible in sentiment; exact words unprovable.  
**Effect:** Among the most repeated last words in Byzantine reception.

### 5. The Kerkoporta

**Story:** A small gate was accidentally left open; a handful of Ottomans entered and raised their banner, causing panic and the fall.  
**Source:** Doukas; elaborated afterward.  
**Evidence:** The location and even identification of the gate are disputed. Other accounts place the decisive breach at the central walls after Giustiniani’s withdrawal.  
**Effect:** Explained civilizational catastrophe through one fatal lapse and encouraged betrayal/accident narratives rather than attritional military analysis.

### 6. Body, head, and grave

**Story:** Mehmed found Constantine through eagles or purple footwear, displayed his head, sent it through his dominions, stuffed or buried the body, or permitted Christian burial. Numerous Istanbul graves have been proposed.  
**Evidence:** Early reports agree neither on recognition nor disposition. No authenticated relic, tomb inscription, or excavated grave exists.  
**Effect:** The absent corpse made transformation, survival, and return possible.

### 7. The surviving empress, daughters, and son

**Story:** Mehmed raped/killed an empress and daughters; a son escaped across the Golden Horn; in other versions Constantine first killed his family to prevent capture.  
**Appearance:** Aeneas Silvius Piccolomini’s July 1453 letter and *Cosmographia*; Mathieu d’Escouchy; Nestor-Iskander recensions; seventeenth-century Hierax and derivative Greek chronicles.  
**Evidence:** Constantine was twice widowed, negotiating another marriage, and childless. The family is fictive.  
**Effect:** It personalized the sack for western and Slavic audiences and supplied dynastic continuations where history supplied none.

### 8. The Marble Emperor

**Story:** An angel seized Constantine just before death, turned him to marble, and concealed him in a cave beneath or near the Golden Gate. At God’s command he will awaken, drive the Turks to the Red Apple Tree, recover Constantinople, proceed to Jerusalem, and complete his mission.  
**Appearance:** Grew from late-fifteenth-century Greek hopes and older “last emperor” apocalyptic traditions; circulated orally, in laments, prophecies, chapbooks, and later nationalist literature.  
**Evidence:** Folklore, not biography. It depends on the unlocated body.  
**Effect:** Preserved Byzantine sovereignty as suspended rather than extinguished. In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries it fused with the Greek **Megali Idea**.

### 9. Constantine I of Greece as Constantine XII

**Story:** Crown Prince and later King Constantine I (born 1868) would fulfill the prophecy as “Constantine XII,” retake Constantinople, and reign where XI had fallen.  
**Circulation:** Royal-national rhetoric intensified after Constantine’s entry into Thessaloniki in 1912 and the Balkan Wars.  
**Evidence:** Political myth. The numbering itself was symbolic. Defeat in Asia Minor in 1922 broke its immediate geopolitical expectation but not the cultural association.

### 10. Sainthood and martyrdom

**Story:** Constantine is a canonized Orthodox martyr with a feast on 29 May.  
**Evidence:** He is popularly called martyr, ethnomartyr, blessed, or saint and is commemorated in some devotional settings. No universally recognized formal canonization by the Ecumenical Patriarchate has been documented. His Florence policy complicates confessional claims.  
**Effect:** Popular veneration blurs national hero, defender of the city, and liturgical saint; assertions of official canonization should be treated as inaccurate unless tied to a specific ecclesial jurisdiction and act.

### 11. Nicholas of Cusa and the mourning world

Nicholas of Cusa did not write a biography or named lament for Constantine personally. The fall generated the setting of **De pace fidei** (September 1453). Its prologue describes a man who had seen the Constantinopolitan region, was inflamed by reports of the Turkish king’s cruelties, groaned and prayed for an end to religious persecution, and received a vision of concord: one faith expressed through diverse rites. Thus Constantine’s lost polity belongs to the immediate historical shock that produced Cusa’s dialogue, but it is inaccurate to turn *De pace fidei* into a personal elegy for the emperor. Cusa’s later position developed toward Qur’anic critique and support for anti-Ottoman action, while retaining a project of Christian demonstration through textual engagement.

## Works by or Issued in the Name of Constantine

Constantine left **no known authored book, treatise, diary, poem, or memoir**. The relevant corpus is administrative and diplomatic.

### Genuine or institutionally authentic material

- **Imperial chrysobulls and privileges**, including the surviving 1451 chrysobull confirming privileges of the Republic of Ragusa/Dubrovnik, with gold seal and imperial subscription in red/purple ink. These are chancery products legally issued in his name; wording need not be personal composition.
- **Treaties, letters, and instructions** to Venice, Genoa, Ragusa, Pope Nicholas V, Alfonso V, John Hunyadi, Chios, his brothers, and other rulers, preserved wholly, partially, or through recipient registers and narrative paraphrase. Again, dictation/authorization is more likely than autograph composition.
- **Coin inscriptions and seals**, official self-representation rather than literary authorship.
- **Reported replies to Mehmed’s surrender demands**, preserved by historians, not as signed originals.

### Attributed but textually mediated

- **Final address to commanders, 28 May 1453.** Leonard of Chios provides the earliest short report. The long form printed as Constantine’s speech derives from pseudo-Sphrantzes and must be labeled a sixteenth-century literary reconstruction.
- **Last words** such as “The city is fallen and I am still alive” belong to Kritovoulos’s narrative.
- Prayers, farewells, and confessions in later Greek and Slavic accounts are attributed speech, not recoverable verbatim compositions.

### Documentary editions relevant to the corpus

- Franz Dölger and successors, *Regesten der Kaiserurkunden des oströmischen Reiches*, for imperial acts.
- Spyridon Lampros, *Palaiologeia kai Peloponnesiaka*, for late Palaiologan texts and documents.
- Agostino Pertusi, *La caduta di Costantinopoli*, and *Testi inediti e poco noti sulla caduta di Costantinopoli*, for contemporary and near-contemporary conquest material.
- Venetian *Senato* deliberations, Marino Sanudo’s documentary traditions, Ragusan archival material, and papal registers preserve recipient-side evidence.

- **Manolis Kalomiris, *Konstantinos Palaiologos***, a “musical legend–tragedy” in three acts, composed 1958–1961 and premiered by the Greek National Opera at the Odeon of Herodes Atticus on **12 August 1962**. No single internationally standard documentary devoted exclusively to Constantine has displaced the book literature. The Netflix dramatized documentary series ** *Rise of Empires: Ottoman* (2020)** similarly combines historians’ commentary with scripted scenes. Greek television, church, museum, and online lectures frequently frame him through the last-emperor and Marble King traditions.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Immediate Christian and Ottoman aftermath

Christian reports turned Constantine rapidly into martyr, tragic ruler, or providential last emperor. Humanists mourned not only territory but libraries, schools, relics, and imperial continuity. Aeneas Silvius Piccolomini—later Pius II—used the conquest in crusading rhetoric and spread inaccurate stories about Constantine’s family. Cardinal Bessarion called Constantinople a school of the best arts. Emperor Frederick III described its fall as a common disaster to the faith and to letters.

Kritovoulos’s double-Constantine symmetry and elegy could mourn the Roman emperor while transferring universal imperial energy to Mehmed. Ottoman histories generally made Constantine a defeated infidel ruler; some allowed courage, others described attempted flight. Mehmed’s claim to sovereignty over the former Roman capital eventually generated the title **Kayser-i Rûm** and incorporation of Roman personnel and institutions, though the precise early use and legal meaning are debated.

### Nicholas of Cusa

News of Constantinople’s violence was the explicit catalyst for Cusa’s *De pace fidei*. His visionary council of heavenly and earthly wisdom answers religious killing with concord—one religion in a diversity of rites. This is one of the most consequential intellectual afterlives of Constantine’s world, but its true object is the crisis of religious violence and universal peace, not a cult of Constantine.

### Greek memory

During Ottoman rule the absent body and failed dynasty became a promise of return. The Marble King lived beneath the Golden Gate; songs made Hagia Sophia and the emperor dormant rather than extinguished. Nineteenth-century independence and irredentism nationalized the legend. Constantine’s statue before Athens Metropolitan Cathedral, modern monuments at Mystras and elsewhere, annual 29 May commemorations, icons, and popular biographies keep together several identities: Roman emperor, Greek national hero, Orthodox defender, unionist pragmatist, and folk saint.

### Historiographical reinterpretation

- **Romantic/heroic tradition:** Constantine as blameless tragic hero whose death redeems political failure.
- **Diplomatic critique:** the Orhan threat, shifting Ottoman policy, coercive Moreot projects, and failed alliance management as evidence of limited statesmanship.
- **Structural history:** no emperor could easily overcome demographic contraction, tributary status, foreign commercial dominance, church division, and Ottoman logistical superiority.
- **Recent military revision:** the city was not simply a corpse waiting to fall; its fortifications and improvised garrison resisted effectively for seven weeks. Sustained combined operations and attrition, not a magical superweapon or open gate alone, produced the breakthrough.
- **Roman reframing:** contemporary inhabitants called themselves Romans. Constantine’s death ended an imperial office and state whose institutional genealogy ran through Constantinople to ancient Rome, even though rival Roman claims and successor identities continued elsewhere.

His reputation is unusually dependent on the manner of his death. The securely documented political life contains compromises, defeats, coercive taxation, and diplomatic error; the securely probable fact that he remained and died in the battle supplied the image through which nearly all later periods reinterpreted those facts.

## Atlas Connections

### Constantine the Great

- **[T9-] Documented fact:** Constantinople is the connecting site. Constantine the Great refounded Byzantium as his imperial capital beginning in 324 and dedicated it in 330; Constantine XI later governed the same Roman capital and died defending it on 29 May 1453. This is a site-transmission connection, not evidence of prophecy or personal contact. [Oxford Classical Dictionary](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548753391)

### Justinian I

- **[T9-] Documented fact:** Hagia Sophia is the shared object/site. Justinian I commissioned the surviving principal structure after the Nika revolt, with construction undertaken in 532–537. Constantine XI subsequently used this inherited imperial cathedral, most consequentially for the proclamation of church union on 12 December 1452 and, according to siege narratives, the final Christian service of 28 May 1453. [Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/hagia-sophia-532-37)

No other roster member has a sufficiently documented crossing under the specified types. In particular, Nicholas/Johannes of Cusa’s *De pace fidei* responded to reports of the conquest and religious violence but did not write about Constantine XI personally, so it does not support a T1 connection. The retrospective “Constantine, son of Helena” symmetry likewise does not support T2 because no rostered earlier figure is documented as its prophet.
