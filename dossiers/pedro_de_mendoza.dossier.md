# Pedro de Mendoza: Research Dossier

## Scope and Identification

This dossier concerns **Pedro de Mendoza y Luján**, the sixteenth-century Castilian courtier, soldier, conquistador, and first adelantado and governor of the Río de la Plata—not later people of the same name.

The surviving record is uneven. Mendoza’s royal capitulation, testamentary documents, administrative records, the judicial proceedings concerning Juan de Osorio, and several eyewitness or near-contemporary narratives survive. Much of the familiar biography, however, was reconstructed centuries later. Exact figures for his fleet, passengers, fortune, and casualties differ between sources.

### Evidentiary labels used

- **Documented fact:** supported by a contemporary official, legal, testamentary, or archival record.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a contemporary or near-contemporary narrator, but not independently established.
- **Later reconstruction:** proposed by historians from incomplete or indirect evidence.
- **Allegation:** a charge made by an interested party or witness and not independently proved.
- **Court finding:** determination by a competent tribunal.
- **Disputed claim:** materially conflicting versions exist.
- **Tradition, legend, or myth:** culturally important narrative not securely demonstrated as fact.

## Names, Titles, and Offices

Mendoza appears in official documents as **Don Pedro de Mendoza** and in the modern archival authority record as **Mendoza Luján, Pedro de**. “Don” expressed noble standing rather than constituting a personal name.

The capitulation of 21 May 1534 describes him as the king’s servant and gentleman of the royal household—“mi criado y gentil hombre de mi casa.” It authorized him to conquer and populate the Río de Solís, “which they call the Plata,” and conferred the offices normally summarized as:

- adelantado;
- governor;
- captain-general;
- chief justice or judicial authority within the concession;
- commander of the proposed occupation and exploration.

“Founder of Buenos Aires” is a posthumous conventional title. Whether the 1536 act constituted a juridical municipal foundation is disputed. No foundation act, cabildo record, formal distribution of municipal offices, or secure contemporary plan equivalent to that of Juan de Garay’s 1580 foundation survives.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

The current PARES authority record identifies his parents as:

- **Hernando—or Fernando—de Mendoza**, associated with Guadix; and
- **Constanza de Luján**.

The Hernando/Fernando variation is normal in early-modern records and later genealogical writing.

### Noble background

**Documented or strongly supported:** Mendoza belonged to the extensive Mendoza-Luján noble network. His access to the royal household, military service, admission to a military order, and ability to negotiate a major capitulation all indicate substantial status.

**Disputed reconstruction:** older and genealogically ambitious accounts sometimes placed him very close to the principal ducal house of the Mendoza and made him a great-grandson or similarly near descendant of Íñigo López de Mendoza, marquis of Santillana. Modern institutional summaries are more cautious. Membership in the wider lineage is secure; every precise line of descent printed in popular biographies should not be treated as equally demonstrated.

### Reported siblings

Later biographical compilations commonly identify:

- **Diego de Mendoza**, his brother, who accompanied the expedition and died in the battle conventionally called Corpus Christi;
- **Catalina de Mendoza**;
- **María de Mendoza**.

Diego’s relationship and presence in the expedition are well supported by expeditionary narratives. The full sibling list depends more heavily on later genealogical reconstruction.

### Kinship and patronage

PARES reports that the offices of a relative, **Pedro González de Mendoza**, helped him enter Charles V’s court around 1517. This cannot refer straightforwardly to the famous cardinal of that name, who died in 1495, unless the record means another member of the same extended family or a more indirect patronage connection. The ambiguity should be retained.

A politically important relationship was his family connection to **María de Mendoza**, wife of the powerful imperial secretary **Francisco de los Cobos**. Later historians have regarded this link as important to his successful pursuit of the Río de la Plata concession.

### Marriage, partners, and children

No securely documented wife or acknowledged child appears in the major biographical and documentary sources consulted. His testaments and codicils made provisions for family, servants, attendants, and religious obligations, but the consulted summaries do not establish a spouse or direct heir.

A codicil mentions **María Dávila**, an ill female servant aboard the returning ship, directing that she receive money for treatment upon reaching Seville. One modern study describes her as suffering from syphilis, but the provision itself does not establish a sexual relationship with Mendoza. Claims that she was his mistress, partner, or the source or recipient of his illness would be speculation.

---

## Childhood and Early Life

Almost nothing certain survives about Mendoza’s childhood. His upbringing was probably that of a provincial noble household connected to wider courtly networks. There is no securely identified school, university, tutor, or formal intellectual curriculum in the sources consulted.

Guadix had been incorporated into the Crown of Castile during the conquest of the Nasrid Kingdom of Granada only shortly before Mendoza’s birth. His formative environment therefore belonged to a militarized frontier society in which noble service, royal patronage, ecclesiastical advancement, and warfare remained closely connected.

No childhood memoir, school record, personal diary, or contemporary physical description has been located.

---

## Education and Formation

### Court education

Around **1517**, according to PARES, Mendoza entered the court of Charles V as a page of the chamber through family patronage. Court service would have supplied practical formation in:

- noble etiquette;
- personal service to the sovereign;
- military culture;
- patronage and petitioning;
- travel with an itinerant imperial household;
- administrative and legal instruments such as grants and capitulations.

This is an inference from the function of a court page, not a surviving curriculum.

### Military formation

Mendoza’s later record shows experience in imperial campaigns. His formative influences were probably practical rather than scholarly: royal service, the Italian Wars, noble military companions, and the expanding Castilian institutions of Atlantic conquest.

### Religious formation

His surviving testamentary language invokes God, the Virgin Mary, and the saints and orders prayers and bequests. These are documentary evidence of formal Catholic identity but do not reveal an idiosyncratic theology or unusually personal devotional system.

## Early Court and Military Career, c. 1517–1533

### Entry into Charles V’s household

**Reported by PARES:** Mendoza became a page of the imperial chamber around 1517.

### Journey to England, 1522

PARES records Mendoza in Charles V’s entourage during the emperor’s journey to England in **1522**. The trip placed him within high imperial politics during Charles’s alliance with Henry VIII against France.

No detailed personal account by Mendoza of the English journey is known.

### Knight of Alcántara

Mendoza was admitted as a **knight of the Order of Alcántara**, usually dated to **1524**. The order was both religious-military and aristocratic. Admission signified noble status and royal favor but also required genealogical proof under the norms of the order.

Some popular accounts say that he later changed to the Order of Santiago through paternal influence. This is not established by the stronger sources consulted and should be treated as a disputed or insufficiently supported claim.

### Italian Wars and the Sack of Rome, 1527

Mendoza served with the imperial forces in Italy and was present at the **Sack of Rome in May 1527**.

**Documented context:** unpaid and mutinous imperial troops—Spanish soldiers, German landsknechts, and Italian contingents—stormed Rome after the death of their commander, Charles, duke of Bourbon. The city experienced killing, rape, extortion, imprisonment, and systematic plunder.

**Reported fact concerning Mendoza:** biographical accounts state that he participated in the campaign and benefited personally from the sack.

**Unresolved financial question:** Paul Groussac and historians following him estimated that Mendoza emerged with a fortune of perhaps **8,000–10,000 ducats**, which later helped finance the Río de la Plata expedition. The amount is a reconstruction, not a surviving audited account. It is safer to say that he possessed or assembled substantial capital and that later tradition connected it to Roman plunder.

### Later imperial travel

PARES states that after returning to Spain, Mendoza again accompanied Charles V to Italy and the Holy Roman Empire. The consulted summaries do not provide a complete itinerary.

## The Río de la Plata Project

### Political and geographical background

Spanish interest in the Río de la Plata preceded Mendoza:

- Juan Díaz de Solís had entered the estuary in 1516.
- Sebastian Cabot explored the river system and established Sancti Spiritus in the late 1520s.
- Reports and legends of a **Sierra de la Plata**—a wealthy inland silver region—circulated among Europeans.
- Francisco Pizarro’s seizure of the Inca ruler Atahualpa and the influx of Andean treasure intensified expectations of another rich interior.
- Portugal’s Atlantic and Brazilian position gave the Crown of Castile an additional reason to occupy the region.

The “Silver Mountain” was part intelligence, part geographical misunderstanding, and part promotional mythology. Andean precious metals existed, but Mendoza had no verified route or territorial knowledge equivalent to what the legend implied.

### Negotiations at court

In **1533–1534**, Mendoza pursued the concession. His noble status, court experience, access to Francisco de los Cobos’s network, military record, and willingness to bear enormous private expense made him an acceptable contractor.

This was not a salaried modern colonial appointment. It was a negotiated public-private enterprise in which the Crown delegated conquest and government while requiring the adelantado to finance ships, men, supplies, horses, weapons, and settlement.

---

## The Capitulation of 21 May 1534

The capitulation was concluded at Toledo on **21 May 1534** in the name of Charles V and his mother, Queen Joanna.

### Principal obligations

Mendoza offered to:

- enter and explore the Río de Solís/Río de la Plata;
- conquer and populate the assigned territory;
- penetrate toward the South Sea or Pacific;
- take **1,000 men**, 500 on the first voyage and another 500 within two years;
- provide provisions for one year;
- take **100 horses and mares**;
- supply arms, artillery, ships, and other necessities;
- bear the cost himself.

### Territorial grant

The Crown assigned a governorship conventionally called **New Andalusia**, extending roughly 200 leagues along the South American sphere allotted to Castile, south of the grant associated with Diego de Almagro. Early-modern geographical language and uncertain mapping make modern boundary conversion problematic.

### Rights and incentives

The agreement included:

- authority to distribute town plots, lands, waters, and cavalry allotments;
- power to grant encomiendas subject to royal instructions;
- temporary customs exemptions for settlers;
- a graduated schedule of royal taxation on mined gold;
- Mendoza’s personal exemption from certain customs duties for household provisions;
- governmental and judicial authority;
- salary or official financial advantages attached to office, conditioned on performance.

### Enslaved Africans

A particularly significant provision authorized Mendoza to carry **200 enslaved Black people**, half men and half women, from Spain, Portugal, Cape Verde, or Guinea without the usual royal duties, subject to confiscation if sold outside the concession.

This is a **documented fact in the capitulation**. Whether all 200 were actually purchased, embarked, or reached the Río de la Plata is not established by the sources consulted. The authorization nevertheless places African slavery inside the enterprise’s planned labor and settlement structure from its inception.

### Indigenous policy

The capitulation combined instructions for conversion and “good treatment” with authority to conquer, make encomienda grants, obtain royal fifths from booty, and conduct what the language called rescates and cabalgadas. These provisions embodied both missionary-legal restraint and coercive colonial extraction.

---

## Financing and Business Structure

### Capital

The expedition was financed substantially at Mendoza’s risk. The capitulation explicitly said that the Crown would not reimburse his expenses.

### Likely sources

His resources probably combined:

- personal or family wealth;
- assets reportedly accumulated through imperial service and Roman plunder;
- credit;
- contributions or self-financed passage by officers and settlers;
- commercial participation connected with shipping and provisioning;
- court patronage facilitating licenses and contracts.

No complete balance sheet survives in the sources consulted.

### Costs and incentives

Mendoza expected returns from:

- gubernatorial office;
- land allocation;
- encomiendas;
- mining and treasure;
- commercial control;
- taxation privileges;
- political advancement.

The enterprise failed to produce the anticipated immediate silver or durable coastal colony during his lifetime.

### Testament and debts

A testament dated at Sanlúcar de Barrameda on **21 August 1535**, immediately before departure, acknowledged debts and instructed that proved claims be paid promptly without unnecessary litigation. Mendoza attributed his inability to remember every debt to illness.

The instrument named executors and made conventional religious, household, and personal provisions. His last codicils, dictated at sea in June 1537, amended or supplemented these arrangements.

The fact that his estate later faced the Osorio judgment complicates any attempt to calculate its final value.

---

## The Armada: Composition and Departure

### Size

Sources give between **11 and 14 ships** and approximately **1,000 to more than 1,500 people**. The differences can reflect:

- vessels initially contracted versus those that actually sailed;
- crew versus soldiers and settlers;
- ships joining or leaving;
- later numerical inflation;
- loss of complete muster records.

It was nevertheless one of the largest Spanish expeditions yet sent to South America.

### Personnel

Important participants included:

- Pedro de Mendoza, captain-general;
- Diego de Mendoza, his brother;
- Juan de Osorio, maestre de campo and Mendoza’s sworn or adoptive “brother” in Schmidl’s terminology;
- Juan de Ayolas;
- Juan de Salazar de Espinosa;
- Domingo Martínez de Irala;
- Gonzalo de Mendoza;
- Francisco Ruiz Galán;
- Galaz de Medrano;
- Pedro or Jorge de Luján;
- Lázaro Salvago;
- the physician Hernando de Zamora;
- the German soldier and later chronicler Ulrich/Ulrico Schmidl;
- several women, servants, craftspeople, clergy, sailors, soldiers, and people of African or Indigenous origin.

Rodrigo de Cepeda, brother of Teresa of Ávila, is often listed among the participants. His presence is reported in later biographical literature, but he had no major documented relationship with Mendoza beyond expeditionary association.

### Women

The familiar image of an entirely male expedition is false. Passenger and testamentary records identify several women, although exact totals and identities remain debated. Later historical memory minimized their presence.

A crucial retrospective witness was **Isabel de Guevara**, whose 1556 letter described women nursing the sick, preparing food, standing guard, rowing, and sustaining the settlements. Her testimony concerns the expedition’s collective history more than Mendoza personally, but it demonstrates how conventional narratives omitted women’s labor.

### Departure

The fleet sailed from **Sanlúcar de Barrameda on 24 August 1535**, although some secondary publications give nearby dates. Mendoza was already seriously ill.

## The Killing of Juan de Osorio

This is the clearest documented legal and political controversy of Mendoza’s career.

### Osorio’s position

Juan de Osorio served as maestre de campo and exercised practical command when Mendoza’s illness left him inactive. Schmidl called him Mendoza’s “sworn brother” and described him as popular with the soldiers.

### Accusations

During the Atlantic voyage, Osorio was accused of:

- plotting a mutiny;
- undermining Mendoza;
- insulting officers;
- possibly committing financial misconduct or disloyalty.

Galaz de Medrano and Juan de Ayolas were among the principal accusers in later reconstructions. The accusation of rebellion was never validated by the later royal judicial review.

### Secret proceeding and sentence

Mendoza conducted or authorized a secret process. On or about **29 November 1535**, he signed a sentence ordering that Osorio be killed wherever found, by stabbing, sword thrusts, or another means, until “the soul leaves the flesh”; declaring him a traitor and mutineer; and confiscating his property.

### Execution

At the fleet’s Brazilian stop—usually located at Rio de Janeiro/Guanabara—Osorio was summoned and stabbed to death by officers acting under Mendoza’s order. Accounts name Juan de Ayolas, Galaz de Medrano, Juan de Salazar, Pedro/Jorge de Luján, and Lázaro Salvago in varying combinations.

His body was publicly displayed with a label identifying him as a traitor. Schmidl stated that Osorio had been unjustly killed.

### Legal aftermath

Osorio’s father, **Juan Vázquez de Orejón**, brought proceedings to restore his son’s honor and recover damages.

- A royal instrument of **7 December 1537** recognized that Osorio had served loyally and had been killed without just cause.
- The Council of the Indies ultimately reversed or nullified the treason judgment.
- On **5 March 1544**, according to the modern study of the proceedings, Osorio’s honor and property were restored and Mendoza’s heirs were ordered to pay **1,000 ducats plus costs**.

**Evidentiary status: court finding.** The later royal proceedings establish that Osorio’s condemnation was legally unjustified. The underlying psychological explanation—jealousy, fear of mutiny, manipulation by rivals, illness-clouded judgment, or deliberate removal of a popular subordinate—remains unresolved.

---

## Atlantic Crossing and Brazil, 1535–1536

The fleet called at the Canary Islands and then proceeded toward Brazil. Mendoza’s incapacity forced delegation of command. The voyage was marked by factional tension, sickness, discipline problems, and the Osorio killing.

Accounts identify a stay of approximately two weeks around Guanabara Bay. The fleet was operating close to Portuguese territory, so prolonged settlement there was politically impractical.

By January 1536 the expedition entered the Río de la Plata and stopped near **San Gabriel Island**, opposite the later Colonia del Sacramento. On **22 January**, according to the conventional reconstruction, officers and men formally swore obedience to Mendoza as governor.

## Santa María del Buen Aire, 1536

### Date

The traditional date is **2 February 1536**; some historians and official commemorations use **3 February**. No surviving foundation act settles the question.

### Name

Recorded forms include:

- Santa María del Buen Aire;
- Nuestra Señora Santa María del Buen Aire;
- Puerto de Nuestra Señora de los Buenos Aires;
- Bonas Ayers in Schmidl’s German transcription.

The invocation probably referred to **Our Lady of Bonaria/Bon Aire**, a Marian devotion associated with sailors and Cagliari, Sardinia.

The title **Ciudad de la Santísima Trinidad y Puerto de Santa María del Buen Ayre** properly belongs to Garay’s 1580 foundation and is sometimes projected backward onto Mendoza’s settlement.

### Was it a “foundation”?

**Disputed claim.**

What Mendoza established was a defended encampment or port settlement with earthen works, huts, a ditch, and a palisade. Contemporary testimony calls it a city, but the customary legal formalities of a Castilian municipal foundation are not securely documented.

Thus:

- “first foundation of Buenos Aires” is the dominant commemorative formula;
- “first European settlement at the site or in the immediate region” is safer;
- “formal legal foundation of the present city” is debatable;
- Garay’s 1580 settlement possessed clearer institutional continuity with the later city.

### Location

The exact site is unresolved. Proposals include:

- the Vuelta de Rocha/La Boca area;
- the Parque Lezama high ground or “Puntas de Buenos Aires”;
- sites farther inland around Parque Patricios;
- alternative positions along former channels of the Riachuelo.

Changes in shoreline, buried waterways, urban construction, contradictory distance measurements, and the absence of conclusive archaeological remains prevent certainty.

## Relations with the Querandí

“Querandí” is the historical ethnonym used by European chroniclers for Indigenous communities inhabiting the pampas around the lower Paraná and Río de la Plata. Its exact linguistic, ethnic, and political scope remains debated.

### Initial exchange

Schmidl reported that approximately 3,000 Querandí men, women, and children were encountered and that they brought the newcomers fish and meat for **fourteen days**.

This exchange was indispensable to the colonists, whose food planning had already failed.

### Breakdown

When provisions ceased, Mendoza sent an alcalde and two companions to demand that the Querandí resume supplying the camp. Schmidl says the messengers behaved in a manner that resulted in their being beaten and driven away.

Mendoza then ordered a punitive expedition under his brother Diego. Schmidl characterized the orders as capturing or killing the Querandí and taking their settlement.

The evidence therefore does not support a simplified account in which an unprovoked Indigenous attack alone caused the conflict. The breakdown followed Spanish dependence, coercive demands, mistreatment or confrontation, and a punitive military response.

## Battle of Corpus Christi

### Date and place

Traditionally dated **15 June 1536**, the feast of Corpus Christi. The battlefield has not been conclusively located; areas associated with the later Río Luján, Matanza basin, or other nearby pampas have been proposed.

### Forces and weapons

Accounts describe approximately:

- 300 Spanish infantry;
- 19–30 horsemen;
- several thousand Querandí and allies.

The Indigenous forces used bows, projectiles, and stone-weighted cords or bolas capable of bringing down horses.

### Result

Spanish accounts treated possession of the battlefield as a nominal victory, but the expedition suffered a serious tactical and leadership loss.

Spanish dead reportedly included:

- **Diego de Mendoza**;
- several captains and noble officers;
- approximately 26–40 Europeans, depending on the source.

Indigenous casualties are sometimes given as approximately 1,000, but this number comes from colonial reporting and cannot be independently verified.

The battle demonstrated that cavalry did not confer automatic dominance on open terrain and that Mendoza’s settlement could not compel a stable local food supply.

## Siege, Famine, and Cannibalism

### Indigenous attack on the settlement

After Corpus Christi, Indigenous forces attacked or besieged Buenos Aires. Schmidl described flaming projectiles setting thatched buildings and ships alight. Several vessels and much of the settlement were damaged or destroyed, though the Europeans retained or rebuilt part of their position.

### Famine

Food shortages became catastrophic. Contemporary and near-contemporary narratives report consumption of:

- rats and mice;
- snakes;
- insects or vermin;
- leather shoes and hides;
- dead horses;
- roots, grasses, and carrion.

Disease and malnutrition killed a large portion of the expedition. Exact mortality totals vary and are complicated by dispersal among ships and inland posts.

### Cannibalism

Schmidl gives the most famous account:

- three Spaniards stole and ate a horse;
- after torture or interrogation, they were executed;
- other starving Spaniards cut flesh from their hanging bodies;
- another man reportedly ate the corpse of his dead brother.

Ruy Díaz de Guzmán and later writers repeated or amplified the story.

**Evidentiary status:** reported fact from a participant’s later chronicle, corroborated in broad outline by other colonial narratives, but with uncertain individual identities and literary shaping.

### Diego González Baitos

Later historian **Enrique de Gandía** identified the brother-eating colonist as the crossbowman Diego González Baitos. A modern textual study finds no colonial source that makes this identification. De Gandía introduced it in the 1930s, after initially relating the episode without naming the man.

**Evidentiary status:** unsupported later attribution, subsequently popularized through historiography and literature.

## Inland Projects and Geographical Movements

### Buena Esperanza and Corpus Christi

Mendoza and his commanders attempted to move people and resources upriver, closer to Indigenous food-producing communities and the route imagined to lead toward silver.

Settlements or posts associated with this phase include:

- **Buena Esperanza**, in the Paraná region;
- **Corpus Christi**, near the earlier Sancti Spiritus area;
- intermediate camps and river stations whose exact locations remain debated.

Mendoza himself traveled upriver despite severe illness, but his physical participation in operations was limited.

### Juan de Ayolas

Mendoza increasingly relied on **Juan de Ayolas**, appointing him lieutenant-general and sending him upriver with several hundred men.

Ayolas proceeded through the Paraná-Paraguay system, seeking:

- food;
- stable Indigenous alliances;
- a route westward;
- the reputed metal-rich interior.

His expedition eventually reached the upper Paraguay and advanced overland. Ayolas was later killed, probably by Payaguá forces, after Mendoza’s death.

### Domingo Martínez de Irala

Ayolas left **Domingo Martínez de Irala** at Candelaria to await his return. Irala later became the dominant political figure in Paraguay.

### Juan de Salazar and Asunción

Mendoza sent **Juan de Salazar de Espinosa** and **Gonzalo de Mendoza** to seek news of Ayolas. Salazar established the fort of **Nuestra Señora de la Asunción** on 15 August 1537, after Pedro de Mendoza had begun his return voyage.

Asunción—not Buenos Aires—became the durable center of the first Río de la Plata colonization.

## Illness

### Symptoms

Sources describe Mendoza as:

- chronically ill before departure;
- bedridden or inactive for substantial periods;
- weakened, “crippled,” or unable to command personally;
- afflicted by bodily ulcers;
- dependent on physician Hernando de Zamora and attendants.

### Syphilis hypothesis

Numerous later biographies diagnose syphilis acquired during or after the Sack of Rome.

Evidence supporting the hypothesis:

- chronic ulcerative illness;
- association with Rome, where sexually transmitted disease was widespread;
- later descriptions of an affected servant aboard his ship;
- long historiographical tradition.

Evidence against certainty:

- no surviving physician’s diagnosis;
- “ulcers,” paralysis, wasting, and pain are nonspecific;
- malnutrition, infection, scurvy, dysentery, or other chronic disease could have contributed;
- retrospective diagnosis from narrative language is medically uncertain.

**Conclusion:** syphilis is a plausible historical diagnosis, not a documented fact.

### Psychological state

Later narratives portray Mendoza as suspicious, withdrawn, despondent, or broken by the enterprise. His killing of Osorio and eventual departure have been interpreted through illness and depression. No contemporary medical assessment supports a formal psychiatric diagnosis.

---

## Collapse of Mendoza’s Personal Command

By early 1537 Mendoza’s authority depended on dispersed subordinates:

- Ayolas was upriver;
- Irala held an advanced position;
- Salazar and Gonzalo de Mendoza undertook searches and supply missions;
- Francisco Ruiz Galán controlled Buenos Aires or the coastal zone;
- royal officials defended their separate legal jurisdictions.

The expedition had failed to find immediate silver, the original settlement was devastated, the population had suffered extreme mortality, and Mendoza was physically incapable of restoring unified command.

Before leaving, he designated **Juan de Ayolas** as his principal successor or lieutenant. Because Ayolas was absent, Mendoza left **Francisco Ruiz Galán** with immediate authority at Buenos Aires. These overlapping appointments later contributed to jurisdictional disputes.

---

## Departure, Codicils, and Death

### Departure

Mendoza left the Río de la Plata on **22 April 1537**, traveling aboard the caravel **Magdalena**. The galeon or ship **Santantón/San Antón** accompanied it. Approximately 150 people reportedly traveled in the returning party.

### Last documents

At sea, Mendoza dictated three short codicils on **11, 12, and 13 June 1537**. Some secondary texts incorrectly give July, which is impossible if the accepted death date is 23 June.

The codicils included:

- religious donations;
- provisions for relatives;
- gifts or wages for servants, attendants, and nurses;
- instructions concerning María Dávila’s treatment;
- confirmation or adjustment of executors and estate arrangements.

Because they were dictated aboard ship shortly before death, historians sometimes call them his “testament made at sea,” although they supplemented the fuller 1535 testament.

### Last voyage and food shortage

Ruy Díaz de Guzmán later told a dramatic story that provisions ran out, Mendoza ordered a female dog killed for food, and after eating it became agitated “as if rabid” and died two days later; others who ate it supposedly also died.

No contemporary medical or ship’s record confirms rabies or poisoning. The tale is a **near-contemporary reported tradition**, not a secure cause-of-death finding.

### Death

Mendoza died on **23 June 1537**, in the Atlantic southwest of Cape Verde or broadly between the Cape Verde and Canary routes.

His body reportedly decomposed rapidly and was committed to the sea the following day. Hernando de Zamora had been unable to reverse his decline.

---

## Later Fate of His Expedition

Mendoza’s death did not immediately end the colonial enterprise.

- Asunción became the central settlement.
- Ayolas disappeared and was presumed killed.
- Domingo Martínez de Irala eventually consolidated power.
- Buenos Aires was formally evacuated and burned in **1541** under Irala’s order.
- Survivors moved to Asunción.
- Juan de Garay established a permanent settlement at Buenos Aires in **1580**.

Mendoza’s enterprise was therefore an immediate failure in its original coastal, commercial, and precious-metal objectives but an institutional precursor of Spanish settlement in Paraguay and the later Río de la Plata.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

No modern company or incorporated business was involved. Relevant structures were:

### Crown of Castile

The sovereign source of legal title, jurisdiction, taxation, and conquest authority.

### Council of the Indies

The administrative and judicial institution that supervised the enterprise and later reviewed the Osorio case.

### Casa de la Contratación

The Seville-based institution regulating transatlantic shipping, passengers, cargo, royal revenue, and colonial litigation.

### Military orders

Mendoza’s membership in the Order of Alcántara contributed to his noble and military standing.

### The adelantado contract

The capitulation functioned as a public-private concession. Mendoza assumed enormous financial risk in exchange for office, jurisdiction, tax privileges, prospective land, and access to colonial wealth.

### Maritime and military technology

The expedition used:

- caravels, naos, and smaller river craft;
- artillery and firearms;
- crossbows;
- steel weapons and armor;
- horses;
- palisades, ditches, and earthen defenses.

Indigenous forces countered cavalry with bows, missiles, fire, and bolas or stone-weighted cords. The effectiveness of these weapons at Corpus Christi contradicted European expectations of easy cavalry superiority.

### Settlement projects

Projects directly or indirectly launched under Mendoza’s authority included:

- Santa María del Buen Aire;
- Buena Esperanza;
- Corpus Christi;
- exploration of the Paraná and Paraguay;
- Ayolas’s westward search for precious metals;
- the chain of expeditions leading to Asunción.

### Property and estate

Mendoza’s property included funds, personal goods, contractual rights, claims arising from his offices, ships or shipping interests, and household assets. A complete inventory has not been reconstructed here. Osorio’s confiscated property became part of the later lawsuit, and Mendoza’s heirs were eventually held financially liable.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Violence, and Financial Questions

### 1. Killing of Juan de Osorio

- **Documented fact:** Mendoza signed the lethal sentence and Osorio was killed without an open adversarial trial.
- **Allegation at the time:** Osorio was a traitor and mutineer.
- **Court finding:** the Council of the Indies found the killing unjustified, restored Osorio’s honor, and imposed financial liability on Mendoza’s heirs.
- **Unresolved:** whether Mendoza sincerely feared mutiny or knowingly accepted fabricated accusations.

### 2. Participation in the Sack of Rome

- **Documented/reported fact:** Mendoza served with the imperial forces and was present during the 1527 sack.
- **Reported fact:** he personally benefited from plunder.
- **Unverified amount:** the frequently cited 8,000–10,000 ducats.
- **Broader documented context:** the sack involved widespread killing, rape, extortion, sacrilege, and theft. No source consulted establishes which specific acts Mendoza personally committed.

### 3. Coercion and violence against Indigenous people

- **Documented through expeditionary testimony:** after the Querandí stopped supplying food, Mendoza sent officers to demand provisions and then ordered a punitive expedition.
- **Reported orders:** capture or kill the Querandí and occupy their settlement.
- **Result:** battle, deaths, siege, reprisals, and collapse of exchange.
- **Unresolved:** exact casualty totals and the complete sequence of provocations.

### 4. Encomienda and slavery

- **Documented fact:** the capitulation authorized encomienda grants and the duty-free transportation of 200 enslaved Africans.
- **Unresolved:** how many enslaved Africans actually accompanied the expedition and what became of them.

### 5. Leadership failure

Later historians have charged Mendoza with:

- inadequate provisioning;
- excessive confidence in silver legends;
- incapacity caused by illness;
- over-delegation;
- factional favoritism;
- rash violence toward the Querandí;
- abandoning the colony.

These are historical assessments rather than criminal findings. Relevant documented facts are that Mendoza was gravely ill, food supply collapsed, his brother’s punitive expedition failed at great cost, his officers dispersed, and he sailed for Spain before the colony stabilized.

### 6. “Abandonment” of his command

Mendoza did leave in April 1537. He also appointed successors, dispatched searches for Ayolas, and left continuing settlements. Whether his return constituted cowardly abandonment, medically necessary withdrawal, an attempt to report and seek aid, or acceptance of failure is interpretive.

### 7. Wealth and debts

Mendoza invested heavily but did not achieve the anticipated return. His 1535 testament acknowledged debts without recalling all creditors because of illness. No evidence found supports modern-style fraud, embezzlement, money laundering, or a clandestine corporate structure.

### 8. Most-recent-two-years adversarial search

Searches combining his name with Spanish and English terms for scandal, controversy, allegations, fraud, lawsuits, conspiracy, criticism, and crime produced no new 2024–2026 archival scandal concerning the sixteenth-century Mendoza. Recent results largely concerned unrelated modern people bearing Pedro or Mendoza as a name.

The principal enduring contested matters remain:

- Osorio’s unlawful killing;
- violence against Indigenous communities;
- slavery and encomienda provisions;
- the provenance and amount of Mendoza’s fortune;
- his medical diagnosis;
- whether Buenos Aires was legally “founded”;
- the expedition’s cannibalism accounts;
- the accuracy of heroic nationalist commemoration.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## The Sierra de la Plata

**Story:** A fabulously rich silver mountain or kingdom lay inland and could be reached through the Río de la Plata.

**Origins and circulation:** The story developed from Indigenous geographical information, earlier Portuguese and Spanish voyages, Aleixo Garcia’s journey, Cabot’s explorations, and reports of Andean metal wealth.

**Evidence:** The Andes and Inca world possessed immense precious-metal resources, but no silver mountain existed in the form imagined by Mendoza’s promoters. The river received its European name from this expectation.

**Effect:** It helped recruit and finance expeditions, drew colonists inland, and made a poorly provisioned estuarine settlement seem like the gateway to rapid enrichment.

## Mendoza as the unquestioned “founder of Buenos Aires”

**Story:** On 2 or 3 February 1536 Mendoza formally founded the city at the site of modern Buenos Aires.

**Evidence for:** A settlement bearing the Buen Aire name was established under his command, and Schmidl called it a city.

**Evidence against or qualification:** No secure foundation act survives; municipal formalities are doubtful; the exact site is unresolved; the settlement was abandoned in 1541; institutional continuity comes from Garay’s 1580 foundation.

**Circulation and effect:** Civic commemorations, monuments, school histories, the 1936 four-hundredth anniversary, and popular biographies elevated Mendoza into the city’s founder.

## Exact foundation at Parque Lezama or La Boca

**Story:** Mendoza’s settlement stood at one precisely identifiable modern landmark.

**Evidence:** Topography and chroniclers’ descriptions support several nearby zones, but urban transformation and lack of conclusive archaeology prevent exact identification.

**Effect:** Competing neighborhoods and historians have attached civic significance to proposed sites.

## The “portrait” of Pedro de Mendoza

A familiar painted image is repeatedly reproduced as Mendoza’s portrait.

Historian Enrique de Gandía argued that a family-gallery portrait made in 1567 derived from an earlier sketch of Mendoza. The chain of identification is conjectural. No securely authenticated life portrait is known.

**Evidentiary status:** disputed later attribution.

## Syphilis contracted in Rome

**Story:** Mendoza acquired syphilis during the Sack of Rome and sought the Río de la Plata climate as a cure.

**Evidence for:** chronic ulcerative illness, his presence in Rome, and longstanding retrospective diagnosis.

**Evidence against:** no contemporary diagnostic record; a colonial expedition was not a recognized reliably curative treatment; symptoms were nonspecific.

**Effect:** The story supplies a dramatic causal link between Roman violence, bodily decay, failed conquest, and death.

## Divine punishment for Osorio’s murder

Ruy Díaz de Guzmán wrote that the colony’s wars, hunger, deaths, and disasters followed Osorio’s unjust killing as divine punishment.

This is a providential interpretation, not demonstrable causation. It became a moral framework through which later generations understood the expedition’s failure.

## Mendoza killed by eating a rabid dog

**Story:** Near starvation on the return voyage, Mendoza ate a female dog, became agitated as though rabid, and died; companions who ate it also died.

**Source:** Ruy Díaz de Guzmán’s later narrative.

**Evidence:** Severe food shortage and illness are credible; no medical or shipboard document confirms rabies, poisoning, or the two-day sequence.

**Effect:** It completed the expedition’s famine narrative with a grotesque and providential death.

## Spanish cannibalism

**Story:** Starving settlers ate executed companions and one man ate his brother.

**Evidence:** Schmidl, an expedition participant writing later, gives a detailed account; later colonial writers repeat it.

**Qualification:** The episodes cannot be verified by forensic evidence, and literary or religious shaping—especially the association with Corpus Christi—may have influenced presentation.

**Effect:** The narrative inverted European accusations of Indigenous “savagery,” becoming central to literature about Buenos Aires.

## Diego González Baitos as the brother-eater

**Story:** The crossbowman Baitos consumed his brother.

**Origin:** Enrique de Gandía introduced the identification in the 1930s; Manuel Mujica Láinez subsequently helped popularize the episode in literary form.

**Evidence:** Colonial sources describe an unnamed man. The surviving texts do not identify him as Baitos.

**Status:** unsupported attribution.

## Horses as Mendoza’s accidental lasting gift

**Story:** Horses abandoned after the settlement’s evacuation multiplied into the vast feral herds of the pampas.

**Evidence:** Horses were imported with the expedition; some survived and reproduced. Later observers reported abundant feral horses.

**Qualification:** The exact number left—often given as five mares and seven horses—is uncertain, and later introductions also contributed to regional horse populations.

**Effect:** The story became part of Argentine pastoral and gaucho origin mythology.

## Mendoza as heroic visionary versus incompetent aristocrat

Two opposed reputational myths developed:

- the noble founder who sacrificed fortune and health to create Buenos Aires;
- the sick, violent, poorly prepared aristocrat who killed a loyal officer and led his followers into famine.

Each selects real evidence but turns it into a single moral character. The record instead shows courtly competence, access to wealth and patronage, serious prior military experience, severe chronic illness, an unjust execution, coercive colonial violence, logistical failure, and an expedition whose subordinates nevertheless established a durable Spanish presence in Paraguay.

## Significant Successes and Failures

### Successes

- Secured one of the largest private royal concessions of the period.
- Organized a major transatlantic fleet.
- Transported a substantial settler population, animals, weapons, and equipment.
- Established the first European settlement bearing the Buenos Aires name.
- Initiated the chain of upriver expeditions that culminated in Asunción.
- Preserved enough of the expeditionary structure for subordinates to continue after his departure and death.

### Failures

- Did not reach the South Sea.
- Did not discover or control the expected silver source.
- Failed to establish a self-sustaining coastal food system.
- Provoked or authorized a punitive war after breakdown of relations with the Querandí.
- Lost his brother and important officers at Corpus Christi.
- Failed to prevent famine and mass death.
- Ordered the legally unjustified killing of Juan de Osorio.
- Could not maintain coherent personal command.
- Left before a durable political center existed at Buenos Aires.
- Died before reaching Spain.
- The Buenos Aires settlement was abandoned in 1541.

## Works by Pedro de Mendoza

Mendoza was not a literary author in the conventional sense. His surviving corpus consists principally of dictated, signed, issued, or received legal-administrative documents.

### Authentic or officially issued under his authority

1. **Capitulación con Don Pedro de Mendoza sobre el descubrimiento y población del Río de la Plata** — 21 May 1534.  
   A royal contract rather than a work solely authored by Mendoza. It records his offer, obligations, offices, privileges, territorial grant, taxation, encomienda power, and slave license.

2. **Testamento de Pedro de Mendoza** — Sanlúcar de Barrameda, 21 August 1535.  
   A notarized testament dictated or approved by him before sailing. It includes religious formulas, executors, debts, restitution, and bequests.

3. **Proceso y sentencia contra Juan de Osorio** — aboard the expedition, November 1535.  
   Mendoza’s sentence survives through documentary copies and the later litigation. It declared Osorio a traitor and ordered his killing.

4. **Instructions and appointments to Juan de Ayolas** — 1536–1537.  
   Administrative documents appointing or directing Ayolas as lieutenant-general and expeditionary commander. Exact texts and dates vary in documentary editions.

5. **Appointment or instructions concerning Francisco Ruiz Galán** — April 1537.  
   Authority for immediate government at Buenos Aires during Ayolas’s absence.

6. **Letters and orders to expeditionary officers and royal officials** — 1535–1537.  
   Scattered items survive or are calendared in the Archivo General de Indias and documentary collections. These are administrative correspondence, not a unified authored book.

7. **Three codicils dictated at sea** — 11, 12, and 13 June 1537.  
   Supplements to his testament containing religious, familial, household, and medical provisions.

### Attributed, edited, or posthumously compiled material

8. **Documentos relativos a la expedición de Don Pedro de Mendoza** — edited by Enrique A. Peña, published in the early twentieth century.  
   A documentary compilation, not a book written by Mendoza.

9. **Documents in Anales de la Biblioteca** — edited archival transcriptions containing his testamentary and expeditionary records.  
   Posthumous scholarly publication.

### No known works

No securely attributed memoir, diary, travel narrative, map, literary manuscript, theological work, technical treatise, patent, artwork, or autobiographical book by Mendoza is known.

## Primary and Near-Contemporary Sources

### Ulrich/Ulrico Schmidl

**Original title variants:** *Wahre Historia einer wunderbaren Schiffahrt*; commonly translated as *Viaje al Río de la Plata* or *Derrotero y viaje a España y las Indias*.

Schmidl participated in Mendoza’s expedition and remained in the region for many years. He is indispensable for:

- Osorio’s killing;
- the Querandí exchange;
- Corpus Christi;
- famine and cannibalism;
- military and geographical detail.

Limitations include retrospective composition, textual variants, translation problems, numerical uncertainty, and European ethnographic conventions.

### Ruy Díaz de Guzmán

*Historia argentina del descubrimiento, población y conquista de las provincias del Río de la Plata*, composed around 1612.

Díaz de Guzmán wrote decades later but had access to settler memory and family traditions. He supplies:

- a developed account of Osorio;
- providential interpretations;
- Mendoza’s final voyage and dog-meat death story;
- early colonial political history.

He was not an eyewitness to Mendoza’s voyage.

### Isabel de Guevara

**Carta a la princesa gobernadora Doña Juana**, Asunción, 2 July 1556.

The letter documents women’s work in sustaining the expedition and challenges male-centered chronicles. It is also a petition for reward and should be read as both testimony and advocacy.

### Martín del Barco Centenera

*Argentina y conquista del Río de la Plata*, Lisbon, 1602.

An epic poem based on colonial experience and testimony. It helped give “Argentina” its literary-geographical currency and transmitted famine and conquest traditions, but poetic form limits literal use.

- Passenger and shipping records of the Casa de la Contratación.
- Archivo General de Indias files.
- Mendoza’s testament and codicils.
- Osorio litigation and Council of the Indies judgments.
- Later royal provisions governing the vacancy after Mendoza’s death.

## Death and Estate Aftermath

Mendoza died without seeing whether Ayolas’s inland expedition had succeeded. News traveled slowly, creating uncertainty over succession.

The Crown issued later provisions for government in case Mendoza and his designated lieutenant were dead. The surviving settlers eventually recognized new leadership, with Irala emerging as the most durable authority.

Mendoza’s estate remained exposed to:

- personal debts;
- testamentary bequests;
- expeditionary financial losses;
- claims by servants and creditors;
- the legal reversal of Osorio’s conviction;
- the 1,000-ducat award and costs assessed against his heirs.

No evidence consulted establishes that Mendoza’s family recovered the fortune invested in the expedition.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Sixteenth and seventeenth centuries

Early narratives remembered:

- the Osorio killing;
- disastrous hunger;
- conflict with the Querandí;
- Mendoza’s sickness;
- failed silver expectations;
- the upriver movement that produced Asunción.

Providential history presented the expedition’s suffering as punishment for Osorio’s death.

### Nineteenth century

As Buenos Aires became a capital and Argentine historians constructed a national past, Mendoza became useful as its “first founder.” Scholars debated:

- his genealogy;
- the exact foundation date;
- the settlement’s location;
- whether the act was legally a foundation;
- the meaning of his failure.

### 1936 quatercentenary

The four-hundredth anniversary generated:

- monuments;
- public ceremonies;
- newspaper and magazine coverage;
- historical books;
- proposed portraits;
- local-site claims;
- a more heroic civic image.

Enrique de Gandía’s biography belongs to this commemorative moment and materially shaped the modern Mendoza legend.

### Indigenous reinterpretation

More recent scholarship and public history place greater emphasis on:

- the Querandí’s initial provisioning of the Europeans;
- Spanish coercion when food deliveries stopped;
- Mendoza’s punitive order;
- Indigenous tactical success at Corpus Christi;
- colonial seizure, encomienda, and slavery;
- the disappearance of Indigenous agency from heroic foundation narratives.

### Urban legacy

The modern city is institutionally continuous with Garay’s 1580 foundation rather than Mendoza’s abandoned settlement. Nevertheless, Mendoza supplied:

- its enduring name;
- its first European encampment tradition;
- a foundational civic date;
- a tragic origin narrative of hunger, violence, and uncertainty.

### Paraguayan and regional legacy

The expedition’s more durable consequence lay upriver. Mendoza’s lieutenants and survivors contributed to:

- Asunción;
- Spanish occupation of Paraguay;
- later colonizing movements downriver;
- the political system from which Garay refounded Buenos Aires.

### Historical balance

The recoverable Mendoza is simultaneously:

- a noble court insider;
- a soldier of Charles V;
- a participant in the army that sacked Rome;
- a high-risk colonial entrepreneur;
- a chronically ill commander;
- authorizer of an unjust killing;
- organizer of coercive Indigenous conquest;
- beneficiary of a legal system permitting encomienda and African slavery;
- leader of a logistically catastrophic expedition;
- initiator of settlements and journeys that outlived him.

These statements describe distinct parts of the record and do not depend on reducing him to either heroic or hostile mythology.
