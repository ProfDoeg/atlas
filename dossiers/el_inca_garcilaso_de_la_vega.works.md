## Works by El Inca Garcilaso de la Vega

Garcilaso is unusual among writer-travelers of his kind for having three substantial works rather than one. He produced a translation early in his Spanish career, then, across roughly two decades in Montilla and Cordoba, built a trilogy of historical writing about the Americas he had left as a young man and never saw again.

### An early translation: the Dialogues of Love

Before turning to history, Garcilaso's first published book was a Spanish translation of an Italian philosophical work, the Dialoghi d'amore of the Jewish Neoplatonist Judah Abravanel, known as Leon Hebreo. Garcilaso finished the translation in the 1580s and published it in Madrid in 1590 as La traduzion del indio de los tres Dialogos de Amor de Leon Hebreo. The phrase "del indio," "by the Indian," in the title was a deliberate public claim of his American origin on a work of European philosophy, and the translation functioned as both a literary exercise and a declaration of authorial identity before he had published anything about Peru.

### La Florida del Inca (1605)

His first historical book concerned a place Garcilaso never visited. Settled near Cordoba, he spent years interviewing Gonzalo Silvestre, a veteran of Hernando de Soto's expedition through the North American southeast in the 1540s, supplementing those conversations with other participants' accounts and published histories. The resulting book, published at Lisbon in 1605, narrates Soto's expedition and its encounters with Indigenous peoples of what is now the southeastern United States. It is not an eyewitness chronicle but a literary reconstruction built from decades-old oral testimony, shaped with the narrative techniques Garcilaso had absorbed from classical and Renaissance historiography; modern historians generally treat it as more valuable for its portrait of expedition veterans' memory and its sympathetic depiction of Indigenous eloquence and resistance than as a precise documentary record of events.

### Comentarios Reales de los Incas (1609)

Garcilaso's central and most influential work turned to the history he did know firsthand, at least in part: the Inca world of his own childhood in Cuzco, reconstructed from family memory, Quechua etymology, and comparison with earlier Spanish chroniclers he considered mistaken or ignorant of Indigenous language and custom. Published at Lisbon in 1609 as the Comentarios reales, its nine books cover Inca origin accounts, the succession of rulers and territorial expansion, government and social structure, religion, agriculture and technology, and relations among Andean peoples. Garcilaso presents himself explicitly as a corrector and glossator of the Spanish historical record, drawing authority from his mixed descent and his access to his mother's family's oral tradition. The book shapes the Inca state as orderly, paternal, and providentially prepared for Christianity, an idealized picture later historians and archaeologists have qualified considerably, particularly regarding warfare, coercion, and human sacrifice, which the work minimizes.

### Historia General del Peru (1617)

Garcilaso considered this book the second part of the same project, and it is sometimes described as such rather than as a fully separate title. Where the Comentarios reales stopped with the Inca state on the eve of conquest, the Historia general carries the narrative through the Spanish invasion, the killing of successive Inca rulers, the civil wars among the conquistadors, the suppression of later rebellions, and the execution of Tupac Amaru I in 1572. It is markedly more personal than the first part, defending his father Sebastian Garcilaso's conduct during the Pizarro rebellions and addressing Indigenous, mestizo, and creole Peruvians directly as compatriots. Garcilaso secured a publication license for the work before his death in April 1616; it appeared in print at Cordoba the following year, 1617, under his widow's imprint.

### A contested source: Blas Valera

A substantial portion of Garcilaso's Andean material in these later books draws on fragments attributed to the mestizo Jesuit Blas Valera, delivered to him around 1598 after most of Valera's own manuscript had reportedly been destroyed. Garcilaso names and quotes Valera repeatedly rather than concealing the debt, but the scale of the borrowing prompted a plagiarism dispute in the early twentieth century that continues to shape how scholars read the authorship of the Comentarios reales.
