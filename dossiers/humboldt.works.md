## Works by Alexander von Humboldt

Humboldt's bibliography resists the simple shape of "a writer and his books." The American-expedition material alone was published as roughly thirty folio and quarto components over three decades, under joint authorship with Aimé Bonpland and with substantial unacknowledged labor from specialist collaborators who classified, engraved, and described the specimens Humboldt and Bonpland collected. His last great project, *Kosmos*, was still unfinished when he died at eighty-nine. And underneath the printed books lies a correspondence so large, by some reconstructions some fifty thousand letters written and over a hundred thousand received, that it functioned less as private communication than as an informal international scientific institution in its own right.

### Early scientific monographs, before the American expedition

- **1790:** *Mineralogische Beobachtungen über einige Basalte am Rhein*. Humboldt's first book, a geological study of Rhineland basalt formations, written while he was still formed by his teacher Abraham Gottlob Werner's "Neptunist" theory that such rocks precipitated from a primordial ocean, a theory Humboldt later abandoned as he observed actual volcanoes.
- **1793:** *Florae Fribergensis specimen*. A study of cryptogamic (non-flowering) plants found in and around the Freiberg mining district, reflecting his training as a mining engineer as much as his botanical interests.
- **1797:** *Versuche über die gereizte Muskel- und Nervenfaser*, two volumes. A physiological study of muscle and nerve irritability under electrical and chemical stimulation, including self-experimentation that left Humboldt with real wounds. It sits in the genre of experimental physiology rather than natural history or travel writing.
- **1799:** *Ueber die unterirdischen Gasarten*. A practical treatise on dangerous underground mine gases and how to reduce their harm, written from his years as a Prussian mining inspector; closer to an engineering safety report than to his later synthetic works.

### The American-expedition publication complex, 1805–1834

Humboldt and Bonpland's travels through Spanish America (1799–1804) generated not one book but an umbrella publishing enterprise, the *Voyage aux régions équinoxiales du Nouveau Continent*, issued in parts over nearly thirty years. Counting its "volumes" depends on how atlases, plates, and text fascicles are tallied; bibliographies disagree. Its major separable components include:

- **Essai sur la géographie des plantes** (1805–1807), with Bonpland. The founding statement of plant geography: vegetation types mapped against altitude, temperature, and humidity, illustrated by the celebrated cross-section of Chimborazo showing plant zones banded up the mountainside. It proposed comparing the distribution of living things against physical conditions rather than simply cataloguing specimens.
- **Relation historique du Voyage aux régions équinoxiales du Nouveau Continent**, published in English as the ***Personal Narrative of Travels to the Equinoctial Regions of the New Continent*** (three volumes appeared, 1814, 1819, 1825). This is the travel narrative proper: the Atlantic crossing, the Orinoco and Casiquiare canoe journeys, the ascent toward Chimborazo's summit, observations of earthquakes, electric eels, missions, and plantation slavery. Humboldt never finished it; the narrative breaks off before the Mexican and Cuban portions of the journey, which were instead treated in separate political-statistical works.
- **Vues des Cordillères et monuments des peuples indigènes de l'Amérique** (1810–1813). A folio of plates and accompanying text on Andean landscapes and Indigenous American monuments and artifacts, produced through a network of engravers and artists rather than by Humboldt's hand alone.
- **Essai politique sur le royaume de la Nouvelle-Espagne** (1808–1811), published in English as *Political Essay on the Kingdom of New Spain*. A statistical and economic description of colonial Mexico, drawn from Humboldt's privileged access to Spanish administrative archives: population, mining output, agriculture, and trade. It became one of his most widely read books, and was also, independent of Humboldt's intent, useful geopolitical intelligence; some of its data reached Thomas Jefferson and other American officials.
- **Essai politique sur l'île de Cuba** (1826). A similar political-economic study of Cuba that documents, and explicitly condemns, the scale of plantation slavery there. A pirated and politically doctored 1856 English edition by the American proslavery writer John Sidney Thrasher cut the antislavery passages and inserted proslavery argument; Humboldt publicly repudiated it, so that edition should never be read as representing his views.
- **Taxonomic and zoological monographs**: *Nova genera et species plantarum* (seven volumes, 1815–1825) and other botanical series were credited to Humboldt and Bonpland but depended heavily on the systematic classification work of Carl Sigismund Kunth; the *Recueil d'observations de zoologie et d'anatomie comparée* likewise drew on contributing specialists. These are collaborative works in substance, however the title pages apportion credit.
- **Atlas géographique et physique des régions équinoxiales du Nouveau Continent** and its New Spain counterpart: cartographic volumes, a different kind of "text" again, closer to a technical reference object than to prose.

### Other synthetic works

- **Ansichten der Natur** (1808; enlarged 1826, 1849), in English ***Views of Nature*** (sometimes *Aspects of Nature*). A collection of essays, more literary and essayistic than the expedition volumes, exploring themes like steppes, cataracts, and night skies drawn from his travels; it reached a wider general readership than the folio volumes aimed at specialists.
- **Fragmens de géologie et de climatologie asiatiques** (1831) and **Asie centrale** (1843). Works arising from Humboldt's 1829 Russian-funded expedition across the Urals and Siberia toward the Chinese frontier. They extend far past ordinary travel narrative into comparative geology and climatology, but they also notably omit sustained criticism of the forced labor and exile system he observed under Russian sponsorship, a silence modern scholarship treats as a real limitation rather than an oversight.

### Kosmos: a late synthesis left unfinished

*Kosmos: Entwurf einer physischen Weltbeschreibung* occupied Humboldt's final decades. It grew out of his 1827–28 Berlin lecture series, including public lectures open to men and women without fee at the Sing-Akademie, and aimed to describe the physical universe as a connected whole, from nebulae and stars down to terrestrial climate, organisms, landscapes, and the history of human engagement with nature. Volumes I through IV appeared during his lifetime (1845, 1847, 1850, 1858); a fifth volume, assembled from his manuscripts and notes, was published posthumously in 1862, incomplete. *Kosmos* is best understood not as a finished treatise but as an ambitious, ultimately overtaken attempt at synthesis: by the time Volume IV appeared, the specialist sciences it tried to unify had already begun to outgrow what one author, however well connected, could master and narrate.

A related text, sometimes catalogued separately, is the ***Kosmos-Vorlesung*** itself: the Sing-Akademie lectures survive mainly through auditors' notes rather than a single manuscript Humboldt himself prepared for print, and were edited into publishable form only after the fact.

### Correspondence

Humboldt's letters are not an appendix to his books but a major part of his intellectual production in their own right. Estimates of roughly fifty thousand letters written and over a hundred thousand received are modern reconstructions rather than a complete surviving count, but they are consistent with his role as the hub of an international network: coordinating simultaneous geomagnetic observations with Gauss and Wilhelm Weber, recommending students and scholars for appointments, corresponding with Simón Bolívar and Thomas Jefferson, and, by his final years, publishing a newspaper appeal asking strangers to stop sending him manuscripts and requests he no longer had time to answer. Large portions of this correspondence have since been dispersed across archives in Berlin, Kraków, Paris, and elsewhere, and a modern scholarly edition project continues to reconstruct it.
