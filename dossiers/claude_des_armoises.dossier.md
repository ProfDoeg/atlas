# Claude des Armoises: Research Dossier

## Scope, identity, and evidentiary method

Claude des Armoises is the conventional modern name for a fifteenth-century woman who appeared in Lorraine in 1436, called herself Claude, and then presented herself—or allowed herself to be presented—as Jeanne la Pucelle, the supposedly surviving Joan of Arc. After marrying the knight Robert des Armoises, she was usually called Jeanne, Jehanne, or Dame des Armoises.

Her biography is unusually difficult to reconstruct because:

- her birth name, birth date, parentage, and early childhood are not securely documented;
- several contemporary or near-contemporary texts describe her under different names;
- some evidence comes from municipal accounts and legal instruments, while other details derive from chronicles compiled or copied later;
- Jeanne des Armoises has sometimes been conflated with other “false Maids,” especially Jeanne de Sermaises;
- the alleged marriage contract publicized in the seventeenth century was produced by the notorious document-finder Jérôme Vignier and is generally regarded as forged or at least unreliable;
- nineteenth- and twentieth-century “survivalist” writers identified her with Joan of Arc and incorporated her into a much larger theory of royal birth, substitution at Rouen, and political conspiracy;
- later popular accounts often repeat narrative embellishments—the king’s disguise, the “secret of Chinon,” residence at Jaulny, a known grave at Pulligny—as if securely documented.

The labels used below are:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a surviving administrative, legal, financial, or contemporaneous narrative record sufficiently close to the event.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a contemporary or near-contemporary source but not independently established.
- **Allegation:** an accusation made against her in a judicial or narrative source.
- **Theory:** a later attempt to connect or interpret incomplete evidence.
- **Rumor or myth:** a story that became part of her historical reputation but lacks adequate documentary proof.
- **Unresolved:** evidence permits more than one reasonable reconstruction.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Primary modern name:** Claude des Armoises.

**Names and designations in the sources and later literature:**

- Claude
- Claude des Armoises
- Jeanne des Armoises
- Jehanne des Armoises
- Johanna des Armoises
- Jeanne-Claude des Armoises
- Jeanne du Lis or du Lys
- Jehanne la Pucelle
- la Pucelle de France
- la dame des Armoises
- la fausse Pucelle
- la fausse Jeanne d’Arc
- “une appelée Jehanne, qui se disoit Pucelle,” in the royal remission concerning Jean de Siquenville

**Sex:** Female.

**Nationality or political-geographical identity:** A woman associated with Lorraine, Metz, the duchy of Luxembourg, Cologne, and later the kingdom of France. Calling her simply “French” conceals the fragmented political geography in which she operated.

**Probable birthplace:** Jarville, near Nancy, according to the reconstruction accepted by Catherine Guyon on the basis of the work of Olivier Bouzy, Pierre-Gilles Girault, and Mireille Chazan. The exact underlying birth record is not known, and no baptismal register establishes the claim.

**Birth date:** Unknown. No defensible exact year survives. She was already old enough to travel independently and take part in armed life by the early-to-mid-1430s. A birth in the first decades of the fifteenth century is possible but cannot be narrowed securely.

**Death date:** Unknown. She was certainly active in 1440. If the woman pardoned by René of Anjou in February 1457 was the same person, she was alive then. References to death in 1449 or burial at Pulligny are later and disputed.

**Occupation or social role:**

- itinerant armed woman;
- probable mercenary or camp follower in papal service in Italy;
- religious-political claimant;
- impersonator of Joan of Arc;
- wife of Robert des Armoises;
- possible commander or symbolic head of an armed company;
- litigant or defendant before ecclesiastical and secular jurisdictions.

**Authorship:** No securely surviving literary, theological, or autobiographical work can be attributed to her. Letters were sent in her name, but neither their text nor her personal authorship is securely preserved.

**BnF classification:** The Bibliothèque nationale de France identifies “Des Armoises, Claude” as a fifteenth-century French adventurer who pretended to be Joan of Arc, obtained the protection of Duke Ulrich of Württemberg, was received at the court of Elisabeth of Luxembourg, and married Robert des Armoises.

## Historical Context

Joan of Arc was executed at Rouen on 30 May 1431 after an ecclesiastical trial conducted under Anglo-Burgundian authority. Her death did not immediately stabilize her public identity. Many French supporters had regarded her victories as providential, while her enemies officially represented her as a heretic and relapsed offender.

Claims that important people had escaped death were a recurring medieval phenomenon. Political pretenders could attract support because identity was established through personal recognition, seals, heraldry, reputation, kinship testimony, and remembered bodily signs rather than modern documentary identification.

Claude’s claim was particularly effective because:

- Joan’s execution occurred in enemy-held Rouen;
- most inhabitants of Lorraine and central France had not witnessed it;
- rumors held that another woman had been burned;
- Joan’s brothers and other acquaintances could lend apparent recognition;
- the name of the Pucelle still carried military, devotional, and financial power;
- uncertainty over whether Joan should be remembered as holy, heretical, or politically inconvenient remained unresolved until the nullification proceedings of 1450–1456.

Claude was not the only woman associated with claims about Joan’s survival. Sources also mention Jeanne de Sermaises and other “Pucelles.” Whether some records concern the same itinerant claimant remains disputed.

## Family and Ancestry

### Biological family

Claude’s parents, siblings, ancestry, and original family name are not securely established.

A Parisian chronicle reports that the false Pucelle said she had accidentally struck her mother while trying to hit someone else. Her mother had tried to restrain her because Claude intended to beat one of her female acquaintances. This was allegedly why she needed to undertake a penitential journey to Rome for a “reserved case”—an offense requiring higher ecclesiastical absolution.

If the report is accurate, it provides the only significant glimpse of her biological family:

- her mother was alive during Claude’s youth or early adulthood;
- Claude had a volatile conflict with her;
- no father, sibling, or family occupation is named.

The wording comes through a hostile Parisian narrator and a public confession or sermon, not through a family record. It should therefore be treated as **reported testimony**, not a fully verified domestic history.

### The du Lys family

In May 1436, Pierre and Jean du Lys, brothers of Joan of Arc, reportedly recognized Claude as their sister. This is one of the central facts behind the later survival legend.

The recognition is reported in the Metz chronicle tradition. The text says the brothers had believed their sister burned, but that when they saw Claude they recognized her, and she recognized them. They then took “their sister” with them.

Possible interpretations include:

1. **Good-faith recognition:** They believed Joan had escaped.
2. **Mistaken recognition:** Similarity, expectation, and Claude’s knowledge persuaded them.
3. **Collusion:** They knowingly helped an impersonator because the revived identity brought influence, hospitality, and gifts.
4. **Political operation:** Claude was being used to revive an Armagnac symbol or test public reaction.
5. **Survivalist interpretation:** They recognized their actual sister.

The documents establish that recognition was claimed; they do not establish what the brothers privately believed. Later writers who state categorically that Pierre and Jean “ran the fraud” go beyond the surviving proof, although their material interest is a reasonable subject of inquiry.

There is no secure evidence that Isabelle Romée, Joan’s mother, recognized Claude. Nineteenth-century accounts sometimes assert that she did, but the municipal evidence more clearly shows Orléans continuing or resuming support for Isabelle as the mother of Jeanne and eventually calling Joan “feue”—deceased.

### Marriage to Robert des Armoises

Claude married Robert des Armoises, a knight and lord associated with Tichémont. The marriage took place by 7 November 1436 and probably at Arlon.

Robert belonged to a Lorraine noble family but appears to have been financially constrained. Later narratives characterize him as substantially older than Claude and as a politically compromised or dispossessed knight. Exact age statements are not securely documented.

A deed dated 7 November 1436 concerns Robert and his wife, styled “Jehanne du Lys, la Pucelle de France,” in a property transaction involving a portion of the lordship of Haraucourt. Unlike the supposed marriage contract, this transaction has been treated as genuine documentary evidence that:

- Claude and Robert were married by that date;
- she was openly using Joan’s ennobled family name, du Lys;
- the “Pucelle de France” claim was incorporated into a formal transaction;
- her assumed identity was accepted, tolerated, or instrumentally employed in an aristocratic and notarial setting.

The alleged marriage contract later found by Jérôme Vignier is a separate item. It has not survived in a reliable original and is generally considered a Vignier fabrication.

### Children

The *Journal d’un bourgeois de Paris* reports that Claude acknowledged being married and having two sons. This is **contemporary reported testimony**, probably deriving from the public proceedings against her in Paris.

The sons’ names and later lives are not securely known. A Louis des Armoises killed at the Battle of Nancy in 1477 has occasionally been proposed as one of them, but the relationship is conjectural.

Claims that she died childless conflict with the Paris account. Claims of a traceable modern line of descendants are likewise unproved.

## Childhood and Early Life

No continuous childhood biography survives.

Recent French scholarship has proposed Jarville, near Nancy, as her place of origin. If correct, she emerged from Lorraine rather than from Domrémy itself. No reliable source establishes that she grew up with Joan, knew the d’Arc family before 1436, or belonged to an aristocratic or royal lineage.

A reconstructed early episode concerns the assault on her mother. Pierre-Gilles Girault’s study of the proceedings against Claude notes that, possibly before 1434, she was associated with a penitential journey to Rome for an offense treated as a reserved ecclesiastical case. The Paris chronicle’s later version says she struck her mother while enraged.

Nothing establishes:

- her original surname;
- her father’s identity;
- whether her family was peasant, urban, military, or minor noble;
- literacy;
- formal education;
- childhood residence beyond the proposed Jarville origin;
- personal acquaintance with Joan of Arc before 1431.

---

## Education and Formation

No school, convent, teacher, or formal mentor is documented.

Her later conduct nevertheless indicates practical formation in several fields:

- familiarity with military dress and armed companies;
- ability to move among knights and noble courts;
- enough command of the Joan narrative to convince or enlist the cooperation of people connected with the real Pucelle;
- knowledge of aristocratic forms of address and patronage;
- capacity to participate in transactions under an assumed noble identity;
- familiarity with penitential pilgrimage and ecclesiastical jurisdiction.

The source tradition also associates her with feats interpreted as magic or conjuring: breaking and restoring glasses or tearing and rejoining cloth. These reports come through hostile ecclesiastical or later narrative sources and may describe stage tricks, rumor, demonological interpretation, or inventions intended to discredit her.

## Early Career and Travels Before 1436

### The penitential journey to Rome

**Reported fact:** Claude said she needed to go to Rome to obtain absolution because she had struck her mother.

The journey’s timing and completion are uncertain. Girault places the possible penitential case before 1434. The older reconstruction by Alfred Lecoy de La Marche put an Italian journey between her first eastern appearances and the Orléans triumph of 1439, but the chronology remains difficult.

### Military activity in Italy

Johannes Nider and later accounts associate Claude with armed life and male clothing. One tradition says she served in Italy in forces connected with Pope Eugene IV, whose pontificate was marked by warfare against Italian rivals and revolts in the Papal States.

This is best classified as a **reported but incompletely documented episode**. No surviving papal muster roll or contract of service has been identified that names her unambiguously.

Her later ability to act among soldiers makes military experience plausible. Claims that she held a regular papal commission or officer’s rank are not established.

## Chronological Life History

## 1436: Appearance near Metz

On 20 May 1436, a woman calling herself Claude appeared at La Grange-aux-Ormes, near Saint-Privat outside Metz. The Metz chronicle tradition describes her as “the Maid Jeanne who had been in France,” while also recording that she “had herself called Claude.”

Pierre and Jean du Lys came to see her. According to the chronicle, they recognized her as their sister and she recognized them. On 21 May they took her with them to Bacquillon.

Several local notables supplied gifts. The chronicle tradition mentions:

- a horse;
- a hood or clothing;
- a sword;
- other equipment appropriate to the identity of an armed noblewoman.

This marked the transformation of an obscure “Claude” into an internationally interesting claimant.

The chronicle often called the *Chronique du doyen de Saint-Thiébault* is more properly connected with the Metz chronicle tradition and Pierre Didier, priest of Saint-Eucaire. Its textual history is complicated: surviving copies are later than the event, and part of its modern prominence results from the discoveries publicized by the Vignier brothers. Its narrative is nevertheless supported at points by independent documents, especially the Cologne records and Orléans accounts.

## 1436: Arlon and Elisabeth of Luxembourg

Claude went to Arlon and entered the circle of Elisabeth of Görlitz, duchess of Luxembourg. Elisabeth was a politically connected widow with a financially troubled court and extensive relations across Luxembourg, Burgundy, and the Empire.

The BnF authority record states that Claude was welcomed at Elisabeth’s court. The duchess’s protection gave Claude:

- noble lodging and visibility;
- access to armor, mounts, and escorts;
- a setting in which the identity “Pucelle of France” could be politically useful;
- contact with German and Lotharingian lords.

A son of the count of Virneburg reportedly took her toward Cologne. She was also associated with the protection of Duke Ulrich of Württemberg. Exact sequences differ between chronicles.

No document proves that Elisabeth privately accepted Claude as the genuine Joan. Patronage could reflect belief, curiosity, political experimentation, entertainment, or protection of a remarkable armed woman.

## 1436: Cologne, ecclesiastical investigation, and excommunication

Claude appeared in Cologne dressed and equipped as a soldier. Johannes Nider’s *Formicarius*, written in the later 1430s, supplies a hostile ecclesiastical account. Nider describes an armed woman who claimed divine or prophetic authority and reportedly boasted that she would install a bishop just as the Maid of France had installed a king.

The Cologne inquisitor Heinrich Kalteisen investigated her. Reported charges or suspicions included:

- wearing male dress;
- assuming a prophetic or divinely commissioned role;
- participation in military affairs;
- irregular social behavior;
- suspected magical performances;
- claims involving ecclesiastical appointments.

She was summoned but did not submit to the proceeding. She was excommunicated for contumacy or nonappearance.

This is the earliest clearly documented confrontation between Claude and formal authority. To contemporaries, the danger was not merely “identity fraud.” She was appropriating the persona of a woman officially condemned as a relapsed heretic and was allegedly claiming supernatural authority.

The Cologne municipal safe-conduct register independently attests her presence in the city. That administrative corroboration strengthens the outline of Nider’s narrative, although not every lurid detail.

## Late 1436: Return to Arlon and marriage

After leaving Cologne, Claude returned to the Luxembourg-Lorraine milieu. By 7 November she was married to Robert des Armoises.

The genuine property deed of that date styles her “Jehanne du Lys, Pucelle de France.” This is among the strongest pieces of evidence for the social penetration of her claim. She was not merely telling stories in taverns: the identity appeared in an instrument involving a noble husband and property.

The couple lived for a time in Metz, reportedly in Robert’s house opposite the church of Sainte-Ségolène.

### Jaulny

Later tradition places the couple at the château de Jaulny and exhibits alleged portraits and arms there. Catherine Guyon reports that Robert did not own Jaulny, undermining the standard château-residence narrative. The painted fireplace medallion presented as Claude’s portrait is probably nineteenth-century or otherwise much later than Claude.

Residence at Jaulny should therefore be labeled **local tradition**, not documented fact.

## 1436–1438: Correspondence, movement, and uncertain military activity

The Orléans accounts record communications connected with the supposed survival of the Pucelle. Messengers carried news or letters between the claimant, Jean du Lys, and the city.

Orléans did not respond uniformly. Its records could entertain correspondence from the supposed living Pucelle while still preserving commemorative observances for the dead Joan. Alfred Lecoy de La Marche interpreted this as evidence that opinion in the city was divided.

The texts of Claude’s letters have not survived in a form that establishes their wording or authorship. Scribes almost certainly prepared such communications, as was normal. Their contents should not be reconstructed from later paraphrases.

## 1438–1439: Armed service and association with Gilles de Rais

A royal remission issued in June 1441 to Jean de Siquenville retrospectively describes events approximately two years earlier. Gilles de Rais had given Siquenville command or government of men-at-arms previously held by “one called Jehanne, who called herself the Maid.”

This document establishes a connection among:

- Gilles de Rais;
- an armed company;
- a woman claiming to be the Pucelle;
- Jean de Siquenville;
- operations contemplated around Le Mans, Anjou, or Poitou.

It does not clearly establish Claude’s formal rank. Older historians sometimes called her “captain” and treated Siquenville as her lieutenant. Lecoy de La Marche observed that the document may instead indicate that Siquenville replaced her as head of a company.

Jacques Chiffoleau characterizes the relationship with Gilles de Rais as poorly documented and difficult to interpret. Claims that Gilles supported her because he recognized the genuine Joan, or because both were practitioners of magic, are theories rather than facts.

The troop’s activities occurred amid civil conflict, private warfare, forced contributions, and ransom-taking. Siquenville was later accused of exacting payments from villages in Anjou and Poitou. Whether Claude personally authorized pillage or profited from it is not documented.

### The supposed expedition to Le Mans

Later narratives describe Claude leading an expedition against Le Mans. The remission says Gilles intended to move against the city and that Siquenville might become its captain if it were taken. It does not record a successful siege or prove Claude led it.

She has also been confused with Jeanne la Féronne, a later visionary called the “Pucelle du Mans.” These were distinct figures.

### The La Rochelle and Castilian-fleet story

A Spanish narrative concerning Álvaro de Luna says that the Pucelle of France appealed to King Henry of Castile and obtained ships and troops that helped take La Rochelle.

Problems include:

- La Rochelle was already aligned with Charles VII;
- the supposed “Chronicle of the Maid” cited by the Spanish writer is lost;
- the timing conflicts with Claude’s documented presence in Lorraine and Luxembourg;
- Castilian vessels did help protect the arrival of Margaret of Scotland in 1436, possibly supplying material for a transformed story.

Claude’s participation in the capture of La Rochelle is therefore **unverified and chronologically improbable**.

## July–August 1439: Triumphal reception at Orléans

Claude reached Orléans by 18 July 1439. Municipal accounts document official hospitality:

- wine was presented on 18 July;
- further wine and food were supplied on 29 and 30 July;
- a farewell meal was held on 1 August;
- the council awarded her 210 livres parisis “for the good that she had done for the said town during the siege.”

The wording is remarkable because it credits her with service during the siege of 1428–1429, when the real Joan helped relieve Orléans. At least for purposes of the payment, municipal authorities treated Claude as the returning heroine.

Two hundred ten livres was a substantial gift, but proposed modern equivalents vary wildly and should not be treated as precise. Medieval purchasing power, accounting money, wages, and commodity values do not convert reliably into a single modern currency figure.

Her departure was abrupt enough that wine ordered for her arrived too late and was redirected to Jean Luillier, possibly the cloth merchant who had helped outfit the real Joan.

Claude returned briefly to Orléans on 4 September.

The reception proves institutional acceptance or accommodation; it does not prove universal belief. Municipal officials may have been divided, politically cautious, or unwilling to insult Joan’s brothers and supporters.

## September 1439: Tours and petition to the king

Claude proceeded toward Touraine. A bailiff sent a letter concerning her to Charles VII, and a petition in her name accompanied it. The king was then moving through the region after the recovery of Paris and preparing for political assemblies.

Later accounts transform this documentary contact into a sustained correspondence between Claude and Charles VII. The surviving evidence is narrower: a message about her and a supplication sent in her name.

## 1440: Royal confrontation

A later story says Claude obtained an audience with Charles VII. According to the version attributed to the chamberlain de Boisy, she greeted a substitute or failed to identify the king, after which Charles asked for the secret shared between himself and the true Joan at Chinon. Unable to answer, she fell to her knees and confessed.

This polished recognition-scene resembles literary stories told about pretenders. It is **later reported tradition**, not a verbatim court record.

The core event—a royal summons or confrontation followed by exposure—is plausible and supported by the subsequent terminology in the 1441 Siquenville remission, which treats the claimant’s status skeptically. The specific “secret of Chinon” test is not independently established.

## 1440: Proceedings at Paris

The *Journal d’un bourgeois de Paris*, a contemporary but strongly partisan chronicle, provides the clearest account of Claude’s public exposure.

According to the journal:

- the University of Paris and Parlement caused her to be brought to Paris, willingly or unwillingly;
- she was exhibited in the great courtyard of the Palais on the marble stone;
- a public sermon narrated her life and condition;
- she admitted that she was not Joan;
- she acknowledged being married and the mother of two sons;
- she recounted the assault on her mother and proposed Roman penitential journey;
- she had previously been involved in war and went back to military life afterward.

This was not necessarily a modern criminal trial with a preserved indictment and judgment. Pierre-Gilles Girault characterizes the outcome as public preaching and *amende honorable*: ritualized exposure, confession, and correction.

The journal’s author was hostile to Joan and to Armagnac religious claims. His language should not be treated as neutral, but the detailed institutional setting makes outright invention unlikely.

An alleged sentence of four years’ imprisonment on bread and water appears in later discussion, coupled with the claim that she served none of it. The precise origin and execution of this sentence are uncertain.

## After 1440

The Paris chronicler says that after the proceedings she returned to war, stayed in a garrison, and then departed. Her destination is not given.

This contradicts accounts that end her public life immediately upon confession. It suggests that her military usefulness or access to protectors survived the destruction of her Joan identity.

No secure record establishes that she retired permanently to domestic life with Robert.

## 1456–1457: Imprisonment, banishment, and remission

A remission issued by René of Anjou in February 1457, or February 1456 in old-style dating, concerns a woman called Jeanne de Sermaises. She had allegedly long called herself Jeanne la Pucelle and deceived people who had seen Joan at Orléans. She had been imprisoned and was released or pardoned under conditions that she dress and conduct herself as a woman.

Older scholarship, including Lecoy de La Marche, identified Jeanne de Sermaises with Jeanne des Armoises through similarity of name and conduct. Andrew Lang and other writers debated the identification.

Modern scholarship remains attentive to the possibility but should not suppress the uncertainty:

- **If identical:** Claude survived into 1457, repeated or continued her claim, was imprisoned and banished, and obtained René’s remission.
- **If distinct:** Claude disappears from secure documentation after the 1440s, while another claimant underwent the 1456–1457 proceeding.

Girault includes the 1456–1457 imprisonment, banishment, and remission in his study of Jeanne-Claude’s judicial career, indicating that he considers the connection historically substantial. It is still safer to label the identification **probable or possible, not certain**.

## Residences, Migrations, and Major Movements

The fullest defensible itinerary is:

- **Jarville near Nancy:** proposed birthplace or early home.
- **Rome/Italy:** intended penitential pilgrimage and reported armed service; exact dates uncertain.
- **La Grange-aux-Ormes near Saint-Privat:** appearance on 20 May 1436.
- **Bacquillon:** taken there by Pierre and Jean du Lys on 21 May 1436.
- **Metz:** contact with local elites and later marital residence.
- **Arlon:** protected at Elisabeth of Luxembourg’s court; probable marriage setting.
- **Cologne:** documented by a safe-conduct record and ecclesiastical investigation.
- **Possibly Trier and other Rhineland locations:** reported in reconstructions but incompletely documented.
- **Metz:** residence with Robert opposite Sainte-Ségolène.
- **Italy:** reported military service; chronology disputed.
- **Poitou, Anjou, and the Le Mans region:** possible armed-company activity under Gilles de Rais’s patronage.
- **Orléans:** documented receptions in July–August and September 1439.
- **Tours/Touraine:** documented petitionary activity in September 1439.
- **Paris:** public exposure and confession in 1440.
- **Unidentified garrison:** military activity after the Paris proceeding.
- **Lorraine or Anjou:** possible later imprisonment and conditional pardon if she was Jeanne de Sermaises.
- **Jaulny:** later tradition, not a securely documented residence.
- **Pulligny:** alleged burial place, not securely established.

## Marriage, Property, and Finances

Claude’s economic history was structured around patronage, gifts, military service, and marriage.

### Gifts and hospitality

Known or reported benefits included:

- horse and equipment from Lorraine notables;
- protection and court access from Elisabeth of Luxembourg;
- armor or military support in the Rhineland;
- lodging and food from towns and patrons;
- municipal expenditure by Orléans;
- 210 livres parisis from the Orléans council;
- possible income, rations, or shares of booty from armed service.

### Marriage and land

The 7 November 1436 transaction involving Robert and “Jehanne du Lys, Pucelle de France” concerned the sale or transfer of a portion of the lordship of Haraucourt. The title used in the deed increased the couple’s prestige and potentially its bargaining position.

The claim that Robert married her solely to profit from the imposture is possible but unproved. His participation in a formal deed using her assumed identity does show that he accepted or exploited the public persona.

### Fraud and enrichment

Claude obtained gifts by allowing donors to believe she was the woman who had relieved Orléans. In modern terms this resembles identity fraud. Fifteenth-century legal authorities, however, were also concerned with:

- heretical impersonation;
- public deception;
- unlawful male dress;
- unauthorized religious claims;
- disorder caused by an armed woman;
- abuse of the political symbol of the Pucelle.

The total amount she accumulated is unknown. No estate inventory, treasury, banking arrangement, or final property settlement survives.

### No documented business structure

There is no evidence of companies, commercial partnerships, formal investments, patents, inventions, or technologies associated with Claude. Her “enterprise,” if understood economically, was a shifting patronage and military network rather than an incorporated business.

## Political, Religious, and Ideological Development

### Political position

By claiming to be Joan, Claude appropriated an Armagnac and Valois political identity. Her supporters were generally located in territories sympathetic to Charles VII or among nobles for whom the Pucelle remained an attractive legitimizing symbol.

She is not known to have produced a political program. Her activity supported, or at least traded upon:

- the legitimacy of Charles VII;
- the memory of the relief of Orléans;
- hostility to English rule;
- belief that divine favor remained with the Valois cause.

The Cologne episode shows that she also claimed influence over episcopal politics, allegedly promising to establish a bishop as Joan had established a king.

### Religion

Claude used a persona inseparable from prophecy and divine mission. Whether she personally believed herself inspired cannot be established.

The known religious-legal elements are:

- a proposed penitential journey to Rome;
- alleged need for absolution after striking her mother;
- suspicion of magical feats;
- refusal to appear before the Cologne inquisitor;
- excommunication for nonappearance;
- public ecclesiastical correction in Paris;
- possible later punishment for appropriating the identity of a condemned heretic.

No creed, prayer book, spiritual writing, confessor, or sustained devotional practice is documented.

### Male dress

Claude wore armor and male clothing in military contexts. Ecclesiastical writers treated this as a sign of religious and social disorder, especially because cross-dressing had been central to the proceedings against Joan of Arc.

The record does not establish Claude’s private gender identity. Her clothing is best understood in its documented contexts: soldiering, public performance of the Pucelle role, physical mobility, and the legal demand that she resume clothing considered appropriate to a woman.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Pierre and Jean du Lys

**Nature:** Reported recognition as their sister; travel and promotional association.

**Evidence:** Metz chronicle tradition and Orléans accounts.

**Unresolved question:** Whether they were duped, hopeful, politically motivated, or complicit.

### Robert des Armoises

**Nature:** Husband; participant in property dealings under her assumed identity.

**Evidence:** The 7 November 1436 deed and chronicle material.

**Unresolved question:** His level of belief and financial motivation.

### Elisabeth of Luxembourg/Görlitz

**Nature:** Noble protector and host at Arlon.

**Evidence:** Chronicle tradition and BnF authority summary.

**Unresolved question:** Whether she believed Claude or used her politically.

### Duke Ulrich of Württemberg

**Nature:** Reported protector.

**Evidence:** BnF authority record and later reconstructions based on German sources.

### Count and family of Virneburg

**Nature:** Escort or patronage connection facilitating travel to Cologne.

**Evidence:** Metz chronicle tradition.

### Heinrich Kalteisen

**Nature:** Dominican inquisitor who summoned or investigated her at Cologne.

**Evidence:** Nider’s *Formicarius* and Cologne documentation.

**Relationship:** Institutional adversary.

### Johannes Nider

**Nature:** Dominican theologian who recorded the Cologne case.

**Evidence:** *Formicarius*, book V, chapter VIII.

Nider did not necessarily meet Claude personally. He transmitted information obtained in the inquisitorial milieu.

### Gilles de Rais

**Nature:** Patron or military employer connected with a company formerly under “Jehanne, who called herself the Maid.”

**Evidence:** Charles VII’s 1441 remission for Jean de Siquenville.

**Limits:** The relationship’s duration, personal intimacy, and political purpose are unknown.

### Jean de Siquenville

**Nature:** Soldier appointed to command men previously associated with the claimant.

**Evidence:** Royal remission of June 1441.

### Charles VII

**Nature:** King whose legitimacy Claude’s assumed identity invoked; recipient of a petition; probable authority behind her exposure.

**Evidence:** Tours account, Paris chronicle, later audience tradition.

### University of Paris and Parlement of Paris

**Nature:** Investigating and disciplinary institutions.

**Evidence:** *Journal d’un bourgeois de Paris*.

### René of Anjou

**Nature:** Possible later pardoning authority if Jeanne de Sermaises and Claude were identical.

**Evidence:** 1457 remission.

## Military Activity

Claude’s reputation as a female warrior has better support than many other elements of her biography, but her rank is frequently exaggerated.

Documented or near-documented elements include:

- wearing male military clothing and armor;
- movement with nobles and armed retainers;
- reported Italian military service;
- association with a body of troops later placed under Jean de Siquenville;
- return to a garrison after her Paris exposure.

Unproved or exaggerated claims include:

- that she commanded papal armies;
- that she captured La Rochelle;
- that she led a major siege of Le Mans;
- that she fought beside Dunois in Spain;
- that she was recognized by all of Joan’s former comrades;
- that she held a royal captaincy;
- that she killed two men in documented battle.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, and Financial Questions

## Impersonation of Joan of Arc

**Documented fact:** Claude used the name and titles of Joan or permitted their use in official and municipal contexts.

**Evidence:**

- the Metz chronicle says she called herself Claude but was presented as the Pucelle;
- the 1436 deed calls her Jeanne du Lys, Pucelle of France;
- Orléans paid her for alleged service during its siege;
- the Paris chronicle records exposure of a woman claiming to be the Pucelle;
- the Siquenville remission refers to “Jehanne, who called herself the Maid.”

**Historical characterization:** Modern scholarship overwhelmingly treats her as an impersonator, because Joan’s execution is independently documented and Claude reportedly confessed.

## Possible complicity of Joan’s brothers

**Allegation or theory:** Pierre and Jean du Lys knowingly promoted Claude to obtain gifts and revive their social position.

**Evidence for:**

- they reportedly recognized her;
- Orléans paid expenses connected with Jean’s communications;
- Claude’s credibility materially depended upon them;
- the group benefited from hospitality and gifts.

**Evidence against categorical assertion:**

- no confession by either brother survives;
- grief, hope, mistaken recognition, or uncertainty remain possible;
- the length and intensity of their later participation are often exaggerated.

**Status:** Unresolved; financial interest is evident, knowing conspiracy is not proved.

## Magical practices

**Allegation:** Claude performed conjuring feats and claimed supernatural authority.

**Source:** Nider’s demonological and inquisitorial milieu.

**Possible basis:** Sleight of hand, rumor, hostile characterization of performance, or genuine claims by Claude.

**Status:** Allegation, not proof of supernatural practice or criminal sorcery.

## Assault on her mother

**Reported admission:** She accidentally struck her mother while trying to attack another woman.

**Status:** Contemporary reported testimony; no separate judicial record has been identified.

## Excommunication

**Documented or strongly reported fact:** The Cologne inquisitor summoned her; she failed to appear and was excommunicated.

**Nature of finding:** Procedural ecclesiastical penalty for contumacy, not proof of every allegation against her.

## Armed depredations

**Association:** A company connected with Claude was later commanded by Jean de Siquenville, who was accused of ransom-taking and coercive exactions.

**Status:** Siquenville’s misconduct is documented in his remission. Claude’s personal responsibility is not.

## Paris proceedings and confession

**Reported fact:** The University and Parlement examined her; she underwent public preaching or correction and acknowledged the imposture.

**Status:** Strong contemporary narrative evidence, although no full trial dossier survives.

## Later imprisonment and banishment

**Documented for Jeanne de Sermaises:** Imprisonment and conditional remission in 1457.

**Identity problem:** The equation with Claude des Armoises is plausible but disputed.

## Gilles de Rais association

Later writers have sometimes implied that Claude’s association with Gilles connected her to his murders, sexual crimes, alchemy, or necromancy.

No surviving evidence implicates Claude in Gilles’s crimes. The documented link concerns an armed company. Any extension beyond that is unsupported.

## Adversarial research findings, including 2024–2026 material

Searches combining Claude’s name with scandal, fraud, accusation, lawsuit, crime, conspiracy, criticism, and controversy produced no newly reported modern criminal, financial, or archival scandal involving her beyond the established imposture and survival debate.

Recent material largely falls into three groups:

1. **Academic reassessment:** Pierre-Gilles Girault’s judicial reconstruction remains the most important specialized analysis; recent legal-history work cites it when discussing women in male dress and the public disciplining of false Pucelles.
2. **Popular retellings:** Radio, podcast, and magazine treatments continue to present her as the most successful Joan impostor, often adding unverified dramatic details.
3. **Survivalist repetition:** Websites and media continue to claim that Joan escaped Rouen and lived as Madame des Armoises. No newly authenticated fifteenth-century document establishing identity has appeared in the reviewed 2024–2026 material.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## “Claude was the real Joan of Arc”

### Story

Joan did not die at Rouen. Another woman was substituted on the scaffold, while Joan escaped, returned to Lorraine as Claude, was recognized by her brothers and former comrades, married Robert des Armoises, and lived into the 1440s or 1450s.

### Emergence

Contemporary rumors already held that the Pucelle had escaped. The Metz claimant made the rumor concrete in 1436.

The systematic identification of Claude with the genuine Joan developed much later:

- the Vignier material appeared in the late seventeenth century;
- Polluche discussed the historical “problem” in 1729;
- Pierre Caze connected survival with royal-bastardy claims in 1819;
- Ernest Lesigne’s *La Fin d’une légende* appeared in 1889;
- Gaston Save’s *Jehanne des Armoises, Pucelle d’Orléans* appeared in 1893;
- later authors including Grillot de Givry, Étienne Weill-Raynal, Marcel Gay, and Roger Senzig revived variants.

### Evidence cited for it

- recognition by Joan’s brothers;
- reception at Orléans;
- the 210-livre payment;
- use of the title “Pucelle de France” in a deed;
- alleged recognition by former companions;
- rumors that the condemned woman’s face was hidden;
- the alleged marriage contract;
- supposed later letters and property documents;
- alleged tomb and portraits.

### Evidence against it

- detailed contemporary testimony records Joan’s public execution and the exposure of her body;
- the Paris chronicle reports Claude’s confession;
- Claude had a distinguishable prehistory involving Cologne, Italy, and her mother;
- the supposed marriage contract has been associated with Jérôme Vignier’s forgeries;
- several claimed recognitions are later embellishments;
- the 1436 deed proves only that Claude used the title;
- Orléans’s payment proves municipal acceptance or expediency, not biological identity;
- the nullification proceedings pursued by Joan’s family treated Joan as executed;
- no continuous chain takes Joan from Rouen to Metz.

### Effect on reputation

The survival theory transformed Claude from a successful impersonator into a central figure in alternative Joan historiography. It ensured continued public interest while making reliable biographical reconstruction more difficult.

## The royal-bastardy theory

### Story

Joan was secretly the daughter of Queen Isabeau of Bavaria and Louis, duke of Orléans, then fostered by Jacques d’Arc and Isabelle Romée. Her royal relatives arranged her escape from Rouen, after which she lived as Claude des Armoises.

### Origin and circulation

Pierre Caze developed a major version in the early nineteenth century. Later survivalist and “bâtardisante” authors combined it with Claude’s story.

### Evidence

No contemporary birth, household, financial, or correspondence record supports the alleged parentage. The theory is a retrospective construction linking political gossip about Isabeau with gaps in Joan’s early biography and Claude’s survival claim.

### Status

**Later theory without documentary support.**

## The hidden or hooded victim at Rouen

Survivalists cite the word *embronchée* in Perceval de Cagny and interpret it as meaning the victim’s face was concealed.

Catherine Guyon notes that:

- Perceval was not present at the execution;
- the term can signify sorrowful or downcast rather than physically hooded;
- other accounts report that the body was exposed so witnesses could verify the condemned woman’s sex and identity;
- the prison physician later confirmed death.

**Status:** Disputed linguistic claim; inadequate evidence for substitution.

## The king’s disguise and secret

### Story

Charles VII hid among courtiers or placed another man in his position. Claude either recognized or failed to recognize him; he then asked for a secret known only to himself and Joan, causing her confession.

### Evidence

The episode rests on later narrative tradition rather than a contemporary protocol.

### Function

It gives the imposture a dramatic ending and restores the king as uniquely able to identify the true Maid.

### Status

**Later reported story with a plausible core but unverified details.**

## The Jaulny portraits and heraldry

Visitors to Jaulny have been shown supposed portraits of Claude and Robert and arms resembling those granted to Joan’s family.

The alleged Claude portrait is probably a nineteenth-century artistic representation. Robert’s ownership or residence at Jaulny is disputed, and recent scholarship states that he did not own it.

**Status:** Local commemorative tradition, not contemporary portraiture.

## The grave at Pulligny

A tradition says Jeanne du Lys, Pucelle of France, was buried in the church at Pulligny. Reports describe a lost tombstone or inscription.

No securely authenticated surviving medieval burial record identifies the grave as Claude’s. The monument’s wording and transmission are entangled with later family traditions.

**Status:** Unverified local tradition.

## Death in 1449

Some modern databases give 1449 as Claude’s death year. The basis is unclear and conflicts with identification of her as the woman pardoned in 1457.

**Status:** Unsupported exact date.

## Two sons and descendants

The two sons are supported by the Paris chronicle’s account of her admission. Their names and descendants are not. Claims of a continuous line from Claude and Robert remain genealogical speculation.

## A conspiratorial Anglo-Burgundian operation

Lecoy de La Marche suggested that some hidden political manipulation might have lain behind the affair, perhaps of English or Burgundian origin. Other writers instead imagine an Armagnac or royal operation.

No administrative correspondence or payment identifies an organizing government. Claude’s patronage network can be explained without positing a centralized intelligence operation.

**Status:** Theory.

## Self-created mythology

Claude’s most consequential act was to embody an already circulating rumor. She combined:

- the name Claude;
- the identity Jeanne;
- the du Lys family name;
- male dress and armor;
- the title Pucelle of France;
- prophetic-political claims;
- recognition performances;
- relationships with Joan’s brothers and former political milieu.

Whether she initiated every part of the construction or was coached by patrons is unknown.

---

## Health, Injuries, and Psychological Questions

No reliable source records chronic illness, addiction, disability, or cause of death.

Nider and later hostile writers portray her as drinking heavily in taverns, dancing with men, boasting, and behaving violently. These descriptions served the moral contrast between the chaste Joan and a supposedly disordered impostor. They may preserve observation but cannot safely support a diagnosis.

No psychiatric diagnosis can be made. The surviving possibilities include:

- conscious fraud;
- partial self-identification with Joan;
- role-playing encouraged by patrons;
- delusional belief;
- opportunistic use of an existing survival rumor.

The documents do not permit a choice among these for every stage of her career.

---

## Offices, Honors, and Titles

No permanent royal office or honor is securely documented.

Titles used or claimed included:

- Pucelle de France;
- Jeanne du Lys;
- Dame des Armoises;
- wife of Robert, sire de Tichémont;
- possible head or symbolic head of an armed company.

The Orléans payment was a civic reward, not a hereditary title.

## Works by or Attributed to Claude des Armoises

Claude left no securely identified book, memoir, manifesto, speech text, diary, artwork, or technical work.

### Letters to Orléans, 1436–1439

**Status:** Documented as communications in municipal accounts; texts not securely preserved.

Messengers carried news or letters attributed to the returning Pucelle. They were probably drafted by clerks. Personal composition by Claude cannot be established.

### Petition to Charles VII, September 1439

**Status:** Documented in account material as a petition sent in her name; text apparently lost or unidentified.

### Alleged correspondence with Charles VII

**Status:** Later claim or exaggeration.

Evidence supports at least one petitionary communication, not an established four-year private correspondence.

### Alleged letter to Henry IV of Castile

**Status:** Disputed attribution in a later Castilian narrative.

The story credits the Pucelle with soliciting naval assistance. The chronology and identity of the sender are unresolved, and the cited “Chronicle of the Maid” has not been found.

### Public confession at Paris, 1440

**Status:** Reported speech, not an authored text.

The *Journal d’un bourgeois de Paris* paraphrases statements concerning her marriage, children, mother, pilgrimage, and imposture. No verbatim transcript survives.

### Alleged memoirs

Some catalogues or derivative pages classify her as a memoirist. No authentic memoir by Claude is known. This classification should not be used as evidence of authorship.

## Primary and Near-Contemporary Sources

### *Chronique dite du doyen de Saint-Thiébault de Metz*

Usually cited from BnF, nouvelles acquisitions françaises 6699, folios 75v–76, and printed by Jules Quicherat.

Important for:

- appearance on 20 May 1436;
- name Claude;
- recognition by Pierre and Jean du Lys;
- travel to Arlon and Cologne;
- marriage and Metz residence.

Textual caution is required because of its copying and publication history.

### Jean Praillon, *Chronique de Metz*

Bibliothèque municipale d’Épinal, manuscript 131. Provides part of the Metz narrative tradition.

### Jacomin Husson, *Chronique de Metz (1200–1525)*

Edited by Henri Michelant, Metz, 1870. Reports Claude’s appearance, recognition, and travels.

### Philippe de Vigneulles, *Chronique*

Edited by Charles Bruneau, 1929, volume II. A later Metz narrative preserving regional memory of the claimant.

### Johannes Nider, *Formicarius*, book V, chapter VIII

Written during the later 1430s and printed in the fifteenth century. Central source for the Cologne investigation, male dress, military conduct, prophetic claims, and alleged magic.

### Cologne, Stadtarchiv, *Geleitsregister* 6, folio 156v

Safe-conduct register independently attesting Claude’s presence in Cologne.

### 7 November 1436 property deed

Instrument naming Robert des Armoises and “Jehanne du Lys, la Pucelle de France.” It is distinct from the alleged Vignier marriage contract.

### Orléans municipal accounts

Archives départementales du Loiret, CC 654 and CC 655. Document correspondence, hospitality, food, wine, and the 210-livre gift.

### *Journal d’un bourgeois de Paris*, entry for 1440

Contemporary narrative of exposure, sermon, confession, marriage, children, earlier violence, and later return to war.

### Charles VII’s June 1441 remission for Jean de Siquenville

Documents the armed company previously associated with “Jehanne, who called herself the Maid,” and its connection with Gilles de Rais.

### René of Anjou’s February 1457 remission

Concerns Jeanne de Sermaises, imprisoned for long impersonating the Pucelle. Its relevance depends on whether she and Claude were the same woman.

### Jules Quicherat, editor, *Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d’Arc dite la Pucelle*, volume V, 1849

The foundational printed collection for most documentary references concerning Claude, especially pages 321–336.

## Death, Burial, and Final Status

Claude’s death date, place, and cause are unknown.

Possible endpoints are:

- **After 1440:** the last period in which her identity in the Paris account is secure.
- **After 1456 or 1457:** if she was Jeanne de Sermaises, she survived imprisonment and received René of Anjou’s conditional pardon.
- **1449:** a frequently repeated date without a clear documentary foundation.
- **Pulligny burial:** local tradition, not verified through an authenticated contemporary tomb or burial register.

No evidence establishes disease, injury, execution, or violent death.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Claude’s historical importance lies less in an independently recoverable private life than in the extraordinary reach of the identity she assumed.

### Fifteenth-century significance

Her success demonstrates:

- the persistence of belief in Joan after 1431;
- the political power of embodied recognition;
- the vulnerability of identity systems based on reputation and personal testimony;
- the ability of an armed woman to move through noble, military, and urban networks;
- the overlap between imposture, prophecy, warfare, patronage, and heresy.

### Early modern transformation

The seventeenth-century publication of material associated with Jérôme and Benjamin Vignier shifted the story from a medieval imposture into a documentary “mystery.” The alleged marriage contract gave later writers what appeared to be formal proof of survival until historians exposed Vignier’s unreliability.

### Nineteenth-century controversy

National interest in Joan intensified after the Revolution and the Franco-Prussian War. Claude became evidence in arguments over whether Joan was:

- a peasant saint;
- a royal agent;
- an illegitimate princess;
- a nationalist heroine;
- a victim of church and state;
- a survivor of a staged execution.

### Twentieth- and twenty-first-century uses

Claude remains prominent in:

- conspiracy literature;
- alternative-history works;
- feminist histories of women warriors;
- histories of imposture;
- television documentary;
- historical comics and fiction;
- local tourism at Jaulny and Pulligny;
- internet debates about Joan’s death.

The scholarly consensus identifies her as a false Joan, while recognizing that the reasons for her success—especially her brothers’ recognition and Orléans’s payment—remain historically significant and not fully explained.

### Joan of Arc

**Evidentiary label: documented identity appropriation; biological identity disputed and rejected by the prevailing documentary reconstruction.**

Claude claimed, or allowed others to claim, that she was Joan of Arc after Joan’s execution in 1431. She used Joan’s titles, the du Lys name, the memory of the Orléans siege, and relationships with Pierre and Jean du Lys. Orléans rewarded her in 1439 for services attributed to Joan. Paris authorities exposed her in 1440, and a contemporary chronicle reports her confession.

This is the defining relationship of Claude’s life, but it was not a documented meeting between two simultaneously living women. No source establishes that Claude met Joan before 1431.

A June 1441 remission for Jean de Siquenville says Gilles de Rais placed Siquenville over men-at-arms previously held by “Jehanne, who called herself the Maid.” The event occurred around 1439, probably in western France. The precise command structure and whether Gilles personally believed or promoted Claude’s claim are unknown.

No evidence connects Claude to Gilles’s murders, sexual crimes, alchemical activity, or necromantic practices.
