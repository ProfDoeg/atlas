# Indra Devi: Research Dossier

**Subject:** Indra Devi (born Eugenia/Evgeniia Vasilievna Peterson; 1899–2002)  
**Prepared:** 31 August 2026  
**Scope:** Documentary reconstruction of her life, work, institutions, writings, networks, controversies, myths, and legacy. Claims are qualified according to the strength and type of surviving evidence.

### Precision concerning the “first” claims

Several different propositions are repeatedly collapsed into “Krishnamacharya’s first Western student”:

- She was an **early Western and female pupil** of T. Krishnamacharya at Mysore, admitted in 1937 or 1938.
- The best-supported formulation is that she was **his first foreign woman pupil**, and very probably the first woman regularly trained in the male Mysore Palace yoga school.
- Some accounts call her the first Western pupil without qualification. This is harder to prove exhaustively because Krishnamacharya’s private and court-connected teaching roster is incomplete.
- “Studied alongside B. K. S. Iyengar and K. Pattabhi Jois” is chronologically plausible and widely repeated, but it does not mean the three followed one identical curriculum or trained together continuously.
- “First Western woman to teach yoga in India” and “first foreigner to teach yoga in India” are promotional formulations. Her 1948 Indian book and teaching are important, but priority across all earlier European and American teachers in colonial India has not been demonstrated.

## Family and Ancestry

### Father: Vasili/Vassili Peterson

Her father is consistently identified as **Vasili Peterson**, of Swedish ancestry, and as a banker or bank director in Riga. Accounts differ on his later fate. One widely repeated version says he served as an officer during the civil-war period and disappeared; another strand of the biography effectively has him vanish from Eugenia’s life after her parents’ early separation. No death certificate, military record, or definitive account of his fate was located.

### Mother: Aleksandra Vasilievna Labunskaia

Her mother, **Aleksandra (Alejandra/Alexandra) Vasilievna**, came from a Russian noble family and worked as a stage actress, especially in operetta, under the name **Labunskaia/Labunskaya**. Russian sources associate her with the Nezlobin Theatre. Some later biographies state that she was only sixteen when Eugenia was born and that the parents’ marriage soon broke down. That exact age and marital chronology were not verified against civil records.

Aleksandra’s theatrical career gave Eugenia access to the late-imperial Russian stage world. The poet and performer Alexander Vertinsky was later said to have been a visitor and to have characterized the young Eugenia as a girl unlike other girls. This belongs to family and theatrical reminiscence rather than securely dated documentary evidence.

### Grandparents, siblings, and extended family

Later biographies say Eugenia spent part of her childhood in a prosperous grandparents’ household because her mother toured or performed. The names and genealogies of those grandparents are not consistently published. No siblings are securely documented. The often-used label “Russian aristocrat” rests chiefly on her mother’s noble origin and the material conditions described in her later recollections; it should not be read as evidence that Eugenia herself possessed a title.

### First fiancé: Viacheslav Tretiakov

Russian institutional photographs identify a youthful fiancé, **Viacheslav Tretiakov**, described as a young officer who disappeared during the civil war. The story is distinct from the later German banker Hermann Bolm. Tretiakov’s service record and fate remain unverified.

### Fiancé and financial sponsor: Hermann Bolm

In Weimar-era Berlin, banker **Hermann Bolm** proposed marriage. Eugenia accepted on the condition that she first visit India. Bolm paid for the trip, expecting India to cure her fascination. On returning after about three months, she ended the engagement and returned his ring. The detailed restaurant scene and dialogue come from her retrospective life narrative; the basic engagement-and-funded-journey story is repeated across independent later accounts but has not been corroborated by Bolm’s papers.

### First husband: Jan Strakaty

In Bombay in 1930 she married **Jan Strakaty**, about forty and connected to the Czechoslovak consulate, variously called commercial attaché, consular employee, or diplomat. He was prominent in expatriate social life and was described as president or chairman of a bachelors’ club before marriage. His diplomatic position supported the couple’s comfortable Bombay and later Shanghai existence.

The marriage lasted until his sudden death in **1946**. Accounts say he died while traveling on business in Europe; the precise place and medical cause were not located. There is no documented divorce or separation. No children are recorded.

### Second husband: Sigfrid/Siegfried Knauer

In **1953** Devi married **Dr. Sigfrid (Siegfried) Knauer**, a German-born physician and humanist frequently described as an anthroposophical doctor. He became her collaborator and financial supporter. In 1961 he purchased the large Rancho Cuchumá property near Tecate, Mexico, for her work. Later sources describe a serious automobile accident and prolonged illness. The couple moved to India in the late 1970s; Knauer died in **Sri Lanka in 1984**, reportedly aged about ninety. Some secondary sources incorrectly place his death in 1977, confusing the closure of the Tecate center or onset of illness with the death itself.

No evidence was found that either marriage produced children. The Argentine teachers **Iana and David Lifar** were disciples and institutional heirs, not biological descendants.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1899–1921

### Riga and imperial Russia

Eugenia was born into a prosperous, culturally connected milieu in Riga. She was baptized Orthodox but later built a religious identity that combined Theosophical, Hindu, yogic, devotional, and universalist elements without formally repudiating Christianity. The retention of her cross is significant in that respect, though the story comes through later remembrance.

The family moved to **St. Petersburg/Petrograd** during the First World War. Eugenia attended a gymnasium and reportedly graduated with a **gold medal in 1917**. The claim appears in Russian biography and her later institutional tradition; a school transcript has not been published.

At roughly fifteen, she encountered **Yogi Ramacharaka’s _Fourteen Lessons in Yogi Philosophy and Oriental Occultism_**, the pseudonymous work of the American New Thought writer William Walker Atkinson, and also read Rabindranath Tagore. These books were not traditional Indian instruction manuals in a simple sense: Atkinson’s work was a Western occult/New Thought construction presented under an Indian persona. It nevertheless supplied the adolescent Eugenia with the vocabulary of yoga, India, occultism, and personal transformation that organized much of her later life.

### Dramatic study

After school she studied acting in Moscow, commonly said to have been at **Fyodor Komissarzhevsky’s theatrical school or studio**. Later hagiographic accounts say Komissarzhevsky predicted a major career for her. She also lived amid her mother’s theatrical network. Exact enrollment dates, productions, and formal qualifications remain uncertain.

### Revolution, civil war, and flight

The revolutions of 1917 and civil war destroyed the family’s secure social and financial world. Her father and fiancé disappear from the record in competing accounts. Eugenia and her mother fled or returned first to **Latvia**, then went to **Poland in December 1920**, and by **1921** reached **Berlin**. They lost most of their wealth. Unlike later spiritualized accounts of effortless detachment, this migration was a material dispossession and a refugee experience.

No imprisonment, criminal charge, or direct political office is documented. She and her mother were anti-Bolshevik refugees by circumstance and class position, but Eugenia did not develop a recorded party-political program.

## Education and Formation

### Theatre and dance

Her formal and practical education combined Russian dramatic training with touring performance. In Berlin she joined **Iakov/Ivan Yuzhny’s Russian cabaret-theatre _Der Blaue Vogel_ (“The Blue Bird”)**, a celebrated émigré company, and toured European capitals as actress and dancer. Russian reporting says Max Reinhardt noticed her and considered recruiting her. This detail has not been matched to a Reinhardt document.

The Blue Bird’s stylized, cosmopolitan “Russian” performance and the need to reinvent identity for foreign audiences prefigure Devi’s later presentation of a visually legible India through saris, selected postures, lectures, celebrity endorsements, and modern media.

In India she studied or performed “temple dance.” She named **Enakshi Rama Rau** as a teacher in later recollections; the story that Rau dismissed her after only a few lessons because she somehow already knew the art is a reincarnation-flavored legend, not an independently verifiable training record.

### Theosophy and Jiddu Krishnamurti

In **1926**, after seeing Theosophical literature or a notice in Tallinn, Eugenia traveled to the **Order of the Star/Theosophical camp at Ommen, Netherlands**, associated with Annie Besant and Jiddu Krishnamurti. Thousands attended. She later said that hearing Krishnamurti chant Sanskrit hymns around a fire felt like a “forgotten call” and overturned her life.

This was an important conversion experience, though not a conversion to a single religion. Theosophy supplied a transnational network, a spiritualized image of India, and contacts at Adyar. Krishnamurti later dissolved the Order of the Star in 1929 and rejected the messianic role constructed for him; Devi retained friendship and admiration but did not reproduce his anti-guru position consistently.

### Indian teachers before Krishnamacharya

Her broader formation included:

- **Indian dance teachers** in Bombay/Madras circles.
- **Swami Kuvalayananda**, founder of Kaivalyadhama and pioneer of physiological research on yoga. Modern yoga scholarship and the academic review of Michelle Goldberg’s biography place Devi among his students or visitors, but the duration and precise curriculum are unclear.
- A Nepalese prince or aristocrat, often called **Prince Mussoorie**, who showed her asanas. The name/title is unstable in secondary accounts.
- The Theosophical Society at **Adyar**, which brought her into the orbit of Indian nationalists, artists, and spiritual seekers.

### T. Krishnamacharya at Mysore

After years of ill health described as cardiac weakness, weight gain, gray complexion, fainting, crying spells, and chronic pain, Devi sought instruction from **Tirumalai Krishnamacharya** at the Mysore Palace yoga school. A Prague medical student named **Ripka** is said to have suggested that, because she believed yogic exertion had contributed to the illness, she should consult yogis about treating it. This causal story is autobiographical and medically unverified.

Krishnamacharya initially refused her because she was a foreign woman and his school trained young Indian males, particularly court-connected boys. The **Maharaja of Mysore** intervened or requested her admission. Sources differ between **1937 and 1938**. Once admitted, she followed an austere regimen: early rising, no heat from a stove or fire, sleep before 9 p.m., and a restrictive vegetarian diet excluding meat, refined sugar and flour, canned food, and even some root vegetables. The Maharani reportedly supplied a personal cook.

Devi later reported that her symptoms resolved and that Krishnamacharya moved her into private instruction in pranayama and teaching methods. When her husband was transferred to Shanghai, he instructed her to teach. The miraculous language attached to this period—Krishnamacharya stopping his heart or switching electricity at a distance, Devi curing another person and absorbing his illness—is not medically or experimentally documented. Her training itself is well established.

## Early Career: Berlin, India, and Film, 1921–1930

### Weimar Berlin performer

Between roughly 1921 and 1927, Eugenia earned her living in émigré theatre and cabaret. This was not yet a yoga career. She performed Russian identity to European audiences, traveled widely, and cultivated the poise and social fluency later useful in diplomatic, Hollywood, and elite wellness circles.

### First Indian journey, 1927–1928

She departed for India on **17 November 1927**, financed by Hermann Bolm. She traveled from south to north, adopted the sari and Indian dining customs, and returned about three months later. She then broke the engagement, sold jewelry and furs, and returned to India intending to remain.

The phrase “there is my home,” the station and restaurant scenes, and the claim that Indians quickly accepted her as one of their own belong to her retrospective self-narration. The underlying movements are credible and consistent.

### Adyar, Nehru, and Indian nationalist society

At Adyar she performed Indian dance and met **Jawaharlal Nehru**. She later called their connection an **“amorous friendship”** or “friendship in love.” Surviving public material does not establish a sexual affair. She also claimed acquaintance with **Mahatma Gandhi, Rabindranath Tagore, the Roerich family**, and other independence-era figures. Some contacts are highly plausible within her consular and Theosophical networks; the intensity of each friendship varies in the evidence and is often amplified by institutional biography.

She later stressed that, as a white diplomatic hostess, she ignored colonial social rules by receiving Indians of different ranks and castes. The statement comes from her memoir but is consistent with her nationalist and Theosophical network.

### Indian silent cinema and the name “Indra Devi”

Director **Bhagwati Prasad Mishra** cast her in **_Sher-e-Arab_ (The Arabian Knight)**, released in 1930. Film databases list her as “Indira Davi/Devi” and “Miss Eugene Peterson.” The production is sometimes said to have starred Prithviraj Kapoor, but surviving database records are inconsistent about the full cast.

An IMDb identity called Indira Devi is also credited with **_Kapalkundala_ (1929)** and **_Ganesh Janma_ (1930)**. It is not certain that every credit belongs to Peterson rather than another actress using the common name Indira Devi. _Sher-e-Arab_ is the safest attribution. Claims that its premiere made her an overnight national film star are partly promotional; it did establish her Indian persona and public name.

Devi said Mishra gave her a list of possible names and she selected “Indra Devi” almost at random. Popular retellings mistranslate it as a single expression meaning “heavenly goddess.” **Indra** is the Vedic divine king; **Devi** means goddess or revered lady. The name created an immediately Indian and numinous screen identity without proving religious initiation.

## Chronological Life History

## Bombay Society and the Turn to Yoga, 1930–1938

Marriage to Jan Strakaty placed Devi in Bombay’s diplomatic and colonial society: receptions, races, balls, salons, travel, and contact with Indian elites. She later portrayed this as comfortable but spiritually empty. The couple also traveled in Europe, including Prague.

Her unexplained illness became the narrative hinge from socialite to yogini. Medical doctors reportedly diagnosed heart weakness but did not cure it. There are no surviving clinical files. The reported four-year duration, fainting, weight change, crying spells, and eventual remission admit many possible physical and psychological explanations. Devi understood the recovery through yoga, diet, discipline, and spiritual reorientation.

By 1937–38 she was studying in Mysore. Krishnamacharya’s approach to her appears to have been therapeutic and individualized, gentler than the strenuous sequences associated with male students. That adaptation later became central to her own method.

## Shanghai, War, and the First Book, 1939–1947

### Move to China

Strakaty was transferred to **Shanghai**, and the couple arrived around 1939. Devi began teaching expatriates and Chinese students. The recurrent claim that she opened “the first yoga school in China” is best narrowed to **one of the first documented modern-postural-yoga schools in China**, and a decisive Western-mediated reintroduction of yoga there. Chinese traditions had long known Indian Buddhist and bodily practices; absolute firstness is untenable without qualification.

### Soong Mei-ling and elite patronage

Her classes were held in, or patronized through, the house of **Soong Mei-ling (Madame Chiang Kai-shek)**. Accounts describe Soong as a yoga enthusiast. Devi taught a slow sequence beginning with prolonged relaxation, then bridge, shoulderstand, cobra or gentle backbends, lotus-related positions, and supported headstand. American and Russian expatriates formed an important part of the clientele. She also reported free teaching in orphanages and shelters.

Shanghai during the Sino-Japanese War and Japanese occupation was politically fragmented, violent, and unstable. As the wife of a Czechoslovak consular official, Devi occupied a protected but precarious expatriate world. Available sources do not connect her to espionage or an intelligence service. Her diplomatic access and later proximity to political figures are not themselves evidence of intelligence work.

### First book

During a 1946 return to India, she completed **_Yoga: The Technique of Health and Happiness_**. The first reliably cataloged edition is Allahabad: Kitabistan, **1948**, under “Eugenie Strakaty (Indira/Indra Devi).” Some later bibliographies give 1946, probably the completion date or a putative earlier printing. The book is significant as an early English-language manual by a woman adapting hatha yoga to lay health.

### Widowhood and departure

Strakaty died unexpectedly in 1946 while away in Europe. Devi was in India and returned to Shanghai to dispose of property. The civil war threatened or resulted in military requisition of her house. She sold possessions and secured an American visa. Late in **1947** she sailed to San Francisco, reportedly aboard the troop transport **USS _General W. H. Gordon_**. The romantic version says she went to the port and took the first available ship; documentation supports the voyage and destination more strongly than that spontaneous-ticket scene.

## Hollywood and the Americanization of Yoga, 1947–1961

### Entry into Los Angeles networks

Devi arrived with limited money but strong elite social skills. Hostess **Bernardine Fritz** helped introduce her into spiritually receptive Hollywood circles. She met **Aldous and Maria Huxley**, renewed contact with **Krishnamurti**, and connected with health promoter **Paul Bragg**. Her earlier book quoted Huxley’s account of nonattachment; she visited the Huxleys and discussed diet and pesticide concerns.

### Sunset Boulevard studio

In **1948** she opened a studio at **8806 Sunset Boulevard**, usually described as Los Angeles’s or Hollywood’s first dedicated yoga studio. “First U.S. yoga studio,” sometimes claimed in obituaries, is false: yoga and yogic exercise had been taught in the United States for decades. Her studio was nevertheless pioneering in sustained, public, celebrity-associated postural yoga.

She usually wore a sari and taught a deliberately accessible system. Rather than foregrounding Sanskrit metaphysics, celibacy, renunciation, or demanding athleticism, she offered relaxation, breathing, a limited repertoire of slowly performed postures, diet, concentration, beauty, youthfulness, and relief from modern stress. She taught factory workers and shop assistants as well as private celebrity clients.

### Elizabeth Arden and the beauty industry

**Elizabeth Arden** became supporter, client, or business ally and incorporated Devi-related yoga elements into elite spa and beauty programs. This alliance placed yoga inside a commercial female wellness market. It helped associate yoga with youth, slenderness, beauty treatment, organic or health food, and luxury self-care. Exact contract terms and royalties have not been located.

### Celebrity students and contacts

Students or close participants reliably associated with Devi include:

- **Gloria Swanson**, a close friend and prominent endorser.
- **Greta Garbo**.
- **Eva Gabor**.
- **Jennifer Jones**.
- **Robert Ryan**.
- **Ramon Novarro**.
- **Yul Brynner**.
- **Yehudi Menuhin**, who wrote or supplied a foreword for an edition and later played a separate role in bringing B. K. S. Iyengar to Western attention.
- Conductor **Serge Koussevitzky** and dancer **Ruth St. Denis** are named in period marketing or later accounts.

The extent of instruction varied: “student” may mean private pupil, occasional attendee, photographed participant, friend, or endorser. Celebrity lists became a marketing instrument.

**Marilyn Monroe is not established as her student.** Monroe owned _Forever Young, Forever Healthy_ and was photographed in positions resembling yoga or gymnastics. A widely circulated image said to show Devi teaching Monroe actually depicts **Eva Gabor**. No meeting record or lesson evidence has been found.

### Books, records, and the mass market

_Forever Young, Forever Healthy_ (Prentice-Hall, **1953**) and _Yoga for Americans_ (Prentice-Hall, **1959**) were commercially successful and widely translated. The latter presented a six-week home course. Her 1963 _Renew Your Life Through Yoga_ made rhythmic breathing and relaxation central. Records such as _Yoga for Americans_ and _Indra Devi Presents Concentration & Meditation_ extended her method into home audio.

She framed yoga as a method of physical, mental, and spiritual development rather than a sectarian religion. This secularizing language made it saleable in Cold War America while leaving room for generalized spirituality. Critics in modern yoga studies observe that the program converted a tradition concerned with liberation and ego-transcendence into a therapeutic technology for strengthening the modern self. That is a historical interpretation, not evidence of fraud.

### Second marriage and citizenship

She married Dr. Sigfrid Knauer in 1953. In the mid-1950s she obtained U.S. citizenship and formally adopted **Indra Devi** as her legal name. Her business structures appear to have remained a mixture of studio income, book and recording revenue, patronage, and her husband’s resources. No complete tax, corporate, or royalty archive was located.

### 1960 Soviet visit

In 1960 Devi returned to the Soviet Union via Finland with an Indian associate, **Balu Krishna**. She visited Leningrad and Moscow. At a reception given by Indian ambassador **K. P. S. Menon** at the Hotel Sovetskaya, she met senior Soviet officials reported as **Andrei Gromyko, Aleksei Kosygin, and Anastas Mikoyan** and explained that yoga was a method, not a religion or magic.

The toast attributed to Gromyko—“to Indra Devi, who opened our eyes to yoga”—and the newspaper label “the woman who put the Kremlin on its head” derive from later retellings. The meeting itself is plausible and widely reported; the claim that she thereby **legalized yoga in the USSR** is false or greatly exaggerated. She left disappointed that no public teaching movement immediately followed. Soviet yoga remained restricted, medically reframed, informally circulated, and institutionally inconsistent until perestroika.

## Mexico, Global Teaching, and Sai Baba, 1961–1977

### Rancho Cuchumá, Tecate

In **1961** Knauer purchased a large estate—often described as a **24-room ranch**—near **Tecate, Baja California, Mexico**, at the foot of Mount Cuchumá. Devi established an international yoga teacher-training center there. It is variously called the Indra Devi Foundation, Rancho Cuchumá, and an international training center. The ranch gave her a residential base close to California while allowing retreats and longer teacher courses.

No reliable purchase price, operating budget, ownership deed, enrollment ledger, or eventual sale record was found. The property demonstrates substantial household resources, largely attributed to Knauer. Her books and international lectures also generated income, but no responsible net-worth estimate is possible.

### Sathya Sai Baba and “Sai Yoga”

In **1966** Devi met **Sathya Sai Baba** in India and became a committed devotee. She traveled repeatedly to Bangalore and Puttaparthi, publicized his teachings, described alleged miracles, and renamed or reframed her synthesis as **“Sai Yoga.”** The system combined Krishnamacharya-derived postures and breathing with bhakti, meditation, prayer, service, and Sai Baba’s language of love.

Her 1975 book **_Sai Baba and Sai Yoga_** is direct evidence of the relationship. A 1971 article, later republished as “SwaMi and Me,” records her devotional testimony. She helped introduce Western seekers and elites to Sai Baba.

The devotion marked a change from the more secular, nonsectarian marketing of her Hollywood period, though she continued to say that she belonged to no religion and that religion was between God and herself. She could present Sai devotion explicitly in devotional settings and minimize it for general audiences.

### Closure

The Tecate operation closed in **1977**. Devi and her seriously ill husband moved to **Bangalore**. Some sources say his automobile accident precipitated the closure; others emphasize Devi’s wish to be near Sai Baba. Knauer did not die until 1984.

## India, Sri Lanka, Latin America, and Panama, 1977–1985

Devi cared for or arranged care for Knauer while remaining active in Sai Baba’s orbit. In 1983–84 they traveled to **Sri Lanka**, where Knauer died in 1984. Devi briefly considered India her final home, but at more than eighty she began another institutional phase.

She first visited **Argentina in 1982**, delivered a seminar and appeared on television. Institutional accounts say a single appearance produced extraordinary demand. She also lectured in Punta del Este and elsewhere in Latin America.

### Roberto Díaz Herrera and Panama

This is a documented but often omitted political connection. In Buenos Aires in **1986**—or during contacts beginning slightly earlier—Argentine singer **Piero** introduced Devi to **Colonel Roberto Díaz Herrera**, then the number-two officer in Panama’s Defense Forces under Manuel Noriega. Devi impressed him with teachings about Sai Baba and demonstrations resembling applied kinesiology: an apple supposedly strengthened his arm while a cigarette and white bread weakened it. She also introduced him to California psychic **Shama Calhoum**.

Díaz Herrera invited Devi and Calhoum to Panama, reportedly at Defense Forces expense. The psychic told him he had a major mission. He later broke with Noriega and made disclosures that contributed to the regime crisis preceding the 1989 U.S. invasion. Popular accounts therefore call Devi his “spiritual adviser” and place her name in the chain of events that destabilized Noriega.

The documented connection does **not** establish that Devi planned the political rupture, worked for the CIA, knew of covert operations, or directed Díaz Herrera’s accusations. Michelle Goldberg later filed a Freedom of Information request with the CIA for records concerning Devi and Díaz Herrera; the CIA’s public reading room preserves the request itself, not proof of an intelligence relationship. No evidence located in this research ties Devi to an intelligence service.

## Argentina and Late International Career, 1985–2002

### Settlement in Buenos Aires

In **1985** Devi settled permanently in Buenos Aires, calling Argentina the country of her love, alongside Russia as her birth country and India as her spiritual country. She rebuilt a teaching organization through lectures, studios, teacher formation, books, television, and tours in Argentina, Uruguay, Brazil, Paraguay, Chile, Mexico, Spain, Germany, and elsewhere.

Early organizer **Francesca Baldi** assisted her. By 1987 **David Lifar**, husband of her student **Iana Lifar**, assumed major administrative responsibility. Devi reportedly disliked organizational detail and relied heavily on collaborators.

### Foundation and yoga institutions

The **Fundación Indra Devi—Yoga, Arte y Ciencia de Vida** was created in **1988** according to its own later history, though some institutional sources use 1987 for its establishment or precursor. It trained teachers, sponsored meetings, issued the periodical _Yoga, Arte y Ciencia de Vida_, organized national and international congresses, and published or republished her Spanish-language works. Iana and David Lifar directed it after her death until 2020, then continued through Indra Devi International.

In **1987**, at Montevideo, she was named honorary president of the **International Federation of Yoga Teachers/Instructors** (later identified with the International Yoga Federation) and of a Latin American yoga confederation or union under Swami Maitreyananda. Organizational names changed and are rendered inconsistently by later institutional sources.

An Argentine academic genealogy of yoga identifies the Foundation, the Argentine Association of Yoga Teachers, and the first Argentine Yoga Congress presided over by Devi as important in consolidating distinct modern-yoga lineages in Argentina.

### Return to the Soviet Union and Russia

In **May 1990** Devi returned to the USSR, visiting Riga, Leningrad, and Moscow and celebrating her ninety-first birthday among admirers. She appeared on Vladimir Molchanov’s widely watched program **_Do i posle polunochi_ (“Before and After Midnight”)**, seated in lotus position. The visit followed the 1989 all-Union conference on yoga and the creation of a yoga association.

Russian sources report further visits in **1992**, at the invitation of the Russian Olympic Committee, and **1994**, at the invitation of the Argentine ambassador. The precise sponsors and programs warrant archival confirmation.

### United Nations and Argentine recognition

Her foundation records a specially invited talk at **United Nations headquarters in June 1992**. No UN program or transcript was located during this research, so the event should be attributed to institutional testimony rather than treated as independently verified.

On **8 March 1999**, the Argentine National Congress honored her in connection with International Women’s Day for her work promoting love, light, and peace. The foundation is the principal online source for the wording. More than **3,000 people** reportedly attended her hundredth-birthday celebration in Buenos Aires in May 1999.

### Late teaching and publications

Devi continued teaching, lecturing, traveling, and appearing on Argentine television into her late nineties. Reports say that at ninety-nine she could still demonstrate demanding postures. Spanish works included _Respirar bien para vivir mejor_ (1995), _Palabras desde el corazón_ (1996), Sai-related books in 1999, and the memoir _Una mujer de tres siglos_ (2000). These late books were produced with substantial editorial and organizational assistance; David and Iana Lifar are specifically credited as collaborators/compilers for the breathing book and institutional series.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Political orientation

Devi was not a systematic political thinker. Her biography passed through the Russian Revolution, Indian anti-colonial nationalism, Japanese-occupied Shanghai, Cold War Hollywood, the USSR, Sai Baba’s global movement, Noriega-era Panama, and democratic Argentina. She preferred personal influence, elite networks, and universalist language to party organization.

Her friendships with Nehru and other nationalist figures, and her refusal to observe colonial social segregation in Bombay, aligned her socially with Indian independence circles. There is no evidence that she joined the Indian National Congress, participated in underground action, or held political office.

Her Soviet visit was cultural diplomacy, not communist affiliation. Her contact with Díaz Herrera was spiritual and personal, with indirect political consequences; no evidence supports intelligence-agent claims.

### Religious and philosophical sequence

1. **Russian Orthodoxy:** baptism and retained cross.
2. **Western occult/New Thought yoga:** Ramacharaka/Atkinson and Tagore in adolescence.
3. **Theosophy and Krishnamurti:** universal religion, reincarnation-inflected Indianism, Ommen and Adyar networks.
4. **Modern therapeutic hatha yoga:** Kuvalayananda and Krishnamacharya; bodily discipline, health, breath, and scientific legitimation.
5. **Hollywood universalism:** yoga as nonsectarian self-development, relaxation, beauty, and anti-stress method.
6. **Sai devotion after 1966:** bhakti, miracles, prayer, love/service, and “Sai Yoga.”
7. **Late universalism:** continued Sai allegiance combined with the declaration that she belonged to no religion and with language accessible to Catholic and secular Latin American audiences.

### Artistic and pedagogical method

Her method emphasized a small group of asanas, relaxation (often beginning in shavasana), rhythmic breathing, concentration, diet, positive thought, and gradual progress. It was noncompetitive and suitable for women, older people, office workers, and beginners. Her theatrical training influenced the visual authority of her sari-clad persona, vocal delivery, staged demonstrations, radio records, and television success.

Her great institutional innovation was not a new posture or technical patent. It was the **social translation of yoga**: from male court school and ascetic imagery into an intimate, feminized, medically and cosmetically legible practice for modern householders, then into a teacher-training and mass-media network.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, and Financial Questions

### No known personal criminal case

No evidence was located that Devi was arrested, indicted, convicted, sued for fraud, investigated for money laundering, or personally accused of sexual or violent abuse. No known criminal activity is attributable to her on the consulted record.

### Health and efficacy claims

Her books and publicity associated yoga with curing or improving heart trouble, stress, fatigue, weight, complexion, aging, and emotional distress. Her own cardiac recovery is autobiographical and unsupported by clinical documentation. Applied-kinesiology-style demonstrations with food and cigarettes in Panama are not controlled scientific evidence. These claims belong to wellness marketing and testimonial culture; calling them proven medical cures would exceed the evidence.

### Celebrity marketing and inflated priority claims

Her organization and later admirers repeatedly enlarged claims:

- “First U.S. yoga studio” instead of first or among the first in Los Angeles/Hollywood.
- “Legalized yoga in the USSR” based on one elite reception.
- “First yoga school in China” without acknowledging earlier Indian/Chinese bodily and religious transmissions.
- Marilyn Monroe as a direct student.
- Universal “first Western student” language where “first foreign woman student” is safer.

These exaggerations enhanced reputation but do not erase the underlying pioneering work.

### Sathya Sai Baba affiliation

Devi was an active propagator for Sathya Sai Baba and published claims of his miracles. Sai Baba was challenged by Indian rationalists over alleged sleight of hand and later faced allegations from former male devotees of sexual abuse. Danish and BBC documentaries publicized those allegations; no criminal conviction or court finding established them during his life. Devi herself was not accused of participating in abuse.

The chronology matters. Skepticism about materializations existed by the 1960s–70s, during her devotion. The most widely publicized sexual-abuse allegations emerged in the 1990s and early 2000s, near the end of her life. No evidence located here shows what she knew, whether she investigated, or whether she privately doubted any claim. Her continued devotional promotion made her part of the movement’s international legitimation, but it is not evidence of complicity in alleged crimes.

### Panama and military financing

Díaz Herrera reportedly brought Devi and a psychic to Panama at Panamanian Defense Forces expense. That is a political-financial fact reported in histories of the Noriega crisis, not an accusation that Devi embezzled funds. The amount, authorization mechanism, and accounting are unknown. Her teachings influenced an officer who later became a major regime dissident, but there is no evidence she was tasked by a government or intelligence service.

### Biographical reliability and self-mythologizing

The principal modern biography, Michelle Goldberg’s _The Goddess Pose_, conducted extensive archival and interview research but has been criticized in an academic review for inconsistent citations and for simplifying modern-yoga history. Devi’s own _Una mujer de tres siglos_ is indispensable testimony but was produced at age one hundred with institutional collaborators and participates in decades of self-fashioning. Specific dialogue, miracle scenes, priority claims, and famous-friend lists require corroboration.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. “She remembered India from a previous life”

**Story:** India felt like home; she “remembered” customs and dance rather than learned them. Enakshi Rama Rau supposedly stopped lessons because Devi already knew the dance.  
**Appearance/circulation:** Devi’s recollections, Russian profiles, foundation narratives.  
**Evidence:** Subjective déjà vu and a teacher anecdote; no independent evidence of reincarnation or instant mastery.  
**Reputational effect:** Cast her adoption of Indian identity as destiny rather than cosmopolitan performance or appropriation.

### 2. Krishnamacharya’s miracles and the foreign woman he could not defeat

**Story:** The guru could stop his heart and switch electricity at a distance, but could not rid himself of the persistent Devi.  
**Appearance/circulation:** Devi’s recollections and popular biographies.  
**Evidence:** Krishnamacharya gave physiological demonstrations, but supernatural control is unverified. Her admission through court patronage and persistence is credible.  
**Effect:** Creates a comic hagiographic contest in which the male miracle-worker recognizes the destined female transmitter.

### 3. Devi absorbed another man’s heart illness

**Story:** She concentrated on curing a guest’s cardiac attack; he recovered and she developed years of heart symptoms.  
**Appearance/circulation:** Her memoir tradition and Russian profile.  
**Evidence:** No medical records establish the guest’s diagnosis, causal transfer, or Devi’s diagnosis.  
**Effect:** Makes her illness both occult sacrifice and providential path to Krishnamacharya.

### 4. Overnight Indian film stardom

**Story:** _Sher-e-Arab_ made her a star in one day.  
**Appearance/circulation:** Foundation and Russian-language biography.  
**Evidence:** A real 1930 screen credit exists. The scale of fame is hard to measure, and credits for other films may conflate actresses.  
**Effect:** Enhances the sequence actress–diplomat’s wife–yogini and proves her charisma before yoga.

### 5. “First Lady of Yoga”

**Story:** Devi uniquely brought yoga to the West.  
**Appearance/circulation:** Press, obituaries, her foundation, the title/marketing of Goldberg’s biography.  
**Evidence:** Western yoga preceded her, including Vivekananda, Yogendra, Yogananda, Pierre Bernard, and others. Devi’s specific role in feminized, therapeutic, celebrity and mass-market postural yoga is exceptionally important.  
**Effect:** A durable shorthand that both recognizes her and obscures predecessors, Indian women, and parallel networks.

### 6. Marilyn Monroe was her student

**Story:** Devi personally taught Monroe; a photograph supposedly shows them together.  
**Appearance/circulation:** Yoga publicity, social media, government tourism promotion, and later profiles.  
**Evidence against:** The famous lesson photograph is of Eva Gabor. Monroe owned Devi’s book and posed in yoga-like positions, but no direct lesson or meeting is documented.  
**Effect:** Retroactively links the world’s most recognizable beauty icon to Devi’s yoga-beauty synthesis.

### 7. She legalized yoga in the Soviet Union

**Story:** After she explained yoga to Gromyko, Kosygin, and Mikoyan in 1960, Soviet authorities permitted it.  
**Appearance/circulation:** Western and Russian profiles, yoga websites, German summary.  
**Evidence:** The reception and conversation are repeatedly attested; a legalization decree or policy change caused by her has not been found. Her own account says she left disappointed.  
**Effect:** Produces the vivid headline that one sari-clad woman “turned the Kremlin upside down.”

### 8. Sai Baba’s materializations and interventions

**Story:** Devi witnessed ash, objects, or divine interventions produced by Sai Baba and received direct spiritual guidance.  
**Appearance/circulation:** Her 1971 testimony, _Sai Baba and Sai Yoga_, Sai movement publications.  
**Evidence:** Devotional witness accounts exist; rationalists argued the acts were sleight of hand and Sai Baba declined controlled tests.  
**Effect:** Shifted Devi from secular health teacher toward charismatic bhakti and helped transmit Sai Baba’s cult internationally.

### 9. The yogini who caused Noriega’s fall

**Story:** Devi spiritually awakened Roberto Díaz Herrera, whose revolt helped bring down Noriega.  
**Appearance/circulation:** Panama political accounts, Goldberg’s biography and publicity.  
**Evidence:** Meetings, invitations, Sai teachings, and introduction to Shama Calhoum are documented in secondary political narratives. Causation is interpretive; Díaz Herrera had military, personal, and political motives.  
**Effect:** Inserts Devi into histories of the 1989 Panama crisis and generates intelligence speculation unsupported by evidence.

### 10. Longevity as proof of yoga

**Story:** Living to 102 and demonstrating postures near one hundred proved the efficacy of her method.  
**Appearance/circulation:** Obituaries, foundation promotion, book marketing.  
**Evidence:** Her longevity and late activity are facts; one life cannot establish causation.  
**Effect:** Her body became the final advertisement for “forever young, forever healthy.”

## Works by Indra Devi

Bibliography is complicated by translated retitlings, revised editions, later compilations, and library records that treat reprints as first editions. The list below distinguishes original/core works from translations and posthumous releases where possible.

### Books and major printed works

1. **_Yoga: The Technique of Health and Happiness_**. Allahabad: Kitabistan, **1948**. Credited in rare-book records to Eugenie Strakaty (Indira/Indra Devi). Often dated 1946 in later bibliographies, likely reflecting composition in India that year. Early English manual; genuine authorship, though normal editorial help is possible.
2. **_Forever Young, Forever Healthy: Simplified Yoga for Modern Living_**. New York: Prentice-Hall, **1953**, 174 pp. First British edition, Blackpool: Thomas, 1955, xi + 169 pp. Frequently reprinted and translated; genuine core authorship.

4. **_Renew Your Life Through Yoga: The Indra Devi Method for Relaxation Through Rhythmic Breathing_**. Englewood Cliffs, NJ: Prentice-Hall, **1963**. Genuine authorship; later audio edition is not a newly authored book.
5. **_Sai Baba and Sai Yoga_**. Delhi: Macmillan Company of India, **1975**, 148 pp. Later editions from Sai publishers. Devotional testimony and yoga synthesis.

10. **_Una mujer de tres siglos_**. Buenos Aires: Sudamericana, **2000**, 275 pp., ISBN 950-07-1726-3 / 978-950-07-1726-7. Autobiographical memoir produced at the end of her life. ## Illness, Injury, Aging, and Death

### Earlier illness

In the 1930s Devi described four years of heart symptoms, fainting, weight and complexion changes, and emotional spells. European and Indian physicians allegedly failed to help. She interpreted recovery under Krishnamacharya as proof of yoga. Because no medical files survive in the accessible record, diagnosis and mechanism cannot be established.

### Later life

She remained unusually active into her late nineties and used her physical condition as evidence for her method. Public reports said she still performed demanding postures at ninety-nine and taught two classes daily in her early nineties. These claims are plausible in broad form but may reflect publicity language and organizational rather than strictly personal teaching schedules.

### Final illness and cause of death

A German-language account says she suffered a **stroke in February 2002**. Russian biography says her health declined and she became unable to travel to India, where she wished to die. Contemporary Los Angeles Times and other obituaries stated that the **cause of death was not disclosed**. Therefore “stroke” is a reported antecedent, not a securely established official cause of death.

She died peacefully in Buenos Aires on **25 April 2002**. There is no indication of addiction, suicide, violent death, or suspicious circumstances.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Yoga history

Devi’s legacy lies less in technical novelty than in successful cultural packaging and transmission. She helped establish that modern postural yoga could be:

- taught by a woman to women and mixed lay audiences;
- therapeutic, gentle, and adaptable rather than acrobatic;
- practiced at home through a six-week course or record;
- marketed through film celebrities and beauty businesses;
- presented as nonsectarian while retaining spiritual depth;
- integrated with stress relief, diet, relaxation, spas, and later New Age culture;
- institutionalized through teacher training in Mexico and Argentina.

The predominantly female Western yoga market, the celebrity-yoga economy, and the association between yoga, wellness food, beauty, and holistic spas all bear her imprint. Later power-yoga and fitness cultures are physically more strenuous than her method but operate within a mass market she helped create.

### Gender and colonial/postcolonial reinterpretation

She broke a real barrier in Krishnamacharya’s male school and became an internationally authoritative female teacher. At the same time, the title “First Lady of Yoga” can reproduce the disappearance of Indian women and earlier teachers from the historical record. Her career also complicates simple “East to West” narratives: an émigré Russian, trained partly through American occult literature and Indian modernizers, reintroduced a hybrid yoga to Shanghai, Hollywood, Mexico, the USSR, and Argentina.

### Russia, India, and Argentina

Russian memory emphasizes the czarist-born refugee who returned with yoga. Indian memory places her among Krishnamacharya’s global disciples and early Western devotees of Sai Baba. Argentina inherited her most durable named foundation, Spanish bibliography, disciples, and mass public image. Her ashes in the Río de la Plata symbolically completed that third national attachment.

### Hagiography and correction

The foundation tradition portrays “Mataji” as a bearer of love, light, and peace and preserves teaching lineages. Critical scholarship emphasizes reinvention, elite access, consumer wellness, dubious medical claims, charismatic-guru affiliation, and selective memory. The documentary record supports both extraordinary agency and extensive self-mythologizing.

### Continuing institutions

The Buenos Aires foundation operated under Iana and David Lifar until 2020; their Indra Devi International virtual school continues the teaching. Books remain in print in Spanish and other languages. Goldberg’s 2015 biography restored Devi to general histories of modern yoga and triggered correction of some myths, especially the Marilyn Monroe photograph and simplistic “ancient unchanged yoga” narratives.
