# Anna Matveyevna Pavlova: Research Dossier

## Scope and Method

This dossier concerns the Russian ballerina commonly known as **Anna Pavlova**, born **Anna Matveyevna Pavlova** in 1881 and known professionally as **Anna Pavlovna Pavlova**.

The reconstruction draws on archival catalogues, performance programs, contemporary and near-contemporary accounts, institutional histories, specialist dance scholarship, memoirs, film records, and later biographical research. Conflicting evidence is retained rather than silently reconciled.

Evidentiary labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a contemporary record, archival object, official institutional record, or multiple independent sources.
- **Reported fact:** stated in a generally reliable secondary account but not independently established here from the underlying record.
- **Claim or allegation:** attributed to a named person or publication without adequate corroboration.
- **Rumor or myth:** a story circulated as part of Pavlova’s legend but unsupported or contradicted by documentary evidence.
- **Unresolved:** available evidence does not permit a firm conclusion.

Dates before the Russian calendar reform are given in both Old Style and New Style when material. Thus Pavlova’s birth was recorded as **31 January 1881 Old Style / 12 February 1881 New Style**.

## Family and Ancestry

### Mother

**Documented fact:** Pavlova’s mother was **Lyubov Fyodorovna Pavlova** (Любовь Фёдоровна Павлова), a woman of peasant background. Biographies commonly describe her as a laundress or domestic worker. Mother and daughter lived in modest circumstances, although the precise economic condition of every stage of Pavlova’s childhood is difficult to reconstruct.

Lyubov was a decisive presence in the early narrative of Pavlova’s life. She took Anna to the theatre, supported her applications to the Imperial Ballet School, and remained part of her daughter’s household or financial concerns after Anna’s ascent. Pavlova’s later public accounts emphasized their closeness and the mother’s sacrifices.

### Official father: Matvey Pavlov

**Documented fact:** A church metrical record names the parents as **Matvey Pavlovich Pavlov**, a reserve private from a peasant family in the village of Bor, Osechenskaya volost, Vyshny Volochyok district, Tver province, and his lawful wife Lyubov Fyodorovna.

The form **Matveyevna** in Anna’s legal birth identity corresponds to Matvey’s given name.

**Reported fact:** Matvey was associated with the Preobrazhensky Regiment and either died, separated from Lyubov, or disappeared from the child’s life when Anna was very young. Published biographies disagree about whether the marriage occurred shortly before Anna’s birth, how long it lasted, and whether Matvey was Anna’s biological father.

### The Lazar Polyakov paternity claim

**Claim or allegation:** Several twentieth-century accounts, including material attributed to members of the Polyakov family, identify the banker **Lazar Solomonovich Polyakov** as Pavlova’s biological father. Lyubov reportedly worked in or had connections with the Polyakov household, and Polyakov was said to have contributed to Anna’s upbringing or education.

The most specific published version derives from statements attributed to Polyakov’s sons, including Vladimir Polyakov. Oleg Kerensky repeated the story in his 1973 biography.

**Evidence supporting the claim:**

- Lyubov Pavlova was reportedly employed in a Polyakov household.
- Members of the Polyakov family later claimed Anna had been raised in proximity to their children and received support from Lazar.
- Pavlova’s professional substitution of “Pavlovna” for “Matveyevna” has sometimes been treated as suggestive, although it proves nothing by itself.

**Evidence against or limiting the claim:**

- The surviving birth and baptismal documentation names Matvey Pavlov and Lyubov as married parents.
- No acknowledged-paternity instrument, contemporary letter from Lazar, DNA evidence, or comparable direct proof has been produced.
- Later family recollections may incorporate hearsay, status claims, or retrospective appropriation of a celebrity.

**Status:** Unresolved. It is a historically important paternity claim, not an established fact.

### The Shabbetai or Savely Shamash claim

**Claim or legend:** Another tradition identifies Pavlova’s father as **Shabbetai/Savely/Matvey Shamash**, described as a Crimean Karaite from Yevpatoria who went to Saint Petersburg and operated or worked around a laundry.

The tradition has been memorialized in Karaite cultural contexts, including an association with Yevpatoria’s kenesas.

**Evidence:** Later community tradition and biographical repetition.

**Limitations:** No direct contemporary evidence establishing Shamash as Pavlova’s father was located. The claim competes with both the metrical record and the Polyakov theory.

**Status:** Unverified tradition.

### Siblings and extended family

No securely documented full siblings appear prominently in reliable accounts. References to Polyakov’s sons as Pavlova’s half-brothers depend entirely on accepting the Polyakov paternity claim. They should therefore be described as **alleged half-brothers**, not established family.

### Marriage, partner, and household

Pavlova’s central adult relationship was with **Victor Emilievich Dandré** (c. 1870–1944), a Saint Petersburg municipal official, aristocratic social figure, impresario, and eventually the administrator of her company.

**Documented facts:**

- Pavlova and Dandré had a long personal, domestic, and financial partnership.
- He managed her tours and business affairs.
- They lived together at Ivy House.
- Dandré publicly described himself as her husband after her death.
- His ashes were ultimately placed beside hers at Golders Green.

**Marriage claim:** Dandré said they married secretly, usually dated to 1914. Some biographies accept this account.

**Contrary evidence:** Researchers have reported that no marriage certificate has been found. In later litigation concerning Pavlova’s estate, Dandré reportedly could not produce documentary proof of marriage. Dutch researcher René Thomassen argued that no legal marriage occurred.

**Status:** Their intimate and managerial partnership is documented. The existence and date of a legally valid marriage remain unresolved. Descriptions such as “husband,” “common-law husband,” “companion,” and “manager” reflect differing interpretations of the same relationship.

### Children and wards

Pavlova had no documented biological children.

**Reported fact:** At Ivy House she supported a home or school for Russian refugee or orphaned girls. Accounts commonly give the number as approximately fifteen. The girls were sometimes called her adopted daughters, but formal individual adoptions have not been established. “Wards,” “resident pupils,” or “supported orphans” are safer documentary descriptions.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1881–1891

Pavlova was born prematurely or was at least remembered as a frail, frequently ill child. Later accounts place part of her early childhood at **Ligovo**, then a semi-rural summer-residence district southwest of central Saint Petersburg, where cleaner air and a quieter environment were thought beneficial.

The childhood legend centers on a visit with her mother to the Mariinsky Theatre to see Marius Petipa’s **The Sleeping Beauty**, which premiered in 1890.

**Reported fact:** The spectacle produced a decisive vocation: Pavlova resolved to become a ballerina.

**Legendary shaping:** Later children’s biographies turn the visit into an instantaneous revelation—the poor and sickly child sees the imperial stage, prays or dreams of dancing, and overcomes rejection. The story is plausible and was endorsed in Pavlova’s autobiographical narrative, but its polished form belongs partly to retrospective self-presentation.

Around age nine, she auditioned for the Imperial Ballet School.

**Reported fact:** Her first application was rejected because she was too young, small, or physically delicate. She was accepted at approximately ten, in 1891 or 1892. Institutional chronologies differ by a year, likely because they count the audition, entry examination, or formal enrollment differently.

## Education and Formation

### Imperial Ballet School

The Imperial Ballet School in Saint Petersburg combined general education with a highly disciplined program of classical dance, mime, music, deportment, and theatrical practice. Pupils lived within an institution designed to feed the Imperial Theatres.

Pavlova’s body was not an obvious match for the prevailing late-nineteenth-century ideal. She had highly arched feet, thin ankles, long limbs, and limited muscular strength by the standards of the virtuoso Italian school. Classmates reportedly mocked her as “the broom” or “la petite sauvage,” although the wording varies among biographies.

Her limitations became formative. She developed:

- fast, delicate footwork;
- an ability to disguise preparation;
- expressive épaulement and arms;
- unusually communicative mime;
- an impression of lightness rather than athletic force;
- phrasing organized around dramatic and musical effect.

### Teachers

Teachers associated with her training and later coaching include:

- **Ekaterina Vazem** — a major exponent of the older Imperial tradition.
- **Pavel Gerdt** — danseur noble, mime, teacher, and partner in the Imperial repertory.
- **Christian Johansson** — Swedish-born teacher central to Saint Petersburg classicism.
- **Nikolai Legat** — dancer, pedagogue, and later caricaturist of the ballet world.
- **Marius Petipa** — choreographer and institutional authority rather than her everyday classroom instructor; he cast and encouraged her.
- **Enrico Cecchetti** — Pavlova studied privately with him after entering the company, especially from about 1905; his systematic technique strengthened her.
- **Michel/Mikhail Fokine** — school contemporary, partner, choreographer, and one of the decisive artistic collaborators of her career.

### Technical controversy: reinforced pointe shoes

**Documented practice; contemporary criticism reported:** Pavlova modified or reinforced her pointe shoes to accommodate extremely arched, unstable feet. The shoes had broader, flatter platforms and stronger boxes or soles than convention prescribed. Photographs were sometimes retouched to make the shoe appear more tapered.

Traditionalists accused her of “cheating,” because the reinforced shoe supplied mechanical support that earlier dancers had lacked.

The practice was not fraud in a legal sense. It was a technical adaptation that helped normalize the modern blocked pointe shoe. The controversy illustrates conflict between inherited visual ideals and changing dance technology.

### Graduation

She graduated in **April 1899**. Unlike many graduates, she entered the Imperial Ballet at a rank above the ordinary corps—usually given as **coryphée**—indicating unusual confidence in her potential.

## Early Career at the Imperial Ballet, 1899–1905

Pavlova made her official professional debut at the Mariinsky Theatre in 1899, although she had appeared in student productions earlier.

Early repertory included roles in or excerpts from:

- *La Fille mal gardée*;
- *The Pharaoh’s Daughter*;
- *The Sleeping Beauty*;
- *Paquita*;
- *Le Roi Candaule*;
- *The Cavalry Halt*;
- *The Little Humpbacked Horse*;
- *Raymonda*;
- *La Bayadère*;
- *Giselle*;
- *The Corsair*;
- *Don Quixote*.

Rank chronologies vary slightly, but the commonly reported sequence is:

- **1902:** second soloist;
- **1903:** first soloist;
- **1905:** ballerina;
- **1906:** prima ballerina.

Pavlova did not prevail by matching the Italian virtuosity of Pierina Legnani or the political and theatrical authority of Mathilde Kschessinska. Critics instead emphasized vulnerability, lyricism, musicality, and the appearance that movement arose from emotion.

### The 1905 political setting

The Revolution of 1905 affected the Imperial Theatres. Ballet artists participated in workplace agitation over conditions and administration. Pavlova’s exact political activity is imperfectly documented.

**Reported fact:** She sympathized with reformist demands within the ballet but was not a systematic revolutionary activist. Later narratives sometimes depict management sending politically troublesome dancers abroad; this may oversimplify a mix of artistic opportunity, company politics, and administrative calculation.

No evidence was found that Pavlova joined a political party, advocated revolutionary violence, held public office, or participated in intelligence activity.

## “The Dying Swan”

### Creation and date

Michel Fokine created the solo generally known as **The Dying Swan**, originally **The Swan** (*Лебедь* / *Le Cygne*), for Pavlova to Camille Saint-Saëns’s *Le Cygne* from *Le Carnaval des animaux*.

The date is variously given as **1905**, **1906**, or **December 1907**.

- Fokine’s recollection places the rapid creation in the mid-1900s.
- Some chronologies date the first public performance to a charity event in December 1907.
- Later publicity often collapsed composition, rehearsal, and premiere into a single date.

**Status:** The work was created during 1905–1907; December 1907 is the strongest date for the celebrated early public presentation.

### Choreographic importance

The solo is not a literal account of Swan Lake’s Odette. It lasts only several minutes. The ballerina travels with trembling bourrées and continuously articulated arms, giving the impression of a creature struggling against death.

Fokine later connected the work to:

- Pavlova’s specific physical and expressive qualities;
- natural observation of swans;
- experiments in expressive dance;
- the influence of Isadora Duncan’s freer use of the upper body.

Pavlova performed the solo thousands of times. It became inseparable from her public identity and one of the most frequently imitated short dances of the twentieth century.

### Mythic afterlife

The solo generated the central Pavlova mythology: the fragile white figure who becomes the dying bird and ultimately dies “for the dance.” Her actual career, however, required stamina, repeated ocean travel, management discipline, and strenuous daily performance.

## International Emergence, 1907–1911

### Early independent touring

Pavlova began arranging or participating in tours outside Russia before permanently leaving the Imperial establishment.

Archival chronologies document:

- **1908:** Riga, Helsingfors/Helsinki, Stockholm, and Copenhagen;
- **spring 1909:** Leipzig, Prague, and Vienna;
- further appearances in Berlin and other European cities.

These journeys established her as an exportable individual celebrity rather than merely a member of the Imperial company.

### Diaghilev’s 1909 Paris season

In 1909 Pavlova joined Sergei Diaghilev’s Russian ballet season at the **Théâtre du Châtelet, Paris**. Her colleagues included Tamara Karsavina and Vaslav Nijinsky.

She appeared in works including:

- *Les Sylphides*/*Chopiniana*;
- *Cléopâtre*;
- *Le Pavillon d’Armide*;
- other Fokine repertory presented under Diaghilev’s direction.

The original Paris cast of *Les Sylphides* included Pavlova, Karsavina, Nijinsky, and Alexandra Baldina. Léon Bakst’s long Romantic tutu for Pavlova contributed to the production’s influential visual identity.

### Departure from Diaghilev’s model

Pavlova’s association with the Ballets Russes was brief and intermittent. Diaghilev was building an ensemble centered on new choreography, modernist scores, visual art, and increasingly on male stars such as Nijinsky. Pavlova wanted control of repertory, billing, schedule, and income, and her aesthetic remained rooted in Romantic and Petipa-era classicism.

### The Firebird refusal

**Documented fact:** Pavlova was considered for the title role in Fokine and Igor Stravinsky’s *The Firebird* in 1910 but declined. Tamara Karsavina created the role.

**Reported explanation:** Pavlova could not find a dance pulse in Stravinsky’s unfamiliar score or regarded it as incomprehensible noise. The often-repeated quotation “I shall never dance to such nonsense” is retrospective and should not be treated as a verbatim transcript without qualification.

The refusal became evidence in two competing narratives:

- hostile or modernist interpretation: Pavlova failed to recognize a major new work;
- sympathetic interpretation: she correctly recognized that the part and musical language did not suit her artistic identity.

### London

London became increasingly central from 1910–1912. Pavlova danced at the Palace Theatre and in Russian seasons, achieving exceptional celebrity with audiences beyond the established ballet public.

## Victor Dandré: Relationship, Finance, and Legal Trouble

Dandré belonged to Saint Petersburg’s administrative and social world and served in the city duma or municipal government. He met Pavlova by about 1900–1904; the exact year varies.

### Patronage and intimacy

**Reported fact:** Dandré initially functioned as patron, lover, and adviser. He helped finance a more luxurious life and supported Pavlova’s career. Later narratives reverse the economic direction: after his prosecution, Pavlova’s earnings enabled him to escape financial and legal jeopardy.

### Dandré’s prosecution

**Documented core, incomplete details:** Dandré was accused in Russia of financial impropriety involving municipal funds or contracts. Sources variously use “fraud,” “embezzlement,” “misappropriation,” or corrupt procurement. He was arrested or detained and released on substantial bail.

Pavlova was reportedly called or proposed as a witness, but her international schedule limited her presence.

Dandré eventually left Russia, violating or evading the constraints of his legal situation, and joined Pavlova abroad. His inability to return safely was one factor in their permanent international life.

### Competing interpretations

- **Dandré’s account:** He devoted himself to Pavlova’s art and created the logistical structure that made her world career possible.
- **Conventional romantic narrative:** Pavlova signed demanding contracts and worked to pay his bail or debts; their secret marriage sealed a partnership of sacrifice.
- **Hostile interpretation:** Dandré exploited Pavlova financially, drove her through an exhausting schedule, controlled her estate, and later fabricated or exaggerated the marriage.
- **Documentary minimum:** He was indispensable to the company’s management and benefited economically from Pavlova’s labor; she also benefited from his administrative competence. The internal division of money, power, and affection is not recoverable in full.

No reliable evidence was found that Pavlova herself was charged with fraud or knowingly participated in Dandré’s alleged Russian offenses.

## Independent Company and Global Touring, 1911–1930

Pavlova formed an independent ensemble that grew from a small touring group into a company of several dozen performers and staff. The organization was commonly billed as **Anna Pavlova’s Ballet Company**, **Les Ballets d’Anna Pavlova**, or variants of **Pavlova and her Ballet Russe**.

### Business structure

The enterprise centered on:

- Pavlova as owner, chief attraction, artistic authority, and principal source of revenue;
- Dandré as business manager and tour organizer;
- ballet masters such as Ivan Clustine;
- changing male partners, including Mikhail Mordkin, Laurent Novikoff, Pierre Vladimiroff, and Alexander Volinine;
- a corps assembled from Russian émigrés and locally recruited dancers;
- impresarios and local presenters, notably Sol Hurok in North America;
- extensive scenery, costumes, musical parts, animals, and personal effects.

One account describes approximately **400 pieces of luggage** on a 1920 American return tour. The detail conveys the scale of moving a full theatrical apparatus before containerized air travel.

### Repertory strategy

Pavlova’s company did not primarily commission avant-garde full-length ballets. Programs mixed:

- abbreviated Petipa classics;
- Fokine works;
- Romantic revivals;
- divertissements;
- national-character dances;
- short dramatic solos;
- accessible orchestral arrangements.

This made ballet portable and intelligible to audiences in cities without permanent ballet institutions. Critics sometimes judged the scenery, orchestration, corps, and choreography inferior to Diaghilev’s productions. Admirers argued that Pavlova disseminated dancing itself rather than presenting ballet as a Gesamtkunstwerk for elite metropolitan audiences.

### North America

Pavlova appeared repeatedly in the United States and Canada from about 1910 onward. Programs preserved by the New York Public Library document seasons from 1915 through 1923.

Her company performed not only in New York, Boston, and Chicago but across vaudeville, opera-house, and touring circuits. This broadened ballet’s American public.

At Midway Gardens in Chicago in 1915, the young **Ruth Page** studied around the Pavlova company. Page later toured South America with Pavlova’s organization in 1918–1919. The episode is documented in the Ruth Page archival collection.

### South America, 1915–1919 and later

Pavlova’s company toured South America more than once, including Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Peru, and other markets. Precise itineraries differ among biographies and surviving programs.

Her arrival could be treated as a major civic event. Latin American poets and critics converted Pavlova into an emblem of ethereal beauty and cosmopolitan modernity. Mexican poet Ramón López Velarde wrote a poem titled “Anna Pavlova,” with imagery presenting divine speech through her feet.

### Film: *The Dumb Girl of Portici*

In 1915–1916 Pavlova worked in California on **The Dumb Girl of Portici**, directed by Lois Weber and Phillips Smalley and released by Universal in 1916. It adapted Daniel Auber’s opera *La muette de Portici*.

Pavlova played **Fenella**, a mute heroine whose bodily expression suited a dancer in silent cinema. It was her only starring feature film.

The film is important for several reasons:

- it preserves Pavlova in an extended dramatic performance;
- it was made under Lois Weber, one of silent cinema’s most important directors;
- it translated mime and dance into film narrative;
- it demonstrates Pavlova’s willingness to use mass media despite her primary commitment to live performance.

Additional fragments and actuality footage preserve Pavlova dancing, rehearsing, or posing, though identification and dating can be uncertain. The Library of Congress holds Arnold Genthe images made in November 1915; the New York Public Library’s Dance Division holds early film material associated with Pavlova and Weber.

### Wartime and revolutionary displacement

The First World War and the Russian revolutions transformed Pavlova’s international touring into de facto emigration. She did not settle in Soviet Russia and never re-established herself at the Mariinsky.

She performed benefits for wartime relief and later aided Russian refugees. Accounts suggesting sympathy with some social aims of the revolution do not amount to Bolshevik affiliation. Her independent company, London property, and émigré household were incompatible with Soviet cultural administration.

### Asia

Pavlova toured India, Ceylon/Sri Lanka, China, Japan, Burma/Myanmar, Malaya, Java/Indonesia, and other parts of Asia during successive tours.

She watched local dancers and commissioned or created theatrical “national” pieces inspired by what she saw. Examples associated with her repertory include *Krishna and Radha*, *Ajanta Frescoes*, and various Japanese, Indian, and Orientalist divertissements.

These works occupied an ambiguous position:

- they reflected real curiosity and direct encounters with Asian performers;
- they introduced Western ballet audiences to stylized references to non-European dance;
- they also transformed complex traditions into short exotic spectacles constructed for Western touring programs.

Her appearances nevertheless had important local effects. In several countries, writers, dancers, and teachers remembered her as the first internationally celebrated classical ballerina they saw.

### Australia and New Zealand, 1926 and 1929

Pavlova toured Australia and New Zealand in **1926** and returned to Australia in **1929**.

The National Library of Australia preserves programs and ephemera, including:

- a Melbourne season beginning 17 April 1926;
- a farewell charity matinée at Her Majesty’s Theatre on 23 July 1926;
- records and photographs connected with the 1929 tour;
- a performer’s day-by-day diary.

She first danced in New Zealand on **26 May 1926**. These tours are frequently credited with stimulating ballet training and professional aspiration in Australasia.

The tours also generated the later dessert mythology surrounding the **pavlova**, discussed separately below.

### Middle East and Africa

Pavlova’s routes included Egypt and South Africa. The 1928–1929 world tour included Egypt, India, Malaya, Java, Burma, Australia, and South America in various sequence. Exact city-by-city reconstruction requires consultation of the company’s surviving programs, press books, and shipping records.

### Final tour years

The pace remained extreme through 1930. Pavlova maintained daily practice and often performed despite exhaustion or illness. The idea that she “never missed a performance” is hagiographic exaggeration, but it reflects an unusually rigid professional ethic.

## Ivy House

Around 1912 Pavlova established her principal home at **Ivy House**, North End Road, Golders Green, London.

The property included:

- a large house;
- a studio;
- grounds and ornamental water;
- swans and other birds;
- facilities for pupils and dependants;
- storage and administrative functions connected to the company.

The image of Pavlova studying live swans at Ivy House became central to her mythology. Although direct observation probably informed her arm movements and publicity photographs, *The Dying Swan* predated her long residence there.

Ivy House was simultaneously:

- a domestic home;
- a rehearsal and teaching site;
- a company headquarters;
- a carefully staged extension of the Pavlova image;
- an asset within Pavlova and Dandré’s financial world.

Later occupants and institutions altered the house. It remains associated with Pavlova in London cultural history.

## Artistic Development and Aesthetic Position

### From academic irregularity to personal style

Pavlova converted physical features once treated as defects into a recognizable aesthetic. Her dancing emphasized:

- continuity rather than poses;
- rapid footwork under a seemingly weightless torso;
- emotional legibility;
- line extended through neck, hands, and gaze;
- dramatic identification with a character;
- the illusion of fragility sustained by formidable discipline.

Her technique could appear less secure in balances or turns than that of more conventionally powerful dancers. Contemporary criticism was divided: some found mannerism, technical shortcuts, sentimentality, or excessive personal display; others described unprecedented spiritual or emotional force.

### Romantic classicism versus modernism

Pavlova was not simply conservative. She collaborated with Fokine, whose early reforms rejected empty display and demanded unity of expression. But her independent repertory gradually privileged emotionally accessible Romantic classicism over the increasingly experimental music and design of Diaghilev’s company.

The *Firebird* refusal became the best-known limit of her musical modernism. She preferred Chopin, Tchaikovsky, Saint-Saëns, Glazunov, and melodically direct arrangements.

### Choreographic authorship

Pavlova supervised, adapted, and sometimes received choreographic credit for solos and divertissements. Precise authorship is difficult because company programs might attribute a work to her even when a ballet master supplied substantial choreography.

Works associated with Pavlova as choreographer, co-creator, adapter, or principal author include:

- *The Dragonfly*;
- *California Poppy*;
- *Christmas*;
- *Autumn Leaves*;
- *The Fairy Doll* adaptations;
- national or character dances inspired by tour encounters;
- revisions of her personal versions of *Giselle*, *Coppélia*, and other classics.

Claims of authorship should be assessed work by work against programs and company records.

### Religion and philosophy

Pavlova was culturally connected to Russian Orthodoxy, and Orthodox memorial services followed her death. No sustained theological writing by her is known.

Her public language about art was idealistic: dance was vocation, beauty, service, and a means of lifting audiences beyond ordinary life. This was an artistic credo rather than a formal philosophical system.

### Politics

No evidence supports a substantial party-political career. Her relevant activities were:

- participation or sympathy in Imperial Theatre labor reform around 1905;
- benefit performances;
- support for Russian refugees and children after revolution and civil war;
- life as a non-returning émigré;
- preservation of an Imperial-Russian ballet identity abroad.

Both Soviet and émigré narratives later appropriated her: Soviet accounts claimed her as a product of Russian schooling while criticizing the commercial touring repertory; émigré and Western accounts presented her as proof of pre-revolutionary Russian artistic greatness.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Victor Dandré

Partner, manager, financial organizer, estate claimant, and first major posthumous biographer. Their relationship combined intimacy, business, dependence, and unresolved legal status.

### Michel Fokine

School contemporary, partner, choreographer, and creator of *The Dying Swan*. Fokine’s ideas about expressive unity gave Pavlova her most durable vehicle. Their professional paths diverged, but each remained central to the other’s historical reputation.

### Marius Petipa

Institutional elder and choreographer of the repertory in which Pavlova was trained. Petipa recognized her usefulness despite her atypical body. Later descriptions calling him her “sponsor” concern artistic patronage, not a private financial arrangement.

### Enrico Cecchetti

Technical teacher and coach. Cecchetti’s method strengthened Pavlova’s control, though she never became merely a specimen of his school.

### Sergei Diaghilev

Impresario who presented Pavlova in the 1909 Paris breakthrough. Their competing enterprises came to represent different routes for Russian ballet abroad: Diaghilev’s collaborative modernism and Pavlova’s star-centered global classicism.

### Vaslav Nijinsky

Colleague and celebrated partner in the Imperial Ballet and Diaghilev seasons, notably in *Giselle* and *Les Sylphides*. Later legend contrasts Nijinsky’s leap with Pavlova’s floating quality. A story says Pavlova examined his shoes to discover the secret of his elevation; this is anecdotal rather than demonstrably documented.

### Tamara Karsavina

Colleague, fellow Imperial ballerina, and alternative Diaghilev star. Karsavina received *The Firebird* after Pavlova declined it. Their relationship is often framed as rivalry, but they also shared training, stages, repertory, and collaborators.

### Mathilde Kschessinska

Senior Imperial ballerina and a figure of great court influence. Later memoirs and biographies position her as a professional rival. Claims that Kschessinska deliberately prevented Pavlova’s return or used Dandré’s scandal against her require caution; the institutional rivalry is real, but particular acts are unevenly documented.

### Mikhail Mordkin

Important partner during the first American period. Publicity made the pair an attraction, but professional conflict developed over billing, authority, and company control. Their separation helped consolidate Pavlova’s insistence that the company be organized around her name.

### Ivan Clustine

Ballet master and choreographic organizer for her company. He supplied or adapted much of the repertory presented as Pavlova’s touring canon.

### Alexander Volinine and Laurent Novikoff

Frequent partners whose role was to frame Pavlova’s dancing while contributing substantial classical expertise.

### Sol Hurok

North American impresario who presented Pavlova and later became one of the century’s major dance managers. His memoirs provide valuable but self-dramatizing evidence about touring culture.

### Harcourt Algeranoff

Dancer in Pavlova’s company and author of *My Years with Pavlova*. His recollections are an important insider source, although written after her death.

### Walford Hyden

Musical director and composer-arranger associated with the touring company. The company depended heavily on adaptable musical personnel because local orchestral conditions varied.

### Ruth Page

Young American dancer influenced directly by Pavlova’s company. Page studied around it in Chicago in 1915 and toured South America with the company in 1918–1919.

### Anna Pavlova’s company dancers

The company depended on many less-famous performers whose labor is obscured by star-centered publicity. Surviving programs and archives identify dancers such as Muriel Stuart, Hilda Butsova, Enid Brunova, Helene Sokolova/Saxova, Amata Grassi, and others. Membership changed frequently across tours.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Imperial Theatres

Pavlova’s state-supported training and career originated in the Russian imperial system. Her salary, rank, repertory, and leave were governed by theatre administration.

### Anna Pavlova touring company

The company was Pavlova’s principal commercial enterprise. Its valuable assets included:

- her name and image;
- bookings and presenter contracts;
- costumes and scenery;
- orchestral arrangements;
- choreographic knowledge;
- a mobile workforce;
- publicity photography;
- film and reproduction rights, where contracted.

No full audited financial history was located. Statements about her exact wealth should be treated cautiously.

### Ivy House property

Ivy House was the principal identifiable real-estate asset associated with Pavlova. Its title history and estate disposition became relevant after her death.

### Publicity and merchandising

Pavlova’s image circulated through:

- photographic portraits;
- signed postcards;
- cigarette cards;
- programs and souvenir books;
- fashion illustrations;
- sculptures and paintings;
- branded theatrical advertising.

This was a modern celebrity business even when the public language surrounding her emphasized spiritual art.

### Film rights

*The Dumb Girl of Portici* represented a commercial venture with Universal. Pavlova’s dissatisfaction or limited interest in continuing a film career is reported, but surviving contract details would be necessary to establish her compensation and control.

### Dance school and refugee support

Ivy House housed teaching and charitable activity. Pavlova’s support for Russian refugee girls joined philanthropy with cultural reproduction: the household educated children while maintaining Russian language, manners, and dance.

### Patents and inventions

No patents by Pavlova were located. Her reinforced pointe shoes were a practical technical innovation or adaptation, not a documented patented invention.

## Travels and Geographical Movements

A complete itinerary would require a database of every program, border crossing, shipping list, railway movement, and newspaper notice. The strongest reconstructable outline is:

1. **1881–1891:** Saint Petersburg and Ligovo.
2. **1891/92–1899:** Imperial Ballet School, Saint Petersburg.
3. **1899–1907:** Primarily Saint Petersburg, with Imperial performance obligations.
4. **1907–1909:** Northern and Central European tours—Riga, Helsinki, Stockholm, Copenhagen, Leipzig, Prague, Vienna, and other cities.
5. **1909:** Paris with Diaghilev.
6. **1910–1914:** Repeated London, continental-European, and North American engagements; increasing independence.
7. **1914 onward:** London/Ivy House as home base; Russia no longer a practical residence.
8. **1915–1916:** United States and film work in California; South American touring.
9. **1916–1924:** Repeated North American, Latin American, British, and European circuits.
10. **1922–1923:** Tours extending into East and Southeast Asia.
11. **1925–1926:** Extensive tours including Asia, Australia, and New Zealand.
12. **1928–1929:** Final great world circuit through South America, Egypt, India, Burma, Malaya, Java, and Australia.
13. **1930–1931:** European touring; final journey toward The Hague.

Her company introduced ballet to audiences far beyond cities with permanent opera institutions. “Introduced ballet” should not be interpreted as meaning that the visited societies lacked dance traditions; it means she brought a particular Russo-European theatrical ballet system.

## Health, Injuries, and Working Conditions

### Childhood frailty

Pavlova was remembered as premature, physically delicate, and frequently ill. The narrative may have been intensified by publicity contrasting weakness with artistic triumph.

### Feet and biomechanics

Her very high arches and unstable feet shaped her shoe modifications and technique. The beauty of her instep was also a structural vulnerability.

### Exhaustion

Repeated rail and sea travel, rehearsal, publicity, and near-nightly performance imposed severe physical strain. Stories of dancing through pain are abundant but seldom medically specific.

### Accident stories

Tour literature contains reports of transport failures, missed connections, cold exposure, and minor injuries. The most consequential is the final-train story, but its details are contested below.

No reliable evidence was found of addiction, a diagnosed psychiatric disorder, or a major psychological breakdown.

## Final Illness and Death

### Journey to The Hague

In January 1931 Pavlova traveled to the Netherlands for engagements at The Hague.

The familiar narrative says her train was involved in an accident or breakdown near Dijon or elsewhere in France, forcing her—thinly dressed—to wait in severe cold. She then developed pneumonia and pleurisy.

**Evidentiary caution:** The breakdown-and-exposure story appears in institutional chronologies and biographies but varies in location and detail. It may describe a real disruption embellished into a fatalistic final journey.

### Medical condition

**Documented core:** Pavlova became gravely ill with pneumonia complicated by pleurisy at the Hôtel des Indes.

An operation to drain or treat the pleural condition was reportedly proposed. Accounts state that surgery might have damaged ribs or musculature and ended her ability to dance. The celebrated response—effectively, “If I cannot dance, I would rather be dead”—belongs to the deathbed legend and cannot be verified verbatim.

### Death

Pavlova died shortly after midnight or in the early hours of **23 January 1931**.

The company was scheduled to perform. According to theatrical tradition, the performance proceeded while a spotlight traced the empty space where she would have danced *The Dying Swan*. Whether this occurred immediately at The Hague or was developed in subsequent memorial performance practice varies by account.

### Last words

The best-known story gives her last words as:

> “Prepare my swan costume.”

Variants include “Get my Swan costume ready” and “Play the last measure very softly.”

**Status:** Legendary or reported deathbed speech, not independently documented verbatim.

René Thomassen offered a contrary claim that Pavlova instead instructed her maid to return a Paris dress for a refund, allegedly for the benefit of an orphanage. This alternative also depends on later testimony and has not displaced the traditional story through independently produced primary documentation.

### Cremation and memorials

Pavlova was cremated. Orthodox memorial services were held in London. Her ashes were placed in the columbarium at Golders Green Crematorium. Dandré’s ashes were placed nearby after his death in 1944.

The urn’s association with ballet shoes became part of the memorial image. Accounts state that shoes displayed there were later stolen.

## Estate, Marriage Litigation, and the Ashes Dispute

### Absence of a clear will

Pavlova apparently died without a valid, readily enforceable will governing all property. This intensified disputes among Dandré, her mother or maternal representatives, and other claimants.

The legal status of Dandré’s marriage claim mattered because a lawful spouse would have stronger inheritance rights. Reports state that he could not produce a marriage certificate and explained that records had been lost in revolutionary Russia.

The precise judgments, jurisdictions, property totals, and final distributions require direct examination of British and possibly Dutch probate records. Popular biographies often simplify the case.

### Allegations against Dandré

Dutch researcher René Thomassen alleged that Dandré:

- was never legally married to Pavlova;
- exploited her earnings;
- removed or controlled money while she was dying;
- shaped the posthumous legend for his own benefit.

These are **published allegations**, not court findings established in the sources consulted here. Dandré’s essential managerial work is independently documented, as is the absence of publicly produced marriage documentation.

### Proposed transfer to Russia

During the 1990s and 2001, campaigners sought to move Pavlova’s ashes from London to Moscow, potentially for burial at Novodevichy Cemetery.

Supporters claimed that Pavlova wished to return to Russia after the fall of Soviet rule.

**Documentary finding:** No will by Pavlova expressing that wish was produced. Dandré’s will reportedly allowed the possibility that both urns might be moved if Russian authorities formally requested it and proper honors were assured; permission is not the same as Pavlova’s dying wish.

In March 2001 arrangements were announced, but the plan collapsed amid objections, lack of family or governmental authorization, and disputes over custodial authority. The ashes remain in London.

## Controversies, Allegations, and Contested Questions

### 1. Paternity

- **Documented:** Matvey Pavlov is named as father in the church record.
- **Allegation:** Lazar Polyakov was the biological father.
- **Alternative tradition:** Shabbetai/Savely Shamash was the biological father.
- **Finding:** Biological paternity remains unresolved; official parentage is documented.

### 2. Legal marriage to Victor Dandré

- **Documented:** intimate, domestic, managerial partnership.
- **Claim:** secret marriage, often dated to 1914.
- **Contrary evidence:** no certificate publicly produced; later estate disputes questioned the marriage.
- **Finding:** “Husband” is Dandré’s asserted status; legal validity remains unresolved.

### 3. Dandré’s financial offenses

- **Documented core:** Russian prosecution, detention or arrest, bail, and flight or permanent departure.
- **Disputed particulars:** exact charges, culpability, amount, and extent to which Pavlova’s money was implicated.
- **Finding:** Pavlova was not shown to have been charged as a participant.

### 4. Exploitation of Pavlova

- **Allegation:** Dandré treated Pavlova as an income-producing asset and made her tour until physical collapse.
- **Counter-evidence:** Pavlova consistently exercised artistic authority, wanted worldwide reach, and was an active principal in the enterprise.
- **Finding:** The partnership was financially interdependent and unequal in ways that cannot be fully reconstructed. A simple victim/controller narrative exceeds the evidence.

### 5. Professional ruthlessness

Company memoirs describe Pavlova as exacting, jealous of billing, intolerant of competition, and capable of dismissing dancers. Such behavior is plausible within a company organized around a single star, but individual anecdotes require source-by-source verification.

The company’s repertory and economics subordinated ensemble members to Pavlova’s public image. This was an observable business structure, not in itself evidence of personal malice.

### 6. Rivalry with Mordkin

Pavlova and Mikhail Mordkin quarreled over prominence and billing during early American work. Public fascination with the pair could undermine Pavlova’s exclusive star status. Their separation is documented; lurid accounts of confrontations may be publicity or retrospective embellishment.

### 7. Conservatism and rejection of *The Firebird*

The refusal is documented. Characterizations of it as cowardice, ignorance, artistic integrity, or aesthetic self-knowledge are interpretations.

### 8. Technical “cheating”

Her reinforced shoes were criticized as artificial assistance. Modern pointe practice broadly validated the technical direction of the adaptation. Retouched photographs concealed the bulky platform to preserve the desired silhouette.

### 9. Orientalism

Pavlova’s national and Asian dances drew on real travel and observation but reduced diverse traditions to short theatrical impressions. Modern criticism describes some of these works as Orientalist appropriation. No evidence indicates that Pavlova claimed ethnographic authenticity; programs nevertheless presented stylized representations to audiences likely unable to distinguish adaptation from tradition.

### 10. Commercial dilution of ballet

Critics contrasted Pavlova’s repetitive touring programs with Diaghilev’s artistic innovation. Her defenders point to the geographic reach, audience formation, and preservation of nineteenth-century performance qualities.

Both propositions can be true: the repertory could be uneven and commercially repetitive while exerting extraordinary institutional influence.

### 11. Misidentified performance at Wentworth Woodhouse

For many years a 1912 royal performance at Wentworth Woodhouse was attributed to Pavlova. Local historical research now identifies the dancer as **Lydia Kyasht**.

- **Old story:** Pavlova danced in the Marble Saloon before King George V and Queen Mary.
- **Reassessment:** programs or related documentation point to Kyasht.
- **Status:** Corrected historical misidentification.

### 12. No substantiated criminality by Pavlova

The dedicated adversarial search located controversies around paternity, marriage, labor and touring conditions, artistic conservatism, cultural representation, estate administration, and Dandré’s finances. It did **not** locate credible evidence that Pavlova herself was convicted of fraud, corruption, violent crime, money laundering, espionage, or political violence.

Recent searches from 2024–2026 mainly returned namesakes, retrospective ballet discussion, exhibitions, dessert disputes, and repetition of older claims. No materially new criminal allegation against the historical Pavlova was identified.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Propaganda

### The child transformed by *The Sleeping Beauty*

**Story:** A poor, frail child sees Petipa’s ballet and immediately knows her destiny.

**Circulation:** Pavlova’s autobiographical accounts, Dandré, popular biographies, children’s books.

**Evidence:** The theatre visit and its importance are plausible and repeatedly attested; dialogue, prayers, and sudden certainty are literary reconstruction.

**Effect:** It made Pavlova’s life a vocation narrative accessible to young dancers.

### The ballerina who could literally fly

Reviews repeatedly said she did not dance but floated or flew. This was metaphorical testimony about stage effect, later treated almost as supernatural description.

The mythology obscures technical mechanisms: rapid bourrée, soft landings, mobile arms, carefully controlled focus, costume, lighting, and cultivated photographic imagery.

### The swan as Pavlova’s natural double

Pavlova kept swans at Ivy House and was photographed with them. In myth she learned *The Dying Swan* directly from a pet swan named Jack.

Direct observation may have influenced later performance detail and publicity, but Fokine’s solo emerged from choreography, music, and expressive experimentation before the mature Ivy House legend.

### “Prepare my swan costume”

The traditional last words perfectly fuse Pavlova’s body, role, and death. Their dramatic fitness is a reason for caution as well as survival. No contemporaneous stenographic evidence establishes the exact phrase.

The words shaped nearly every later biography and memorial.

### The empty spotlight

The story says that, at the performance scheduled after her death, the orchestra played Saint-Saëns while a spotlight followed the path of the absent dancer.

Empty-light tributes became a real theatrical memorial practice. Whether every detail belongs to the first Hague memorial or later reenactment is uncertain.

### Dying because she chose dance over surgery

The story states that Pavlova refused a potentially life-saving operation because it would end her dancing.

Pleurisy and pneumonia were often fatal before antibiotics, and surgery carried substantial risk. The stark choice between survival and dancing may be a dramatized compression of medical discussions.

### Secret aristocratic or banking birth

The Polyakov theory elevated the daughter of a laundress into a concealed banking dynasty; the Karaite theory made her an emblem of another community. Both demonstrate how celebrity produces competitive ancestry.

Neither should replace the documentary record without stronger evidence.

### Secret wife versus exploited mistress

Dandré’s posthumous presentation made the relationship a hidden marriage of devotion. Hostile revision made it a story of a criminal manager exploiting an unmarried woman and appropriating her legacy.

Available documentation supports the partnership but not either totalizing narrative.

### The pavlova dessert

The meringue-and-cream dessert called **pavlova** was named in Pavlova’s honor in Australasia. Australia and New Zealand both claim its invention.

Stories often identify a single hotel chef who created it during or just after her 1926 or 1929 tour and declared it “as light as Pavlova.”

Food historians have located earlier uses of the name and predecessor desserts, preventing confident attribution to one inventor. What is secure is the association of her name with lightness and Australasian celebrity culture.

### “Pavlova introduced dance” to entire countries

This publicity formula erases indigenous, court, temple, folk, and theatrical dance traditions. More precisely, her company introduced or popularized a particular international form of Russian classical ballet in many markets.

## Awards, Honors, and Offices

Formal state honors were less central to Pavlova’s career than theatrical rank and celebrity.

Documented or widely reported distinctions include:

- promotion to **prima ballerina** of the Imperial Ballet;
- royal and court performances;
- leadership of a company bearing her name;
- election as the first president of the organization that became the **Royal Academy of Dance**, established in 1920 as the Association of Operatic Dancing of Great Britain;
- extensive civic receptions and commemorative honors during tours.

She held no documented governmental office after leaving Russia.

## Works by or Under the Name of Anna Pavlova

Pavlova was principally a performing artist, not a prolific author. Bibliographic records frequently mix her own words with books assembled after her death.

### Autobiographical and essay material

#### “Pages of My Life”

- Autobiographical reminiscences published in periodical or later collected form.
- Original-language and first-publication details vary in catalogues.
- **Authorship status:** attributed to Pavlova and based on her first-person recollections, but editorial assistance is likely.
- Contents emphasize childhood vocation, school, teachers, and artistic ideals while saying relatively little about private relationships or finances.

#### “A Few Thoughts on Dancing”

- Frequently dated to approximately **1912**.
- **Authorship status:** published under Pavlova’s name; possible journalistic editing cannot be excluded.
- Expresses the idea that dance had governed her life from childhood.

#### Interviews and signed articles

Pavlova gave numerous newspaper and magazine interviews during tours. Some were written in first person or published as signed articles. In early celebrity journalism, such pieces could be edited, translated, reconstructed from conversation, or supplied by publicists. Each requires individual textual verification before being treated as verbatim authorship.

### Correspondence

Letters by Pavlova survive in institutional and private collections, including correspondence with:

- Victor Dandré;
- managers and presenters;
- company members;
- admirers and collectors;
- artistic colleagues.

No definitive complete edition of her correspondence was identified. Letters reproduced by Dandré and later biographers are selections mediated by editors.

### Choreographic works and arrangements

Works carrying Pavlova’s name as choreographer, co-creator, adapter, or performer-author include:

- *The Dragonfly*;
- *California Poppy*;
- *Christmas*;
- *Autumn Leaves*;
- various Russian, Mexican, Japanese, Indian, and “Oriental” dances;
- adaptations of classical ballets for touring.

Attribution is frequently shared or disputed because Ivan Clustine and other ballet masters supplied choreography while Pavlova shaped her own roles.

### Film performance

#### *The Dumb Girl of Portici* (1916)

- Directors: Lois Weber and Phillips Smalley.
- Role: Fenella.
- **Status:** Pavlova’s only known feature-film starring role.
- Not authored by Pavlova, but an essential work performed under her name.

### Film fragments and actuality recordings

Short footage of Pavlova dancing, rehearsing, or demonstrating movements survives in archives. Some material was assembled posthumously into documentaries. These should not be confused with films she directed or authored.

### Posthumous publications under her name

Collections titled as Pavlova’s memoirs, thoughts, letters, or autobiographical writings may combine:

- genuine first-person texts;
- interviews;
- letters;
- recollections transcribed by others;
- editorial linking prose;
- Dandré’s narrative.

They are posthumous compilations unless a contemporary publication history demonstrates otherwise.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Transformation of the ballerina ideal

Pavlova displaced a purely muscular or virtuoso standard with a persona based on mobility, fragility, dramatic identification, and continuous line. Later dancers did not necessarily imitate her technique, but the ideal of the ballerina as an emotionally legible individual star owes much to her.

### Worldwide ballet infrastructure

Her tours helped create audiences that later supported:

- ballet schools;
- visiting companies;
- local professional troupes;
- critics and dance societies;
- Royal Academy of Dance examination networks;
- national ballet institutions in Britain, North America, Latin America, Australia, New Zealand, and parts of Asia.

Causation should not be overstated: local teachers and dancers did the institutional labor. Pavlova often supplied the catalytic spectacle and prestige.

### British ballet

Pavlova’s London presence preceded the consolidation of British ballet institutions. Her presidency of the Association of Operatic Dancing and the example of Ivy House linked Russian classical authority to emerging British training.

Marie Rambert later offered a useful distinction: Pavlova excited the desire to dance, whereas Diaghilev inspired love of ballet and choreography. The contrast is schematic but captures differing legacies.

### The *Dying Swan* lineage

The solo has been interpreted by generations of ballerinas, including Olga Spessivtseva, Galina Ulanova, Maya Plisetskaya, Alicia Markova, Margot Fonteyn, Natalia Makarova, and others. Some follow Fokine’s choreographic structure; others quote, parody, deconstruct, or transform it.

Pavlova remains the reference point even when later dancers use different technique.

### Modern reassessment

Recent scholarship increasingly examines:

- the company rather than Pavlova alone;
- labor and touring logistics;
- photographic retouching and celebrity manufacture;
- colonial routes and Orientalist repertory;
- women’s control of theatrical enterprises;
- the difference between embodied influence and surviving choreography;
- how Dandré controlled the early archive and narrative.

### Popular culture

Her name survives through:

- the pavlova dessert;
- schools and competitions;
- streets, theatres, and commemorative plaques;
- sculptures and paintings;
- ballet costumes and commercial brands;
- recurring portrayals in film and literature;
- “Pavlova” as shorthand for ethereal classical dancing.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are included. Shared geography or contemporaneity alone is excluded.

### Sergei Pavlovich Diaghilev — documented fact

Pavlova danced in Diaghilev’s 1909 Paris season at the Théâtre du Châtelet and in later related appearances. Diaghilev helped establish her Western European fame, but she did not remain a stable member of his Ballets Russes. Their artistic and commercial models subsequently diverged.

### Vaslav Nijinsky — documented fact

Pavlova and Nijinsky were Imperial Ballet colleagues and partners. They danced together in *Giselle* and appeared in the original 1909 Paris cast of *Les Sylphides*. Their contrasting stage qualities became part of ballet legend.

### Igor Fyodorovich Stravinsky — documented fact

Pavlova was approached for the title role in *The Firebird* in 1910 but rejected the role because she disliked or could not interpret Stravinsky’s score. Tamara Karsavina created the part. No substantial later collaboration is documented.

### Isadora Duncan — documented influence; personal contact insufficiently established here

Fokine stated that the expressive-dance climate associated with Duncan influenced his thinking around *The Dying Swan*. Sol Hurok later wrote that Pavlova encouraged his enthusiasm for bringing Duncan back to the United States in 1922. This supports documented professional awareness and indirect influence. A close friendship between Pavlova and Duncan was not established from the consulted evidence.

### Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky — documented artistic connection, not personal meeting

Pavlova’s vocation narrative began with Petipa’s *The Sleeping Beauty*, set to Tchaikovsky’s music, and she later performed in Tchaikovsky ballets. Tchaikovsky died in 1893, while Pavlova was a child at the Imperial school; no personal meeting was established.
