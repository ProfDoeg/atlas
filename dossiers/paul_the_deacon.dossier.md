# Paul the Deacon: Research Dossier

**Subject:** Paul the Deacon (Latin: *Paulus Diaconus*)  
**Prepared:** 25 August 2026  
**Scope:** Reconstructable life, family, education, movements, patronage, political and religious position, writings, textual transmission, disputed attributions, myths, later reputation, and the Cynocephali episode in *Historia Langobardorum* I.11.  
**Evidence standard:** Statements are separated, where necessary, into documented fact, contemporary or near-contemporary testimony, scholarly reconstruction, disputed attribution, and legend. Exact dates are not supplied where the evidence does not establish them.

## Executive Identification

Paul the Deacon was an eighth-century Lombard deacon, Benedictine monk, grammarian, poet, hagiographer, compiler, and historian. Born at Cividale del Friuli probably between 720 and 730, he was educated first locally and then at the Lombard royal court at Pavia. He became closely associated with Princess Adelperga and her husband Arechis II of Benevento, entered Monte Cassino at an uncertain date, traveled to Francia in 782 to plead with Charlemagne for his captive brother, and remained in the Frankish court orbit until about 786–787. Back at Monte Cassino, he produced or completed major educational, liturgical, antiquarian, hagiographical, and historical works. He died on 13 April, probably between 797 and 799; the year 799 is traditional but not documentary certainty.

His six-book *Historia Langobardorum*, written at Monte Cassino in roughly 787–796 and left either deliberately truncated or unfinished, is the principal continuous narrative of Lombard history. It preserves written sources now lost, legal and ecclesiastical material, court memories, family traditions, oral narrative, ethnographic lore, miracle stories, and political interpretation. Its Cynocephali episode does **not** assert that dog-headed soldiers really served in a Lombard vanguard. Paul says the outnumbered Lombards **pretended** to possess dog-headed, blood-drinking warriors and amplified the deception with extra tents and fires. In Paul’s narrative it is a story of psychological warfare, itself embedded in legendary migration history.

## Evidence Base and Its Limits

The reconstruction depends on four main classes of evidence:

1. **Paul’s own writings.** The most personal are *Historia Langobardorum* IV.37, which gives his family genealogy; the pleading poem *Verba tui famuli* to Charlemagne; the verse reply *Sensi cuius*; dedicatory letters; references to places he had seen; and remarks locating composition at Monte Cassino.
2. **Near-contemporary institutional evidence.** The Monte Cassino necrology gives 13 April as the day of his death. A passage in the *Gesta episcoporum Neapolitanorum* describes clerics being sent to study at Monte Cassino under “Paul the deacon.” Pope Hadrian I calls him “Paul the grammarian.”
3. **The acrostic epitaph attributed to Paul’s pupil Ilderic (Italian: Ilderico).** It is the principal early narrative witness to his court upbringing, movement toward sacred learning, monastic conversion, and reputation. Its authenticity has been questioned but not decisively overthrown; it probably belongs to the first half of the ninth century rather than Paul’s own lifetime.
4. **Later medieval biography and modern inference.** Cassinese writers from the tenth century onward enlarged Paul’s image. Modern biographies often combine evidence with conjectural itineraries. The [Friulian biographical dictionary](https://www.dizionariobiograficodeifriulani.it/paolo-diacono) and Capo’s Treccani entry are particularly useful because they identify these layers rather than silently merging them.

No contemporary civil dossier, ordination record, monastic profession record, property charter in Paul’s name, or exact birth or death certificate survives. Any exact year assigned to his childhood, ordination, profession, or death should therefore be read as reconstruction rather than documentary fact.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paul’s own genealogy

In *Historia Langobardorum* IV.37 Paul inserts a family history unusually personal for an early medieval ethnographic narrative:

- **Leupchis**, Paul’s ancestor, entered Italy with King Alboin in 568/569 and settled in Friuli.
- Leupchis had five sons. During an Avar incursion they were carried away from Friuli.
- One son, **Lopichis** (also represented as a second Leupchis in some summaries), escaped captivity, returned to his ruined home, and rebuilt the family line with help from relatives and friends.
- Lopichis fathered **Arechis**.
- Arechis fathered **Warnefrid**.
- Warnefrid and **Theodelinda** were Paul’s parents.

The number of generations may be defective: modern scholars have noted that the genealogy is difficult to fit perfectly between the migration of 568/569 and Paul’s birth in the 720s. That does not make the family narrative worthless. It documents Paul’s own remembered lineage and the family’s association of identity with migration, captivity, dispossession, return, and restoration.

### Parents and siblings

- **Father:** Warnefrid (*Warnefrit*, *Warnefridus*).
- **Mother:** Theodelinda (*Theudelinda*).
- **Brother:** Arechis (*Arichis*, *Arachis*). After the revolt of Duke Hrodgaud/Rotgaud of Friuli in 776, Arechis was taken into Frankish territory as a captive or hostage. Paul’s 782 petition says he had been held for nearly seven years.
- **Unnamed sister:** Paul’s poem says she had been dedicated to Christ from youth and was suffering grievously over the family disaster. A later suggestion places her at Santa Maria in Valle in Cividale, but that location is not established by Paul.
- **Sister-in-law and nephews/nieces:** *Verba tui famuli* says Arechis’s wife had been excluded from paternal property and begged for food in the streets to sustain **four children**. Their names and later lives are unknown.
- **Niece Sophia:** Paul wrote an epitaph for a young niece, Sophia, who died shortly before marriage. Whether she was a child of Arechis is not securely established.

### Status, property, and family collapse

Paul and his pupil’s epitaph call the family noble. “Noble” in eighth-century Lombard Italy did not necessarily denote a fixed later aristocratic rank; it indicated inherited prestige, economic standing, and likely connections with the ducal or royal sphere. Paul’s petition to Charlemagne claims that after Arechis’s captivity the household goods were plundered, the family’s nobility “perished,” and poverty reduced them to a level resembling rustic dependents. The poem is advocacy and uses the conventions of supplication, but it supplies the closest evidence for Paul’s finances and the material effect of the 776 settlement.

There is no evidence that Paul personally owned a business, invested capital, held patents, maintained a commercial partnership, or accumulated landed wealth in his own name. His economic world was that of family property, court and ducal patronage, monastic provision, books, copyists, and scholarly labor.

### Marriage, partners, and children

No evidence indicates that Paul married, had a sexual partner, or fathered children. His status as deacon and later monk and the silence of the sources make “none documented” the appropriate conclusion, not proof about every period of his life.

## Childhood and Early Life, c. 720–740s

Paul was born in Cividale during the mature Lombard kingdom. Cividale was not a remote backwater: it was the political center of Friuli, connected to Aquileian ecclesiastical culture, transalpine routes, and the Adriatic and Byzantine worlds. Paul’s family traditions linked the household to the first Lombard conquest and to subsequent Avar violence.

His first schooling probably occurred in Cividale. In *Sensi cuius*, written in old age, he says that as a *puerulus* he learned only “three or four syllables” of Greek and Hebrew and that the rest had escaped him. The phrase is deliberately comic and self-deprecating, since the same poem offers a small translation display. It establishes exposure, not mastery. Capo finds no proof that Patriarch Callistus of Aquileia personally directed his education, nor that Paul belonged to the court of Duke Pemmo.

Paul’s childhood influences included:

- family oral history and Lombard genealogical memory;
- Christian Scripture and liturgy;
- late-antique school grammar and rhetoric;
- the legal and political culture of the Lombard kingdom;
- a regional environment in which Greek remained useful, though the extent of Paul’s competence is uncertain.

No childhood illness, injury, psychological crisis, or early bereavement is securely recorded.

## Education and Formation at Pavia

### Arrival and royal-court schooling

Paul was probably brought to the royal palace at Pavia in the later reign of Liutprand or by the accession of Ratchis in 744. His pupil’s epitaph says he was raised in the palace and later directed by King Ratchis toward *sacra sophia*, sacred learning. Paul himself says in *Historia Langobardorum* II.28 that he had been at the palace. Pavia’s topography and court traditions are described with firsthand familiarity in his works.

### Teacher Flavian

Paul’s teacher is named **Flavian** (*Flavianus*), a grammarian. His identity is uncertain. He may have belonged to a family of palace teachers connected with the earlier grammarian Felix, or “Flavian” may preserve a name without enough context for secure identification.

### Curriculum

His education combined:

- Latin grammar, morphology, and syntax;
- classical and late-antique poetry;
- biblical and patristic literature;
- rhetoric and the techniques of verse composition;
- historical epitomes and chronography;
- some law, inferred from his knowledge of Justinian’s *Corpus iuris*, Lombard legislation, royal enactments, and multiple manuscripts of Lombard law;
- rudimentary Greek and perhaps Hebrew.

Paul’s Latin is learned and at times archaizing, but also absorbs Christian and spoken forms. His theology is less systematic than his grammar and historical learning. Capo notes imprecision in his treatment of Monothelitism and the Three Chapters controversy; this may reflect limited formal theological training, the confusion of his sources, or his tendency not to make doctrinal precision the sole criterion of salvation.

### Entry into the clergy

Paul became a **deacon**, probably while connected to the church of Pavia and before entering Monte Cassino. There is no secure ordination year, no proof of ordination at Aquileia, and no sound basis for the occasionally repeated claim “ordained in 782.” The stable contemporary-style designation *Paulus levita/diaconus* indicates a real clerical grade, not a literary nickname.

## Early Career: Lombard Courts and Southern Italy

### Relationship with Desiderius

Older accounts often call Paul King Desiderius’s secretary, chancellor, or adviser. His access to the royal family is certain; a formal chancery office is not. The critical evidence supports the following narrower claims:

- Paul was trained in the royal environment.
- He became tutor or learned adviser to Desiderius’s daughter **Adelperga**.
- His writings show knowledge of royal ideology, law, and Pavia.
- Later authors’ description of him as a chancery official may be inference or legend.

### Adelperga and Arechis II

Adelperga married **Arechis II**, duke of Benevento, around 758–762. Paul followed or later joined her in southern Italy and remained connected with the Beneventan court for a long period, plausibly from the early 760s to his departure for Francia in 781/782, though residence was not necessarily continuous.

Adelperga was not a passive dedicatee. Paul’s preface to the *Historia Romana* describes her as an eager reader who found Eutropius’s *Breviarium* too abbreviated and deficient in sacred history. Paul expanded the text for her, integrated Christian and pre-Roman material, and continued it beyond Eutropius. Their relationship is among the clearest examples of a learned woman acting as reader, critic, and patron in the eighth-century West.

Arechis II likewise appears as a politically and culturally ambitious patron. Paul wrote for the ducal buildings at Salerno, praised the transfer and cult of St. Mercurius, and eventually composed Arechis’s epitaph after the duke’s death in 787.

### First major compositions

- **763:** *A principio saeculorum* / *Versus de annis a principio saeculorum*, an acrostic chronological poem whose initials form “Adelperga pia.”
- **c. 766–774:** *Historia Romana*, sixteen books, adapting Eutropius and continuing the narrative through the Gothic war and the age of Justinian.
- **Uncertain dates before 782:** verses on Lake Como; the epitaph for Sophia; inscriptions and poems for Benevento and Salerno; praise and epitaphic poems for the ducal and royal circle.

### The Frankish conquest of 774

Charlemagne conquered the Lombard kingdom and took Pavia in 774. Paul’s exact location during the siege is unknown. There is no evidence that he fought, organized resistance, was tried, or was imprisoned after the conquest. His southern association and possible earlier monastic profession make the common picture of a royal courtier fleeing directly from Pavia too simple.

Paul’s political response developed over time. His Salerno verses contrast Arechis’s Christian city with the predatory magnificence of pagan Rome and reflect southern Lombard resistance to Frankish and papal expansion. Later writings accept Charlemagne’s kingship in Italy and incorporate Carolingian purposes without erasing Lombard historical identity.

## Monte Cassino: Profession, Teaching, and Institutional Role

The date of Paul’s monastic profession is one of the central biographical disputes.

### Competing reconstructions

1. **Early profession, before 774:** Ilderic’s epitaph presents monastic conversion as the culmination of Paul’s Lombard-world career and does not connect it with the Frankish conquest. Capo therefore argues that Paul probably became a monk before 774 while continuing relations with the Beneventan court.
2. **Profession after Ratchis’s retirement:** Some older writers link Paul’s entry to King Ratchis’s monastic retirement at Monte Cassino in 749 or 757. Direct evidence is lacking.
3. **Profession after the conquest, c. 774–776:** Another common reconstruction makes monastic life a response to the fall of the kingdom or even a Frankish punishment. No conviction or banishment order supports the punitive version.

The best formulation is: **Paul was certainly a monk of Monte Cassino by the early 780s and may well have entered before 774; the exact date and immediate motive are unknown.**

At Monte Cassino he worked with Abbot **Theodemar** and became a major teacher. The Neapolitan episcopal history reports that Bishop Stephen sent clerics to study with “Paul the deacon” at the monastery. Paul’s school activity likely included elementary grammar, textual correction, and the organization of books. His *Expositio Artis Donati* is an expanded school version of Donatus’s *Ars minor*, though whether it was composed at Monte Cassino before 782 or in the Frankish orbit remains debated.

Monte Cassino had recently been rebuilt and was becoming a leading intellectual center. Paul’s role was not that of abbot, bishop, proprietor, or independent institution-builder. It was that of learned monk, teacher, writer, and broker between the abbey, southern Lombard patrons, Rome, and the Carolingian court.

## Family Crisis and Journey to Charlemagne, 776–783

### Revolt and captivity

In 776 Duke Hrodgaud/Rotgaud of Friuli rebelled against Frankish rule. Charlemagne suppressed the revolt. Paul’s brother Arechis was taken away into Francia. The sources do not establish that Paul himself participated in the revolt. His poem assumes collective family responsibility in order to plead for mercy, not to confess a personal crime.

### *Verba tui famuli*

In spring 782 Paul sent or presented Charlemagne with a twenty-eight-line elegiac petition. The [Latin text](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/pauldeacon/carmina.shtml) says that the brother was captive, naked, and needy; his wife begged in the streets for four children; the sister had nearly blinded herself with weeping; household goods had been seized; and nobility had given way to poverty. Paul acknowledges the justice of punishment, then asks the king to restore the captive to homeland and civic fields with modest property.

The poem is both evidence and performance:

- **Documented:** Paul petitioned the king on behalf of his brother and family.
- **Strongly supported:** the petition eventually succeeded, though the precise act and date of release are unknown.
- **Advocacy language:** the complete destitution may be literal, conventionally intensified, or both.
- **Not established:** Paul was himself a prisoner, rebel, defendant, or convicted conspirator.

### Route and first audience

Paul obtained permission from Theodemar to travel north. He first received hospitality from an unnamed abbot. A first meeting with Charlemagne at Rome during Easter 781 is possible but not certain. By 782 Paul was in the Frankish realm; by January 783 he wrote to Theodemar from the royal environment.

## Charlemagne’s Court, c. 782–786/787

### Status at court

Paul was not a modern salaried civil servant with a fixed office, nor can the court be reduced to Aachen. Charlemagne’s court was itinerant. Paul stayed at monasteries and moved in and out of the royal circle. In his January 783 letter to Theodemar, he complained that palace life felt like prison and longed to return to Monte Cassino. This is evidence of discomfort and dependence, **not a record of judicial imprisonment**.

The king treated him favorably and retained his services after the family petition. Paul became a respected but ambivalent member of the learned network often called the Carolingian Renaissance.

### Court network

- **Charlemagne:** conqueror of the Lombard kingdom, recipient of Paul’s plea, patron, poetic interlocutor, and recipient of later scholarly works.
- **Peter of Pisa:** grammarian, probably known from the Lombard court, who exchanged verse with Paul and acted as the king’s poetic voice.
- **Paulinus of Aquileia:** Friulian churchman and intellectual in the Carolingian orbit; a colleague rather than demonstrable intimate friend.
- **Angilram of Metz:** bishop and royal archchaplain who commissioned Paul’s history of the bishops of Metz.
- **Adalhard of Corbie:** recipient of a collection of Gregory the Great’s letters revised or copied by a “Paul the suppliant,” probably Paul the Deacon, though the degree of Paul’s autograph contribution remains disputed.
- **Alcuin:** entered Charlemagne’s service during Paul’s later court period. They belonged to the same program, but a close personal friendship or rivalry is not documented. Later narratives that make Alcuin’s superiority drive Paul away are conjectural.
- **Royal women:** Paul wrote epitaphs for members of Charlemagne’s family, including the infant Hildegard, and engaged with the projected Byzantine marriage of the king’s daughter Rotrude. The claim that he was formally Rotrude’s Greek teacher is doubtful, since Paul himself mocked the idea that he knew enough Greek to train an embassy.

### Poetic exchange and self-presentation

Peter, writing for the king, praised Paul as comparable to Homer, Horace, Virgil, and other authorities and asked him to teach Greek. Paul’s *Sensi cuius* rejects the praise as irony. He says he knows neither Greek nor Hebrew, has no gold, silver, purse, or wealth, and can offer only affection. He compares pagan writers to dogs following wrong paths and insists that letters are a means of livelihood rather than vainglory.

The poem is not a transparent confession of incompetence. It combines monastic humility, irritation, courtly game, Christian polemic, and social negotiation. It shows that Paul initially felt alien to the competitive praise culture of the court while also participating skillfully in it.

### *Liber de episcopis Mettensibus*, c. 783–784

At Angilram’s request Paul wrote the *Liber de episcopis Mettensibus* (also *Gesta episcoporum Mettensium*), the earliest influential episcopal history north of the Alps. It traced Metz from the supposed Petrine disciple Clement through Chrodegang and linked the Carolingian family to St. Arnulf of Metz. The text supplied a sacred genealogy for the ruling house and elevated Metz’s institutional status.

Whether to call it “propaganda” depends on definition. It served dynastic and ecclesiastical legitimation, but Damien Kempf’s modern edition argues that it was not merely royal propaganda; it also advanced Metz’s own position within the kingdom. Late-tenth- or eleventh-century interpolation altered the Clement section, so not every medieval copy preserves Paul’s original text. The [Peeters edition description](https://www.peeters-leuven.be/detail.php?id=4000) summarizes the political and textual issues.

### Other court-period or court-connected work

- poems, riddles, epigrams, dedicatory verses, and royal-family epitaphs;
- possible composition or revision of *Expositio Artis Donati*;
- research in fuller copies of Pliny, Gregory of Tours, Bede, Gregory the Great, and possibly Fredegar;
- beginnings of work later used in the *Vita Gregorii* and *Historia Langobardorum*;
- participation in the correction and transmission of texts.

Paul was important to the Carolingian program, but the evidence does not make him the sole architect of the palace school, Caroline minuscule, standardized Latin, or the entire ecclesiastical reform.

## Return to Italy and Final Monte Cassino Period, c. 786/787–797/799

Paul returned south by 787. Proposed stops at Corbie, Metz, Rome, Cividale, and Pavia cannot all be dated with confidence. A journey through Friuli with his released brother is attractive but unproved. A letter to Adalhard mentions travel in the vicinity of Corbie and a period of illness from September to Christmas; its itinerary is difficult to reconcile with older reconstructions and may indicate that some editorial work was completed in Francia.

He was certainly back in the southern sphere after Arechis II died on 26 August 787, for whom he composed *Lugentum lacrimis populorum*, a major epitaph.

### Illness

Paul reports a serious illness lasting from September to Christmas in connection with delayed work on Gregory’s letters. The exact year and diagnosis are unknown. No chronic disease, disability, addiction, or psychiatric condition is documented.

### Scholarly and liturgical projects for Charlemagne

Even after leaving Francia, Paul remained linked to the king.

#### Epitome of Festus

Probably at Monte Cassino, Paul abridged Pompeius Festus’s twenty-book *De verborum significatione*, itself an epitome of Verrius Flaccus. He dedicated the work to Charlemagne and emphasized its value for understanding Rome and Latin. He omitted much antiquarian detail and privileged material useful for grammar and schools. The result became the principal medieval vehicle for much of Festus. Authorship was later forgotten or disputed and was only firmly reidentified by early modern and modern philology.

#### Benedictine Rule

Charlemagne requested a reliable copy of the Rule of Benedict from Monte Cassino. A letter sent in Abbot Theodemar’s name may have been dictated by Paul, and Paul may have helped copy or annotate the text. The evidence does **not** support the old attribution to Paul of a full commentary on the Rule. The specialist [Geschichtsquellen entry](https://geschichtsquellen.de/autor/4242) explicitly identifies that commentary as long misattributed.

#### Homiliary

At Charlemagne’s request, Paul compiled a two-part homiliary for the night office and church year. It collected patristic homilies and excerpts, traditionally counted as 244 items, arranged in winter and summer volumes. Charlemagne’s prefatory *Epistola generalis* authorized its dissemination as a corrected alternative to corrupt sermon collections.

Modern manuscript research has considerably revised earlier reconstructions. Zachary Guiliano identified more than eighty Carolingian witnesses and evidence of use in at least thirty-three monasteries, cathedrals, and churches. The collection was completed in the late 790s, around 797, and remained influential for centuries. The [Brepols description of Guiliano’s study](https://www.brepols.net/products/IS-9782503577913-1) stresses its theological, liturgical, and institutional importance.

### *Vita Gregorii Magni*

Paul wrote a life of Pope Gregory the Great, probably between 782 and 787 or shortly thereafter, using Gregory’s own works, Gregory of Tours, and Bede. A ninth-century interpolated recension added legends and circulated even more widely than Paul’s original. Later readers often treated the enlarged text as entirely his; modern textual criticism separates BHL 3639, Paul’s work, from the expanded BHL 3640.

### *Historia Langobardorum*

Paul’s final and most famous work was written at Monte Cassino after his Frankish sojourn. He explicitly locates himself at the monastery in Books I and VI. Book I appears to have been composed before 796 because it speaks of Avar dominion as still current; that terminus may not date every book.

The work has six books:

1. legendary northern origins, migrations, wars, entry into Pannonia;
2. Alboin and the conquest of Italy;
3. the age of Authari, Theodelinda, Gregory the Great, Franks, and Byzantines;
4. seventh-century kings, duchies, church affairs, and Paul’s family history;
5. civil wars and the rise of later dynasties;
6. the kingdom through Liutprand’s death in 744.

Its source base includes the *Origo gentis Langobardorum*, Rothari’s law-code prologue, Lombard laws, the lost history of Secundus of Trent, Gregory the Great, Gregory of Tours, Bede, the *Liber pontificalis*, legal and archival material, inscriptions, oral traditions, court memory, family memory, and sources now lost.

The *Historia* is neither a neutral archive dump nor simple propaganda. Paul selects, rearranges, critiques, and sometimes refuses traditions. He calls the pagan Wotan/Frea naming story “a ridiculous fable” yet preserves it. He uses Roman, Christian, Lombard, Frankish, and local frameworks while making the Lombard *gens* the narrative protagonist.

### Deliberate ending or unfinished work?

The narrative stops with Liutprand in 744, before the final rulers and the conquest of 774.

- **Deliberate-ending thesis:** Paul chose an exemplary high point and refused or saw no need to narrate the humiliating end of independent Lombard rule.
- **Unfinished-work thesis:** the lack of dedication, prologue, and epilogue; declining linguistic finish in later books; forward reference to material never supplied; and formal irregularities indicate that death interrupted the work.
- **Capo’s position:** the claim that Paul simply could not bear to narrate the conquest is inadequate. Elsewhere he had already discussed Lombard failures and papal responsibility. The text looks not fully released by its author and probably anticipated continuation.

The question remains unresolved. It should not be converted into a psychological fact about Paul.

## The Cynocephali Passage: Text, Context, and Interpretation

### What Paul says

The episode occurs in *Historia Langobardorum* I.11 during the legendary migration period. The Lombards, few in number, want to pass from Scoringa into Mauringa but are blocked by the Assipitti. Paul writes that necessity suggested a plan. In William Dudley Foulke’s translation:

> “They pretend that they have in their camps Cynocephali, that is, men with dogs’ heads.”

The passage continues that the Lombards spread a report that these fighters wage stubborn war, drink human blood, and drink their own blood when they cannot reach an enemy. They enlarge their apparent camp with widely spread tents and many fires. The Assipitti believe what they hear and see and abandon their threatened attack. The complete English context is available in the [1907 translation, Book I, chapter 11](https://elfinspell.com/MedievalMatter/PaultheDeacon-PaulusDiaconus/HistoryOfTheLangobards/PaulTheDeacon-Book1.html), and the critical Latin is accessible in the [1878 Waitz edition](https://archive.org/details/paulihistorialan00paul).

### What it does **not** say

- Paul does not state that actual dog-headed men existed in the Lombard army.
- He does not call them a normal vanguard unit.
- He does not claim personal observation.
- He does not locate the event in eighth-century Italy or Charlemagne’s wars.
- He presents the Cynocephali as the content of a **fabricated rumor** within an ancestral tale.

### Narrative function

The episode is a stratagem: an outnumbered migrating people manipulates enemy perception through a monstrous reputation and deceptive camp scale. It is followed by another ordeal, single combat against an Assipitti champion, and then by emancipation of enslaved persons to enlarge the Lombard fighting body. The sequence turns weakness into survival through rumor, courage, incorporation, and mobility.

Emanuele Piazza’s study, [“Guerre e inganni in età longobarda”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ista_0000-0000_2021_act_1516_1_3859), places the episode within Paul’s repertoire of military deception rather than treating it as zoological reportage.

### Source and historicity

The immediate written source is not securely identified. The *Origo gentis Langobardorum* supplies other migration material but not necessarily this developed Cynocephali stratagem. Paul may have preserved court or oral epic tradition, adapted wider ethnographic lore, or combined them. Mauringa and the Assipitti cannot be securely mapped; older identifications with Elbe, Baltic, Usipetes, or specific German districts are conjectural.

The historical claim that a real Lombard migration party used exactly this deception is unverified. The passage’s historical value lies securely in showing what late-eighth-century Lombard memory and Paul’s literary culture found intelligible: dog-headed peoples belonged to the available ethnographic imagination, and their imagined blood-drinking ferocity could function as psychological weaponry.

### Wider Cynocephali tradition

Cynocephali circulated from ancient Greek and Roman ethnography through late-antique and medieval geography, hagiography, exegesis, maps, and stories of distant peoples. Paul’s use is distinctive because the monsters are not encountered at the world’s edge: the Lombards claim them as allies. Later Carolingian culture also used “dog-head” language for frightening northern enemies, and Ratramnus of Corbie debated whether reported Cynocephali, if rational and social, should be considered human and evangelized. These later texts show the concept’s vitality but do not prove Paul’s story factual or directly cause it.

### Reputation effect

Modern retellings often compress the distinction between “the Lombards claimed to have” and “the Lombards had.” This converts Paul’s subtle account of rumor into a literal monster story. The original passage is more revealing: the Cynocephali exist in the narrative as a believed story whose belief changes military behavior. Myth is the weapon.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Lombard identity

Paul’s identity remained deeply Lombard. He calls the Lombards his people, inserts his family into their history, preserves vernacular terms and traditions, and treats their kingdom as a serious political and cultural project. This does not require interpreting him as an anti-Frankish nationalist in a modern sense.

### Rome and Italy

His view of Rome changed with genre and political moment:

- In the *Historia Romana*, Roman history is integrated into a Christian and Italian continuum into which the Lombards could fit.
- The Salerno verses condemn pagan Rome’s grandeur as the product of world plunder and contrast it with Arechis’s Christian construction.
- In works for Charlemagne, Roman antiquity becomes part of the king’s inherited realm and learning.
- In the *Historia Langobardorum*, Rome is once the head of the world, but papal temporal interventions are treated with suspicion.

### Papacy

Paul was a Catholic cleric and hagiographer of Gregory the Great, but he did not automatically endorse the territorial politics of eighth-century popes. In *Historia Langobardorum* IV.29 he indirectly criticizes popes whose actions contributed to Christian bloodshed and the fall of the Lombard kingdom. He largely omits papal territorial claims from the political frame. This is a source position, not evidence of heresy or hostility to the Church as such.

### Charlemagne and political accommodation

The conquest harmed Paul’s family, and his early southern poetry reflects opposition to expansion. Personal gratitude after his brother’s release, intellectual experience in Francia, and recognition of the new regime gradually reshaped his position. His Metz history, Festus epitome, homiliary, and continued correspondence supported Carolingian rule and reform. The *Historia Langobardorum* can therefore be read as an attempt to secure Lombard memory within a Carolingian present, though scholars disagree about how directly the Frankish court commissioned or shaped it.

### Monastic ideal

Paul presents learning as service rather than autonomous glory. His attachment to Monte Cassino, praise of Benedict, longing for cloister, textual correction, and teaching all fit a Benedictine intellectual vocation. His verse nevertheless shows mastery of classical form and active participation in elite literary exchange.

## Institutions, Projects, and Material Interests

### Institutions

- Lombard royal palace, Pavia — education and royal-family connections.
- Beneventan ducal/princely court — patronage, tutoring, histories, inscriptions, and poetry.
- Monte Cassino — monastic home, school, library, scriptorium, final residence.
- Charlemagne’s itinerant court — literary, grammatical, dynastic, and reform projects.
- Church of Metz — episcopal history and Carolingian genealogy.
- Corbie network — transmission of Gregory the Great’s letters.
- Frankish monasteries, cathedrals, and churches — later users and copyists of the homiliary.

### Technologies and projects

Paul invented no documented mechanical technology and held no patent. His practical technologies were textual:

- abridgement and excerpting;
- correction and collation of manuscripts;
- school grammar;
- chronological tables and acrostic verse;
- homiliary architecture across the liturgical year;
- dynastic genealogy;
- historical synthesis from oral and written sources;
- controlled dissemination through court and monastic networks.

### Finances

No salary, benefice income, tax exemption, property inventory, or estate is documented for Paul. His texts refer to:

- inherited family standing and property destroyed or confiscated after 776;
- lack of money, purses, and scribes, sometimes in conventional humility language;
- dependence on monastic hospitality and patronage;
- book production as costly labor requiring exemplars and copyists;
- letters as a means of livelihood, as he says in verse, though this need not mean a market wage.

## Controversies, Allegations, and Disputed Historical Claims

No evidence has been found for criminal prosecution, fraud, corruption, money laundering, sexual scandal, abuse, intelligence activity, or a lawsuit involving Paul. The relevant controversies are historiographical and political.

### Participation in rebellion

- **Documented:** his brother was captive after Hrodgaud’s revolt.
- **Not documented:** Paul’s own participation.
- **Later inference:** monastic retirement as punishment for rebellion.
- **Assessment:** unsupported by a charge, judgment, or contemporary narrative.

### Secretary or chancellor of Desiderius

- **Story:** Paul held a formal chancery post.
- **Evidence:** court education, royal-family access, knowledge of law and politics.
- **Against certainty:** no appointment document or contemporary title.
- **Assessment:** possible, not established.

### Imprisoned by Charlemagne

- **Story:** Charlemagne held Paul at court as a prisoner.
- **Evidence:** Paul compared palace life to imprisonment while waiting on royal mercy.
- **Against literal reading:** he traveled with monastic permission, received hospitality, wrote and moved within the court network, and suffered no documented sentence.
- **Assessment:** rhetorical self-description, not a legal imprisonment record.

### Carolingian propagandist

- **Documented:** Paul wrote dynastic genealogy for Metz, served royal educational and liturgical projects, dedicated works to Charlemagne, and accepted the new political order.
- **Qualification:** he retained Lombard identity, criticized papal policy, wrote for southern Lombard patrons, and did not simply erase the conquered kingdom.
- **Assessment:** some works performed legitimating functions; “mere propagandist” is reductive.

### Historical reliability

Paul sometimes conflates chronology, accepts miracle stories, preserves legends, misstates doctrine, and integrates oral traditions. He also criticizes sources, flags fables, reconciles contradictions, consults laws and documents, and preserves otherwise lost material. Claims must be evaluated episode by episode.

### The endpoint of the *Historia*

Deliberate refusal, literary design, and unfinished manuscript remain competing explanations. No final authorial statement settles them.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. The Lombard Cynocephali

1. **Story:** the Lombards said dog-headed warriors in their camp fought relentlessly and drank human or their own blood.
2. **Appearance:** *Historia Langobardorum* I.11, written late eighth century, recounting legendary migration time.
3. **Circulation:** Paul preserved the story; later manuscripts, translations, monster studies, and popular history repeated it.
4. **Evidence:** the text proves the story existed by Paul’s time. It explicitly identifies the Cynocephali claim as a pretense. There is no independent evidence for actual dog-headed beings or the exact stratagem.
5. **Reputation:** it has made Paul a recurring source in the history of monsters and medieval ethnography; popular retellings often incorrectly literalize the deception.

### 2. Wotan, Frea, the Winnili women, and the “Longbeards”

1. **Story:** Frea advises Winnili women to bind their hair like beards; Wotan sees them and names them Longobards, thereby granting victory.
2. **Appearance:** earlier in *Historia Langobardorum* I, derived substantially from the *Origo gentis Langobardorum*.
3. **Circulation:** Lombard origin tradition, then Paul and later histories.
4. **Evidence:** evidence for an origin myth, not for a divine event. Paul calls it a ridiculous fable and substitutes an etymology from long beards.
5. **Reputation:** central to Lombard ethnic memory and modern representations of Lombard women, paganism, and naming.

### 3. Paul could not bear to narrate 774

1. **Story:** grief or shame prevented him from describing Charlemagne’s conquest.
2. **Appearance:** medieval continuation tradition and modern romantic-national interpretations.
3. **Circulation:** biographies and introductions emphasizing Paul as the elegist of a defeated people.
4. **Evidence:** the *Historia* stops at 744, but formal incompleteness and unfulfilled forward references point to interruption. Paul discussed the kingdom’s decline elsewhere.
5. **Reputation:** powerful image of the conquered historian; psychologically plausible but unproved.

### 4. Paul as inventor of “do–re–mi”

1. **Story:** Paul wrote *Ut queant laxis*, whose successive phrases begin UT–RE–MI–FA–SOL–LA; Guido of Arezzo used them for solmization, making Paul the indirect inventor of note names.
2. **Appearance:** the hymn predates or belongs near 800; attribution to Paul is first securely traceable much later, in the twelfth-century Cassinese tradition. Guido’s use is eleventh century.
3. **Circulation:** musical histories, church education, popular articles, recordings.
4. **Evidence:** the hymn certainly underlies the syllables used by Guido. Paul’s authorship is **traditional and disputed**. The Canterbury hymnological notice says the attribution is often made and dates it to the twelfth century. Recent stylometric/philological work argues Paul is the strongest known candidate but stops short of unequivocal proof. A [Cambridge music-history article](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/early-music-history/article/singing-horace-in-antiquity-and-the-early-middle-ages/5BFBDB7C34DC8AE3311BF281291B7B05) treats the poem within Paul’s Horatian context; the [Canterbury Dictionary of Hymnology](https://hymnology.hymnsam.co.uk/u/ut-queant-laxis) preserves the attribution caveat.
5. **Reputation:** it gives Paul a vast musical afterlife often presented with more certainty than the manuscripts permit.

### 5. Paul as Charlemagne’s captive Greek master

1. **Story:** Charlemagne forcibly retained a famous Greek scholar to teach the royal household.
2. **Appearance:** built from the palace-prison metaphor and Peter of Pisa’s request that Paul teach Greek for Rotrude’s Byzantine marriage project.
3. **Circulation:** compressed biographies of the Carolingian Renaissance.
4. **Evidence:** Paul mocked his own Greek as a few childhood syllables and warned that clerics trained only by him would be like mute statues. Court retention was real; legal captivity and Greek mastery are not.
5. **Reputation:** magnifies both Charlemagne’s cultural recruitment and Paul’s learning.

### 6. The legendary Cassinese Paul

From the tenth century into the early modern period, southern Italian monastic tradition expanded Paul into an exemplary founder-teacher, authoritative commentator, hymn writer, and universal scholar. This helped preserve his memory but also attached texts to him that modern philology rejects or questions, especially a full commentary on the Benedictine Rule, parts of the enlarged *Vita Gregorii*, hymns, poems, and possibly the Latin *Vita Mariae Aegyptiacae*.

### Letters

The [specialist letter catalogue](https://geschichtsquellen.de/werk/3885) identifies six secure or attributed letters:

1. dedication of *Historia Romana* to Adelperga;
2. letter to Abbot Theodemar promising a swift return to Monte Cassino;
3. dedication of the Festus epitome to Charlemagne;
4. letter by “Paulus supplex” to Adalhard of Corbie sending Gregory the Great’s letters—probably Paul, autograph contribution disputed;
5. letter in Theodemar’s name to Charlemagne accompanying the Benedictine Rule—possibly dictated by Paul;
6. a letter from a “Paul” to an unnamed priest—attribution uncertain.

### Poetry, inscriptions, epitaphs, and verse letters

The corpus is textually unstable. Dümmler printed 56 items in 1881; Neff printed 41 with 11 doubtful pieces in 1908; Alessandro Russo’s 2024 critical edition prints 34 items and two *dubia*. The [current specialist poetry catalogue](https://geschichtsquellen.de/werk/3884) should be preferred to older blanket attributions.

Principal secure or widely accepted items include:

- *A principio saeculorum* / *Versus de annis a principio saeculorum* (763), acrostic for Adelperga and Arechis;
- *Versus in laudem Larii* / *Ordiar unde tuas laudes* (praise of Lake Como);
- *Roscida de lacrimis* (epitaph for niece Sophia);
- *Verba tui famuli* (petition to Charlemagne, c. 782);
- *Sensi cuius verba* (verse reply to Peter of Pisa/Charlemagne, c. 782);
- *Paule, sub umbroso* and *Sic ego suscepi* (courtly exchange with Charlemagne);
- *Cynthius occiduas* (court poem, before Queen Hildegard’s death in 783);
- epitaphs for Carolingian women and children, including the infant Hildegard;
- *Aemula Romuleis* and related Salerno building inscriptions for Arechis II;
- *Lugentum lacrimis populorum* (epitaph for Arechis II, after 26 August 787);
- verses on St. Benedict, some incorporated into *Historia Langobardorum* I.26;
- dedicatory verses associated with the homiliary.

### Editorial, scribal, and glossing work—not simple authorship

- **Collectio Pauli:** fifty-four letters of Gregory the Great, copied/revised for Adalhard. Paul did not compose Gregory’s letters. His exact hand in the St. Petersburg manuscript is disputed.
- **Benedictine Rule:** Paul may have arranged copying and added grammatical notes; the Rule is Benedict’s, and the full commentary once attributed to Paul is rejected.
- **Scholia on Isidore:** Vallicelliana glosses may derive from Paul’s Festus work or scholarly notes; the surviving manuscript is later.
- **Homiliary:** Paul selected and arranged patristic texts; authorship remains with the church fathers whose sermons he excerpted.

### Disputed or rejected attributions

- *Ut queant laxis* / *Hymnus in honorem Sancti Iohannis Baptistae*: traditional, plausible to some modern scholars, not secure.
- *Commentarium in Regulam sancti Benedicti*: long attributed, now rejected; associated instead with the Hildemar commentary tradition.
- Expanded *Vita Gregorii* BHL 3640: anonymous ninth-century interpolation, not Paul’s full text.
- Latin translation of Sophronius’s *Vita Sanctae Mariae Aegyptiacae*: attributed in older scholarship; not part of the secure core in current specialist catalogues.
- Various hymns, riddles, fables, and epigrams: attribution must be checked item by item against modern editions.
- Hymn to St. Mercurius: recent scholarship dates the attributed text to the twelfth century, excluding Paul’s authorship.

## Death and Burial

The Monte Cassino necrology records: “On the Ides of April [13 April] died lord Paul, deacon and monk, of venerable memory.” The [Waitz edition’s introductory material](https://archive.org/stream/paulihistorialan00paul/paulihistorialan00paul_djvu.txt) preserves the notice.

The year is absent. Proposals range from 797 to 799, with occasional older dates extending to c. 800. The year 799 became conventional because Paul appears to have died before Charlemagne’s imperial coronation in December 800 and because of later Cassinese tradition; these are arguments from silence, not a dated death record.

Cause of death is unknown. The earlier illness reported in correspondence cannot safely be identified as terminal. No poisoning, violence, accident, execution, or epidemic cause is documented.

Later tradition placed his burial at Monte Cassino near the church of St. Benedict, before the chapter room. Rebuilding and repeated destruction of the abbey erased a securely identifiable grave. There is no formal canonization, feast cult comparable to a saint’s, or authenticated relic tradition centered on Paul.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Medieval reception

Paul’s works began circulating quickly.

- *Historia Langobardorum* survives in at least 115 known manuscripts by one count and more than 100 in current specialist catalogues. Northern Italian manuscripts are early, but use in Naples, Monte Cassino, Salerno, and Farfa also shows rapid southern transmission.
- It was excerpted, continued, and remodeled by writers including Erchempert, Andreas of Bergamo, the *Chronicon Salernitanum*, Landolfus Sagax, John the Deacon of Venice, and other Italian chroniclers.
- *Historia Romana* became an exceptionally successful school and universal-history text; Landolfus Sagax expanded it into the *Historia miscella*.
- The *Vita Gregorii* shaped Gregory’s medieval image, especially through the interpolated recension.
- The homiliary standardized access to patristic preaching across Carolingian institutions and generated derivatives, abbreviations, expansions, and local adaptations for roughly a millennium.
- The Metz book helped establish the *gesta episcoporum* genre and linked Carolingian genealogy to episcopal sanctity.
- Paul’s Festus epitome preserved otherwise lost lexical material while also replacing the fuller ancient author for many medieval readers.

### Humanist and early modern reception

Humanists used and criticized Paul’s Festus epitome. Some blamed the abridger for damaging or displacing ancient learning; identifying which “Paul” made the epitome became a philological problem. Printed editions of the *Historia Langobardorum* and *Historia Romana* made him foundational to early modern reconstruction of post-Roman Italy.

### Nineteenth- and twentieth-century national readings

Germanic, Italian, and regional scholarship variously cast him as:

- historian of a Germanic *Volk*;
- mediator between Germanic and Roman civilization;
- Italian historian of a territorial kingdom;
- Friulian cultural ancestor;
- elegist of a defeated Lombard nation;
- Carolingian court intellectual and propagandist;
- Benedictine guardian of classical learning.

Each image selects real elements but can become misleading when made exclusive.

### Current scholarly interpretation

Recent work concentrates on memory, narrative construction, ethnicity, institutional patronage, manuscript circulation, political accommodation, emotion, liturgy, and the instability of the poetic corpus. Paul is treated less as a transparent eyewitness and more as an author whose choices require analysis. At the same time, his works remain indispensable because so many of his written and oral sources are lost.

### Cultural commemoration

Cividale, Friuli, Benevento, Salerno, Metz, and Monte Cassino all claim parts of his geography. Schools, streets, cultural associations, lectures, museum exhibitions, and heritage programming bear his name or feature his manuscripts. The ninth-century Cividale witness makes his legacy materially visible in the region whose remembered history he embedded in the wider Lombard story.

## Research Limitations and Unresolved Questions

- Exact birth and death years remain unknown.
- “Winfrid” as a birth name is insecure.
- The nature of Paul’s formal role under Desiderius is unknown.
- The date and motive of his monastic profession cannot be fixed.
- His route to and from Francia cannot be reconstructed stop by stop.
- The date and legal form of Arechis’s release are not preserved.
- Paul’s level of Greek and Hebrew is limited and filtered through self-deprecating poetry.
- The degree of autograph work in the St. Petersburg Gregory manuscript is disputed.
- The original endpoint intended for *Historia Langobardorum* is unknown.
- The source of the Cynocephali stratagem is unknown; historical occurrence is unverified.
- The poetic corpus remains fluid, including the authorship of *Ut queant laxis*.
- Cause of death and exact burial location are unknown.

## Atlas Connections

### Aeneas

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Paul narrates Aeneas’s arrival in Italy, war with Turnus, marriage to Lavinia, foundation of Lavinium, and three-year reign over the Latins in *Historia Romana* I, composed for Adelperga around 766–774. [Latin text](https://thelatinlibrary.com/pauldeacon/histrom1.shtml)

### Alexander the Great

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Paul’s *Historia Romana* recounts Alexander’s Macedonian and Asian conquests as part of its pre-Roman and Roman chronological framework. This is literary treatment of a figure dead for more than a millennium, not a personal crossing. [Critical-work record](https://geschichtsquellen.de/werk/3890)

### Archimedes

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** In his account of the Roman capture of Syracuse during the Second Punic War, Paul reports Archimedes’s death while absorbed in geometrical figures. The passage belongs to the ancient-history portion of *Historia Romana*. [Latin text collection](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/pauldeacon.html)

### Hannibal

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Paul treats Hannibal extensively in *Historia Romana*, including the Second Punic War, the invasion of Italy, Roman defeats, and Hannibal’s eventual flight and death. [Latin text collection](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/pauldeacon.html)

### Julius Caesar

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** *Historia Romana* VI narrates Caesar’s civil war, victory over Pompey, Alexandrian campaign, relationship with Cleopatra, and subsequent wars. [Latin text](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/pauldeacon/histrom6.shtml)

### Cicero

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Paul includes Cicero in *Historia Romana*’s narrative of the late Republic, particularly the Catilinarian conspiracy, Cicero’s consulship, and the political violence preceding Augustus’s ascendancy. [Latin text collection](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/pauldeacon.html)

### Cleopatra VII

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Paul writes about Cleopatra in *Historia Romana* VI–VII, first in connection with Julius Caesar and then with Mark Antony and Octavian. His account states that Caesar awarded her the Egyptian kingdom and later describes her defeat. [Books VI](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/pauldeacon/histrom6.shtml) and [VII](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/pauldeacon/histrom7.shtml)

### Jesus of Nazareth

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Paul integrated Christian chronology and sacred history into *Historia Romana*, including Christ’s incarnation, crucifixion, resurrection, and the beginnings of the Church under the Roman emperors. These passages were among the additions that distinguished his Christianized history from Eutropius’s pagan *Breviarium*. [Work description](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/11591b.htm)

### Paul the Apostle

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Paul the Deacon’s expanded Roman history recounts the apostolic age and the activity and martyrdom traditionally assigned to Paul the Apostle under Nero. Paul also selected Pauline material, directly or through patristic exposition, for his late-eighth-century homiliary. [Historia Romana collection](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/pauldeacon.html) and [homiliary study](https://www.brepols.net/products/IS-9782503577913-1)

### Peter the Apostle

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Paul writes about Peter as apostle and Roman martyr in the Christian continuation embedded in *Historia Romana*. In the *Liber de episcopis Mettensibus* he also presented Metz’s first bishop, Clement, as belonging to a Petrine missionary succession; part of the surviving Clement material was subsequently interpolated. [Edition description](https://www.peeters-leuven.be/detail.php?id=4000)

### Constantine the Great

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** *Historia Romana* narrates Constantine’s accession, wars, imperial rule, conversion-era religious policies, and relationship to the Christian Church. The treatment forms part of Paul’s continuation and Christian recasting of Roman imperial history. [Latin text collection](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/pauldeacon.html)

### Justinian I

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Justinian is a major figure in the final books of *Historia Romana*, whose narrative Paul extended through the Gothic War and approximately 552/553. Paul also discusses Justinian’s reign, councils, legislation, and Italian reconquest in *Historia Langobardorum*. [Work record](https://geschichtsquellen.de/werk/3890) and [Latin *Historia Langobardorum*](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/pauldeacon.html)

### Empress Theodora

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Paul treats Theodora within his account of Justinian’s reign in the concluding portion of *Historia Romana*, including imperial religious and political affairs. [Latin text collection](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/pauldeacon.html)

### Charles Martel

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Paul records Charles Martel in *Liber de episcopis Mettensibus* as a descendant of St. Arnulf’s family and an ancestor of Charlemagne. This genealogy helped connect the Carolingian dynasty to Metz’s episcopal sanctity and institutional history. [Modern edition and description](https://www.peeters-leuven.be/detail.php?id=4000)

### Charlemagne

- **[T3] Documented fact:** Paul addressed writings directly to Charlemagne, including the verse petition *Verba tui famuli* around 782, the dedication of his Festus epitome, poems in the courtly exchange involving Peter of Pisa, and works produced at the king’s request. Charlemagne commissioned or authorized projects including the homiliary and the transmission of a reliable Benedictine Rule. [Poetic-exchange study](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/handle/10023/20278), [letters catalogue](https://geschichtsquellen.de/werk/3885), and [homiliary study](https://www.brepols.net/products/IS-9782503577913-1)
- **[T5] Documented fact:** Paul personally entered Charlemagne’s Frankish court orbit by 782–783 while seeking relief for his captive brother, remained associated with the itinerant royal court until approximately 786–787, and participated in literary exchanges with the king. The precise location of their first audience is uncertain; an Easter 781 meeting at Rome remains a reconstruction rather than a documented fact. [Critical biography](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/paolo-diacono_(Dizionario-Biografico)/)

### Homer

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Paul discusses Homer in the poetic exchange conventionally dated around 782: Peter of Pisa’s court poem compared Paul with Homer and other canonical authors, and Paul’s reply rejected the extravagant comparison while judging pagan literary authority from a monastic-Christian standpoint. [Study and edition of the exchange](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/handle/10023/20278)

### Virgil

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Paul names and evaluates Virgil within the same Carolingian poetic exchange in which classical authors were invoked as measures of his learning. His historical writing also displays direct engagement with Virgilian material, especially traditions concerning Aeneas and Italy. [Poetic-exchange study](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/handle/10023/20278) and [*Historia Romana* I](https://thelatinlibrary.com/pauldeacon/histrom1.shtml)

### Pliny the Elder

- **[T3] Documented fact:** Paul made substantial one-sided textual use of Pliny’s *Natural History*, especially for the geography and antiquarian learning of *Historia Langobardorum*. This is documented literary engagement, not personal contact and not evidence that a particular surviving manuscript passed directly between them. [Source overview](https://elfinspell.com/MedievalMatter/PaultheDeacon-PaulusDiaconus/HistoryOfTheLangobards/PaulTheDeacon-TableOfContents.html)

### Saint Mercurius of Caesarea

- **[T1-] Allegation or theory:** A poem concerning Mercurius’s relics and their transfer into the Beneventan sphere has been assigned to Paul and associated with Arechis II’s promotion of the saint’s cult. The broader Paul–Benevento context is historical, but modern attribution research excludes at least one formerly Paulian hymn to Mercurius as a twelfth-century work; the precise poetic connection therefore should not be treated as uniformly secure. [Current specialist poetry catalogue](https://geschichtsquellen.de/werk/3884)

### Thomas Aquinas

- **[T9+] Documented fact:** Paul lived, taught, wrote, and died at Monte Cassino in the eighth century; Thomas Aquinas was sent there for his early education in the thirteenth century. Their connection is the same Benedictine site across approximately five centuries, without shared occupancy, personal contact, or evidence that Thomas handled a specific object belonging to Paul. [Paul’s institutional biography](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/paolo-diacono_(Dizionario-Biografico)/)

No defensible T2 prophecy or hyperstition connection was found within the supplied roster. Mere chronological overlap, reuse of a general legend such as the Cynocephali tradition, or later residence in the same broad region has not been counted without the textual, personal, or site-specific evidence required by the atlas taxonomy.
