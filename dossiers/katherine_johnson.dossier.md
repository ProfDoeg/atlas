# Katherine Johnson: Research Dossier

**Fullest reconstructable documentary profile prepared 30 August 2026**

> **Name note.** The request supplied the form “ÉKatherine Johnson.” No reliable source consulted records an initial *É* or the spelling *ÉKatherine*. The subject is **Creola Katherine Coleman Goble Johnson** (1918–2020), generally published as **Katherine G. Johnson**. This dossier therefore uses *Katherine Johnson*.

> **Method note.** This is a documentary dossier, not a narrative essay. It distinguishes contemporary or institutional records, Johnson’s own later recollections, family accounts, and dramatization. NASA’s retrospective pages are valuable but not independent of the post-2015 recovery of Johnson’s reputation; where possible, their claims are checked against NASA Technical Reports Server records, legislation, archives, contemporaneous interviews, and the work of space historians. “Computed by hand” means analytic work performed with pencil, tables, slide rule, and desktop mechanical/electromechanical calculating machines—not unaided mental arithmetic.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and household

Katherine was the youngest of four children of Joshua McKinley Coleman and Joylette Roberta Lowe. MacTutor, drawing on genealogical records, reports that Joshua and Joylette married in Danville, Virginia, on 29 September 1909. Joylette, born in 1887 in Caswell County, North Carolina, was the daughter of Lee Lowe and Roberta Johnson and worked as a teacher. Joshua is variously described as a lumberman, farmer, handyman, and employee of the Greenbrier resort hotel. The descriptions are compatible with a working life made up of several forms of labor rather than rival biographies.

Johnson repeatedly credited both parents with treating education as nonnegotiable. Family recollection preserved by NASA and the 2011 WHRO interview says her father had formal schooling only through approximately sixth grade but was exceptionally quick with numbers; she called him the smartest man she knew. Her mother’s work as a teacher supplied an immediate professional model.

The decisive family circumstance was Jim Crow schooling. Greenbrier County did not provide public secondary education for Black children after eighth grade. To educate all four children, the Colemans established a school-year household in **Institute**, approximately 120 miles from White Sulphur Springs, where West Virginia State College operated a laboratory high school. Accounts differ in their shorthand: some say the family moved; others emphasize Joshua’s long drives and that the family divided the year between Institute and White Sulphur Springs. The stable core is that the family incurred substantial travel and household costs to obtain education unavailable locally by race.

### Marriages and descendants

Katherine married **James Francis Goble** in 1939. They had three daughters. Her acceptance into West Virginia University’s graduate program followed the marriage; she withdrew after becoming pregnant and built the next phase of life around family and teaching. The Gobles relocated to Newport News in 1953 when Katherine began work at Langley. James became ill with an inoperable brain tumor and died in December 1956. Katherine was then a working widow supporting three daughters.

In 1959 she married **James A. “Jim” Johnson**, a U.S. Army officer and Korean War veteran. They met through family and church networks; later retellings often stage their courtship more elaborately than the documentary record supports. They remained married for about sixty years, until his death at 93 in March 2019. Public biographies record six grandchildren and eleven great-grandchildren.

There is no substantial evidence in the consulted record of inherited wealth, private businesses, patents held for personal profit, major investments, or dynastic financial structures. The household’s documented economic foundation was salaried professional and military work. Later honoraria, book royalties, film-related arrangements, and estate terms are not publicly itemized in reliable sources consulted. Her descendants established or supported the Katherine Johnson Foundation; this is a legacy and education organization, not evidence that Johnson accumulated unusual private wealth.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1918–1933

Johnson’s autobiographical story begins with counting: dishes, silverware, steps to church, steps to the road, and anything else that could be enumerated. The motif appears repeatedly in NASA education material, interviews, and both memoirs. It is plausible first-person memory but also became the organizing childhood legend of the mature public figure.

She entered school early. Later accounts say she attended kindergarten briefly at four, was advanced to second grade, skipped another grade, entered high school at ten, and finished at fourteen. Exact grade-by-grade paperwork is not reproduced online in the sources consulted, but the age-at-entry and age-at-graduation claims are consistent across the university archive, NASA, family foundation, and memoir-based biographies.

At the West Virginia State laboratory school, one of her formative teachers was **Angie Turner King**, a chemist and mathematician who taught her in high school and later at college. The campus environment placed her among Black teachers and scholars with graduate training at a time when most white institutions excluded them. She later joined **Alpha Kappa Alpha**, whose networks remained socially important through the rest of her life.

## Education and Formation, 1933–1940

### West Virginia State College

Johnson entered West Virginia State College at fourteen. She studied mathematics and French and exhausted the college’s existing mathematics curriculum. Her most consequential mentor was **William Waldron Schieffelin Claytor**, among the first African Americans to earn a Ph.D. in mathematics. Claytor created advanced courses for her, including analytic geometry and aspects of higher mathematics, and told her she would make a good research mathematician. Johnson later remembered asking what such a person did and being told that she would have to find out.

She also studied with Angie Turner King. The combination matters: Johnson was not a miraculous autodidact appearing from nowhere, but a prodigiously capable student formed within an HBCU intellectual institution that deliberately built coursework around her abilities.

She graduated **summa cum laude in 1937**, age eighteen, with a Bachelor of Science and double major in mathematics and French. Some popular accounts call these two separate degrees; the NASA and WVSU records describe a B.S. with majors in both fields.

### West Virginia University

After the U.S. Supreme Court’s 1938 decision in *Missouri ex rel. Gaines v. Canada*, West Virginia moved to open graduate education rather than finance equivalent out-of-state training for Black residents. West Virginia State president **John W. Davis** selected Johnson as one of three Black students, and the only woman, to enter West Virginia University. In 1939 she became the first Black woman admitted to WVU graduate study.

She studied graduate mathematics in Morgantown, including advanced analysis, but left after the first session or academic year when she became pregnant. Sources variously date her attendance to 1939 or 1940 because enrollment crossed academic-year boundaries. She did not earn a graduate degree. Later popular wording sometimes calls her WVU’s first Black female graduate; that is incorrect—she was its first Black female graduate student. The distinction is confirmed by the WVU archive.

## Early Career: Teaching and Interrupted Opportunity, 1937–1953

With research positions largely closed to a young Black woman, Johnson entered teaching, then one of the principal professional avenues available. Her first clearly identified post was in a segregated Black public school in **Marion, Virginia**, where she taught mathematics, French, and music. She left after marrying James Goble and entering WVU, then returned to teaching after leaving graduate school.

The precise school-by-school chronology of the 1940s is not fully fixed in accessible institutional sources. The family moved among teaching posts in Virginia and West Virginia; some biographies refer generally to teaching for more than a decade. She also at times left paid work to raise her children. A sound reconstruction is therefore episodic rather than a falsely exact employment ledger.

In 1952 a relative told the Gobles that NACA’s Langley laboratory was recruiting Black women with mathematical training for the segregated **West Area Computing** unit. Johnson first applied after positions had been filled, then applied successfully the following year. The family’s move to Newport News was a career migration: James found work and Katherine reported to Langley in June 1953.

## Chronological Life History and NASA Career

### 1953–1957: NACA computer and aeronautical flight research

Johnson entered the **National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics**, NASA’s predecessor, in June 1953. Her federal classification was a computer: a mathematician assigned numerical reductions, data analysis, and analytic calculations in support of engineers. She began in the West Area Computing unit supervised by **Dorothy Vaughan**. The office was composed of Black women and was administratively segregated under Virginia’s Jim Crow regime.

Within roughly two weeks, Vaughan assigned Johnson temporarily to the **Maneuver Loads Branch** of the Flight Research Division. Engineers kept her. She analyzed flight-test data, gust loads, and wake turbulence and participated in the investigation of an aircraft crash attributed to wake effects. This early work is sometimes omitted by space-centered biographies, but it occupied approximately four years and situated her in research rather than routine tabulation.

Johnson asked why women were excluded from technical briefings and editorial meetings. Her remembered method was direct: ask whether a rule or law forbade attendance; when told that convention rather than law was the barrier, attend. This was not a single cinematic breakthrough but a repeated negotiation over access to the information required for calculations and to the authorship process that turned work into institutional credit.

Her first husband’s illness and death in December 1956 coincided with the end of this aeronautical phase. She continued federal work while raising three daughters.

### 1957–1960: Sputnik, Space Task Group, and authorship

The Soviet launch of **Sputnik 1** on 4 October 1957 accelerated Langley’s movement into spaceflight. Johnson supplied mathematics for **Notes on Space Technology**, a roughly 600-page internal compendium of lectures by engineers who formed the nucleus of Langley’s space program. She became associated with the Space Task Group and orbital-mechanics work when NACA was reorganized as NASA on 1 October 1958.

Her pivotal early publication was written with engineer **Ted H. Skopinski**. *Determination of Azimuth Angle at Burnout for Placing a Satellite over a Selected Earth Position* (NASA TN D-233, September 1960) derived relationships among launch-site coordinates, burnout position and velocity, orbital inclination, Earth rotation and oblateness, and a desired ground position. The aim was not merely “drawing a trajectory”: it was to select the burnout azimuth that would place a vehicle over a specified Earth position after a chosen number of orbital passages.

Later interviews describe resistance from a supervisor to listing a woman as author. Skopinski reportedly insisted that Johnson had done most of the work and should finish the report. Whatever the exact dialogue, the report itself documents joint authorship and NASA identifies it as the first Flight Research Division research report credited to a woman. It supplied part of the theoretical basis for human orbital planning.

### 1961: Alan Shepard and the first U.S. crewed spaceflight

For **Mercury-Redstone 3/Freedom 7**, launched 5 May 1961, Johnson performed trajectory analysis for Alan Shepard’s suborbital flight. The task involved launch timing, powered and ballistic flight, reentry, and prediction of a splashdown area close enough for recovery forces. NASA and the family foundation say she calculated the trajectory “entirely by hand.” That formulation should be read in period terms: the equations were analytically developed and numerically evaluated by a human using mechanical calculating aids, not run end-to-end by a general-purpose digital trajectory program.

The commonly circulated claim that she alone “sent Shepard into space” is false by implication. Mission design was collaborative and involved large engineering, computing, range, military, and operations teams. Her trajectory analysis was a documented and important component.

### 1962: Echo I and John Glenn’s Friendship 7

Johnson and Gertrude C. Westrick published *The Orbital Behavior of the Echo I Satellite and Its Rocket Casing During the First 500 Days* (NASA TN D-1366, June 1962), analyzing orbital changes caused by drag, solar pressure, and other perturbations.

Before **Mercury-Atlas 6/Friendship 7** on 20 February 1962, NASA used IBM electronic computers to calculate the trajectory from liftoff through orbital flight, retrofire, reentry, and splashdown. Astronaut **John Glenn**, wary of the new machines’ reliability, requested a human check. In Johnson’s later recollection, he told engineers to “get the girl”—meaning her—and said that if her numbers agreed, he was ready to go. She reran the programmed equations using her desktop calculating machine, and the results agreed.

What is firmly supported: Johnson, NASA, her family, Margot Lee Shetterly, and later Smithsonian accounts agree that she checked the electronic computation at Glenn’s request. What is less secure: the viral wording that Glenn “refused to fly” until she checked it, and the exact quotation, are retrospective. Space historian Robert Pearlman’s investigation could not locate a contemporaneous checklist entry or determine exactly which of Glenn’s postponed launch dates prompted the request. The core event is credible; its ultimatum form and dialogue belong to remembered history, not a surviving verbatim 1962 record.

The technical distinction is also important. Johnson did not distrust mathematics performed by computers as such. She translated and checked an electronic implementation of orbital equations, helping establish confidence in digital calculation. Later she worked with electronic computers herself.

### 1963–1968: reentry, interplanetary geometry, optical navigation

Her verified publications show a widening technical program:

- In 1964, with Jack A. White, she derived approximate solutions for the flight-path angle of a reentry vehicle in the upper atmosphere.
- In 1965, with W. Thomas Blackshear, she studied solar-system geometric parameters as interplanetary navigation aids.
- In 1967, with Blackshear and Harold A. Hamer, she developed a midcourse-guidance procedure based on a single onboard optical position fix.

The work moved from Mercury-era Earth orbit toward lunar, Earth-return, and planetary navigation. It emphasized procedures astronauts or onboard systems could use when continuous ground guidance or fully functioning electronics could not be assumed.

NASA group awards associated her with the **Lunar Orbiter Spacecraft and Operations Team**, whose five robotic spacecraft mapped the Moon for Apollo landing-site selection, and the **Apollo Group Achievement Award**. Sources disagree on whether the Lunar Orbiter award was dated 1967 or 1977; the mission series flew in 1966–1967, making 1967 the more contextually likely date, while some later award lists print 1977. This dossier preserves the discrepancy.

### 1969–1972: Apollo, rendezvous, emergency return, and planetary guidance

Johnson later identified her most important contribution not as Glenn’s check but as calculating conditions that synchronized the **Apollo Lunar Module** with the Moon-orbiting **Command and Service Module**. Lunar-orbit rendezvous required launch from the surface at the correct time and into the correct plane and phase so the two vehicles could meet. Her role belonged to a team, but NASA’s biography records the rendezvous calculations as the contribution she herself valued most.

She worked on Apollo 11 trajectory and navigation planning. On 20 July 1969 she was at an Alpha Kappa Alpha gathering in the Pocono Mountains and watched the lunar landing on a small television with others. The film image of her inside a tense control room is not historical.

Her work included backup navigational charts for use if electronic navigation failed. NASA says earlier backup parameters and charts contributed to the safe return of **Apollo 13** after the oxygen-tank explosion in April 1970. Popular children’s accounts sometimes inflate this into Johnson personally “saving Apollo 13” in real time. The stronger formulation is that preexisting navigation and contingency work to which she contributed formed part of the mission’s recovery resources; the live rescue involved mission controllers, flight dynamics officers, contractors, astronauts, and many other specialists.

Her reports from 1970–1972 addressed single-measurement approach guidance, fixed-angle translunar guidance, simplified Moon-to-Earth control, and interplanetary optical navigation. They show sustained theoretical work beyond the few celebrity missions attached to her name.

### 1973–1986: large flexible spacecraft, Landsat, Shuttle, and space-station work

Johnson’s later career extended into **Earth Resources Technology Satellite/Landsat**, the **Space Shuttle**, and concepts for a crewed Mars mission. The family foundation reports that she spent nearly fifteen years on development work related to NASA’s first space-station plans. The accessible public summaries do not provide a complete year-by-year project assignment ledger.

The late technical papers that can be securely identified concern control of large flexible structures in orbit: error propagation in decoupled control systems, control of a long flexible beam, a large flexible space antenna, and comparisons between decoupled and linear-quadratic-regulator methods. These are not Mercury trajectory papers; they reflect the dynamics and controls problems of large future spacecraft and antennas.

Johnson learned and used electronic computation as the occupation changed. The historical arc is therefore not “human genius versus computer” but human mathematical modeling, verification, and judgment migrating into computer-assisted aerospace engineering.

She retired in **August 1986**, after 33 years at Langley. NASA award lists record Langley Special Achievement Awards in 1971, 1980, 1984, 1985, and 1986.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Institutions

- West Virginia State College laboratory school and college: secondary and undergraduate education.
- West Virginia University: graduate mathematics study; first Black female graduate student.
- Segregated Virginia and West Virginia public schools: teacher.
- NACA Langley Memorial Aeronautical Laboratory: West Area Computing; Maneuver Loads Branch; Flight Research Division.
- NASA Langley Research Center: Space Task Group-linked work, guidance and control, Space Mechanics Division and later aerospace systems/control projects.
- Carver Memorial Presbyterian Church: long-term religious and musical community.
- Alpha Kappa Alpha Sorority: lifelong social, educational, and service network.
- National Technical Association: professional/community work; named Johnson its Mathematician of the Year in 1997 according to WVSU.

### Technologies and mathematical methods

- Desktop mechanical/electromechanical calculators, slide rules, tables, and hand-derived analytic equations.
- IBM mainframe trajectory calculations, especially the IBM 7090 era; Johnson independently checked programmed results and later used digital computation.
- Orbital mechanics on a rotating, oblate Earth; burnout azimuth and ground-track placement.
- Atmospheric reentry approximations.
- Satellite perturbation analysis, including drag and solar radiation pressure.
- Onboard optical navigation, single-position fixes, translunar and Moon-to-Earth guidance.
- Lunar-orbit rendezvous and contingency navigation.
- Decoupled and linear-quadratic-regulator control of flexible space structures.

### Missions and projects

Documented or institutionally attributed work includes aeronautical flight testing; wake-turbulence investigation; *Notes on Space Technology*; Project Mercury; Freedom 7; Friendship 7; Echo I; Lunar Orbiter; Apollo lunar rendezvous and Apollo 11; Apollo 13 contingency foundations; ERTS/Landsat; Space Shuttle; early space-station studies; and interplanetary/Mars trajectory studies.

### Patents, businesses, and private finance

No patent in her name, privately owned aerospace company, consulting corporation, or personal commercialization of NASA equations was identified. Technical reports were federal research products. No credible evidence of financial misconduct, bribery, fraud, money laundering, intelligence work, or undisclosed business interests appears in the consulted record.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

Johnson was not a public partisan political operator. Her life nevertheless intersected directly with state policy: segregated schooling; *Gaines*-era graduate-school integration; wartime and Cold War federal expansion; civil-service sex and race hierarchies; and the Space Race. Her method inside Langley was usually described as procedural insistence—asking for the meetings, the data, and the authorship necessary to do the work—rather than public agitation.

She did not describe mathematics as isolated from collective purpose. Her repeated formulation was team-centered: she was proud of her work but “didn’t do anything alone.” This corrects both the older institutional erasure of women’s labor and the newer heroic myth that one exceptional individual performed an entire mission’s mathematics.

Her religious life was durable and public but not doctrinally elaborate in the sources. She belonged to Carver Memorial Presbyterian Church for approximately fifty years, sang in the choir, and met Jim Johnson through church/community connections. After retirement, education, church, sorority, and professional-service networks structured her public life.

Her often-repeated family maxim—“You are no better than anyone else, and no one is better than you”—encapsulated equality without self-effacement. She urged students to master fundamentals, ask questions, and like the work well enough to do it carefully.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, or Financial Questions

No credible source consulted reports that Johnson was arrested, indicted, sued for professional misconduct, investigated for corruption, involved in fraud or money laundering, connected to intelligence services, implicated in political violence, or accused of abuse. Absence of such material is itself the relevant finding; manufacturing a controversy category would distort the record.

The genuine disputes concern **historical attribution and later dramatization**:

1. **Thirteen or twenty-six reports.** A 2017 NASA profile says she authored or coauthored **13** research reports. NASA’s Shetterly biography, WVSU, the family foundation, and the WVU archive say **26**. Thirteen named NASA technical reports can be reconstructed with reasonable confidence from the public catalog and connected bibliographies. The figure 26 may include internal reports, contributions cataloged under variants, or a broader institutional tally. No complete authoritative 26-item list was located. The discrepancy remains unresolved.

2. **Individual versus team credit.** Later publicity often says Johnson “calculated the trajectory” for a mission in the singular. NASA mission design was collective. The documented record supports specific analysis and report authorship, not sole ownership of Mercury, Apollo, or Apollo 13 navigation.

3. **Precision of Apollo claims.** NASA retrospective biographies attribute Apollo 11 trajectory work and Apollo 13 benefit from her backup charts. Public sources consulted do not reproduce task orders or a contemporaneous personal calculation sheet for each viral claim. These are institutional attributions, not fabrications, but should not be expanded into sole-rescuer narratives.

4. **Segregation testimony.** Johnson said she knew segregation existed but did not personally experience the NASA workplace in the same way the film represents it; she used the white restroom when challenged and continued doing so. This does not establish that Langley was unsegregated. NACA maintained segregated West Computing, cafeteria seating, and restroom arrangements, and Black women navigated both formal and informal exclusion. Her individual recollection and the institutional record coexist.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “John Glenn refused to fly until she checked the math”

1. **Story:** Glenn refused to launch until Johnson personally verified the IBM computer’s numbers by hand.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** The core appears in Johnson’s later recollections, NASA biographies, Shetterly’s book, the film, obituaries, and viral social-media versions. The phrasing “get the girl” and “if she says they’re good” is repeatedly attributed to Glenn.
3. **Evidence:** NASA confirms the verification; Johnson remembered it; the request fits astronaut distrust of early mainframes. A later historical inquiry could not establish the exact date, launch attempt, checklist entry, or ultimatum language from contemporary documents.
4. **Assessment:** **Documented retrospective account with uncertain verbatim wording and an amplified refusal motif.**
5. **Effect:** It became the single most memorable symbol of human expertise validating machine computation and of a white male national hero depending on a Black woman mathematician.

### The half-mile run to the “colored” bathroom

1. **Story:** Johnson repeatedly ran half a mile from her office to a segregated restroom until a white director destroyed the sign.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** Created for Theodore Melfi’s 2016 feature film, drawing partly on a bathroom incident involving Mary Jackson in Shetterly’s book.
3. **Evidence:** Johnson explicitly said this did not happen to her as shown; she used the white restroom. NACA’s segregated facilities are historical fact, but NASA had administratively dissolved the segregated computing pools by 1958, before much of the film’s 1961 action.
4. **Assessment:** **Dramatic fiction built from real institutional segregation and another woman’s experience.**
5. **Effect:** The scene made workplace segregation visible to mass audiences but reassigned Jackson’s experience and invented a white managerial savior.

### Al Harrison broke segregation with a crowbar

1. **Story:** Johnson’s director, Al Harrison, smashed the restroom sign and announced that everyone at NASA used the same toilets.
2. **Appearance:** The film.
3. **Evidence:** Harrison is a composite, often associated loosely with Robert Gilruth and other managers; no such crowbar episode occurred.
4. **Assessment:** **Fiction.**
5. **Effect:** It simplified slow institutional change into one decisive act and attracted criticism for a white-savior narrative.

### Johnson, Vaughan, and Jackson as an inseparable trio

1. **Story:** The three women carpooled daily, socialized constantly, and solved parallel 1961 crises together.
2. **Appearance:** The feature film and promotional material.
3. **Evidence:** They worked at Langley and knew one another within overlapping Black professional networks, but their assignments and career milestones occurred at different times. Johnson’s documented carpool companion included Eunice Smith. Vaughan had become supervisor in 1949; Jackson became an engineer in 1958.
4. **Assessment:** **Composite friendship and compressed chronology.**
5. **Effect:** It created a coherent ensemble narrative while obscuring other women and stretching a decade of institutional history into months.

### “She did the math in her head” or “without machines”

1. **Story:** Johnson mentally performed entire mission trajectories or used only pencil and paper.
2. **Circulation:** Headlines, children’s stories, memes, motivational material.
3. **Evidence:** She had exceptional numerical fluency but used desktop calculating machines, tables, slide rules, written derivations, colleagues’ data, and later mainframes. Her published papers are collaborative technical mathematics.
4. **Assessment:** **Heroic simplification.**
5. **Effect:** It makes her brilliance legible but erases the technological ecology and collaborative labor of aerospace calculation.

### “NASA hid her”

The phrase is true in one sense and misleading in another. Johnson’s name appeared on federal technical reports from 1960 onward, she received internal awards, and Black press reported her Mercury contribution. She was not literally secret or erased from every record. Yet technical staff seldom became public celebrities; sex and race affected authorship, promotion, access, and whose work entered national memory. *Hidden Figures* describes delayed cultural recognition more accurately than total documentary invisibility.

### “Katherine Johnson saved Apollo 13”

NASA states that Apollo 13 made use of earlier backup parameters and charts connected to her work. Later retellings convert this distributed preparedness into a lone, real-time rescue. The first statement is an institutional attribution; the second is **hagiographic compression**. Its cultural effect has been to attach an already famous rescue to a recognizable individual, sometimes at the expense of the flight-control teams and other mathematicians.

1. **Katherine Johnson, *Reaching for the Moon: The Autobiography of NASA Mathematician Katherine Johnson*.** Atheneum Books for Young Readers, 2019. A young-readers autobiography published during her lifetime; genuine first-person authorship shaped through normal editorial production.

### Other technical contribution

- **“Notes on Space Technology”** (1958-era internal compilation, approximately 600 pages). Johnson contributed calculations alongside dozens of Langley colleagues. It should not be cataloged as a sole-authored book.
- Additional internal reports or technical contributions may account for the frequently cited total of 26. Without a complete catalog-level list, assigning titles would risk false attribution.

### Speeches, interviews, and correspondence

Johnson gave school and university talks, interviews, and award remarks, but no authoritative collected edition exists. Important preserved audiovisual testimony includes the 2011 WHRO *What Matters* interview, MAKERS/National Visionary Leadership Project interviews, NASA event footage, and post-*Hidden Figures* press interviews. The **Katherine G. Johnson Papers, A&M 4536**, at West Virginia University contain correspondence, family photographs, awards, honorary degrees, clippings, fan mail, and born-digital material; the collection’s bulk dates from 2016–2021 and is open subject to reproduction permissions.

### Scholarly and contextual studies

- **Beverly E. Golemba, *Human Computers: The Women in Aeronautical Research* (1994).** Early study of women computers at Langley.
- **Claire L. Evans, *Broad Band: The Untold Story of the Women Who Made the Internet* (2018).** Broader women-and-computing context; Johnson appears in popular histories of human computation.

No serious hostile biography, criminal exposé, or evidence-based investigative book alleging wrongdoing by Johnson was identified. Critical writing concentrates on the film’s white-savior devices, chronology, and individualization of collective work.

## Later Life, Illness, and Death

After retiring in 1986, Johnson spoke in schools, advised students to pursue mathematics and science, and remained active in church, Alpha Kappa Alpha, and professional/community education. Recognition grew gradually through reference works and internal NASA history, then rapidly after the Medal of Freedom and *Hidden Figures*.

She appeared publicly at the 2016 Hampton premiere and NASA ceremonies. At the 89th Academy Awards on 26 February 2017, Taraji P. Henson, Janelle Monáe, and Octavia Spencer introduced her onstage; the audience gave her a standing ovation. She attended the 2017 opening of the Langley facility carrying her name.

No source consulted documents addiction, psychiatric crisis, major disabling accident, or public scandal. Advanced age increasingly limited travel. Her husband Jim died in March 2019. Johnson died on the morning of **24 February 2020**, at 101, in Newport News. Public institutional notices did not specify a disease or proximate medical cause. Reporting that she died “at home” and “at a retirement home” likely describes the same residential-care setting but cannot be conclusively reconciled from the accessible record.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Johnson’s posthumous standing operates on three levels.

First is the technical archive: named reports on orbit placement, reentry, optical navigation, interplanetary guidance, and flexible-spacecraft control. These permit assessment beyond anecdotes. Her 1960 paper is the documentary center of her early spaceflight reputation; the 1964–1986 reports show a much longer and broader technical career.

Second is institutional recovery. NASA, universities, Congress, the Smithsonian, schools, companies, and the military attached her name to facilities, medals, spacecraft, scholarships, curricula, and consumer objects. The 2024 Congressional Gold Medal ceremony expanded commemoration from four famous women to all women who served as NACA/NASA computers, mathematicians, and engineers in the relevant decades.

Third is mythic memory. Johnson became the personification of exact human judgment at the threshold of electronic computing: the mathematician whose result an astronaut trusted more than a machine. The image is powerful because its core is real, even though later tellings sharpen the wording and isolate her from the team. *Hidden Figures* corrected public ignorance while introducing its own distortions—composite managers, transferred experiences, invented confrontations, and compressed time.

Historical reinterpretation now moves in two directions at once: restoring Johnson’s individual name to work long narrated through astronauts and male engineers, and restoring the collective around Johnson so that celebrity does not erase Vaughan, Jackson, Darden, Smith, Westrick, the West Computers, flight-dynamics groups, and thousands of mission workers. Johnson’s own statement—pride in her work coupled with insistence that she did nothing alone—supports that double correction.
