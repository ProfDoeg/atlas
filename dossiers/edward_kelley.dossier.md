# Edward Kelley: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

This dossier concerns the English alchemist and spirit medium **Edward Kelley**, also known as **Edward Talbot**, who collaborated with John Dee and later pursued an independent career at the court of Rudolf II.

The record is radically uneven. Kelley’s activities from 1582 through early 1589 are unusually well documented because John Dee recorded household events and their purported angelic conferences. Kelley's earlier life is known mostly through seventeenth-century antiquarians, while his final years must be reconstructed from Bohemian administrative records, diplomatic intelligence, correspondence, court petitions, property documents, and retrospective testimony.

The following labels are used throughout:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary manuscript, diary, letter, legal, financial, or administrative evidence.
- **Reported fact:** related by a near-contemporary or generally credible later source but not independently established.
- **Allegation:** a contemporary accusation not demonstrated by a surviving judgment or equivalent evidence.
- **Later tradition:** a story recorded substantially after the supposed event.
- **Legend or myth:** a story elaborated through occult, literary, or popular tradition.
- **Unresolved:** the evidence permits no firm conclusion.

Dates in Dee’s records may involve the difference between English Old Style and continental New Style dating. Modernized place-names are followed, where useful, by historical forms.

## Basic Identifying Information

| Field | Information |
|---|---|
| Principal name | Edward Kelley |
| Variant spelling | Edward Kelly; Latinized as *Eduardus Kelleus*, *Edwardus Kellæus*, or similar forms |
| Early alias | Edward Talbot |
| Later style | Sir Edward Kelley; Edward Kelley of Imany/Imanii/Imamyi; “Baron of Bohemia” in some English accounts |
| Claimed lineage | Kelley represented himself as descended from an Irish house of “Imany” in Connacht, apparently an allusion to Uí Maine; the genealogy is unverified |
| Occupations | Apothecary or medically trained practitioner, alchemical operator, scryer or crystal-gazer, purported spirit medium, estate holder, court-dependent practitioner |
| Birth | Traditionally **1 August 1555**, at approximately 4 p.m. |
| Birthplace | Worcester, England |
| Death | Probably late 1597, possibly around Christmas; **1 November 1597** is also widely given but may mark the end of a prison-accounting period rather than the exact death date |
| Place of death | Hněvín Castle above Most, Bohemia—then Brüx in the Habsburg lands, now Most in the Czech Republic |
| Cause of death | Unresolved. The strongest near-contemporary account says he was injured in an escape attempt and died after drinking water, possibly containing poison. Other accounts say he died of his injuries |
| Religion | Publicly Christian; Kelley and Dee framed their spirit work in Christian language. His precise confessional adherence in Central Europe is uncertain |
| Children | No proven biological children. He became stepfather to Joan Cooper’s children, including the poet Elizabeth Jane Weston |
| Portraiture | The familiar capped and fur-clad portrait is a posthumously disseminated image; the cap became associated with the story that his ears had been cropped |

The birth date and hour derive from a horoscope preserved by Dee and later printed by Elias Ashmole. They are stronger than many other early-life claims, but they are not independently corroborated by a surviving parish baptismal record.

## Names, Titles, and Claimed Status

### Kelley, Kelly, and Talbot

**Documented fact:** When he first entered Dee’s record in March 1582, Kelley called himself **Edward Talbot**. He subsequently used Kelley or Kelly. Why he used Talbot is unknown.

Later writers assumed that Talbot was an alias adopted to conceal criminal punishment or a fraudulent past. That explanation is possible but remains an inference. No surviving Tudor criminal judgment has been located that conclusively identifies Dee’s associate under either name.

### “Sir Edward Kelley”

**Documented fact:** By the 1590s he was addressed as a knight. Rudolf II issued an instrument on **23 February 1590** identifying him as a “golden knight” or *eques auratus*. Dee later referred to “Sir Edward Kelly, Knight, at the Emperor’s Court at Prague.”

The precise legal significance of his associated styles is less certain. Kelley claimed noble Irish descent and used “of Imany.” English reports elevated this to “Baron of Bohemia,” but the evidence more securely supports imperial knighthood and admission to the Bohemian noble estate than a conventional hereditary barony.

### Imany, Imanii, or Imamyi

Kelley claimed descent from a noble “house of Imany” in the county of “Conneghaku,” evidently intended as Connacht. Uí Maine was an actual Gaelic territorial and dynastic designation, but no reliable genealogical evidence connects Kelley to it. The title could have been an attempt to create a noble identity acceptable in the Rudolfine court.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and origins

Kelley’s parents have not been securely identified. The surviving evidence supports Worcester as his birthplace, but not a detailed pedigree.

The claim that he came from Irish nobility is **unverified self-presentation**. No established documentary chain joins the Worcester-born Kelley to the Uí Maine dynasties.

### Siblings

- **Thomas Kelley:** Documented as Edward’s younger brother and confidant. Thomas joined the Dee–Kelley household abroad. He married a woman named Lydia. Dee recorded that Lydia miscarried a girl and, on 19 January 1589 at Třeboň, premature male twins.
- **Elizabeth or Lydia:** Later biographical notices identify a sister in Worcester, though the evidence is confused. William Lilly reportedly knew of a sister living there well into the seventeenth century. Some accounts call her Lydia; others Elizabeth.
- The family relationships beyond Thomas are insufficiently documented.

### Wife: Joan Kelley

**Documented fact:** Kelley married **Joan**, commonly identified as **Joan Cooper**, an English widow. The marriage occurred no later than 1583. Dee consistently called her Mistress Kelley or Joan/Jone Kelley.

Joan travelled with him through northern Europe, Poland, and Bohemia. She participated—at least formally—in the 1587 covenant by which the Kelleys and Dees agreed to hold “all things” in common. During Edward’s imprisonment, she petitioned imperial authorities, managed what remained of the household, and tried to defend the family’s seized property.

A petition described Kelley’s harsh conditions and Joan’s own poverty, saying that he was fed pieces of old cow meat while she lacked enough money to repair her bowl. After his arrest she was confined or closely supervised, probably at Nová Libeň.

### Stepchildren

Joan had children from her earlier marriage:

- **Elizabeth Jane Weston** (also Elisabeth Johanna Westonia, c. 1581–1612), later a recognized Neo-Latin poet. Contemporary and near-contemporary records show that she regarded Kelley as her stepfather and that she and her mother fought in Bohemian courts over property lost after his downfall. She later married the Prague lawyer Johann or John Leo, who assisted the family.
- A son often called **John Francis Weston** or simply John Weston is mentioned in family reconstructions, but his biography is much less secure.

Kelley has sometimes been described as a caring stepfather. Elizabeth’s later attachment to his memory and her role in defending the family property support a meaningful family bond, although idealized accounts should not be treated as independent proof of his private conduct.

### Theodorus Trebonianus Dee and the paternity question

John Dee’s wife Jane gave birth to **Theodorus Trebonianus Dee on 28 February 1588**. Because the wife-sharing covenant occurred during the likely period of conception, later writers have suggested that Kelley was Theodore’s biological father.

This is an **unresolved inference**, not a documented fact. Dee accepted and raised Theodore as his son. No surviving source establishes biological paternity.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1555–1582

### Worcester

Kelley was reportedly born at Worcester on 1 August 1555. Nothing dependable is known about his childhood household, parents, or economic status.

Anthony à Wood later wrote that Kelley acquired adequate grammar-school learning at Worcester and elsewhere. This is plausible: by the 1580s Kelley could operate in learned circles, use Latin, compose or transmit technical material, and participate in complex textual work.

### Possible Oxford education

**Later report:** Wood was told that Kelley spent time at Gloucester Hall, Oxford. Wood could not find him in the matriculation register. He noticed several students named Talbot in 1573 and speculated that Kelley might have entered under that name.

This does not prove attendance. The proposition rests on hearsay plus an attempted identification in the university register. Kelley received no known degree.

### Apothecary training

Later biographers repeatedly call Kelley an apothecary or say that he was trained as one. His practical familiarity with chemicals, furnaces, medicines, and laboratory processes makes some medical or artisanal training plausible, but no apprenticeship record has been securely attached to him.

### Alleged forgery, counterfeiting, and mutilation

The most persistent early-life story says Kelley worked as a scrivener or attorney, forged title deeds, and was pilloried at Lancaster with his ears cropped. Another version substitutes coin counterfeiting for documentary forgery.

The evidence must be separated:

- **Documented fact:** Kelley later used the name Talbot and was portrayed wearing a close-fitting cap.
- **Near-contemporary testimony:** Around 1604, the Bohemian mining specialist Simon Tadeáš Budek described Kelley as having missing ears.
- **Seventeenth-century report:** John Weever wrote that Kelley fled England after losing both ears at Lancaster.
- **Unproven element:** No surviving sentence, indictment, or municipal record has conclusively established the crime, place, and punishment.
- **Interpretive conclusion:** Physical mutilation is supported by near-contemporary testimony; the cause—title-deed forgery, counterfeiting, or something else—remains unresolved.

The common claim that the cap in Kelley’s portrait was specifically intended to hide cropped ears is plausible but not independently documented.

### Walton-le-Dale necromancy

John Weever’s *Ancient Funerall Monuments* (1631) relates that Kelley and Paul Waring went by night to Walton-le-Dale in Lancashire, exhumed a newly buried corpse, and compelled it to speak about hidden money and a gentleman’s future.

Evidentiary status:

- The story was published more than three decades after Kelley’s death.
- Weever named Waring as participant and apparent informant, but the supposed client and corpse have never been identified.
- No known diary, legal proceeding, parish record, or contemporary newspaper verifies the episode.
- Dee was later inserted into some retellings, although Weever’s story does not establish his presence.
- The episode is therefore **later reported tradition**, not documented necromancy.

It became one of the most durable visual legends surrounding Kelley. Nineteenth-century engravings of Dee and Kelley raising a corpse helped turn a thinly sourced story into a defining popular image.

## Education and Formation

Kelley’s formation appears to have combined several bodies of knowledge:

- Grammar-school Latin and scribal competence.
- Practical chemical or apothecary techniques.
- English alchemical poetry, especially texts attributed to George Ripley.
- Astrology and the preparation or interpretation of horoscopes.
- Scrying: gazing into a crystal, polished stone, or reflective surface.
- Ritual and learned magic derived from Christian angelology, apocalypticism, biblical exegesis, cabala, and medieval ceremonial texts.

No securely identified teacher or master survives from before 1582. John Dee became Kelley’s senior collaborator and intellectual organizer rather than a conventional teacher: Kelley supplied visions and much of the vocal content, while Dee recorded, questioned, systematized, translated, and contextualized it.

## Early Career with John Dee, 1582–1583

### First meeting at Mortlake

**Documented fact:** Kelley, then calling himself Edward Talbot, came to John Dee’s house at Mortlake in **March 1582**. Dee had already experimented with scryers, including Barnabas Saul, but believed Kelley unusually effective.

Their working arrangement divided the séance:

1. Dee prayed, posed questions, and recorded proceedings.
2. Kelley looked into a “shew-stone,” crystal, or reflective object.
3. Kelley described figures, tableaux, letters, and speech that he said appeared.
4. Dee wrote the material down and treated it as communication from angels or other spirits.

Dee did not leave a simple statement that Kelley was a salaried servant. Their relationship evolved into a volatile mixture of household dependency, intellectual collaboration, spiritual partnership, and economic enterprise.

### The angelic system

Between 1582 and 1587, the sessions produced:

- The **Heptarchia Mystica**, involving seven planetary kings and princes.
- The **Sigillum Dei Aemeth**, a complex ritual seal.
- Tables of divine or angelic names.
- The **Liber Loagaeth**, consisting of lettered tables.
- An “Angelical,” “Adamic,” or “Enochian” alphabet and vocabulary.
- The later “Calls” or “Keys,” invocations central to modern Enochian magic.
- Prophecies about European rulers, religious reform, apocalyptic upheaval, and a divinely mandated mission for Dee and Kelley.

The name “Enochian” became standard largely through later occult reception. Dee himself more often used terms such as “Angelical” and connected the language with Adam and Enoch.

### Who authored the revelations?

Three principal interpretations remain:

- **Dee and Kelley’s own understanding:** genuine revelation mediated through Kelley’s sight.
- **Fraud interpretation:** Kelley invented voices, visions, languages, and dramatic commands to control or exploit Dee.
- **Psychological or collaborative-production interpretation:** the material emerged from trance, dissociation, improvisation, conscious and unconscious invention, and Dee’s systematizing editorial work.

The record does not permit a definitive choice. The texts exist in Dee’s hand, but Kelley supplied much of their ostensible visionary content. Linguistic study has not demonstrated a supernatural language; its grammar and translation are strongly conditioned by English. Nor has scholarship produced a universally accepted method by which Kelley mechanically encoded a prior English text.

### Tension and attempted withdrawal

Kelley repeatedly expressed fear of the spirits, doubts about their goodness, and a wish to end the sessions. These protests have been interpreted in incompatible ways:

- As genuine religious anxiety.
- As resistance to Dee’s pressure.
- As dramatic manipulation intended to increase his value.
- As an attempt to withdraw from an invention becoming too demanding.

The diaries document the tension but do not settle his motive.

### Alchemy alongside scrying

Kelley’s value to Dee was not limited to crystal-gazing. He claimed knowledge of metallic transmutation and possessed or later obtained a red powder or “tincture” supposedly capable of turning base metal into gold. Dee increasingly treated angelic wisdom and practical alchemy as linked parts of a program of religious and natural reform.

## Departure from England and Continental Journey, 1583–1584

### Albrecht Łaski

The Polish nobleman **Albrecht or Olbracht Łaski**, Palatine of Sieradz, met Dee in England in 1583. The angelic sessions flattered Łaski with prophecies of power and territorial rule. Łaski invited Dee and Kelley eastward.

### Departure

**Documented fact:** Dee, Kelley, their wives and household left England with Łaski on **21 September 1583**.

The party travelled through the Low Countries and northern Germany. They reached Stettin, now Szczecin, around Christmas 1583 and Łaski’s Polish territories early in 1584. Accounts variously date arrival at Łask to February and Kraków to March 1584.

### Economic reality

Łaski was heavily indebted and could not indefinitely support the large travelling household or expensive experiments. The prophecy-mediated relationship offered him dynastic promise while giving Dee and Kelley access to continental patrons, but it did not restore his finances.

Claims that Kelley and Dee deliberately bankrupted Łaski are too categorical. They consumed resources and encouraged extravagant hopes, but Łaski’s financial difficulties preceded their arrival.

### Poland and Hungary

The party travelled among Kraków, Łask, and territories then associated with Poland and Upper Hungary. Some accounts locate an alchemical laboratory on a Łaski property at Spiš, now in Slovakia.

The household’s movements were driven by patronage, political prophecy, alchemy, and the need for accommodation. Kelley was not merely Dee’s silent assistant: as the person who voiced the purported angels, he influenced the party’s geographical and political decisions.

---

## Prague, Kraków, and the Courts of Rudolf II and Stephen Báthory, 1584–1586

### First Prague period

Dee and Kelley reached Prague in August 1584. Their objective was access to **Emperor Rudolf II**, whose court was a major center of collecting, astronomy, medicine, artisanal experimentation, natural magic, and alchemy.

Dee presented himself as the bearer of a divine message concerning religious and political reform. Kelley remained the indispensable medium.

### Rudolf II

Dee obtained an imperial audience in September 1584. Rudolf did not adopt Dee’s prophetic program. Papal nuncio **Giovanni Francesco Bonomi**, concerned about heterodoxy or demonic magic, opposed the Englishmen.

A famous story says Kelley so angered the nuncio by criticizing corrupt clergy that Bonomi wanted to throw him from a window. It rests on correspondence and later retelling but captures the antagonistic setting better than it proves a literal attempted defenestration.

Imperial or ecclesiastical pressure required Dee and Kelley to leave Prague. They moved again toward Poland.

### Kraków and Stephen Báthory

In 1585 Dee and Kelley sought the patronage of **Stephen Báthory**, king of Poland and grand duke of Lithuania.

A séance before Báthory occurred at Niepołomice Castle on **27 May 1585**. Kelley, gazing into the crystal, delivered a severe prophetic message. Báthory remained skeptical and insisted that purported revelations be compatible with Christian doctrine and ecclesiastical authority. He did not adopt their program or offer lasting patronage.

This was a significant failure. The sessions generated records and publicity but not the political transformation or stable support promised by the revelations.

### Religious and political implications

The angelic material predicted upheaval, criticized rulers and clergy, and positioned Dee and Kelley as agents in a universal reform. Such claims were politically dangerous. Yet there is no firm evidence that Kelley was a formal English intelligence officer.

Dee did communicate with Francis Walsingham and other officials, and travelling Englishmen were routinely suspected of espionage. Kelley later corresponded with Lord Burghley and supplied information concerning Christopher Parkins. These contacts establish political usefulness and intelligence exchange, not membership in a clearly documented secret service.

## Třeboň and the Rosenberg Patronage, 1586–1589

### Vilém of Rožmberk

After unstable years, the pair found a major patron in **Vilém of Rožmberk**—William of Rosenberg—one of the most powerful Bohemian nobles. They settled at **Třeboň**, historically Wittingau or Trebona, in 1586.

Rožmberk offered accommodation, laboratory facilities, money, political protection, and access to mining and metallurgical networks. This was the most stable period of the Dee–Kelley continental enterprise.

### Laboratory network

The Třeboň circle included or intersected with:

- John Dee and his household.
- Edward and Joan Kelley.
- Thomas Kelley and Lydia.
- Vilém of Rožmberk and his administrators.
- The Bohemian experimenter Jan Kapr of Kaprštejn, called John Carpio in Dee’s records.
- Karl Widemann of Augsburg, a manuscript collector and sometime secretary associated with Kelley and Rosenberg.
- English travellers and intermediaries including Francis Garland, Edmund Hilton, Edward Rowley, and Thomas Simkinson.
- Edward Dyer, poet, courtier, and emissary of Elizabeth I.
- Various German, French, Italian, Polish, and Bohemian practitioners seeking recipes, patronage, or access to Kelley.

### Purported transmutations

Dee’s diary reports alchemical successes at Třeboň. A noted episode on **19 December 1586** involved what was described as the transmutation of mercury into gold. Another diary entry in April 1587 celebrated Kelley’s disclosure of the “great secret.”

The evidence establishes that demonstrations were performed and believed by some witnesses. It does not establish nuclear or chemical transmutation of one element into another.

Possible fraud mechanisms proposed by historians and chemists include:

- Introducing concealed gold into a crucible.
- Using a hollow stirring rod filled with gold.
- Employing mercury–gold amalgams.
- Switching samples.
- Using compounds that produced a gold-colored surface.
- Providing genuine gold while falsely describing its origin.

No surviving apparatus or securely continuous assay record permits reconstruction of a particular demonstration. “Kelley produced gold” is therefore a **documented claim and witnessed performance**, not a demonstrated physical transmutation.

### The red powder and the Book of St Dunstan

Later tradition says Kelley and John Blokley discovered an alchemical manuscript and red powder in the ruins of Glastonbury Abbey, sometimes specifically in a bishop’s tomb. Ashmole helped disseminate the story.

Problems with the account include:

- The earliest detailed form is posthumous.
- Dee’s diaries refer to powder, books, and a “bone,” but do not confirm the later discovery narrative.
- Ownership changed within the Dee–Kelley circle.
- No material survives with a verifiable chain of custody and composition.

The Glastonbury story is best classified as **alchemical foundation legend** built around real objects of uncertain origin.

### Edward Dyer and English interest

Elizabeth I’s court became interested in reports that Kelley could make gold. **Edward Dyer** visited Třeboň in 1588 as an intermediary connected with the queen and Lord Burghley. He experimented or observed work with Kelley and carried correspondence.

Kelley declined to return permanently to England. In July 1590 he emphasized his lands and status in Bohemia while professing loyalty to Elizabeth. English official interest is documented; a proven government contract for successful transmutation is not.

## The Wife-Sharing Covenant

### What the record says

In April and May 1587, Kelley reported messages from a female spirit, commonly identified as Madimi, requiring Dee and Kelley to hold all things in common—including their wives.

Dee was distressed and suspicious. Nevertheless, on **3 May 1587 at Třeboň**, John Dee, Edward Kelley, Jane Dee, and Joan Kelley signed a covenant for “indissoluble and inviolable” unity and common possession. The surviving printed version states that this unity was both spiritual and corporal.

A subsequent diary notation indicates reconciliation and sacramental participation. The arrangement appears to have been acted upon, though the exact number and circumstances of sexual encounters are unknown.

### Evidentiary classifications

- **Documented fact:** The command appears in Dee’s record, and all four signed a covenant.
- **Strong inference:** Sexual exchange or “cross-matching” occurred.
- **Unresolved:** Whether all participants freely consented in a modern sense; whether Kelley invented the command; whether it was intended to terminate the sessions; and whether Kelley fathered Theodore Dee.
- **Unsupported embellishment:** Later fictional scenes describing the participants’ emotions or exact sexual arrangements.

### Interpretations

The episode has been read as:

- Kelley’s deliberate sexual manipulation.
- A test designed to make Dee reject the spirits.
- A manifestation of antinomian religious ideas.
- A crisis produced by the internal logic of the angelic system.
- A coercive arrangement in which both wives had limited power.
- A sincere but short-lived attempt to obey what the group believed was a divine command.

The record demonstrates the covenant but not Kelley’s private intent.

---

## Breakdown of the Dee–Kelley Partnership

After the covenant, formal angelic conferences diminished. Kelley concentrated increasingly on laboratory work, patrons, travel, and his independent reputation. Dee felt marginalized by Bohemians, Italians, and Englishmen gathering around Kelley.

On **4 December 1588**, Dee gave Kelley a valued glass. On **4 February 1589**, he transferred powder, books, glass, and a bone intended for Rosenberg; Kelley supplied a signed and sealed discharge.

Kelley left Třeboň for Prague on 16 February 1589. Dee departed Bohemia in March and eventually returned to England. They never again resumed their earlier partnership.

The separation was not a single theatrical rupture. It was the gradual dissolution of a household, spiritual, technical, and financial collaboration in which Kelley had become more valuable to patrons than Dee.

---

## Independent Career in Bohemia, 1589–1591

### Rapid advancement

After separating from Dee, Kelley experienced a dramatic rise.

On **23 February 1590**, Rudolf II recognized him as a golden knight. Kelley entered the Bohemian landed order and styled himself of Imany. English correspondence records his claim that he possessed inheritable lands yielding approximately **£1,500 annually**, an enormous stated income.

He said that he had been invited to join Rudolf’s council but had not taken the oath because of loyalty to Elizabeth. This is Kelley’s own report; the invitation is not as securely established as his knighthood.

### Property and businesses

Kelley’s assets reportedly included:

- **Nová Libeň** and Libeřice estates near Jílové.
- Nine associated villages.
- Houses, farms, a former mint, brewery, and mill around Jílové.
- Twelve houses in Jílové according to one reconstruction.
- Two adjoining houses in Prague’s New Town, one later associated with the “Faust House.”
- An alchemical laboratory near Prague Castle in a building traditionally called the House at the Donkey in the Cradle.

An estimate cited in research on Central European alchemy valued his real property at **34,369 schock of Meissen groschen**. Such historical valuation should not be converted casually into modern currency.

Some assets were granted by Rosenberg; others were acquired through purchase or structured in Joan’s name. The portfolio demonstrates extraordinary access to credit and patronage, whether or not Kelley ever produced alchemical gold.

### Why patrons invested

Kelley offered several possible returns:

- Metallic transmutation.
- Medicines and chemical preparations.
- Mining and metallurgical expertise.
- Recovery of precious metal from mine waste.
- Prestige from possessing a famous adept.
- Access to texts and secret recipes.
- Political intelligence and international contacts.

The possibility that Rosenberg hoped Kelley could revive declining gold production around Jílové is plausible but not conclusively documented as the sole reason for the grants.

### Disseminating alchemical manuscripts

Research on Dee’s Central European circle shows that Kelley built authority by copying, commissioning, and distributing alchemical texts, particularly works attributed to George Ripley. Manuscripts associated with his network contain Ripleian treatises, poems, recipes, and correspondence.

This practice complicates the image of Kelley as either pure fraud or isolated genius. He participated in a functioning manuscript economy in which recipes, authority, secrecy, patronage, and experimental reputation were exchangeable assets.

## First Arrest and Imprisonment, 1591–1593

### Reasons for arrest

Rudolf II’s officers moved against Kelley at the end of April 1591. Multiple explanations circulated:

- He failed to reveal or reproduce the philosopher’s stone.
- Rudolf feared he would leave Bohemia with the secret.
- Kelley had killed or gravely injured an official named **Jiří or Georg Hunkler** in an illegal duel.
- He had incurred debts or financial obligations.
- Court rivals engineered his fall.
- His contacts with England made him politically suspect.
- His alchemical claims had been exposed as fraudulent.

No single surviving document proves one exclusive cause. The imperial edict treated him as a criminal and fugitive, while the Rosenberg annalist Václav Březan called him “the cheating alchemist.” The duel story is recurrent but incompletely documented.

### Flight and capture

Archival reconstruction by Charles Nicholl provides a detailed sequence:

- **29–30 April 1591:** Kelley secretly left his Prague house.
- Officials and soldiers raided the house, sealed the laboratory, seized elixirs and decorated boards, and detained or mistreated servants, including Thomas Kelley.
- **2 May:** Rudolf issued an edict for Kelley’s capture.
- **3 May:** Quartermaster Gregor Böul’s force found Kelley asleep at an inn in Soběslav, south of Tábor.
- He was searched, bound, and taken north through Tábor, Sedlčany, Dobříš, and Beroun.
- **6 May 1591:** He was delivered to Křivoklát Castle.

### Conditions at Křivoklát

Kelley was confined in the Huderka Tower. Fugger commercial newsletters reported:

- He was denied even a bread knife.
- He refused food and appeared despairing.
- He was kept in a confined space with air and food passing through a small opening.
- Access to him was severely restricted.

Joan was placed under restraint and petitioned the imperial authorities.

### Fine and confiscation

Kelley was assessed a fine of approximately **15,000 thalers**, though the documented expenses for his arrest were only about 160 thalers. His estates at Nová Libeň were impounded in autumn 1591. By January 1592 imperial officials were administering the house and lands.

Kelley’s family was therefore pressured not only through imprisonment but also through seizure, debt, and loss of income.

### Alleged first escape and leg injury

A common story says Kelley attempted to escape Křivoklát with a rope, fell, shattered a leg, and required amputation.

The archival evidence does **not** confirm that this occurred at Křivoklát. Contemporary reports of his release fail to mention an escape or injury. He was, however, later described as having a wooden leg. The injury may have occurred later, or the two imprisonments may have become conflated.

### Release

Kelley was released in **October 1593**, or by late 1593. Dee recorded receiving news in December that “Sir Edward Kelley” had been freed. The death of Rosenberg in 1592 had removed his strongest aristocratic protector, so the mechanism of release remains imperfectly understood.

## Final Years and Second Imprisonment, 1593–1597

### Life after release

The interval from 1593 to 1596 is poorly documented. Kelley remained in Bohemia, pursued alchemy, attempted to recover his fortunes, and retained some household and property interests. Claims that he led a wholly “vagabond” life are not well matched to the evidence of continued family, legal, and textual activity.

He appears to have accumulated substantial debt. The imperial court still wanted useful alchemical disclosure, while Kelley continued to protect or claim a secret he could not—or would not—deliver reliably.

### Second arrest

Kelley was confined again by **7 November 1596**. This imprisonment is frequently described as imprisonment for debt, although imperial interest in his alchemical knowledge remained part of the context.

He was sent to **Hněvín Castle**, overlooking Most/Brüx in northern Bohemia. A surviving account for his maintenance runs from 7 November 1596 through 1 November 1597. It lists firewood, food, a servant, and four guards, with 334 *kopa* outstanding.

### Writing in prison

During this confinement Kelley composed or completed material addressed to Rudolf II, including *De Lapide Philosophorum*. Its prefatory letter presents him as a persecuted seeker of truth who, although twice chained in Bohemia, kept his mind free through philosophy.

A German manuscript copy of the Bohemian land constitution bears a colophon associating it with “Herrn Edwardo Kelleo von Imanii” at Most Castle on **22 May 1597**. Parts are reportedly in Kelley’s hand. It may have related to a legal effort concerning confiscated estates, or simply to prison occupation.

## Death

### The strongest surviving account

The most credible narrative comes from the Bohemian mineral prospector and alchemist **Simon Tadeáš Budek of Leslin**, writing around 1604 and probably acquainted with Kelley or his circle.

Budek says:

1. Kelley was held at Most with a wooden leg, missing ears, and long hair.
2. Around Christmas 1597 he attempted to descend from the prison.
3. Thomas Kelley waited below with a cart.
4. Edward fell into the moat and broke his remaining leg in three places.
5. He was returned to the castle and informed that he would be transported to the emperor.
6. He asked to see his wife and daughter.
7. He spoke English with Joan and Flemish and Latin with Elizabeth.
8. He requested water, drank it, and died.

The description suggests suicide by poison concealed in or added to the water, but Budek does not explicitly say what it contained.

### Competing dates and causes

| Version | Status |
|---|---|
| 1 November 1597 | Possible, but may derive from the endpoint of the prison expense account |
| Christmas 1597 | Supported by Budek’s near-contemporary account |
| 1595 | Older English biographies, including the original DNB, followed chronologies now superseded by 1596–97 records |
| 1598 | Appears in later retellings and may reflect calendar uncertainty |
| Died of injuries from a fall | Reported by John Weever and later by Arthur Dee |
| Suicide by poison after the fall | Strong inference from Budek’s account |
| Died during the escape itself | Later simplification |
| Executed by Rudolf | No satisfactory evidence |
| Murdered to suppress secrets | Conspiracy narrative without documentary support |

The safest conclusion is that Kelley died in late 1597 following a failed escape from Hněvín Castle; whether the immediate cause was trauma, poison, or their combination remains unresolved.

### Burial

No identified grave survives. He may have been buried in the cemetery of Most’s old Church of the Assumption. The old town was demolished during twentieth-century lignite mining, although the church itself was moved. Any grave location is conjectural.

## Residences, Travels, and Geographical Movements

### England

- Worcester: probable birthplace and childhood.
- Possibly Oxford: alleged but unproven attendance at Gloucester Hall.
- Lancaster: alleged site of punishment; unverified.
- Walton-le-Dale: alleged necromantic episode; later tradition.
- London and Mortlake: work with Dee from 1582.
- Glastonbury: alleged discovery of powder and manuscript; legend.

### Continental Europe

- Departure from England in September 1583.
- Passage through the Low Countries and northern Germany.
- Stettin/Szczecin by Christmas 1583.
- Łask and Kraków in early 1584.
- Possible residence or laboratory work in the Spiš region.
- Prague from August 1584.
- Return to Kraków and Niepołomice in 1585.
- Třeboň from 1586, with repeated journeys to Prague and Poland.
- Prague and Jílové region during independent career.
- Soběslav: capture in 1591.
- Křivoklát: first imprisonment.
- Hněvín at Most: final imprisonment and death.

Kelley’s movements were not exile in a single legal sense. They included voluntary patronage-seeking, compelled departure from Prague, flight from arrest, and imprisonment.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

Kelley operated before the modern corporation and patent systems. No company or patent attributable to him is known. His economic activities were organized through households, aristocratic patronage, estates, workshops, manuscript exchange, and court privilege.

### Laboratories and technologies

His laboratories likely used:

- Furnaces and athanors.
- Crucibles and cupels.
- Distillation vessels.
- Sublimation equipment.
- Mercury, sulfurous materials, salts, acids, and metallic ores.
- Assaying and refining procedures.
- Pigments and compounds capable of changing a metal’s surface.
- Secret powders or tinctures.

The exact technical content of Kelley's operations cannot be reconstructed from the literary texts alone. Early modern alchemical language deliberately mixed material instruction, allegory, secrecy, and spiritual symbolism.

### Estates and revenue

Kelley became, for a short period, a substantial Bohemian property holder. His assets linked him to:

- Agricultural rents.
- Mills and brewing.
- Urban property.
- Mining districts.
- Credit and debt.
- Rosenberg grants.
- Imperial confiscation.
- Litigation by Joan and Elizabeth Weston.

His reported £1,500 annual landed income comes from his own correspondence and may be boastful. The separate estate valuation of 34,369 schock of Meissen groschen nevertheless confirms that the property interests were not purely fictional.

### No verified invention or patent

Kelley left no demonstrated invention comparable to a modern chemical process, and no confirmed patent. His enduring technical project was the supposed philosopher’s stone, whose efficacy was never established under reproducible conditions.

## Religious, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

### Christian framework

Dee and Kelley opened their sessions with prayer, fasting, confession, and appeals to God. The purported spirits used biblical names and Christian eschatology.

Neither man understood the work simply as secular fortune-telling. They believed—or presented themselves as believing—that it concerned:

- Restoration of Adamic knowledge.
- Recovery of a prelapsarian language.
- Reform of corrupted Christianity.
- Revelation of natural secrets.
- Preparation for apocalyptic transformation.
- Advice to rulers.

### Kelley’s ambivalence

Kelley repeatedly questioned whether the spirits were good. He sometimes described frightening or obscene visions and threatened to abandon scrying. This ambivalence is a major feature of the primary record.

### Alchemy

Kelley’s surviving works use the traditional language of:

- Sulfur and mercury.
- Death and regeneration.
- Dissolution and coagulation.
- Red and white tinctures.
- The union of body, soul, and spirit.
- Concealment of the work from the unworthy.

His intellectual practice belonged to an early modern culture in which practical metallurgy, medicine, cosmology, scriptural interpretation, and allegory were not sharply separated.

### Enochian language

The Angelical material has a distinctive alphabet, vocabulary, and textual structure. Modern linguists generally treat it as an invented or constructed language rather than evidence of supernatural speech. Its exact authorship remains divided between Kelley’s oral production and Dee’s written organization.

The claim that Kelley alone consciously designed the entire system to deceive Dee is possible but unproven. Conversely, the fact that no simple cipher has been discovered does not prove angelic origin.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### John Dee

The central relationship of Kelley’s life. Dee supplied reputation, household, ritual method, literacy, transcription, political access, and intellectual organization. Kelley supplied visionary performance, purported voices, images, languages, and later the promise of laboratory transmutation.

Their association was cooperative, dependent, competitive, and increasingly unequal. By the late 1580s patrons valued Kelley’s promised gold more than Dee’s universal reform.

### Joan Kelley

Wife, traveller, household manager, petitioner, and defender of family property. She endured house arrest, impoverishment, and litigation after Kelley’s downfall.

### Jane Dee

Participant in the household and the 1587 covenant. Jane’s own voice survives mainly through her husband’s record, limiting what can be known about her attitudes.

### Elizabeth Jane Weston

Stepdaughter and later Neo-Latin poet. She remained involved in the family’s property struggle and helped preserve the memory of Kelley’s final years.

### Thomas Kelley

Brother, laboratory and household associate, and alleged accomplice in the final escape plan.

### Albrecht Łaski

Initial continental patron and political aspirant. Kelley’s prophecies supported Łaski’s ambitions, while Łaski opened Central European routes and courts to the English party.

### Vilém of Rožmberk

Kelley’s most important financial patron. He provided laboratories, estates, protection, and credibility. His death in 1592 materially weakened Kelley.

### Rudolf II

Imperial patron, source of knighthood, and ultimately imprisoning sovereign. Their relationship moved from skepticism to conditional favor, lavish status, frustrated expectation, and coercion.

### Edward Dyer

English courtier and poet who visited Třeboň, observed or participated in alchemical work, corresponded with Kelley, and served as a bridge to Elizabeth I and Burghley.

### William Cecil, Lord Burghley

Correspondent and prospective English patron. Burghley sought Kelley's return or the transfer of his supposed knowledge to England.

### Karl Widemann

German manuscript collector and sometime secretary associated with Kelley. His activity helps explain how texts connected with Kelley circulated through Central European collections.

### Jan Kapr of Kaprštejn

Bohemian associate called John Carpio in Dee’s diary. He participated in the practical and manuscript networks surrounding Třeboň.

### Giovanni Francesco Bonomi

Papal nuncio who opposed Dee and Kelley as religiously dangerous or suspect practitioners.

### Stephen Báthory

Polish king whom Dee and Kelley unsuccessfully attempted to recruit into their prophetic mission.

### Court rivals and officials

Gregor Böul organized Kelley’s capture. Václav Březan recorded him as a cheating alchemist. Imperial administrators Bernard Taba and Gabriel Dejmek took control of seized property. The precise role of alleged duel victim Jiří Hunkler remains unclear.

---

## Political Activity and Intelligence Questions

Kelley held no demonstrated English political office. His activity nevertheless had political dimensions:

- Prophecies concerning the succession and territorial ambitions of Łaski.
- Efforts to influence Rudolf II and Stephen Báthory.
- Correspondence with Burghley.
- Contact with English emissaries.
- Reports about Christopher Parkins and suspected treason.
- Possible use as an informal intelligence source in Prague.
- Declared loyalty to Elizabeth I while holding Habsburg rank and property.

### Was Kelley a spy?

**Documented fact:** He exchanged politically relevant information with English officials.

**Unresolved theory:** He may have acted as an informal intelligence asset.

**Unsupported overstatement:** No surviving commission, payment series, cipher correspondence, or service record proves that Kelley was a formally recruited English agent.

Likewise, suspicions that Dee and Kelley were spies arose partly because international scholars, astrologers, and alchemists moved between courts and asked politically sensitive questions. Suspicion is not equivalent to proven employment.

## Controversies, Allegations, Crimes, Fraud, and Financial Questions

### Alleged Tudor forgery or counterfeiting

- **Allegation/later report:** Forged title deeds or counterfeit currency.
- **Reported punishment:** Pillory and cropping of both ears at Lancaster.
- **Evidence:** Missing ears are supported by Budek; the offense and venue lack a verified court record.
- **Conclusion:** Mutilation probably occurred, but the legal narrative remains unproven.

### False identity

- **Documented fact:** Used “Edward Talbot.”
- **Unresolved:** Whether Talbot was a criminal alias, family name, travelling identity, or situational pseudonym.

### Manipulation of John Dee

- **Documented fact:** Kelley controlled the visual and vocal channel of the séances.
- **Allegation:** He fabricated messages to dominate Dee.
- **Evidence supporting suspicion:** Convenient revelations, changes in position, failed prophecies, demands increasing his influence, and the wife-sharing command.
- **Evidence against a simple fraud model:** His repeated reluctance, fear, lack of an obvious financial payoff during early sessions, and the extraordinary labor required to sustain the system.
- **Conclusion:** Conscious fraud is plausible but not judicially or experimentally established.

### Sexual exploitation through purported revelation

- **Documented fact:** Kelley reported the command to share wives, and the four-person covenant was signed.
- **Interpretation/allegation:** Kelley manufactured divine authority to obtain sexual access to Jane Dee.
- **Unresolved:** His intention and the participants’ degrees of belief, resistance, or consent.
- The consequences were real even though motive cannot be recovered.

### Fraudulent transmutation

- **Documented fact:** Kelley claimed and demonstrated transmutation before witnesses.
- **Scientific finding:** No credible evidence establishes elemental transmutation by his methods.
- **Contemporary accusation:** Březan called him a cheating alchemist; Rudolf’s actions imply loss of confidence.
- **Probable historical interpretation:** At least some demonstrations involved deception, contaminated material, sample substitution, or misunderstood metallurgical processes.
- **Unresolved:** Whether Kelley himself believed he possessed a genuine tincture or consciously staged every demonstration.

### Financial extraction from patrons

Kelley obtained unusually valuable estates and privileges on the promise of secret knowledge. Patrons assumed the risk common to alchemical patronage: large advances for an art whose secrecy allowed failure to be attributed to impure materials, incorrect timing, insufficient support, or the adept’s refusal to disclose.

There is no surviving comprehensive civil judgment labeling all his grants fraudulent. Confiscation followed arrest and imperial displeasure, not a modern fraud trial.

### Łaski’s impoverishment

The Dee–Kelley enterprise consumed Łaski’s resources, but he was already deeply indebted. Describing Kelley as the sole cause of his ruin is not supported.

### Duel and homicide

The first arrest is sometimes said to have followed Kelley’s killing of Jiří Hunkler in a forbidden duel.

- No complete trial record has been identified.
- The claim is repeated in Czech institutional histories.
- Contemporary arrest records conceal or leave ambiguous the cause.
- It should therefore be treated as a **reported allegation**, not a proven homicide conviction.

### Debts

By the final imprisonment, debt was a material factor. The seizure of property, disputed ownership, outstanding maintenance costs, and family litigation show a complex insolvency and confiscation crisis.

### Treatment by Rudolf II

Kelley was confined harshly and pressured to surrender alchemical knowledge. The imperial government imposed a 15,000-thaler penalty and seized estates. Whether this was punishment for fraud, preventive detention of a valuable secret-holder, debt enforcement, criminal detention, or a combination remains unresolved.

### Adversarial search findings, including 2024–2026 material

A dedicated search using combinations of Kelley’s name with *controversy*, *scandal*, *allegation*, *lawsuit*, *fraud*, *accusation*, *conspiracy*, and *criticism* found:

- No newly published 2024–2026 primary evidence materially altering the Tudor or Bohemian criminal allegations.
- Recent search results are heavily contaminated by unrelated modern people named Edward Kelley.
- Modern Czech popular histories continue to describe Kelley as a fraud and repeat the duel, escape, amputation, and poisoning stories, often without distinguishing archival evidence from later legend.
- The strongest modern adversarial reconstruction remains Charles Nicholl’s use of the 1591 edict, Böul’s expense complaint, English intelligence reports, Fugger newsletters, Joan’s petition, property records, and Budek’s near-contemporary death account.
- No evidence surfaced for money laundering, a modern-style company fraud, a patent dispute, organized political violence, or membership in a secret society. Applying those categories to the evidence would be anachronistic.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. Kelley raised a corpse at Walton-le-Dale

**Story:** Kelley and Paul Waring exhumed a newly buried man and forced the corpse to reveal hidden treasure and prophecy.

**Appearance:** Published by John Weever in 1631, based on a story associated with Waring.

**Evidence for:** Weever was relatively near Kelley’s lifetime and named a supposed participant.

**Evidence against:** No legal, parish, diary, or eyewitness document; unidentified corpse and client; later addition of Dee.

**Reputational effect:** Established Kelley as the archetypal English necromancer and inspired enduring images of Dee and Kelley in a graveyard.

### 2. His ears were cut off for forgery

**Story:** Kelley forged deeds or coins and lost both ears in the Lancaster pillory.

**Evidence for:** Budek describes missing ears; Weever connects the mutilation to flight from England.

**Evidence against:** No verified sentence or exact offense.

**Effect:** The cap in his portrait became a symbol of concealed criminality.

### 3. Kelley discovered the philosopher’s stone at Glastonbury

**Story:** Kelley and John Blokley found a manuscript and red powder in the ruins of Glastonbury Abbey, sometimes inside a tomb.

**Appearance:** Most influentially circulated by Elias Ashmole.

**Evidence for:** Dee’s diaries confirm the existence of a powder and books treated as valuable.

**Evidence against:** The diaries do not corroborate the discovery story; no authenticated substance survives.

**Effect:** Supplied Kelley with an origin myth as an accidental possessor of ancient English monastic wisdom.

### 4. Kelley genuinely made gold

**Story:** A tiny quantity of red powder transformed large quantities of mercury or base metal into gold.

**Evidence for:** Several reported demonstrations and witnesses; patrons committed major resources.

**Evidence against:** No controlled replication, surviving assay chain, or chemically plausible process; ample opportunity for substitution and concealment.

**Effect:** Produced his wealth, knighthood, and enduring occult prestige.

### 5. Kelley invented Enochian to defraud Dee

**Story:** Kelley fabricated an angelic alphabet and language as part of an elaborate confidence trick.

**Evidence for:** Kelley alone controlled the visions; the system reflects English linguistic features; revelations sometimes benefited him.

**Evidence against:** No confession or recovered construction key; the production was elaborate and often burdensome; some messages frustrated Kelley’s immediate wishes.

**Effect:** Central to skeptical accounts and modern debates about invented language, mediumship, and collaborative authorship.

### 6. The Voynich Manuscript was forged by Dee and Kelley

**Story:** Dee and Kelley created the Voynich Manuscript and sold or attempted to sell it to Rudolf II as an ancient or occult book.

**Origin and circulation:** A modern theory based on their presence at Rudolf’s court, their cipher and alchemical interests, and a later report that Rudolf bought the manuscript for 600 ducats.

**Evidence for:** Circumstantial overlap of place, patron, occult book culture, and cryptographic ability.

**Evidence against:** No document links either man to the manuscript; radiocarbon dating places its vellum in the early fifteenth century; authorship, writing date, and Rudolf’s ownership remain disputed.

**Effect:** Attached Kelley to one of the most famous undeciphered manuscripts, despite the absence of direct evidence.

### 7. Kelley was the real Faust

Prague traditions attach Kelley to the Faust House. He appears to have owned property on the site or in the relevant Prague New Town complex, but the Faust legend predated or developed independently of him. His alchemical reputation was later absorbed into the building’s mythology.

### 8. Kelley cursed Most

A modern Czech legend says Kelley cursed Most and foretold its destruction. The old town was indeed demolished in the twentieth century for lignite mining, which encouraged retrospective interpretation of the event as fulfillment.

No contemporary text securely records such a curse. It is **local folklore**, historically significant as part of Most’s memory of destruction and displacement.

### 9. Rudolf imprisoned Kelley solely to steal the secret

This is a simplified legend. The emperor certainly wanted alchemical disclosure and had reason to prevent Kelley’s flight, but surviving accounts also mention alleged criminality, debt, court disputes, and perceived fraud.

### 10. Kelley was murdered by the emperor

No good evidence establishes execution or assassination. The escape-and-injury narrative is substantially better supported.

### 11. Kelley was a heroic martyr to truth

Kelley promoted this interpretation himself in the preface to *De Lapide Philosophorum*, comparing the rejection of truth to the release of Barabbas and crucifixion of Christ.

This is **self-created mythology**: valuable evidence for how he wished posterity to understand his imprisonment, not independent proof of innocence.

## Health, Injuries, and Psychological Crises

### Missing ears

Near-contemporary evidence indicates that Kelley’s ears had been cut off. The date and reason remain uncertain.

### Violent temperament

Dee’s record and later associates describe quarrels, anger, and emotional volatility. “Violent temperament” is a reported behavioral description, not a psychiatric diagnosis.

### Despair and refusal of food

Fugger newsletters from Křivoklát reported that Kelley refused food and appeared in profound despair in May 1591. This may indicate protest, depression, fear, or physical illness. The evidence does not justify a modern clinical diagnosis.

### Leg injury and amputation

Budek’s final narrative depicts Kelley with a wooden leg before the last escape. The injury’s origin is unclear. Later tradition places it in an escape from Křivoklát, but surviving contemporary release reports do not confirm that account.

### Final injuries

During the last escape from Hněvín, he reportedly broke his remaining leg in three places. In the sixteenth century such fractures presented major risks of shock, infection, hemorrhage, and death.

### Possible suicide

Budek’s description of Kelley requesting water and immediately dying, after speaking privately with his family, strongly suggests poison. It remains an inference because the account does not identify a substance or autopsy.

No dependable evidence indicates addiction.

## Successes and Failures

### Successes

- Became Dee’s most consequential scryer.
- Co-produced one of the most elaborate bodies of recorded ritual revelation in Renaissance Europe.
- Gained access to English, Polish, Bohemian, and imperial elites.
- Acquired a major reputation as an alchemist.
- Secured Rosenberg patronage and substantial estates.
- Received imperial knighthood.
- Built an international network of manuscript collectors, laboratory practitioners, patrons, and political correspondents.
- Left writings that continued to circulate through European alchemical print culture.

### Failures

- Did not produce independently reproducible gold.
- Failed, with Dee, to recruit Rudolf II or Stephen Báthory into their universal religious program.
- Contributed to or became trapped in the collapse of Łaski’s patronage.
- Lost the trust of Rudolf’s government.
- Suffered arrest, confiscation, a massive fine, and prolonged imprisonment.
- Failed to restore his estates or financial independence.
- Died after an unsuccessful escape.

## Offices, Honors, Appointments, and Dismissals

- **Imperial golden knight, 23 February 1590:** documented.
- **Admission to the Bohemian noble order:** substantially supported.
- **“Baron of Bohemia”:** used in English accounts but less precisely documented.
- **“Of Imany”:** adopted noble territorial or genealogical style.
- **Proposed imperial councillor:** claimed by Kelley but uncertain; he said he had not been sworn.
- No university degree, English public office, or formal court appointment as “court alchemist” has been located in the modern bureaucratic sense. The label describes patronage and access more than a securely salaried office.

## Works by Edward Kelley

Attribution is difficult because Kelley worked through oral dictation, collaborative séances, manuscript copying, and posthumous print. The following categories should remain distinct.

### A. Works substantially attributable to Kelley

#### 1. *Sir Edward Kelle’s Worke*

An English alchemical poem preserved by Elias Ashmole in *Theatrum Chemicum Britannicum* (London, 1652), pp. 324–28 in standard references.

- **Original composition:** probably late sixteenth century.
- **First known print:** 1652.
- **Attribution:** accepted as Kelleyan in the seventeenth-century manuscript tradition, but no author-supervised edition survives.

#### 2. *Sir Ed: Kelly concerning the Philosophers Stone, written to his especiall good Freind, G.S. Gent.*

English verse treatise printed by Ashmole in 1652.

- **Subject:** preparation and properties of the philosopher’s stone.
- **Attribution:** traditional and plausible; exact identity of “G.S.” remains uncertain.

#### 3. *De Lapide Philosophorum*

Latin prose treatise addressed or dedicated to Rudolf II, written or completed during imprisonment at Most, around 1596–97.

- **First print:** in *Tractatus duo egregii de Lapide Philosophorum, una cum Theatro Astronomiae Terrestri*, Hamburg, 1676.
- **Attribution:** strong, supported by its prison address and manuscript tradition.
- **Function:** technical-alchemical exposition and personal vindication.

#### 4. *Via Humida, sive Discursus de Menstruo Vegetabili Saturni*

“The Humid Way, or Discourse on the Vegetable Menstruum of Saturn.”

- **Survival:** posthumous manuscript and print tradition.
- **Attribution:** conventional but not supported by an authorial publication.

#### 5. *Theatrum Astronomiae Terrestris*

“The Theatre of Terrestrial Astronomy,” an emblematic alchemical work printed with the 1676 treatises.

- **Attribution:** associated with Kelley in the print tradition.
- **Qualification:** editorial transmission through Johann Lange, John Lilly, Meric Casaubon, or initials interpreted as their names complicates the exact textual history.

#### 6. *Fragmenta quaedam Edovardi Kellei ex ipsius epistolis excerpta*

Fragments extracted from Kelley’s letters.

- **Publication:** related material appeared as *Fragmenta aliquot edita a Cambacis*, Geismar, 1647, and in later alchemical compilations.
- **Nature:** excerpted correspondence or recipes rather than a unified authorial book.
- **Attribution:** individual fragments vary in security.

#### 7. Letters to Edward Dyer and Lord Burghley

Surviving manuscript letters concern alchemy, loyalty, political information, court prospects, and Kelley's status.

- **Original dates:** mainly late 1580s and 1590.
- **Status:** genuine correspondence where preserved in recognized manuscript collections.
- **Publication:** scattered through antiquarian editions and studies rather than issued by Kelley as a book.

#### 8. Copy of the Bohemian land constitution

A German-language manuscript completed or annotated at Most Castle in 1597 and associated in its colophon with Kelley and a person named Ritter.

- **Status:** scribal or legal compilation, not an original constitutional treatise.
- **Significance:** one of the last datable products connected with his hand.

### B. Collaborative revelatory corpus

The following should not be credited to Kelley alone:

#### 1. Dee’s spiritual diaries or “Books of Mysteries”

The manuscripts record sessions in which Kelley served as principal scryer and Dee as interrogator, scribe, compiler, and interpreter. These include material later called:

- *Mysteriorum Libri Quinque*
- *Liber Mysteriorum Sextus et Sanctus* or *Liber Loagaeth*
- *Heptarchia Mystica*
- *Liber Scientiae, Auxilii et Victoriae Terrestris*
- The Angelical Calls or Keys
- The Kraków, Prague, and Třeboň spiritual actions

**Authorship classification:** collaboratively produced documents written principally by Dee, containing speech and visions attributed to spirits through Kelley.

#### 2. *A True & Faithful Relation of What Passed for Many Yeers between Dr. John Dee … and Some Spirits* (1659)

- **Editor:** Meric Casaubon.
- **Source:** selections from Dee’s manuscripts.
- **Authorship:** not a book authored by Kelley; it preserves his reported séance speech.
- **Editorial purpose:** Casaubon framed the material as a warning about demonic deception and religious enthusiasm.
- **Textual caution:** the edition contains transcription and editorial problems and should be checked against manuscripts or modern critical editions where possible.

### C. Works merely attributed or associated

- Various alchemical recipes circulated under Kelley’s name because his reputation increased their value.
- Ripleian works copied within his circle are not Kelley’s compositions.
- The “Book of St Dunstan” is an attributed medieval or pseudonymous alchemical work, not a Kelley-authored book.
- The Voynich Manuscript attribution is speculative and unsupported by direct evidence.
- Modern “Enochian” manuals published under Dee’s and Kelley’s names are posthumous reconstructions.

## Selected Publication History of Kelley’s Works

- **1647:** *Fragmenta aliquot edita a Cambacis*, Geismar.
- **1652:** Elias Ashmole prints two English poems in *Theatrum Chemicum Britannicum*.
- **1659:** Casaubon publishes selections from Dee’s spiritual records containing extensive Kelleyan material.
- **1670:** B. N. Petraeus includes associated alchemical material in *Drey vortreffliche und noch nie im Druck gewesene chymische Bücher*.
- **1676:** *Tractatus duo egregii de Lapide Philosophorum, una cum Theatro Astronomiae Terrestris*, Hamburg.
- **1691:** Later edition of Petraeus’s compilation.
- **1732:** German translations and related material appear in volume 3 of the *Deutsches Theatrum Chemicum*.
- **1893:** Arthur Edward Waite issues *The Alchemical Writings of Edward Kelly* in English translation and compilation.
- Subsequent occult publishers repeatedly reprinted Waite, Ashmole, and Casaubon, often without returning to manuscripts.

Kelley’s popular Czech identity is strongly influenced by films and television about Rudolf II’s alchemical Prague. Productions often merge him with the generic fraudulent court alchemist, the Faust legend, or other Rudolfine practitioners.

- The Faust House.

Such productions vary widely in reliability and commonly repeat the cropped ears, corpse-raising, wife-sharing, and fatal-escape narratives without evidentiary distinctions.

- Ashmolean Museum and Bodleian Library, Oxford: alchemical manuscripts, correspondence, and later collections.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Seventeenth century

Kelley’s reputation divided almost immediately:

- To alchemists, he could be a persecuted adept who possessed genuine tincture.
- To Protestant polemicists, the Dee–Kelley records showed the danger of demonic delusion.
- To antiquarians, he was a picturesque necromancer and criminal.
- To satirists, his imperial reputation embodied the grandest possible alchemical boast.

Ben Jonson invoked “Kelly” in *The Alchemist* as a benchmark for an alchemist courted by an emperor. Samuel Butler later referred to him in *Hudibras*. Elias Ashmole preserved his poems and promoted the Glastonbury powder story.

### Casaubon’s intervention

Meric Casaubon’s 1659 publication preserved otherwise inaccessible portions of the spiritual record while framing Dee and Kelley as victims or instruments of evil spirits. This hostile editorial purpose decisively shaped their later reputations.

### Romantic and occult revival

Nineteenth-century occultists rehabilitated Kelley as an adept, medium, or martyr. Waite translated and republished his alchemical works while acknowledging the hostile biographical tradition.

The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn adapted Dee–Kelley material into ritual practice. Later figures including Aleister Crowley expanded “Enochian” magic far beyond the surviving Elizabethan sessions.

### Modern scholarly reinterpretation

Recent scholarship tends to resist two older caricatures:

1. Kelley as nothing more than an ignorant trickster.
2. Kelley as a proven supernatural magician and gold-maker.

Instead, he is studied as:

- A skilled visionary performer.
- A collaborator in textual production.
- An alchemical broker and manuscript disseminator.
- A participant in courtly patronage.
- A migrant English practitioner navigating confessional and political boundaries.
- A household figure whose career materially affected women, children, servants, and dependents.
- A case study in how experimental credibility was constructed before modern laboratory institutions.

### Local Czech memory

In Prague, Jílové, Třeboň, Křivoklát, and Most, Kelley belongs to the cultural landscape of Rudolfine alchemy. Statues, exhibitions, legends, tourist narratives, and reconstructed sites often present him simultaneously as charlatan, magician, scientist, and tragic prisoner.

### Scientific and linguistic legacy

Kelley’s alchemy did not yield verified elemental transmutation. His lasting intellectual effect lies instead in:

- The enormous Dee–Kelley manuscript corpus.
- The history of constructed and sacred languages.
- Studies of altered states and mediumship.
- Ritual magic and Western esotericism.
- Early modern manuscript exchange.
- The social history of experimental credibility.
- The boundary between performance, belief, fraud, and collaborative imagination.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted evidence are included. Mere contemporaneity, shared interest in alchemy, or later comparison is excluded.

### John Dee — documented fact

- **Nature:** Principal collaborator, host, senior intellectual partner, ritual interrogator, recorder, and later rival for patronal attention.
- **Dates:** March 1582 through February–March 1589.
- **Places:** Mortlake; northern European travel route; Poland; Prague; Kraków and Niepołomice; Třeboň.
- **Evidence:** Dee’s diaries and spiritual manuscripts document hundreds of sessions, household events, journeys, disputes, transfers of objects, and the dissolution of the partnership.

### Frances Amelia Yates — documented posthumous interpretive connection

- **Nature:** Twentieth-century historian whose work placed Dee, Kelley, angelic magic, alchemy, and later Rosicrucian currents within a large Renaissance intellectual narrative.
- **Dates and places:** No lifetime meeting was possible; this is a posthumous scholarly connection.
- **Qualification:** Yates’s interpretive model was highly influential but has been qualified and revised by later scholarship. She should not be treated as a witness to Kelley’s life.

### Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa — documented intellectual-context connection; direct personal influence unresolved

- **Nature:** Agrippa’s writings on occult philosophy, angelic hierarchies, cabala, number, and learned magic formed part of the intellectual world in which Dee’s ritual program developed.
- **Qualification:** Kelley could not have met Agrippa, who died in 1535. The consulted evidence does not establish Kelley’s own direct reading of a particular Agrippa edition; the connection is therefore contextual and mediated principally through Dee.

### Paracelsus — documented alchemical-context connection; no personal meeting

- **Nature:** Paracelsian medicine and alchemy circulated strongly at Rudolf II’s court and among Kelley’s Central European associates.
- **Qualification:** Paracelsus died before Kelley’s birth. No direct relationship existed, and specific intellectual dependence by Kelley must be demonstrated work by work.

### Francis Bacon — no documented personal crossing found

Both men were English contemporaries with interests later grouped under histories of natural knowledge, but the consulted evidence does not establish a meeting, correspondence, collaboration, or acknowledged influence.

### Giordano Bruno — no documented personal crossing found

Bruno and Kelley both moved within late-sixteenth-century European occult and courtly environments, but no reliable source consulted for this dossier establishes contact.

- **Arthur Edward Waite — documented posthumous connection:** Waite translated, compiled, and republished Kelley’s alchemical writings in the nineteenth century and wrote a biographical preface that helped shape the English occult revival’s view of him.
