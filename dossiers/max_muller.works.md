## Works by Friedrich Max Müller

### Editorial projects and critical editions

- **1849–1873:** *Rig-Veda-Sanhita: The Sacred Hymns of the Brahmans, together with the Commentary of Sayanacharya*. A six-volume critical edition of the Sanskrit text with Sāyaṇa’s commentary, financed by the East India Company and conceived by Müller as a principal life-project. [1][2]
- **1866:** *Hitopadeśa: The Sanskrit Text, with a Grammatical Analysis, Alphabetically Arranged*. A teaching edition rather than a newly authored narrative; Müller made its philological apparatus and arrangement. [1]
- **1869:** *Rig-Veda-Prātiśākhya*. An edition and German translation of the ancient phonetic treatise, with critical and explanatory work by Müller. [1]
- **1873:** *Rig-Veda-Sanhita: The Sacred Hymns of the Brahmans, in the Samhita and Pada Texts*. A textual edition presenting two traditional forms of the Vedic text. [1]
- **1875:** *Schiller’s Briefwechsel mit dem Herzog Friedrich Christian von Schleswig-Holstein-Augustenburg*. An edition, with introduction, of correspondence by Schiller and the duke; the letters are not Müller’s compositions. [1][3]
- **1879–1910:** *The Sacred Books of the East*. Müller conceived and supervised this comparative-scripture project, originally planned as twenty-four volumes; it became a fifty-volume collaborative translation series. It resists treatment as solely Müller’s “work”: numerous scholars translated its texts, Müller died before the 1910 general index, and his role was editor, organizer, contributor, and architect of the collection. [4][5]
- **1890–1892:** *Rig-Veda-Sanhita, together with the Commentary of Sayanacharya*, second edition. A substantially revised manifestation of the earlier critical edition, not a separate newly composed work. [1]

### Translations

- **1844:** *Hitopadeśa*. Müller’s German translation of the Sanskrit collection of instructive animal tales; the underlying work is traditional and not authored by him. [1]
- **1847:** *Meghadūta*. A German verse translation of Kālidāsa’s poem, making Müller the translator rather than the poem’s creator. [1]
- **1869:** *Rig-Veda, Volume I: Hymns to the Maruts*. The first volume of Müller’s English translation of selected Vedic hymns; his planned complete translation did not appear as such. [1]
- **1870:** *The Dhammapada*. An English translation of the Buddhist verse collection, first issued separately and later incorporated into *The Sacred Books of the East*. [1]
- **1879:** *The Upanishads, Part I*. Müller’s translation of five Upanishads, published as volume 1 of *The Sacred Books of the East*. It is simultaneously an individual translation and a component of the larger editorial project. [1][4]
- **1881:** *The Critique of Pure Reason*. An English translation of Immanuel Kant’s philosophical work; Müller supplied translation and scholarly framing, not the original argument. [1][3]
- **1884:** *The Upanishads, Part II*. Müller’s second collection of translated Upanishads, volume 15 of *The Sacred Books of the East*. The family bibliography subsumes it under “Upanishads, translation” rather than listing the second part independently. [1][4]
- **1893:** *The Sacred Laws of the Âryas, Part II: Vâsishtha and Baudhâyana*. The family bibliography calls this “Apastamha Sutras,” but that description is erroneous or compressed: the 1893 volume Müller translated was volume 14 of *The Sacred Books of the East*, containing Vasiṣṭha and Baudhāyana; Georg Bühler translated the series’ Āpastamba material. Attribution therefore requires correction rather than silent repetition. [1][4]

### Philology, language, and textual scholarship

- **1847:** *On the Relation of Bengali to the Aryan and Aboriginal Languages of India*. A linguistic essay, not a book-length system. Its title appears in abbreviated form in the family bibliography. [1]
- **1853:** *Essay on the Turanian Languages*. A comparative-linguistic essay associated with the Bunsen prize competition; “Turanian” is Müller’s historical classification, not a category accepted without qualification today. [1]
- **1853:** *Essay on Indian Logic*. A scholarly essay on Indian systems of reasoning. [1]
- **1854:** *Proposals for a Uniform Missionary Alphabet*. A protocol-like text: it proposes practical transcription rules for missionaries recording unwritten or differently written languages. [1]
- **1854:** *The Languages of the Seat of War in the East*. A topical linguistic handbook prompted by the Crimean War, combining grammatical and ethnolinguistic description. [1]
- **1861:** *Lectures on the Science of Language, First Series*. A published lecture-cycle that attempts to establish language study as a historical science; later editions materially revised its manifestation. [1]
- **1862:** *On Ancient Hindu Astronomy and Chronology*. A scholarly study of astronomical evidence used for dating Sanskrit literature; the bibliography abbreviates its title as *Ancient Hindu Astronomy*. [1]
- **1864:** *Lectures on the Science of Language, Second Series*. A second lecture-cycle, related to but distinct from the 1861 series. [1]
- **1866:** *A Sanskrit Grammar for Beginners*. A pedagogical grammar—a protocol for learning and analysis—rather than a descriptive history of Sanskrit alone. [1][3]
- **1868:** *Stratification of Language*. A lecture or essay proposing that languages contain historically layered formations; its separate-publication status should not be mistaken for a large monograph. [1]
- **1873:** *Darwin’s Philosophy of Language*. A polemical essay engaging evolutionary accounts of language rather than a work by Darwin. [1]
- **1876:** *On Spelling*. A public intervention in English orthographic reform, functioning partly as an argumentative proposal or protocol. [1]
- **1881:** *Selected Essays on Language, Mythology and Religion*. An authorized selection of earlier essays; it is a new arrangement and manifestation of existing texts, not wholly new composition. [1]
- **1887:** *The Science of Thought*. A large philosophical-linguistic treatise arguing for the inseparability of thought and language. [1][3]
- **1888:** *Three Introductory Lectures on the Science of Thought*. A compact lecture version of Müller’s linguistic philosophy; the family bibliography calls it “Science of Thought, three Lectures.” [1]
- **1888:** *Biographies of Words and the Home of the Aryas*. A collection joining word histories to an argument about Indo-European origins; its racial-era terminology is historically situated and should not be silently naturalized. [1][3]
- **1889:** *Three Lectures on the Science of Language*. A concise late restatement of Müller’s linguistic program, distinct from the two earlier, longer lecture series. [1]

### Comparative mythology and religion

- **1856:** *Comparative Mythology*. An essay, first published in *Oxford Essays*, proposing a linguistic method for interpreting Indo-European myths. [1]
- **1857:** *Buddhist Pilgrims*. A study of Chinese pilgrims as sources for Indian Buddhism; it is a historical-philological essay, not a pilgrimage narrative by Müller. [1]
- **1873:** *Introduction to the Science of Religion*. Four Royal Institution lectures with essays, presenting comparative religion as a field of inquiry. [1]
- **1878:** *Lectures on the Origin and Growth of Religion as Illustrated by the Religions of India*. The Hibbert Lectures: a delivered lecture-cycle subsequently made into a book. [1][3]
- **1888–1892:** *Gifford Lectures*. A four-year intellectual project that appeared as four related but separately titled books; “the Gifford Lectures” is therefore a project and cycle rather than a single text. [1]
- **1889:** *Natural Religion*. The published 1888 Gifford course and first part of the cycle. [1]
- **1891:** *Physical Religion*. The published 1890 Gifford course and second part of the cycle. [1]
- **1892:** *Anthropological Religion*. The published 1891 Gifford course and third part of the cycle. [1]
- **1893:** *Theosophy, or Psychological Religion*. The published 1892 Gifford course and fourth part of the cycle. [1]
- **1894:** *Three Lectures on the Vedânta Philosophy*. A lecture-cycle introducing Vedānta to a general English-language audience. [1]
- **1896–1897:** *Contributions to the Science of Mythology*. A two-volume synthesis of Müller’s comparative mythology; the family bibliography dates it 1897, while bibliographic records commonly divide publication across 1896–1897. [1][3]
- **1898:** *Râmakrishna: His Life and Sayings*. A biographical and interpretive study assembled from testimony and translated sayings. Its subject’s words are transmitted through Müller and his informants, so it is not simply an independently witnessed biography. [1]
- **1899:** *The Six Systems of Indian Philosophy*. A comparative exposition of six orthodox Sanskrit philosophical traditions. [1][3]
- **1899:** *Das Pferdebürla: Fragen der Zeit beantwortet*. A popular religious-philosophical apologetic that grew from Müller’s published exchange with a Silesian immigrant calling himself “Pferdebürla.” Because correspondence, replies, and revised essays accreted into the book, it resists classification as a single-origin treatise. [1][6]

### Collected essays, biographies, and literary prose

- **1857:** *Deutsche Liebe: Aus den Papieren eines Fremdlings*. A sequence of fictionalized literary recollections or prose meditations, published pseudonymously in form as papers “from a stranger”; it is Müller’s principal work of imaginative prose. [1]
- **1858:** *The German Classics from the Fourth to the Nineteenth Century*. An anthology and teaching collection edited by Müller, with translated or explanatory matter; its constituent German works are not his creations. [1]
- **1867–1875:** *Chips from a German Workshop*, first series, four volumes. A cumulative body of essays on religion, mythology, language, and literature; it is both a collection and a deliberately shaped scholarly “workshop,” not one continuous treatise. [1][7]
- **1875:** *J. B. Basedow*. A biographical-historical essay or small monograph on the German educational reformer. [1]
- **1884:** *Biographical Essays*. A collection of intellectual portraits, some previously published; its made object is the selection and arrangement as well as the individual essays. [1]
- **1894:** *Chips from a German Workshop*, new edition, four volumes. Müller extensively altered and reorganized the earlier collection, making this more than a mechanical reprint but not an entirely separate corpus. [1][7]
- **1898:** *Auld Lang Syne, First Series: Memories of Other Days*. A volume of autobiographical recollections rather than a systematic autobiography. [1]
- **1899:** *Auld Lang Syne, Second Series: My Indian Friends*. A second memoir volume centered on relationships conducted largely through correspondence; Müller never visited India. [1][3]
- **1901:** *My Autobiography: A Fragment*. An unfinished autobiographical project published after Müller’s death. Its fragmentary status is integral: it resists being treated as a completed life narrative, and the posthumous book-form was produced editorially by others. [1][3]
- **1901:** *Last Essays, First Series: Essays on Language, Folklore and Other Subjects*. A posthumous collection assembled from Müller’s papers; the essays are his, but the final collected object depends on editorial intervention after his death. [1]
- **1901:** *Last Essays, Second Series: Essays on the Science of Religion*. The companion posthumous collection, likewise a manifestation made jointly by Müller’s prior writing and his survivors’ editorial work. [1]

### Art-historical study

- **1887:** *La Carità of Andrea del Sarto in the Chiostro dello Scalzo at Florence*. A short illustrated art-historical study of a fresco, issued by the Fine Art Society; Müller made the interpretive text, not the artwork it examines. [1][8]

### Political interventions

- **1871:** *Letters on the War between Germany and France*. A collected intervention in contemporary politics, largely originating as public letters; it acts rhetorically on Anglo-German opinion and thus approaches a manifesto more than a detached history. [1]
- **1900:** *The Transvaal War*. A political intervention on the South African War. The family bibliography supplies only this shortened title, so no more elaborate title should be invented without firmer bibliographical evidence. [1]
