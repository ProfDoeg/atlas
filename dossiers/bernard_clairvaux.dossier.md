# Bernard of Clairvaux: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

Bernard lived before modern civil registration, administrative archives, journalism, or systematic financial accounting. His life must be reconstructed chiefly from:

1. his surviving Latin writings and correspondence;
2. institutional records and chronicles;
3. the *Vita prima*, begun during his lifetime;
4. later medieval hagiography;
5. modern textual, historical, theological, and institutional scholarship.

The *Vita prima* is indispensable but not neutral. William of Saint-Thierry, Bernard’s friend, wrote its first book before Bernard died. Arnold of Bonneval and Geoffrey of Auxerre continued it. Its authors possessed direct testimony, but their purpose was to establish sanctity, not produce a critical biography. Miracles, prophetic dreams, exemplary speeches, and dramatized scenes must therefore be distinguished from independently recoverable events.

Dates before Bernard’s entry into Cîteaux are particularly uncertain. Modern authorities commonly give 1090, while older accounts give 1091. His death is variously dated 20 or 21 August 1153; 20 August is now his liturgical feast and is preferred by recent scholarship, while 21 August remains common in older reference works.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Principal name:** Bernard of Clairvaux  
**Latin:** *Bernardus Claraevallensis*; *Bernardus abbas Clarae Vallis*  
**French:** Bernard de Clairvaux  
**German:** Bernhard von Clairvaux  
**Birth name/designation:** Bernard of Fontaine; Bernard de Fontaine  
**Religious title:** Abbot of Clairvaux  
**Later ecclesiastical titles:** Saint Bernard; Doctor of the Church  
**Honorific:** *Doctor Mellifluus*, “Honey-Flowing Doctor” or “Mellifluous Doctor”  
**Other traditional description:** “Last of the Fathers”  
**Religious affiliation:** Latin Christianity; Benedictine monasticism; Cistercian movement  
**Profession and offices:** Monk, founding abbot, preacher, theologian, biblical commentator, spiritual director, ecclesiastical diplomat, controversialist, letter writer, monastic administrator  
**Born:** probably 1090; 1091 is an older alternative  
**Birthplace:** the family castle at Fontaine, now Fontaine-lès-Dijon, Duchy of Burgundy  
**Died:** 20 August 1153 according to recent scholarship; 21 August in some older sources  
**Place of death:** Clairvaux Abbey, in present-day Aube, France  
**Burial:** Clairvaux Abbey  
**Canonized:** 1174, by Pope Alexander III  
**Declared a Doctor of the Church:** 1830, by Pope Pius VIII  
**Feast:** 20 August  
**Iconographic attributes:** white Cistercian habit, abbatial crozier, book, beehive or bees, instruments of Christ’s Passion, the Virgin Mary, or the devil in chains

Bernard was never a bishop, cardinal, pope, university master, crusade commander, or founder of the Cistercian Order. He became its most visible representative and greatest promoter, but Cîteaux and the early Cistercian reform preceded his arrival.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

Bernard’s father was **Tescelin**, conventionally called Tescelin le Roux, Tescelin Sorrel, or Tescelin de Fontaine. He belonged to the Burgundian knightly landholding class and was associated with the service of the dukes of Burgundy. Older hagiography inflated the family’s standing into very high nobility. Recent work describes Tescelin more cautiously as a member of the lower military aristocracy or knightly gentry possessing the lordship of Fontaine and other interests.

Bernard’s mother was **Aleth, Aleidis, or Alèthe of Montbard**, from a family whose landed and social position may have exceeded Tescelin’s. Her father is generally identified as Bernard of Montbard. Aleth died while Bernard was young, usually placed around 1105–1107, and was buried at Saint-Bénigne in Dijon.

Later literature portrays both parents as models of Christian nobility. Aleth, in particular, became the subject of an extensive family cult. Claims about her ascetic household practices, dedication of each child to God, and supernatural knowledge come chiefly through hagiographical transmission.

### Siblings

The standard sibling order is:

1. **Guy or Guido**, the eldest son; a knight before entering religious life.
2. **Gerard**, initially associated with military life; later a monk at Clairvaux and an important collaborator of Bernard.
3. **Bernard**.
4. **Andrew**.
5. **Bartholomew**.
6. **Nivard**, the youngest brother.
7. **Humbeline, Hombeline, or Ombeline**, the only sister.

The exact birth order of the younger children and some details of their careers vary among accounts. Guy, Gerard, Andrew, and Bartholomew accompanied or followed Bernard into the Cistercian life. Nivard, still young when the older brothers departed, joined later. Humbeline was married and subsequently became a nun, eventually serving as prioress at Jully.

A 2023 study of Bernard’s brothers stresses that there is no objective evidence that the sons had originally been destined for monastic careers. Their collective conversion represented a radical redirection of the family’s military, dynastic, and property expectations.

Bernard never married and had no documented sexual partner or children.

### Wider kinship

**André de Montbard**, one of the early leaders and later grand master of the Knights Templar, is traditionally identified as Bernard’s maternal uncle. Medieval kinship terminology can be elastic, and the exact degree of relationship deserves caution, but the Montbard family connection is well established in the tradition surrounding Bernard and the Templars.

The figure **Gaudry of Touillon**, described as an *avunculus*, accompanied the group to Cîteaux. The Latin term may mean maternal uncle or, more loosely, an older quasi-familial protector. Recent scholarship considers it possible that he was a close associate of Tescelin rather than a blood uncle.

## Childhood and Early Life

Bernard grew up at Fontaine, a fortified noble residence near Dijon, in a household oriented toward landholding, ducal service, warfare, and ecclesiastical patronage. Very little about his childhood can be independently documented.

The *Vita prima* says that Aleth dreamed during pregnancy that she carried a white dog which barked. A holy interpreter allegedly told her that the child would become a faithful watchdog of God’s house and a powerful preacher. This is hagiographical symbolism, not verifiable prenatal history. The white dog later suited Cistercian identity and Bernard’s reputation as an opponent of heresy and ecclesiastical disorder.

Aleth’s death appears to have been a major formative event. Later accounts associate Bernard’s vocational crisis with grief for his mother and describe a decisive experience in a church during a journey. These narratives may preserve authentic memory, but their literary construction prevents exact reconstruction.

Bernard was physically slight and apparently suffered throughout adult life from serious gastrointestinal problems, poor digestion, weakness, and an inability to retain ordinary food. Hagiography interpreted his frailty as the result of asceticism, spiritual intensity, or providential discipline. Modern retrospective diagnosis is impossible.

---

## Education and Formation

Bernard studied at the school attached to the collegiate church of **Saint-Vorles at Châtillon-sur-Seine**. His curriculum included the liberal arts, especially grammar, rhetoric, and dialectic. He acquired the Latin command evident in his mature prose and an extensive working knowledge of Scripture.

His writings reveal saturation in:

- the Latin Bible;
- the Rule of Saint Benedict;
- liturgical language;
- Augustine of Hippo;
- Gregory the Great;
- Ambrose;
- Jerome;
- Origen’s exegetical inheritance, sometimes indirectly;
- patristic and monastic interpretations of the Song of Songs;
- classical Latin rhetoric and vocabulary.

He did not receive the later Parisian scholastic education associated with Abelard’s milieu. His theology emerged through monastic reading, liturgy, meditation, communal discipline, correspondence, and pastoral controversy.

### Principal formative figures

**Stephen Harding**, abbot of Cîteaux, received Bernard and later commissioned him to lead the Clairvaux foundation.

**William of Champeaux**, bishop of Châlons and formerly a major Parisian teacher, became an ecclesiastical ally and reportedly ordained Bernard abbot.

**William of Saint-Thierry** became Bernard’s friend, theological interlocutor, advocate, and first biographer. Their relationship was intellectually important, particularly for affective theology and exegesis of the Song of Songs.

Bernard’s mother, his schooling at Saint-Vorles, and the collective conversion of his brothers were also presented by early sources as formative.

---

## Entry into Cîteaux

The date of Bernard’s entry is disputed between **1112 and 1113**. The traditional narrative says that after deciding on monastic life he persuaded approximately thirty relatives and companions to join him. They spent a period together near Châtillon preparing for entry, then entered Cîteaux.

The exact number is hagiographically rounded and cannot be verified, but the collective nature of the recruitment is credible. Bernard’s brothers Guy, Gerard, Andrew, and Bartholomew were among those associated with the movement; Nivard joined later.

Cîteaux had been founded in 1098 by Robert of Molesme and developed under Alberic and Stephen Harding. It sought stricter observance of the Rule of Saint Benedict, simplicity in worship and architecture, withdrawal from aristocratic display, manual labor, and institutional independence.

Bernard’s entry supplied the struggling monastery with a substantial body of recruits and kinship connections. It did not, however, begin the Cistercian reform.

---

## Founding and Government of Clairvaux

In **1115**, Stephen Harding sent Bernard with a group of monks to establish a daughter house in the valley of the Aube. The location became **Clara Vallis**, Clairvaux, “Clear Valley.” Bernard, approximately twenty-five, became its first abbot and remained in office until death.

William of Champeaux probably blessed him as abbot. Early Clairvaux was materially austere. Bernard favored plain buildings, restricted diet, simple vestments, silence, manual work, scriptural meditation, and discipline. He criticized the ornamental and culinary wealth of older Benedictine houses.

His early rigor was excessive even by reformed monastic standards. Accounts say that the monks’ health suffered and that ecclesiastical superiors temporarily removed practical control from him or compelled him to moderate his regime. Bernard himself later acknowledged errors of severity. The evidentiary core—that he initially imposed extreme austerity and subsequently moderated it—is stronger than the dramatic details.

Clairvaux expanded rapidly. By Bernard’s death it had sent out numerous daughter houses and stood at the center of a large filiation. Traditional figures assign sixty-eight foundations directly or indirectly to Bernard, but totals depend on whether incorporations, daughter houses, and foundations by former Clairvaux monks are counted together.

Bernard was not detached from property. Clairvaux required land, water, forests, agricultural labor, mills, granges, livestock, manuscripts, and patronage. Its institutional wealth expanded even while its monks rejected personal property and ornamental display. Surviving evidence does not permit a modern balance sheet for Bernard personally; as a monk, he had no legitimate private fortune. His influence nevertheless directed substantial corporate resources.

## Early Writings and Monastic Controversies

### *The Steps of Humility and Pride*

Bernard’s first major treatise, conventionally dated to the 1120s, was *De gradibus humilitatis et superbiae*. It recast the Rule of Saint Benedict’s ladder of humility by describing a contrary descent through pride. It combined moral psychology, satire, monastic observation, and scriptural exegesis.

### The dispute with Cluny

In the *Apologia ad Guillelmum abbatem*, written for William of Saint-Thierry, Bernard defended Cistercian simplicity and attacked extravagance in established Benedictine houses. He mocked elaborate food, expensive clothing, enormous churches, sculptural monsters, and visual distractions in cloisters.

His target was associated especially with Cluny, whose abbot **Peter the Venerable** became both critic and respected correspondent. Bernard’s position was not a total repudiation of Cluniac monasticism. The *Apologia* admits that different disciplines may suit different communities and rebukes Cistercians for pride toward other monks.

The document remains a major source for Romanesque art, monastic consumption, institutional competition, and ascetic ideology.

### Relations with Carthusians and other reformers

Bernard corresponded with Carthusians, Premonstratensians, canons regular, hermits, bishops, and monasteries. He served as adviser, adjudicator, recruiter, and critic across institutional boundaries.

## The Knights Templar and Sacralized Warfare

Bernard became the most important theological advocate of the early **Knights Templar**.

At the **Council of Troyes in 1129**—sometimes dated 1128 according to local calendrical reckoning—the Templars received formal ecclesiastical recognition. Bernard’s precise role in composing their Latin rule is debated. Later tradition made him its principal author; modern scholarship more cautiously sees him as a sponsor or influential participant within a collective process.

His *Liber ad milites Templi de laude novae militiae*—*In Praise of the New Knighthood*—was addressed to **Hugh of Payns**, the Templars’ first master. Bernard contrasted secular knights, whom he condemned for vanity and private violence, with disciplined religious warriors defending pilgrims and holy places.

The treatise’s most controversial element is its theory that killing an enemy of Christ in a licit sacred struggle could be “malicide,” the killing of evil rather than ordinary homicide. Bernard did not supply a general license for indiscriminate violence, but he gave an influential spiritual justification to a permanent military-religious order.

No evidence establishes that Bernard personally invested in the Templars, owned their property, commanded troops, or traveled to the Levant.

---

## Papal Schism, 1130–1138

Following the disputed papal election of 1130, two claimants emerged:

- **Innocent II**, elected by a minority of cardinals;
- **Anacletus II**, elected by a larger body and supported by powerful Roman connections and Roger II of Sicily.

Bernard became the leading international advocate for Innocent II. At the Council of Étampes, the French king and clergy accepted Innocent, reportedly under Bernard’s influence. Bernard traveled through France, Germany, and Italy, negotiated with rulers and churchmen, preached Innocent’s legitimacy, and attempted to detach supporters from Anacletus.

He helped secure or consolidate recognition from:

- Louis VI of France;
- Henry I of England;
- Lothair III;
- ecclesiastical leaders in France and the Empire;
- the city of Milan.

Bernard’s case rested on judgments about canonical legitimacy, personal reputation, and the need for unity. Polemical accounts also invoked Anacletus’s descent from the converted Jewish Pierleoni family. The degree to which Bernard himself relied on antisemitic lineage arguments, rather than later partisans amplifying them, requires care.

Bernard opposed **Roger II of Sicily**, Anacletus’s principal royal supporter. In 1137 he debated or negotiated with Anacletus’s representative **Peter of Pisa**, who subsequently changed sides. Anacletus died in January 1138. His successor Victor IV soon submitted to Innocent, ending the schism.

Bernard declined episcopal advancement and repeatedly represented himself as desiring monastic withdrawal. Nevertheless, his conduct during the schism made him one of Latin Christendom’s most powerful unelected political actors.

## Ecclesiastical and Political Networks

Bernard’s correspondence contains more than five hundred letters in traditional editions, though authenticity and numbering vary. His correspondents included:

- popes;
- cardinals;
- kings and queens;
- emperors;
- dukes and counts;
- bishops;
- abbots and monks;
- nuns;
- students;
- crusaders;
- persons seeking protection or intervention.

He intervened in episcopal elections, disputed jurisdictions, monastic discipline, noble marriages, political reconciliation, clerical conduct, benefices, and patronage. His influence depended on moral authority, family networks, Cistercian expansion, literary skill, and access to rulers—not on formal jurisdiction outside Clairvaux.

He could use severe rhetoric. Opponents were described as proud, corrupt, fraudulent, heretical, or spiritually diseased. His letters also show affection, grief, humor, tactical retreat, self-criticism, and persistent intercession.

## Abelard, William of Saint-Thierry, and the Boundaries of Theology

### Background

**Peter Abelard** developed a highly dialectical theology and had already faced condemnation at Soissons in 1121. William of Saint-Thierry compiled objections to Abelard’s writings and alerted Bernard.

Bernard’s criticism centered on Abelard’s treatments of:

- the Trinity;
- Christ’s redemption;
- grace and free will;
- sin;
- the relationship between faith and rational inquiry.

Bernard believed Abelard submitted mysteries to methods incapable of containing them and risked turning faith into intellectual conjecture. Abelard and his defenders believed Bernard misunderstood, excerpted, or distorted complex arguments and used ecclesiastical influence to suppress a rival mode of theology.

### Council of Sens

In 1140—or 1141 under another dating system—Abelard requested a public hearing at Sens. Bernard prepared propositions extracted from Abelard’s works. When the assembly met, Abelard refused substantive debate and appealed to Rome. The bishops condemned propositions attributed to him; Pope Innocent II confirmed the condemnation and ordered Abelard’s writings restricted.

Abelard found protection with Peter the Venerable at Cluny. Peter mediated reconciliation with Bernard and secured Abelard’s peaceful final years. Abelard died in 1142 in communion with the Church.

### Evidentiary classification

- **Documented fact:** Bernard organized and prosecuted doctrinal objections against Abelard.
- **Documented fact:** propositions were condemned at Sens and by Rome.
- **Documented fact:** Abelard appealed rather than completing the planned confrontation.
- **Disputed interpretation:** whether Sens was a fair doctrinal proceeding, a prearranged political prosecution, or both.
- **Hostile contemporary representation:** Berengar of Poitiers satirized Bernard’s conduct and accused him of manipulation and hypocrisy.
- **Unresolved textual problem:** whether every condemned proposition accurately represented Abelard’s intended meaning.

The conflict was not simply “mysticism versus reason.” Bernard used sophisticated reasoning; Abelard was also religious and contemplative. The dispute concerned authority, method, language, and acceptable theological risk.

---

## Gilbert of Poitiers

Bernard also participated in proceedings concerning **Gilbert of Poitiers**, whose technical language about God and divine attributes provoked suspicion. At the Council of Reims in 1148, Gilbert was questioned before Pope Eugenius III.

Bernard attempted to formulate a confession of faith, but several cardinals resisted what they saw as his effort to impose a new doctrinal formula without proper process. Gilbert clarified or modified contested formulations and retained his bishopric.

This episode reveals a limit to Bernard’s power. Unlike the Abelard affair, it did not end in Gilbert’s destruction or deposition. It also exposed friction between Bernard and the papal curia.

---

## Pope Eugenius III and *De consideratione*

**Bernard of Pisa**, formerly a monk of Clairvaux, became Pope **Eugenius III** in 1145. Bernard thus acquired a former subordinate as pope.

His five-book *De consideratione*, composed over several years for Eugenius, combined spiritual counsel with institutional criticism. Bernard warned that constant litigation, administration, ceremonial demands, and political business could destroy the pope’s inner life. He criticized ambition, patronage, curial excess, and the confusion of spiritual office with domination.

The relationship was not simple control by a “puppet master.” Eugenius sometimes followed Bernard, sometimes resisted him, and possessed his own curial and political constraints. Bernard could admonish the pope but could not dictate every policy.

---

## Second Crusade, 1146–1149

### Commission and preaching

After the fall of Edessa in 1144, Eugenius III issued the crusading bull *Quantum praedecessores*. Bernard became the campaign’s most famous preacher.

At **Vézelay on 31 March 1146**, in the presence of **Louis VII of France** and **Eleanor of Aquitaine**, Bernard preached the crusade. Later accounts say the crowd exhausted prepared cloth crosses and that Bernard tore his clothing into strips. The mass recruitment is credible; the clothing episode is a dramatic tradition.

Bernard then preached across northern and eastern France, Flanders, and the Rhineland. At **Speyer in December 1146**, he helped persuade **Conrad III of Germany** to take the cross. He preached through interpreters where necessary.

His letters promised spiritual remission and presented the expedition as penitential service. He also supported crusading activity on other fronts, including Iberia and the Baltic, reflecting the widened papal conception of crusade.

### Radulf and attacks upon Jews

A monk named **Radulf, Raoul, Rudolf, or Ralph** independently preached violence against Jewish communities in the Rhineland. Ecclesiastical authorities appealed to Bernard.

Bernard wrote against killing Jews and traveled to confront Radulf. He ordered or persuaded him to return to monastic obedience. Jewish narrative traditions preserved awareness of Bernard’s intervention.

Bernard’s protection was not based on modern religious equality. He regarded Judaism as theological error and accepted Jewish subordination. His position was that Jews should not be killed or forcibly destroyed because they survived as scriptural witnesses and might ultimately be converted.

Thus:

- **Documented fact:** Bernard opposed Radulf’s massacres and forced preaching campaign.
- **Documented fact:** he intervened personally and rhetorically to restrain anti-Jewish violence.
- **Documented fact:** his theology remained supersessionist and treated Jewish continued existence instrumentally.
- **Disputed quantitative question:** the number of victims before Bernard stopped Radulf cannot be securely established.

### Failure

The crusade ended disastrously. Conrad’s and Louis’s armies suffered enormous losses in Anatolia; the attack on Damascus collapsed in 1148. Bernard’s preaching had attached his reputation to an expedition he did not command and whose strategy he did not determine.

Critics accused him of false prophecy and reckless mobilization. Bernard responded that God’s promises had not failed; Christian sin and unworthiness had made the crusaders undeserving, paralleling biblical explanations for Israelite defeat.

This explanation protected the divine mandate while relocating responsibility to participants. It did not erase damage to Bernard’s reputation. Plans for a new expedition under his leadership or inspiration did not materialize.

---

## Hildegard of Bingen

Around 1146–1147, **Hildegard of Bingen** wrote to Bernard seeking judgment or encouragement concerning her visions. Bernard’s surviving response was courteous but cautious. He acknowledged reports of her gifts, urged humility, and did not give an expansive endorsement.

Their letters occupy a complicated manuscript tradition. The correspondence is accepted as substantially historical, but scholars have debated ordering, redaction, and the extent to which Hildegard’s later letter collections reshaped individual documents.

Bernard may have helped make Hildegard’s case known in circles associated with Pope Eugenius III and the Synod of Trier, but claims that he personally “approved” all her revelations or launched her career overstate the evidence.

---

## Malachy of Armagh and Ireland

Bernard formed a close relationship with **Malachy of Armagh**, the Irish reforming churchman. Malachy visited Clairvaux, sought Cistercian participation in Irish reform, and died there in Bernard’s arms or presence on 2 November 1148.

Bernard wrote the *Vita sancti Malachiae*, a life of Malachy, and sermons commemorating him. The work is a major source for Malachy but is hagiographical and shaped by Bernard’s program for ecclesiastical reform.

The later **“Prophecy of the Popes,”** attributed to Malachy, is not a work of Bernard and does not appear in Bernard’s life of him. It surfaced in print only in the late sixteenth century and is generally treated as a later fabrication.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Friends, mentors, and collaborators

**William of Saint-Thierry:** intimate friend, theological interlocutor, initiator of the case against Abelard, first biographer.

**Geoffrey of Auxerre:** Abelard’s former student who became Bernard’s secretary, monk, biographer, and later abbot.

**Gerard of Clairvaux:** Bernard’s brother and close monastic collaborator. Gerard’s death produced one of Bernard’s most personal passages in the Song of Songs sermons.

**Stephen Harding:** abbot of Cîteaux and institutional superior who commissioned the Clairvaux foundation.

**William of Champeaux:** bishop, ally, and likely consecrator or blesser of Bernard as abbot.

**Pope Innocent II:** papal claimant whose cause Bernard promoted throughout the schism.

**Pope Eugenius III:** former Clairvaux monk and recipient of *De consideratione*.

**Malachy of Armagh:** friend, guest at Clairvaux, and subject of Bernard’s hagiography.

**Hugh of Payns:** Templar leader and recipient of *In Praise of the New Knighthood*.

**Suger of Saint-Denis:** major ecclesiastical and political figure in Capetian France; their relationship combined cooperation with differing institutional and aesthetic commitments.

### Opponents and rivals

**Peter Abelard:** principal theological opponent.

**Berengar of Poitiers:** Abelard’s defender and author of a hostile satire against Bernard.

**Anacletus II and his supporters:** political and ecclesiastical opponents during the papal schism.

**Roger II of Sicily:** leading royal supporter of Anacletus.

**Radulf:** monk whom Bernard compelled to abandon violent anti-Jewish preaching.

**Gilbert of Poitiers:** subject of a doctrinal intervention which Bernard did not control as successfully as the Abelard case.

### Complex relationships

**Peter the Venerable:** institutional counterpart, sometime critic, mediator, and respected correspondent.

**Louis VII:** crusading king whom Bernard advised and sometimes rebuked.

**Conrad III:** German king recruited to the Second Crusade.

**Eleanor of Aquitaine:** present in the crusading political environment and at Vézelay; no secure evidence supports later stories of an intimate personal relationship or prolonged private spiritual direction.

## Religious and Intellectual Development

Bernard’s theology was monastic, biblical, affective, and rhetorical. Its recurring movement was from self-knowledge through humility and purification toward love and experiential knowledge of God.

### Love

In *De diligendo Deo*, Bernard described ascending forms of love:

1. loving oneself for oneself;
2. loving God for benefits received;
3. loving God for God’s own sake;
4. in rare moments, loving oneself only for God’s sake.

The final condition is not a permanent earthly achievement but a momentary participation in transformed desire.

### Humility and self-knowledge

Humility was not merely social abasement. It was accurate knowledge of one’s condition before God. Bernard paired it with compassion: recognizing one’s own weakness should produce mercy toward others.

His own government did not always conform to this ideal. His career contains recurrent tension between denunciations of pride and the extraordinary confidence with which he judged abbots, bishops, theologians, kings, and popes.

### Scripture and the Song of Songs

Bernard’s eighty-six *Sermones super Cantica Canticorum* cover only the opening portion of the biblical Song. They interpret the bride and bridegroom as soul and Word, Church and Christ, desire and divine response.

The sermons were literary compositions related to chapter conferences, not necessarily verbatim transcripts. Their “experience” language made them foundational for later Western mysticism.

### Christology

Bernard emphasized the humanity and name of Jesus, affective attachment to Christ, the Passion, and the soul’s conformity to the Word.

### Mary

Bernard wrote influential Marian homilies, especially those on *Missus est*. He promoted confidence in Mary’s intercession and helped shape later Marian devotion.

However, the famous prayer **“Memorare”** is not securely authored by him. Nor did he teach every later doctrine associated with “Bernardine” Marian spirituality. He opposed or questioned celebration of the Immaculate Conception as it was being introduced at Lyon, arguing that the feast lacked sufficient warrant and that sanctification should not be confused with conception.

### Grace and free will

In *De gratia et libero arbitrio*, Bernard followed Augustine in affirming both divine grace and the reality of human willing. Freedom was damaged by sin but not annihilated; salvation remained impossible without grace.

### Church and office

Bernard defended papal primacy while criticizing curial bureaucracy, litigation, luxury, and political distraction. He distinguished spiritual care from domination but participated deeply in political church government.

## Institutions, Projects, Property, and Financial Interests

### Clairvaux

Clairvaux was Bernard’s central institutional project. It accumulated estates through donations, purchases, exchanges, and patronage. Its monks and lay brothers developed granges, mills, vineyards, woodland, pasture, and agricultural production.

Bernard’s rhetoric of poverty concerned personal renunciation and institutional simplicity rather than the permanent absence of corporate wealth. Cistercian expansion altered local economies, landscapes, labor relations, and patterns of landholding.

### Cistercian network

Clairvaux’s daughter houses formed a transregional administrative and spiritual network. Bernard traveled among monasteries, advised abbots, resolved disputes, and cultivated patrons. The network amplified his correspondence and political leverage.

### Labor

Cistercian houses employed or incorporated **lay brothers**, tenants, hired laborers, and dependent rural populations. The romantic image of choir monks personally transforming untouched wilderness obscures the role of pre-existing settlements and non-choir labor.

### Personal finances

There is no evidence of private investment, commercial partnership, personal treasure, patents, or individually owned businesses. Bernard had renounced personal property. He nevertheless exercised decisive influence over a large ecclesiastical corporation and solicited material support for foundations and political-religious campaigns.

### Templars

Bernard supplied ideological and reputational capital to the Knights Templar. No source consulted establishes a personal financial share in Templar property or banking activity. Later conspiracy narratives connecting Bernard to secret Templar treasure lack contemporary evidence.

## Journeys and Geographical Movements

Bernard’s principal locations and journeys included:

- Fontaine-lès-Dijon: birth and childhood;
- Châtillon-sur-Seine: schooling;
- Cîteaux: entry into monastic life;
- Clairvaux: residence and abbacy from 1115;
- Châlons: relationship with William of Champeaux;
- Paris: preaching to clerics and ecclesiastical business;
- Étampes: support for Innocent II;
- England: diplomatic activity associated with recognition of Innocent;
- Germany and the Empire: papal diplomacy and later crusade preaching;
- Milan, Pavia, Pisa, Genoa, and Rome: schism-related activity;
- Aquitaine: efforts against the Anacletan alignment and ecclesiastical conflict;
- Sens: Abelard proceedings;
- Vézelay: crusade preaching;
- Speyer, Mainz, Frankfurt, Constance, and other Rhineland or imperial centers: crusade recruitment;
- Trier: ecclesiastical affairs associated with Eugenius III and Hildegard’s milieu;
- Reims: Gilbert of Poitiers proceedings;
- Metz: final major peace mission, probably in 1153.

He never traveled to Jerusalem or participated physically in the Second Crusade.

## Illness, Asceticism, and Physical Condition

Bernard experienced recurrent and sometimes severe illness from early in his abbacy. Sources mention:

- stomach cramps;
- inability to digest food;
- vomiting or regurgitation;
- extreme weakness;
- emaciation;
- episodes requiring seclusion or nursing;
- difficulty riding and traveling;
- progressive decline near death.

Some symptoms may have been aggravated by fasting and early ascetic practices. Claims that he suffered a specific modern disease—cancer, peptic ulcer, Crohn’s disease, anorexia, or another diagnosis—are retrospective speculation.

Hagiography treats his endurance as evidence of sanctity and sometimes describes healings occurring through him even while he remained chronically ill.

No reliable evidence supports addiction, psychiatric hospitalization, or a defined psychological disorder. Accounts of visions, ecstasy, scruples, grief, or altered states belong to medieval spiritual categories and cannot responsibly be converted into modern diagnoses.

## Controversies and Adverse Evidence

### Coercive ecclesiastical influence

Bernard possessed no judicial office beyond his abbacy but helped initiate or shape proceedings against theologians. His campaign against Abelard is the strongest example. Critics accused him of using reputation, episcopal alliances, and advance condemnation to prevent a fair hearing.

**Status:** documented intervention; fairness and intent remain disputed.

### Rhetorical violence

His polemics frequently personalized disagreement and depicted opponents as spiritually diseased or dangerous. Such language had consequences in institutions capable of censorship and punishment.

**Status:** documented in his letters and controversial works.

### Crusade advocacy

Bernard was the chief public preacher of a military expedition that caused mass death and failed strategically. He offered remission of sins within the authorized crusading system and supplied theological legitimation for war.

**Status:** documented fact.

He did not command the armies, choose the Anatolian routes, or plan the Damascus attack.

**Status:** documented limitation of responsibility.

### “Malicide”

His defense of Templar killing remains one of his most controversial formulations. It was bounded by his account of legitimate sacred warfare but participated in the sanctification of lethal force.

**Status:** documented theological position.

### Jews

Bernard opposed massacres and forced anti-Jewish preaching in 1146. Simultaneously, his theology described Jews as unbelieving witnesses preserved under Christian domination.

**Status:** both elements documented; portraying him either as a modern defender of religious equality or simply as an advocate of massacre falsifies the record.

### Heresy and religious repression

Bernard supported action against doctrines he considered heretical and participated in the ecclesiastical machinery of condemnation. Claims that he personally ordered mass executions are unsupported by the sources consulted.

### Institutional poverty versus wealth

Bernard condemned luxury while Clairvaux became a powerful landholder. Medieval monastic theory distinguished communal resources from personal ownership, but the material contrast was visible even to contemporaries.

**Status:** documented institutional tension, not evidence of personal embezzlement.

### Family recruitment

Bernard’s recruitment of brothers, relatives, and companions dismantled much of his natal family’s secular future. Hagiography presents it as inspired persuasion; modern social history recognizes pressure, charisma, kinship obligation, and collective conversion.

There is no evidence of kidnapping or a legally adjudicated inheritance fraud.

### Treatment of Bartholomew

A family tradition says Bernard angrily expelled his brother Bartholomew from Clairvaux and later admitted fault and restored him. The story fits a broader record of Bernard acknowledging excessive severity, but its details depend on religious biography rather than administrative documentation.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### Aleth’s white dog

**Story:** Bernard’s pregnant mother dreamed of a white barking dog. An interpreter predicted that her son would guard the Church and preach against its enemies.

**Appearance and circulation:** incorporated into Bernard’s early hagiographical tradition and repeated in later biographies and art.

**Evidence:** no independent contemporary verification; it is structured like a biblical or saintly prenatal omen.

**Effect:** established Bernard as divinely appointed watchdog and preacher.

**Classification:** hagiographical legend.

### The swarm of bees and “Mellifluous Doctor”

Later iconography associates Bernard with bees, a beehive, or honey because of his eloquence. The title *Doctor Mellifluus* refers to the perceived sweetness of his teaching and language.

**Classification:** symbolic association grounded in reception, not a literal biographical event.

### Lactation of Saint Bernard

**Story:** while Bernard prayed before an image of the Virgin, the statue came alive and squirted milk from Mary’s breast into his mouth or eye.

**Appearance:** prominent in later medieval and early modern art, especially Iberian and Central European representations.

**Evidence:** absent as a securely documented event from Bernard’s life; derived from devotional legend.

**Effect:** symbolized Marian favor, infused wisdom, and Bernard’s privileged eloquence.

**Classification:** later devotional legend and iconographic myth.

### “Bernard founded the Cistercians”

The Cistercian reform began at Cîteaux before Bernard entered. Robert of Molesme, Alberic, and Stephen Harding preceded him.

**Classification:** popular historical error generated by Bernard’s later dominance.

### Sole authorship of the Templar Rule

Bernard is often called author of the Templar Rule. His advocacy and presence in the relevant ecclesiastical network are secure, but the rule developed through collective institutional processes and subsequent revisions.

**Classification:** disputed attribution or simplification.

### Secret Templar knowledge and treasure

Modern occult and conspiracy narratives connect Bernard, the Templars, Solomon’s Temple, hidden relics, sacred geometry, the Grail, and secret bloodlines.

No contemporary source consulted establishes Bernard’s possession of occult technology, Temple treasure, hidden gospels, or a dynastic secret. His known Templar interest was public, theological, and ecclesiastical.

**Classification:** modern conspiracy mythology without documentary support.

### Bernard and Gothic architecture

Bernard’s aesthetic of simplicity influenced Cistercian architecture. Claims that he secretly designed Gothic cathedrals or encoded esoteric geometry go beyond the evidence. His criticism of ornament also sits uneasily beside the later magnificence of institutions influenced by his cult.

**Classification:** influence is documented broadly; secret authorship is myth.

### The bees and the infant Jesus

Later stories present the infant Jesus appearing to Bernard or Bernard receiving miraculous sweetness directly from Christ. These reflect the affective Christology of his writings but cannot be treated as witnessed events.

**Classification:** devotional legend.

### Miracles

The *Vita prima* attributes healings, exorcisms, prophecy, supernatural discernment, and control over natural events to Bernard. The narratives were collected partly for canonization and frequently contain named places or witnesses, but they were selected within a sanctity-producing genre.

**Classification:** reported miracle testimony within hagiography; supernatural causation is not historically verifiable.

### Bernard as Europe’s ruler

The label “uncrowned pope” expresses his influence but is not an office. Kings, bishops, cardinals, cities, and rival churchmen repeatedly resisted him.

**Classification:** reputational metaphor.

### Clairvaux as an untouched wilderness

Cistercian memory emphasized a desolate valley transformed by monks. Archaeological and social approaches to monastic colonization warn that “wilderness” rhetoric often concealed previous settlement, land use, tenants, and negotiated displacement.

**Classification:** institutional foundation myth containing a factual core of rural development.

## Death

By 1153 Bernard believed death was near. Despite severe weakness and longstanding digestive illness, he remained active. His last important political journey was a peace mission to Metz, where civic conflict had produced violence.

He returned to Clairvaux and died there, probably on **20 August 1153**. Older authorities say **21 August**. He was approximately sixty-three.

No precise modern cause of death can be established. Chronic gastrointestinal disease, malnutrition, the cumulative effects of asceticism, and an unidentified terminal illness are possibilities rather than diagnoses.

He was buried at Clairvaux near the high altar. The abbey and tomb experienced later rebuilding, revolutionary suppression, dispersal, and transformation. Clairvaux was converted into a prison under Napoleon, complicating the physical continuity of Bernard’s memorial landscape.

---

## Canonization, Cult, and Relics

Bernard’s cult developed immediately. The *Vita prima* had begun before his death and was completed with canonization in view. He was canonized by **Alexander III in 1174**, only twenty-one years after death.

His feast became 20 August. Churches, monasteries, schools, parishes, and places were dedicated to him. Relic claims multiplied and portions of his remains were reportedly transferred or dispersed after the French Revolution. Exact chains of custody for individual relics require case-by-case investigation.

Pope Pius VIII declared him a Doctor of the Church in 1830. Pope Pius XII’s 1953 encyclical *Doctor Mellifluus*, issued for the eighth centenary of Bernard’s death, reinforced his modern Catholic status.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Cistercian legacy

Bernard became the defining public face of Cistercian spirituality. His texts supplied the order with a vocabulary of humility, desire, reform, simplicity, and contemplative experience.

His fame sometimes obscured Stephen Harding, other early Cistercians, women’s communities, lay brothers, patrons, tenant populations, and the institutional machinery that enabled expansion.

### Mysticism

The Song of Songs sermons influenced medieval and early modern accounts of spiritual marriage, contemplation, desire, and union with God. His influence passed through monastic, Franciscan, Dominican, Rhineland, Devotio Moderna, and later Catholic traditions.

### Protestant reception

Reformation writers, including Martin Luther and John Calvin, could cite Bernard favorably on grace, faith, and the limits of human merit while rejecting his Marian devotion and ecclesiology. Protestants sometimes represented him as an evangelical voice trapped inside medieval Catholic structures.

### Dante

Dante made Bernard his final guide in the *Paradiso*. Bernard replaces Beatrice and prays to Mary before Dante’s culminating vision. This fixed Bernard’s reputation as contemplative and Marian intercessor in European literature.

### Marian reception

Later devotion magnified Bernard as a supreme Marian doctor. Some prayers and sayings were attached to his name without secure attribution. His actual Marian theology was influential but narrower and more historically specific than the later Bernardine corpus.

### Crusade historiography

Confessional biographies often treated the Second Crusade as a tragic but spiritually sincere episode. Modern scholarship examines Bernard as a producer of crusading ideology, mass mobilization, and sanctified violence while also documenting his restraint of anti-Jewish attacks.

### Abelard historiography

Enlightenment and Romantic narratives frequently cast Abelard as reason and Bernard as authoritarian faith. Catholic and monastic histories often reversed the moral polarity. Current scholarship tends to reconstruct institutional procedure, textual misunderstanding, genuine doctrinal difference, and personal politics without reducing either man to a slogan.

### Art and culture

Bernard appears in:

- illuminated manuscripts;
- Cistercian sculpture and painting;
- Marian lactation scenes;
- Dante illustration;
- altarpieces;
- stained glass;
- Counter-Reformation imagery;
- modern religious film, radio, and television treatments.

### Historical reinterpretation

Modern research has shifted attention toward:

- the composite and strategic character of the *Vita prima*;
- Bernard’s family as knightly rather than simplistically “high noble”;
- corporate Cistercian property and labor;
- the textual history of his works;
- women correspondents and religious communities;
- coercion in doctrinal controversy;
- Jewish evidence for 1146;
- the distinction between Bernard’s authentic writings and the enormous pseudonymous corpus.

## Works by Bernard

Dates are approximate because many works circulated in stages, grew from oral conferences, or survive without autograph manuscripts.

### Authentic or substantially authentic treatises

1. ** *De gradibus humilitatis et superbiae* ** — *The Steps of Humility and Pride*; approximately 1120s. Bernard’s earliest major treatise.
2. ** *Apologia ad Guillelmum abbatem* ** — *Apology to Abbot William*; approximately 1124–1125. On Cistercian and Cluniac observance, luxury, art, food, and monastic pride.
3. ** *De diligendo Deo* ** — *On Loving God*; probably 1120s–1130s. Sometimes translated *On the Love of God*.
4. ** *De gratia et libero arbitrio* ** — *On Grace and Free Choice*; approximately late 1120s.
5. ** *De praecepto et dispensatione* ** — *On Precept and Dispensation*; approximately 1130–1144. On abbatial authority and the Rule.
6. ** *Liber ad milites Templi de laude novae militiae* ** — *In Praise of the New Knighthood*; probably 1128–1136.
7. ** *De conversione ad clericos* ** — *On Conversion, Addressed to Clerics*; based on an address to Parisian clerics, commonly placed around 1140.
8. ** *De consideratione libri quinque ad Eugenium III* ** — *Five Books on Consideration to Pope Eugenius III*; composed approximately 1148–1153.
9. ** *De moribus et officio episcoporum* ** — *On the Conduct and Office of Bishops*; addressed to Henry of Sens, commonly dated around 1127–1128.
10. ** *Vita sancti Malachiae episcopi* ** — *Life of Saint Malachy, Bishop*; written after Malachy’s death in 1148.
11. ** *Sententiae* ** — collections of theological and spiritual sentences. Their relation to Bernard’s oral teaching, secretaries, and later compilation requires textual qualification.
12. ** *Parabolae* ** — parables or exemplary narratives associated with Bernard’s teaching; individual textual histories vary.

### Sermons

1. ** *Sermones super Cantica Canticorum* ** — eighty-six sermons on the Song of Songs, composed over many years and unfinished at Bernard’s death.
2. ** *Sermones de tempore* ** — sermons for the liturgical year.
3. ** *Sermones de sanctis* ** — sermons for saints’ feasts.
4. ** *Sermones de diversis* ** — sermons on diverse subjects.
5. ** *Sermones in festivitate Omnium Sanctorum* ** and other liturgical groups.
6. ** *Homiliae super “Missus est”* ** — four homilies on Luke 1:26–38, important for Bernard’s Marian reception.
7. Sermons for the Nativity, Advent, Epiphany, Easter, Pentecost, Assumption, dedication of churches, deaths, and monastic occasions.

The sermon corpus includes authentication problems. Some texts represent edited literary compositions rather than stenographic records of preaching.

### Letters

Traditional editions contain approximately **530 letters**, but totals and numbering vary. They include correspondence with popes, rulers, bishops, abbots, monks, nuns, theologians, and lay patrons.

Important groups include:

- letters concerning the papal schism;
- letters against Abelard;
- correspondence with Peter the Venerable;
- correspondence with Hildegard of Bingen;
- letters promoting the Second Crusade;
- letters opposing Radulf’s anti-Jewish violence;
- advice to Eugenius III;
- monastic administrative letters;
- letters of intercession and recommendation.

Some letters were drafted by secretaries, revised for collection, or transmitted in multiple recensions. Medieval authorship did not always require autograph composition.

### Liturgical and musical work

Bernard participated in or authorized revision of Cistercian chant. The **letter on the revision of Cistercian chant**, *Epistola de revisione cantus Cisterciensis*, is associated with this reform. The precise division of labor between Bernard, musicians, scribes, and the order’s leadership is not always recoverable.

### Works attributed to Bernard but disputed or spurious

The pseudonymous Bernardine corpus is large. Notable examples include:

- ** *Meditationes* ** — not securely Bernard’s.
- ** *Soliloquium* / *Soliloquia* ** — transmitted under his name but not authentic as traditionally claimed.
- ** *De interiori domo* ** — *On the Interior House*; generally not accepted as Bernard’s.
- ** *Scala claustralium* ** — *The Ladder of Monks*; authored by Guigo II the Carthusian, not Bernard.
- **“Memorare”** — famous Marian prayer traditionally associated with Bernard but not securely his composition.
- ** *Floretus* ** and various moral verse collections — attribution doubtful or false.
- Numerous sermons assigned in manuscripts or early print to Bernard but now attributed to **Nicholas of Clairvaux**, **Guerric of Igny**, or other authors.
- Various Passion prayers and Marian texts circulating as “Prayer of Saint Bernard” belong to later devotional traditions.
- Bernard did not write the sixteenth-century “Prophecy of the Popes” attributed to Malachy.

### Principal scholarly editions

The standard modern Latin edition is:

**Jean Leclercq, Henri Rochais, and collaborators, eds., *Sancti Bernardi Opera*, 8 volumes, Rome, 1957–1977.**

Important earlier editions include:

- Jacques Merlin, *Opera*, early sixteenth century;
- Jean Mabillon, *Sancti Bernardi Opera*, 1667 and later revisions;
- J.-P. Migne, *Patrologia Latina*, volumes 182–185;
- the bilingual French *Sources Chrétiennes* series based substantially on modern critical work.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted evidence are included. Mere contemporaneity has not been treated as a connection.

### Bernard of Clairvaux

**Nature:** subject of this dossier and member of the atlas roster.  
**Dates and places:** c. 1090–1153; Burgundy, Cîteaux, Clairvaux, France, England, Italy, Germany, and other parts of Latin Europe.  
**Evidentiary label:** documented fact.

### Hildegard von Bingen

**Nature:** correspondence. Hildegard sought Bernard’s judgment concerning her visions around 1146–1147. His surviving reply was encouraging but reserved. The exchange belongs to the earliest phase of her external correspondence.  
**Dates and places:** c. 1146–1147; Hildegard’s community in the Rhineland and Bernard at or traveling from Clairvaux.  
**Evidentiary label:** documented fact, subject to manuscript-redaction questions.

### Aurelius Augustinus

**Nature:** major acknowledged intellectual and theological influence. Bernard’s theology of grace, freedom, love, self-knowledge, and scriptural interpretation repeatedly operates within the Latin Augustinian tradition. *De gratia et libero arbitrio* is particularly indebted to Augustine. This was textual influence, not a personal meeting.  
**Dates and places:** throughout Bernard’s writing career, especially the 1120s–1150s; Clairvaux and the wider Latin monastic world.  
**Evidentiary label:** documented intellectual influence.

### Origen of Alexandria

**Nature:** indirect and sometimes direct exegetical influence, especially through the patristic tradition of interpreting the Song of Songs as the union of soul or Church with the divine bridegroom. Bernard did not meet Origen and did not merely reproduce him; the influence reached Bernard through Latin patristic and monastic transmission.  
**Dates and places:** especially during composition of the Song of Songs sermons, c. 1130s–1153, Clairvaux.  
**Evidentiary label:** documented intellectual tradition; the exact pathway of individual passages can be textually disputed.

### Jesus of Nazareth

**Nature:** central religious object and declared source of Bernard’s monastic vocation, Christology, devotion, preaching, and mystical theology. This is a documented intellectual and devotional relationship, not a historical face-to-face meeting.  
**Dates and places:** throughout Bernard’s religious life and writings, 1110s–1153.  
**Evidentiary label:** documented religious influence; supernatural encounters belong to faith testimony or hagiography.

### Dante Alighieri

**Nature:** posthumous literary reception. Dante made Bernard the final human guide in *Paradiso*, where Bernard directs Dante toward contemplation and offers the final prayer to the Virgin. Bernard could not have influenced Dante through personal contact; the connection is textual and reputational.  
**Dates and places:** early fourteenth century, Italy; approximately 150 years after Bernard’s death.  
**Evidentiary label:** documented posthumous influence.

### Francis of Assisi

**Nature:** no direct meeting was possible: Francis was born approximately twenty-eight years after Bernard’s death. Both appear within histories of medieval reform, poverty, affective devotion, and crusading, but the consulted sources do not establish a specific, explicitly acknowledged Franciscan dependence sufficient for a firm atlas connection.  
**Evidentiary label:** absence of documented personal connection; broader influence remains a research question.

### Maimonides

**Nature:** no documented meeting, correspondence, or institutional relationship. They were near-contemporaries for only part of Maimonides’ childhood; Bernard died when Maimonides was about fifteen. Bernard’s intervention in Rhineland Jewish affairs does not create a connection to Maimonides.  
**Evidentiary label:** no documented connection.

### Richard I of England

**Nature:** no personal connection. Richard was born in 1157, four years after Bernard’s death. Later crusading and Templar traditions sometimes place them in the same symbolic history, but this is not a biographical crossing.  
**Evidentiary label:** no documented connection; later associative mythology only.

### Saint Anthony of Padua, Albertus Magnus, Thomas Aquinas, Ramon Llull, Roger Bacon, Dante Alighieri, and other later medieval atlas figures

Except for Dante’s explicit literary use of Bernard, no specific relationship has been recorded here merely on the basis of belonging to later medieval Christianity. Any textual influence would require work-specific documentation.

**Evidentiary label:** absence is the present finding.
