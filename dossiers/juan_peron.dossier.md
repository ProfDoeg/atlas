# Juan Domingo Perón: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

Juan Domingo Perón’s life is unusually difficult to separate from the documentary war conducted around him. His governments produced an official leader cult; the regime that overthrew him in 1955 created investigatory commissions and an equally programmatic counter-narrative; exile-era Peronism circulated authenticated, edited, and sometimes disputed directives; and later Peronist factions claimed incompatible versions of his legacy.

This dossier uses the following labels:

- **Documented fact:** supported by official records, contemporaneous documents, authenticated correspondence, legislation, or convergent archival evidence.
- **Reported fact:** stated by credible witnesses or scholarship but not independently established here from a primary record.
- **Allegation:** a claim formally or publicly made but not established by adjudication or conclusive evidence.
- **Court finding:** a judicially determined matter, noting jurisdiction and limitations where relevant.
- **Disputed claim:** materially conflicting evidence or interpretations remain.
- **Rumor or myth:** historically consequential narrative lacking adequate proof.
- **Propaganda:** a claim circulated primarily for political mobilization; this classification concerns use, not necessarily underlying truth.

Dates generally follow official civil and military records. Disputed birth information is treated separately.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Juan Domingo Perón.
- **Recorded baptismal name:** Juan Domingo Sosa, according to accounts of the 1898 baptismal register, which reportedly omitted his father’s name.
- **Common forms:** Juan Perón; Juan D. Perón; General Perón.
- **Political titles:** President of the Argentine Nation; founder and conductor of the Justicialist/Peronist movement; president of the Partido Justicialista.
- **Military rank:** Lieutenant general in later usage. He graduated as a second lieutenant and rose through the Army before being compulsorily retired after the October 1945 crisis. His rank and honors were removed after 1955 and restored in the democratic period.
- **Names used in exile:** Perón sometimes signed or was addressed through coded or indirect correspondence, but no stable public pseudonym comparable to a literary pen name is established.
- **Supporters’ names for him:** *el General*, *el Conductor*, *el Líder*, and, in movement rhetoric, *el primer trabajador*.
- **Hostile political names:** *el tirano prófugo* and descriptions of his government as the *segunda tiranía*, terminology institutionalized by the post-1955 regime.
- **Official birth date and place:** 8 October 1895, Lobos, Province of Buenos Aires, Argentina.
- **Civil-registration complication:** Frequently cited descriptions of the civil record say Mario Tomás Perón registered the child on 8 October and declared that he had been born the preceding day, 7 October. Other official biographies simply give 8 October.
- **Alternative birth claim:** Hipólito Barreiro argued that Perón was born in Roque Pérez on 7 October 1893 and that the record was later altered to overcome military-college restrictions concerning legitimacy and ancestry. This is a **disputed claim**, not the accepted civil identity.
- **Death:** 1 July 1974, Quinta Presidencial de Olivos, Vicente López, Province of Buenos Aires.
- **Recorded immediate cause:** cardiac arrest or cardiac failure in the context of chronic ischemic heart disease and worsening cardiopulmonary health.
- **Nationality:** Argentine.
- **Religion:** baptized and publicly identified as Roman Catholic; his political relationship with Catholicism ranged from alliance to violent conflict.
- **Principal occupation:** professional Army officer, military educator, government minister, vice president, politician, political exile, and three-time elected president.
- **Presidential terms:**
  - 4 June 1946–4 June 1952.
  - 4 June 1952–21 September 1955, terminated by military overthrow.
  - 12 October 1973–1 July 1974, terminated by death.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Mario Tomás Perón** (1867–1928), his father, worked in agricultural and livestock enterprises and moved the family between Buenos Aires Province and Patagonia. He was the son of the physician and public figure Tomás Liberato Perón.

**Juana Salvadora Sosa** (1874–1953), his mother, came from a less socially prominent Buenos Aires provincial family. Perón’s parents were not married when Juan and his older brother were born; they married in Buenos Aires on 25 September 1901.

The official Argentine archival authority lists Perón as the son of Mario Tomás Perón and Juana Salvadora Sosa.

### Grandparents and ancestry

His paternal grandfather, **Tomás Liberato Perón Hughes** (1839–1889), was a physician, university teacher, public-health official, and provincial legislator. The paternal line included Italian/Sardinian, British, French, and Franco-Basque ancestry in published genealogical reconstructions.

His maternal grandparents are generally identified as **Juan Ireneo Sosa Martínez** and **María de las Mercedes Toledo Gaona**. Claims of substantial Indigenous ancestry through the maternal line are discussed below. The surviving record supports a less elite maternal background but does not conclusively resolve the more specific Tehuelche identity asserted in later literature.

### Siblings

- **Mario Avelino Perón** (1891–1955), older brother.
- Some genealogies list an infant brother, **Alberto Perón**, who died very young. Documentation is less consistently reproduced than for Mario.

### Marriages

1. **Aurelia Gabriela Tizón** (“Potota”), married 5 January 1929 in the military church Nuestra Señora de Luján. An educator and artist, she died from uterine cancer on 10 September 1938. They had no documented children.

2. **María Eva Duarte** (“Evita”), civil marriage on 22 October 1945 in Junín and a religious ceremony later that year, according to the customary reconstruction. She became his most important political partner, organized women and charitable-welfare networks, led the Fundación Eva Perón, and served as a central intermediary between Perón, labor, and the public. She died of cervical cancer on 26 July 1952. No children are documented.

3. **María Estela Martínez Cartas** (“Isabel,” “Isabelita”), married in Madrid on 15 November 1961. She had met Perón during his exile, commonly dated to Panama in 1955–56. She acted as emissary to Argentina, became his 1973 vice-presidential running mate, and succeeded him as president after his death. They had no documented children.

### Children and paternity claims

Perón left no legally recognized biological children.

**Martha Holgado claim:** Holgado maintained for decades that she was Perón’s daughter and pursued judicial recognition. His remains were exhumed for testing. In 2006, court-commissioned analysis by the Fundación Favaloro’s immunogenetics center excluded paternity; a laboratory retained by Isabel Perón reportedly reached the same conclusion. A sample sent by Holgado to Britain was reportedly inconclusive because of sample condition. The legally operative conclusion was that she was not Perón’s daughter. This is a **court-supported scientific exclusion**, notwithstanding Holgado’s continued disagreement.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1895–1911

Perón’s early geography was divided between the Buenos Aires pampas and Patagonia. His parents moved south around 1899–1900, living or working near Río Gallegos and later in Chubut. Accounts associate the family with ranches or rural properties including Chaok-Aike, Kamesa-Aike, and Coy-Aike, followed by residence around Camarones.

The boys were later sent to Buenos Aires for formal schooling and lived with paternal relatives, including their grandmother Dominga Dutey and aunts connected with teaching. Perón subsequently fashioned Patagonia as a formative landscape: horses, hunting, rural labor, Indigenous vocabulary, distance from metropolitan society, and the masculine independence of the frontier all entered his autobiographical persona.

The family was not part of Argentina’s landed oligarchy. It possessed educated and professionally distinguished paternal connections but experienced unstable provincial agricultural fortunes. This intermediate position—neither urban proletarian nor great landowning elite—later permitted competing representations of Perón as a provincial outsider, aspiring officer, *criollo*, or concealed member of the establishment.

### Birthplace and Indigenous-ancestry controversy

The accepted institutional account identifies the birthplace as Lobos and the date as 8 October 1895. Barreiro’s *Juancito Sosa, el indio que cambió la historia* argued for Roque Pérez, 7 October 1893, and direct Tehuelche ancestry through Juana Sosa. The theory proposes that records were rearranged because the Colegio Militar would have rejected an illegitimate or Indigenous candidate.

Evidence cited for the revision includes anomalies between civil and baptismal entries, family testimony, the parents’ delayed marriage, and racial exclusions within contemporary military society. Against it stand Perón’s lifelong official identity, the extant civil registration, institutional biographies accepted during his lifetime, and genealogical research favoring Lobos. The existence of nonmarital birth is well supported; the changed year and birthplace and specific Tehuelche identification remain **disputed claims**.

Perón himself occasionally embraced an Indigenous or *criollo* symbolic identity. Whether this reflected precise genealogy or political-cultural identification cannot be conclusively determined. The story became important in efforts to recover a subaltern, non-European Perón.

## Education and Formation

### Civil schooling

His early schooling took place after his return from Patagonia to Buenos Aires. Published biographies identify attendance at schools run or influenced by his paternal aunts and later preparation for military admission. Accounts that he originally wanted to study medicine connect that desire to his grandfather Tomás Liberato Perón.

### Colegio Militar de la Nación

- Entered: 1 March 1911.
- Graduated: 18 December 1913.
- Rank: second lieutenant, Infantry.

His professional formation emphasized discipline, topography, military history, physical education, organization, and the Army’s conception of itself as a national institution above party politics.

### Escuela Superior de Guerra

Perón entered the Staff College in 1926. He later taught military history there and joined the General Staff. His early publications included military hygiene, morale, campaign history, strategy, and Patagonia.

### Athletic formation

Perón practiced boxing, fencing, skiing, athletics, and equestrian activities. Sport was not merely recreational: he treated physical training as an instrument of discipline, morale, military preparation, and mass organization. His governments later invested heavily in organized sport and its political symbolism.

### Influences

His thought was eclectic rather than reducible to one doctrine:

- Argentine Army nationalism and the institutional memory of José de San Martín.
- Catholic social teaching, especially corporative and social-organic conceptions.
- European interwar military theory.
- Italian Fascism’s organization of labor, propaganda, and state mediation.
- Social-democratic welfare measures, although Perón rejected class-based Marxism.
- Syndicalism and practical negotiations with Argentine union leaders.
- German military organization and the idea of national mobilization.
- The Argentine nationalist critique of foreign economic dependency.
- Pragmatism: he repeatedly described doctrine as adaptable to historical circumstances.

## Early Military Career, 1913–1930

After graduation Perón served with the 12th Infantry Regiment in Paraná, Entre Ríos, from 1914 to 1919. He was promoted to lieutenant in 1915 and first lieutenant in December 1919. He subsequently served in a succession of infantry, training, and staff assignments.

His professional writings included instructional texts on:

- military hygiene;
- military morals;
- the Upper Peru campaigns;
- the eastern front in the First World War;
- the Russo-Japanese War;
- operational and strategic thought.

He was promoted to captain in 1924 and entered the Escuela Superior de Guerra in 1926.

### The 1930 coup

Perón participated on the military side of the movement that overthrew President Hipólito Yrigoyen on 6 September 1930, although biographers differ over his precise role and factional alignment. He was initially connected with officers around General José Félix Uriburu but was closer politically to the legalist-nationalist current associated with General Agustín P. Justo than to Uriburu’s explicitly corporatist project.

This was Perón’s first direct involvement in the overthrow of a constitutional government. Later official Peronist narratives minimized it or presented it as youthful military duty; opponents treated it as evidence that his later electoralism was instrumental. The participation itself is a **documented fact**; the extent of his enthusiasm and operational importance is debated.

## Military Educator, Chile, and Europe, 1930–1941

By 1930 Perón was attached to the Army General Staff and teaching military history. His first wife, Aurelia Tizón, maintained an intellectually and artistically inclined household. Her death in 1938 left him a widower.

### Chile, 1936–1938

As a major and then lieutenant colonel, Perón served as military attaché at the Argentine embassy in Santiago. He observed Chilean military organization and assembled military information. Chilean authorities reportedly suspected him of intelligence activity; accounts differ on whether a subordinate’s acquisition of restricted material precipitated his departure. This episode belongs to ordinary military-intelligence competition rather than a proven criminal espionage conviction.

### Patagonia scholarship

In 1935 he published *Toponimia patagónica de etimología araucana*, an attempt to organize Indigenous-derived place names. It combined military-geographical interest, philology of uneven scholarly quality, and his continuing self-identification with Patagonia.

### Europe, 1939–1941

The Army sent Perón on a study mission centered in Italy. He trained or observed mountain warfare and skiing, attended military institutions, and traveled through Italy, Spain, Germany, France, Hungary, Yugoslavia, and Albania. Claims that he formally studied for a semester at the University of Turin recur in biographies but require qualification regarding enrollment status.

He closely observed Mussolini’s regime. Perón later admired its ability to integrate organized labor into the state and create what he saw as unity between government, employers, and workers. He also distinguished this organizational admiration from racial doctrine and from totalitarian repression when later explaining his “justicialist” alternative.

A personal meeting with Benito Mussolini is often asserted in popular biographies. The evidentiary basis is not strong enough to treat it as established. Perón unquestionably observed Fascist Italy and later spoke favorably of aspects of Mussolini’s labor-state system; an actual meeting should be labeled **reported but not conclusively documented**.

He returned to Argentina by early 1941 and worked in mountain training in Mendoza. He became a colonel that year.

## The 1943 Revolution and Rise to Power

### Grupo de Oficiales Unidos

Perón was associated with the secret military lodge generally known as the GOU. Its exact expanded name, membership structure, and the authorship of some attributed documents remain disputed. The group combined nationalism, anti-communism, military professional concerns, hostility to the corrupt conservative electoral system, and opposition to immediate alignment with the Allied powers.

On 4 June 1943 the Army overthrew President Ramón Castillo. Perón initially occupied a relatively minor position in the Labor Department under General Edelmiro Farrell.

### Labor Secretariat

Perón transformed the weak Departamento Nacional del Trabajo into the **Secretaría de Trabajo y Previsión**. Between late 1943 and 1945 he built direct relationships with labor leaders, including socialists, syndicalists, railway workers, meatpackers, and autonomous unionists.

Measures associated with his secretariat included:

- extension and enforcement of collective bargaining;
- labor tribunals;
- pension and retirement expansion;
- paid holidays;
- severance protections;
- the rural labor statute, *Estatuto del Peón*;
- recognition and state incorporation of unions;
- government intervention in labor disputes frequently favorable to workers.

The relationship was reciprocal. Perón used state authority and benefits to organize a durable political constituency; labor leaders used access to him to obtain long-delayed social legislation. Independent or communist-led unions could also face intervention, displacement, or pressure.

### Accumulation of office

Perón became minister of war in February 1944 and vice president in July 1944 while retaining influence over labor administration. His simultaneous control of military and labor channels alarmed rival officers, employers, conservative elites, portions of the Left, and the United States embassy.

### Eva Duarte

Perón met radio and film actor Eva Duarte in January 1944, conventionally at a benefit for victims of the San Juan earthquake. They began living together. Eva helped connect Perón with broadcasting and popular-cultural networks, but later claims that she single-handedly organized his rescue in October 1945 simplify a labor mobilization planned by multiple union leaders and activists.

### Foreign-policy crisis

Argentina maintained neutrality for most of the Second World War and severed relations with the Axis only in January 1944. It declared war on Germany and Japan in March 1945. Washington officials, especially Ambassador Spruille Braden, treated Perón and his military allies as Axis-sympathetic authoritarians.

The opposition coalition used the slogan “Braden or Perón”; Perón converted foreign criticism into a sovereignty-centered electoral weapon. His ideological admiration for elements of Fascism was real, but the claim that his 1946 movement was simply an overseas branch of Nazism was also campaign propaganda.

---

## October 1945: Arrest, Mobilization, and Marriage

On 9 October 1945 Perón was forced to resign his offices by hostile officers. He was detained and taken to Martín García Island, later transferred to the military hospital in Buenos Aires.

The CGT declared a general strike for 18 October. On 17 October, however, large columns of workers—particularly from industrial districts south of Buenos Aires—entered the city and occupied the Plaza de Mayo, demanding his release. Union organizers, spontaneous neighborhood mobilization, police passivity or sympathy, and divisions within the government all contributed.

Perón addressed the crowd from the Casa Rosada late that night. The event became the foundational date of Peronism, commemorated as the **Día de la Lealtad**.

He married Eva Duarte within days. The marriage helped regularize a relationship that the military and conservative Catholic society had treated as scandalous.

## First Presidency, 1946–1952

### Election

Perón defeated the Unión Democrática candidate José P. Tamborini in the election of 24 February 1946. The opposition coalition included Radical, Socialist, Communist, and Democratic Progressive forces and benefited politically from support by Ambassador Braden. Perón’s coalition joined labor, nationalist, provincial, Catholic, and military constituencies.

He took office on 4 June 1946.

### Political system

Perón consolidated the parties supporting him into a centralized organization, ultimately the Partido Peronista. The labor-based Partido Laborista was dissolved; its independent leader Cipriano Reyes resisted and later became an opponent.

The state increasingly blurred distinctions among government, party, unions, and presidential leadership. The executive pressured courts, universities, civil servants, opposition media, and rival unions. Supporters described this as national and social democratization against entrenched elites; opponents described an authoritarian plebiscitary regime.

### Supreme Court and judiciary

The Peronist Congress impeached most members of the Supreme Court in 1947, citing their validation of earlier coups and opposition to the new government. The replacement of the Court increased executive influence over the judiciary. The proceeding was constitutional in form but politically transformative.

### Labor and redistribution

Real wages, union membership, social insurance, paid vacations, workplace protections, and labor’s share of national income rose sharply in the favorable early postwar period. The government encouraged mass consumption, internal tourism, housing, health programs, and worker access to leisure.

The CGT became a pillar of government. This brought workers unprecedented institutional influence but reduced union independence: leadership selection and disputes were subject to state and party intervention.

### Women’s political rights

Law 13,010, enacted in September 1947, established women’s equal national political rights. Eva Perón campaigned intensely for the measure and later organized the Partido Peronista Femenino. Women first voted in a national election in 1951; female Peronist legislators entered Congress in significant numbers.

### Nationalizations and state enterprises

The government nationalized or expanded state control over:

- British- and French-owned railways;
- the Central Bank;
- telephones;
- gas;
- urban transport;
- merchant shipping;
- civil aviation;
- portions of electricity and other utilities.

The railway purchase became a central symbol of “economic independence.” Economic historians disagree about its price and condition. Nationalist interpretations emphasize recovery of infrastructure from foreign ownership; critical accounts stress overpayment, deferred maintenance, operating deficits, and the political theatricality of the transaction.

### IAPI

The Instituto Argentino de Promoción del Intercambio bought agricultural products domestically and sold them abroad, transferring resources from export agriculture to industrialization, public spending, and consumption. During high postwar prices this financed development; when terms of trade deteriorated, it became a major source of rural resentment and fiscal difficulty.

### Industrial policy and planning

The First Five-Year Plan promoted import-substitution industrialization, public works, energy, transport, social policy, and state coordination. Argentina’s accumulated wartime reserves initially made rapid expansion possible.

The system encountered constraints:

- depletion of foreign exchange;
- insufficient energy and capital goods;
- declining agricultural incentives and output;
- drought;
- inflation;
- state-enterprise deficits;
- conflict between consumption objectives and investment requirements.

The later first term shifted from exuberant redistribution toward stabilization.

### Fundación Eva Perón

The Fundación Eva Perón built hospitals, schools, homes, holiday facilities, and distributed material assistance. It created rapid access for people excluded by conventional charity and bureaucracy.

Its finances were opaque by modern nonprofit standards. Contributions came from unions, businesses, state-linked institutions, and sometimes coercive pressure. Critics alleged extortion and personal enrichment. The foundation’s large-scale social work is documented; precise separation among voluntary contributions, political compulsion, public resources, and personal authority remains incomplete.

### Education and culture

The government expanded access to education and eliminated university tuition in 1949. It also intervened universities, dismissed or marginalized opposition professors, promoted official doctrine in textbooks, and cultivated compulsory or quasi-compulsory public reverence for Perón and Eva.

### Media

Government allies acquired radio and newspaper properties, while opposition media faced newsprint controls, labor actions, licensing pressure, prosecution, and closure. In 1951 Congress expropriated *La Prensa* and transferred it to the CGT after an intense political and labor conflict. The government’s defenders cited tax and labor violations; opponents regarded the episode as the destruction of the leading independent newspaper.

### Political prisoners and coercion

Opponents including Cipriano Reyes were imprisoned. Socialist deputy Alfredo Palacios, Radical activists, communists, military conspirators, and others reported surveillance or detention. Testimony exists concerning police torture, notably involving the Federal Police’s Special Section and provincial forces.

The 1955 killing of communist physician **Juan Ingallinella** following torture by Rosario police became one of the best-documented lethal abuses associated with the late Peronist period. This was a state crime by police under a Peronist provincial administration, though no evidence establishes that Perón personally ordered the killing.

Post-coup commissions gathered extensive abuse allegations, but their political mandate and evidentiary practices varied. Later scholarship shows both that political imprisonment and mistreatment occurred and that the new regime sometimes failed to substantiate its broadest accusations.

### Foreign policy and the “Third Position”

Perón sought a “Third Position” formally independent of both United States capitalism and Soviet communism. Argentina restored relations with the Soviet Union, promoted Latin American agreements, maintained close relations with Francisco Franco’s isolated Spain, and pursued pragmatic trade.

Third Position rhetoric combined genuine nonalignment, bargaining between blocs, nationalism, and Perón’s ambition for regional leadership.

### Nazi and collaborationist immigration

**Documented fact:** Argentina under Perón admitted numerous European fascists, Nazi officials, collaborators, and war-crime suspects through migration and intelligence networks. Adolf Eichmann, Josef Mengele, Erich Priebke, Josef Schwammberger, Croatian Ustaša figures, and others reached or lived in Argentina.

Research by Ronald Newton, Uki Goñi, Argentine investigators, and declassified archives connects escape networks to Argentine officials, diplomats, Rodolfo Freude, European intermediaries, and false or irregular travel documentation.

Perón regarded many postwar prosecutions, especially Nuremberg, as victors’ justice and sought European technical and military personnel. The government also admitted ordinary displaced persons and Jewish refugees; Argentine Jewish citizens gained forms of political access, and the state established diplomatic relations with Israel. These facts do not negate the ratlines.

What remains case-specific is whether Perón personally knew the identity and crimes of every entrant. A centrally permissive policy and official facilitation are well supported; individualized personal authorization for each fugitive is not.

### Jewish relations

Peronism included both nationalist antisemites and Jewish Peronists. Perón publicly opposed racial discrimination, cultivated the Organización Israelita Argentina, recognized Israel in 1949, and appointed Jewish officials. At the same time, antisemitic nationalists retained places within his wider coalition, immigration procedures discriminated in practice, and Nazi fugitives received protection. Characterizing the regime as simply philosemitic or simply antisemitic obscures this documented contradiction.

### Project Huemul and Ronald Richter

Perón backed Austrian-German physicist Ronald Richter’s project on Huemul Island near Bariloche. In March 1951 he announced that Argentina had achieved controlled thermonuclear reactions. Scientific inspection failed to validate Richter’s claim; the project was terminated after substantial expenditure.

This was a significant technological and political failure. The episode reflected Perón’s interest in scientific sovereignty, willingness to fund spectacular strategic projects, insufficient peer oversight, and Richter’s unsupported claims. Argentina’s later nuclear program became substantial, but it did not validate the Huemul announcement.

### Constitution of 1949

The new constitution incorporated social rights, labor protections, state responsibility for economic welfare, and the “social function” of property. It also permitted immediate presidential reelection, enabling Perón to run in 1951.

Supporters emphasize constitutional social rights; opponents emphasize irregular political conditions and personal reelection. The post-1955 military regime annulled it by proclamation rather than through an elected constituent procedure.

## Second Presidency, 1952–1955

### Reelection

Perón won the 11 November 1951 election by a large majority, the first national election in which Argentine women voted. Eva Perón had been promoted by mass organizations as a possible vice-presidential candidate but withdrew amid military resistance, illness, and political negotiation. She died on 26 July 1952.

Her death removed Perón’s most powerful emotional and organizational intermediary. Her embalmed body became an object of public devotion and, after the coup, state concealment.

### Economic adjustment

The Second Five-Year Plan emphasized productivity, agricultural recovery, restraint, and selected foreign investment. Wage gains slowed. Perón negotiated with foreign capital, including the controversial proposed California Standard Oil arrangement, contradicting simplistic accounts of unwavering economic autarky.

The stabilization measures reduced some immediate imbalances but alienated constituencies accustomed to the first term’s expansion.

### Death of Juan Duarte

Eva’s brother Juan Duarte, Perón’s private secretary, was found shot dead in April 1953. The official finding was suicide. Rumors alleged murder connected to corruption, internal conflict, or knowledge of financial affairs. No conclusive proof has displaced the suicide finding. The episode became a central component of anti-Peronist corruption mythology.

### April 1953 bombing and retaliation

Bombs exploded during a Peronist rally at Plaza de Mayo on 15 April 1953, killing and injuring participants. Perón responded with an inflammatory speech. Peronist groups attacked and burned opposition premises, including the Casa del Pueblo and Jockey Club. The bombing and retaliatory destruction are documented; responsibility for specific retaliatory orders and the full organization behind the bombing require care.

### Conflict with the Catholic Church

Relations had initially been close. The 1943 regime restored religious instruction; Catholic personnel entered government; Perón and Eva used Catholic social language.

By late 1954, conflict erupted over autonomous Catholic political organizing, youth organizations, education, divorce, prostitution regulation, religious processions, and control of public symbolism. The government legalized divorce, removed religious instruction, restricted public religious activity, and contemplated church-state separation. Catholic networks became a major opposition infrastructure.

### 16 June 1955 bombing

Naval aircraft and supporting military-civilian conspirators bombed and strafed the Plaza de Mayo and surrounding government targets in an attempt to kill Perón and overthrow the government. More than 300 people were killed according to the later reconstructed official count, with hundreds injured. Most victims were civilians.

That night Peronist groups burned or damaged churches and church buildings. Police and fire-service nonintervention has been reported; direct presidential authorization has not been established. Perón condemned the destruction afterward.

### “Five for one” speech

On 31 August 1955, after an apparent resignation maneuver and mass rally, Perón delivered his most violent public threat, declaring in substance that for every Peronist killed, five opponents would fall. The speech is a **documented fact** and marked the failure of his attempted pacification strategy.

### Overthrow

A military revolt began on 16 September 1955 under Generals Eduardo Lonardi and Pedro Eugenio Aramburu and Admiral Isaac Rojas. With the threat of civil war and naval bombardment, Perón delegated authority and sought asylum. He left aboard the Paraguayan gunboat *Paraguay*.

Whether his communication constituted a formal resignation was disputed. Congress did not constitutionally process a resignation; the military removed him by force. His government had become increasingly coercive, but its termination was a coup, not a constitutional succession.

## Exile, 1955–1972

### Geographical movements

Perón’s exile proceeded through several states:

1. **Paraguay, 1955:** protection from Alfredo Stroessner’s government; he moved between the gunboat, official residences, and guarded locations.
2. **Panama, 1955–56:** residence in Colón and Panama City; he encountered the dancer María Estela Martínez.
3. **Venezuela, 1956–58:** residence under Marcos Pérez Jiménez; forced to leave after the regime’s fall.
4. **Dominican Republic, 1958–60:** protected by Rafael Trujillo and resident in or near Ciudad Trujillo.
5. **Spain, 1960–1972:** admitted under Francisco Franco; ultimately settled at the villa **17 de Octubre**, Puerta de Hierro, Madrid.

He traveled or maintained contacts across Latin America and Europe through emissaries. The 1964 attempt to return to Argentina was stopped in Brazil, whose authorities sent him back to Spain after an Argentine governmental intervention.

### Legal and political persecution

The post-coup regime:

- proscribed Peronism;
- dissolved or intervened its organizations;
- prohibited Peronist names, symbols, and songs under Decree-Law 4161/1956;
- confiscated or investigated property;
- annulled the 1949 Constitution;
- tried Perón in military and civil proceedings;
- removed his military rank and political rights;
- concealed Eva Perón’s corpse.

Perón was accused and in some matters convicted in absentia by institutions created under the dictatorship. These proceedings cannot be ignored, but their political context, lack of his presence, and exceptional legal framework limit their reliability as neutral adjudication.

### Peronist Resistance

From exile Perón sent directives encouraging organization, strikes, sabotage, political abstention or tactical voting, and the recovery of unions. Resistance included workplace action, clandestine propaganda, bombings, attempted uprisings, and later armed organizations.

His communications deliberately sustained multiple constituencies. He could validate revolutionary language to the Left while assuring union bureaucrats, nationalists, military contacts, or conservatives that they remained indispensable. This was not merely confusion: ambiguity preserved his control over a prohibited, heterogeneous movement.

### John William Cooke

Perón designated former deputy **John William Cooke** as his representative and potential heir of command in the late 1950s. Their extensive correspondence documents resistance strategy, internal disputes, the Frondizi negotiations, and Cooke’s movement toward revolutionary Marxism and Cuba.

Perón later reduced Cooke’s authority and balanced him against competing delegates. Cooke remained a major theorist of revolutionary Peronism but never became Perón’s uncontested successor.

### Pact with Arturo Frondizi

Perón instructed followers to support Arturo Frondizi in the 1958 presidential election in return for normalization of unions, political opening, and restoration of Peronist rights and property.

A text bearing signatures associated with Perón, Cooke, Rogelio Frigerio, and Frondizi circulated; Perón, Cooke, and Frigerio affirmed the pact, while Frondizi denied signing it. The voting instruction and negotiation are **documented facts**; the authenticity or precise execution of every surviving signed copy remains disputed.

When Perón judged Frondizi noncompliant, he resumed opposition. The relationship illustrates his ability to influence Argentine elections while formally excluded.

### Cuba, Che Guevara, and the revolutionary Left

Cooke and Alicia Eguren developed close relations with the Cuban Revolution and Che Guevara. Perón did not become a Marxist and did not subordinate himself to Havana, but he treated revolutionary Cuba as an anti-imperialist development and praised Guevara after his death in 1967 in a message whose archival transmission and versions have been studied critically.

Claims of a personal Perón–Guevara meeting are not securely established. Their connection was mediated principally through Cooke, Eguren, Cuban officials, messages, and the symbolic politics of the revolutionary movement.

### Union leadership and Augusto Vandor

Metalworkers’ leader **Augusto Timoteo Vandor** pursued “Peronism without Perón,” seeking autonomous union power and electoral negotiation. Perón countered through rival unionists and his emissary Isabel. Vandor’s faction was defeated politically but remained powerful. Vandor was assassinated in 1969; competing organizations claimed or were blamed for the crime. No adequate evidence establishes a personal murder order from Perón.

### Armed organizations

The late 1960s and early 1970s brought the Fuerzas Armadas Peronistas, Fuerzas Armadas Revolucionarias, and Montoneros. Montoneros kidnapped and killed former president Aramburu in 1970. Perón publicly exploited the act’s political effect and called militants a “marvelous youth,” but his precise advance knowledge or operational authorization is unproved.

He endorsed “all forms of struggle” in some contexts while also seeking negotiation with military authorities. His exile strategy combined electoral, union, insurrectionary, and diplomatic methods.

### Return negotiations

General Alejandro Agustín Lanusse’s dictatorship proposed a controlled political opening. Perón appointed Héctor J. Cámpora as delegate, reorganized alliances, and frustrated Lanusse’s attempt to exclude him permanently.

He returned temporarily to Argentina on 17 November 1972, met political leaders including his long-time Radical rival Ricardo Balbín, and helped assemble the Frente Justicialista de Liberación. Because residency rules prevented his immediate candidacy, Cámpora ran under the slogan “Cámpora al gobierno, Perón al poder.”

## Return and Third Presidency, 1973–1974

### Cámpora government

Cámpora won the 11 March 1973 election and took office on 25 May. Political prisoners were released amid mass mobilization, and Congress enacted an amnesty.

Perón returned permanently on 20 June 1973. Armed right-wing groups controlling the reception site near Ezeiza fired on left-wing Peronist columns. At least 13 deaths and hundreds of injuries are commonly reported. The massacre exposed the movement’s internal civil conflict.

Perón’s exact advance knowledge of the attack remains disputed. Organizers close to his right wing, including Jorge Osinde and figures linked to José López Rega, controlled the site. The following day Perón publicly defended hierarchy and criticized infiltrators, politically siding against the revolutionary wing.

### Cámpora’s resignation

Cámpora and Vice President Vicente Solano Lima resigned in July 1973, allowing a new election. Raúl Lastiri, López Rega’s son-in-law, became interim president. The procedure was legal in form but orchestrated to reopen the presidency for Perón.

### Election and office

Perón and Isabel won the 23 September 1973 election with approximately 62% of the vote. He began his third term on 12 October.

His choice of Isabel rather than a stronger labor, military, or cross-party figure prevented an alternative power center but left an inexperienced successor. José López Rega’s influence over both members of the couple was already substantial.

### Social Pact

Economy Minister José Ber Gelbard negotiated the **Pacto Social** among the CGT, employers, and government. Wages and prices were coordinated to reduce inflation and restore investment. It initially moderated conflict but deteriorated under supply constraints, international oil shocks, distributive struggle, and political violence.

### Foreign policy

Argentina restored relations and trade with Cuba, pursued nonalignment, and cultivated Latin American relations. Perón condemned the overthrow of Salvador Allende publicly as a continental misfortune, while his government also maintained state relations with the new Chilean junta. Claims that Perón positively endorsed Pinochet rest partly on disputed readings of diplomatic minutes and should not be merged with his public denunciations.

### Conflict with Montoneros

After Montoneros was blamed for the 25 September 1973 assassination of CGT secretary-general José Ignacio Rucci, Perón’s relationship with the revolutionary Left rapidly deteriorated. Montoneros did not formally claim the killing at the time; later participant accounts and scholarship commonly attribute it to the organization. A judicial conviction conclusively resolving organizational responsibility did not occur.

On 1 May 1974, Montoneros and allied youth groups challenged Perón during the May Day rally. He denounced them from the balcony, and their columns left the Plaza de Mayo. The rupture became public and irreversible.

### José López Rega and Triple A

López Rega—former policeman, occultist, Isabel’s confidant, Perón’s private secretary, and minister of social welfare—accumulated extensive influence. The **Alianza Anticomunista Argentina (Triple A)** emerged as a parapolice death squad targeting leftists, lawyers, artists, union dissidents, and political opponents.

The Triple A’s relationship to López Rega, the Social Welfare Ministry, and security officials is extensively documented and later became the subject of crimes-against-humanity proceedings.

Perón’s personal relationship to the organization requires differentiation:

- **Documented fact:** he elevated and retained López Rega, endorsed hardening against “subversion,” and presided over a government during which the Triple A emerged.
- **Reported fact:** planning and personnel for the organization drew on Peronist right-wing security networks close to the presidency.
- **Unresolved question:** no conclusive judicial finding established that Perón personally founded, operationally directed, or ordered individual Triple A murders.
- **Political responsibility:** this is an interpretive question, not a substitute for documentary proof of command.

### Police and military policy

Perón appointed hard-line police figures including Alberto Villar. His government introduced more severe security legislation and supported the removal of governors or officials associated with the Left, including the federal intervention in Córdoba after the police coup known as the *Navarrazo*.

The full apparatus of clandestine state disappearance associated with the 1976 dictatorship postdated his death. Nonetheless, personnel, doctrines, and parapolice violence active in 1973–74 formed part of its prehistory.

### Final illness

Perón had chronic cardiovascular and respiratory problems. The demands of office, cold weather, and political crisis worsened his condition. Isabel temporarily exercised executive authority in June 1974.

His last major public appearance occurred on 12 June 1974, when he addressed supporters in the Plaza de Mayo and delivered the line remembered as carrying “in my ears the most wonderful music, which is the word of the Argentine people.”

## Death, Funeral, and Remains

Perón died on 1 July 1974 at the Quinta Presidencial de Olivos. The civil death record confirms the date and location. Isabel announced the death and succeeded constitutionally as president.

His body lay in state at the National Congress, attracting an enormous procession despite severe weather. He was buried in the family vault at Chacarita Cemetery.

In October 2006 his remains were transferred to a purpose-built mausoleum at the former San Vicente country residence, now the Museo Histórico 17 de Octubre. Fighting among rival union groups during the transfer ceremony caused death and injuries, demonstrating the continued contest over ownership of Peronist memory.

### Theft of his hands

On 29 June 1987 unidentified intruders entered his Chacarita tomb, opened the coffin, amputated both hands, and took his sword and cap or other objects. A ransom letter alerted the Partido Justicialista.

The crime remains officially unsolved. Investigators considered extortion, political destabilization, access to alleged financial accounts requiring fingerprints, occult ritual, intelligence operations, and internal Peronist conflict. None became a proven explanation.

The death or murder of several people tangentially associated with the investigation fed conspiracy narratives. The basic profanation is a **documented fact**; claims about Swiss accounts, Masonic ritual, military intelligence, or a curse are **unresolved theories or myths**.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Justicialism

Perón presented Justicialism as a national doctrine between individualist capitalism and collectivist communism. Its stated “three flags” were:

- social justice;
- economic independence;
- political sovereignty.

Its institutional model favored an organized community: unions, employers, professions, churches, the military, and the state coordinated under political leadership. It rejected liberal society conceived as atomized, but Perón alternately operated through elections, constitutional reform, corporative representation, executive centralization, and personal arbitration.

### Class and labor

Perón rejected Marxist class struggle while making the organized working class his central constituency. Labor would receive material dignity and political recognition in exchange for integration into a national movement rather than revolutionary independence.

### Democracy

His three presidencies began through competitive elections and mass participation. His governments also weakened liberal checks, subordinated institutions, restricted opposition media, used political imprisonment, and cultivated leader-centered plebiscitary legitimacy. “Democratic” and “authoritarian” therefore describe different documented components of the same system rather than mutually exclusive verdicts.

### Fascism

Perón’s debt to Fascist labor organization, corporatism, mass spectacle, leadership theory, and anticommunism is documented in his European observations and later statements.

Equating Peronism wholly with European Fascism is inadequate because:

- its working-class coalition operated through powerful unions;
- it expanded electoral participation;
- it lacked a comparable racial-imperial project;
- it adapted to electoral competition and ideological pluralism inside the movement;
- it later incorporated revolutionary socialist and Third World currents.

Conversely, denying any Fascist influence conflicts with Perón’s own favorable observations of Mussolini’s system and the personnel and methods surrounding early nationalist Peronism.

### Catholicism and spirituality

Perón used Catholic social concepts and initially relied on Church support. The 1954–55 rupture demonstrated his refusal to accept an autonomous national institution competing for youth, welfare, and moral authority.

López Rega later introduced esoteric ideas, astrology, spiritualism, and occult ritual into the Madrid household. Perón tolerated him and used his services; evidence that Perón shared López Rega’s full occult worldview is weak.

### Geopolitics and universalism

In exile Perón increasingly wrote about continental integration, technological change, population, ecology, resource limits, and an approaching “universalism.” *Modelo argentino para el proyecto nacional* tried to reconcile organized social interests with national planning. Some texts anticipated environmental politics, though posthumous admirers sometimes detach these passages from their immediate 1970s political setting.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

Perón was not principally a private entrepreneur. His financial history concerns public institutions, politically connected business networks, personal property, and exile financing.

### State and movement institutions

- Secretaría de Trabajo y Previsión.
- Partido Laborista, later absorbed into centralized Peronist parties.
- Partido Peronista/Partido Justicialista.
- Confederación General del Trabajo as the principal allied labor federation.
- Partido Peronista Femenino.
- Fundación Eva Perón.
- IAPI.
- Five-Year Plans and the nationalized railway, telephone, aviation, shipping, and utility sectors.
- Comisión Nacional de Energía Atómica and Project Huemul.
- Escuela Superior Peronista, an institution for doctrinal training.

### Personal property

Properties associated with Perón included residences used during office, the San Vicente country estate, and the Puerta de Hierro villa in Madrid. Distinguishing state, foundation, party, donated, and private property became contentious after 1955.

### Exile financing

Perón was supported by:

- personal property and funds that escaped confiscation;
- donations from supporters and unions;
- contributions from politically connected businessmen;
- assistance from host governments;
- the financial and logistical network of businessman **Jorge Antonio**.

The complete accounting remains unavailable.

### Jorge Antonio network

Antonio acquired Mercedes-Benz Argentina interests and major holdings in media, banking, and agribusiness. His fortune grew rapidly during the Peronist decade. Opponents treated him as evidence of presidential cronyism; Antonio described himself as a loyal industrialist persecuted for political affiliation.

He later helped finance Perón in exile. Adolf Eichmann, under the name Ricardo Klement, worked at a Mercedes-Benz facility, and Antonio acknowledged learning his identity. This connection illustrates overlap among postwar German business migration, Peronist patronage, and Nazi refuge networks, but it does not by itself prove that every commercial actor was part of a single clandestine structure.

### Corruption investigations

The 1955 regime created commissions to investigate “ill-gotten gains” involving Perón, officials, the Eva Perón Foundation, Juan Duarte, Jorge Antonio, and public projects. Assets were confiscated or interdicted.

Later scholarship describes these commissions as both repositories of valuable documentation and instruments of political “de-Peronization.” Some cases revealed opaque enrichment, conflicts of interest, coerced donations, or weak controls; other broad charges failed to produce sustainable prosecutions. The commissions’ conclusions must therefore be assessed file by file, not accepted or rejected collectively.

No final neutral accounting establishes a single reliable figure for Perón’s personal fortune.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Political Violence

### 1. Authoritarian government

**Documented:** executive pressure on courts, universities, media, unions, public employment, and opposition politicians; political imprisonment; expansive contempt and state-security laws; official leader cult.

**Qualification:** elections retained mass competitive importance, opposition parties continued to exist, and the regime’s social base was not merely coerced. It was neither a conventional liberal democracy nor identical to a one-party totalitarian state.

### 2. Torture

Survivor testimony and later scholarship support the use of torture by police organs against some opponents. Specific responsibility must be assigned to perpetrators and chains of command where known. The broad opposition assertion that all detention was systematically torturous exceeds what individual records prove; blanket denial is equally unsustainable.

### 3. Nazi fugitives

Official facilitation of postwar escape and entry networks is documented. Perón’s ideological sympathy for defeated Axis personnel and desire for technical expertise were important. Claims that every German immigrant was a Nazi, or that Argentina alone operated the ratlines, are false generalizations.

### 4. Nelly Rivas

Nélida Haydeé “Nelly” Rivas, born in 1939 and associated with the Unión de Estudiantes Secundarios, was repeatedly present in Perón’s post-Eva social environment. After 1955, investigators and newspapers alleged that she began a sexual relationship with him at fourteen.

Rivas was detained after the coup, and her family was punished. Accounts attribute to her later testimony describing a romantic or sexual relationship. Perón denied impropriety and disputed letters attributed to the relationship.

A criminal accusation involving *estupro* did not result in conviction and eventually prescribed. Recent defender Ignacio Cloppet argues that accusatory papers were falsified and that an original military honor-court file described paternal treatment. Other published accounts treat Rivas’s testimony and correspondence as authentic.

Classification:

- Her presence in Perón’s circle: **documented fact**.
- A sexual relationship beginning at age fourteen: **serious allegation/disputed claim**.
- Criminal guilt: **not established by conviction**.
- Assertions that the entire story is conclusively forged or conclusively proved require more confidence than the contested record permits.

### 5. Personal enrichment

Political-connected fortunes and opaque transfers existed, especially around Juan Duarte, Jorge Antonio, the foundation, and media acquisitions. No reliable final accounting proves the extravagant totals circulated in 1955 propaganda as Perón’s personal holdings. The destruction, seizure, and politicized presentation of records complicate reconstruction.

### 6. Political murders

Perón was accused in political literature of ordering various killings. For Juan Ingallinella, Augusto Vandor, Pedro Eugenio Aramburu, José Ignacio Rucci, and Triple A victims, evidentiary chains differ. Direct personal orders from Perón are not established for these deaths. His rhetoric, appointments, factional strategy, or governmental position may be relevant context but should not be presented as a substitute for proof of an order.

### 7. Ezeiza

The right-wing organizational role is documented. Perón’s political support for the organizers after the event is evident. Direct evidence that he planned the shooting remains absent or disputed.

### 8. Triple A

The organization’s state links and López Rega’s central role are strongly documented. Perón’s appointment and protection of the responsible network are documented; his personal operational command remains unresolved.

### 9. Excommunication

After the expulsion of bishops Manuel Tato and Ramón Novoa in June 1955, reports said Perón had been excommunicated. The canonical status was more complicated: Vatican documentation concerned those responsible for violent expulsion and did not necessarily constitute a simple personalized papal excommunication of Perón by name. Paul VI later regularized his standing before the 1973 return. “Perón was personally excommunicated” should be treated as an oversimplified reported claim.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Perón was born Juan Sosa in Roque Pérez in 1893”

The story says his true identity was concealed to overcome racial and legitimacy barriers in the Army. It emerged prominently in Barreiro’s late twentieth-century research and was circulated by Indigenous and revisionist interpretations.

The irregularity of his parents’ marital status and varying civil/baptismal entries support part of the story. The changed year and place are not accepted by the principal archives. It strengthened the image of Perón as a concealed Indigenous and popular figure.

### “Perón invented the Argentine middle class”

This is movement mythology. His governments expanded working-class consumption, social mobility, education, and public employment, but Argentina had substantial middle sectors before 1946.

### “Perón alone gave women the vote”

Law 13,010 was enacted under his presidency, and Eva Perón’s mobilization was decisive. Feminists, socialists, provincial experiments, and earlier legislative campaigns had demanded suffrage for decades. The personal gift narrative became central to Peronist commemoration but compresses a longer history.

### “17 October was entirely spontaneous” versus “entirely staged”

Neither extreme fits the evidence. Union leaders planned action; workers mobilized through workplace and neighborhood networks; government and police divisions created opportunity; and mass initiative exceeded centralized direction.

### “Eva rescued Perón alone”

Eva participated in contacts and became the event’s principal later symbol. Evidence does not support the cinematic version in which she personally organized the entire 17 October mobilization.

### “Perón was a Nazi”

This hostile formula arose during the 1945–46 campaign and was reinforced by his European observations and later refuge policy. It accurately points to Fascist influence and Nazi-protection networks but becomes misleading when used as a complete institutional description of Peronism.

### “Perón was a communist”

Conservative and Catholic opponents used this charge during periods of labor mobilization and the 1955 conflict. Perón was consistently anti-Marxist, suppressed communists in various contexts, and proposed class collaboration. The charge was propaganda, although revolutionary Marxists later entered Peronism.

### “Argentina became poor solely because of Perón”

This long-duration political claim attributes Argentina’s entire relative economic decline to his governments. His policies contributed enduring institutions, protection, fiscal pressures, and distributional conflicts, but relative decline began earlier and involved later governments, commodity cycles, political instability, debt, and repeated policy reversals. The monocausal claim is political mythology.

### “Perón met Mussolini and received a personal lesson in Fascism”

His study of Fascist Italy is documented; the personal meeting is repeatedly reported but inadequately documented. The story magnified both sympathetic images of the attentive statesman and hostile images of the Fascist disciple.

### “Perón possessed secret Swiss accounts accessible with his fingerprints”

This theory became attached to the 1987 theft of his hands. No identified bank record has established it. It remains a conspiracy narrative.

### “The hands were stolen for a Masonic or occult rite”

The theory draws on López Rega, Licio Gelli, Propaganda Due, and ritual interpretations of hands and authority. It has not been proved. Its influence lies in joining Perón’s body to Argentina’s political-occult folklore.

### “Perón’s hands curse investigators”

Deaths and violence involving some investigators generated a curse narrative. Selective association and absence of a proven causal network place it in folklore.

### “Martha Holgado was Perón’s hidden daughter”

The claim received extensive media attention and produced exhumation and DNA testing. Court-commissioned DNA excluded paternity. It survives as personal legend but is contradicted by the strongest scientific evidence.

### “Perón ordered every major act of Peronist violence from exile”

His directives sometimes authorized sabotage and “all forms of struggle,” and he cultivated armed groups politically. The movement was nevertheless fragmented, and operational organizations often acted autonomously. Individual attribution requires individual evidence.

### “Perón returned to pacify the nation”

This was the dominant 1972–73 public expectation and part of Perón’s self-presentation. He pursued reconciliation with Balbín and a Social Pact, but also empowered hard-line figures and expelled the revolutionary Left. The pacifier image was both a political project and a myth contradicted by the escalation surrounding his final government.

## Works by Juan Domingo Perón

Authorship requires caution. Perón wrote military studies, delivered lectures and speeches, dictated material to aides, authorized doctrinal manuals, and later edited or approved transcriptions. Many “books” are posthumous or movement-edited compilations rather than conventionally authored monographs.

### Military and geographical writings

- *Higiene militar* (1924). Early military instructional work; publication details vary by bibliography.
- *Moral militar* (1925). Military instructional text.
- *Campaña del Alto Perú* (1925). Historical-military study.
- *El frente oriental de la Guerra Mundial de 1914: estudios estratégicos*. Published in installments or instructional form; dates vary across editions.
- *Apuntes de historia militar*. Early 1930s; later editions and reprints.
- *La guerra ruso-japonesa de 1904–1905*. Lectures/studies later collected in military-history volumes.
- *Memoria geográfica sintética del Territorio Nacional del Neuquén* (1934, in bibliographic reconstructions).
- *Toponimia patagónica de etimología araucana* (1935).
- *La idea estratégica y la idea operativa de San Martín en la Campaña de los Andes* (1937).

### Early political speeches and compilations

- *El pueblo quiere saber de qué se trata* (1944). Collection of speeches and explanations associated with his labor administration; compilation status should be distinguished from a newly written monograph.
- *Doctrina revolucionaria* (1946). Political-speech compilation under his name; editions differ.
- *Los mensajes de Perón*. Multiple contemporaneous and later collections.

### Doctrinal works

- *La comunidad organizada* (1949). Associated with Perón’s closing address to the First National Congress of Philosophy at Mendoza. The published work derives from a speech/text prepared in a governmental intellectual environment; editorial or collaborative assistance is probable but exact ghostwriting claims remain disputed.
- *Conducción política* (1951). Lectures delivered at the Escuela Superior Peronista, later transcribed and published. Genuine oral authorship with institutional editing.
- *Justicialismo*. Various editions compiling doctrine, speeches, and lectures.
- *Filosofía peronista*. Institutional doctrinal compilation; should not automatically be treated as a solely written monograph by Perón.
- *Política peronista*. Compilation derived from doctrinal teaching.
- *Economía peronista*. Government or party instructional compilation rather than secure sole authorship.
- *Sociología peronista*. Doctrinal instructional publication with institutional production.
- *Doctrina peronista*. Numerous editions with changing selections.

### Exile-era works

- *La fuerza es el derecho de las bestias* (1956). Political account and indictment of the 1955 overthrow.
- *Los vendepatria* (1957). Polemical exile work; several editions.
- *Del poder al exilio: quiénes me derrocaron* (1958). Memoir/interview-derived publication; editorial mediation should be noted.
- *La realidad de un año de tiranía* (1958).
- *Latinoamérica, ahora o nunca* (1967). Essays and geopolitical reflections; compilation history varies.
- *La hora de los pueblos* (1968). Geopolitical and anti-imperialist work.
- *Actualización política y doctrinaria para la toma del poder* (1971). Filmed interviews and statements to Fernando Solanas and Octavio Getino; an audiovisual work later transcribed.
- *Perón-Cooke: correspondencia* (letters, 1956–66; published in later edited editions). Genuine correspondence, editorially selected and annotated.
- Messages, directives, letters, interviews, and recorded addresses from exile, collected across numerous volumes.

### Final-period work

- *Modelo argentino para el proyecto nacional* (1974). Presented in the final presidency and published in official and later editions. Its production involved advisers and state drafting, but it represents Perón’s authorized final program.
- *Mensaje ambiental a los pueblos y gobiernos del mundo* (1972). A brief environmental-political statement widely republished.
- *Yo, Juan Domingo Perón: relato autobiográfico* and similarly titled interview memoirs. These generally derive from interviews, especially those conducted by Torcuato Luca de Tena or other journalists; they are not diaries written without mediation.
- *Las veinte verdades peronistas* (1950). Doctrinal maxims publicly presented by Perón; often reproduced as a separate work.

### Collected editions

- *Obras completas de Juan Domingo Perón*, multi-volume editions published by Fundación Pro Universidad de la Producción y del Trabajo/Fundación Hernandarias and related institutions. These mix:
  - works written by Perón;
  - transcribed lectures;
  - speeches;
  - interviews;
  - correspondence;
  - government plans involving multiple authors;
  - posthumous editorial arrangements.
- Biblioteca del Congreso de la Nación, *JDP: Los trabajos y los días*. Source-based chronological collection of speeches, declarations, documents, interviews, correspondence, and writings.

No definitive critical edition has fully resolved textual variants, ghostwriting, dictation, institutional drafting, and competing versions of exile documents.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Perón left no uncontested ideological or familial heir. This made his name available to mutually hostile successors.

### Political continuity

The Partido Justicialista survived proscription, dictatorship, factional warfare, and ideological transformation. Governments identifying as Peronist have included:

- labor-nationalist and welfare-statist administrations;
- neoliberal privatization under Carlos Menem;
- center-left or national-popular Kirchnerism;
- provincial conservative machines;
- trade-union, feminist, Catholic, secular, and market-oriented factions.

This elasticity reflects Perón’s own coalition management and the absence of a single binding economic program across eras.

### Labor

His most durable institutional legacy is organized labor’s position in Argentine politics: collective bargaining, union-based social services, political identification of workers as national citizens, and the CGT’s continuing role.

### Social rights

Paid holidays, pensions, labor courts, mass welfare, university access, women’s voting rights, constitutional social rights, state economic planning, and public-sector expansion remain central to positive Peronist memory. Some measures predated him or resulted from collective struggle; his governments consolidated them into a new political settlement.

### Institutional criticism

Critics associate his legacy with:

- executive personalism;
- politicization of public administration;
- weak judicial and press independence;
- corporative interest bargaining;
- fiscal and monetary instability;
- leader cults;
- political tolerance of coercion;
- the use of ideologically heterogeneous factions until they became violent rivals.

The degree to which later Argentine crises can be causally attributed to Perón remains a major historiographical dispute.

### Cult and material memory

Perón’s portraits, busts, residences, speeches, songs, and relics became movement objects. The date 17 October functions as a political sacred day. San Vicente, the Lobos birthplace museum, and the Casa Rosada museum collections preserve rival forms of official memory.

His body underwent a political afterlife comparable to Eva’s: guarded, mutilated, exhumed for DNA, transferred, and fought over physically. The missing hands became an emblem of unresolved state secrecy.

### Historical reinterpretations

Major interpretive phases include:

1. **1945–55 official Justicialist narrative:** liberator of workers and architect of the New Argentina.
2. **Anti-Peronist “second tyranny” narrative:** Fascist demagogue, corrupt dictator, and destroyer of institutions.
3. **Modernization sociology:** leader of newly mobilized internal migrants and mass society.
4. **Labor history:** product of bargaining between an autonomous working class and an innovative state actor.
5. **Revisionist nationalism:** anti-imperialist national leader.
6. **Revolutionary Peronism:** potential leader of national liberation, selectively reconstructed from exile rhetoric.
7. **Late twentieth-century institutional critique:** source of personalism and corporative instability.
8. **Recent transnational scholarship:** Peronism as part of global welfare, authoritarian, Catholic, fascist, populist, and postcolonial circulations without reduction to any one of them.
9. **Memory and gender studies:** analysis of the Perón–Eva political partnership, women’s mobilization, domestic symbolism, and the gendered leader cult.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the researched record are included. Mere ideological similarity or shared Argentine cultural space is excluded.

### Eva Duarte de Perón

**Label: documented fact.** Perón met Eva Duarte in Buenos Aires in January 1944 during fundraising connected with the San Juan earthquake. They became partners, married in October 1945, and formed the central political partnership of early Peronism. Eva organized women, welfare activity, and relations with labor until her death in Buenos Aires on 26 July 1952.

### María Estela Martínez Cartas de Perón

**Label: documented fact.** Perón met Martínez during exile, commonly placed in Panama in 1955–56. They married in Madrid on 15 November 1961. She served as his political emissary, became his vice president on 12 October 1973, and succeeded him after his death on 1 July 1974.

### José López Rega

**Label: documented fact.** López Rega entered the Perón household through Isabel’s circle, became Perón’s private secretary in Madrid, accompanied his return, and served as minister of social welfare from 1973. His access made him a principal figure in the Peronist Right and the state-linked Triple A network.

### Benito Mussolini

**Label: documented influence; personal meeting reported but unverified.** Perón studied and observed Fascist Italy during his 1939–40 European mission and later acknowledged admiration for its labor organization and national mobilization. Popular accounts claim he personally met Mussolini, but the consulted evidence does not securely establish the encounter.

### Ernesto “Che” Guevara

**Label: documented indirect political connection; direct meeting unverified.** Perón’s delegate John William Cooke and Alicia Eguren developed close relationships with Guevara and revolutionary Cuba. After Guevara’s death in Bolivia in October 1967, a message attributed to Perón praised him as a revolutionary. Archival scholarship treats the document and its versions critically. No direct personal Perón–Guevara meeting was established.

### Fidel Alejandro Castro Ruz

**Label: reported political connection, principally indirect.** Perón maintained a favorable tactical position toward revolutionary Cuba in exile, partly through Cooke, and his 1973 government restored trade and diplomatic engagement. The dossier found no adequately documented face-to-face meeting between Perón and Castro.

### Augusto Pinochet Ugarte

**Label: documented contemporaneous state relationship; disputed political interpretation.** Perón’s final government maintained relations with Chile after Pinochet’s September 1973 coup. Perón publicly described Allende’s overthrow as a continental misfortune. Later claims that he privately endorsed Pinochet rely on contested interpretations of diplomatic records; no personal meeting between Perón and Pinochet was established.

### Salvador Guillermo Allende Gossens

**Label: reported fact with disputed details.** Testimony attributed to Juan Manuel Abal Medina says Perón sent warnings to Allende in 1973 that a military conspiracy was under way and identified Pinochet. Perón publicly condemned Allende’s overthrow. The exact messages and delivery chain require further archival corroboration; no personal meeting was established here.

### Ronald Richter

**Label: documented fact.** Perón personally sponsored Richter’s thermonuclear research project on Huemul Island and publicly announced its claimed success in Buenos Aires on 24 March 1951. Independent investigators failed to verify the claim; the project was closed.

### Licio Gelli

**Label: reported fact/allegation requiring caution.** Gelli circulated in Argentine and Spanish political networks connected with López Rega and later Peronist officials. Accounts link him with Puerta de Hierro and claim Masonic relations involving Perón. A close personal Perón–Gelli relationship or formal initiation of Perón into Propaganda Due was not established by the sources consulted. Later stories connecting Gelli to the theft of Perón’s hands remain conspiracy theories.

### Enrique Santos Discépolo

**Label: documented political-cultural association.** Discépolo became an outspoken radio defender of Perón in 1951 through the “Mordisquito” broadcasts. His work contributed to Peronist popular culture. This was political advocacy and shared movement activity, not a demonstrated intimate friendship.

### Jorge Luis Borges

**Label: documented antagonistic political relationship, not a personal rivalry.** Borges was an outspoken opponent of Perón. Under the Peronist administration he lost or was pushed from his municipal-library post and was assigned the mocking position of poultry and rabbit inspector, according to his and later biographical accounts. The episode became a major symbol of Peronist cultural persecution, though administrative particulars have been debated.

### Victoria Ocampo

**Label: documented political antagonism.** Ocampo opposed Perón and was imprisoned for several weeks in 1953. Her detention became an international cause among writers and intellectuals. No significant personal meeting or correspondence with Perón was established.

### Pablo Ruiz Picasso

**Label: reported symbolic intersection only.** Picasso’s name circulated within international intellectual opposition and solidarity networks concerning Argentine political prisoners, but the research did not establish a direct relationship with Perón. It is therefore not counted as a substantive personal connection.

### Cristina Elisabet Fernández de Kirchner

**Label: documented posthumous political influence, no lifetime meeting possible.** Fernández de Kirchner’s political career developed after Perón’s death and explicitly operated within the Justicialist movement. This is a legacy connection rather than a biographical crossing.

### Diego Armando Maradona

**Label: documented posthumous symbolic association, no lifetime relationship established.** Maradona publicly identified with Peronist and national-popular symbols in later decades. No documented meeting with Perón was found.

### Juan Domingo Perón

The roster includes the dossier subject himself; this is not treated as a connection.

For the overwhelming majority of the 896-person roster, no documented meeting, correspondence, collaboration, family tie, or acknowledged influence was found. Shared nationality, later ideological appropriation, or mere contemporaneity was not counted.

## Compact Chronology

- **7 or 8 October 1895:** Born and/or officially registered at Lobos; 8 October becomes the official date. Alternative 1893 Roque Pérez theory remains disputed.
- **1898:** Baptized, reportedly as Juan Domingo Sosa.
- **c. 1899–1900:** Family moves to Patagonia.
- **1901:** Parents marry in Buenos Aires.
- **c. 1904 onward:** Sent to Buenos Aires for schooling.
- **1 March 1911:** Enters Colegio Militar de la Nación.
- **18 December 1913:** Graduates as infantry second lieutenant.
- **1914–1919:** Serves with 12th Infantry Regiment at Paraná.
- **1915:** Promoted lieutenant.
- **1919:** Promoted first lieutenant.
- **1924:** Promoted captain; publishes military instructional work.
- **1926:** Enters Escuela Superior de Guerra.
- **5 January 1929:** Marries Aurelia Tizón.
- **6 September 1930:** Participates on the military side of the coup against Yrigoyen.
- **Early 1930s:** General Staff work and military-history teaching.
- **1935:** Publishes *Toponimia patagónica de etimología araucana*.
- **1936–38:** Military attaché in Chile; promoted lieutenant colonel.
- **10 September 1938:** Aurelia Tizón dies.
- **1939–40/41:** European military-study mission centered in Fascist Italy.
- **1941:** Returns to Argentina; colonel; mountain-training duties.
- **4 June 1943:** Military coup; Perón begins political ascent.
- **Late 1943:** Takes control of labor administration.
- **1944:** Becomes secretary of labor and welfare, minister of war, and vice president; meets Eva Duarte.
- **9–17 October 1945:** Removed, arrested, and released after mass worker mobilization.
- **17 October 1945:** Foundational Peronist mobilization in Plaza de Mayo.
- **22 October 1945:** Civil marriage to Eva Duarte.
- **24 February 1946:** Elected president.
- **4 June 1946:** Begins first presidency.
- **1947:** Women’s political-rights Law 13,010; major nationalization program advances.
- **1948:** State purchase of principal foreign-owned railways completed.
- **1949:** New constitution; *La comunidad organizada*; university tuition eliminated.
- **1950:** Proclaims the Twenty Peronist Truths.
- **24 March 1951:** Announces Richter’s claimed thermonuclear success.
- **11 November 1951:** Reelected; women vote nationally for first time.
- **4 June 1952:** Begins second term.
- **26 July 1952:** Eva Perón dies.
- **April 1953:** Juan Duarte dies; Plaza de Mayo bombing and retaliatory attacks.
- **Late 1954:** Open conflict with Catholic Church.
- **16 June 1955:** Failed coup bombs Plaza de Mayo; churches burned that night.
- **31 August 1955:** “Five for one” speech.
- **16–21 September 1955:** Military revolt overthrows Perón.
- **1955:** Exile begins in Paraguay and Panama; meets Isabel Martínez during early exile.
- **1956–58:** Venezuela; directs Peronist Resistance.
- **1958:** Negotiates support for Frondizi; moves to Dominican Republic.
- **1960:** Settles in Spain.
- **15 November 1961:** Marries Isabel Martínez in Madrid.
- **1964:** Attempted return stopped in Brazil.
- **1967:** Issues or authorizes message praising Che Guevara after his death.
- **1968:** Publishes *La hora de los pueblos*.
- **1969:** Cordobazo; Vandor assassinated.
- **1970:** Montoneros kidnaps and kills Aramburu.
- **1971:** Filmed interviews become *Actualización política y doctrinaria para la toma del poder*.
- **17 November 1972:** Returns temporarily to Argentina.
- **11 March 1973:** Cámpora elected.
- **25 May 1973:** Cámpora takes office.
- **20 June 1973:** Perón returns permanently; Ezeiza massacre.
- **July 1973:** Cámpora resigns.
- **23 September 1973:** Perón and Isabel win presidential election.
- **25 September 1973:** José Ignacio Rucci assassinated.
- **12 October 1973:** Third presidency begins.
- **Late 1973:** Triple A begins visible operations.
- **1 May 1974:** Public rupture with Montoneros.
- **12 June 1974:** Last major Plaza de Mayo address.
- **1 July 1974:** Dies at Olivos; Isabel succeeds him.
- **29 June 1987:** His tomb is violated and his hands stolen.
- **2006:** DNA testing excludes Martha Holgado’s paternity claim; remains transferred to San Vicente.
- **Present:** Peronism remains a principal Argentine political tradition; the hand-theft case and several biographical controversies remain unresolved.
