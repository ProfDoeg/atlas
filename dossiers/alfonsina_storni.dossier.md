# Alfonsina Storni: Research Dossier

**Prepared:** 24 August 2026  
**Scope:** Documentary life history, writings, networks, controversies, death narratives, posthumous reception, and bibliography.  
**Editorial note:** Dates and claims below are attributed where the record is disputed. “Poet” is used in the narrative; contemporary Spanish sources frequently use *poetisa*.

## Family and Ancestry

### Swiss-Italian background

- **Father:** Alfonso Storni, born in Luggagia in Ticino. He followed brothers Ángel, Antonio, and Pablo to San Juan, Argentina, arriving in 1883. The brothers first worked in construction and then developed a soda-water, ice, and beer concern, remembered through bottles labeled **“Cerveza Los Alpes, de Storni y Cía.”** A San Juan local history places Ángel and Antonio’s arrival in 1879 and Alfonso’s in 1883; it also notes that the 1894 earthquake damaged the family position ([San Juan al Mundo](https://www.sanjuanalmundo.com/articulo?id=55648)).
- **Mother:** Pasqualina, generally called **Paulina Martignoni** (born about 1866), from the Lugano region. She had a teacher’s education, painted, read widely, sang soprano, played or studied music, and wrote social chronicles in French and Italian. Family tradition called her “la pequeña Patti,” after soprano Adelina Patti; this is family/social legend, not an independently documented professional stage name.
- The parents married at Origlio on **16 October 1886**, then returned to San Juan. Their early household occupied a prosperous provincial social world of musical and literary gatherings.

### Siblings and household

- **María** (born 1887), elder sister.
- **Romeo** (the Cervantes chronology gives this spelling; some derivative accounts say Romero), born 1888.
- **Alfonsina**, third child.
- **Hildo Alberto**, born **28 August 1898**, six years younger. Alfonsina performed substantial childcare for him; he became an early confidant for her writing.
- After Alfonso’s death, Paulina later married **Juan Perelli**. Alfonsina’s maternal household included at least one half-sister, **Olimpia Perelli**, cited in later recollection.

### Father’s illness, alcohol, and economic collapse

From about 1890 Alfonso suffered what relatives and later biographers called melancholy or depression, withdrew from business, disappeared for days on hunting or mining excursions, and drank heavily. Modern diagnostic certainty is impossible. The evidence supports serious mood disturbance and alcohol misuse; claims about a precise psychiatric disorder would be retrospective speculation. His decline, the deterioration of the Storni enterprise, and family moves from prosperity to poverty are central to Alfonsina’s childhood record and recur in her poems about father and mother.

### Son and disputed paternity

Alfonsina’s only child, **Alejandro Alfonso Storni**, was born at Buenos Aires’s Hospital San Roque (later Ramos Mejía) on **21 April 1912** and died in 2009. She never publicly named his father and never married. The child bore her surname, and her public identity as a self-supporting unmarried mother became both a social vulnerability and a literary subject, most conspicuously in “La loba.”

Later genealogical and popular accounts identify **Carlos Tercero Arguimbau**, a married journalist/political figure whom she knew in Rosario, as the father. The consulted institutional biographies do not document an acknowledgment, birth-record declaration, court finding, or statement by Storni establishing this. Accordingly:

- **Documented:** Storni became pregnant in 1911; moved alone to Buenos Aires; gave birth in April 1912; did not name the father publicly.
- **Plausible but unproved in the consulted record:** Arguimbau was the father.
- **Not established:** stories assigning other men or treating the paternity as conclusively solved.

An interview record with Alejandro reiterates that his mother did not name his father ([SEPA Argentina](https://sepaargentina.com/2020/05/04/16-06-2002-alejandro-hijo-de-alfonsina-storni/)).

## Childhood and Early Life, 1892–1908

### Switzerland to San Juan

The family had returned from Argentina to Ticino in 1890/91 on medical advice, taking María and Romeo. Alfonsina was born at Sala Capriasca in 1892. In August 1896 the household sailed from Genoa for Argentina. At four, she had acquired Italian as her first language.

In February 1897 she entered the kindergarten attached to San Juan’s Normal School, participating in recitation, song, dance, and small plays. Her later autobiographical sketch portrayed a conspicuously imaginative child: pretending to read an upside-down book, stealing a primer when her distressed household would not buy it, and inventing sensational crimes, fires, estates, and invitations. These are self-fashioned autobiographical anecdotes. They illuminate how she later narrated herself, but their exact details are not independently verifiable.

### Rosario poverty and child labor

In August 1900 the family moved to Rosario. Alfonso’s first café near the Sunchales station failed and was auctioned in 1901. Paulina opened a home school that reportedly enrolled about fifty pupils. A second venture, the **Café Suizo** on Mendoza Street, lasted only months in 1902. Alfonsina washed dishes and waited tables; women in the household sewed for income. She left regular schooling amid the crisis.

Her father died in August 1906. At fourteen she took work as an apprentice in a cap/hat factory. Later accounts also place her in sewing and other poorly paid employment. No business ownership, investments, patents, or independent property holdings are documented for her.

### Touring theatre

In 1907 Storni joined a touring dramatic company, usually identified with actor-director **José Tallavi**. The troupe traveled through Santa Fe, Córdoba, Mendoza, Santiago del Estero, and Tucumán, presenting plays associated with Ibsen, Pérez Galdós, Florencio Sánchez, and others. The tour was a practical theatrical education—memorization, voice, gesture, staging, touring discipline—but unstable and socially risky for a young woman. She left the company and returned in August to her mother at **Bustinza**, Santa Fe Province.

The theatre episode later fed two competing narratives: heroic independence and scandal. Some retellings claim a scandal over singing on stage drove her to a suicide attempt. The consulted institutional chronology does not substantiate such an attempt; it should be treated as an unverified biographical story unless corroborated by contemporary records.

## Education and Formation, 1909–1911

In 1909 Storni entered the newly created **Escuela Normal Mixta de Maestros Rurales** at Coronda. The admissions register reportedly describes “Alfonsina Storni, 17 años, suiza.” She lacked a full primary certificate and did not pass the ordinary entrance examination, but director **Mercedes Gervasoni** admitted her in a school needing students and recognizing her determination.

She worked as a *celadora* for about forty pesos monthly while paying roughly twenty-five pesos for lodging. A claimed thirty-peso state scholarship secured by deputy Mardoquio Contreras is not documented firmly. At Coronda she studied pedagogy, literature, recitation, and school practice; teachers and classmates remembered theatrical flair and emotional intensity. She qualified as a rural teacher in 1910.

She taught at Rosario-area schools, including in Rosario and Bustinza, and participated in local literary and political circles. Important early figures included:

- **Juan Julián Lastra**, poet-lawyer, her first consequential literary sponsor and later author of the prologue to her first book.
- **Carlos Tercero Arguimbau**, journalist/political associate and possible father of Alejandro; the relationship’s precise character is unresolved.
- Local periodicals such as *Monos y Monadas*, which printed “Anhelos” on 8 January 1912, near the transition to Buenos Aires.

Late in 1911 she learned she was pregnant. Rather than remain under provincial scrutiny, she moved to Buenos Aires, carrying little money, poems, and books by Rubén Darío. This relocation was not an exile in legal terms but a decisive self-directed migration.

## Early Career in Buenos Aires, 1912–1918

### Work, motherhood, and commercial writing

After Alejandro’s birth, Storni supported them through varied employment:

- cashier or shop employee at a pharmacy and at **A la Ciudad de México**;
- small paid contributions to *Caras y Caretas* (a figure of twenty-five pesos is often given);
- unpaid writing for the socialist newspaper *La Acción* and Latin-Americanist *Proteo*;
- “corresponsal psicológico” for the oil-importing firm **Freixas Hermanos**, a position won after a competitive writing examination.

The frequently repeated story says she was the only woman among roughly one hundred applicants, drafted a commercial letter plus advertisements for yerba mate and oil, and received 200 pesos where a male predecessor had earned 400. It comes through later biography rather than surviving payroll files in the sources consulted. It is credible context but the exact numbers remain secondarily attested.

### First book and entry into literary institutions

In March 1916 she financed or arranged publication of **_La inquietud del rosal_**, with Lastra’s prologue. Sales were poor, debts burdensome, and some reviewers objected to its erotic and female-speaking subject. *Nosotros* reviewer Nicolás Coronado treated it as immature but promising. Storni’s presentation of herself as an unmarried mother in “La loba” intensified its notoriety.

On 9 May 1916 she attended a *Nosotros* dinner honoring Manuel Gálvez at the Restaurante Génova. There she entered a network including **Roberto Giusti**, **Alfredo Bianchi**, **Alberto Gerchunoff**, **José Ingenieros**, **Manuel Gálvez**, and later **Horacio Quiroga**. The claim that she was the “first woman” admitted to Buenos Aires literary cenacles should be understood as a contemporary/reputational formulation, not a legally defined office.

Her relations with **Leopoldo Lugones** were chilly. She sent him letters and sought comment; he did not respond. Later explanations—that he feared a female rival—are interpretations, not documented admissions by Lugones.

### Emin Arslan and *La Nota*

The Lebanese-Ottoman diplomat, editor, and writer **Emín (Amīn) Arslán** founded the Buenos Aires weekly **_La Nota_** in 1915. It became an anti-German wartime literary forum open to Río de la Plata intellectuals. Storni began placing poetry and prose there in 1916; “Canto a los niños” appeared in December. From **28 March to 21 November 1919** she held a regular section, first “Feminidades,” later “Vida femenina,” publishing gender analysis, satire, chronicles, and poems including “Convalecer” and “Golondrinas.”

The connection is therefore concrete and institutional: **Arslan was an editor-platform builder who gave Storni sustained periodical space during her emergence**. Sources also list them within overlapping Buenos Aires literary networks, but evidence consulted does not establish a romance, financial partnership, or hidden political alliance. Scholarship on Arslan describes their friendship/social connection while studies of *La Nota* analyze his magazine as the material venue for her insurgent gender journalism ([Scielo Argentina](https://www.scielo.org.ar/scielo.php?pid=S1852-96152012000100008&script=sci_arttext); [Tania Diz bibliography](https://www.aacademica.org/tania.diz)).

### Prize, pacifism, and teaching

Under **Piéridas**, Storni submitted the long “Canto a los niños” to the Consejo Nacional de Mujeres. It won first prize—reported as **655.55 pesos**, donated by the Jockey Club—at the ninth Fiesta del Libro on 30 November 1916. In “Por los niños que han muerto,” printed once in socialist *La Acción* on 6 May 1916, she attacked the slaughter of the First World War through Christian-pacifist imagery. This was political writing, but there is no evidence that she held party office.

In June 1917 she returned to formal teaching at the **Colegio Marcos Paz**, run by the Association for the Protection of Children of Police and Firefighters. A library of more than two thousand volumes assisted her self-education. In 1918 she published **_El dulce daño_** and was honored at another Génova dinner; Ingenieros and Giusti spoke. A period of nervous exhaustion interrupted her teaching.

## Chronological Life History, 1919–1926

### 1919–1920: popularity, journalism, citizenship, awards

**_Irremediablemente_** appeared early in 1919. Storni lectured and recited in socialist halls, neighborhood libraries, and modest theatres, drawing women from popular and middle-class audiences. She traveled to Montevideo to speak on Delmira Agustini and to read her Spanish translations of Delfina Bunge de Gálvez’s French poems.

**_Languidez_** was published in 1920. It received the **First Municipal Poetry Prize** and **Second National Literature Prize**, substantially improving her status. In *La Nación*, often under **Tao Lao**, she created ironic social types and examined women’s legal, occupational, sexual, and cultural subordination. Scholarship treats these columns as strategic gender performance: the masculine/Chinese-sage mask permitted satire of prescriptive femininity. A specialist study is Claudia Edith Méndez’s dissertation on her chronicles ([University of Maryland repository](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/items/52a1a767-e5f2-4515-89dc-7564ae5e46c3)).

On 9 November 1920 she obtained Argentine citizenship. Alfonso Depascale also issued an Italian selection, **_Poesie scelte di Alfonsina Storni_**, an early marker of international circulation.

### 1921–1923: institutional security and nervous illness

In April 1921 she joined the **Escuela de Niños Débiles** in Parque Chacabuco, a public educational-health institution for undernourished children. She attended **Anaconda** gatherings at Emilio Centurión’s studio and other meetings above the Teatro Empire. Work, public recitation, journalism, and single parenthood coincided with fatigue, depression, anxiety, fears of tuberculosis, and feelings of persecution or interpersonal guilt. José Ingenieros, friend, patron, and sometimes medical adviser, recommended annual rest. She began recurring stays at **Los Cocos, Córdoba**, and **Mar del Plata**.

In December 1921 the young Jorge Luis Borges dismissed her poetry in “La lírica argentina contemporánea” in Madrid’s *Cosmópolis*. This was an aesthetic polemic—ultraist avant-garde against her then late-modernist idiom—not evidence of an enduring personal conspiracy. Later accounts sometimes exaggerate Borges into an “enemy”; the record supports harsh criticism and different literary positions.

In 1922 she began teaching at the **Teatro Infantil Labardén**. In 1923 a chair of reading/declamation was created for her at the **Escuela Normal de Lenguas Vivas**. Her income rested chiefly on public teaching, journalism, honoraria, prizes, and book publication, not inherited wealth.

### Horacio Quiroga

Storni and Uruguayan story writer **Horacio Quiroga** became close friends. Letters from 1919–1922 mention her; the two went to cinemas with their children and moved in shared circles. A party anecdote says Quiroga removed his watch while they were to kiss opposite sides, causing them to kiss; her mother was allegedly angered. The story is colorful but not proof of a sexual relationship.

Biographers disagree over whether they were lovers. It is safest to describe an emotionally and intellectually intimate friendship with possible romance, not a documented partnership. Quiroga treated her seriously as a writer. His suicide in February 1937 affected her deeply; she wrote “A Horacio Quiroga,” interpreting suicide as a self-chosen exit while also registering grief.

### 1924–1926: transatlantic recognition and formal change

In 1924 Barcelona’s Editorial Cervantes published an anthology in “Las mejores poesías de los mejores poetas,” and Julio Cejador y Frauca included her in his literary history. She was read in Uruguay, Chile, Mexico, and Spain.

Storni organized the first **Fiesta de la Poesía** at Mar del Plata on 29 January 1925. In May she published **_Ocre_**, whose irony, self-portraiture, and more cerebral line marked transition from accessible late-Romantic/modernist verse toward her own experimental modernity.

In 1926 she taught scenic art at the **Conservatorio Nacional de Música y Declamación** and evening Spanish and arithmetic at the **Escuela de Adultos Bolívar**. She joined **La Peña / Agrupación Gente de Artes y Letras**, led by Benito Quinquela Martín in the Café Tortoni cellar. Its circle included Luis Perlotti, Baldomero Fernández Moreno, Conrado Nalé Roxlo, Juana de Ibarbourou, musicians, painters, actors, and visiting intellectuals.

That September she issued **_Poemas de amor_**, prose poems tracing a romance from infatuation to loss. It was reprinted three times and translated into French by Max Daireaux. Storni’s prefatory disclaimer—that they were states of love rather than “a work of literature”—is authorial positioning, not grounds to exclude them from her canon.

## Chronological Life History, 1927–1934

### Theatre failure and dramatic work

Her three-act comedy **_El amo del mundo_** (originally **_Dos mujeres_**) premiered at the Teatro Cervantes on **10 March 1927** and closed after three performances. Critics attacked its dramaturgy; Storni believed its treatment of women, desire, and male double standards had been misunderstood. Both technical criticism and gender hostility were present in reception; reducing the failure entirely to either one is not warranted.

She wrote **_La debilidad de Mister Dougall_** around 1927. It was not staged and remained unpublished until Delfina Muschietti’s 2002 prose/ theatre volume.

### SADE and literary labor organization

Storni was an important early organizer of what became the **Sociedad Argentina de Escritores (SADE)**. From October 1925 she pressed for an association to protect writers, served as secretary of a provisional commission, convened meetings—including at her apartment at Córdoba 807—and helped draft the institutional path. SADE formally assembled on 8 November 1928 under Leopoldo Lugones, with Quiroga, Samuel Glusberg, Gálvez, Borges, Giusti, and others in leadership.

Contemporary *Nosotros* credited Storni’s energy with overcoming the project’s difficulties. Yet she was absent from the durable top directorate. The reasons are unclear. It is reasonable to note the gendered structure of the field, but no surviving dismissal order or proved conspiracy was found.

### Residences and travel

Addresses documented in correspondence and biographies include Acevedo 2161, José Bonifacio 2011, and the tenth-floor apartment at **Córdoba 807**, Buenos Aires. She also lived for about a year in Rosario around 1928, and regularly stayed at Mar del Plata, Los Cocos, Montevideo, Pocitos, and María Sofía “Fifí” Kusrow’s house at Real de San Carlos, Colonia, Uruguay.

In early 1930 she traveled with **Blanca de la Vega** through Spain, France, and Switzerland. She encountered the milieu of the Spanish Generation of 1927 and published “Diario de navegación” and other travel impressions in *La Nación*. Her “Kodak” sketches adapted modern machines, city fragments, and cinematic perception without submitting to a manifesto.

Early in 1932 she returned to Europe with Alejandro. In Buenos Aires she frequented the **Signo** group at the Hotel Castelar and met **Federico García Lorca** during his 1933 Argentine stay.

### Dramatic recovery and mature poetry

**_Dos farsas pirotécnicas_**—**_Cimbelina en 1900 y pico…_** and **_Polixena y la cocinerita_**—appeared in 1931/32. Berta Singerman staged *Polixena* successfully; *Cimbelina* was not mounted during Storni’s lifetime. She freely reworked Shakespeare and Euripides.

Her children’s theatre included **_Blanco… negro… blanco…_**, whose dependence on Lugones’s “El pierrot negro” prompted criticism. Storni answered that classical motives belonged to no one. This is a documented controversy over adaptation, not a plagiarism judgment by a court or formal body. Other children’s works addressed autonomy, domestic gender roles, freedom, and cruelty to animals.

In November 1934 **_Mundo de siete pozos_** broke sharply with the “love poem”: irregular verse, urban anatomy, objects, fractured perception, and a less anecdotal voice. Gabriela Mistral admired it. The book was dedicated to Alejandro.

## Illness and Final Years, 1935–1938

### Breast cancer

In 1935 Storni discovered a breast mass. Stories connect its discovery to a wave striking her chest while swimming; friend Blanca de la Vega remembered Storni asking her to feel the hard area, and Benito Quinquela Martín helped her seek a specialist. On **20 May 1935** she underwent surgery for a malignant tumor—sources disagree on the affected side; the Cervantes chronology says the **right breast**, while many derivative biographies say left. Without the operative record, the side remains unresolved.

The radical mastectomy, bodily pain, fear of recurrence, and disfigurement intensified her withdrawal, anxiety, and depression. Later sources report morphine for severe pain in October 1938 and a prognosis of roughly six months. These details are biographical testimony rather than a medical chart available here. There is no basis for labeling therapeutic morphine use an addiction.

Claims that she suffered “paranoia” appear in later biographies and government commemorative writing. The record supports suspiciousness, fear that friends were angry, nervous exhaustion, depression, and suicidal thoughts. A definitive psychiatric diagnosis cannot be made from letters and retrospective anecdotes.

### Work despite illness

In February 1936 she published the hybrid chronicle “Film marplatense” in *Crítica*. On 23 May she spoke at events surrounding Buenos Aires’s Obelisk, reportedly composing “Entre un par de maletas a medio abrir y las manecillas del reloj” during the car ride. She continued teaching and directing.

In January 1937 she traveled south through Argentina and Chile; *La Nación* printed her window-view travel series **“Carné de ventanilla.”** Quiroga’s February suicide, then those of his daughter Eglé and Leopoldo Lugones, formed a grim sequence in her circle.

On **27 January 1938** at Montevideo she shared a major public event with **Gabriela Mistral** and **Juana de Ibarbourou**, speaking “Entre un par de maletas a medio abrir y la manecilla del reloj.” The meeting symbolized the later canonical triad of Spanish-American women poets, though their aesthetics and politics were distinct.

Her selected **_Antología poética_** appeared through Espasa-Calpe in August, and **_Mascarilla y trébol: círculos imantados_** in September. The latter’s “antisonnets,” masks, mineral/cosmic images, and severe condensation constitute a late avant-garde phase, not merely a suicide note in book form.

## Death

### Last journey and letters

On 18 October 1938 Storni left Buenos Aires by train for Mar del Plata, seen off at Constitución by Alejandro and Lidia Oriolo de Pizzigatti. She lodged in the Oriolo establishment on Tres de Febrero Street. Letters to Alejandro dated 19 and 22 October show ambivalence and need; a final letter to **Manuel Gálvez** asked him to help ensure Alejandro’s welfare. The surviving Gálvez letter bears judicial stamps from the death inquiry and is catalogued in the [SADE manuscripts repository](https://repositorio.bpm.parquedelconocimiento.com/handle/bpm/349/browse?type=author&value=Storni%2C+Alfonsina).

She mailed **“Voy a dormir”** to *La Nación*. The paper published it after her death. The poem’s final telephone message—she has “gone out”—became inseparable from the event, but it is a literary text, not a forensic description.

### What can be reconstructed

- Around 1:00 a.m. on Tuesday, 25 October, she left her lodging.
- She went toward La Perla and the breakwater associated with the Club Argentino de Mujeres.
- Government commemoration records the claim that one shoe caught in the breakwater ironwork, identifying the point of entry ([Argentina.gob.ar](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/noticias/alfonsina-storni-87-anos-de-su-fallecimiento)).
- Two workers found her body washed onto the beach that morning.
- Her death was investigated judicially as a suicide; surviving farewell material bears case markings.
- Her body was viewed in Mar del Plata, transported to Buenos Aires, and publicly mourned. Sources vary on whether to call the Buenos Aires site a wake at the Club Argentino de Mujeres or emphasize later funeral rites; both may describe stages of the ceremonies.

The immediate causal context includes recurrent cancer, severe pain, mastectomy trauma, depression/suicidal ideation, and the recent suicides of associates. A single motive cannot be proved. Claims that she died “because of a man,” solely because of cancer, or to imitate Quiroga simplify a multi-factor record.

### Funeral and burial

Manuel Ugarte spoke at the funeral. Her remains were placed in the Recoleta vault of her friend **Salvadora Medina Onrubia de Botana** and family, then moved in 1963 to Chacarita. The Chacarita memorial is associated with sculptor Julio César Vergottini/Vergotini; spelling varies in sources.

No patents, inventions, shell companies, investments, political funds, money-laundering structures, or significant real-estate holdings are documented. Financially she moved from childhood business prosperity to poverty, then achieved a precarious professional middle-class life through multiple jobs. The 655.55-peso 1916 prize and later municipal/national awards were material lifelines, not evidence of wealth.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Feminism

Storni’s feminism was expressed through documentary subjects: unequal civil capacity; the sexual double standard; marriage and divorce; women’s education and paid work; fashion and the disciplining of bodies; the woman writer’s exclusion; maternity outside marriage; and masculine entitlement. She wrote in socialist venues and associated with **Carolina Muzzilli**, **Alicia Moreau de Justo**, **José Ingenieros**, **Alfredo Palacios**, and **Manuel Ugarte**, but she was not a party functionary.

Her prose often uses masks, irony, overheard dialogue, mock taxonomy, and a male pseudonym rather than a systematic manifesto. “Tú me quieres blanca” reverses the demand for female purity; “Hombre pequeñito” turns captivity into a miniature cage; the *La Nota* and *La Nación* columns anatomize institutions and habits. The documentary basis for this journalistic formation is the digitized 1992 [*Diario de Poesía* dossier](https://ahira.com.ar/ejemplares/diario-de-poesia-n-23/) and studies collected by Tania Diz.

### Religion and metaphysics

She drew on Christianity as symbol and ethical language—especially in anti-war poetry—without a documented institutional religious career. Her mature poetry moves among materialism, nature, cosmic imagery, death, eroticism, and skeptical invocations. Describing her as conventionally devout or programmatically atheist would exceed the evidence.

### Aesthetic phases

1. **1916–1920:** late Romantic and modernist diction, confession, erotic protest, maternal self-assertion, formal meters.
2. **1925–1926:** *Ocre* and prose poems introduce irony, analytic self-portraiture, and sharper formal control.
3. **1930–1934:** travel, urban “Kodak” perception, theatre, and irregular verse lead toward an idiosyncratic avant-garde.
4. **1938:** *Mascarilla y trébol* develops antisonnets, magnetic/cosmic structures, mask, abstraction, and compressed sound.

This development corrects a common myth that Storni wrote only sentimental love verse. Her most popular poems belong largely to the early phase; her late work is materially different.

## Controversies, Allegations, and Unresolved Questions

### Sexual respectability and unmarried motherhood

Contemporaries attacked early erotic poems and her unapologetic unmarried maternity as immoral. This is documented reception, not evidence of misconduct. The identity of Alejandro’s father remained private; later naming of Arguimbau is not conclusively documented.

### Alleged plagiarism/adaptation

*Blanco… negro… blanco…* closely follows the scheme of Lugones’s “El pierrot negro.” Storni defended reuse of inherited/classical motifs. There was literary criticism but no consulted evidence of litigation, formal plagiarism finding, or financial penalty.

### SADE exclusion

She was crucial to organizing SADE yet did not remain in top leadership. Contemporary acknowledgment of her work is strong; the exact mechanism and motivation of her exclusion are undocumented. Gender bias is a historically grounded hypothesis, not a proved individual plot.

### Mental-health language

Later writers use “paranoia,” “neurasthenia,” and depression. Neurasthenia was a period diagnosis; paranoia is often a retrospective label. Her letters and recollections document anxiety, suspiciousness, exhaustion, and suicidality, but not a modern standardized diagnosis.

### No criminal or financial scandal

No credible evidence was found of criminal activity, fraud, corruption, money laundering, intelligence connections, political violence, abuse, arrest, imprisonment, lawsuit, indictment, or conviction involving Storni. Her controversies were principally sexual-literary, aesthetic, and institutional.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. “She slowly walked into the sea”

1. **Story:** Storni walked calmly into the waves, continuing until the sea took her.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** The image crystallized in posthumous popular biography and especially Ariel Ramírez and Félix Luna’s 1969 zamba **“Alfonsina y el mar.”** It is repeated in commemorative journalism, tourism, and visual art.
3. **Evidence:** Biographical reconstruction places her at a La Perla breakwater/espigón; a caught shoe and recovery of the body are cited. No witness is documented as watching a slow walk into the sea.
4. **Assessment:** Poetic legend, not the best-supported forensic account.
5. **Effect:** It transformed a painful suicide into an internationally legible ritual return to the sea and made the manner of death more famous than much of her writing.

### 2. “She was born at sea”

1. **Story:** Her affinity with the sea was predestined because she was born aboard the emigrant ship.
2. **Circulation:** Popular lore and derivative biographies.
3. **Evidence:** Institutional chronology and family history place the family in Ticino and the birth at Sala Capriasca.
4. **Assessment:** Unsupported legend, probably retrofitted to the maritime death.
5. **Effect:** Strengthens a fate/sea mythology at the cost of Swiss documentary geography.

### 3. “Alfonsina means ‘ready for anything’”

1. **Story:** She said her Arabic-derived name meant *dispuesta a todo*.
2. **Circulation:** Storni’s own autobiographical account and later biographies.
3. **Evidence:** The Cervantes chronology notes scholar Carlos Alberto Andreola’s Germanic derivation from roots meaning noble lineage and ready/prepared.
4. **Assessment:** Self-created etymological mythology rather than accepted linguistics.
5. **Effect:** Became a compact emblem of will, independence, and tragic courage.

### 4. The purely lovelorn suicide

1. **Story:** A failed male love drove her into the sea.
2. **Circulation:** Romanticized press, film, song, and simplified biographies.
3. **Evidence:** No single final lover is documented as causal. The record instead includes recurrent cancer, pain, depression, fear of dependency, literary farewell, and exposure to several friends’ suicides.
4. **Assessment:** Reductionist narrative; emotional relationships mattered, but a one-man cause is unsupported.
5. **Effect:** It domesticates a professional intellectual’s death into the conventional fate of an abandoned woman.

### 5. The permanently sentimental “poetess”

1. **Story:** Storni was primarily a confessional writer of wounded feminine love.
2. **Circulation:** Early mass readership, school anthologies, hostile avant-garde criticism, and the popularity of a small number of poems.
3. **Evidence against:** Journalism, theatre, labor organizing, *Ocre*, urban chronicles, *Mundo de siete pozos*, and *Mascarilla y trébol* demonstrate satire, public argument, dramatic experiment, and formal avant-gardism.
4. **Assessment:** Partial truth turned into a canonical distortion.
5. **Effect:** Made her accessible but delayed serious treatment of late poetry and prose.

### 6. The father of Alejandro as a solved secret

1. **Story:** Carlos Tercero Arguimbau was definitively the father.
2. **Circulation:** Genealogical websites and later popular accounts.
3. **Evidence:** Circumstantial relationship/timing; no consulted acknowledgment, court record, or public statement by Storni.
4. **Assessment:** Possible, not established.
5. **Effect:** Encourages biography to revolve around disclosure rather than her deliberate construction of an autonomous mother-son household.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Immediate memorialization

Her death produced national mourning, Senate homage, front-page reporting, and a striking iconography of a composed, well-dressed body returned by the sea. That newspaper image merged with “Voy a dormir” and later with the song, creating the enduring sea-death cult.

### Luis Perlotti’s monument

Storni and sculptor **Luis Perlotti** were friends through La Peña at Café Tortoni. After La Peña dissolved in 1943/44, proceeds from sale of its furnishings—especially its Steinway piano—helped buy the stone for a memorial. Perlotti carved Storni in profile, garments driven by wind and sea, with lines from her 1925 poem **“Dolor.”** Buenos Aires city’s museum account explicitly documents their friendship and the work’s iconography ([Museo Perlotti/GCBA](https://buenosaires.gob.ar/gcaba_historico/noticias/datos-curiosos-escultura-de-alfonsina-storni)).

Sources sometimes date the work “1942,” apparently referring to conception/creation, while the best specific inauguration account gives **14 February 1948**. The monument first stood high on the barranca, reportedly facing away from the sea for visibility, and was later reoriented/re-sited to face it. It now stands on Paseo Dávila opposite La Perla, but not at the exact point of death. A Buenos Aires historical account confirms that Tortoni assets funded the stone and that inauguration occurred in 1948 ([Buenos Aires Historia](https://buenosaireshistoria.org/juntas/quinquela-y-la-pena-del-cafe-tortoni-el-sotano-mas-celebre-de-buenos-aires/)).

The monument is a pilgrimage site, frequently marked by graffiti, flowers, recitation, and tourist ritual. A related sculptural memorial stands at her Chacarita grave; other monuments exist in Santa Teresita and Punta Arenas, Chile.

### Canon formation and reinterpretation

Storni never disappeared from popular reading, but the object of admiration changed:

- Early readers favored accessible erotic, rebellious, and maternal poems.
- Mid-century memorial culture emphasized the wounded woman and maritime suicide.
- Feminist scholarship recovered her journalism, civil-rights arguments, unmarried maternity, labor, and strategic satire.
- Formalist and avant-garde criticism restored *Ocre*, *Mundo de siete pozos*, and *Mascarilla y trébol* to the center of her achievement.
- Archival editions by Muschietti established that poetry was only one part of a corpus spanning fiction, essays, journalism, adult drama, and children’s theatre.

Her place beside Gabriela Mistral, Juana de Ibarbourou, and Delmira Agustini remains common, but current scholarship treats those women less as a homogeneous “female lyric” school than as distinct writers navigating gendered literary markets.

Street names, schools, libraries, prizes, busts, theatre pieces, musical settings, public readings, and translations sustain her presence. The continued dominance of “Alfonsina y el mar” is double-edged as historical evidence: it has made her name globally recognizable while continually replacing the documented breakwater death with an aesthetic walk into the waves.

## Research Limitations and Evidentiary Notes

- No Swiss civil or parish scan was accessible in the consulted corpus; hence the 22/29 May conflict remains bounded rather than “solved.”
- No birth certificate, acknowledgment, DNA evidence, or first-person declaration establishing Alejandro’s paternity was located.
- No complete police, autopsy, or judicial file was consulted; the death reconstruction relies on institutional chronology, surviving court-marked farewell correspondence, and later biographies drawing on contemporary reporting.
- Exact salaries, prize figures, and medical prognosis are preserved as reported rather than converted into modern values.
- Storni’s periodical corpus is large and dispersed. The works list aims at bibliographic completeness by book and genre, but does not pretend to enumerate every newspaper item; Muschietti’s scholarly volumes and periodical databases remain the authoritative route for item-level work.

## Atlas Connections

### Alberto Gerchunoff

- **[T4]** **Documented fact:** Storni and Gerchunoff attended the *Nosotros* banquet honoring Manuel Gálvez at Restaurante Génova, Buenos Aires, on 9 May 1916. They later occupied the same Anaconda literary-artistic milieu in the early 1920s; the evidence establishes repeated shared settings, though not necessarily a close friendship. [Centro Virtual Cervantes chronology](https://cvc.cervantes.es/actcult/storni/cronologia/cronologia02.htm); [University of Chile study citing Roberto Giusti’s contemporary recollection](https://repositorio.uchile.cl/bitstream/handle/2250/108872/Subjetividad-femenina-y-experiencia-moderna-en-la-escritura-de-Alfonsina-Storni.pdf?sequence=4)

### Jorge Luis Borges

- **[T3]** **Documented fact:** Borges addressed Storni’s work directly, and dismissively, in “La lírica argentina contemporánea,” published in Madrid’s *Cosmópolis* in December 1921. This is a one-sided written engagement rather than evidence of a personal feud. [Centro Virtual Cervantes biography](https://cvc.cervantes.es/actcult/storni/biografia.htm)
- **[T4]** **Documented fact:** Both belonged to the Buenos Aires literary milieu involved in establishing the Sociedad Argentina de Escritores. Storni drove the preliminary organizing work and hosted meetings at Córdoba 807; Borges was named among SADE’s board members when the association formally assembled on 8 November 1928. The record supports institutional proximity, not close friendship. [Centro Virtual Cervantes chronology](https://cvc.cervantes.es/actcult/storni/cronologia/cronologia03.htm)

### Emín Arslán

- **[T3]** **Documented fact:** Arslán, director of *La Nota*, published Storni’s poetry and prose from 1916 and personally proposed that she take charge of its “Feminidades” section. Storni recorded that proposal in her inaugural column of 28 March 1919; she remained a regular columnist through November 1919. Their crossing was therefore a direct editorial and written engagement, not merely publication in the same periodical. [La Nación account reproducing Storni’s description](https://www.lanacion.com.ar/cultura/con-la-mira-en-la-mujer-futura-nid1702838/); [scholarly anthology of *La Nota*](https://www.memoria.fahce.unlp.edu.ar/libros/pm.23/pm.23.pdf)

### Enrique Santos Discépolo

- **[T5]** **Reported fact:** Retrospective cultural histories identify Storni as a friend of Discépolo and his partner, Tania, within Buenos Aires theatrical and literary society, and describe her participating in their social gatherings during the 1930s. The friendship is repeatedly reported, but the sources consulted are later reconstructions rather than contemporary correspondence between Storni and Discépolo. [Buenos Aires Historia](https://buenosaireshistoria.org/juntas/enrique-santos-discepolo/); [La Nación profile of Discépolo and Tania](https://www.lanacion.com.ar/lifestyle/enrique-santos-discepolo-y-tania-nid475042/)

### Federico García Lorca

- **[T5]** **Documented fact:** Storni met García Lorca in Buenos Aires during his 1933–34 Argentine visit, in the Signo circle at the Hotel Castelar. Institutional accounts place them among the writers who saw one another during his stay, but no substantial collaboration or correspondence between them is documented. [Centro Virtual Cervantes Storni chronology](https://cvc.cervantes.es/actcult/storni/cronologia/cronologia03.htm); [Centro Virtual Cervantes Lorca chronology](https://cvc.cervantes.es/literatura/lorca_america/introduccion.htm)

### Luis Perlotti

- **[T5]** **Documented fact:** Storni and sculptor Luis Perlotti became friends through La Peña’s gatherings in the Café Tortoni cellar, which both regularly attended from the mid-1920s. Museum documentation also records that they conversed there. Perlotti later created her Mar del Plata memorial, but that posthumous tribute is not classified as T9 because no object passed between the two during their lives. [Museo de Esculturas Luis Perlotti](https://buenosaires.gob.ar/gcaba_historico/noticias/datos-curiosos-escultura-de-alfonsina-storni); [Museo Benito Quinquela Martín collection record](https://coleccion.quinquela.ar/index.php/Detail/entities/677)

No other roster member produced a sufficiently documented crossing in the sources consulted. Mere literary comparison, shared nationality, overlapping chronology, posthumous influence, or appearance in the same anthology was excluded.
