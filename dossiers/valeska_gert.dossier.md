# Valeska Gert: Research Dossier

**Subject:** Gertrud Valesca Samosch, known as Valeska Gert (1892–1978)  
**Prepared:** 31 August 2026  
**Scope:** Chronological life history; family, formation, performance, film, exile, cabaret businesses, politics, finances, legal disputes, writings, myths, reception, archives, and legacy.  

## Research Note and Evidentiary Method

Valeska Gert repeatedly narrated her own life in four autobiographical books and in late interviews. Those sources are indispensable but are also performances: vivid, compressed, self-dramatizing, and sometimes inconsistent in dates or sequence. This dossier therefore distinguishes among:

- **documented facts:** supported by dated programs, contemporary newspapers, official records, film credits, archival catalogues, or several independent scholarly sources;
- **Gert's account:** an event principally known through one of her memoirs or interviews;
- **later recollection:** testimony written years after the event by an associate;
- **scholarly reconstruction:** a conclusion based on archival comparison;
- **rumor or reception myth:** a story circulated in publicity or later cultural memory but not established by primary documentation.

The most detailed modern documentary biography remains Frank-Manuel Peter's *Valeska Gert: Tänzerin, Schauspielerin und Kabarettistin* (1985; rev. 1987). The strongest freely accessible research used here includes the Deutsches Tanzarchiv Köln's program-based work catalogue, Karen A. Mozingo's archive-based dissertation, the Akademie der Künste and Deutsches Tanzarchiv collection descriptions, filmportal.de, the Deutsches Filminstitut's Volker Schlöndorff collection, and contemporary or near-contemporary texts by Kurt Tucholsky and Gert herself. A 2025 UCLA dissertation by Jacqueline Davis is a major new study; its abstract and project description were consulted, but the full ProQuest PDF was access-blocked, so no unverified details from it are used.

## Family and Ancestry

Gert was born into a prosperous, assimilated Berlin Jewish commercial family. Her father was **Theodor Samosch**, described variously as a merchant, manufacturer, or manager of **Zade und Falk**, a concern making or selling hat accessories. Her mother was **Augusta Samosch, née Rosenthal**. She was the eldest child or eldest daughter. The available public sources do not securely name all younger siblings.

The household was upper-middle-class and materially comfortable before the First World War. Her mother's interest in theater and dance gave the child access to ballet, while her father disapproved of the theater and wanted conventional domestic respectability. The war damaged the father's business and family finances, making paid work more urgent for Gert.

One brother survived Nazi persecution. Gert later wrote that she used her travel privileges in the late 1930s to help him obtain a visa and escape to Paris. His name is not consistently established in the accessible sources consulted and is therefore not supplied here. In her postwar memoir she described learning that most of her extended family had been murdered: a cousin Albert allegedly died after beatings while forced to work in a powder factory; Aunt Elly died in the street; a friend Lilly was killed at Auschwitz; her school friend Else returned disabled from Theresienstadt. Mozingo's archival reconstruction quotes Gert's statement that only she and her brother survived from her family. This is **Gert's retrospective account**, not a complete independently verified genealogy.

No evidence was found for aristocratic ancestry, inherited title, or a family artistic dynasty. The “von” in her first married name belonged to her husband Helmuth von Krause.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1892–1914

Gert grew up in Berlin in a bourgeois home whose expectations she later represented as suffocating. She disliked formal schooling and office work. Sources differ between age seven and age nine for the beginning of dance lessons; the better German summaries and her mother's role point to lessons beginning at about **seven**, followed by more serious study later.

Her mother enrolled her in ballet classes at the opera. She later recalled becoming the best student in the class. A formative childhood encounter was seeing **Anna Pavlova** perform in Berlin. Gert and her friend Else waited at the stage door; Pavlova gave her a lily-of-the-valley from her bouquet, which Gert pressed into her diary. She called Pavlova her “third love.” The incident is known through Gert's memoir and should be treated as autobiographical memory.

Her autobiographies also construct an early psychology of performance. She wrote of becoming terrified by the certainty of her own death and of being repelled by dolls because they were lifeless. Acting and dance became a means to make temporary forms—*Gestalten*—against transience. This lifelong preoccupation produced her celebrated dance *Tod* and her persistent effort to archive herself in books, films, photographs, and stories.

Gert was not a member of a formal Laban, Wigman, or Duncan school and did not found a codified school of her own. Her later hostility to “grace,” harmony, and system was partly a refusal of precisely such lineages.

## Education and Formation

### Formal and private training

- General school education in Berlin; she left without pursuing an academic career.
- Childhood ballet classes arranged by her mother.
- **1915–1916:** acting study with **Maria Moissi** and **Alfred Breiderhoff**.
- Dance study and first public opportunity with the Italian-German dancer **Rita Sacchetto**.
- No conservatory diploma or university education is documented.

### Formative artistic influences and oppositions

Gert's development was defined as much by opposition as apprenticeship:

- She admired Pavlova's presence but rejected ballet's decorative ideal.
- She acknowledged **Isadora Duncan** as a liberator from ballet but called Duncan's return to Greek harmony romantic and untrue to modern dislocation.
- She saw **Alexander Sacharoff** and **Mary Wigman** as modernizers who still retained “pseudo-classical” form. Her caricature of Wigman's stamping and dust-raising became part of a public aesthetic rivalry.
- Film supplied compositional devices: close-up, cut, acceleration, slow motion, montage, and interruption. She translated these into bodily rhythm in pieces such as *Kino*, *Verkehr*, and *Wochenschau*-type material.
- Berlin street life, prostitution, sport, journalism, cinema, jazz, political meetings, and commodity culture provided subjects more directly than mythology or “pure” movement.
- Her theatrical collaborators placed her near Expressionism, Dada, cabaret, and Brechtian epic theater, but she resisted permanent membership in any movement.

Her own concise artistic program was to represent “people and the many-colored gestures and movements of daily life,” not empty movement detached from the time. The contrast of sweet and ugly, gentle and violent, still and convulsive, was central. Makeup—white face, black hair, sharply marked eyes and mouth—and self-designed saturated costumes enlarged the face and body into graphic signs.

## Early Career and First World War, 1915–1918

The First World War weakened her father's business. Gert entered paid performance at a moment when conventional bourgeois protection was becoming less secure.

### 1916 debut

Her first documented public dance appearance came in **February 1916** in a Rita Sacchetto program in Berlin. The Deutsches Tanzarchiv's program-based catalogue records *Tanz in Orange* at the U.T. Lichtspiele by 27 February 1916 and notes an apparent premiere a few days earlier, probably at the Apollo-Theater. Mozingo, following Gert, places a Sacchetto performance in the Blüthner-Saal.

Gert made an orange silk/harem-pants costume, whitened her face, and danced barefoot. In her later account she exaggerated the same steps that the other pupils rendered sweetly, broke into giant strides and swinging arms, then abruptly returned to exaggerated sweetness. She described the audience as shouting, whistling, and cheering and called the event the birth of modern dance satire. This is a **self-created origin story**, but it accords with the rapid appearance of the “Gert genre” in criticism.

She next appeared between films at the Ufa Theater at Nollendorfplatz, performing *Tanz in Orange* and *Golliwogg's Cakewalk/Pierrot und Colombine* with Sidi/Brigitte Riha (later wife of Erich Heckel). The setting between film reels was important to her later relation to cinema, but it must not be confused with the disputed “Pause” story discussed below.

### Munich, Berlin, Bucharest

By late 1916 and 1917 she was appearing in Munich and Berlin with works including *Humoreske*, *Gavotte*, *Bauerntanz*, *Japanische Groteske*, *Modedame*, *Samoanischer Tanz*, and *Spanischer Tanz*. In March **1918** she toured Bucharest with **Erik Charell**, her first known foreign tour. On **28 March 1918** she married physician **Helmuth von Krause** (1893–1980), son of jurist and Prussian politician Paul von Krause.

For the 1918–1919 season **Max Reinhardt** engaged her at the Deutsches Theater and Kammerspiele in Berlin. Stage work associated with this early period includes Oskar Kokoschka's *Hiob*, Ernst Toller's *Die Wandlung*, and Frank Wedekind's *Franziska*; she received notice especially as Karaminka in *Franziska*. Her stage career and solo dance career proceeded in alternation rather than a single linear ascent.

## Chronological Life History

## 1919–1923: The Grotesque-Dance Breakthrough

The program record shows a rapid expansion in 1919–1922: *Alt-Wien*, *Canaille/Kanaille*, *Tango*, *Zirkus*, *Menuett*, *Ballett*, *Fromme Helene*, *Messalina*, *Variété*, *Kupplerin*, *Boston-Cancan*, *Kino*, *Salome*, *Alt Paris*, *Clown*, *Diseuse*, *Jugend im Mai*, *Kameliendame*, a work billed under racially offensive period titles as *Negertanz* or variants, and *Tod*.

### *Canaille*

The first presently documented performance of *Canaille* is **30 January 1919** in Hamburg's Weißer Saal—not 1915 or 1916, dates sometimes given in later summaries. The work portrays a prostitute's labor: display, sexual transaction, orgasmic spasm, exhausted pause, awakening, and contempt at the economic use of her body. Gert used high heels and black stockings rather than the bare feet of concert dance. The work's sexual explicitness became the center of her scandal reputation.

Later sources often say that the audience called the police when she danced an orgasm in 1922. Gert herself openly described “the spasm (orgasm)” in Schlöndorff's 1977 film. A specific contemporary police report was not located in the sources consulted; the police-call detail should therefore be identified as a **repeated anecdote**, not a demonstrated legal incident.

### *Tod*

First documented in Berlin in **November 1922**, *Tod* reduced dance toward immobility. In a long black garment she enacted breathing, bodily contraction, resistance, stillness, relaxation, and the final drop of the head. Unlike decorative danse macabre, it made micro-movement and physiological dying the entire event. This work, not the probably nonexistent independent *Pause*, is securely documented as her radical performance of near-stasis.

### Theater experiments

Gert moved among solo recitals, Reinhardt theater, the Tribüne, and mixed avant-garde evenings. On **20 April 1923** she mounted a radically reduced production of Oscar Wilde's *Salomé* at Berlin's Tribüne. She also performed in Expressionist and Dada-adjacent contexts. The evidence supports proximity and collaboration with Dadaists, not simple membership in a Dada organization.

In **1923** she began a long relationship with actor and writer **Aribert Wäscher**. Sources place its end in 1938, though Nazi racial law had made it dangerous earlier. She remained legally married to von Krause until 1935.

## 1924–1932: International Fame, Film, Sound-Dance, and Cabaret

### New dances and “sound dances”

The documented repertoire grew to include:

- **1924:** *Demut*, *Jazzband*, *Sport*, *Laster*, *Zärtlicher Walzer*.
- **1926:** *Großer und kleiner Schritt*, *Amme*, *Chansonette*, *Charleston*, *Gruß aus dem Mumienkeller*, *Verkehr*.
- **1927:** *Erzengel*, *Nervosität*, *Boxen*.
- **1928:** *Koloratursängerin*.
- **1929:** *Song/Dirnenlied/Barbarasong*, *Opus I, Komposition auf ausgeleiertem Klavier/La célèbre pianiste*, *Tragödie*, *Operette*.
- **1930–1931:** *Persische Blume*, *Schlafwandlerin*, *Kummerlied*, *Japanisches Theater*, *Schriftsteller*, *Versammlung*, *Zauberer*.

From about **1926** she increasingly used the voice—groans, cries, speech, distorted song, consonants, and noises—in what she called *Tontänze* (sound dances). Her later recordings of *Baby*, *Tod*, and *Kummerlied* are important because most live dances were never filmed in their original period.

She toured widely in German cities and performed in **Paris** and **Moscow**, including a Moscow Proletkult context. In Paris she appeared at the Comédie/Théâtre des Champs-Élysées by March 1930. Contemporary reception was polarized but substantial. Kurt Tucholsky, writing as Peter Panter in *Die Weltbühne* in 1921, ended a review by calling her “a crazy act, an outstanding dancer, an extraordinary woman.” Other critics described each jump as a punchline or whip-blow and treated her compressed satires as a theater of the age.

Her first film experiences made her anxious. Photographer/filmmaker **Suse Byk**, who filmed *Kupplerin* in 1925, helped her understand the camera as an amplifier rather than captor. She then used facial micro-gesture and abrupt physical rhythm to redirect attention from conventionally beautiful stars toward socially abrasive supporting figures.

### Relationships with Brecht and the avant-garde

Gert knew **Bertolt Brecht** and later met him in American exile. The frequently repeated exchange in which she asked him to define epic theater and he answered “what you do” fits her practice, but no contemporaneous document was located in this research; it should be treated as **Gert/Brecht lore** unless tied to a precise memoir passage or letter.

She worked with or circulated among Max Reinhardt, G. W. Pabst, Jean Renoir, Kurt Weill/Brecht film circles, Friedrich Hollaender, Walter Ruttmann, Hans Richter, photographers Lili Baruch, Suse Byk, Lotte Jacobi, Lisette Model, Erna Lendvai-Dircksen, Elli Marcus, Otto Umbehr, Man Ray, and numerous Berlin cabaret figures. Association did not always mean friendship or sustained collaboration.

### First cabaret ownership

In **1932** she briefly opened a Berlin cabaret commonly called **Kohlkopp** (“Cabbage Head”). It was short-lived. Later sources sometimes project her work backward into Werner Finck's Katakombe or Hollaender's Tingel-Tangel from 1920, but the Deutsches Tanzarchiv has demonstrated that those venues did not yet exist then. This is a concrete example of a secondary-source chronological error.

## 1933–1939: Nazi Exclusion and Mobile Exile

After the Nazi seizure of power, Gert was attacked both as Jewish and as an avant-garde/left-associated artist. In 1933 Joseph Goebbels's *Das erwachende Berlin* reportedly included her photograph among figures presented as the rejected theatrical culture of Weimar. By September, Jews were excluded from the Reich cultural chambers and from ordinary theater, film, music, literary, and journalistic employment. Gert retained only limited possibilities in the **Kulturbund Deutscher Juden/Jüdischer Kulturbund**.

“Degenerate artist” accurately summarizes Nazi denunciation of her, but care is needed: the famous 1937 *Entartete Kunst* exhibition concerned seized visual art and did not literally exhibit Gert's dances. She was excluded under racial-cultural policy and hostile propaganda, not as a painter whose object appeared in that show.

Her marriage to Helmuth von Krause deteriorated under fear and prolonged absences. They divorced on **23 November 1935**. The Nuremberg Laws made her relationship with the non-Jewish Aribert Wäscher legally dangerous as *Rassenschande*. According to Gert, she left partly to protect him and von Krause.

On **24 April 1936** in London she married **Robin Hay Anderson**, a British writer and cultural organizer, described in later sources as homosexual. The marriage was principally or partly a **marriage of survival**, providing a British passport and safe haven. Von Krause reportedly encouraged it. Anderson organized solo appearances for her in 1936–1938. The evidence does not require that the marriage be reduced to fraud: it was a legal marriage serving protection and professional needs, with the intimate character not fully recoverable.

From 1933 through 1938 she circulated among Germany, London, Paris, and the United States, still occasionally returning to Berlin despite increasing danger. In London she appeared in Alberto Cavalcanti's experimental sound short **Pett and Pott: A Fairy Story of the Suburbs** (1934), long her last screen role. After she criticized conditions in Germany to a British journalist who had promised discretion, her remarks and photograph appeared in newspapers; she feared the Nazi response.

She helped her brother escape to Paris. In late 1938, after performances at **Raymond Duncan's** Paris theater, an American manager offered a round-trip ticket, a contract, and **$500**. In early **1939** she sailed for New York aboard the **Île de France**. Some summaries say 1938; her own chronology and the strongest research place final American emigration in 1939.

## 1939–1941: Failed American Restart

Gert arrived with little grasp of American professional networks or household finance. She later admitted that in Germany husbands and lovers had handled money and domestic structure: she did not know what they or even she earned.

Her first promised manager died suddenly. A second manager helped with clothes and American dance forms, then also became ill and died. She briefly hired the seventeen-year-old émigré pianist **Georg Kreisler** as accompanist, but no stable engagement followed.

She traveled by bus to Hollywood after contacting **Salka Viertel**. Meetings with émigré film figures failed. Producer **Walter Wanger** told her she was “not the type”; she angered **Ernst Lubitsch** by bluntly saying American actors looked like picture postcards. These details derive from her memoir and expose both industrial typecasting and her self-described impulsiveness.

In New York she presented an October **1940** program at the Cherry Lane Theatre including *Boxen*, *Baby*, *Tod*, *Kupplerin*, and *Koloratursängerin*. Critics admired *Tod* but often regarded the program as overly European, theatrical, or nostalgic and demanded American subjects. Unable to establish herself in American modern dance, she went to **Provincetown, Massachusetts**.

There she rented a rough shed room for **$25 a month**. She briefly washed dishes at **$0.30 an hour**, earning $2.40 for an eight-hour shift, then posed nude for **Hans Hofmann's** painting school—$12.50 for five morning sessions—and for another art class at $1 an hour. Her posing became performance: she successively assumed postures from her theatrical and dance roles.

By summer **1941** she was performing at Provincetown's **White Whale**. Her European reputation drew an audience, and Tennessee Williams later encouraged her to create a New York cabaret.

## 1941–1945: The Beggar Bar, Greenwich Village

### Establishment and address

Gert opened the **Beggar Bar/Beggar's Bar/Bettlerbar** in late 1941 at **3 Morton Street**, at or near the corner of Morton and Bleecker Streets in Greenwich Village. A 9 October 1942 advertisement survives in *Aufbau*. It operated until early 1945.

The cellar had black-painted walls on which guests added graffiti, mismatched tables, improvised chairs, a small stage, a bar mirror painted with dancers, and a back room with benches or mattresses. Staff waited tables and performed; guests could become performers. Gert explained that she called it the Beggar Bar because she had begged for nearly everything in it.

### Capital structure and operating finances

The best reconstructable capitalization comes from Gert's memoir, as checked by Mozingo:

- A painter named Marjorie initially promised **$300** but withdrew.
- A doctor advanced **$50** and asked her to find a co-investor.
- Émigré art dealer **Karl Nierendorf** contributed **$200**.
- Cabaret and restaurant licensing cost **$135**.
- Negotiated rent was reportedly **$30 per month**, with tenant responsibility for repairs.
- Gert deposited **$20** with Consolidated Edison to obtain electricity.
- First-night receipts were only **$7.50**.

No corporate filing, partnership agreement, audited accounts, or evidence of accumulated wealth was located. It appears to have been a precarious personally operated small business, dependent on low overhead, unpaid or poorly paid labor, informal investment, and Gert's own dishwashing and cleaning.

### Performers, staff, and patrons: evidence grades

**Well-supported by Gert's memoir, contemporary coverage, institutional research, or participant memoir:**

- **Kadidja Wedekind**, reciting her father Frank Wedekind's work.
- **Maria Collm**, **Sada Gordon**, **Sonja Wronkow**, pianists **Erwin Strauss** and **Oskars Sohn**, and various multilingual émigré performers.
- **Judith Malina**, later cofounder of the Living Theatre, worked in the cloakroom.
- **Tennessee Williams** recited poetry and worked as a waiter. His own memoir confirms the association. Gert fired him; accounts variously say for sloppiness or refusal to pool tips.

**Frequently repeated but less securely documented in the accessible material:**

- **Julian Beck**, **Jackson Pollock**, and **Jack Kerouac** are often said to have worked there. Recent scholarship includes them in the circle, but the consulted primary staffing lists do not establish dates, jobs, or duration. These should not be repeated as equal in certainty to Williams and Malina.

**Patrons named by Gert and later research:** Judy Garland, Vincente Minnelli, Simone Simon, Fredric March, Patsy Kelly, Beverley Roberts, Maxine Sullivan, June Havoc, Marcel Duchamp, Hans Richter, Walter Mehring, and members of Katherine Dunham's troupe. Garland's alleged remark that it was the only New York cabaret worth seeing is **Gert's reported quotation**, not independently verified testimony.

### Social and political character

The venue did not enforce racial segregation and attracted Black patrons, gay patrons, émigrés, sailors, artists, and affluent uptown visitors. It supplied an unusually mixed social space in wartime New York. Gert told Brecht that gay patrons were better audiences than heterosexual ones. Her own writings nevertheless used racial language normal in her German period but offensive now, and some earlier dance titles employed racial caricature. Both inclusionary practice and stereotyped representation belong in the record.

Programs mixed Gert's Berlin works with songs, multilingual acts, satirical current-events commentary, *The Berlin Girl*, *The Strange Journey of Professor Blitz*, and an *Interview with a Ninety-Year-Old about Hitler*. The bar functioned simultaneously as livelihood, exile network, improvised school, performance installation, and public extension of Gert's persona.

### Licensing disputes, self-publicity, and closure

Neighbors initially objected to a nightclub on Morton Street. At a licensing hearing none appeared, and she obtained the cabaret license. The Alcoholic Beverage Control authorities refused a liquor license because the establishment was not a bona fide restaurant. She therefore served coffee, sandwiches, knackwurst, and potato salad.

Gert tells an extraordinary story about filling an empty second night: she telephoned intelligence, narcotics, and other agencies with fabricated tips that the German owner might be a Nazi spy or that opium cigarettes were smoked there. Plainclothes officers arrived and filled the room. This is **self-reported publicity theater**, not evidence of an intelligence connection. No evidence was found that Gert worked for any intelligence service.

The immediate cause of closure was a licensing violation involving the **Beggar Sip**, coffee mixed with egg liqueur containing a small percentage of alcohol. Gert said the law had changed six months earlier without her knowledge. Police removed the cabaret license, leaving only a restaurant license. She continued by moving from table to table and narrating—an unauthorized performance tolerated by one officer—but the Beggar Bar closed in **February or spring 1945**. Claims that it was simply closed “despite success” omit the unlicensed-alcohol issue; claims of criminal racketeering have no evidentiary basis.

## 1945–1947: Provincetown Cabaret and Jail

After the Beggar Bar closed, Gert returned to Provincetown. In May **1946** she opened **Valeska's** in a waterfront studio. She painted it light blue under pink bulbs, wrote the menu across the wall, made stools from nail barrels, used two drifted crates as a stage, and lit it with candles. Performers included Esme and the elderly small-statured performer **Mademoiselle Pumpernickel**. The setting was lighter than the demonic Beggar Bar but followed the same fusion of domestic, restaurant, and performance space.

Neighbors complained about noise, and authorities required her to close at 11 p.m. while other bars reportedly operated until 1 a.m. She faced proceedings involving garbage thrown from a window and nonpayment of a dance partner. Tennessee Williams appeared as a character witness according to later accounts. Gert tried to defend herself, made admissions damaging to her case, refused to pay a fine, was held in contempt, and spent **one night in jail**. Cabaret regulars secured her release. The precise docket and disposition were not located; details come largely from memoir and Williams-related biography.

Friends then asked her to leave town to avoid further conflict with neighbors. In **March 1947** she returned to Europe.

## 1947–1951: Paris, Zurich, and Return to Berlin

Gert spent roughly a year and a half moving through **Paris and Zurich** while seeking permission to enter Berlin. In Zurich she operated or performed in a café-cabaret known as **Valeska und ihr Küchenpersonal** (“Valeska and Her Kitchen Staff”) during 1948. Sources vary on its exact duration, usually about six months.

In **December 1948** she entered Germany through Baden-Baden with a six-month permit from American occupation authorities. Some summaries date her Berlin return to 1949; both can be reconciled as entry in December 1948 followed by establishment in Berlin in 1949.

She found rupture rather than restoration. Anderson would not see her. Wäscher would meet only in the presence of other women; their relationship was over. Her possessions were missing or destroyed. Another family occupied a house in Kampen, and she said she lost a legal effort to recover it. This is difficult to reconcile with later accounts that she operated the Ziegenstall from a house built for her around 1930; the sources may refer to different property rights, temporary loss, later recovery, or autobiographical compression. The exact property history requires land and court records not found in this research.

## 1949–1956: Berlin Cabarets and the Postwar Taboo

Gert opened **Bei Valeska** in the former Opernkeller at the Theater des Westens in 1949–1950, then the **Hexenküche** (“Witch's Kitchen”) in 1950. The Hexenküche operated in winter seasons, with some sources carrying it to April 1956. Performers and staff included young or marginal artists; **Klaus Kinski** is documented as appearing and serving there.

Her postwar material addressed German amnesia and the Nazi past. *Der Remigrant* mocked the ceremonial welcome and rapid marginalization of returning exiles. Her “KZ-Kommandeuse,” later described or reenacted as **Ilse Koch**, presented an apparently harmless older woman knitting or embroidering while barking orders about burning bodies and sorting victims' property. The number has sometimes been misread as impersonation without critique; its structure depended on collision between grandmotherly image and perpetrator speech.

The Hexenküche failed to reproduce the Beggar Bar's success. Berlin audiences and the Cold War cultural field had changed. Gert wrote that she was isolated because she would not make anti-Communism a loyalty performance. A city authority tripled her entertainment tax by classifying the cabaret as **striptease**, making continued operation unaffordable. This is a documented claim through her memoir; the administrative tax file was not consulted.

## 1951–1978: Kampen, Sylt, and the Ziegenstall

Gert first visited Kampen around **1930** and had a house built there at her husband's initiative, according to the local Sylt memorial account. In **1951** she converted her Kampen home or an adjoining space into the **Ziegenstall** (“Goat Shed”), a seasonal artists' bar and cabaret. Other institutional chronologies emphasize 1956 as its mature or continuous phase. It remained associated with her until death.

Guests sat on hay sacks; straw covered the floor; a cow once reportedly tried to enter after smelling the hay. Gert cooked, hosted, performed, quarreled, hired outsiders, and made the establishment an extension of herself. It was locally famous but never a conventional scalable business. No reliable net income or valuation was found.

The Ziegenstall's social reputation includes claims that it was a major lesbian meeting place. Queer patrons and nonconforming performers are well attested across Gert's venues, but the superlative claim “best lesbian gathering place in Europe” appears in later secondary description and is not supported by business records or systematic contemporary evidence.

Gert alternated between Sylt and a Berlin apartment for years. Her final memoir frames her as the *Katze von Kampen* (“Cat of Kampen”): territorial, watchful, solitary, and self-mythologizing.

## 1962–1978: Recordings, Film Return, Television, and Final Recognition

### Recordings and preserved performances

- **1962:** seven-inch EP *Absurdes Kabarett – Zu Gast bei Valeska Gert*.
- **1969:** Ernst Mitzka filmed/video-recorded *Tod* and *Baby* and studies of Gert's face. A *Baby* single recorded in 1969 was released posthumously in 2010.
- Her vocal work *Kummerlied* consists of crying, whimpering, and lament rather than conventional lyrics.

Federico Fellini cast her in **Giulietta degli spiriti/Juliet of the Spirits** (1965). The role restored screen visibility after three decades. She then sought work from younger German filmmakers:

She appeared on Hansjürgen Rosenbauer's talk show **Je später der Abend** on 19 November 1975. Her outspoken discussion of sex education and her undiminished physical manner introduced her to a younger audience. Artist **Wolfgang Müller**, then eighteen, later described this broadcast as his discovery of the “first punk.”

In **1970** she received the **Filmband in Gold** for long-standing and outstanding work in German film. The official Deutscher Filmpreis history confirms the award.

### Final project

Werner Herzog offered her the role of the estate agent **Knock** in *Nosferatu: Phantom der Nacht*. She signed the contract on **1 March 1978** but died before filming. The role went elsewhere. The story that she died “two weeks after signing Nosferatu” is substantially correct, although the exact death day remains uncertain.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Politics

Gert's work repeatedly attacked bourgeois respectability, sexual hypocrisy, militarism, commodity relations, social abandonment, political meetings, and the anesthetic effect of entertainment. She was broadly identified with the anti-authoritarian and left cultural field of Weimar Berlin. The Nazis treated her as leftist and “degenerate.” No evidence was found that she held party office or belonged to the Communist Party.

In exile she created anti-Hitler material and an integrated venue. After returning, she refused to make anti-Communism a condition of belonging in West German culture. That stance contributed to isolation but does not by itself establish Communist membership or Soviet intelligence ties. No intelligence connection is supported.

### Jewish identity

She came from an assimilated Jewish family and was persecuted as Jewish under Nazi racial law. Her work was not primarily religious, and no evidence of observant practice was located. Jewishness became an imposed legal identity under Nazism and a lived axis of exile. Later scholars examine how she performed the “bad Jewish woman” and refused both respectable assimilation and victim-only representation.

### Artistic development

1. **Early satirical dance (1916–1919):** exaggeration of existing dance vocabulary, aggressive color, facial distortion.
2. **Grotesque social types (1919–mid-1920s):** prostitute, procuress, nurse, clown, film, circus, death.
3. **Montage and sound-dance (mid-1920s–1933):** filmic cuts, acceleration, voice, noise, text, political and media forms.
4. **Exile cabaret (1939–1947):** autobiographical commentary, multilingual ensemble, bar as social artwork and survival economy.
5. **Postwar memory performance (1948–1950s):** remigration, perpetrators, German amnesia, deliberately uncomfortable speech pieces.
6. **Late self-archiving (1960s–1978):** memoir, records, video, documentary reenactment, television persona, collaboration with younger filmmakers.

Her “anti-grace” was not lack of technique. Contemporary critics and later historians stress exact preparation, compression, and timing. She opposed graceful continuity because she thought modern people were internally discontinuous.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, and Financial Questions

### Established or substantially documented

- Nazi racial and cultural exclusion after 1933.
- Divorce from von Krause in 1935 amid Nazi danger.
- Protective 1936 marriage to Anderson.
- Beggar Bar lacked a liquor license; sale of egg liqueur in the Beggar Sip led to loss of the cabaret license.
- Provincetown disputes over noise, garbage, and payment; contempt and one night in jail after refusing a fine.
- Postwar property litigation in Kampen, according to Gert, ending against her; exact file not identified.
- Hexenküche tax reclassification as striptease, according to Gert, tripling the entertainment tax.
- Many civil disputes and impulsive quarrels are acknowledged in her memoir and the 2019 edition's scholarly afterword, but a full docket-level list is not publicly reconstructed in the sources consulted.

### Allegation, anecdote, or insufficiently documented

- **Police called during *Canaille*:** widely repeated; no police record found.
- **Intelligence attention:** Gert says she herself planted false tips to draw agents to the Beggar Bar. This is not evidence she was an agent or target of a sustained investigation.
- **Gangster/racketeer connections:** she was warned that New York nightclubs were run through with gangsters, and later wrote that gangsters caused trouble. No criminal partnership is shown.
- **Financial exploitation/nonpayment:** the Provincetown dance-partner dispute is documented only through narrative sources; no judgment record was located.
- **Sexual scandal:** her work simulated sex, orgasm, prostitution, and gender crossing. Scandal was public reception, not proof of prostitution or sexual crime.

### Negative findings

No credible evidence was found of fraud convictions, corruption, money laundering, organized crime, political violence, abuse, espionage, patent disputes, or large concealed wealth. Absence from the sources does not prove that no minor civil actions existed, but sensational criminal characterization would be unsupported.

## Health, Psychological Crises, Accidents, and Death

Gert's memoirs describe intense childhood death anxiety and periods of despair, alienation, and anxious dreams after the loss of the Beggar Bar. These are autobiographical psychological states, not diagnoses. She suffered a fall and injuries during her cross-country bus trip to Hollywood; bandaged legs and sunburn impaired a meeting with Walter Wanger.

No well-documented chronic illness, psychiatric hospitalization, addiction, or substance dependency emerged from the consulted evidence. Her association with Weimar nightlife should not be converted into an addiction narrative. One late commentator explicitly distinguishes her from the cocaine culture of the Berlin demi-monde.

Neighbors and friends had not seen her for several days in March 1978. Police forced entry on **18 March** and found her dead in the apartment beside the Ziegenstall. The exact death day lies between 15 and 18 March; 16 March is inferred most often. The consulted authoritative sources do **not** establish a specific medical cause. Statements that she died of a particular disease would exceed the evidence.

She had wanted a bright red coffin. The burial reportedly used a conventional coffin covered with a red cloth instead. Volker Schlöndorff gave the funeral address. Her house went to close friend and television journalist **Werner Höfer**, who later demolished the Ziegenstall structure and replaced it with a modern house, according to the official Sylt memorial text.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. “Pause,” the motionless dance between film reels

**Story:** In the 1920s, during a cinema reel change, Gert supposedly walked onstage, stood completely motionless with raised arms, and forced the audience to experience waiting. It is often presented as a foundational work of performance art anticipating John Cage.

**Appearance and circulation:** The story grew from a photograph in Fred Hildenbrandt's 1928 book captioned *Pause*. Artist Wolfgang Müller made it central to exhibitions, writing, editions, and the 2010 Hamburger Bahnhof show *PAUSE. Valeska Gert: Bewegte Fragmente*. It then spread through museum, press, and silent-film accounts.

**Evidence:** The Deutsches Tanzarchiv's program-based catalogue finds **no independent dance titled *Pause***. Frank-Manuel Peter compared the photograph with Schlöndorff's film, in which Gert teaches *Canaille* to Pola Kinski: the pose is the prostitute's repose after intercourse, a pause **within *Canaille***. Programs document Gert performing dances between films in 1916, which may have made the later fusion plausible, but they do not document a motionless reel-change work.

**Assessment:** **Disputed reception myth; strong evidence against a standalone work.** The historically real object is the myth's twenty-first-century productivity: exhibitions, prints, scholarship, and performance art were built around it.

### 2. “Mother,” “grandmother,” or “first” punk

**Story:** Gert was the first punk or the mother/grandmother of punk, decades before the movement.

**Origin and circulation:** Wolfgang Müller dates his discovery to the 19 November 1975 talk show *Je später der Abend*. Punk and post-punk artists and writers later emphasized her white face, aggressive grotesque, anti-professional persona, sound eruptions, gender defiance, and disdain for cultural hierarchy. Nina Hagen has been reported as naming Gert a model; young punks wrote to Gert shortly before her death.

**Evidence:** There is no genealogical evidence that 1970s punk musicians derived the movement directly from her. There is evidence that individual German post-punk artists retrospectively recognized her and adopted her as an ancestor.

**Assessment:** **Influential retrospective affiliation, not literal origination.** It changed her reputation by moving her from dance history into performance-art and subcultural canons.

### 3. Sergei Eisenstein's “love of his life”

**Story:** Soviet filmmaker Sergei Eisenstein was paralyzed by love for Gert, or she was the love of his life.

**Circulation:** German cultural journalism repeats the story; Schlöndorff's own synopsis carefully says “it was rumored.”

**Evidence:** Gert did perform in Moscow and knew international avant-garde circles, but no letter, diary entry, or independently verified affair was located in the consulted sources.

**Assessment:** **Rumor.** It enhances her myth as an irresistible grotesque anti-beauty and international avant-garde muse.

### 4. Brecht's “Epic theater is what you do”

**Story:** When Gert asked Brecht what epic theater meant, he replied, “It's what you do.”

**Circulation:** Popular profiles and performance histories use the exchange to place her bodily montage before Brechtian theory.

**Evidence:** Their acquaintance is established and the aesthetic comparison is strong. A contemporaneous letter or dated witness for the exact dialogue was not found here.

**Assessment:** **Plausible but unverified anecdote.** It should not substitute for analysis of their documented work.

### 5. The Beggar Bar's all-star staff

**Story:** Tennessee Williams, Judith Malina, Julian Beck, Jackson Pollock, and sometimes Jack Kerouac all worked for Gert.

**Evidence:** Williams and Malina have strong support; Williams's memoir corroborates his waiter work. Beck, Pollock, and Kerouac appear in later institutional/scholarly lists, but precise employment evidence was not recovered here.

**Assessment:** **Mixed:** Williams and Malina documented; others require archival confirmation. The legend makes the bar a retrospective cradle of several postwar avant-gardes.

### 6. “Pause” versus the real near-immobility of *Tod*

The exposure of the *Pause* myth does not eliminate Gert's experiments with duration and stillness. *Tod* is documented from 1922 and reduces action to breath, contraction, release, and death. *Canaille* contains a significant resting interval. The myth exaggerated real formal tendencies by inventing a discrete title and cinematic occasion.

### 7. Self-created “witch,” “beast,” and “cat” identities

Gert adopted or embraced the “witch” (*Ich bin eine Hexe*), “wonderful beast,” and “Cat of Kampen” as metamorphic public figures. The white face, black hair, animal grimace, shriek, and refusal of graceful femininity became both hostile caricature and self-brand. These identities allowed her to turn stigmatizing descriptions—ugly, old, Jewish, masculine, vulgar—into stage resources. They are not biographical facts about occult belief. No evidence was found that she practiced witchcraft as a religion.

### 8. Nazi “degenerate art” shorthand

Later accounts say she was displayed or officially listed as part of “Degenerate Art.” She was indeed defamed as *entartet*, racially persecuted, and professionally excluded. But the 1937 visual-art exhibition was not a dance exhibition. The shorthand is broadly descriptive of Nazi cultural ideology but should not be transformed into the false claim that her body or a dance was literally displayed in the Munich show.

## Death, Burial, Estate, and Posthumous Reputation

Gert died alone at Kampen in March 1978 while a new screen role was pending. The uncertainty of the date—15, 16, or an interval through 18 March—should remain visible in any precise biography. She was buried in Berlin, the city she had called her birthplace and desired place of death.

Her physical business space did not survive intact: Werner Höfer inherited the Kampen property, and the Ziegenstall was demolished. Her documentary estate was placed, through biographer Frank-Manuel Peter and the heirs, in the Akademie der Künste; other bodies of material remain dispersed across Cologne, Düsseldorf, Oldenburg, Berlin, Frankfurt, and film archives.

### Institutional honors and memorialization

- **1970:** Filmband in Gold for long-standing and outstanding contribution to German film.
- **1978:** Berlin honorary grave.
- **2004:** star on the Walk of Fame of Cabaret/Stern der Satire in Mainz.
- **2006:** Valeska Gert Visiting Professorship for Dance and Performance founded jointly by Freie Universität Berlin, DAAD, and Akademie der Künste; also a Valeska-Gert-Straße named in Berlin-Friedrichshain.
- **2009:** art stele installed in Kampen.
- **2010:** *PAUSE. Valeska Gert: Bewegte Fragmente* at Hamburger Bahnhof, Berlin, intensifying her performance-art and punk reception.
- **2012:** Kampen artists' wine edition dedicated to her.
- **2023:** a path in Kampen named Valeska-Gert-Weg.
- **2026:** twentieth-anniversary lecture series for the Valeska Gert Visiting Professorship, emphasizing dance, film, sound, experimental theater, queer/feminist culture, and activism.

### Lines of influence and reinterpretation

Gert left no codified “Gert technique,” school, or institutional company comparable to Wigman's. This made direct pedagogical lineage weak but allowed later artists to claim her across genres. Her legacy appears in:

- German *Tanztheater* histories, often as a prehistory to Pina Bausch and other theatrical dance makers;
- performance art, body art, sound poetry, and conceptual work;
- feminist and queer histories of nonconforming bodies and self-representation;
- exile studies and histories of alternative economic/social structures;
- punk and post-punk genealogy, especially Wolfgang Müller, Nina Hagen reception, and Berlin art scenes;
- reenactment and speculative historiography, notably Eszter Salamon's **Valeska Gert Monuments** (from 2015) and *Study for the Valeska Gert Pavilion* (2022);
- contemporary vocal-dance research by artists such as Jule Flierl.

Her late fame also produced distortion. The *Pause* myth converted an ambiguous photograph into a canonical conceptual performance; the “first punk” label made a historical analogy into a genealogy; Beggar Bar lore retrospectively gathered famous future artists into one origin space. These afterlives are historically consequential even where literal claims fail.

## Unresolved Questions and Archival Priorities

1. Full names, dates, and fates of all siblings and extended family members.
2. Exact corporate/license files and full financial records for Beggar Bar and the German cabarets.
3. New York and Provincetown court dockets for licensing, garbage, wage, and contempt proceedings.
4. Documentary support for Pollock, Beck, and Kerouac as Beggar Bar employees rather than patrons or later-associated names.
5. Exact legal status and end date of the Anderson marriage.
6. Land and restitution records clarifying the competing accounts of the Kampen house.
7. Medical cause of death and precise death date.
8. Complete annotated bibliography of Gert's approximately twenty periodical essays.
9. Primary evidence for the Eisenstein romance and the exact Brecht “epic theater” exchange.
10. Full incorporation of Jacqueline Davis's 2025 dissertation and its archival findings.
