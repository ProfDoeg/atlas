# Pasquale Simonetti: Research Dossier

## Scope and Identification Note

This dossier concerns **Pasquale Simonetti (1926–1955)**, the Campanian criminal and agricultural-market intermediary known as **“Pascalone ’e Nola.”** It does not concern contemporary people, including the sculptor of the same name born in Carbonara di Nola in 1954.

The surviving public record is uneven. Simonetti died at twenty-nine and left no known writings, memoirs, corporate archive, or accessible personal papers. Much of the detailed narrative comes from:

- contemporaneous and near-contemporaneous crime reporting;
- evidence and reporting surrounding the 1959 trials of his killer and his widow;
- parliamentary investigations into organized crime;
- historical research by Felia Allum, Francesco Barbagallo, Gigi Di Fiore, John Dickie, Paolino Nappi, Vittorio Paliotti, and others;
- later recollections by participants and relatives.

Facts established by judicial proceedings are distinguished below from police or journalistic attribution, later historical interpretation, and folklore.

### Date discrepancies

The death date is sometimes given as **15 July 1955**, but contemporaneous-trial references, later judicial reporting, the parliamentary record, and most historical works use **16 July 1955**. One modern newspaper article mistakenly gives 16 April. The latter is incompatible with the April wedding and the repeatedly documented eighty-day interval between marriage and murder.

Accounts place Maresca’s killing of Antonio Esposito variously on **4 August, 4 October, or 16 September 1955**. The 4 October date appears in the Time report, the 1959 trial material reproduced by Fototeca Gilardi, and much academic literature and is therefore the strongest available date. The conflicting dates are evidence of extensive later mythologization and copying errors.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and social background

**Reported fact:** Simonetti was born into a poor or working-class household at Palma Campania. Felia Allum’s historical reconstruction describes his father as a **carter** and his mother as a **housewife**. A French-language summary instead calls the father a carpenter; this appears to be a translation or transmission discrepancy. No accessible civil record was found to resolve it.

No reliable published source consulted gives either parent’s full name. No documented genealogy, property inheritance, notable ancestry, or authenticated list of siblings was located.

Palma Campania lay within the agricultural territory feeding the Naples wholesale markets. Simonetti’s origins in this environment were central to his later role as an intermediary among growers, transporters, wholesalers, concessionaires, and urban dealers.

### Marriage into the Maresca family

On **27 April 1955**, Simonetti married **Assunta Maresca**, generally known as **Pupetta**, at the time a young woman from Castellammare di Stabia who had won the local title Miss Rovigliano.

The Marescas were known locally as the **Lampetielli**. Later parliamentary reporting characterized them as a powerful criminal family. Pupetta’s father, Alberto Maresca, was identified in later accounts as a contrabandist. Her brother Ciro was known as **Lampetiello**.

The marriage accordingly joined Simonetti to a family with an established reputation in the illicit economy. One later interpretation maintains that this alliance helped elevate him from a powerful provincial guappo to a figure of wider status. This is plausible but not reducible to a formal initiation or documented organizational merger.

### Child

Pupetta was pregnant when Simonetti died. Their son:

- was also named **Pasquale Simonetti**;
- was commonly called **Pasqualino**, **Pascalino**, or **Pascalino ’o Pascalotto**;
- was born while Maresca was held in Poggioreale prison;
- disappeared in January 1974, at about eighteen;
- was presumed murdered, although his body was not publicly recovered and the crime was never judicially solved.

The later disappearance has sometimes been attributed to Maresca’s subsequent partner, Umberto Ammaturo. Ammaturo was investigated or suspected in contemporary reporting but was reportedly cleared at the investigative stage and denied involvement. This later crime cannot be attributed to the elder Simonetti, who had died nineteen years earlier, but it became part of the family mythology surrounding him.

## Childhood and Early Life

Very little documentary evidence survives about Simonetti’s childhood.

### Established or consistently reported circumstances

- He grew up in **Palma Campania**, in the eastern Neapolitan hinterland.
- His family had limited means.
- His formative environment was rural-commercial rather than metropolitan-professional.
- He came of age during Fascism, the Second World War, Allied occupation, rationing, and the collapse of formal distribution systems in southern Italy.

### Education

No school, teacher, apprenticeship, literacy record, military service file, or educational qualification has been identified in the sources consulted. Descriptions of him as having risen from the **bassaforza**, or lower ranks of the underworld, imply no recognized professional education, but they do not prove that he lacked elementary schooling.

### Formative context

The Allied occupation of Naples produced a huge informal market in food, fuel, clothing, military supplies, and American cigarettes. Conventional economic institutions were weak, rationing was widespread, and violent intermediaries could provide transport, credit, enforcement, and protection.

This environment—not a documented teacher or formal ideological mentor—was Simonetti’s principal known formation.

## Early Criminal Career

### Wartime ration fraud and black-market food

**Reported fact:** Simonetti’s criminal career began during the 1940s. Historical accounts say he falsified or manipulated ration books, diverted food, and resold it on the black market.

The record does not disclose:

- the precise years;
- whether he was arrested or convicted for these acts;
- the quantities involved;
- his suppliers or accomplices;
- the amount of money earned.

### Cigarette smuggling

After the war, the state tobacco monopoly could not fully satisfy demand for American-style cigarettes. Once Allied military supplies diminished, contraband networks imported cigarettes from such points as:

- Marseille;
- Nice;
- Gibraltar;
- Tangier.

Corsican, Marseille, Sicilian, and other international traffickers relied on local Campanian distributors. Simonetti and Alfredo Maisto were among the ambitious guappi who reportedly progressed beyond street distribution into larger-scale organization.

**Documented historical interpretation:** Simonetti participated in this contraband economy.

**Not established:** No accessible customs file, financial account, vessel record, or final judgment was found documenting the exact scale of his cigarette business or proving that he personally traveled to France, Spain, Gibraltar, Morocco, or Tangier.

## The Agricultural Market System

### The “presidents of prices”

By the early 1950s, Simonetti had become one of three men repeatedly identified as **presidenti dei prezzi**:

- **Pasquale Simonetti**, associated with Palma Campania and Nola;
- **Alfredo Maisto**, associated with Giugliano;
- **Antonio Esposito**, called Totonno or Tatonno ’e Pomigliano.

Their power lay in occupying the pivotal point between country producers and the Naples wholesale market.

The system could include:

- deciding or coercively influencing daily wholesale prices;
- controlling who could bring produce into the market;
- collecting commissions on loads sold;
- arbitrating disputes;
- arranging protection;
- enforcing debts and commercial promises;
- excluding disobedient producers or dealers;
- threatening persons, crops, farms, vehicles, or premises;
- coordinating transport and distribution.

Simonetti became especially associated with potatoes but also dealt in tomatoes, melons, fruit, flowers, and vegetables.

### Economic structure

This was not necessarily a conventional corporation with shares, offices, and formal payrolls. It was a network of personal authority, illicit agreements, commissions, intimidation, and selective protection layered over legal agricultural commerce.

Historians describe the arrangement as a **mediation racket**. The same activity could appear to different participants as:

- an indispensable brokerage service;
- cartelized price regulation;
- private protection;
- debt enforcement;
- market governance;
- extortion.

Large processors, reportedly including the Cirio concern, could also be affected by coercive intervention in prices or supplies. Proving particular threats was difficult because witnesses and victims rarely cooperated.

### Reported earnings

A later local account asserts that Simonetti collected **100 lire for every quintal of potatoes** sold and that roughly **40,000 quintals** might pass through the market daily. The implied four million lire per day should not be treated as a verified personal income:

- no ledger or tax record supports it;
- the volume may refer to peak market throughput;
- it is unclear whether every load was assessed;
- the commission might have been divided among an organization.

The figures nevertheless illustrate the scale later writers associated with the racket.

### “Alternative justice”

Residents reportedly brought private disputes to Simonetti when official institutions were slow or ineffective. His decisions were enforced by personal reputation and threatened violence.

A frequently retold example concerns a man who impregnated and abandoned a woman. Simonetti supposedly offered him money for flowers and asked whether the flowers should be used for his wedding or funeral.

Versions disagree on the amount—**10,000 or 100,000 lire**—and no contemporaneous complaint or named witness has been found. It should therefore be classified as **reported folklore illustrating his reputation**, not a fully verified incident.

## Rivalries and Armed Conflict

### Alfredo Maisto

Simonetti and **Alfredo Maisto** competed over territorial and economic supremacy.

In **1952**, their groups fought a gun battle, usually located at or near **Melito di Napoli**. The judicial consequences are clearer than the exact origin of the encounter:

- Simonetti was prosecuted for attempted murder and weapons-related violence;
- in 1953 he received a sentence reported as **eight years and three months**;
- Maisto was subsequently described as having been acquitted on self-defense grounds.

Later courtroom testimony concerning Maisto recalled that he “did not tolerate” Simonetti’s attempt at domination.

### Imprisonment

Simonetti was incarcerated, including at **Poggioreale prison**. He served approximately two years before being released around **Christmas 1954**, despite the longer sentence reported in the Maisto case. The available sources do not explain whether this resulted from appeal, credit for detention, provisional release, remission, or another procedure.

A historical source states that Simonetti and associates were accused of participating in a prison disturbance in which mattresses were burned. The event is sometimes dated incorrectly as 1933, when Simonetti was seven; the intended date was almost certainly **1953**. The accusation was reportedly not proved.

### Antonio Esposito

While Simonetti was imprisoned, **Antonio Esposito**, his business associate and later wedding witness, took control of part of their market operation. According to Allum’s reconstruction from contemporary reporting:

- Esposito promised Simonetti’s associates that part of the profits would be reserved for Simonetti;
- Esposito allegedly failed to honor this arrangement;
- Simonetti returned from prison determined to recover his commercial authority;
- each man believed himself entitled to dominate the Vasto/Corso Novara market sector.

This provides the most coherent economic background to Simonetti’s murder, but important distinctions remain:

- their former partnership is well supported;
- their rivalry is well supported;
- Esposito’s diversion of profits is a reported claim;
- Esposito’s role as the murder’s principal is a judicial and historical attribution complicated by his own death before trial.

### Other intermediaries

**Francesco Antonio Tuccillo** is reported to have mediated repeatedly between Simonetti and Esposito. Vittorio Nappi and members of the Orlando network also appear in descriptions of the wider market underworld.

## Courtship and Marriage to Pupetta Maresca

Simonetti courted Pupetta Maresca while already a prominent guappo. Accounts emphasize:

- his exceptional physical size;
- her beauty-queen reputation;
- the approval of her family;
- her pregnancy before the wedding;
- an elaborate celebration attended by approximately 500 people.

The wedding took place on **27 April 1955**. Sources variously locate the religious ceremony at the **Sanctuary of Pompeii** or at **Santa Maria di Pozzano**, between Naples and Sorrento. These versions cannot presently be reconciled. Wedding photographs survive.

Guests reportedly included Simonetti’s allies and rivals, among them Maisto, Esposito, Nappi, and Orlando-connected figures. Esposito is widely described as a wedding witness.

### The surrender-of-the-pistol story

A parliamentary report, later journalism, and family legend state that Simonetti removed his pistol at the altar and handed it to his pregnant wife, or placed it before the Madonna, as a promise to abandon violence.

Versions differ materially:

- the weapon was handed directly to Pupetta;
- it was placed at the Virgin’s feet;
- Pupetta later used the same weapon to kill Esposito.

No contemporaneous church record or ballistic documentation proving continuity of the firearm has been located. The episode is best classified as **reported tradition with strong symbolic significance**.

### Intended retirement

Some accounts say Simonetti planned to become a small landowner and reduce or abandon criminal activity under his wife’s influence. Other evidence shows that after release he actively attempted to regain his market position. The two are not necessarily incompatible—he may have expressed an intention to retire while continuing the struggle—but no property deed or business reorganization establishing a real withdrawal has been found.

## Murder, 16 July 1955

### Setting

On the morning of **16 July 1955**, Simonetti was in **Corso Novara**, close to Naples Central Station and the fruit-and-vegetable market where his authority was concentrated.

He encountered **Carlo Gaetano Orlando**, generally called **Gaetano Orlando** or **Tanino ’e Bastimento**. Orlando was the son of Angelo Orlando, a former mayor of Marano di Napoli, and was associated with Antonio Esposito.

### Immediate confrontation

Later narratives say Simonetti challenged Orlando for failing to greet him—a public insult in the honor code of the guapperia. Harsh words were exchanged.

This public-insult account may describe the immediate trigger, but it does not exclude a prearranged killing grounded in the Simonetti–Esposito struggle.

### Ballistics and cause of death

Accounts differ:

- some say Orlando fired **one shot**;
- the parliamentary organized-crime report says Simonetti was struck by **three shots**;
- another widely circulated account says Orlando’s bullet passed through Simonetti’s wrist and entered his abdomen;
- some sources name an additional participant, **Gaetano Nuvoletta**;
- later journalism says the wrist wound prevented effective compression and Simonetti died from blood loss.

The judicially secure core is that Orlando shot Simonetti and was convicted of the homicide. The exact number of shots and participation of others remain uncertain in the public sources consulted.

Simonetti was taken to hospital. Some later accounts identify the **Ospedale degli Incurabili**, but searchable reproductions contain date and transcription errors, so the exact institution should be treated cautiously.

### Simonetti’s final statements

The traditional narrative says police asked Simonetti to identify his attacker and that he refused, observing the underworld code of silence. Another version says he privately named Esposito as the instigator to Pupetta shortly before dying.

These claims are compatible but not independently verified by a surviving deposition. They became essential elements of the revenge narrative.

### Legal finding concerning Orlando

Orlando was tried with Maresca in proceedings that began in April 1959. He was convicted and initially sentenced to **thirty years’ imprisonment**.

The Court of Cassation later annulled an appellate judgment and ordered reconsideration of evidence favorable to him. A 1960s legal commentary states that his eventual sentence was **twenty-one years**. Thus:

- **documented fact:** Orlando killed Simonetti and was convicted;
- **first-instance judgment:** thirty years;
- **later reported sentence:** twenty-one years after further proceedings;
- **disputed issue:** whether he was an independent adversary acting in a confrontation or a paid killer acting for Esposito.

Orlando maintained that the encounter was not an assassination for hire and later published his own version.

### Antonio Esposito’s responsibility

Esposito was widely identified as the person who ordered Simonetti’s murder. The attribution rests on:

- the economic rivalry;
- Orlando’s association with Esposito;
- Pupetta’s accusation;
- prosecutorial theory;
- later historical consensus.

Esposito was killed before he could be tried for Simonetti’s death. Consequently, describing his role requires care:

- **Historical and prosecutorial attribution:** Esposito ordered the killing.
- **Not a final conviction:** Esposito never received a judicial determination of guilt for commissioning it.
- **Alternative version:** Orlando maintained that the fatal confrontation arose directly between him and Simonetti.

## Pupetta Maresca’s Revenge and the 1959 Trial

This occurred after Simonetti’s death but decisively shaped his historical reputation.

### Killing of Antonio Esposito

On the strongest chronology, **4 October 1955**, the pregnant Pupetta Maresca went to the Corso Novara area, accompanied by her younger brother Ciro. She confronted Esposito near or outside the **Grandone bar** and shot him.

She was arrested on **14 October 1955**.

Maresca later declared in court that she had acted personally and would do so again. The trial generated national and international attention. Microphones were reportedly installed in the Naples courtroom, and foreign correspondents attended.

### Disputed number of shooters

The romanticized account describes Maresca emptying a Smith & Wesson into Esposito by herself. Investigators reportedly found evidence that shots came from multiple weapons—sometimes stated as four—and prosecutors treated the killing as organized retaliation.

The final judgment nevertheless convicted Maresca as the responsible killer. Her lawyers later emphasized that the court treated her motive as personal fury and provocation following her husband’s murder, rather than proving her participation in a Camorra association.

### Sentences

- Maresca received **eighteen years** or, in some accounts, eighteen years and six months.
- On appeal, the sentence was reduced to **thirteen years and four months**.
- Ciro Maresca was initially sentenced to twelve years but was later acquitted on appeal.
- Orlando received thirty years at first instance for Simonetti’s murder; later proceedings reportedly reduced the effective sentence to twenty-one years.

The courtroom divided spectators into popularly labeled **“Pupettisti”** and **“Antipupettisti”**—those who viewed her as an avenging wife and those who saw the killings as an organized-market feud.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Known or attributed interests

Simonetti’s economic interests were concentrated in:

- wholesale fruit and vegetables;
- potato distribution;
- brokerage between growers and wholesalers;
- produce transport and market access;
- protection and debt enforcement;
- postwar cigarette contraband;
- wartime black-market food distribution.

### Business structure

No registered company, partnership agreement, balance sheet, bank account, formal investment portfolio, patent, or corporate office has been identified.

His network appears to have operated through:

- personal partnerships;
- territorial affiliations;
- concessionaires and stallholders;
- commissions;
- reciprocal enforcement;
- cash payments;
- control over access and price information.

### Property and wealth

Simonetti was widely described as prosperous or rich by the standards of his origins. The lavish wedding, market revenues, and underworld status support substantial cash resources. No reliable estate inventory, landholding schedule, probate document, tax assessment, or bank balance was found.

Claims that he intended to become a landowner remain uncorroborated by located deeds.

### Technologies and inventions

No invention, patent, engineering project, or technical paper is known. His operational methods were organizational: communications among markets, coercive enforcement, control of transport and distribution, and exploitation of price-setting bottlenecks.

## Political Activity and Connections

No evidence was found that Simonetti:

- held public office;
- belonged formally to a political party;
- ran for election;
- published a political program;
- acted as a registered union or agricultural representative.

Antonio Esposito was reputed to have political contacts. A later report claimed that twelve deputies sent funeral wreaths after Esposito’s killing, but no verified list of those deputies was located.

Simonetti’s market power inevitably touched local administration, policing, licensing, transport, and agricultural policy. The available evidence does not, however, establish a specific bribery arrangement or party patronage network in his name.

## Religious, Philosophical, and Ideological Development

There is no known personal statement of Simonetti’s religious or political beliefs.

The wedding-pistol story associates him symbolically with popular Catholic devotion to the Madonna. Whether it records sincere repentance, ceremonial theater, or retrospective myth cannot be established.

His practical code, as described by contemporaries and historians, drew on the **guappo** culture of:

- public honor;
- personal courage;
- recognition through greetings and precedence;
- protection of dependents;
- silence before authorities;
- violent enforcement;
- arbitration outside the state.

This was a social and criminal ethos, not a documented philosophical doctrine.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Assunta “Pupetta” Maresca

Wife, mother of his posthumous son, and avenger of his death. Their eighty-day marriage became central to both figures’ public mythology.

### Antonio Esposito, “Totonno/Tatonno ’e Pomigliano”

Business associate, reported wedding witness, later commercial rival, and alleged instigator of Simonetti’s murder. His responsibility was widely accepted by prosecutors and historians but never adjudicated because Maresca killed him before trial.

### Alfredo Maisto

Competitor among the “presidents of prices.” Their rivalry culminated in a 1952 gun battle and Simonetti’s imprisonment.

### Carlo Gaetano Orlando, “Tanino ’e Bastimento”

The man who shot and killed Simonetti. Convicted of the homicide. Orlando disputed the characterization of himself as Esposito’s hired assassin and later sued journalists who called him a sicario.

### Francesco Antonio Tuccillo

Reported market power broker and mediator in the conflicts between Simonetti and Esposito.

### Ciro Maresca, “Lampetiello”

Simonetti’s young brother-in-law. He accompanied Pupetta during the confrontation in which Esposito was killed; convicted at first instance and reportedly acquitted on appeal.

### Gaetano Nuvoletta

Named in some secondary accounts as participating in the attack on Simonetti. The accessible judicial reporting centers on Orlando, and Nuvoletta’s exact legal status in the case remains unclear.

### Growers, wholesalers, concessionaires, and haulers

Most are unnamed in public narratives. Collectively they formed the economic constituency over which Simonetti exercised coercive and arbitrational power.

## Dedicated Adversarial Research Pass

Searches were conducted in Italian and English combining his name or nickname with terms corresponding to controversy, scandal, allegations, accusations, lawsuit, fraud, corruption, conspiracy, criticism, extortion, smuggling, homicide, and recent-year filters.

### Findings

1. **The core negative record is not a late accusation.** It consists of contemporaneous criminal proceedings, market-racket reporting, his attempted-murder conviction, and the prosecution of Orlando for killing him.

2. **No new 2024–2026 criminal revelation about Simonetti was located.** Because he died in 1955, recent material is retrospective.

3. A significant **2024 publication and interview** concerns Francesco Aloia’s novel *Questo sangue masticato*, inspired by his grandfather Gaetano Orlando. It reopens the killer’s family perspective but is a literary reconstruction, not newly discovered judicial proof.

4. Orlando contested the description **“sicario.”** A legal essay reports that he brought a defamation action against journalists who applied the word to him. His objection was not to having killed Simonetti—he had been convicted—but to the claim that he did so as Esposito’s hired killer. This is an important distinction between established homicide and disputed commissioning.

5. The popular claim that Simonetti slapped **Lucky Luciano** appears to be a false attribution. Historians instead identify **Francesco Pirozzi**, associated with Alfredo Maisto, as the man involved.

6. Some modern articles repeat inconsistent dates, weapons, marriage locations, and shot counts. These discrepancies demonstrate dependence on legend and secondary copying rather than new evidence.

7. Later descriptions of Simonetti as a “boss” or “capo della Camorra” should not be read as proof that he headed a single pyramidal organization. Historians describe postwar Campanian criminality as fragmented among localized groups and economic brokers.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## The benevolent guappo

**Story:** Simonetti was a generous giant who protected the weak, settled disputes, and supplied justice where the state failed.

**Circulation:** Local memory, later journalism, popular histories, and accounts of guappo culture.

**Evidence:** Multiple reports say townspeople approached him as an arbitrator. No comprehensive set of named cases survives.

**Counterevidence:** The authority enabling such “justice” was inseparable from armed reputation, market coercion, and documented violence.

**Classification:** **Reported social practice surrounded by hagiographic mythology.**

**Effect:** It allowed Simonetti to be remembered simultaneously as criminal predator and folk protector.

## The wedding ultimatum involving funeral flowers

**Story:** Simonetti offered an absconding seducer money for flowers and instructed him to choose whether they would decorate his wedding or funeral.

**Where it appears:** Later biographies and Clare Longrigg’s account.

**Discrepancy:** The sum is variously 10,000 and 100,000 lire.

**Evidence:** No named contemporary witness or judicial record located.

**Classification:** **Folklore, possibly based on a real intimidation.**

**Effect:** It became a compact parable of guappo arbitration—ostensibly protective, explicitly violent.

## The pistol before the Madonna

**Story:** At his wedding Simonetti handed his pistol to Pupetta or placed it before the Madonna, promising reform; she later used it against Esposito.

**Where it appears:** Parliamentary narrative, journalism, and later histories.

**Evidence:** The wedding and Pupetta’s later shooting are documented; the ceremonial continuity of the weapon is not.

**Classification:** **Reported tradition / unresolved factual claim.**

**Effect:** It converts the weapon into a symbol linking marriage, repentance, betrayal, widowhood, and revenge.

## The slap delivered to Lucky Luciano

**Story:** Simonetti publicly slapped Italian-American gangster Charles “Lucky” Luciano at Agnano racecourse.

**Circulation:** Popular biographies and crime lore.

**Evidence against:** Historical treatments identify Francesco Pirozzi, a man connected with Alfredo Maisto, rather than Simonetti.

**Classification:** **False or misattributed legend.**

**Effect:** The story enlarged Simonetti’s stature by depicting a provincial guappo humiliating an internationally famous mafioso.

## Refusal to name his killer

**Story:** Mortally wounded, Simonetti refused to answer police questions because a guappo did not cooperate with the state.

**Evidence:** Repeated in later narrative sources; no authenticated deposition located.

**Classification:** **Plausible reported fact, not independently verified.**

**Effect:** It reinforced his posthumous image as an exemplary adherent of the underworld code.

## Secret identification of Esposito

**Story:** Although silent to police, Simonetti whispered to his wife that Esposito had ordered his death.

**Evidence:** Central to Maresca’s account and motive; no independent witness has been established.

**Classification:** **Reported claim by an interested participant.**

**Effect:** It supplied the moral structure of the revenge story and helped transform Maresca into a popular tragic heroine.

## Murder over a missing greeting

**Story:** Orlando killed Simonetti because Orlando failed to greet him and Simonetti publicly objected.

**Evidence:** A confrontation and exchange of insults are reported.

**Countervailing evidence:** Longstanding market rivalry and Orlando’s Esposito connection indicate a larger background.

**Classification:** **Reported immediate trigger; insufficient as a complete causal explanation.**

## Simonetti as supreme head of the Camorra

**Story:** He was the undisputed chief of the entire Neapolitan Camorra.

**Evidence:** He exercised major market power and was called the single “president of prices.”

**Qualification:** Postwar Camorra networks were decentralized. Other powerful figures controlled separate territories and trades.

**Classification:** **Popular exaggeration built on documented regional influence.**

## Films, Television, Interviews, and Archival Material

### *La sfida* (The Challenge), 1958

Francesco Rosi’s first solo-directed feature was freely inspired by Simonetti’s rise and death.

- José Suárez plays **Vito Polara**, the fictionalized Simonetti figure.
- Rosanna Schiaffino plays **Assunta**, modeled partly on Maresca.
- The screenplay was by Suso Cecchi D’Amico, Enzo Provenzale, and Francesco Rosi.
- It won the **Silver Lion** at the 1958 Venice Film Festival and three Nastri d’Argento in 1959.

The film is not a literal biography. It alters names and events and focuses on structural control of the agricultural market. Its importance lies in turning the Simonetti story into an early work of Italian political cinema.

### *Pupetta – Il coraggio e la passione*, 2013

A television miniseries starring Manuela Arcuri, centered on a fictionalized Pupetta. The production generated criticism that it romanticized Camorra culture. Its characters and chronology should not be treated as documentary evidence.

### Other screen material

Pupetta Maresca appeared in films and later television interviews, often discussing her own life. These interviews preserve her self-created narrative about Simonetti but were recorded after years of media mythmaking.

### Photographic and documentary archives

- Wedding photographs of Simonetti and Maresca survive on Wikimedia Commons and in press archives.
- A 1955 photograph of Simonetti was reproduced by *L’Unità*.
- Fototeca Gilardi preserves the *Domenica del Corriere* illustration and caption for the 1959 trial.
- Material objects associated with the Simonetti case, including weapons, have been discussed in connection with a proposed Naples museum of criminal evidence.
- Radio Radicale’s later Camorra-trial archive contains retrospective courtroom references to the Simonetti–Maisto conflict.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Simonetti’s historical importance exceeds the length of his life because his career stands at the intersection of three developments.

### Transformation of the Camorra

He represents the postwar transition from the traditional neighborhood guappo to an entrepreneur who controlled economic bottlenecks. His influence was exercised through market mediation rather than a modern drug cartel or formally named clan.

### Visibility of agricultural racketeering

The affair exposed the degree to which organized violence could govern the passage of ordinary food from farms to cities. The “president of prices” became a durable shorthand for private criminal regulation of markets.

### The Pupetta Maresca narrative

Maresca’s revenge, pregnancy, beauty, defiant testimony, and imprisonment overshadowed Simonetti himself. In popular memory he became:

- the murdered husband;
- the betrayed market king;
- the giant guappo;
- the silenced victim whose widow completed the vendetta.

### Cinematic influence

Rosi stripped away much of the romantic revenge narrative and used the underlying market story to analyze the relations among criminal authority, modernization, wholesalers, landowners, and commercial expansion.

### Historical reinterpretation

Later historians have tended to qualify three older claims:

1. Simonetti was not necessarily chief of a unified Camorra.
2. His role as market mediator combined real economic services with coercion.
3. His benevolent-guappo reputation cannot be separated from violent enforcement.

No formal honor, public appointment, decoration, or legitimate institutional commemoration was located. His commemoration has been cultural, journalistic, criminological, and familial.

## Atlas Connections

### Assunta “Pupetta” Maresca

**Evidentiary label: Documented fact**

Simonetti courted and married Assunta Maresca on 27 April 1955. She was pregnant with their son when he was murdered on 16 July. She subsequently killed Antonio Esposito, whom she accused of ordering Simonetti’s murder, and was convicted. Their marriage, the killing, and her 1959 trial created the principal public narrative through which Simonetti is remembered.

**Dates and places:** Courtship before April 1955; marriage on 27 April 1955, with sources differing between Pompeii and Santa Maria di Pozzano; shared connections to Palma Campania, Castellammare di Stabia, and Naples.

### Charles “Lucky” Luciano

**Evidentiary label: Rumor or myth / probable false attribution**

A durable story says Simonetti slapped Luciano at the Agnano racecourse. Historical research instead attributes the confrontation to Francesco Pirozzi, a man in Alfredo Maisto’s circle. No reliable evidence establishes that Simonetti struck Luciano or had a substantive personal relationship with him.

The myth connected Simonetti symbolically to an internationally famous Mafia figure and enlarged his image as a fearless local guappo. It should not be recorded as a documented meeting.
