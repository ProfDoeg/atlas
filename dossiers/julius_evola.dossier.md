# Julius Evola: Research Dossier

*Prepared 30 August 2026. This dossier distinguishes contemporaneous documentation, Evola's retrospective self-report, later scholarship, judicial findings, and legend. Dates in Evola's private life are sometimes uncertain because he deliberately minimized ordinary biographical detail and fashioned his memoir as an intellectual rather than domestic autobiography.*

## Evidentiary Note

Evola is unusually easy to misdescribe because four partial portraits are repeatedly substituted for the whole: the Dada artist; the apolitical Traditionalist; the Fascist/Nazi racial theorist; and the postwar guru. Each is documented, but none by itself explains the sequence. His own *Il cammino del cinabro* (1963; revised 1972) is indispensable for intellectual chronology, drug use, suicidal crisis, occult practice, mountaineering, war injury, and his retrospective interpretation of Fascism. It is not a neutral memoir: it suppresses domestic life, arranges events as stages of spiritual realization, and often converts political failure into evidence of principled distance. Police surveillance, Fascist publication controls, Mussolini's patronage, SS security reports, the 1951 criminal proceeding, and the recollections of followers must therefore be read alongside it.

## Family and Ancestry

Evola was born in Rome to Vincenzo Evola and Concetta Frangipane, both from Sicilian Catholic backgrounds. The *Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani* describes the family as aristocratic, Catholic, and of distant Spanish descent. Later sympathetic biographies often call him a baron and sometimes elaborate an old Norman or Spanish-Sicilian lineage. The basic family claim is plausible; the leap to Evola's personal legal title is not securely demonstrated in the sources consulted. Contemporary people who knew him, including Aleramo, did not consistently style him baron. Postwar followers made “the Baron” a compact symbol of his cultivated bearing, monocle, hierarchy doctrine, and distance from mass society. Thus:

- **Documented:** parents' names; Roman birth; Sicilian Catholic family; claims of aristocratic and Spanish ancestry in major Italian reference works.
- **Disputed:** whether Giulio/Julius possessed or formally inherited a legally valid baronial title.
- **Historically consequential myth:** “Baron Evola” became a personal brand after the war and remains pervasive on editions, websites, clothing, and political imagery.

The household's exact wealth and Vincenzo's profession are not adequately established in the accessible record. Evola had enough educational and cultural capital to attend engineering courses, paint, travel, publish small avant-garde journals, and move in Roman salons. No company ownership, inherited estate, investment portfolio, patent, salaried chair, or sustained commercial enterprise has been documented. In adult life he appears to have lived from family means, journalism, translations, books, editorial work, patrons/publishers, and in later years whatever disability or private support was available. Exact income and property records remain unestablished.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1898–1916

Evola grew up in a Catholic environment but later rejected Christianity as a central instance of the egalitarian and “priestly” tendency he opposed. He gave few childhood recollections. As an adolescent he was directed toward technical and mathematical study while independently immersing himself in art, poetry, philosophy, and the culture of *Leonardo*, *Lacerba*, and *La Voce*. His early reading, by his own later account and the Treccani biography, included Oscar Wilde, Gabriele D'Annunzio, Friedrich Nietzsche, Max Stirner, Otto Weininger, Carlo Michelstaedter, and Giovanni Papini. He admired Papini's individualist “titanism” but repudiated Papini's later Catholic conversion.

His earliest known paintings date to 1915 and poems to 1916. He entered the Roman Futurist environment around **Giacomo Balla**, meeting or associating with **Filippo Tommaso Marinetti, Enrico Prampolini**, and **Fortunato Depero**. Evola accepted the avant-garde demand to break inherited aesthetic forms but found Futurism physically materialist, nationalist, and—during the First World War—crudely anti-German. His initial “sensory idealism” combined Futurist dynamism with an increasingly inward and esoteric conception of abstraction.

## Education and Formation

Evola attended technical studies and enrolled in engineering at the University of Rome. Treccani reports that he completed the course of study but deliberately did not take the degree, later saying he despised bourgeois titles such as doctor or engineer. Other short biographies say simply that he left without completing the degree. The safe conclusion is that he undertook substantial engineering study and never received a university degree; his claim to have completed all coursework rests principally on his own account.

He had no conventional academic career and no permanent university appointment. His education after the war was self-directed and network-based:

- philosophy through Nietzsche, Stirner, Michelstaedter, Weininger, German Idealism, and Giovanni Gentile;
- avant-garde practice through Balla, Marinetti, Prampolini, Depero, Tristan Tzara, Anton Giulio Bragaglia, and Herwarth Walden;
- Western esotericism through Arturo Reghini, Giovanni Colazza, Giulio Parise, theosophical and neo-Pythagorean circles, Hermetic and alchemical texts, and the Ur Group;
- Asian traditions through translations and Orientalist literature on Taoism, Buddhism, Vedānta, and Tantra rather than documented apprenticeship under an Asian monastic lineage;
- Traditionalism through René Guénon, with whom he corresponded, argued, and from whom he adopted the transhistorical idea of Tradition while rejecting Guénon's preference for contemplative/sacerdotal authority over political-warrior action;
- comparative religion and Asian studies through contacts that included Giuseppe Tucci and contributions to *Asiatica* and later *East and West*.

The repeated description of Evola as a “professor” or academic philosopher is therefore misleading. In 1939 Giuseppe Bottai reportedly offered him a university lectureship/chair connected with racial doctrine and arranged lectures at Milan and Florence, but no stable academic office followed.

## Chronological Life History

### First World War, drugs, and the postwar crisis, 1917–1919

After artillery-officer training, Evola served in mountain positions near Asiago in 1917–1918. He was not recorded as participating in a major action. His later presentation of war emphasized impersonal discipline, danger, and the possibility of transcendence, not Italian nationalism. The stance prefigured his later distinction between an external “small holy war” and an inner “great holy war.”

Treccani, following his autobiographical record, dates his first use of narcotics to the war and says it continued until about 1925. Evola described drugs not as recreation but as an attempt to break ordinary consciousness. He also said these experiments, occult practices, and disgust with ordinary postwar life brought him close to psychic disintegration. No clinical diagnosis is documented. His account should be treated as **self-report**, not independently verified medical history.

In April 1919 he exhibited at Marinetti's National Futurist Exhibition at Palazzo Cova, Milan. Representative early works include *Fucina, studio di rumori*, *Five o'clock tea*, and *Mazzo di fiori*.

### Dada and “mystical abstraction,” 1920–1923

Evola wrote to **Tristan Tzara** on 5 January 1920 formally affiliating himself with Dada. He interpreted Dada less as negation or play than as liberation of an absolute interior will. In January 1920 he held a solo exhibition at the Casa d'Arte Bragaglia in Rome. He published *Arte astratta: posizione teorica* and the French Dada poem *La parole obscure du paysage intérieur*. With Aldo Fiozzi and Gino Cantarelli he founded the Mantua review *Bleu*, which produced three issues in roughly a year. He collaborated with Bragaglia's *Cronache d'attualità* and co-directed Prampolini's *Noi*.

Herwarth Walden arranged a January 1921 exhibition at Berlin's Galerie Der Sturm at which Evola showed approximately sixty paintings. He participated in the Paris Salon Dada at Galerie Montaigne in June 1921, another Bragaglia group show, and Dada evenings in Rome's Grotte dell'Augusteo, for which he painted a backdrop. His second painterly phase—*astrattismo mistico*—included the *Paesaggi interiori* and works titled *Astrazione* and *Composizione Dada*.

Around 1922–1923 he abandoned sustained painting and poetry. His memoir casts this as the completion, not failure, of the avant-garde route. He believed Dada had exhausted its liberating function and was becoming a style and market. He later resumed painting sporadically, especially after 1960, and did not destroy the surviving work.

At approximately the same moment he reported contemplating suicide. In his retrospective story, a sentence from an early Buddhist text—on the extinction of the craving for personal existence—revealed that the desire to annihilate himself was itself a bond to the personal self. The crisis passed. This is one of the foundational episodes of his self-created spiritual biography. It is supported by his own memoir, not an independent witness.

### Magical idealism and the Roman esoteric milieu, 1923–1929

From 1923 Evola announced a philosophical phase. He Latinized Giulio to Julius. In *Saggi sull'idealismo magico* (1925), *L'uomo come potenza* (1925), *L'individuo e il divenire del mondo* (1926), *Teoria dell'individuo assoluto* (1927), and *Fenomenologia dell'individuo assoluto* (1930), he attempted to push Idealism beyond logical self-consciousness toward an operative “absolute individual” capable of overcoming the conditioned empirical self. “Power” meant ontological efficacy as well as will.

He lectured in 1925 at the Independent Theosophical League in Rome, contributed to *Ultra*, *Atanòr*, and *L'Idealismo realistico*, and encountered **Arturo Reghini**, **Giovanni Colazza**, **Giulio Parise**, **Guido De Giorgio**, **Arturo Onofri**, and other occultists. With Reghini he helped publish *Ignis* in 1925. A polemic with Guénon over Vedānta began on *L'Idealismo realistico*; the disagreement became a long, productive, unequal exchange. Evola later acknowledged Guénon as a master of Traditional doctrine while refusing to become his disciple.

His 1925–1926 relationship with **Sibilla Aleramo**, twenty-two years his senior, belongs to this milieu. Aleramo's papers and autobiographical fiction are the principal evidence. Her *Amo, dunque sono* presents Evola as “Tellegra,” an emotionally withholding esoteric intellectual from whom she becomes estranged before her relationship with Giulio/Guido Parise (“Luciano”). The literary portrait cannot be read as stenographic fact, but it is contemporaneous evidence against the later image of an entirely celibate, impersonal sage.

In late 1926 or 1927 Evola, Reghini, Colazza, and associates formed the **Gruppo di Ur**, a small experimental circle that combined Hermetic, Buddhist, Tantric, anthroposophical, magical, and initiatic practices. Its monthly *Ur* (1927–1928) and *Krur* (1929) articles appeared under pseudonyms and were later collected as *Introduzione alla magia quale scienza dell'Io*. The group reportedly created “chains” of coordinated practice. Some members hoped to supply Fascism with a non-Christian Roman-sacral core or magically affect its direction. This intention is recorded in participant and scholarly accounts; claims that the group successfully exercised supernatural influence are matters of belief, not demonstrable political causation.

Personal and doctrinal conflict ended the collaboration. Reghini, a Freemason and neo-Pythagorean, accused Evola of appropriation and blamed him for the handling of *Imperialismo pagano*; Evola accused Reghini and Parise of attempting to subordinate the review to Masonic interests. Evola retained control of the periodical as *Krur* in 1929; the circle dissolved.

### First political interventions and conflict with Fascist normalization, 1925–1933

Evola's first explicit political article, “Stato, potenza, libertà,” appeared on 1 May 1925 in Duke Giovanni Colonna di Cesarò's anti-Fascist but antidemocratic *Lo Stato democratico*. It rejected liberal democracy and criticized existing Fascism for failing to establish an authentic spiritual-aristocratic order. This double opposition remained characteristic: Evola was against parliamentary egalitarianism and against Fascism whenever it appeared populist, nationalist, Catholic, bourgeois, biological-materialist, or administratively totalitarian.

*Imperialismo pagano: il fascismo dinanzi al pericolo euro-cristiano* (1928) demanded a pagan, Roman, imperial reorientation and attacked the Church immediately before the Lateran settlement. Catholic and Fascist critics reacted sharply. Evola was not rejecting dictatorship in favor of democracy; he was attacking Mussolini's regime from a position he considered farther to the hierarchical right.

In 1930 he founded the fortnightly *La Torre*. It attracted writers from several anti-modern currents but attacked Fascist demography, party conformism, and compromises with Catholicism. Party secretary **Achille Starace** intervened; Roman printers were forbidden to print it, street distribution was obstructed, and the journal ended after ten issues. Police reports treated Evola as dangerous or opportunistic, and circulated allegations that he was connected to Jiddu Krishnamurti's Order of the Star or the Ordo Templi Orientis. These were **police suspicions**, not proven memberships. Evola was placed under surveillance.

He temporarily withdrew to mountain climbing. During the late 1920s and 1930s he climbed in the Alps and described the high mountain as a direct school of detachment, danger, silence, and solar transcendence. Claims that he completed a major first ascent were not substantiated in the sources consulted. His alpine essays were collected posthumously/at the end of his life as *Meditazioni delle vette*.

He also made brief incognito stays in Carthusian, Carmelite, and old-rule Benedictine monasteries in the early 1930s. He considered original Christianity a possible severe path of salvation while placing it below “regal” and initiatic traditions. *La tradizione ermetica* (1931) interpreted alchemy as an operative royal art; *Maschera e volto dello spiritualismo contemporaneo* (1932) criticized Theosophy, spiritualism, psychoanalysis, and contemporary occult currents as largely passive or confused.

### *Revolt*, German networks, Fascist journalism, 1934–1937

*Rivolta contro il mondo moderno* appeared with Hoepli in 1934. Its first part constructs an ideal-typical “world of Tradition”: sacral kingship, initiation, caste, warrior asceticism, ritual, and hierarchy. Its second part narrates history as decline through a four-age/four-caste pattern, combining Indo-European myth, Hindu yugas, Roman and medieval materials, Bachofen, Guénon, and selective ethnology. It reverses progressive evolution: modern democracy, capitalism, communism, material science, feminism, and mass society are terminal dissolution. The book was initially little noticed in Italy but became his central work.

A German *Heidnischer Imperialismus* appeared in 1933, substantially recasting the Italian book. Evola lectured in Berlin and Bremen in 1934. In Germany he cultivated the aristocratic-conservative **Deutscher Herrenklub** milieu, Prince Karl Anton Rohan's networks, and contacts connected to the “Conservative Revolution,” rather than initially relying on the Nazi Party mass organization. Important interlocutors or contacts included **Edgar Julius Jung**, **Othmar Spann**, **Heinrich von Gleichen**, and later **Carl Schmitt**, **Ernst Jünger**, and racial theorist **Ludwig Ferdinand Clauss**. Not every claimed friendship was intimate: surviving correspondence shows intellectual contact with Schmitt; a 1953 letter indicates Evola admired Jünger and sought translation rights but may never have met him.

From 1934 until 18 July 1943 Evola edited “Diorama filosofico: Problemi e prospettive nell'etica fascista,” a cultural page in **Roberto Farinacci's** *Il Regime Fascista*. This was his longest quasi-institutional platform. Contributors included Guénon, Spann, and European conservative or Traditionalist writers. Farinacci's protection gave him access but did not make his doctrine official policy.

The relationship with Nazi institutions was ambivalent and increasingly concrete. Evola admired the SS as a possible political-military order, the Axis as Europe's defense against the United States and USSR, and National Socialism's attempt to form a new elite. He criticized mass völkisch nationalism, biological reductionism, Hitler's plebeian style, and the quasi-religious Führer cult. SS and Sicherheitsdienst evaluators, in turn, often found him foreign, reactionary, “Roman,” aristocratic, and doctrinally unreliable. The German secret documents published by Cospito and Neulen record surveillance and rejection as well as access. “Rejected by the SS” and “SS collaborator” are therefore not mutually exclusive descriptions; they refer to different moments and offices.

In *Tre aspetti del problema ebraico* (1936) and his 1937 introduction/edition of the *Protocols of the Elders of Zion*, Evola participated directly in antisemitic propaganda. The *Protocols* were already a demonstrated forgery; he treated them as revealing a true pattern even if not authentic as a document. *Il mito del sangue* (1937) surveyed modern racial theories. *Il mistero del Graal e la tradizione ghibellina dell'Impero* (1937) detached the Grail from purely Christian devotion and read it as initiation and Ghibelline imperial symbolism.

### Romania and the Iron Guard, 1937–1938

Evola traveled to Romania in 1937 and interviewed **Corneliu Zelea Codreanu**, leader of the Legion of the Archangel Michael/Iron Guard. He also knew **Mircea Eliade**, who reviewed *Rivolta*, and moved through circles associated with Nae Ionescu. Evola presented Codreanu as an ascetic, anti-bourgeois captain whose movement united political militancy with religious transformation. After Codreanu's 1938 killing by the Romanian state, Evola wrote “La tragedia della Guardia di Ferro.” The Iron Guard remained one of his preferred models of an order-like political elite, although its Orthodox Christianity differed from his pagan-imperial emphasis and its record included assassinations and mass violence.

### Race doctrine and access to Mussolini, 1938–1943

After Fascist Italy's 1938 racial laws, race became a major part of Evola's paid and political writing. He contributed to **Telesio Interlandi's** *La difesa della razza* and other regime organs. Giuseppe Bottai invited him to lecture on race at universities in Milan and Florence and reportedly proposed a chair of racism at Rome in 1939. The chair did not become an enduring appointment.

Evola divided race into body, soul, and spirit. He borrowed from Ludwig Ferdinand Clauss and other German race psychology but subordinated anatomy to an inner “style.” This allowed him to criticize crude skull-measurement while retaining heredity, antisemitic typology, eugenic selection, anti-miscegenation, and an “Aryan-Roman” leading race. **“Spiritual racism” was an alternative formulation within Fascist racism, not a rejection of racial hierarchy or antisemitic state policy.** Peter Staudenmaier's archival study shows Evola trying to reconcile Italian and German racisms and encountering resistance from officials in both systems.

*Sintesi di dottrina della razza* (1941) brought his greatest official success. Mussolini summoned him in September 1941, praised or endorsed the work, and directed attention to Evola's conception. Evola proposed an Italian-German review, variously reported as *Sangue e Spirito* or *Sangue e Razza*. He drafted a program and sought German partners. Rival Fascist, Catholic, and German racial offices obstructed it, and it never appeared. He traveled to Germany in February 1942 to obtain backing and cultivated officials in the racial apparatus.

Mussolini's relationship to Evola was therefore episodic but real:

1. For most of the regime Evola was a freelance critic without party membership.
2. Fascist authorities suppressed *La Torre* and monitored him.
3. Farinacci gave him a durable newspaper platform.
4. Bottai opened lecture opportunities.
5. Mussolini personally received him in 1941 and sponsored his racial initiative.
6. His proposed journal failed and he never became a central policy official.

During the war Evola requested assignment to the Russian front. Approval arrived, by his account, only after the Italian retreat; he blamed his nonmembership in the party. This was an unrealized request, not military service on the Eastern Front.

### Collapse of Fascism, Berlin, Rastenburg, Rome, and Vienna, 1943–1945

After Mussolini's fall on 25 July 1943, extreme antisemite **Giovanni Preziosi** recommended Evola to German authorities for a project exposing alleged Jewish-Masonic influence in the fallen Italian establishment. Evola accepted a German invitation and went to Berlin in late August. Following Mussolini's rescue on 12 September, Evola was at or around Hitler's East Prussian headquarters at Rastenburg/Wolfsschanze in the group receiving the restored dictator. Accounts that he personally held a substantive meeting with Hitler are less securely documented than his presence in the entourage.

Evola did not embrace the Italian Social Republic as a sufficient restoration. He tried to develop a **Movimento per la Rinascita dell'Italia**, conceived as a nucleus for a postwar authoritarian right. Back in German-occupied Rome, he circulated among RSI and German contacts. When Allied forces approached in 1944 he moved north through the RSI and then to Vienna.

In Vienna he worked with material seized from Masonic and Jewish organizations, in the orbit of the SS/Sicherheitsdienst and **RSHA Amt VII**, the ideological research and library office. His exact status is disputed. His memoir and later defenders sometimes describe scholarly examination rather than intelligence work; other historians infer or state Sicherheitsdienst service. The documented minimum is protected collaboration with German security-linked research offices using confiscated collections. No evidence found establishes that he commanded police operations, participated personally in deportations, or held SS rank. Nor does the absence of such evidence turn the archive work into politically neutral scholarship: the collections were products of Nazi seizure, and the project served an antisemitic and anti-Masonic security apparatus.

In April 1945, as Soviet forces fought for Vienna, Evola deliberately remained above ground during bombardment instead of using shelters. An explosion threw or struck him and damaged his spinal cord. Sources differ on whether the projectile came from a Soviet artillery shell, aerial bomb, or general bombardment. Treccani says April and a bombing; sympathetic biographies have attempted to reconstruct a specific air raid. He survived but never recovered functional use of his legs.

### Hospitals and return to Italy, 1945–1948

Evola spent approximately two years in Austrian hospitals and clinics. Details vary among biographies, and claims about miraculous opportunities to recover mobility are difficult to verify. The injury became the axial fact of his final three decades. He interpreted it neither as punishment nor random tragedy but as an encounter with fate that he had not inwardly “solved.” He continued writing and receiving visitors despite pain, limited mobility, and dependence on attendants.

In 1948 he returned to Italy, first to Bologna and then to Rome. He settled in the apartment at Corso Vittorio Emanuele II 197, where young rightists, occultists, writers, diplomats, and admirers visited by appointment.

### Postwar neo-Fascist mentor and the FAR trial, 1949–1953

Evola supplied younger militants inside and around the **Movimento Sociale Italiano (MSI)** with a language of elite formation, impersonality, anti-bourgeois conduct, and “standing among the ruins.” His short program **Orientamenti** circulated in 1950. He wrote in *Imperium*, *Meridiano d'Italia*, *La rivolta ideale*, and later *Ordine Nuovo*. Important younger readers included **Pino Rauti, Enzo Erra, Clemente Graziani**, and **Adriano Romualdi**. Evola refused the compromises and electoral routine of MSI politics but remained engaged in its cultural field.

Young neo-Fascists connected directly or indirectly to this milieu carried out or attempted bombings in 1949–1950. Police arrested Evola in May 1951 with defendants associated with the **Fasci di Azione Rivoluzionaria (FAR)**. He spent roughly a month in custody and appeared in court on a stretcher/wheelchair. The charges concerned apology/glorification of Fascism and attempting to reconstitute the dissolved Fascist Party under the 1948 Scelba constitutional framework and criminal law.

His **Autodifesa** argued that antidemocratic, hierarchical, and aristocratic ideas preceded Fascism and could not be criminalized merely because Fascists adopted some of them. He denied being a Fascist in the ordinary party-regime sense and called himself, in the phrase attached to the trial, a **“superfascista”**—whether “above,” “beyond,” or more radical than Fascism is linguistically and politically debated. On 20 November 1951 the court acquitted him; twenty-three of thirty-six defendants were acquitted and thirteen received sentences. The court did **not** find Evola legally responsible for the militants' violent acts. Acquittal establishes the failure of the criminal case, not absence of ideological influence.

*Gli uomini e le rovine* (1953), introduced in its original edition by **Prince Junio Valerio Borghese**, developed a postwar organic state, order of politically qualified men, rejection of party democracy, and possible action by a minority in a crisis. Evola corresponded with **Carl Schmitt** (letters survive for 1951–1963) and contacted **Ernst Jünger** in 1953 about translating *Der Arbeiter*. He supported aspects of **Francis Parker Yockey's** *Imperium* while finding Yockey strategically superficial.

### Later work, apolitìa, visitors, and renewed painting, 1954–1967

Evola wrote political commentary and major esoteric studies from his apartment. *Metafisica del sesso* (1958) presented sexuality as a potential route to transcendence through polar complementarity, Tantra, ritual union, and selected literary and ethnographic examples. It also codified strongly hierarchical male/female roles. He did not marry and left no acknowledged children; evidence of later sexual partners is fragmentary and colored by disciple lore.

*L'operaio nel pensiero di Ernst Jünger* (1960) examined Jünger's Worker as a type beyond bourgeois individualism. *Cavalcare la tigre* (1961) addressed a differentiated person living after the practical collapse of Tradition. Its **apolitìa** meant inner nonattachment to the political forms of the age, not liberal neutrality. Elisabetta Cassina Wolff argues that his continuing instruction of neo-Fascist youth calls a purely apolitical reading into question.

*Il cammino del cinabro* (1963) organized his works as phases in an alchemical path. A Rome retrospective at Galleria La Medusa the same year reportedly sold every exhibited painting. He resumed making abstract works around this period. The renewed art market complicates his earlier claim that painting had been completely transcended, though it does not establish substantial wealth.

His correspondents and visitors included Italian militants and intellectuals, foreign Traditionalists, researchers, and figures in the old European right. Surviving letters include exchanges with Guénon, Schmitt, and Egyptologist **Boris de Rachewiltz** (43 letters/postcards, 1958–1967, auctioned in 2025). **Giorgio Almirante** later called him “our Marcuse—only better,” summarizing the MSI right's search for a counter-theorist to the New Left.

### Miguel Serrano and Evola, chiefly 1970

Chilean diplomat and writer **Miguel Serrano** did not shape Evola's interwar career and should not be retrojected into it. While ambassador in Europe, Serrano sought out veterans and intellectuals of Fascism, Nazism, Traditionalism, and occultism. His own chronology records that in **1970** he visited Evola in Rome after unsuccessfully seeking Ezra Pound in Venice. Serrano later recalled leaving the apartment, being called back by Evola, and wondering whether Evola meant to reveal a secret. He published “El secreto de Julius Evola” in *Hiperbórea* no. 2 (1992) and in *Nuestro honor se llama lealtad* (1994).

The relationship was thus a late personal encounter followed by Serrano's retrospective appropriation. Evola's polar/Hyperborean Tradition, Aryan myth, initiation, SS ideal, and spiritual race supplied elements that Serrano radicalized into **Esoteric Hitlerism**, a Gnostic cosmic war in which Hitler became an avatar. Serrano combined Evola with Jung, Guénon, Savitri Devi, Nazi survival mythology, Antarctic lore, and his own revelations. Evola did not publish Serrano's full later doctrine, and Serrano sometimes criticized Traditionalists while claiming to go beyond them. Describing Serrano simply as Evola's collaborator or disciple overstates the documented contact.

### Radical-right rediscovery and final years, 1968–1974

The global revolt of 1968 brought a paradoxical revival. Young rightists read Evola's attacks on bourgeois consumerism alongside, or against, Marxism, Marcuse, and the Frankfurt School. **Pino Rauti's** *Ordine Nuovo* environment and the journal *Civiltà* promoted him. Some right radicals proposed tactical convergence with the extra-parliamentary left against capitalism and the Atlantic/Soviet order; “Nazi-Maoist” currents drew selectively on Evola, Mao, and Third World revolt.

Evola published *L'arco e la clava* (1968), the poetry collection *Raâga Blanda* (1969), revised earlier books, and supervised collections. From 1968 to his death he directed Edizioni Mediterranee's **Orizzonti dello Spirito** series. His journalism continued in *Roma*, *Il Borghese*, *Il Ghibellino*, *Vie della Tradizione*, *Ordine Nuovo*, *La Destra*, and *East and West*.

He remained physically isolated but socially accessible by appointment. Accounts of his last hours say that he asked to be moved to a desk or window facing the Janiculum and died upright or looking toward the hill. These details come from followers and are part testimony, part deathbed iconography. The basic fact is that he died in his Rome apartment on 11 June 1974.

Following cremation, **Renato Del Ponte** and Evola's former mountain guide **Eugène David** carried his ashes to Monte Rosa in August 1974 and placed/scattered them in a glacier crevasse, fulfilling his instructions. Exact descriptions vary between burial “on” Monte Rosa and deposition in a crevasse. The rite joined his alpine metaphysics to his posthumous cult.

### Money and property

No evidence was found of patents, inventions, manufacturing companies, securities holdings, money laundering, commercial fraud, or significant real property. Evola's “projects” were overwhelmingly editorial, artistic, philosophical, esoteric, and political. His books were issued by numerous commercial houses—Atanòr, Bocca, Laterza, Hoepli, Scheiwiller, Edizioni Mediterranee, and others—but publication does not establish ownership. His wartime access depended on political patrons and German institutions; his postwar apartment life depended on publishing income and private support whose exact structure is not publicly reconstructed. The 1963 La Medusa retrospective reportedly sold out, but prices and beneficiaries are not established.

### Religion and metaphysics

Evola denied modern secular materialism but was not conventionally theistic. “Tradition” meant a primordial, supra-human body of truth instantiated differently in Indo-European, Hindu, Buddhist, Roman, Hermetic, medieval imperial, and initiatic forms. He distinguished a “regal” or warrior spirituality from “sacerdotal” contemplation and ranked the former more highly. He rejected Christianity's equality of souls, salvation of the weak, creationism, and church universalism, while selectively admiring ascetic Christianity, medieval orders, the Grail, and Ghibelline imperial conflict with the papacy.

His method was morphological and analogical rather than historical-critical: a symbol or institution from different civilizations could manifest the same primordial structure. This produces the density of *Rivolta* but also makes disconfirmation difficult. Historical sequences are organized as sacred decline rather than reconstructed through conventional chronology.

### Sex and gender

Evola treated sexual polarity as cosmic and asymmetrical. Masculinity signified transcendent form, solar constancy, and initiative; femininity signified nature, becoming, and telluric power, though certain feminine forms could be initiatic or divine. He attacked feminism as dissolution of differentiation. *Metafisica del sesso* also drew wide comparative material on Tantra, courtly love, ritual sexuality, homosexuality, and magical eros. Later admirers sometimes isolate its initiatic account from its political gender hierarchy; critics sometimes reduce the entire book to misogyny. Both elements are explicit in the text.

### Art

Evola's genuine place in Italian Dada is documented by correspondence with Tzara, exhibitions, journals, and surviving canvases. His work is represented in Italian collections and the Dada archive. The 2022 MART exhibition in Rovereto displayed roughly fifty works; the 2025 Electa catalogue raisonné, edited by Guido Andrea Pautasso, reports verification of historical data and illustrates 176 items/images. The late institutional revival has generated controversy over whether aesthetic presentation sanitizes his politics; the catalogue is nevertheless important documentary work.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, and Intelligence Connections

### Fascism and the claim “Evola was not a Fascist”

- **Documented fact:** Evola criticized Mussolini's actual regime, was not proven a PNF member, suffered suppression of *La Torre*, and rejected Fascist populism, nationalism, Catholic accommodation, and bureaucratic totalitarianism.
- **Documented fact:** he wrote for Fascist institutions for nearly two decades, accepted Farinacci's platform, contributed to racial propaganda, sought to radicalize the regime, received Mussolini's patronage, supported Axis victory, and collaborated with the RSI/German apparatus after 1943.
- **Conclusion warranted by the record:** nonmembership and heterodoxy distinguish him from an ordinary party Fascist; they do not establish political opposition to authoritarian hierarchy or racial Fascism. His own “superfascist” formulation captured this position beyond and to the regime's radical right.

### Antisemitism, racial law, and the Holocaust

Evola's 1930s–1940s record includes antisemitic journalism, *Tre aspetti del problema ebraico*, an edition/introduction to the forged *Protocols*, articles in *La difesa della razza*, *Il mito del sangue*, *Sintesi di dottrina della razza*, and *Indirizzi per una educazione razziale*. He proposed legal-cultural selection and an Aryan-Roman ruling type. His disagreement with biological materialism was a dispute within racist doctrine. Claims that his “spiritual racism” was therefore nonracist are contradicted by his explicit treatment of bodily heredity and political race measures.

Whether he directly drafted the 1938 Italian racial laws is **not established**. His public work supported and elaborated the environment of their enforcement; scholars disagree about how much policy influence he exercised. The clearest personal access came after their promulgation, especially in 1941. No evidence found makes him an operational participant in deportation or mass murder. He did not produce a clear postwar reckoning with the Holocaust and continued to publish antisemitic conspiracy themes, while later criticizing Hitler's antisemitism as an obsessive idée fixe and distinguishing his doctrine from crude hatred.

### Nazi and intelligence connections

- German security reports on Evola exist; they record both suspicion and contact.
- He lectured in Nazi Germany and sought official racial collaboration.
- After July 1943 he traveled under German protection, entered the Rastenburg/RSI orbit, and worked in Vienna with confiscated Masonic/Jewish records connected to RSHA Amt VII.
- Claims that he held formal SS rank or was a senior operational spy were not substantiated.
- Claims that he was wholly rejected by Nazism ignore his later wartime utility; claims that he penetrated the innermost Nazi leadership exaggerate a marginal intellectual's access.

### Political violence and the postwar terrorist right

The 1951 FAR court acquitted Evola. No later court convicted him of terrorism, conspiracy, homicide, bombing, or reconstituting the Fascist Party. He died before the full judicial history of the Years of Lead unfolded.

At the level of intellectual transmission, however, **Ordine Nuovo**, Rauti, Graziani, Franco Freda, Paolo Signorelli, and other militants read or invoked Evola. Scholars such as Franco Ferraresi describe his doctrine as an “essential mortar” for generations of the radical right. Evolian concepts—warrior elite, depersonalized action, contempt for bourgeois morality, order, catastrophe as selection—could be and were read as legitimation for violence. Other passages recommended inner distance, a “rectification” from above, or rejection of chaotic activism. The documentary distinction is:

- **court finding:** no personal criminal responsibility established in the FAR case;
- **historical finding:** substantial ideological influence on milieus that produced political violence;
- **unproven allegation:** that Evola personally ordered the later bombings of the strategy of tension or acted as an intelligence controller. No such evidence was found.

### Drugs, occult practice, and psychological crisis

Evola acknowledged narcotics experimentation from the war until about 1925, a suicidal crisis, and magical/altered-state practices that brought him near breakdown. There is no documented lifelong addiction, psychiatric hospitalization, psychosis diagnosis, or drug-related criminal prosecution. Stories of supernatural feats within the Ur Group cannot be established empirically.

### Fraud, corruption, and money laundering

No documented case of commercial fraud, embezzlement, bribery, corruption prosecution, tax offense, or money laundering was found. The major controversies concern political ideology, racial propaganda, collaboration, confiscated archives, and influence on violent movements rather than financial crime.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. “Baron Evola”

1. **Story:** Evola was a hereditary Sicilian baron.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** sympathetic biographies, followers, publishers, journalists, and visual culture, especially after 1945.
3. **Evidence:** aristocratic family traditions are reported by Treccani; conclusive documentation of his personal legal title is lacking and some genealogical researchers dispute it.
4. **Against:** inconsistent contemporary use; Aleramo did not make the title central.
5. **Effect:** fused the man with his doctrine of caste and made “the Baron” an instantly recognizable aristocratic counter-image to mass democracy.

### 2. Dadaist turned anti-modern sage

1. **Story:** Evola exhausted Dada and passed beyond art into metaphysics.
2. **Appearance:** chiefly *Il cammino del cinabro* and disciple biographies.
3. **Evidence:** a real break in intensive artistic production occurred around 1922–1923.
4. **Complication:** he resumed painting and sold work in the 1960s; the phase structure is partly retrospective self-fashioning.
5. **Effect:** made apparent contradiction—avant-garde destroyer versus archaic Traditionalist—into an initiatic ascent.

### 3. The Buddhist sentence that prevented suicide

1. **Story:** while preparing suicide, Evola read a Buddhist teaching and realized that the desire for extinction remained attachment.
2. **Appearance:** his spiritual autobiography and later biographies.
3. **Evidence:** his own testimony; no independent witness or contemporaneous diary was located.
4. **Effect:** legitimized his later Buddhist scholarship as repayment of a personal debt and turned crisis into initiation.

### 4. The Ur Group magically changed Fascism

1. **Story:** Ur's ritual chain exerted occult force on Mussolini or caused political events.
2. **Appearance:** participant recollections, esoteric histories, hostile occult-Fascism narratives.
3. **Evidence:** Ur existed, performed coordinated practices, and some members intended metapolitical influence.
4. **Against:** no historical method can isolate supernatural causation; political outcomes do not demonstrate it.
5. **Effect:** placed Evola at the center of an “occult history” of Fascism disproportionate to his administrative power.

### 5. Mussolini's secret master or rejected outsider

1. **Story A:** Evola spiritually directed Mussolini and Fascist racism. **Story B:** the regime rejected him completely.
2. **Circulators:** hagiographers and sensationalists favor A; apologetic accounts often favor B.
3. **Evidence:** direct 1941 audience and endorsement, Farinacci platform, Bottai support; also suppression, surveillance, failed projects, no party membership.
4. **Finding:** he was a marginal insider and would-be radicalizer with episodic access, not Mussolini's controlling occult adviser and not a detached dissident.
5. **Effect:** both myths enlarge his autonomy—one as hidden power, the other as pure thinker.

### 6. SS initiate, SS officer, or man banned by the SS

1. **Story:** variants make Evola an SS initiate/officer, or say the SS prohibited all contact with him.
2. **Appearance:** occult-Nazi literature, polemic, and selective reading of German files.
3. **Evidence:** SS/SD reports distrusted his “reactionary” and Roman-aristocratic doctrine; later the German security research apparatus protected and employed/collaborated with him in Vienna.
4. **Against formal-rank story:** no reliable service record establishing SS rank was found.
5. **Effect:** the contradiction sustains both demonizing and exculpatory legend.

### 7. Injury while fearlessly walking through bombs

1. **Story:** he deliberately walked Vienna's streets during bombardment to test fate and was struck.
2. **Appearance:** Evola's account and disciple recollections.
3. **Evidence:** refusal to shelter and April 1945 spinal injury are consistently reported; precise projectile, date, and circumstances vary.
4. **Alternative rumors:** punishment for black magic, failed sexual magic, suicide attempt, or Soviet attack directed at him lack evidence.
5. **Effect:** the wheelchair became physical proof, for admirers, of his ethic of confronting destiny; enemies recast it as grotesque consequence.

### 8. Paralysis as occult failure or chosen immobility

1. **Story:** Evola could have been cured but psychically refused, or his paralysis expressed magical backlash.
2. **Appearance:** oral lore and sensational esoteric biographies.
3. **Evidence:** a grave spinal-cord lesion is medically sufficient; no reliable record proves a refused curative operation would have restored walking.
4. **Effect:** converted disability into metaphysical drama rather than bodily trauma.

### 9. Direct architect of the strategy of tension

1. **Story:** Evola designed or commanded Italian neo-Fascist terrorism.
2. **Appearance:** polemics after the bombings and “black mastermind” narratives.
3. **Evidence:** militants read him; he advised their milieu; some direct visitors later joined violent organizations.
4. **Against:** 1951 acquittal; no order, operational file, financing trail, or conviction tied him personally to later massacres.
5. **Effect:** made him a posthumous explanatory key for ideologically diverse terrorist cells; obscured distinctions between texts, organizations, state contacts, and individual operations.

### 10. Serrano as disciple receiving Evola's final secret

1. **Story:** Evola nearly disclosed an ultimate initiatic secret to Miguel Serrano.
2. **Appearance:** Serrano's later “El secreto de Julius Evola” and *Nuestro honor se llama lealtad*.
3. **Evidence:** Serrano visited the apartment in 1970 and remembered being called back.
4. **Against:** Serrano himself asks whether a secret was intended and says he does not know; no transmission document exists.
5. **Effect:** inserted Evola into Serrano's lineage of hidden Hyperborean authorities and linked Traditionalism to later Esoteric Hitlerism.

### 11. Death upright before the Janiculum and ashes in the eternal ice

1. **Story:** Evola died seated upright, facing the Janiculum, and his ashes entered Monte Rosa's glacier.
2. **Appearance:** follower testimony and commemorative literature.
3. **Evidence:** death at home and deposition by Del Ponte and David on Monte Rosa are well attested; exact last posture depends on witnesses.
4. **Effect:** completed a deliberately crafted solar/alpine life narrative: Rome, vertical posture, mountain, impersonal ice.

## Films, Documentaries, Interviews, and Archival Material

No canonical feature-film biography or universally accepted documentary of Evola emerged from the search. The audiovisual record is dominated by Italian television/radio features, conference recordings, ideological video essays, and interviews with biographers rather than archival footage of Evola speaking at length. Such works require provenance checks.

Important non-film archival resources include:

- **Archivio Centrale dello Stato, Rome, Polizia politica, fascicolo personale Julius Evola** — surveillance and regime reports.
- **Fondazione Julius Evola, Rome** — correspondence, papers, editorial stewardship, including Guénon letters.
- **German security records** reproduced in Cospito and Neulen, *Julius Evola nei documenti segreti del Terzo Reich*.
- **International Dada Archive, University of Iowa** — Dada publications and substantial holdings by/about Evola.
- **Fondazione Istituto Gramsci, Fondo Sibilla Aleramo** — notebooks and papers relevant to the Aleramo relationship and Roman esoteric milieu.
- **Ernst Jünger archive** — Evola's 1953 contact/letter.

## Death, Funeral, and Disposition of Remains

Evola died in Rome on 11 June 1974. Congestive/cardiac failure is the commonly reported cause. Accounts from his circle say he arranged to face the Janiculum at death, maintaining a vertical posture despite paralysis; the scene should be attributed to witnesses rather than presented as independently certified. He was cremated. In August 1974 Renato Del Ponte and Eugène David climbed Monte Rosa and deposited or scattered the ashes in a glacier crevasse. The remains are therefore not in a conventional grave and are not recoverable in any ordinary sense.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Italy

Evola's immediate institutional legacy was a small but durable network of editors, foundations, study centers, and neo-Fascist readers. The Fondazione Julius Evola preserved manuscripts, promoted editions, and defended his reputation. Edizioni Mediterranee kept major Italian works in print. The radical right divided over whether to follow electoral MSI politics, Rauti's cultural metapolitics, Freda's revolutionary synthesis, Roman pagan revival, or Evola's late apolitìa.

His influence on violent groups was real at the level of formation and vocabulary, but variable and nonexclusive: militants also drew on Fascist republicanism, Nazism, Catholic integralism, nationalism, Maoism, Third Worldism, and intelligence-linked anti-communism. Judicial responsibility for specific massacres cannot be assigned by a reading list.

### International reception

French, German, Spanish, Hungarian, and later English translations turned Evola from an Italian radical-right reference into a global author. **Alain de Benoist** and Nouvelle Droite circles read him selectively; **Aleksandr Dugin** translated *Heidnischer Imperialismus* into Russian in the early 1980s and integrated Traditionalism into Eurasianism; **Miguel Serrano** absorbed and mythologized his Aryan-Hyperborean and initiatic themes; occult-Nazi and white-nationalist subcultures placed him beside Savitri Devi. Some Hindu, Buddhist, Hermetic, masculinist, New Age, martial-arts, and countercultural readers consume isolated esoteric books without accepting his political system; the texts themselves nevertheless connect metaphysics, hierarchy, race, and politics across the corpus.

In the United States, English translations from Inner Traditions and later radical-right publishers greatly expanded his availability after roughly 1990. **Steve Bannon** referred to Evola in discussions of Traditionalism but is not accurately described as a systematic Evolian. Online alt-right culture made the monocled portrait and “Revolt Against the Modern World” slogan into memes and merchandise.

### Scholarship and museums

Academic study has moved from treating Evola chiefly as a source of postwar terrorism to examining the full archive: Dada, esotericism, race science, Italian-German exchange, gender, political religion, and globalization. Scholarship remains divided over classification—Fascist, superfascist, radical Traditionalist, Conservative Revolutionary—but current archival work generally rejects both the claim that he was Mussolini's secret master and the claim that he was politically innocent because he criticized Fascism.

The 2022 MART retrospective and 2025 catalogue raisonné reinserted him into histories of Italian abstraction and Dada. This recognition concerns genuine artistic significance; it neither erases nor proves the later political doctrine. The recurrence of controversy around exhibitions is itself part of his posthumous reception.

## Atlas Connections

### Aleksandr Gelyevich Dugin

- **[T1+] Documented fact:** Evola was an important written-about figure for Dugin. In 1981 or the early 1980s, Dugin translated the 1933 German version of Evola’s *Heidnischer Imperialismus* into Russian and circulated it in samizdat; he subsequently published essays interpreting Evola and incorporated parts of Evolian Traditionalism into his own political thought. This is a posthumous intellectual reception, not a personal meeting. [Andreas Umland’s historical study](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1016/j.euras.2010.04.008); [ETH Zürich research paper](https://www.files.ethz.ch/isn/20571/OP294.pdf)

### Annie Besant

- **[T3] Documented fact:** Evola discussed Besant’s leadership of the Theosophical Society and the promotion of Jiddu Krishnamurti as a “World Teacher” in *Maschera e volto dello spiritualismo contemporaneo* (1932 and revised editions). The engagement was entirely textual and generally critical; no meeting or correspondence is documented. [Google Books record and indexed text](https://books.google.com/books/about/Maschera_e_Volto_dello_Spiritualismo_Con.html?id=wZTLCQAAQBAJ); [Joscelyn Godwin’s historical study](https://hermetic.com/godwin/julius-evola)

### Blavatsky

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Evola repeatedly examined Helena P. Blavatsky’s Theosophy, especially in *Maschera e volto dello spiritualismo contemporaneo* (1932), criticizing its mediumistic and syncretic features while drawing on or paralleling parts of its prehistoric and cyclical cosmology. Blavatsky died in 1891, before Evola’s birth, so the connection is exclusively through her writings and their reception. [Joscelyn Godwin’s historical study](https://hermetic.com/godwin/julius-evola); [Google Books record and indexed text](https://books.google.com/books/about/Maschera_e_Volto_dello_Spiritualismo_Con.html?id=wZTLCQAAQBAJ)

### Dante Alighieri

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Evola interpreted Dante as an initiate and as a representative of the Ghibelline, imperial current, most explicitly in chapter 26 of *Il mistero del Graal e la tradizione ghibellina dell’Impero* (1937). This was Evola’s esoteric-political reading of Dante, not evidence of an initiatic lineage passing historically from Dante to Evola. [Discussion of Evola’s Dante interpretation](https://arktos.com/2023/06/24/dante-alighieri-and-the-philosophy-of-right/)

### Gabriele d’Annunzio

- **[T3] Documented fact:** Evola read d’Annunzio closely in adolescence and later wrote critically about the D’Annunzian “superman,” distinguishing it from the impersonal, transcendent type he wished to derive from Nietzsche and Tradition. Both men lived in Italy during 1898–1938, but the evidence reviewed supports one-sided literary engagement rather than a securely documented personal meeting. [Treccani biography of Evola](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/giulio-cesare-andrea-evola_(Dizionario-Biografico)/)

### F. T. Marinetti

- **[T5] Documented fact:** Evola entered Marinetti’s Italian Futurist circle during the First World War and exhibited at the National Futurist Exhibition organized at Palazzo Cova in Milan in April 1919. He later rejected Marinetti’s nationalism, industrialism, and anti-German wartime orientation. Contemporary artistic activity and Evola’s retrospective testimony support personal acquaintance, although the surviving evidence does not establish a close friendship. [Treccani biography of Evola](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/giulio-cesare-andrea-evola_(Dizionario-Biografico)/); [International Dada Archive](https://sdrc.lib.uiowa.edu/dada/dadas/evola.htm)

### Fichte

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Evola engaged Johann Gottlieb Fichte’s absolute idealism while constructing his own “magical idealism” in *Saggi sull’idealismo magico* (1925), *L’individuo e il divenire del mondo* (1926), and the two volumes on the “absolute individual” (1927–1930). His project attempted to turn philosophical self-positing into operative, transformative power and was an appropriation and revision rather than simple adherence to Fichte. [Treccani biography of Evola](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/giulio-cesare-andrea-evola_(Dizionario-Biografico)/); [University of Victoria study](https://dspace.library.uvic.ca/server/api/core/bitstreams/4324d561-ca42-44b7-ab14-dbad1f67f9e0/content)

### Freud

- **[T3] Documented fact:** Evola criticized Sigmund Freud’s psychoanalysis in *Maschera e volto dello spiritualismo contemporaneo* and later writings, regarding its interpretation of the unconscious and sexuality as reductive and “subpersonal.” This was one-sided written engagement; no correspondence or meeting is documented. [Google Books record and indexed text](https://books.google.com/books/about/Maschera_e_Volto_dello_Spiritualismo_Con.html?id=wZTLCQAAQBAJ)

### Gurdjieff

- **[T3] Documented fact:** Evola devoted critical discussion to G. I. Gurdjieff and his movement in *Maschera e volto dello spiritualismo contemporaneo*. He regarded aspects of Gurdjieff’s discipline and “awakening” more seriously than ordinary spiritualism but rejected the movement as an adequate initiatic authority. No reliable evidence of a meeting or correspondence was found. [Google Books record and indexed text](https://books.google.com/books/about/Maschera_e_Volto_dello_Spiritualismo_Con.html?id=wZTLCQAAQBAJ)

### Hegel

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Evola’s philosophical books of 1925–1930 engaged and attempted to move beyond Hegelian and post-Hegelian Idealism. He opposed what he considered the merely logical or historical resolution of the absolute and proposed instead an “absolute individual” possessing operative power. This is a textual-philosophical connection only. [Treccani biography of Evola](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/giulio-cesare-andrea-evola_(Dizionario-Biografico)/); [University of Victoria study](https://dspace.library.uvic.ca/server/api/core/bitstreams/4324d561-ca42-44b7-ab14-dbad1f67f9e0/content)

### Jiddu Krishnamurti

- **[T3] Documented fact:** Evola devoted a substantial section of *Maschera e volto dello spiritualismo contemporaneo* to Krishnamurti. He discussed both the Theosophical construction of Krishnamurti as “World Teacher” and Krishnamurti’s later rejection of that role, treating the latter with qualified respect. Fascist police reports alleging that Evola belonged to Krishnamurti’s Order of the Star were suspicions, not proof of membership or personal contact. [Joscelyn Godwin’s historical study](https://hermetic.com/godwin/julius-evola); [discussion of Evola’s Krishnamurti chapter](https://www.paulbrunton.org/publications/commentaries/beyond-the-cosmic-ladder/)

### Jung

- **[T3] Documented fact:** Evola repeatedly engaged Carl Gustav Jung’s psychology, archetypes, alchemical interpretation, and theory of the unconscious, including a 1950 article commonly titled “Lo svizzero Jung aggiorna Freud” and discussions incorporated into later editions of *Maschera e volto dello spiritualismo contemporaneo*. Evola considered Jung less reductive than Freud but rejected the psychologization of metaphysical and initiatic symbols. No correspondence or meeting is documented. [Google Books record and indexed text](https://books.google.com/books/about/Maschera_e_Volto_dello_Spiritualismo_Con.html?id=wZTLCQAAQBAJ)

### Mircea Eliade

- **[T3] Documented fact:** Eliade reviewed Evola as early as 1927, and the two maintained an intermittent correspondence from the interwar period through the 1950s. Evola reviewed and cited Eliade, helped connect his work with Italian publishers, translated some of his writing, and later contributed five articles to *Antaios*, the journal co-edited by Eliade and Ernst Jünger. Surviving Evola letters and Eliade’s journals document both cooperation and later estrangement. [Encyclopedia of Religion entry](https://www.encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/evola-julius); [Liviu Bordaș’s archival study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/369236062_The_difficult_encounter_in_Rome_Mircea_Eliade%27s_post-war_relation_with_Julius_Evola_-_new_letters_and_data_International_Journal_on_Humanistic_Ideology_IV_no_2_Autumn-Winter_2011_pp_125-158)
- **[T5] Documented fact:** Evola and Eliade met in Bucharest in 1937 in the circle of Nae Ionescu. Archival research also documents two postwar meetings in Rome, in May 1952 and April 1955. The evidence supports repeated personal meetings, but descriptions of an enduring close friendship overstate a relationship marked by caution and eventual rupture. [Persée scholarly article](https://www.persee.fr/doc/arss_0335-5322_1995_num_106_1_3134); [Liviu Bordaș’s archival study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/369236062_The_difficult_encounter_in_Rome_Mircea_Eliade%27s_post-war_relation_with_Julius_Evola_-_new_letters_and_data_International_Journal_on_Humanistic_Ideology_IV_no_2_Autumn-Winter_2011_pp_125-158)

### Mussolini

- **[T3] Documented fact:** Evola wrote repeatedly about Mussolini and Fascism from the 1920s onward, sometimes addressing the regime as a potential vehicle for pagan-imperial transformation and sometimes attacking its nationalism, Catholic accommodation, populism, and bureaucratic character. Mussolini also read or was briefed on *Sintesi di dottrina della razza* in 1941 and authorized support for Evola’s proposed Italian-German racial journal. [Treccani biography of Evola](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/giulio-cesare-andrea-evola_(Dizionario-Biografico)/); [Peter Staudenmaier’s archival article](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0022009419855428)
- **[T5] Documented fact:** Mussolini personally received Evola at the Palazzo Venezia in Rome in September 1941 to discuss *Sintesi di dottrina della razza*. Evola was later present in the German-controlled milieu surrounding Mussolini’s restoration in September 1943, but this does not establish a continuous advisory relationship or personal intimacy. [Treccani biography of Evola](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/giulio-cesare-andrea-evola_(Dizionario-Biografico)/); [Peter Staudenmaier’s archival article](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0022009419855428)

### Nietzsche

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Nietzsche was one of Evola’s formative adolescent authors and remained a major explicit interlocutor. Evola discussed the “death of God,” nihilism, eternal recurrence, the Übermensch, and the will to power, most extensively in *Cavalcare la tigre* (1961), while faulting Nietzsche for naturalism, individualism, and the absence of a fully transcendent foundation. [Treccani biography of Evola](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/giulio-cesare-andrea-evola_(Dizionario-Biografico)/)

### Oscar Wilde

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Evola identified Wilde among the writers who shaped his adolescent revolt against bourgeois life and conventional morality. The presently documented crossing is reception through reading and later autobiographical discussion; no direct contact was possible, since Wilde died in 1900 when Evola was two years old. [Treccani biography of Evola](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/giulio-cesare-andrea-evola_(Dizionario-Biografico)/)

### René Guénon

- **[T3] Documented fact:** Evola and Guénon conducted a substantial correspondence from approximately 1930 until Guénon’s death in 1951, following earlier public engagement with Guénon’s writings. Guénon contributed to Evola’s *Diorama filosofico*, while Evola translated, reviewed, and debated him on Vedānta, initiation, political action, and the relative authority of priestly and warrior functions. Published collections preserve numerous letters, but no securely documented personal meeting was found. [Published correspondence description](https://www.ilgiardinodeilibri.it/libri/__lettere-julius-evola-gue-non-libro.php); [Fondazione Julius Evola](https://www.fondazionejuliusevola.com/)

### Rudolf Steiner

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Evola examined Steiner’s Anthroposophy at length in *Maschera e volto dello spiritualismo contemporaneo*, criticizing its Christian-evolutionary structure and claims of supersensible investigation while also drawing on themes circulating through Anthroposophical associates in the Ur milieu. Steiner died in 1925; no personal meeting or correspondence is documented. [Joscelyn Godwin’s historical study](https://hermetic.com/godwin/julius-evola); [Google Books record and indexed text](https://books.google.com/books/about/Maschera_e_Volto_dello_Spiritualismo_Con.html?id=wZTLCQAAQBAJ)

### Giuseppe Tucci

- **[T3] Documented fact:** Evola participated in Tucci’s IsMEO publishing environment, contributing to *Asiatica* in 1940 and fifteen essays to its successor *East and West* between 1950 and 1960. A surviving letter dated 9 June 1952 shows Evola submitting an article directly to Tucci. This establishes written and institutional engagement; the available evidence does not justify calling Evola Tucci’s pupil or asserting a close friendship. [History of Evola’s IsMEO contributions](https://www.fondazionejuliusevola.com/contributi/saggi-su-julius-evola/julius-evola-ponte-tra-oriente-e-occidente/); [Treccani biography of Tucci](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/giuseppe-tucci_%28Dizionario-Biografico%29/)

### Miguel Serrano

- **[T5] Reported fact:** Serrano’s chronology and later recollections place a personal visit to Evola’s apartment at Corso Vittorio Emanuele II 197 in Rome in 1970. Serrano described a conversation with the wheelchair-bound Evola and a moment in which Evola called him back as he was leaving. The visit is credible self-report; Serrano’s suggestion that Evola might have intended to disclose a final secret is explicitly speculative. [Serrano chronology](https://www.miguelserrano.cl/site/vida-cronologia/1967-1970/); [Serrano’s later recollection](https://miguelserrano.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/The-Golden-Thread.pdf)
- **[T1+] Documented fact:** After Evola’s death, Serrano wrote about the encounter in “El secreto de Julius Evola,” published in *Hiperbórea* no. 2 (1992) and incorporated into *Nuestro honor se llama lealtad* (1994). Serrano appropriated Evolian themes within his own Esoteric Hitlerism, but this later synthesis cannot be attributed back to Evola as a shared doctrine. [Serrano bibliography](https://www.miguelserrano.cl/site/obra-bibliografia/1990-2000/); [Nuestro honor se llama lealtad](https://www.miguelserrano.cl/site/nuestro-honor-se-llama-lealtad/)
