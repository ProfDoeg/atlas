# Antoni Gaudí i Cornet: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier reconstructs Antoni Gaudí’s life from surviving academic records, institutional and architectural archives, contemporary reports, published recollections, later scholarship, and official heritage documentation. Much of his professional archive—drawings, models, correspondence, notes, and workshop records—was destroyed when the Sagrada Família workshop was attacked and burned on 20 July 1936. Consequently:

- **Documented fact** means supported by contemporary records, surviving documents, or strong institutional archival evidence.
- **Reported fact** means repeated by named witnesses or reputable later scholarship but not independently confirmed here from the original document.
- **Disputed claim** means substantial sources disagree.
- **Attributed work** means authorship is plausible but not securely established.
- **Legend or myth** means culturally significant but inadequately documented.
- **Inference** is explicitly identified and not presented as fact.

Dates for commissions may differ between design, licensing, construction, completion, and later alteration. Those distinctions are retained wherever possible.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Antoni Plàcid Guillem Gaudí i Cornet.
- **Common name:** Antoni Gaudí.
- **Spanish-language form:** Antonio Gaudí y Cornet; also Antonio Gaudí Cornet.
- **Family name pronunciation:** The Catalan surname is stressed on the final syllable: Gaudí.
- **Professional description:** Architect, architectural designer, furniture and decorative-arts designer, structural experimenter, and director of the Temple Expiatori de la Sagrada Família.
- **Associated movement:** Catalan *Modernisme*, although his mature work does not fit neatly within a single historical style.
- **Religious title:** Declared **Venerable** by the Roman Catholic Church in April 2025 following recognition of his “heroic virtues.” “God’s architect” or “architect of God” is a devotional and journalistic epithet, not a professional title.
- **Birth:** 25 June 1852, reportedly at approximately 9:30 a.m.
- **Birthplace:** **Disputed between Reus and Riudoms**, province of Tarragona, Catalonia, Spain. His baptism in Reus the next day is documented. Some later documents signed by Gaudí identify him as a son or native of Reus; Riudoms tradition and some witness testimony place the birth at the family’s Mas de la Calderera. No surviving civil birth record conclusively resolves the dispute.
- **Baptism:** 26 June 1852, Prioral Church of Sant Pere, Reus.
- **Death:** 10 June 1926, Hospital de la Santa Creu, Barcelona.
- **Cause of death:** Injuries sustained when struck by a Barcelona tram on 7 June 1926.
- **Burial:** Chapel of Our Lady of Carmel, crypt of the Sagrada Família, Barcelona.
- **Marital status:** Never married.
- **Known children:** None.
- **Common forms of address:** His associates reportedly called him *Anton*, *senyor Gaudí*, or *don Antoni*.
- **Nickname attached to principal work:** The Sagrada Família became popularly known as the “Cathedral of the Poor,” although it is technically an expiatory basilica rather than Barcelona’s cathedral.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and occupational background

Gaudí was the youngest child of:

- **Francesc Gaudí i Serra** (1813–1906), a coppersmith or boilermaker from Riudoms.
- **Antònia Cornet i Bertran** (1819–1876), from a Reus family also associated with metalworking.

The family’s repeated identification with coppersmithing became central to later accounts of Gaudí’s development. Gaudí and his interpreters connected the trade’s transformation of flat metal into three-dimensional vessels with his ability to imagine spatial form. This is credible as a formative environment, but the stronger claim that his architectural genius was essentially inherited from generations of coppersmiths belongs partly to retrospective mythology. The 2021–22 Museu Nacional exhibition explicitly challenged the reduction of his education to innate craft knowledge, emphasizing his formal architectural, scientific, and historical training.

The paternal line was rooted in Riudoms. The maternal family lived in Reus and operated a workshop there. A genealogy prepared for the Càtedra Gaudí survives in the Memòria Digital de Catalunya.

### Siblings

The surviving evidence identifies five children in the family, with Antoni the youngest. The siblings ordinarily listed are:

- Rosa Gaudí i Cornet.
- Francesc Gaudí i Cornet, who studied medicine and died young.
- Maria Gaudí i Cornet, who died in childhood.
- Francesc or another child bearing a repeated family name, also reported to have died before age five.
- Antoni Gaudí i Cornet.

The exact presentation and numbering of the children differs in secondary biographies, partly because of repeated names following childhood deaths. The secure point is that Antoni was the youngest and experienced several close family deaths.

His sister Rosa married Francesc Egea. Their daughter, **Rosa Egea i Gaudí**, later lived with Gaudí and his father. She died in 1912. Gaudí assumed responsibility for her after her parents’ deaths.

### Family losses

- His mother died in 1876.
- His physician brother Francesc died shortly afterward, commonly dated 1876.
- His sister Rosa died in 1879.
- His father died in 1906 at an advanced age.
- His niece Rosa Egea died in early 1912.

These losses gradually removed the family household around him. Later biographers often treat them as part of his movement toward solitude and religious concentration. The deaths are documented; their precise psychological consequences remain interpretive.

### Romantic relationships

Gaudí never married. The most frequently reported attachment was to **Josefa “Pepeta” Moreu**, a teacher connected with the Cooperativa Obrera Mataronense. Accounts say Gaudí fell in love with her and proposed marriage, but she rejected him or was already attached to another man.

- **Evidentiary label:** Reported fact with romanticized later elaboration.
- The relationship is accepted by major biographers, but details of a formal proposal, her exact response, and the assertion that this rejection caused Gaudí’s religious conversion are not securely documented.
- Stories of two additional rejected courtships circulate in biographies but are poorly substantiated.
- Claims that Gaudí’s lifelong celibacy establishes a particular sexual orientation are speculative; no reliable documentary evidence supports such a conclusion.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1852–1868

Gaudí divided his childhood between Reus and the family’s rural property near Riudoms, commonly called **Mas de la Calderera** or *el Maset*. Ill health reportedly kept him from ordinary physical activity for periods and gave him time to observe plants, animals, stones, and landscape forms.

The illness is usually identified as rheumatism or recurrent rheumatic pain.

- **Documented or strongly reported:** He suffered chronic physical complaints from childhood.
- **Uncertain:** A precise retrospective diagnosis is impossible.
- **Later narrative:** His enforced contemplation of nature supposedly supplied the entire vocabulary of his architecture. Nature unquestionably became central to his thought, but the childhood-illness-to-genius sequence is partly biographical compression.

He attended the school of the Piarist Fathers in Reus. He reportedly contributed drawings to a handwritten school periodical, *El Arlequín*. His academic performance was not uniformly exceptional; drawing and spatial subjects were stronger than some other areas.

With school friends **Eduard Toda i Güell** and **Josep Ribera i Sans**, he developed a youthful plan for restoring the ruined monastery of Poblet, around 1870. The project was imaginary or preliminary rather than an executable commission. Toda later became a diplomat, Egyptologist, collector, and a major restorer of Poblet.

The natural environment of the Camp de Tarragona, Mediterranean light, local masonry, agricultural forms, ironwork, and the family workshop remained recurring reference points in Gaudí’s later explanations of his art.

## Education and Formation, 1868–1878

### Move to Barcelona

Gaudí moved to Barcelona at approximately seventeen, in 1868 or 1869, initially for preparatory studies. He lived modestly and combined school with drafting work.

His documented education included:

- Preparatory scientific courses at the Faculty of Sciences, University of Barcelona.
- Provincial School of Architecture of Barcelona, entered formally in September 1873.
- Degree in architecture awarded 15 March 1878.

His studies were interrupted or prolonged by employment and military obligations. His academic record was irregular overall, but he achieved strong results in drawing, mathematics, and design projects.

### Military service

Gaudí was registered for military service during the Third Carlist War period. Because of ill health and administrative circumstances, his active service was limited and intermittent. Accounts differ over the exact number of days he served. No evidence indicates combat experience.

### Teachers and professional formation

Important teachers and senior architects included:

- **Elies Rogent**, director of the architecture school.
- **Joan Torras i Guardiola**, engineer and specialist in iron structures.
- **Lluís Domènech i Montaner**, teacher and later leading Modernista architect.
- **Francisco de Paula del Villar y Lozano**, architect for whom Gaudí drafted and with whom he worked at Montserrat.
- **Joan Martorell i Montells**, whom Gaudí regarded as a master.
- **Josep Fontserè i Mestre**, with whom he worked on the Parc de la Ciutadella.
- **Leandre Serrallach**, for whom he performed drafting work.

The famous remark attributed to Rogent at Gaudí’s graduation—roughly, “I do not know whether we have given this degree to a madman or a genius”—was published through later recollection. It is a durable legend but not a securely authenticated verbatim contemporary quotation.

### Student employment and projects

To finance his education, Gaudí worked in architectural and building offices. His pre-degree activities included:

- Drawings and hydraulic or ornamental work at the Parc de la Ciutadella under Fontserè.
- Work on the Ciutadella cascade, water tank, railings, and related details.
- Drafting for Villar, including work connected with Montserrat.
- Projects for Leandre Serrallach.
- A tram-line project from Montjuïc toward Can Tunis for the Padrós y Borràs concern.
- Designs for a cemetery gate, hospital, university assembly hall, monumental fountain, royal pier, and an exhibition pavilion.
- The youthful Poblet restoration scheme with Toda and Ribera.

### Formative intellectual influences

Gaudí’s education exposed him to:

- Medieval Catalan architecture.
- French structural rationalism, especially Eugène-Emmanuel Viollet-le-Duc.
- Gothic Revival theory.
- Orientalist, Mudéjar, Islamic, and Byzantine design.
- The Arts and Crafts movement and ideas associated with John Ruskin and William Morris.
- Contemporary engineering, iron construction, photography, natural science, geometry, and stereostatics.
- Catalan *Renaixença* history and symbolism.
- Catholic liturgy, Benedictine renewal, and later the writings associated with Dom Prosper Guéranger of Solesmes.

Direct borrowing is often difficult to isolate. Gaudí transformed rather than merely reproduced these sources.

## Early Career, 1878–1883

After qualification, Gaudí printed a professional card giving an office at Carrer del Call, Barcelona. His first independent municipal commission was for street lamps in the Plaça Reial and Pla de Palau.

Other early work included:

- A display case for glove maker Esteve Comella, shown at the Paris Universal Exhibition of 1878.
- Furniture for the chapel-pantheon at the Palacio de Sobrellano, Comillas.
- Work for the Cooperativa Obrera Mataronense.
- Designs for kiosks, public lavatories, electrical illumination, pharmacy interiors, altars, liturgical objects, and private furniture.
- Competition entries and unrealized civic schemes.

### Eusebi Güell

At the 1878 Paris exhibition, industrialist **Eusebi Güell i Bacigalupi** reportedly noticed Gaudí’s Comella display case. Whether this was their literal first contact or the emblematic beginning of a relationship arranged through Joan Martorell, it accurately represents the start of Gaudí’s most important patronage network.

Güell commissioned:

- Furniture for the Sobrellano chapel.
- Finca Güell gates and pavilions.
- Palau Güell.
- Projects at Garraf.
- The Colònia Güell church.
- Park Güell.
- Numerous smaller alterations, furnishings, and studies.

Their relationship was both professional and personal. Güell supplied capital, social access, and unusual artistic freedom. Gaudí supplied an architectural language through which the Güell family projected wealth, Catholicism, cultural ambition, and Catalan identity.

### Cooperativa Obrera Mataronense

Gaudí’s designs for the Mataró workers’ cooperative included factory structures, housing, a casino or social center, a flag, and service buildings. Only fragments were built or survive.

The commission connected him to the cooperative movement and to Salvador Pagès, and probably introduced him to Pepeta Moreu. It is often used to characterize young Gaudí as a utopian socialist.

- **Documented fact:** He worked for the cooperative and designed structures for it.
- **Reported or interpretive claim:** He personally adhered to utopian socialism.
- His later political and religious outlook was conservative Catholic and corporatist rather than socialist. No surviving party membership or systematic socialist writing is known.

## Chronological Life History

### 1883–1889: First major buildings and the Sagrada Família

In 1883 Gaudí received several decisive commissions:

- **Casa Vicens**, Gràcia, for tile manufacturer Manuel Vicens.
- **El Capricho**, Comillas, for Máximo Díaz de Quijano.
- Direction of the **Sagrada Família**, succeeding Francisco de Paula del Villar after Villar resigned following a dispute over the project and its financing.

Gaudí initially inherited a neo-Gothic crypt already under construction. He progressively transformed the scheme into a monumental symbolic basilica using increasingly experimental structure and geometry. On 28 March 1884 he signed his first official document as its architect.

Casa Vicens combined exposed brick, patterned ceramic, ironwork, plant motifs, and Islamic or Mudéjar references. El Capricho similarly used ceramic surfaces, towers, and calculated solar orientation.

Between 1884 and 1887 he created the Finca Güell entrance ensemble, including the celebrated wrought-iron dragon gate. The work brought together architecture, metalwork, mythology, and estate representation.

From 1886 to 1889 he built **Palau Güell** near the Rambla. Its parabolic arches, central hall, roofscape, ironwork, stone construction, and carefully orchestrated circulation announced his mature integration of engineering and decorative design.

In 1887 he began the **Episcopal Palace of Astorga** for Bishop Joan Baptista Grau. After Grau’s death and disputes with diocesan authorities, Gaudí resigned. Ricardo García Guereta completed the building in altered form in 1909.

In 1888–90 Gaudí took over the nearly built **Col·legi de les Teresianes**, adapting its constrained budget and existing structure into an austere brick building with parabolic arches.

### 1890–1899: León, Tangier project, crisis, and Casa Calvet

Gaudí designed **Casa Botines** in León in 1891–94 for textile businessmen Simón Fernández and Mariano Andrés. Its construction prompted criticism from local professionals who doubted the foundations and structural decisions; no structural failure followed.

A projected complex for **Franciscan missions in Tangier**, conventionally dated 1892–93, remained unbuilt. Its tower composition anticipated aspects of the Sagrada Família. Claims that Gaudí personally traveled to Tangier or Málaga to inspect the site are disputed; some chronologies explicitly call the journey undocumented.

Around 1894 Gaudí undertook an extreme Lenten fast that seriously weakened him. Bishop Josep Torras i Bages reportedly persuaded him to stop.

- **Documented core:** A dangerous fast and illness are reported by early biographers.
- **Interpretive claim:** This was a depressive or psychological crisis.
- No clinical psychiatric diagnosis can be established.

The **Güell wine cellars** at Garraf, built in the 1890s, were produced with Francesc Berenguer. Division of authorship remains debated.

Gaudí’s only conventional municipal prize came for **Casa Calvet**: Barcelona’s award for the best building completed in 1900. The façade is comparatively restrained, while interiors and furniture use organic, ergonomic forms.

### 1900–1906: Park Güell, Bellesguard, Mallorca, Casa Batlló

From 1900 Gaudí worked on several major commissions simultaneously:

- **Park Güell**, conceived as an English-style residential development.
- **Casa Figueras/Bellesguard**, invoking the medieval royal site associated with Martin I of Aragon.
- The First Mystery of Glory for the monumental rosary at Montserrat.
- Casa Miralles wall and gate.
- Restoration and liturgical reordering of Mallorca Cathedral.
- Casa Batlló.
- Can Artigas gardens and the Catllaràs chalet near La Pobla de Lillet.

Park Güell was planned for roughly sixty residential plots. Gaudí designed roads, viaducts, drainage, entrance buildings, market hall, stairs, and the serpentine plaza bench. Only two houses were built. Restrictive conditions, distance from central Barcelona, lack of transport, and weak buyer interest caused the real-estate project to fail.

This was a **commercial failure**, not an architectural or structural collapse. Güell’s heirs later sold the property to Barcelona, which opened it as a public park in 1926.

At Mallorca Cathedral, Gaudí moved the choir, improved sightlines and lighting, designed furnishings and baldachin elements, and collaborated with artists including Josep Maria Jujol. His relationship with cathedral authorities deteriorated; he left in 1914 before completing the program. Later alterations complicated attribution.

**Casa Batlló** was a radical remodeling rather than a wholly new building. Its bone-like façade, colored ceramic skin, enlarged interior court, roof form, ventilation, and joinery created an integrated interior and exterior composition. Interpretations include the legend of Saint George and the dragon, Carnival imagery, aquatic forms, and a memento mori. Gaudí left no definitive iconographic key, so exclusivist readings remain theories.

### 1906–1914: Casa Milà and structural experimentation

In 1906 Gaudí moved into the Park Güell show house with his father and niece. The house had been designed principally by Francesc Berenguer and signed or supervised by Gaudí because Berenguer lacked an architecture degree. Gaudí did not design it as his own residence.

Between 1906 and 1910 he built **Casa Milà**, known as *La Pedrera*, for Pere Milà and Roser Segimon.

Innovations included:

- A self-supporting stone façade.
- Structural columns permitting flexible floor plans.
- Underground vehicle accommodation.
- Complex courtyards for light and ventilation.
- A roofscape combining stair exits, ventilation towers, and chimneys.
- Integrated furniture, doors, plasterwork, and ornamental programs.

The building exceeded municipal height and volume regulations. The city treated parts as infractions and considered whether the work’s artistic character justified exceptions.

Gaudí clashed with the owners, particularly over costs, interior changes, and a projected monumental Marian group on the façade. The sculpture was abandoned, probably amid the anticlerical atmosphere surrounding the 1909 Tragic Week and the owners’ objections.

Gaudí sued over unpaid professional fees and ultimately obtained a judgment conventionally dated 1916 for approximately 105,000 pesetas. Accounts state he donated the recovered money to charity or the Sagrada Família.

- **Court finding:** Gaudí prevailed in the fee dispute.
- **Reported detail:** The exact disposition of all proceeds is repeated in biographies but should ideally be checked against the judgment and financial records.

Casa Milà was mocked in contemporary cartoons as a quarry, wasp nest, animal enclosure, or wreck. The nickname *La Pedrera*—“the stone quarry”—originated as ridicule before becoming the accepted name.

### Colònia Güell structural laboratory

Gaudí developed a hanging-chain or funicular model for the Colònia Güell church. Weighted strings represented loads in tension; inverted, the curves provided compression-only structural forms. Mirrors and photographs helped translate the model into drawings.

Only the crypt or lower church was constructed, principally in 1908–14. Work stopped after Güell’s business interests ceased funding the project. The surviving crypt became a laboratory for inclined columns, ruled surfaces, hyperbolic paraboloids, material integration, and the structural system later expanded at the Sagrada Família.

### Illnesses, deaths, and the turn toward exclusive religious work

- In 1906 his father died.
- In 1910 severe anemia or exhaustion led him to recuperate in Vic.
- In 1911 he suffered what contemporaries called Malta fever or brucellosis while in Puigcerdà and made a will on 9 June.
- His niece died in 1912.
- His collaborator Francesc Berenguer died in 1914.
- Bishop Torras i Bages died in 1916.
- Eusebi Güell died in 1918.

In 1910 Gaudí’s work was exhibited at the Société Nationale des Beaux-Arts in Paris. He did not develop an international practice from the exhibition, but it helped establish recognition beyond Spain.

By 1914 he stopped accepting substantial secular commissions and concentrated on the Sagrada Família. This was simultaneously a religious decision, a professional concentration, and a practical response to the loss of patrons and the ending of major civil commissions.

### 1914–1926: Sagrada Família and final years

Gaudí increasingly worked through large plaster models rather than complete conventional drawings. He altered designs as construction and experimentation advanced. Assistants translated his instructions into drawings and calculations.

Important collaborators at different stages included:

- Francesc Berenguer i Mestres.
- Josep Maria Jujol.
- Llorenç Matamala and Joan Matamala.
- Domènec Sugrañes i Gras.
- Francesc de Paula Quintana.
- Isidre Puig i Boada.
- Lluís Bonet i Garí.
- Cèsar Martinell.
- Ricard Opisso.
- Jaume Bayó.
- Joan Rubió i Bellver.
- Josep Canaleta.

Gaudí developed the Nativity façade as a densely sculptural proclamation of incarnation, nature, labor, and Christian doctrine. Human and animal casts were taken from life. The towers used ruled geometries, perforation, color, and sound-related concepts.

Because the temple depended on private donations rather than government or diocesan capital, progress was slow. Gaudí solicited funds personally. The recurring claim that he spent his entire personal fortune on the basilica is not demonstrably quantifiable. He undoubtedly lived austerely and directed his professional life toward the project.

By the 1920s his routine centered on Mass, the workshop, and visits to the Oratory of Sant Felip Neri, where his confessor and spiritual director was Father Agustí Mas i Folch. He eventually slept in or near the Sagrada Família workshop, though accounts differ on how continuously.

In November 1925 the first completed bell tower, dedicated to Saint Barnabas, was finished—the only Sagrada Família tower Gaudí saw completed.

## Residences, Travel, and Geographical Movements

### Principal residences

- **1852–late 1860s:** Reus, with periods at Mas de la Calderera near Riudoms.
- **From 1868/69:** Barcelona, at a succession of student lodgings and professional addresses.
- **1906–approximately 1925:** House in Park Güell, shared initially with his father and niece.
- **Final years:** Increasingly at the Sagrada Família workshop or associated quarters.

### Documented or strongly supported travel

- Repeated travel between Reus, Riudoms, Tarragona, and Barcelona.
- Excursions through Catalonia with the Associació Catalanista d’Excursions Científiques.
- Visits to southern France.
- Travel to Montserrat.
- Comillas and other Cantabrian locations for Güell/López-family commissions.
- Astorga and León for the episcopal palace and Casa Botines.
- Mallorca, especially Palma, during the cathedral restoration.
- Mataró for cooperative work.
- Garraf and La Pobla de Lillet for Güell projects.
- Vic and Puigcerdà for recuperation.

### Disputed or poorly documented journeys

- **Tangier and Málaga, approximately 1891–92:** Repeated in many biographies in connection with the Franciscan missions project; some specialist chronologies call the trip undocumented.
- **Paris 1878:** Gaudí’s display case was exhibited there, but evidence that he personally attended is less secure than the display’s presence.
- **Paris 1910:** His work was exhibited; reports differ on whether he attended.
- **New York:** No journey. The “Hotel Attraction” or New York hotel project attributed to him was never built and its documentation appeared later.

Gaudí was not an exile, emigrant, colonial administrator, or habitual international traveler. His career remained overwhelmingly Catalan and Spanish.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Professional structure

Gaudí was an independent architect rather than the proprietor of a large incorporated firm. His practice operated through:

- A personal office and workshop.
- Project-specific artisans and contractors.
- Draftsmen and architectural collaborators.
- Patronage relationships.
- The Sagrada Família’s governing construction board.
- Municipal permitting systems and ecclesiastical clients.

His workshop practice blurred modern distinctions between architect, engineer, sculptor, furniture designer, and building-site director.

### Patrons and financial networks

Major patrons included:

- **Eusebi Güell i Bacigalupi**, industrialist and later count.
- The Güell and López families.
- **Manuel Vicens i Montaner**, tile and stock-exchange businessman.
- **Pere Milà i Camps** and **Roser Segimon i Artells**.
- **Josep Batlló i Casanovas**, textile industrialist.
- Bishop **Joan Baptista Grau i Vallespinós**.
- **Claudio López Bru**, second Marquess of Comillas.
- The Calvet textile-manufacturing family.
- **Baldomer de Gili i Roig** and other private clients.
- The Associació Espiritual de Devots de Sant Josep and the Junta Constructora of the Sagrada Família.

### Wealth and property

Young Gaudí cultivated the appearance of a successful Barcelona professional: tailored clothing, restaurants, theater or opera attendance, cigars, and social participation. Later he became conspicuously austere.

He purchased the Park Güell house in which he lived, rather than designing a personal mansion. No substantial portfolio of businesses, shares, patents, or speculative investments has been established. His income came chiefly from professional fees.

Claims that he became literally destitute should be qualified. He chose severe personal simplicity and lacked money at moments—most famously during his 1924 detention—but remained an architect with institutional relationships and owned or had owned property. His appearance at death contributed to the “unknown beggar” story.

### Technologies and methods

Gaudí’s principal innovations were architectural methods, not patented inventions:

- Funicular hanging-chain models.
- Catenary and parabolic arches.
- Hyperboloids, helicoids, conoids, and hyperbolic paraboloids.
- Inclined and branching columns aligned with load paths.
- Three-dimensional stereostatic modeling.
- Modular geometric generation.
- Lightweight Catalan tile vaulting.
- Integrated ventilation, daylight, circulation, and acoustic design.
- Recycling of ceramic fragments in *trencadís* mosaic.
- Ergonomic furniture and door hardware.
- Structural façades independent of internal partitions.
- Life casting for sculpture.
- Use of photography and mirrors to analyze inverted models.

No verified patent under Gaudí’s name was identified.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Catalanism

Gaudí was closely associated with Catalan cultural revival:

- Member of the Associació Catalanista d’Excursions Científiques.
- Connected with the Cercle Artístic de Sant Lluc and Lliga Espiritual de la Mare de Déu de Montserrat.
- Used Catalan in professional and personal life.
- Incorporated Catalan historical, religious, and heraldic references in his work.
- Participated in religious and cultural acts with Catalanist meaning.

He was not known to hold elective office or formal leadership in a political party. Whether he should be called an autonomist, nationalist, separatist, traditionalist, or cultural Catalanist depends partly on later terminology. His documented conduct supports committed Catalan linguistic and cultural nationalism; stronger claims about a defined separatist program are less certain.

### Arrest in 1924

On 11 September 1924, under Miguel Primo de Rivera’s dictatorship, Gaudí attempted to attend a Mass at Sants Just i Pastor associated with the Lliga Espiritual and remembrance of the defenders of Barcelona in 1714. Police blocked entry.

Gaudí addressed the police in Catalan and refused demands to switch to Castilian. He was arrested and held for approximately four hours. The commonly reported fine was fifty pesetas. A priest reportedly supplied seventy-five pesetas: fifty for Gaudí and twenty-five for another detainee whom Gaudí wished to assist.

- **Evidentiary label:** Documented fact, with some dialogue preserved through later transcription.
- Exact wording of celebrated quotations should be treated cautiously unless checked against the surviving police or witness account.
- Claims that police severely beat him that day are less consistently supported than the arrest, language dispute, detention, and fine.

### Religion

Gaudí was baptized Catholic and became increasingly observant. His mature religious outlook included:

- Daily Mass and frequent confession.
- Marian devotion.
- Membership in Catholic cultural organizations.
- Study of scripture and liturgy.
- Interest in the Benedictine liturgical revival.
- Association with Bishop Torras i Bages, poet-priest Jacint Verdaguer, Jesuit Ignasi Casanovas, and the Oratorians of Sant Felip Neri.
- Fasting, personal austerity, and devotional work.

The common narrative describes a transformation from fashionable young professional or socialist sympathizer to ascetic Catholic. The development was real but gradual; Catholic, Catalanist, artisanal, and socially reformist concerns overlapped rather than replacing one another abruptly.

### Artistic theory

His surviving statements emphasize:

- Nature as a source of structural law rather than surface imitation.
- Geometry as a practical instrument of construction.
- Architecture as an ordered synthesis of structure, light, color, sculpture, craft, and liturgy.
- The Mediterranean as a culture of visual clarity.
- Originality as a return to origins.

Many famous quotations attributed to Gaudí were recorded after the fact by pupils, including Joan Bergós, Isidre Puig i Boada, Cèsar Martinell, and others. They represent valuable testimony but are not equivalent to stenographic transcripts.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Eusebi Güell i Bacigalupi

**Documented fact:** Patron, client, friend, and social intermediary from approximately 1878 until Güell’s death in 1918. Their partnership generated Gaudí’s largest connected body of secular work.

### Joan Martorell i Montells

**Documented fact:** Employer, recommender, collaborator, and acknowledged master. Martorell helped connect Gaudí to the Güell/Comillas circle and recommended him for major ecclesiastical work.

### Francisco de Paula del Villar

**Documented fact:** Early employer and immediate predecessor at the Sagrada Família. Their relationship is sometimes recast as rivalry, but the central documented issue was Gaudí’s succession after Villar’s resignation.

### Josep Maria Jujol

**Documented fact:** Important collaborator on color, collage, lettering, furniture, surfaces, and liturgical work, especially Casa Batlló, Casa Milà, Park Güell, and Mallorca. Popular accounts sometimes assign all decorative invention to Gaudí; scholarship has increasingly emphasized Jujol’s distinct contribution.

### Francesc Berenguer i Mestres

**Documented fact:** Longtime assistant and designer who never obtained the architect’s degree. Gaudí signed certain projects attributed substantially to Berenguer because legal submission required a licensed architect. Authorship disputes remain for the Garraf cellars, Park Güell house, and other works.

### Llorenç and Joan Matamala

**Documented fact:** Sculptural collaborators at the Sagrada Família. Their recollections became important sources for Gaudí’s working methods and personal habits.

### Bishop Joan Baptista Grau

**Documented fact:** Client and ally for the Astorga episcopal palace. Grau’s death deprived Gaudí of institutional protection and contributed to his withdrawal from the commission.

### Josep Torras i Bages

**Documented fact:** Bishop, Catholic Catalanist thinker, spiritual influence, and personal acquaintance. He reportedly intervened during Gaudí’s dangerous fast.

### Jacint Verdaguer

**Documented fact:** Poet-priest encountered through Catalanist and Güell circles. Both worked within the cultural world of Catholic Catalan revival, although claims of intimate friendship vary.

### Joan Maragall

**Documented fact:** Poet and correspondent. Surviving letters and recollections help document Gaudí’s intellectual circle.

### Pepeta Moreu

**Reported fact:** Unrequited romantic attachment connected to the Mataró cooperative. Later retellings magnified it into the decisive emotional event of his life without conclusive evidence.

### Clients with whom he quarreled

- **Roser Segimon and Pere Milà:** Documented financial, aesthetic, and legal conflict.
- **Astorga cathedral chapter or diocesan authorities:** Documented professional breakdown after Bishop Grau’s death.
- **Mallorca cathedral authorities:** Documented tensions and unfinished withdrawal.
- Gaudí’s authoritarian control of projects is frequently reported by collaborators and clients, but descriptions range from exacting professionalism to irascibility.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### Dedicated adversarial-search findings

Searches conducted across English, Catalan, and Spanish for combinations of Gaudí’s name with *controversy, scandal, allegations, lawsuit, fraud, accusations, conspiracy,* and *criticism*, including material from 2024–26, found no credible evidence that Gaudí was investigated or charged for fraud, corruption, money laundering, violent crime, sexual abuse, intelligence activity, or comparable criminal conduct.

The major substantiated controversies concern patronage, labor and class symbolism, architectural authorship, fees, municipal violations, politics, and posthumous completion.

### Colonial and slavery-linked patronage

Gaudí’s chief patron Eusebi Güell married Isabel López Bru, daughter of Antonio López y López, first Marquess of Comillas. Recent historical work describes Antonio López’s fortune as connected to Cuban colonial commerce, including enslavement and illegal slave trafficking, alongside shipping, tobacco, banking, and railways.

The Güell family’s larger wealth also derived from colonial and industrial networks. Palau Güell, Finca Güell, Comillas furnishings, Park Güell, and Colònia Güell therefore belong materially to a patronage system partly rooted in colonial capital.

- **Documented fact:** The family and commission network.
- **Documented historical finding concerning the patrons:** Colonial commerce and slavery-linked accumulation were significant within the López fortune.
- **Not established:** That Gaudí personally participated in slave trading, managed colonial enterprises, or was accused contemporaneously of doing so.
- **Current controversy:** Museums and scholars debate how prominently this source of capital should appear in interpretation of Gaudí and Catalan Modernisme.

### Industrial paternalism and labor

Colònia Güell joined worker housing, factory organization, social facilities, and a church within an employer-controlled industrial settlement.

- **Documented fact:** Gaudí designed the church for an industrial colony owned by his patron.
- **Historical interpretation:** Such colonies provided services while extending employer oversight over workers’ housing, worship, education, and social life.
- Claims that Gaudí designed the colony as an instrument of repression exceed the evidence; his surviving major contribution was the church, not total managerial control of the settlement.

### Casa Milà lawsuit and regulatory disputes

- Municipal officials found portions of Casa Milà exceeded approved limits.
- Gaudí fought with the Milàs over design changes, the abandoned Marian sculpture, costs, and fees.
- He obtained a favorable judgment in the fee case.
- There is no evidence of fraud by Gaudí; the dispute was contractual and aesthetic.

### Authorship and assistants

Gaudí’s fame has often obscured the creative labor of Jujol, Berenguer, Rubió, the Matamalas, craftspeople, builders, and later Sagrada Família architects.

- **Documented fact:** Gaudí directed large collaborative teams.
- **Disputed attribution:** Several smaller projects signed by Gaudí were substantially designed by unlicensed Berenguer; decorative work may be jointly attributable to Jujol or artisans.
- **No established fraud:** Signing an assistant’s plans within the period’s professional system raises authorship questions, but available evidence does not establish criminal misrepresentation.

### Religious and political iconography

Modern scholarship has read elements of the Sagrada Família as a conservative Catholic response to industrial conflict, secularization, anarchism, and revolutionary violence. The Portal of the Rosary includes a sculptural tempter associated with an Orsini bomb, connecting sacred admonition with the bomb attacks of late nineteenth-century Barcelona.

- **Documented fact:** The sculpture and contemporary political environment.
- **Interpretation:** Gaudí sought to resolve class conflict symbolically within Catholic social order.
- Labels such as “counter-revolutionary” describe a scholarly interpretation of the program, not a criminal accusation.

### Completion of the Sagrada Família

The most persistent controversy is whether post-1926 construction can authentically realize Gaudí’s design.

Problems include:

- Gaudí continually revised the project.
- Much of the workshop archive was destroyed in 1936.
- Surviving models were shattered and later reconstructed.
- Successors rely on photographs, publications, fragments, geometry, oral instruction, and extrapolation.
- Modern reinforced concrete, digital modeling, and contemporary engineering differ from Gaudí’s materials and process.

In 1965 architects, artists, writers, and critics—including Le Corbusier, Oriol Bohigas, Ricardo Bofill, Joan Miró, Antoni Tàpies, Camilo José Cela, Jaime Gil de Biedma, and Josep Maria Subirachs—signed or supported a public appeal opposing continuation in its then-current form. Some later revised their position.

- **Documented controversy:** Whether to stop, preserve as a ruin, continue literally, or continue creatively.
- **Institutional position:** UNESCO recognizes only Gaudí’s Nativity façade and crypt as the authentic component of the World Heritage property while describing continuation as based on studied evidence.
- **Unresolved question:** No documentary test can prove that every posthumous feature is what Gaudí would have built.

### Tourism and urban conflict

Gaudí’s works have become instruments of mass tourism and property branding. Park Güell access restrictions, neighborhood crowding, commercial reproductions, and planned Sagrada Família approaches have generated protests. In 2000 protesters reportedly threw stones at the Park Güell dragon as a symbol of touristification.

These are posthumous conflicts over the use of his legacy, not misconduct by Gaudí.

### Death-conspiracy theory

A recent Catalan nationalist article proposed reconsidering whether Gaudí’s tram death might have followed political hostility or aggression.

- **Theory:** His death may not have been a simple accident, given earlier confrontations with authorities.
- **Evidence for:** Documented Catalanist activity, the 1924 arrest, and reports of prior harassment.
- **Evidence against:** Contemporary accounts consistently describe a tram collision; no reliable evidence of deliberate targeting, conspiracy, order, or perpetrator has emerged.
- **Classification:** Unsupported theory, not an established investigation or historical finding.

### Supposed 1922 film appearance

In 2026 a researcher identified a bearded man appearing briefly in footage of a 1922 Barcelona wedding as possibly Gaudí.

- **Classification:** Unverified identification.
- The researcher herself requested expert confirmation.
- Social-media circulation converted a cautious possibility into confident assertion.
- No definitive authentication was established in the sources consulted.

## Illness, Diet, Accidents, and Psychological Claims

- **Childhood rheumatic illness:** Strongly reported; exact diagnosis uncertain.
- **Vegetarianism and restrictive diet:** Reported in biographies. His diet was sometimes medically directed and sometimes ascetic.
- **1894 fast:** Reportedly became life-threatening before Torras i Bages intervened.
- **1910 anemia/exhaustion:** Reported and associated with recuperation at Vic.
- **1911 Malta fever/brucellosis:** Reported; severe enough that he made a will.
- **Possible depression:** Later biographers use this label for episodes of exhaustion, bereavement, fasting, and spiritual crisis. No contemporary clinical diagnosis establishes a depressive disorder.
- **Addictions:** No credible evidence of addiction. Young Gaudí smoked cigars; later he lived austerely.
- **Temperament:** Witnesses described intensity, impatience, sarcasm, anger, warmth, humor, courtesy, and generosity in differing contexts. No reliable psychiatric diagnosis follows from these recollections.
- **Fatal accident:** Tram collision, 7 June 1926.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### “Madman or genius”

**Story:** Elies Rogent declared at Gaudí’s graduation that the school had awarded a degree either to a madman or a genius.

**Origin and circulation:** Early twentieth-century recollections and biographies; repeatedly reproduced by Gaudí institutions and popular histories.

**Evidence:** No contemporary transcript identified. Rogent did sign or participate in the academic process.

**Classification:** Plausible recollected remark, not verified verbatim.

**Reputational effect:** Established the archetype of Gaudí as visionary outsider recognized at the moment of qualification.

### Birth in Reus versus Riudoms

**Story:** Each town claims Gaudí.

**Evidence for Reus:** Baptism in Reus; maternal residence and workshop; later documents in which Gaudí described himself as from Reus.

**Evidence for Riudoms:** Paternal origin, Mas de la Calderera tradition, witness testimony, and reported statements by Gaudí identifying himself with Riudoms.

**Classification:** Unresolved birthplace dispute. Reus is conventionally listed; Riudoms remains a serious competing claim.

**Reputational effect:** Became a local identity contest and a symbol of the Camp de Tarragona’s claim on Gaudí.

### Child genius taught entirely by nature

**Story:** Sickly Gaudí learned everything by watching plants and inherited spatial genius from coppersmith ancestors.

**Evidence:** Childhood illness, rural experience, metalworking family, and mature praise of nature are supported.

**Against literal reading:** His architectural education, paid drafting work, study of historical sources, engineering, photography, geometry, and collaboration are extensively documented.

**Classification:** Hagiographic simplification built from factual elements.

### Pepeta Moreu caused his religious conversion

**Story:** Rejection by his great love drove Gaudí from fashionable society into mysticism.

**Evidence:** A romantic attachment is reported.

**Against:** His religious and political evolution was gradual and connected to multiple institutions, patrons, bereavements, and intellectual influences.

**Classification:** Romantic biographical myth or unproven causal claim.

### Gaudí as a penniless beggar

**Story:** He gave everything to the Sagrada Família, became indistinguishable from a beggar, and was abandoned after the tram strike.

**Evidence:** He dressed austerely, carried no identification, was not immediately recognized, and was taken to the public Hospital de la Santa Creu.

**Qualification:** Accounts do not prove that all passersby knowingly refused aid or that earlier private medical attention would certainly have saved him. He was transported to hospital and subsequently identified.

**Classification:** Factual core with moralizing amplification.

### Taxi drivers refused him

**Story:** Several taxi drivers refused to carry the apparently destitute injured man.

**Evidence:** Repeated in later popular narratives; details and number vary.

**Classification:** Reported tradition not securely established here from contemporaneous records.

### “There are no straight lines in nature”

This and similar aphorisms are widely attributed to Gaudí. Nature contains both curved and apparently straight structural phenomena, so the line is better read as a compressed artistic maxim. Exact wording and source frequently go uncited.

**Classification:** Attributed quotation; verify individually before treating as verbatim.

### Secret sacred geometry, Freemasonry, alchemy, or occult codes

Numerous popular books and websites interpret Park Güell and the Sagrada Família through Masonic, alchemical, Templar, Atlantis, or esoteric codes.

- Gaudí used complex geometry and rich Christian, Catalan, classical, and natural symbolism.
- Eusebi Güell’s intellectual interests and the period’s symbolic culture allow multiple readings.
- No reliable evidence establishes Gaudí as a Freemason, occult initiate, Templar successor, or designer of a hidden conspiratorial code.
- His mature public identity and practice were explicitly Catholic.

**Classification:** Speculative interpretation or conspiracy narrative unless tied to a specific documented source.

### Hotel Attraction in New York

**Story:** Gaudí designed an enormous futuristic New York hotel in 1908 for unidentified American clients.

**Evidence for:** Drawings and recollections publicized decades later, particularly through Joan Matamala.

**Problems:** Lack of normal contemporary commission records, client identity, contract, or submission.

**Classification:** Disputed attribution.

**Reputational effect:** Became a powerful image of Gaudí as skyscraper visionary and was revived in proposals after September 2001.

### Premonition that others would finish the basilica

Gaudí is frequently quoted as saying his client was not in a hurry, meaning God, and as planning construction so later generations could continue.

The long-term strategy is consistent with the project’s donation financing and his decision to complete the Nativity façade as a visible model. Exact aphorisms survive largely through disciples.

**Classification:** Reported statements consistent with documented practice, but not always verifiable verbatim.

## Death and Funeral

On Monday, 7 June 1926, Gaudí left the Sagrada Família area for his customary religious visit, generally identified as the Oratory of Sant Felip Neri. Near the intersection of Gran Via de les Corts Catalanes and Carrer de Bailèn or Girona—accounts differ slightly—he was struck by a number 30 tram.

He was unconscious, poorly dressed, and carried no conventional identification. A Gospel book was reportedly found on him. He was taken to the Hospital de la Santa Creu, then the principal charitable hospital for Barcelona’s poor.

His absence was noticed at the Sagrada Família. Friends and collaborators searched hospitals and identified him. He reportedly declined transfer after recognition, preferring to remain among the poor, although the precise wording assigned to him comes through recollection.

Gaudí died on 10 June 1926, aged seventy-three. Reported medical consequences include severe trauma, fractures, internal injury, and deterioration after the collision; the exact modern diagnostic formulation varies.

On 12 June an immense procession accompanied his coffin through Barcelona to the Sagrada Família. He was buried in the crypt with ecclesiastical and civil authorization. Contemporary commentary noted the paradox that a figure frequently mocked in life received extraordinary collective mourning.

## Works by Gaudí: Writings, Documents, Designs, and Attributions

Gaudí was not a conventional author. His primary oeuvre consists of buildings, projects, models, furniture, liturgical objects, and ornamental designs. Most “books by Gaudí” are posthumous editorial constructions.

### Authentic surviving writings and records

- **“Manuscrits de Reus” / “Manuscrito de Reus”** (c. 1873–78). Student notebook containing reflections on ornament, construction, the Christian church, the ancestral or family house, design, and a desk project. Genuine manuscript; not published as a book during his lifetime.
- **Academic projects and explanatory memoranda** (1870s). Genuine student material, fragmentary.
- **Municipal applications, plans, specifications, certificates, and technical reports** for individual buildings. Genuine professional documents; dispersed among Barcelona, Reus, UPC, COAC, Sagrada Família, and other archives.
- **Article on the decorative-arts exhibition at the Institut del Foment del Treball Nacional**, published in *La Renaixensa*, 1–2 February 1881. Genuine journalistic/art-critical text.
- **Letters and notes**, including correspondence associated with Joan Maragall, clergy, clients, collaborators, and municipal authorities. Genuine but incomplete and usually published posthumously.
- **1924 detention note requesting payment of his fine.** Reported surviving documentary item.
- **Will**, made 9 June 1911 during illness; legal document.
- **Professional card**, 1878.
- **Dedication to the Orfeó Català**, 1922, with polychromy by Jujol.

### Posthumous edited collections

- *Manuscritos, artículos, conversaciones y dibujos* (edited collection, 1982). Not a lifetime book; assembles authentic documents and attributed recollections.
- *Escritos y documentos* / *Escrits i documents*, edited by Laura Mercader, 2002. Scholarly compilation of manuscripts, technical texts, academic documentation, biographical records, and correspondence.
- *El pensament de Gaudí*, edited from sayings recorded by disciples. Posthumous compilation; individual statements vary in documentary proximity.
- *Conversaciones de Gaudí* and similarly titled collections. Based on recollections by pupils and associates, not transcripts authorized as a book by Gaudí.
- Albums or guides to the Sagrada Família issued during or shortly after his lifetime include photographs and explanations reflecting workshop intentions, but should not automatically be treated as solely authored by Gaudí.

### Lost corpus

Destroyed or badly damaged in 1936:

- Large plaster models.
- Project drawings.
- Workshop notebooks.
- Sculptural studies.
- Photographs.
- Correspondence and business records.
- Furniture and personal material.

Reconstructed models are historically significant but are post-1936 restorations assembled from fragments, photographs, measurements, testimony, and later technical interpretation.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Destruction and continuity

Ten years after his death, revolutionary forces attacked and burned the Sagrada Família workshop. Anti-clerical violence, not a narrowly documented personal vendetta against Gaudí, explains the immediate setting. Models were smashed and tombs or religious spaces damaged.

Assistants and later architects recovered model fragments. Francesc de Paula Quintana restored the burned crypt; Domènec Sugrañes, Isidre Puig i Boada, Lluís Bonet i Garí, Francesc Cardoner, Jordi Bonet, Jordi Faulí, and others successively directed or shaped construction.

### Mid-century eclipse and revival

For decades, orthodox modernist criticism treated Gaudí as an eccentric decorator, regional curiosity, or formal excess. His revival involved:

- Salvador Dalí’s surrealist advocacy.
- International exhibitions.
- The 1952 centenary.
- The 1957 Museum of Modern Art exhibition.
- Friends of Gaudí organizations.
- Scholarship by Collins, Cirlot, Martinell, Bassegoda, and later structural researchers.
- Tourism and photographic publication.
- Japanese reception, reinforced by Teshigahara’s 1984 film.

### UNESCO

Park Güell, Palau Güell, and Casa Milà entered the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1984. The designation was expanded in 2005 to include:

- Casa Vicens.
- Casa Batlló.
- The Nativity façade and crypt of the Sagrada Família.
- The crypt of the Colònia Güell.

UNESCO recognizes Gaudí’s contribution to architecture and building technology and his synthesis of Arts and Crafts, Symbolism, Expressionism, Rationalism, Catalan traditions, and industrial-era innovation.

### Influence

Documented or widely recognized fields of influence include:

- Expressionist architecture.
- Organic architecture.
- Shell and tensile-form experimentation.
- Biomimetic and computational design.
- Parametric modeling.
- Integrated architecture and decorative arts.
- Structural form-finding.
- Mosaic and recycled-material practices.
- Theme-park, fantasy, and cinematic visual design.
- Contemporary Barcelona’s global identity.

Direct stylistic followers have been fewer than admirers because his method depended upon site, workshop craft, iterative modeling, patronage, and personal supervision.

### Beatification cause

An association promoting Gaudí’s beatification was founded in 1992. The diocesan cause gathered testimony and documentary evidence concerning his religious life.

In April 2025 Pope Francis authorized promulgation of a decree recognizing that Gaudí practiced theological, cardinal, and associated virtues to a heroic degree. He thereby received the Catholic title **Venerable**.

- **Formal ecclesiastical finding:** Recognition of heroic virtues.
- **Not yet established:** Beatification or canonization.
- A recognized miracle attributed to his intercession would ordinarily be required for beatification unless a different canonical route applied.
- The cause also reinforces a devotional image that scholars must distinguish from ordinary historical biography.

### Competing modern appropriations

Gaudí has been represented as:

- A universal Spanish genius.
- A specifically Catalan nationalist.
- A Catholic mystic and prospective saint.
- A structural rationalist.
- A proto-surrealist.
- A proto-environmentalist.
- A bourgeois architect financed by colonial-industrial capital.
- A socially concerned designer.
- An anti-modern medievalist.
- A precursor of digital architecture.

Each representation selects authentic elements but becomes misleading when treated as exhaustive.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are included. Mere contemporaneity, presence in Barcelona, or stylistic similarity is excluded.

### John Ruskin

- **Nature of connection:** Documented intellectual influence at the level of the wider Gothic Revival and Arts and Crafts environment; direct personal meeting is not known.
- **Dates/places:** Gaudí encountered Ruskinian architectural ideas during his Barcelona education and early career in the 1870s and 1880s.
- **Evidence label:** **Reported/documented intellectual influence**, but not a documented personal relationship.
- Ruskin’s defense of Gothic craft, moralized construction, natural ornament, and integrated artisan labor formed part of the theoretical culture from which Gaudí emerged. The extent of Gaudí’s direct reading, as opposed to mediation through teachers and patrons, is not fully documented.

### William Morris

- **Nature of connection:** Influence through the Arts and Crafts movement and the ideal of integrated architecture, craft, furniture, and ornament.
- **Dates/places:** Indirect, principally from the 1870s onward.
- **Evidence label:** **Documented movement-level influence; no documented meeting or correspondence.**

### Wilhelm Richard Wagner

- **Nature of connection:** Cultural and possibly programmatic influence within the Güell circle; Wagnerian ideas of total artwork and recurring symbolic motifs have been applied to Park Güell.
- **Dates/places:** Barcelona, principally c. 1890–1914.
- **Evidence label:** **Reported intellectual/artistic connection**, not a personal relationship. Gaudí and Wagner never met.
- Some Park Güell research situates the project within a Wagnerian cultural network, but specific iconographic interpretations remain debated.

### Pablo Ruiz Picasso

- **Nature of connection:** Shared Barcelona artistic environment and later hostile critical reception.
- **Dates/places:** Picasso lived in Barcelona during Gaudí’s mature career; direct substantive collaboration or friendship is not documented.
- **Evidence label:** **Documented critical association; reported quotation.**
- Picasso is repeatedly credited with a hostile remark consigning Gaudí and the Sagrada Família “to hell.” The sentiment is well established in reception history, though the precise wording and context require caution.

### Le Corbusier

- **Nature of connection:** Posthumous admirer, critic of continuation, and participant in Gaudí reception.
- **Dates/places:** Le Corbusier examined Gaudí’s work during a Barcelona visit in May 1928, two years after Gaudí’s death. In 1965 he was associated with the manifesto opposing continuation of the Sagrada Família.
- **Evidence label:** **Documented posthumous reception; no meeting.**
- His relation to Gaudí was not simply hostile: sources also record admiration for Gaudí’s plastic and structural work.

### George Orwell

- **Nature of connection:** Hostile posthumous literary reception of the Sagrada Família.
- **Dates/places:** Barcelona, 1936–37; *Homage to Catalonia*, 1938.
- **Evidence label:** **Documented posthumous criticism; no personal connection.**
- Orwell called the building extremely ugly and remarked that anarchists had shown poor taste by not destroying it. The workshop had in fact been attacked in 1936, although the basilica survived.

### Agustí Querol i Subirats

- **Nature of connection:** Both belonged to the Catalan/Spanish monumental-art world of the period.
- **Evidence label:** **No sufficiently documented personal crossing found.**
- No meeting, correspondence, collaboration, or rivalry was established in the consulted material.

### Achille-Claude Debussy, Alphonse Mucha, Auguste Rodin, Alexandre Gustave Eiffel, Charles Rennie Mackintosh, Frank Lloyd Wright, Gustav Klimt, Antoni Gaudí’s other roster contemporaries

- **Finding:** No reliable direct meeting, correspondence, collaboration, or explicitly acknowledged personal influence was established from the sources consulted.
- Comparisons between Gaudí and these figures are common in stylistic literature but do not by themselves satisfy the atlas standard.
- A supposed Gaudí–Rodin encounter was not confirmed.
- Gaudí’s work was displayed at Paris exhibitions in 1878 and 1910, but shared exhibition geography does not establish contact with Eiffel, Mucha, Rodin, or Debussy.

## Compact Chronology

- **1852, 25 June:** Born, conventionally in Reus; Riudoms remains a competing claim.
- **1852, 26 June:** Baptized at Sant Pere, Reus.
- **1860s:** Educated in Reus; recurrent ill health; stays at Mas de la Calderera.
- **c. 1867:** Associated with school periodical *El Arlequín*.
- **1868/69:** Moves to Barcelona.
- **c. 1870:** Poblet restoration project with Eduard Toda and Josep Ribera.
- **1873:** Enters Barcelona School of Architecture.
- **1872–78:** Works under Fontserè, Villar, Martorell, and other architects.
- **1876:** Mother and physician brother die.
- **1878, 15 March:** Receives architecture degree.
- **1878:** Plaça Reial lamps; Comella display case; begins Mataró cooperative work; association with Güell begins.
- **1879:** Sister Rosa dies.
- **1881:** Publishes decorative-arts article in *La Renaixensa*.
- **1883:** Begins Casa Vicens and El Capricho; assumes direction of Sagrada Família.
- **1884:** First formal signature as Sagrada Família architect; Finca Güell work begins.
- **1886–89:** Builds Palau Güell.
- **1887–93:** Episcopal Palace, Astorga; resigns after Bishop Grau’s death and institutional disputes.
- **1888–90:** Col·legi de les Teresianes.
- **1891–94:** Casa Botines.
- **1892–93:** Tangier Franciscan mission project; journey itself disputed.
- **1894:** Dangerous fast and illness.
- **1895–97:** Güell wine cellars.
- **1898–1904:** Casa Calvet.
- **1900:** Casa Calvet receives Barcelona municipal prize; Park Güell and Bellesguard projects underway.
- **1900–14:** Park Güell.
- **1903–14:** Mallorca Cathedral intervention.
- **1904–06:** Casa Batlló.
- **1906:** Moves to Park Güell house; father dies.
- **1906–10:** Casa Milà.
- **1908–14/16:** Colònia Güell crypt.
- **1909:** Sagrada Família Schools.
- **1910:** Paris exhibition; anemia/exhaustion and recuperation at Vic.
- **1911:** Malta fever at Puigcerdà; executes will on 9 June.
- **1912:** Niece Rosa Egea dies.
- **1914:** Ends major secular practice; devotes himself to Sagrada Família.
- **1916:** Reported favorable judgment in Casa Milà fee lawsuit; Torras i Bages dies.
- **1918:** Eusebi Güell dies.
- **1920s:** Lives and works increasingly at Sagrada Família workshop.
- **1924, 11 September:** Arrested after confronting police in Catalan at Sants Just i Pastor; fined.
- **1925:** Saint Barnabas tower completed; first and only Sagrada Família tower completed during his life.
- **1926, 7 June:** Struck by tram in Barcelona.
- **1926, 10 June:** Dies at Hospital de la Santa Creu.
- **1926, 12 June:** Public funeral and burial in Sagrada Família crypt.
- **1936, 20 July:** Workshop attacked and burned; archive and models largely destroyed.
- **1952:** Centenary commemorations accelerate revival.
- **1957:** Major Museum of Modern Art exhibition.
- **1963:** Casa Museu Gaudí opens.
- **1965:** International manifesto disputes continuation of Sagrada Família.
- **1984:** Park Güell, Palau Güell, and Casa Milà inscribed by UNESCO; Teshigahara film released.
- **1992:** Association promoting beatification established.
- **2005:** UNESCO listing expanded to seven Gaudí components.
- **2010:** Sagrada Família consecrated as a basilica by Benedict XVI.
- **2021–22:** Major MNAC/Musée d’Orsay exhibition reassesses Gaudí beyond conventional mythology.
- **2025, April:** Catholic Church recognizes heroic virtues; Gaudí declared Venerable.
- **2026:** Centenary of death stimulates new biographies, exhibitions, disputed film identification, political reinterpretation, and renewed controversy over completion and ownership of his legacy.
