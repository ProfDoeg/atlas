# Maslama ibn Qasim al-Qurtubi: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

Maslama ibn Qāsim al-Qurṭubī is poorly documented by modern biographical standards. Most information about his life survives in Arabic biographical dictionaries, especially Ibn al-Faraḍī’s *Taʾrīkh ʿulamāʾ al-Andalus*, supplemented by later authorities including Ibn Ḥazm, al-Dhahabī, Ibn Ḥajar, and manuscript annotations. These sources preserve a remarkably detailed itinerary and many teachers but almost nothing about his household, finances, or daily life.

A second body of evidence comes from the *Rutbat al-ḥakīm* and *Ghāyat al-ḥakīm*, two anonymous or pseudonymously transmitted works that Maribel Fierro attributed to Maslama in 1996. That identification has since won broad scholarly acceptance, particularly through research by Godefroid de Callataÿ, Sébastien Moureau, and Wilferd Madelung, but it remains a reconstruction rather than an explicit authorial signature preserved in an autograph manuscript.

Evidentiary labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** directly attested in early biographical evidence, an identified manuscript, or the work itself.
- **Reported fact:** transmitted by a later source relying on earlier material.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** a conclusion derived from converging textual, chronological, and biographical evidence.
- **Disputed attribution:** supported by scholarship but not beyond conceivable challenge.
- **Allegation:** an adverse claim reported by a source without demonstration.
- **Legend or dream narrative:** a story transmitted as meaningful biography but not independently verifiable.
- **Unsupported modern claim:** a recent assertion for which the consulted evidence is insufficient.

## Basic Identifying Information

### Name

**Full recorded name:** Abū al-Qāsim Maslama ibn al-Qāsim ibn Ibrāhīm ibn ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ḥātim al-Qurṭubī al-Andalusī al-Zayyāt.

Arabic:

**أبو القاسم مسلمة بن القاسم بن إبراهيم بن عبد الله بن حاتم القرطبي الأندلسي الزيات**

Recorded forms include:

- Maslama ibn Qāsim al-Qurṭubī
- Maslama ibn al-Qāsim al-Qurṭubī
- Maslama ibn Qāsim al-Andalusī
- Abū al-Qāsim Maslama
- Maslama al-Qurṭubī
- al-Ḍarīr, “the blind man,” in references made after his loss of sight
- al-Zayyāt, probably an occupational or familial designation connected with oil, although no secure evidence establishes whether Maslama personally traded in oil

### Important disambiguation

He was not the astronomer and mathematician:

**Abū al-Qāsim Maslama ibn Aḥmad al-Majrīṭī**, who died around 395–398 AH/1004–1007 CE.

Both men were Andalusian, Cordoban-associated scholars named Maslama, used the *kunya* Abū al-Qāsim, and were called al-Qurṭubī or al-Andalusī in some sources. This near-identity of names produced one of the most durable authorship confusions in medieval intellectual history.

### Birth

- **Islamic date:** usually given as 293 AH.
- **Common Era equivalent:** approximately 905–906 CE; some modern notices give 906 or 908.
- **Place:** Córdoba, al-Andalus.
- **Status:** **reported fact**, calculated primarily from the statement that he died aged sixty in 353 AH. Exact conversion depends on whether “sixty” means lunar years and whether it is exact or approximate.

### Death

- **Date:** Monday, eight nights remaining in Jumādā I, 353 AH.
- This is generally converted to **23 May 964 CE**.
- **Place:** Córdoba.
- **Age:** reported as sixty lunar years.
- **Cause:** not recorded.

### Principal occupations

- Hadith scholar and transmitter
- Biographer of hadith transmitters
- Compiler or historian
- Extensive scholarly traveler
- Practitioner or expert in charms and *nīranjāt*
- According to the prevailing modern attribution: alchemist, theorist of occult sciences, and compiler of astral magic

No governmental appointment, judgeship, salaried teaching post, court office, military position, or commercial enterprise is securely documented.

## Family and Ancestry

The paternal genealogy preserved in biographical sources runs:

**Maslama → al-Qāsim → Ibrāhīm → ʿAbd Allāh → Ḥātim.**

This is the full extent of securely reconstructable ancestry.

No consulted source identifies:

- his mother;
- the professions or social rank of his parents;
- siblings;
- a wife or wives;
- partners;
- children;
- descendants;
- inherited property;
- household slaves or dependants;
- a family scholarly dynasty.

The designation *al-Zayyāt* may suggest a family or occupational association with oil dealing, but treating Maslama as an active oil merchant would go beyond the evidence.

## Childhood and Early Life

No narrative of Maslama’s childhood survives.

He grew up in Córdoba during the emirate and early caliphate of the Umayyad ruler ʿAbd al-Raḥmān III. Córdoba was then a major center of Mālikī jurisprudence, hadith study, court culture, medicine, astronomy, and book collection.

The earliest identifiable phase of his education consisted of hearing hadith in al-Andalus. Ibn al-Faraḍī’s notice lists among his Andalusian teachers:

- Muḥammad ibn ʿUmar ibn Lubāba
- Abū Ḥafṣ ibn Abī Tammām
- Aḥmad ibn Khālid
- Muḥammad ibn Qāsim
- Muḥammad ibn ʿAbd Allāh ibn Qāsim
- ʿAbd Allāh ibn Yūsuf
- Muḥammad ibn Zakariyyāʾ
- Qāsim ibn Aṣbagh
- Sīd Abīhi ibn al-ʿĀṣī al-Murādī al-Ishbīlī

The surviving lists establish participation in formal hadith-transmission networks. They do not reveal whether he studied in a single institution; tenth-century Andalusian scholarly education was normally organized through individual masters, mosque sessions, reading circles, and authorized transmission rather than a university in the modern sense.

His later works, if the attribution is accepted, demonstrate training or self-education well beyond hadith: philosophy, arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, astrology, natural science, alchemy, pharmacology, talismanic construction, and the literature associated with Hermetic, pseudo-Aristotelian, Sabian, Ismaili, and Jābirian traditions.

## Education and Formation

### Andalusian hadith formation

**Documented fact:** Maslama heard extensively from Cordoban and other Andalusian authorities before traveling east.

Qāsim ibn Aṣbagh was especially important in the Andalusian transmission of eastern hadith scholarship. Maslama’s exposure to this circle helps explain his later ambition to compile biographical works on transmitters omitted by al-Bukhārī.

### Departure on the *riḥla*

Ibn al-Faraḍī says Maslama departed eastward **“before [the year] twenty”**, conventionally understood as before 320 AH/932–933 CE.

Modern reconstruction places the major journey through the early and middle 930s. He was therefore probably in his twenties when he left al-Andalus.

### The eastern itinerary

The itinerary below is not a securely dated, sequential travel diary. It is reconstructed from places in which biographical dictionaries say he heard or wrote from particular teachers.

#### Qayrawān

Teachers included:

- Aḥmad ibn Mūsā, known as Ibn al-Tammār
- ʿAbd Allāh ibn Muḥammad ibn Faṭīs
- ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masrūr

This stop placed Maslama in a major Mālikī intellectual center under Fatimid political rule.

#### Tripoli

He heard from:

- Ṣāliḥ ibn Aḥmad ibn Ṣāliḥ al-Kūfī, associated with the family of the hadith critic Aḥmad ibn ʿAbd Allāh al-ʿIjlī

#### Crete

He heard from:

- Aḥmad ibn Muḥammad ibn Khalaf
- Yaḥyā ibn ʿUthmān al-Andalusī, an Andalusian then resident in Crete

The dating is historically consequential. The Muslim emirate of Crete fell to Byzantine conquest in 961, after Maslama’s journey.

#### Alexandria

He heard from:

- Ibn Abī Maṭar

#### Egypt, probably Fusṭāṭ and its scholarly environment

Teachers included:

- Muḥammad ibn Zabān or Ibn Abān al-Ḥaḍramī—the manuscripts and later notices vary
- Abū Jaʿfar Aḥmad ibn Muḥammad al-Ṭaḥāwī
- Abū al-Ṭāhir al-ʿAllāf
- Muḥammad ibn ʿAbd Allāh al-Bahrānī

Al-Ṭaḥāwī was the prominent Egyptian jurist and hadith scholar. Contact with him is among Maslama’s most significant documented scholarly relationships.

#### al-Qulzum

At the head of the Red Sea, Maslama heard from:

- Muḥammad ibn Aḥmad, the local judge
- Muḥammad ibn ʿAbd Allāh, known as Ghassān
- Muḥammad ibn ʿAbd Allāh ibn Qunqul al-Qulzumī
- Sulaymān ibn Muḥammad ibn Daws al-Mālikī

#### Jeddah

He heard from:

- ʿAbd Allāh ibn Aḥmad ibn Ḥamawayh al-Janābī

#### Mecca

Teachers included:

- Muḥammad ibn Ibrāhīm al-Daybulī
- ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn Muḥammad ibn ʿAbd Allāh al-Muqriʾ
- Abū Jaʿfar al-ʿUqaylī
- Abū Saʿīd ibn al-Aʿrābī
- Muḥammad ibn al-Muʾammal al-ʿAdawī

Abū Jaʿfar al-ʿUqaylī was the author of an influential biographical work on weak transmitters. Maslama’s later interest in transmitter criticism belongs to the same scholarly field.

Abū Saʿīd ibn al-Aʿrābī was associated with early Sufi transmission as well as hadith. Modern scholars have treated this encounter as a possible channel for Maslama’s exposure to eastern mystical traditions, but the content of their interaction is not recorded.

#### Basra

Teachers included:

- Abū Rawq al-Hazzānī
- Abū ʿAlī al-Luʾluʾī, Muḥammad ibn Aḥmad
- Muḥammad ibn ʿAlī al-Zaʿfarānī
- Aḥmad ibn Muḥammad ibn Sālim al-Tustarī
- Qāsim ibn Muṭarrif al-Qaṭṭān, according to de Callataÿ and Moureau’s reconstruction

Basra is central to the disputed history of the *Rasāʾil Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ*. Maslama’s presence there makes his early access to material connected with the Brethren of Purity possible.

Some modern accounts go further, claiming that he “joined” the Brethren, helped revise their encyclopedia, or contributed particular epistles. These are **scholarly theories or unsupported elaborations**, not documented facts. The surviving evidence supports contact with Basran intellectual networks and later textual familiarity; it does not supply a membership record.

#### Wāsiṭ

He wrote from:

- ʿAlī ibn ʿAbd Allāh ibn Mubashshir

#### Baghdad

Teachers included:

- al-Ḥusayn ibn Ismāʿīl al-Maḥāmilī
- Muḥammad ibn Aḥmad ibn al-Jahm, a Mālikī judge or scholar
- ʿAbd Allāh ibn Muḥammad ibn Ziyād al-Nīsābūrī, also called Abū Bakr ibn Ziyād

#### al-Madāʾin

He heard from:

- Sahl ibn Ibrāhīm ibn Sahl, a judge

#### Sīrāf

He heard from:

- Jaʿfar ibn Muḥammad ibn al-Ḥasan al-Iṣfahānī

Sīrāf was a Persian Gulf entrepôt connecting Iraq with India and the Indian Ocean. The record proves scholarly activity there but not commercial investment or travel to India.

#### Yemen

Named contacts included:

- Yaḥyā ibn ʿAbd Allāh ibn Kulayb, judge of Ṣanʿāʾ
- ʿAbd al-Aʿlā ibn Muḥammad ibn al-Ḥusayn al-Būsī, preacher of Ṣanʿāʾ
- Hārūn ibn Aḥmad ibn Muḥammad, encountered at ʿAlqān between Ṣanʿāʾ and Aden
- Abū Sulaymān Rabīʿ ibn Sulaymān, associated with the prayer office at al-Janad

#### Syria and Palestine

He wrote from:

- Yaʿqūb ibn Ḥajar al-ʿAsqalānī
- Ibn Abī Qurṣāfa
- Yaḥyā ibn Mūsā at Ramla

The Jerusalem dream narrative discussed below places him in the sanctuary at Jerusalem. Even if the dream itself is treated as legend, the geographical detail is compatible with the rest of his Palestinian itinerary.

### Intellectual consequences of the journey

**Scholarly reconstruction:** the journey changed Maslama from a primarily Andalusian hadith student into a collector of eastern religious, philosophical, mystical, and occult materials.

The works attributed to him incorporate or reflect:

- Jābirian alchemy
- the *Filāḥa Nabaṭiyya* or *Nabataean Agriculture*
- philosophical and magical texts attributed to Plato and Aristotle
- Hermetic materials
- astrological doctrines
- traditions connected with al-Kindī
- the *Rasāʾil Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ*
- material associated with Sahl al-Tustarī and Dhū al-Nūn al-Miṣrī

Precisely where and from whom he obtained each text cannot normally be established.

## Early Career and Return to al-Andalus

Maslama returned to al-Andalus carrying “much knowledge” and a large body of hadith material. The exact return date is uncertain.

De Callataÿ and Moureau argue that he must have returned by approximately **September–October 937**, because one of his later pupils was executed for conspiracy no later than 339 AH/950 and because other teacher–student chronologies require his presence in Córdoba.

Following his return:

- he transmitted hadith to many listeners;
- he compiled biographical and transmitter-critical material;
- he apparently composed works in occult philosophy;
- he lost one eye and eventually became blind.

The sources do not identify a permanent teaching institution, patron-funded laboratory, court appointment, or dedicated alchemical workshop.

## Chronological Life History

### c. 905–906: Birth in Córdoba

Maslama was born into an otherwise undocumented Cordoban family. The later surname *al-Zayyāt* is the only possible clue to occupational background.

### Before c. 932–933: Education in al-Andalus

He heard hadith from a substantial group of local teachers. His formation was conventionally Sunni and closely connected with the Mālikī-hadith environment, notwithstanding later accusations of esotericism, anthropomorphism, or heterodoxy.

### Early to mid-930s: Mediterranean and eastern journey

He crossed North Africa and traveled through Qayrawān, Tripoli, Crete, Alexandria, Egypt, the Red Sea corridor, Mecca, Iraq, the Persian Gulf, Yemen, Syria, and Palestine.

This was not merely pilgrimage. His biographers repeatedly use the technical language of hearing, writing, and receiving from named scholars.

### By c. 937: Return to Córdoba

Modern chronological analysis favors a return by the later 930s.

He thereafter taught and transmitted the materials collected during the journey. Ibn al-Faraḍī says many people heard from him.

### After the journey: Loss of sight

The biographical record states that he lost his sight after returning from the east. One narrative says:

1. Maslama slept one night inside the closed sanctuary at Jerusalem.
2. He awoke and saw an enormous lion beside him.
3. A dream interpreter told him the lion represented Gabriel and predicted that his sight would be taken.
4. Maslama hurried home.
5. One eye failed during the sea voyage.
6. He became fully blind in al-Andalus.

The blindness is a **reported fact**. The lion, Gabrielic identification, and prophetic sequence are a **dream legend**. The story may have supplied religious meaning to an otherwise unexplained degenerative illness.

No diagnosis survives. Cataract, glaucoma, infection, injury, or another cause would be speculation.

### 339–342 AH/950–953 CE: *Rutbat al-ḥakīm*

The *Rutba* gives internal dates interpreted as composition between 339 and 342 AH.

The work treats alchemy as the first of the two ultimate “results” or culminations of philosophy. It discusses laboratory operations, substances, furnaces, mercury, textual secrecy, symbols, ciphers, and the ethical and intellectual qualifications of the adept.

The work’s reference to *fitna* once encouraged a later date linked to the Cordoban civil war beginning in 1009. Fierro argued that *fitna* could refer to earlier political or religious conflict, removing one obstacle to authorship by a man who died in 964.

### 343–348 AH/954–959 CE: *Ghāyat al-ḥakīm*

The *Ghāya* was composed after the *Rutba*, conventionally between 343 and 348 AH, although some manuscript-based discussions place its final stages in 346–348 AH.

It presents astral magic as the second culmination of philosophical training. Its subjects include:

- the philosophical basis of magic;
- celestial causation;
- correspondences among planets, zodiacal signs, minerals, plants, animals, colors, perfumes, languages, and human activities;
- planetary images and talismans;
- fumigations and compounded substances;
- invocations, prayers, and ritual operations;
- magical cities and legendary sages;
- astrological timing;
- the preparation required of the philosopher-magician.

The text includes operations that later religious readers classified as illicit magic, including spirit invocation, sacrifice, necromantic or divinatory material, and the ritual use of animal substances. Description in the book does not establish that Maslama personally performed every operation he compiled.

### Final years

Maslama continued to be remembered as a transmitter, compiler, and controversial religious personality. He was active under the Umayyad caliphs ʿAbd al-Raḥmān III and al-Ḥakam II, but no secure evidence makes him a court officer.

A later manuscript annotation claims caliphal favor. Scholars regard the note as contaminated by the conflation of Maslama ibn Qāsim with Maslama al-Majrīṭī. It cannot securely establish patronage.

### 23 May 964: Death

He died in Córdoba on a Monday, reportedly aged sixty. No account identifies his final illness, burial place, estate, heirs, or funeral.

## Institutions, Projects, Technologies, and Financial Interests

### Hadith transmission

Maslama participated in a transregional network rather than a single formal institution. His project involved:

- collecting hadith;
- recording names and judgments concerning transmitters;
- bringing eastern transmissions to Córdoba;
- teaching numerous Andalusian listeners.

### Alchemical practice

The *Rutba* describes laboratory technologies and experimental procedures, including heating mercury. A modern study by de Callataÿ and Moureau examined its mercury-heating experiment as evidence of active engagement with alchemical procedure.

This is evidence for technical knowledge and perhaps experimentation. It is not evidence that Maslama discovered elemental oxygen, anticipated modern chemistry, or operated a commercial laboratory.

### Talismanic technology

The *Ghāya* treats talismans as constructed technologies joining:

- selected materials;
- images;
- inscriptions;
- planetary configurations;
- ritual speech;
- fumigations;
- theories of celestial sympathy.

Whether these operations were considered “science,” “magic,” or both depended on the classificatory and religious assumptions of the reader.

### Finances and property

No reliable information survives concerning:

- personal wealth;
- landholdings;
- workshop ownership;
- patron payments;
- investments;
- trade partnerships;
- debts;
- inheritance;
- fees from teaching;
- manuscript sales.

The *al-Zayyāt* surname is insufficient to reconstruct a business structure.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Sunni and Mālikī setting

Maslama’s teachers and transmitter activity place him within Sunni scholarly society. Modern databases commonly classify him as Mālikī.

### Esoteric or *bāṭinī* interests

Ibn al-Faraḍī called him a practitioner or possessor of:

- **ruqā**, charms or incantations;
- **nīranjāt**, magical artifices, enchantments, or talismanic operations.

He also said Maslama was a man of “opinion, secret, and book” in a formulation preserved or paraphrased by later authorities.

Fierro interpreted Maslama as an Andalusian *bāṭinī*: a scholar interested in inner or esoteric meanings and possibly connected intellectually with the milieu of Ibn Masarra. This does not prove formal membership in a named sect.

### Alleged connection with Ibn Masarra’s followers

Later polemical evidence places a “Maslama ibn Qāsim” among Andalusian people of error alongside Ibn Masarra or his current. Fierro treated the identification as significant.

The evidence supports:

- the existence of a later tradition connecting Maslama with condemned esoteric thought;
- thematic parallels between his attributed writings and eastern mystical or cosmological materials.

It does not prove:

- a personal meeting with Ibn Masarra;
- initiation into an organized Masarrī order;
- participation in political conspiracy;
- prosecution by the Umayyad state.

### Philosophy as a curriculum

In the *Rutba* and *Ghāya*, the perfect sage advances through a hierarchy of disciplines. Alchemy and magic are not presented as isolated folk practices but as the two final products of philosophical mastery.

The attributed system integrates:

- logic and philosophical reasoning;
- number and geometry;
- astronomy and astrology;
- natural philosophy;
- alchemy;
- astral magic.

### The Brethren of Purity

The *Ghāya* contains many close parallels with the *Rasāʾil Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ*. The *Rutba* also refers to a body of epistles, although de Callataÿ’s later comparison argues that Maslama’s familiarity with the complete *Rasāʾil* was weaker when writing the *Rutba* than when composing the *Ghāya*.

The strongest cautious conclusion is:

- Maslama acquired or gained increasing access to materials associated with the Brethren;
- he became an early agent in their transmission or reception in al-Andalus;
- the exact moment, location, and form of the corpus he obtained remain unresolved.

The claim that he personally authored or co-authored the entire *Rasāʾil* is not established.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Teachers and mentors

The named teachers above constitute his best-documented network. Especially significant were:

- al-Ṭaḥāwī in Egypt;
- al-ʿUqaylī in Mecca;
- Abū Saʿīd ibn al-Aʿrābī in Mecca;
- Aḥmad ibn Muḥammad ibn Sālim al-Tustarī in Basra;
- Qāsim ibn Muṭarrif al-Qaṭṭān;
- Qāsim ibn Aṣbagh in al-Andalus.

### Students and transmitters

The complete pupil list is not securely reconstructable from the sources consulted. Ibn al-Faraḍī says many people heard from Maslama.

Modern reconstruction identifies pupils including men whose careers help date Maslama’s return. One was associated with the Umayyad prince ʿAbd Allāh, son of ʿAbd al-Raḥmān III, and was executed following a conspiracy. This supplies chronological evidence but does not show that Maslama joined the conspiracy.

### Ibn al-Faraḍī

Ibn al-Faraḍī was born shortly before Maslama’s death and transmitted evidence obtained from Maslama’s associates. He preserved the most important early biography but also reported accusations of lying, weak intellect, magical practices, and questionable theological speech.

### Muḥammad ibn Aḥmad ibn Yaḥyā ibn Mufarrij

When questioned about Maslama, this Cordoban judge or scholar reportedly answered:

- Maslama was not a liar;
- he was, however, “weak in intellect.”

This testimony is important because it rejects the harshest accusation while retaining an adverse personal judgment.

### Yaḥyā ibn al-Haytham

He transmitted the Jerusalem lion-and-blindness story. His report is evidence for the circulation of the narrative, not for the supernatural interpretation.

### Enemies and critics

No personal enemy is securely named as the author of a campaign against him. Ibn Ḥazm says some Andalusians were hostile to him and sometimes accused him of lying. Ibn Ḥajar later maintained that the anthropomorphism allegation came from an enemy.

No lawsuit, formal heresy trial, imprisonment, exile order, confiscation, or execution proceeding is documented.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, and Financial Questions

### Reliability as a hadith transmitter

**Allegation:** Ibn al-Faraḍī heard people accuse Maslama of lying.

**Counter-testimony:** Muḥammad ibn Aḥmad ibn Yaḥyā ibn Mufarrij said he was not a liar, although “weak in intellect.”

**Later evaluations:**

- al-Dhahabī called him weak or unreliable;
- Abū Jaʿfar al-Mālaqī reportedly said “there is something to examine in him”;
- Ibn Ḥazm described him as a prolific transmitter and said some Andalusians bore hostility toward him and sometimes called him a liar;
- Ibn Ḥajar called him a man of considerable rank while preserving criticism of aspects of his transmitter judgments.

**Status:** unresolved medieval hadith-critical dispute. There was no court finding and no surviving demonstration that he fabricated a particular report.

### Alleged anthropomorphism

Sources say objectionable statements concerning *tashbīh*—likening God to created things—were preserved against him.

Later al-Dhahabī said he was reportedly among the *mushabbiha*. Ibn Ḥajar countered that only an enemy attributed this position to him.

**Status:** allegation, disputed by later authority. No complete creed or trial record survives.

### Charms, enchantments, and magic

Ibn al-Faraḍī’s characterization of Maslama as a man of charms and *nīranjāt* is early and specific.

If he wrote the *Ghāya*, expertise in talismanic and magical literature is independently corroborated by the text.

What remains unproven is the extent of personal ritual practice. An author may compile, theorize, transmit, or prescribe operations without performing all of them.

### *Bāṭinism* and heterodoxy

Modern scholarship associates him with esoteric currents and perhaps with the intellectual aftermath of Ibn Masarra. A later polemical source classes a Maslama ibn Qāsim among the misguided.

**Status:** plausible intellectual characterization and documented later accusation; formal sectarian affiliation unproved.

### Political connections

A manuscript note associates “Maslama” with al-Ḥakam’s favor. Because the note combines traits of both Maslamas, its subject is unstable.

No reliable evidence establishes:

- political office;
- intelligence work;
- espionage;
- participation in the prince ʿAbd Allāh’s conspiracy;
- imprisonment;
- exile;
- confiscation;
- Fatimid agency.

His travel through Fatimid North Africa does not by itself imply political collaboration.

### Authorship controversy

The central controversy is whether Maslama ibn Qāsim wrote the *Rutba* and *Ghāya*.

Evidence favoring him includes:

1. The books’ internal dates fit his lifetime.
2. Those dates fit al-Majrīṭī very poorly; al-Majrīṭī was probably a child or not yet born when composition began.
3. The author claims extensive travel, matching Ibn Qāsim but not the known biography of al-Majrīṭī.
4. The author combines religious learning with occult sciences, matching Ibn Qāsim’s biography.
5. Ibn al-Faraḍī explicitly associates Ibn Qāsim with charms and talismans.
6. The intellectual materials fit an eastern journey through the regions Ibn Qāsim visited.
7. Early manuscript annotations preserve mixed biographies showing how the two men became conflated.

Limitations include:

1. No autograph identifies Ibn Qāsim.
2. The extant manuscript tradition is later and frequently names al-Majrīṭī.
3. Some arguments depend on reading internal chronology and *fitna* in particular ways.
4. The textual history remains layered.
5. Certain modern scholars, including Mourad Kacimi, have urged greater caution about treating the reconstruction as proven beyond doubt.

**Assessment:** **disputed attribution with strong and now broadly accepted scholarly support**.

### Adversarial search: lawsuits, crimes, fraud, scandal

Dedicated searches in English and Arabic, including material published or indexed in 2024–2026, did not disclose evidence of:

- a lawsuit;
- criminal charge;
- conviction;
- financial fraud;
- corruption;
- money laundering;
- abuse;
- imprisonment;
- documented political violence;
- an intelligence relationship;
- a modern scandal specific to the historical individual.

Searches did recover the medieval accusations already detailed: lying, weak intellect, anthropomorphism, magical practice, and heterodoxy.

Recent internet discussions largely concern practicing magic from *Picatrix*, hallucinogenic ingredients, supposed Vatican suppression, demonic secrets, or sensational descriptions of the book as forbidden technology. These are discussions of the later text and its reception, not evidence about Maslama’s conduct.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### The lion in Jerusalem

**Story:** Maslama awoke inside Jerusalem’s closed sanctuary and found an enormous lion beside him. An interpreter identified it as Gabriel and foretold his blindness. Maslama then lost one eye at sea and became blind at home.

**Appearance and circulation:** preserved through Yaḥyā ibn al-Haytham and transmitted in later biographical literature.

**Evidence for:** the independently reported fact of blindness and a plausible journey through Palestine.

**Evidence against or limitations:** dreams and angelic identities are not independently verifiable; the tale supplies no medical cause.

**Effect on reputation:** transformed disability into a providential sign and linked Maslama’s journey with sacred geography.

### “Picatrix” as the author’s personal name

The Latin title *Picatrix* came to be treated as the name of the book’s supposed author. Proposed explanations derive it from a corrupted form of an Arabic or Greek name inside the text, sometimes *Buqrāṭīs* or Hippocrates.

The Arabic work does not identify Maslama as “Picatrix.” Calling the historical Maslama “Picatrix” is therefore a product of translation and manuscript reception.

### Maslama al-Majrīṭī as author

For centuries, Arabic and European scholarship assigned the *Rutba* and *Ghāya* to al-Majrīṭī. Ibn Khaldūn helped perpetuate the attribution of the *Ghāya* to that celebrated mathematician.

This was not necessarily deliberate fraud. Similar names, lost biographical context, anonymous composition, and later manuscript notes created a cumulative conflation.

### Founder or member of the Brethren of Purity

Some modern accounts say Maslama joined the Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ in Basra, revised fifty epistles, wrote additions, or authored the *Risāla al-jāmiʿa*.

**Evidence:** his Basran presence and the strong textual relationship among the *Rutba*, *Ghāya*, and *Rasāʾil*.

**Limitations:** no membership roll, contemporary statement, autograph, or universally accepted textual demonstration establishes those specific acts.

**Status:** theory, sometimes overstated online as fact.

### Secret patronage by the Cordoban caliphs

Later manuscript material associates Maslama with Caliph al-Ḥakam. The note also incorporates information belonging to al-Majrīṭī and is therefore composite.

**Status:** unresolved and textually contaminated claim.

### Suppressed sorcerer or occult master

Modern occult publicity often recasts Maslama as a hidden grand master deliberately erased by religious authorities.

There is evidence that secrecy structured alchemical and magical writing, and evidence that contemporaries regarded Maslama suspiciously. There is no evidence for an organized suppression program that erased his name from every manuscript.

### Vatican or conspiratorial suppression of *Picatrix*

Recent online narratives describe the Vatican, secret orders, or Renaissance elites as concealing a uniquely dangerous manual.

The text did circulate selectively, and learned magic could attract ecclesiastical condemnation. Manuscripts were nevertheless copied, catalogued, owned, and studied. No consulted source substantiates a coordinated centuries-long Vatican conspiracy centered on Maslama personally.

## Works by or Attributed to Maslama

## Works with comparatively secure biographical attribution

### *al-Taʾrīkh al-kabīr fī asmāʾ al-muḥaddithīn*

**English:** *The Great History of the Names of Hadith Transmitters*.

Ibn Ḥajar says Maslama compiled a one-volume transmitter history designed to include persons omitted from al-Bukhārī’s history. He called it rich in useful information.

**Status:** genuine attribution; apparently lost as a complete independent work.

### *al-Ṣila*

A supplement or continuation related to his transmitter history and ultimately to al-Bukhārī’s biographical project. Later authors quoted it repeatedly.

Some notices blur the distinction between *al-Ṣila*, Maslama’s own history, and a work called *al-Zāhir*. Modern scholarship does not possess an identified complete manuscript.

**Status:** genuine attribution, title and precise relationship among recensions uncertain; lost or surviving only in quotations.

### *Mā rawā al-kibār ʿan al-ṣighār*

**English:** *What the Elders Narrated from the Young*.

A work on reverse-generation transmission.

**Status:** reported bibliographical attribution; apparently lost.

### *Kitāb fī al-khaṭṭ fī al-turāb*

**English:** *Book on Drawing/Writing in the Dust*.

The title may refer to geomancy or another divinatory procedure.

**Status:** reported attribution; contents and survival uncertain.

## Major works now attributed to him

### *Rutbat al-ḥakīm wa-madkhal al-taʿlīm*

Arabic: **رتبة الحكيم ومدخل التعليم**

Translations include *The Rank of the Sage*, *The Degree of the Sage*, or *The Scale of the Sage*.

- **Date:** 339–342 AH/950–953 CE.
- **Subject:** alchemy as the first culmination of philosophy.
- **Authorship:** traditionally assigned to Maslama al-Majrīṭī; now attributed by strong scholarly reconstruction to Maslama ibn Qāsim.
- **Textual state:** survives in multiple manuscripts. Wilferd Madelung published a preliminary Arabic edition based principally on Paris Arabe 2612, Süleymaniye Hacı Mahmud 6224, and Tehran Malek 5346. Other research uses Süleymaniye Ragıp Paşa 965 and 963.
- **Notable content:** mercury-heating experiment, laboratory operations, alchemical symbols, ciphers, Jābirian materials, *Nabataean Agriculture*, and discussion of *ramz* or coded expression.

### *Ghāyat al-ḥakīm wa-aḥaqq al-natījatayn bi-l-taqdīm*

Arabic: **غاية الحكيم وأحق النتيجتين بالتقديم**

Translations include *The Aim of the Sage*, *The Goal of the Wise*, and *The Goal of the Sage*.

- **Date:** usually 343–348 AH/954–959 CE.
- **Subject:** astral and talismanic magic as the second culmination of philosophy.
- **Authorship:** same disputed but strongly supported attribution as the *Rutba*.
- **Structure:** four books.
- **Transmission:** translated into Castilian by order of Alfonso X, conventionally dated 1256–1258; translated from Castilian into Latin; circulated as *Picatrix*.
- **Textual caution:** the Latin *Picatrix* is not identical to the Arabic *Ghāya*. Translation, abridgment, interpolation, and rearrangement affected the European text.

### Hadith reports

Modern hadith databases locate approximately eighteen reports or transmitter appearances connected with Maslama, including chains preserved through later compilations. These are transmitted materials rather than an authored book.

## Works problematically or secondarily attributed

### *Rasāʾil Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ*

Medieval and modern writers sometimes attributed the encyclopedia to “Maslama,” often meaning al-Majrīṭī or a conflation of both men.

**Status:** not accepted as Maslama ibn Qāsim’s sole-authored work. His role as transmitter or adapter is plausible but incompletely established.

### *al-Risāla al-jāmiʿa*

Sometimes attributed to Maslama in theories concerning the Brethren of Purity.

**Status:** disputed and unproven.

### *Taʾrīkh al-falāsifa al-ʿArab*

A later manuscript note assigns to “Maslama” a history of Arab philosophers.

Because the note conflates Ibn Qāsim and al-Majrīṭī, this attribution is insecure.

### Epistle on derivation or transmutation of bodies

The same manuscript tradition credits “Maslama” with a short epistle on derivation and the transformation of bodies.

**Status:** insecure, possibly alchemical, and affected by the two-Maslamas confusion.

## Death and Final Status

Maslama died in Córdoba on 23 May 964, according to the standard conversion of the recorded Islamic date.

Documented near the end of his life:

- blindness;
- continued reputation as a prolific transmitter;
- circulation of criticism concerning his reliability and theology;
- completion, if the modern attribution is accepted, of both the *Rutba* and *Ghāya*.

Not documented:

- cause of death;
- final illness;
- burial site;
- will;
- heirs;
- estate;
- deathbed statement;
- formal rehabilitation or condemnation.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### As a hadith scholar

Maslama’s transmitter judgments continued to be quoted after his death. Later hadith critics valued information in his lost histories while questioning some of his evaluations and his own reliability.

This produced a divided reputation:

- useful compiler and knowledgeable traveler;
- weak or controversial transmitter;
- “great in rank” according to a later favorable evaluation;
- suspected by detractors of lying or theological error.

### As an occult author

His occult authorship disappeared behind the name of al-Majrīṭī. The more famous scientist became the conventional author of the *Rutba* and *Ghāya*, while Ibn Qāsim remained a short, ambiguous entry in hadith dictionaries—the “man of charms and talismans.”

Fierro’s 1996 study reunited those two documentary identities.

### *Picatrix* in Europe

The *Ghāya* was translated into Castilian under Alfonso X and subsequently into Latin. The Latin *Picatrix* became one of medieval and Renaissance Europe’s most substantial works of astral magic.

Its readers or users included intellectual circles associated with:

- Marsilio Ficino;
- Giovanni Pico della Mirandola;
- Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa;
- learned astrologers and physicians;
- manuscript collectors interested in image magic and celestial causation.

Ficino’s correspondence provides evidence that he borrowed a *Picatrix* manuscript and extracted material he considered useful for *De vita*. Agrippa later criticized the author for mixing natural magic with superstition.

Influence on any particular thinker must be established textually; the mere presence of *Picatrix* in Renaissance culture does not prove direct dependence.

### Modern reputation

Maslama now occupies overlapping fields:

- hadith and transmitter criticism;
- Andalusian intellectual history;
- Islamic esotericism;
- alchemy;
- history of science;
- history of magic;
- manuscript studies;
- Brethren of Purity scholarship;
- Renaissance transmission studies.

Popular occult culture often removes his Mālikī and hadith background, while traditional biographical literature often omits or minimizes the two occult works. The historically reconstructable figure belongs to both contexts.

## Atlas Connections

### Maslama ibn Ahmad al-Majriti

- **Nature:** posthumous identity and authorship conflation, not a documented meeting.
- **Dates and places:** the confusion developed in medieval Andalusian and later Arabic manuscript traditions and passed into European scholarship through the *Picatrix* tradition.
- **Evidence label:** **documented fact**.
- The two men shared the name Maslama, the *kunya* Abū al-Qāsim, and Cordoban/Andalusian designations. The *Rutba*, *Ghāya*, and sometimes the *Rasāʾil Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ* were consequently attributed to al-Majrīṭī.

### Abu Yusuf Yaʿqub ibn Ishaq al-Kindi

- **Nature:** textual and intellectual influence through theories of celestial causation, rays, astrology, and magical efficacy.
- **Dates and places:** indirect, through Arabic scientific and philosophical literature available to the author of the *Ghāya*.
- **Evidence label:** **scholarly reconstruction based on textual relationship**.
- No personal meeting was possible; al-Kindī died before Maslama’s birth.

### Plato

- **Nature:** textual authority in the philosophical and magical source-world of the *Ghāya*.
- **Evidence label:** **documented textual reception**, often through pseudonymous or late antique Arabic works rather than Plato’s authentic dialogues.
- No historical personal connection existed.

### Aristotle

- **Nature:** textual authority in the attributed works’ philosophical curriculum.
- **Evidence label:** **documented textual reception with attribution caution**.
- A number of medieval Arabic occult works circulated under Aristotle’s name without being authentic Aristotelian compositions.

### Zoroaster

- **Nature:** legendary ancient authority within the genealogy of astral and magical wisdom drawn upon by the *Ghāya* tradition.
- **Evidence label:** **mythic or pseudepigraphic textual association**, not a historical connection.

### King Solomon

- **Nature:** paradigmatic ancient possessor of occult wisdom in later magical traditions relevant to *Picatrix* reception.
- **Evidence label:** **legendary association**, not a biographical meeting or demonstrable direct influence on Maslama personally.

### Ibn Arabi

- **Nature:** later Andalusian esoteric thinker occasionally compared with or placed downstream from traditions transmitted through Maslama.
- **Evidence label:** **scholarly comparison only**.
- No meeting was possible; Ibn ʿArabī was born nearly two centuries after Maslama’s death, and the consulted evidence does not establish an explicit acknowledgment of Maslama by Ibn ʿArabī.

No documented meeting, correspondence, family tie, patronage relationship, or direct collaboration was found between Maslama and any other person on the atlas roster. Shared geography, broad participation in occult traditions, or later similarity has not been counted as a connection.
