# Nizami Ganjavi: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary limits

Nizami left no autobiography, independent contemporary biography, securely identified portrait, correspondence archive, administrative record, or surviving autograph manuscript. Almost everything recoverable about his private life comes from autobiographical passages embedded in poems whose manuscript traditions contain additions and variant readings. Later biographical dictionaries were written generations or centuries after his death.

Accordingly, this dossier uses these labels:

- **Documented fact:** supported by Nizami’s text, an early manuscript, or a reasonably contemporary external source.
- **Reported fact:** stated by later biographers or modern scholars but not independently documented.
- **Disputed:** competing textual readings or scholarly reconstructions exist.
- **Legend or myth:** a culturally important story lacking adequate historical evidence.
- **Modern political claim:** an interpretation substantially shaped by modern national institutions or identity politics.

Dates are given in Common Era, with Islamic Hijri dates where relevant. Medieval years often overlap two Common Era years.

## Historical and Geographical Setting

Nizami lived in Ganja when the city belonged culturally and geographically to **Arran**, north of the Aras River. It lay within the broader Persianate and Islamic world but passed among Turkic-ruled dynasties, principally the Seljuqs, Eldiguzid atabegs, Shirvanshahs, and neighboring local houses.

Ganja was a prosperous urban center associated with textiles, trade, Islamic scholarship, Persian court culture, Armenian and Georgian neighbors, and recurrent warfare. A destructive earthquake struck the city in 1139, probably shortly before Nizami’s birth. Political authority in his lifetime was fragmented: rulers competed, succeeded one another rapidly, and maintained Persian secretarial and literary cultures despite varied dynastic origins.

“Nizami was born in Azerbaijan” can be historically defensible only if “Azerbaijan” is carefully defined. Medieval Arabic and Persian geography normally distinguished Arran, where Ganja stood, from Azerbaijan south of the Aras. Modern Ganja is in the Republic of Azerbaijan. Neither medieval classification maps cleanly onto twentieth- or twenty-first-century ethnicity or citizenship.

[Encyclopaedia Iranica’s history of Ganja](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/ganja-/) identifies the city as an important center whose cultural flourishing included Nizami.

## Family and Ancestry

### Father

Nizami’s father is generally reconstructed as **Yūsuf ibn Zakkī ibn Muʾayyad**.

- **Documented fact:** Nizami names or alludes to his paternal lineage in autobiographical passages.
- **Reported fact:** Yūsuf may have been an official, merchant, or educated urban notable. No contemporary occupational record survives.
- **Disputed:** Some manuscript versions contain a line connecting the family with **Qom** in central Iran. Textual critics including Waḥīd Dastgerdī and later de Blois have treated this line as probably interpolated. Consequently, claims that Nizami or his father was demonstrably born in Qom exceed the evidence.
- **Likely fact:** His father died while Nizami was young.

### Mother

A frequently printed passage in *Layli o Majnun* describes his mother as **raʾisa-ye kord**, read either as “Raʾisa the Kurd” or “a Kurdish lady/chieftain’s daughter.”

- **Traditional scholarly reading:** His mother was named or titled **Raʾisa** and was Kurdish.
- **Lexical qualification:** *Raʾisa* can mean a woman of high position and need not be a personal name.
- **Historical qualification:** Medieval *Kurd* could denote a range of Iranian-speaking tribal or pastoral populations rather than a modern nationality.
- **Textual dispute:** Some Azerbaijani researchers argue that the relevant couplets are absent from particular early manuscripts and are later interpolation. Absence from one manuscript does not by itself establish interpolation across the entire tradition, but it makes categorical genealogical conclusions unsafe.
- **Best reconstruction:** A passage long accepted by major Persian and Western editors associates Nizami’s mother with Kurdish identity or status; her personal name and the textual originality of the verses remain disputed.

The controversy is documented in both the manuscript-oriented critique of modern politicization and recent Azerbaijani scholarship advancing the opposite conclusion.

### Maternal uncle

Nizami commemorates a maternal uncle, **Khwāja ʿUmar**.

- **Probable fact:** After the early death of Nizami’s parents, Khwāja ʿUmar exercised some guardianship or formative role.
- **Uncertain:** Later claims that “Khwāja” proves he headed a palace guard or occupied a specified court office are conjectural; *khwāja* was a broad title of respect.

### Brother or relative: Qivami Mutarrizi

Some biographical traditions call the poet **Qivāmī Muṭarrizī** Nizami’s brother.

- **Reported fact:** Qivami was a Persian poet associated with Ganja and is known for highly artificial rhetorical verse.
- **Disputed kinship:** The brotherhood is not securely documented by contemporary evidence and may result from later biographical conflation.

### Marriages and children

Nizami’s poems indicate three bereavements involving wives, but details are sparse.

#### First wife: Afaq

The first wife is conventionally called **Āfāq/Afaq**.

- **Reported from poetic evidence:** She was a young woman given to Nizami by the ruler of Darband as part of a royal gift.
- Some accounts identify her as a **Qipchaq/Kipchak Turkic slave woman**. The precise legal status implied by the poetic language—slave, concubine subsequently married, or politically gifted bride—is not recoverable with certainty.
- Nizami’s commemoration is unusually personal. He compares her death with the death of Shirin in *Khosrow o Shirin*.
- She probably died around **1180–81**.
- The familiar proper name “Afaq” may itself be uncertain: the word can mean “horizons” or “world,” and some scholars question whether it is a personal name.

#### Son: Muhammad

Nizami had at least one son, **Muḥammad**, generally considered Afaq’s child.

- In a passage added to *Khosrow o Shirin* around 1180–81, Muhammad is described as approximately **seven**, placing his birth around **1173–74**.
- In *Layli o Majnun* in 1188 he is addressed at about **fourteen**.
- Nizami counsels him to pursue learning, medicine or law rather than rely upon inherited literary prestige.
- No secure later career, marriage, descendants, or death date for Muhammad is known.

#### Later wives

Nizami alludes to the deaths of a second wife in connection with *Layli o Majnun* and a third in connection with the *Eskandar-nama*.

- Their names, origins, marriage dates, and children are unknown.
- The recurring association between completing a poem and losing a wife became part of Nizami’s self-presentation and later biography.
- The common statement that he “married three times” is a reasonable inference, not a fully documented marital register.

## Childhood and Early Life

### Birth and orphanhood

The standard birth year, 535 AH/1140–41, is calculated retrospectively from Nizami’s statements about his age in various poems. No medieval birth notice is known.

His parents apparently died while he was young. The poems’ memorial references to his father, mother, and uncle have encouraged the reconstruction that his uncle Khwāja ʿUmar raised or supervised him.

### Social position

Nizami was evidently not illiterate, destitute, or socially marginal. His eventual command of Persian literary tradition, Arabic religious learning, technical vocabulary, court etiquette, and book culture points to access to substantial education. Whether this came through a madrasa, private tutors, a family scholarly circle, or self-directed reading is unknown.

There is no reliable evidence that he belonged to a hereditary aristocracy, clerical dynasty, craft guild, or state bureaucracy.

---

## Education and Formation

No teacher can be named securely. Later biographies sometimes supply mentors without documentary support.

His writings demonstrate formation in:

- Persian poetry, particularly **Ferdowsi**, **Sanāʾi**, **Fakhr al-Din Asʿad Gorgani**, and traditions represented by *Varqa o Golshah*;
- Qurʾanic narrative and exegesis;
- hadith and stories of the prophets;
- Arabic and Persian rhetoric;
- adab, ethics, and advice-to-kings literature;
- Islamic theology and devotional thought;
- Sufi vocabulary and ascetic teaching;
- Iranian royal and heroic legend;
- astronomy and astrology;
- medicine, pharmacology, and descriptions of the body;
- geometry, arithmetic, music theory, and cosmology;
- stories transmitted from Greek, Indian, Christian, Jewish, and Iranian traditions.

The *Khamsa* article in *Encyclopaedia Iranica* identifies Sanāʾi’s *Hadiqat al-haqiqa*, Gorgani’s *Vis o Ramin*, Ferdowsi’s *Shah-nama*, ʿAyyuqi’s *Varqa o Golshah*, and Nizam al-Mulk’s *Siyasat-nama* among his literary resources.

His learning should not automatically be treated as proof of formal professional status in every discipline. Medieval poets incorporated technical lexicons as intellectual and metaphorical resources.

## Early Career

Very little can be reconstructed before the first major poem.

Nizami probably wrote lyric poetry—qasidas, ghazals, fragments, and possibly rubāʿis—before or alongside his narrative works. He later stated that he had assembled a **divan** by 1188. The original collection is lost, and surviving lyric anthologies contain many doubtful attributions.

Unlike poets who lived continuously at a royal court, Nizami presents himself as based in Ganja, valuing seclusion and resisting the humiliations of professional panegyric. That image must be qualified:

- he dedicated every major narrative poem to rulers;
- he negotiated patronage through praise;
- he complained about delayed or inadequate compensation;
- he apparently accepted gifts, including the woman conventionally called Afaq;
- one meeting with the atabeg Qizil Arslan is described in *Khosrow o Shirin*.

He was therefore not outside court patronage. His distinctive position was that of a locally resident author who cultivated rulers at a distance rather than a continuously salaried court poet.

## Chronological Life History

### c. 1141: Birth at Ganja

Nizami was probably born in Ganja around 1141. The alternative Qom birth rests largely on a contested verse. His nisba, lifelong association, literary geography, and burial tradition favor Ganja.

### 1140s–1150s: Family losses and education

He apparently lost both parents early. Khwāja ʿUmar may have supervised his upbringing. Nizami acquired advanced literary and religious education, but no institution or teacher is securely identified.

### Before or around 1166–1176: *Makhzan al-asrar*

The chronology of **مخزن الاسرار (*Makhzan al-asrār*, “Treasury of Mysteries”)** is disputed.

- Older editions commonly proposed **570 AH/1174–75** or nearby dates.
- De Blois argued for **561 AH/1165–66 or shortly afterward**.
- Other reconstructions extend composition into the 1170s.
- It was dedicated to **Fakhr al-Din Bahramshah**, Mengüjekid ruler of Erzincan in Anatolia.

The poem, approximately 2,250–2,300 couplets, differs from the four romances. It contains twenty moral and spiritual discourses accompanied by illustrative tales. Subjects include justice, vanity, oppression, speech, knowledge, mortality, asceticism, and the responsibilities of rulers.

The dedication demonstrates a patronage connection extending from Ganja to eastern Anatolia. It does not prove that Nizami traveled to Erzincan; transmission could occur through envoys.

### c. 1173–74: Birth of Muhammad

The son’s approximate age in later passages suggests birth around 1173–74.

### 1175–1191: Composition and revision of *Khosrow o Shirin*

**خسرو و شیرین (*Khosrow o Shirin*)** recounts the relationship of the Sasanian king Khosrow II and the Armenian princess Shirin, with Farhad as the tragic rival.

Its textual history is layered:

- work probably began around **571 AH/1175–76**;
- relevant patrons include Seljuq sultan **Tughril III**, Eldiguzid atabeg **Muhammad Jahan Pahlavan**, and later **Qizil Arslan**;
- sections were revised or added through approximately **1191**;
- manuscripts preserve competing dates.

The poem transforms material found in Ferdowsi and other Iranian traditions into a sustained psychological romance. Shirin possesses unusual agency: she chooses, refuses, negotiates, rules, and requires lawful marriage.

Nizami’s first wife died during the textual life of this work, probably in 1180–81. An autobiographical passage links her with Shirin and addresses seven-year-old Muhammad.

### c. 1180–81: Death of Afaq

Afaq’s probable death is among the most personal events recoverable from Nizami’s poetry. The evidence does not establish the cause of death.

### 1186–1191: Qizil Arslan episode

A concluding section of *Khosrow o Shirin* describes Nizami meeting **Muzaffar al-Din Qizil Arslan**, Eldiguzid ruler from 1186 to 1191.

- **Documented literary report:** Nizami’s text depicts the meeting and royal favor.
- **Probable historical core:** A meeting took place during Qizil Arslan’s rule, perhaps at a royal encampment near Ganja.
- **Uncertain elaboration:** Later biographies and paintings embellish the reception.
- A village or estate called **Hamdanian/Hamduniyan** is sometimes reported as the ruler’s gift. Its precise location, tenure, and economic value cannot be firmly established.

The poem also complains that earlier patrons delayed or inadequately rewarded it. These passages constitute evidence about literary economics but not an auditable account of Nizami’s income.

### 1188: *Layli o Majnun*

The Shirvanshah **Akhsatan I** requested a Persian version of the Arabic Layla-Majnun material. Nizami initially resisted, according to his own prologue, partly because he thought the austere Arabian setting offered insufficient courtly ornament. His son Muhammad urged acceptance.

He completed **لیلی و مجنون (*Layli o Majnun*)** in approximately four months, dating it to **Rajab 584 AH/September–October 1188**. It contains roughly 4,600 couplets.

The poem reorganizes scattered Arabic reports about Qays—called Majnun, “possessed” or “mad”—into a continuous tragedy. It explores obsessive love, social prohibition, poetic fame, wilderness, madness, and death.

Nizami’s mother and maternal uncle are commemorated in autobiographical passages transmitted in this poem, although the wording concerning his mother is textually disputed.

A later wife appears to have died around this part of his career. Her identity and exact date are unknown.

### c. 1190s: *Sharaf-nama*

The first half of Nizami’s Alexander cycle, **شرف‌نامه (*Sharaf-nāma*, “Book of Glory/Honor”)**, recounts Alexander’s conquest, kingship, encounters with foreign societies, and search for knowledge.

Its exact date and its order relative to *Haft Paykar* are disputed because manuscripts differ. De Blois concluded that both Alexander books postdate *Layli o Majnun* and were probably composed before *Haft Paykar*, though some manuscripts imply another order.

### 1197: *Haft Paykar*

**هفت پیکر (*Haft Paykar*, “Seven Portraits,” “Seven Beauties,” or “Seven Figures”)**, also called **Haft Gonbad** and **Bahram-nama**, dates itself to **Ramadan 593 AH/July–August 1197**.

It was dedicated to **ʿAlaʾ al-Din Körpe Arslan**, ruler of Maragha from the Ahmadili/Aq Sunqurid line.

The poem centers on Sasanian king Bahram V Gur. Seven princesses inhabit seven domes associated with planets, colors, weekdays, climates, and symbolic narratives. Bahram hears one story in each pavilion and ultimately confronts the damage caused by a corrupt minister.

The work joins romance, political instruction, cosmology, color symbolism, erotic narrative, and the reform of kingship. Its nested tales became especially influential in manuscript painting and comparative narrative studies.

### Late 1190s–early 1200s: *Iqbal-nama/Khird-nama*

The philosophical second half of the Alexander cycle is called:

- **اقبال‌نامه (*Iqbal-nāma*, “Book of Fortune”)**
- **خردنامه (*Khird-nāma*, “Book of Wisdom”)**

Alexander becomes not only conqueror and king but seeker, philosopher, and in some readings prophet. He consults sages, investigates the world, travels to its limits, and finally dies.

The *Eskandar-nama* as a whole contains some 10,000–10,500 couplets in many editions. Its two parts were textually independent enough that scribes sometimes transmitted them separately.

The dedicatees are difficult to reconstruct. Manuscripts name different rulers, and verses may have been revised when patrons died. Proposed associations include Nusrat al-Din Abu Bakr and rulers of Mosul. No single simplified patronage statement covers the entire transmission.

### c. 1202–1209: Final years

There is no continuous record of Nizami’s last years. He appears to have remained near Ganja, completing or revising the Alexander poems and possibly arranging his lyric corpus.

No reliable evidence documents:

- exile;
- imprisonment;
- pilgrimage to Mecca;
- long residence at another court;
- diplomatic office;
- military service;
- prosecution or scandal;
- a final illness.

### c. 1209: Death

The conventional death date is **4 Ramadan 605 AH**, often converted to **12 March 1209**. It appears on the modern-era tombstone and is accepted by many modern reference works.

Alternative dates include:

- 575 AH/1179–80 in Dawlatshah and derivative traditions—impossible if the securely dated 1188 and 1197 poems are accepted as his;
- 599–600 AH/1202–04;
- 602 AH/1205–06;
- 607 AH/1210–11;
- as late as 613 AH/1216–17.

The most defensible formulation is **died probably in 1209, certainly after 1197, at Ganja**. His age was probably in the sixties. Cause of death is undocumented.

## Residences, Travel, and Geographical Movement

### Documented or strongly supported

- **Ganja:** principal and probably lifelong residence.
- **Vicinity of Ganja:** traditional burial and possible meeting with Qizil Arslan.
- Intellectual geography in the poems includes Iran, Arabia, Byzantium, Armenia, the Caucasus, Central Asia, India, China, Rus, Egypt, the Mediterranean, and imagined edges of the world.

### Reported but not securely demonstrated

- A visit to Qizil Arslan’s camp or court.
- Receipt of a rural estate from Qizil Arslan.
- Contacts with Darband through its ruler’s gifts or emissaries.

### Unsupported as personal travel

Nizami’s vivid descriptions of Armenia, Arabia, India, Central Asia, Byzantium, and other regions do not prove he visited them. His works combine books, oral reports, geographical traditions, and imagination.

## Profession, Patronage, Property, and Finances

### Profession

Nizami’s only securely established profession is poet and learned author. There is no proof that he held a bureaucratic, judicial, medical, or teaching office.

### Patronage network

His major named or reconstructed patrons include:

- Fakhr al-Din Bahramshah of Erzincan;
- Tughril III, Seljuq sultan;
- Muhammad Jahan Pahlavan, Eldiguzid atabeg;
- Qizil Arslan, Eldiguzid ruler;
- Nusrat al-Din Abu Bakr;
- Akhsatan I, Shirvanshah;
- ʿAlaʾ al-Din Körpe Arslan of Maragha;
- one or more rulers associated with the Alexander books whose identities vary by manuscript.

### Economic arrangements

Courtly poems were exchanged for gifts, protection, land, money, clothing, animals, or status. Nizami’s texts imply that he expected compensation and could resent delay or stinginess.

No accounts survive showing:

- annual income;
- amount paid for a poem;
- tax status;
- household labor;
- ownership of urban property;
- book-copying revenue;
- debts;
- inheritance;
- business investments.

### Village or estate

The Qizil Arslan episode is associated in later scholarship with a grant of a village, commonly transcribed **Hamduniyan/Hamdanian**.

- **Reported fact:** Nizami received land or a village.
- **Unresolved:** Whether it was ownership, tax revenue, usufruct, or a literary convention; its location and subsequent history are uncertain.
- Claims that he possessed the land surrounding the later Imamzadeh complex depend on much later traditions.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

## Political Thought

Nizami held no known political office and did not lead a faction. Politics enters his work through advice to rulers.

Recurring positions include:

- kingship requires justice;
- rulers must hear the oppressed directly;
- corrupt ministers can destroy a kingdom;
- victory without self-mastery is incomplete;
- royal legitimacy is ethical as well as dynastic;
- tyranny causes cosmic and social disorder;
- wise speech and consultation restrain power.

These themes belong partly to Persian advice literature and partly to the practical court environment in which poems solicited rulers’ attention.

## Religious Position

Nizami was a Muslim poet whose works praise God and Muhammad and include accounts of the Prophet’s ascension.

- His poetry is compatible with mainstream Sunni devotional culture, but a precise legal school is not securely documented.
- Later authors have variously claimed him for Sunni, Shiʿi, Sufi, and heterodox traditions.
- References to ʿAli or the Prophet’s family do not independently prove formal Shiʿi affiliation; such devotion crossed sectarian lines.
- There is no reliable evidence that he belonged to a named Sufi order.
- “Ḥakīm” and “shaykh,” frequently applied posthumously, indicate sage-like reputation rather than a documented ecclesiastical office.

## Mysticism and Philosophy

Nizami repeatedly treats:

- the insufficiency of appearances;
- self-discipline;
- divine unity;
- the relation of love to knowledge;
- the moral danger of ego and appetite;
- the universe as a system of signs;
- poetic speech as both concealment and disclosure.

He draws on Sufi-inflected language without writing a systematic Sufi manual. His intellectual world also includes Neoplatonic, Aristotelian, Iranian royal, astrological, and popular scientific traditions mediated through Islamic scholarship.

## Artistic Development

His sequence can be understood—without assuming a simple progression—as:

1. concentrated moral and symbolic discourse in *Makhzan al-asrar*;
2. psychologically expanded historical romance in *Khosrow o Shirin*;
3. tragic Bedouin love narrative in *Layli o Majnun*;
4. universal kingship and philosophy in the Alexander cycle;
5. intricately patterned cosmological romance and political correction in *Haft Paykar*.

Manuscript chronology complicates this apparent order, especially for *Haft Paykar* and the *Eskandar-nama*.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Afaq

Wife, household partner, mother of Muhammad in the usual reconstruction, and a formative emotional presence in Nizami’s autobiographical poetics.

### Muhammad

Son and addressee of paternal counsel. In Nizami’s telling, Muhammad encouraged him to accept Akhsatan’s commission for *Layli o Majnun*.

### Khwaja Umar

Maternal uncle, memorialized as a lost family protector.

### Akhsatan I

Shirvanshah patron who requested *Layli o Majnun*. The commission connected Nizami to the Shirvan court without proving residence there.

### Qizil Arslan

Ruler whose meeting with Nizami forms the clearest claimed face-to-face encounter with a patron.

### Other patrons

Tughril III, Jahan Pahlavan, Bahramshah, Körpe Arslan, and Abu Bakr formed a geographically dispersed patronage network mediated by manuscripts and envoys.

### Khaqani Shirvani and Mahsati Ganjavi

Both belonged to the wider literary culture of the Caucasus.

- **No securely documented meeting or friendship** between Nizami and Khaqani has been established.
- **No securely documented relationship** with Mahsati survives.
- Later local cultural memory frequently groups them together, but shared region is not personal connection.

### Rivals and enemies

No personal enemy or literary rival can be securely identified. Nizami attacks envious, incompetent, or mercenary poets generically, but attaching those passages to named contemporaries is speculative.

## Women, Gender, and Social Representation

Nizami’s heroines have generated sharply different readings.

Documentary features of the poems include:

- Shirin insists upon consent and legitimate marriage;
- she rules and makes political decisions;
- Layli is constrained by household and tribal authority;
- Fitna challenges Bahram’s pride;
- the seven princesses control narrative spaces and educate the king through stories;
- women can be sovereign, scholar, musician, strategist, lover, captive, or victim.

At the same time:

- the works reproduce medieval patriarchal marriage and kingship;
- women may be exchanged as diplomatic or enslaved persons;
- beauty is repeatedly objectified through courtly conventions;
- some episodes contain coercion, violence, or severe sexual double standards.

Modern claims that Nizami was either straightforwardly “feminist” or simply a spokesman for patriarchal authority are interpretive conclusions, not biographical facts.

## Successes, Failures, and Professional Friction

### Successes

- Established the Persian **Khamsa** as a canonical five-poem model.
- Converted dispersed Layla-Majnun anecdotes into the most influential Persian version.
- Reworked Sasanian and Alexander traditions into psychologically and philosophically ambitious romances.
- Attracted patrons from the Caucasus, Azerbaijan, Anatolia, and northern Mesopotamia.
- Became one of the most frequently illustrated poets of the Persianate manuscript tradition.
- Generated centuries of imitations in Persian, Ottoman Turkish, Chagatai, Kurdish, Urdu, Punjabi, Pashto, and other languages.

### Difficulties or failures

- Complained of delayed or inadequate patronal reward.
- Endured the deaths of his parents, uncle, and apparently three wives.
- His lyric divan was largely lost.
- No autograph survives; his text accumulated interpolations and variant dedications.
- Later historical identity disputes often displaced study of his actual language and manuscript history.

## Controversies and Disputed Questions

## Birthplace: Ganja or Qom?

**Ganja case:**

- Nisba Ganjavi;
- continuous association with Ganja;
- burial tradition there;
- lack of evidence for another residence;
- disputed status of the Qom verse.

**Qom case:**

- a line in some manuscripts says his ancestry or origin was from Qom;
- later biographers accepted the line.

**Finding:** Ganja is substantially better supported as birthplace and residence; paternal ancestry from Qom is possible but unproved.

## Ethnicity and National Classification

### Persian designation

International literary scholarship generally calls Nizami a **Persian poet** because:

- his surviving major corpus is in Persian;
- he operated within Persian literary forms and Iranian narrative traditions;
- medieval literary classification was commonly linguistic and cultural rather than based on modern nationality.

### Azerbaijani designation

Azerbaijan officially and popularly treats him as a foundational **Azerbaijani poet** because:

- he was born, lived, died, and was buried at Ganja, now in Azerbaijan;
- his landscape and political environment were Caucasian;
- Azerbaijani translations and institutions made him central to national culture.

### Ethnic-Turkic claims

Some Soviet and post-Soviet Azerbaijani scholarship argues that Nizami was ethnically Turkic/Azerbaijani and that supposed Kurdish or Qom ancestry rests on interpolation.

Objections include:

- no surviving Azerbaijani Turkic work by him;
- modern Azerbaijani national identity and standardized language postdate him;
- disputed genealogical arguments cannot prove a specific paternal or maternal ethnicity.

### Iranian or Kurdish claims

Iranian scholarship emphasizes his Persian language and Iranian literary tradition. Kurdish claims emphasize the line about his mother.

**Finding:** Place, language, ancestry, and modern national canon are different categories. The documentary minimum is: Nizami was a Persian-language Muslim poet of Ganja; the exact ethnic identities of both parents are unresolved; Azerbaijan and Iran both developed powerful, but differently grounded, claims to his legacy.

## The Soviet “national poet” campaign

From the late 1930s, Soviet institutions increasingly presented Nizami as a great Azerbaijani poet rather than an Iranian/Persian poet geographically associated with Ganja. A major **800th-anniversary celebration**, planned for 1941 and held amid wartime conditions, institutionalized this classification.

Scholars dispute the mechanics:

- Critics describe a state-directed reclassification encouraged under Stalin and supported by Soviet Orientalists.
- Azerbaijani accounts treat the process as recovery of a native poet previously misappropriated.
- Soviet-era translations into Azerbaijani were sometimes discussed rhetorically as restoring him to a national language, although the surviving originals are Persian.

The state campaign is documented. Claims that Stalin personally invented every component are harder to substantiate and should not replace examination of Soviet Academy publications, jubilees, monuments, and translations.

## Mother’s Kurdish identity

- **Traditional textual fact:** widely used editions include the Kurdish reference.
- **Modern challenge:** Azerbaijani scholars cite manuscript absence and argue interpolation or misreading.
- **Unresolved:** A full stemmatic survey of early manuscripts is required; political utility on either side does not decide textual authenticity.

## Purported Turkic verse

Verses in Turkic have occasionally been attributed to Nizami.

- No secure early manuscript or accepted critical edition establishes a Turkic corpus by Nizami Ganjavi.
- Confusion with later poets named Nizami is common.
- These attributions are **disputed or spurious**, not established authorship.

## Portraits

No contemporary portrait survives. Images commonly reproduced as “Nizami” are:

- later manuscript illustrations of a poet meeting a ruler;
- idealized Soviet and Azerbaijani portraits;
- statues and carpet images based on modern artistic invention.

They document reception, not physical appearance.

## Relationship with Khaqani

Stories of friendship, rivalry, mutual mourning, or poetic correspondence circulate in modern regional literature. No secure contemporaneous exchange has been established. They should be treated as **later literary tradition**, not documented biography.

## Crime, prosecution, and scandal

No credible source documents fraud, corruption, criminal prosecution, imprisonment, political violence, intelligence work, sexual scandal, or formal religious investigation involving Nizami. His criticisms of rulers and corrupt officials are literary and ethical, not proof of participation in a prosecutable opposition movement.

---

## Illness, Psychology, Injury, and Death

No diagnosed physical illness, addiction, accident, or injury is documented.

Nizami writes extensively about grief, melancholy, seclusion, aging, bodily decline, madness in love, and mortality. These are partly autobiographical and partly conventional. They do not permit retrospective psychiatric diagnosis.

The cause of death is unknown. Natural illness or age is possible, but no evidence identifies a specific disease.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## The holy recluse who never approached a court

**Story:** Nizami rejected court life completely and lived as an independent ascetic.

**Origin and circulation:** Later hagiographic biographies, literary histories, and national commemoration.

**Evidence for:** He remained based at Ganja and criticized courtly servility.

**Evidence against:** Every major poem has royal patronage; he accepted commissions and gifts and describes meeting Qizil Arslan.

**Reputational effect:** Cast him as a morally pure sage rather than a professional participant in patronage culture.

## Afaq as the perfect beloved

**Story:** Afaq was a Kipchak beauty, Nizami’s sole true love, and the direct model for Shirin.

**Evidence:** A personal memorial links the wife and heroine; she was probably associated with a gift from the ruler of Darband.

**Limitations:** Her name, ethnicity, status, biography, and degree of “modeling” remain uncertain.

**Effect:** Humanized Nizami’s romantic epics and supplied modern novels, paintings, and popular biographies with an emotional center.

## Three poems, three dead wives

**Story:** Each of Nizami’s three wives died just as he completed one of his masterpieces.

**Evidence:** He associates marital bereavements with several poems.

**Against literalization:** Composition and revision lasted years; the pattern may have been rhetorically arranged.

**Effect:** Produced an image of creativity repeatedly purchased through personal tragedy.

## Gift of an entire village

**Story:** Qizil Arslan, astonished by the poet’s genius, granted him a village.

**Evidence:** A patronal gift has a plausible textual and later historical basis.

**Uncertainty:** No deed, fiscal register, boundary, or succession record survives.

**Effect:** Demonstrates the ideal reward owed to genius and supports local property traditions.

## Nizami as a national poet in the modern sense

**Story:** He possessed an unambiguous modern Iranian, Azerbaijani, Turkic, or Kurdish national consciousness.

**Evidence:** Each claim selects genuine elements—Persian language, Ganja birthplace, possible Kurdish maternity, Turkic-ruled environment.

**Against:** Modern nation-state identities and their terminology cannot be transferred intact to twelfth-century Arran.

**Effect:** Generated monuments, translated editions, diplomatic disputes, anniversary campaigns, and competing museum narratives.

## Nizami and Khaqani as companions

**Story:** The two great Caucasian Persian poets met, exchanged poems, or mourned one another.

**Evidence:** Regional proximity and overlapping lifetimes.

**Against:** No securely attributable correspondence or encounter.

**Effect:** Creates a coherent “Azerbaijan school” or Caucasian literary pantheon.

## The tomb as unchanged medieval monument

**Story:** The present mausoleum is Nizami’s original medieval tomb.

**Evidence:** A shrine or tomb tradition existed at the site by the Safavid period; Shah Abbas I camped near “Shaykh Nizami’s tomb” in 1606.

**Against:** The structure collapsed and was rebuilt repeatedly. A Soviet monument was erected in 1947 and the present monumental form dates substantially from 1991.

**Effect:** The site operates simultaneously as burial place, pilgrimage locus, national monument, and state ceremonial landscape.

## Works by Nizami

## The Khamsa / Panj Ganj

The collective title **خمسه (*Khamsa*, “Quintet”)** or **پنج گنج (*Panj Ganj*, “Five Treasures”)** was consolidated through later manuscript and imitative traditions. The Alexander work has two parts, so the corpus may physically contain six books while conceptually counting as five poems.

### 1. *Makhzan al-asrar*

- Original title: **مخزن الاسرار**
- Translation: *Treasury/Storehouse of Mysteries*
- Date: probably c. 1166–1170s; disputed
- Genre: didactic, ethical, mystical masnavi
- Approximate length: 2,250–2,300 couplets
- Patron: Fakhr al-Din Bahramshah of Erzincan
- Authorship: genuine
- Principal model: Sanāʾi’s *Hadiqat al-haqiqa*
- Structure: invocations and twenty moral discourses with exemplary stories

### 2. *Khosrow o Shirin*

- Original title: **خسرو و شیرین**
- Date: begun c. 1175–76; revised through c. 1191
- Genre: historical romance
- Approximate length: 6,100–6,500 couplets, depending on edition
- Patrons: Tughril III, Muhammad Jahan Pahlavan, Qizil Arslan, and later Abu Bakr in different textual layers
- Authorship: genuine
- Sources: Iranian historical-romance traditions, especially material also known from Ferdowsi
- Important figures: Khosrow Parviz, Shirin, Farhad, Maryam, Shapur

### 3. *Layli o Majnun*

- Original title: **لیلی و مجنون**
- Date: 584 AH/1188
- Genre: tragic romance
- Approximate length: 4,600 couplets
- Patron: Shirvanshah Akhsatan I
- Authorship: genuine
- Sources: Arabic oral and written traditions about Qays/Majnun and Layla
- Textual importance: earliest surviving manuscript reported from 718 AH/1318

### 4. *Haft Paykar*

- Original title: **هفت پیکر**
- Alternative titles: **هفت گنبد (*Haft Gonbad*)**, **بهرام‌نامه (*Bahram-nama*)**
- Date: Ramadan 593 AH/July–August 1197
- Genre: royal romance, frame-tale cycle, ethical kingship narrative
- Approximate length: about 5,000 couplets
- Patron: ʿAlaʾ al-Din Körpe Arslan
- Authorship: genuine
- Central figure: Bahram V Gur

### 5. *Eskandar-nama*

- Collective Persian title: **اسکندرنامه**
- Date: after 1188, probably largely in the 1190s or early 1200s
- Genre: heroic, geographical, philosophical, and prophetic Alexander romance
- Approximate total length: 10,000–10,500 couplets
- Authorship: genuine
- Patronage: manuscript-dependent and disputed

#### 5a. *Sharaf-nama*

- Original title: **شرف‌نامه**
- Translation: *Book of Honor/Glory*
- Emphasis: conquest, kingship, geographical exploration

#### 5b. *Iqbal-nama* / *Khird-nama-ye Eskandari*

- Original titles: **اقبال‌نامه** / **خردنامه اسکندری**
- Translation: *Book of Fortune* / *Alexander’s Book of Wisdom*
- Emphasis: philosophy, sages, prophecy, knowledge, death

## Divan

Nizami indicates that he assembled a **دیوان (*Dīvān*)** before 1188.

- The authorial collection is lost.
- Later manuscripts and anthologies preserve qasidas, ghazals, qitʿas, and rubāʿis under his name.
- The corpus has suffered contamination from other poets named Nizami.
- Individual lyric authenticity must be assessed manuscript by manuscript.
- Modern “complete divans” are editorial reconstructions, not the intact authorial volume.

## Attributed or dubious works

Various verses, short poems, and Turkic compositions have been attributed to Nizami. Unless supported by early manuscripts and linguistic-textual analysis, these should be labeled **uncertain, misattributed, or spurious**.

## Manuscript History and Visual Afterlife

No autograph manuscript survives. The gap between Nizami’s death and most extant copies complicates recovery of:

- original wording;
- poem order;
- dedication passages;
- dates;
- line counts;
- autobiographical passages.

Important manuscript witnesses and collections include:

- Tehran University Library manuscript dated **718 AH/1318**, containing an early *Layli o Majnun* witness;
- British Library **Or. 13297**, an illustrated late-fourteenth-century *Khamsa* associated with Baghdad;
- British Library **Or. 6810**, with extensive ownership inscriptions and the complete sequence;
- British Library **Or. 12208**, produced for Mughal emperor Akbar in the 1590s;
- Metropolitan Museum manuscripts and dispersed folios;
- Topkapı Palace Library manuscripts;
- Walters Art Museum and Matenadaran collections.

The *Khamsa* became, with Ferdowsi’s *Shah-nama*, one of the principal vehicles of Persianate manuscript painting. Scenes such as Layli and Majnun at school, Farhad before Shirin, Bahram in the seven pavilions, and Alexander with philosophers circulated across Iran, Central Asia, Ottoman lands, and Mughal India.

## Posthumous Reception and Influence

### Persian literary tradition

Nizami’s decisive formal legacy was the **khamsa imitation** or *javāb/naẓira*: poets composed five corresponding masnavis in the same meters and related genres.

Major respondents include:

- Amir Khusrow Dehlavi;
- Khwaju Kermani;
- Jami;
- Navaʾi;
- Hatifi;
- Fuzuli;
- numerous Persian, Ottoman, Chagatai, Kurdish, and Indo-Muslim poets.

*Encyclopaedia Iranica* describes the *Khamsa* as a model emulated for centuries, while its survey of *Layli o Majnun* records dozens of later versions.

### South Asia

Nizami became central to Persian literary education in the Delhi Sultanate and Mughal world. Amir Khusrow’s *Khamsa* directly answered his. Mughal patrons commissioned lavishly illustrated copies; Akbar’s manuscript involved numerous court painters.

### Ottoman and Turkic traditions

Ottoman poets translated, adapted, and answered individual romances. Fuzuli’s Azerbaijani Turkish *Leyli vü Mecnun* is among the most famous transformations. Ali-Shir Navaʾi produced a Chagatai counterpart to the five-poem structure.

### Europe

From the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, Orientalist editions and translations introduced Nizami to European scholarship. Goethe, comparative folklorists, and historians of romance encountered Persian narrative partly through this expanding translation culture, though claims of direct influence require case-specific proof.

### Music, theater, ballet, and opera

Adaptations include:

- Uzeyir Hajibeyov’s opera **Leyli and Majnun** (1908), generally recognized as the first opera of the Muslim East;
- Kara Karayev’s ballet **Seven Beauties** (1952);
- Kara Karayev’s symphonic poem **Leyli and Majnun** (1947);
- Niyazi’s and other Azerbaijani composers’ Nizami-related works;
- numerous Iranian, Azerbaijani, Central Asian, Indian, and Turkish theatrical and musical adaptations.

These are works based on Nizami’s narratives, not biographical depictions of his life.

## Monuments, Commemoration, and Institutional Legacy

### Tomb and mausoleum

Nizami’s burial place outside Ganja became a local shrine.

- Safavid chronicler Iskandar Beg Munshi recorded Shah Abbas I near the tomb in **1606**.
- The area became a battlefield during the Russo-Persian War of 1826.
- The older structure deteriorated.
- A Soviet monument was constructed in **1947**.
- The present octagonal granite mausoleum was rebuilt in **1991**.

### Soviet commemoration

The Soviet Union sponsored translations, scholarship, monuments, visual art, and an 800th-anniversary program. Wartime celebrations in Leningrad became part of the poet’s Soviet mythology: scholars and soldiers commemorated a humanistic poet while the city was under siege.

### Azerbaijan

Institutions and commemorations include:

- Nizami Museum of Azerbaijani Literature in Baku;
- Nizami Institute of Literature;
- Nizami district and metro station;
- monuments in Ganja, Baku, Moscow, St Petersburg, Rome, and elsewhere;
- state designation of **2021 as the “Year of Nizami Ganjavi”**;
- use of his image on currency, stamps, carpets, and public buildings.

### Iran

Iranian scholarship, publishing, and commemoration emphasize Nizami’s Persian language, Iranian historical material, and place within classical Persian literature. His works remain widely edited and taught.

### UNESCO

The **850th anniversary in 1991** was celebrated internationally in association with UNESCO. The anniversary consolidated Nizami’s status as a transnational literary figure while also intensifying competing national claims.

### Sympathetic, national, and hostile interpretive traditions

There is no major “hostile biography” in the modern investigative sense because the documentary life is too sparse. Instead, polemic concentrates on national attribution:

- Soviet Azerbaijani studies frame Nizami as an Azerbaijani national poet;
- post-Soviet Azerbaijani scholarship often defends Turkic ancestry or indigenous Azerbaijani ownership;
- Iranian and some Western scholarship criticizes this as anachronistic nationalization;
- Kurdish cultural writing emphasizes his maternal ancestry;
- some popular Iranian narratives overstate his connection to Qom.

Each corpus contains useful evidence mixed with national interpretation.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the reviewed sources are included. Mere contemporaneity, shared geography, or modern comparison is excluded.

### Alexander the Great

- **Nature:** Central literary subject of Nizami’s *Eskandar-nama*.
- **Dates and places:** Composed at Ganja, probably during the 1190s or early 1200s; narrative geography extends across the legendary known world.
- **Label:** **Documented fact—literary and intellectual connection, not personal contact.**
- Nizami reconfigured Alexander as conqueror, just ruler, philosopher, seeker, and in the later book a prophetic figure. The historical Alexander died more than fourteen centuries before Nizami.

### Aristotle

- **Nature:** Appears as Alexander’s philosopher and counselor within the Alexander tradition.
- **Dates and places:** Literary treatment in the *Eskandar-nama*, probably 1190s–early 1200s.
- **Label:** **Documented fact—literary representation, not personal contact.**
- Nizami knew Aristotle through Arabic-Persian philosophical and legendary transmission rather than direct Greek study demonstrable from surviving evidence.

### Plato

- **Nature:** One of the Greek sages incorporated into the philosophical world of the *Eskandar-nama*.
- **Dates and places:** Literary treatment at Ganja, probably 1190s–early 1200s.
- **Label:** **Documented fact—literary representation.**
- There was necessarily no personal connection.

### Muhammad ibn Abdullah

- **Nature:** Prophet praised throughout Nizami’s prologues; the miʿraj or ascension receives elaborate poetic treatment.
- **Dates and places:** Across the *Khamsa*, especially invocatory sections written in Ganja during the later twelfth century.
- **Label:** **Documented fact—religious and literary influence.**
- This is confessional and textual influence, not contemporaneous contact.

### Jesus of Nazareth

- **Nature:** Appears within Nizami’s Islamic prophetic and wisdom traditions and in material connected to Alexander’s philosophical world.
- **Label:** **Documented fact—religious-literary reference, not personal contact.**
- Nizami’s Jesus is mediated by Qurʾanic, exegetical, and Persian ethical traditions.

### King Solomon

- **Nature:** Exemplary prophet-king and master of wisdom within Nizami’s religious and royal imagery.
- **Label:** **Documented fact—symbolic and literary influence.**
- No historical contact is possible.

### Amir Khusrow Dehlavi

- **Nature:** Major later imitator of Nizami’s *Khamsa*.
- **Dates and places:** Amir Khusrow composed his five-poem response in India in the late thirteenth century, roughly a century after Nizami’s death.
- **Label:** **Documented fact—posthumous literary influence.**
- His *Ayina-ye Eskandari* directly answers Nizami’s Alexander poem, and his complete *Khamsa* helped transmit the Nizamian model throughout South Asia.

### Farid al-Din Attar

- **Nature:** Near-contemporary Persian poet sometimes linked to Nizami through broad literary and mystical comparison.
- **Label:** **No documented personal connection found.**
- The reviewed sources do not establish a meeting, correspondence, teacher-student relationship, or explicit direct influence between them.

### Jalal al-Din Rumi

- **Nature:** Later Persian poet operating in a literary world influenced broadly by earlier Persian masnavi.
- **Label:** **No direct documented connection found in the consulted sources.**
- Rumi was a child or young man around Nizami’s death; no meeting or correspondence is documented.

### Nizam al-Din Auliya

- **Nature:** No personal connection. The similar name “Nizam” is coincidental; the Delhi Sufi belonged to a later generation.
- **Label:** **No documented connection found.**

### Dante Alighieri

- **Nature:** Modern comparative studies sometimes juxtapose Nizami’s cosmic journeys and ethical allegory with Dante.
- **Label:** **Theory or comparison only.**
- No documentary chain of transmission from Nizami to Dante was established in the sources consulted.

### Homer and Aeneas

- **Nature:** Nizami is sometimes compared with epic poets and Mediterranean heroic traditions.
- **Label:** **No documented direct textual connection found.**
- Nizami’s Alexander and Iranian romances came through Persian, Arabic, and Islamicate sources, not a demonstrated reading of Homer or Virgil.

### Zoroaster

- **Nature:** Nizami’s Sasanian settings preserve Iranian royal and religious memories in Islamicized literary form.
- **Label:** **Documented cultural-literary association; no evidence of adherence to Zoroastrianism.**

### Roster finding

Beyond the literary, religious, and posthumous influence relationships above, **no documented lifetime meeting, correspondence, collaboration, family tie, shared office, or direct patronage connection with another person on the atlas roster was found**. In particular, claims involving Rumi, Attar, or later Sufis should not be inferred from shared Persian literary culture alone.
