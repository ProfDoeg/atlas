# Federico García Lorca: Research Dossier

**Research date:** 24 August 2026  
**Scope:** Documentary reconstruction of Lorca’s life, work, networks, politics, sexuality, finances, murder, afterlife, and principal disputed narratives. Dates attached to compositions distinguish writing, première, and first publication wherever the evidence permits.

## Basic Identifying Information

| Field | Documentary position |
|---|---|
| Full baptismal name | **Federico del Sagrado Corazón de Jesús García Lorca**. He was baptized six days after birth. |
| Usual name | **Federico García Lorca**; in criticism and conversation, often simply **Lorca**. “García Lorca,” not García alone, is the conventional short surname. |
| Birth | **5 June 1898**, at about midnight, 4 Calle de la Trinidad, Fuente Vaqueros, Granada, Spain. |
| Death | Extrajudicially shot by forces of the military rebellion near Víznar/Alfacar, Granada, probably in the early hours of **19 August 1936**. The literature also gives 17 or 18 August; there is no contemporaneous death certificate fixing the moment and no identified body. |
| Occupations | Poet, dramatist, prose writer, lecturer, theatre director, adaptor of Golden Age drama, pianist, harmonizer/collector of traditional songs, draftsman, magazine editor, and author of an experimental film script. He qualified in law but never practiced. |
| Literary placement | Central member of the **Generation of 1927**, though the label was retrospective and describes a network rather than a formal school. |
| Languages | Wrote principally in Spanish; also wrote the six *Seis poemas galegos* in Galician, with linguistic assistance from Galician friends. |
| Marital/parental status | Never married; no documented children. |
| Estate/remains | His manuscripts and family-held papers form the core of the Federico García Lorca Archive. His physical remains have not been located or identified. |

The basic dates, residences, and year-by-year creative record below are anchored in the [Centro Federico García Lorca’s institutional chronology](https://www.centrofedericogarcialorca.es/es/fgl), supplemented by the [Biblioteca Virtual Miguel de Cervantes chronology](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/portales/federico_garcia_lorca/cronologia/) and sources linked in the relevant sections.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

- **Federico García Rodríguez** (1859–1945), father, was a prosperous farmer and landowner in the fertile Vega of Granada. Sugar-beet expansion and careful property dealings enlarged his means. He had been married before; Vicenta Lorca was his second wife. He financed his son’s studies, travel, first book, and long period of artistic apprenticeship. The father’s support was not passive: Lorca repeatedly had to negotiate permission and money, but the family subsidy made a non-salaried literary career possible. A specialist family profile calls him “fundamental” to that career because he paid for Lorca’s projects [Universo Lorca](https://www.universolorca.com/en/personaje/garcia-rodriguez-federico/).
- **Vicenta Lorca Romero** (1870–1959), mother, was born in Granada’s Realejo district and taught the girls’ infant class at Fuente Vaqueros from 1893 to 1897. Her father died before her birth. Lorca associated her with education, music, and reading. Her family history is summarized by [Universo Lorca](https://www.universolorca.com/personaje/lorca-romero-vicenta/). After the murder, she refused to return to Granada; the family eventually went to the Americas, and she later lived in Madrid.

### Siblings

1. **Federico** (1898–1936), eldest surviving child.
2. **Luis** (1900–1902), died of pneumonia at two.
3. **Francisco “Paquito” García Lorca** (1902–1976), lawyer, diplomat, scholar, and later professor at Columbia University. He helped select *Libro de poemas*. In New York in 1942 he married Laura de los Ríos Giner, daughter of Lorca’s teacher Fernando de los Ríos.
4. **María de la Concepción “Concha” García Lorca** (1903–1962), married the physician and Socialist politician **Manuel Fernández-Montesinos**, mayor of Granada when the rebellion began. He was executed on 16 August 1936, the day Lorca was arrested. Their descendants became central custodians of the estate.
5. **Isabel García Lorca** (1909–2002), teacher and memoirist; later founded and led the Federico García Lorca Foundation. The sibling order and Luis’s early death are documented in the [Lorca Center chronology](https://www.centrofedericogarcialorca.es/es/fgl) and [Universo Lorca’s Isabel profile](https://www.universolorca.com/personaje/garcia-lorca-isabel/).

### Household, class, and property

Lorca grew up in a materially comfortable landowning family, not in poverty. The household moved from Fuente Vaqueros to **Asquerosa** (renamed Valderrubio) in 1906–07, and then to Granada in 1909. In the city it occupied increasingly substantial addresses—Acera del Darro, Gran Vía 34, Acera del Casino 31—and in 1925 the father bought the country property renamed **Huerta de San Vicente**, used by the family from 1926. The house and garden became Lorca’s summer workplace and the setting in which he completed or advanced *Así que pasen cinco años*, *Bodas de sangre*, *Yerma*, *Diván del Tamarit*, and *La casa de Bernarda Alba*. There is no evidence that Lorca owned an independent business, large investment portfolio, patent, or industrial concern. His economic structure was family capital plus book, lecture, and theatre income.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1898–1914

Lorca’s first landscape was the irrigated Vega: fields, farm laborers, servants, horses, popular songs, religious festivals, children’s games, and the class divisions of rural Granada. These materials later entered his work in transformed rather than ethnographic form. He was close to domestic workers and village performers but was also the landlord’s son; both vantage points matter.

He attended primary school locally. His mother’s colleague **Antonio Rodríguez Espinosa** was an early teacher and family friend. In 1908–09 Lorca boarded with him in Almería to begin secondary education. Fever and malaise from an abscess or phlegmon prompted his return before the school year ended; the episode is documented in [Rodríguez Espinosa’s profile](https://www.universolorca.com/en/personaje/rodriguez-espinosa-antonio/). Later biographies describe flat feet, a slight limp, delayed physical confidence, and dislike of sports. These are biographical recollections, not a surviving clinical diagnosis. No credible record supports drug addiction or chronic alcoholism.

In Granada he attended the Colegio del Sagrado Corazón de Jesús and studied piano and harmony with **Antonio Segura Mesa**. Music came before literature as a vocation. Family music-making, an uncle’s playing, the liturgy, folk song, flamenco, and classical repertory shaped his sense of rhythm and performance. Segura’s death in 1916 helped turn him from a possible musical career toward writing.

## Education and Formation, 1915–1923

In 1915 Lorca entered the University of Granada, initially taking Arts and Law. His most consequential teachers were:

- **Martín Domínguez Berrueta**, professor of theory of literature and the arts, who led study journeys across Spain and encouraged descriptive prose.
- **Fernando de los Ríos**, professor of comparative political law, socialist intellectual, future Republican minister, family friend, and the adult who helped persuade Lorca’s father to let him move to Madrid.
- **Antonio Segura Mesa**, music teacher and compositional mentor.
- Informally, **Manuel de Falla**, whom Lorca met in 1919 and with whom he developed the 1922 cante jondo festival.

Study journeys in 1916–17 took him to Baeza, Úbeda, Córdoba, Ronda, El Escorial, Ávila, Medina del Campo, Salamanca, Santiago de Compostela, A Coruña, Lugo, León, Burgos, Segovia, Madrid, and other places. He met **Antonio Machado** in Baeza and **Miguel de Unamuno** in Salamanca and played piano publicly. Travel sketches became *Impresiones y paisajes* (1918), printed at his father’s expense.

In Granada the café group **El Rinconcillo** connected him with José Mora Guarnido, Melchor Fernández Almagro, Constantino Ruiz Carnero, Francisco Soriano, Antonio Gallego Burín, Miguel Pizarro, the Fernández-Montesinos brothers, Hermenegildo Lanz, Ángel Barrios, and Manuel Ángeles Ortiz. Their pranks included inventing the apocryphal poet **Isidoro Capdepón Fernández**; texts assigned to “Capdepón” are collaborative/attributed play, not a serious Lorca pseudonym.

Lorca moved to Madrid in spring 1919, stayed in boarding houses, and entered the **Residencia de Estudiantes** in October. The Residencia connected him to Juan Ramón Jiménez, José Ortega y Gasset’s circles, Pedro Salinas, Gerardo Diego, José Moreno Villa, Pepín Bello, Luis Buñuel, Salvador Dalí, and visiting international figures. It was a residence and cultural institution, not proof that he completed a Madrid degree there. Lorca did complete a **law degree in 1923**, moving administratively between Granada and Madrid; he never practiced. His indifferent academic performance and the decisive influence of Berrueta and de los Ríos are summarized in [Universo Lorca’s university account](https://www.universolorca.com/en/biography-of-federico-garcia-lorca/trips-1916/).

## Early Career, 1917–1924

- **1917–18:** First dated poem, “Canción (Ensueño y confusión),” 29 June 1917; prose in Granada and Burgos periodicals; *Impresiones y paisajes* issued April 1918.
- **1919:** Madrid literary entry. Gregorio Martínez Sierra and actress Catalina Bárcena agreed to stage a work. Lorca met Falla in Granada.
- **1920:** *El maleficio de la mariposa* opened at Teatro Eslava. Its insect love tragedy was mocked and withdrawn after four performances—a conspicuous failure, though its mixture of symbol, music, dance, and doomed desire anticipated later theatre.
- **1921:** *Libro de poemas* appeared; Juan Ramón Jiménez printed parts of the *Suites* in *Índice*. Lorca and Falla began organizing the Concurso de Cante Jondo.
- **1922:** Lorca lectured on cante jondo; the Granada competition was held 13–14 June in the Plaza de los Aljibes. It aimed to preserve an “ancient” deep-song tradition, but it also selected and staged that tradition for elite cultural purposes. Later scholarship debates the romantic essentialism of the project.
- **1923–24:** He developed puppet theatre with Falla and Hermenegildo Lanz; worked on *Mariana Pineda*, *Canciones*, *Romancero gitano*, and the *Suites*; met Dalí, whose arrival intensified the Residencia circle.

The detailed institutional record for these years appears in the [Lorca Center 1919–24 chronology](https://www.centrofedericogarcialorca.es/es/fgl/bio-1919-1924).

## Chronological Life History

### 1925–1928: Dalí, Aladrén, Barcelona, and fame

In 1925 Lorca stayed with the Dalí family in Cadaqués and read *Mariana Pineda*. His intense attachment to **Salvador Dalí** combined aesthetic collaboration, emotional intimacy, rivalry, and unreciprocated or incompletely reciprocated erotic desire. Dalí later said he refused Lorca’s physical advances. The surviving correspondence supports intense affection and artistic exchange; it does not support a simple, fully documented consummated partnership. Dalí designed the scenery for the 1927 Barcelona première of *Mariana Pineda*. Lorca’s “Oda a Salvador Dalí” appeared in *Revista de Occidente* in 1926.

From about 1925 to 1929 Lorca had a love relationship with sculptor **Emilio Aladrén Perojo**. Its collapse was one factor in the emotional crisis preceding New York. Public success and private secrecy coexisted: homosexual acts were socially stigmatized and could expose a man to family, police, and professional danger.

The 1927 Barcelona period brought theatrical and visual-art recognition: *Mariana Pineda* opened at Teatro Goya with Margarita Xirgu; Lorca exhibited drawings at Galeries Dalmau; he moved between Barcelona, Sitges, Figueres, Cadaqués, Madrid, and Granada. The December Góngora commemoration in Seville—with Alberti, Alonso, Diego, Guillén, Bergamín, Cernuda, and others—became the symbolic founding episode of the “Generation of 1927.”

In 1928 Lorca co-edited the short-lived Granada magazine **Gallo** (two issues); **Pavo** was its joking counter-magazine. *Romancero gitano* brought national and transatlantic celebrity. Lorca resisted being reduced to a picturesque “gypsy poet”: the Roma figures are highly stylized, and the book is not documentary Roma self-representation. Its success also sharpened tensions with Dalí and Buñuel, whose avant-garde aesthetic was moving elsewhere. Lorca interpreted *Un chien andalou* (1929) as possibly aimed at him; no decisive evidence shows that the film title was a coded attack.

### 1929–1930: New York, Vermont, and Cuba

Lorca left with Fernando de los Ríos, traveled through Paris and London, sailed from Southampton on the **RMS Olympic** on 19 June 1929, and reached New York about a week later. He enrolled in Columbia University’s summer and fall sessions, studying English, and lived in **Furnald Hall room 617** and later **John Jay Hall room 1231**. Columbia confirms his enrollment and surviving letters in its [Rare Book & Manuscript Library account](https://blogs.library.columbia.edu/rbml/2022/12/07/federico-garcia-lorca-dormlife-in-1929/).

His New York network included Ángel del Río, Federico de Onís, León Felipe, José Camprubí, Gabriel García Maroto, Mildred Adams, Philip Cummings, Campbell Hackforth-Jones, Henry Herschel Brickell, John Crow, Francis C. Hayes, and Hart Crane. He spent time in Eden Mills, Vermont, and the Catskills. He witnessed the Wall Street crash in October, encountered Harlem music and Black cultural life, and read the city through economic violence, racial oppression, mechanization, loneliness, and apocalyptic religious imagery. *Poeta en Nueva York* is therefore both urban critique and intensely personal transformation; it should not be treated as literal reportage of every scene.

He wrote the experimental film scenario *Viaje a la luna* for possible collaboration with Emilio Amero. The Aladrén rupture, tension with Dalí and Buñuel, unwanted “gypsy poet” celebrity, and concealment of his sexuality contributed to depression or melancholy. “Nervous breakdown” is too precise a diagnosis for the evidence. Letters show alternating anguish, theatrical sociability, productivity, and reassurance to his family.

In March 1930 he went to Cuba at the invitation of the Institución Hispano-Cubana de Cultura. In Havana, Caibarién, Cienfuegos, and Santiago de Cuba he lectured on poetry, Góngora, lullabies, architecture of cante jondo, and his own practice. He befriended the Loynaz siblings, Lydia Cabrera, José María Chacón y Calvo, Nicolás Guillén, José Lezama Lima, Luis Cardoza y Aragón, and others; attended an Afro-Cuban Abakuá/ñáñigo ceremony with Cabrera; wrote “Son de negros en Cuba” and “Oda a Walt Whitman”; and worked on *El público*. He entered a clinic for removal of warts, the only notable documented medical intervention in this trip. He sailed home on 12 June and reached Cádiz on 30 June. The itinerary is detailed in the [Lorca Center 1929–30 chronology](https://www.centrofedericogarcialorca.es/es/fgl/bio-1929-1930).

### 1930–1933: Republican cultural work and La Barraca

Back in Spain, Lorca completed *El público*, a formally radical drama about theatrical truth, masks, and same-sex desire that he did not see professionally staged. *La zapatera prodigiosa* opened in December 1930. In 1931 he and **La Argentinita** recorded five 78-rpm discs containing ten **Canciones populares españolas**, sung by her with Lorca at the piano. These are authentic recordings of his piano playing, not of his speaking voice.

The proclamation of the Second Republic in April 1931 opened a new cultural-policy field. With Education Ministry/Federación Universitaria Escolar support, Lorca and **Eduardo Ugarte** directed **La Barraca**, a university touring theatre taking Lope, Calderón, Cervantes, Tirso, and other classics to towns and rural audiences. Lorca directed, acted, adapted, composed or selected music, coached speech, and helped design the visual world. The company began touring in July 1932 and traveled widely through Castile, Galicia, Asturias, Cantabria, Navarre, Aragón, Valencia, Andalusia, and elsewhere.

The project was publicly subsidized: Lorca told Silvio d’Amico that an initial annual grant of **100,000 pesetas** for roughly thirty people, sets, costumes, props, and fuel was reduced to 50,000 and then removed by a later government. The statement is preserved in scholarship quoted by a [UAB study of Lorca’s theatre](https://ddd.uab.cat/pub/tesis/2016/hdl_10803_399720/sufe1de1.pdf). It was cultural democratization and also state cultural policy; conservative critics attacked its Republican sponsorship, repertory choices, and supposed partisanship.

He completed *Así que pasen cinco años* (1931), *Bodas de sangre* (1932), and continued *Diván del Tamarit*. *Bodas de sangre* opened in Madrid on 8 March 1933. That year he signed an anti-Hitler manifesto, publicly associated with anti-fascist intellectual causes, and entered a relationship with **Rafael Rodríguez Rapún**, the athletic secretary of La Barraca. Rodríguez Rapún later died from wounds in 1937 while serving the Republican army. The [Lorca Center 1930–33 record](https://www.centrofedericogarcialorca.es/es/fgl/bio-1930-1933) documents the tours and relationship.

### 1933–1934: Argentina and Uruguay

Invited by actress **Lola Membrives** and her husband/manager **Juan Reforzo** after the Buenos Aires success of *Bodas de sangre*, Lorca sailed from Barcelona on the *Conte Grande* on 29 September 1933 and arrived **13 October** after calls at Las Palmas, Rio de Janeiro, and Montevideo. He lived at the **Hotel Castelar**, Avenida de Mayo, through most of the visit.

Buenos Aires treated him as a celebrity. He supervised or participated in productions of *Bodas de sangre*, the complete *La zapatera prodigiosa*, *Mariana Pineda*, *Retablillo de don Cristóbal*, and an adaptation of Lope’s *La dama boba*; delivered *Juego y teoría del duende*, *Cómo canta una ciudad de noviembre a noviembre*, *Un poeta en Nueva York*, and cante jondo lectures; and gave a joint lecture with Pablo Neruda on Rubén Darío. His network included Neruda, Victoria Ocampo, Oliverio Girondo, Norah Lange, Ricardo Molinari, Raúl González Tuñón, Salvador Novo, Jorge Larco, Alfonsina Storni, and Carlos Gardel. Stories of the Gardel meeting derive from later recollection but are plausible and widely transmitted.

He visited Rosario in December and spent January–February 1934 in Montevideo, lodging at Hotel Carrasco and seeing Enrique Díez-Canedo, Enrique Amorim, José Mora Guarnido, and Juana de Ibarbourou. He returned to Buenos Aires and sailed on the *Conte Biancamano* on 27 March, reaching Barcelona on 11 April. Argentina’s National Library gives the same arrival and departure dates in its [Granada-to-Buenos Aires program](https://www.bn.gov.ar/agenda-cultural/federico-garcia-lorca-de-granada-a-buenos-aires).

The commercial success was decisive. *Bodas de sangre* reached about **150 performances**, and royalties, production, and lecture income gave Lorca economic independence for the first time, according to [Universo Lorca’s Argentina account](https://www.universolorca.com/en/biography-of-federico-garcia-lorca/argentinean-transcendence-1933/). Exact ledgers and net totals are not publicly established in the sources consulted.

### 1934–July 1936: mature theatre and widening political exposure

After returning, Lorca finished *Yerma* and *Diván del Tamarit*. The bullfighter, playwright, patron, and friend **Ignacio Sánchez Mejías** died after a goring in August 1934; Lorca’s four-part *Llanto por Ignacio Sánchez Mejías* became one of his major elegies. *Yerma*, starring Margarita Xirgu, opened at Madrid’s Teatro Español on 29 December 1934 amid success and right-wing hostility.

In 1935 he finished *Doña Rosita la soltera*, saw *Yerma* triumph in Barcelona, worked on the **Sonetos del amor oscuro**, and left La Barraca because other work consumed his time. *Seis poemas galegos* appeared at Nós in Santiago de Compostela under Eduardo Blanco-Amor’s care. He reconnected with Dalí in Barcelona and received a mass banquet at the Hotel Majestic.

In 1936 he published *Bodas de sangre* and *Primeras canciones*, participated in support for imprisoned Brazilian communist Luís Carlos Prestes, joined tributes to Alberti, Cernuda, Valle-Inclán, and others, adhered to a Popular Front welcome for French writers, and signed a manifesto against António de Oliveira Salazar’s Portuguese dictatorship. He completed *La casa de Bernarda Alba* on 19 June and wrote the first act of *Los sueños de mi prima Aurelia* and the surviving act of *Comedia sin título*/*El sueño de la vida*.

On 10 June, in his last major interview with cartoonist **Luis Bagaría** for *El Sol*, Lorca rejected blind nationalism—“before this I am a man of the world and brother of everyone”—and discussed Granada’s fearful bourgeois culture. The interview is catalogued by the [Open University of Catalonia archive](https://ace.uoc.edu/items/show/1186). On 13 July he left the manuscript packet of *Poeta en Nueva York* at José Bergamín’s office; on 14 July he reached the Huerta de San Vicente. Friends warned against Granada, but the proposition that he consciously chose martyrdom is retrospective interpretation, not documentary fact.

## Political / Religious / Intellectual / Artistic Development

### Politics

Lorca was not proved to have held a card in the Socialist or Communist parties. He was a friend and former pupil of Socialist Fernando de los Ríos, directed a Republican-funded company, signed anti-fascist and left-cultural petitions, supported political prisoners, and increasingly spoke of poverty and social obligation. In 1933 his name appeared among the signatories establishing the **Asociación de Amigos de la Unión Soviética**; the surviving manifesto expressed curiosity and sympathy toward Soviet construction, not party membership [1933 manifesto transcription](https://nodulo.org/bib/drio/19330211.htm).

His politics were anti-authoritarian, socially sympathetic, Republican in cultural practice, and deliberately nonsectarian in personal relations. He maintained conservative and Falangist acquaintances, including Luis Rosales and José María Pemán’s circles, while also being close to Neruda, Alberti, María Teresa León, Cernuda, and de los Ríos. Claims that he was “apolitical” omit his signatures and public cultural work; claims that he was a disciplined Communist equally exceed the evidence.

### Religion

He was baptized and saturated in Catholic scripture, ritual, saints, Marian imagery, Holy Week, sacramental theatre, and the sound world of the liturgy. Early poetry struggles directly with God, sin, Christ, and unbelief; *Oda al Santísimo Sacramento del Altar* was dedicated to Catholic Falla. He could also be irreverent and anticlerical in letters. “Devout Catholic,” “atheist,” and “anti-religious” each simplify an unstable, artistically productive relation to Christianity.

### Aesthetic development

His synthesis joined children’s song, romance, ballad, flamenco, Andalusian Arabic and Jewish memory, Gongorism, symbolism, modernist image, cubism, surrealist techniques, silent cinema, jazz, puppet theatre, and Golden Age drama. Lorca denied automatic writing as his method: even his most “surreal” work is tightly constructed. His “duende” lecture described an embodied, death-aware power of performance—not a scientific theory or a simple synonym for flamenco soul.

His theatre moved from symbolist verse drama through puppet farce and historical romance to experimental “impossible theatre” (*El público*, *Así que pasen cinco años*), rural tragedy, and the austere prose of *La casa de Bernarda Alba*. Recurring structures include frustrated desire, sterility, social surveillance, gendered confinement, class power, blood, moon, water, horse, knife, and theatrical masks.

Lorca had no spouse or children. Because legal and social conditions forced discretion, the surviving romantic record is uneven. “Friend,” “secretary,” and “companion” in older editions may conceal desire, but it is equally unsafe to convert every intimate male friendship into a sexual relationship.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

- **University of Granada / University of Madrid:** formal education; law degree, 1923.
- **Residencia de Estudiantes:** residence and principal Madrid intellectual network, 1919–26 intermittently.
- **Centro Artístico de Granada / El Rinconcillo:** early performance and literary circles.
- **Gallo and Pavo:** Granada magazines, 1928; brief collaborative editorial projects, not durable businesses.
- **La Barraca:** Republican-subsidized university theatre, founded 1931–32; artistic director Lorca, co-director Eduardo Ugarte. It was not Lorca’s private company or property.
- **Theatre companies:** Martínez Sierra/Catalina Bárcena, Margarita Xirgu/Cipriano Rivas Cherif, Lola Membrives/Juan Reforzo, Club Anfistora/Pura Ucelay, and La Argentinita were the principal production partnerships.
- **Publishers:** Maroto; Revista de Occidente; Litoral; Ulises; Cruz y Raya/Ediciones del Árbol; Héroe; Nós; Séneca; Norton; and numerous posthumous houses. Lorca often delayed or revised publication and earned less from print than his fame might suggest.
- **Technology/media:** Five commercial phonograph discs with La Argentinita; radio appearances in Spain, Argentina, and Cuba; experimental film script *Viaje a la luna*. No verified recording of his speaking voice is known. Claims that 1933 Buenos Aires radio wires preserve it have not yielded accessible authenticated audio.
- **Intellectual property:** copyrights and manuscripts passed through the family estate. No patents or inventions are documented.

## Arrest, Political Violence, and Death

### Immediate setting

The military rebellion reached Granada on 20 July 1936 and produced systematic killings of Republican officeholders, trade unionists, teachers, intellectuals, and suspected leftists. Lorca’s brother-in-law and mayor **Manuel Fernández-Montesinos** was arrested on 20 July and shot on 16 August. Falangist groups searched and harassed the Lorca household between 6 and 9 August; episodes included abuse of household staff and pressure connected with local political and property feuds.

On 9 August Lorca moved to the home of the **Rosales**, a prominent Falangist family, believing their status would protect him. On 16 August a group led by former CEDA deputy **Ramón Ruiz Alonso** arrested him and took him to the Civil Government. Luis Rosales, José Rosales, Manuel de Falla, and others sought his release. Civil governor **José Valdés Guzmán** held authority locally; later accounts say he consulted General **Gonzalo Queipo de Llano**, whose reported authorization—often rendered “give him coffee, lots of coffee”—is hearsay, not a surviving order.

Lorca was transferred toward Víznar, probably held at **La Colonia**, and shot without trial with other prisoners. The traditional group is schoolteacher **Dióscoro Galindo González** and anarchist banderilleros **Francisco Galadí Melgar** and **Joaquín Arcollas Cabezas**. Some accounts add a fourth victim. The precise firing party, sequence, and burial location are unresolved.

### Date and place

- **Best-supported conventional reconstruction:** night of 18–19 August or early 19 August 1936, between Víznar and Alfacar. Agustín Penón and Ian Gibson rely especially on servant Angelina Cordobilla’s recollection that she brought Lorca food on two days before being told he was gone. The [Gobierno Civil site history](https://www.universolorca.com/en/lugar/civil-government-1933-1944/) explains this evidentiary chain.
- **Other dates:** 17 and 18 August appear in witnesses, commemorations, news reports, and later reconstructions. A 2026 report argued for dawn on the 17th. Because no body, execution register, or contemporaneous death record has fixed the event, the dossier preserves the range.
- **Place:** along the Víznar–Alfacar road, in or near the Barranco de Víznar, Fuente Grande/Ainadamar, or an adjacent burial zone. “At the cemetery wall” is inconsistent with the strongest transport testimony.

### Motives: overlapping rather than exclusive

1. **Political/cultural:** He was internationally prominent, associated with the Republic, de los Ríos, La Barraca, anti-fascist statements, and social criticism. Killing him silenced a potent cultural voice.
2. **Homophobic:** Enemies used slurs; the 1965 police report treated “homosexual practices” as incriminating; later testimony attributes sexual taunts to killers. Sexual hostility plausibly intensified his vulnerability.
3. **Local faction and family/property conflict:** Ruiz Alonso’s CEDA background, rivalry with Falangists who sheltered Lorca, disputes involving the García/Roldán/Alba families, and Granada vendettas supplied proximate actors and motives.
4. **Freemasonry:** The 1965 report called Lorca a Mason of the “Alhambra lodge.” No lodge register, initiation record, or independent contemporary documentation located in this research establishes membership. Treat it as a hostile police allegation, not fact.

The first official Franco-state narrative known publicly is a two-page **9 July 1965 Granada police report**, prepared after pressure arising from Marcelle Auclair’s inquiries. It admits arrest by forces of the rebel authorities and execution after an alleged confession, labels him socialist and Mason, and cites homosexuality. It is valuable as regime admission but was written 29 years later, contains ideological slurs, and is not a judicial finding. RTVE summarizes the document and reproduces its core claims [RTVE, 23 April 2015](https://www.rtve.es/noticias/20150423/informe-policial-1965-prueba-asesinato-lorca-por-mason-practicas-homosexuales/1134650.shtml).

### Body searches and legal status

No court tried Lorca; there was no indictment or conviction. His killing was an extrajudicial political murder. The crime has not produced a criminal conviction of an individual perpetrator.

Agustín Penón investigated in Granada in 1955–56, collecting testimony later organized by Marta Osorio. Claude Couffon, Gerald Brenan, Marcelle Auclair, Eduardo Molina Fajardo, José Luis Vila-San-Juan, and Ian Gibson offered competing reconstructions. Excavations in **2009** at the long-favored Alfacar site found no grave there; later searches in 2014 and 2016 also failed to locate Lorca. New mass graves and 132 bodies found in the broader Víznar ravine led Granada prosecutors in the 2020s to examine crimes against humanity, but they did not identify Lorca. A victim’s descendant continues to seek Galindo and the other men [El País, 30 May 2024](https://elpais.com/espana/2024-05-30/la-nieta-de-dioscoro-galindo-pide-personarse-en-la-investigacion-para-que-se-busquen-los-restos-de-garcia-lorca-y-de-su-abuelo.html).

The Lorca family long opposed a targeted spectacle around his bones, while allowing broader searches and possible identification arrangements. Its position has been criticized by other victims’ descendants; the family has answered that Lorca should remain among the anonymous dead and that media exposure would be intrusive. Neither position proves that the family secretly removed the body.

## Controversies, Allegations, and Disputed Narratives

### “Red,” Communist, or apolitical

- **Documented:** Republican cultural office, progressive social statements, anti-fascist and pro-Prestes actions, Soviet-friendship manifesto signature, left-wing friendships.
- **Not documented:** Communist Party membership, revolutionary conspiracy, espionage, or violent activity.
- **Assessment:** “Socialist” in the 1965 report is a police classification. “Apolitical poet” is equally misleading.

### Freemason

- **Claim:** member of Granada’s Alhambra lodge, coded name “Homero.”
- **Source:** Franco-era police files and later retellings.
- **Evidence:** no verified lodge documentation surfaced in the consulted record.
- **Status:** uncorroborated allegation used to rationalize repression.

### Sexuality and retrospective censorship

Lorca’s homosexuality is established by correspondence, love relationships, testimony, and work; it is not merely a rumor invented after death. The interpretive controversy concerns how particular texts map onto particular lovers. Under Franco, publishers and family editors often euphemized the subject. The eleven late sonnets circulated privately, appeared in French, then in a clandestine red 1983 Spanish edition sent to about 250 recipients, apparently to force publication; ABC printed an authorized edition in 1984 as *Sonetos de amor*, dropping “oscuro.” A 2025 account reconstructs that episode [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/culture/2025/jun/13/spanish-publisher-reprints-sonnets-of-dark-love-federico-garcia-lorca).

### Foundation finances—not Lorca’s conduct

The later Federico García Lorca Foundation became entangled in financial and governance controversy during construction of the Granada center. In 2015 Laura García Lorca accused former manager **Juan Tomás Martín** of misappropriating millions of euros; Spanish authorities also blocked export of the archive amid fears of sale. These events concern posthumous estate administration, not fraud by Federico. They should not be projected backward onto him.

### Roma representation and orientalism

*Romancero gitano*, cante jondo work, and Andalusian-Arabic imagery generated enormous visibility but can romanticize marginalized communities through an educated landowner’s avant-garde lens. Lorca insisted that “the gypsies are a theme and nothing more” and rejected being marketed as primitive. Both cultural preservation and appropriation debates belong to the reception history; neither makes his stylized poetry ethnography.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### The poet who foresaw and authored his own death

**Story:** death imagery, the *Llanto*, and his return to Granada prove Lorca foresaw execution or deliberately chose martyrdom.  
**Appearance/circulation:** elegies after 1936, biographies, commemorative journalism, and readings of recurring moon/blood/knife symbols.  
**Evidence:** he wrote obsessively about death and ignored warnings, but also planned Mexico travel, future plays, publications, and life with Ramírez de Lucas.  
**Effect:** turns an administrative murder into tragic destiny, sometimes obscuring perpetrators and contingency.

### “Killed only because he was gay” / “only because he was left-wing”

Each mono-causal version serves later political memory. Documentary evidence shows homophobic stigma, political profiling, Republican associations, and local vendettas working together. The relative weight cannot be measured exactly.

### Shot in the buttocks or tortured for homosexuality

Later testimony and retellings claim executioners fired into his backside and used sexualized torture. No body exists for forensic confirmation; witness chains are late and sometimes contradictory. Sexual taunts are plausible and supported more broadly, but the precise ballistic story is **unverified**.

### Secret burial, family removal, or transfer to the Valley of the Fallen

The failure of excavations produced stories that the family privately removed him, Franco’s agents moved him, or his bones were transported with unidentified Republican remains to the Valley of the Fallen before its 1959 inauguration. Marta Osorio’s publication of Penón/Llanos correspondence revived removal possibilities [El País, 19 August 2015](https://english.elpais.com/elpais/2015/08/19/inenglish/1439971024_385486.html). No chain of custody, remains, or definitive document proves any version. Their historical importance lies in exposing Spain’s unresolved mass-grave politics.

### Franco tried to save Lorca / the Rosales delivered him

Apologetic narratives sometimes claim the central rebel leadership would have saved him if informed; hostile narratives blame all Rosales brothers. Surviving evidence places the arrest under Granada rebel authority and shows Falangist Luis Rosales attempting intervention. A signed Franco rescue order does not exist, nor does proof that Luis conspired in the arrest.

### Secret weekly dinners with José Antonio Primo de Rivera

Gabriel Celaya and later writers circulated a story that Lorca dined regularly with the Falange founder. They moved in intersecting Madrid circles and a meeting is possible; the weekly ritual is not securely documented. The story has been used both to depoliticize Lorca and to imagine cross-ideological cultural fellowship.

### A lost recording of Lorca’s voice

Lorca made radio appearances, including a farewell from Radio Stentor, and reports periodically claim that Buenos Aires wire recordings survive. Searches in Argentine and Uruguayan sound archives have not produced authenticated speech. The La Argentinita discs preserve his piano, not voice. The missing voice has become an archival legend.

### Juan Eduardo Cirlot connection

No documented personal meeting or collaboration has been established. **Juan-Eduardo Cirlot was born in 1916 and was twenty when Lorca was killed.** The link is a postwar line of reception: both began with music, both used surrealist/visionary imagery, symbols, death, and permutation or sound, and later scholarship compares their treatments of *Hamlet* and Spanish poetic modernism [Liverpool University Press](https://www.liverpooluniversitypress.co.uk/doi/10.3828/bhs.2015.39). Cirlot’s *Diccionario de símbolos* is frequently used to interpret Lorca, but applying its fixed entries cannot prove Lorca’s own intended meanings.

### Music and recordings

- **Canciones populares españolas** / **Canciones populares antiguas** (1931): five commercial 78-rpm discs, ten traditional songs, Encarnación López Júlvez “La Argentinita” singing and castanets, Lorca piano and harmonization. Genuine performance; the underlying melodies/texts are traditional, not wholly composed by Lorca.
- Additional youthful piano compositions and song harmonizations survive in manuscript or edited reconstruction. Attribution should distinguish Lorca’s arrangement from folk source.
- No authenticated recording of his spoken or singing voice has been made public.

### Drawings and film

Hundreds of drawings survive—portraits, saints, clowns, sailors, moons, hands, blood, and hybrid figures. They are genuine visual works, though individual dating and titles are often editorial. The standard corpus is Mario Hernández, ed., *Libro de los dibujos de Federico García Lorca* (1990).

*Viaje a la luna* (1929) is Lorca’s genuine experimental film script. Emilio Amero did not complete the intended film with him; Frederic Amat’s 1998 film is a posthumous realization, not a Lorca-directed movie.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

News of the murder circulated unevenly in wartime. Nationalist authorities denied, minimized, or blamed uncontrolled actors; Republican and international writers made Lorca a symbol of murdered Spain. Antonio Machado’s “El crimen fue en Granada,” Neruda’s commemorations, and Xirgu’s exile productions helped establish the martyr image.

Francoist censorship prohibited open discussion of the murder and restricted publication and performance. The 1954 Aguilar *Obras completas* was a landmark return but was not genuinely complete and muted homosexuality and politics. Abroad, especially in Mexico, Argentina, the United States, France, and Britain, editions, productions, and translations kept the work active. Buenos Aires hosted the world première of *La casa de Bernarda Alba* in 1945.

The post-Franco period produced competing Lorcas: national classic, Andalusian folkloric emblem, Republican martyr, gay/queer ancestor, international modernist, theatre innovator, and victim among tens of thousands. Recovery of *El público*, the sonnets, letters, drawings, and the *Poeta en Nueva York* manuscript repeatedly changed the canon. His archive remains incomplete; letters, manuscripts, drawings, and any authentic speaking voice may still surface.

### Buenos Aires and the Jardín de los Poetas

Lorca’s 1933–34 stay is not a minor tour: it supplied mass audiences, money, collaborators, and a durable Argentine performance tradition. His Hotel Castelar room became a memorial space; Teatro Avenida remains central to the story.

His bust stands in the **Jardín de los Poetas**, within El Rosedal, Parque Tres de Febrero, Palermo. Buenos Aires’s official tourism page lists Lorca among the poets honored there [City of Buenos Aires](https://buenosaires.gob.ar/gcaba_historico/noticias/se-cumplen-110-anos-del-rosedal-de-palermo). The municipal-art inventory reproduced by WebOeba identifies **MOA no. 2477**, sculptor **Eduardo Carretero (1920–2011)**, and inauguration on **19 August 1986**, the fiftieth anniversary [monument record](https://weboeba.com/palermo/jardinpoetas/oe/fgarcialorca.html). This corrects web claims that the bust was inaugurated in 2014 or designed by Takutaro Yabushi; those details belong to other Rosedal objects or anniversaries.

### Institutional memorials and honors

Lorca received acclaim, banquets, and civic tributes during life but no major competitive literary prize comparable to the later awards bearing his name. Posthumous honors include museums at Fuente Vaqueros, Valderrubio, and Huerta de San Vicente; the Centro Federico García Lorca; parks and statues in Granada, Madrid, Alfacar, Buenos Aires, and many other cities; a San Francisco Rainbow Honor Walk plaque; innumerable schools and theatres; and the City of Granada Federico García Lorca International Poetry Prize, created in 2004.

## Research Limits

- No complete public execution file, body, autopsy, contemporaneous death certificate, or signed execution order was located.
- Exact private finances, royalties, and contracts are incompletely published.
- The romantic archive is damaged by secrecy, censorship, destroyed or withheld correspondence, and retrospective testimony.
- “Complete works” is editorial: lost projects, variants, juvenilia, adaptations, scattered journalism, and hundreds of drawings prevent a final closed list.
- Research stopped when the main chronological and bibliographic slots had institutional or scholarly support, consequential disputes were explicitly bounded, and further searches were returning derivative repetition rather than new primary evidence.

## Atlas Connections

### Alberto Gerchunoff

- **[T5]** **Documented fact:** Gerchunoff and Lorca attended a Sociedad Argentina de Autores/PEN gathering honoring Lorca at Buenos Aires’s Café Tortoni in November 1933. An archival photograph, identified by contemporary annotations on its reverse, shows Gerchunoff seated beside Lorca. [PEN 100 Archive](https://www.pen100archive.org/garcia-lorca-at-cafe-tortoni/)

### Alfonsina Storni

- **[T5]** **Documented fact:** Lorca met Storni through the Signo literary gathering at the Hotel Castelar during his 1933–34 Buenos Aires stay; contemporary chronologies place their acquaintance in 1933. [Centro Virtual Cervantes](https://cvc.cervantes.es/actcult/storni/cronologia/cronologia03.htm)

### Carlos Gardel

- **[T5]** **Reported fact:** Later accounts place a brief meeting or greeting between Gardel and Lorca on Calle Corrientes, Buenos Aires, during the night of 6 November 1933, when Lorca was accompanied by Pablo Suero and César Tiempo. Neither principal publicly recorded the encounter, and the surviving account depends on later recollection; it should therefore not be elevated to fully documented fact. [Account and evidentiary qualification](https://www.cazarabet.com/conversacon/fichas/fichas1/tangofederico.htm)

### H. G. Wells

- **[T3]** **Documented fact:** After Lorca disappeared in 1936, Wells, then president of PEN International, sent an inquiry to the rebel authorities requesting news of his “distinguished colleague.” This constitutes one-sided written engagement concerning Lorca, although not correspondence received by him. **Reported fact:** a recent PEN-based account additionally says Wells had met Lorca at an Argentine congress in 1933; absent a located contemporary attendance record, that personal meeting remains reported rather than independently established. [Cadena SER/PEN account](https://cadenaser.com/nacional/2026/08/18/el-telegrama-de-hg-wells-sobre-lorca-que-cambio-la-lucha-por-la-libertad-de-prensa-cadena-ser/)

### Jorge Luis Borges

- **[T5]** **Documented fact:** Borges later recalled an approximately hour-long conversation with Lorca in Buenos Aires during Lorca’s 1933–34 visit; the Centro Virtual Cervantes also includes Borges among the writers Lorca saw there. Borges subsequently characterized him critically as an “andaluz profesional,” confirming personal acquaintance rather than mere contemporaneity. [Centro Virtual Cervantes](https://cvc.cervantes.es/literatura/lorca_america/introduccion.htm), [Harvard Instituto Cervantes study citing Borges’s recorded recollection](https://cervantesobservatorio.fas.harvard.edu/sites/default/files/88_sp_historias_de_hispanistas_en_wellesley_1.pdf)
- **[T3]** **Documented fact:** Borges published two Lorca poems in *Proa* in June 1925 and later discussed Lorca repeatedly in interviews, usually critically. This is documented editorial and one-sided written/oral engagement independent of their meetings. [Harvard Instituto Cervantes study](https://cervantesobservatorio.fas.harvard.edu/sites/default/files/88_sp_historias_de_hispanistas_en_wellesley_1.pdf)

### Miguel de Cervantes

- **[T3]** **Documented fact:** Lorca engaged directly with Cervantes’s writings by adapting and directing several of the *Entremeses* for La Barraca beginning in 1932. This is a one-sided textual and theatrical engagement with Cervantes’s work, not a personal crossing.

### Victoria Ocampo

- **[T5]** **Documented fact:** Ocampo personally knew Lorca and belonged to his Argentine intellectual network during the 1933–34 visit. Her journal *Sur* issued an edition of *Romancero gitano* before the end of 1933. [Biblioteca Nacional Argentina](https://museo.bn.gov.ar/noticias/7-de-abril-de-1890-nace-victoria-ocampo), [scholarly account](https://revistachilenadederecho.uc.cl/index.php/alch/article/download/32831/25411/82605)
- **[T3]** **Documented fact:** After Lorca’s murder, Ocampo published an address to him in *Sur* in 1936, ending by asking whether he could hear her. Together with *Sur*’s publication of his work, this establishes continuing written and editorial engagement. [Fundación Juan March study](https://digital.march.es/fedora/objects/fjm-pub%3A6129/datastreams/OBJ/content)

### Juan Eduardo Cirlot

- **[T3]** **Absence finding:** No personal meeting, correspondence, or direct one-sided writing by either man about the other has been established. Later scholars compare their symbolism and treatments of *Hamlet*, but third-party comparison does not itself satisfy an Atlas connection type; no connection is assigned.

### Pablo Picasso

- **[T4]** **Absence finding:** Lorca sought or hoped for contact with Picasso and admired his painting, but the available biographical account states that the two never met. Shared Spanish and Parisian artistic milieus, later joint exhibitions, and posthumous comparisons do not establish proximity at a particular event or a personal relationship; no positive Atlas connection is assigned. [Contemporary report on the Lorca–Picasso research](https://www.eltiempo.com/archivo/documento/MAM-367181)
