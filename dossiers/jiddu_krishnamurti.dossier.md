# Jiddu Krishnamurti Research Dossier

**Prepared:** 24 August 2026  
**Scope:** Life, institutions, writings, disputes, mythology, and legacy, 1895–1986 and after  
**Method:** Documentary reconstruction using primary texts, legal judgments, institutional archives, contemporaneous reporting, memoirs, and modern scholarship. Claims are labeled where the evidence is disputed. Dates before 1909 and private-life claims carry the greatest source limitations.

---

## Source and Evidence Note

Krishnamurti’s record is unusually stratified:

1. **Theosophical promotional records (1909–29)** present clairvoyant discovery, initiations, Masters, overshadowing, and World Teacher prophecy as fact within their own system.
2. **Krishnamurti’s own later statements** reject spiritual authority but do not offer a conventional autobiography. He often spoke of himself in the third person and left the nature of his altered states unresolved.
3. **Authorized or foundation-adjacent biographies**, especially Mary Lutyens and Pupul Jayakar, have unmatched access but are sympathetic and sometimes protective.
4. **Independent biographies**, notably Roland Vernon and Christine V. Williams, historicize the “invention of a messiah” and test institutional narratives.
5. **The Rajagopal family memoir** by Radha Rajagopal Sloss disclosed the sexual relationship and alleged abortions and institutional hypocrisy. Mary Lutyens later acknowledged the affair while contesting parts of Sloss’s account.
6. **Legal and corporate papers** document the guardianship case and later property/copyright conflict more securely than recollection, although a complete digitized docket of the California litigation is not readily available.

Accordingly, this dossier distinguishes court findings, contemporary documentation, recollection, institutional interpretation, occult claim, and unresolved medical or historical question.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and social background

- **Father:** **Jiddu Narayaniah** (also rendered Narayaniah/Narianiah). A Telugu-speaking Brahmin and employee of the British colonial administration, later retired. He joined the Theosophical Society in 1882 and, after retirement, obtained work at its Adyar headquarters. In the 1914 Privy Council judgment he was described as a Hindu Brahmin of modest means.
- **Mother:** **Sanjeevamma** (spellings vary). Krishnamurti remembered her with exceptional intensity. She died in 1905, when he was about ten. Later narratives connect his childhood reports of seeing her after death with his openness to invisible presences; these reports are memories and family lore, not independently verifiable events.
- **Children in the family:** Sources usually state that Narayaniah and Sanjeevamma had eleven children, six surviving childhood. Krishnamurti was conventionally described as the eighth child/son, and his given name was associated with Krishna, himself an eighth child in Hindu tradition.
- **Key sibling:** **Jiddu Nityananda (“Nitya”)**, born 30 May 1898 according to the Privy Council record; died 13 November 1925 from tuberculosis. The brothers’ emotional dependence was profound, intensified by removal from India and their Theosophical upbringing. Nitya’s death was the decisive personal crisis of Krishnamurti’s twenties.

### Ancestry and caste

The family belonged to the Telugu Brahmin milieu of the Madras Presidency. “Brahmin” in the sources identifies caste and ritual-social location, not proof that Krishnamurti received advanced Sanskritic schooling. His later speech rarely rested on inherited Hindu authority, and he explicitly treated caste, nationality, and organized religion as conditioning. No credible evidence establishes aristocratic ancestry, inherited wealth, or a family commercial empire.

### Surrogate and constructed family

- **Annie Besant** became guardian, patron, and surrogate mother. Krishnamurti’s letters called her “Amma” (“mother”).
- **C. W. Leadbeater** was discoverer, occult trainer, and architect of the World Teacher identity, but not legal parent.
- **Nitya** remained his closest biological relationship until 1925.
- **D. Rajagopal, Rosalind Rajagopal, and their daughter Radha** formed his Ojai household and quasi-family from the late 1920s into the postwar period.
- **Mary Zimbalist**, **Pupul Jayakar**, **Mary Lutyens**, **Sunanda Patwardhan**, **Nandini Mehta**, and other long-term associates formed later circles of care, travel, publishing, and conversation.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1895–1909

### Madanapalle, Kadapa, and maternal loss

Krishnamurti was born in Madanapalle, a small administrative and market town. His childhood was marked by illness, periodic malaria, physical frailty, daydreaming, poor school performance, and punishments by teachers. The family spent time in Kadapa (Cuddapah); accounts place a move there in 1903. Biographers describe him as vague, inattentive, and sometimes assumed intellectually disabled by adults. These descriptions come substantially from later recollection and should not be retrodiagnosed.

His mother’s death in 1905 was the central early bereavement. The surviving household’s finances deteriorated after Narayaniah’s retirement. Seeking work and proximity to the movement he admired, Narayaniah moved the family to the Theosophical Society’s large estate at **Adyar**, near Madras, in January 1909.

### “Discovery” on the Adyar beach

In April or May 1909, **Charles Webster Leadbeater** noticed the undernourished, lice-infested boy near the Adyar beach. The canonical Theosophical story says Leadbeater clairvoyantly perceived an aura wholly without selfishness—the “most wonderful” he had seen—and identified Krishnamurti as a possible vehicle for Maitreya. Ernest Wood and others remembered the boy as academically slow, making the prophecy especially dramatic.

This episode has three evidentiary levels:

- **Documented fact:** Leadbeater selected Krishnamurti in 1909; intensive grooming and tutoring followed.
- **Contemporary occult claim:** Leadbeater saw an extraordinary aura and received confirmation from Masters.
- **Later mythic function:** The neglected Indian child recognized by clairvoyant insight became the movement’s annunciation story. Its evidentiary force depends entirely on accepting Leadbeater’s claimed clairvoyance.

The selection must also be read against Leadbeater’s controversial history. He had resigned from the Theosophical Society in 1906 amid allegations concerning sexual advice to boys and was later restored. No court established that he sexually abused Krishnamurti. Narayaniah’s later distrust and the guardianship litigation were nevertheless shaped by controversy around Leadbeater and access to the boys.

## Education and Formation

### Theosophical tutoring

Leadbeater and associates bathed, fed, exercised, and privately tutored Krishnamurti and Nitya. Krishnamurti rapidly acquired functional English, received etiquette and physical training, and was taught Theosophical cosmology: Masters, initiations, subtle bodies, karma, reincarnation, and the expected Maitreya. He was separated from ordinary Indian childhood and prepared for an international public role.

### Guardianship and removal to Europe

On 6 March 1910 Narayaniah signed a letter authorizing Besant to take charge of the boys and finance English education, including hoped-for Oxford study. The brothers left India with Besant in 1911/12 and lived or studied in England and continental Europe. Tutors included Theosophical associates; they mixed with wealthy supporters and learned languages, public manners, tennis, golf, motoring, and upper-class social conventions.

Krishnamurti repeatedly failed university entrance examinations. Plans for Oxford collapsed; he attended lectures or preparatory study in London and Paris but earned no university degree. Nitya was academically more successful and studied law. Krishnamurti’s formal education was therefore extensive but irregular: private tutoring, travel, elite socialization, and religious grooming rather than a completed credential.

### Formative teachers and influences

- **Leadbeater:** occult vocabulary, bodily discipline, imagined initiatory advancement, and the messianic role.
- **Besant:** maternal attachment, public service, internationalism, oratory, and organizational protection.
- **Nitya:** intellectual companion, translator of social circumstances, emotional anchor.
- **Theosophical critics:** Hostility from Hindus, journalists, dissident Theosophists, and Rudolf Steiner’s circle exposed the political and institutional costs of the project.
- **Grief and the “process”:** Nitya’s death and Krishnamurti’s recurrent altered states contributed more directly to his mature position than any completed academic course.
- **Later interlocutors:** Aldous Huxley, David Bohm, Pupul Jayakar, educational colleagues, Buddhist and Hindu figures, scientists, psychologists, and students helped shape the dialogical form of his mature work without becoming authorities he professed to follow.

## The Guardianship Litigation, 1912–1914

Narayaniah revoked Besant’s authority on 11 July 1912 and sued for custody. The Madras proceedings ordered that the boys be treated as wards and restored to their father. The litigation publicly aired the World Teacher scheme, Leadbeater controversy, the boys’ removal, and the authorship of *At the Feet of the Master*.

On 25 May 1914 the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council [allowed Besant’s appeal](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/in/56b49611607dba348f0166db), but on jurisdictional and procedural grounds rather than by declaring her occult project true. It held that a father’s delegation of custody was revocable in principle, yet the Chingleput court lacked jurisdiction over boys then abroad; their welfare and wishes required proper consideration by an English court. The Indian decree was discharged and the suit dismissed without prejudice to a proceeding in England. The boys, who expressed a desire to remain, stayed under Besant’s protection. The preceding [Madras High Court opinion](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/1214788/) preserves the competing lower-court reasoning.

**Court finding:** The order returning them to Narayaniah could not stand under the procedure used.  
**Not a court finding:** That Besant was an irrevocable guardian, that Leadbeater’s claims were true, or that Narayaniah was an unfit father.

## Early Career and the World Teacher Project, 1909–1925

### Alcyone and the first books

In 1910 *At the Feet of the Master* appeared under **Alcyone**, with a preface by Besant. The narrative given by Leadbeater was that the Master Koot Hoomi taught the sleeping boy, who wrote notes on waking; Leadbeater corrected and arranged them. The original notes disappeared.

**Authorship status:** disputed. Narayaniah alleged that the book had been “fathered” on his son to manufacture messianic credentials; supporters said witnesses saw Krishnamurti write and that Leadbeater did not compose it. Krishnamurti later said he did not remember writing it and did not resolve the question. The work should be catalogued as **attributed to Krishnamurti/Alcyone, probably heavily mediated within Leadbeater’s circle**, not as an uncontested autonomous composition by a fourteen-year-old.

### Order of the Star

In January 1911 a preparatory group became the **Order of the Star in the East (OSE)**, with Krishnamurti as its young head and Besant and Leadbeater as protectors, as summarized in the [Theosophical Society Adyar record](https://www.ts-adyar.org/j-krishnamurti-1895-1986). It was formally distinct from the Theosophical Society but grew through its network. Its organ, *The Herald of the Star*, tours, camps, youth groups, lodges, and donor appeals created a transnational messianic organization.

Krishnamurti’s first formal words are attributed to a 23 December 1910 Adyar meeting. Through the 1910s he spoke infrequently and shyly; by the 1920s he addressed large audiences in India, Britain, the Netherlands, Australia, and the United States. The foundation archive counts more than 5,300 events over his lifetime, beginning in 1910.

### Rudolf Steiner: a structural relationship, not a personal friendship

The promotion of Krishnamurti directly intersected with **Rudolf Steiner**, general secretary of the German Section of the Theosophical Society. Steiner rejected a new physical incarnation of Christ in an Indian boy, holding that Christ’s incarnation in Jesus was unique and that a future manifestation would be “etheric,” not another body. Steiner’s section refused admission to Order of the Star members. Besant revoked its charter in 1913; most German-speaking members followed Steiner into the Anthroposophical Society, founded in 1912. The sequence is treated in a [modern historical overview](https://wrldrels.org/2018/05/03/anthroposophy/) and a [focused study of Steiner’s transition](https://www.ej-theology.org/index.php/theology/article/view/74).

Thus Krishnamurti was a **catalyst and symbol** in the Theosophy–Anthroposophy schism, though there is no strong evidence of a sustained personal relationship or significant face-to-face exchange between the two men. Other doctrinal and governance conflicts preceded the break; it should not be reduced to Krishnamurti alone.

### Tours, properties, and patronage

Wealthy adherents supplied houses, travel, publishing infrastructure, and camp estates. Baron Philip van Pallandt made the **Eerde estate near Ommen, Netherlands**, available to the Star movement. Trusts held property rather than Krishnamurti operating a conventional personal business. Donations financed international publicity and gatherings; Krishnamurti himself generally showed little interest in accounts or manuscripts, leaving administration to others—a habit that later enabled severe control and ownership disputes.

### Ojai, Nitya’s tuberculosis, and “the process”

In 1922 Krishnamurti and Nitya traveled via Australia to California, seeking a climate helpful to Nitya’s tuberculosis. At **Ojai** they met **Rosalind Williams**, who helped nurse them. Supporters acquired Pine Cottage and surrounding land; the Ojai Valley became Krishnamurti’s enduring base.

Beginning 17 August 1922 he experienced neck pain, extreme sensitivity, appetite loss, childlike speech, apparent lapses of consciousness, and intense altered states. On 19 August he described identity with a road worker, pickaxe, stone, grass, and trees, followed by peace, compassion, and a sense of the Buddha’s presence. Related episodes recurred intermittently for decades and became known privately as **“the process.”**

Competing interpretations:

- **Krishnamurti’s report:** intense pain accompanied a qualitatively different consciousness or “otherness.”
- **Theosophical interpretation:** purification, initiatory preparation, or overshadowing by the World Teacher.
- **Psychological interpretation:** dissociation, regression under pain, grief, expectation, or trauma.
- **Neurological speculation:** epilepsy, migraine, or another paroxysmal condition.
- **Documentary limit:** no adequate contemporary neurological workup survives; retrospective diagnosis is not possible. Describing the events as “seizure-like” is phenomenological, not a diagnosis.

### Nitya’s death, 1925

Theosophical assurances had encouraged hope that Nitya would be protected. While Krishnamurti traveled by ship toward India, news arrived that Nitya had died on 13 November 1925. Witnesses described overwhelming grief. In later accounts, this shattered his confidence in the Masters as Leadbeater’s system presented them. It did not instantly produce the 1929 break, but it accelerated the erosion of inherited belief and the emergence of a language centered on direct perception rather than hierarchy.

## Chronological Life History, 1925–1929: From Proclamation to Renunciation

### Huizen and Adyar declarations

In 1925 Besant announced that the World Teacher’s manifestation was near and named prospective “apostles,” intensifying expectation and ridicule. At Ommen and Adyar, Krishnamurti’s speech sometimes shifted into a voice followers interpreted as Maitreya speaking through him. “Overshadowing” claims circulated. Whether he consciously accepted literal possession, used inherited language for his own experience, or was carried by collective expectation changed over time and cannot be reduced to a single moment.

By 1927 he increasingly said that the “Beloved” could not be monopolized by names or organizations. The OSE shortened its name to the **Order of the Star**. He rejected ceremonies, spiritual grades, dependence, and claims to exclusive access.

### Dissolution at Ommen, 3 August 1929

Before approximately 3,000 people at the Ommen Star Camp, with the address broadcast by radio, Krishnamurti dissolved the Order. In the [surviving dissolution address](https://www.organism.earth/library/document/dissolution-speech), his central declaration was: **“Truth is a pathless land.”** No organization, creed, priest, ritual, or psychological technique could lead another person to truth. His “only concern,” he said, was to set humanity “absolutely, unconditionally free.”

This was not merely resignation from office. He abolished the organization constructed around him, rejected discipleship and spiritual authority, and said he wanted no followers. Property and money given to the Order were, so far as practicable, returned to donors; Eerde reverted to van Pallandt. Some publishing and practical bodies continued in reorganized form.

**Documented act:** dissolution of the Order and rejection of exclusive spiritual authority.  
**Interpretive dispute:** whether this was heroic renunciation, a maturation already incubated by Theosophical teaching, a reaction to Nitya’s death, institutional failure, or a new charismatic strategy. These explanations are not mutually exclusive.  
**Common error:** saying he “dissolved the Theosophical Society.” He did not; he dissolved the Order of the Star and then severed Theosophical ties.

Besant remained personally devoted and continued to believe he was the World Teacher until her death in 1933. Leadbeater reportedly said that the Coming had “gone wrong,” though the exact wording is transmitted secondhand.

## Independent Work, 1930–1945

### Star Publishing Trust and the Ojai household

Krishnamurti continued worldwide talks without the old messianic institution. **Desikacharya Rajagopal (D. Rajagopal)** ran the **Star Publishing Trust**, later publishing bodies, copyrights, correspondence, donations, and property. Rosalind married Rajagopal in 1927; their daughter **Radha** was born in 1931. Krishnamurti, the Rajagopals, and Radha lived in overlapping household intimacy at **Arya Vihara** in Ojai.

The arrangement combined friendship, employment, fiduciary dependence, domestic life, and—secretly—sexual attachment. Krishnamurti’s lack of administrative engagement allowed Rajagopal extraordinary practical control. Conversely, Rajagopal carried decades of burdensome editorial and managerial labor. Later hostile accounts reduce one man to victim and the other to thief; the record supports a long mutual dependency that hardened into mistrust.

### The relationship with Rosalind Rajagopal

Radha Rajagopal Sloss alleged that Krishnamurti and Rosalind began a sexual relationship in 1932 and continued for roughly twenty-five years. She further alleged three pregnancies and abortions; a later [Sloss interview](https://beezone.com/current/the-shadow-side-of-krishnamurti-interview-with-radha-rajagopal-sloss.html) makes the evidentiary challenge explicit. Mary Lutyens’s rebuttal accepted that a long affair occurred; later associates reported that Krishnamurti privately admitted sexual relations. The relationship itself is therefore best treated as **documented to a high probability**, though never publicly acknowledged by Krishnamurti during his life.

The abortion count and some episodes of violence, manipulation, jealousy, and motive rest primarily on Sloss’s family account and contested recollection. They are **serious allegations, not court findings**. No evidence found establishes a criminal charge. Rosalind was married to Rajagopal; secrecy protected all three adults, their household, and Krishnamurti’s public image. Although Krishnamurti denied being a guru, many listeners assumed celibate saintliness. The gap between that image and the concealed affair became a major posthumous controversy.

### Aldous Huxley and wider literary networks

Krishnamurti met **Aldous Huxley** in the Ojai region in 1938. Their friendship centered on perception, conditioning, nationalism, war, and education. Huxley helped connect the work to a major publisher and later wrote the foreword to *The First and Last Freedom*. Other acquaintances or admirers included George Bernard Shaw, Henry Miller, Christopher Isherwood, and artists in California’s intellectual milieu; degrees of closeness vary and later admirer lists often overstate contact.

### Second World War and FBI surveillance

Unable to maintain normal international tours, Krishnamurti lived quietly at Ojai from 1940 to 1944, worked the land, and gave surplus produce to relief. His denunciations of nationalism and war were interpreted as pacifist or potentially subversive. FBI agents attended talks and questioned his solitary walks. The [1935–44 archival timeline](https://thelifeofkrishnamurti.kfa.org/timelines/timeline-1935-1944/) says surveillance ended after agents concluded he was not dangerous.

**Documented fact:** federal scrutiny related to antiwar speech.  
**No evidence found:** espionage, an intelligence-service relationship, sabotage, prosecution, imprisonment, or membership in a clandestine political organization.

## Postwar International Career, 1945–1967

### Resumption of speaking

Public talks resumed at Ojai in 1944 and internationally after the war. In 1947 Krishnamurti returned to India after a long absence and met national leaders and intellectuals, including **Jawaharlal Nehru**. He spoke in Bombay, Madras, Delhi, Varanasi, and educational centers, while continuing annual or frequent gatherings in Ojai, London, Paris, and later Saanen, Switzerland.

He did not join the Indian National Congress or accept state office. He opposed nationalism, ideological revolution, organized belief, and authority. His political content was psychological but concrete: war, communal division, fear, ambition, conformity, violence, and the state’s use of education.

### Publishing expansion

The postwar talks became *Commentaries on Living* and other collections. With Huxley’s foreword, *The First and Last Freedom* (1954) brought him a wider readership. Much of the apparent “authorship” was oral: stenographers or tape recorders captured talks; editors selected, titled, arranged, and sometimes smoothed them. Bibliographic descriptions must therefore distinguish handwritten works, dictated journals, transcripts, edited thematic compilations, and later anthologies.

### Saanen and European gatherings

From 1961, annual summer gatherings at **Saanen, Switzerland**, became a major institution without formal membership. Thousands camped nearby to hear talks and discussions. Krishnamurti continued until 1985, when age and health ended the series.

### David Bohm

The theoretical physicist **David Bohm** met Krishnamurti in the early 1960s. Their recorded dialogues examined thought, time, fragmentation, insight, the observer/observed division, and collective consciousness. Bohm served as a foundation trustee and gave the work unusual access to scientific audiences. Their relationship later became strained over governance, method, and Krishnamurti’s private behavior, but dialogue and personal regard survived in reduced form. Books including *The Ending of Time*, *The Future of Humanity*, and *The Limits of Thought* are edited transcripts, not jointly drafted treatises.

## Foundations, Schools, and Institutional Reorganization, 1968–1979

### New foundations

To move archives and publications beyond Rajagopal’s control and to secure an impersonal legacy, Krishnamurti and associates established new nonprofit foundations:

- **Krishnamurti Foundation Trust (United Kingdom)**, [registered in 1968](https://kfoundation.org/brockwood/).
- **Krishnamurti Foundation of America**, developed in the late 1960s/early 1970s.
- **Krishnamurti Foundation India**.
- A Spanish/Latin American foundation, later **Fundación Krishnamurti Latinoamericana**.

Their stated function was custodial—preserve and disseminate the teaching—without doctrinal or spiritual authority. Inevitably, they also became institutions interpreting authenticity, controlling archives and copyrights, appointing trustees, and operating schools. This tension between anti-organization teaching and institutional preservation remains part of the legacy.

### Schools and educational projects

Krishnamurti treated education as inquiry into the whole human being, not only job preparation. Schools were to unite academic rigor, care for nature, freedom from fear, scientific temper, and examination of conditioning.

| Institution | Role and approximate date |
|---|---|
| **Rishi Valley School**, near Madanapalle | Founded within the Krishnamurti educational movement in **1926**; later a major KFI residential school and rural-education center. Some secondary sources give 1928, reflecting acquisition/opening phases. |
| **Rajghat Besant School**, Varanasi | Founded in **1926** on/near the Ganges; developed as an Indian school and education center. |
| **Brockwood Park School**, Hampshire, England | Foundation established 1968; property acquired and school opened in **1969**. International residential school. |
| **Oak Grove School**, Ojai | [Established **1975**](https://www.kfa.org/the-oak-grove-school/) by KFA; began at Arya Vihara with three students and two teachers, later moved to Meiners Oaks. |
| Other KFI schools | The Valley School (Bengaluru), The School KFI (Chennai), and later schools grew through the foundation network; not all were personally founded in the same direct sense by Krishnamurti. |

He held hundreds of discussions with students, teachers, and parents. He did not create a fixed pedagogical method or curriculum carrying certification as “Krishnamurti technique.”

### Rajagopal litigation and financial/property conflict

By the 1960s the relationship with D. Rajagopal had collapsed. Disputes concerned:

- ownership of Ojai land and donated assets;
- control of Krishnamurti’s manuscripts, correspondence, recordings, and publication rights;
- accounting for funds and the legal identity of successive publishing organizations;
- whether Rajagopal held assets personally, as trustee, or for Krishnamurti’s work.

KFA and Krishnamurti pursued lengthy California litigation. A settlement in the mid-1970s recovered significant property and materials for the new foundation and ended parts of the dispute. Foundation-aligned narratives accuse Rajagopal of withholding or misusing assets; the Rajagopal family portrays the suits as oppressive and denies simplistic theft claims. Without the full docket and audited historical accounts, the safest finding is: **a documented civil fiduciary, property, and copyright dispute ending largely by settlement; no located criminal conviction for fraud, embezzlement, corruption, or money laundering.**

Krishnamurti’s conduct is also open to scrutiny. He had entrusted affairs without sustained oversight, later signed or supported aggressive legal action, and publicly taught relationship without conflict while privately participating in a bitter struggle. That is a documented contradiction in circumstance; whether it invalidates his thought is a philosophical judgment outside this dossier.

## Later Life, 1980–1986

### Dialogues, public recognition, and politics

In his eighties Krishnamurti continued a demanding circuit among Ojai, Brockwood Park, Saanen, and India. He held discussions with Bohm, scientists, psychologists, Buddhist scholars, teachers, and students. He met the Dalai Lama and Indian leaders. **Pupul Jayakar** arranged or witnessed serious conversations with Prime Minister **Indira Gandhi**, including during and after the Emergency. Jayakar believed his challenge may have affected Gandhi, but there is no documentary basis for attributing a specific governmental decision to him.

At the United Nations in New York in 1985, during the organization’s fortieth-anniversary period, he spoke on the causes of conflict and received a **United Nations Peace Medal**, as described in the [foundation’s archival presentation](https://kfoundation.org/how-to-find-peace/). This was an honor, not a UN office or Nobel-type adjudicated prize.

### Final talks and death

Increasing fatigue, fever, and weight loss became apparent in 1985. His final public talk took place at Adyar, Madras/Chennai, on **4 January 1986**. He returned to Ojai on 10 January after a difficult flight. Tests found inoperable pancreatic cancer. Curative intervention was stopped; friends and professionals cared for him at home.

He died at 12:10 a.m. on 17 February 1986. The [contemporaneous obituary](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1986-02-18-mn-9208-story.html) records pancreatic cancer, cremation, and the decision against a memorial service. His ashes were divided among Ojai, England, and India and dispersed, including in the Ganges and near Adyar, limiting the possibility of a shrine centered on remains.

In final private statements he reportedly said that nobody had fully understood the extraordinary intelligence that had operated through the body and that no comparable body would appear for many hundreds of years. These remarks sit uneasily beside his rejection of personal authority and became material for both hagiography and criticism. Their interpretation is unresolved: literal exceptionalism, description of an impersonal state, illness-period reflection, or a last remnant of World Teacher mythology.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

Krishnamurti did not build ordinary for-profit companies, hold patents, or promote a proprietary technology. His material world consisted of trusts, publishing entities, schools, donated estates, copyrights, recordings, and travel support.

### Organizational sequence

1. **Order of the Rising Sun** (brief precursor, 1910/11).
2. **Order of the Star in the East** (1911; later Order of the Star; dissolved by Krishnamurti in 1929).
3. **The Herald of the Star** and related magazines.
4. **Star Publishing Trust** (formed in the 1920s; administered by D. Rajagopal).
5. Successive Ojai publishing/custodial bodies, including **Krishnamurti Writings, Inc. (KWINC)**.
6. **Krishnamurti Foundation Trust**, **Krishnamurti Foundation of America**, **Krishnamurti Foundation India**, and the Spanish/Latin American foundation.
7. Schools, study centers, archives, and the continuing **Complete Teachings/Complete Works** project.

### Property and wealth

- Eerde/Ommen was donated for the Star project and returned after dissolution.
- Ojai properties were acquired through supporter trusts and became the center of later litigation.
- Brockwood Park was purchased by the UK foundation in 1969, aided by donors.
- Krishnamurti lived comfortably, traveled internationally, wore well-made clothes, drove cars, and accepted donor hospitality. This was materially privileged compared with his childhood.
- There is no strong evidence that he amassed a large personally controlled fortune, ran investment vehicles, engaged in money laundering, or enriched heirs. The durable assets resided mainly in nonprofit entities.
- His indifference to bookkeeping is repeatedly reported; it does not prove personal probity or wrongdoing, but it helps explain later control failures.

### Media technology

His career tracked twentieth-century media: stenographic transcripts, print periodicals, radio broadcast at Ommen, gramophone/spoken-word releases, reel-to-reel audio, film, television, videocassette, and digital archives. The [foundation biography](https://kfoundation.org/krishnamurti-biography/) reports roughly 600 videos, more than 2,500 audios, and transcripts of more than 5,000 events. These numbers describe archival scale and may evolve as cataloguing continues.

## Political, Religious, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

### From esoteric hierarchy to anti-authority

Krishnamurti’s early language accepted Masters, initiation, discipleship, reincarnation, and the World Teacher. Between 1925 and 1929 he progressively rejected privileged spiritual mediation. His mature position was not simple atheism or secular rationalism; he continued to speak of the sacred, benediction, immeasurable, and “otherness,” while refusing to turn these into a creed.

### Core themes

- **Truth is “pathless”:** no institution, scripture, guru, method, or ideology guarantees access.
- **Freedom is at the beginning:** discipline imposed to reach liberation merely reproduces conditioning.
- **Choiceless awareness:** observation without selection, condemnation, motive, or division between observer and observed.
- **Relationship as mirror:** self-knowledge appears in daily relation, not isolation from life.
- **Thought and time:** thought is useful technologically but, as accumulated memory, cannot touch the timeless and often manufactures psychological continuity.
- **Fear, pleasure, sorrow, and death:** examined directly rather than managed through belief.
- **Meditation:** not system, repetition, posture, or control, but attention and silence without a meditator seeking achievement.
- **Revolution:** psychological transformation now, not gradual idealistic progress or violent political seizure.
- **Education:** freedom from fear and comparison, care for the earth, and global rather than nationalist consciousness.

### Politics

He condemned nationalism as glorified tribalism, rejected ideological parties, opposed organized violence, and treated capitalism, communism, and religious nationalism as manifestations of conditioned thought. His position was often called pacifist, yet he avoided membership in pacifist parties and did not supply a program of policy reform. Critics regard this as politically quietist; supporters regard it as inquiry into the roots of violence. He advised or spoke with leaders but did not become their formal adviser or accept office.

### Relation to Indian traditions

Parallels exist with Advaita, Buddhism, and apophatic mysticism: nonduality, insight into the self’s constructed nature, attention, and freedom from attachment. Krishnamurti denied belonging to a tradition and resisted Sanskrit terminology. Scholars therefore debate whether he represents a “tradition of no tradition,” modernized Indian spirituality, global religious individualism, or a genuinely anomalous teaching.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### 1. Manufacture of a messiah

**Documented:** Adults selected, renamed, groomed, publicized, and institutionally elevated a vulnerable adolescent.  
**Occult claim:** Clairvoyant recognition and Masters authorized the choice.  
**Critical interpretation:** Besant and Leadbeater manufactured charisma through imperial privilege, racial exoticism, and mass organization.  
**Counterpoint:** Krishnamurti later destroyed the office built for him and returned major gifts; he was both beneficiary and object of the project.

### 2. Leadbeater and child-safety questions

Leadbeater’s earlier sexual-instruction scandal is documented in Theosophical history. Narayaniah feared his influence. **No located evidence establishes that Krishnamurti accused Leadbeater of sexually abusing him, and no court convicted Leadbeater of such abuse.** Claims of direct abuse of Krishnamurti should be labeled unverified.

### 3. Authorship of *At the Feet of the Master*

The lost manuscript, the boy’s weak English, Leadbeater’s editorial role, and the book’s propaganda value support doubt. Witness statements and the possibility of rapid language acquisition support some Krishnamurti involvement. **Unresolved attribution**, not proven fraud by Krishnamurti.

### 4. Secret affair, alleged abortions, and public image

The affair with Rosalind is strongly supported posthumously and acknowledged even by Lutyens’s rebuttal. The alleged three abortions and particular violent episodes derive chiefly from Sloss and remain disputed. The concealment materially affects assessments of transparency and the celibate image projected by followers. Krishnamurti did not explicitly preach mandatory celibacy for all; therefore “violation of a vow” is not established, while secrecy and adultery are documented ethical circumstances.

### 5. Rajagopal litigation

The civil struggle over property, funds, copyrights, correspondence, and archives is documented. Partisan accounts accuse Rajagopal of misappropriation or accuse the new foundations of dispossession. Settlement, rather than a located criminal verdict, resolved the central fight. Avoid translating fiduciary allegations into a criminal conviction.

### 6. FBI surveillance

He was monitored during wartime because of antiwar statements. The surveillance did not produce a prosecution. Claims that he was an intelligence asset or enemy agent are unsupported by the consulted record.

### 7. Institutional paradox

He dissolved an order and rejected followers, then created foundations, schools, annual gatherings, archives, and a recognizable global network. He addressed the paradox by saying foundations had no spiritual authority and existed only to preserve texts and operate schools. Critics argue custodianship inevitably creates authority. This is a real institutional contradiction, not evidence of financial crime.

### 8. Claims of healing or extraordinary power

Associates sometimes described a healing presence, benediction, clairvoyant sensitivity, or unusual energy. These are testimonials, not controlled evidence. Krishnamurti generally discouraged pursuit of powers, though he also spoke privately of “otherness” and exceptional bodily sensitivity.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### The aura without selfishness

1. **Story:** Leadbeater saw a uniquely pure aura around the neglected boy.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** Theosophical recollections and biographies after the 1909 selection.
3. **Circulators:** Leadbeater, Besant’s network, Order of the Star literature, later devotees.
4. **Evidence:** Selection is factual; clairvoyant perception is untestable and rests on Leadbeater’s testimony.
5. **Effect:** Created an annunciation myth that made apparent intellectual dullness proof of spiritual purity rather than disconfirmation.

### The reincarnated or overshadowed Christ/Maitreya

1. **Story:** Krishnamurti would become the vehicle through which Maitreya/World Teacher spoke.
2. **Appearance:** The 1909–11 project, amplified by 1925 proclamations and “apostles.”
3. **Circulators:** Besant, Leadbeater, Liberal Catholic and Star networks; opposed by Steiner and dissident Theosophists.
4. **Evidence:** Large organization and altered speech are documented; supernatural identity is not independently verifiable. Krishnamurti rejected the exclusive claim.
5. **Effect:** Split the Theosophical movement, generated global publicity, and supplied the dramatic contrast for his 1929 renunciation.

### Instant enlightenment under the pepper tree

1. **Story:** Krishnamurti became enlightened at Ojai in August 1922 beneath a pepper tree.
2. **Appearance:** Letters and recollections describing the experience, later devotional retelling.
3. **Circulators:** Inner circle, biographers, foundations, spiritual readers.
4. **Evidence:** First-person experience and witnessed pain are documented; “enlightenment” is an interpretive label. The process continued, arguing against a simple one-time conversion.
5. **Effect:** Created a sacred geography at Ojai and an origin event for the mature teaching.

### Nitya’s death single-handedly caused the break

1. **Story:** One bereavement disproved the Masters and caused the 1929 revolt.
2. **Appearance:** Biographical compression after 1929.
3. **Evidence:** The death was devastating and weakened belief; his language changed over several years, and institutional doubts already existed.
4. **Effect:** Gives the break a psychologically intelligible turning point but oversimplifies a four-year development.

### “He returned every penny and owned nothing”

1. **Story:** On dissolution he gave away all wealth and thereafter lived without institutions or property.
2. **Evidence:** Major Order properties and donations were returned; yet publishing trusts, donor-supported residences, travel, schools, and later foundations continued.
3. **Effect:** Supports saintly renunciation mythology while obscuring the complicated trust/property history.

### Perfect celibate sage versus fraudulent hypocrite

1. **Devotional story:** A wholly unattached, celibate, conflict-free sage.
2. **Hostile story:** A calculated sexual and financial fraud.
3. **Evidence:** The secret Rosalind relationship and bitter litigation contradict the perfect public image. Evidence does not establish that his entire teaching was a deliberate confidence scheme, personal enrichment fraud, or criminal enterprise.
4. **Effect:** Since 1991, reception often divides between suppression of Sloss and reduction of the life to her allegations.

### The incomparable final vehicle

1. **Story:** No one understood the intelligence operating through him, and no similar manifestation would occur for centuries.
2. **Appearance:** Reported final conversations in 1986.
3. **Circulators:** Close associates and biographers; critics cite the statement as self-mythologizing.
4. **Evidence:** Witness report, not a public doctrinal declaration; metaphysical content unverifiable.
5. **Effect:** Reopened the messianic question he appeared to close in 1929.

## Works by or Under the Name of J. Krishnamurti

### Bibliographic caution

Krishnamurti’s corpus exceeds a normal author bibliography because thousands of oral events were repeatedly excerpted and repackaged. The foundation archive describes more than 80 books in 60 languages, approximately 600 video recordings, more than 2,500 audio recordings, and transcriptions from more than 5,000 events. Many commercial titles are **posthumous thematic compilations**, not books composed or approved as books by him. The following is a substantial, provenance-aware bibliography rather than a claim to enumerate every translation, pamphlet, cassette, reissue, or thematic extraction.

### Early Theosophical works and periodicals, 1910–1933

- *At the Feet of the Master* (1910), as **Alcyone** — attribution disputed; Leadbeater’s mediation certain, precise authorship unresolved.
- *Education as Service* (1912), as Alcyone — early Theosophical work; attribution likewise questioned.
- *The Immortal Friend* (1928) — poems and prose from the transitional period.
- *The Pool of Wisdom* / *Who Brings the Truth?* / *By What Authority?* and associated poems (late 1920s pamphlet material).
- “The Dissolution of the Order of the Star” (1929), statement/address; primary programmatic text.
- Contributions, editorials, poems, and speeches in *The Herald of the Star*, *International Star Bulletin*, and related Star publications — often edited from talks.
- *Life in Freedom* (1928) and *The Kingdom of Happiness* (1927/28) — transitional texts; editions and dating vary.
- *The Song of Life* (1931) — poetry; closely tied to the “Beloved” vocabulary.
- *Early Writings of J. Krishnamurti* — later compilation of genuine/attributed early material, not an original single work.

### Major lifetime books and edited talk collections

- *Authentic Report of Sixteen Talks Given in 1945 & 1946* (1947).
- *Education and the Significance of Life* (1953) — one of the more deliberately organized prose books.
- *The First and Last Freedom* (1954), foreword by Aldous Huxley — selected talks/writings.
- *Commentaries on Living*, Series I–III (1956, 1958, 1960) — encounters recast into reflective narratives, editorially mediated.
- *Life Ahead* (1963) — talks and discussions with students.
- *This Matter of Culture* (1964; later issued as *Think on These Things*) — educational talks.
- *Freedom from the Known* (1969), edited by Mary Lutyens — compilation, not a continuously authored manuscript.
- *The Only Revolution* (1970) — meditative narrative/dialogue form.
- *The Urgency of Change* (1970/71) — dialogues.
- *The Awakening of Intelligence* (1973) — talks and dialogues, including scientific and religious interlocutors.
- *Beyond Violence* (1973).
- *Krishnamurti’s Notebook* (1976) — diary kept June 1961–March 1962; principal first-person record of the process/otherness.
- *The Wholeness of Life* (1978), including dialogues with David Bohm and David Shainberg.
- *Truth and Actuality* (1977).
- *Exploration into Insight* (1979/80), dialogues at Brockwood Park.
- *The Network of Thought* (1982).
- *Krishnamurti’s Journal* (1982) — entries from 1973–75.
- *The Flame of Attention* (1984).
- *The Last Talks at Saanen, 1985* (published in various forms).

### Dialogues and co-credited transcript books

- *Tradition and Revolution* — dialogues with Pupul Jayakar and others in India.
- *The Ending of Time* (1985), dialogues with **David Bohm** — edited transcripts.
- *The Future of Humanity* (1986), dialogues with Bohm.
- *The Limits of Thought* (posthumous selection), dialogues with Bohm.
- *Can Humanity Change?* / *A Wholly Different Way of Living*, dialogues with **Allan W. Anderson** (recorded 1974; later book/video editions).

- *Krishnamurti to Himself: His Last Journal* (1987) — transcribed from recordings made 1983–84.

## Illnesses, Psychological Crises, Injuries, and Cause of Death

- Childhood malaria and recurrent frailty are repeatedly reported.
- Dental and general health problems occurred during early grooming; records are biographical rather than full medical charts.
- The recurrent “process” involved severe pain, sensitivity, altered consciousness, and regression-like speech from 1922 onward. No defensible retrospective medical diagnosis can be made.
- Grief after Nitya’s death was acute and publicly witnessed; no evidence establishes a formal psychiatric diagnosis.
- No documented addiction to alcohol or drugs was found. He generally lived with dietary and physical discipline.
- Increasing exhaustion, fevers, and weight loss in 1985 led to diagnosis of inoperable pancreatic cancer in January 1986.
- Cause of death: pancreatic cancer, 17 February 1986.

## Awards, Honors, Offices, Appointments, and Dismissals

- Head of the Order of the Star in the East/Order of the Star, 1911–1929; he abolished the office by dissolving the body.
- Numerous ceremonial honors within the Theosophical movement, including projected apostolic/initiatory status; these were esoteric designations, not state awards.
- Founder or central inspiration of Krishnamurti foundations and schools; typically not a salaried executive in the conventional corporate sense.
- United Nations Peace Medal, 1985, following a UN address.
- No Nobel Prize, elected political office, formal professorship, earned university degree, military rank, or state decoration of comparable evidentiary prominence was found.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Institutional survival

Four principal foundations preserve and distribute the corpus; schools continue in India, Britain, and the United States. Digital release has changed his audience from annual camp participants and book readers to a global video/podcast public. The archival abundance makes him one of the most extensively recorded twentieth-century religious speakers.

### Intellectual influence

His vocabulary—conditioning, choiceless awareness, observer and observed, freedom from the known—entered transpersonal psychology, nonsectarian meditation, educational theory, dialogue practice, and modern spiritual individualism. David Bohm’s dialogue work is frequently connected to their conversations, though Bohm’s physics should not be described as derived from Krishnamurti.

Readers and admirers have included writers, artists, scientists, spiritual teachers, and political figures. Influence claims involving Bruce Lee, Jackson Pollock, Van Morrison, Terence Stamp, Toni Packer, Eckhart Tolle, and others range from documented reading to loose resemblance; “influenced” should not automatically imply personal contact.

### Competing historical reinterpretations

1. **Liberator narrative:** the chosen messiah renounced power and refused followers.
2. **Modernist religious narrative:** Theosophy created the global platform and conceptual problems from which an anti-guru teacher emerged.
3. **Postcolonial narrative:** an Indian child was appropriated and fashioned by British occultists, then reclaimed agency by dismantling their role.
4. **Psychological narrative:** childhood loss, separation, grooming, the process, and Nitya’s death generated a distinctive inquiry into dependency and authority.
5. **Critical private-life narrative:** secrecy with Rosalind and warfare with Rajagopal expose a gap between teaching and conduct.
6. **Institutional paradox:** an anti-authority message survives through organizations that authenticate, edit, own, and teach around it.

None alone exhausts the evidence. The enduring historical fact is the unusual sequence: colonial child selection; international messianic construction; self-dissolution of the office; five decades of anti-authoritarian teaching; extensive institutional preservation; and posthumous reopening of the private life.

## Research Limits and Unresolved Questions

- The exact birth documentation and some sibling details remain uncertain.
- No surviving original notes settle authorship of *At the Feet of the Master*.
- The “process” cannot be medically diagnosed retrospectively from the available narratives.
- Full California litigation dockets and historical audited accounts were not accessible in this research; criminal claims are therefore unwarranted.
- The affair is well supported, but abortion counts and some alleged episodes remain dependent on contested family testimony.
- No reliable evidence was found for a personal Krishnamurti–Dion Fortune meeting or sustained correspondence.
- Exact counts of books, events, recordings, translations, and sales vary because archival and editorial projects continue.

## Atlas Connections

### Annie Besant

- **[T5] Documented fact:** Besant personally knew Krishnamurti from 1909, assumed responsibility for his upbringing and education, took him and Nitya to Britain in 1911, and became his guardian and surrogate mother. Their personal attachment survived his repudiation of the World Teacher organization; Besant remained devoted to him until her death in 1933. The institutional sequence is documented by the [Theosophical Society, Adyar](https://www.ts-adyar.org/j-krishnamurti-1895-1986) and the [1914 Privy Council judgment](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/in/56b49611607dba348f0166db).
- **[T2- hyperstition] Documented fact, with metaphysical fulfillment unverified:** Besant proclaimed the coming World Teacher, promoted Krishnamurti as his prospective vehicle, installed him as head of the Order of the Star in the East, and built the international apparatus intended to produce public recognition of that role. The prophecy demonstrably helped manufacture Krishnamurti’s career and institutional identity, although his supernatural identification as Maitreya was never independently established and was ultimately rejected by him. [Theosophical Society account](https://www.ts-adyar.org/j-krishnamurti-1895-1986); [Harvard Center for the Study of World Religions](https://cswr.hds.harvard.edu/news/2026/07/01/jiddu-krishnamurtis-out-body-initiation).

### Helena Petrovna Blavatsky

- **[T2- hyperstition] Documented historical transmission; occult claim unverified:** Blavatsky died in 1891 and never knew Krishnamurti, but her late Theosophical expectation that the Society should prepare for a coming World Teacher was revived by Besant and Leadbeater and applied to him after 1909. That inherited expectation causally structured his selection, grooming, publicity, and Order of the Star role; it does not establish fulfillment of the prophecy in any supernatural sense. [Harvard Center for the Study of World Religions](https://cswr.hds.harvard.edu/news/2026/07/01/jiddu-krishnamurtis-out-body-initiation); [Theosophical Society, Adyar](https://www.ts-adyar.org/j-krishnamurti-1895-1986).

### Dion Fortune

- **[T3] Reported fact; no personal meeting established:** Fortune briefly participated in the Theosophical Society’s Christian Mystic Lodge, initially treated the expected World Teacher as a live possibility, and later wrote polemically against the Besant–Leadbeater tendency after the Krishnamurti project’s collapse. This is a one-sided engagement through the institutional controversy, not evidence of correspondence or face-to-face contact. The account is preserved in Alan Richardson’s biography, available through the [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/stream/priestess-alan-richardson-dion-fortune-biography/PRIESTESS%20Alan%20Richardson%20dion%20fortune%20biography_djvu.txt).

### Mahatma Gandhi

- **[T3] Documented fact:** In a letter dated 11 February 1929, Gandhi answered questions explicitly based on Krishnamurti’s formulations of life and the relation between individual and world problems, stating agreement with the latter proposition. Krishnamurti subsequently discussed Gandhi’s example and ideas in public talks, including at Madras on 22 and 26 October 1947. This establishes reciprocal written/public-discourse engagement, but the consulted record does not securely establish a personal meeting. [Gandhi’s collected works, vol. 45](https://library.bjp.org/jspui/bitstream/123456789/615/1/THE%20COLLECTED%20WORKS%20OF%20MAHATMA%20GANDHI%20-%20VOL%20%2845%29.PDF); [Krishnamurti’s 22 October 1947 talk](https://kfoundation.org/transcript/public-talk-1-madras-22-october-1947/); [26 October 1947 talk](https://kfoundation.org/transcript/public-talk-2-madras-26-october-1947/).

### Indra Devi

- **[T3] Reported fact:** Eugenie Peterson, later known as Indra Devi, attended the 1926 Theosophical gathering at Ommen and heard Krishnamurti speak and chant Sanskrit hymns. She later described the experience as a decisive “forgotten call” that intensified her determination to go to India. The evidence supports seeing him perform; it does not by itself establish friendship or a private meeting. [Russia Beyond biographical account](https://www.rbth.com/articles/2010/11/22/first_lady_of_yoga04910); [Theosophy Wiki biography](https://theosophy.wiki/en/Indra_Devi).

### Nicholas Roerich

- **[T3] Reported fact:** Nicholas and Helena Roerich rejected the Besant–Leadbeater claim that Krishnamurti was the vehicle of the World Teacher. Scholarship cites Helena Roerich’s secretary Zinaida Fosdik’s diary for this position, making it a one-sided written engagement with the Krishnamurti controversy; no personal encounter or correspondence between Nicholas Roerich and Krishnamurti is established. [Brill study of Roerich Theosophy](https://brill.com/previewpdf/book/edcoll/9789004235977/B9789004235977-s012.xml).

### Rudolf Steiner

- **[T3] Documented fact:** In 1911–13 Steiner publicly and textually opposed the teaching that Christ could return physically through Krishnamurti, arguing instead for the uniqueness of the incarnation in Jesus and a future “etheric” manifestation. His followers’ exclusion of Order of the Star members and Besant’s subsequent revocation of the German Section’s charter made Krishnamurti’s projected role a major issue in the Theosophy–Anthroposophy schism. This was a consequential one-sided doctrinal engagement; no sustained personal exchange between the two men is established. [Rudolf Steiner Archive](https://rsarchive.org/Books/GA015/English/AP1992/GA015_intro.html); [historical study of the World Teacher project](https://www.theosophyforward.com/pdf/Krishnamurti_and_the_World_teacher.pdf).

No additional roster member was retained merely because of shared ideas, membership in a broad spiritual or intellectual milieu, contemporaneity, or later comparison by third parties. In particular, the consulted evidence did not establish a personal Krishnamurti crossing with Carl Jung, Sigmund Freud, Maria Montessori, W. B. Yeats, G. I. Gurdjieff, René Guénon, Rabindranath Tagore, or Nicholas Roerich beyond the one-sided Roerich engagement recorded above.
