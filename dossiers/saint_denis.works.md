## Works by Saint Denis of Paris

### Securely attributable works

- **Third century:** *No surviving work*. No text, building, artwork, or other named obra can be securely attributed to the historical Denis, an early bishop and martyr of Paris about whom little contemporary evidence survives. The dates “c. 250–1987” conflate his approximate historical period with the publication year of a modern English translation of the Pseudo-Dionysian corpus; 1987 is not a biographical date.

### Legendary foundation

- **No securely fixed date:** *Church at Paris*. Hilduin’s ninth-century *Passio* says that Denis bought land from Lisbius and built a church there. This is a legendary architectural obra, not a historically verified building by the third-century saint; the story belongs to Hilduin’s conflation of Denis of Paris with the apostolic Dionysius the Areopagite ([Krönert, pp. 82–83](https://www.persee.fr/doc/bsnaf_0081-1181_2018_num_2015_1_12419)).

### Pseudepigraphic treatises traditionally made into works of Saint Denis

The following texts survive as the *Corpus Dionysiacum*. Medieval Saint-Denis identified their purported author, Dionysius the Areopagite, with Denis of Paris, but modern scholarship dates their anonymous author between about 485 and 518–528—more than two centuries after the Parisian martyr. They are therefore manifestations of Denis’s constructed authorial identity, not works securely made by him ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-dionysius-areopagite/)).

- **c. 485–528:** *On the Divine Names* (*De divinis nominibus*). A thirteen-chapter theological treatise on scriptural names for God and the limits of affirmative language; it survives, but its attribution to Saint Denis of Paris is rejected by historical-critical scholarship.

- **c. 485–528:** *On the Celestial Hierarchy* (*De coelesti hierarchia*). An angelological treatise organizing the celestial beings into hierarchical orders; it is pseudepigraphic rather than an obra of the historical Parisian bishop.

- **c. 485–528:** *On the Ecclesiastical Hierarchy* (*De ecclesiastica hierarchia*). A liturgical and ecclesiological treatise interpreting rites, clerical orders, monastic profession, and funerals as a human hierarchy corresponding to the celestial one.

- **c. 485–528:** *Mystical Theology* (*De mystica theologia*). A short apophatic treatise presenting an ascent beyond assertion, negation, language, and knowledge.

### Pseudepigraphic letters

- **c. 485–528:** *Letter 1, to Gaius*. A theological letter on God’s transcendence of knowledge; its apostolic setting is part of the corpus’s assumed Dionysian persona.

- **c. 485–528:** *Letter 2, to Gaius*. A theological letter arguing that the divine exceeds even the predicates “God” and “goodness.”

- **c. 485–528:** *Letter 3, to Gaius*. A brief Christological letter on the “sudden” manifestation of Christ.

- **c. 485–528:** *Letter 4, to Gaius*. A Christological letter concerning the relation of divine and human activity in Christ.

- **c. 485–528:** *Letter 5, to Dorotheus*. A brief apophatic meditation on divine darkness and light.

- **c. 485–528:** *Letter 6, to Sosipater*. An admonitory letter against making theological refutation an end in itself; it acts partly as a protocol for controversy.

- **c. 485–528:** *Letter 7, to Polycarp*. A letter against polemics that also claims its author witnessed extraordinary events associated with Christ’s death, helping manufacture the corpus’s apostolic-era authorship.

- **c. 485–528:** *Letter 8, to Demophilus*. A disciplinary letter ordering a monk to respect ecclesiastical hierarchy; as an intervention in conduct, it approaches a protocol rather than mere description.

- **c. 485–528:** *Letter 9, to Titus*. A theological letter explaining bodily and material symbols used for divine realities.

- **c. 485–528:** *Letter 10, to John*. A consolatory letter addressed to John the Evangelist concerning persecution, suffering, and final deliverance; its recipient is another device locating the pseudonymous writer in apostolic time.

### Projects claimed inside the corpus but absent

These titles are mentioned by Pseudo-Dionysius, but no independent copies or citations establish that the texts ever existed. They resist classification as lost works because they may instead be fictive projects designed to imply a larger apostolic corpus ([Oxford Research Archive discussion](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:f0a643f1-a43e-4e9f-bb48-e1df7161b15a/files/rrf55z851k); [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-dionysius-areopagite/)).

- **c. 485–528, if ever made:** *Theological Representations* (also translated *Theological Outlines*). A projected or purported treatise on the Trinity and Incarnation; it is known only through references in the surviving corpus and may never have existed as a separate text.

- **c. 485–528, if ever made:** *Symbolic Theology*. A purported extended interpretation of sensible and bodily scriptural images of God; whether it was lost or merely imagined remains disputed.

- **c. 485–528, if ever made:** *Divine Hymns*. A purported work on the hymns or praises of celestial beings; its textual existence is unverified.

- **c. 485–528, if ever made:** *Concerning Justice*. A purported theological work known only from internal reference; it may be a fictive bibliographical object rather than an unfinished or lost obra.

- **c. 485–528, if ever made:** *Concerning the Judgment of God*. A purported treatise whose independence from *Concerning Justice* is uncertain in modern enumerations; no text survives.

- **c. 485–528, if ever made:** *On the Soul*. A purported work known only from the corpus’s self-references; there is no evidence that Saint Denis of Paris, or even Pseudo-Dionysius, completed such a text.

- **c. 485–528, if ever made:** *On the Conceptual and the Perceptual*. A purported discussion of intelligible and sensible realities. Some bibliographies divide the conceptual and perceptual subjects into two titles, which accounts for differing totals among lists of the absent works; neither form survives.
