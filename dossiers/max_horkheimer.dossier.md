# Max Horkheimer: Research Dossier

**Prepared:** 24 August 2026  
**Scope:** Documentary reconstruction of Horkheimer’s life, work, institutions, relationships, controversies, mythology, and reception, 1895–1973 and posthumously.

The Frankfurt University Library’s [archival chronology](https://www.ub.uni-frankfurt.de/archive/horkheimer_vita_en.html) is the most concise official biographical control source. Its date “1929” for assuming Institute leadership conflicts with the commonly documented formal appointment/inaugural lecture in 1930/31; the distinction appears to be between the transition after Carl Grünberg’s illness and formal assumption of office.

## Family and Ancestry

Horkheimer was the only son of **Moses/Moriz Horkheimer** (1858–1945), textile manufacturer and *Kommerzienrat*, and **Babette, née Lauchheimer** (1869–1946). [LEO-BW](https://www.leo-bw.de/detail/-/Detail/details/PERSON/kgl_biographien/118553615/Horkheimer%2BMax) identifies his paternal grandparents as the merchant Feist Horkheimer and Babette, née Schwarzenberger, and his maternal grandparents as the merchant Samuel Lauchheimer and Fanny, née Ottenheimer. The family was affluent, conservative, and Jewish; sources vary between “Orthodox” and “observant/conservative.”

Moses/Moritz owned textile factories in and around Zuffenhausen. His wealth placed Max in a very different material position from a typical German student: the son was prepared for commercial succession, entered the firm as a teenager, became a junior manager, traveled on business, and retained access to private means. This background mattered institutionally. Horkheimer was never the founding financier of the Institute—that was principally **Felix Weil**, using wealth derived from his father Hermann Weil’s Argentine grain enterprise—but Horkheimer’s own class position, management experience, and partnership with Friedrich Pollock helped him administer an unusually independent research body.

The most consequential family conflict concerned **Rosa Christine Riekher**, his father’s Christian private secretary and the daughter of Hermann Riekher and Rosina, née Roberts. Horkheimer called her Maidon. His parents opposed what they considered a religious and class *mésalliance*, and she was dismissed when the relationship became known. A widely repeated claim makes her “eight years older,” sometimes assigning an 1887 birth; the specialist Baden-Württemberg genealogy gives **1897**, two years younger than Max, and is preferred here pending a civil certificate. The couple married at Kronberg in 1926 and remained together until her death in 1969.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1895–1918

### Schooling, commerce, Pollock, and Maidon

Horkheimer grew up in the industrializing Stuttgart region. He attended the Gymnasium but left before the Abitur, in 1910 or 1911 depending on how sources count the transition, to undertake commercial training and work in his father’s factory. The [Frankfurt archive](https://www.ub.uni-frankfurt.de/archive/horkheimer_vita_en.html) records completion of grammar school and the start of commercial training in 1911.

His commercial formation included training travel to Belgium, France, and England. During this period he met **Friedrich Pollock** (1894–1970), another manufacturer’s son. Their friendship became the central non-marital relationship of Horkheimer’s life. Pollock combined intellectual collaboration with financial and organizational management; their papers became so interwoven that archivists later separated portions into distinct estates. Horkheimer also began his relationship with Maidon.

The young Horkheimer wrote fiction, diary entries, and aphorisms. These juvenilia, preserved against his later wish that some not be republished, reveal alienation from bourgeois family expectations, sympathy for suffering, and literary-philosophical interests before his systematic encounter with Marxism. They appeared posthumously as *Aus der Pubertät: Novellen und Tagebuchblätter* (1974) and in volume 1 of the *Gesammelte Schriften*.

### First World War

The archive chronology records factory work from 1914 to 1919 and “participation” in the war in 1917/18. Biographical narratives often say that he was drafted but served only briefly, was repeatedly ill or medically unfit, and saw no combat. These formulations are reconcilable: induction or military attachment does not prove front-line service. Claims that he simply “avoided service” overstate the evidence. His letters show growing hostility to the war, but John Abromeit cautions that his earlier position was politically unformed rather than consistently revolutionary. No evidence was found of decorations, combat wounds, desertion, or military prosecution.

## Education and Formation, 1918–1930

### Abitur and universities

After the imperial collapse, Horkheimer obtained an external Abitur in Munich in 1919. He and Pollock studied at Munich, Frankfurt, and Freiburg (winter 1920/21 through summer 1921). His subjects included philosophy, psychology, and economics; at Freiburg he heard **Edmund Husserl** and **Martin Heidegger**.

During the Bavarian revolutionary crisis of 1918–19, Horkheimer was briefly arrested after being mistaken for the revolutionary writer **Ernst Toller**. The anecdote is repeated in major biographies, but publicly accessible documentation is thin; it should be treated as a well-attested biographical episode rather than a fully reconstructed arrest record. No lasting charge or conviction followed.

### Hans Cornelius and academic credentials

At Frankfurt Horkheimer studied under **Hans Cornelius**, a philosopher and psychologist influenced by neo-Kantianism, empiricism, Gestalt problems, and the critique of metaphysics. Cornelius became his doctoral and habilitation sponsor. Horkheimer initially pursued a Gestalt-psychology dissertation that did not succeed. In 1922 he received the doctorate for **“Zur Antinomie der teleologischen Urteilskraft”** (On the Antinomy of Teleological Judgment), a study of Kant. In 1925 he completed the habilitation **“Über Kants Kritik der Urteilskraft als Bindeglied zwischen theoretischer und praktischer Philosophie”** (Kant’s *Critique of Judgment* as a Link between Theoretical and Practical Philosophy).

He became *Privatdozent* in 1926 and lectured on modern philosophy, German idealism, and the history of philosophy. His early work was not yet the mature “critical theory” of the 1930s. Formative sources included Kant, Schopenhauer, Hegel, Marx, Nietzsche, Freud, and contemporary psychology. Schopenhauer remained particularly important to his late pessimism; Marx supplied the theory of historically organized domination; Freud and Fromm enabled the Institute’s early social psychology.

### Adorno

Through Cornelius and Frankfurt musical-philosophical circles, Horkheimer met **Theodor Wiesengrund-Adorno**, nine years younger. Adorno’s early proximity should not be back-projected into an equal partnership from the start. During the early 1930s Erich Fromm was more central to Horkheimer’s interdisciplinary program. Adorno became indispensable in the later 1930s and especially during the California collaboration that produced *Dialectic of Enlightenment*.

## Early Career and the Institute for Social Research

### Felix Weil, not Horkheimer, as founder

The Institute originated in the wealth, planning, and political-intellectual initiative of **Felix José Weil**. Weil organized the 1923 First Marxist Work Week and persuaded his father Hermann to endow a permanent institute. The Prussian Ministry approved the foundation on 23 January 1923; the building opened in 1924 under **Carl Grünberg**. Recent Weil scholarship corrects a retrospective myth that Horkheimer, Pollock, or an already coherent “Frankfurt School” founded the Institute. The evidence summarized in [Hans-Peter Gruber’s account](https://www.historicalmaterialism.org/an-interview-with-hans-peter-gruber-on-the-life-and-work-of-felix-weil-argentine-millionaire-marxist-and-founder-of-the-frankfurt-school/) places Weil at the center and says Weil promoted Horkheimer as Grünberg’s successor.

After Grünberg suffered a stroke, Pollock served as interim director. Horkheimer obtained a newly endowed chair in social philosophy and assumed the directorship in 1930/31. His inaugural lecture of 24 January 1931, **“Die gegenwärtige Lage der Sozialphilosophie und die Aufgaben eines Instituts für Sozialforschung,”** proposed cooperative research joining philosophy, economics, sociology, psychology, and history.

### Horkheimer’s program

As director he moved the Institute away from Grünberg’s archival, more orthodox Marxist labor history toward interdisciplinary diagnosis of modern society. He recruited or coordinated Pollock, Fromm, Leo Löwenthal, Herbert Marcuse, Franz Neumann, Otto Kirchheimer, Henryk Grossmann, Karl August Wittfogel, and later Adorno. Membership was never uniform: the group contained conflicting theories of capitalism, law, psychology, fascism, and political strategy.

In 1932 Horkheimer founded and edited the ***Zeitschrift für Sozialforschung***. His essays—often programmatic and sometimes published without the visibility later attached to them—developed materialism, social philosophy, the relation of history and psychology, philosophical anthropology, truth, morality, authority, and the difference between traditional and critical theory. The [Institute’s centenary history](https://www.ifs.uni-frankfurt.de/schlaglichter.html) identifies the journal as the central organ of the new program.

## Chronological Life History

### 1930–1933: Director under the Weimar Republic

Horkheimer’s directorship coincided with depression, mass unemployment, collapse of parliamentary government, Communist–Social Democratic conflict, and Nazi ascent. The Institute studied authority and the psychological dispositions of workers and employees. Fromm led the survey work that eventually became *The Working Class in Weimar Germany*. Horkheimer’s public institute remained academically cautious and organizationally independent of political parties. This caution later drew opposing readings: prudent protection of research and endangered scholars; or withdrawal of Marxist theory from political practice.

### 1933–1934: Nazi seizure, closure, and escape

Because the Institute was Marxist-associated and largely Jewish, it anticipated danger. Assets and operations had been partly transferred abroad before the Nazi seizure of power. Suppression occurred in stages: SA men occupied the Kronberg Horkheimer–Pollock residence in January; police closed the Institute in March; its building passed to the Nazi student organization in May; the Institute chronology dates formal Gestapo closure for “subversive activities” to July. The regime confiscated its library/building and dismissed Horkheimer from his university post under anti-Jewish legislation. He left Germany for Switzerland, directing an institutional base in Geneva. Branches or working centers operated in Paris and London. His habilitation/teaching rights and German status were stripped; he was not imprisoned by the Nazi regime.

In 1934 the Institute moved its principal center to New York. Columbia University president **Nicholas Murray Butler** provided affiliation and premises at 429 West 117th Street. Columbia did not simply absorb or fully fund the Institute: the exiles retained their own legal/financial structure and depended heavily on the Weil endowment, whose income diminished during the Depression and exile.

### 1934–1939: New York and “critical theory”

Horkheimer rebuilt the journal and scholarly network in English-speaking exile while continuing to publish much work in German. The *Zeitschrift* appeared from Paris and later as *Studies in Philosophy and Social Science*. Research culminated in the collective ***Studien über Autorität und Familie*** (Paris, 1936), edited by Horkheimer with major contributions by Fromm and Marcuse.

In 1937 Horkheimer published **“Traditionelle und kritische Theorie”** and its postscript with Marcuse. The essay defined “critical theory” not as neutral expertise but as historically situated inquiry oriented toward a rational society. It was also a coded formulation: explicit revolutionary language could endanger the Institute and its personnel.

Horkheimer returned briefly to Europe in 1937 and met Walter Benjamin. Benjamin remained financially supported by the Institute but institutionally marginal and vulnerable. Horkheimer, Adorno, and Löwenthal edited and sometimes pressed Benjamin to revise work. The relationship combined material rescue, intellectual collaboration, editorial control, and unequal dependence. Benjamin died by suicide at Portbou in 1940 while fleeing the Nazis.

### The break with Erich Fromm

**Erich Fromm** supplied the psychoanalytic bridge in Horkheimer’s early program and directed empirical studies of workers and families. The partnership deteriorated over theory, personalities, Adorno’s increasing role, and finances. Fromm revised Freud in a more socially and humanistically oriented direction; Adorno and Horkheimer considered parts of this revision insufficiently dialectical and too accommodating. In 1939, as the Institute’s funds contracted, Fromm left under a financial settlement.

The neutral word “left” conceals conflict. Later Institute-centered histories minimized Fromm’s role and delayed publication of his Weimar worker study. Neil McLaughlin’s “origin myths” analysis and the [New York Public Library’s Fromm finding aid](https://archives.nypl.org/mss/1090) support the view that Fromm was integral, not peripheral, before 1939. The evidence does not support a simple claim that Horkheimer dismissed him solely for theoretical heresy; intellectual conflict, Adorno’s arrival, personal antagonism, and fiscal contraction interacted.

### 1940–1944: Citizenship, California, and wartime projects

Horkheimer became a U.S. citizen in 1940. He relocated much of his personal and intellectual life to **Pacific Palisades, Los Angeles**, joining the German-speaking émigré world of Thomas Mann, Bertolt Brecht, Arnold Schoenberg, Lion Feuchtwanger, and others, though degrees of intimacy varied. Adorno followed, and the pair worked in close daily collaboration.

Horkheimer became chief research consultant and scientific head of the **American Jewish Committee** (AJC) antisemitism research department in 1943/44 (sources differ on the administrative start date), continuing through 1949. The AJC relationship helped finance research into antisemitism and authoritarianism. He planned the multi-volume ***Studies in Prejudice***; the best-known result, *The Authoritarian Personality* (1950), was authored by Adorno, Else Frenkel-Brunswik, Daniel Levinson, and Nevitt Sanford—not by Horkheimer, although he was a principal institutional organizer and series editor.

Several Institute associates entered U.S. wartime agencies: Marcuse, Neumann, Kirchheimer, and others worked for the Office of Strategic Services or related bodies. Horkheimer himself is not documented as an OSS intelligence officer. He urged Neumann and Kirchheimer toward outside wartime employment partly because Institute funds could not retain them. Accordingly, “Horkheimer/Frankfurt School worked for U.S. intelligence” is true only when disaggregated: some associates did; Horkheimer led the institution from which they came, but direct personal intelligence employment is not established.

### *Dialectic of Enlightenment*

Horkheimer and Adorno’s collaborative manuscript circulated in mimeographed form in 1944 as ***Philosophische Fragmente***. A revised book appeared in Amsterdam in 1947 as ***Dialektik der Aufklärung: Philosophische Fragmente***. Gretel Adorno assisted substantially with notes, typing, editing, and the working process, but the published authors were Horkheimer and Theodor W. Adorno.

The book’s central historical problem was how Enlightenment reason, intended to free human beings from fear and myth, could turn into domination, mass deception, and administrated barbarism. Its “Excursus” chapters treated Odysseus and Sade; “The Culture Industry” analyzed standardized mass culture; “Elements of Antisemitism” linked persecution to social and psychic mechanisms. It was not a linear claim that Enlightenment automatically “caused” Nazism, although hostile summaries often reduce it to that proposition.

### 1944–1949: Reason, antisemitism, and return planning

Horkheimer delivered the Columbia lectures that became ***Eclipse of Reason*** (New York, 1947), distinguishing objective from subjective/instrumental reason and describing the erosion of substantive rational ends. The English text preceded the later German redaction, ***Zur Kritik der instrumentellen Vernunft*** (1967).

The Institute’s empirical work on prejudice expanded. Horkheimer cultivated U.S. philanthropic and Jewish organizational connections while considering a return to Germany. The postwar choice was controversial among émigrés: return could assist democratic reconstruction, but it also meant working amid restored officials, institutions, and corporations compromised by Nazism.

### 1949–1958: Return to Frankfurt and institutional restoration

Horkheimer first revisited Europe in April 1948 on a Rockefeller-supported research trip and returned in 1949, against Pollock’s initial advice. The Institute was publicly re-established in 1950, first working from damaged premises; its new building opened in 1951. U.S. High Commissioner **John J. McCloy’s fund**, the city, and private donors supplied important reconstruction money. American citizenship and connections remained assets. Horkheimer resumed his chair and directorship and became rector of the university from 1951 to 1953, the first Jewish rector in its postwar history.

His rectorate emphasized academic reconstruction, Western democratic integration, and international exchange. He became a public intellectual of the Federal Republic, less openly Marxist and more guarded than in the 1930s. He and Adorno conducted or sponsored empirical research on antisemitism, political consciousness, group experiment, youth, family, and industry.

The Institute accepted commissions, including a 1954 workforce study for **Mannesmann**. Mannesmann had participated in Nazi rearmament and forced/slave labor under Wilhelm Zangen. The contract is documented in Institute histories; the further claim that the study’s intended purpose was to prevent socialist organizing is an interpretation, not a demonstrated contractual clause. The episode illustrates the tension between autonomous critique and dependence on government/corporate research funds in the restored Federal Republic.

Horkheimer was a visiting professor at the University of Chicago in the mid-1950s and again later in the decade. He retired from his Frankfurt chair in 1959; sources differ on whether he relinquished practical Institute leadership in 1958 or in stages. Adorno became the central director.

### 1957–1969: Montagnola, late pessimism, religion, and the New Left

In 1957 Max and Maidon, with Pollock nearby, settled increasingly in **Montagnola**, Ticino, Switzerland, while Horkheimer retained German institutional ties. The archive’s short chronology associates the move with 1959; the detailed NDB/LEO-BW chronology favors 1957. His later lectures and notes moved from the 1930s’ revolutionary horizon toward a negative philosophy of resistance, compassion, and longing for the “wholly Other.” This was not a formal conversion to a church. He described theology as preserving a longing that injustice not be final, while denying positive knowledge of God.

He revisited Schopenhauer, defended the family as a possible refuge from total administration, and became culturally conservative on some questions. In a 1970 interview published as ***Die Sehnsucht nach dem ganz Anderen***, he expressed sympathy for Pope Paul VI’s opposition to artificial contraception and feared that the pill would instrumentalize sexuality and extinguish love. The position is documented, frequently criticized, and not reducible to the earlier Institute program.

Horkheimer and Adorno were deeply suspicious of the West German student movement. They feared authoritarian dynamics within revolutionary rhetoric, antisemitism in anti-Zionism, and institutional destruction. Horkheimer defended the Western alliance and, in private or reported comments, took positions sympathetic to U.S. policy in Vietnam and strongly supportive of Israel in 1967. Marcuse, by contrast, engaged sympathetically with the New Left. The divergence became a public fracture inside the retrospective “Frankfurt School.”

Claims about Horkheimer’s blanket support for the Vietnam War require source-specific qualification: his correspondence and interviews reveal anti-communism and acceptance of U.S. power as a barrier to totalitarianism, but slogans such as “he supported the war” often omit changes over time and the difference between strategic preference and endorsement of every military act.

Maidon died in 1969. Adorno died the same year, and Pollock in 1970. The clustering of these deaths left Horkheimer personally isolated. Public evidence supports grief and declining health; it does not support a clinical diagnosis of a specific late psychological disorder.

### 1970–1973: Final years

Horkheimer continued interviews, notes, and occasional public statements. In 1970 *Die Sehnsucht nach dem ganz Anderen* and *Vernunft und Selbsterhaltung* appeared. His late work joined metaphysical skepticism, Jewish memory, Schopenhauerian pessimism, and insistence that solidarity with finite suffering remains possible without a guaranteed philosophy of history.

He died on 7 July 1973 in Nuremberg. Some biographical accounts say death followed a routine medical examination, but a precise publicly documented medical cause was not located. It is therefore safer to record the place and date and leave the proximate cause **not established in the consulted public sources**. He was buried beside Maidon in Bern’s Jewish cemetery.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Family textile business

Horkheimer worked in the family textile enterprise from adolescence through the war years and reached junior management. Public sources do not supply reliable factory names, shareholdings, balance sheets, inheritance figures, or a complete disposition of family property. Statements that he was personally a “millionaire” should be treated as loose descriptions of family affluence, not a verified net worth.

### Institute legal and financial structure

The IfS was attached to Frankfurt University but originally financed by a private endowment organized by Felix and Hermann Weil. This gave it unusual autonomy. Pollock managed administrative and financial affairs; Horkheimer set intellectual and personnel strategy. The Institute moved assets abroad before Nazi confiscation, enabling survival in Geneva and New York. Depression-era losses and declining Weil support forced salary reductions and personnel choices, including the conflict around Fromm and the outside employment of Neumann and Kirchheimer.

Columbia supplied institutional shelter and premises but did not convert the IfS into an ordinary Columbia department. In the United States Horkheimer pursued Rockefeller Foundation and other grants, AJC sponsorship, university arrangements, and contract research. Not every approach succeeded; the Institute repeatedly faced dwindling funds.

After return, West German public authorities, the university, foundations, and commissioned research supported the Institute. The Mannesmann contract shows corporate work. The archival catalogue identifies contracts and confirmations involving monthly Pollock payments, life-insurance policies, the Hermann Weil and Kurt Gerlach memorial foundations, the Institute, and Columbia’s Bureau of Applied Social Research; the public catalogue does not disclose amounts or establish impropriety. No evidence in the consulted sources establishes fraud, embezzlement, money laundering, secret corporate ownership, patents, inventions, or personal profiteering by Horkheimer.

### Research and editorial projects

- *Zeitschrift für Sozialforschung* / *Studies in Philosophy and Social Science* (editorial and institutional leadership, 1932–41).
- *Studien über Autorität und Familie* (collective project, 1936).
- Unpublished/partly published worker and employee studies led by Fromm.
- AJC ***Studies in Prejudice*** (planner/director/series sponsor; 1940s–50).
- “Racket theory” research on protection, monopoly, and domination, much of it unpublished in his lifetime.
- Postwar German group experiments and political-consciousness research.
- A proposed Institute experimental film (1943 notes survive); no completed Horkheimer film resulted.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### From bourgeois revolt to interdisciplinary materialism

Horkheimer’s youthful revolt was ethical and literary before it became Marxian. The First World War, German revolution, failed socialism, and exposure to Marx shifted his focus from Kantian academic philosophy to historically grounded social theory. He never joined the Communist Party. His Institute maintained distance from the SPD, KPD, Comintern, and exile party politics.

### Early critical theory

The mature 1930s model sought to combine:

1. Marxian political economy and class analysis;
2. empirical sociology;
3. Freudian/Frommian social psychology;
4. philosophical critique of reason and ideology;
5. historical analysis of family, authority, and culture.

Unlike “traditional theory,” critical theory understood the theorist and categories as part of society. Its practical interest was emancipation, but Horkheimer refused to identify an existing party or state as its guaranteed agent.

### Fascism, state capitalism, and disagreements inside the Institute

Pollock’s “state capitalism” thesis influenced Horkheimer’s wartime view that market capitalism was becoming administratively controlled. Neumann’s *Behemoth* instead stressed conflict among Nazi power blocs and continuing monopoly-capitalist competition. Horkheimer leaned toward Pollock; the disagreement mattered for *Dialectic of Enlightenment* and later accounts of an “administered world.”

### Instrumental reason and domination of nature

In *Dialectic* and *Eclipse*, reason narrows from judging ends to calculating means. Human domination of nature rebounds as social domination and self-repression. Myth and Enlightenment are not simple opposites: myth already classifies; Enlightenment can regress into myth when calculation becomes absolute.

### Religion and Judaism

Horkheimer came from Jewish observance but was philosophically non-orthodox. The Holocaust and antisemitism intensified his attachment to Jewish memory and the biblical prohibition on representing God. Late “theology” was negative: humans cannot affirm divine justice as fact, but can preserve the longing that the murderer not triumph eternally over the victim. He supported Israel as refuge and, especially after 1967, defended its survival; he did not simply embrace religious Zionism.

### Political trajectory after 1945

Horkheimer’s postwar position combined anti-Nazism, anti-Stalinism, defense of constitutional democracy, Atlantic orientation, and fear of mass movements. Critics call this a conservative or anti-communist retreat; defenders emphasize the historical experience of fascism and Stalinism. The record supports a real movement rightward relative to the 1930s, without showing that he abandoned every Marxian diagnostic category.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### 1. Did Horkheimer purge Fromm?

**Documented:** Fromm’s theoretical direction conflicted with Adorno and Horkheimer; Institute finances deteriorated; Fromm departed in 1939 with a settlement; his worker study was not then published; later narratives marginalized him.  
**Disputed interpretation:** whether this was chiefly a financial layoff, a theoretical expulsion, Adorno’s personal campaign, or a combined institutional rupture. The combined explanation fits the record best.  
**Not established:** criminal conduct, breach adjudicated by a court, or fraud.

### 2. Editorial power over Walter Benjamin

**Documented:** the Institute paid Benjamin, commissioned/rejected/revised texts, and controlled publication; Horkheimer and Adorno urged changes.  
**Competing readings:** material rescue under desperate conditions versus coercive editorial domestication of Benjamin. Both capture parts of an unequal dependency. There is no evidence that Horkheimer caused Benjamin’s suicide; that claim belongs to hostile exaggeration.

### 3. Political abstention in the Nazi era

Rolf Wiggershaus and later critics document Horkheimer’s insistence that the Institute avoid overt party politics and revolutionary publicity. Critics describe failure to support émigrés or resistance adequately; supporters stress the survival of a targeted institution and continuing analysis of fascism. The Institute did materially support selected refugees, especially Benjamin, but was neither a resistance organization nor a general relief agency.

### 4. U.S. intelligence and the CIA

**Documented:** Marcuse, Neumann, Kirchheimer and other associates worked for OSS/U.S. agencies; Horkheimer’s Institute interacted with U.S. foundations, AJC, universities, and postwar reeducation networks. Some Institute-connected intellectuals later published in Congress for Cultural Freedom settings.  
**Not established:** that Horkheimer was a CIA officer, that the CIA created or secretly directed the Frankfurt School, or that *Dialectic of Enlightenment* was an intelligence operation. The CIA was created in 1947, after the Institute’s 1923 founding and after the first 1944 *Dialectic* typescript.  
**Legitimate unresolved research question:** how Cold War patronage, anti-communism, and Western institutions shaped the postwar reception and choices of Horkheimer’s circle.

### 5. Mannesmann and compromised postwar elites

**Documented:** the Institute undertook a 1954 industrial sociology project for Mannesmann; the firm and senior management had deep Nazi-era involvement.  
**Interpretation:** critics argue that corporate research served labor management and anti-socialism. Without the full contract and project archive, a specific covert union-suppression purpose should not be stated as fact. The broader compromise with restored West German corporate power is real.

### 6. Anti-communism, Vietnam, Israel, and 1968

Horkheimer’s late defense of the Western bloc, hostility to Soviet communism, skepticism about decolonizing and student movements, sympathy for U.S. strategic power, and support for Israel are documented in correspondence/interviews and historical studies. Whether this constituted betrayal of critical theory is an ideological evaluation, not a factual finding. He was not charged with political violence or illegal activity. There is no evidence that he directed police action comparable to Adorno’s famous 1969 calling of police on occupying students.

### 7. Elitism, mass culture, and alleged cultural pessimism

Critics argue that “culture industry” theory treats audiences as passive, generalizes from U.S. commercial entertainment, and privileges European high art. Defenders note that Horkheimer and Adorno analyzed industrial organization and standardized choice rather than claiming all popular art is worthless. Both the textual elitism and the caricature require attention.

### 8. Sex, family, and contraception

The late endorsement of Paul VI’s anti-contraception stance is documented and contrasts with later sexual-liberation politics. No evidence was found of sexual abuse, addiction, criminal sexual conduct, or a secret family. Assertions beyond the documented marriage and philosophical remarks are rumor.

### 9. Wealth and conflicts of interest

Horkheimer came from wealth and directed a privately endowed institute. Patronage relationships affected institutional choices. No audited personal net worth is publicly established; no evidence was found of corruption, embezzlement, money laundering, tax prosecution, or illegal enrichment.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### Myth A: “Horkheimer founded the Frankfurt School/Institute”

1. **Story:** Horkheimer created the Institute and assembled the Frankfurt School.  
2. **Appearance/circulation:** Short biographies, textbooks, popular videos, and both admirers and critics.  
3. **Evidence:** The Institute was planned and financed by Felix Weil, approved in 1923, and opened under Grünberg in 1924; Horkheimer took over in 1930/31.  
4. **Assessment:** False if “founded” means legal/financial creation; substantially true that his directorship created the program later known as Frankfurt School critical theory.  
5. **Effect:** Marginalized Weil, Gerlach, Grünberg, Pollock, and Fromm while personalizing a collective institution.

### Myth B: “Cultural Marxism was a Frankfurt School plot to destroy Western civilization”

1. **Story:** Horkheimer and a secretive Jewish/Marxist group deliberately infiltrated U.S. universities, feminism, media, race politics, and sexuality to dissolve Christianity, family, and nation.  
2. **Emergence:** Late-20th-century U.S. paleoconservative accounts, especially around William S. Lind and the Free Congress Foundation; amplified online and connected to the older Nazi term “cultural Bolshevism.”  
3. **Circulators:** Far-right activists, conspiracy sites, some mass shooters’ manifestos, and partisan media.  
4. **Evidence for/against:** The Institute really was Marxian, privately funded, exiled to Columbia, critical of bourgeois family/culture, and enormously influential. There is no evidence of a coordinated secret program to control institutions or of the often-circulated “eleven-point plan” attributed to the Frankfurt School. Horkheimer’s late defense of family, religion’s longing, contraception restrictions, Israel, and Western anti-communism contradicts the simplified plot.  
5. **Effect:** Turned a heterogeneous scholarly network into an ethnicized conspiracy symbol and made Horkheimer more famous outside philosophy.

### Myth C: “The Frankfurt School invented political correctness”

The claim appears in the same polemical genealogy. Horkheimer did analyze language, prejudice, and authoritarian personalities, but neither coined the modern term nor designed modern speech codes. It is a retrospective association, not a documented project.

### Myth D: “The CIA funded/created critical theory”

The story combines genuine facts—OSS service by Institute associates, U.S. foundation patronage, Cold War anti-communism, CCF cultural networks—with anachronism. Critical theory’s core program and major texts predate the CIA. Research into indirect postwar networks is legitimate; claims of CIA creation or command remain unsupported.

### Myth E: “Horkheimer was a revolutionary communist” / “Horkheimer was never really Marxist”

These opposing hostile myths each isolate one phase. His 1930s theory was unmistakably built through Marx and aimed beyond capitalism, yet he avoided party membership and distrusted Soviet orthodoxy. His later anti-communism and pessimism were real. The trajectory changed; a single fixed label erases the chronology.

### Myth F: Arrest as Ernst Toller

The anecdote says Bavarian authorities mistook Horkheimer for Toller during the Munich revolution and briefly jailed him. It appears in serious biographies and popular accounts, but the accessible evidentiary chain usually terminates in later biographical narration rather than an arrest file. It is plausible and well established in the literature, but exact date, detention site, and duration remain uncertain.

## Works by Max Horkheimer

### Authorship and editorial cautions

- *Dialectic of Enlightenment* is genuinely co-authored with Theodor W. Adorno; attribution of discrete sentences is often impossible because they revised one another’s drafts. Gretel Adorno’s uncredited editorial labor should be noted without converting her automatically into a third legal author.
- *Studies in Prejudice* and *The Authoritarian Personality* were organized/sponsored by Horkheimer, but he did **not** author all volumes or the latter book.
- The 19-volume *Gesammelte Schriften* contains lifetime publications, lectures, juvenilia, notes, institutional memoranda, and correspondence edited posthumously; inclusion does not mean Horkheimer published each item as a finished work.
- *Dämmerung* first appeared under **Heinrich Regius**.

## Books and Major Works About Horkheimer

### Biographies and intellectual studies

- John Abromeit, ***Max Horkheimer and the Foundations of the Frankfurt School*** (Cambridge University Press, 2011). The fullest intellectual biography of 1895–1941; especially strong on early development and changing concepts of history.
- Rolf Wiggershaus, ***Die Frankfurter Schule*** (1986); English, ***The Frankfurt School: Its History, Theories, and Political Significance*** (MIT Press, 1994/95). Broad institutional history with extensive archival use.
- Martin Jay, ***The Dialectical Imagination: A History of the Frankfurt School and the Institute of Social Research, 1923–1950*** (1973; later editions). Foundational English-language institutional history, based on interviews and archives; later scholarship revises aspects.
- Helmut Gumnior and Rudolf Ringguth, ***Max Horkheimer in Selbstzeugnissen und Bilddokumenten*** (1973). Contemporary illustrated introduction.
- Zoltán Tar, ***The Frankfurt School: The Critical Theories of Max Horkheimer and Theodor W. Adorno*** (1977).
- Seyla Benhabib, Wolfgang Bonß, and John McCole, eds., ***On Max Horkheimer: New Perspectives*** (MIT Press, 1993).
- John Abromeit, “Max Horkheimer” in the ***Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy***, a reliable synthetic intellectual biography with bibliography.
- Nicola Emery, ***For Nonconformism: Max Horkheimer and Friedrich Pollock—The Other Frankfurt School*** (English trans., Brill, 2023). Reconstructs the Pollock–Horkheimer symbiosis.
- Thomas Wheatland, ***The Frankfurt School in Exile*** (University of Minnesota Press, 2009). Detailed U.S. institutional and reception history.
- Stuart Jeffries, ***Grand Hotel Abyss: The Lives of the Frankfurt School*** (Verso, 2016). Readable collective biography; interpretive and sometimes dramatized.
- Detlev Claussen, ***Theodor W. Adorno: One Last Genius*** (English 2008). Valuable on the Horkheimer–Adorno relationship.
- Rolf Tiedemann and others’ editorial work in the Horkheimer and Adorno collected editions and correspondence.
- Manfred Gangl, studies of Friedrich Pollock and state capitalism, important for correcting Horkheimer-centered histories.
- Neil McLaughlin, “Origin Myths in the Social Sciences: Fromm, the Frankfurt School and the Emergence of Critical Theory” (1999), on Fromm’s erasure and institutional memory.
- Peter M. R. Stirk, ***Max Horkheimer: A New Interpretation*** (1992).
- Alfred Schmidt, studies on Horkheimer’s materialism, Schopenhauer, and critical theory.
- Brian J. Shaw, “Reason, Nostalgia, and Eschatology in the Critical Theory of Max Horkheimer” (1985), on late thought.

### Hostile and polemical literature

- Georg Lukács’s “Grand Hotel Abyss” image—originally aimed broadly at Western Marxist intellectuals—became a durable criticism of comfortable radical pessimism.
- Perry Anderson and later Marxist critics treated Frankfurt critical theory as Western Marxism’s displacement from political economy and practice into philosophy/culture.
- Gabriel Rockhill’s essays allege deep integration into Cold War anti-communist cultural power. They assemble real institutional connections but make contested inferences about their meaning; they should be read alongside Wheatland, Jay, Wiggershaus, correspondence, and recent rebuttals such as the [Historical Materialism exchange](https://www.historicalmaterialism.org/article/theory-betrayed-an-essay-on-gabriel-rockhills-who-paid-the-pipers-of-western-marxism-part-one/).
- William S. Lind and Free Congress Foundation media popularized the “cultural Marxism” conspiracy genealogy. These are sources for reception/propaganda, not reliable biographies.

## Illnesses, Psychological Crises, and Death

- Wartime medical unfitness/illness limited his 1917–18 military service; exact diagnosis is inconsistently reported in accessible sources.
- Exile correspondence records anxiety, overwork, insomnia/health concerns, and financial/institutional strain, but retrospective psychiatric diagnoses are unwarranted.
- The deaths of Maidon and Adorno in 1969 and Pollock in 1970 were major late-life losses.
- No documented addiction was found.
- Exact medical cause of death was not established in the consulted public sources. Some accounts say he died following a routine examination in Nuremberg. Date and place are secure; proximate pathology is not.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Horkheimer’s reputation has passed through several phases:

1. **1930s–40s:** known within European and émigré social science as Institute director and journal editor; less individually famous than later.
2. **1950s:** West German university leader and organizer of democratic social research; critical Marxian vocabulary muted in public reception.
3. **1960s–70s:** *Dialectic of Enlightenment* rediscovered by students, often against its authors’ politics; Marcuse became the movement icon while Horkheimer was cast as conservative elder.
4. **1970s–90s:** Jay, Wiggershaus, Habermas, and collected editions established “Frankfurt School” canons; Horkheimer was often treated mainly as Adorno’s co-author and administrator.
5. **1990s–present:** renewed study of early critical theory, religion, state capitalism, social psychology, antisemitism, and Horkheimer’s independent philosophy; Abromeit restored the pre-1941 intellectual biography; Fromm and Weil scholarship challenged Horkheimer-centered origin stories.
6. **Internet political mythology:** “cultural Marxism” narratives recast him as architect of civilizational subversion, while some Marxist polemics recast him as a Cold War asset. Both rely on selective real facts joined by unsupported totalizing claims.

His durable conceptual legacy includes **critical versus traditional theory**, **instrumental reason**, **culture industry** (with Adorno), interdisciplinary study of authority and prejudice, and the paradox that rational mastery can reproduce myth and domination. His institutional legacy is equally large: he kept the IfS alive through expropriation and exile and reestablished it in Frankfurt. The same record also preserves exclusions, financial dependencies, political caution, postwar compromises, and a late estrangement from radical movements.

## Research Limitations and Unresolved Questions

- A full factory/corporate genealogy and audited Horkheimer personal finances were not available in consulted public records.
- Exact military unit, dates, and medical diagnosis for 1917–18 require personnel records or the printed correspondence.
- The Ernst Toller mistaken-identity arrest lacks an accessible primary arrest record in the sources consulted.
- Exact cause of death remains unspecified.
- The complete item-level bibliography exists primarily in *Gesammelte Schriften*, vol. 19; the present dossier gives the principal works and edition structure rather than reproducing hundreds of archival items.
- Claims about CIA direction, corporate union suppression, and an intentional program of cultural destruction exceed the located documentary evidence and are labeled accordingly.
