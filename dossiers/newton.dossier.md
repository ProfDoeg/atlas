# Sir Isaac Newton: Research Dossier

## Scope, identity, dating, and evidentiary conventions

This dossier concerns **Sir Isaac Newton**, the English natural philosopher, mathematician, astronomer, biblical scholar, alchemical investigator, public official, and Master of the Royal Mint.

Dates before Britain adopted the Gregorian calendar in 1752 require care:

- Newton was born **25 December 1642 Old Style**, equivalent to **4 January 1643 New Style**.
- He died **20 March 1726/27 Old Style**, equivalent to **31 March 1727 New Style**.
- This dossier normally gives the date as it appeared in contemporary English records, followed by the modern equivalent where confusion is likely.

Evidentiary labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary documents, manuscripts, official records, or securely established material evidence.
- **Reported fact:** recorded by a reasonably informed witness or early biographer but not independently documented.
- **Disputed interpretation:** the underlying evidence is genuine, but its meaning remains contested.
- **Allegation:** an accusation made by a contemporary or later writer without a conclusive adjudication establishing it.
- **Rumor or myth:** a culturally important story lacking adequate historical proof.
- **False or unsupported claim:** contradicted by surviving evidence or unsupported after investigation.

The principal documentary backbone is the Newton Project’s editions of Newton’s manuscripts and correspondence, Cambridge University Library’s Newton holdings, Royal Society material, Royal Mint records, the papers assembled by John Conduitt, and the manuscript collections associated with Portsmouth, John Maynard Keynes, Abraham Shalom Yahuda, and Grace K. Babson.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal family

Newton’s father, also named **Isaac Newton**, was a yeoman farmer and lord of the small manor of Woolsthorpe. Surviving family material describes him as unable to sign his name but economically comfortable. He died in October 1642, roughly three months before his son’s birth. The cause is unrecorded.

Newton’s paternal grandfather was **Robert Newton**, who acquired Woolsthorpe Manor in 1627. Later claims that the Woolsthorpe Newtons were closely connected to the baronetical Newton family of Haydor or Culverthorpe were investigated during Newton’s lifetime and afterward. The evidence did not establish the socially advantageous relationship with confidence.

**Documented fact:** Newton inherited land through the paternal line and was born into a prosperous rural household, not destitution.

**Disputed or unsupported family mythology:** Attempts to elevate the family into an ancient or aristocratic Newton lineage exceed what the surviving records prove.

### Mother and Ayscough family

Newton’s mother was **Hannah Ayscough**, daughter of James Ayscough of Market Overton, Rutland. The Ayscoughs included clergy and landholders. Her brother, the Reverend **William Ayscough**, later helped persuade her that Newton should return to school and prepare for university.

Newton was reportedly premature and extremely small. John Conduitt preserved Hannah’s recollection that the infant could have fitted into a quart pot. This is an early family report rather than a medical record, but it is plausible and became part of Newton’s birth legend.

### Barnabas Smith and the half-family

On 27 January 1646 OS, Hannah married **Barnabas Smith**, the rector of North Witham, who was considerably older than she was. She moved to Smith’s rectory and left the approximately three-year-old Newton at Woolsthorpe with his maternal grandmother, **Margery Ayscough**.

The separation was psychologically significant. In a private list of sins made in 1662, Newton remembered having threatened his “father and mother Smith” that he would burn them and their house. The entry is authentic. Whether it proves a lasting clinical trauma is interpretive, but it documents childhood rage toward the household from which he had been excluded.

Hannah and Barnabas Smith had three children, Newton’s maternal half-siblings:

- **Mary Smith**, born 1647.
- **Benjamin Smith**, born 1651.
- **Hannah Smith**, born 1652.

Barnabas Smith died in 1653. Hannah returned to Woolsthorpe with her three younger children. Newton was then about ten.

### Nieces, nephews, household, and heirs

Newton never married and had no documented children. His most important later family relationship was with his half-niece **Catherine Barton** (1679–1739), daughter of his half-sister Hannah Smith and Robert Barton. Catherine became his London household manager and social intermediary.

She married **John Conduitt** in 1717. Conduitt later became Newton’s deputy and successor as Master of the Mint. Their daughter, Catherine Conduitt, married into the Portsmouth family; through that descent, many Newton manuscripts became the **Portsmouth Papers**.

Newton died intestate. His personal estate, valued by an early account at roughly **£32,000**, was divided among eight nephews and nieces of the half-blood. The Woolsthorpe and Sustern real property passed to the heir-at-law, John Newton. The exact net value depends on how securities, debts, and property are counted, but Newton plainly died wealthy.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1642–1661

### Woolsthorpe and North Witham

Newton’s first years unfolded amid the English Civil War, although no evidence places him in military action. Woolsthorpe lay in rural Lincolnshire, away from the principal theaters of his later life.

His father’s death, his mother’s remarriage, and his separation from her formed the central family events of his childhood. There is no evidence that Newton was physically imprisoned, exiled, or forcibly displaced.

### King’s School, Grantham

In 1654 Newton entered the **King’s School, Grantham**, about seven miles from Woolsthorpe. He lodged with **William Clark**, an apothecary. The household probably gave him access to medicinal and chemical materials and has often been treated as one stimulus to his later experimental interests.

Early biographical traditions describe Newton:

- making mechanical models, clocks, sundials, lantern-bearing kites, and a model windmill;
- drawing geometric and architectural figures on walls;
- first performing poorly and then rising to the top of his class after a fight or rivalry with another boy;
- forming an attachment to Clark’s stepdaughter, usually identified as **Catherine Storer**, later Mrs Vincent.

These stories derive largely from reminiscences collected decades later by Conduitt, William Stukeley, and early biographers.

**Reported fact:** Newton built models and devices as a boy; the reports are consistent with his later mechanical aptitude.

**Reported romantic attachment:** Catherine Storer or her family reportedly remembered a youthful attachment. No contemporary courtship letters or engagement documents survive. Claims that she was his fiancée are stronger than the evidence supports.

### Failed attempt to make him a farmer

Around 1658 Hannah removed Newton from school to prepare him to manage the family property. Reports say that he neglected markets and agricultural business in favor of reading and mechanical pursuits. His schoolmaster **Henry Stokes** and uncle William Ayscough persuaded Hannah to return him to Grantham and prepare him for Cambridge.

This episode is well established in outline, though anecdotes of deliberate incompetence may have been sharpened to foreshadow the genius unsuited to ordinary work.

## Education and Formation

### Admission to Trinity College

Newton entered **Trinity College, Cambridge**, on 5 June 1661 OS. College records classify him first as a **subsizar** and then a **sizar**—a student receiving financial support and traditionally undertaking service obligations. This did not mean that his family was destitute; the exact mixture of economy, status, and college custom is uncertain.

The prescribed curriculum remained Aristotelian, emphasizing logic, ethics, rhetoric, and natural philosophy. Newton increasingly pursued modern authors outside it.

### Early notebooks and intellectual sources

His surviving notebooks include the **Waste Book** and the **Quaestiones quaedam philosophicae**. They document intensive engagement with:

- Aristotle and scholastic natural philosophy;
- René Descartes;
- Galileo Galilei;
- Johannes Kepler;
- Nicolaus Copernicus;
- Pierre Gassendi, often through Walter Charleton;
- Thomas Hobbes;
- Robert Boyle;
- Henry More;
- Kenelm Digby;
- Euclid, Viète, Wallis, Oughtred, and Descartes in mathematics.

The notebook motto commonly rendered “Plato is my friend, Aristotle is my friend, but my best friend is truth” was written by Newton. It should not be mistaken for an original Newtonian aphorism; it adapts an older saying.

Newton became largely self-directed in advanced mathematics. **Isaac Barrow**, first Lucasian Professor, recognized and supported him, but describing Barrow as the inventor of Newton’s ideas or Newton as merely Barrow’s pupil misrepresents the surviving mathematical papers.

### Degrees and fellowship

- **1664:** elected a scholar of Trinity.
- **1665:** BA.
- **1667:** elected Fellow of Trinity after returning from the plague interruptions.
- **1668:** MA.
- **1669:** succeeded Isaac Barrow as Lucasian Professor of Mathematics, at approximately twenty-six.

The fellowship statutes normally required ordination. Newton obtained a royal dispensation in 1675. His private rejection of the Trinity and other orthodox doctrines would have made ordination perilous or dishonest, but the dispensation itself did not publicly identify him as a heretic.

## Chronological Life History

## 1661–1665: Cambridge apprenticeship and rapid mathematical development

Newton’s early Cambridge course began conventionally but soon became independent. By 1664–1665 he had worked through contemporary algebra and geometry and begun constructing new infinite-series techniques. He studied refraction, colors, motion, matter, and celestial phenomena.

The idea that he arrived at Cambridge already recognized as a prodigy is retrospective. Surviving evidence shows a young man whose intellectual development accelerated dramatically after sustained private study.

## 1665–1667: Plague years at Woolsthorpe

Cambridge dispersed students during outbreaks of plague. Newton returned for extended periods to Woolsthorpe, though he moved between Lincolnshire and Cambridge rather than living in total two-year isolation.

During these “years of wonders” he developed:

- generalized binomial expansions;
- infinite series;
- central parts of the method later called **fluxions**, one form of differential and integral calculus;
- experiments showing that white light is composed of differently refrangible rays;
- early investigations of circular motion and terrestrial–celestial gravity.

The often-repeated claim that Newton completed calculus, optics, and universal gravitation in final form during a single miraculous year is false as stated. He formed decisive ideas in 1665–1667, but the theories were repeatedly revised over the next two decades.

### The apple

The apple story is treated separately below. Its core—a falling apple prompting thought about the reach of terrestrial gravity—was told by Newton late in life to several people. There is no evidence that an apple struck his head.

## 1667–1671: Fellow, professor, mathematics, and optical instrument

After election to a Trinity fellowship in 1667, Newton consolidated his mathematical work.

In 1668 he constructed a small **reflecting telescope**. It used a concave metal mirror rather than a large refracting objective and thereby avoided chromatic aberration in the primary image. Earlier reflecting-telescope concepts existed, including designs associated with James Gregory, but Newton produced the first practically successful instrument of its type known to have been demonstrated publicly.

In 1669 Barrow communicated Newton’s manuscript **De analysi per aequationes numero terminorum infinitas** to John Collins. Barrow resigned the Lucasian chair, and Newton took office in October.

Newton’s lectures covered optics, algebra, and mathematical method. Stories that he sometimes lectured to empty rooms derive from later reports and are plausible, but not every such anecdote is independently documented.

## 1671–1676: Royal Society, light controversy, and retreat

Newton’s telescope was shown to the Royal Society in 1671. He was elected a Fellow in January 1672. In February the Society published his paper generally called **“A New Theory about Light and Colours.”**

Newton argued that white light is a mixture of rays with differing refrangibility and that color is an original property of the rays, not a modification imposed by glass or another medium. His prism experiments—especially attempts to isolate and refract rays a second time—were designed as “crucial experiments.”

**Robert Hooke**, an established authority on optics, criticized Newton’s interpretation and priority claims. Other critics included Christiaan Huygens and Jesuit investigators. Newton reacted defensively, engaged in detailed correspondence, and increasingly withdrew from public controversy.

The popular formula that Hooke “rejected Newton’s discovery” is too simple. Hooke accepted important experimental results but disputed Newton’s corpuscular and theoretical conclusions and believed Newton undervalued earlier optical work.

In 1675 Newton submitted **“An Hypothesis explaining the Properties of Light”**, involving an ether and speculative mechanisms. This shows that the later slogan *hypotheses non fingo* did not mean he never framed hypotheses; it concerned what he would admit as established within a particular demonstrative argument.

## 1670s–1690s: Alchemical and theological work

Alongside mathematics and optics, Newton undertook extensive “chymical” experimentation and transcription. Indiana University’s Chymistry of Isaac Newton project estimates about a million surviving words relating to alchemy.

He studied authors including George Starkey, writing as Eirenaeus Philalethes, and copied, compared, decoded, and experimentally tested recipes concerning:

- metallic transformations;
- antimony, mercury, lead, gold, and volatile salts;
- the “vegetation” or active principles of matter;
- the philosophers’ stone and the mercury of the philosophers;
- ancient prisca sapientia, or primordial wisdom.

No surviving evidence shows that Newton successfully transmuted base metal into gold. Nor is the alchemical work simply fraudulent or decorative: laboratory notebooks document sustained material experiments. The relationship between this work and his physics remains debated. Some historians find conceptual traffic between alchemical active principles and Newtonian forces; others warn against reducing the mechanics to alchemy.

His theological studies were at least as extensive. By the 1670s Newton had concluded that fourth-century church authorities had corrupted primitive Christianity. He rejected the co-equality and co-eternity of the Son with the Father and treated worship of Christ as the supreme God as idolatrous.

The labels **Arian**, **Socinian**, **Unitarian**, or **anti-Trinitarian** capture parts of his position but not its entire development. Newton kept these views private because denial of the Trinity threatened his university position, public office, and reputation.

## 1679–1684: Hooke correspondence and orbital dynamics

In 1679 Hooke reopened correspondence and challenged Newton to consider planetary motion as the combination of tangential inertia and attraction toward a center. Hooke asserted that an inverse-square attraction would generate elliptical motion.

**Documented fact:** Hooke supplied Newton with a conceptual challenge involving compounded motion and inverse-square attraction.

**Disputed priority:** Hooke lacked Newton’s general mathematical demonstrations and never produced the *Principia*. Newton nevertheless minimized Hooke’s role and resisted the acknowledgments Hooke sought. Historians generally distinguish Hooke’s significant stimulus from Newton’s comprehensive derivation and synthesis.

## 1684–1687: Halley, *De motu*, and the *Principia*

In August 1684 **Edmond Halley** visited Newton in Cambridge and asked what orbit would follow an inverse-square attraction. Newton answered “an ellipse” and later sent the tract **De motu corporum in gyrum**.

With Halley’s persistent encouragement, Newton expanded the tract into three books of **Philosophiæ naturalis principia mathematica**.

The *Principia* established:

- definitions of mass, momentum, impressed force, and centripetal force;
- the three laws of motion;
- the mathematical theory of central forces;
- derivations of Keplerian orbital relations;
- a universal inverse-square gravitation applying to terrestrial and celestial bodies;
- treatment of tides, comets, the Earth’s figure, and lunar and planetary inequalities.

The Royal Society had exhausted much of its publishing capacity on the commercially unsuccessful *Historia Piscium*. Halley supervised and substantially financed the printing, managed corrections and illustrations, and promoted the book. The first edition appeared in 1687, probably in 300–400 copies.

Hooke asserted that Newton had taken the inverse-square idea from him. Newton replied that Hooke had not demonstrated the results and that earlier figures had considered related principles. Newton nearly removed parts of Book III during the dispute. The final work acknowledged Hooke, Wren, and Halley for recognizing the inverse-square rule in the solar system, but not to the degree Hooke wanted.

## 1687–1690: University resistance to James II and Parliament

In 1687 King James II’s government attempted to force Cambridge to grant an MA to the Benedictine monk Alban Francis without the statutory oaths. Newton served among the university delegates defending its charter and Protestant legal position before the Court of High Commission.

After the Glorious Revolution, Cambridge University elected Newton to the Convention Parliament in January 1689. He sat until the dissolution in 1690.

His documented parliamentary importance lies principally in his representation of university and Protestant interests. The famous story that his only speech was a request to close a window is not securely supported by parliamentary records and should be treated as anecdote, not fact.

Newton was aligned with Whig political and intellectual networks, notably Charles Montagu and John Locke, but he was not a major parliamentary or party theorist.

## 1688–1693: Locke, Huygens, Fatio, Bentley, and theology

### John Locke

Locke reviewed or summarized the *Principia* and corresponded with Newton about natural philosophy, theology, biblical criticism, and alchemy. Newton sent him **An Historical Account of Two Notable Corruptions of Scripture**, arguing that 1 John 5:7 and 1 Timothy 3:16 had been textually altered in ways favoring Trinitarian doctrine.

Locke praised Newton’s scriptural learning. Their correspondence also involved secret alchemical material associated with Robert Boyle.

### Christiaan Huygens

Newton and Huygens corresponded and met through the European mathematical community. Huygens respected Newton’s mathematical achievement but disputed parts of his optical and gravitational philosophy, especially action at a distance without an identified mechanism.

### Nicolas Fatio de Duillier

Newton met the young Swiss mathematician **Nicolas Fatio de Duillier** around 1689. Their surviving correspondence shows intense friendship, intellectual alliance, plans or wishes to live near one another, concern about health, and a bitter rupture or cooling in 1693.

The relationship has generated speculation about Newton’s sexuality. The emotional closeness is documented; a sexual relationship is not.

### Richard Bentley

In 1692–1693 Newton answered theological questions from the clergyman Richard Bentley, who was preparing the first Boyle Lectures. Newton argued that the ordering of the solar system supported intelligent divine agency and that an initially uniform distribution of matter could not form the system without divine intervention.

## 1693: Crisis or breakdown

In 1693 Newton sent disturbed and accusatory letters to Samuel Pepys and John Locke. He told Pepys he was “extremely troubled at the embroilment I am in,” denied seeking favors, and withdrew from acquaintance. He accused Locke of attempting to entangle him with women and of other designs, then apologized.

The episode followed prolonged work, insomnia, chemical experimentation, disappointment in securing public employment, and tension with Fatio.

**Documented fact:** Newton suffered insomnia, agitation, suspiciousness, social rupture, and impaired judgment for a period in 1693.

**Disputed diagnosis:** Later writers have called it a nervous breakdown, psychosis, paranoid episode, severe depression, exhaustion, or mercury poisoning. No diagnosis can be established retrospectively.

Hair attributed to Newton has yielded high mercury and other metal concentrations. Authentication and the relationship between hair chemistry and the 1693 episode remain uncertain. Mercury exposure through alchemical work is plausible; “mercury poisoning caused Newton’s madness” is not a proven finding.

By 1694 Newton was again engaged in demanding mathematical and administrative work.

## 1694–1696: Flamsteed, lunar theory, and search for public office

Newton sought accurate observations from **John Flamsteed**, Astronomer Royal, for lunar theory and the second generation of *Principia* calculations. Their collaboration deteriorated because Newton pressed for data that Flamsteed considered unfinished and proprietary, while Flamsteed believed Newton failed to acknowledge his contributions adequately.

Newton also sought a remunerative government appointment. His Cambridge income and estates made him comfortable, but office offered wealth, influence, and escape from an increasingly unsatisfying university life.

## 1696–1699: Warden of the Mint and the Great Recoinage

On the recommendation of **Charles Montagu**, Chancellor of the Exchequer and later Earl of Halifax, Newton became **Warden of the Royal Mint** in March 1696. He moved toward permanent London residence, taking office at the Tower during the Great Recoinage.

The Recoinage replaced clipped and degraded hammered silver with milled coin. Branch mints operated at Bristol, Chester, Exeter, Norwich, and York. Newton dealt with:

- bullion intake and coin output;
- machinery, personnel, accounts, and supply;
- disputes among moneyers and officers;
- the legal and evidentiary apparatus for counterfeiting cases.

The wardenship was traditionally capable of sinecure treatment, but Newton pursued it intensively. Royal Mint historians caution, however, against Macaulay’s heroic claim that he revolutionized the institution: Newton improved exactness and administration but left much of its medieval contractor structure intact.

## Counterfeit investigations and William Chaloner

Coin clipping and counterfeiting were high treason and could carry death sentences. Newton cultivated informers, examined prisoners, gathered sworn testimony, visited prisons and taverns through agents, and prepared cases.

His most famous target was **William Chaloner**, a counterfeiter and confidence man who accused the Mint of corruption before Parliament and proposed reforms that would have given him access or influence.

Chaloner survived an earlier prosecution but was convicted at the Old Bailey in March 1699 and hanged at Tyburn.

**Documented fact:** Newton personally assembled testimony and directed the Crown’s evidentiary campaign against Chaloner.

**Documented legal result:** Chaloner was convicted and executed under the contemporary law of high treason.

**Allegation by Chaloner’s allies:** Newton manipulated trial timing and sought a hostile recorder or jury. Affidavits repeating such claims survive.

**Resolution:** No judicial finding established criminal misconduct by Newton. Chaloner was himself demonstrably involved in counterfeiting and had strategic reasons to attack the Mint. Nevertheless, Newton’s private prosecutorial role, use of paid or compromised informers, and investment in obtaining capital convictions are established—not later fictional embellishments.

Newton’s papers document many additional suspects, including women and poor participants in counterfeiting networks. The extent to which his actions should be called ordinary enforcement or personal ruthlessness is interpretive; the investigations themselves are not disputed.

## 1699–1703: Master of the Mint, London establishment, and second Parliament

In 1699 Newton became **Master and Worker of the Mint**, succeeding Thomas Neale. The Mastership was technically below the Warden in hierarchy but substantially more lucrative because the Master contracted for coin production.

He formally resigned the Lucasian professorship and his Trinity fellowship in 1701. His protégé **William Whiston** succeeded him as Lucasian Professor.

Newton was again returned as MP for Cambridge University in 1701 and served until 1702. He declined to conduct another contested campaign.

London residences included:

- a house in or near **Jermyn Street** by 1697;
- temporary residence near Chelsea around 1709;
- **Martin Street, Leicester Square**, from about 1710 to 1725;
- Kensington from 1725 for health and cleaner air.

He maintained an increasingly elite household, with Catherine Barton acting as hostess.

## 1703–1705: Royal Society presidency, *Opticks*, and knighthood

After Robert Hooke’s death, Newton was elected **President of the Royal Society** in 1703 and was reelected annually until his death.

In 1704 he published **Opticks: or, A Treatise of the Reflexions, Refractions, Inflexions and Colours of Light**. Unlike the Latin *Principia*, it appeared in English and emphasized experimentally ordered propositions.

It treated:

- the decomposition and recombination of white light;
- spectra and refrangibility;
- thin-film colors, later called Newton’s rings;
- reflection, refraction, diffraction or “inflexion”;
- the design limitations of refracting telescopes;
- speculative “Queries” on matter, forces, ether, chemistry, heat, and physiology.

Newton favored a corpuscular account of light but entertained wave-like or medium-based processes. Describing him simply as having “proved light is particles” overstates both his evidence and his position.

Queen Anne knighted him at Trinity College on 16 April 1705. Political considerations surrounding the Cambridge election contributed to the occasion. He was the second English natural philosopher knighted, after Francis Bacon, and the first to receive the honor primarily in the context of scientific eminence and public service.

## Catherine Barton and Charles Montagu

Catherine Barton was admired for wit and beauty and moved within the Whig elite. Charles Montagu left her a substantial annuity, property, and a generous legacy, describing his affection and the pleasure of her conversation.

Contemporary and later rumor held that she was Montagu’s mistress.

- **Documented fact:** Barton and Montagu were close; he made unusually generous financial provisions for her.
- **Contemporary rumor:** Their relationship was sexual.
- **Unresolved:** No surviving document conclusively proves or disproves the allegation.
- **Unsupported extension:** There is no proof that Newton obtained Mint office as payment for facilitating a sexual relationship. Montagu and Newton were already Cambridge and political associates, and Newton’s appointment preceded the clearest evidence of Barton’s place in Montagu’s household.

## 1705–1710: Elections, patronage, and institutional authority

Newton unsuccessfully contested the Cambridge University election of 1705. The loss was a significant public failure despite royal and ministerial favor.

As Royal Society president and Mint master he built patronage networks, promoted associates, controlled access to manuscripts and observations, and defended institutional precedence. Protégés included Samuel Clarke, William Whiston before their theological divergence, Roger Cotes, John Keill, Brook Taylor, and later Henry Pemberton.

Newton’s relations with Whiston deteriorated. Whiston openly embraced anti-Trinitarian positions and was expelled from his Cambridge professorship in 1710. Newton shared significant heterodox beliefs but did not publicly defend Whiston. Whether this was hypocrisy, prudence, doctrinal difference, or an unwillingness to expose himself is interpretive.

## 1710: Trial of the Pyx

A jury at the Trial of the Pyx found gold coins from Newton’s Mint below standard. Newton reacted vigorously and demonstrated that the recently introduced trial plate, not the coins, was too fine. The older standard plate was restored.

This incident illustrates his concern with precision and his sensitivity to anything implying personal or institutional dishonor.

## 1708–1716: Leibniz calculus controversy

Newton developed his method of fluxions in the 1660s but delayed full publication. **Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz** developed differential calculus independently in the 1670s and published in 1684 and 1686. Leibniz’s notation proved more adaptable and became standard.

John Keill publicly accused Leibniz of borrowing from Newton. Leibniz appealed to the Royal Society. A committee was appointed, and in 1712–1713 the Society issued the **Commercium Epistolicum**, concluding that Newton had priority and implying that Leibniz derived key ideas from material originating with Newton.

The procedure was not impartial:

- Newton was president of the adjudicating institution.
- The committee consisted largely of his associates.
- Newton assembled or controlled key documentary material.
- Newton substantially wrote or shaped the report.
- In 1715 he anonymously reviewed and defended the report in the *Philosophical Transactions*.

**Modern historical judgment:** Newton reached major calculus concepts earlier; Leibniz developed and published his system independently; neither should be treated as the sole inventor of all calculus. The plagiarism case against Leibniz is not accepted as established.

**Documented institutional misconduct by modern standards:** Newton used an officially “impartial” process to advocate his own case without transparent disclosure of authorship. Whether “misconduct” is applied as a contemporary or modern procedural category should be specified.

The dispute hardened English–Continental mathematical divisions. British adherence to fluxional notation contributed, though was not the sole cause, to relative isolation from Continental analysis.

## 1704–1715: Flamsteed publication dispute

Prince George of Denmark had supported publication of Flamsteed’s astronomical observations. Newton, Halley, and institutional trustees pressed Flamsteed to surrender manuscripts. An incomplete version of **Historia Coelestis Britannica**, edited by Halley, was published in 1712 without Flamsteed’s final consent and with alterations he opposed.

Flamsteed obtained many unsold copies after Prince George’s death and burned them in 1715.

- **Documented fact:** Newton used his Royal Society and official authority to pressure Flamsteed and facilitate publication of unfinished work.
- **Flamsteed’s position:** His intellectual property and reputation had been violated.
- **Newton’s position:** Publicly funded observations were being improperly withheld from necessary scientific use.
- **Historical assessment:** Newton’s conduct is commonly judged coercive and procedurally improper; Flamsteed was also chronically slow and possessive, but that does not erase the unauthorized publication.

The 1713 second edition of the *Principia* reduced acknowledgments of Flamsteed and Leibniz. Roger Cotes edited the edition and supplied a preface attacking rival natural philosophies. The new **General Scholium** discussed God, space, gravitation, and Newton’s refusal to frame an unsupported physical cause of gravity.

## 1713–1717: European authority, Princess Caroline, and monetary report

Newton corresponded indirectly or directly with European scholars and received distinguished visitors. His ideas entered the Leibniz–Clarke correspondence of 1715–1716; Samuel Clarke was the named writer, but Newton advised him, and Princess Caroline understood the exchange as substantially representing Newton’s position.

In 1716 Newton gave Princess Caroline a short account of his revisionist ancient chronology on condition of confidentiality. A copy circulated and was translated into French.

On 21 September 1717 Newton reported on the relative value of gold and silver coin. The government reduced the guinea’s official value to **21 shillings**. This helped stabilize the guinea but overvalued gold relative to silver and encouraged silver’s disappearance from circulation.

**Documented fact:** Newton recommended the rate underlying the 21-shilling valuation.

**Disputed interpretation:** The claim that Newton single-handedly and accidentally “invented the British gold standard” is an oversimplification. Britain’s movement toward de facto gold was the cumulative result of market flows, legal tender rules, monetary practice, and later legislation.

The gold used in some guineas came through companies involved in colonial trade and slavery, including the Royal African Company, and such coins sometimes bore an elephant emblem. Newton managed the institution that processed imperial bullion. No evidence found establishes that he personally organized slave voyages or held an office in the Royal African Company.

## 1717–1722: Conduitt, investments, and late administration

Catherine Barton married John Conduitt in 1717. Conduitt became increasingly important in Newton’s household and Mint work.

Newton held a diversified investment portfolio including government securities and shares associated with the Bank of England, East India Company, and South Sea Company. He also owned land and derived substantial income from the Mint.

### South Sea Bubble

The familiar story says Newton sold South Sea shares profitably, reentered near the peak, lost **£20,000**, and remarked that he could calculate celestial motions but not the madness of people.

- **Documented fact:** Newton owned and traded South Sea securities and made financially damaging decisions during the 1720 bubble.
- **Strong reconstruction:** Surviving portfolio evidence indicates sales followed by renewed exposure near high prices.
- **Uncertain amount:** The often-repeated £20,000 loss rests mainly on later family testimony and cannot be established precisely. Economic historians describe the evidence as inconclusive.
- **Probably apocryphal wording:** The celebrated “madness of people” quotation is not securely documented in a contemporary Newton letter.
- **Documented outcome:** He was not ruined. His estate at death remained exceptionally large.

The South Sea Company held a state-backed monopoly involving trade with Spanish America, including the asiento slave trade. Ownership of its securities linked Newton financially to a company engaged in enslavement, although share ownership does not by itself establish operational involvement or a recorded position on slavery.

## 1722–1726: Illness, final editions, and declining activity

From approximately 1722 Newton suffered recurring urinary or bladder-stone symptoms. He increasingly delegated Mint work to Conduitt and Royal Society tasks to trusted associates.

The unauthorized French **Abrégé de la chronologie de M. le chevalier Newton** appeared in 1725 with critical commentary. Newton protested that the underlying manuscript had been circulated against his wishes and published a sharp reply.

The third edition of the *Principia*, edited with **Henry Pemberton**, appeared in 1726. It incorporated corrections, refinements, new astronomical material, and changes in presentation.

For better air Newton moved from Leicester Square to Kensington around 1725.

## 1727: Final illness and death

Newton presided at his last Royal Society meeting on 2 March 1727 NS-equivalent dating, depending on calendar convention. Soon afterward his urinary illness worsened.

He died at Kensington on 20 March 1726/27 OS, aged eighty-four by modern completed-year reckoning.

Contemporary reports attribute death to complications associated with a bladder or urinary stone. Later accounts say he declined the sacrament or last rites. Because his private theology rejected orthodox Trinitarian doctrine, the report is significant, but it does not prove a formal deathbed repudiation of Christianity.

No reliable evidence supports poisoning, murder, suicide, laboratory explosion, or a staged death.

His body lay in state in the Jerusalem Chamber at Westminster Abbey. He was buried in the Abbey on 28 March. Distinguished noblemen served as pallbearers, and many Royal Society Fellows attended.

## Scientific, Mathematical, Technological, and Administrative Projects

## Mathematics

Newton’s mathematical work included:

- generalized binomial series;
- methods of infinite series;
- fluxions and fluents;
- quadrature;
- classification of cubic curves;
- numerical approximation, including what is now associated with the Newton–Raphson method;
- identities related to symmetric polynomials;
- interpolation and finite differences;
- algebraic and geometric methods;
- power-series solutions to equations.

Attribution must be handled carefully. Several named “Newton” methods have precursors or were completed by later mathematicians. His importance rests not on sole creation of every component but on extraordinary synthesis and development.

## Mechanics and gravitation

Newton’s major physical achievements were:

1. Formulation of three general laws of motion.
2. Mathematical treatment of centripetal forces.
3. Universal gravitation with inverse-square dependence.
4. Demonstration that terrestrial falling, lunar motion, planetary motion, cometary paths, and tides belonged to a common dynamical system.
5. Recognition of mutual gravitational interaction and perturbation.
6. Analysis of the oblate Earth and related measurements.
7. Development of absolute space and time as theoretical structures.

The familiar equation \(F=ma\) is a later algebraic condensation, not a formula printed by Newton in that modern form.

Newton did not provide a successful mechanical cause of gravity. He rejected the claim that matter could act at a distance without mediation as an inherent absurdity, yet left the operative cause unresolved.

## Optics

Newton demonstrated that:

- white light is heterogeneous;
- color corresponds to differing refrangibility;
- a dispersed spectrum can be recombined into white;
- chromatic aberration limits ordinary refracting telescopes;
- thin films produce systematic color patterns.

His treatment of diffraction remained incomplete. His corpuscular preference later lost ground to wave optics, but twentieth-century quantum theory did not simply “prove Newton right”: photons are not Newtonian corpuscles, and modern optics incorporates wave and quantum behavior.

## Reflecting telescope

Newton’s telescope was a real technological achievement. It was demonstrated to the Royal Society and helped secure his election. There is no surviving Newton patent for it.

Earlier proposals for reflecting instruments mean that “inventor of the reflecting telescope” should be qualified: he built the first known successful working Newtonian reflector, not the first imaginable mirror-telescope design.

## Speed of sound and other experiments

Newton estimated the speed of sound and treated acoustics mathematically in the *Principia*. His original model required later corrections by Laplace concerning thermodynamic behavior.

He also conducted experiments on:

- cooling and temperature;
- fluid resistance;
- capillarity;
- chemical affinities;
- air, vapors, and combustion;
- the elasticity and rarefaction of gases.

## Royal Mint administration

Newton’s work at the Mint encompassed:

- Great Recoinage administration;
- assaying and fineness standards;
- metallic and copper experiments;
- financial accounts;
- contracts and personnel;
- anti-counterfeiting investigation;
- Scottish Mint coordination after the 1707 Union;
- medals, including work connected with Queen Anne’s coronation;
- the 1717 gold–silver valuation.

He claimed to have brought coinage to unprecedented exactness. Royal Mint evidence supports substantial improvement in precision, though not a wholesale organizational revolution.

## Wealth, Property, Investments, and Financial Relationships

### Inherited and earned wealth

Newton inherited interests in the Woolsthorpe estate. His academic offices supplied status and income, but the Mint transformed his finances.

Reported annual values vary:

- Warden: often placed around **£400–£600** including allowances.
- Master: commonly estimated around **£1,500** annually, fluctuating with coinage activity and contractual income.

These figures were very high relative to ordinary English incomes.

### Property and household

His relevant property relationships included:

- Woolsthorpe Manor and rural family land;
- London leases or residences in Jermyn Street, Chelsea, Martin Street, and Kensington;
- securities and government obligations;
- household support involving Catherine Barton and later John Conduitt.

An account says Newton paid £4,000 for a Wiltshire estate worth substantially less and declined litigation to unwind the bargain. This is an early biographical report, not a fully reconstructed court record.

### South Sea and imperial finance

Newton held shares in institutions embedded in Britain’s developing fiscal-military and imperial economy. The South Sea Company’s slave-trading monopoly is directly relevant to the nature of the asset.

No evidence reviewed establishes:

- that Newton directed South Sea commercial operations;
- that he laundered money;
- that he committed investment fraud;
- that he was prosecuted over the Bubble;
- that he became insolvent.

## Political, Religious, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

## Politics

Newton’s politics were Protestant, anti-Catholic in the specific confessional politics of the period, aligned with the post-1688 settlement, and generally Whig-connected.

He defended Cambridge’s corporate privileges against James II. He accepted office under William III, Anne, and George I, demonstrating institutional adaptability across ministries.

His anti-Catholicism was not merely casual prejudice; it was integrated with his reading of prophecy and church history, in which Roman ecclesiastical power represented apostasy. He also regarded religious persecution itself as antichristian, creating a tension between tolerationist principles and confessional hostility.

## Theology

Newton believed:

- one supreme God, the Father, alone possessed underived divinity;
- Christ was the Messiah and exalted Son but subordinate to the Father;
- the Trinity was a post-apostolic corruption;
- church history had been falsified, especially around Athanasius;
- biblical prophecy encoded a recoverable history of apostasy and restoration;
- Scripture should be investigated philologically and historically;
- God actively governed the cosmos.

His positions could have exposed him to legal and professional penalties. He therefore practiced restricted manuscript circulation and selective disclosure.

The claim that Newton was an atheist is false. The claim that he was an orthodox Anglican is also false if orthodoxy includes acceptance of the Trinity. He outwardly conformed enough to hold office while privately rejecting central Anglican doctrine.

## Prophecy and chronology

Newton analyzed Daniel and Revelation through the historicist method: prophetic “days” could represent years, and apocalyptic periods mapped onto church and political history.

A manuscript calculation places the end of a 1,260-year period **no earlier than 2060** under one starting point. Newton did not predict that the physical world would certainly end in 2060. He explicitly sought to restrain reckless date-setting.

He also reconstructed ancient chronology, radically shortening traditional Egyptian and Greek timelines. His work used astronomical references, dynasties, genealogies, and mythic synchronization. Much of it is rejected by modern archaeology and chronology, but it was a serious component of his attempt to unify sacred and profane history.

## Philosophy of nature

Newton’s public method emphasized:

- mathematical derivation from phenomena;
- cautious generalization;
- rejection of arbitrary mechanical hypotheses as demonstrated causes;
- experimental analysis and synthesis;
- distinction between established relations and speculative explanations.

“Hypotheses non fingo” does not mean “I make no hypotheses.” His private papers and the *Opticks* Queries contain many. It means, in context, that he would not pretend to have derived a physical cause of gravity from phenomena when he had not.

## Alchemy and ancient wisdom

Newton did not recognize a modern border separating chemistry, alchemy, theology, and natural philosophy. He believed ancient sources might preserve fragments of a once-unified wisdom.

There is no documentary basis for turning this into membership in a continuous secret occult order. His manuscript secrecy is adequately explained by alchemical convention, intellectual priority, the illegality or suspicion attaching to transmutation claims, and theological danger.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Isaac Barrow

Teacher, senior mathematician, and patron. Barrow communicated Newton’s work and resigned the Lucasian chair in his favor.

### Edmond Halley

Collaborator, editor, patron, and scientific ally. Halley elicited *De motu*, encouraged expansion into the *Principia*, supervised publication, financed it substantially, and later participated in disputes involving Flamsteed and Leibniz.

### Robert Hooke

Critic, correspondent, rival, and intellectual stimulus. Their disputes concerned optics, planetary dynamics, gravitation, experimental credibility, and credit.

### Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz

Independent co-creator of calculus, correspondent, philosophical opponent, and priority rival. They never established a productive personal reconciliation.

### Christiaan Huygens

Respected correspondent and critic. He engaged Newton on optics, mechanics, gravity, and mathematics.

### John Locke

Friend and confidential correspondent on Scripture, theology, natural philosophy, and alchemy. Locke was one of the limited circle trusted with Newton’s anti-Trinitarian textual criticism.

### Nicolas Fatio de Duillier

Close friend and mathematical ally from about 1689 to 1693. The intensity and collapse of the relationship are documented; its sexual nature is not.

### John Flamsteed

Astronomical collaborator turned bitter adversary. Flamsteed supplied observations; Newton and Halley participated in coercive publication of his unfinished work.

### Charles Montagu, Earl of Halifax

Cambridge acquaintance, Whig associate, political patron, and the minister who placed Newton at the Mint.

### Catherine Barton

Half-niece, housekeeper, social hostess, and important family confidante. Her relationship with Halifax produced contemporary sexual rumor and substantial financial consequences.

### John Conduitt

Nephew by marriage, Mint subordinate and successor, household associate, and collector of biographical testimony. He became custodian of Newton’s papers after death.

### Samuel Clarke

Theological and philosophical disciple; public voice in the Leibniz–Clarke correspondence.

### Roger Cotes

Editor of the 1713 *Principia* and important mathematical collaborator. Cotes died in 1716, deeply affecting Newton.

### William Whiston

Newton’s chosen Lucasian successor, later estranged. Whiston’s public anti-Trinitarianism contrasted with Newton’s secrecy.

### Henry Pemberton

Physician, editor of the third *Principia*, and late associate.

### Humphrey Newton

No close relation despite the surname. Served as Newton’s amanuensis in the 1680s and later left valuable recollections of his intense work habits.

### Richard Bentley

Correspondent on gravitation, cosmology, and natural theology.

### Robert Boyle

Experimental model and alchemical associate through shared networks. Boyle’s papers and recipes passed into exchanges among Newton and Locke.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Adverse Material

## Priority and credit

### Calculus

- **Fact:** Newton developed fluxional methods before Leibniz’s first publication.
- **Fact:** Leibniz published first and created the notation predominantly used today.
- **Finding:** Independent development is the prevailing historical conclusion.
- **Adverse finding:** Newton secretly shaped a Royal Society judgment issued under the appearance of institutional neutrality.

### Hooke and gravitation

- **Fact:** Hooke proposed important ideas about compound orbital motion and inverse-square attraction.
- **Fact:** Newton supplied the mathematical system and universal synthesis.
- **Dispute:** Newton’s acknowledgments were narrower than Hooke believed deserved.
- **Unsupported extreme:** Hooke did not possess a hidden completed *Principia* that Newton merely stole.

### Flamsteed

- **Fact:** Newton and Halley facilitated publication of unfinished observations over Flamsteed’s objections.
- **Adverse finding:** The procedure violated Flamsteed’s control over his scholarly work.
- **Context:** Public funding and Flamsteed’s delays were used to justify access but do not make his consent immaterial.

## Use of institutional power

Newton’s long Royal Society presidency enabled him to control committees, publication venues, elections, and access. His role in the Leibniz dispute is the clearest documented conflict of interest.

The characterization of Newton as a “tyrant” is a biographical judgment, not a legal finding. The underlying actions—anonymous self-defense, controlled inquiry, removal or reduction of rival credit—are documented.

## Counterfeiting prosecutions

Newton’s investigation of Chaloner and others was legal under contemporary authority. It also contributed directly to executions for economic crimes.

There is no evidence that Newton personally tortured suspects. Informers, imprisonment, harsh interrogation environments, and capital prosecution were part of the system he used.

Claims that Newton “personally hanged” Chaloner are rhetorical, not literal. He assembled the prosecution; the court convicted; the state executed.

## Religious concealment

Newton subscribed outwardly to an institution whose Trinitarian doctrine he rejected.

- **Fact:** He concealed heterodox beliefs.
- **No formal finding:** He was not tried or convicted for heresy.
- **Interpretive question:** Whether concealment constitutes deception, prudent self-protection, or both cannot be resolved by documentary description alone.

## Mental-health claims

The 1693 letters document a crisis. They do not establish schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, autism, poisoning-induced psychosis, or any other retrospective diagnosis.

Claims that Newton’s genius arose from mental illness or neurodivergence are unverified.

## Sexuality

Newton never married and left no securely identified sexual relationship.

- A youthful affection for Catherine Storer is reported.
- His emotional intimacy with Fatio is documented.
- Lifelong virginity was asserted by later sources but cannot be independently verified.
- Labels such as heterosexual, homosexual, bisexual, asexual, or celibate may be used as modern hypotheses, not documented identities.

## Catherine Barton and Halifax

The mistress allegation was contemporary and connected to real money and intimacy. It remains unresolved. It should neither be presented as established fact nor erased from the record.

## Finance and the gold standard

The £20,000 South Sea loss is uncertain. The claim that Newton’s mathematical brilliance should have made him immune to speculation is moralizing hindsight.

The claim that his 1717 report alone created the gold standard mistakes a policy contribution for a complete monetary transformation.

## Fraud, corruption, crime, and litigation

No evidence consulted establishes that Newton was convicted of fraud, corruption, money laundering, theft, violent crime, or scientific forgery.

Newton faced accusations by counterfeiters and rivals, institutional complaints, and hostile memoirs. None produced a criminal conviction against him.

He is not known to have held patents, founded a private company, operated an intelligence service, or served as a formal spy. His informer network at the Mint was a law-enforcement apparatus, not evidence of a secret-intelligence affiliation in the modern sense.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## The apple struck Newton’s head

**Story:** An apple fell on Newton’s head and instantaneously revealed gravity.

**Earliest evidence:** Newton late in life told William Stukeley, John Conduitt, Catherine Barton, and others that seeing an apple fall prompted reflection on why bodies fall perpendicular to Earth and whether gravity extends to the Moon.

**Evidence for:** Multiple early accounts trace to Newton himself.

**Evidence against the familiar form:** None of the strongest early accounts says it struck his head; gravity was not completed in that instant.

**Reputational effect:** It became perhaps the most recognizable scientific-discovery legend, presenting theory as a sudden flash rather than decades of mathematics and revision.

## The quart-pot baby

**Story:** Premature Newton was so small that he could fit into a quart pot.

**Source:** Family recollection transmitted through Conduitt’s biographical notes.

**Status:** Reported family tradition, plausible but not independently measured.

**Effect:** Supports a providential survival narrative: the physically negligible infant who came to measure the universe.

## “Standing on the shoulders of giants”

Newton wrote to Hooke in 1676: “If I have seen further it is by standing on ye sholders of Giants.”

**Mythic interpretation:** It was a sarcastic insult about Hooke’s short stature or deformity.

**Evidence:** The phrase belongs to an older tradition associated with Bernard of Chartres. The letter’s immediate context concerns intellectual predecessors. Sarcasm cannot be conclusively ruled out, but the physical-insult interpretation has no direct proof.

**Effect:** The sentence became a universal emblem of cumulative knowledge, often detached from the contentious relationship in which it appeared.

## Newton destroyed Hooke’s portrait

**Story:** After becoming Royal Society president, Newton destroyed the Society’s only portrait of Hooke.

**Evidence:** No documented portrait securely identifiable as Hooke’s is known, and no record shows Newton ordering one destroyed. The Royal Society itself treats the accusation as an enduring speculation without proof.

**Status:** Rumor or myth.

**Effect:** It symbolizes Newton’s reputed vindictiveness and has influenced novels, television, and popular histories.

## Newton discovered gravity

**Story:** Before Newton, nobody knew gravity existed.

**Correction:** Falling bodies, weight, planetary motion, and attraction had long been studied. Newton’s achievement was a quantitatively universal theory unifying terrestrial and celestial motion.

## Newton invented calculus alone

**Correction:** Newton and Leibniz developed distinct calculi independently, drawing on a larger mathematical inheritance. Newton was earlier in private manuscripts; Leibniz was earlier in publication.

## Newton was the first modern scientist

This is a retrospective title, useful only as shorthand. His theology, alchemy, textual scholarship, public administration, and ancient chronology were not marginal eccentricities detachable from a “modern” scientific core.

## Newton was “the last of the magicians”

John Maynard Keynes used this characterization after studying manuscripts hidden from conventional scientific biography. It accurately challenged sanitized accounts but can create a new distortion if “magician” is taken to mean stage magician, sorcerer, or member of a secret order.

## Secret Rosicrucian or Priory of Sion grand master

Newton’s alchemical, prophetic, and Hermetic interests are documented. Membership in a Rosicrucian body is unproved. Claims that he was a grand master of the alleged Priory of Sion derive from modern pseudohistorical lists tied to the twentieth-century Priory hoax.

**Status:** Unsupported conspiracy narrative.

## Newton predicted the world would end in 2060

Newton calculated that a prophetic period would not end before 2060 under one chronology.

He did not state that the planet would be destroyed in that year. His note was partly directed against irresponsible apocalyptic date-setting.

**Status:** Genuine manuscript transformed into a misleading media myth.

## Newton could calculate the heavens but not human madness

The statement attached to the South Sea Bubble is memorable but lacks a securely located contemporary source in Newton’s own hand.

**Status:** Traditional attribution; wording unverified.

## Newton’s dog Diamond burned the manuscript

A story says Newton’s dog Diamond knocked over a candle and destroyed years of work, causing Newton to cry, “O Diamond, Diamond, thou little knowest the mischief thou hast done.”

The story appeared late; evidence that Newton owned such a dog is weak. Similar laboratory-fire traditions occur in heroic biography.

**Status:** Probably apocryphal.

## Newton never laughed

Early descriptions emphasize seriousness and report that he laughed rarely. Absolute claims that he laughed only once or never laughed are anecdotal exaggerations.

## Newton’s only parliamentary speech concerned a window

No adequate parliamentary record confirms the story.

**Status:** Anecdote of uncertain origin.

## Mercury made Newton a genius or madman

Authenticity-qualified hair analysis supports exposure to metals. It cannot assign a single cause to his intellectual powers or the 1693 crisis.

**Status:** Plausible exposure; unproved causal diagnosis.

## Newton was murdered, exploded, or achieved immortality

No supporting evidence exists. Contemporary reports, burial, estate administration, and monument records establish an ordinary death after urinary illness.

**Status:** Modern fringe rumor.

## Works by Isaac Newton

A literally complete item-level bibliography would include millions of manuscript words, thousands of drafts, calculations, letters, Mint papers, notes, and transcriptions. The Newton Project catalogue and Cambridge, Yahuda, Keynes, Babson, Portsmouth, and Mint collections are therefore the authoritative item-level continuation of this bibliography.

### Major works published during Newton’s lifetime

1. **“A Letter of Mr. Isaac Newton … containing his New Theory about Light and Colors”** — *Philosophical Transactions*, 1672. Genuine authorship; first major optical publication.

2. **“An Hypothesis explaining the Properties of Light”** — submitted 1675; published in the *Philosophical Transactions*. Genuine authorship; speculative optical and ether theory.

3. **De motu corporum in gyrum** — 1684 manuscript, circulated rather than issued as a commercial book. Genuine authorship; precursor to the *Principia*.

4. **Philosophiæ naturalis principia mathematica** — London, 1687. Genuine authorship; Edmond Halley managed and financed publication substantially.

5. **Philosophiæ naturalis principia mathematica**, second edition — Cambridge, 1713. Revised by Newton; edited by Roger Cotes; includes the General Scholium.

6. **Opticks: or, A Treatise of the Reflexions, Refractions, Inflexions and Colours of Light** — London, 1704. Genuine authorship.

7. **Enumeratio linearum tertii ordinis** — appended to the 1704 *Opticks*. Genuine mathematical work.

8. **Tractatus de quadratura curvarum** — appended to the 1704 *Opticks*. Genuine authorship.

9. **Arithmetica universalis; sive de compositione et resolutione arithmetica liber** — Cambridge, 1707. Based on Newton’s lectures and manuscripts; edited and published by William Whiston without Newton’s full enthusiasm or direct authorial supervision. Genuine Newtonian material but editorially mediated.

10. **Optice** — Latin version, 1706, translated and augmented under Newton’s supervision; Samuel Clarke produced the Latin translation.

11. **Commercium epistolicum D. Johannis Collins et aliorum de analysi promota** — 1712/1713. Formally a Royal Society documentary report; substantially compiled, controlled, and partly written by Newton. Institutional publication with concealed authorial involvement.

12. **“An Account of the Book entituled Commercium Epistolicum”** — *Philosophical Transactions*, 1715. Written by Newton and published anonymously; effectively a review of his own report.

13. **Opticks**, second English edition — 1717/1718. Revised, with expanded Queries.

14. **“Remarks upon the Observations made upon a Chronological Index of Sir Isaac Newton”** — *Philosophical Transactions*, 1725. Genuine response to the unauthorized French chronology.

15. **Philosophiæ naturalis principia mathematica**, third edition — London, 1726. Revised by Newton; edited with Henry Pemberton.

### Important posthumous publications

16. **The Chronology of Ancient Kingdoms Amended** — 1728. Genuine Newton manuscript, edited and published posthumously by the Conduitt circle.

17. **Opticks**, fourth edition — 1730. Posthumous edition based on Newton’s work.

18. **Observations upon the Prophecies of Daniel, and the Apocalypse of St. John** — 1733. Genuine theological work, posthumously edited; not a fully author-supervised final publication.

19. **The Method of Fluxions and Infinite Series** — English translation by John Colson, 1736, from Newton’s Latin *De methodis serierum et fluxionum*, composed principally in 1671. Genuine work, posthumously published and translated.

20. **A Treatise of the System of the World** — 1728 English; derived from an early prose draft related to Book III of the *Principia*. Genuine Newtonian material, posthumously published.

21. **Lectiones opticae** — 1729. Derived from Newton’s Cambridge optical lectures; posthumous publication.

22. **Four Letters from Sir Isaac Newton to Doctor Bentley** — 1756. Genuine correspondence from 1692–1693, posthumously collected.

23. **Two Letters of Sir Isaac Newton to Mr. Le Clerc … containing a Dissertation upon the Reading of the Greek Text** — 1754. Version of the work now known as **An Historical Account of Two Notable Corruptions of Scripture**; genuine anti-Trinitarian textual criticism, posthumously published without Newton’s lifetime authorization.

24. **An Historical Account of Two Notable Corruptions of Scripture** — fuller English publication, 1785. Genuine authorship.

25. **Opuscula mathematica, philosophica et philologica** — edited by Johann Castillioneus, 1744. Posthumous compilation, not a single Newton-designed work.

26. **Isaaci Newtoni Opera quae exstant omnia** — edited by Samuel Horsley, 1779–1785. Five-volume posthumous collected edition; selective and editorially mediated.

27. **The Mathematical Papers of Isaac Newton** — edited by D. T. Whiteside, 1967–1981. Eight-volume scholarly edition of authentic manuscripts and printed work; editorial collection, not a lifetime Newton publication.

28. **The Correspondence of Isaac Newton** — edited by H. W. Turnbull, J. F. Scott, A. Rupert Hall, and Laura Tilling, 1959–1977. Seven-volume scholarly edition; includes incoming as well as outgoing letters.

### Major manuscript works and bodies of papers

29. **Waste Book**, Cambridge University Library MS Add. 4004 — mathematical work of the 1660s; genuine autograph manuscript.

30. **Quaestiones quaedam philosophicae** — early philosophical notebook; genuine autograph notes.

31. **De analysi per aequationes numero terminorum infinitas** — 1669; genuine mathematical tract circulated in manuscript.

32. **De methodis serierum et fluxionum** — principally 1671; genuine calculus treatise, unpublished in Newton’s lifetime.

33. **Geometria curvilinea** and related mathematical manuscripts — genuine but variously unfinished.

34. **De gravitatione et aequipondio fluidorum** — undated, usually placed in the Cambridge period; genuine philosophical and mathematical manuscript, unpublished.

35. **“Of Natures Obvious Laws & Processes in Vegetation”** — c. 1670s; genuine manuscript associated with chymical theory.

36. **Index Chemicus** — extensive alchemical index; genuine working compilation.

37. **Praxis**, **Clavis**, **The Key**, laboratory notebooks, recipe books, transcriptions of Philalethes, and many untitled alchemical papers — mixed original composition and copying. Text copied by Newton is not necessarily authored by him; each item requires manuscript-level attribution.

38. **Theologiae gentilis origines philosophicae** — unfinished work on the philosophical origins of pagan theology; genuine manuscript corpus.

39. **Paradoxical Questions concerning the morals and actions of Athanasius** — extensive anti-Athanasian historical prosecution; genuine manuscript, unpublished in his lifetime.

40. **Irenicum** and related ecclesiological writings — genuine theological manuscripts.

41. **Rules for interpreting the words and language in Scripture** — genuine prophetic-hermeneutic manuscript.

42. **Treatise on Revelation**, drafts on Daniel, church history, apostasy, idolatry, and prophetic chronology — genuine but often unfinished and multiply redrafted.

43. **Solomon’s Temple studies** — manuscripts reconstructing the Temple’s dimensions, rites, and historical meaning.

44. **Short Scheme of the True Religion** and related creedal drafts — genuine private theological texts.

45. **Mint papers, 1696–1727** — hundreds of reports, memoranda, examinations, accounts, letters, assays, coinage calculations, and prosecution papers. Many are autograph; others were produced by clerks under Newton’s authority.

46. **Parliamentary and university papers** — material connected with the 1687 Cambridge case and his parliamentary service.

47. **Personal correspondence** — letters to or involving Oldenburg, Hooke, Collins, Barrow, Halley, Huygens, Leibniz, Locke, Flamsteed, Fatio, Bentley, Cotes, Pemberton, Pepys, Montagu, Conduitt, and many others.

48. **List of sins, 1662** — genuine private religious memorandum written partly in shorthand, later deciphered.

### Works falsely attributed, mischaracterized, or requiring qualification

- Newton did **not** write a book predicting that the physical world would certainly end in 2060.
- The *Commercium Epistolicum* was published as a Royal Society report, but Newton’s concealed authorship and control were extensive.
- *Arithmetica Universalis* contains genuine Newton material but was not published through an ordinary author-controlled process.
- Alchemical transcriptions in Newton’s hand are not automatically Newton’s original compositions.
- The *Leibniz–Clarke Correspondence* was formally written by Samuel Clarke; Newton’s advice and influence were substantial, but simple co-authorship is not established.
- Aphorisms widely placed on inspirational posters—especially versions of the apple and “madness of men” statements—often lack secure textual sources.

## Death, Burial, Estate, and Papers

Newton died without a will. Conduitt posted a bond concerning the estate and took possession of papers. Appraisers considered only a few manuscripts fit for publication, reflecting both eighteenth-century standards and the danger posed by Newton’s theology.

The papers descended through Catherine Conduitt’s daughter into the Portsmouth family. In 1872 the scientific papers were offered to Cambridge. Cataloguers returned much theological, chronological, alchemical, and administrative material to the family as supposedly of lesser value.

In July 1936 Sotheby’s sold the remaining Portsmouth papers. The sale dispersed the archive:

- **John Maynard Keynes** acquired much of the alchemical material and later left it to King’s College, Cambridge.
- **Abraham Shalom Yahuda** acquired major theological manuscripts, eventually deposited in Jerusalem.
- **Grace K. Babson** assembled another major collection, now associated with the Huntington Library.
- Dealers and private buyers dispersed additional leaves and groups; some remain lost or privately held.

The 1936 sale transformed Newton studies by exposing the enormous scale of his alchemy and heterodox theology.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Eighteenth century

Newton received an elite state-like funeral and monumental burial. Alexander Pope’s couplet—

> Nature and Nature’s laws lay hid in night:  
> God said, Let Newton be! and all was light.

—helped create the image of a divinely sanctioned intellectual monarch.

Voltaire and Émilie du Châtelet promoted Newtonian physics in France. Du Châtelet’s French translation and commentary on the *Principia* became foundational.

Newtonianism extended beyond mechanics into theology, political language, aesthetics, and models of rational order. It was never a single doctrine.

### Nineteenth century

Victorian biographies emphasized piety, modesty, and rational scientific genius while minimizing anti-Trinitarianism and alchemy. David Brewster initially resisted evidence incompatible with the orthodox heroic image but eventually acknowledged more of Newton’s non-scientific work.

Classical mechanics became a central educational framework. Newton’s monument and portraits helped make him a symbol of British national genius.

### Twentieth century

Relativity and quantum theory limited the universality of Newtonian mechanics without making it obsolete. Newton’s equations remain extraordinarily accurate at ordinary speeds, weak gravitational fields, and macroscopic scales.

The recovery and study of private manuscripts displaced the purified rationalist image:

- Keynes described him as “the last of the magicians.”
- Historians integrated alchemy, theology, chronology, administration, and psychology into his biography.
- Westfall’s *Never at Rest* presented an ambitious, driven, defensive, and intellectually omnivorous Newton.
- Scholars increasingly treated “science” and “occultism” as categories that did not map neatly onto Newton’s seventeenth-century practice.

### Twenty-first century

Digitization has made large parts of the archive globally accessible. Current scholarship emphasizes:

- the textual evolution of his discoveries;
- experimental reconstruction of alchemy;
- anti-Trinitarian theology;
- patronage and institutional power;
- the Mint and coercive state;
- finance, empire, and the South Sea Company;
- collaborative and rivalrous networks behind the solitary-genius image.

Newton remains foundational to mechanics, celestial dynamics, optics, applied mathematics, engineering, and scientific culture. His name survives in units, laws, methods, instruments, educational institutions, spacecraft, craters, and prizes.

The SI unit of force, the **newton (N)**, was named for him: one newton is the force required to accelerate one kilogram at one metre per second squared.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the reviewed sources are included. Mere chronological overlap is excluded.

### Edmond Halley — documented fact

Halley visited Newton at Cambridge in 1684, asked the orbital question that elicited *De motu*, pressed him to expand it, carried the manuscript through the Royal Society, supervised printing, corrected proofs, and substantially financed the 1687 *Principia*. They later cooperated in Royal Society affairs and the Flamsteed publication.

### Robert Hooke — documented fact

Hooke criticized Newton’s optical paper beginning in 1672, corresponded with him on orbital motion in 1679–1680, asserted priority for the inverse-square conception during preparation of the *Principia*, and remained his most important English scientific rival. The story that Newton destroyed Hooke’s portrait is a rumor without documentary proof.

### Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz — documented fact

Newton and Leibniz corresponded indirectly and directly through mathematical networks from the 1670s. Each developed calculus independently. Their priority conflict culminated in the Newton-controlled *Commercium Epistolicum* and continued through the Leibniz–Clarke correspondence.

### Christiaan Huygens — documented fact

Huygens examined Newton’s optics, corresponded with him, met him within European scholarly networks, and disputed aspects of the corpuscular theory of light and action at a distance while respecting his mathematics.

### John Locke — documented fact

Locke and Newton were friends and confidential correspondents. They exchanged ideas about natural philosophy, Robert Boyle’s alchemy, scriptural criticism, toleration, and anti-Trinitarian theology. Newton sent Locke his work on two disputed New Testament passages.

### René Descartes — documented intellectual influence and opposition

Newton studied Descartes intensively in his Cambridge notebooks. Cartesian geometry and natural philosophy contributed to his formation, while Newton later rejected Cartesian vortex cosmology and criticized Descartes’s accounts of motion, matter, and space.

### Galileo Galilei — documented intellectual influence

Newton studied Galileo’s writings and incorporated the mathematical study of falling bodies, inertia, and projectile motion into a more general mechanics. The connection is textual and intellectual; the two could not have met.

### Johannes Kepler — documented intellectual influence

Newton mathematically derived Kepler’s empirical laws from central-force dynamics and universal gravitation. This is an explicit intellectual connection, not a personal one.

### Nicolaus Copernicus — documented intellectual influence

Newton’s astronomical system presupposed and transformed the heliocentric tradition associated with Copernicus. There was no personal contact.

### Aristotle — documented intellectual engagement

Aristotelian logic and natural philosophy formed part of Newton’s Cambridge curriculum. His *Quaestiones* records both study and growing resistance to scholastic Aristotelian physics.

### Plato — documented intellectual and symbolic engagement

Newton wrote the adapted notebook motto placing truth above both Plato and Aristotle. He also used Platonic and Neoplatonic materials in studies of ancient philosophy, but no simple label such as “Platonist” captures his system.

### Archimedes of Syracuse — documented intellectual influence

Newton inherited and extended the mathematical-mechanical tradition of Archimedes, including geometrical demonstration and hydrostatics. The connection is textual and disciplinary.

### Francis Bacon — documented contextual influence; direct methodological dependence disputed

Bacon supplied an influential English model of experimental and inductive reform. Newton knew Baconian traditions, but claims that he simply followed “the Baconian method” are rejected by historians because his practice combined mathematics, experiment, deduction, analogy, and speculative inquiry.

### John Dee — reported documentary/intellectual connection

Newton owned or consulted literature belonging to the English mathematical and alchemical tradition in which Dee was prominent. This is a connection through texts and antiquarian-alchemical culture, not evidence of personal contact or membership in Dee’s supposed secret networks.

### Paracelsus — documented textual tradition

Paracelsian and post-Paracelsian concepts entered the alchemical literature Newton copied and tested. Direct adherence to a single Paracelsian school is not established.

### Albert Einstein — documented posthumous influence

Einstein treated Newtonian mechanics and gravitation as the indispensable predecessor to relativity. Relativity revised Newton’s absolute space, absolute time, and gravitational action while recovering Newtonian predictions as a limiting approximation. There was necessarily no personal contact.

### Immanuel Kant — documented posthumous influence

Kant’s philosophy of space, time, causality, and natural science was deeply shaped by the Newtonian physical achievement and debates between Newtonian and Leibnizian frameworks. This is posthumous intellectual influence.

### Pierre-Simon Laplace — documented posthumous development and correction

Laplace extended celestial mechanics on Newtonian foundations and corrected Newton’s treatment of the speed of sound by incorporating thermal effects.

### Leonhard Euler, Joseph-Louis Lagrange, Carl Friedrich Gauss, and Siméon Denis Poisson — documented posthumous mathematical inheritance

Each developed analytical mechanics, potential theory, celestial dynamics, or mathematical physics from problems structured by Newton’s mechanics and gravitation. These are intellectual-successor connections, not personal relationships.

### Michael Faraday, James Clerk Maxwell, Albert Einstein, Niels Bohr, Werner Heisenberg, Erwin Schrödinger, Paul Dirac, and Richard Feynman — documented posthumous relation to Newtonian physics

Their work revised or extended domains that Newtonian mechanics and optics had organized. Listing them does not imply direct influence personally acknowledged in every case; the documented connection is disciplinary succession. No meetings were possible.

### Other atlas names

No adequately documented personal or direct intellectual crossing was established in the consulted sources for the remainder of the roster. Shared nationality, residence in Cambridge or London at another date, general fame, or participation in broad traditions was not treated as a connection.
