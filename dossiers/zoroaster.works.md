## Works by Zoroaster

### Ahunavaitī Gāthā: metrical songs

- **Undated; Old Avestan:** *Yasna 28*. A metrical liturgical song and the first of seven songs conventionally collected as the *Ahunavaitī Gāthā*. Its attribution to Zoroaster is traditional and accepted by many specialists, although the historical Zoroaster’s date—and even the unity of Gathic authorship—remains disputed. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Gathas i”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/gathas-i-texts/))
- **Undated; Old Avestan:** *Yasna 29* (“The Complaint of the Cow”). A dramatic, dialogic ritual poem rather than a narrative work; it belongs to the *Ahunavaitī Gāthā* and is conventionally attributed to Zoroaster. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Gathas i”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/gathas-i-texts/))
- **Undated; Old Avestan:** *Yasna 30*. A metrical religious song within the *Ahunavaitī Gāthā*, concerned with fundamental choices and opposing ways of thought and action. Its survival inside the later *Yasna* makes it simultaneously a poem and a component of enacted liturgy. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Gathas i”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/gathas-i-texts/))
- **Undated; Old Avestan:** *Yasna 31*. A metrical song within the *Ahunavaitī Gāthā*, conventionally attributed to Zoroaster but transmitted only as part of the composite *Yasna* liturgy. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Gathas i”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/gathas-i-texts/); [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Yasna”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/yasna/))
- **Undated; Old Avestan:** *Yasna 32*. A metrical polemical and devotional song within the *Ahunavaitī Gāthā*. It is an oral-liturgical obra rather than a “book” independently issued by its maker. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Gathas i”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/gathas-i-texts/))
- **Undated; Old Avestan:** *Yasna 33*. A metrical song or prayer within the *Ahunavaitī Gāthā*, conventionally assigned to Zoroaster. Its present chapter number belongs to the later arrangement of the *Yasna*, not necessarily to the original composition. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Gathas i”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/gathas-i-texts/); [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Yasna”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/yasna/))
- **Undated; Old Avestan:** *Yasna 34*. The seventh metrical song of the *Ahunavaitī Gāthā*. Like the other Gathic songs, it survives through ritual performance and manuscript redaction, so its received form cannot simply be equated with an authorial document. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Gathas i”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/gathas-i-texts/))

### Uštavaitī Gāthā: metrical songs

- **Undated; Old Avestan:** *Yasna 43*. A metrical visionary and devotional song, the first of four songs grouped as the *Uštavaitī Gāthā*. Scholars have used its first-person presentation to reconstruct Zoroaster’s religious experience, but such biographical readings remain interpretive. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Gathas i”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/gathas-i-texts/))
- **Undated; Old Avestan:** *Yasna 44*. A metrical song structured largely through questions addressed to Ahura Mazdā. It acts as ritual inquiry as much as theological description. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Gathas i”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/gathas-i-texts/))
- **Undated; Old Avestan:** *Yasna 45*. A metrical proclamatory song within the *Uštavaitī Gāthā*; in function it approaches a manifesto because its utterance calls hearers to a religious choice. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Gathas i”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/gathas-i-texts/))
- **Undated; Old Avestan:** *Yasna 46*. A metrical song incorporating personal, social, and patronage concerns. Although conventionally Zoroaster’s, its first-person voice is evidence within a highly formal ritual poem, not an independently verifiable signature. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Gathas i”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/gathas-i-texts/))

### Spəṇtāmainyu Gāthā: metrical songs

- **Undated; Old Avestan:** *Yasna 47*. A tightly patterned metrical song and the first of four songs grouped as the *Spəṇtāmainyu Gāthā*. Its formal interweaving of doctrinal terms makes it simultaneously poetic construction and liturgical utterance. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Gathas i”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/gathas-i-texts/))
- **Undated; Old Avestan:** *Yasna 48*. A metrical religious song within the *Spəṇtāmainyu Gāthā*, conventionally attributed to Zoroaster and preserved as part of the recited *Yasna*. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Gathas i”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/gathas-i-texts/))
- **Undated; Old Avestan:** *Yasna 49*. A metrical song within the *Spəṇtāmainyu Gāthā*, combining devotional address with conflict involving named or identifiable opponents. Its precise historical setting cannot be securely recovered. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Gathas i”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/gathas-i-texts/))
- **Undated; Old Avestan:** *Yasna 50*. A metrical song of prayer and praise, concluding the *Spəṇtāmainyu Gāthā*. It is best classified as an oral-liturgical poem rather than a book or doctrinal treatise. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Gathas i”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/gathas-i-texts/))

### Vohuxšaθrā Gāthā: metrical song

- **Undated; Old Avestan:** *Yasna 51*. A single long metrical song constituting the entire *Vohuxšaθrā Gāthā*. It belongs to the sixteen-song body, *Yasna* 28–51, that offers the strongest case for common authorship, conventionally identified as Zoroaster’s. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Gathas i”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/gathas-i-texts/))

### Vahištōišti Gāthā: disputed metrical song

- **Undated; Old Avestan:** *Yasna 53*. A nine-stanza song constituting the *Vahištōišti Gāthā*, traditionally associated with the marriage of Zoroaster’s daughter Pouručistā. Many translators have treated the Gathas as Zoroaster’s work while judging this final song to be a composition from his immediate circle rather than by him; it therefore resists secure inclusion among his own obras. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Gathas i”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/gathas-i-texts/); [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Gathas ii”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/gathas-ii-translations/))

### Disputed Old Avestan liturgy

- **Undated; Old Avestan:** *Yasna Haptaŋhāiti* (“Worship in Seven Chapters,” *Yasna* 35–41). A seven-part prose liturgy placed between the first and second Gathas; it is a communal ritual manifestation more readily than an individually signed work. Johanna Narten and some other scholars have argued that nothing requires an author different from the Gathas’ author, while attribution to Zoroaster has not achieved general agreement. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Zoroaster iii”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/zoroaster-iii-zoroaster-in-the-avesta/); [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Yasna”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/yasna/))

### Disputed prayer-formulas

- **Undated; Old Avestan:** *Ahuna Vairiia* (also *Ahunwar* or *Yathā ahū vairiiō*, *Yasna* 27.13). A short performative prayer-formula, not a book: its recitation is understood to accomplish protection and ritual efficacy. Some modern scholarship has included it among Zoroaster’s compositions, but Zoroastrian tradition presents it as a primordial utterance of Ahura Mazdā and calls Zoroaster its first human reciter; maker and transmitter therefore cannot be securely identified. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Ahunwar”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/ahunwar-middle-persian-form-of-avestan-ahuna-vairya-name-of-the-most-sacred-of-the-gathic-prayers-y/); [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Bag Nask”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/bag-nask/))
- **Undated; linguistically disputed:** *Yeŋ́hē hātąm* (*Yasna* 27.15). A short liturgical prayer traditionally said to have been spoken by Zoroaster, unlike two companion prayers assigned to Ahura Mazdā. Modern analysis has challenged Zoroaster’s authorship and has classified its language as archaizing Younger Avestan, so it belongs here only as a disputed attribution. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Yeŋ́hē hātąm”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/yenhe-hatam-prayer/); [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Bag Nask”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/bag-nask/))

### Corpus and limits

- **Undated; assembled later:** *The Gathas*. The collective obra comprises five metrical groupings containing seventeen songs (*Yasna* 28–34, 43–46, 47–50, 51, and 53), embedded in the larger, composite *Yasna*. “The Gathas” names a transmitted poetic-liturgical corpus rather than a book Zoroaster designed in its surviving codex form; tradition assigns all five groupings to him, while scholarship disputes particular pieces, especially *Yasna* 53, and occasionally the entire attribution. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Gathas”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/gathas/); [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Yasna”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/yasna/))
- **No securely recoverable date:** *Zoroaster’s religious-poetic project*. The Gathas manifest a project of ritual speech, ethical exhortation, praise, controversy, and community formation; it is not a separately preserved text beyond the songs themselves. The dates c. 628–551 BCE are one traditional modern reconstruction, not a secure dating of the compositions: specialist accounts also place the historical Zoroaster near the end of the second millennium BCE, while his date, homeland, historicity, and authorship remain debated. ([Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Zoroaster ii”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/zoroaster-ii-general-survey/); [Encyclopaedia Iranica, “Zoroaster iii”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/zoroaster-iii-zoroaster-in-the-avesta/))
