# Antonin Artaud: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

This dossier reconstructs Antonin Artaud’s life from institutional chronologies, catalogues, archival descriptions, contemporary records, correspondence, memoir-based sources, film records, and scholarship. Dates and spellings vary across editions, especially for early films, psychiatric transfers, the Mexican and Irish journeys, and posthumously assembled texts.

Evidentiary labels used:

- **Documented fact:** supported by institutional records, publications, correspondence, archival holdings, or contemporary documentation.
- **Reported fact:** stated in credible biographies or testimony but not independently established here from the underlying record.
- **Disputed claim:** competing documentary or scholarly interpretations exist.
- **Self-reported belief:** asserted by Artaud, often in letters or writings, without independent confirmation.
- **Rumor or myth:** a story important to his reputation but inadequately supported as literal history.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full baptismal/birth name:** Antoine Marie Joseph Paul Artaud.
- **Usual name:** Antonin Artaud. “Antonin” is a diminutive of Antoine and became his permanent literary and professional name.
- **Other signatures and self-designations:**
  - **Louis des Attides:** early pseudonym used for adolescent writing, approximately 1910.
  - **Eno Dailor:** signature used in the one-man review *Bilboquet* in 1923.
  - **Le Révélé:** anonymous or quasi-anonymous prophetic persona attached to *Les Nouvelles Révélations de l’Être* in 1937.
  - **Artaud le Mômo:** late self-designation; “mômo” is Marseille slang suggesting a fool, simpleton, or child.
  - **Antonin Nalpas:** identity invoked during his later psychiatric period, drawing on his mother’s family name.
  - Late notebooks also contain unstable invented genealogies, doubles, daughters, persecutors, and alternative identities. These belong to his personal cosmology, not civil genealogy.
- **Nationality:** French.
- **Occupations:** poet, essayist, dramatist, theatrical theorist, actor, stage director, designer, cinema scenarist and critic, editor, lecturer, radio performer, translator-adaptor, and graphic artist.
- **Birth:** 4 September 1896, Marseille, France.
- **Death:** morning of 4 March 1948, Maison de santé du docteur Delmas, Ivry-sur-Seine, France.
- **Age at death:** 51.
- **Burial:** initially at Ivry; his remains were later transferred to the family vault at Marseille. Published accounts differ on the precise dates and sequence of reinterment.
- **Known legal marital status:** never married.
- **Children:** no documented biological children.
- **Principal long relationship:** Génica Athanasiou, Romanian-born actress, from 1921 into the later 1920s, with intermittent contact thereafter.
- **Engagement:** Cécile Schramme, 1937; marriage did not occur.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and commercial background

**Documented fact:** Artaud’s father was Antoine-Roi Artaud, a Marseille shipowner or operator of a small navigation concern. His mother was Euphrasie Nalpas, whose family came from Smyrna, now İzmir in Turkey. The Bibliothèque nationale de France identifies the father’s small shipping company and the mother’s Smyrniot origin in its biographical chronology.

The family occupied a commercially established Marseille milieu rather than poverty. This background gave Artaud a family support system, but he never developed a stable business career or independent fortune. Acting fees, publication income, family money, medical allowances, loans, gifts, and later benefit events supported him irregularly.

### Cousin marriage and eastern Mediterranean ancestry

**Reported fact, strongly supported by genealogical research:** Antoine-Roi Artaud and Euphrasie Nalpas were first cousins. Their mothers, Catherine Chilé and Mariette Chilé, were sisters from Smyrna. Catherine married the Frenchman Marius Artaud and settled in Marseille; Mariette married Louis Nalpas, a ship chandler in Smyrna.

The Nalpas and Chilé lines connected Artaud to a Levantine, Greek-Ottoman, and Mediterranean commercial world. The exact ethnic and confessional descriptions vary among biographies: “Greek,” “Levantine Greek,” “Smyrniot,” and “Ottoman Greek” all appear. Artaud later mythologized this maternal ancestry, sometimes repudiating the civil identity “Artaud” in favor of “Nalpas.”

### Siblings

Euphrasie reportedly experienced nine births. Four children were stillborn and two died during childhood; only Antonin, Marie-Ange, and Fernand survived into adulthood. Exact lists and dates differ across secondary accounts.

The repeated deaths formed part of the family environment in which Artaud grew up. Later biographers frequently treat them as psychologically formative, but causal claims about their effect on his illness remain interpretation rather than documentary fact.

### Extended family and cinema

Louis Nalpas, a cousin on his mother’s side, became an important French film producer and artistic director associated with the Société des Cinéromans. **Documented fact:** this relationship helped Artaud obtain early film work, including appearances in productions connected to the company.

### Marriage and descendants

Artaud had no documented spouse or children. His late writings name or construct female figures as daughters or persecuted family members. These are part of the notebooks’ visionary genealogy; they are not supported by civil records.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1896–1914

Artaud grew up in Marseille in a Catholic household. Family prosperity was sufficient for private religious schooling and repeated medical treatment.

### Childhood illness

At approximately five years old he was diagnosed with meningitis.

- **Traditional biographical version:** the illness caused lasting nervous and physical effects.
- **Disputed medical interpretation:** biographer David A. Shafer and others have questioned whether true meningitis is likely, given the diagnostic practices of the period, the absence of effective treatment, and Artaud’s survival.
- **Reported fact:** his father used or obtained a static-electricity apparatus, a contemporary therapeutic device, to treat him.

The illness became the first episode in a lifelong history of pain, depression, nervous crises, medicalization, and attempts to locate bodily causes for mental suffering.

### Education

From approximately 1907 to 1914 Artaud attended the Collège du Sacré-Cœur in Marseille. His education was Catholic and classical. He read Baudelaire, Edgar Allan Poe, Rimbaud, and probably Mallarmé; the Centre Pompidou chronology specifically associates his first writing with Poe, Baudelaire, and Rimbaud.

Around 1910 he wrote under the name **Louis des Attides** and participated in or created a private literary review with school friends. Surviving evidence for the contents and circulation of this adolescent publication is limited.

### Formative themes

His early formation combined:

- Catholic ritual and doctrine;
- Mediterranean family identity;
- poetry conceived as a bodily and spiritual operation;
- recurrent illness;
- awareness of death within the family;
- a sense that ordinary language failed to represent his internal condition.

These themes recur throughout the Rivière correspondence, the Theatre of Cruelty, the prophetic writings of 1937, the asylum letters, and the late notebooks.

## Medical Crisis, War, and Sanatoria, 1914–1920

### 1914–1915: breakdown and first institutions

In 1914 Artaud experienced a severe depressive or nervous crisis. In 1915 he consulted Joseph Grasset, a Montpellier specialist in neurological and psychiatric disorders, and entered La Rouguière, a rest home near Marseille.

Terminology such as “neurasthenia,” “nervous depression,” “hereditary syphilis,” and later “schizophrenia” appeared at different stages of his medical history. These diagnoses reflect changing medical systems and should not be retrospectively treated as interchangeable.

### Military service

**Documented fact:** he was mobilized on 9 August 1916 and incorporated at Digne. He was discharged on medical grounds in 1917. Accounts sometimes repeat colorful stories about sleepwalking or deliberate simulation; the firmly established point is medical discharge, not the precise symptom that secured it.

### 1917–1920: therapeutic migrations

- **1917:** treatment at Divonne-les-Bains, an establishment specializing in neuropsychiatric disorders.
- **Summer 1918:** Bagnères-de-Bigorre.
- **Autumn 1918–early 1920:** the Chanet clinic near Neuchâtel, Switzerland, directed by Dr. Dardel.

At Chanet he painted and drew landscapes and portraits. The Centre Pompidou identifies these as his earliest sustained visual works and describes their affinities with Symbolism, Fauvism, and Edvard Munch.

### Opiates

Around 1919 doctors prescribed laudanum to combat what Artaud described as wandering pains and anxiety. This began a lifelong dependence on opiates and other drugs. Morphine, laudanum, heroin, opium preparations, chloral hydrate, and attempted withdrawal cures all appear in his history.

The distinction between treatment and addiction is essential: exposure began within medical care, but his later procurement and consumption exceeded ordinary prescription. Drug dependence affected his finances, behavior, travel, working rhythms, and encounters with police and doctors.

## Paris, Dr. Toulouse, and Entry into the Arts, 1920–1923

### Dr. Édouard Toulouse

In 1920 Artaud moved to the Paris area under the care of psychiatrist Édouard Toulouse at Villejuif. Toulouse believed in occupational and cultural activity as treatment and encouraged his writing.

Artaud became co-secretary or editorial collaborator on Toulouse’s review *Demain*. This was his first sustained entry into professional literary and intellectual networks.

### Theatre

He performed as an extra for Aurélien Lugné-Poë at the Théâtre de l’Œuvre. In 1921 Charles Dullin engaged him in the company that became the Théâtre de l’Atelier.

With Dullin he received practical formation in:

- gesture and mime;
- physical training;
- masks and stylized acting;
- non-naturalistic mise-en-scène;
- costume and set design;
- ensemble theatrical work.

Dullin was employer, teacher, and formative influence, although their relations became strained by Artaud’s temperament and absences.

Artaud subsequently worked with Georges and Ludmilla Pitoëff’s company. His stage credits included roles in works by Calderón, Molière, Pirandello, Claudel, and others; surviving cast lists and memoirs are not wholly consistent.

### Génica Athanasiou

At Dullin’s company in 1921 he met Génica Athanasiou. Their correspondence documents an intense, unstable romantic and sexual relationship marked by declarations of dependence, jealousy, illness, financial insecurity, and separation.

She was not merely a muse: she was a working actress and professional collaborator. Artaud hoped she would play in projects including *La Coquille et le Clergyman*. The relationship weakened during the later 1920s but correspondence continued much longer.

### André Masson and rue Blomet

In 1922 Artaud met painter André Masson and entered the rue Blomet artistic circle. Masson later designed the cover of *Le Pèse-Nerfs*. This milieu connected Artaud to emerging Surrealist artists and writers.

### First publications and pseudonym

In 1923 he issued:

- *Tric Trac du ciel*, poems, Galerie Simon;
- *Bilboquet*, a small review written entirely or nearly entirely by him under the signature **Eno Dailor**.

The initial printing of *Tric Trac du ciel* was small. Artaud later rejected or revised parts of his earliest poetry.

## Jacques Rivière and the Problem of Authorship, 1923–1924

Artaud submitted poems to Jacques Rivière, editor of *La Nouvelle Revue française*. Rivière declined the poems but became interested in Artaud’s letters explaining why his thought disintegrated before it could become acceptable literature.

Their exchange was published in the NRF on 1 September 1924 as *Une correspondance*, later retitled *Correspondance avec Jacques Rivière*.

The episode established several durable features of Artaud’s authorship:

- inability to “possess” thought was made the subject of writing;
- rejection became documentary evidence of the writer’s condition;
- correspondence crossed into literary composition;
- illness was neither external biography nor metaphor alone, but a condition of production.

Rivière was editor, interlocutor, and gatekeeper rather than a conventional teacher.

## Surrealism, 1924–1927

### Entry and organizational role

In 1924 Artaud met André Breton and joined the Surrealist movement. He directed or took a leading role in the Bureau de recherches surréalistes and contributed to *La Révolution surréaliste*.

The third issue, dated 15 April 1925 and organized largely by Artaud, carried the theme **“1925: fin de l’ère chrétienne.”** Its addresses and manifestos attacked institutions including the pope, the Dalai Lama, rectors, doctors, and colonial power.

### Major Surrealist-period works

- *L’Ombilic des limbes* (1925)
- *Le Pèse-Nerfs* (1925)
- manifestos, addresses, letters, and prose poems in Surrealist publications

### Political divergence

Artaud shared Surrealism’s revolt against bourgeois culture, rationalism, organized religion, and colonial civilization. He resisted subordinating inward and metaphysical revolt to Communist Party discipline.

His exclusion is variously dated to late 1926 or publicly formalized in 1927.

- **Documented fact:** *Au grand jour* publicly announced the exclusion of Artaud and Philippe Soupault.
- **Documented fact:** Artaud replied with *À la grande nuit ou le Bluff surréaliste*.
- **Disputed emphasis:** some accounts make refusal of Communist alignment decisive; others emphasize theatrical commercial activity, personal conflict, rival ideas of revolution, and Breton’s consolidation of authority.

Artaud never developed a stable party-political program. His texts could be fiercely anti-colonial and anti-bourgeois while rejecting Marxist organization and materialist explanation.

## Theatre Alfred Jarry, 1926–1930

Artaud, Roger Vitrac, and Robert Aron founded the Théâtre Alfred Jarry in 1926. Yvonne and René Allendy provided important backing.

The company invoked Alfred Jarry as a precursor of anti-naturalist, destabilizing theatre. It sought to treat performance as an event capable of exposing social falsity and attacking the audience’s habits.

### Productions

- **1927, first program:** Vitrac’s *Les Mystères de l’amour*; Artaud’s *Ventre brûlé ou la Mère folle*; Aron’s *Gigogne*.
- **1928, second program:** screening of Vsevolod Pudovkin’s *La Mère* and Act III of Paul Claudel’s *Partage de midi*, staged without Claudel’s authorization.
- **1928, third program:** August Strindberg’s *Le Songe*; Surrealists organized a disruption.
- **1929, fourth and last program:** Vitrac’s *Victor ou les Enfants au pouvoir*.

The theatre suffered from limited funds, poor attendance, scandal, unstable rehearsal conditions, and conflict with both cultural authorities and former Surrealist allies. Its historical importance far exceeded its commercial success.

In 1930 Artaud and Vitrac published *Le Théâtre Alfred Jarry et l’hostilité publique*, illustrated with Éli Lotar’s staged photomontages.

### *La Coquille et le Clergyman*

Artaud wrote the scenario in 1927; Germaine Dulac directed the film. It premiered at the Studio des Ursulines on 9 February 1928.

- **Documented fact:** the finished film was Dulac’s direction from Artaud’s scenario.
- **Documented conflict:** Artaud believed Dulac had displaced or psychologized his conception and objected to his exclusion from production and editing.
- **Reported fact:** hostile spectators shouted insults at Dulac during the premiere; Artaud’s exact responsibility for initiating the disturbance is disputed.
- **Later myth:** Artaud is sometimes credited as the film’s director. This is false; he was the scenarist.
- The film is often called the first Surrealist film, though its relation to institutional Surrealism and Artaud’s repudiation of Dulac’s interpretation make that classification contested.

Artaud wrote numerous additional scenarios and film essays, but the industrial cinema rarely realized his projects.

## Theatre of Cruelty and Intellectual Development, 1931–1935

### Balinese theatre

At the 1931 Paris Colonial Exposition Artaud saw a Balinese performance. He interpreted its controlled gesture, percussion, costume, spatial pattern, and nonverbal signs as evidence of a theatre not subordinated to literary dialogue.

His account projected his own theatrical desires onto a performance presented within a colonial exposition. Later scholarship therefore treats it both as a major theatrical encounter and as an instance of primitivist or Orientalist appropriation.

### Lectures and manifestos

- 1 October 1931: “Le Théâtre balinais à l’Exposition coloniale,” NRF.
- 10 December 1931: “La Mise en scène et la métaphysique,” Sorbonne.
- 1932: first manifesto of the Theatre of Cruelty.
- 1933: second manifesto and organizational efforts.

“Cruelty” did not simply mean stage sadism. Artaud used it for necessity, rigor, cosmic pressure, danger, and the exposure of spectators to forces ordinarily neutralized by conventional drama.

### *Héliogabale*

*Héliogabale ou l’Anarchiste couronné* appeared in 1934. It fused historical narrative, occult symbolism, sexuality, religious conflict, invented anthropology, and political metaphor.

Its Roman and Syrian material cannot be treated as dependable ancient history. The book deliberately merges source material and visionary reconstruction. Some passages employ racial, ethnic, sexual, and essentializing language characteristic of interwar esotericism and primitivism; this is part of the text’s documentary record.

### *Les Cenci*

Artaud adapted material from Shelley and Stendhal into *Les Cenci*, staged in 1935 at the Théâtre des Folies-Wagram. Balthus designed sets and costumes; Roger Désormière composed or arranged the music. Artaud directed and played Count Cenci.

The production used sound, movement, machinery, and spatial assault as an attempted realization of cruelty. It closed after seventeen performances. Financially and institutionally it was a failure; historically it became the principal production mounted under Artaud’s own Theatre of Cruelty program.

## Mexico, 1936

Artaud left Europe on 10 January 1936 and returned to France on 12 November.

### Mexico City

He lectured at the Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México and published or prepared articles in Mexican periodicals. Subjects included:

- Surrealism and revolution;
- theatre and culture;
- the decadence of European rationalism;
- ancient Mexican civilization;
- opposition to colonial destruction;
- the search for a living sacred culture.

He sought support from Mexican cultural officials and intellectuals. His finances were precarious; lectures, journalism, assistance, and personal contacts supported the journey.

### Sierra Tarahumara

Artaud traveled north into the Sierra Tarahumara, probably during August–October 1936, and reported participation in a peyote rite among the Rarámuri/Tarahumara.

- **Documented fact:** he traveled in the region and subsequently wrote extensively about it.
- **Reported fact:** he took peyote in a ritual setting.
- **Unresolved questions:** scholars dispute the exact route, communities visited, duration, names of participants, and whether the ceremony occurred exactly as later narrated.
- **Interpretive caution:** the Tarahumara writings mix travel report, retrospective composition, visionary experience, polemic, and personal mythology. They are not straightforward ethnography.

He represented the Sierra as a landscape of signs and the Tarahumara as custodians of a pre-European metaphysical order. This shaped his later rejection of Western Christianity, anatomy, and social identity, while also reproducing primitivist idealization.

## Prophecy, Engagement, and Ireland, 1937

### Cécile Schramme

Artaud became engaged to Cécile Schramme. The projected marriage was abandoned. Accounts attribute the rupture variously to his drug use, erratic conduct, escalating prophetic ideas, family opposition, and conflict around sexuality and religion.

### *Les Nouvelles Révélations de l’Être*

Published in 1937 under the persona “Le Révélé,” this work presented apocalyptic and astrological claims. Artaud believed a catastrophic transformation was imminent and sent letters in a prophetic register.

### The cane

Artaud possessed a knotted walking stick that had come through René Thomas and was associated in his mind with Saint Patrick, Jesus, Lucifer, or ancient sacred power.

- **Self-reported belief:** the cane was a sacred relic that had to be returned to Ireland.
- **Rumor or myth:** later narratives call it unequivocally “Saint Patrick’s staff.” No evidence establishes it as an ancient relic.
- **Documented consequence:** the object became a central motive in Artaud’s journey and posthumous Irish legend.

### Irish route

Artaud traveled through Cobh, Galway, Inishmore in the Aran Islands, and Dublin. Surviving letters run principally from 14 August to 21 September.

He arrived with little money and inadequate English. He sought priests, ancient sites, and persons he believed could help complete his mission. Stories that he sought a document hidden in Saint Patrick’s tomb appear in later testimony.

### Arrest, Mountjoy, and deportation

By September he was destitute and behaving in ways authorities regarded as threatening or disorderly.

- **Documented fact:** he was arrested in Dublin, held for approximately six days in Mountjoy Prison, and deported as an undesirable or destitute alien on 29 September 1937 aboard the *Washington*, bound for Le Havre.
- **Reported fact:** police beat or injured him during arrest or deportation.
- **Unresolved:** Irish and French accounts differ over the precipitating conduct, treatment in custody, the fate of the cane, and the loss of manuscripts or possessions.
- **Mythic afterlife:** the lost staff became the central emblem of “Artaud in Ireland,” often narrated as a failed sacred quest.

## Psychiatric Confinement, 1937–1946

### Le Havre and Sotteville-lès-Rouen

On return from Ireland Artaud was confined at the Hôpital général in Le Havre, then transferred to Quatre-Mares at Sotteville-lès-Rouen. His family initially had difficulty locating him.

### Sainte-Anne

In 1938 he was transferred to Sainte-Anne in Paris. Psychiatric descriptions increasingly treated him as chronically psychotic. The diagnosis most often associated with this phase is schizophrenia, but terminology and criteria changed over the period.

*Le Théâtre et son double* appeared from Gallimard in 1938 while he was institutionalized.

### Ville-Évrard, 1939–1943

Artaud was confined at Ville-Évrard from 22 February 1939 to 22 January 1943. Dr. Léon Fouks was among the doctors he encountered.

He wrote large numbers of letters and produced “sorts”: written and drawn spells, often burned, perforated, stained, or aggressively addressed to friends, doctors, public figures, and imagined persecutors.

During wartime occupation, psychiatric hospitals suffered catastrophic hunger, cold, disease, and excess mortality. Artaud became severely emaciated. His mother sought help from Robert Desnos in arranging transfer to the unoccupied zone.

### Rodez, 1943–1946

Artaud reached the psychiatric hospital at Rodez in February 1943. The hospital was directed by Gaston Ferdière, who encouraged writing, translation, drawing, and occupational activity.

#### Electroconvulsive treatment

- First series: June 1943.
- Second series: October–November 1943.
- Further series: May–June and August–December 1944.
- Published totals vary, often around fifty or fifty-one treatments; some accounts give fifty-six.
- **Documented adverse event:** during or after one treatment Artaud sustained a vertebral fracture, leaving serious pain and temporary immobility.
- **Documented dispute:** Ferdière later defended the treatment as medically justified and credited it with restoring Artaud’s capacity to write; Artaud denounced electroshock as an assault and torture.
- **Historical caution:** the claim that electroshock simply “cured” him or simply created his late work reduces a complex record. He resumed prodigious production under Rodez conditions while also suffering grave injury and coercion.

#### Religion and reversal

Early Rodez writings contain an intense Catholic mystical phase, including translations and invocations of Christ and Mary. By 1 April 1945 he had violently rejected Christianity, God, baptism, family generation, and institutional religion.

His late worldview centered on:

- the body without organs;
- rejection of anatomical and reproductive destiny;
- glossolalia;
- exorcism of God, spirits, doubles, and persecutors;
- refusal of heredity and social identity;
- language as breath, percussion, cry, drawing, and bodily attack.

#### Late visual and notebook practice

In January 1945 he resumed large-format drawing. From February he filled school notebooks continuously with text, invented syllables, diagrams, drafts, names, bodily formulas, and images.

The notebooks cannot be cleanly separated into diary, artwork, literary manuscript, and medical document. Later editorial efforts have differed over whether to normalize them into readable texts or preserve their material disorder.

## Release, Ivry, and Final Creative Period, 1946–1948

### Campaign for release

Jean Paulhan, Jean Dubuffet, Arthur Adamov, Marthe Robert, Henri and Paule Thomas/Thévenin, Roger Blin, André Breton, and others participated in varying ways in efforts to improve Artaud’s circumstances.

Dr. Achille Delmas agreed to receive him at his private clinic in Ivry under conditions allowing substantial freedom. Artaud left Rodez on 25 May 1946 and arrived in Paris the next day.

### Benefit events and finances

On 7 June 1946 a benefit and homage was held at the Théâtre Sarah-Bernhardt. André Breton opened the event; Arthur Adamov and Jean-Louis Barrault were among its organizers.

On 13 June, works donated by artists were auctioned at Galerie Pierre for Artaud’s support. Picasso is associated with the benefit through a contributed work and later illustrated publication. Funds from the theatrical event, auction, publication rights, friends, and family allowed Artaud to live at Ivry without ordinary employment.

No evidence indicates investments, companies, property holdings, or durable wealth. His financial structure was personal and precarious: clinic costs, drugs, café expenses, small publisher advances, gifts, and benefit proceeds.

### Vieux-Colombier performance

On 13 January 1947 Artaud gave a three-hour performance, *Histoire vécue d’Artaud-Mômo. Tête à tête par Antonin Artaud*, at the Théâtre du Vieux-Colombier.

The event combined lecture, recitation, shouting, silence, bodily gesture, and breakdown of conventional presentation. André Gide later recorded his impression. Reports differ as to whether it was a triumph, disaster, ordeal, or revelation; the disagreement is itself evidence of the event’s effect.

### Drawings

From 4 to 20 July 1947 Galerie Pierre exhibited *Portraits et dessins*. Works included large pencil and colored-chalk sheets, self-portraits, pierced and scarred pages, and images Artaud described as neither conventional drawings nor aesthetic objects.

Jean Dubuffet admired Artaud’s drawings and visited him. Paule Thévenin later preserved and donated a major group; Centre Pompidou holdings include *Autoportrait*, *L’Inca*, *Les Corps de terre*, *La Machine de l’être*, and related works.

### Major final publications

- *Artaud le Mômo* (written principally 1946; published 1947)
- *Ci-gît précédé de La Culture indienne* (1947)
- *Van Gogh, le suicidé de la société* (1947)
- preparations for *Suppôts et Suppliciations*
- hundreds of notebooks, letters, drawings, and dictated texts

*Van Gogh* was prompted by the Orangerie exhibition and encouragement from gallery owner Pierre Loeb. Artaud disputed psychiatric claims about Van Gogh and argued that society drove the painter to suicide. The text won the Prix Sainte-Beuve in 1948.

### Paule Thévenin

Paule Thévenin became Artaud’s friend, listener, typist, performer, custodian, and later editor. He dictated material to her and authorized her to collect monies due on specified works.

After his death she spent decades editing Gallimard’s *Œuvres complètes*. Her editorial authority later generated litigation and dispute with Artaud’s heirs.

## *Pour en finir avec le jugement de dieu*

Fernand Pouey commissioned a radio work from Artaud. It was recorded between 22 and 29 November 1947 with Artaud, Maria Casarès, Roger Blin, and Paule Thévenin. Artaud supplied cries, percussion, xylophone-like sounds, and vocal effects.

The work attacked American militarism, organized religion, fecal shame, anatomy, social discipline, and God. It culminated in the “body without organs.”

It was scheduled for broadcast on 2 February 1948. Wladimir Porché, director-general of French broadcasting, prohibited transmission after a private hearing.

- **Documented fact:** it was commissioned, recorded, scheduled, and administratively censored.
- **Documented fact:** a committee hearing produced conflicting judgments; some artists defended it.
- **Posthumous myth:** it is sometimes described as destroyed or wholly unheard until the 1970s. The recording survived, was heard privately and in limited presentations, and circulated before its eventual official broadcast.
- INA identifies the preserved recording as Artaud’s final chosen version and notes its first radio transmission approximately twenty-five years later.

## Illness, Drugs, and Death

### Cancer

In early 1948 Artaud was diagnosed with inoperable rectal cancer. The diagnosis was late. Pain management included chloral hydrate, and he continued seeking opiates.

### Discovery of the body

On the morning of 4 March 1948 he was found dead in his room at Ivry, seated at the foot of or beside his bed, reportedly holding a shoe.

### Cause

The most widely accepted account is accidental overdose of chloral hydrate against the background of terminal cancer, chronic drug dependence, emaciation, and severe pain.

- **Documented:** terminal rectal cancer and access to chloral hydrate.
- **Probable, not conclusively established:** accidental overdose.
- **Unresolved:** whether the dosage was deliberate, mistaken, or physiologically fatal because of his condition.
- **No adequate evidence of suicide:** some narratives loosely call the death suicide, but the documentary basis is insufficient for that conclusion.

## Employment, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

Artaud’s institutional affiliations were artistic and medical rather than corporate.

### Principal institutions

## Political, Religious, and Artistic Development

### Politics

Artaud opposed colonialism, bourgeois cultural authority, conventional psychiatry, organized Christianity, and mechanized civilization. He did not maintain a consistent party affiliation.

His break with the Surrealists arose partly from their movement toward Communist political discipline. He rejected the idea that psychic and metaphysical revolt could be reduced to economic revolution.

His Mexico writings praised Indigenous cultural survival and condemned European destruction, while also transforming living Indigenous people into symbols within his private theory. The two elements—anti-colonial argument and primitivist projection—coexist.

### Religion

His trajectory was not linear:

1. Catholic childhood.
2. Early revolt against Christianity.
3. Surrealist anti-clericalism and “end of the Christian era.”
4. Occult, alchemical, astrological, and ancient-religion interests.
5. Mexican search for sacred culture.
6. Apocalyptic Christian and prophetic phase around 1937–44.
7. Violent late repudiation of God, Christ, baptism, sexuality, family generation, spirits, and metaphysical doubles.

Claims that he was straightforwardly Catholic, atheist, Gnostic, Satanist, or occult initiate each isolate one phase or rhetorical position.

### Artistic principles

- language begins in breath, cry, rhythm, and bodily pressure;
- the stage should organize space, light, sound, gesture, and danger;
- performance should affect the spectator physiologically;
- theatre should not illustrate a literary script;
- art should act like plague, ritual, exorcism, or catastrophe;
- drawings and notebooks are operations on the body and “subjectile,” not representations alone.

## Controversies and Disputed Questions

### Psychiatric diagnosis

Artaud’s institutional record documents profound distress, delusions, hallucination-like experiences, disorganized conduct, and inability at periods to care for himself. “Schizophrenia” became the dominant institutional diagnosis.

Modern retrospective labels remain disputed. Literary admirers sometimes deny illness entirely; medicalizing accounts sometimes reduce the writings to symptoms. Neither position adequately represents the combined documentary record.

### Electroshock

The treatment was legal medical practice, administered under institutional authority. It was not a criminal prosecution or clandestine experiment. Nevertheless, consent was absent or compromised, grave injury occurred, and Artaud denounced it as destructive violence.

Ferdière’s later claim that shock restored Artaud’s creativity is contested by the patient’s own testimony and by scholars who emphasize coercion and bodily damage.

### Drugs and possible illegal procurement

Artaud was medically introduced to laudanum but became dependent on narcotics. Letters and memoirs document persistent efforts to obtain opium derivatives and heroin. This involved behavior outside ordinary medical practice, but no major narcotics conviction is documented.

### Irish arrest

The arrest and deportation are documented. Assertions about assault by police, the exact offense, a confrontation at a Jesuit house, destruction of a manuscript, and disappearance of the staff have uneven evidence.

### Dulac controversy

Artaud wrote the scenario; Dulac directed the film. Accounts that erase Dulac’s authorship are historically incorrect. Conversely, accounts that ignore Artaud’s substantive dispute over transformation of his scenario omit a documented conflict.

### Editorial litigation

After Artaud’s death, Paule Thévenin edited the multi-volume *Œuvres complètes*. Artaud’s nephews and other heirs challenged control, transcription, and publication.

Thévenin maintained that Artaud entrusted the work and rights administration to her. The heirs invoked legal succession. Critics also questioned whether she regularized spelling, segmented notebooks, or transformed fluid material into discrete “works.” Defenders emphasize that she rescued, deciphered, and published an otherwise inaccessible corpus.

Gallimard’s 2004 *Œuvres*, edited by Évelyne Grossman, and subsequent facsimile-oriented notebook editions reflect a different editorial approach. This is a genuine textual-history dispute, not merely personal scandal.

### Racial, sexual, and violent language

Artaud’s works contain ethnic typologies, primitivist constructions, misogynistic or sexually hostile imagery, scatology, threats, and violent fantasies. Some late letters name real people as persecutors or doubles. Such passages document his rhetoric and cosmology; they are not evidence that the named persons committed the acts attributed to them.

No substantial evidence ties him to organized fascism, intelligence services, political terrorism, financial crime, or corruption.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Self-Created Mythology

### “Mad genius”

- **Story:** Artaud’s art flowed directly and automatically from insanity.
- **Circulation:** obituaries, avant-garde reception, psychiatric debate, popular biography.
- **Evidence:** mental illness and institutionalization are documented, but so are deliberate revision, publication planning, correspondence, collaboration, and detailed technical thinking.
- **Effect:** produced both romantic cult and medical reductionism.

### Saint Patrick’s staff

- **Story:** Artaud carried the authentic staff of Saint Patrick back to Ireland.
- **Origin:** Artaud’s own prophetic beliefs in 1937, amplified by later Irish accounts and films.
- **Evidence for authenticity:** none.
- **Evidence for an actual cane and mission:** letters and travel records.
- **Effect:** transformed the Irish collapse into sacred pilgrimage narrative.

### Tarahumara initiation

- **Story:** tribal priests formally initiated Artaud into an immemorial peyote mystery.
- **Origin:** Artaud’s published narratives and later countercultural reception.
- **Evidence:** travel to the Sierra and engagement with peyote culture are credible; exact ritual details are difficult to corroborate.
- **Effect:** central to psychedelic, theatrical, anthropological, and occult appropriations of Artaud.

### “Inventor” of the first Surrealist film

Artaud wrote *La Coquille et le Clergyman* but did not direct it. Calling it the first Surrealist film is defensible as film history but does not make Artaud its sole author.

### The censored radio masterpiece destroyed by the state

The broadcast was censored, but the recording was preserved. The story of total suppression exaggerates the archival facts while accurately reflecting institutional prohibition.

### Deliberate suicide

The circumstances permit speculation, but evidence favors probable accidental chloral overdose during terminal illness. Suicide remains unproved.

### The outsider with no institutional support

Artaud repeatedly clashed with institutions, but he also depended on editors, patrons, theatres, film companies, physicians, galleries, broadcasters, and family. His outsider status was real but never socially absolute.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Artaud’s reputation expanded dramatically after 1948, especially after publication of *The Theatre and Its Double* in English in 1958 and the gradual appearance of the *Œuvres complètes*.

### Theatre and performance

His influence is documented or explicitly claimed in relation to:

- Peter Brook;
- Jerzy Grotowski;
- the Living Theatre;
- the Open Theatre;
- environmental and immersive theatre;
- performance art;
- ritual theatre;
- body art;
- experimental voice and sound poetry.

These practitioners rarely reproduced Artaud’s program literally. “Theatre of Cruelty” became a broad label, sometimes reduced to violent spectacle despite Artaud’s more complex definition.

### Philosophy and critical theory

Derrida made Artaud central to arguments about speech, writing, representation, and the “subjectile.” Deleuze and Guattari transformed the “body without organs” into a major philosophical concept. Foucault treated Artaud as a boundary figure in the relation between madness and language.

This reception detached phrases from their medical, religious, and notebook contexts while giving Artaud an influence far beyond theatre.

### Psychiatry and anti-psychiatry

Artaud became an emblem in debates about psychiatric confinement, electroshock, patient testimony, and the institutional designation of madness. His case has been used by both critics and defenders of psychiatry. The documentary record supports neither a simple heroic anti-psychiatric legend nor a purely clinical account.

### Visual art

The late drawings entered major museum collections and are studied independently of the literary work. Their classification remains unstable: modern art, outsider art, manuscript art, graphic poetry, and psychiatric art have all been applied.

### Editorial legacy

Paule Thévenin’s edition made the immense corpus accessible but also became part of the work’s history. Later editors sought closer reproduction of the notebooks’ spatial and graphic form. There is no neutral “complete Artaud”: each edition must decide how to separate work, draft, letter, image, and fragment.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the researched sources are included. Mere contemporaneity has been excluded.

- **Alfred Jarry — documented influence.** The Théâtre Alfred Jarry, founded in Paris in 1926 by Artaud, Roger Vitrac, and Robert Aron, explicitly adopted Jarry’s name and anti-naturalist theatrical legacy.

- **André Breton — documented fact.** Artaud joined Breton’s Surrealist circle in Paris in 1924, directed the Bureau de recherches surréalistes, and contributed to *La Révolution surréaliste*. Breton’s group expelled him in 1926–27 amid political and artistic conflict. Breton later supported him and opened the 7 June 1946 Sarah-Bernhardt benefit.

- **Carl Theodor Dreyer — documented fact.** Dreyer directed Artaud as the monk Jean Massieu in *La Passion de Jeanne d’Arc*, filmed in France in 1927 and released in 1928.

- **Renée Jeanne Falconetti — documented fact.** Falconetti played Joan of Arc opposite Artaud’s Massieu in Dreyer’s *La Passion de Jeanne d’Arc*.

- **Edgar Allan Poe — documented influence.** Centre Pompidou and biographical chronologies identify Poe among Artaud’s adolescent readings. This was literary influence, not a personal connection.

- **Friedrich Nietzsche — reported intellectual affinity.** Artaud read and was repeatedly compared with Nietzsche in relation to cruelty, anti-Christianism, tragedy, and the body. Direct line-by-line influence is less securely documented than the broader affinity.

- **Pablo Ruiz Picasso — documented support/association.** Picasso was connected with postwar efforts benefiting Artaud and supplied an original etching for the 1957 posthumous publication *Autre chose que l’enfant beau*. This was not a close personal partnership.

- **Albert Camus — reported fact.** Camus belonged to the postwar literary-theatrical milieu mobilized around Artaud and is reported among supporters of the 1946 benefit campaign. The available sources do not establish a sustained personal friendship.

- **Jacques Derrida — documented posthumous intellectual connection.** Derrida wrote major studies of Artaud, including “La parole soufflée,” “Le théâtre de la cruauté et la clôture de la représentation,” and *Forcener le subjectile*. They did not meet; Derrida’s engagement was posthumous.

- **Gilles Louis René Deleuze — documented posthumous influence.** Deleuze used Artaud’s language in *Logique du sens*; with Félix Guattari he developed the “body without organs” in *L’Anti-Œdipe* and *Mille Plateaux*. No personal meeting occurred.

- **Pierre-Félix Guattari — documented posthumous influence.** With Deleuze, Guattari transformed Artaud’s “body without organs” into a philosophical and political concept. No personal connection is documented.

- **Paul-Michel Foucault — documented posthumous intellectual connection.** Foucault repeatedly invoked Artaud in work on madness, literature, silence, and language. No personal meeting occurred.

- **Jean-Paul Charles Aymard Sartre — reported posthumous reception.** Sartre’s theatrical and intellectual milieu discussed Artaud, but the consulted sources do not establish a significant direct lifetime relationship.

- **William Blake — reported intellectual and symbolic affinity.** Artaud translated or adapted English religious-poetic material at Rodez and has often been compared with Blake as a visionary artist-poet. The consulted evidence does not establish Blake as a comparably central named influence in Artaud’s early formation.

No reliable researched connection was found with the overwhelming majority of the 894-person roster. Absence from this section means that the consulted evidence did not support a meeting, correspondence, collaboration, institutional tie, family relationship, or explicit documented influence.

- **1931:** sees Balinese theatre at Colonial Exposition; Sorbonne lecture; publishes adaptation of *Le Moine*.
- **1932:** first Theatre of Cruelty manifesto.
- **1934:** publishes *Héliogabale ou l’Anarchiste couronné*.
- **1935:** stages *Les Cenci*; production closes after seventeen performances.
- **1936, January–November:** Mexico journey; lectures in Mexico City; travels to Sierra Tarahumara and reports peyote rite.
- **1937:** engagement to Cécile Schramme fails; publishes *Les Nouvelles Révélations de l’Être*; travels to Ireland.
- **1937, 23–29 September:** arrested, held at Mountjoy, deported.
- **1937–38:** confined at Le Havre and Sotteville-lès-Rouen.
- **1938:** transferred to Sainte-Anne; *Le Théâtre et son double* published.
- **1939–1943:** interned at Ville-Évrard.
- **1943, February:** transferred to Rodez.
- **1943–44:** repeated electroshock series; vertebral injury; translation and writing resume.
- **1945:** rejects Christianity; begins continuous school notebooks and large drawings.
- **1946, 25–26 May:** leaves Rodez and returns to Paris; enters clinic at Ivry.
- **1946, 7 June:** Sarah-Bernhardt benefit.
- **1946, 13 June:** Galerie Pierre benefit auction.
- **1947, 13 January:** Vieux-Colombier performance.
- **1947:** publishes *Artaud le Mômo*, *Ci-gît*, and *Van Gogh, le suicidé de la société*.
- **1947, 4–20 July:** Galerie Pierre exhibition.
- **1947, 22–29 November:** records *Pour en finir avec le jugement de dieu*.
- **1948, 2 February:** scheduled broadcast prohibited.
- **1948, February:** terminal rectal cancer documented.
- **1948, 4 March:** found dead at Ivry; probable accidental chloral-hydrate overdose.
- **1948 onward:** expanding posthumous publication and international influence.
- **1956–1994:** Gallimard issues *Œuvres complètes* under Paule Thévenin’s principal editorship.
- **1970s onward:** radio work receives official broadcast and broader circulation.
- **1986:** Thévenin and Derrida publish *Antonin Artaud: Dessins et portraits*.
- **1994:** Thévenin bequest brings a major body of drawings into public collections.
- **1996–97:** MoMA exhibition *Antonin Artaud: Works on Paper*.
- **2004:** Gallimard publishes Grossman’s chronological *Œuvres*.
- **2006–07:** major BnF retrospective.
- **2012:** publication of the extensive *Cahiers d’Ivry* edition.
