# Rudolf Laban: Research Dossier

## Scope and Evidentiary Conventions

This dossier reconstructs Rudolf Laban’s life from published books, institutional histories, archival catalogues, digitized primary materials, contemporaneous periodicals, and historical scholarship. The surviving record is uneven. Laban frequently mythologized his own life; important memoirs were written retrospectively; portions of his papers are undated; and many theatrical works survive only through photographs, programs, reviews, sketches, partial notation, or reconstructions.

Evidentiary labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary documents, archival catalogues, institutional records, publications, or convergent specialist scholarship.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a generally reliable biographer, memoirist, or institutional history but not independently verified here from a primary record.
- **Disputed claim:** substantial sources disagree.
- **Unverified claim:** repeated in secondary literature without adequate documentary confirmation.
- **Myth or legend:** a story important to Laban’s self-presentation or reception but not securely historical.
- **Interpretation:** an analytical conclusion advanced by named scholars rather than a directly recorded event.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and social background

**Documented fact:** Laban’s father was **Rudolf Laban de Váralja (1843–1907)**, an Austro-Hungarian officer who rose to field-marshal-lieutenant and held senior military responsibilities in Bosnia-Herzegovina. He was associated with Pressburg, Vienna, Sarajevo, and Mostar. His advancement brought the family into the imperial military elite.

Laban’s mother was **Marie Laban, née Bridling (1858–1926)**. The available open sources provide much less information about her origins than about her husband.

Laban reportedly had a sister, generally identified as **Renée/Renée Laban**, who lived with the family in Vienna. The full sibling history remains incompletely documented in the accessible sources.

The father’s career exposed the younger Laban to several cultural environments:

- Magyar and German-speaking Pressburg;
- the imperial court and military world of Vienna;
- Sarajevo and Mostar under Austro-Hungarian administration;
- South Slavic, Islamic, Orthodox Christian, Catholic, and Jewish communities in Bosnia-Herzegovina.

Later Laban institutions emphasized this mixed Central and Southeastern European formation. Claims that he thereby received a systematic “Eastern” education are better treated as retrospective characterization than as a documented curriculum.

### First marriage: Martha Fricke

**Reported fact, with disputed dates:** Around **1900**, Laban married the painter **Martha Fricke**, reportedly from the Lüneburg Heath area. They lived at Arcisstraße 44 in Munich and later moved to Paris.

Their children are usually identified as:

- **Azraela/Azra von Laban**
- **Arpad von Laban**

Martha died in either **1905 or 1907**; 1907 appears most frequently in recent biographical accounts, but the year is not uniform across the literature. The children subsequently grew up principally with their maternal grandparents. This separation became one of the enduring ruptures in Laban’s family life.

### Second marriage: Maja Lederer

Laban met **Maja Lederer**, a Munich singer, during his unsettled post-1907 period. They married in Pressburg on **8 May 1910**.

Their children are generally identified as two daughters, including **Juana de Laban**, who later participated in preserving and interpreting his work. Published summaries are inconsistent about the names and exact birth details of all children in this household; claims should therefore be checked against the family correspondence collections before being treated as complete.

The marriage deteriorated during and after the First World War. Laban’s increasingly itinerant work, financial instability, and relationships with other women contributed to the breakdown. Maja nevertheless assisted him at several points and remained important in the transmission of family papers and artworks.

### Suzanne Perrottet and the triangular household

Swiss musician and movement teacher **Suzanne Perrottet (1889–1983)** became Laban’s collaborator and lover after meeting him in the Swiss-German reform-culture milieu. She worked with Laban and Maja Lederer in developing a movement practice centered on individual expressive possibility.

Perrottet and Laban had a son:

- **Allar Perrottet**, later known as **André Perrottet von Laban**

During the First World War Laban, Maja, Perrottet, and their children lived in an unconventional shared household, first associated with the Monte Verità summer schools and later at Hombrechtikon near Zürich. Photographs and correspondence support the existence of this family-collaborative group. Descriptions of it as a harmonious *ménage à trois* are retrospective and may simplify a relationship marked by emotional and financial pressures.

Perrottet took over the Zürich school when Laban left Switzerland. She subsequently developed an independent career, while remaining central to the history of his early movement pedagogy.

### Dussia Bereska

Russian-born dancer **Dussia Bereska** became Laban’s performer, collaborator, and intimate partner during his German career. She directed or co-directed the Kammertanzbühne and contributed to choreography, including *Ritterballett*. Their relationship aggravated the collapse of Laban’s domestic arrangements with Maja and Perrottet. Later biographical literature describes him during this transition as effectively homeless and dependent on professional associates.

### Lisa Ullmann

**Lisa Ullmann (1907–1985)** became Laban’s closest late-life collaborator and companion. She assisted him after his move to Britain, helped systematize his teaching, established the Art of Movement Studio, edited and revised his English books, and organized his papers. Laban bequeathed his English archive to her.

Whether “partner” is intended romantically or principally as companion and professional collaborator varies by source. Their practical and intellectual partnership is documented; more specific claims about the private relationship should not be asserted beyond the evidence.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1879–1898

Laban was born in Pressburg, a multilingual city in the Hungarian half of the Habsburg monarchy. His childhood was divided among Pressburg, Vienna, Sarajevo, and Mostar according to his father’s postings.

He later connected several formative motifs to this environment:

- military parades and disciplined mass formations;
- folk dances and communal festivities;
- craft labor and rhythmic work;
- Islamic and Balkan architecture;
- theatrical spectacle;
- geometrical organization of bodies in space.

These influences are plausible and recur in his writings and biographies, but individual childhood episodes often come from Laban’s own retrospective accounts. His autobiography is a stylized artistic memoir, not a neutral chronology.

His father intended him for military service. Laban entered the **Theresian Military Academy at Wiener Neustadt**, reportedly in 1898. He abandoned the officer’s path after a short period.

## Education and Formation

### Munich

Around 1899–1900 Laban went to Munich to study art rather than remain in the army. His exact enrollment history is not fully consistent across sources. He attended art instruction and became associated with the progressive **Lehr- und Versuchsatelier für freie und angewandte Kunst**, founded by Hermann Obrist and Wilhelm von Debschitz.

Munich’s Schwabing milieu exposed him to:

- Jugendstil and Symbolism;
- experimental design and architecture;
- Lebensreform;
- alternative education;
- occult and esoteric currents;
- new bodily cultures;
- the visual abstraction developing around artists later associated with the Blaue Reiter.

Direct personal acquaintance with every celebrated Munich modernist is not established. Institutional proximity must not be converted into meetings without evidence.

### Paris

In approximately **1904**, Laban, Martha, and their children moved to Paris. Laban studied architecture at the **École des Beaux-Arts** or in its orbit, worked as an illustrator and graphic artist, and observed theatre, dance, cabaret, and popular entertainment.

Paris contributed several lasting elements:

- an architectural understanding of the moving body;
- interest in spatial proportion and geometric solids;
- exposure to systems of expressive gesture associated with **François Delsarte**;
- awareness of theatre as a synthesis of movement, design, light, and social gathering;
- a desire to devise a script for movement comparable to musical notation.

The documentary record does not support a completed architecture degree.

### Influences

Laban acknowledged or discussed several predecessors:

- **François Delsarte:** expressive correspondences among bodily gesture, emotion, and spiritual state. Laban referred to Delsartean mime and credited Delsarte in *Gymnastik und Tanz*.
- **Isadora Duncan:** liberation from academic ballet and recovery of bodily/natural movement. Laban knew her work and reportedly saw her dance in Zürich, though one account says he was unimpressed by the performance.
- **Émile Jaques-Dalcroze:** rhythmic education and the systematic union of music and movement. Laban departed from Dalcroze by insisting that dance need not remain subordinate to music.
- **Nietzsche, Wagner, Romantic Naturphilosophie, craft revival, Lebensreform, and contemporary esotericism:** recurrent intellectual contexts, though the weight assigned to each differs among scholars.
- **Visual modernism:** Kandinsky, Cézanne, Matisse, Picasso, Klimt, Schiele, and others are cited in later institutional accounts as parallels for his search for an autonomous movement art.

## Early Career, 1907–1913

The death of Martha and, shortly afterward, Laban’s father in 1907 left him in an unstable position. His two children remained with their maternal family. Laban moved between Paris, Vienna, Nice, and Munich, earning money through visual art, illustration, design, teaching, and theatrical work.

After marrying Maja Lederer in 1910, he established a movement school in Munich. This date is conventionally treated as the beginning of his organized dance career, though he had already been studying bodily expression.

His emerging program rejected several assumptions of theatrical ballet:

- codified steps as the exclusive basis of dance;
- music as the necessary origin and regulator of movement;
- narrative mime as the primary source of meaning;
- professional virtuosity as the sole legitimate form of dance.

Instead he investigated weight, time, direction, rhythm, bodily inclination, spatial pathways, and group relations. The term **Bewegungskunst**—movement art—better describes some of this early activity than “modern dance” in its later institutional sense.

## Monte Verità and Switzerland, 1913–1919

### Summer schools at Monte Verità

Beginning in **1913**, Laban conducted summer courses at **Monte Verità**, above Ascona in Ticino. The colony had become a center of Lebensreform, vegetarianism, nudism, anarchism, Theosophy, experimental education, crafts, and alternative sexuality.

Laban’s group practiced outdoors, experimented with movement independent of conventional music, made costumes and masks, and created festival forms tied to landscape and communal ritual. Nudity or minimal dress belonged to the wider body-culture environment and to some Laban practice, although later accounts sometimes exaggerate Monte Verità into an entirely unstructured erotic commune.

### Collaborators and pupils

Important participants included:

- **Mary Wigman**
- **Suzanne Perrottet**
- **Katja Wulff**
- **Sophie Taeuber**
- **Maja Lederer**
- other artists, students, and reform-colony visitors whose degree of participation varied.

Wigman became Laban’s assistant and one of the most consequential independent artists to emerge from his circle. Their later relationship combined acknowledged influence with differentiation: Wigman built her own aesthetic and institution rather than remaining merely a transmitter of Laban’s method.

### War and Zürich

When the First World War began, Laban, as an Austro-Hungarian reserve officer, risked mobilization. After the 1915 summer course he remained in neutral Switzerland rather than returning to Munich.

Laban, Maja, Suzanne Perrottet, and the children moved to **Hombrechtikon**, outside Zürich. He also worked in Zürich, where wartime émigré and avant-garde networks converged.

His school offered professional and amateur training. He developed:

- improvisational movement;
- group “movement choirs”;
- spatial scales;
- movement analysis;
- early notation experiments;
- theatre without necessary dependence on music;
- festival dance as collective social practice.

### *Sang an die Sonne*

On the night of **18–19 August 1917**, Laban’s group presented *Sang an die Sonne* (“Song to the Sun”) at Monte Verità. The work’s progression through darkness, dawn, and illumination became emblematic of his ritual-festival theatre.

Descriptions of the performance vary because no complete film or definitive score survives. It is documented as a major event, but later reconstructions inevitably combine photographs, programs, memoirs, and Laban principles.

### Departure from Switzerland

In 1918–19 Laban’s Swiss enterprise fragmented. Perrottet continued the Zürich school. Laban sought opportunities in postwar Germany, where his artistic reputation expanded rapidly despite unstable finances.

## German Career, 1919–1930

### Stuttgart and Mannheim

Laban worked briefly in Stuttgart, including association with painter **Max Ackermann**, and then developed theatrical activity in Mannheim. At the Mannheim National Theatre he worked with conventional theatre personnel while introducing his own dancers.

Productions included:

- a version of the Bacchanale from Wagner’s *Tannhäuser*;
- *Die Geblendeten*;
- *Epische Tanzfolge*;
- early movement-choir and dance-theatre experiments.

The Mannheim engagement demonstrated both the possibilities and recurring limitations of his practice: he generated systems, images, ensembles, and experimental structures but often relied on collaborators to refine productions.

### Hamburg and the Tanzbühne Laban

In **1922–23**, Laban concentrated his activities in Hamburg and founded the **Tanzbühne Laban** and later the **Kammertanzbühne Laban**, associated especially with Dussia Bereska.

The ensembles toured Germany, Austria, Italy, and Yugoslavia. The Hamburg operation combined:

- professional stage productions;
- teacher training;
- amateur movement choirs;
- children’s movement education;
- experimental notation;
- publishing and advocacy.

The system expanded through affiliated schools and teachers. Later institutional accounts speak of approximately twenty-five Laban schools or choirs in Germany, Latvia, Zagreb, Paris, and other cities. These were not always centrally owned branches in a modern corporate sense; the “Laban” network combined direct institutions, licensed or affiliated teachers, and groups using his principles.

### Major stage works

The repertory included:

- *Der schwingende Tempel* (“The Swinging Temple”)
- *Faust II*
- *Prometheus*
- *Die Gaukelei* (“Jugglery”)
- *Casanova*
- *Don Juan*, using Gluck
- *Die Nacht*
- *Narrenspiegel*
- *Ritterballett*, with Bereska
- *Das lebende Idol*
- *Götzendienst*
- *Die grünen Clowns*
- *Faustreigen*

Several titles existed in revised versions. Attribution may be divided among Laban as originator, a collaborator as choreographic finisher, a composer, and the performing ensemble. Modern catalogues therefore should not imply that every movement was fixed personally by Laban.

### Movement choirs

The **Bewegungschor** was one of Laban’s characteristic institutions. Large groups of amateurs performed organized movement patterns, sometimes with trained leaders or a smaller “crystal” group demonstrating themes.

The movement choir could serve different purposes:

- community participation;
- festival ceremony;
- worker or civic culture;
- aesthetic education;
- disciplined mass spectacle.

Its political valence was not fixed. The same techniques could support participatory reform culture, municipal pageantry, socialist or pacifist themes, commercial festivals, or authoritarian national spectacle. This portability later helped make Laban useful to Nazi cultural administration.

### Choreographic institutes

Laban founded or directed choreographic institutes in:

- **Würzburg**, approximately 1926–27;
- **Berlin**, approximately 1928–29.

He sought to create a research-and-training institution where dance could possess theory, written transmission, trained choreographers, archives, and professional standing comparable to music.

### Dance congresses and notation

Laban participated prominently in German dance congresses. In **1928**, the system that became **Kinetography Laban** was publicly consolidated through *Schrifttanz* and associated teaching.

The notation used a vertical staff, with symbols indicating:

- timing through vertical extent;
- direction;
- level;
- body part;
- support;
- spatial relationship.

Laban’s system was developed and standardized by collaborators, particularly **Albrecht Knust** in Central Europe. In Britain and the United States other traditions emerged, including what became known as **Labanotation**. The two major contemporary lineages are related but not identical.

### Financial structure

Laban’s enterprises were chronically vulnerable. Schools, tours, publishing schemes, theatre companies, and experimental festivals required more resources than they reliably generated. He depended on:

- tuition;
- theatre appointments;
- municipal or festival commissions;
- publishers;
- local organizers;
- collaborators who performed administrative labor;
- patrons and hosts.

No substantial investment portfolio or lasting private fortune is documented. The recurrent picture is of ambitious institutional expansion accompanied by debt, dependence, and unstable residence.

## Berlin State Opera and Public Eminence, 1930–1933

From **1930 until 1934**, Laban held a senior dance position at the **Prussian State Opera/State Opera Unter den Linden** in Berlin, commonly described as ballet director or movement-and-dance director. The exact institutional title changed.

This appointment placed an experimental movement theorist inside a major state theatre. He choreographed or supervised opera ballets, including Wagnerian material, and operated master workshops.

By his fiftieth birthday in 1929–30, Laban had become:

- a leading theorist of German modern dance;
- an organizer of professional education;
- an internationally known notation innovator;
- the center of a network of schools and teachers;
- an advocate for a national dance theatre.

His theatrical works themselves did not enter a stable repertory comparable to the ballets of classical choreographers. Even sympathetic institutional histories acknowledge that Laban was more effective as an experimenter, theorist, and initiator than as a finisher of durable stage products.

## Nazi Germany, 1933–1937

## Positions and institutional collaboration

**Documented fact:** Laban remained in Germany after the Nazi seizure of power and collaborated with institutions controlled and funded by the regime. He was not merely a passive private teacher during these years.

In 1933–34:

- the German theatre system was brought under political and racial control;
- Jewish and politically unacceptable personnel were dismissed;
- dance organizations were reorganized within the Reich cultural bureaucracy;
- Laban cultivated cooperation with **Otto von Keudell**, a dance official in Joseph Goebbels’s Propaganda Ministry.

Laban became the artistic center of the **Deutsche Tanzbühne**, created in 1934 with ministerial support. He directed or organized the **Deutsche Tanzfestspiele 1934**, held in Berlin from 9 to 16 December, and edited the accompanying publication.

The festival assembled important German dancers and schools under a national framework. Its texts and organization participated in official claims about a specifically “German” dance culture.

### Racial exclusions

Scholarship based on German archival records reports that Laban adapted his institutions to the regime’s racial requirements and excluded “non-Aryan” personnel. Exact responsibility for individual dismissals can differ between administrative records, but the broader accommodation is documented.

No evidence found in the consulted sources supports treating Laban as a clandestine anti-Nazi resister during 1933–36. Conversely, formal membership in the Nazi Party is not established by the sources used here. Institutional collaboration and ideological conformity do not require party membership and should be recorded separately from it.

### Public language

Laban’s 1930s publications and statements adopted terms compatible with the regime’s cultural nationalism. Historians disagree about whether this represented:

- opportunistic adaptation intended to preserve modern dance;
- genuine ideological convergence;
- a continuation of Laban’s older organic-national and communal thought;
- or some mixture of these.

The documentary minimum is that he accepted official authority, funding, and organizational power, and publicly framed dance in terms usable by National Socialist cultural policy.

### Deutsche Tanzbühne and archive

The Deutsche Tanzbühne sponsored touring, professional coordination, festivals, and research. In **1935**, Laban created the **Deutsches Tanzarchiv**, placing Albrecht Knust in charge. This institutional ancestor is part of the history of the present German Dance Archive Cologne, although holdings and organizational continuity changed after 1945.

### *Vom Tauwind und der neuen Freude*

For the 1936 Berlin Olympic festivities, Laban prepared a large movement-choir work generally titled:

*Vom Tauwind und der neuen Freude*  
(variants translate *Tauwind* as “spring wind,” “dew wind,” or “warm wind”)

Approximately one thousand performers were involved. After a dress rehearsal, Goebbels rejected the production. A diary remark attributed to Goebbels described it as dressed in the regime’s clothing but having nothing to do with it.

**Documented outcome:** the work was cancelled and Laban lost official favor.

**What the cancellation does not prove:** It does not retroactively establish that Laban had opposed Nazism throughout the preceding years. The record shows several years of active cooperation followed by rejection when his aesthetic failed a major propaganda test.

**Disputed details:** Accounts differ on whether the cancellation occurred immediately before the intended performance, exactly how comprehensive the subsequent ban was, and whether Laban was formally “confined” at Staffelberg. He continued to exist in Germany for months afterward but lost the official trajectory on which his career depended.

### Collapse and departure

Laban’s physical and psychological health deteriorated. He spent time at **Schloss Banz** or in the Staffelstein/Banz area and was reportedly hospitalized or convalescent. Accounts describe severe exhaustion, depression, emaciation, financial destitution, and collapse.

In **August 1937**, he reached Paris. In **February 1938**, through connections with former pupils, especially **Kurt Jooss** and **Sigurd Leeder**, he entered Britain and went to Dartington Hall.

“Fled Nazi Germany” is broadly accurate as a description of his final departure from a regime that had rejected him, but it can obscure his prior collaboration. “Exile after official disgrace” is more precise.

## Britain, 1938–1958

### Dartington Hall

At the end of February 1938 Laban arrived at **Dartington Hall, Devon**, where Leonard and Dorothy Elmhirst supported an arts and educational community. Jooss and Leeder had established a dance school there after leaving Germany.

Laban initially lived with or near the Jooss circle and used the Warren House studio. He was in poor health and no longer capable of reproducing his German institutional scale.

His relationship with Jooss was complicated:

- Jooss had been his pupil and collaborator;
- Jooss’s *The Green Table* became one of the most durable works associated with the broader Laban lineage;
- Jooss was now an established director in his own right;
- Laban increasingly turned away from theatre production toward theoretical research, movement education, and observation.

### Lisa Ullmann and Sylvia Bodmer

**Lisa Ullmann** and **Sylvia Bodmer** helped Laban translate his work into British educational and theatrical contexts. Ullmann became indispensable as teacher, organizer, editor, and institutional founder.

The British “Art of Movement” approach softened some of the German metaphysical vocabulary and adapted the work for:

- teacher education;
- children’s creative dance;
- amateur movement;
- acting;
- observation of behavior;
- therapy-related applications.

### Wartime status

The open evidence is inconclusive about Laban’s legal treatment as an enemy alien. One later account says he was not interned and speculates that age, illness, statelessness, or Dorothy Elmhirst’s influence may have helped. Another secondary chronology contains ambiguous wording suggestive of internment. No primary internment record was located in the sources consulted.

**Unresolved question:** Laban’s exact alien registration classification and any period of detention should be checked in British Home Office and tribunal files before a definitive claim is made.

### Newtown and Manchester

During the war Laban and his associates moved through **Newtown, Montgomeryshire**, and later **Manchester**. The decentralized wartime environment and need to train workers created new applications for movement observation.

The **Laban Art of Movement Guild** was founded in **1945**. The **Art of Movement Studio** opened in Manchester in **1946**, principally through Ullmann’s organizational work, with instruction by Ullmann, Bodmer, and Laban.

### Industrial movement work

Laban collaborated with management consultant **F. C. Lawrence** and the firm **Paton Lawrence & Company**. Their work, called **Laban–Lawrence Industrial Rhythm**, observed workers’ actions in factories and docks.

Recorded clients or studies included:

- Mars Confections;
- J. Lyons & Company’s Greenford tea factory;
- dock work;
- industrial and engineering operations.

They analyzed effort through factors later commonly expressed as:

- space: direct/indirect;
- weight: strong/light;
- time: sudden/sustained;
- flow: bound/free.

The stated objectives included reducing fatigue, matching people to jobs, improving satisfaction, coordinating production, and increasing efficiency. Promotional language promised “lilt in labour.”

The system also functioned as labor discipline and production management. Later studies note attempts to use effort assessment in job placement, selection, bonus calculation, and responses to worker complaints. There is no evidence in the consulted sources of a patent owned by Laban. The commercial structures were consultancies, tests, training, reports, and jointly authored methods rather than a patented machine.

### *Effort*

Laban and Lawrence published *Effort: Economy of Human Movement* in **1947**. It combined movement theory with workplace analysis. Authorship was genuinely collaborative; it should not be listed as a solely authored Laban book.

Later **Warren Lamb** extended this work into Action Profiling and Movement Pattern Analysis, particularly for management selection and decision-making studies.

### Educational books

Laban’s principal English-language publications followed:

- *Modern Educational Dance* (1948)
- *The Mastery of Movement on the Stage* (1950)
- *Principles of Dance and Movement Notation* (1956)

Ullmann revised later editions. Consequently, editions published after Laban’s death may contain significant editorial reorganization and should not automatically be treated as identical to his original text.

### Addlestone

In **July 1953**, the Art of Movement Studio moved to a donated country property at **Addlestone, Surrey**. Laban’s last years centered on:

- movement as behavior;
- spatial harmony;
- education;
- actor training;
- industrial and occupational observation;
- the relation of movement to psychiatric care;
- diagrams, geometrical models, and unpublished theoretical writing.

A 1955 photograph shows him standing within an icosahedral spatial model, an enduring image of his “kinesphere” and space-harmony research.

## Health, Psychological Crises, and Death

Laban experienced repeated periods of exhaustion, collapse, depression, and impaired functioning. Trinity Laban’s institutional biography retrospectively describes his condition as resembling “spasmodic manic depression,” especially after extreme creative exertion or perceived rejection.

That language is **retrospective and non-clinical**. No adequate medical record establishing a modern bipolar-disorder diagnosis was located. The documented or strongly reported features are:

- cycles of intense creative labor;
- financial and personal disorganization;
- physical exhaustion;
- depressive collapse;
- severe illness after the 1936 rejection;
- chronic poor health during exile;
- inadequate wartime nutrition contributing to later illness.

No reliable evidence was found of addiction.

Laban died on **1 July 1958** in **Weybridge, Surrey**, aged seventy-eight. The accessible sources consistently give the date and place but do not provide a securely documented immediate medical cause. A cause of death should therefore not be invented.

The November 1958 *Laban Art of Movement Guild Magazine* announced his death and reviewed his work. His archive passed to Lisa Ullmann.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Institutions founded or directed

- Munich movement school, from approximately 1910.
- Monte Verità summer school, from 1913.
- Zürich/Hombrechtikon school and movement groups, wartime period.
- Tanzbühne Laban, Hamburg.
- Kammertanzbühne Laban, associated with Dussia Bereska.
- Laban schools and affiliated movement choirs across Central Europe.
- Choreographisches Institut, Würzburg and Berlin.
- Dance division/master workshops connected with Berlin state theatres.
- Deutsche Tanzbühne, Berlin, 1934–36.
- Deutsches Tanzarchiv, created under the Deutsche Tanzbühne in 1935.
- Deutsche Gesellschaft für Schrifttanz.
- *Schrifttanz* journal, 1928–31.
- Laban Art of Movement Guild, Britain, 1945.
- Art of Movement Studio, Manchester, 1946; Addlestone from 1953.

### Technologies and methods

#### Kinetography Laban / Labanotation

A symbolic writing system for human movement. Laban initiated the conceptual system; Knust and others standardized and extended it. The system became useful for dance preservation, reconstruction, analysis, and research.

#### Choreutics / space harmony

Study of movement through spatial pathways, directional scales, geometric structures, and relations among body, kinesphere, and surrounding space.

#### Eukinetics / Effort

Analysis of the dynamic qualities of movement, especially combinations involving weight, space, time, and flow.

#### Movement choirs

A compositional and organizational technology for coordinated amateur group movement, transmitted through leaders and notation.

#### Industrial Rhythm and Effort Assessment

A management and occupational-observation system developed with F. C. Lawrence. It analyzed bodily action, fatigue, group coordination, personnel selection, and work organization.

### Patents and ownership

No Laban patent was identified. “Invention” in his case generally refers to conceptual systems, notation, pedagogy, and analytical methods.

### Property and wealth

Laban moved frequently and relied heavily on institutions, partners, hosts, patrons, and commissions. His companies did not produce lasting personal wealth. Accounts repeatedly describe debt, financial precariousness, homelessness or temporary lodging, and late-life dependence on supporters.

The Addlestone property belonged to the educational enterprise rather than representing evidence of a private landed fortune.

## Political, Religious, Philosophical, and Artistic Development

### Lebensreform and communal culture

Before 1914 Laban’s thought grew within Lebensreform: nature, bodily emancipation, alternative community, anti-industrial longing, craft, vegetarian and nudist cultures, and spiritual experimentation.

He imagined dance as an integral human activity rather than specialized entertainment. His often-quoted proposition that every person carries a dancer within them condensed this outlook.

### Esotericism

Laban used concepts of harmony, crystal forms, cosmic order, ritual, and spiritual transformation. Monte Verità placed him amid Theosophy, occultism, alternative Christianity, anarchism, and Eastern-religion appropriations.

Specific claims that Laban held formal membership in a particular occult order are not established by the sources consulted. His work is accurately described as esoterically inflected, but not securely assignable to a single creed.

### National and conservative elements

Recent scholarship has challenged the assumption that Laban’s pre-Nazi work was straightforwardly progressive. Organic community, hierarchy, anti-liberal cultural criticism, national regeneration, and suspicion of urban-industrial modernity appear in his 1919–26 writings.

This does not make all movement choirs proto-Nazi. It does help explain why portions of his vocabulary could be adapted to authoritarian nationalism.

### Social and pacifist themes

Some works in Laban’s wider circle addressed war, poverty, and collective suffering. Kurt Jooss’s *The Green Table* is the most famous anti-war work to emerge from this lineage, though it is Jooss’s creation and must not be credited to Laban as choreographer.

### Accommodation to National Socialism

From 1933 to 1936 Laban placed his organizational vision in the service of state-supported “German dance.” He participated in Gleichschaltung-era institutions and racialized exclusions. The regime then rejected his Olympic choreography and displaced him.

### British humanist and educational reframing

In Britain, Laban’s system was presented increasingly through creativity, personal development, humane work organization, education, and behavioral observation. This was both an evolution and a selective reconstruction. Nazi-era institutional activity was frequently minimized in postwar movement communities.

### Teachers and formative contacts

### Rivals and tensions

Laban’s relationships with former pupils were not always simple rivalries. Wigman and Jooss owed formative debts to him but produced more repertorially durable theatrical work. Professional competition, differing aesthetics, and Laban’s desire to remain the central authority strained some relationships.

No personal “enemy list” is documented. The decisive hostile political relationship was with Nazi cultural officials after 1936, but even that followed years of patronage.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### Collaboration with the Nazi regime

**Documented fact:** Laban cooperated with Nazi cultural administration from 1933 through 1936, held official responsibility, accepted propaganda-ministry support, organized national dance festivals, and participated in institutional racial conformity.

**Not established:** Nazi Party membership, involvement in political violence, intelligence work, criminal prosecution, or war crimes.

**Postwar distortion:** Accounts that present him simply as a refugee banned for anti-Nazi art omit the period of official collaboration. Accounts that present him as a uniformly committed Nazi ideologue can likewise flatten contradictions and the regime’s eventual rejection of his work.

### Dismissal of “non-Aryan” personnel

Historians report that Laban’s administration complied with racial dismissals. Further case-by-case research in personnel files is required to allocate responsibility among Laban, theatre management, and ministry officials. The institutional result—racial exclusion—is not in serious doubt.

### Olympic cancellation mythology

The cancellation is documented. The idea that the work was banned because Laban heroically refused censorship is less securely supported. Goebbels’s criticism concerned the work’s ideological and aesthetic incompatibility with the regime. It does not establish an intentional act of resistance by Laban.

### Industrial labor control

Laban and Lawrence presented Industrial Rhythm as humane efficiency: reducing fatigue and aligning work with natural movement. Historical analysis also identifies it as a technique of workplace surveillance, personnel selection, production coordination, and management control.

No lawsuit or finding of unlawful practice was located. The controversy concerns function and interpretation, not a documented criminal proceeding.

### Authorship and system ownership

Modern users sometimes credit all of Labanotation, Effort-Shape, Bartenieff Fundamentals, Movement Pattern Analysis, and dance therapy directly to Laban.

That is historically inaccurate:

- Laban originated major concepts.
- Knust standardized Kinetography.
- Ann Hutchinson Guest and others developed Labanotation.
- Bartenieff created her own synthesis.
- Warren Lamb developed Movement Pattern Analysis.
- Ullmann reorganized and transmitted the educational work.
- Dance-movement therapy emerged through several practitioners and institutions.

### Sexual and domestic life

The shared household with Maja Lederer and Suzanne Perrottet, Laban’s relationship with Dussia Bereska, and his repeated emotional attachments are supported by biographical and photographic evidence. Sensational descriptions of Monte Verità as a sexual cult exceed the available documentation.

No evidence was located of a criminal sexual accusation against Laban.

### Financial disorder

Reported debts, dependency on collaborators, and failed or short-lived institutions recur throughout his life. No evidence was found of fraud, embezzlement, money laundering, or corrupt private enrichment.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The Hungarian marquis”

**Story:** Laban was an exotic Hungarian marquis with an elaborate string of baptismal names.

**Circulation:** His own self-presentation, early publicity, and later popular biographies.

**Evidence:** His father’s ennoblement and the predicate *de Váralja* are supported. The rank “marquis” and the full theatrical name are not securely supported.

**Effect:** It gave Laban aristocratic and cosmopolitan charisma while also encouraging later claims that his entire noble identity was fraudulent.

### “A Balkan dervish initiation”

Some accounts romanticize his Bosnian childhood as direct initiation into Islamic or dervish movement traditions.

**Evidence:** He lived in Bosnia-Herzegovina and observed its multicultural life. No reliable record establishes formal Sufi initiation.

**Effect:** The story reinforced an image of Laban as a mediator between “East” and “West.”

### Monte Verità as utopian dance commune

**Story:** Laban created an emancipated, nude, egalitarian ritual community where modern dance was born.

**Evidence:** Outdoor practice, experimental dress, communal living, reform culture, and major dance experiments are documented.

**Against the simplified story:** His organization retained teacher authority, gendered labor, unstable finances, emotional conflict, and personal hierarchy.

**Effect:** Monte Verità became the foundational myth of liberated European modern dance.

### Laban as sole inventor of movement notation

**Story:** Laban invented the first dance notation.

**Evidence against:** Dance-notation systems existed for centuries. Laban created one of the twentieth century’s most influential general movement-writing systems.

**Complication:** Knust, Hutchinson Guest, and many others materially developed it.

**Effect:** The “sole inventor” story obscures both predecessors and collaborators.

### Laban as anti-Nazi resistance artist

**Story:** A humanist artist refused Nazi censorship, had his Olympic work banned, and immediately fled.

**Evidence:** The cancellation and later emigration are documented.

**Evidence against the heroic compression:** He had cooperated with the regime for several years, organized officially sponsored festivals, and adapted institutions to racial policy. No deliberate resistant intention behind the Olympic work has been demonstrated.

**Effect:** The narrative facilitated postwar rehabilitation and allowed institutions to inherit his methods without confronting their Nazi-era use.

### Laban as simply “Hitler’s choreographer”

**Story:** Laban was the choreographic engineer of Nazi mass spectacle and the 1936 Olympics.

**Evidence:** He received official patronage and prepared a thousand-person Olympic work.

**Qualification:** His work was cancelled and did not become the performed Olympic spectacle commonly imagined. He was one actor within a much larger state cultural apparatus.

**Effect:** The phrase corrected sanitized histories but can become misleading when it implies uninterrupted regime favor or authorship of all Olympic choreography.

### The universal geometric key to movement

Laban sometimes presented spatial scales, crystalline structures, and notation as approaching a universal grammar of human movement.

This was his theoretical aspiration, not a scientifically proven universal law. Subsequent practitioners have found the framework productive, revised it, or criticized its culturally specific assumptions.

### Retrospective psychiatric diagnosis

Institutional accounts describe “manic depression.” This is a retrospective label derived from cycles of energy and collapse. Without clinical records, it remains a reported interpretation rather than a secure diagnosis.

## Films, Documentaries, Interviews, and Archival Material

### Historical film appearances

**_Wege zu Kraft und Schönheit_** (“Ways to Strength and Beauty,” 1925), directed by Wilhelm Prager and Nicholas Kaufmann.

The film includes representatives of German body culture and modern dance. Dussia Bereska performs *Die Orchidee*; Mary Wigman and her group appear; and material from Laban’s *Das lebende Idol* includes Laban himself. The film is evidence of the overlap among dance reform, physical culture, health propaganda, and mass media.

### Documentary and television treatment

- Documentary and television programs on German modern dance and the Third Reich have frequently included Laban’s Deutsche Tanzbühne and the cancelled Olympic work. Titles vary by national broadcaster and release market; catalogue verification is advisable before citing a particular translated title.
- Reconstructed or pedagogical films made by Laban institutions document movement scales, Effort, movement choirs, and notation. These are demonstrations or reconstructions, not films of Laban’s early choreography.
- Surviving interviews and recorded lectures are limited. *Laban Speaks About Movement and Dance* is an edited textual collection rather than a verbatim audiovisual archive.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Institutional continuity

The Art of Movement Studio became the Laban Centre for Movement and Dance, moved to New Cross, London, and merged with Trinity College of Music in 2005 to form **Trinity Laban Conservatoire of Music and Dance**.

Other continuing institutions include:

- International Council of Kinetography Laban;
- Laban/Bartenieff Institute of Movement Studies;
- Dance Notation Bureau;
- Laban Guild and international successor groups;
- notation and movement-analysis programs in Europe, the Americas, and Asia.

### Dance notation

Kinetography/Labanotation became one of the principal systems for recording dance. It has been used to preserve and reconstruct choreography, including works for which film is absent or insufficient.

Its historical importance does not mean it is culturally neutral or capable of representing every movement practice without interpretation. Debates concern the relation between universal notation and culturally specific bodies, genres, intentions, and performance traditions.

### Dance and movement education

Laban’s British educational work shaped creative dance in schools and teacher education. Through Ullmann and other teachers, children were encouraged to explore movement qualities rather than merely copy fixed steps.

### Theatre and acting

Laban-based Effort analysis entered actor training through practitioners including Yat Malmgren. It influenced approaches to character, intention, tempo, weight, and physical action.

### Dance-movement therapy and somatics

Laban did not single-handedly found dance therapy. His concepts supplied tools later adapted by Bartenieff, Marian Chace, Judith Kestenberg, and other practitioners. Bartenieff Fundamentals is a later synthesis, not simply a Laban technique under another name.

### Management and behavioral analysis

Laban–Lawrence work led to personnel-selection and decision-making systems. Warren Lamb’s Movement Pattern Analysis developed this lineage in management consulting. These applications remain controversial because they move from descriptive observation toward claims about suitability, motivation, or decision-making.

### Choreographic reputation

Few Laban works survive as continuously performed repertory. His largest legacy lies in:

- processes of improvisation;
- group composition;
- movement vocabulary;
- notation;
- analysis;
- pedagogy;
- institutional structures.

Jooss, Wigman, and later choreographers produced more durable stage works, but within intellectual and pedagogical fields partly opened by Laban.

### Historical reinterpretation

Postwar commemorative histories often emphasized:

- universal creativity;
- exile from Nazism;
- educational humanism;
- notation and movement science.

From the late twentieth century, archival scholarship restored:

- his cooperation with Nazi institutions;
- racial exclusions;
- the political flexibility of movement choirs;
- conservative and nationalist elements in his earlier thought;
- collaborators’ contributions;
- the role of women in sustaining his schools and publications.

The resulting historical picture is neither the uncomplicated emancipator of institutional memory nor the caricature of an omnipotent Nazi mass choreographer.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are included. Mere presence in the same city or artistic generation is excluded.

### Mary Wigman — documented fact

Wigman studied with Laban and became his assistant in the Monte Verità/Zürich period, approximately 1913–19. She participated in his movement research and group performances before founding an independent school and becoming a principal figure in Ausdruckstanz. Their relationship was formative but not one of lifelong artistic subordination.

### Suzanne Perrottet — documented fact

Perrottet worked with Laban and Maja Lederer in Switzerland, taught in the Laban school, joined the Monte Verità circle, became Laban’s lover, and bore their son Allar/André Perrottet von Laban. She took over the Zürich institution after Laban’s departure.

### Katja Wulff — documented fact

Wulff participated in the Monte Verità and Swiss Laban circle as dancer, artist, and teacher. She helped develop and transmit the early movement practice.

### Sophie Taeuber-Arp — documented fact

Taeuber took modern expressive-dance courses with Laban and Wigman in Zürich and Ascona during the First World War. Dance, geometry, costume, and abstraction became continuing components of her work, although she developed an independent Dada and Constructivist career.

### Hilde Holger — documented fact

Holger trained within the Central European Laban movement and is consistently listed among his pupils and associates. She later developed an independent teaching and choreographic career in Vienna, India, and Britain.

### Hanya Holm — reported fact

Holm belonged to the German modern-dance lineage closely connected with Laban through Mary Wigman. Sources sometimes call her a Laban student, but her principal documented teacher and institutional affiliation was Wigman. The direct Laban connection is therefore less precisely established than Wigman’s.

### Isadora Duncan — reported fact and documented influence

Laban discussed Duncan’s contribution to dance reform in *Gymnastik und Tanz* and was aware of her performances. A scholarly source reports that he saw her dance in Zürich but was unimpressed. Their documented connection is one of acknowledged influence and observation, not collaboration.

### François Delsarte — documented intellectual influence

Laban credited Delsarte’s contribution to expressive movement theory and encountered Delsartean mime during his Paris formation. They could not have met: Delsarte died before Laban’s birth.

### Émile Jaques-Dalcroze — documented intellectual and institutional relationship; direct meeting unverified

Dalcroze’s rhythmic pedagogy preceded and influenced the environment from which Laban’s work emerged. Wigman studied at Hellerau before joining Laban. Laban’s insistence on movement independent of musical control differentiated his system from Dalcroze’s. No direct teacher-student bond is supported.

### Rudolf Steiner — reported intellectual parallel; direct relationship unverified

Laban and Steiner developed contemporaneous movement systems with spiritual and pedagogical dimensions. Scholarship places Steiner’s eurythmy within Laban’s wider intellectual environment, but the consulted sources do not establish a documented personal meeting or correspondence.

### Friedrich Nietzsche — documented intellectual influence

Nietzsche’s concepts of dance, transformation, Dionysian culture, and critique of modernity formed part of Laban’s intellectual environment. This is influence through texts and cultural reception, not personal contact.

### Wilhelm Richard Wagner — documented artistic influence

Laban’s ideal of synthetic theatre and several productions drew on Wagner. He staged the *Tannhäuser* Bacchanale and shared the period’s preoccupation with Gesamtkunstwerk. Wagner died before Laban’s mature career; there was no personal relationship.

### Johannes Itten — reported shared reform-art network; direct relationship not established here

Itten and Laban moved through overlapping Swiss and German reform-art environments, and both used geometry, bodily training, and spiritualized pedagogy. The sources consulted do not establish a specific meeting, correspondence, or collaboration; this connection is therefore not entered as a biographical crossing.

### Oskar Schlemmer — reported institutional crossing; precise personal evidence insufficient

Schlemmer and Laban occupied overlapping German modernist dance and theatre circuits, particularly around dance congresses and debates on body-space geometry. No specific relationship was sufficiently documented in the consulted material to classify it as a firm Atlas crossing.

### Anna Pavlova — documented critical/intellectual connection

Laban published an article on Pavlova in *Schrifttanz* in 1931. This establishes critical engagement, not a documented personal collaboration.

### Dore Hoyer — influence through the Ausdruckstanz lineage; direct connection unverified

Hoyer developed within German expressive dance after Laban’s foundational period. No direct meeting or teacher-student bond was established in the consulted sources.

### Martha Graham — indirect historical influence only

Laban’s methods affected American modern dance and later movement analysis, but no documented personal crossing with Graham was found.

### Merce Cunningham, John Cage, José Limón, Jennifer Muller, Sara Rudner, Viola Farber — no documented personal crossing found

These later choreographers worked in fields affected directly or indirectly by modern-dance analysis and notation. The sources consulted did not establish a meeting, correspondence, collaboration, or explicit personal influence from Laban sufficient for an Atlas entry.

### Absence finding

No supported connection was found between Laban and the overwhelming majority of the Atlas roster. Shared modernist, esoteric, political, geographic, or body-culture contexts were not treated as biographical connections without evidence of contact or explicitly documented influence.
