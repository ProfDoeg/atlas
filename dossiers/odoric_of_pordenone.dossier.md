# Odoric of Pordenone: Research Dossier

**Prepared:** 24 August 2026  
**Scope:** Reconstructable life, journey, text, disputed narratives, cult, and reception  
**Method note:** Medieval evidence is uneven. This dossier labels contemporary or near-contemporary evidence, later hagiography, modern reconstruction, and legend separately. Place-name identifications are given as probabilities rather than silently converted into modern certainties.

## Basic Identifying Information

| Field | Best-supported finding |
|---|---|
| Principal name | **Odoric of Pordenone**; Italian **Odorico da Pordenone** |
| Medieval forms | *Odoricus de Portu Naonis*, *Odericus de Foro Julii*, *Odoricus Boemus*; scribal spellings include Oderic, Oderigo, Odolricus, Ordericus and many corrupt forms |
| Geographical epithets | “of Pordenone,” “of Friuli,” “the Bohemian”; occasionally “of Udine” in later devotional literature |
| Religious status | Professed friar of the Order of Friars Minor (Franciscan); traditionally associated with the Udine convent and later usually claimed by the Conventual Franciscan tradition |
| Birth | Exact date unknown; probably **about 1280**, in or near **Pordenone**, traditionally Villanova di Pordenone, then in the Patriarchate of Aquileia/Holy Roman Empire |
| Older birth dates | 1265, 1274/75, 1286 and other dates occur. The 1265/Mattiussi reconstruction is rejected by Andrea Tilatti; examination of the remains in 2002 indicated a man of about fifty at death, supporting c. 1280. |
| Death | **14 January 1331**, Franciscan convent of San Francesco, Udine |
| Cause of death | Contemporary sources establish a final illness, not its diagnosis. Modern examination of the partly mummified remains attributed death to cardiac complications associated with respiratory insufficiency; this is retrospective forensic interpretation. |
| Canonical status | Cult confirmed *ab immemorabili* by Pope Benedict XIV, **2 July 1755**—commonly described as equipollent beatification. Not canonized as of the dossier date. Feast/memorial: 14 January. |
| Principal work | A dictated travel account conventionally called the **Relatio**, **Itinerarium**, *Descriptio orientalium partium*, or *Itinerarium de mirabilibus orientalium Tartarorum* (Padua, May 1330). No authorial autograph survives. |

The modern documentary baseline is Andrea Tilatti’s 2013 *Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani* entry, which integrates the notarized Friulian record, the 2002 examination, textual research, and the cult dossier. Older biographies often repeat incompatible genealogies and dates without distinguishing their evidentiary status ([Tilatti, Treccani](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/odorico-da-pordenone_(Dizionario-Biografico)/)).

## Family and Ancestry

No securely documented parent, sibling, spouse, partner, or child is known. As a professed friar, Odoric would have lived under vows including chastity and individual poverty. There is no reliable evidence of marriage or descendants.

### Competing ancestry traditions

1. **Bohemian/garrison tradition — near-contemporary, unconfirmed.** John of Viktring wrote that Odoric came from one of the families left to garrison Pordenone by King Ottokar II of Bohemia in the early 1270s. This may explain *Boemus* (“the Bohemian”) in the textual tradition. It does not identify a father or prove ethnic Czech descent.
2. **Mattiussi/Mattiuzzi family tradition — late and rejected by recent scholarship.** Early-modern local writers attached Odoric to the Mattiussi family, showed visitors a supposed birth house, room, and bed at Villanova, and supplied a Czech soldier as father. Henry Yule recorded the living local tradition but not contemporary proof. Tilatti concludes that Odoric did **not** belong to the Mattiussi-Mattiuzzi family.
3. **Local Friulian origin — secure at the broad level.** His own geographical names, the legal documents, and his institutional network securely place him in Friuli. “Bohemian” and “Friulian” are therefore not necessarily mutually exclusive: a descendant of an Ottokar-era garrison family could have been locally born. The descent itself remains unproved.

No evidence supports later precision about the name or career of his father. The claimed family home is a site of memory, not authenticated real property belonging to Odoric.

## Childhood and Early Life

Odoric’s childhood is almost blank in contemporary evidence. Fourteenth-century hagiographers say he entered the Friars Minor very young. A legal act made at Gemona del Friuli on **9 February 1296** includes an *Odolricus puer fratrum Minorum* among five friar-witnesses. Tilatti regards identification with the traveler as attractive but not certain. If correct, Odoric was already attached to a Franciscan house while still described as *puer*, compatible with a birth around 1280.

Later saint-lives impose a conventional ascetic template: bread and water, bare feet, scourging, a hair shirt or iron mail, refusal of promotion, solitary retreat, preaching, and miracles before departure. These claims appear in Franciscan hagiographic transmission rather than the surviving contemporary legal record or Odoric’s own travel narrative. They document how his sanctity was constructed, not independently verified childhood facts.

## Education and Formation

No school, degree, named teacher, or curriculum is documented. His formation was Franciscan and practical:

- liturgy, scripture, confession, preaching, mendicant discipline, and obedience within the Order;
- enough spoken and written Latin to participate in legal and administrative business, though his narrative was written down by another friar;
- experience of travel and urban Franciscan networks before Asia;
- likely exposure to the missionary culture created by John of Plano Carpini, William of Rubruck, John of Montecorvino, and the papal missions to the Mongol world.

The Council of Vienne (1311–12) ordered schools of Arabic, Hebrew, and “Chaldean” at major centers for eastern mission. Tilatti places Odoric in this institutional climate, but there is **no evidence** that he attended one of these language schools. Likewise, no evidence establishes competence in Persian, Arabic, Mongolian, or Chinese; interpreters and multilingual Franciscan houses may have mediated communication.

## Early Career and Documentary Record Before Asia

Odoric appears, or probably appears, in four Friulian legal settings:

- **1296, Gemona:** possible identification as *Odolricus puer fratrum Minorum*.
- **1316, Cividale; 1317, Castello di Porpetto; 1318, Portogruaro:** three notarized acts name him while alive, as witness or participant. Their parties and contexts show access to high-status institutional networks and possibly contact with Avignon.

These records undermine two common portraits: the completely undocumented peasant recluse and the persecuted Spiritual Franciscan fleeing the Order. Tilatti instead sees a friar integrated into provincial structures whose eastern travel probably had institutional authorization. The exact commissioning document, however, has not survived.

Odoric held no securely documented episcopal, diplomatic, political, or commercial office. His observable institution was the Order of Friars Minor, especially its Province of St Anthony and houses linking Friuli, Padua, the Black Sea, Persia, India, and Yuan China.

## Chronological Life History

### c. 1280–1318: Friuli and Franciscan integration

Born at Pordenone or traditionally Villanova, Odoric entered the Franciscan world young. By 1316–18 he moved among Cividale, Porpetto, and Portogruaro. These appearances suggest mobility, trust, and participation in legal affairs, but do not reveal his daily duties.

### After 1318: departure from Venice

The strongest modern reconstruction places departure **after 1318**, probably from Venice. Older chronologies say 1316, 1317, or 1318. Odoric’s account provides travel durations but almost no absolute dates. A 1317 arrival at Trebizond, repeated from d’Avezac, lacks traceable authority; it should not be presented as certain.

### Black Sea, Anatolia, and Ilkhanid Persia

The outward route is reconstructed as:

**Venice → Constantinople → Trebizond → Erzurum/Arziron → Tabriz/Tauris → Soltaniyeh/Soldania → Kashan → Yazd → Persepolis region → probably Shiraz and perhaps Kurdistan → Baghdad → lower Mesopotamia/Persian Gulf → Hormuz.**

The narrative notices Trebizond’s trade, Armenia and the legendary mountain of Noah’s Ark, Tabriz’s wealth, Soltaniyeh, Persian cities, and ruins identified with ancient Persepolis. Yule suggested that otherwise unexplained years before 1322 may have been spent in Franciscan houses at Trebizond, Tabriz, or Soltaniyeh. That is reasonable institutional inference, not direct testimony.

At Hormuz he entered the Indian Ocean shipping system. The journey was neither a private European expedition with dedicated vessels nor a commercial company: Odoric used existing caravan roads, ports, ships, mendicant houses, and Mongol imperial communications.

### After April 1321: western India and the Thane martyrs

He reached **Thane/Tana**, near present Mumbai, after the killing of four Franciscans there in April 1321. This supplies the journey’s firm *terminus post quem*. The executed friars were Thomas of Tolentino, James of Padua, Peter of Siena, and Demetrius of Tiflis. Odoric recovered the bones of Thomas, James, and Demetrius—not Peter’s body—and carried them east.

His Relatio gives a long martyrdom narrative involving a Muslim judge, theological confrontation, fire, and execution. The deaths themselves and movement of relics belong to the historical Franciscan mission record; dramatic speeches and ordeal details require the caution applied to martyr narratives. Bishop Andrew of Perugia’s **1326 letter** confirms reception of the relics at Quanzhou, anchoring Odoric’s presence in coastal China by that date ([Tilatti](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/odorico-da-pordenone_(Dizionario-Biografico)/); [Yule–Cordier edition](https://archive.org/stream/no33works02hakluoft/no33works02hakluoft_djvu.txt)).

He traveled down the western coast through places commonly identified as Pandarani, Cranganore/Kodungallur, and Quilon/Kollam, then visited the shrine of St Thomas at Mylapore near Chennai. His ethnographic notices include pepper, coconuts, religious specialists, cattle veneration, funerary customs, and widow-burning. They mix direct observation, interpreter-mediated explanation, conventional Christian categories (“idolaters”), and inherited marvel lore.

### Indian Ocean and Southeast Asia: route problems

After India, manuscript order and geography become unstable. Places conventionally associated with his account include Ceylon/Sri Lanka, the Andaman and Nicobar groups, Sumatra, Java, perhaps Borneo, Champa in present Vietnam, and possibly other island landfalls. The narrative’s order does not create a navigationally coherent itinerary in every recension.

Important reports include:

- **Sumatra/Lamori:** cannibalism, shared wives, trade, and abundance; Odoric is among the earliest western writers after Marco Polo to use a recognizable Sumatra name.
- **Java:** a powerful, wealthy king, spices, and a lavish palace.
- **Thalamasyn/Panten:** sago production, blowpipes, poison, large bamboo, piracy, and a “dead sea.” The sago description contains mistakes but also details long treated as evidence of direct observation.
- **Champa/Zampa:** elephants, royal polygyny, fishing abundance, tortoises, and widow-burning.
- **Ceylon/Sillan:** Adam’s Peak tradition, gems, leeches, and extraordinary fauna.
- **Dondin:** ritual killing/eating of the sick after divination. Its location is unresolved; efforts to make it Luzon are not textually secure.

The possibility that Odoric touched the Philippines cannot be absolutely excluded from an opaque island itinerary, but his Relatio names neither Bolinao nor Luzon and does not report a Mass or baptisms there.

### The “cynocephali witness”: what the text actually says

In the chapter conventionally headed “Of the island of Nicoveran, where the men have dogs’ faces,” the first-person narrative says he found an island called Nicoveran whose men and women had faces “like great dogs.” It adds nakedness, an ox-image worn on the forehead, worship of that figure, a huge royal ruby, body-sized shields, ransom or consumption of prisoners, and the statement that the people were just toward one another ([chapter and variants in Yule–Cordier](https://archive.org/stream/no33works02hakluoft/no33works02hakluoft_djvu.txt)).

Evidence assessment:

- **Documented textual fact:** dog-faced people occur in the medieval Odoric tradition; a Latin heading explicitly reads *ubi Cynocephali* in one version.
- **First-person framing:** the chapter is grammatically embedded in an eyewitness itinerary.
- **Not established:** that Odoric saw humans with canine heads, or even that every element derives from his oral dictation. The autograph is lost; the text survives in divergent redactions.
- **Composite character:** Yule and Cordier observed that the chapter is an “anomalous jumble”: the name resembles Marco Polo’s Nicobar; dog-heads were conventionally located in the Andamans; the forehead ox resembles a Maabar motif; the ruby belongs to Ceylon traditions; large shields and cannibalism match other parts of the archipelago; the final praise of intra-group justice resembles Ctesias’s ancient cynocephali.
- **Best historical reading:** Odoric’s transmitted narrative is a genuine medieval cynocephali witness-text, but “Odoric personally saw literal dog-headed humans” is an unverified marvel claim. It may combine sailor report, ethnographic misunderstanding, conventional monster geography, recollection, and scribal/redactional accretion.

### Yuan China

The itinerary becomes more linear at **Guangzhou/Canton** (*Censcalan/Chin-Kalan*), continuing to **Quanzhou/Zaiton**, **Fuzhou**, **Hangzhou/Cansay**, **Nanjing**, **Yangzhou**, and by canal toward **Khanbaliq/Dadu (Beijing)**. Identifications of minor stops remain disputed.

Odoric’s China material is his strongest claim to eyewitness value. It includes:

- huge cities, bridges, internal waterways, salt revenue, markets, shipping, paper currency, and state post stations (*yam*);
- fishing with trained cormorants and another form of diving-based fishing;
- elite long fingernails and women’s compressed feet;
- Hangzhou’s size and food supply;
- Quanzhou’s Franciscan house and the martyrs’ relics;
- the palace at Khanbaliq, artificial hill and lake, jade vessel, court hierarchy, banquets, imperial travel and hunts;
- friars singing and raising a cross when the Great Khan passed, followed by imperial reverence toward the cross.

He says he remained in Khanbaliq **three years**. This likely falls somewhere between c. 1322/25 and 1328, but exact dates are not recoverable. John of Montecorvino, archbishop of Khanbaliq, was still alive until 1328. Odoric probably encountered him, yet never names the meeting. No evidence proves a formal working relationship; claims that he spent three fruitful years converting the population are later inference.

The narrative’s independent details—especially cormorant fishing, foot binding, nails, and specific urban practices absent from Marco Polo—are major reasons historians accept that its core author reached China. Doubts have existed, but the 1326 Quanzhou relic evidence and the texture of the China descriptions weigh strongly for presence.

### Return: Prester John, Tibet, and the missing route

Odoric’s return narrative moves through the land of Prester John, Kansan (often Shaanxi), and a place called Tibet, with felt tents, women’s appearance, a religious superior, and funerary dismemberment/skull vessels. Older reference works confidently made him the first European visitor to Lhasa. Modern scholarship is more cautious:

- *Tibet* could designate a broad Tibetan cultural region rather than Lhasa.
- the text does not name Lhasa securely;
- the route after northern China is compressed and sometimes arranged topically rather than chronologically;
- some information may be reported rather than seen.

“Odoric visited Tibetan territory” is plausible; “Odoric entered Lhasa” is disputed and unproved. The rest of the route—often reconstructed through Kashmir or Afghanistan, Khorasan, south of the Caspian, Tabriz, Trebizond, and Venice—is conjectural.

### 1329/30: return to Italy and dictation

Odoric probably reached Italy in late 1329 or early 1330. Later biographers say hardship and starvation changed him so greatly that relatives did not recognize him; this is hagiographic tradition rather than contemporary medical evidence.

In **May 1330**, at the Franciscan house of St Anthony in **Padua**, he dictated his report to **Friar William of Solagna**. The colophon says William wrote it simply as Odoric spoke, without ornate Latin. Another attestation states that Odoric saw the contents himself or heard them from trustworthy people, and omitted still more because Europeans would not believe them. The provincial minister’s order to report suggests an official institutional document, possibly intended for Avignon.

Later tradition sends him from Padua to Pisa to sail for Avignon and request missionary reinforcements. Illness prevented the journey; a dream of St Francis ordered him back to his “nest.” The proposed Avignon journey and dream are not contemporary documented facts.

### Final illness and death

Odoric returned to Udine and died in the Franciscan convent on **Monday, 14 January 1331**. The date is supported by narrative postscripts and the subsequent May inquiry. Later deathbed words assert assurance of divine pardon while submitting to priestly absolution; these belong to saint-life tradition.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Institutions

- **Order of Friars Minor:** lifelong institutional identity.
- **Province of St Anthony / Franciscan houses:** Udine, Padua, and the Eurasian mission network.
- **Franciscan archbishopric and convents in Yuan China:** context for his Quanzhou and Beijing residence; exact post not known.
- **Papacy at Avignon:** probable intended recipient of the Relatio; direct commission and planned audience remain unproved.
- **Municipality of Udine and Patriarchate of Aquileia:** principal posthumous patrons of his shrine and cult.

### Finance and property

No personal estate, business, investment, patent, or financial holding is documented. The vow of poverty makes individual commercial ownership unlikely. Travel necessarily depended on institutional hospitality, alms, commercial transport, and Mongol infrastructure, but no accounts survive.

Two precise posthumous financial facts survive: Udine paid **two marks** on 5 April 1331 to Friar James of Ireland, identified as Odoric’s companion; and the municipality financed the miracle process and the 1332 shrine. These are institutional/civic expenditures, not Odoric’s wealth.

### Technologies and systems observed

Odoric invented none known. His importance lies in reporting technologies and systems: paper money, imperial relay posts, the Grand Canal, cormorant fishing, sago extraction, blowpipes, large ocean-going ships, salt revenue, court automata or marvels, and urban supply networks.

## Political / Religious / Intellectual Development

Odoric was a Latin Christian mendicant operating through Mongol-ruled Eurasia. His categories are missionary: Christians, “Saracens,” “idolaters,” schismatics, martyrs, relics, and marvels. Yet the Relatio is unusually light on theology and sustained conversion narrative. It behaves as an administrative travel report and marvel-book as much as a missionary memoir.

He lived during the Franciscan poverty crisis and conflict among Pope John XXII, Minister General Michael of Cesena, Emperor Louis IV, and antipope Nicholas V (Peter of Corbara). No evidence makes Odoric a Spiritual dissident or partisan. Later reports that news of the schism distressed him at Pisa are hagiographic.

His intellectual method is observational but not modern ethnography. He compares foreign cities to Italian ones, records production and custom, repeats interpreters’ explanations, and incorporates inherited marvels. The final text also reflects William of Solagna and later redactors. “Eyewitness” and “literary tradition” therefore coexist sentence by sentence.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

No evidence connects Odoric personally to crime, corruption, fraud, lawsuits, imprisonment, intelligence activity, sexual abuse, financial misconduct, or political violence. Relevant controversies concern truth, authorship, missionary claims, and later appropriations.

### Reliability and authorship

The Relatio is dictated, multiply redacted, and transmitted in more than a hundred manuscripts. It contains demonstrably valuable observation alongside legendary beings, impossible quantities, composite geography, and second-hand material. Odoric’s own attestation explicitly allows both sight and trustworthy hearsay. It is therefore inaccurate either to dismiss the entire journey as fraud or to treat every first-person clause as verified observation.

### Claimed 20,000 baptisms

Later Franciscan writers credit him with baptizing more than 20,000 “Saracens.” The Relatio contains no such claim and little direct missionary activity. Yule judged it imaginative hagiographic expansion. Status: **late claim, unsupported**.

### Lhasa claim

Often repeated as fact in older encyclopedias. Status: **disputed reconstruction**. Tibetan-region travel is possible; entry into Lhasa is not established.

### Philippine/Bolinao Mass claim

A modern local tradition says Odoric landed at Bolinao during a storm and celebrated a thanksgiving or Christmas Mass in 1324, baptized inhabitants, and thus preceded the 1521 Magellan expedition. A marker and popular retellings circulated the story. Odoric’s account contains none of these details; Dondin/Thalamasyn cannot securely be equated with Luzon; William Henry Scott’s source criticism rejected the story. Status: **modern local legend without primary support**, not a documented first Mass ([discussion of the manufacture and Scott’s critique](https://bobcouttie.wordpress.com/2021/06/04/the-manufactured-bolinao-massof-odoric-of-pordenone/); [popular account of the legend](https://lifestyle.inquirer.net/91214/first-christmas-in-the-philippines-fr-odorico-ayuvallos-20201224/)).

### China skepticism

Some scholarship has asked whether Odoric reached China. Against wholesale skepticism stand the independently anchored relic transmission and observations not derived from Marco Polo. The prudent conclusion is that a real China journey underlies the work, while not every reported marvel or route segment is eyewitness.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### Ascetic wonder-worker before Asia

**Story:** youthful austerities, iron mail, solitary retreat, miracles, refusal of office.  
**Appearance/circulation:** fourteenth-century and later Franciscan hagiography, amplified in early-modern lives.  
**Evidence:** no corroborating contemporary personal record; compatible with saint-life conventions.  
**Effect:** prepared Odoric’s identity as holy missionary rather than merely traveler.

### The Great Khan’s commission

**Story:** the Khan sent Odoric west to recruit more friars.  
**Appearance:** later ecclesiastical biographies; absent as a firm commission in the Relatio.  
**Evidence:** the Chinese church needed personnel, but no Yuan or papal mandate survives.  
**Effect:** converted an uncertain return motive into an apostolic mission.

### Devil on the road and St Francis’s dream

**Story:** a demonic figure—specified in one telling as a pregnant woman—mocked his mission; later St Francis ordered the sick friar to return home.  
**Appearance:** Wadding, Mark of Lisbon, and later hagiography.  
**Evidence:** devotional narrative only.  
**Effect:** made his failed Avignon journey providential and foreshadowed death.

### Cynocephali

**Story:** on Nicoveran, Odoric encountered people with dog faces.  
**Appearance:** the medieval Relatio family itself, then translations, illuminations, Mandevillean reception, and modern monster lore.  
**Evidence:** secure as a textual report; literal beings unverified; chapter assembled from several geographical and literary motifs.  
**Effect:** made Odoric a central node in the long cynocephali tradition and helped the Relatio’s “book of marvels” afterlife.

### Miraculous corpse and posthumous healings

**Story:** a woman’s contracted arm healed by touching the corpse; scissors could not cut off his ear; sick petitioners recovered after vows; a six-day-dead friar was raised; “powder of Blessed Odoric” cured fevers.  
**Appearance:** immediate local miracle collections, the May 1331 sworn inquiry, later oral traditions, inscriptions, Franciscan histories, and the 1755 process.  
**Evidence:** the inquiry and testimony are historical documents; supernatural causation is a devotional claim. The report’s heading advertised seventy miracles but the surviving form enumerated twenty-seven. Resurrection and scissors stories are outside or later than the authenticated core.  
**Effect:** generated a civic cult, shrine, bequests, relic traffic, beatification, and later canonization campaigns.

### Unrecognized by his family

**Story:** hardship left him so wasted that close relatives did not recognize him.  
**Appearance:** later Franciscan biographies.  
**Evidence:** no contemporary confirmation.  
**Effect:** dramatized bodily sacrifice and reinforced the ascetic missionary image.

### Apostle of China

**Story/title:** Odoric as *Apostolo dei Cinesi*.  
**Appearance:** especially twentieth-century Catholic promotion.  
**Evidence:** he spent three years in Khanbaliq, but documented conversions are minimal and the 20,000 figure is unsupported.  
**Effect:** reoriented an older local miracle cult toward global mission and supplied momentum for canonization efforts.

## Works by the Person

### Genuine core work, dictated

1. **Relatio / Itinerarium / Descriptio orientalium partium / Itinerarium de mirabilibus orientalium Tartarorum** — dictated in Padua to William of Solagna, May 1330. The title is conventional and varies by manuscript. The lost archetype survives through numerous recensions rather than one authorially fixed book.

Major textual forms include the recension of William, the Marchesino recension, the recension of Henry of Glatz (copied from an Avignon exemplar in 1331 and revised), the Guecelli/Udine cult version, Germanic and abridged recensions, and Ramusian forms. Modern scholarship counts roughly eighty surviving Latin witnesses and numerous vernacular copies; wider counts exceed 117 or 130 depending on definitions. Annalia Marchisio’s study reconstructs a complex lost archetype and warns against treating one late printed text as “the original” ([Marchisio, “Many Versions, One Edition”](https://www.jstor.org/stable/48501924)).

### Principal medieval translations/adaptations

- early Italian vernacular versions, including the fourteenth-century *Libro delle nuove e strane e meravigliose cose*;
- Jean de Vignay, *Merveilles de la terre d’outremer*, fourteenth century;
- Jean le Long of Ypres, French translation, **1351**;
- German translations, notably Konrad Steckel’s;
- Catalan-Castilian and Welsh versions;
- excerpts absorbed into *Mandeville’s Travels*.

British Library Royal MS 19 D I preserves Jean de Vignay’s French version ([British Library catalogue](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/040-002107624)). BnF Français 2810, produced c. 1410–12, places Jean le Long’s Odoric beside Marco Polo, Mandeville, Hayton, Riccoldo, and diplomatic texts; Odoric occupies fols. 97r–115v ([Gallica digitization](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/btv1b52000858n/f1.planchecontact)).

### Early and major printed editions

- **1513, Pesaro:** *Odorichus de rebus incognitis* / early printed Italian tradition; commonly identified as the first print.
- **1574/1583:** Giovanni Battista Ramusio, *Navigationi et viaggi*, two differing Italian versions.
- **1599:** Richard Hakluyt, English *Iournall of Frier Odoricus*.
- **1643:** Bollandist text and cult materials in *Acta Sanctorum*, 14 January.
- **1881:** Teofilo Domenichelli, *Sopra la vita e i viaggi del beato Odorico da Pordenone*, with documents, Latin and Italian texts.
- **1891:** Henri Cordier, *Les voyages en Asie au XIVe siècle du bienheureux frère Odoric de Pordenone*.
- **1913:** Henry Yule, revised by Henri Cordier, *Cathay and the Way Thither*, vol. II, English translation, extensive notes, texts, manuscript census, cult documents, and bibliography ([full text](https://archive.org/stream/no33works02hakluoft/no33works02hakluoft_djvu.txt)).
- **1929:** Anastasius van den Wyngaert, ed., *Sinica Franciscana*, vol. I, *Itinera et relationes fratrum Minorum saeculi XIII et XIV*, pp. 381–495; long the standard Latin edition.
- **1990:** D. A. Trotter, ed., Jean de Vignay, *Les merveilles de la terre d’Outremer*.
- **2000:** Alvise Andreose, ed., *Libro delle nuove e strane e meravigliose cose*.
- **2001/2002:** *The Travels of Friar Odoric*, Yule translation with Paolo Chiesa introduction.
- **2010:** Alvise Andreose and Philippe Ménard, eds., *Le voyage en Asie d’Odoric de Pordenone traduit par Jean le Long OSB*.

### Works falsely or doubtfully attributed

- **Sermons and letters:** later Franciscan bibliographers say he left them; none is securely known.
- **Universal chronicle to the death of John XXII:** impossible as stated because the pope outlived Odoric; attribution rejected, sometimes associated with John of Udine/Mortiliano.
- **De Terra Sancta:** a fifteenth-century Berlin manuscript attributes a Palestine itinerary to Odoric in a colophon, but authorship is doubtful.
- **Chronica compendiosa:** sometimes attached to him in reference literature; not accepted as his securely authored work.

## Books and Major Works About the Person

### Primary-source collections and older biographies

- Bartholomew of Pisa, *De conformitate vitae beati Francisci ad vitam Domini Iesu* (1385–90), early hagiographic notice.
- *Chronica XXIV generalium Ordinis Minorum* (1369–73), attributed to Arnold of Sarrant.
- Luke Wadding, *Annales Minorum*, relevant entries under 1321/1331.

## Death, Burial, Relics, and Cult

The friars reportedly intended a quiet same-day burial. Udine’s gastald Conrad Bernardiggi intervened; crowds touched and cut at the habit and hair, and claims of healing began. Burial was delayed, then reversed as veneration expanded. Pagano della Torre and Beatrice of Bavaria participated in the public solemnities according to the cult record.

In May 1331 the patriarch appointed Canon Melioranzi, Maffeo Cassini, and notary Guecelli/Guccelli to receive sworn miracle testimony. This imitates a canonization process, but no completed papal proceeding from 1331 survives. A stone sarcophagus by Philip de Sanctis was completed in 1332 with civic funds, and the body was elevated in an episcopal “canonization.”

Later movements:

- **1332:** first solemn elevation into the new sarcophagus.
- **1735:** transfer to a new chapel during renovation.
- **1749–55:** formal Roman inquiry into immemorial cult; Benedict XIV confirmed it on 2 July 1755.
- **1769–71:** Venetian suppressions displaced the Franciscan community; relics were moved with civic and ecclesiastical ceremony to the former Carmelite church, now Santa Maria del Carmine.
- **2002:** medical examination of the partly mummified remains; diocesan canonization phase formally opened.
- **2006:** diocesan phase closed positively and documentation sent to Rome; no final canonization followed by the dossier date.

The cult was civic as well as Franciscan: bequests appear from the year of death, the city financed the tomb and inquiry, the chapel carried narrative frescoes, and powdered relic matter was sold or distributed as a fever remedy as far as Florence in the eighteenth century. The documented existence of these practices does not verify miraculous efficacy.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

1. **Travel knowledge:** Odoric supplemented Marco Polo with distinct observations of maritime Southeast Asia and Yuan China.
2. **Textual afterlife:** more than a hundred manuscripts and many languages made the Relatio a late-medieval bestseller, though often anonymously or under shifting titles.
3. **Mandeville:** large portions were appropriated by the author/compiler of *Mandeville’s Travels*. Odoric’s observations thereby reached a readership much larger than his name did.
4. **Cartography:** details from Odoric entered the informational world of the Catalan Atlas of 1375; modern cartographic scholarship treats his Relatio alongside Polo and other travel sources ([ICA study](https://ica-proc.copernicus.org/articles/1/69/2018/)).
5. **Marvel tradition:** cynocephali, cannibals, pygmies, the vegetable lamb, Prester John, and court marvels preserved the porous border between report and inherited geography.
6. **Franciscan mission:** later centuries recast him as the “Apostle of the Chinese,” despite the thin record of his own conversions.
7. **Local identity:** Pordenone and Udine memorialized him through shrines, art, monuments, institutes, anniversaries, and canonization campaigns.
8. **Modern philology:** attention shifted from proving every route point to reconstructing a fluid, multi-redaction textual tradition.
9. **Astronomical commemoration:** main-belt asteroid **4637 Odorico**, discovered in 1989, bears his name; the naming is commemorative, not an award received in life ([Minor Planet Center authority](https://www.minorplanetcenter.net/)).

## Research Limitations

- No autograph diary, letters, family archive, school record, commission, expense account, or Yuan administrative record naming Odoric is known.
- The Relatio’s textual plurality prevents mechanically equating a passage in one recension with Odoric’s exact spoken words.
- Absolute dates between 1318 and 1330 are sparse; many popular year-by-year routes create false precision.
- Modern place identifications, especially Southeast Asian islands and the return through Tibet, remain hypotheses.
- The miracle collections are evidence for testimony, practice, and reputation, not laboratory proof of supernatural causation.
- No evidence was found for personal business structures, romantic relationships, descendants, imprisonment, criminal proceedings, or political office.

## Atlas Connections

### Adam

- **[T1-] Documented textual fact:** In the *Relatio*, Odoric recounts the Ceylon/Sri Lanka tradition that Adam mourned Abel for five hundred years on a mountain and that the tears of Adam and Eve formed a fountain. Odoric questions the fountain explanation while still transmitting the Adam narrative ([Yule–Cordier edition](https://archive.org/details/cathaywaythither02yule); [historical survey of Adam’s Peak](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/d2/Adam%27s_Peak_-_legendary%2C_traditional%2C_and_historic_notices_of_the_Samanala_and_Sr%C3%AD-P%C3%A1da_-_with_a_descriptive_account_of_the_pilgrims%27_route_from_Colombo_to_the_Sacred_Foot-Print_%28IA_cu31924012567057%29.pdf)).

### Noah

- **[T1-] Documented textual fact:** While crossing Armenia or the adjoining highlands, Odoric writes about the mountain on which Noah’s Ark was said to rest. He wished to ascend it, but local people reportedly told him that nobody could reach the summit. The passage documents Odoric’s transmission of a Noah/Ark tradition, not the historical presence of the Ark ([Yule–Cordier edition](https://archive.org/details/cathaywaythither02yule)).

### Saint Anthony of Padua

- **[T9-] Documented fact:** In May 1330 Odoric dictated the *Relatio* to William of Solagna at the Franciscan “place of Saint Anthony” in Padua. This belonged to the religious complex that developed from Anthony’s dwelling and the adjacent convent founded around 1229; Anthony’s tomb and the basilica erected over it made the site his principal shrine. Anthony died in 1231, approximately a century before Odoric used the complex, so this is a successive shared-site connection rather than a meeting ([Odoric’s colophon and site history](https://www.odorichus.it/index.php/beato-odorico/viaggio); [Basilica friary history](https://basilicadelsanto.it/en/ba-category-archive/friary-and-novitiate-cloister); [Comune di Padova](https://www.comune.padova.it/luogo/basilica-di-santantonio)).
