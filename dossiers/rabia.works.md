## Works by Rabia al-Adawiyya

### Oral teachings and pedagogical project

- **Eighth century; transmitted from the ninth century onward:** *Masāʾil (ethical and spiritual teachings)*. A body of brief oral lessons, maxims, answers, and admonitions rather than a written book; early witnesses characterize Rābiʿa as an authoritative teacher whose sayings addressed conduct, renunciation, sincerity, devotion, and knowledge of God. The surviving corpus was selected and reshaped by later transmitters, so neither its original extent nor the wording and authorship of every item can be recovered ([Cornell, pp. 51–52, 65–66](https://research.vu.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/42127650/complete%20dissertation.pdf)).

- **Eighth century; no fixed title:** *The way of taʾdīb*. A pedagogical project of moral and spiritual formation enacted through teaching rather than composed as a text: Rkia Elaroui Cornell describes its component *masāʾil* as exercises or stages cultivating religious and social virtues, upright conduct, and wisdom. It resembles a protocol, but resists being called a discrete work because no authorial manual survives and its unity is reconstructed from reports ([Cornell, pp. 65–66](https://research.vu.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/42127650/complete%20dissertation.pdf)).

### Supplications and devotional utterances

- **Eighth century; transmitted posthumously:** *Supplications, prayers, and intimate addresses to God*. These are short oral manifestations of devotional practice, not a securely delimited authorial collection; they survive embedded in biographical and Sufi narratives, particularly al-Sulamī’s *Dhikr al-niswa al-mutaʿabbidāt al-ṣūfiyyāt* and later compilations. Individual attribution and wording remain uncertain because there is no autograph, contemporary recension, or collection made by Rābiʿa herself ([al-Sulamī, *Early Sufi Women*, discussed by Cornell, pp. 17, 52](https://research.vu.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/42127650/complete%20dissertation.pdf); [Arabic text of al-Sulamī’s *Dhikr al-niswa*](https://fomlar.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/%D8%B0%D9%83%D8%B1-%D8%A7%D9%84%D9%86%D8%B3%D9%88%D8%A9-%D8%A7%D9%84%D9%85%D8%AA%D8%B9%D8%A8%D8%AF%D8%A7%D8%AA-%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%B5%D9%88%D9%81%D9%8A%D8%A7%D8%AA.pdf)).

### Poetry attributed to her

- **Eighth century?; first securely attested attribution in the late tenth century:** *Poem of the Two Loves*. A short lyric on two modes of loving God and the strongest—though still unprovable—poetic attribution to Rābiʿa: Abū Ṭālib al-Makkī names four plausible near-contemporary Basran transmitters, but the slightly earlier al-Kalābādhī gives a variant to an unnamed Sufi. No extant source before al-Makkī names Rābiʿa as its maker, its text varies substantially, and Cornell concludes that its actual composer cannot now be determined ([Cornell, pp. 146–54](https://research.vu.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/42127650/complete%20dissertation.pdf)).

- **Date unknown; attribution appears in medieval transmission:** *Poem of Love* (one of three state-poems reported in al-Kharkūshī’s *Tahdhīb al-asrār*). A lyric assigned to an unspecified “Rābiʿa” and associated with the spiritual state of love; the transmitter Aḥmad ibn Abī al-Ḥawārī’s connection to Rābiʿa bint Ismāʿīl of Damascus makes attribution to Rābiʿa al-ʿAdawiyya unsafe. It survives as attributed speech, not as part of an authorially constituted collection ([Cornell, pp. 155–57](https://research.vu.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/42127650/complete%20dissertation.pdf)).

- **Date unknown; attribution appears in medieval transmission:** *Poem of Fear* (one of three state-poems reported in al-Kharkūshī’s *Tahdhīb al-asrār*). A lyric associated with the spiritual state of fear and likewise assigned only to an unspecified “Rābiʿa”; confusion between the Basran Rābiʿa and Rābiʿa bint Ismāʿīl prevents secure inclusion in either woman’s oeuvre. Its resistance to classification is fundamental: it is a transmitted attribution, not a demonstrably authored work ([Cornell, pp. 155–57](https://research.vu.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/42127650/complete%20dissertation.pdf)).

- **Date unknown; extant by the early eleventh century:** *Poem of the Intimate Gift*. A short lyric associated with the spiritual state of intimacy. Modern collections often assign it to Rābiʿa al-ʿAdawiyya, but Ibn al-Jawzī attributes it to the married Syrian ascetic Rābiʿa bint Ismāʿīl, and Cornell judges that woman’s biography and the early chain through her husband Aḥmad ibn Abī al-Ḥawārī to be the fitting provenance; it should therefore be recorded as a famous misattribution, not accepted as Rābiʿa al-ʿAdawiyya’s work ([Cornell, pp. 154–57](https://research.vu.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/42127650/complete%20dissertation.pdf)).

### Posthumous corpus that resists authorship

- **Medieval period onward:** *Sayings, poems, prayers, dialogues, and miracle narratives attributed to Rābiʿa*. This is a collaborative manifestation produced by oral transmitters, hagiographers, anthologists, and later devotees rather than a stable oeuvre made by one historical author. Its layers include early remembered aphorisms, material transferred from other women named Rābiʿa, and much later literary inventions; Cornell consequently warns that statements about what “Rābiʿa said” or did require explicit qualification ([Cornell, pp. 266, 269–73](https://research.vu.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/42127650/complete%20dissertation.pdf)).

- **1999:** *Dīwān Rābiʿa al-ʿAdawiyya wa-akhbāruhā*, compiled by Muwaffaq Fawzī al-Jabr. This modern gathering of attributed poetry and reports is a reception-history object, not a collected-works edition authorized by Rābiʿa and not evidence that she made a *dīwān*. Cornell uses it as an example of modern attribution while demonstrating that even its best-known poems have divergent or mistaken provenances ([Cornell, pp. 155–57](https://research.vu.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/42127650/complete%20dissertation.pdf)).
