## Works by Aeneas

### Ships and provisional settlements

- **After the fall of Troy:** *The fleet built below Antandros*. A collective construction directed by Aeneas to carry the Trojan refugees and their household gods westward; its size and the individual ships’ makers are not securely transmitted (Virgil, *Aeneid* 3.5–7; Dionysius 1.47.6).
- **First winter after the fall of Troy:** *Aeneia*. A city and refuge founded in Pallene for Trojans unable or unwilling to continue; Dionysius says it survived until Cassander’s foundation of Thessalonica, while Livy independently preserves the attribution to Aeneas (Dionysius 1.49.4; Livy 40.4.9).
- **Early in the migration:** *The Aeneadae*. An attempted city in Thrace whose walls Aeneas says he began and named for himself, then abandoned after the horrifying discovery of Polydorus’s murder. It is explicitly an unfinished project rather than an enduring city (Virgil, *Aeneid* 3.13–68).
- **Early in the migration:** *Pergamea in Crete*. A settlement collectively built and named by the Trojans under Aeneas, then abandoned when disease and a divine warning disclosed that Crete was the wrong destination (Virgil, *Aeneid* 3.132–191). It belongs to Virgil’s itinerary and is not included in Dionysius’s otherwise extensive catalogue.
- **Disputed migration tradition:** *Capyae*. Ariaethus and other writers attributed this Arcadian town, named for the Trojan Capys, to Aeneas and the Trojans; rival accounts have Aeneas die in Thrace or bypass permanent settlement in Arcadia (Dionysius 1.49.1–2).
- **Second year after the fall of Troy, in Dionysius’s chronology:** *Aegesta and Elyma*. Cities built in western Sicily for Aegestus, Elymus, and the Trojans left there; Dionysius reports disagreement over whether this was Aeneas’s planned refuge for exhausted followers or a necessity after Trojan women burned part of the fleet (Dionysius 1.52.4–53.1).
- **During the Sicilian sojourn, in Virgil’s version:** *Acesta*. Aeneas marks out the city, assigns inhabitants, gives laws, and leaves its rule to Acestes; the settlement substantially overlaps the Aegesta/Segesta tradition and should not be mistaken for a securely distinct foundation (Virgil, *Aeneid* 5.746–761).
- **Arrival at Laurentum:** *The Trojan shore camp*. Dionysius calls it an entrenched camp thereafter known as Troy; in Virgil the Trojans likewise enclose their landing place with rampart, battlements, and ditch. This is military infrastructure rather than a city (Dionysius 1.53.3; Virgil, *Aeneid* 7.157–159, 9.8–76).
- **Traditionally about two years after Troy:** *Lavinium*. Aeneas’s principal city, named for Lavinia and built as the permanent Trojan settlement in Latium (Dionysius 1.56.4–63.3; Livy 1.1.11). Its archaeological remains do not verify a Bronze Age founder, and traditions instead making Aeneas founder of Rome, or denying that he reached Italy at all, remained current in antiquity (Dionysius 1.53.4, 1.72).

### Sanctuaries, altars, and sacred installations

- **First winter after Troy:** *Temple of Aphrodite at Pallene*. A promontory sanctuary built collectively by Aeneas’s migrants beside Aeneia; no ancient source preserves a more specific title or design (Dionysius 1.49.4).
- **During the Mediterranean migration:** *Temple of Aphrodite on Cythera*. A collective Trojan foundation attributed to the company led by Aeneas (Dionysius 1.50.1).
- **During the Mediterranean migration:** *Temple of Aphrodite on Zacynthus*. A sanctuary built by the Trojans and linked to a sacrifice and games instituted during their stay (Dionysius 1.50.3).
- **During the Mediterranean migration:** *Temple of Aphrodite Aeneias at Leucas*. A sanctuary on the small island between Dioryctus and the city, reportedly still standing in Dionysius’s day (Dionysius 1.50.4).
- **During the Mediterranean migration:** *Sanctuaries at Actium*. Dionysius records a temple of Aphrodite Aeneias and a neighbouring temple of the Great Gods as monuments of the Trojans’ arrival; his collective wording does not establish Aeneas as their individual builder (Dionysius 1.50.4).
- **During the Mediterranean migration:** *Temple of Aphrodite at Ambracia*. Another sanctuary treated by Dionysius as a memorial of the expedition. The nearby hero-shrine and wooden image of Aeneas were honours subsequently made for him, not works by him (Dionysius 1.50.4).
- **During the Mediterranean migration:** *Temple of Aphrodite at the Harbour of Anchises*. A collectively constructed sanctuary in Epirus, at a harbour whose ancient name Dionysius says had already changed by his time (Dionysius 1.51.2).
- **During the Sicilian sojourn:** *Altar of Aphrodite Aeneias on Mount Eryx*. Dionysius specifically distinguishes this altar, built by Aeneas for his mother, from the temple to Aeneas at Aegesta, which the colonists made after his departure (Dionysius 1.53.1).
- **On reaching Latium:** *Trojan altars at the sacred spring*. Two altars, one facing east and one west, were remembered as Trojan structures on which Aeneas made his first Italian thanksgiving sacrifice to the Sun (Dionysius 1.55.2). The attribution is local sacred tradition, not an archaeologically demonstrated authorship.
- **At the site of Lavinium:** *Altar and sacred enclosure of the white sow*. Aeneas sacrificed the sow and her thirty young to his household gods at a place later occupied by an inaccessible Lavinian chapel; the later building itself is not attributed to him (Dionysius 1.57.1).
- **At Lavinium:** *Installation of the Trojan gods*. Aeneas selected the best part of the new settlement and placed the transported divine images there; a later temple housed them, but Dionysius does not clearly say that Aeneas built that temple (Dionysius 1.55.5–57.1, 1.67.1).

### Rituals and instituted performances

- **During the stay on Zacynthus:** *The Course of Aeneas and Aphrodite*. Games for young men, including a race whose finish was the goddess’s temple, instituted by the Trojan visitors and reportedly maintained by the Zacynthians (Dionysius 1.50.3). As a repeatable ritual protocol, it resists classification as a single material work.
- **First anniversary of Anchises’s death, in Virgil’s version:** *Annual rites and funeral games for Anchises*. Aeneas establishes a recurring commemoration and stages contests in rowing, running, boxing, and archery, with prizes and sacrifices (Virgil, *Aeneid* 5.42–603). This is a designed civic-religious performance rather than a text or object.
- **During the Sicilian resettlement, in Virgil’s version:** *Priesthood and sanctuary of Venus at Eryx*. Aeneas appoints the priest and orders a sanctuary associated with his mother as part of the new settlement’s institutional design (Virgil, *Aeneid* 5.759–761); its relation to Dionysius’s altar on Mount Eryx is uncertain.

### Tombs and funerary constructions

- **During the Thracian landing, in Virgil’s version:** *Tomb of Polydorus*. Aeneas and the Trojans heap a mound and perform funeral rites after discovering the murdered prince’s remains. It is a collective funerary work created immediately before they abandon the Aeneadae project (Virgil, *Aeneid* 3.62–68).
- **During the voyage past the Peloponnese:** *Burial of Cinaethus*. Aeneas’s company buries its dead companion on the promontory subsequently called Cinaethion; the source does not identify a constructed tomb or Aeneas’s individual handiwork (Dionysius 1.50.2).
- **At Cumae, in Virgil’s version:** *Pyre and tomb of Misenus*. The Trojans build the pyre collectively, while Aeneas raises a monumental tomb bearing Misenus’s arms, oar, and trumpet (Virgil, *Aeneid* 6.212–235). Dionysius instead connects Misenus’s death with the naming of the harbour and does not assign Aeneas a tomb there (Dionysius 1.53.2).
- **On the Italian coast, in Virgil’s version:** *Tomb of Caieta*. Aeneas performs the funeral rites and raises the mound of his nurse Caieta, whose name the narrative gives to the place (Virgil, *Aeneid* 7.1–7). Dionysius preserves the place-naming tradition but attributes it collectively to the travellers (Dionysius 1.53.2).
- **During the Italian war, in Virgil’s version:** *Funeral bier and escort for Pallas*. Aeneas prepares Pallas’s body on an elaborate bier and sends it home with spoils, attendants, and sacrificial captives (Virgil, *Aeneid* 11.59–99). It is an ephemeral ceremonial assemblage, not a permanent monument.

### Dedications and victory monuments

- **During the migration:** *Trojan offerings at Dodona*. Aeneas and his companions dedicate several objects, including inscribed bronze mixing bowls; Dionysius reports having evidence from their ancient inscriptions but does not name individual makers (Dionysius 1.51.1).
- **During the voyage along southern Italy:** *Bronze patera dedicated to Juno*. Dionysius reports a vessel bearing an inscription naming Aeneas as its dedicator. Dedication is securely the attributed act; manufacture by Aeneas is not claimed (Dionysius 1.51.3).
- **At Actium, in Virgil’s version:** *Shield of Abas dedicated at Apollo’s temple*. Aeneas fixes a captured Greek shield to the temple entrance and inscribes it as his own dedication (Virgil, *Aeneid* 3.286–288). It is a repurposed object and manifesto of victory, not an artefact he fabricated.
- **After the defeat of Mezentius, in Virgil’s version:** *Trophy of Mezentius*. Aeneas erects an oak trunk dressed in the defeated warrior’s armour and dedicates it to Mars (Virgil, *Aeneid* 11.5–16). This temporary assemblage is the clearest object in the epic personally fashioned under his name.

### Political and genealogical creations

- **After alliance with Latinus:** *The united Latin people*. Livy says that Aeneas gave the common name “Latins” to Trojans and Aborigines so that both communities would share one law and designation (Livy 1.2.4–5). This is a political creation or project of collective identity, not a made object; Virgil’s alternative settlement has the indigenous Latins retain their name, language, and customs (*Aeneid* 12.819–840).
- **Before and after the fall of Troy:** *Ascanius, also called Iulus*. Aeneas’s son and the dynastic link claimed by the Julian family. His mother and even his identity are disputed: Virgil makes him Creusa’s Trojan son, while Livy records a competing tradition in which an Ascanius is born to Lavinia in Italy (Virgil, *Aeneid* 2.674–729; Livy 1.1.11, 1.3.2–3).
- **Conceived before Aeneas’s death, in Virgil’s prophecy:** *Silvius*. Anchises foretells a posthumous son of Aeneas and Lavinia who will begin the Alban royal line (*Aeneid* 6.760–766). Livy instead places Silvius one generation later as the son of Ascanius, so neither the parentage nor Aeneas’s direct authorship of this line is stable (Livy 1.3.6–10).
- **Traditional future extending beyond Aeneas’s lifetime:** *The Roman line*. Ancient narratives treat Aeneas as progenitor of the Alban kings, Romulus, and Rome, but these are manifestations attributed to his lineage rather than things he personally made. Even antiquity offered incompatible genealogies and rival claims that Aeneas founded Rome, returned to Troy, died in Thrace, or never reached Italy (Dionysius 1.49, 1.53.4, 1.72–73).
