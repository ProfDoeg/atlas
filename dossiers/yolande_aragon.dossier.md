# Yolande of Aragon: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

Yolande of Aragon lived in a period for which evidence is uneven. Royal acts, seals, accounts, marriage agreements, diplomatic records, chronicles, manuscripts, and later archival inventories survive, but there is no continuous personal diary or contemporary biography. Many familiar anecdotes first appear in chronicles written decades after the events.

The following labels are used:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary acts, accounts, correspondence, official records, or multiple closely contemporary sources.
- **Reported fact:** recorded by a contemporary or near-contemporary narrative source but not independently documented.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** a modern historian’s interpretation of incomplete evidence.
- **Disputed claim:** supported by conflicting or inconclusive evidence.
- **Allegation or theory:** a proposed explanation lacking documentary proof.
- **Rumor or myth:** a culturally important story that cannot be accepted as established history.
- **Later error:** a demonstrably or probably mistaken statement in modern reference works.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Principal name:** Yolande of Aragon.

**Names and spellings:**

- French: *Yolande d’Aragon*
- Catalan: *Violant d’Aragó*
- Spanish: *Violante de Aragón*
- Latin and documentary variants: *Violans*, *Violanta*, *Iolanda*, *Jolanda*
- English variants: Yolanda, Yolande, Violante
- After marriage she was frequently styled Yolande of Anjou or queen of Sicily.

**Dynasty:** House of Barcelona, through her father; incorporated by marriage into the second Capetian House of Anjou-Valois.

**Birth:** Usually given as **11 August 1381**, at Zaragoza in the Kingdom of Aragon. Some sources give 1380, 1383, or 1384. The 1381 reconstruction, adopted in Zita Eva Rohr’s modern biography, is the strongest current scholarly position. Older genealogies and institutional catalogues remain inconsistent.

**Death:** **14 November 1442**, probably at the residence traditionally called the house of the Queen of Sicily in Saumur, then in the duchy of Anjou.

Some old notices and catalogues give 1443, probably through calendar, transcription, or chronicle error. Yolande made her testament at Saumur on 12 November 1442, two days before the conventionally accepted death date.

**Burial:** Cathedral of Saint-Maurice, Angers, beside or near her husband Louis II. The original dynastic funerary arrangements were later damaged or dispersed.

**Principal titles:**

- Infanta of Aragon
- Duchess consort, later dowager duchess, of Anjou
- Countess consort or dowager countess of Maine, Provence, and Forcalquier
- Lady of Guise
- Duchess of Touraine, through a grant from Charles VII
- Titular queen of Sicily or Naples
- Titular queen of Jerusalem
- Sometimes styled queen of Aragon by dynastic claim

The retrospective epithet **“Queen of Four Kingdoms”** normally refers to some combination of Aragon, Sicily/Naples, Jerusalem, and Cyprus. It exaggerates the territory she personally governed. The Angevin claim to Cyprus was especially remote, while her family’s effective power lay mainly in Anjou, Maine, Provence, and parts of the French royal sphere.

**Occupation in modern terms:** Dynastic ruler, regent, estate administrator, diplomat, political broker, patron, and royal councillor. She held no “profession” in the modern occupational sense.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal line

Yolande’s father was **John I of Aragon** (*Joan I d’Aragó*, 1350–1396), called “the Hunter” or “the Lover of Elegance.” He succeeded Peter IV in 1387 and ruled Aragon, Valencia, Majorca, Sardinia, and the Catalan counties until his death.

John’s court was Francophile, literary, musical, and ceremonially elaborate. It was also criticized by political opponents for expense, favoritism, astrology, hunting, and the influence of courtiers. Those criticisms form part of the political environment in which Yolande was raised.

John I was the son of:

- **Peter IV of Aragon**, “the Ceremonious”
- **Eleanor of Sicily**

This ancestry connected Yolande to the ruling houses of Barcelona and Sicily.

### Maternal line

Her mother was **Violant or Yolande of Bar** (c. 1365–1431), daughter of:

- **Robert I, Duke of Bar**
- **Marie of Valois**, daughter of King John II of France

Yolande of Bar brought French aristocratic culture and Capetian connections to the Aragonese court. She exercised political influence during her husband’s reign and, after his death, defended her daughter’s rights and household against the new regime.

Through her maternal grandmother, Yolande of Aragon was a great-granddaughter of **John II of France** and a relative of the French Valois dynasty. This ancestry helped make her marriage into the Angevin branch politically plausible.

### Siblings and half-siblings

John I had children by two marriages. Mortality among them was high. Yolande’s full siblings included several children who died young, among them:

- James, Duke of Girona
- Ferdinand
- Joanna
- Antonia
- Peter

Exact lists and dates vary because infants who died shortly after birth were inconsistently recorded. Yolande became the principal surviving child of John I and Violant of Bar.

She also had paternal half-siblings from John’s first marriage to Martha of Armagnac. The most politically relevant was **Joanna of Aragon**, Countess of Foix. Joanna and her husband, Matthew of Foix, advanced a claim to Aragon after John I’s death but failed to displace John’s brother Martin.

### Marriage

Yolande married **Louis II of Anjou** (1377–1417), duke of Anjou and claimant king of Naples, at Arles on **2 December 1400**.

The marriage reconciled, or attempted to reconcile, two dynasties competing over southern Italy. The Barcelona-Aragonese and Angevin houses had rival claims arising from the division between island Sicily and the mainland Kingdom of Naples. The union did not end that rivalry permanently, but it converted Yolande from a potential dynastic opponent into a bearer of claims useful to the Angevins.

A marriage arrangement had been considered while the parties were younger. Surviving reports indicate that Louis initially resisted or delayed the match. Later representations describe the marriage as successful, but intimate evidence is sparse.

### Children

Six children are generally attributed to Yolande and Louis II:

1. **Louis III of Anjou** (1403–1434)  
   Duke of Anjou; count of Provence and Maine; titular king of Naples, Sicily, and Jerusalem. He pursued the Angevin claim in Italy and was at one stage adopted as heir by Queen Joanna II of Naples. He married Margaret of Savoy but died without legitimate surviving issue.

2. **Marie of Anjou** (1404–1463)  
   Betrothed in childhood and eventually married to Charles VII of France. She became queen of France and was the mother of Louis XI.

3. **An unnamed or short-lived daughter**  
   Some genealogies include a daughter born around 1406–1407 who died young. Her identity and dates are uncertain.

4. **René of Anjou** (1409–1480)  
   Later duke of Bar and Lorraine, duke of Anjou, count of Provence, and titular king of Naples, Sicily, and Jerusalem; remembered as “Good King René.” His first marriage, to Isabella of Lorraine, was central to Yolande’s eastern dynastic policy.

5. **Yolande of Anjou** (c. 1412–1440)  
   Married Francis of Montfort, later Duke Francis I of Brittany. The marriage cemented a Breton-Angevin rapprochement.

6. **Charles of Anjou** (1414–1472)  
   Count of Maine. He became an influential member of Charles VII’s court and benefited from his mother’s territorial provisions.

The order and exact birth dates vary in genealogical literature.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1381–1400

### Aragonese upbringing

**Documented context:** Yolande was raised at the royal court of Aragon, principally within an itinerant court that moved among Zaragoza, Barcelona, and other royal centers. Her mother’s household preserved strong French and Barrois connections.

No surviving school curriculum can be assigned to Yolande. Her later activity nevertheless indicates competence in:

- Dynastic genealogy and succession
- Household and estate administration
- Diplomatic negotiation
- Ceremonial and devotional culture
- The political uses of marriage
- Written commands and correspondence, usually produced through secretaries

Her father’s court patronized poetry, music, manuscripts, astrology, and elaborate ceremony. Her mother maintained correspondents and political agents. These were formative institutional environments, even where a particular teacher cannot be named.

### Death of John I and succession crisis, 1396

John I died suddenly in May 1396 after a hunting accident or collapse. He left no surviving legitimate son. His brother became **Martin I of Aragon**.

Yolande’s elder half-sister Joanna and Matthew of Foix attempted to assert a claim but failed. Yolande’s mother lost influence and faced hostility from the new administration. Associates of the former queen were removed, arrested, or investigated amid accusations that the previous court had mismanaged government and finances.

For Yolande, this established three enduring political facts:

- Female descent could generate a dynastic claim but did not guarantee recognition.
- Possession, elite consent, and military strength could outweigh genealogy.
- Her future security depended on alliances outside Aragon.

### Journey to Provence and marriage

Yolande traveled from the Iberian Peninsula to Provence for her marriage. She married Louis II in Arles Cathedral on 2 December 1400. The famous Apocalypse Tapestry belonging to the Angevins was reportedly used in the wedding decoration at Arles.

This was a permanent dynastic migration from the Crown of Aragon into the Angevin territories of France and Provence, though she continued to maintain Iberian claims and connections.

## Marriage, Dynastic Government, and Italian Ambitions, 1400–1417

### Angevin residences

Yolande and Louis II moved among:

- Angers and the Angevin Loire territories
- Saumur
- Provence, including Aix and Arles
- Paris and the French royal court
- Other residences required by royal and dynastic politics

Louis spent substantial periods engaged with his Italian claims. Yolande consequently acquired practical responsibility for households, revenues, children, and political representation.

### Relationship with Marie of Blois

Her mother-in-law, **Marie of Blois-Penthièvre** (1345–1404), had preserved Angevin authority during earlier crises and provided a model of active female regency. Marie had governed, negotiated with Provençal estates, raised revenue, and defended the claims of her son Louis II.

Modern historians regard Marie’s career as an important institutional precedent for Yolande. Direct evidence for a formal mentor-student relationship is lacking, but Yolande entered an administration already accustomed to rule by a widowed duchess.

### Children as dynastic policy

Yolande and Louis treated their children’s marriages and placements as instruments of territorial survival.

The most consequential arrangement was the childhood betrothal of their daughter Marie to **Charles of Valois**, a younger son of Charles VI and Isabeau of Bavaria. Charles was not originally expected to inherit the French throne. He was sent into the Angevin household and spent part of his youth under Yolande’s supervision.

### Claim to Aragon, 1410–1412

Martin I died in 1410 without a surviving legitimate heir. Yolande’s family advanced the claim of her son Louis through descent from John I.

The succession was settled by the **Compromise of Caspe** in 1412. Delegates chose **Ferdinand of Antequera**, a Castilian prince descended in the male-preferred line from Eleanor of Aragon.

**Documented fact:** The Angevin-Yolande party conducted diplomacy in support of Louis’s claim.

**Outcome:** Failure. Ferdinand became Ferdinand I of Aragon.

**Interpretive question:** Some writers present Yolande herself as the principal claimant. In practice, her son Louis was the candidate most readily advanced, while her own descent supplied the genealogical basis. The claim survived symbolically in Angevin titles but not in effective rule.

### French civil war

France was divided between Armagnac and Burgundian factions during the mental illness of Charles VI. The Angevins aligned broadly with the Armagnac-Valois side.

Yolande’s interests were not reducible to French patriotism. The survival of the Valois claimant was bound to:

- Her daughter Marie’s future queenship
- Angevin lands in France
- Her sons’ security
- Her family’s Italian and Aragonese ambitions
- Resistance to Anglo-Burgundian domination

## Widowhood and Regency, 1417–1424

Louis II died at Angers on **29 April 1417**. Yolande became the effective head of the Angevin dynasty and regent or guardian for Louis III.

Her authority included oversight of:

- Anjou, Maine, Provence, and Forcalquier
- Household appointments
- Diplomatic relations
- Her children’s marriages
- Revenues and feudal rights
- Italian policy
- Relations with the French crown

The administrative structure was not a private “company.” It was a territorial princely government composed of councils, clerks, treasurers, receivers, judges, ecclesiastical councillors, household officers, captains, and local estates.

### Protection of Charles, the future Charles VII

In 1415 and 1417, Charles’s elder brothers Louis and John died. Charles unexpectedly became dauphin.

A famous story says that Queen Isabeau demanded his return and Yolande replied:

> “We have not nourished and cherished this one for you to make him die like his brothers, go mad like his father, or become English like you. I keep him for my own. Come and take him if you dare.”

**Evidentiary label: Reported fact / probably embellished anecdote.**

The wording comes from later Angevin chronicling, especially Jean de Bourdigné, rather than a surviving letter by Yolande. Its political substance is plausible: Charles remained closely connected to the Angevin household, and Yolande had compelling reasons to protect him. The polished speech should not be treated as a verbatim contemporary transcript.

### Paris, Bourges, and the “kingdom of Bourges”

After the Burgundian seizure of Paris in 1418, Charles escaped south. The Valois government operated from Bourges and Loire centers, controlling only part of France.

Yolande supplied familial, political, and territorial support. Her household and network furnished administrators and intermediaries. Her influence fluctuated and was never absolute; Charles had his own favorites, councillors, and priorities.

### Murder of John the Fearless, 1419

Duke John the Fearless of Burgundy was killed during a meeting with the dauphin’s party on the bridge at Montereau on 10 September 1419.

**Documented fact:** Men associated with Charles’s party carried out the killing.

**Unresolved question:** Charles’s prior knowledge and the precise responsibility of individual councillors remain disputed.

**Theory involving Yolande:** Some later reconstructions place the Angevin faction, and sometimes Yolande personally, behind the killing.

**Assessment:** No surviving evidence establishes that Yolande ordered, planned, or knew in advance of the assassination. Her political circle suffered consequences because Burgundy demanded the removal or punishment of those considered responsible. Association of the entire Angevin network with the murder should not be converted into proof of Yolande’s personal complicity.

### Treaty of Troyes, 1420

The Treaty of Troyes recognized Henry V of England as heir to Charles VI and excluded the dauphin. Charles and his adherents rejected it.

The treaty threatened Yolande directly: if Charles were disinherited, her daughter Marie would not become queen of France, and Angevin lands could become vulnerable to Anglo-Burgundian pressure.

### Marriage of Marie and Charles

Marie of Anjou married Charles at Bourges on **22 April 1422**. When Charles VI died later that year, Charles claimed the throne as Charles VII, while the Anglo-Burgundian party recognized the infant Henry VI.

Yolande thereby became mother-in-law of the disputed French king.

## Political Ascendancy and Court Faction, 1424–1435

### Touraine and territorial rewards

Charles VII granted Yolande the duchy of Touraine in 1424. Grants of this kind rewarded service but also converted royal dependence into territorial and financial obligations.

Yolande’s material base included:

- Dower and marital rights
- Revenues from Angevin territories
- Provençal and Maine rights
- Royal grants
- Baronies and seigneuries later distributed among her children
- Household and ecclesiastical patronage

Exact yearly wealth cannot be reconstructed from available published evidence. She was a major territorial princess but also belonged to a dynasty repeatedly strained by warfare, ransom, Italian expeditions, and competing claims.

### Arthur de Richemont

Yolande helped negotiate a rapprochement with **John V, Duke of Brittany**, and supported the appointment of John’s brother **Arthur de Richemont** as constable of France in 1425.

**Documented political relationship:** Yolande and Richemont shared an interest in widening Charles VII’s coalition and strengthening military government.

**Limits:** Richemont’s appointment required negotiation among the king, Brittany, Burgundy, and court factions. It cannot be attributed to Yolande alone.

Burgundian reconciliation required the removal of councillors associated with Montereau. The resulting dismissals were later represented as Yolande purging incompetent favorites. The evidence points instead to a negotiated political concession mixed with factional competition.

### Pierre de Giac and Le Camus de Beaulieu

Charles VII’s court was marked by violent changes of favorite.

**Pierre de Giac**, a royal favorite, was seized, summarily tried, and drowned in 1427 by enemies associated with Richemont. Later narratives connected Yolande to the faction that removed him.

Shortly afterward, another favorite, **Le Camus de Beaulieu**, was murdered.

**Evidentiary assessment:**

- The violent removals are documented.
- Richemont and his allies had clear involvement in Giac’s overthrow.
- Yolande’s political hostility to certain favorites is plausible.
- Direct evidence that she ordered either killing is lacking.
- Statements that she personally orchestrated the murders should be labeled allegation or inference, not fact.

### Georges de La Trémoille

Georges de La Trémoille became Charles VII’s principal chamberlain and displaced Richemont’s faction. Relations between La Trémoille and the Angevin party were hostile and unstable.

In 1427 Yolande and Richemont temporarily withdrew from court. La Trémoille’s dominance restricted their influence.

A prolonged effort to remove him culminated in June 1433, when Pierre d’Amboise, Jean de Bueil, Prigent de Coëtivy, and Pierre de Brézé attacked and abducted him at Chinon.

**Documented fact:** La Trémoille was violently removed and the Angevin party benefited; Yolande’s son Charles of Maine rose at court.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** Yolande and Richemont probably approved or encouraged the plot.

**Unproven detail:** No surviving order signed by Yolande commands the attack. “Yolande’s coup” is a useful shorthand but stronger than the direct evidence.

## Yolande and Joan of Arc, 1429–1431

This relationship is the largest source of modern exaggeration.

### What is securely established

- Joan entered the Valois-Angevin political sphere in early 1429.
- She traveled from Vaucouleurs through territory connected with the king’s supporters and reached Chinon.
- Charles VII’s council examined her claims.
- Theological inquiry was conducted at Poitiers.
- Joan received armor, equipment, attendants, and military access through the royal government.
- An expedition relieved Orléans.
- Yolande had an immediate political interest in saving Orléans and preserving Charles VII.
- Contemporary rehabilitation testimony says that matrons, commonly understood to have acted under royal or queenly authority, examined Joan to confirm her virginity.
- Yolande was an important member of the wider political network that admitted and supported Joan.

### The virginity examination

Modern accounts often state that Yolande personally examined Joan. More cautiously, women appointed or supervised within the royal household conducted the examination; Yolande is traditionally identified as directing it.

**Evidentiary label:** Reported fact with uncertain procedural detail.

### Financing Orléans

Yolande is frequently said to have personally financed the entire relief army.

**Documented core:** The Angevin house and its financial networks contributed materially to the royal war effort, and Yolande was involved in efforts to sustain Orléans.

**Qualification:** Medieval military finance came from multiple royal, municipal, noble, ecclesiastical, and credit sources. The claim that Yolande alone organized and paid for Joan’s army is not supported by a single comprehensive account.

### Did Yolande “create” Joan?

Marina Warner, Nancy Goldstone, and popular historical narratives have advanced versions of a theory that Yolande recognized Joan’s propaganda value, arranged her reception, or effectively manufactured her career.

The strongest version says that Yolande’s agents identified Joan in Lorraine, trained or coached her, supplied prophecies, and brought her to Chinon as a political instrument.

**Evidence offered:**

- Joan’s route crossed territories and networks connected to the Angevins.
- René of Anjou and his father-in-law Charles of Lorraine were geographically close to Joan’s early activity.
- Joan asked Charles of Lorraine whether René could accompany her.
- Yolande controlled access and resources around Charles VII.
- The “armed virgin” prophecy was politically useful.
- Joan’s arrival benefited the Angevin-Valois faction.

**Evidence against the strong theory:**

- No surviving letter, payment order, instruction, or testimony says Yolande recruited or trained Joan.
- Joan’s own trial testimony describes a locally developing mission and repeated attempts to persuade Robert de Baudricourt.
- The route through friendly territory was necessary for any supporter attempting to reach Charles.
- Contamine characterized the two women’s direct intersection as an “improbable meeting of two political trajectories,” cautioning against turning circumstantial alignment into a hidden master plan.
- A scholarly review of Rohr’s biography similarly warned that attributing numerous court outcomes to Yolande can exceed demonstrable evidence.

**Assessment:** Yolande’s political network helped make Joan’s reception and deployment possible. The claim that she invented Joan’s mission is an **allegation or theory**, not documented fact.

### Joan’s capture and death

Joan was captured in May 1430 and executed at Rouen on 30 May 1431.

There is no reliable evidence that Yolande engineered Joan’s abandonment, capture, trial, or death. Nor is there evidence of a successful rescue effort by Yolande. The absence of a rescue must be understood within fragmented authority, jurisdiction, diplomacy, ransom practice, and the fact that Joan passed through Burgundian into English custody.

Claims that Yolande deliberately “disposed of” Joan once she had served her purpose are **modern conspiracy narratives without documentary proof**.

## Brittany, Lorraine, and Dynastic Marriages

### René and Lorraine

Yolande arranged or supported René’s marriage to **Isabella of Lorraine**, heiress of Duke Charles II. René was transferred into the Bar-Lorraine political sphere and eventually succeeded to Bar and, through Isabella, claimed Lorraine.

This policy expanded Angevin interests eastward but produced war. René was captured at Bulgnéville in 1431 by forces supporting Antoine of Vaudémont and was held by Philip the Good of Burgundy.

Yolande participated in the dynastic and financial response, which involved negotiations, guarantees, and ransom burdens. René’s imprisonment illustrates the limits of her strategy: marriage created claims but not uncontested possession.

### Breton alliance

In May 1431, on the island of Béhuard near Angers, Yolande and Duke John V of Brittany witnessed oaths involving their children. Yolande’s daughter Yolande married Francis of Montfort in August 1431.

The marriage secured a direct family tie to Brittany and complemented the political relationship with Richemont.

Yolande’s surviving seal records include arrangements for her daughter’s dowry and dower, including rights connected to Beaufort and Château-Fromont and financial guarantees of 4,000 or 6,000 livres under specified succession conditions.

## Later Life, 1434–1442

### Death of Louis III

Louis III died in Calabria in November 1434 without surviving legitimate children. René became heir to the central Angevin titles and claims.

### Joanna II and Naples

Queen Joanna II of Naples had alternately adopted Alfonso V of Aragon and Louis III of Anjou as heirs. At her death in 1435, René inherited the Angevin claim.

This renewed the war with Alfonso V. René’s captivity initially prevented his effective intervention. He later reached Naples but ultimately lost the kingdom to Alfonso in 1442.

Yolande therefore lived to see the collapse of effective Angevin rule in Naples. The family retained the titles “king of Sicily,” “king of Naples,” and “king of Jerusalem,” but these increasingly expressed claims rather than possession.

### Peace of Arras

The Treaty of Arras in 1435 reconciled Charles VII and Philip the Good of Burgundy. Yolande’s long-standing objective of detaching Burgundy from England was thereby substantially realized, though the treaty was the work of many diplomats and political actors.

### Withdrawal and continuing administration

Yolande increasingly resided at Angers and Saumur, but “retirement” should not be understood as total withdrawal. She continued to manage rights, patronage, family settlements, and testamentary arrangements.

Her final testament was made at Saumur on **12 November 1442**. It confirmed or distributed rights including:

- Baronies and seigneuries of Lunel and Berre
- Rights in the Île de Martigues
- Interests in Bar and Pont-à-Mousson
- Provisions benefiting her sons Charles and René
- Religious and funerary dispositions

She died on 14 November.

## Residences, Movements, and Geographical Sphere

Places directly associated with Yolande include:

- **Zaragoza:** probable birthplace
- **Barcelona and other Aragonese royal centers:** childhood court
- **Arles:** marriage in 1400
- **Aix-en-Provence and Provence generally:** comital government
- **Angers:** principal dynastic capital and burial place
- **Saumur:** residence and place of death
- **Paris:** French royal politics before the Burgundian seizure
- **Bourges:** Valois court and marriage of Marie and Charles
- **Chinon:** royal court, Joan of Arc’s reception, and factional struggles
- **Tours and Touraine:** duchy granted by Charles VII
- **Béhuard:** Breton-Angevin oaths in 1431
- **Lorraine and Bar:** dynastic interests managed through René

There is no good evidence that Yolande personally campaigned in Italy. Her Italian activity was conducted through her husband, sons, envoys, money, correspondence, and legal claims.

## Government, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### The Angevin polity

Yolande operated through overlapping institutions rather than a centralized state:

- Ducal councils in Anjou
- Comital administration in Provence
- Local estates and municipal governments
- Ecclesiastical institutions
- Royal councils around Charles VII
- Household offices
- Diplomatic embassies
- Marriage and dower contracts
- Credit, taxation, feudal revenues, and pledges

The Angevin territories were geographically discontinuous. This made travel, delegated government, correspondence, and family placements essential.

### Financial pressures

Major expenses included:

- War in France
- Defense of Provence and Anjou
- Italian expeditions
- Court and household maintenance
- Dowries
- René’s ransom and Lorraine conflict
- Diplomatic missions
- Religious foundations and patronage

No defensible single estimate of Yolande’s personal net worth survives. Titles such as “queen of four kingdoms” should not be read as evidence of liquid wealth or control of four royal treasuries.

### Property

Her testament and marriage records show rights in or claims upon:

- Touraine
- Anjou, Maine, Provence, and Forcalquier
- Guise
- Beaufort-en-Vallée
- Château-Fromont
- Lunel
- Berre
- Martigues
- Bar and Pont-à-Mousson

The legal character of these interests varied: dower, appanage, usufruct, grant, family inheritance, or claim.

### Religious patronage

Yolande supported churches, abbeys, and mendicant networks. A surviving archival description records a letter in favor of the Abbey of Saint-Serge at Angers dated **16 March 1431**.

Her religious activity was conventional for a territorial princess but also political: patronage linked households, clerics, towns, and dynastic memory.

### Manuscript patronage

Yolande is associated with elite literary and devotional culture.

The **Grandes Heures de Rohan**, produced around 1430–1435, has often been attributed to her commission, possibly in connection with the Anjou-Rohan/Brittany marriage project.

**Evidentiary label:** Probable or disputed attribution.

Rohan arms and the artist’s Angevin context support an Angevin-Rohan connection, but no surviving commission contract proves Yolande ordered the manuscript.

A different manuscript conventionally called the **Heures de Yolande d’Aragon**, now at Aix-en-Provence, dates from approximately 1460–1470—after her death. Its title therefore reflects later association or provenance, not authorship or a commission by Yolande.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Political outlook

Yolande’s actions show durable commitments to:

- Preservation of Angevin territorial autonomy
- Her children’s inheritance
- The Valois claim of Charles VII
- Opposition to English and Anglo-Burgundian domination where it threatened Angevin interests
- Diplomatic reconciliation with Brittany and eventually Burgundy
- Expansion through marriage rather than direct conquest where possible
- Maintenance of claims in Aragon and southern Italy

Calling her a “French nationalist” is anachronistic. Her politics were dynastic, territorial, familial, and confessional. They could nevertheless contribute to the reconstruction of French royal power.

### Religion

Yolande was a Latin Christian princess whose public life included:

- Masses and funerary provisions
- Ecclesiastical patronage
- Abbey protection
- Devotional manuscripts
- Use of religious legitimacy in dynastic politics

There is no evidence of heresy, conversion, anticlericalism, or unusual personal mysticism. Claims that she fabricated Joan’s voices imply religious manipulation but remain speculative.

### Intellectual culture

She was raised in a multilingual court influenced by Catalan, Aragonese, Occitan, French, and Latin culture. Her later patronage and her children’s artistic interests suggest familiarity with courtly literature and illuminated manuscripts.

No original literary composition by Yolande has been securely identified.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Louis II of Anjou

Husband and dynastic partner. Their marriage joined rival Mediterranean claims and produced the generation through which Yolande exercised later power.

### Violant of Bar

Mother and formative dynastic model. Both women defended claims through correspondence, household organization, and marriage politics.

### Marie of Blois

Mother-in-law and predecessor in Angevin female government. Her regency demonstrated that a dowager duchess could govern the dispersed Angevin territories.

### Charles VII

Son-in-law, former ward, king, and principal political associate. Yolande supported him, but claims that she controlled him continuously are exaggerated. He accepted, resisted, and balanced competing factions.

### Marie of Anjou

Daughter and queen of France. Marie’s marriage made the fortunes of the Valois monarchy inseparable from those of Yolande’s house.

### Louis III, René, and Charles of Maine

Sons used in distinct political theaters:

- Louis III in Naples
- René in Bar, Lorraine, and later Naples
- Charles in the French royal court

### John V of Brittany and Arthur de Richemont

Partners in Breton rapprochement. Richemont was both military collaborator and factional actor.

### Georges de La Trémoille

Court rival whose dominance marginalized the Angevin-Richemont network. His abduction in 1433 restored Angevin influence.

### Philip the Good of Burgundy

Adversary and eventual negotiating partner. Burgundy’s demands affected Yolande’s associates after Montereau; reconciliation became essential to Valois recovery.

### Joan of Arc

A politically aligned but poorly documented relationship. Yolande belonged to the governing network that evaluated, equipped, and used Joan’s mission; direct personal mentorship is unproved.

### Clerical councillors

The Angevin administration employed educated canons and clerks. Jean Boucher, a canon of Angers cathedral, is documented as a late councillor associated with Yolande’s service during the Joan of Arc period.

## Successes and Failures

### Significant successes

- Preserved Angevin government after Louis II’s death.
- Protected and promoted her children’s dynastic interests.
- Sustained Charles VII during the monarchy’s weakest period.
- Helped arrange the marriage that made Marie queen of France.
- Helped build a Breton alliance and supported Richemont’s constableship.
- Contributed to the political and financial environment in which Orléans was relieved.
- Restored Angevin influence after La Trémoille’s fall.
- Extended the dynasty into Bar, Lorraine, Brittany, and the French royal family.
- Maintained administrative continuity across geographically divided territories.

### Significant failures or incomplete projects

- Failed to secure the Crown of Aragon for herself or Louis III.
- Failed to establish lasting Angevin rule in Naples.
- Could not prevent René’s capture or the heavy financial consequences of his Lorraine conflict.
- Could not eliminate recurrent factional violence at Charles VII’s court.
- Her Italian and eastern claims consumed resources disproportionate to their durable returns.
- Several children died before her, including Louis III and her daughter Yolande.
- Much of her power depended on informal access and kinship rather than secure constitutional office.

## Controversies, Allegations, Violence, and Financial Questions

### Court assassinations

**Allegation:** Yolande arranged the deaths of Pierre de Giac and Le Camus de Beaulieu.

**Evidence:** Her allies opposed the favorites; Richemont’s faction participated in Giac’s seizure.

**Finding:** The killings occurred, but no surviving document proves Yolande ordered them. Personal responsibility remains unresolved.

### Abduction of La Trémoille

**Reported fact:** Yolande probably supported the coalition that removed La Trémoille in 1433.

**Documented fact:** Armed men attacked and abducted him; her son Charles benefited.

**Finding:** Political complicity is plausible; operational command is unproved.

### Montereau assassination

**Allegation or theory:** Yolande’s faction helped plan John the Fearless’s murder.

**Finding:** Men associated with the dauphin committed the killing. Yolande’s personal participation has not been demonstrated.

### Manipulation of Joan of Arc

**Theory:** Yolande manufactured Joan’s religious persona as wartime propaganda.

**Finding:** Circumstantial political connections exist, but the strong theory lacks direct documentary evidence. Yolande’s support after Joan reached the royal sphere is more defensible than prior recruitment.

### Abandonment of Joan

**Conspiracy narrative:** Yolande and Charles VII deliberately allowed Joan to be captured and executed.

**Finding:** No documentary evidence establishes such a plan. Political inaction and failure to rescue Joan are historically important but do not prove a coordinated disposal.

### Misappropriation or corruption

No credible surviving record establishes that Yolande was formally investigated, indicted, tried, or convicted for fraud, embezzlement, money laundering, or corruption.

“Money laundering” is an anachronistic category for her period. Her use of revenues, grants, marriage settlements, and offices was part of medieval dynastic government. It could attract political hostility, but no equivalent judicial finding against her is known.

### Lawsuits and inheritance disputes

Her life was surrounded by legal and diplomatic claims over:

- Aragonese succession
- Naples and Sicily
- Lorraine
- Dowers and appanages
- Bar and Pont-à-Mousson
- Children’s marriage settlements

These were dynastic disputes rather than modern personal lawsuits. Surviving acts document negotiation, guarantees, property transfers, and contested succession.

## Health, Illness, and Cause of Death

Louis II died from illness in 1417; detailed diagnosis is unavailable.

For Yolande:

- No reliable record establishes chronic illness, addiction, psychiatric crisis, major injury, or disability.
- Her testament two days before death suggests that her final illness or expectation of death was recognized.
- The immediate cause of death is not securely documented.
- Later statements assigning a particular disease should be treated as unsupported unless tied to an unidentified medical or household record.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Queen of Four Kingdoms”

**Story:** Yolande ruled or was queen of four kingdoms.

**Origins:** Dynastic titulature and later romantic biography.

**Evidence:** She possessed claims through marriage and descent to Aragon, Naples/Sicily, Jerusalem, and sometimes Cyprus.

**Against:** She did not exercise effective government over four kingdoms. Aragon rejected her line in 1412; Naples remained contested; Jerusalem and Cyprus were titular claims.

**Reputational effect:** The phrase turns a complex territorial princess into an almost imperial monarch.

### “The woman who won the Hundred Years’ War”

**Story:** Yolande was the hidden strategist chiefly responsible for France’s victory.

**Circulation:** Modern biographies, popular histories, journalism, and Gérard de Senneville’s title *La reine qui a gagné la guerre de Cent Ans*.

**Evidence:** She played a major role in dynastic support, diplomacy, finance, Brittany, court appointments, and the survival of Charles VII.

**Against:** The war continued until 1453, eleven years after her death. Victory also depended on Charles VII, Richemont, military commanders, artillery reform, taxation, Burgundian defection, English political weakness, and many local actors.

**Assessment:** Interpretive exaggeration built on a substantial political role.

### The defiant speech to Isabeau

**Story:** Yolande refused to return Charles and accused Isabeau of making him die, go mad, or become English.

**Appearance:** Later Angevin chronicle tradition.

**Evidence:** Charles was protected within the Angevin orbit.

**Against:** No contemporary verbatim record.

**Effect:** Became a defining image of Yolande as Charles’s true mother and Isabeau as a traitor.

### “A man’s heart in a woman’s body”

Later tradition attributes this description to her grandson Louis XI. Other chronicles praised her as the wisest and most beautiful princess in Christendom; Jean Jouvenel des Ursins reportedly called her the most beautiful woman in the kingdom.

These are gendered formulas of praise, not psychological descriptions. They reflect admiration for political capacity while presenting that capacity as conventionally masculine.

### Secret creator of Joan of Arc

This modern myth ranges from a plausible claim of sponsorship to an elaborate conspiracy involving staged prophecy, coaching, intelligence networks, and fabricated visions.

No documentary chain supports the full narrative. Its persistence reflects a modern desire to replace supernatural explanation with a hidden political organizer. Yolande’s genuine access to money, court, Lorraine connections, and royal policy makes her a compelling candidate for that role, but plausibility is not proof.

### Invisible ruler or *éminence grise*

Yolande is often described as the secret ruler behind Charles VII.

She was influential, but periods of exclusion—especially La Trémoille’s ascendancy—demonstrate that she did not continuously control the king. “Éminence grise” is a retrospective metaphor.

### Sanitized national heroine

French commemorative writing sometimes recast this Aragonese-born dynast as a selfless savior of France. That account suppresses her family’s claims in Aragon, Naples, Provence, Lorraine, and Brittany.

Her support for Charles VII served both the Valois monarchy and Angevin dynastic survival. These motives were compatible rather than mutually exclusive.

## Works by or Attributed to Yolande

Yolande was not an author in the conventional literary sense. Her documentary corpus consists principally of acts issued in her name.

### Authentic documentary material

- **Letters patent, grants, mandates, and administrative acts**, 1400–1442. Dispersed among the Archives nationales de France, departmental archives, Provençal holdings, and later manuscript copies.
- **Diplomatic communications concerning the Aragonese succession**, especially 1410–1412. Often preserved in registers, abstracts, or copied dossiers.
- **Marriage, dowry, and dower instruments for her children.**
- **Letter in favor of the Abbey of Saint-Serge**, dated 16 March 1431; known through a later printed-on-vellum or copied witness catalogued at Angers.
- **Testament**, Saumur, 12 November 1442; archival reference cited in modern scholarship as Archives nationales, P 1334, no. 52.

These were composed through chancery officials. “Authorship” therefore means legal issuance and authorization, not necessarily autograph composition.

### Correspondence

No complete modern critical edition of Yolande’s correspondence appears to exist. Individual letters and summaries are distributed across archival catalogues and older documentary publications.

### *L’Avis à Yolande d’Aragon*

This political “mirror for princes” is **addressed to or associated with Yolande**, not securely authored by her. It should not be listed as her own work.

### Manuscripts associated with her

- **Grandes Heures de Rohan**, c. 1430–1435: probably or possibly commissioned by Yolande; not written or illuminated by her.
- **Heures dites de Yolande d’Aragon**, c. 1460–1470: postdates her death and is not her work.
- Other books appearing in reconstructed Angevin collections represent ownership, patronage, or later association, not authorship.

## Films, Television, Fiction, and Cultural Representations

### Film

- **The Messenger: The Story of Joan of Arc** (1999), directed by Luc Besson. Yolande is played by Faye Dunaway. The characterization is dramatically stylized and not a documentary reconstruction.
- Numerous Joan of Arc films omit Yolande or compress her role into composite court characters.

### Television

- **Catherine**: Yolande was portrayed by Geneviève Casile.
- Historical documentaries about Joan of Arc, Charles VII, and the Hundred Years’ War occasionally present Yolande as Joan’s patron or Charles’s political mentor, with varying degrees of evidentiary caution.

### Theatre

- Jean Anouilh, **L’Alouette** (*The Lark*, 1953): Yolande appears as a dramatic character; Denise Perret created the role in the original production.

### Fiction

- Princess Michael of Kent, *The Queen of Four Kingdoms*.
- Milja Kaunisto’s Olaus Magnus trilogy includes Yolande as a significant secondary figure, although its chronology and setting are fictionalized.
- Historical fiction about Joan of Arc frequently depicts Yolande as either a wise sponsor, intelligence organizer, or manipulative architect of Joan’s public identity.

### Visual and botanical commemoration

- The rose cultivar **‘Yolande d’Aragon’**, introduced by Jean-Pierre Vibert in 1843, commemorates her.
- Stained-glass, manuscript, and later historical images have been identified as portraits of Yolande, but secure life portraits are difficult to establish. Some images are idealized dynastic representations rather than physiognomic likenesses.

## Death and Burial

Yolande made her will at Saumur on 12 November 1442 and died on 14 November, probably in the residence later known as the house of the Queen of Sicily.

Her body was carried to Angers and buried in Saint-Maurice Cathedral. She joined a dynastic memorial landscape that linked the Angevins to the church, city, and principality.

No contemporary medical diagnosis or detailed deathbed narrative is known. Claims that she died in Paris or in 1443 are inconsistent with the testamentary and Angers-Saumur tradition.

---

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Fifteenth-century memory

Angevin chroniclers celebrated Yolande as extraordinarily wise, beautiful, and politically capable. This tradition served the prestige of the house of Anjou.

Her grandson Louis XI’s supposed description of her as possessing a man’s heart in a woman’s body became a durable formula. Whether the exact sentence is authentic is less certain than the gendered commemorative tradition it expresses.

### French national history

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century historians increasingly placed her within the recovery of France under Charles VII. She became:

- Protector of the dauphin
- Opponent of Isabeau and Burgundy
- Patron of Joan
- Diplomatic architect of national recovery
- Hidden founder of Charles VII’s successful government

This narrative restored a neglected female political actor but sometimes converted inference into certainty.

### Women’s and queenship history

Modern scholarship treats Yolande as an example of power exercised through:

- Motherhood
- Widowhood
- Regency
- Household government
- Property
- Marriage brokerage
- Patronage
- Informal access to kings

This approach rejects the assumption that lack of a crowned sovereign office meant lack of political authority.

### Revisionist limits

Recent scholarship also cautions against solving the documentary invisibility of women by assigning every successful event to a hidden female mastermind. Yolande’s importance is demonstrable; the exact boundaries of her agency remain case-specific.

### Dynastic legacy

Her descendants and children linked the House of Anjou to:

- The French monarchy through Marie and Charles VII
- Brittany through her daughter Yolande
- Lorraine through René and Isabella
- England through her granddaughter Margaret of Anjou
- Later French royal succession through Louis XI

Her long-term influence therefore operated as much through kinship as through individual policy.

## Atlas Connections

Only intersections supported by sources are included. Mere contemporaneity, indirect thematic resemblance, or presence in the same broad geographical area has been excluded.

### Joan of Arc

**Evidentiary label: Documented political intersection; direct personal meeting uncertain.**

Joan entered the royal court at Chinon in February or March 1429, when Yolande was Charles VII’s mother-in-law and a leading member of the Angevin political network. Yolande is associated with the matrons’ examination of Joan and with the financing and organization surrounding the relief of Orléans.

The evidence supports institutional and political connection. It does not establish the popular claim that Yolande discovered, trained, or invented Joan. Philippe Contamine deliberately characterized their relationship as the “improbable meeting” of two political trajectories.

### Gilles de Montmorency-Laval, Baron de Rais

The atlas name **Gilles Louis Rene de Montmorency-Laval** corresponds to **Gilles de Rais**.

**Evidentiary label: Reported political and military association within the same Valois-Angevin coalition; no close personal relationship documented.**

Gilles served in Charles VII’s forces during Joan of Arc’s campaigns and was made marshal of France in 1429. He therefore operated within the military coalition supported by Yolande’s house.

Later accounts also place men connected to Gilles’s political world among the armed participants in the 1433 removal of La Trémoille, a change favorable to Yolande and Richemont. This does not prove that Yolande and Gilles planned political violence together, nor that she knew of the crimes for which Gilles was prosecuted in 1440.

### Claude des Armoises

**Evidentiary label: No documented personal connection.**

Claude appeared after Joan’s execution claiming to be Joan of Arc. Although her story belongs to the wider mythology of the political world in which Yolande had operated, no consulted source documents a meeting, correspondence, patronage, or family tie between Claude and Yolande.

### Charlemagne, Charles Martel, Clovis, and other remote dynastic figures on the roster

**Evidentiary label: No qualifying connection.**

Medieval royal genealogies could trace Yolande’s houses to earlier Frankish rulers, but remote genealogical descent is not treated here as a documented personal crossing. No meeting, correspondence, collaboration, or acknowledged formative influence was possible.

### Remaining atlas roster

No other roster member was found to have a securely documented meeting, correspondence, collaboration, rivalry, patronage relationship, shared institution, close family tie, or explicit acknowledged influence involving Yolande. Given the roster’s chronological breadth, absence is the principal finding.
