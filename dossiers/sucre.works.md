## Works by Antonio José de Sucre

### Documentary obra: correspondence and official papers

- **1812–1830:** *Correspondence, dispatches, orders, reports, proclamations, decrees, speeches, and private papers*. This is Sucre’s principal surviving obra: thousands of individually dated documents rather than a book conceived as a unified work. The standard documentary edition is *Archivo de Sucre* (fourteen volumes plus an index, 6,838 documents); its editors describe it as containing practically everything then known to have been written by him, although the archive also preserves incoming and jointly signed material, so not every item is solely his composition.[1][2]

- **1812–1830:** *Private and political correspondence*. Letters to Bolívar, relatives, officers, civil authorities, clergy, and foreign representatives form a distinct epistolary body within the archive, but not a work arranged or titled by Sucre. *De mi propia mano* is a modern selection—235 documents in its expanded edition—not an authorial book; its individual headings are editorial phrases extracted from the documents.[1]

- **1821–1830:** *Copiadores, borradores, oficios y otros documentos*. These manuscript copybooks and drafts preserve the working record of Sucre’s military and governmental writing. They resist classification as finished works: many are secretarial copies, some bear autograph corrections, and the surviving corpus is conditioned by archival loss and dispersal.[1][3]

### Protocols and negotiated instruments

- **26 November 1820:** *Tratado de Armisticio*. A protocol suspending hostilities between Colombia and the Spanish forces. Sucre was one of Colombia’s three commissioners, alongside Pedro Briceño Méndez and José Gabriel Pérez; it is therefore a negotiated and collective instrument, not a solely authored Sucre text.[1][4]

- **26 November 1820:** *Tratado de Regularización de la Guerra*. A humanitarian protocol prescribing the treatment of prisoners, wounded personnel, civilians, and the dead. Tradition frequently calls Sucre its drafter, but the signed text belongs to six commissioners representing the two belligerents; sole authorship should not be inferred from Sucre’s decisive role in its negotiation.[1][4][5]

- **25 May 1822:** *Capitulación de Quito*. A negotiated surrender instrument following Pichincha, signed through commissioners for Sucre and the defeated royalist commander Melchor de Aymerich and ratified by the principals. Its articles and replies make it a jointly produced juridical act rather than a conventional authored work.[1]

- **9 December 1824:** *Capitulación de Ayacucho*. A negotiated military and political settlement between José de Canterac and Sucre after the battle. Canterac’s proposed articles and Sucre’s concessions and qualifications are visibly separate within the document, making shared, dialogic authorship essential to its classification.[1]

### Manifestos, proclamations, and military narratives

- **1819–1830:** *Proclamations to soldiers and peoples*. This is a dispersed body of manifesto-like texts: public speech acts intended to command, persuade, commemorate victories, or constitute political allegiance. They were issued separately and only later gathered by editors; the corpus has no authorial collective title.[1]

- **24 May 1822:** *Parte de la batalla de Pichincha*. An official battle report: simultaneously a military record, an account of victory, and an act assigning public credit to particular units and combatants. Surviving versions and copies belong to Sucre’s official documentary corpus rather than to an independently published book.[1]

- **7 October 1823:** *Orden y proclama dadas por el general Antonio José de Sucre al ejército de su mando*. A compound military object—general order and proclamation—abolishing honors, treatments, and distinctions within the army. It acts upon military practice and therefore combines protocol with manifesto.[1]

- **9–10 December 1824:** *Partes y proclamas de Ayacucho*. Reports to Bolívar and public addresses to the victorious army narrate the battle while also distributing honor and declaring its continental meaning. They survive as several documents, not as one stable authorial work.[1]

- **1828–1829:** *La campaña de los treinta días: documentos de la guerra de 1828–1829*. This title belongs to a later documentary compilation of Sucre’s campaign papers, not to a book composed under that title by Sucre. The underlying dispatches, orders, and reports are his military-administrative obra; their later binding into a volume is editorial.[2]

### Constitutive acts and governmental protocols

- **9 February 1825:** *Decreto de convocatoria a la Asamblea General de Diputados de las Provincias del Alto Perú*. A constitutive protocol directing the provinces to elect an assembly empowered to deliberate their political future. It helped make possible the institutional appearance of Bolivia, but the republic itself was a collective historical creation and should not be treated as Sucre’s individual “work.”[6][7]

- **1 July 1825; read 10 July 1825:** *Memoria que el General en Jefe del Ejército Libertador, encargado de los departamentos del Alto Perú, presenta a la Asamblea General de los mismos el día de su instalación*. A governmental memoria accounting for Sucre’s conduct and explaining the assembly’s political situation. The manuscript is dated 1 July, while Sucre’s autograph note records its reading at the assembly’s installation on 10 July.[1][8]

- **1825–1828:** *Decrees and administrative acts of the government of Bolivia*. This is a body of operative texts rather than a single authored work: appointments, fiscal and military measures, institutional regulations, and executive decrees issued under Sucre’s authority. Authorship varies—some texts are autograph, some corrected by him, and others were prepared by ministers or secretaries and merely promulgated or signed by the president.[1]

- **3 February–5 March 1826:** *Decrees establishing colleges of sciences and arts and institutions for orphaned children*. Thirteen educational and welfare protocols, issued across Bolivia’s departments, constitute an institutional project rather than a single text. Their status as Sucre’s personal compositions is not uniform, although they belong to the acts of his government preserved in the documentary corpus.[1]

- **1826:** *Instructions to Bolivia’s representatives at the Congress of Panama*. A diplomatic protocol defining the delegation’s mission and Sucre’s project of American confederation. The modern heading “Con el sello de la cultura moral y de la libertad” is editorial, not Sucre’s original title.[1]

- **2 August 1828:** *Último mensaje del general Sucre al Congreso de Bolivia*. A resignation-era presidential message and public account of his administration. It is both retrospective narrative and political act; later editions also call it *Mensaje de despedida*, but that publishing title should not be mistaken for a separate work.[1][2]

### Personal legal act

- **10 November 1829:** *Testamento cerrado*. A private juridical protocol reportedly sealed and entrusted to Vicente Aguirre before Sucre departed for the Congreso Admirable. Its documentary history generated competing claims about the “true” testament, and later publications distinguish it from other testamentary testimony and copies; its text and status should therefore be cited with that dispute intact.[2][9][10]

### Institutional and military projects that resist treatment as authored works

- **1821–1822:** *Campaign for the liberation of Quito*. A military-political project culminating at Pichincha, planned and commanded by Sucre but made collectively by armies, local allies, governments, and negotiators. Calling the campaign a personal “work” would conceal that distributed agency; Sucre’s own made objects within it are the plans, orders, reports, proclamations, and capitulation preserved in the archive.[1][11]

- **1823–1824:** *Campaign of Peru and Ayacucho*. A military project culminating in the battle and capitulation of Ayacucho. It is neither a text nor Sucre’s solitary creation; its traceable Sucrean obra consists of operational correspondence, orders, battle reports, proclamations, and his portion of the capitulation.[1]

- **1825–1828:** *Organization of the Republic of Bolivia*. A state-building project encompassing representative government, administration, education, public finance, and the army. Sucre made many of its operative documents and institutions, but neither Bolivia nor its 1826 Constitution is properly his individual work: the constitution was drafted by Simón Bolívar and adopted and modified through Bolivian institutions.[1][12]
