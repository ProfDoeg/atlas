# Alexander Baring: Research Dossier

## Scope, method, and evidentiary cautions

This dossier reconstructs the life of Alexander Baring, 1st Baron Ashburton, from published primary documents, archival descriptions, parliamentary records, historical scholarship, museum and heritage research, and bibliographical catalogues. It distinguishes Alexander's personal acts from acts of the partnership that he led or had left, and contemporary documentation from later family or institutional legend.

Two recurring date discrepancies require notice. Modern parliamentary, museum, peerage, and archival authorities generally give **27 October 1774** as Baring's birth date; older reference works and some modern heritage pages give **1773**. A genealogical transcription says that he was baptized at St Gabriel Fenchurch on 25 November and gives 1774. This dossier uses 1774 while flagging the variant. His death is securely dated **12 May 1848** in modern records, although the 1885 *Dictionary of National Biography* article and derivative texts printed 13 May.

The most important correction to the capsule description is one of sequence. Baring did negotiate the Louisiana financing personally, but not solely while Britain and France were at war. The bankers' preparatory work and the Paris negotiations began during the Peace of Amiens. War resumed in May 1803; Baring then crossed to the United States and completed the American side while the war continued. British policy toward the remittance changed during the operation.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Alexander Baring.
- **Later style and title:** The Right Honourable Alexander Baring, 1st Baron Ashburton, of Ashburton in the County of Devon; Privy Counsellor; often called Lord Ashburton after 1835.
- **Commercial style:** Mr Alexander Baring; partner and eventually senior or managing partner of Francis Baring & Co / Baring Brothers & Co.
- **No documented pseudonym or stable nickname** has been found. “The sixth great power” referred to the House of Baring, not to Alexander personally.
- **Born:** 27 October 1774, normally identified as London, probably at or near the family's Mincing Lane commercial residence. Variant in older literature: 27 October 1773.
- **Baptized:** reported as 25 November 1774 at the parish of St Gabriel Fenchurch, City of London.
- **Died:** 12 May 1848, aged 73, at Longleat House, Wiltshire, the seat of his son-in-law's family.
- **Burial:** St John the Evangelist churchyard, Northington, Hampshire, according to the church memorial record.
- **Nationality and allegiance:** British; English merchant banker, politician, peer, and diplomat.
- **Principal professions:** merchant banker, sovereign-loan contractor, international merchant, land investor and trustee, Bank of England director, parliamentarian, cabinet minister, diplomat, art collector, and museum trustee.
- **Political trajectory:** entered Parliament as a broadly Whig commercial member, increasingly separated from the Whigs over parliamentary reform, and became a Tory/Conservative.
- **Principal residences and properties:** London (including 82 Piccadilly/Bath House); The Grange, Northington, Hampshire; Rudhall Manor, Herefordshire; interests in Buckenham Tofts, Norfolk, and other estates; long periods in Amsterdam, Philadelphia, Paris, Washington, and other American and European commercial centers.
- **Arms/title succession:** the new United Kingdom barony was created by letters patent dated 10 April 1835. It passed at his death to his eldest son, William Bingham Baring.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal ancestry

The English Baring dynasty descended from **Johann/John Baring**, a German Lutheran wool merchant from Bremen who migrated to England in the early eighteenth century and settled in Exeter. Johann married **Elizabeth Vowler**. Their sons John and Francis developed the Exeter family's textile and merchant operations; Francis transferred the family's center of gravity to London.

Alexander's father, **Sir Francis Baring, 1st Baronet** (1740–1810), was the central founder of the London merchant house established as John & Francis Baring & Co in 1762. Francis became a major government contractor, East India Company figure, financier, and MP. His commercial formation combined textile exports, colonial commodities, government supply, marine insurance, and eventually state finance. The father's wealth and network were Alexander's principal institutional inheritance.

The family's origins were mercantile rather than aristocratic. The baronetcy given to Francis in 1793 and Alexander's peerage in 1835 record the family's conversion of commercial capital into political and landed status.

### Mother and maternal connections

Alexander's mother was **Harriet Herring** (1750–1804), daughter of the Croydon merchant William Herring and Dorothy Dawson. The Herring family included a connection to Thomas Herring, Archbishop of Canterbury. Harriet married Francis Baring in 1767.

### Siblings

Surviving pedigrees do not always enumerate the family identically, but the children of Francis and Harriet included:

- **Dorothy Elizabeth Baring** (1771–1859), who married Pierre César Labouchère of Hope & Co in 1796. This marriage made Alexander's Amsterdam colleague and Louisiana collaborator his brother-in-law.
- **Sir Thomas Baring, 2nd Baronet** (1772–1848), initially sent to India and later a partner and collector.
- **Alexander Baring** (1774–1848).
- **Henry Baring** (1776–1848), banker and MP, who married first Maria Matilda Bingham, sister of Alexander's wife, and then Cecilia Anne Windham. The Baring brothers therefore married the two Bingham sisters.
- **William Baring** (1779–1820).
- **George Baring** (1781–1854), associated in some accounts with the Asian trading world.
- Daughters commonly listed as **Maria, Lydia, Frances**, and/or others whose dates and ordering vary in genealogical summaries.

Three brothers—Thomas, Henry, and Alexander—died within weeks of one another in spring 1848: Thomas on 3 April, Henry on 13 April, and Alexander on 12 May.

### Marriage and the Bingham-Willing alliance

On **23 August 1798**, in Philadelphia, Alexander married **Ann Louisa Bingham** (6 January 1782–5 December 1848), daughter of US Senator **William Bingham** and **Ann Willing Bingham**. She is also called Anne, Anna, or Louisa in different sources.

William Bingham was a merchant, privateer investor, land speculator, banker, and political figure, commonly described as one of the richest Americans of his generation. Ann's maternal grandfather, **Thomas Willing**, headed the Bank of North America and the First Bank of the United States. The union gave Alexander an intimate connection to the Philadelphia Federalist commercial elite. It also brought substantial property. A frequently repeated figure of **$900,000** for Ann's dowry appears in later institutional histories; it should be treated as an estimate rather than a verified marriage-settlement total unless the settlement itself is consulted.

The marriage was simultaneously personal, dynastic, and commercial. Alexander had been dispatched to negotiate the purchase and management of Bingham's immense Maine holdings. His brother Henry married Ann's sister Maria Matilda in 1802. When William Bingham died in 1804, Alexander and Henry became among the trustees of the Bingham estate. Their descendants remained involved in American land administration for generations.

### Children

Peerage accounts normally give **nine children—five sons and four daughters**. Names and details are:

1. **William Bingham Baring** (1799–1864), born in Philadelphia; succeeded as 2nd Baron Ashburton; MP, businessman, and later Paymaster General. He married Lady Harriet Mary Montagu and, after her death, Louisa Caroline Stewart-Mackenzie.
2. **Francis Baring** (1800–1868), born in Philadelphia; succeeded his brother as 3rd Baron Ashburton; married Hortense Eugénie Claire Maret, daughter of Hugues-Bernard Maret, duc de Bassano.
3. **Harriet Baring** (1804–1892), married Henry Thynne, 3rd Marquess of Bath. This family connection explains Alexander's death at Longleat.
4. **Frederick Baring** (1806–1868), Anglican clergyman and rector of Itchen Stoke; married Frederica Ashton.
5. **Anne Eugenia Baring** (d. 1839), married Humphrey St John-Mildmay.
6. **Alexander Baring** (1810–1832), naval officer, who died aboard HMS *Alfred* in the Mediterranean.
7. **Louisa Emily Baring** (d. 1888).
8. **Lydia Emily Baring** (d. 1868).
9. **Arthur Baring** (1818–1838), died unmarried.

Some online trees report ten children, but the standard peerage tradition and the contemporary obituary tradition say nine. No reliable evidence of a partner or children outside the marriage was located.

## Childhood and Early Life

Alexander grew up inside a merchant-banking household whose home, countinghouse, and international correspondence were closely connected. His father's firm already traded across Europe, India, the Caribbean, and North America. Alexander's elder brother Thomas received an East India Company appointment; Alexander was consequently trained for the London and Atlantic business.

Evidence for a named school, university, or formal degree before his career has not been found. The post-1832 History of Parliament database classifies him as **privately educated**. His education was chiefly commercial: languages, accounts, exchange, correspondence, political economy, commodity markets, sovereign credit, and the law and practice of bills and securities.

His strongest early mentors were:

- **Sir Francis Baring**, who trained him, financed him, criticized his risk-taking, and eventually relied on his independent judgment.
- **Henry Hope**, senior figure of Hope & Co, the pre-eminent Amsterdam issuing house. Alexander worked with Hopes and became a favorite of Henry Hope.
- **Pierre César Labouchère**, Hope partner, later Alexander's brother-in-law, and one of the principal strategists of their joint operations.
- The commercial and political circle around **William Bingham**, Thomas Willing, Robert Morris, and Philadelphia finance.

## Education and Formation: Amsterdam, 1794–1795

By 1794 Alexander was working with **Hope & Co in Amsterdam**. The French invasion of the Netherlands forced the Hopes to move men and assets toward London. This was an unusually advanced apprenticeship: Hopes dominated the Dutch sovereign-bond market and had experience syndicating foreign loans to European investors. The techniques Alexander later used for Louisiana and post-Waterloo France—joint accounts, underwriting, bearer securities, geographical distribution of risk, and government negotiation—were learned in this milieu.

The Hope-Baring partnership was also embedded in colonial commerce. Hope & Co and Barings financed commodities and properties connected to Atlantic slavery. The connection is important because Alexander's later public defense of gradual rather than immediate emancipation did not arise at a distance from plantation finance.

## Early American Career, 1795–1801

### Mission to William Bingham

Late in 1795, aged about twenty-one, Alexander went to the United States to inspect and negotiate American land investments, especially William Bingham's Maine acreage. Bingham had acquired huge tracts between the Penobscot and St Croix rivers and was burdened by their financing and development. Barings and Hopes sought a large investment at a discount and needed a trusted agent on the ground.

Alexander lived principally in Philadelphia but traveled to inspect land and negotiate with agents. A surviving memorandum to Bingham dated **2 February 1796** addressed the complications of alien landholding. He visited the Maine property and worked on plans for surveys, maps, settlement, lumbering, roads, and resale. The scheme was not an immediate colonization success. The remoteness of the tracts, legal problems, weak settlement demand, and the need for long-term management slowed realization. It nevertheless embedded Barings deeply in American land and generated valuable political and financial relationships.

Place names preserve this association: **Baring Plantation** and the town of **Alexander** in Maine were named in the Baring orbit. This is a documented commemorative geography, not evidence that he founded modern towns single-handedly.

### American network

Between 1795 and 1801 Baring developed contacts among Federalist merchants, financiers, and politicians. The central nodes were William Bingham, Thomas Willing, the Biddle family, Robert Gilmor, the Willing family, and land agents in Pennsylvania and Maine. His marriage made the network familial.

He returned to Britain in 1801, but America remained the defining specialization of his business life. He understood American credit from inside both its merchant elite and its chronic shortage of long-term capital.

## Chronological Life History

### 1801–1803: return to Europe and rise in the family firm

On returning to London, Alexander joined the management of the family house, then styled Francis Baring & Co. The exact legal dates of partnership and changes of style differ slightly among compressed accounts. The name **Baring Brothers & Co** came into use in 1807, with Alexander, Thomas, and Henry associated in the partnership. After Sir Francis's death in 1810, Alexander became the dominant head of the house.

In 1803 the firm became the European financial agent of the United States government. The appointment was both a cause and consequence of the Louisiana operation.

### 1803–1804: financing the Louisiana Purchase

#### Preliminary design

Spain had secretly retroceded Louisiana to France under the Treaty of San Ildefonso. The United States initially sought New Orleans and the Floridas, fearing loss of access to the Mississippi. Napoleon's failure in Saint-Domingue, renewed war with Britain, and inability to defend Louisiana made a larger sale attractive.

On **30 January 1803**, Labouchère wrote to Alexander about a structure by which Hopes and Barings could finance the cession. Larry Neal's reconstruction of the archived papers argues that the houses anticipated acting separately with the French and American governments and contemplated payments to intermediaries described by the historian as bribes to “grease the wheels.” This is not the same as evidence that Alexander personally paid a named official; the surviving planning correspondence establishes that corrupt inducements were contemplated, while execution and recipients require further archival proof.

#### Paris negotiations

Alexander Baring and Labouchère went to Paris in **April 1803**. They worked alongside the American envoys **Robert R. Livingston and James Monroe** and Napoleon's treasury minister **François Barbé-Marbois**. Because communication with London and Amsterdam took weeks and letters could be intercepted, Sir Francis and Henry Hope gave their representatives wide discretion: “We must leave the decision to Alexander,” the houses agreed.

Baring argued that France's demand of 100 million francs was beyond what the United States or European investors could support. The total price was reduced to **80 million francs / $15 million**. Of this, the United States would assume **20 million francs / $3.75 million** in American citizens' claims against France. The remaining **60 million francs / $11.25 million** would be paid in US six-percent stock.

The purchase treaty and claims convention were agreed on **30 April 1803**. A separate banker-government agreement was executed in early May. Barings and Hopes contracted to buy the $11.25 million face amount of American bonds from France for approximately **52 million francs**, roughly 86.5–86.7 percent of face value, then place them in Amsterdam and London. The bonds paid six percent, with interest in Amsterdam, London, or Paris, and were redeemable on a schedule extending to 1822/23.

#### War resumes

Britain declared war on France in May 1803. The transaction now risked financing Britain's enemy. Sir Francis consulted Prime Minister **Henry Addington**. In June, Addington reportedly approved the strategic result, saying Britain might wisely pay £1 million to transfer Louisiana from France to the United States. By **16 December 1803**, amid fear of French invasion, Addington ordered Sir Francis to decline participation in remittances to France on the Louisiana debt and to divert any funds available for that purpose.

The archival exhibition states that what followed is not fully clear. The order prohibited remittances; it did not necessarily prohibit selling American bonds. Barings circulated a London prospectus in April 1804. A careful account therefore cannot say simply that the British government continuously licensed direct wartime payment to Napoleon. British strategic approval, later restriction, continental payment mechanisms, bond distribution, and the joint Hope account all formed part of the solution.

#### Washington completion

After Paris, Alexander crossed the Atlantic “to complete the agreement in a regular manner.” In Washington he negotiated the formal delivery arrangements with the US Treasury. His letter to Sir Francis of **21 October 1803** described congressional ratification and a delay caused by Spain's protest against the cession. Congress authorized the bonds in November. The first tranche was delivered to Baring in Washington on **16 January 1804**.

Napoleon needed cash before the scheduled process. The bankers made or arranged a **10-million-franc advance in July 1803**. In April 1804 they compressed the remaining installment schedule from twenty-three months into days in return for another discount of 1.65 million francs. By late April they were discharged from their obligation to France.

#### Risk and profit

The exact final division of profit is not established. The firms bought the bonds well below face and reportedly sold the market allocation at or above par. The initial gross discount was about **8 million francs / $1.5 million**, before expenses, risk, later concessions, and the division between houses. In November 1803, $4.75 million of stock remained unallocated—more than the Barings partnership capital and a large part of its total balance sheet—so the underwriting risk was real.

Baring did not “buy Louisiana” in the territorial-law sense. France conveyed sovereignty to the United States. Barings and Hopes purchased the US securities delivered to France and financed their conversion into cash. The popular formulation that America bought Louisiana “from Barings” describes the intermediation economically, not the legal vendor.

### 1804–1810: Bank of England, Parliament, and Anglo-American trade

Baring became a **director of the Bank of England in 1805**, serving until 1817 with rotation customary for directors. He entered the House of Commons for **Taunton in November 1806**. His first recorded Hansard contribution is in February 1808 on trade and navigation.

In **1808** he published *An Inquiry into the Causes and Consequences of the Orders in Council; and an Examination of the Conduct of Great Britain towards the Neutral Commerce of America*. He attacked British restrictions on neutral trade, defended American commercial grievances, and warned that the policy would push the United States toward war. It ran to multiple British and American editions and is his principal sustained work of political economy.

His position did not make him a simple American partisan. It reflected a merchant's view that maritime coercion, seizures, and retaliatory restrictions damaged British trade and strategic interests. The War of 1812 proved that the Anglo-American rupture he warned about was possible.

### 1810–1815: leadership, War of 1812 finance, and transatlantic contradiction

Sir Francis died in September 1810. Alexander became the leading partner. Hope's London office and the Baring operations became more closely integrated after Henry Hope's death in 1811.

During the War of 1812, Barings remained closely tied to American finance even while Britain and the United States were enemies. The US **$16 million loan of 1813** was rescued by a syndicate centered on Stephen Girard, David Parish, and John Jacob Astor. Scholarship on Parish identifies Alexander Baring as part of the transatlantic network and correspondence behind the operation, though the American contractors—not Baring alone—made the subscription that prevented fiscal collapse. Barings' position as US European agent and its trade relationships survived the conflict.

This duality—nationally British, financially transnational—is not evidence of a secret supranational command. Merchant banks could maintain private and governmental relationships across political crises, subject to blockades, licenses, interception, and state intervention. Their profit depended on making otherwise incompatible payment systems connect.

### 1815–1820: post-Waterloo France and the “sixth great power”

After Waterloo, France owed indemnities and occupation costs to the victorious allies. French public credit was broken. Alexander, working with Hopes and in contact with the Duke of Wellington and the duc de Richelieu, became the main foreign banker in the restoration of French credit.

The 1817–1818 loans converted immediate political claims into marketable French rentes held across Europe. Research by Kim Oosterlinck and co-authors shows the relationship between military occupation and the perceived security of the loans: Baring regarded allied troops as protection for repayment, while Wellington helped create incentives for France, allies, and bankers to avoid default. Baring at one point halted negotiations when the risk-return terms did not satisfy him, despite allied political interest in completion.

Historic England's synthesis estimates **profits over £700,000** to Baring Brothers from wartime and French-reparation finance, but this is a firm-level estimate, not Alexander's personal income. The operation made the house Europe's best-known merchant bank.

The famous saying that Europe had six great powers—Britain, France, Austria, Prussia, Russia, and **Baring Brothers**—is attributed to **Armand-Emmanuel du Plessis, duc de Richelieu**, French chief minister. The line circulated widely in nineteenth-century financial lore. Its precise first utterance is difficult to document; it is best treated as a contemporary or near-contemporary bon mot whose circulation accurately records the perceived political weight of the house, not literal sovereign status.

### 1817–1825: The Grange, free trade, political economy, and sovereign loans

In **1817** Baring bought **The Grange at Northington, Hampshire**, a Greek Revival house and park. He employed Robert Smirke and Charles Robert Cockerell on extensions, a dining room, and an innovative iron-and-glass conservatory. He expanded gardens and parkland. The architecture transformed new banking wealth into landed and classical display.

In **1820**, Baring presented the **London Merchants' Petition** to the Commons. Drafted by the merchant-economist **Thomas Tooke**, it stated a general case against commercial restrictions and discriminatory protection. It became an important document in the long British movement toward freer trade. Baring belonged to the network around Tooke, David Ricardo, and the Political Economy Club, although his monetary views did not always follow Ricardo.

His free-trade principles were selective and changed with circumstances. He supported freer commerce but defended colonial property, resisted some currency contraction, and later opposed Peel's Corn Law repeal and the Bank Charter Act.

Barings expanded sovereign finance: loans or issues for France, Russia, Austria, American states and institutions, and the **first external bond issue for Buenos Aires/Argentina in 1824**. The firm's modern timeline credits Barings with managing that Argentine issue. Alexander was still the leading partner, but individual negotiation details should not be assigned to him without the relevant house papers.

### Slavery debates and plantation finance, 1823–1832

Baring spoke repeatedly against immediate emancipation. In the Commons debate of **15 May 1823** on Thomas Fowell Buxton's motion, he criticized abolitionists William Wilberforce and Buxton for, in his view, exaggerating colonial conditions and lacking plantation experience. His speech was printed separately. He advocated amelioration and eventual, locally managed change rather than an immediate parliamentary end to slavery.

On **14 and 20 April 1826**, he presented petitions from Antigua's council and assembly and London West India merchants defending colonial property and asking that change proceed by “moderate and well digested measures.” In 1828 he rejected the abolitionist denial of a property right in enslaved people. In April 1831 he argued that immediate emancipation would destroy British West Indian capital, force Britain to consume slave-produced foreign sugar, and possibly revive the transatlantic trade. He asserted that published accounts generalized exceptional atrocities into ordinary life. On 24 May 1832 he again opposed immediate abolition.

These were documented political positions, not merely guilt by association. They coincided with material connections:

- During Alexander's partnership, Barings lent against plantation and slave property and dealt in commodities including sugar, cotton, coffee, indigo, rum, and tobacco.
- The firm was heavily exposed to **Wolfert Katz**, a Berbice planter, and to the American merchant and enslaver **Burrill Carnes**, taking security connected to enslaved people.
- A heritage study reports Baring Brothers' ownership or control of plantations in St Croix and approximately 500 enslaved people in 1831, though the timing crosses Alexander's retirement and must be attributed carefully to the firm.
- At mid-1830, Barings reportedly had about **£250,000** in West Indian mortgages—roughly half its capital.
- The National Gallery/UCL research records compensation claims involving Alexander as trustee or beneficiary after emancipation. For the **Spooners estate on St Kitts**, claims listed Charles Bosanquet, James William Freshfield, Sir John Bosanquet, Alexander Baring, Jeremiah Harman, Sir Edward Hyde East, and John Pearse in trust/beneficial structures, first for 126 and later 164 enslaved people. A Spring Garden counterclaim connected to Carnes and 64 enslaved people failed. The exact personal sum economically received by Alexander cannot safely be equated with the gross awards without resolving trustee and beneficiary shares.
- A recent art-history study reports Alexander as proprietor of the **Mon Bijou estate** in 1824, with 111 enslaved people listed. This finding should be checked against the original slave register for the capacity in which “proprietor” was recorded.

Baring did not leave a documented abolitionist record. The National Gallery project states “No known connections with abolition.” The eventual withdrawal of Barings from some British Caribbean estates appears in the scholarship as commercial risk management, not proven humanitarian divestment.

The Louisiana financing also had a structural connection to slavery: US acquisition enabled the westward expansion of plantation agriculture and the interstate slave trade. That consequence does not establish that Baring designed the purchase for that purpose; it establishes that his finance facilitated a territorial transformation whose slave economy became central to Barings' later American commodity business.

### 1825–1830: crisis, monetary policy, and retirement from the house

The Panic of 1825 produced bank failures and a severe liquidity crisis. Baring was a leading parliamentary authority on currency, small notes, banking, and commercial credit. He opposed policies he believed would contract circulation too abruptly and harm trade. His monetary thought was practical and balance-sheet-centered: money and credit were not neutral veils, and forced deflation could intensify distress.

American **Joshua Bates** became a partner in 1828 and gradually assumed the leading American role. Alexander withdrew from daily management during the mid-to-late 1820s and **formally retired in 1830**. Acts of Baring Brothers after retirement—especially 1830s cotton finance, slave-compensation claims, and dealings with Nicholas Biddle—should not automatically be treated as Alexander's personal decisions, though he retained family, reputational, and sometimes trust interests.

His wealth moved increasingly into land, art, and politics. He bought Bath House in Piccadilly in 1821, acquired Buckenham Tofts and other properties, and bought **Rudhall Manor** in Herefordshire around 1830. A later property history estimates his estate at death at **£800,000**; this is an estimate, not a probate inventory reproduced here. His will was proved on 31 August 1848.

### Parliamentary representation and borough control

Baring's Commons constituencies were:

- **Taunton:** November 1806–June 1826.
- **Callington:** June 1826–July 1831.
- **Thetford:** May 1831–December 1832.
- **North Essex:** December 1832–April 1835.

Callington and Thetford were small, patron-controlled constituencies; hostile descriptions called them rotten boroughs. Baring owned or influenced electoral interests, and opposition to the Reform Bill protected a political system from which he benefited directly. Contemporary caricatures portrayed his party movement and borough interest as political theft or ratting.

He opposed Grey's Reform Bill throughout 1831–1832 and moved from the Whig side toward the Tories. During the May 1832 crisis, he agreed in principle—after hesitation—to serve as Chancellor of the Exchequer if Wellington could form a government. Wellington failed. The anecdote that Baring would rather face “a thousand devils” than the reformed Commons belongs to the political tradition around this episode; its exact wording and occasion should be verified in contemporary correspondence before treating it as verbatim.

### 1834–1835: cabinet office and peerage

In Sir Robert Peel's first ministry Baring served as:

- **President of the Board of Trade**, 15 December 1834–8 April 1835.
- **Master of the Mint**, 23 December 1834–8 April 1835.
- **Privy Counsellor**.

The ministry was brief and lacked a Commons majority. Baring's tenure therefore produced no long independent program. On its fall he was raised to the peerage as **Baron Ashburton**, letters patent dated 10 April 1835.

The title had family symbolism. An earlier Baron Ashburton was **John Dunning**, lawyer and politician; Dunning's family was connected by marriage to the Barings. Alexander's new creation was legally distinct.

### 1835–1841: House of Lords, museums, currency, and art

Baring sat in the Lords until death. Hansard records extensive interventions on banking, paper currency, railways, trade, bankruptcy, Ireland, the Corn Laws, colonial affairs, and foreign policy.

He became a **British Museum trustee in 1829** and a **National Gallery trustee from 1835 to 1848**. He also participated in the Fine Arts Commission, appearing in the group portrait of the commissioners. He collected Dutch, Flemish, Italian, Spanish, and other Old Master paintings, displayed at Bath House and The Grange. Gustav Friedrich Waagen admired the Bath House collection. Paintings with Baring provenance later entered major public and private collections; the National Gallery of Art and National Gallery provenance databases identify individual works.

The house and collection were also material expressions of wealth derived from state loans, American land and securities, colonial commodities, and plantation-connected credit. This does not mean every purchase can be matched to a specific slave-derived pound; it means the fortune was integrated and its documented revenue streams included such business.

### 1842: special mission to the United States

#### Appointment and arrival

Relations between Britain and the United States had deteriorated over the Maine–New Brunswick boundary, the Caroline incident, Alexander McLeod's prosecution, Great Lakes boundaries, maritime search and the slave trade, impressment, the *Creole* revolt, and extradition. The Peel government and Foreign Secretary Lord Aberdeen chose Ashburton because he knew America, had American family connections, and was personally trusted in elite circles.

He was appointed minister plenipotentiary on a special mission and arrived in **Washington on 4 April 1842**. This was his most important public diplomatic office. He negotiated directly with Secretary of State **Daniel Webster**, while Webster consulted President John Tyler, the Senate, Maine and Massachusetts representatives, and Ashburton consulted Aberdeen and British North American interests.

#### Webster–Ashburton Treaty, 9 August 1842

The treaty:

- Divided the disputed Maine–New Brunswick territory: the US State Department summarizes the result as **7,015 square miles to the United States and 5,012 to Britain**. Britain secured the strategically important Halifax–Quebec winter road.
- Defined the border from Lake Superior toward the Lake of the Woods and adjusted other portions of the northern boundary.
- Preserved navigation provisions.
- Established reciprocal extradition for murder, assault with intent to murder, piracy, arson, robbery, forgery, and uttering forged paper.
- Committed each power to maintain a West African naval force of at least eighty guns for slave-trade suppression, instead of granting Britain a general right to search American-flag vessels.
- Resolved the legal principle arising from the *Caroline* affair through exchanged notes: anticipatory self-defense required a necessity that was instant, overwhelming, and left no choice of means or moment for deliberation, followed by proportionality. The formulation became influential in international law.

The Oregon boundary was not settled and remained for the 1846 treaty. The *Creole* case and impressment were handled through understandings or later settlement rather than a straightforward treaty article. The State Department history says Britain eventually paid $110,330 in the *Creole* claims settlement; that payment postdated the mission.

#### Criticism and “secret map” story

In Britain, Palmerston and other opponents accused Ashburton of conceding too much and writing in an overly conciliatory tone. In the United States, Webster used private funds to influence favorable press and showed senators a map said to bear Benjamin Franklin's marking of a boundary favorable to Britain. The so-called Franklin/red-line map became a controversy because Webster did not share it with Ashburton and used it to persuade Americans that compromise was prudent. Its provenance and probative value were disputed.

The map story should not be converted into a claim that Ashburton was knowingly tricked by a definitive secret map. Boundary negotiations relied on multiple contradictory maps, and modern scholarship emphasizes the political compromise rather than a single cartographic revelation.

On **11 April 1843**, the Lords voted thanks to Ashburton for the mission; the Commons followed in May after partisan debate. The treaty was ratified and marked a durable Anglo-American rapprochement.

### 1843–1848: final monetary campaign

Ashburton opposed the **Bank Charter Act 1844**, which separated the Bank of England's issue and banking departments and restricted note issue. During the **Panic of 1847**, he argued that excessive rigidity in currency law aggravated commercial distress. His pamphlet *The Financial and Commercial Crisis Considered* appeared in 1847 and went through at least three English editions and a German translation. He supported a broader metallic and credit basis often described as bimetallist or anti-restrictionist.

This late position put him at odds with Peel's monetary policy and with the free-trade direction of the Conservative government. His opposition to repeal of the Corn Laws also contrasts with his 1820 free-trade petition. The apparent contradiction is real: his political economy combined commercial liberalization with protection of agriculture, colonial capital, and flexible bank credit. It did not develop as a single abstract doctrine.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Baring firms

- **John & Francis Baring & Co** (founded 1762 by his father and uncle): Alexander's inherited institutional base.
- **Francis Baring & Co**: style around the turn of the century.
- **Baring Brothers & Co** (from 1807): Alexander, Thomas, and Henry; Alexander the principal leader after 1810 until his gradual withdrawal and 1830 retirement.
- **Hope & Co**: employer/partnering house and joint-account collaborator; not merely a subsidiary of Barings.

The firm combined acceptance of bills, trade finance, commodity consignments, securities distribution, government contracting, sovereign lending, land investment, agency services, and private banking. It was a partnership with unlimited liability, not a modern limited-liability bank.

### Major documented operations associated with his leadership

- Bingham/Maine land investment, mapping, settlement, and resale.
- European agency for the US government.
- Louisiana Purchase US bond underwriting and placement, 1803–1804.
- American trade and securities during and after the War of 1812.
- British war finance through the Bank of England and government contracting network.
- French stabilization and indemnity loans, 1817–1818.
- Sovereign and public issues involving Russia, Austria, France, Argentina/Buenos Aires, and American borrowers.
- Trade in or finance of tea, sugar, coffee, indigo, cotton, rum, spices, tobacco, flour, wool, copper, and iron.
- Plantation mortgages, securities over enslaved people, and management or ownership of foreclosed estates during his partnership.
- Relationship with the Bank of the United States and Nicholas Biddle. The house correspondence shows agreed credit limits and later tensions. Much of the decisive 1837 rupture was managed after Alexander's retirement by Joshua Bates and other partners.

### Bank of England

Baring served as director from 1805 to 1817. His later criticism of currency restriction did not make him a permanent opponent of the Bank. He had inside knowledge of its discount policy, reserves, government financing, and director rotation.

### Property and architectural projects

- **The Grange, Northington:** purchased 1817; west wing by Robert Smirke; Cockerell dining room and conservatory, 1823–1825; landscaped park, kitchen gardens, prefabricated iron structures, rainwater collection, and advanced horticultural heating/glazing.
- **Bath House, Piccadilly:** bought 1821; London residence and picture gallery.
- **Buckenham Tofts, Norfolk:** acquired around 1821; associated with his Thetford electoral interest.
- **Rudhall Manor, Herefordshire:** bought around 1830.
- Other Bingham trust and American land interests.

No invention or patent by Alexander has been found. The Grange conservatory was technologically innovative, but it was designed and fabricated by architects, gardeners, and Jones & Clark rather than patented by Baring.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Political economy

Baring's thought grew from merchant practice. Persistent elements were opposition to arbitrary trade obstruction, concern for public credit, belief in international capital markets, distrust of forced deflation, and defense of property. These principles could produce liberal and conservative positions in different settings.

His 1808 attack on the Orders in Council and his 1820 presentation of the Merchants' Petition placed him on the liberal-commercial side of British policy. His defense of colonial slave property, borough patronage, opposition to parliamentary reform, later Corn Law position, and resistance to Peel moved him into Tory conservatism.

### Monetary thought

He rejected a purely metallic or automatic account of money when it threatened contraction. In debates surrounding cash resumption, the crises of 1825 and 1847, small notes, and the 1844 act, he emphasized liquidity, credit circulation, prices, and demand. He was neither a systematic academic economist nor a simple inflationist; his evidence came from merchant balance sheets and international settlements.

### Religion

The Baring family had German Lutheran origins but assimilated into the Church of England. Alexander's peerage, parish patronage, son's Anglican clerical career, and burial were Anglican. No evidence was found of a conversion, intense devotional movement, heterodoxy, or a body of theological writing.

### Art

He was a major collector rather than a practicing artist. His museum trusteeships and participation in the Fine Arts Commission connected private collecting to emerging national institutions. His taste favored Old Masters, and contemporary connoisseurs noted the quality of Bath House. Portraits were made by Sir Thomas Lawrence and George Peter Alexander Healy; he also appears in George Hayter's Commons painting and John Partridge's Fine Arts Commissioners group.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, and Financial Questions

### Louisiana and contemplated bribes

The most specific corruption issue comes from Neal's 2024 reconstruction of a January 1803 planning letter: the proposed structure contemplated inducements to key persons in both governments. The documentary status is **a plan discussed in private commercial correspondence**. This dossier has not located proof of completed payment, a named recipient, a criminal inquiry, or a conviction. It should neither be erased nor expanded into an established bribery scheme without the full papers.

### Financing an enemy in wartime

After May 1803, performance of the Louisiana contracts could benefit Napoleonic France. This was known and debated. Addington first regarded removal of France from North America as a strategic British gain; in December he explicitly prohibited British participation in remittances. The firms continued bond marketing and completed their obligation through an incompletely reconstructed mechanism. There is no located prosecution of Baring for treason or illegal enemy trading. The controversy is documented policy conflict, not a criminal finding.

### Slavery and compensation

Baring's anti-emancipation speeches, firm-level plantation finance, slave-property security, and appearances in compensation structures are documented. Later summaries sometimes state that he personally “owned nearly 500 slaves” and received exactly £10,090. The underlying claims include trustees, creditors, counterclaimants, and beneficiaries; gross award totals cannot always be treated as his personal beneficial receipt. The more defensible findings are those itemized above.

### Rotten boroughs and reform

He benefited from and defended unreformed representation and exercised influence over small boroughs. This was legal within the pre-1832 system but was a major contemporary political controversy. Caricatures titled *A Most Extraordinary Rat!* and *A Clear Case of Political Petty Larceny* attest hostile public mythology around his shifts and patronage. No criminal election conviction has been identified.

### Conflicts between public office and finance

His combined roles—banker, Bank of England director, MP, government financial expert, minister, landowner, and diplomatic envoy—would be treated under much more formal conflict rules today. In his era, commercial expertise was a reason for office. No located court or parliamentary finding convicted him of self-dealing. Nevertheless, sovereign-loan policy, currency law, plantation property, and American diplomacy affected markets and assets connected to his class and former house.

### Bank of the United States and Panic of 1837

Barings was a European correspondent and creditor of Nicholas Biddle's bank. It reduced exposure before the Panic and later ended cooperation after credit limits and payment problems. Alexander had retired in 1830; claims that he personally engineered Biddle's 1837 fall or controlled the later cotton operations confuse him with the continuing firm and Joshua Bates.

### No documented criminal case

No evidence was found of Alexander's indictment, conviction, imprisonment, personal bankruptcy, addiction, or participation in political violence. Nor was evidence found of modern-style money laundering, a category that should not be retroactively attached to ordinary nineteenth-century cross-border settlements without proof of concealment of criminal proceeds.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Barings bought Louisiana and sold it to the United States”

1. **Story:** Napoleon sold the territory to Barings, which resold it to America.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** later bank histories, popular finance accounts, and corporate timelines.
3. **Evidence:** Barings and Hopes bought the US bonds from France at a discount and marketed them; the territorial treaty conveyed Louisiana directly from France to the United States.
4. **Assessment:** economically suggestive, legally false if taken literally.
5. **Reputational effect:** magnifies the bank into a quasi-sovereign territorial dealer and obscures Hope & Co, the US Treasury, and French/American negotiators.

### “He financed Napoleon while Britain and France warred”

1. **Story:** a British banker calmly financed Britain's enemy during war.
2. **Origin:** simplified histories of Louisiana and Barings.
3. **Evidence:** preliminary and Paris negotiations occurred during the Peace of Amiens; war resumed before completion; Addington first approved the strategic transfer but later barred remittances. The firms completed the finance and France obtained cash.
4. **Assessment:** substantially true only with the chronology and government intervention restored.
5. **Effect:** used both as celebration of borderless finance and as proof of banker disloyalty.

### “Sixth great power of Europe”

1. **Story:** Richelieu named Barings alongside five states.
2. **Circulation:** nineteenth-century and later financial histories.
3. **Evidence:** widely attributed to the duc de Richelieu after the French loans; precise first text is elusive.
4. **Assessment:** plausible bon mot, not a constitutional fact and not necessarily exact verbatim wording.
5. **Effect:** canonical symbol of merchant-bank power.

### “Baring single-handedly lowered Napoleon's price”

1. **Story:** Alexander personally cut the price from 100 million to 80 million francs.
2. **Circulation:** family/archive narratives and popular purchase histories.
3. **Evidence:** archival material supports his argument that 100 million could not be financed and places him centrally between the parties. Livingston, Monroe, Barbé-Marbois, Napoleon, Labouchère, and the two houses also shaped the outcome.
4. **Assessment:** he was instrumental, not solitary.
5. **Effect:** turns a multilateral negotiation into a banker-hero story.

### Secret Franklin map and Ashburton's supposed humiliation

1. **Story:** Webster possessed a map proving Britain's claim, concealed it, and duped Ashburton into surrendering British territory.
2. **Circulation:** treaty controversy and later diplomatic history.
3. **Evidence:** Webster did show senators a purported Franklin-marked map favorable to the British claim; it was not shared with Ashburton. Many maps conflicted, and provenance was contested.
4. **Assessment:** concealment in Webster's domestic persuasion is documented; decisive fraud causing the treaty is not.
5. **Effect:** furnished British critics with a betrayal/incompetence narrative and American admirers with a Webster cunning narrative.

### “The peerage was payment for banking services”

The temporal proximity of office, political loyalty, commercial eminence, and the 1835 creation invited the usual charge that money bought aristocratic status. The peerage clearly embodied the political ascent of banking wealth. No document located proves a corrupt quid pro quo or cash purchase of the title.

### Conspiracy narratives about a supranational banking cabal

Barings did possess confidential government access, cross-border family partnerships, and the capacity to move sovereign credit during war. Those facts explain why later narratives cast the house as a hidden power above states. The surviving record instead shows negotiation with, dependence on, and sometimes restriction by governments; exposure to market risk; disagreement among partners; and state power capable of blocking remittance. No evidence was found that Alexander belonged to a secret order directing the Louisiana cession, Waterloo settlement, or Anglo-American border.

## Illness, Death, and Estate

No source consulted gives a documented chronic disease, addiction, psychiatric crisis, or specific fatal diagnosis. He remained active in Lords monetary debate through December 1847. He died on **12 May 1848 at Longleat**. Statements of cause beyond age-related illness would be speculative.

His will was proved on 31 August 1848. A later landed-family reconstruction estimates the estate at **£800,000**. The figure should be treated as a contemporary-style estate estimate, not a complete net-worth calculation: partnership withdrawals, trusts, real estate settlements, art, American land, wife-controlled property, and encumbrances complicate any single number.

Ann Louisa survived him by less than seven months, dying on 5 December 1848 at Bay House, Gosport. Their eldest son inherited the barony and The Grange.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Finance

Baring helped establish the model of the nineteenth-century international merchant bank: private partnerships underwriting public debt across several markets, combining political access with reputational capital. Louisiana demonstrated joint Amsterdam–London placement of New World sovereign securities; the French loans demonstrated how private credit could convert reparations into tradable debt.

The Baring house continued until its 1995 collapse under ING ownership. Later corporate histories retrospectively made Alexander part of a continuous brand story. His own period should be separated from later Argentine crises, the 1890 rescue, and Nick Leeson's 1995 trading losses.

### Anglo-American relations

His life links two major settlements: financing the 1803 territorial expansion of the United States and negotiating the 1842 boundary compromise that reduced the risk of another Anglo-American war. The treaty's extradition machinery and *Caroline* correspondence had long afterlives in international law.

### Political economy

He is remembered as the parliamentary spokesman of commercial expertise: an early free-trade petitioner, critic of the Orders in Council, monetary expansionist during crises, opponent of Reform, defender of colonial property, and critic of Peel's later program. Historians therefore resist assigning him to a clean liberal or conservative economic school.

### Slavery reassessment

Older biographies treated slavery chiefly as one parliamentary subject. Recent UCL, National Gallery, Historic England, and art-history research reconstructs the bank's plantation mortgages, enslaved collateral, compensation interests, St Croix holdings, and slave-produced commodity revenues. This has shifted his reputation from neutral financier to an important participant in the financial infrastructure of Atlantic slavery, while also forcing more exact distinctions among personal ownership, trusteeship, creditor security, partnership exposure, and post-retirement firm conduct.

### Art and landscape

The Grange remains a Grade I monument and performance venue. Its Greek Revival form and iron-and-glass additions embody the translation of international finance into British landed culture. His paintings dispersed through descendants, sales, and museums. His trusteeships connect the Baring collecting dynasty to the early National Gallery and British Museum.

### Names and commemorations

Baring and Alexander in Maine retain the geographical memory of the land speculation. Baron Ashburton remains a hereditary title. The financial bon mot about Europe's sixth power remains the most durable piece of Baring mythology.
