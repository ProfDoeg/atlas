## Works by Muhammad ibn Abdullah

### Revelatory recitations

- **c. 610–632:** *Qurʾānic proclamations*. A corpus of oral proclamations recited by Muhammad as revelations from God: in Islamic terms a manifestation transmitted through him, not a book authored by him. Their collection, ordering, and redaction as the Qurʾān continued after his death, and scholars dispute how much of the received text reached its final form during his lifetime. [McAuliffe; Shoemaker; Shah and Abdel Haleem]

### Covenants, protocols, and correspondence

- **c. 622–627:** *The Kitāb of Medina* (commonly “the Constitution of Medina”). A covenant or protocol establishing an *umma* and regulating alliance, warfare, blood-money, captives, expenditure, and relations among participating groups; “constitution” is an imperfect modern label. It survives only in later recensions, and scholars disagree whether it was one document or several agreements made at different times. [Lecker; Arjomand]

- **628:** *Treaty of al-Ḥudaybiyya*. A negotiated truce-protocol between Muhammad’s community and Quraysh, traditionally dictated by Muhammad and written by ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib. Its wording is preserved through later narrative and hadith recensions rather than an extant contemporary original, so the transmitted versions should not be treated as a single autograph text. [Hamidullah; Mirza]

- **c. 622–632:** *Documents attributed to Muhammad*. A body of roughly two hundred surviving textual traditions comprising contracts, land and water grants, tax instructions, safe-conducts, tribal agreements, appointments, and diplomatic or personal letters; these are administrative acts, usually said to have been dictated to scribes, rather than a conventional authored oeuvre. They survive principally as citations in later historiography, often in divergent forms, and their individual authenticity and dating remain disputed. [Hamidullah; Mirza]

- **c. 628–632:** *Letters of invitation attributed to Muhammad*. Diplomatic manifestos traditionally addressed to such rulers as Heraclius, Khusrau II, the Negus, al-Muqawqis, al-Ḥārith ibn Abī Shamir, Hawdha ibn ʿAlī, and the rulers of Oman: texts intended to produce allegiance or conversion rather than merely describe doctrine. The purported physical originals are generally late copies or forgeries, while even the transmitted texts show literary reshaping and possible anachronism. [Mirza; Hamidullah]

- **c. 631:** *Instruction for ʿAmr ibn Ḥazm*. A composite administrative and legal protocol associated with Muhammad’s governor in Yemen, covering taxation, alms, blood-money, and religious duties. It survives in several recensions and appears to have accumulated material in transmission, making its extent and original form uncertain. [Hamidullah; Mirza]

### Spoken acts and transmitted practice

- **c. 610–632:** *Prophetic utterances and enacted practice* (*sunna*). Muhammad’s reported statements, rulings, prayers, sermons, gestures, and tacit approvals form an attributed body of performed teaching, not a book or a stable text made by him. Individual reports were selected, organized, and written in hadith collections generations later; their attribution is assessed report by report and remains a central field of dispute. [Mirza]

- **632:** *The Farewell address or addresses*. Sermonic manifestos associated with Muhammad’s final pilgrimage, preserved as dispersed and partly conflicting reports rather than one securely recoverable speech with a fixed title or text. The familiar unified “Farewell Sermon” is therefore a later reconstruction and resists classification as a single work. [Mirza]

### Foundations and collective constructions

- **622–632:** *The Medinan umma*. A political-religious community made through preaching, allegiance, migration, covenant, adjudication, ritual, warfare, and administration: a project and social creation rather than a discrete object. Its boundaries and confessional character changed during Muhammad’s lifetime and cannot be reduced to a single founding text. [Lecker; Arjomand]

- **622–623:** *Muhammad’s mosque-and-residence complex at Medina* (later al-Masjid al-Nabawī, the Prophet’s Mosque). A collaboratively built compound serving as dwelling, prayer ground, assembly place, court, and administrative center—a Gesamtkunstwerk of architecture, ritual, government, and domestic life. The first structure has not survived, and the present mosque is the result of repeated enlargements and reconstructions. [Oxford Bibliographies, “Medina”; Encyclopaedia Britannica]

- **c. 628–631:** *The minbar at Medina*. A commissioned preaching seat that converted Muhammad’s spoken address into a spatial and ceremonial institution; sources differ over its precise date and construction. The original object does not survive, and later structures and relic traditions should not be identified with it. [Mostafa]

- **c. 628:** *Signet ring bearing Muhammad’s name and title*. A silver sealing device reportedly commissioned when Muhammad began sending formal letters to rulers who expected authenticated correspondence. The ring is known through hadith and later historical tradition, which says it passed to the first caliphs and was lost under ʿUthmān; no securely authenticated original survives. [Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim; Mirza]
