# Jacob Frank: Research Dossier

## Scope, Method, and Evidentiary Note

This dossier reconstructs the life of Jacob Frank from the surviving Frankist manuscript tradition, contemporary Jewish and Catholic accounts, archival studies, later histories, and modern scholarship. Frank did not leave a conventional autobiography, signed theological treatise, diary, or orderly corpus. The central text attributed to him, usually called *The Collection of the Words of the Lord* (*Zbiór słów Pańskich* or *Księga słów Pańskich*), consists of sayings, parables, reminiscences, commands, and interpretations recorded and arranged by followers, principally in 1784 and 1789–1790. It therefore preserves Frank’s voice at second hand. The Frankist *Chronicle* is an internal sectarian source. Rabbinic polemics, ecclesiastical investigations, conversion records, diplomatic correspondence, municipal reports, memoirs, and newspapers preserve external testimony, but they too reflect institutional interests.

The principal modern reconstructions used here are Aleksander Kraushar’s archival monograph (1895); Gershom Scholem’s studies of Sabbateanism and “redemption through sin”; Jan Doktór’s work on Frank’s doctrine and messianic itinerary; Paweł Maciejko’s archival history of the movement; Harris Lenowitz’s edition and translation of the sayings; Ada Rapoport-Albert’s work on women and Sabbatean messianism; and Jay Michaelson’s close analysis of Frank’s late mythology. Where they disagree, the disagreement is stated.

Evidence labels used below:

- **Documented:** supported by contemporary institutional records, multiple independent testimonies, or a strong archival chain.
- **Frankist testimony:** asserted in internal Frankist manuscripts or sayings recorded by disciples.
- **Hostile contemporary report:** asserted by opponents close to the events; historically important but not automatically factual.
- **Later tradition:** recorded substantially after the event.
- **Disputed / unresolved:** evidence permits no confident conclusion.
- **Legend / propaganda / conspiracy narrative:** part of Frank’s reputation or self-presentation, without adequate documentary proof as literal history.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and origins

Frank’s father is variously named **Leib**, **Judah Leib**, **Leyb**, or **Leib Buchbinder**. Lenowitz describes him from the Frankist tradition as an itinerant peddler and perhaps a bookbinder or preacher. Encyclopaedia Judaica-derived accounts describe the family as middle class and the father as a contractor and merchant of some local standing. The father’s Sabbatean affiliation is widely reported and fits the family’s movement into Ottoman lands, but the details come through later reconstruction rather than a surviving confession by him. Frank’s paternal grandfather reportedly lived for a time in Kalisz.

Frank’s mother is identified in later sources as **Rachel**, sometimes **Rachel Herschel of Rzeszów/Reischa**. Her ancestry is poorly documented. The names of Frank’s siblings, if any, and the exact degree of his relationship to several later Frankist families remain uncertain.

The family left Podolia when Jacob was very young. Jan Doktór dates the flight to about **1727**, stating that the parents were accused of Sabbatean heresy and fled to Wallachia. Other accounts place the family in **Czernowitz/Chernivtsi** about 1730. These are compatible as stages in a movement through Moldavia, Wallachia, and Bukovina, but the precise route is not established.

### Marriage

In **1752**, at Nikopol in Ottoman Bulgaria, Frank married **Hannah** (also Hanele; some literary sources use Tovah), daughter of a respected Ashkenazi merchant. Two Sabbatean emissaries from Podolia reportedly witnessed the wedding, evidence of Frank’s integration into a transregional Sabbatean network. Hannah accompanied the movement into the Commonwealth. She died in **1770**, during Frank’s Częstochowa confinement or residence. Her individual role is obscured by the overwhelmingly male authorship of surviving records.

### Children

The couple had at least three children who survived into adulthood:

- **Rachel/Eve Frank** (usually dated **1754–1816**, with 1817 in some sources), born in Nikopol. She received or adopted the Christian name **Ewa/Eva** after the family’s baptism. Within the movement she became “the Lady,” “the Virgin,” or *Matronita*, an embodiment or vehicle of the Shekhinah and an increasingly central devotional figure. Frank circulated royal-parentage stories about her and presented her as **Eva Romanovna**. She succeeded him as leader at Offenbach.
- **Joseph/Józef Frank**, who helped administer the Offenbach court after his father’s death and died in **1807**, apparently without heirs.
- **Roch/Rochus Frank**, also active in the post-1791 leadership, died in **1813**, apparently without heirs.

Sources occasionally imply other children or use “sons” and “daughters” spiritually rather than biologically. No reliable complete birth register has been located. Later claims that Frank committed incest with Eve belong to the controversy section below; they are not established biographical fact.

### Extended network

Frank’s Moravian kinship network included **Schöndl/Schoendel Dobruška**, wife of a wealthy Brno merchant, and her family. Her son **Moses Dobruška**—later Franz Thomas von Schönfeld and, in revolutionary France, Junius Frey—combined Frankist, Masonic, Habsburg, and revolutionary identities before being guillotined in 1794. The exact biological degree of kinship is described inconsistently as cousin or related family. The Dobruška household was essential to Frank’s entry into Brno society.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1726–ca. 1748

Frank grew up on the borderlands of the Commonwealth and Ottoman Europe, among Jewish communities shaped by trade, migration, rabbinic authority, Kabbalah, and clandestine Sabbatean expectation. His later claim to be an *am ha’aretz*—a plain, ignorant, or unlearned man—should not be treated as a school transcript. It was a polemical and charismatic posture against rabbinic textual mastery. He plainly absorbed biblical narrative, Zoharic motifs, Sabbatean myth, Christian imagery, Islamic and Balkan folklore, mercantile geography, courtly spectacle, and popular tales. His late sayings transform these materials with confidence even when they disregard learned exegesis.

Accounts name an early teacher called **Leib**, a Sabbatean and wonder-worker in Wallachia or Moldavia, and later teachers or initiators including **Rabbi Issachar**, associated with Hayim Malakh’s Sabbatean lineage, and **Mordecai ben Elias Margalit of Prague**. The biographical precision of these teacher lists varies. They represent a credible chain connecting East European crypto-Sabbateans to the radical followers of **Barukhiah Russo** (Beruchiah, Osman Baba, d. 1720) among the Salonican Dönmeh.

No formal yeshiva, degree, guild apprenticeship, or sustained school attendance is documented. His formation was itinerant, oral, commercial, and sectarian.

## Education and Formation

### Sabbatai Zevi and Nathan of Gaza

The indispensable background was the movement of **Sabbatai Zevi** (1626–1676), acclaimed as messiah in 1665–1666 and converted to Islam under Ottoman pressure in September 1666. **Nathan of Gaza** interpreted apostasy as a descent into the realm of impurity necessary to liberate divine sparks. Some later Sabbateans preserved Jewish practice secretly; others ritualized reversal, transgression, or conversion. Frank inherited this field but did not merely repeat it.

### Barukhiah Russo / Osman Baba and the Karakaş Dönmeh

Frank’s closest Ottoman matrix was the radical Dönmeh faction associated with **Barukhiah Russo**, revered by followers as Osman Baba and as a further messianic embodiment. Frank traveled to Smyrna/İzmir and Salonica and entered circles that treated public religious identity as a garment covering an esoteric faith. Jan Doktór describes Frank as sent to Poland in December 1755 by the **Koniozos** sect to bring Polish Sabbateans out of concealment and toward “Esau,” interpreted as Christianity. Other historians allow more independent initiative by Frank. The “mission” is therefore strong scholarly reconstruction, not a surviving commission signed by a Dönmeh council.

### Trade and travel as education

As a merchant in cloth, textiles, and possibly jewels or precious stones, Frank traversed Moldavia, Wallachia, the Balkans, Anatolia, Smyrna, Salonica, and Podolia. “Frank/Frenk” in Ottoman and Sephardi environments denoted a European or Western/Ashkenazi outsider; the nickname became his durable surname and persona. His trade gave him routes, multilingual competence, patrons, intelligence about borders, and a method of leadership based on messengers and mobile cells.

## Early Career and Ottoman Years, ca. 1748–1755

The chronology is approximate. Frank later told stories of danger, visions, disguise, poverty, mercantile ventures, and encounters with Sabbatean teachers. Some are spiritualized adventure tales and cannot be independently dated.

- **ca. 1750–1752:** associated with Sabbatean teachers in Smyrna/İzmir; later tradition names Rabbi Issachar.
- **1752:** married Hannah in Nikopol. The presence of Podolian Sabbatean witnesses connects the marriage to a larger network.
- **November 1753:** reportedly traveled with Mordecai Margalit to Salonica and encountered or joined the Karakaş/Barukhian Dönmeh.
- **Islamic conversion or conformity:** Frank became, or was represented as, a Muslim and Ottoman subject. The date and ritual circumstances are not securely fixed. In a 1760 Catholic interrogation he reportedly explained the conversion pragmatically—as a means to Ottoman protection and support—whereas Jewish opponents interpreted it as deliberate imitation of Sabbatai Zevi. Both explanations served the speaker’s later interests.
- **1754:** birth of Rachel/Eve at Nikopol, according to the standard reconstruction.
- **December 1755:** returned or was sent to Podolia to organize local Sabbateans.

## Chronological Life History

### Return to Podolia and the Lanckorona affair, 1755–1756

Frank entered a landscape containing clandestine Sabbatean groups but no unified Frankist church. He gathered “believers,” taught revelations, and pressed followers toward an open break with rabbinic authority. His Ottoman subjecthood initially protected him from local Jewish jurisdiction.

In early **1756**, a gathering at **Lanckorona/Lanckorona (Landskron)** was interrupted or exposed. The standard hostile story says participants danced around a woman—often identified as the local rabbi’s wife or a teenage girl—who was naked or bare-breasted and wore a Torah crown, representing the Shekhinah. Later tellings expand the scene into an orgy.

What is established is narrower: authorities arrested participants after complaints; Jewish leaders accused the group of magic, sexual immorality, and violations of halakhah; Frank, as an Ottoman subject, was released or removed beyond the Commonwealth; and a rabbinic process followed at Satanów and Brody. The sect denied the lurid account. Modern scholarship notes that no neutral eyewitness deposition proves an orgy, and that the most detailed narrative comes through committed heresiologists. The event demonstrates a suspect or transgressive ritual and the political efficacy of sexual accusation; its exact choreography is unresolved.

At a rabbinic court in **Satanów**, some men and women reportedly confessed to breaches of Sabbath, dietary, marital, and sexual rules. How freely these statements were given, and whether they described systematic rites, isolated conduct, or charges shaped by interrogation, cannot be established from the surviving polemical record. In **June 1756**, a rabbinic assembly at Brody pronounced a *ḥerem* against unrepentant sectarians and required communities to expose them.

### Alliance with Bishop Dembowski and the Kamieniec disputation, 1756–1757

The Frankists appealed to **Mikołaj Dembowski**, bishop of Kamieniec Podolski, presenting themselves as **Contra-Talmudists** who accepted the Hebrew Bible, rejected rabbinic authority, privileged the Zohar, and believed in a triune God. The alliance converted a Jewish disciplinary crisis into a Christian ecclesiastical controversy.

The **Kamieniec Podolski disputation** was held in **1757** under Dembowski’s authority. The Contra-Talmudists submitted propositions asserting, among other things, that the Talmud contained blasphemies and that the Zohar’s Godhead resembled the Christian Trinity. Rabbis were compelled to answer within a tribunal whose patron favored the accusers. Dembowski declared the Contra-Talmudists victorious, imposed an indemnity on Jewish communities, and ordered copies of the Talmud burned. The burning was carried out publicly.

Dembowski died suddenly in **November 1757**, shortly after the burnings. Jewish communities interpreted the death as divine punishment; the Contra-Talmudists lost their protector and faced retaliation, flight, and economic isolation. The death became a providential counter-myth in Jewish memory.

### Exile, Iwania, and preparation for conversion, 1757–1759

Frank moved among Ottoman territories, Wallachia, and border communities, while his adherents divided over strategy. At **Iwania/Iwanie** he asserted a new stage of revelation and presented himself as successor to Sabbatai Zevi and Barukhiah/Osman Baba. Whether he explicitly declared “I am the Messiah” at this time is disputed. Followers clearly assigned him a redemptive role; his own later language often preferred guide, Jacob, brother, or vehicle within a chain of embodiments.

He taught that the company must “go to Esau” or enter **Edom**, usually meaning the Roman Catholic world, as an intermediate descent. Christianity was not the terminus. Beyond it lay **Daas/Da’at**—knowledge, a new dispensation, or the true religion still to be disclosed. Baptism was at once theological passage, political shelter, and an exit from Jewish communal jurisdiction.

### Lwów disputation and mass baptism, 1759–1760

Negotiations with church officials produced a second public disputation at **Lwów/Lemberg/Lviv**, held from **17 July to 19 September 1759**. Frank himself did not conduct the formal argument, though he came to Lwów. The Frankists’ propositions included a version of the Christian ritual-murder accusation: that the Talmud required Jewish use of Christian blood.

The accusation was not proved. The Frankist side did not supply credible evidence adequate to establish ritual murder, and the rabbis rejected it. Modern research suggests that Christian officials may have proposed, demanded, or shaped the blood-libel plank as the price of protection. Some Frankist converts later amplified the charge; **Jan Serafinowicz**, for example, published an extraordinary confession alleging participation in ritual murders. Such claims belong to the history of blood-libel propaganda and conversion polemic, not to established criminal history.

After the disputation, church authorities required the Contra-Talmudists to demonstrate sincerity through baptism. Large groups were baptized in Lwów, Kamieniec, Warsaw, and other towns. Noble godparents gave converts Christian names and, in some cases, access to patronage networks.

The number is contested:

- Jan Doktór’s public summary gives about **2,000** baptisms after the second dispute.
- Paweł Maciejko estimates roughly **3,000–4,000 adults and children** in the immediate wave.
- Contemporary or later partisan claims rose to 10,000, 20,000, 26,000, or more, often conflating all Jewish baptisms in Poland with Frankists or counting descendants.
- “Tens of thousands” is defensible only for later persons connected to Frankist-descended networks, not as a register of people personally led to the font in 1759.

Frank was baptized at the **Latin cathedral in Lwów on 17 September 1759**, taking the name **Józef/Joseph**. His godparents were **Anna Brühl** and **Franciszek Rzewuski**, according to Polish accounts. Because the first ceremony was treated as baptism “from water,” or because court patronage demanded a second solemnity, he underwent another ceremony in the **royal chapel of the Saxon Palace in Warsaw on 18 November 1759**. King **Augustus III** served as godfather through the royal rite or representation. Some older English summaries mistakenly say the Warsaw baptism occurred the day after the Lwów ceremony; the documented interval is about two months.

Frank and leading followers adopted noble styles and the designation *generosus*. Baptism brought forms of social elevation, but it did not automatically make every convert a hereditary Polish noble, and Frank’s later title “baron” was self-assumed or courtly rather than a securely documented Polish patent of nobility.

### Catholic investigation, arrest, and Częstochowa confinement, 1759–1772

Church officials quickly suspected that the new converts remained an esoteric sect obedient to Frank. Testimonies described him as a redemptive figure, reported private rites, and indicated that baptism was only a stage in a separate faith. The papal nuncio **Niccolò Serra** and Polish ecclesiastical authorities investigated.

Frank was arrested in Warsaw on **6 February 1760**. He was interrogated and judged a teacher of heresy or a dangerous sectarian. Popular accounts call the result a criminal “conviction”; more precisely it was an ecclesiastical and administrative detention under the confessional state. He was first held in or near Warsaw and then confined at the Pauline monastery-fortress of **Jasna Góra, Częstochowa**, seat of the Black Madonna.

His confinement lasted about **thirteen years**. It was restrictive but not equivalent throughout to a dungeon sentence. Family and selected disciples had access; followers settled nearby, carried letters and money, and treated him as a persecuted holy master. The shrine’s Marian imagery contributed to Frank’s growing doctrine of the **Virgin**, eventually focused on Eve. The prison thus became the institutional center of the movement.

During the 1760s, Frank issued commands, parables, and letters; directed marriages, separations, travel, and money; demanded obedience; and repeatedly revised the next stage of the mission. He explored a possible conversion of the company to **Russian Orthodoxy**, sending emissaries and claiming very large numbers of prospective converts. Russian church and state officials did not accept the plan on his terms.

His wife Hannah died in **1770**. Eve, about sixteen, rose into a central sacred role. Frank’s theology associated the Black Madonna, Shekhinah, feminine divine presence, Virgin Mary, and Eve without simply equating them in a stable creed.

### Release amid the First Partition, 1772–1773

Russian forces under General **Alexander Bibikov** occupied Częstochowa during the suppression of the Bar Confederation. Frank was freed in **August 1772**, or released by order following the occupation; some sources place his effective departure in early 1773. The prior Polish sentence was not formally annulled.

Later Offenbach lore says Russian liberation rewarded Frank’s espionage for the tsar. The evidence securely establishes Russian military control and Bibikov’s order or intervention, not a proven intelligence contract. Frank and his followers were skilled in secret correspondence and courted Russian protection, which made the spy story plausible to contemporaries, but no payment ledger, commission, or operational report now establishes service as a Russian agent. It should be listed as an unresolved allegation, not a fact.

Frank went to Warsaw and then, in **March 1773**, traveled with Eve and about eighteen associates—reportedly disguised as servants of a wealthy merchant—to **Brno/Brünn in Moravia**.

### Brno court and Habsburg contacts, 1773–1786

In Brno, the wealthy Dobruška family supplied an entry point. Frank constructed a court rather than a synagogue or ordinary church congregation. It included household officials, pilgrims, guards or uniformed “Uhlans,” disciplined young men and women, and a continuous flow of Polish and Moravian followers. He cultivated aristocratic dress, military drill, healing claims, initiation, and secrecy.

His followers financed the establishment through gifts, levies, pilgrim offerings, and remittances from Poland and Moravia. Exact annual accounts have not survived. The court’s expenditure—uniforms, horses, servants, guards, travel, and later castle rent—was conspicuous. Frank’s organization had no modern incorporated business, bank, patent, or transparent balance sheet. It was a charismatic household economy.

Frank and Eve traveled repeatedly to **Vienna**. Empress **Maria Theresa** regarded or was encouraged to regard Frank as a missionary capable of converting Jews to Christianity. **Joseph II**, then co-ruler and later emperor, received information about the group; stories also connect Eve to the imperial court. A famous claim says Joseph II and the future Russian emperor Paul visited Frank together. High-level contacts are credible, but popular versions exaggerate intimacy and stable imperial favor.

The Habsburg authorities also investigated Frank. A former disciple, **Jakub Goliński**, complained that Frank had abducted or retained his wife and exercised destructive authority over followers. The complaint and related reports helped make the court a police matter. The 2011 film *Daas* builds its plot around this episode but fictionalizes the investigation.

By **1786**, Habsburg toleration had ended. Frank was ordered to leave or made unwelcome as an unmanageable foreign sect leader maintaining an armed-looking retinue and dubious finances. He moved west through **Oberrad** near Frankfurt while a new residence was prepared.

### Offenbach and the Gottes Haus, 1786/1788–1791

Frank settled in **Offenbach am Main**, ruled by the financially strained house of Isenburg-Birstein. Municipal history dates his arrival in the city to **1788**; other accounts date the Offenbach establishment from **1786**, including a preparatory residence at Oberrad. The difference reflects the move into the castle versus arrival in the region.

Prince **Wolfgang Ernst II of Isenburg-Birstein** leased Isenburg Castle to Frank. The city’s historical account gives the extraordinary figure of **three million gulden**; this may refer to a nominal long-term obligation, aggregate financing, or later tradition, and should not be read uncritically as cash paid at entry. Older sources say he bought the castle; modern local history describes a lease. The prince’s debt and Frank’s need for a protected court made the arrangement mutually useful.

The residence became the **Gottes Haus** (“House of God”). Several hundred newcomers, many baptized Jews from East-Central Europe, affected a town of roughly 5,000 inhabitants. Frank entered with a uniformed Uhlan guard and appeared to townspeople as an eastern potentate. Locals called him the **Polish prince** (*Pollakenfürst*) or Baron of Offenbach. His followers maintained a separate social world, drilled and celebrated in a site remembered as the **Pollakengarten**, and attended Catholic worship while preserving esoteric devotions. Reports say Frank prayed prostrate on a carpet in church, a visible remnant or performance of Ottoman practice.

Pilgrims carried money, news, recruits, and manuscripts between Offenbach, Warsaw, Prague, Brno, and other centers. Frank promised protection, healing, transformation, and ultimately liberation from death. His late teachings were recorded here and at Brno. They increasingly presented the world as ruled by a murderous demiurgic power, with the company required to enter danger, find **Big Brother/Esau**, encounter the **Virgin**, and reach Daas. The stories combine Genesis, Jesus, Kabbalah, fairy tale, court ritual, and personal adventure.

Offenbach rumor repeated charges of orgies and sexual control. Outsiders had limited access. The closed household, display of young attendants, Frank’s earlier notoriety, and theological language of transgression sustained the rumors; they do not independently prove every alleged practice.

### Final illness and death, 1791

Frank promised, or followers understood him to promise, bodily immortality and victory over death. He nonetheless became ill and died at Isenburg Castle on **10 December 1791**, reportedly at 4:00 a.m. The immediate medical cause is not securely recorded in the major sources consulted. Claims of a specific disease should therefore be treated cautiously.

His body was displayed in state and buried with pomp. Followers called him the Holy Lord of Offenbach and continued pilgrimage to his grave. The death required reinterpretation: Frank had descended, remained mystically present, or transferred authority to Eve and the family. For opponents, the corpse disproved his promises.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

Frank founded no company in the modern sense and held no patent, elected public office, university appointment, or recognized clerical benefice. His durable institutions were relational:

### The Company

The core body of disciples functioned through sworn loyalty, arranged marriages, messengers, local treasurers, household service, and periodic pilgrimage. Frank’s personal commands could govern work, residence, marriage, sex, religious conformity, and the disposition of money.

### Contra-Talmudist representation

In 1757–1759, designated spokesmen acted before Catholic tribunals. This was a political-religious front capable of petitions, propositions, fundraising, and negotiation. It was not simply a transparent statement of internal belief.

### Częstochowa communications network

During confinement, nearby followers maintained contact, moved documents and funds, and constructed martyrdom around Frank. The center survived despite official detention.

### Brno and Offenbach courts

These were quasi-aristocratic households with guards, uniforms, servants, pilgrims, ritual attendants, and family leadership. Their income came principally from adherents. Precise sums and ownership structures remain obscure. The apparent wealth coexisted with debt and dependency.

### Financial controversies

- Frank extracted or received substantial contributions from followers, sometimes under spiritual pressure. This is documented as a system of remittances, though surviving accounts do not permit a complete total.
- Followers sometimes sold property, moved households, or undertook costly pilgrimages. Former members accused the court of ruinous exploitation.
- The three-million-gulden Offenbach figure is reported by the city’s historical account but requires archival contextualization.
- After Frank’s death, Eve, Joseph, and Roch continued the court without his authority. Contributions declined; debts mounted. By Eve’s death, the household was insolvent or near collapse. Later reports give spectacular debt figures, but they concern the post-Frank court and vary substantially.
- No evidence consulted supports “money laundering” in the modern legal sense. Secret transfers and concealed sectarian finance are not by themselves money laundering.

## Religious, Political, and Intellectual Development

### From Sabbatean succession to an independent myth

Frank began within a Sabbatean chain: Sabbatai Zevi, Nathan of Gaza, Barukhiah Russo/Osman Baba, and then a new Polish mission. Yet his late teachings marginalize detailed Lurianic Kabbalah and sometimes criticize predecessors for failing to complete the path. Modern scholars disagree on whether “Sabbatean” adequately captures the mature system.

### “Going to Esau” and “Edom”

Esau/Edom traditionally symbolized Rome and Christianity. Frank transformed the image. The company had to enter Esau’s domain, don Christianity, and find the true elder brother displaced by Jacob. This could mean Catholic baptism, reconciliation with a primordial power, or passage beyond Jewish-Christian antagonism. “Going to Esau” was both historical program and mythic map.

### Daas / Da’at

Frank repeatedly invoked **Daas**—knowledge—as the future religion or state beyond existing law. It was never codified as a creed. The absence was functional: revelation remained Frank’s monopoly, and each achieved threshold disclosed another hidden stage.

### Law and antinomianism

Frank rejected the binding authority of rabbinic halakhah and also denied that Christianity’s public law was the final truth. His sayings call for the destruction or passage beyond laws and religions. This is **documented theological antinomianism**. It does not prove that every criminal or sexual prohibition was routinely reversed in practice.

### The alien or “God of death”

In the late mythology, the visible world is often governed by a cruel, death-dealing power distinct from the hidden good God. Frank recast biblical and Christian figures within a quasi-gnostic drama. Jesus had opened a path but did not finish the confrontation; Frank’s company must pass farther.

### The Virgin and Eve

The feminine divine principle became essential. The Virgin could appear through the Shekhinah, Virgin Mary, Black Madonna, a hidden princess, and Frank’s daughter Eve. This elevated a woman to unprecedented ritual centrality while also placing her inside her father’s authoritarian symbolic system. After Hannah’s death, Eve’s political and devotional role increased.

### The Jacob–Esau reversal and “Big Brother”

Frank’s “Big Brother” is not merely the biblical villain Esau. He is elder, powerful, concealed, and necessary. The myth reverses inherited Jewish valuations and makes redemption dependent on the recovered brother. Jay Michaelson treats this as one of Frank’s most original constructions.

### Immortality

Frank repeatedly spoke of conquering death and led followers to expect bodily transformation or immortality. The expectation mobilized devotion and became a reputational disaster at his death. Whether he issued an unqualified calendrical guarantee to every baptized follower varies by source.

### Political theology

Frank sought protection successively from Ottoman, Polish Catholic, Russian Orthodox, Habsburg, and Isenburg powers. Conversion, court imitation, uniforms, titles, and royal-parentage myths made sovereignty part of his religion. He did not develop a conventional party program, but his movement changed social status, created converted kin networks, and entered the political cultures of late Commonwealth and Habsburg Europe.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, and Abuses

### 1. Sexual rites and the Lanckorona “orgy”

**Claim:** Frankists performed ritual orgies, beginning with a 1756 ceremony around a naked or half-naked woman crowned with a Torah ornament.

**Evidence:** Arrest and investigation are documented. Jewish opponents reported a sexually transgressive rite; confessions or admissions at subsequent rabbinic proceedings alleged sexual violations. The sect denied the story. There is no neutral record proving the expanded orgy narrative. Modern historians accept that gendered and sexual transgression mattered to some Sabbatean and Frankist practice but dispute its frequency, form, and coerciveness.

**Finding:** A suspect and probably deliberately heterodox gathering is established; the canonical orgy scene is a hostile contemporary report elaborated by later retelling, not a fully documented fact.

### 2. Incest with Eve

**Claim:** Frank maintained an incestuous sexual relationship with his daughter and commanded father-daughter incest among followers.

**Evidence:** Opponents accused Sabbateans and Frankists of incest; late rumors interpreted Eve’s sacred role and proximity to Frank sexually. Frankist writings use provocative familial and erotic symbolism. Modern close study, including Michaelson’s, emphasizes that direct documentary evidence is extremely weak.

**Finding:** **Unverified allegation.** It should not be stated as established biography. The story’s historical importance lies in the sexualization of the closed father-daughter cult and in later portrayals of Frank as absolute transgressor.

### 3. Coercive control

**Claim:** Frank exercised authoritarian control over followers’ marriages, bodies, money, movement, and family relationships.

**Evidence:** His sayings and the organization of the courts demonstrate commands, tests of obedience, humiliations, arranged relationships, financial demands, and a hierarchy centered on his person. Complaints by defectors, including Goliński, add evidence but are adversarial.

**Finding:** **Substantially documented**, though individual allegations require separate corroboration. “Cult leader” is a modern analytic label, not an eighteenth-century legal verdict.

### 4. Blood libel

**Claim promoted by Frankist representatives:** Rabbinic Jews required Christian blood.

**Evidence:** Formally advanced at the 1759 Lwów disputation and amplified by converts such as Serafinowicz. It was unsupported by credible evidence. Christian authorities may have imposed or encouraged it as a condition of alliance.

**Finding:** **Documented propagation of a false and dangerous accusation.** Frank’s exact personal authorship of the plank is less certain than his movement’s institutional participation.

### 5. Fraudulent conversion and heresy

**Claim:** Frank accepted Catholic baptism only as disguise and continued a separate religion.

**Evidence:** His teachings explicitly treated Edom/Christianity as a stage rather than the final dispensation; church inquiries found continuing private obedience to Frank.

**Finding:** **Documented esoteric reservation.** Whether this is called “fraud” depends on legal and theological categories, but the public profession did not exhaust his private doctrine.

### 6. Messiah claim

**Claim:** Frank straightforwardly proclaimed himself the reincarnated Messiah, Sabbatai Zevi, Jacob, and Jesus.

**Evidence:** Followers treated him as the decisive redemptive leader and successor in a chain; hostile witnesses reported explicit claims; later Frankist devotion called him Holy Lord. In the late sayings he often avoids the simple title and distributes messianic roles among Jacob, Esau, the Virgin, and the company.

**Finding:** His **messianic function is established**; the formula “I am the Messiah/reincarnation of X” at every stage is an oversimplification and, for the church investigation, may have been a tendentious charge.

### 7. False nobility and royal identity

**Claim:** Frank was a Polish noble or recognized Baron of Offenbach; Eve was a Russian imperial daughter.

**Evidence:** He used *generosus*, maintained a noble court, and called himself baron. No secure hereditary patent corresponding to the grander claims has been demonstrated. Eve’s alleged mother shifts in retellings among Empress Elizabeth and Catherine II; chronology and evidence contradict literal royal maternity.

**Finding:** **Self-created court mythology**, effective socially but not verified genealogy.

### 8. Espionage and intelligence connections

**Claim:** Frank spied for Russia and was released as payment.

**Evidence:** Russian forces freed him; he had sought Russian Orthodox and political patronage; secret messengers crossed borders. Offenbach local tradition records the rumor. No operational archive proving intelligence service was identified in the consulted scholarship.

**Finding:** **Unresolved allegation / local rumor**, not established fact.

### 9. Armed force and political violence

Frank maintained uniformed guards or Uhlans in Brno and Offenbach. They drilled and projected sovereignty. Evidence consulted does not establish that they conducted a sustained campaign of political violence. The display alarmed authorities and may have protected the household. Claims of a private army should be qualified as a ceremonial and protective guard unless further records demonstrate combat operations.

### 10. Financial exploitation

The courts depended upon remittances, gifts, and pressure on followers. Defectors alleged ruin. The posthumous Offenbach court accumulated major debts. This supports a finding of an opaque, extractive charismatic economy, but the surviving evidence does not yield a complete forensic accounting or a modern criminal judgment for fraud.

### 11. Criminal adjudication

No modern-style criminal conviction for rape, incest, homicide, financial fraud, or espionage is documented. Frank’s principal incarceration followed ecclesiastical investigation for heresy and sectarian deception within an eighteenth-century confessional regime. Rabbinic excommunication was communal law, not a state criminal conviction.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### Frank as the third and final Messiah

**Story:** Sabbatai Zevi was followed by Barukhiah Russo and finally by Frank, who would complete redemption.

**Appearance/circulation:** Internal Frankist succession traditions, opponents’ descriptions, and later histories.

**Evidence:** The succession structure is real as belief. Whether Frank was “sent” by a formal Dönmeh body or declared literal reincarnation in a fixed creed varies by source.

**Effect:** Legitimated Frank’s authority and allowed apostasy to be framed as repetition and completion rather than failure.

### The naked Shekhinah at Lanckorona

**Story:** Frankists danced around a nude or bare-breasted woman wearing a Torah crown and then held an orgy.

**Appearance/circulation:** Rabbinic heresiology after the 1756 discovery; later Jewish and Christian histories; modern popular biography.

**Evidence:** Arrest and accusations are secure; precise scene and orgy are uncorroborated and denied by the group.

**Effect:** Became the master image of Frankism as sexual antinomianism and often substitutes for analysis of the movement.

### Bishop Dembowski’s providential death

**Story:** God struck down the bishop for burning the Talmud.

**Appearance/circulation:** Jewish communal memory immediately after his sudden death in 1757.

**Evidence:** Temporal proximity is real; divine causation is a religious interpretation.

**Effect:** Reversed the disputation’s verdict and encouraged retaliation against the now-unprotected sectarians.

### Baptism as a guarantee of immortality

**Story:** Frank promised that those who accepted baptism would never die.

**Appearance/circulation:** Conversion-era and later accounts; consonant with his continuing language of bodily immortality.

**Evidence:** Immortality teaching is well attested. Exact universal wording and its connection to each baptismal decision vary.

**Effect:** Increased recruitment and made ordinary deaths, especially Frank’s, crises requiring reinterpretation.

### Eve Romanovna, daughter of the empress

**Story:** Eve was the illegitimate daughter of a Russian empress—usually Catherine II, sometimes Elizabeth—and Frank only her foster father.

**Appearance/circulation:** Frank’s court, Offenbach town rumor, and posthumous Frankist representation.

**Evidence:** No credible genealogical evidence; versions contradict one another. Hannah’s maternity and Eve’s Nikopol birth are the standard historical reconstruction.

**Effect:** Gave the Virgin dynastic charisma, connected the household symbolically to Russia, and justified princely display.

### Joseph II’s love affair with Eve

**Story:** Eve’s beauty won imperial protection, possibly through a sexual relationship with Joseph II.

**Appearance/circulation:** Court gossip and later biographies.

**Evidence:** Visits and petitions in Vienna are credible; an affair is unproved.

**Effect:** Explained otherwise puzzling Habsburg toleration through erotic intrigue and fed later fiction.

### Russian rescue for espionage

**Story:** Bibikov released Frank to reward spying for Catherine II.

**Appearance/circulation:** Offenbach lore and later popular history.

**Evidence:** Release by Russian power is documented; quid pro quo is not.

**Effect:** Recast a geopolitical accident as evidence of Frank’s hidden power and treason.

### Freemasonry, Illuminism, and revolutionary conspiracy

**Story:** Frankism became a secret engine of Freemasonry, the Illuminati, Jacobinism, Communism, Zionism, sexual liberation, banking power, or a continuing elite pedophile network.

**Appearance/circulation:** Nineteenth- and twentieth-century occult genealogies, antisemitic literature, political polemic, and twenty-first-century online conspiracy culture.

**Evidence:** Specific Frankist descendants and relatives entered Masonic, Enlightenment, revolutionary, Polish patriotic, and bourgeois networks; Moses Dobruška/Junius Frey is the clearest example. Converted families often intermarried and preserved some endogamy. This does not establish a single continuous command organization or secret control of later ideologies. Modern claims of an unbroken Frankist cabal generally proceed by genealogical insinuation rather than organizational records.

**Effect:** Frank’s genuine secrecy and border-crossing are used to provide an eighteenth-century origin myth for unrelated modern anxieties.

### Famous Polish descendants

Claims attach **Adam Mickiewicz**, his wife Celina Szymanowska, Maria Szymanowska, Frédéric Chopin, Juliusz Słowacki, Tadeusz Boy-Żeleński, and numerous noble or banking families to Frankist ancestry. Some converted-family lines, especially around the Szymanowski/Szymanowska and Wołowski networks, are historically plausible or documented. Other claims depend on hostile nineteenth-century memoirs or expansive definitions of “Frankist.” Descent does not demonstrate inherited secret belief.

### Frank’s skull

Kraushar reproduced or discussed an alleged skull removed when the Offenbach cemetery was cleared in 1866 and associated with Emil Pirazzi. The object’s chain of custody is insufficient for modern identification. It is an artifact of posthumous fascination, not verified remains.

## Works by or Attributed to Jacob Frank

Frank was primarily an oral teacher. Authorship below means attributed speech or direction, not necessarily autograph composition.

### *Zbiór słów Pańskich* / *Księga słów Pańskich* / *The Collection of the Words of the Lord*

- **Composition:** sayings recorded by disciples mainly in **1784** and **1789–1790**, with material referring back to earlier life.
- **Language:** Polish manuscript idiom with Yiddish, Ladino, Hebrew, Aramaic, Turkish, and regional elements. Some material may have moved orally from Yiddish or mixed speech into Polish.
- **Manuscripts:** Jagiellonian/University of Kraków manuscripts conventionally cataloged as **6968, 6969/1, 6969/2, 6969/3** (catalog numbers appear with occasional variation in secondary literature), plus a portion in Lublin Public Library manuscript **2118**.
- **Authorship status:** dictated or spoken by Frank and written, selected, translated, and arranged by followers. Not an autograph book and not safely treated as verbatim stenography.
- **Modern editions:** Jan Doktór edited Polish Frankist writings; Harris Lenowitz prepared a complete English translation and annotation in 2004. Stanford and National Library of Israel catalogs record the Lenowitz edition/translation. A short *Sayings of Yakov Frank* edition was produced in a limited Tree Tzaddikim printing.

### Frankist *Chronicle* (*Kronika*)

An internal chronological narrative appended to or transmitted alongside the sayings. It supplies otherwise unavailable biographical details, including early movements. **Authorship:** follower compilation, not Frank’s personal memoir.

### Letters, epistles, commands, and circulars

Frank issued letters from Częstochowa, Brno, Vienna, and Offenbach to local companies and emissaries. Surviving pieces are dispersed through archival publications and Frankist manuscript collections. They are dictated or secretarial documents; autograph status varies.

### Disputation propositions and conversion petitions

The Contra-Talmudist theses of 1757 and 1759, petitions to Catholic officials, and negotiations with Russian Orthodoxy were collective political-theological documents produced under Frankist leadership. They should not be assigned to Frank alone without manuscript evidence.

### *Red Letters*

Red-ink circulars were sent to Jewish communities about 1799–1800 urging entry into the religion of Edom. They belong to the leadership of **Eve, Joseph, Roch, and the posthumous Offenbach court**, not to Frank, who had died in 1791.

### No established corpus in the following categories

No verified patents, scientific papers, artworks, compositions, corporate filings, signed memoir, conventional sermon collection published during his life, or personal diary are known.

## Death, Succession, and the End of the Offenbach Court

After the funeral, many Polish followers left Offenbach for Warsaw. Eve became the **Holy Mistress**, assisted by Joseph and Roch. The family retained ritual objects, correspondence networks, and the Gottes Haus. In **1799–1800**, the leadership dispatched the Red Letters calling Jews to the religion of Edom at the turn of the century.

The children lacked Frank’s authority and inherited an expensive court. Joseph died in 1807 and Roch in 1813. Eve’s finances collapsed; she died in 1816 or 1817 with heavy debts. Stories that she escaped arrest and lived elsewhere belong to rumor. Organized pilgrimage and endogamous family networks persisted, but no single central Frankist authority replaced the Offenbach household.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Within Jewish historiography

Frank became a boundary marker: the heretic whose movement demonstrated the danger of Sabbateanism, apostasy, charismatic authority, and the abandonment of halakhah. Jacob Emden and later Graetz fixed a strongly hostile portrait. Scholem made Frank important to a dialectical history in which messianic antinomianism helped dissolve tradition and prepare modern secular consciousness. Later historians have retained the rupture while questioning the linear genealogy from heresy to Enlightenment.

### Within Polish social history

Mass baptism inserted thousands of converts and descendants into Catholic urban, professional, noble, and patriotic networks. Some families retained endogamy and private memory; others assimilated fully. Frankism therefore belongs to the histories of Jewish-Christian relations, conversion, Polish nobility, bourgeois formation, the Great Sejm, nineteenth-century patriotism, and disputes over national genealogy.

### Women and religious authority

Eve’s status as Virgin and posthumous leader makes Frankism central to the study of women in Sabbatean prophecy. The same system that made a woman the visible sacred center also subordinated her identity to Frank’s mythology and left her managing an insolvent dynastic court.

### Religious thought

Frank’s late teachings are now studied as an original esoteric mythology: law must be crossed; the visible deity may be the God of death; Jesus began but did not finish the work; Esau is elder brother rather than mere enemy; the Virgin is indispensable; and redemption demands embodied passage through political religions. Michaelson argues that this cannot be reduced to opportunism or sexual license. Critics answer that doctrine and authoritarian practice cannot be separated.

### Literature and film

Tokarczuk’s *The Books of Jacob* transformed Frank from a specialist subject into a major figure of European historical fiction. Its many perspectives recover women, servants, rabbis, priests, converts, merchants, and borderland languages while refusing a single authoritative Frank. Panek’s *Daas* concentrates the material into political thriller and conspiracy.

### Conspiracy afterlife

Because Frankism was secretive, transreligious, connected by marriage, and followed by the social success of some descendants, it is repeatedly invoked as an invisible ancestor of modern elite power. These theories typically combine true nodes—conversion, endogamy, Dobruška’s revolutionary career, Masonic contacts—with unsupported claims of continuous centralized control. The mythology is historically significant as a form of modern political demonology, but the continuous cabal is not documented.

## Atlas Connections

### Abraham

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** In teachings recorded by disciples principally at Brno and Offenbach in 1784 and 1789–1790, Frank repeatedly reinterpreted Abraham. He taught that Abraham, like the other patriarchs, had sought but failed to reach the feminine redemptive power he called the Virgin; elsewhere he recast God’s appearance to Abraham under the name *El Shaddai* through his own esoteric wordplay. This is textual engagement, not a historical encounter. [Lenowitz’s translation of *The Collection of the Words of the Lord*](https://archive.org/details/TheCollectionOfTheWordsOfTheLordJacobFrank), [overview of Frankist doctrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankism)

### King David

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Frank’s recorded teachings extensively remodel King David as a messianic predecessor and as a figure connected with the feminine redemptive principle—most provocatively in the teaching summarized as “David was secretly a woman.” Jan Doktór has devoted a scholarly study specifically to David’s reception in Frank’s thought. [*The Variety of Davids in Monotheistic Traditions*](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004465978/BP000013.pdf)

### Jesus of Nazareth

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Frank’s late teachings repeatedly interpret Jesus as a genuine but incomplete redemptive predecessor: Jesus opened a path and entered death, but did not complete the work that Frank assigned to his own company. Christianity was consequently treated as a necessary passage rather than the final religion. The connection is documented in the disciple-recorded sayings and modern close readings of them; it does not imply personal contact or ordinary Christian orthodoxy. [Oxford University Press, *The Heresy of Jacob Frank*](https://academic.oup.com/book/43750), [Lenowitz’s translation of the sayings](https://archive.org/details/TheCollectionOfTheWordsOfTheLordJacobFrank)

### Moses

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Frank’s recorded teachings repeatedly contrast the Mosaic law with the still-unrevealed religion of *Daas* and portray Moses as one of the predecessors who approached but failed to reach the Virgin. Conversion-era Frankist teaching also invoked Moses and biblical passages attributed to him when arguing for a triune God. Because some such statements survive in collective or externally transmitted Frankist documents, Frank’s personal wording should not always be assumed. [English translation of the conversion-era *Teaching of Jacob Newly Baptized*](https://andrewjacobs.org/translations/doctrina.html), [overview of Frankist doctrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankism)

### Sabbatai Zevi

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Frank’s movement arose directly from post-1666 Sabbateanism, and his disciple-recorded teachings discuss and judge Sabbatai Zevi as an earlier messianic predecessor whose work remained incomplete. Frank variously presented himself, or was presented by followers, as Zevi’s successor or later embodiment, while his mature teachings also criticized and moved beyond inherited Sabbatean Kabbalah. This is a documented intellectual and sectarian succession, not a personal meeting: Zevi died in 1676, about fifty years before Frank’s birth. [Oxford University Press, *The Heresy of Jacob Frank*](https://academic.oup.com/book/43750), [YIVO Encyclopedia, “Frankism”](https://encyclopedia.yivo.org/article.aspx/frankism)

### King Solomon

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** In *The Collection of the Words of the Lord*, Frank retells and interrogates Solomon’s encounter with the Queen of Sheba, asking why Solomon did not disclose the knowledge she needed. The passage converts the biblical meeting into a lesson about guarded or incomplete revelation. [Lenowitz’s translation of *The Collection of the Words of the Lord*](https://archive.org/details/TheCollectionOfTheWordsOfTheLordJacobFrank)

### The Queen of Sheba

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** The same disciple-recorded saying invokes the Queen of Sheba as a seeker from whom Solomon withheld necessary knowledge. Frank therefore wrote—or, more precisely, delivered an oral teaching later recorded—about both roster figures in the biblical pair. [Lenowitz’s translation of *The Collection of the Words of the Lord*](https://archive.org/details/TheCollectionOfTheWordsOfTheLordJacobFrank)
