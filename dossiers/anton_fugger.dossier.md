# Anton Fugger: Research Dossier

**Subject:** Anton Fugger von der Lilie (1493–1560)  
**Scope:** Documentary life history, family, education, business, finance, politics, religion, patronage, controversies, myth, writings, historiography, and legacy  
**Prepared:** 30 August 2026  

## Research Note and Evidentiary Conventions

Anton Fugger is unusually well documented for a sixteenth-century merchant because the surviving Fugger commercial archive contains contracts, accounts, correspondence, inventories, estate records, and testamentary papers. He is nevertheless not well served by short modern biographies. The indispensable life is the five-part, three-volume *Anton Fugger* begun by Götz Freiherr von Pölnitz and completed by Hermann Kellenbenz (1958–1986); its documentation remains fundamental, while some of its judgments and rhetoric belong to an older historiographical tradition. Mark Häberlein’s *The Fuggers of Augsburg* (2012) provides the best modern English synthesis. The online *Neue Deutsche Biographie* article, also written by Pölnitz, is exceptionally detailed but should be read as a compressed interpretation, not as a neutral inventory of facts.

This dossier uses the following labels:

- **Documented:** supported by contemporary records or a modern scholarly edition based on them.
- **Strongly supported:** accepted in modern scholarship but not always recoverable here to a single surviving document.
- **Disputed:** sources or scholars disagree.
- **Legend:** a later story without contemporary substantiation.
- **Inference:** a conclusion drawn from documented circumstances rather than directly stated by a source.

Money amounts are left in contemporary gulden/florins or ducats. These were not invariant units, and conversions into modern currency are usually misleading. “Company capital,” “assets,” “claims,” and Anton’s personal wealth are not interchangeable.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal line

Anton belonged to the **Fugger von der Lilie** (“of the Lily”), the branch descended from Jakob Fugger the Elder (d. 1469), not the separate and ultimately unsuccessful Fugger vom Reh branch. The family’s rise began with Hans Fugger’s migration from Graben to Augsburg in 1367. Hans entered the textile economy as a weaver and, more importantly, a distributor or putting-out merchant. Over successive generations the family moved from textile production and trade into Italian commerce, metals, sovereign finance, and territorial lordship.

Anton’s paternal grandparents were:

- **Jakob Fugger the Elder** (d. 1469), merchant and founder of the Lily line’s durable commercial base.
- **Barbara Bäsinger** (1419–1497), daughter of the Augsburg mint master Franz Bäsinger. She continued the family’s affairs after her husband’s death and raised a large family.

Anton’s father was **Georg Fugger** (1453–1506), a merchant, co-founder/partner in the family company, and for a substantial period its representative in Nuremberg. Georg was a brother of **Ulrich Fugger** (1441–1510) and **Jakob Fugger “the Rich”** (1459–1525). Jakob, Anton’s paternal uncle, became the dominant manager of the family enterprise and ultimately selected Anton as his managerial successor.

### Maternal line

Anton’s mother was **Regina Imhof** (also Imhoff; d. 1526), daughter of the Augsburg patrician merchant **Peter Imhof** (d. 1504) and Regina Walther. The marriage joined two commercially powerful families. It also placed Anton within the kinship world of the Augsburg upper class before the Fuggers’ formal admission to the city patriciate in 1538.

### Siblings and close cousins

- **Markus Fugger** (1488–1511), Anton’s elder brother, accumulated cathedral provostships and papal offices, becoming a protonotary and scriptor. As a cleric he was excluded from active partnership in the family company.
- **Raymund Fugger** (1489–1535), elder brother, merchant, collector, and husband of Katharina Thurzo, thereby connecting the family to its former Upper Hungarian mining partners. Raymund was a company partner but did not become its effective ruler; illness, tensions with Jakob, and an inclination toward scholarship, collecting, and country life limited his managerial role.
- **Regina Fugger** (d. 1533), sister, married the merchant Hans Baumgartner the Younger in 1512.
- **Ulrich Fugger the Younger** (1490–1525), first cousin and originally a serious succession candidate, died shortly before Jakob.
- **Hieronymus Fugger** (1499–1538), first cousin and partner, was judged unsuitable for command and excluded from sole succession by Jakob’s final arrangements.

### Marriage

Anton married **Anna Rehlinger von Horgau** (3 November 1505–25 March 1548), daughter of the patrician Hans Rehlinger von Horgau and Anna Dietenhaimer. The marriage contract is dated 5 February 1527; sources give 25 February or 3 March for the wedding, while a few derivative accounts give 25 December. The February contract and early-March celebration are the strongest reconstruction.

The match was socially important. Anna is often described as the first woman of Augsburg’s established patriciate to marry into the Fugger house. A contemporary report said that no one remembered so costly and honorable a wedding. The marriage consolidated Anton’s status within Augsburg’s elite and founded the surviving “Antonius line.”

Anna died at Schwaz in Tyrol on 25 March 1548, reportedly in or following childbirth, aged forty-two. She was buried at Babenhausen. Anton did not remarry.

### Children: a documented numerical discrepancy

The *Neue Deutsche Biographie* says **five sons and six daughters** (eleven children). Most modern family summaries list **four sons and six daughters** (ten named children). Anna’s biographical tradition says she died giving birth to an eleventh child, which may explain the discrepancy: an infant who died at or near birth may not appear in later genealogies. The ten consistently named children are:

1. **Markus (Marx) Fugger** (1529–1597), later lord of Nordendorf and founder of the Markus/Nordendorf line; married Countess Sibylla von Eberstein. He wrote on horse breeding and translated Nicephorus Callistus’s church history.
2. **Anna Fugger** (1530–1549), died young and unmarried.
3. **Hans (Johannes) Fugger** (1531–1598), later associated with Kirchheim and Glött; married Elisabeth Notthafft von Weißenstein and became ancestor of the Fugger von Glött line.
4. **Catharina (Katharina) Fugger** (1532–1585), married Jakob, Count of Montfort (d. 1572).
5. **Hieronymus (Jeronimus) Fugger** (1533–1573), landholder; died unmarried and without a surviving line.
6. **Regina Fugger** (1537–1584), married Wolfgang Dietrich, Count of Hardegg.
7. **Susanna Fugger** (1539–1588), married Balthasar II Trautson, Baron of Matrei.
8. **Jakob Fugger** (1542–1598), landholder of Babenhausen/Wellenburg; married Anna Ilsung von Tratzberg and became ancestor of the Fugger-Babenhausen line.
9. **Maria Fugger** (1543–1583), married Michael von Eitzing/Aitzing, historian, publicist, and cartographic author.
10. **Veronika Fugger** (1545–1590), married Gaudenz von Spaur.

The unlisted eleventh child, if the total of eleven is correct, appears to have died at birth in 1548; sex and name have not been securely established in the sources consulted.

Anton organized marriages, dowries, education, and territorial inheritances to move his descendants into the imperial nobility. Yet he was disappointed that his sons and nephews did not share his commercial appetite. His testament reportedly required two Montfort grandsons to spend three years in France and three in Italy so they would learn both spoken and written languages, then serve at a Catholic court—an unusually concrete expression of his educational and confessional program.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1493–c. 1512

Anton was born into wealth but not into a settled hereditary nobility. When he was thirteen, his father Georg died. His uncle Jakob consequently became still more important as employer, guardian-like authority, and model. No evidence establishes a university education. Anton’s education was the elite merchant formation of the period: literacy and numeracy, correspondence, accounting, languages, appraisal of metals and cloth, contract law in practice, political intelligence, and prolonged work in branch offices.

He did not begin as the predetermined heir. His elder brother Raymund and cousins Ulrich and Hieronymus all stood between him and sole management. The competition was real. Jakob’s company agreements admitted male family members as fully liable partners and normally ran for fixed terms; authority was increasingly concentrated in a single *Regierer* or *Schaffierer*.

An agreement with Jakob’s nephews was concluded in 1512 and renewed thereafter. Anton’s rise therefore occurred within a contractual family partnership, not through the simple personal inheritance suggested by later popular biographies.

## Education and Formation, c. 1512–1525

### Rotating apprenticeship through the network

Anton trained in the Augsburg center and in major branches or operating zones. The documentary biography names **Nuremberg, Breslau/Wrocław, Buda (often rendered Budapest in modern sources), Italy, Rome, and Tyrol**. This was not leisure travel: each location exposed him to a different part of the enterprise—German textile and metal markets, eastern-central European mining and logistics, Italian finance and papal business, and Tyrolean mines and smelters.

In 1523–24 Jakob sent Anton and cousin Ulrich with the experienced factor **Georg Hörmann** to Tyrol. A surviving registry concerns copper and silver deliveries under a sovereign contract, while the appointment gave the young men legal power to represent the company. The operation combined advances to the ruler, compelled ore deliveries at fixed prices, smelting at Rattenberg, and mine ownership acquired from bankrupt operators. This assignment illustrates Anton’s practical education in the integrated credit–mining–metallurgy system he later inherited.

### Rome and humanist formation

Anton spent several years in Rome. The experience gave him:

- familiarity with the papal court and curial finance;
- Italian and Latin cultural competence;
- access to humanists, clerics, diplomats, and printers;
- a reputation for judgment under conditions where commerce and church politics were inseparable.

He helped introduce the theologian **Johann Eck** at the papal court. Eck was already linked to the Fuggers’ defense of interest-bearing commercial contracts and became Luther’s prominent Catholic antagonist. Anton’s successful Roman service appears to have been a decisive reason Jakob trusted him with succession.

Anton later maintained a genuine patronal friendship with **Erasmus of Rotterdam**. Erasmus’s correspondence documents gifts and benefits from Anton. A letter of 7 July 1529 thanks him for a gold cup; in 1531 Erasmus wrote of Anton’s “extraordinary friendship” and accumulated favors. Anton offered 100 gold florins for moving expenses and another 100 annually if Erasmus would settle in Augsburg. Erasmus declined. This relationship is evidence of Anton’s humanist social world, not proof that he shared every Erasmian religious position.

### Selection as successor

Jakob Fugger changed or confirmed his testamentary arrangements on 22 December 1525, eight days before his death. Raymund and Anton inherited partnership interests, but Anton received decisive managerial responsibility. Hieronymus remained a partner without command. Modern summaries often say Anton inherited “the firm”; legally and economically, he inherited a controlling position within a family partnership and a complex estate whose capital belonged in different shares to several participants and depositors.

## Chronological Life History

### 1525–1527: succession, Hungary, and the first inventory

Jakob died on 30 December 1525. Anton, aged thirty-two, assumed the leadership of an enterprise exposed to Habsburg debt, mining risk, and a major dispute in Hungary. His first large success was a negotiated reconciliation with the Hungarian crown in 1526, shortly before King Louis II died at Mohács. The Fugger–Thurzo Upper Hungarian copper enterprise had been under political attack; Anton restored its legal position and commercial possibilities.

After Mohács, Anton financed **Ferdinand of Habsburg’s claims to the Bohemian and Hungarian crowns**, placing the company against Ferdinand’s rival **John Zápolya**. This was at once a political choice and protection of the firm’s mining and credit interests.

Charles V renewed or extended the family’s noble/comital privileges in 1526. The exact sequence has produced simplified but conflicting dates in popular accounts: 1526, 1530, and 1535 all mark different confirmations or authorizations. It is safest to say Anton’s comital status developed through a sequence of Habsburg privileges rather than a single clean “promotion.”

The **1527 company inventory**, ordered after Jakob’s death and edited by Jakob Strieder, is a central primary source. Figures differ depending on whether one cites total assets, liabilities, surplus, or a partner’s “proper capital.” The NDB gives Anton’s company capital in 1527 as about **1.6 million gulden**. Other reconstructions of the 1527 inventory give total assets around 3.0 million, liabilities around 0.87 million, and net surplus around 2.13 million gulden. The frequently repeated 2,032,652-gulden “inheritance” compresses company and estate accounting into a single number and should not be treated as Anton’s personal cash fortune.

The **Sack of Rome in May 1527** made the company’s open imperial alignment untenable at the papal court. Anton formally closed the Roman factoría, though indirect contacts and services continued. In the same year he married Anna Rehlinger.

### 1528–1531: Transylvanian salt, Vienna, Augsburg, and a second Habsburg election

In 1528 Ferdinand imposed, or induced the firm to accept, a restitution arrangement involving the **Transylvanian salt lease**. The venture was politically important but commercially uncertain. It deepened the firm’s exposure to war, broken communications, and unstable royal promises.

Fugger credits and arms deliveries assisted Habsburg defense during the Ottoman siege of Vienna in 1529. Anton repeatedly wanted a more independent or neutral commercial policy, but the scale of Habsburg indebtedness and the value of Habsburg concessions made disengagement difficult.

At the **Diet of Augsburg in 1530**, Charles V and Ferdinand pressed Anton and the Welsers to finance Ferdinand’s election as King of the Romans. The Fugger house already faced more than a million gulden in Habsburg obligations. One scholarly reconstruction gives Anton’s additional advance at 275,333 gulden plus an annuity of 7,000 gulden for Elector Albrecht of Mainz; Pölnitz’s NDB narrative places the combined Fugger–Welser resources mobilized for Ferdinand’s 1531 election and coronation at **1.5 million ducats**. These figures refer to different layers of the operation and are not necessarily contradictory, but they should not be silently merged.

### Spanish revenues, Almadén, and colonial plans

The Spanish trade was built on **asientos**, contracts by which the firm advanced money to the crown against designated revenues. Beginning in 1525, repayment securities included the **Maestrazgos**, revenues of the military orders of Santiago, Calatrava, and Alcántara. These brought cash and agricultural tithes and provided access to the **Almadén cinnabar and mercury complex**.

Mercury became strategically valuable when used to amalgamate American silver. Under Anton, Almadén and the Maestrazgo revenues were among the firm’s potentially richest but politically contested securities. The lease was repeatedly renewed, interrupted, or competed for. It is important not to project later conditions backward: Spanish convict labor at Almadén was introduced in 1566, six years after Anton’s death, and the large documented use of enslaved labor belongs principally to later Fugger leases. Anton profited from the dangerous mercury economy, but the specific convict regime often associated with “the Fuggers at Almadén” was not instituted by him.

In **1531**, Charles V considered broad concessions to the Fuggers along the Pacific coast of South America and offshore islands, especially in areas corresponding to Peru and Chile. The plan may have assumed a passage or transport connection across Panama. The surviving evidence indicates a draft or projected agreement, not a successfully ratified Fugger colony. Anton abandoned or allowed the project to lapse by about 1533, plausibly because returns did not justify the risk. Claims that he “owned Peru and Chile” are false; claims that the firm developed transatlantic trade connections to the West Indies, Mexico, and the Río de la Plata world are broadly supported but should not be confused with territorial rule.

### 1532–1535: consolidated command, rivals, and war finance

The 1532 partnership renewal made Anton’s personal command explicit. After Raymund’s death in 1535 and Hieronymus’s in 1538, Anton was the last active senior partner of his generation.

His rivalry with the **Höchstetter** company was severe. When Ferdinand favored the Höchstetters and sought to place the Almadén opportunity with them, Anton defended the Fugger concession. The NDB says that, working through Antwerp and with Lazarus Tucher, he helped bring about the competitors’ ruin and pursued the conflict in Lyon. This is evidence of aggressive commercial warfare—use of credit, market position, court influence, and information—not a criminal conviction. Ambrosius Höchstetter’s larger overextension and the failure of his attempted mercury/copper monopoly were also material causes of the firm’s collapse.

Anton financed unsuccessful or only partially successful Habsburg projects:

- Ferdinand’s 1534 campaign to recover Württemberg;
- plans for Catholic restoration in Denmark;
- an intervention in the cardinalate intended to favor a German papal candidate rather than Alessandro Farnese/Paul III;
- formation efforts for a Catholic league under Austrian leadership;
- Charles V’s 1535 Tunis expedition, supported through Spanish contracts.

In 1534 the family received a coinage privilege. Such rewards were not charitable honors detached from finance: they formed part of the reciprocal political economy of credit, jurisdiction, and status.

### Augsburg’s Reformation and Anton’s retreat to Weißenhorn, 1533–1536

Anton’s relationship with Augsburg’s increasingly Protestant government deteriorated. A conflict with Zwinglian reformers led to a brief detention or confinement; the details and legal form are less consistently summarized than the event itself. From 1533 he withdrew to the family lordship of **Weißenhorn**, returning to Augsburg around 1536. The move was simultaneously confessional, political, and practical: country lordships offered refuge from urban religious conflict, epidemic disease, and civic jurisdiction.

His Catholic identity sharpened after Augsburg’s municipal Reformation in 1537. Yet commerce prevented a clean confessional bloc policy. Anton negotiated with Protestant rulers such as **Christian III of Denmark**, **Albrecht of Prussia**, and later the English Tudors when trade or transit required it.

### 1533–1545: building a territorial base

Anton steadily transferred some wealth from risky commerce and sovereign credit into jurisdictional land. The acquisitions recorded by the *Historisches Lexikon Bayerns* include:

| 1533 | Glött |
| 1534 | Hirbishofen |
| 1537 | Oberndorf am Lech; Kleinkissendorf |
| 1536/39 | Babenhausen (purchase and consolidation occurred in stages; popular accounts usually say 1538) |
| 1539 | Brandenburg near Dietenheim |

| 1547 | Markt Rettenbach; Pleß |

He also bought the imperial bailiwick or pledge of **Donauwörth** in connection with the 1536 imperial campaign in France. Babenhausen became his most important personal residence and the nucleus of a surviving dynastic line. Construction or major rebuilding of Babenhausen castle began around 1541.

This program was not mere retirement. Lordship supplied rents, courts, labor obligations, textile workers, offices for clients, marriage capital, safe residences, and a path to durable noble status. It also subjected villagers and townspeople to Fugger manorial jurisdiction. The estate was later tied through entail-like family rules and divided among male heirs.

### 1536–1543: global diversification amid Habsburg risk

Anton’s network extended through Antwerp, Iberia, Italy, Central Europe, Scandinavia, and the Ottoman frontier. The firm traded or financed:

- copper, silver, mercury/cinnabar, lead, brass, and manufactured copperware;
- fustian/Barchent and other textiles;
- spices and Mediterranean goods;
- livestock, later including projected Balkan ox trading;
- bills of exchange and transfers for courts;
- sovereign loans secured on taxes, mines, rents, or future revenues;
- news and political intelligence carried by factors and correspondence.

The business sought mines in Sweden and Norway, mercury at Idrija, and renewed eastern connections. It participated in Atlantic and Iberian trade and received proceeds linked to American silver fleets. The older NDB uses the period term “Negerhandel” and states that profits from African trade supported the company. A more precise documentary statement is possible for 1548: Anton’s firm contracted to deliver huge quantities of brass rings/manillas and copper/brass vessels to Portugal’s **Casa da Índia e Mina** for Guinea and Elmina. Such objects functioned as money and exchange goods in West African commerce, including the purchase of enslaved people. The contract therefore materially supplied the Portuguese African trading system and its slave economy, even where surviving accounting does not identify each final transaction.

The firm also invested in voyages toward the Moluccas and claimed compensation for losses. Anton threatened withdrawal from Hungary when Habsburg guarantees failed. In 1541 he renewed relations with the Tudor and Vasa monarchies, and in 1543 the Fuggers and Welsers raised a new 300,000-ducat bill for Charles V’s wars against France and the Ottoman Empire.

### 1544–1548: England, the Schmalkaldic War, and the peak balance sheet

From the mid-1540s England became a major diversification strategy. Under **Henry VIII**, **Edward VI**, **Mary I**, and at the very end of Anton’s life **Elizabeth I**, the firm made loans, sold copper, handled exchange, and attempted to establish a market for south German fustian. A probable London branch or agency is documented. English naval expansion created copper demand; Tudor purchases could also serve as in-kind or market support for loan renewals.

The English business was profitable enough to offset some Spanish risk, but it was not frictionless. Channel piracy caused transport losses. Ulm’s textile interests attacked Fugger competition, and Anton ended the English-oriented fustian branch in 1555. Thomas Gresham became an important financial counterpart and later helped collect English and Spanish obligations.

The firm’s own capital reached its documented high point in **1546**, usually given as about **five million gulden**. This was company equity, not a pile of cash and not wholly Anton’s private fortune. One often-cited posthumous accounting gives about 5.6 million gulden in assets and about 5.4 million in outstanding obligations or claims; interpretation depends on whether “Schulden” means debts owed by the firm or sums owed to it in the source being paraphrased. By 1558, 1.7 million ducats were reportedly tied up in Spanish claims with little prospect of prompt payment. The best conclusion is that Anton controlled a firm with unprecedented gross resources but declining liquidity and immense sovereign-credit concentration.

#### Financing the Schmalkaldic War

In 1546 Charles V secretly informed Anton at Donauwörth that imperial resources would be directed not only against the Ottoman enemy but against the Protestant Schmalkaldic League. Anton had hoped for religious negotiation and a crusading focus. He nevertheless helped mobilize funds through Fugger and Welser networks. The resulting position was structurally contradictory:

- he was a committed Catholic and creditor of Charles V;
- his home city joined the Protestant side;
- many customers, routes, and allies were Protestant;
- the company’s existing loans made a rupture with the emperor financially dangerous;
- both sides exaggerated his freedom to determine imperial policy.

Anton moved company headquarters first to neutral Bavarian territory and then to **Schwaz in Tyrol**. He delayed some imperial financing and refused subsidies to the opposing league, attempting to limit the war without openly defecting. Fugger agents nevertheless accompanied imperial forces and money reached Charles before and after the victory at Mühlberg.

In early 1547 Anton returned to Augsburg and acted as mediator. At Ulm he knelt before Charles V in a celebrated “footfall,” asking clemency for Augsburg and assisting with its contribution payments. Later memory sometimes says he “saved Augsburg from destruction.” The mediation and financial sacrifice are documented; the counterfactual claim that only Anton prevented a sack is not provable.

Relations with Charles worsened after victory. The emperor stayed in the Fugger house, but Anton initially avoided visiting him. The company faced compelled payments that Pölnitz characterized as bordering on extortion. In 1547 Anton terminated the firm’s Hungarian partnership exposure and began liquidations. These facts are the historical background from which the later debt-note-burning legend drew its emotional plausibility—but they point toward conflict over repayment, not voluntary cancellation.

### 1548: family settlement, foundations, and bereavement

1548 was a hinge year.

- Anna Fugger died in March at Schwaz, probably following childbirth.
- Anton and the Raymund branch divided territorial property and established rules designed to retain land within the male line. Daughters received dowries and suitable settlements but not equal participation in the entailed lordships.
- Anton separated the Tyrolean and Carinthian trade from the central company structure.
- On **31 July 1548**, he renewed Jakob Fugger’s 1521 foundation charter in explicitly Catholic form after the Schmalkaldic War and the Augsburg Interim.
- He made the existing **Holz- und Blatternhaus** a separate foundation. It treated poor patients suffering from syphilis and related diseases, using the contemporary “wood” and mercury cures; revenue paid doctors, attendants, treatment, and aftercare.
- He implemented cousin Hieronymus’s bequest by creating the hospital at **Waltenhausen**, with places for twenty-five women and men, prioritizing Fugger servants, subjects, distressed relatives, and local poor.

The renewed Fuggerei rules required residents to be needy Catholic citizens of Augsburg and to say prescribed prayers. The foundations combined material welfare, confessional discipline, commemoration of the founders, and governance of dependents; none of these purposes cancels the others.

### 1549–1554: attempted withdrawal and renewed dependence

In 1549 Anton separated his personal interest from the Spanish business and contemplated dissolving the company. His second testament, dated 1550, may indicate an expectation of declining health or early death. He sought alternatives in Antwerp, Lisbon, England, Russia, and the Levant.

Yet Habsburg dependence persisted. Company money supported:

- imperial forces under Maurice of Saxony before Magdeburg;
- the Medici–Spanish side against Siena and France;
- Charles V during the **Princes’ Revolt of 1552**;
- the failed reconquest of Metz;
- defense activity around Brunswick;
- diplomatic finance for Philip’s proposed marriage to Mary Tudor.

The **Asiento of Villach**, 28 May 1552, was designed to rescue Charles V during the princes’ uprising. Once the immediate crisis passed, repayment again became irregular. The firm was forced to accept weak or “unusable” securities in the Netherlands and was excluded in 1553 from the Guadalcanal silver opportunity. A large loan in 1553 did not restore trust.

Anton sent the veteran factor and scholar **Hans Dernschwam** with Ogier Ghiselin de Busbecq’s Habsburg embassy to Constantinople and Anatolia in 1553–55. Dernschwam’s surviving diary is a major travel source. The mission combined scholarship, observation, diplomacy, and probable reconnaissance for southeastern trade; it was not proof of a covert intelligence service in the modern sense, but Fugger factors plainly collected strategic commercial and political intelligence.

### 1555–1560: illness, Spanish crisis, Catholic consolidation, and final plans

Anton’s enemies included followers of **Margrave Albrecht Alcibiades**, who threatened his life and possessions during the Second Margraves’ War. From 1555 the firm financed Franconian ecclesiastical territories resisting Albrecht.

At the 1555 Diet of Augsburg Ferdinand lodged in the Fugger palace, but Anton’s relations with both Habsburg branches remained cold. In 1556 the firm increased loans to Philip II in an effort to recover favor and protect its Spanish position. Competition also came from the Frankfurt financier **Joseph of the Golden Swan**. The Antwerp and Iberian books were dangerously overextended.

By 1557 chronic illness impaired Anton’s daily management. When the Antwerp factor **Matthäus Örtel** made unauthorized or overly compliant deals with Philip II and Spanish authorities seized Fugger silver transports, Anton intervened: he dismissed Örtel, reduced Spanish investment, and wound down the Weißenhorn textile operation. He still renewed lending after Spanish military victories because a direct break threatened existing assets. The firm survived the financial crises of 1557 and restored its Antwerp credit in 1558, but at lower profitability.

Anton cultivated English relations through Thomas Gresham and Elizabeth I and explored Russian capital. Plans included Venetian spice trade, Balkan cattle, a return to Hungarian mining, Levant ventures, and Tuscan mining. At the same time he considered abandoning unprofitable Tyrolean mining. His late policy was therefore not simple decline but rapid experimentation under severe liquidity constraints.

Religiously, Anton moved from Erasmian humanist tolerance toward a more confessional Catholic program. His contacts included **Ignatius of Loyola**, **Peter Canisius**, **Stanislaus Hosius**, and **Charles Borromeo**. He promoted Catholic renewal in Augsburg, supported plans for a Jesuit college, and attempted to orient publishing and education toward Catholic authors. Pölnitz also connects him to efforts to encourage a Catholic war associated with Aegidius Tschudi in Switzerland; the extent of Anton’s direct operational role deserves cautious treatment, but financial encouragement of anti-Protestant action is supported in the specialist biography.

On **26 July 1560**, weeks before his death, Anton added charitable and personal provisions to his third will. The surviving distribution record lists **57 legacies totaling 10,900 gulden**, plus **1,000 gulden for alms**. Beneficiaries included lawyers, the Spanish representative, financial clerks, estate administrators, chief accountant Matthäus Schwarz, and household servants such as his waiter, cook, stable keeper, maids, and other long-term dependents. The will also endowed the **Schneidhaus**, a surgical foundation providing free operations for poor patients suffering from hernias, bladder stones, and related conditions.

Anton died in Augsburg on **14 September 1560** after a long illness, aged sixty-seven. He was buried in Babenhausen. His choice of burial reflected the confessional loss of St. Anna in Augsburg as a secure Catholic family tomb.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### The Fugger company’s structure under Anton

The “Fugger bank” was not a modern incorporated bank. It was a family partnership—the **Gemeiner Handel**—renewed by fixed-term contracts, with male family partners personally invested and liable. It employed factors, accountants, correspondents, mine managers, smelter operators, lawyers, estate officials, transport agents, and court brokers. Anton exercised monarchical managerial authority but did not own every asset personally.

Key officials and collaborators included:

- **Matthäus Schwarz**, chief accountant and creator of sophisticated ledgers and inventories;
- **Georg Hörmann**, Tyrolean representative and trusted deputy;
- **Lazarus Tucher**, ally in commercial struggle against the Höchstetters;
- **Veit Hörl**, Antwerp factor and later co-benefactor of the medical foundation;
- **Matthäus Örtel**, Antwerp factor dismissed in the Spanish crisis;
- **Hans Dernschwam**, factor, mining observer, scholar, and Ottoman traveler;
- **Johannes Mylius**, Madrid legal counsel;
- **Thomas Gresham**, English royal financial agent and business counterpart;
- the **Welser company**, both competitor and repeated co-financier.

### Integrated metals business

Anton inherited and expanded a vertically connected system:

1. lending to rulers and mine owners;
2. receiving mining output, purchase rights, tax income, or leases as security;
3. operating or investing in mines and smelters;
4. transporting metals through central European, Baltic, Italian, and Atlantic routes;
5. selling copper, silver, mercury, brass, and finished wares;
6. recycling the proceeds into exchange and sovereign loans.

Principal zones included Tyrol/Schwaz, Carinthia, Upper Hungary (today chiefly Slovakia), Transylvania, Almadén in Spain, Idrija, Scandinavia, and late Tuscan ventures. Technologies included ore dressing and smelting, copper–silver separation, cinnabar roasting, and the mercury supply needed for silver amalgamation. Anton did not personally invent these techniques and held no “patents” in the modern sense; his innovation lay in capitalization, contracting, logistics, integration, and political security.

### Textiles and rural industry

The company retained the family’s older fustian/Barchent trade. Anton developed production among rural and small-town weavers in his Swabian lordships, especially around Weißenhorn, and sought English markets. The arrangement supplied workers with materials or market access but also brought them under merchant control. Ulm’s opposition and poor trading conditions contributed to closure by 1555–57.

### Sovereign finance

Borrowers included Charles V, Ferdinand I, Philip II, Henry VIII, Edward VI, Mary I, Elizabeth I, Christian III of Denmark, Portuguese authorities, Italian states and princes, and ecclesiastical territories. Loans were typically tied to:

- tax farms and military-order revenues;
- mining leases and metal deliveries;
- customs or regional rents;
- bills of exchange in Antwerp, Spain, Italy, or Germany;
- dynastic marriages, elections, campaigns, or emergency military payrolls.

The system gave Anton political access but produced concentrated default risk. Habsburg “repayment” often meant a new concession, extension, forced rollover, or inferior security. This was not money laundering in the modern legal sense; there is no documented criminal prosecution for laundering, fraud, or embezzlement. It was opaque, influence-driven public finance in a period before modern parliamentary budgets, disclosure law, or banking regulation.

### News and intelligence

The Fugger correspondence network carried prices, shipping notices, military news, diplomatic rumors, court decisions, and political analysis. Factors asked what fleets and armies were doing and transmitted information across borders faster than many governments. This is properly described as a commercial intelligence network. No evidence consulted establishes that Anton belonged to a secret state intelligence agency. The later famous “Fugger newsletters” mainly survive from after his death (especially 1568–1605) and should not be falsely attributed to him, though they grew from the communications system he operated.

### Territorial lordship

Anton’s land program created a portfolio of castles, courts, villages, market rights, rents, forests, farms, and dependent labor. He acquired Babenhausen, Glött, Kirchheim, Boos, and numerous smaller lordships while maintaining the inherited Kirchberg–Weißenhorn complex with the Raymund line. In 1548 and through testamentary rules, the properties were bound to male-line family continuity. This stabilized the dynasty while limiting daughters to dowries and excluding them from equal succession to the core lordships.

### Foundations

Anton preserved or founded institutions with unusually long afterlives:

- legal reorganization of Jakob’s Fuggerei and church foundations (1548);
- Holz- und Blatternhaus Foundation for syphilis and skin disease (1548);
- Waltenhausen hospital (1548, implementing Hieronymus Fugger’s testament);
- Schneidhaus surgical foundation (testamentary, 1560);
- archive regulations in 1554 and 1560 that helped preserve the commercial and family records.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Political method

Anton held power without becoming a territorial prince or elected city ruler. His methods were lending, hosting, intercession, information, dynastic brokerage, market pressure, strategic delay, and the placement of trusted agents. He financed elections and wars yet repeatedly sought peace when war endangered routes, mines, customers, and Augsburg.

His record cannot be reduced to “Habsburg loyalist.” He financed Ferdinand’s crowns and Charles’s armies, but threatened withdrawal, diversified to England and Russia, assisted a captive Saxon elector, negotiated with Protestant rulers, and ended the Hungarian connection. Loyalty, coercion, sunk exposure, Catholic allegiance, and profit operated together.

### Religious evolution

Anton’s early world joined Catholic institutional loyalty with Erasmian humanism. His friendship with Erasmus, Italian cultural patronage, and cross-confessional business show that confessional separation was initially incomplete. Augsburg’s Reformation, his detention and exile-like withdrawal, the Schmalkaldic War, and Jesuit contacts progressively hardened his Catholic program.

By the late 1550s he supported:

- restoration and endowment of Catholic worship;
- Catholic eligibility rules in foundations;
- Jesuit settlement and education;
- Catholic publishing and scholarly networks;
- finance for Catholic territories and, in some cases, anti-Protestant political action.

There is no evidence that Anton converted, was excommunicated, or experienced a documented personal crisis of faith. The shift is reconstructed from patronage, correspondence, and political action.

### Humanism, books, and music

Anton associated with Erasmus, Mariangelo Accursio, Johann Eck, Hieronymus Wolf, and other scholars. **Mariangelo Accursio** collaborated with or received support from him in poetry, philology, epigraphy, book publication, and plans connected with the Fugger chapel. Anton supported editions rather than writing their scholarly texts.

Works dedicated to or patronized by members of the Fugger family include classical and musical publications. The 1534 Augsburg edition *Poëtae tres egregii nunc primum in lucem editi*, edited by Georg von Logau, is associated with Anton’s patronage. He should not be listed as its author.

The Fuggers maintained major book and music collections and employed scholars such as **Hieronymus Wolf**, later Anton’s librarian and a foundational editor of Byzantine historical texts. Anton’s cultural role was that of collector, commissioner, financier, and network builder. The exact boundary between his collection and those of Raymund and Hans Jakob Fugger is often blurred in popular accounts.

### Art and self-representation

Hans Maler’s 1525 portrait presents Anton in costly but controlled dress, with fine gloves and a combined toothpick/earpick pendant. It projects cultivated elite restraint rather than mercantile inventory. Anton commissioned or supported architecture, inscriptions, furnishings, medals, portraits, castle building, chapel projects, and genealogical memory. The **Ehrenbuch der Fugger** (Book of Honors), commissioned around 1545 by his nephew Hans Jakob Fugger, helped transform commercial ascent into dynastic history. Anton was a subject and family authority behind that culture, not the book’s author.

## Wealth and Financial Reconstruction

### What can be stated securely

- The 1527 post-Jakob inventory showed a firm with net resources above two million gulden; Anton’s own “proper capital” is given at approximately 1.6 million.
- By 1546 Fugger company equity reached approximately **five million gulden**, its historical high.
- The company used extensive outside deposits and borrowed capital. Gross assets therefore cannot be treated as Anton’s net personal fortune.
- A late accounting tradition gives approximately **5.6 million gulden** in assets and **5.4 million** in debts/claims at or after Anton’s death, but summaries translate the accounting categories inconsistently.
- By 1558 approximately **1.7 million ducats** in Spanish claims were considered unlikely to be repaid promptly.
- Anton’s 1560 will distributed 10,900 gulden in 57 personal/business legacies and another 1,000 in alms, separate from the main inheritance and foundation capital.

### Why modern-dollar estimates fail

A gulden can be converted by silver or gold weight, wage purchasing power, grain prices, state revenue, GDP share, or relative command over labor; each produces radically different results. Claims that Anton possessed “hundreds of billions” in modern dollars are illustrative analogies, not historical balance sheets. The strongest relative claim is that he headed probably the wealthiest private commercial organization in Europe and, in company capital, surpassed the high point reached under Jakob.

### Successes

- stabilized succession after 1525;
- restored the Hungarian position in 1526;
- defended Almadén/Maestrazgo interests;
- more than doubled company capital by the 1546 peak;
- diversified into English, Atlantic, Scandinavian, and Mediterranean markets;
- expanded territorial assets and dynastic status;
- preserved the company through the Schmalkaldic War and 1557 credit crisis;
- created legal and archival structures that outlived the trading firm.

### Failures and burdens

- inability to disengage from Habsburg sovereign risk;
- repeated involuntary rollovers and weak securities;
- unrealized Pacific colony and mining projects;
- loss and final abandonment of Upper Hungarian operations;
- failure of several Habsburg military and dynastic projects he financed;
- declining profitability after 1546;
- Spanish sequestration and 1.7 million ducats in doubtful claims;
- collapse of the English fustian project;
- no equally committed commercial successor among his sons.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### Financing war and confessional violence — documented

Anton’s money and exchange network supported armies in the Ottoman wars, Charles V’s North African campaigns, wars against France, the Schmalkaldic War, the siege of Magdeburg, the campaign against Siena, the failed recovery of Metz, and resistance to Albrecht Alcibiades. He attempted mediation in 1546–47, but this does not erase the material role of Fugger credit in imperial military capacity. The degree to which he knowingly financed a war against German Protestants before his private 1546 conversation with Charles is disputed; after that point, awareness is clear.

### Bribery and imperial elections — documented system, anachronistic terminology

Anton participated in financing inducements, annuities, and electoral costs for Ferdinand’s election as King of the Romans in 1530–31, analogous to Jakob’s role in Charles’s 1519 election. Contemporary electoral politics treated gifts, pensions, and negotiated payments as routine instruments, though many observers condemned them. Calling every payment “criminal bribery” imposes later statute law; denying the purchase of political support would be equally misleading.

### Monopolistic power — documented controversy

The Fuggers sought privileged access and near-monopoly positions in copper, silver, mercury, and related transport. Reichstag debates attacked “monopolies,” and miners and rival cities resented fixed-price delivery arrangements, long hours, unreliable pay, and Fugger rights. Anton’s company used court influence and contractual privileges to defeat competitors. No surviving judgment criminally convicted Anton of unlawful monopoly in the modern sense.

### Labor conditions in mines and lordships — structural, incompletely individualized

Mining was dangerous: collapse, dust, fumes, water, heat, and mercury exposure injured workers. Tyrolean miners protested working hours, delayed pay, and merchant privileges. Anton benefited as owner, smelter, purchaser, and concession holder. Sources consulted do not document him personally ordering a specific fatal safety violation. At Almadén, the notorious convict workforce began in 1566 after Anton’s death; later enslaved labor likewise should not be retroactively attributed to his personal administration.

Anton’s Swabian properties included seigneurial courts and dependent or unfree peasants. Land acquisition created durable aristocratic rule as well as a safe investment. Later legal histories of Kirchberg–Weißenhorn explicitly study **Leibeigenschaft** (serfdom/bondage) under Fugger rule.

### Atlantic slavery connection — documented supply relationship, limits of attribution

In 1548 Anton’s firm contracted to supply Portugal with vast quantities of brass rings/manillas and manufactured brass/copper goods destined for Guinea and Elmina. Manillas were a currency of West African exchange and were used in the purchase of enslaved Africans. The contract embeds the Fugger enterprise in the material supply chain of Portuguese slave trading. It is stronger evidence than a vague assertion that Anton made “profits from Africa,” but it does not by itself calculate how many enslaved persons were bought with Fugger metal or prove that Anton directly owned a slave ship.

### Colonial expansion — proposed, partly commercial, not a Fugger colony

Anton pursued American commerce and considered an enormous Pacific concession. The agreement was not ratified or implemented, and he withdrew. The firm nevertheless profited from imperial revenue streams, mercury used in colonial silver production, Atlantic metal sales, and goods moving through Seville and Portuguese routes. He was a financier and supplier within Iberian empire, not a conquistador governor.

### Destruction of the Höchstetters — aggressive practice, not a proven crime

The NDB states that Anton used Antwerp and Lyon operations and Lazarus Tucher’s help to accelerate the Höchstetter collapse. The rival firm was already dangerously leveraged. “Ruined his rivals” is a fair description of strategy; fraud or criminal conspiracy has not been established by the consulted evidence.

### Insider dealing, corruption, and money laundering — no modern finding

Anton’s world had no modern securities regulator, anti-money-laundering law, corporate disclosure regime, or public procurement code. He traded on private court information, combined credit with political concessions, and used agents to transfer funds across jurisdictions. These practices would raise modern conflict-of-interest and corruption questions, but there was no investigation, indictment, trial, or conviction of Anton for fraud, corruption, laundering, or embezzlement. It is better to document the transactions than attach a modern criminal label without a governing statute.

### Imprisonment and threats

- A conflict with Augsburg Zwinglians produced a brief imprisonment or detention in the early 1530s; details require consultation of the full Pölnitz volumes for exact legal form.
- Followers of Albrecht Alcibiades threatened Anton’s life and property in the mid-1550s.
- No exile sentence is documented, but Anton repeatedly withdrew from Augsburg to Weißenhorn, neutral Bavaria, or Schwaz for safety and political reasons.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### The burning of Charles V’s debt notes

#### 1. What the story says

In its best-known form, Emperor Charles V visited Anton Fugger in Augsburg, sometimes dated to 1530 or 1535. Anton supposedly burned costly cinnamon or other exotic wood in the fireplace, then threw the emperor’s bonds or promissory notes into the flames, demonstrating that a Fugger could afford both the world’s dearest fuel and the cancellation of an imperial debt. Some versions place the scene in the Fugger house; modern Augsburg tourism has also attached it to a fireplace at the Drei Mohren hotel. Other retellings substitute Jakob Fugger for Anton.

#### 2. When and where it appears

Fugger historian Richard Ehrenberg traced the Anton version only to the **end of the seventeenth century**, more than a century after Anton’s death. Similar tales circulated about other magnanimous merchants. An eighteenth/nineteenth-century literary tradition elaborated it. Karl Ludwig Friedrich Becker’s painting **“Kaiser Karl V. bei Fugger” / “Anton Fugger burning the debenture bonds of Charles V” (1866)** fixed the scene in German historical imagination. Cataloguers have sometimes identified the merchant as Jakob because the debts recall the 1519 election; chronological analysis makes Anton the possible host if Charles is already emperor.

#### 3. Who circulated it

Family and civic anecdote, antiquarian histories, popular biographies, historical painters, schoolbooks, tourist literature, and later online retellings. The tale served Augsburg civic pride and the image of unwavering Fugger loyalty.

#### 4. Evidence for and against

**Against:** no contemporary chronicle, letter, ledger, imperial record, or company account records a voluntary destruction of the obligations. The Fugger archive’s own historical account calls the story invented. Pölnitz’s documentary biography likewise labels it legend. Anton’s actual correspondence and actions show repeated attempts to secure repayment, resistance to compelled lending, cooling relations after 1547, and alarm over doubtful Spanish paper. Destroying enforceable instruments would contradict the documented struggle, though human inconsistency alone would not disprove it.

**For:** nothing beyond late tradition and the fact that Charles did lodge in Fugger properties. The story’s internal plausibility comes from real imperial debts and lavish Fugger hospitality, not from evidence of the event.

#### 5. Effect on reputation

The legend recast a hard creditor repeatedly coerced by sovereign borrowers as a feudal retainer whose loyalty transcended profit. It also dramatized the inversion of status: the merchant was rich enough to forgive an emperor. Becker’s painting made this invented act one of the most recognizable images of Fugger history.

**Finding:** legend; no historically secure evidence that either Anton or Jakob burned imperial debt notes.

### “Richest man in history”

The epithet is more commonly attached to Jakob but increasingly transferred to Anton because company capital peaked under him. The underlying fact—Anton headed an extraordinary concentration of private commercial capital—is sound. Ranking him against Mansa Musa, Rockefeller, or modern billionaires requires incompatible conversions and cannot be established as a fact.

### “He owned Peru and Chile” or traded personally in Buenos Aires

This compresses proposed concessions and commercial reach into territorial possession. The 1531 Pacific project was not implemented. “Buenos Aires” in older biographies is a geographical shorthand for trade reaching the Río de la Plata zone, not evidence that Anton visited the city or governed it. There is no evidence he traveled to the Americas.

### “The first multinational corporation” or “first global corporation”

These are modern analogies. The Fugger company had branches, agents, vertically integrated production, multinational finance, and information systems. It was nevertheless a personally liable family partnership embedded in princely privilege, not a joint-stock corporation with limited liability and impersonal shares.

### “He controlled kings”

Fugger credit constrained rulers and bought access. Rulers also coerced the company, changed repayment terms, seized metal, imposed weak paper, and used sovereign law and violence. The relationship was reciprocal dependence, not simple private command of the state.

### The savior of Augsburg

Anton’s 1547 mediation, kneeling at Ulm, and aid with indemnities are documented. The claim that he alone prevented Charles from burning or sacking Augsburg is a heroic counterfactual. It belongs to civic memory rather than provable event history.

### Dynastic self-mythology in the *Ehrenbuch*

The illustrated *Ehrenbuch der Fugger* (1545–47), commissioned by Hans Jakob Fugger, presented the family’s lineage and honors as a coherent ascent. Modern scholarship shows that some contrasts—especially the virtuous, steady Lily line versus the quickly rising and collapsing Deer line—were shaped by retrospective family ideology. The manuscript is a primary source for how Anton’s generation wanted its past remembered, not a transparent genealogy.

## Works by Anton Fugger

Anton was not an author in the normal literary sense. No book, treatise, manifesto, technical paper, patent, or memoir is securely known to have been composed by him for public circulation. His genuine corpus is administrative and epistolary.

### Genuine documentary works

1. **Business correspondence**, c. 1510s–1560. Thousands of letters or references to letters survive or are calendared across the Fugger Archive: instructions to factors, sovereign-finance negotiations, market intelligence, family orders, and political reports. No complete modern edition of Anton’s outgoing and incoming correspondence exists.
2. **Partnership agreements and managerial instructions**, especially renewals surrounding 1525, 1532, 1548, and the planned liquidations. These were collective legal documents, often drafted by clerks or counsel and authorized by Anton.
3. **The company inventory of 1527**, ordered/authorized under Anton and later edited by Jakob Strieder as *Die Inventur der Firma Fugger aus dem Jahre 1527* (1905). Anton was responsible for the accounting event, not sole scribe or author of every entry.
4. **Foundation charter of 31 July 1548**, renewing the 1521 Fugger foundations and establishing/reorganizing the Holz- und Blatternhaus and Waltenhausen hospital. A juridical act issued under Anton’s authority, drafted in chancery form.
5. **Testaments**: at least three wills or major testamentary stages, including a second testament of 1550 and third/final dispositions dated 26 July 1560; edited within Georg Simnacher and Maria Gräfin Preysing, *Die Fuggertestamente des 16. Jahrhunderts*.
6. **Archive and preservation regulations**, 1554 and 1560, directing the retention and ordering of trade documents. These created the institutional ancestor of the Fugger Archive.
7. **“Titelbuch” of Anton Fugger**, a book of forms/titles for correctly addressing princes, prelates, nobles, cities, and officials in correspondence. Götz von Pölnitz published a study, *Das Titelbuch des Anton Fugger* (1955). The manuscript belonged to Anton’s chancery; its exact scribal authorship should not be equated automatically with Anton’s hand.
8. **Autograph signatures, receipts, powers, and contractual instruments**, dispersed through the Fugger Archive and other European repositories.

### Letters to and from Erasmus

Erasmus’s published correspondence contains letters to Anton and references to Anton’s lost or unextant replies, gifts, invitation, and financial assistance. These are not a Fugger-authored book, but they are the most accessible published segment of his intellectual correspondence.

### Works sometimes confused with authorship

- **Georg von Logau (ed.), *Poëtae tres egregii nunc primum in lucem editi* (Augsburg: Heinrich Steiner, 1534):** an edition of classical hunting, fishing, bucolic, and related poems associated with Fugger patronage; not written by Anton.
- **The *Ehrenbuch der Fugger* (1545–47):** commissioned by Hans Jakob Fugger; Anton is a represented family authority, not author.
- **Fugger newsletters / *Fugger-Zeitungen*:** the famous surviving collections largely date from 1568 onward and belong to later generations, not Anton’s authorship.
- **Hans Dernschwam’s Ottoman diary (journey 1553–55):** the journey was connected to Fugger interests, but Dernschwam is the author.

### Archives and primary collections

#### Fürstlich und Gräflich Fuggersches Familien- und Stiftungsarchiv, Dillingen an der Donau

The essential repository. It holds:

- the Common Trade archive;
- Tyrolean, Hungarian, and Spanish Trade sections;
- account books, inventories, contracts, correspondence, and factor reports;
- family, manorial, and foundation records;
- Anton’s testaments and archive regulations;
- Hans Dernschwam’s original travel diary.

The trade archive is famous but smaller than the estate and foundation holdings. Anton’s own 1554 and 1560 preservation rules were decisive in its formation. Parts have been digitized for reading-room use, not comprehensively published online.

#### Other repositories

- Augsburg City Archive and State and City Library;
- Bavarian State Library, including digitized Fugger genealogical manuscripts;
- Austrian State Archives and Tyrolean regional collections for Habsburg and mining contracts;
- Spanish royal/financial archives, especially Simancas, for asientos and Maestrazgo/Almadén business;
- Belgian/Antwerp archives for bills, certifications, and exchange;
- The National Archives and British Library collections for Tudor loans and Thomas Gresham;
- printed volumes of Erasmus’s correspondence.

## Death, Estate Settlement, and Immediate Aftermath

Anton’s long illness worsened from at least 1557, but no secure diagnosis appears in the consulted sources. Reports of “long suffering” do not establish cancer, mercury poisoning, stroke, or a psychiatric condition. No documented addiction is known.

He died at Augsburg on 14 September 1560. His interment at Babenhausen inaugurated the use of separate Catholic burial places for the new Fugger lines. His wife had already been buried there.

The estate and territorial division empowered three surviving line-founding sons—Markus, Hans, and Jakob—and the descendants of Raymund. Hieronymus, though surviving Anton, left no line. The family firm continued for roughly another century despite Anton’s repeated wish to liquidate or reduce it. Its center of gravity moved toward estates, noble service, collections, architecture, and court culture.

The company’s delayed Spanish claims did not disappear. Philip II’s 1557 suspension/restructuring and later fiscal measures converted and postponed obligations rather than producing a clean bankruptcy liquidation. Anton died before the long-term consequences could be fully resolved.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Dynastic legacy

Anton is the common ancestor of the surviving **Fugger-Babenhausen** and **Fugger von Glött** houses. His marriage policy, entails, and territorial purchases converted commercial capital into a noble dynasty. Babenhausen castle remains associated with his line. Later branches rose to princely rank.

### Institutional legacy

His 1548 legal reorganization helped the original Fugger foundations survive confessional and political change. The Fuggerei continues, while the medical foundations were historically important early forms of endowed care. His archival rules preserved one of Europe’s richest early modern business archives.

### Economic-historical reputation

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century writers treated Anton as a hero or villain of nascent capitalism: either the prudent “king of merchants” or the monopolist whose loans subordinated emperors and workers. Later scholarship emphasizes the inseparability of family, household, firm, court finance, confessional politics, and lordship. The language of a “multinational corporation” illuminates reach but obscures personal liability, family partnership, and sovereign privilege.

### Comparison with Jakob

Jakob created the iconic system and remains more famous because of the 1519 imperial election and Fuggerei. Anton inherited a mature but vulnerable firm, increased its capital to a higher peak, widened its geographical range, and preserved it through wars and state-credit crises. His accomplishment was managerial endurance and restructuring rather than a single foundational breakthrough.

### The mythic Anton

The debt-burning story made Anton the model of the loyal, magnificently generous merchant. The actual archive shows nearly the opposite financial lesson: sovereign debt could trap even Europe’s strongest private creditor, and Anton spent his last decade trying to recover, diversify, and withdraw without provoking rulers who controlled his securities.

### Modern commemoration

- Augsburg named the **Anton-Fugger-Brücke**, completed in 1997, for him.
- His portrait remains on display at the Louvre and in Karlsruhe.
- Exhibitions in 1993 marked his 500th birthday, especially his musical and cultural patronage.
- Family and regional museums present him as Jakob’s great successor and founder of the Antonius line.
