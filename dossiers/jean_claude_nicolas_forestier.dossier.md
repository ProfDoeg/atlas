# Jean-Claude Nicolas Forestier: Research Dossier

**Prepared:** 20 August 2026  
**Subject:** Jean-Claude Nicolas Forestier (1861–1930), French forest engineer, *jardiniste*, landscape designer, municipal official, and urban planner  
**Scope note:** This dossier reconstructs the documentary record presently accessible in French, Spanish, Portuguese, English, Argentine, Cuban, and Moroccan sources. It distinguishes an authored design from a collaborative plan, a proposal from an executed work, and contemporary evidence from later attribution. Where the published record is silent—especially on Forestier’s parents, marriage, descendants, religion, private wealth, and the exact medical cause of death—the absence is stated rather than filled by inference.

### Identification problems

Forestier is occasionally confused in catalogues and web results with other men named Jean Forestier and with the Anglo-French illustrator Charles Amédée Forestier. His three forenames, dates, municipal office, and Léonore identifier—**19800035/292/39246**—are the safest identifiers. “Nicolas Forestier” alone is especially prone to ambiguity.

## Family and Ancestry

### Documented record

The major institutional biographies, the published proceedings devoted to Forestier, the official Léonore index, and the sources consulted for this dossier do **not** identify his parents, siblings, spouse, partner, or children. No reliable genealogy can therefore be supplied from the present evidence.

Jean-Claude Guérin says only that Forestier was “born and raised in Savoie.” A 2013 article notes that the Forestier and Randet families knew one another in Morocco, based on a dedicated Forestier project preserved by the Randet family. That proves a social connection between families, not kinship and not the composition of Forestier’s own household.

### What remains to be checked

The following records could answer the family questions but were not available in searchable full text during this research:

- the complete Aix-les-Bains civil birth register for 9 January 1861, including marginal annotations;
- Forestier’s École polytechnique matriculation dossier and Water and Forests personnel file;
- the full digitized Légion d’honneur dossier rather than its authority notice;
- Paris marriage and death registers and the notarial succession record;
- private correspondence and address books in the Forestier and Théodore Leveau fonds.

Accordingly, claims on unsourced family-history sites should not be treated as evidence. The occupational meaning of the French surname *Forestier* does not establish that his ancestors were foresters.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1861–1880

Forestier was born at Aix-les-Bains in the recently annexed department of Savoie. His childhood therefore unfolded in a mountain and spa region incorporated into France only in 1860. Guérin’s description—“born and raised in Savoie”—is the only substantive published statement located about his upbringing. It is reasonable to identify Alpine terrain and forestry as environmental context, but no childhood diary, school register, parental profession, property record, or autobiographical account was found to demonstrate a specific formative episode.

The accessible record begins securely with his admission to the École polytechnique in 1880, when he was nineteen. No evidence was found of exile, imprisonment, military combat, childhood illness, family scandal, or inherited fortune.

## Education and Formation, 1880–1887

### École polytechnique, Paris, 1880–1882

Forestier studied at the École polytechnique from **1880 to 1882**. This supplied the mathematical, engineering, surveying, and state-service formation typical of the French technical elite. His later park-system plans repeatedly combine topography, circulation, land reservation, water, planting, and administrative law; the polytechnic education is the institutional background for that synthetic method.

### Ministry of Finance and École libre des sciences politiques

After leaving the École polytechnique in 1882, he was initially attached to the Ministry of Finance. Guérin reports that he used the period to take courses at the **École libre des sciences politiques**. Sources do not establish a diploma there, so “studied” or “attended courses” is more exact than “graduated.” The short Finance posting also complicates the simplified story that he moved directly from engineering school into landscape work.

### École forestière de Nancy, to 1885

Forestier changed direction and asked to continue at the **École forestière de Nancy**, leaving in 1885 as a trainee *garde général*. The Nancy training provided forestry science, botany, soils, hydraulic and territorial management, and administrative practice. His portrait is preserved in the album of the school’s 59th class at what is now the AgroParisTech archival center.

### Early field postings, 1885–1887

Guérin gives the most precise sequence:

- **1885:** trainee general forest guard at **Argelès**;
- then **Annecy**, where he was confirmed in office;
- **early 1887:** promoted to **Sallanches**;
- later in **1887:** called to Paris.

The consulted source does not disambiguate which Argelès posting was meant. Because the record is an administrative-career summary rather than the personnel decree, the locality should remain “Argelès” pending examination of the original file.

### Teachers, mentors, and influences

No class-by-class teacher list was recovered. The most important direct professional senior was **Jean-Charles Adolphe Alphand**, head of the Paris public-works and promenades tradition, who recruited Forestier. Calling Forestier a “disciple of Haussmann” is defensible only as an institutional lineage: Georges-Eugène Haussmann shaped the Paris service in which Alphand worked, but no evidence establishes Haussmann as Forestier’s teacher.

Later intellectual influences were explicit and plural:

- **Frederick Law Olmsted** and the American *park system*;
- English and American public-park practice and the garden-city movement;
- **Daniel Burnham** and City Beautiful planning;
- **Eugène Hénard** and French urban reform;
- the classical spatial discipline associated with **André Le Nôtre**;
- Mediterranean, Hispano-Islamic, Maghrebi, Italian, and Greco-Roman garden traditions studied through travel and historical inquiry;
- site observation, plant science, and practical gardening rather than a single stylistic school.

## Early Career in Paris, 1887–1905

### Entry into the municipal service

In **1887**, aged twenty-six, Forestier entered the City of Paris Service des Promenades et Plantations. He did not leave municipal service until retirement in 1927. He began as assistant conservator at the **Bois de Vincennes**. The incumbent Lepaute remained until early 1889 and retained control of the arboriculture school and forestry museum until his death in 1893; Forestier only then assumed the full associated responsibilities.

One recurrent claim credits Forestier with Paris’s first municipal cycle track in the Bois de Vincennes in **1890**. It appears in municipal-history and landscape literature, but the original works order was not located in this search; it is therefore best treated as a well-established secondary attribution rather than a newly verified “first.”

### The Formigé conflict and administrative ceiling

The flattering version of Forestier’s Paris career omits a long institutional frustration. Alphand’s architect-deputy **Jean-Camille Formigé** was older, prominent, already an architect of historic monuments, and maintained a private architectural office while working on departmental projects. After Alphand’s retirement in 1889, the once-autonomous promenades service was subordinated inside the architecture administration. Budgets were small while land prices rose.

Forestier’s promotion on **1 January 1898** to conservator of the western sector became his last substantial field promotion for more than twenty-nine years. Guérin reports that Forestier radically revised Formigé’s design for the Avenue de Breteuil in 1898, but the revision was rejected as more expensive. Proposals for Parc Monceau and Square Lefebvre met the same fate. This was not a public lawsuit or formal disciplinary case; it was an internal competition over authority, cost, and professional jurisdiction. Guérin describes Forestier as socially adept, assertive, and unsuccessful in trying to make life difficult for Formigé, a formulation that should be understood as a historian’s characterization, not a proven misconduct finding.

### Avenue de Breteuil, Champ-de-Mars, fortifications

Institutional biographies nevertheless associate Forestier with the Avenue de Breteuil works from **1898**, the preservation and reworking of the **Champ-de-Mars** from **1904**, and continuing discussions over reuse of the obsolete Paris fortifications from **1905**. The exact authorship of individual drawings can be divided among Forestier, Formigé, and the municipal service. Forestier’s importance lies in his persistence in treating planted promenades and open land as an urban system rather than decorative remnants.

### Bagatelle, 1904–1910

Forestier led the campaign for municipal acquisition of the privately held **Domaine de Bagatelle**, an enclave in the Bois de Boulogne. The purchase was completed in early **1905**. The City of Paris says he restored it as a site for botanical collections while preserving the inherited composition. His plans were again reduced administratively, but the new rose garden became a major success. The international new-rose competition began in **1907**. The rose garden’s model partly drew on Jules Gravereaux and Édouard André’s garden at L’Haÿ.

Bagatelle was more than a design: Forestier used it for botanical practice, public horticulture, exhibitions, professional sociability, and tea gatherings with potential patrons. It helped convert a frustrated municipal official into an internationally visible garden authority.

## Chronological Life History

### 1905–1910: a theory of metropolitan open space

Forestier dated the text of *Grandes villes et systèmes de parcs* to December **1905**; Hachette catalogues and digitized copies support a **1906** Paris publication, although later bibliographies often give **1908**. The small book set out a hierarchy of reserves and landscapes, suburban parks, large urban parks, neighborhood parks and gardens, recreation grounds, children’s gardens, and planted avenue-promenades. These were to be connected and reserved before rising land prices made acquisition impossible.

The book neither simply copied Olmsted nor rejected the Paris tradition. It retained hierarchical composition and formal axes while giving parks greater autonomous value in metropolitan planning. Forestier emphasized survey, historical study, current use, future population, topography, and continuity. The system was a land-policy instrument, a public-health program, and a compositional method.

He broadened his publication record in these years: *Notes sur la composition d’un jardin* (1907), the Spanish-language “Los jardines modernos” (Buenos Aires, 1907), *Les Gazons* (1908), an illustrated report on Claude Monet’s garden at Giverny (1908), and articles in 1909 on the Paris fortifications and embellishment. *Bagatelle et ses jardins* followed in 1910.

### 1908–1919: construction of an urbanist profession

Forestier’s organizational work was as important as any one garden:

- **1908:** founding participant in the Urban and Rural Hygiene section of the Musée social;
- **1911:** cofounder of the Société française des architectes urbanistes with figures including Donat-Alfred Agache, Léon Jaussely, Eugène Hénard, Henri Prost, Édouard Redont, and others; the body became the Société française des urbanistes in 1919;
- **1912:** active in the Société des amateurs de jardins, serving as vice-president of its exhibitions committee;
- **1916:** founding member of the École d’art public;
- **1919:** founding member of the École des hautes études urbaines.

The Musée social and urbanist network advocated municipal plans and planning law. Forestier’s circle helped prepare the climate for the **Cornudet law of 14 March 1919**, requiring plans of improvement, beautification, and extension for many French towns. Forestier was neither the legislator nor the law’s sole author; the statute is associated with parliamentary rapporteur Honoré Cornudet. The documentary claim is collective advocacy and technical influence.

### 1910–1914: Andalusia and the first Mediterranean synthesis

Forestier’s Spanish commissions gave him scope unavailable in Paris.

- **Parque de María Luisa, Seville:** engaged around 1911 for the projected Hispano-American exposition; works continued through the long-delayed event eventually held in 1929. The former San Telmo palace gardens had been donated to Seville in 1893 by Infanta María Luisa. Forestier reorganized the park through shaded axes, fountains, pools, tiled details, intimate enclosures, and botanical display. Local architects and gardeners carried out and extended the work; it is not accurate to treat every later element as his sole design.
- **Casa del Rey Moro, Ronda (1912):** commissioned by the Duchess of Parcent. On a steep site, he laid out terraces, stairs, pergolas, water, ceramics, myrtle, oleander, laurel, palm, and cedar. The design is a modern evocation of Andalusian and Hispano-Islamic precedents, not a preserved medieval garden.
- Other Spanish private work associated with this phase includes Moratalla in Córdoba; documentary dating and the division of authorship vary among inventories.

These encounters informed the “gardens of the climate of the orange tree” later articulated in *Jardins* (1920): a constructed Mediterranean family linking Greco-Roman, Andalusian, Maghrebi, Italian, and modern French design.

### 1913–1914: French Protectorate Morocco

Resident-General Hubert Lyautey sent Forestier to study open-space reserves in the principal Moroccan cities. Forestier delivered in **December 1913** a roughly seventy-page administrative report, *Rapport des réserves à constituer au dedans et aux abords des villes capitales du Maroc*. It addressed Rabat, Fez, Meknes, and Marrakesh; studied “Arab gardens”; proposed urban and landscape reservations; and included draft decrees.

Forestier urged Lyautey to bring **Henri Prost** into the work. Prost returned in 1914 and spent about a decade planning the protectorate’s new European quarters. Rabat’s park system and the protection or incorporation of Marrakesh’s Menara, Agdal, palm groves, and olive landscapes are linked to this collaboration. The Rabat Jardin d’Essais was created in **1914** on about seventeen hectares.

This work was done for the French colonial state. Its conservation measures cannot be separated from the protectorate’s segregated administration, land controls, and construction of new European quarters outside the medinas. Forestier sometimes argued for preserving indigenous urban fabrics and existing productive landscapes; that documented position does not make the mission politically independent of colonial rule.

### 1915–1923: Barcelona, private gardens, and publication

Forestier arrived in **Barcelona in 1915** to landscape Montjuïc for a projected exhibition of electrical industries. The program expanded into a set of gardens and urban proposals:

- the Laribal gardens, completed by 1922;
- Miramar and other Montjuïc terraces and promenades;
- the garden associated with the Greek Theatre and the Umbracle area;
- proposals for the Guinardó and other park spaces;
- a much larger Pedralbes park-and-expansion scheme.

The essential collaborator and disciple was **Nicolau Maria Rubió i Tudurí**, son of Mariano Rubió i Bellver. The elder Rubió asked Forestier to train his son. In 1917 Nicolau prepared a municipal park-system memorandum directly indebted to Forestier and later led Barcelona’s park service. Many works conventionally labeled “Forestier” were developed, supervised, completed, or adapted by Rubió.

The proposed Pedralbes operation—at one stage imagined on more than 1,000 hectares, with about 400 relatively level hectares—was not executed as planned. Eusebi Güell’s refusal to sell land and Francesc Cambó’s departure from local politics in 1918 were major obstacles. Rubió’s later, smaller royal-garden work retained Forestier’s influence. In **1923**, the Primo de Rivera-era authorities dispensed with Forestier’s services. The Barcelona exhibition did not open until 1929.

Other work in this period included the **Palacio de Liria** gardens in Madrid (1916), the **Joseph Guy garden** at Béziers (1918), and various private estates. Attribution sometimes rests on project archives rather than a signed built-work record.

Forestier published *Jardins: carnet de plans et de dessins* in **1920**. Its garden types, plans, sketches, plant use, terraces, steps, water, walls, and furniture offered a practical-poetic program for the modern private garden. Helen Morgenthau Fox translated it into English in 1924.

### 1920–1925: Greater Paris, Cité universitaire, Sceaux, and Art Deco

Within Louis Bonnier’s Direction de l’Extension, Forestier contributed in **1920** to a plan for Parisian open spaces at a regional scale, including park connections and the former fortification zone. Financing and divided authority prevented the comprehensive realization he sought.

From **1921**, Lucien Bechmann and Forestier were charged with the park of the Cité internationale universitaire de Paris, conceived as a garden-city joining residences, walks, and sports grounds. Other institutional histories associate Léon Azéma with the overall landscape work. Forestier also helped frame the preservation and restoration of the **Domaine de Sceaux**, acquired by the department in the 1920s, with Azéma responsible for much of the executed rebuilding and landscape restoration.

In the forestry hierarchy Forestier became a conservator in **1921**. Guérin reports retirement two years later from that corps as first-class conservator and municipal chief engineer; the Cité authority gives the end of his City of Paris service as **1927**. These are compatible if the forestry and municipal statuses are distinguished.

For the **1925 Exposition internationale des arts décoratifs et industriels modernes** in Paris, he directed or inspected the garden component and published on the exhibition gardens. The work placed him between horticulture, Art Deco, architecture, and display design.

### 1923–1925: Buenos Aires

#### Invitation and working process

Argentine president-to-be and ambassador in France **Marcelo T. de Alvear** urged Buenos Aires mayor **Carlos Noel** to invite Forestier. The invitation was issued in October 1923. Forestier arrived on **12 November 1923** and stayed approximately one month. He did not relocate to Argentina.

Noel’s Comisión de Estética Edilicia (CEE), created in 1923, was producing what became the **Proyecto Orgánico para la Urbanización del Municipio: El plano regulador y de reforma de la Capital Federal** (Peuser, 1925), often called the Plan Noel. Forestier worked as its foreign park-and-landscape adviser. **Carlos León Thays**, director of Parks and Gardens and son of Charles/Carlos Thays, was his principal local collaborator. Forestier described him as active and valuable. The relationship joined two French-Argentine professional lineages; it did not erase the work already done by Carlos Thays, Benito Carrasco, or the municipal parks staff.

Forestier’s memoranda were delivered in **May 1924** and incorporated into the 1925 publication. The exact internal titles vary in later references; the safely documented published object is the CEE volume and its Forestier sections and plates.

#### What Forestier proposed

His work had two main scales:

1. a citywide and metropolitan hierarchy of reserves, parks, gardens, recreation spaces, and planted avenue-promenades; and
2. a coastal system along the Río de la Plata, including Costanera proposals.

The Observatorio Metropolitano describes the open-space proposal as extending from Constitución station to Avenida del Libertador and as Buenos Aires’s first plan to treat the metropolitan area through a hierarchical green-space network. The approximately ten-kilometre Costanera concept used 200–300-metre-wide reclaimed land, in part from channel dredging, to turn the city toward the river. Individual proposals included or affected Costanera Sur and Norte, Parque Saavedra, Parque Avellaneda, Palermo/Parque 3 de Febrero, and other reserves and avenues. Cost, jurisdiction, reclamation, and political instability limited execution.

The CEE acknowledged that Forestier adopted ideas supplied by the Parks Directorate and the commission. It also considered some recommendations too costly. The appropriate authorship formula is therefore “Forestier’s advisory park-system and coastal plan within the collaborative Plan Noel,” not “Forestier designed Buenos Aires.”

#### Parque 3 de Febrero

Forestier did not create Parque 3 de Febrero. The park predated his visit and had already been reshaped by Carlos Thays and **Benito Carrasco**. Forestier’s broader plan addressed the Palermo park system, and a specific, executed link is the **Patio or Glorieta Andaluza** at the Spanish garden entrance. A municipal decree of **22 February 1929** called for the work according to Forestier’s project; plans moved through Public Works that year, and it opened on **13 October 1929**. A local chronology gives a cost of **35,457 pesos moneda nacional**, a figure that should be checked against the original municipal account before financial comparison.

#### Plaza San Martín and Retiro: an attribution correction

The user-supplied identification associates Forestier with a reworking of Plaza San Martín. The available evidence does **not** support crediting him with the executed plaza design. The official Buenos Aires tourism history says the present design owes much to **Carlos Thays**. The later expansion and treatment around Arenales and Retiro are associated with **Carlos León Thays** and municipal works of the 1930s. In the Observatorio’s breakdown of the 1925 plan, the **Explanada de Retiro** is attributed to the CEE, whereas Costanera and open-space projects are separately attributed to Forestier.

The most likely source of the claim is attribution drift: Forestier advised on the citywide open-space structure and the Retiro–Constitución corridor, while the Thays family designed or executed the plaza itself. Unless a signed Forestier drawing specifically for Plaza San Martín is produced, the documented position is **metropolitan/Retiro influence, not established authorship of the plaza reworking**.

#### Conflict with Benito Carrasco

Carrasco, a former Parks and Gardens director and a major designer of the Rosedal, opposed the decision to hire an eminent foreign adviser. Later municipal-history scholarship presents Forestier’s selection as a serious professional frustration for Carrasco, who had his own 409-hectare park proposal and green-belt ideas. This was a rivalry over credit, local expertise, money, and the direction of Buenos Aires planning—not evidence that either man plagiarized the other. The surviving plan is intrinsically collaborative.

### 1925–1929: Havana

#### Two separate Cuban engagements

Forestier’s Cuban history began before Machado. In **1918**, President Mario García Menocal invited him to design the grounds around the Castillo de San Salvador de la Punta. The much larger episode began in **1925**, when Public Works secretary **Carlos Miguel de Céspedes** brought him into Havana planning under President **Gerardo Machado**.

Forestier made **three visits** to Cuba. On the first, in 1925–26, he surveyed the city and reportedly inspected it from the air. Plans were then developed by a Franco-Cuban team, worked on in Paris, and sent through diplomatic channels. **Théodore Leveau**, Forestier’s pupil and collaborator, and Cuban architect-planner **Pedro Martínez Inclán** were among the important professionals. The evidence contradicts the recurring web claim that Forestier simply “moved to Havana for five years.”

#### Plan and works

The plan sought to preserve and reframe Old Havana, create a new civic center at the Loma de los Catalanes, connect monuments by avenues and parks, open the waterfront, and reserve a large landscape system around the Almendares River and the outskirts. Major elements included:

- gardens around the Capitolio;
- redesign of the Paseo del Prado (1928), including its paving, benches, and promenade character;
- Parque de la Fraternidad, associated with the 1928 Pan-American Conference and its symbolic ceiba;
- Avenida del Puerto and Avenida de las Misiones;
- a proposed civic center and radiating avenues;
- a proposed Gran Parque Nacional / Almendares landscape reserve;
- a university belvedere and campus connection.

Some components were executed; the metropolitan structure, civic center, and great park were largely not. Funding and scope shifted under Céspedes. The **1929 financial crash**, Forestier’s death, and Machado’s later fall all contributed to abandonment or alteration.

#### Dictatorship, race, and state representation

Forestier worked for Machado’s Public Works apparatus while Machado’s regime was becoming dictatorial. Joseph R. Hartman shows that the plans participated in a state campaign of monumental modernization and nation-making and were entangled with Cuba’s colonial histories of race, class, labor, and spatial power. This is documented political context. No source consulted proves Forestier’s personal adherence to Machado’s politics, receipt of illicit payments, or participation in repression. Conversely, treating the work as politically neutral would conceal the client and purpose of the commission.

### 1926–1930: retirement years, Lisbon, Mexico, and final projects

#### Lisbon, 1927–1928

The Lisbon Câmara Municipal invited Forestier in **February 1927** to advise on a general plan of improvements, the extension of Avenida da Liberdade, and Parque Eduardo VII. His stay lasted only about six days. The mature proposal—*Ville de Lisbonne. Étude de la continuation de l’Avenue de la Liberté et de l’aménagement d’un Grand Parc avec cités-jardins et terrains de jeux*—joined a 150–200-metre planted avenue, terraces over the city and Tagus, garden-city housing, playing fields, routes to outlying centers and transport infrastructure, and a riverside avenue.

Forestier proposed financing expensive public works partly by selling enhanced building land along the new avenue, citing the Champ-de-Mars and Avenue de Tervueren. This is the clearest documented example of his land-value financing logic. It was a proposal, not a company or personal investment.

Lisbon newspapers debated the plan in 1928. Supporters presented Forestier’s name and built work as a guarantee; critics, including the young Cassiano Branco, objected that six days’ inspection and distant design were insufficient and that the project’s cost was unrealistic. The plan was not built. Cristino da Silva reused aspects in 1932–33, while later planners Étienne de Gröer and Keil do Amaral developed Monsanto and Eduardo VII along different lines.

#### Mexico, 1928

Forestier’s long friendship with Mexican forest engineer and parks advocate **Miguel Ángel de Quevedo** dated to Quevedo’s Paris contacts around 1907. Forestier visited Mexico from approximately **23 September to 9 October 1928**. He received professional honors, lectured, consulted, and traveled; an anticipated plan was not completed. A strong earthquake occurred on his final night. His system-of-parks ideas circulated through Quevedo, the Sociedad Forestal Mexicana, and *México Forestal*. The claim is one of professional influence and exchange, not sole authorship of Mexico City’s park program.

#### Ligue urbaine and final commissions

In **1928**, Forestier and writer-diplomat **Jean Giraudoux** were among the founders of the Ligue urbaine. Forestier served as its first president, with Giraudoux and Henri Prost among the vice-presidential circle. The organization did not survive him in its original form; Giraudoux and Raoul Dautry revived it in 1943 as the Ligue urbaine et rurale.

Late private and architectural collaborations included:

- **La Bastide du Roy**, near Antibes/Biot, commissioned by Count Jean de Polignac and designed with architect Louis Süe from 1926–27; Forestier organized arrival, cypress theatre, meadow, Spanish garden, santolina garden, and colored parterre within existing olive, orange, pine, and irrigation landscapes;
- **Villa Barbarénia**, Biarritz (1927), with architect Walter-André Destailleur;
- **Villa Berriots**, Ustaritz, for couturier Jean Patou (project dated 1929–32), with Louis Süe and other designers; Forestier’s contribution began before his death;
- a Santo Domingo commission reportedly under study when he died. The accessible sources do not establish a finished signed plan, and later Dominican planning should not be attributed to him without archival proof.

Forestier’s last known letter to Carlos León Thays is dated **5 July 1930** and is reported in the École du Breuil archives. It shows that the Buenos Aires professional relationship continued after the 1923 visit.

## Death / Later Life / Present Status

Forestier died in Paris on **26 October 1930**. Several biographical accounts, including Cuban cultural summaries, state that death followed an operation. The exact illness, procedure, hospital, length of final hospitalization, and certified cause were not found. “Postoperative death” is therefore a secondary report, not a complete medical finding.

No evidence was found of a prolonged terminal illness, addiction, psychiatric crisis, disabling accident, suicide, or violence. No cemetery or probate source was established in the consulted material.

An early substantial obituary was Helen Morgenthau Fox’s “Jean C. N. Forestier” in *Landscape Architecture* (January 1931). Giraudoux later transformed the administrative frustration of his career into a memorable posthumous story: France had named a creator “conservator” while foreign cities gave him room to found landscapes. That tribute is evidence of friendship and reputation, not a neutral measure of how little he actually accomplished in France.

- Ligue urbaine: cofounder and first president, 1928.

### Techniques and technologies

Forestier was not an inventor in the patent-law sense. No patent was found. His technical contribution was combinatory:

- topographic survey and site reconnaissance;
- metropolitan classification and advance reservation of open land;
- planted avenues as connective infrastructure;
- use of dredged fill in the Buenos Aires coastal proposal;
- integration of road, park, recreation, botanical, and water systems;
- aerial reconnaissance in Havana, according to contemporary-derived accounts;
- plant selection by climate, soil, color, season, and maintenance;
- land-value recapture by resale of serviced building plots, explicitly proposed in Lisbon;
- administrative decrees and legal instruments to protect reserves, especially in Morocco.

### Money, property, and business structure

Forestier’s documented economic base comprised a French civil-service salary plus fees for private and foreign commissions. Bagatelle, the Paris parks, and colonial or municipal plans were not his personal property. No reliable salary scale, consulting fee, bank record, shareholding, land speculation, inheritance, estate valuation, or net-worth estimate was found.

The Lisbon plan proposed municipal recovery of infrastructure cost through sale of high-value residential land. That was a project-finance recommendation, not proof that Forestier bought the land or held a beneficial interest. No conflict-of-interest investigation was found.

## Political / Religious / Intellectual / Artistic Development

### Politics

Forestier held no elected office and no party membership has been documented. His political activity was professional and associational: public-health reform, advance planning, municipal expertise, preservation of open land, and institution-building. He worked comfortably with Third Republic officials, aristocratic patrons, colonial administrators, Argentine municipal elites, and the Machado government. These client relationships show access and professional pragmatism; they do not by themselves establish a coherent party ideology.

### The Le Play question

A long-running characterization made Forestier a committed follower of social theorist **Frédéric Le Play**, partly because the École polytechnique, forestry corps, and Musée social all contained Le Play networks. Guérin calls this a rumor that must be qualified: Forestier is absent from Kalaora and Savoye’s specialized inventory of roughly twenty-two Le Play-associated foresters. Absence is not proof of non-adherence, but no direct declaration of Le Play allegiance was located. The defensible position is **institutional proximity without established doctrinal membership**.

### Religion

No reliable source consulted identifies Forestier’s religious belief, observance, conversion, anticlerical activity, or affiliation. Garden references to Islamic, Christian, classical, or royal traditions are artistic and historical evidence, not proof of personal faith.

### From *promenades* to park systems

His intellectual development can be traced through four overlapping stages:

1. **Paris municipal forestry:** trees, roads, nurseries, botanical collections, maintenance, and the Alphand hierarchy.
2. **Metropolitan theory:** *Grandes villes* reconceived isolated gardens as a connected, anticipatory land system.
3. **Mediterranean synthesis:** Spain and Morocco supplied spatial, climatic, horticultural, and decorative materials for a modern garden that rejected indiscriminate picturesque imitation.
4. **Regional urbanism:** Buenos Aires, Havana, and Lisbon joined parks to waterfronts, metropolitan routes, new civic centers, recreation, and land finance.

Forestier resisted reducing the garden to floral display. He favored clearly structured ground plans, hedges, walls, terraces, pools, steps, and masses of vegetation, while adapting species and color to climate and site. His work therefore combines classical order, engineering, horticultural empiricism, City Beautiful monumentality, and Olmstedian connectivity.

### “Latinity” and the Mediterranean

Forestier used a cultural category of Latin/Mediterranean gardens that joined southern France, Spain, Italy, North Africa, and parts of Latin America. Camille Lesouef shows that the “climate of the orange tree” was not a neutral botanical zone: it was a modern typology assembled from multiple historical traditions and tied to French debates about a national modern garden. In colonial and transatlantic commissions, this language could make diverse places legible within a French design vocabulary. Its historical significance lies both in the hybrid designs it enabled and in the asymmetry of who classified whom.

### Conservation and modernization

Later admirers call Forestier an environmentalist or precursor of green infrastructure. The supporting evidence is real: he advocated early land reservation, landscape protection, regional continuity, children’s play areas, existing productive groves, and long-term planning against speculation. The label remains retrospective. His own vocabulary centered on hygiene, beauty, order, parks, reserves, and urban art; his projects could also enable elite real estate, monumental state representation, colonial separation, and automobile boulevards.

## Successes, Failures, and Constraints

### Documented successes

- transformation of Bagatelle into a durable municipal botanical and rose-garden institution;
- formulation of the park system as a French urban-planning instrument;
- central role in institutions that professionalized French urbanism;
- influential Mediterranean garden language visible in Seville, Ronda, Barcelona, and private estates;
- operational effect in Morocco through Prost and protectorate planning;
- lasting built fragments and urban structure in Havana;
- transatlantic transmission through Thays, Rubió, Leveau, Martínez Inclán, and Quevedo;
- official decorations and international recognition by the late 1920s.

### Documented failures or partial failures

- administrative marginalization in Paris and repeated rejection/reduction of proposals;
- comprehensive Paris fortification and regional open-space plans not realized in his lifetime;
- Pedralbes plan blocked by land and political conditions;
- dismissal/termination of his Barcelona role in 1923;
- most of the Buenos Aires Plan Noel and Costanera system not executed as designed;
- Havana’s civic center and metropolitan park system unfinished;
- Lisbon plan rejected or superseded amid cost and competence criticism;
- no completed Mexico plan;
- Santo Domingo work apparently interrupted by death;
- later deterioration, alteration, or loss at important gardens.

These outcomes should not be collapsed into either triumphal “visionary” history or a list of failures: incomplete plans often influenced later municipal action without being built in authored form.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

### Findings of this research

No documented criminal charge, conviction, civil judgment, corruption investigation, fraud allegation, money-laundering allegation, patent dispute, sexual scandal, intelligence-service link, political-violence participation, or professional disciplinary dismissal was found for Forestier.

That negative finding is bounded by the surviving published record. It does not substitute for a search of all personnel, police, court, tax, and notarial files.

### Matters requiring political or evidentiary qualification

#### Colonial Morocco — documented institutional role

Forestier’s 1913 mission was an official instrument of the French Protectorate. His landscape-preservation proposals and separation of new European construction from medinas operated inside colonial sovereignty. The fact is **service to a colonial administration**. No evidence located accuses him personally of violence, expropriation for private gain, or criminal abuse; land consequences require project-by-project colonial archive research.

#### Machado’s Havana — documented regime patronage, no personal political finding

The Havana plan was commissioned and funded through Machado’s state. It contributed to the regime’s monumental self-representation. Hartman’s scholarship analyzes the racial and colonial politics of both built and unbuilt plans. No evidence located establishes Forestier as a party operative, secret police collaborator, or beneficiary of corrupt payments. The appropriate classification is **documented professional collaboration with an authoritarian government; personal ideological allegiance unresolved**.

#### Paris conflict — documented administrative rivalry, no legal case

Forestier and Formigé competed over designs, costs, and authority. Guérin’s colorful account of Forestier trying to trouble Formigé is a secondary characterization. No formal complaint, sanction, or lawsuit was found.

#### Foreign-expert controversy in Buenos Aires and Lisbon

Benito Carrasco’s opposition in Buenos Aires and Cassiano Branco’s criticism in Lisbon questioned the need, cost, local knowledge, and realism of importing a famous French expert. Those are contemporaneous/professional controversies, not evidence of fraud. Both cases expose a recurring power structure: a prestigious international adviser received visibility while local professionals supplied data and implementation.

#### Monumentality, elitism, and land values — scholarly interpretation

Vicente Casals situates Forestier’s mature plans among conservative Catalan nationalism, Iberian and Latin American elites, and monumental national representation. Lisbon’s proposal explicitly anticipated luxury housing and land resale. Such evidence supports analysis of class and state symbolism. It does not prove that every park was exclusively elite, nor that Forestier personally profited from speculation.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. “Forestier was Haussmann’s disciple”

1. **Story:** Haussmann personally trained Forestier, who carried Haussmannization abroad.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** Repeated in short Spanish and English biographies and tourism summaries.
3. **Evidence:** Forestier entered the Paris service created under Haussmann and was recruited by Alphand. No source found documents a teacher-student relationship with Haussmann.
4. **Assessment:** **Institutional lineage presented as personal mentorship.** Alphand is the direct professional link.
5. **Reputational effect:** Makes Forestier legible as an exporter of Parisian boulevards but obscures his criticism of the inherited service and his Olmstedian park-system turn.

### 2. “Forestier created the first cycle path”

1. **Story:** He created the first cycle track, usually specified as Paris’s first municipal one, in the Bois de Vincennes in 1890.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** French landscape histories, municipal exhibition material, and modern biographical blurbs.
3. **Evidence:** The attribution is consistent and plausible within his Vincennes office, but the original decree or engineering file was not located here; the unqualified global “first” is especially unsafe.
4. **Assessment:** **Well-established secondary attribution; scope of ‘first’ requires primary verification.**
5. **Reputational effect:** Supports the image of Forestier as an early multimodal and green-infrastructure innovator.

### 3. “Forestier was a committed Le Play disciple”

1. **Story:** His forestry, Musée social, and public-health connections made him a doctrinaire Leplaysian.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** Earlier professional historiography, addressed explicitly by Guérin.
3. **Evidence:** Institutional proximity is real. A specialist prosopography of Leplaysian foresters does not list him; no declaration of adherence was found.
4. **Assessment:** **Unresolved association, not established ideological identity.**
5. **Reputational effect:** Places his social urbanism in a conservative-reform genealogy, sometimes too neatly.

### 4. “Forestier designed Parque 3 de Febrero”

1. **Story:** The French visitor redesigned or created the entire Palermo park.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** Tourism copy, social media, simplified histories, and conflation of the 1925 plan with executed parks.
3. **Evidence:** Parque 3 de Febrero long predates his mission; Carlos Thays and Benito Carrasco had major roles. Forestier advised on the system and designed the documented 1929 Andalusian patio/glorieta.
4. **Assessment:** **Partial truth expanded into sole authorship.**
5. **Reputational effect:** Raises Forestier’s Buenos Aires visibility while diminishing local authors and chronological layers.

### 5. “Forestier reworked Plaza San Martín”

1. **Story:** Forestier was the landscape author of the plaza’s modern form.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** Biographical shorthand joining famous Buenos Aires parks to the 1925 foreign consultant.
3. **Evidence:** Official Buenos Aires history credits Carlos Thays substantially; the 1930s extension is associated with Carlos León Thays. The 1925 project index attributes the Retiro esplanade to the CEE and the open-space system to Forestier. No signed Forestier plaza plan was found.
4. **Assessment:** **Unsubstantiated specific attribution, probably produced by conflation with the Retiro corridor and Plan Noel.**
5. **Reputational effect:** Contributes to a “French master designed Buenos Aires” myth and collapses Forestier and the two Thays generations.

### 6. “Forestier moved to Havana for five years”

1. **Story:** In 1925 he relocated continuously to Cuba until his death.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** Repeated in encyclopedia-derived web biographies.
3. **Evidence:** Scholarship documents three visits, a Franco-Cuban team, work in Paris, and plans sent by diplomatic pouch.
4. **Assessment:** **False or seriously misleading residence claim.** He was repeatedly “called to Havana” between 1926 and 1929, not continuously resident.
5. **Reputational effect:** Turns a distributed collaborative practice into a lone-master residency.

### 7. “Forestier authored the Cornudet law”

1. **Story:** His organizations or Forestier himself created France’s 1919 planning law.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** Institutional biographies sometimes compress advocacy into origin.
3. **Evidence:** Forestier’s Musée social/SFU circle campaigned for planning legislation. Parliament enacted the law under Honoré Cornudet’s rapporteurship.
4. **Assessment:** **Collective intellectual influence, not sole legal authorship.**
5. **Reputational effect:** Magnifies his statutory power while hiding legislators and the larger reform coalition.

### 8. “Forestier was a precursor or teacher of Le Corbusier”

1. **Story:** Forestier’s green systems directly generated Le Corbusier’s modern city.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** Posthumous scholarly comparisons, especially the formulation “between Le Nôtre and Le Corbusier.”
3. **Evidence:** They knew and respected one another; Forestier reportedly defended Le Corbusier at the 1925 exhibition. Their urban forms and professional politics also diverged. Direct conceptual dependence remains debated.
4. **Assessment:** **Interpretive genealogy, not demonstrated teacher-pupil relation.**
5. **Reputational effect:** Repositions a garden designer inside the canon of modern architecture.

### 9. “Forestier was buried historically with Machado”

1. **Story:** Association with the Cuban dictator discredited or erased his Havana work; alternatively, restoration narratives detach the plan entirely from dictatorship.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** Modern scholarship on Havana’s unfinished plans and heritage recovery.
3. **Evidence:** The commission and regime context are certain. Personal ideological adhesion is not. Later neglect also followed the crash, death, changing regimes, incomplete construction, and disciplinary canon formation.
4. **Assessment:** **A reputational narrative built on a real patronage relationship but insufficient as a complete explanation of forgetting.**
5. **Reputational effect:** Alternately stigmatizes and sanitizes the Havana plan.

### 10. “Forgotten in France, revered everywhere else”

1. **Story:** France gave him no meaningful work and forgot him immediately, whereas Spain and Latin America remembered him continuously.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** Giraudoux’s 1939 tribute and late twentieth-century rediscovery narratives.
3. **Evidence:** His Paris authority was constrained, but Bagatelle, Champ-de-Mars, Cité universitaire, Sceaux, exhibitions, associations, honors, and publications were substantial. French attention did decline after mid-century; Spanish and Latin American attributions also fluctuated and sometimes became inaccurate.
4. **Assessment:** **Powerful hagiographic simplification rooted in genuine administrative frustration and later canon loss.**
5. **Reputational effect:** Helped motivate the 1990 Paris colloquium and modern recovery of his archive.

## Films, Documentaries, Interviews, and Archival Material

### Film, television, audio, and online video

No authoritative feature-length documentary devoted solely to Forestier was identified in the catalogues and searches consulted. Numerous short heritage videos and tourism segments about Seville, Barcelona, Buenos Aires, and Havana mention him, but they frequently reproduce the attribution myths analyzed above. A recent YouTube title, “The Parisian who designed Buenos Aires and never wanted…,” exemplifies the lone-genius framing; it was identified but not relied on as evidence.

Forestier died before sound-broadcast interviews became common, and no verified recording of his voice or moving image of him was found. The Lisbon and Mexican newspaper interviews are textual, not audiovisual.

### Archival collections and primary visual material

- **Jean-Claude-Nicolas Forestier fonds**, Centre d’archives d’architecture contemporaine / Cité de l’architecture et du patrimoine, Paris; archive authority entry and FranceArchives finding aid. Holdings cover drawings, plans, photographs, writings, and professional papers, approximately 1900–29.
- **Théodore Leveau fonds**, same center. The repository explicitly warns that the Leveau and Forestier inventories should be read together; Leveau preserved Forestier graphics and collaborative project material.
- **Base Léonore**, Archives nationales: official Légion d’honneur dossier, identifier 19800035/292/39246.
- **AgroParisTech / Nancy forestry-school archives:** portrait album of the 59th class and likely training/personnel evidence.
- **École du Breuil archives:** correspondence with Carlos León Thays, including the reported 5 July 1930 letter.
- **Buenos Aires municipal and legislative library:** the 1925 CEE *Proyecto Orgánico*, decrees, Parks and Gardens reports, and project plates.
- **Cuban national and public-works archives:** Havana plans and team records; Hartman reproduces and analyzes material from the Cuban Office of Public Works.
- **Getty Research Institute exhibition checklist, *The Metropolis in Latin America, 1830–1930*:** identifies the 1924 Forestier plan for Parque Saavedra and other Plan Noel visual material.
- **Gallica / Bibliothèque nationale de France:** digitized *Grandes villes et systèmes de parcs* and authority/catalogue records.
- **National Library of Australia:** digitized 1906 *Grandes villes* copy.
- **Internet Archive:** catalogue/borrow record for the 1990 colloquium proceedings and digitized periodical materials.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### 1930s–1950s

Fox’s 1931 obituary and Giraudoux’s 1939 tribute kept an international and literary memory alive. His pupils and collaborators continued to shape cities: Rubió in Catalan landscape practice, Prost in planning and teaching, Leveau in design archives, Thays in Buenos Aires, and Quevedo in Mexican forestry advocacy. This transmission sometimes detached concepts from Forestier’s name.

### Mid-century eclipse

After about 1950, French architectural and planning histories often privileged heroic modern architecture, zoning, housing, and Le Corbusier over garden and park-system practice. Forestier’s hybrid position—civil servant, forester, garden artist, urbanist, colonial consultant—fit no single disciplinary canon. Built works were also altered, neglected, or credited to collaborators and exposition architects.

Spain retained a stronger garden-history memory, especially at María Luisa and Montjuïc. In Latin America, the persistence of roads, gardens, and fragments could sustain local memory while also generating inflated attributions.

### Rediscovery from the late 1980s

Bénédicte Leclerc’s research, the **1990 international colloquium in Paris**, the 1994 proceedings, and the 1997 critical edition of *Grandes villes* restored Forestier to landscape and urbanism scholarship. Later work shifted from stylistic celebration to institutional, colonial, transnational, financial, and racial analysis.

### Current influence

Forestier is now frequently read as a precursor of:

- metropolitan green infrastructure and greenways;
- landscape urbanism;
- ecological and recreational networks;
- climate-responsive Mediterranean garden design;
- advance open-space reservation and regional planning;
- professional collaboration between landscape architecture and urbanism.

These are productive historical comparisons, not claims that he used present-day ecological science or social-equity standards. Contemporary French landscape architect **Jacqueline Osty**, for example, has identified Forestier’s hierarchical Parisian landscape tradition as an influence while also contrasting its rigidity with the greater formal flexibility of the English-American/Olmsted line.

### Commemoration and preservation

- A Paris garden bears the name **Jardin Jean-Claude-Nicolas-Forestier**.
- Bagatelle contains a commemorative medallion and remains the clearest Parisian site of his public legacy.
- Seville and Barcelona commemorate him in park history and statuary or place names.
- Restoration programs in Havana and heritage interpretation in Buenos Aires continue to debate what was actually his.

### Historiographical balance

Forestier’s durable legacy is not a list of sites he “designed” but a method carried through institutions and collaborators. Its record includes:

- real innovations in connected open space and long-range reservation;
- a sophisticated garden art attentive to plants, climate, geometry, light, and local precedents;
- dependence on elite, municipal, colonial, and authoritarian patrons;
- large gaps between plan and execution;
- recurring erasure of local coauthors;
- later myths that alternately make him a universal environmental prophet, a simple Haussmannian exporter, or an apolitical artist.

## Atlas Connections

### Carlos Thays

- **[T5]** **Reported fact.** Bénédicte Leclerc identifies Carlos León Thays as “the son of [Forestier’s] friend Charles Thays,” documenting a personal friendship between Forestier and the elder Franco-Argentine landscape designer. Both men were alive and professionally active when Forestier visited Buenos Aires in November–December 1923, but the accessible source does not specify when or where they first met. [Leclerc, “Jean Forestier en París y su influencia en América Latina,” pp. 177–185](https://www.academia.edu/22114365/VIII_Encuentro_de_Revitalizaci%C3%93n_de_CH_El_paisaje_urbano_en_ciudades_hist%C3%B3ricas)

### Le Corbusier

- **[T3]** **Documented fact.** Forestier discussed Le Corbusier’s *Pavillon de l’Esprit Nouveau* and the conflict over the surrounding fences and plantings in his signed 1925 article on the gardens of the Paris Exposition of Decorative Arts. Le Corbusier later recorded a substantive conversation in which Forestier explained the pressures that garden-suburb commuting placed on central Paris. This constitutes reciprocal written documentation of their professional exchange, although not agreement between their urban doctrines. [COAM, *Arquitectura*, no. 337, discussion of Forestier’s 1925 account](https://www.coam.org/media/Default%20Files/fundacion/biblioteca/revista-arquitectura-100/2000-2008/docs/revista-articulos/revista-arquitectura-2004-n337-pag50-59.pdf); [Vicente Casals Costa, “Barcelona, Lisboa y Forestier”](https://www.ub.edu/geocrit/sn/sn-296/sn-296-2.htm)

- **[T5]** **Documented fact.** The conversation recalled by Le Corbusier establishes a personal meeting between the two men, associated in the scholarly account with their exchanges over Paris, garden suburbs, and metropolitan circulation. Their direct professional proximity is additionally documented at the **1925 Paris Exposition**, where Forestier directed the garden program and held official landscape responsibility around the *Pavillon de l’Esprit Nouveau*, while Le Corbusier controlled the pavilion and made detailed planting decisions. [Vicente Casals Costa, “Barcelona, Lisboa y Forestier”](https://www.ub.edu/geocrit/sn/sn-296/sn-296-2.htm); [Journal of the Korean Institute of Landscape Architecture, study of Le Corbusier’s gardens](https://www.jkila.org/archive/view_article?pid=jkila-49-5-12)
