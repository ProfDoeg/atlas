## Works by Calvert Vaux

### Books and composite design works

- **1857:** *Villas and Cottages: A Series of Designs Prepared for Execution in the United States*. An illustrated architectural treatise and portfolio containing thirty-two designs, some speculative and others derived from buildings by Downing & Vaux or Vaux & Withers; it is simultaneously a book, a design catalogue, and a protocol for picturesque domestic architecture. Later editions are manifestations of this obra, not additional works. ([Google Books](https://books.google.com/books?id=relPnjDDjikC); [Kowsky, pp. 92–135](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

### Houses, estates, and domestic architecture

- **1850–51:** *Cedar Lawn (Joel T. Headley House)*. A house and estate composition at New Windsor, New York, produced in Downing’s office; Vaux’s precise share in the collaborative authorship cannot be completely separated from Andrew Jackson Downing’s. ([Kowsky, pp. 28–42](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1851:** *Algonac, alterations for Warren Delano*. A remodeling project at Balmville, New York, undertaken by Downing & Vaux; subsequent alteration makes the realized object an unstable record of their design. ([Kowsky, pp. 28–42](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1851–52:** *Dr. William A. M. Culbert House*. A picturesque residence in Newburgh, designed through Downing’s practice with Vaux’s participation; damaged by fire in 1981, it survives chiefly as a ruin. ([Kowsky, pp. 28–42](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1851–52:** *William L. Findlay House*. A Newburgh residence by Downing & Vaux, subsequently demolished. Its obra now consists principally in surviving documentation rather than a standing building. ([Kowsky, pp. 28–42](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1852–55:** *Daniel Parish House*. A Newport, Rhode Island, villa begun in the Downing partnership and continued after Downing’s death; its divided chronology makes sole attribution to Vaux misleading. ([Kowsky, pp. 28–54](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1850–53:** *Robert Dodge House*. A Washington, D.C., residence designed through Downing & Vaux; the surviving historical attribution is collaborative. ([Kowsky, pp. 28–42](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1853:** *William E. Warren House*. A picturesque Newburgh house from Vaux’s independent practice after Downing’s death. ([Kowsky, pp. 54–91](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1853:** *Idlewild (Nathaniel Parker Willis House)*. A house-and-landscape project at Cornwall-on-Hudson; the house survives greatly altered and its designed landscape has disappeared, so the original Gesamtkunstwerk no longer exists intact. ([Kowsky, pp. 54–91](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1853:** *Wodenethe, alterations for Henry Winthrop Sargent*. A remodeling and estate project at Fishkill Landing, now Beacon, New York; the house was demolished. ([Kowsky, pp. 54–91](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1853:** *Edward S. Hall House*. An unexecuted project for Middletown, Connecticut. It is properly the architectural intention and surviving design, not a nonexistent building. ([Kowsky, pp. 54–91](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **c. 1855:** *James Walker Fowler House*. An apparently unexecuted house project for Newburgh by Vaux & Withers; surviving evidence establishes a design, not a completed residence. ([Kowsky, pp. 92–135](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1855:** *The Point (Lydig and Blanche Livingston Hoyt House and grounds)*. A bluestone house, winding approach, siting, and estate landscape at Staatsburg, New York—an unusually clear Gesamtkunstwerk in which architecture and landscape were conceived together. ([TCLF](https://www.tclf.org/landscapes/hoyt-house))

- **1855:** *Halsey R. Stevens House*. A Newburgh residence designed by Vaux & Withers. ([Kowsky, pp. 92–135](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1856:** *Leonard H. Lee Cottage*. A small rural house at New Windsor, New York, by Vaux & Withers and subsequently published in *Villas and Cottages*. ([Kowsky, pp. 92–135](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1856:** *Thomas Earle House*. A Worcester, Massachusetts, residence by Vaux & Withers. ([Kowsky, pp. 92–135](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1856–57:** *John A. C. Gray House*. A New York City townhouse, with interior decorative work associated with Jacob Wrey Mould; it was later demolished. ([Kowsky, pp. 103–135](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1857:** *Eugene Dutilh House*. A country house at Garrison, New York, belonging to Vaux’s mature picturesque domestic work. ([Kowsky, pp. 141–48](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1857:** *The Squirrels, alterations for John Bigelow*. A remodeling at Highland Falls, New York; because an existing house was transformed, the obra is an intervention rather than an autonomous new building. ([Kowsky, pp. 141–48](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1859:** *Point of Rocks (Peter Chardon Brooks III House)*. A Medford, Massachusetts, country house, subsequently demolished. ([Kowsky, pp. 141–48](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1859–61:** *Ammadelle (Thomas E. B. Pegues House)*. An Italianate villa at Oxford, Mississippi, designed by Vaux and identified by him as one of his strongest domestic works. ([Kowsky, pp. 141–48](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959); [National Register documentation](https://npgallery.nps.gov/AssetDetail/NRIS/74001076))

- **1859–60:** *Federico Barreda House (Beaulieu)*. A Newport villa by Vaux; Eugene Baumann, rather than Vaux, is credited with laying out its grounds, so house and landscape should not be collapsed into a single-authored Vaux work. ([TCLF](https://www.tclf.org/pioneer/eugene-baumann); [Kowsky, pp. 141–48](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1861:** *Francis Tomes House*. A picturesque residence at Greenwich, Connecticut, later incorporated into Christ Church’s property. ([Kowsky, pp. 141–48](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1862:** *Ashcroft (Stephen B. Hammond House)*. A country residence at Geneva, New York. ([Kowsky, pp. 141–48](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1869:** *Dore Cottage*. A house at Riverside, Illinois, designed within the suburban environment planned by Olmsted and Vaux. ([Kowsky, pp. 198–251](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959); [Olmsted Online](https://olmstedonline.org/Job/Details/00607))

- **1870–72:** *Olana, consultation and design collaboration*. Vaux advised Frederic Edwin Church on the house and its relation to the Hudson landscape. Church’s extensive design authorship makes this a collaboration—and an evolving Gesamtkunstwerk—not simply a “Vaux house.” ([TCLF](https://www.tclf.org/sites/default/files/atoms/files/landslide_2009_booklet.pdf); [Kowsky, pp. 198–251](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1874–75:** *George J. Bull House*. A Worcester, Massachusetts, residence by Vaux’s practice. ([Kowsky, pp. 198–251](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1880:** *Raphael Pumpelly House*. A Newport residence, subsequently demolished. ([Kowsky, pp. 261–319](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1881–84:** *Samuel J. Tilden House, remodeling*. A transformation of adjoining Gramercy Park row houses into a richly ornamented residence; because substantial earlier fabric was retained, it is an architectural intervention rather than a wholly new building. ([Kowsky, pp. 261–319](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959); [NPS](https://www.nps.gov/places/samuel-j-tilden-house.htm))

- **1883:** *Boothden (Edwin Booth House)*. A residence at Middletown, Rhode Island, designed for the actor Edwin Booth. ([Kowsky, pp. 261–319](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

### Public, institutional, commercial, and religious architecture

- **1856:** *Bank of New York*. A commercial building in New York City by Vaux & Withers, subsequently demolished. ([Kowsky, pp. 92–135](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1860:** *Trinity Church Parish School*. A New York school building associated with Vaux and Jacob Wrey Mould; the documentary division of design labor between them is not exact. ([Metropolitan Museum of Art, Mould catalogue appendix](https://www.jstor.org/stable/3258511))

- **1863–65:** *West Presbyterian Church*. A Manhattan church by Vaux and Mould, subsequently demolished. ([Metropolitan Museum of Art, Mould catalogue appendix](https://www.jstor.org/stable/3258511))

- **1865–77:** *Jefferson Market Courthouse*. A courthouse complex in Greenwich Village designed through Vaux & Withers, with Frederick Clarke Withers generally credited as the principal architectural designer; calling it Vaux’s alone would erase the partnership. ([NYC Landmarks Preservation Commission](https://s-media.nyc.gov/agencies/lpc/lp/0016.pdf))

- **1872–77:** *American Museum of Natural History, original building and master plan*. A polychrome Gothic museum designed with Jacob Wrey Mould. Later museum wings engulfed or replaced much of the original exterior, so the surviving object is only a fragmentary manifestation of the plan. ([NYC Parks environmental-history appendix](https://www.nycgovparks.org/download/AMNH-FEIS/16DPR004M_FEIS_APPENDIX_A.pdf))

- **1874:** *New York City Prison (“The Tombs”), replacement design*. An unbuilt project: Vaux made an architectural proposal, not the prison eventually constructed. ([Kowsky, pp. 198–251](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1874–80:** *Metropolitan Museum of Art, original Fifth Avenue building*. A polychrome Gothic museum building designed with Jacob Wrey Mould and later almost entirely enclosed by additions; the original survives substantially as internal fabric. ([Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/about-the-met/collection-areas/the-metropolitan-museum-of-art-archives/history-of-the-museum); [Kowsky, pp. 198–251](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1875:** *The Parade Refectory*. A park restaurant and gathering building for Buffalo’s Parade, destroyed by fire in 1877; its smaller replacement was by C. K. Porter and is not a Vaux work. ([Olmsted in Buffalo](https://www.olmstedinbuffalo.com/calvert-vaux/))

- **1875–76:** *The Parade Carriage Sheds and Barn*. Service architecture for Buffalo’s Parade; the sheds were demolished, while the relocated barn survives in altered context. ([Olmsted in Buffalo](https://www.olmstedinbuffalo.com/calvert-vaux/))

- **1875:** *The Park Boathouse*. A boathouse for Buffalo’s Delaware Park, enlarged in 1883–84 and demolished in 1899. ([Olmsted in Buffalo](https://www.olmstedinbuffalo.com/calvert-vaux/))

- **1875:** *The Park Farmstead*. A superintendent’s residence and outbuildings for Buffalo’s Delaware Park, demolished in 1950. ([Olmsted in Buffalo](https://www.olmstedinbuffalo.com/calvert-vaux/))

- **1875:** *The Park Viaduct*. A grade-separated park structure carrying Buffalo’s park circulation across Delaware Street; later reconstruction means the extant fabric is not wholly Vaux’s. ([Buffalo Architecture and History](https://buffaloah.com/a/archs/vaux/time.html))

- **1879:** *East Side Boys’ Lodging House and Industrial School*. The first purpose-built Children’s Aid Society building by Vaux & Radford, at East Broadway and Gouverneur Street; it was demolished. ([NYC Landmarks Preservation Commission](https://s-media.nyc.gov/agencies/lpc/lp/2274.pdf))

- **1883:** *West Side Lodging House*. A Children’s Aid Society lodging house at Seventh Avenue and 32nd Street by Vaux & Radford, subsequently demolished. ([NYC Landmarks Preservation Commission](https://s-media.nyc.gov/agencies/lpc/lp/2274.pdf))

- **1883:** *Trinity Cemetery gatehouse, keeper’s lodge, and suspension bridge*. A funerary-architectural ensemble connecting the two halves of Trinity Cemetery across Broadway, credited to Vaux, Withers & Co. and George K. Radford; the collaborative attribution resists reduction to a sole-authored work. ([Biographical Dictionary of Architects in Canada](https://dictionaryofarchitectsincanada.org/node/1406))

- **1884:** *Health House*. A seaside convalescent building for the Children’s Aid Society at Coney Island, designed by Vaux & Radford and demolished after 1921. ([Biographical Dictionary of Architects in Canada](https://dictionaryofarchitectsincanada.org/node/1406))

- **1885:** *Cemetery Chapel for the Spanish and Portuguese Synagogue*. A chapel at Cypress Hills, Long Island, designed by Vaux & Radford. ([Biographical Dictionary of Architects in Canada](https://dictionaryofarchitectsincanada.org/node/1406))

- **1885–87:** *Tompkins Square Lodging House and Industrial School*. A combined lodging house and school for the Children’s Aid Society, designed by Vaux & Radford; it survives, though no longer in its original institutional use. ([NYC Landmarks Preservation Commission](https://s-media.nyc.gov/agencies/lpc/lp/1255.pdf); [Village Preservation](https://villagepreservation.org/2019/10/16/the-childrens-aid-societys-deep-roots-in-greenwich-village-and-the-east-village/))

- **1887:** *Newsboys’ Lodging House and School*. A Children’s Aid Society building at Second Avenue and East 44th Street, designed by Vaux & Radford. ([Biographical Dictionary of Architects in Canada](https://dictionaryofarchitectsincanada.org/node/1406))

- **1888–89:** *Fourteenth Ward Industrial School*. A Children’s Aid Society school at 256–258 Mott Street, designed by Vaux & Radford and also known as the Astor Memorial or Mott Street Industrial School. Sources vary between 1888–89 and 1890 because design, construction, and opening dates are treated differently. ([NYC Landmarks Preservation Commission](https://s-media.nyc.gov/agencies/lpc/lp/0960.pdf); [Biographical Dictionary of Architects in Canada](https://dictionaryofarchitectsincanada.org/node/1406))

- **1889:** *Sixth Street Industrial School*. A Children’s Aid Society school at 630–634 East Sixth Street by Vaux & Radford; sources variously date its design, construction, and opening to 1889–91. ([Village Preservation](https://villagepreservation.org/2019/10/16/the-childrens-aid-societys-deep-roots-in-greenwich-village-and-the-east-village/); [Children’s Aid Society archive](https://findingaids.library.nyu.edu/nyhs/ms111_childrens_aid_society/))

- **1890:** *Rhinelander Industrial School*. A Children’s Aid Society school by Vaux & Radford; surviving architectural drawings establish the firm’s authorship even where later accounts differ over the opening date. ([Children’s Aid Society archive](https://findingaids.library.nyu.edu/nyhs/ms111_childrens_aid_society/contents/aspace_ref8_3xf/))

- **1890:** *Jones Memorial Industrial School*. A Children’s Aid Society building on East 73rd Street by Vaux & Radford, demolished in 1965. ([Biographical Dictionary of Architects in Canada](https://dictionaryofarchitectsincanada.org/node/1406))

- **1890:** *House of Reception, New York Juvenile Asylum*. A reception building on West 27th Street by Vaux & Radford, subsequently demolished. ([Biographical Dictionary of Architects in Canada](https://dictionaryofarchitectsincanada.org/node/1406))

- **1891–92:** *Elizabeth Home for Girls*. A Children’s Aid Society residence at 307 East 12th Street by Vaux & Radford—the organization’s only Vaux-designed lodging house specifically for girls. ([NYC Landmarks Preservation Commission](https://s-media.nyc.gov/agencies/lpc/lp/2274.pdf))

- **1891–92:** *Sullivan Street Industrial School*. A Children’s Aid Society school at 221 Sullivan Street. Vaux & Radford prepared the design, but evidence indicates that Nicholas Gillesheimer modified it before construction, so the executed building is not securely a pure manifestation of their project. ([Village Preservation](https://villagepreservation.org/2018/12/20/calvert-vaux-and-the-village/))

- **1880s–91:** *Sheppard Asylum*. An institutional complex at Towson, Maryland, now part of Sheppard Pratt; its long construction history and later expansion mean the present campus should not be treated as a single intact Vaux object. ([Kowsky, pp. 261–319](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959); [Sheppard Pratt history](https://www.sheppardpratt.org/about/history/))

### Central Park: landscape, structures, and Gesamtkunstwerk

- **1858:** *Greensward Plan*. The competition-winning project for Central Park, made with Frederick Law Olmsted. It is both a named graphic-textual proposal and the generative intention behind the park; the built and repeatedly altered landscape is a later, collective manifestation rather than a fixed object. ([National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/people/calvert-vaux.htm); [Olmsted Papers chronology](https://rotunda.upress.virginia.edu/founders/default.xqy?keys=OLMS-print-01-03-03-0002&printable=yes))

- **1858:** *Bow Bridge*. A cast-iron pedestrian bridge within Central Park, designed with Jacob Wrey Mould as part of the larger Olmsted–Vaux circulation and landscape system. ([Kowsky, pp. 103–135](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1859:** *Ramble Arch*. A stone arch and grade-separation structure in Central Park, simultaneously architecture, circulation device, and landscape incident. ([Kowsky, pp. 103–135](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1858–71:** *Central Park bridges, arches, and transverse-road structures*. A distributed body of architecture designed chiefly by Vaux and Mould within Olmsted and Vaux’s park plan. It resists enumeration as a set of wholly autonomous works because each structure functions as part of the park’s circulation Gesamtkunstwerk. ([Central Park Conservancy](https://www.centralparknyc.org/articles/bridges-and-arches); [National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/people/calvert-vaux.htm))

- **1862–73:** *Bethesda Terrace and its approaches*. A monumental architectural-landscape composition by Vaux with ornamental design by Jacob Wrey Mould; construction and embellishment extended over more than a decade. ([Central Park Conservancy](https://www.centralparknyc.org/locations/bethesda-terrace); [Kowsky, pp. 103–135](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1862:** *Central Park Bandstand*. A music pavilion designed with Mould and subsequently demolished. ([Metropolitan Museum of Art, Mould catalogue appendix](https://www.jstor.org/stable/3258511))

- **1867–71:** *Belvedere*. A scenic architectural folly and lookout designed with Mould; “Belvedere Castle” is the later familiar name. Its status is deliberately hybrid: building, stage scenery, viewing apparatus, and landscape incident. ([Central Park Conservancy](https://www.centralparknyc.org/locations/belvedere-castle); [Kowsky, pp. 103–135](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1868:** *Boys’ Play House*. A Central Park recreational building, subsequently demolished. ([Kowsky, pp. 103–135](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1869:** *The Dairy*. A picturesque shelter and refreshment building in Central Park; its present fabric has undergone restoration and changes of use. ([Central Park Conservancy](https://www.centralparknyc.org/locations/the-dairy-visitor-center-and-gift-shop); [Kowsky, pp. 103–135](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

### Parks, park systems, campuses, cemeteries, and urban plans

- **1850–52:** *Springside*. An ornamental farm and estate landscape for Matthew Vassar at Poughkeepsie, planned by Downing with Vaux’s assistance. The surviving landscape cannot be cleanly partitioned into independent Downing and Vaux authorship. ([TCLF Hudson Valley guide](https://www.tclf.org/sites/default/files/2023-08/WOTW-Rhinebeck-HudsonValley_Guidebook_009.pdf))

- **1860:** *Retreat Park, Hartford Retreat for the Insane*. A therapeutic institutional landscape made with Olmsted, organized around meadow, curving drives, garden, and orchard. ([TCLF](https://www.tclf.org/institute-living))

- **1865–73:** *Prospect Park*. A Brooklyn public landscape by Olmsted and Vaux, incorporating terrain, water, woodland, circulation, structures, and social program; like Central Park, it is best understood as a Gesamtkunstwerk rather than a single stable artifact. ([National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/people/calvert-vaux.htm); [TCLF](https://www.tclf.org/pioneer/calvert-vaux))

- **1866–67:** *Fort Greene Park, redesign*. A Brooklyn park plan by Olmsted and Vaux, developed from an earlier park and subsequently altered; the work is therefore a redesign layered onto an existing landscape. ([NYC Parks](https://www.nycgovparks.org/parks/fort-greene-park/history))

- **1866:** *Columbia Institution for the Deaf and Dumb grounds (now Gallaudet University)*. A campus-landscape project by Olmsted and Vaux in Washington, D.C. ([Kowsky, pp. 198–251](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1867:** *Hudson River State Hospital grounds*. An institutional landscape at Poughkeepsie designed with Olmsted; subsequent development means the historic plan survives incompletely. ([TCLF](https://www.tclf.org/pioneer/calvert-vaux); [Kowsky, pp. 198–251](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1867–74:** *Brooklyn parkways: Eastern Parkway and Ocean Parkway*. Linear parks and transportation corridors planned by Olmsted and Vaux as extensions of Prospect Park. They are urban infrastructure as much as parks and therefore resist treatment as ornamental landscapes alone. ([National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/people/calvert-vaux.htm); [TCLF](https://www.tclf.org/pioneer/calvert-vaux))

- **1868–76:** *Buffalo park and parkway system*. A linked urban landscape by Olmsted and Vaux comprising The Park (Delaware Park), The Parade (Martin Luther King Jr. Park), The Front (Front Park), and connecting parkways. Individual buildings belong to Vaux’s architectural œuvre, but the system itself is a distinct metropolitan-scale obra. ([Olmsted in Buffalo](https://www.olmstedinbuffalo.com/calvert-vaux/); [TCLF](https://www.tclf.org/pioneer/calvert-vaux))

- **1868–69:** *Riverside*. A planned suburban community in Illinois by Olmsted and Vaux, integrating curvilinear streets, common landscape, planting, drainage, and residential development. ([Olmsted Online](https://olmstedonline.org/Job/Details/00607))

- **1869–71:** *South Park, Chicago*. Olmsted and Vaux’s plan for the linked South Park system, including the landscapes later called Washington Park, Jackson Park, and the Midway Plaisance. Later redesign—especially for the 1893 exposition—means their original project was only partly manifested and does not equal the parks as they now stand. ([Olmsted Online](https://olmstedonline.org/Job/Details/01902))

- **1871:** *New York State Hospital for the Insane grounds, Buffalo*. An institutional landscape planned with Olmsted in conjunction with the hospital complex. ([Olmsted in Buffalo](https://www.olmstedinbuffalo.com/calvert-vaux/))

- **1870s:** *Morningside Park*. A steep-site Manhattan park initially designed by Olmsted and Vaux; execution, interruption, and later work by Jacob Wrey Mould and others make sole or complete attribution inappropriate. ([NYC Parks](https://www.nycgovparks.org/parks/morningside-park/history); [National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/people/calvert-vaux.htm))

- **1870s–80s:** *Trinity Cemetery improvements*. Landscape and architectural work at Manhattan’s Trinity Cemetery by Vaux’s successive partnerships; the commission included grounds, circulation, a lodge, gatehouse, and bridge rather than one self-contained object. ([TCLF](https://www.tclf.org/pioneer/calvert-vaux); [Biographical Dictionary of Architects in Canada](https://dictionaryofarchitectsincanada.org/node/1406))

- **1870s–90s:** *Cemetery of the Evergreens improvements*. Landscape work in Brooklyn and Queens associated with Vaux; the documentary record supports a continuing commission more readily than a single fixed design date. ([TCLF](https://www.tclf.org/pioneer/calvert-vaux))

- **1881:** *Grace Church garden and grounds*. An urban churchyard landscape in Manhattan. It is an intervention among existing buildings, not a freestanding park. ([TCLF](https://www.tclf.org/pioneer/calvert-vaux); [Kowsky, pp. 261–319](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1880s:** *Bryn Mawr College grounds*. Campus-landscape work attributed to Vaux; later campus growth makes the limits and survival of his contribution difficult to isolate. ([TCLF](https://www.tclf.org/pioneer/calvert-vaux))

- **1880s:** *Wisner estate grounds (now Reeves-Reed Arboretum)*. An estate-landscape commission at Summit, New Jersey, associated with Vaux; later owners and designers altered the property. ([TCLF](https://www.tclf.org/pioneer/calvert-vaux))

- **1887–88:** *Canal Street Park*. A small Manhattan urban park designed during Vaux’s later municipal career; later street and park reconstruction altered or erased the original scheme. ([Kowsky, pp. 261–319](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1887:** *New York State Reservation at Niagara improvements*. A landscape-preservation and visitor-access project made in renewed collaboration with Olmsted. Because the reservation was conceived around protecting an existing natural phenomenon, its workhood lies in restraint, circulation, removal, and framing rather than in fabricating the Falls. ([National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/people/calvert-vaux.htm); [TCLF](https://www.tclf.org/pioneer/calvert-vaux))

- **1889–97:** *Downing Park*. A memorial public park at Newburgh begun by Vaux and Olmsted. After both men became unable to complete their portions, Downing Vaux and John Charles Olmsted continued the work; the realized park is consequently a multigenerational manifestation of the original project. ([TCLF](https://www.tclf.org/pioneer/calvert-vaux); [Kowsky, pp. 261–319](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959))

- **1891:** *Wilderstein grounds*. A plan by Vaux & Company, including a topographic survey and picturesque reorganization of the Suckley estate at Rhinebeck. The design was produced with Vaux’s son Downing and belongs to the firm’s œuvre as well as Calvert Vaux’s. ([TCLF](https://www.tclf.org/wilderstein))

- **1894:** *Rockwood Park*. A large public-park project at Saint John, New Brunswick, designed by Vaux; later implementation and modification mean the present park is not identical with his project. ([TCLF](https://www.tclf.org/pioneer/calvert-vaux); [Stonehammer UNESCO Global Geopark](https://stonehammergeopark.com/rockwood-park/))

- **1894:** *New York University University Heights landscape plan*. A preliminary campus project, probably unexecuted. It survives as intention rather than realized landscape and should not be confused with Stanford White’s built campus plan. ([TCLF](https://www.tclf.org/american-pantheon-hall-fame-great-americans))

- **1895:** *Mulberry Bend Park*. A Lower Manhattan park project prepared in the last year of Vaux’s life; the park opened after his death and was subsequently redesigned and renamed Columbus Park, so its built form is not a wholly intact Vaux work. ([Kowsky, pp. 261–319](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/country-park-and-city-9780195114959); [NYC Parks](https://www.nycgovparks.org/parks/columbus-park/history))

- **1893–95:** *Harlem River Speedway plans*. A municipal landscape-and-roadway project whose design became politically contested; Olmsted’s correspondence records attempts to discredit Vaux’s plans. The eventual Speedway therefore cannot be treated without qualification as a straightforward realization of his project. ([Olmsted Papers chronology](https://rotunda.upress.virginia.edu/founders/default.xqy?keys=OLMS-print-01-09-03-0002))

### Competition designs, unrealized projects, and uncertain attributions

- **1859:** *Plymouth Church of the Pilgrims*. An unsuccessful competition project made with Jacob Wrey Mould; it exists as a design rather than a church. ([Metropolitan Museum of Art, Mould catalogue appendix](https://www.jstor.org/stable/3258511))

- **1859:** *Brooklyn Academy of Music*. An unsuccessful competition project with Mould, not the building that was ultimately erected. ([Metropolitan Museum of Art, Mould catalogue appendix](https://www.jstor.org/stable/3258511))

- **1861:** *National Academy of Design*. A competition project associated with Vaux and Mould; it was not the executed building. ([Metropolitan Museum of Art, Mould catalogue appendix](https://www.jstor.org/stable/3258511))

- **1871:** *Jackson Park proposal*. The southern division of Olmsted and Vaux’s Chicago South Park plan, including lagoons, open ground, beach, and pier. Only parts were implemented before later transformations, so its principal existence is as a project embedded in the larger South Park obra. ([Olmsted Online](https://olmstedonline.org/Job/Details/01902))

- **1873:** *Main Building, Centennial Exhibition*. An architectural proposal submitted for the Philadelphia exhibition of 1876; it was not the design selected for construction. ([Philadelphia Architects and Buildings](https://www.philadelphiabuildings.org/pab/app/ar_display.cfm/21716))

- **c. 1870s:** *Touro Park and music stand*. One theory assigns the Newport park and its vanished bandstand to Vaux, but contemporary evidence more directly names landscape gardener Eugene Baumann. The attribution must remain disputed. ([TCLF](https://www.tclf.org/landscapes/touro-park-and-old-stone-mill))

- **1894:** *Hall of Fame/University Heights preliminary grounds plan*. The plan is documented but was probably never carried out; it is a project, not a surviving designed landscape. ([TCLF](https://www.tclf.org/american-pantheon-hall-fame-great-americans))
