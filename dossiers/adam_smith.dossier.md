# Adam Smith: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier concerns **Adam Smith, the Scottish moral philosopher and political economist**, not later people of the same name.

Labels used for uncertain or controversial material:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a contemporary record, Smith’s own writing, official institutional record, or surviving correspondence.
- **Reported fact:** recorded by an early biographer or contemporary witness but not independently established.
- **Allegation or theory:** an interpretive or accusatory claim for which evidence is contested or incomplete.
- **Rumor or myth:** a story lacking adequate contemporary corroboration, included because it has influenced Smith’s image.

Smith ordered most of his unpublished manuscripts destroyed shortly before his death. His surviving correspondence is also incomplete. Any reconstruction of his private life, religious beliefs, early lectures, intellectual debts, or unfinished projects is therefore necessarily partial.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Adam Smith.
- **Alternative forms:** Dr. Adam Smith; Adam Smith, LL.D.; Professor Adam Smith.
- **Titles and offices:** Professor of Logic and then Moral Philosophy at the University of Glasgow; tutor and travelling companion to Henry Scott, later 3rd Duke of Buccleuch; Commissioner of Customs and Salt Duties for Scotland; Rector of the University of Glasgow.
- **Later honorific descriptions:** “father of economics,” “father of political economy,” “father of capitalism,” and “apostle of free trade.” These are posthumous labels and can obscure the breadth and qualifications of his arguments.
- **Pseudonyms:** None documented.
- **Birth:** Exact date unknown. Baptized **5 June 1723** at Kirkcaldy, Fife, Scotland. The baptismal date is often presented as his birthday, including by Dugald Stewart; modern institutional accounts distinguish baptism from an unknown birth date.
- **Death:** **17 July 1790**, Edinburgh, Scotland, aged about sixty-seven.
- **Burial:** Canongate Kirkyard, Edinburgh.
- **Cause of death:** The Kirkcaldy/Old Parish Register description cited by the University of Glasgow is “decay,” an imprecise period term. Smith had been seriously ill for an extended period, apparently with a painful intestinal or abdominal disorder. A precise modern diagnosis cannot be established.
- **Nationality and political setting:** Scottish; born after the 1707 union into the Kingdom of Great Britain.
- **Religion:** Baptized and raised within the Church of Scotland. His mature private beliefs remain uncertain. His writings employ providential and theological language but often analyze morality and social order without relying on revelation. Interpretations range from moderate natural theology to guarded skepticism.
- **Marital status:** Never married.
- **Known children:** None documented.
- **Principal residences:** Kirkcaldy; Glasgow; Oxford; Edinburgh; Toulouse; Geneva; Paris; London; Edinburgh again, principally Panmure House from 1778 until his death.

## Family and Ancestry

### Father

Smith’s father, also **Adam Smith**, was a lawyer or legally trained official who served as comptroller of customs at Kirkcaldy. He had connections with the Scottish administrative establishment and had previously been secretary to Hugh Campbell, Earl of Loudoun. He died several months before the philosopher’s birth.

The father’s customs career is biographically significant because the younger Smith later became a customs commissioner. It does not by itself prove that Smith inherited particular doctrines concerning taxation or commerce.

### Mother

**Margaret Douglas Smith**, daughter of **Robert Douglas of Strathendry**, came from a relatively prosperous landed Fife family. Widowed before her son’s birth, she never remarried. Contemporary and early biographical accounts describe an unusually close lifelong attachment between mother and son.

She lived with Smith during much of his Glasgow professorship, in Kirkcaldy during the composition of *The Wealth of Nations*, and later at Panmure House. She died in 1788, when approximately ninety years old. Her death reportedly affected him deeply.

### Siblings and household family

Smith was the only child of his parents’ marriage. He had a paternal half-brother, **Hugh Smith**, from his father’s earlier marriage. Hugh became associated with customs administration but was not a central figure in the philosopher’s mature household.

Smith’s cousin **Janet Douglas**—sometimes called Miss Douglas—lived with Smith and his mother, helped manage the household, and survived Margaret by only a few years. Early biographies treat both women as Smith’s closest domestic relations.

### Heir

Smith left his estate and library principally to his cousin **David Douglas**, later Lord Reston, the youngest son of Colonel Robert Douglas of Strathendry. His will transferred lands, debts, securities, salary arrears, money, furniture, plate, pictures, and books, subject to specified burdens and legacies.

Smith’s library was subsequently divided through the Douglas-Bannerman family. A substantial surviving portion—around 900 titles—is now associated with Edinburgh University Library; other books are dispersed among institutional and private collections.

### Marriage and romantic reports

**Documented fact:** Smith never married, and no children are known.

**Reported fact:** Dugald Stewart wrote that Smith had once been attached to a young woman of “great beauty and accomplishment” who likewise favored him, but circumstances prevented marriage. Stewart did not name her.

**Reported fact:** Henry Mackenzie later recalled that Smith had been seriously interested in a “Miss Campbell,” whom Mackenzie regarded as temperamentally unlike him.

**Reported fact:** Letters and later reminiscences have been used to propose another attachment while Smith was in France, possibly involving a woman associated with the household of Madame Riccoboni or the French social world Smith entered.

**Unresolved question:** None of these reports establishes an engagement, sexual relationship, or the woman’s identity. Modern attempts to reconstruct “Smith in love” combine early testimony with conjecture. Claims that Smith’s bachelorhood proves homosexuality, celibacy, impotence, or a secret partnership with David Hume are unsupported by surviving documentary evidence.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1723–1737

Smith grew up in **Kirkcaldy**, a Fife burgh and port on the Firth of Forth. Its population was small, but its commercial connections exposed inhabitants to shipping, customs regulation, manufacture, agriculture, and international trade.

Dugald Stewart described Smith as physically delicate in infancy. His mother’s close protection was criticized by some relatives as indulgent, but Stewart maintained that it produced no harmful effect on his character.

### The alleged Romani abduction

**Reported fact becoming legend:** John Rae and other biographers recount that, at about three or four years old, Smith was taken while visiting Strathendry by a passing group described in older sources as “gypsies.” Searchers reportedly found the group, and the child was recovered.

The story’s earliest evidentiary chain is weak. It rests on family or local tradition transmitted long after the event; no contemporary police, parish, court, or family record is known.

The anecdote was repeatedly circulated because it supplied biographers with a dramatic childhood episode. Rae added the joke that Smith would probably have made “a poor gypsy.” Modern repetitions often upgrade the story into fact while omitting the uncertain provenance and the stigmatizing assumptions embedded in nineteenth-century presentations.

### Schooling

From approximately **1729 to 1737**, Smith attended the **Burgh School of Kirkcaldy**, where the schoolmaster was **David Miller**. The curriculum included Latin, writing, mathematics, and history. Smith read widely and displayed an early capacity for sustained absorption.

Reports of his absent-mindedness begin in accounts of childhood or youth and later became an organizing feature of his public image. Some anecdotes are plausible because several independent acquaintances described similar behavior; individual stories often remain unverifiable.

## Education and Formation

## University of Glasgow, 1737–1740

Smith entered the University of Glasgow in 1737, at about fourteen—normal for a precocious Scottish university student of the period.

His most important teacher was **Francis Hutcheson**, Professor of Moral Philosophy. Hutcheson taught natural religion, ethics, jurisprudence, and politics and emphasized benevolence, moral sense, natural liberty, and criticism of inherited privilege. Smith later spoke warmly of him and adopted his practice of lecturing in English rather than relying exclusively on Latin.

Other formative disciplines included logic, classical literature, mathematics, and natural philosophy. Smith encountered Newtonian explanatory ideals and the Scottish natural-law tradition. His mature work retained Hutcheson’s concern with moral judgment but replaced a simple moral-sense account with the social mechanisms of sympathy, spectatorship, and self-command.

### Snell Exhibition

In 1740 Smith received a **Snell Exhibition**, a scholarship enabling Glasgow students to attend Balliol College, Oxford. The foundation had originally been associated with preparing Scots for Anglican orders, though by Smith’s time it functioned substantially as an educational endowment.

## Balliol College, Oxford, 1740–1746

Smith matriculated at Balliol in 1740 and remained about six years.

He later contrasted Oxford unfavorably with Glasgow, criticizing professors whose guaranteed incomes had weakened incentives to teach. His hostile passages in *The Wealth of Nations* helped create the enduring picture of an intellectually dormant Oxford.

**Documented and reported elements:**

- Smith largely educated himself through intensive reading.
- He studied Greek and Latin authors, literature, philosophy, natural science, law, and French.
- He experienced recurring illness and made journeys for his health.
- His scholarship required residence but did not lead to an Oxford degree.
- He left in 1746 and formally resigned the exhibition in February 1749.

**Reported fact:** Smith was discovered reading David Hume’s *Treatise of Human Nature*, after which the book was confiscated and Smith was reprimanded. The episode comes through later testimony and is plausible given Hume’s reputation and Balliol’s religious atmosphere, but no disciplinary record has been identified.

**Qualification:** Smith’s judgment of Oxford cannot be generalized without evidence to every tutor or college. Modern historians treat it partly as the testimony of a dissatisfied student and partly as evidence for real institutional problems.

---

## Early Career, 1746–1751

Smith returned to Kirkcaldy after Oxford. He had no inherited profession and initially lacked a university office.

In **1748**, with the encouragement and patronage of **Henry Home, Lord Kames**, he began delivering public lectures in Edinburgh. The lectures were sponsored through Edinburgh intellectual networks and covered rhetoric, belles lettres, the history of literature, and later jurisprudence or political subjects.

Smith’s Edinburgh teaching brought him into contact with:

- Lord Kames;
- David Hume;
- James Oswald of Dunnikier;
- Alexander Wedderburn;
- William Robertson;
- Hugh Blair;
- members of the Philosophical Society and Edinburgh legal and literary circles.

Student notes published much later as *Lectures on Rhetoric and Belles Lettres* reflect Glasgow teaching from the 1760s rather than a verbatim record of these first Edinburgh lectures, but their subjects probably overlap.

---

## University of Glasgow Career, 1751–1764

In January **1751**, Smith was appointed Professor of Logic at Glasgow. In **1752**, following Thomas Craigie’s death or vacancy, he transferred to the more congenial chair of **Moral Philosophy**.

His course encompassed:

1. natural theology;
2. ethics;
3. jurisprudence;
4. “police,” revenue, and arms—the political-economic administration of society.

The fourth component developed arguments about division of labor, markets, money, public finance, and economic development that later entered *The Wealth of Nations*.

### University offices

Smith served in several administrative roles:

- library **quaestor**, approximately 1758–1760;
- Dean of Faculty, 1760–1762;
- presiding officer or *praeses* when senior officers were absent;
- Vice-Rector in 1763;
- much later Rector, beginning in 1787.

As quaestor he purchased books, including volumes of Diderot and d’Alembert’s *Encyclopédie*. His institutional responsibilities contradict the caricature of a wholly impractical recluse.

### Teaching practice

Students and later observers described Smith as a compelling lecturer who began somewhat hesitantly, became animated, and appeared to develop arguments while speaking. His income combined a salary with student fees. When he resigned, he attempted to return fees for the unfinished portion of the term; students reportedly refused to accept them.

### Honorary degree

The University of Glasgow conferred the degree of **LL.D.** on Smith in **1762**.

### *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*, 1759

Published in London in late April 1759 by Andrew Millar, with Edinburgh partners Alexander Kincaid and John Bell, *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* established Smith’s European reputation. William Strahan’s printing records indicate an initial run of 1,000 copies.

Its central mechanisms include:

- sympathy as imaginative entry into another person’s situation;
- the distinction between actor and spectator;
- the “impartial spectator” as an internalized standpoint;
- propriety, merit, demerit, virtue, self-command, and conscience;
- the human desire not merely to be praised but to be praiseworthy;
- unintended coordination represented by an “invisible hand” in a discussion of the rich.

The work went through six lifetime editions: 1759, 1761, 1767, 1774, 1781, and 1790. The sixth contained substantial revisions, especially on the corruption of moral judgment by admiration of wealth and greatness.

---

## Continental Journey, 1764–1766

The politician **Charles Townshend**, impressed by *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*, arranged for Smith to tutor his stepson, **Henry Scott**, the young Duke of Buccleuch. Smith resigned his chair in 1764.

The appointment gave Smith a salary during the tour and a lifetime pension generally reported as **£300 annually**, a large income that enabled him to work independently afterward.

### Toulouse

Smith and the duke traveled through Paris to **Toulouse**, where they remained for roughly eighteen months. Smith initially found local society dull and resumed work on a projected book—eventually *The Wealth of Nations*.

### Southern France and Geneva

In 1764 the party toured southern France and spent about two months in **Geneva**. Smith met **Voltaire** at or near Ferney. He admired Voltaire’s literary stature while disagreeing with aspects of his philosophy and public posture.

### Rousseau

Smith had already anonymously reviewed Rousseau’s *Discourse on Inequality* in the *Edinburgh Review* in 1756 and translated substantial passages.

**Unresolved question:** Smith and Jean-Jacques Rousseau were simultaneously within the Parisian orbit in late 1765 or early 1766, but no correspondence survives and modern scholarship concludes they probably never met. Their intellectual relationship is textual rather than securely personal.

### Paris

In Paris Smith entered the circles of:

- **François Quesnay**;
- **Anne-Robert-Jacques Turgot**;
- **Victor Riqueti, Marquis de Mirabeau**;
- **Pierre Samuel du Pont de Nemours**;
- **d’Alembert**;
- **Helvétius**;
- **Baron d’Holbach**;
- the Abbé Morellet;
- Madame de Boufflers and other salon figures;
- David Hume, then serving diplomatically in France.

Smith regarded the Physiocrats’ agricultural “system of natural liberty” as seriously mistaken in making agriculture uniquely productive, but also as the closest published approximation to truth in political economy then available.

### End of the tour

The duke’s younger brother, Hew Campbell Scott, fell ill and died in Paris in 1766. The party returned to Britain. Smith retained cordial relations with Buccleuch and received the promised pension.

---

## Composition and Publication of *The Wealth of Nations*, 1766–1776

Smith returned principally to **Kirkcaldy**, living with his mother and Janet Douglas. He spent approximately a decade developing a work whose elements had appeared in his lectures.

He also made extended visits to **London**, particularly during final composition and printing. He participated in elite literary and political networks and attended dinners involving Edmund Burke, Edward Gibbon, Samuel Johnson, Joshua Reynolds, and others.

### Benjamin Franklin

**Documented or strongly reported fact:** Smith and **Benjamin Franklin** were acquainted in London by the mid-1770s. They shared interests in colonial policy, population, wages, and American economic development.

**Qualification:** No direct Smith–Franklin correspondence survives. Claims that Franklin “read every chapter,” corrected the manuscript, or substantially co-authored *The Wealth of Nations* derive from later reminiscence and inference, not demonstrable textual documentation. The defensible conclusion is acquaintance and probable discussion, not a measurable editorial role.

### David Hume

Hume was Smith’s closest major intellectual friendship. They exchanged manuscripts, publication news, travel information, personal confidences, and judgments about contemporaries. They agreed on much concerning commercial society, convention, and criticism of superstition, but differed in emphasis on sympathy, utility, religion, and political economy.

In 1776 Hume, already terminally ill, praised *The Wealth of Nations* while teasing Smith that it required too much thought to become immediately popular. Smith visited Hume during his final illness.

### Publication

*An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations* appeared on **9 March 1776**, in two quarto volumes. The first edition sold out within months.

Its main architecture included:

- division of labor;
- the propensity to exchange;
- money;
- nominal and real prices;
- wages, profit, and rent;
- accumulation of stock;
- historical development;
- agriculture and manufactures;
- criticism of mercantilism;
- colonies and imperial monopoly;
- the Physiocratic system;
- taxation;
- defense, justice, education, infrastructure, and public debt.

The famous “invisible hand” appears only once in this book. Smith did not formulate a universal theorem that private greed invariably produces public good. His analysis repeatedly addresses monopoly, collusion, unequal bargaining power, fraud, rent-seeking, regulatory capture, public goods, and the moral and intellectual injuries caused by repetitive labor.

### American Revolution

Smith completed the book amid the imperial crisis. He argued that Britain’s colonies imposed military and fiscal burdens disproportionate to their benefits under monopoly. He considered alternatives including colonial representation in Parliament and eventual political separation.

He did not participate in the American Revolution. His analysis was neither a revolutionary manifesto nor a defense of continued imperial coercion.

---

## Hume’s Death and Religious Controversy, 1776–1777

Hume died on 25 August 1776. Smith published a letter to William Strahan describing Hume’s serenity, humor, and moral character during his final illness. Smith concluded that Hume approached “as nearly to the idea of a perfectly wise and virtuous man” as human frailty permitted.

### Reaction

**Documented controversy:** Clerical and religious critics attacked Smith for publicly praising a notorious skeptic. One hostile pamphlet styled itself a letter to Smith “by one of the people called Christians.” Smith later remarked that the defense of Hume brought him “ten times more abuse” than his criticism of the commercial system.

### Hume’s unpublished *Dialogues*

Hume wanted his *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* published after his death. Smith refused to undertake publication, citing personal and prudential reasons, and the work was eventually issued by Hume’s nephew.

**Interpretive dispute:** Critics have characterized Smith’s refusal as cowardice or career protection. Defenders point to Smith’s responsibility for executing Hume’s wishes without accepting every legal and reputational risk. The surviving correspondence establishes refusal and anxiety; it does not establish a single motive.

---

## London and Government Consultation, 1776–1778

Smith continued to spend time in London after publication. He advised or conversed with politicians and administrators about taxation, trade, America, and public finance.

He was a member of the **Royal Society of London**, elected in 1773. In 1783 he became a founding fellow of the **Royal Society of Edinburgh**.

Although later tradition sometimes credits immediate policy revolutions to *The Wealth of Nations*, its governmental influence was cumulative. William Pitt the Younger later publicly invoked Smith, but major commercial and fiscal reforms had multiple political and intellectual sources.

---

## Commissioner of Customs, Edinburgh, 1778–1790

In 1778 Smith was appointed a **Commissioner of Customs and Salt Duties for Scotland**. He moved to Edinburgh, eventually occupying **Panmure House** off the Canongate with his mother and Janet Douglas.

### Duties

The commission was not merely ceremonial. Smith attended board business, dealt with seizures, tariffs, staffing, trade regulation, and anti-smuggling measures, and corresponded on administrative questions.

His income has been estimated at around **£900 annually** when his customs salary, Buccleuch pension, and book income were combined—placing him among the comfortably affluent professional elite, though not among great landowners or merchant magnates.

### Apparent contradiction with free trade

**Documented fact:** Smith, critic of mercantilist tariffs and monopolies, administered the existing customs system and participated in enforcement against smugglers.

**Interpretive controversy:** Critics call this hypocrisy. The countervailing evidence is that Smith’s argument was for reform through law, not individual evasion of law. He distinguished ideal “natural liberty” from feasible policy and endorsed customs duties for revenue, retaliation in limited circumstances, national defense, and gradual reform where abrupt abolition would cause severe dislocation.

The office nevertheless embedded him in the coercive machinery he criticized. Surviving records show diligent administration rather than an attempt to sabotage the system.

### Personal seizure of goods

Smith wrote that soon after becoming commissioner he discovered that many items in his own clothing or household had been imported contrary to customs rules. He gathered them and had them burned.

**Documented self-report:** The episode illustrates both the reach of eighteenth-century trade restrictions and Smith’s insistence on official compliance. The exact inventory and independent verification are unavailable.

### Panmure House and social life

Smith hosted Sunday suppers and participated in the **Oyster Club**, whose circle included Joseph Black, James Hutton, Adam Ferguson, and other Edinburgh intellectuals. Visitors or associates included Edmund Burke, the Duke of Buccleuch, Henry Mackenzie, William Robertson, and Dugald Stewart.

His 1781 library catalogue and reconstructed holdings show an extensive collection in classical languages, history, theology, law, philosophy, economics, travel, literature, and natural science. Estimates of the collection commonly exceed 3,000 volumes.

### Rector of Glasgow

Smith was elected Rector of the University of Glasgow in **1787** and served through 1789. In accepting, he described his Glasgow professorship as the most useful, happy, and honorable period of his life.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

## Moral psychology

Smith’s mature ethical theory treated moral judgment as socially formed. People imaginatively change places with others, compare passions with what an observer can share, and internalize an impartial spectator.

Self-interest was one motive among several—not a universal psychological axiom. Affection, resentment, generosity, vanity, status-seeking, duty, habit, and self-command all play important roles.

## Jurisprudence and stages of society

Student notes attribute to Smith a four-stage scheme:

1. hunting;
2. pastoralism;
3. agriculture;
4. commerce.

Property forms, law, political authority, and military organization change with modes of subsistence. This “conjectural history” influenced later social science but rested partly on speculative generalization rather than archival or anthropological fieldwork.

## Political economy

Smith associated growing productivity with specialization and the extent of the market. Accumulation and secure institutions could expand output, but concentrated merchants and manufacturers repeatedly sought privileges at public expense.

He favored:

- security of person and property;
- predictable justice;
- freer domestic and international exchange;
- relatively simple and proportionate taxation;
- public infrastructure where private return was inadequate;
- state-supported elementary education;
- defense and certain navigation restrictions;
- regulation of banks in some circumstances;
- gradual rather than reckless dismantling of entrenched systems.

He opposed or criticized:

- exclusive trading companies;
- colonial monopoly;
- guild restrictions;
- statutes restricting labor mobility;
- policies fixing wages in employers’ favor;
- collusion among merchants;
- slavery;
- wars and empire sustained for sectional commercial interests.

## Religion

Smith’s early teaching included natural theology, and editions of *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* refer to Providence, a final judge, and moral government. Yet his method explains social and moral order through human faculties and institutions.

His close friendship with Hume, avoidance of orthodox dogma, revisions reducing certain explicitly religious passages, and refusal to disclose private belief have produced competing readings.

- **Documented fact:** Smith was not prosecuted for irreligion and retained respectable civic and university standing.
- **Theory:** He was a concealed Humean skeptic who used theological language defensively.
- **Competing theory:** He held a sincere but nonsectarian natural religion.
- **Unresolved:** His private creed cannot be reconstructed with confidence.

## “Adam Smith problem”

Nineteenth-century German scholars formulated *Das Adam-Smith-Problem*: the alleged contradiction between sympathy in *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* and self-interest in *The Wealth of Nations*.

Modern scholarship generally rejects a simple contradiction. Sympathy in Smith means a process of fellow-feeling and judgment, not universal benevolence; self-interest explains many exchanges but does not exhaust morality or politics. Debate continues over how fully the two books form one system.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Margaret Douglas Smith

Mother, household anchor, and longest personal relationship. Smith lived with her through much of adulthood and cared for her until her death.

### David Hume

Friend, correspondent, intellectual interlocutor, and public religious liability. Their friendship is among the best-documented relationships of the Scottish Enlightenment.

### Francis Hutcheson

Teacher and formative influence at Glasgow. Smith inherited but modified Hutcheson’s natural-law, moral-sense, and natural-liberty traditions.

### Henry Home, Lord Kames

Patron of Smith’s Edinburgh lectures, judge, agricultural improver, and theorist of historical development.

### Henry Scott, Duke of Buccleuch

Pupil, travelling companion, patron, and provider of Smith’s pension. Their relationship remained friendly after the continental tour.

### Charles Townshend

Political patron who arranged Smith’s tutorship. Townshend’s later fiscal policies toward America were not Smith’s policies.

### Joseph Black

Chemist, physician, executor, friend, and member of Smith’s dining circle. Black attended Smith during his last illness.

### James Hutton

Geologist, friend, executor, and dining companion. With Black he supervised the destruction of Smith’s manuscripts and publication of authorized surviving essays.

### William Strahan

Printer, publisher, correspondent, and mutual friend of Smith and Hume.

### Andrew Millar and Thomas Cadell

Publishers involved in Smith’s major works and their commercial circulation.

### François Quesnay and Turgot

French economic interlocutors. Smith praised the Physiocrats’ conceptual ambition while rejecting their exclusive emphasis on agricultural productivity.

### Edmund Burke

Political and literary associate. Burke reviewed or promoted Smith’s work and moved in overlapping London circles.

### Adam Ferguson

Friend, colleague, and at times intellectual rival. Both developed analyses of division of labor and commercial society.

### Dugald Stewart

Younger philosopher, acquaintance, intellectual heir, and author of the first substantial biographical memoir.

### William Robertson, Hugh Blair, James Oswald, John Millar, and Henry Mackenzie

Members of overlapping university, literary, legal, political, and social networks in Glasgow and Edinburgh.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

Smith did not found a company, register patents, or operate a manufacturing enterprise.

His economically relevant institutional affiliations were:

- Burgh School of Kirkcaldy;
- University of Glasgow;
- Balliol College, Oxford;
- Philosophical Society of Edinburgh;
- Select Society;
- Royal Society of London;
- Royal Society of Edinburgh;
- British customs administration;
- the Buccleuch household;
- informal Edinburgh dining societies including the Oyster Club.

### Income and assets

Known or estimated sources included:

- professorial salary and student fees;
- the Buccleuch pension, commonly given as £300 annually;
- publication payments and royalties;
- customs salary;
- investments, securities, money, furniture, plate, books, and any real property encompassed by his will.

Smith lived comfortably and spent heavily on books. The exact value of his estate at death is not securely established in the sources consulted. His will is broad but does not supply a complete modern balance sheet.

### Intellectual and publishing structure

Smith’s major books were commercial products issued through London and Edinburgh printers and booksellers. He actively revised them across multiple editions. He was not merely a detached theorist: publishing negotiations, print runs, corrections, and royalties formed part of his career.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

No credible evidence was found that Smith was personally prosecuted for fraud, corruption, theft, violence, or another serious crime. No substantiated lawsuit, indictment, or criminal investigation involving him emerged in the dedicated adversarial search.

### Slavery

**Documented fact:** Smith condemned slavery morally and economically. He argued that free labor was generally more productive because enslaved people lacked incentives and that masters’ domination could corrupt judgment. He described the pride and cruelty of slaveholders and rejected racial slavery as evidence of natural inferiority.

**Documented limitation:** Smith’s Scottish and imperial world was materially sustained by slave-produced tobacco, sugar, and cotton. Glasgow merchants and some students or associates participated in Atlantic commerce. He did not organize abolitionist campaigns, construct a systematic account of metropolitan complicity, or center enslaved people’s testimony.

**Scholarly criticism:** Recent work argues that Smith identified slavery’s cruelty but inadequately analyzed how consumer demand, investment, commercial law, and imperial administration reproduced it. That is an interpretive criticism, not evidence that Smith owned enslaved people or invested in a plantation. No such ownership or direct investment was found.

### Empire and colonialism

Smith attacked monopoly empire and calculated that Britain gained less from colonial dominion than privileged merchants claimed. He nevertheless wrote in an eighteenth-century civilizational vocabulary, distinguishing “savage,” “barbarous,” and “civilized” societies.

**Documented issue:** These categories rank societies by subsistence and institutional development and can flatten or misdescribe Indigenous cultures.

**Competing interpretations:** Smith is presented variously as an anti-imperial critic, a reformer of empire, or a theorist whose commercial developmental scheme remained Eurocentric. The texts support criticism of conquest and monopoly but not a complete rejection of every colonial relationship.

### “Laissez-faire” and corporate power

**Mythologized claim:** Smith believed all government action harmful and all business activity socially beneficial.

**Evidence against:** Smith supported defense, justice, infrastructure, public education, some bank regulation, taxes, and restrictions justified by national security. He repeatedly warned that merchants conspire against the public, seek monopoly, and possess political influence disproportionate to their numbers.

He did not use the expression *laissez-faire* in his major works and was not the inventor of that slogan.

### Customs commissioner “hypocrisy”

**Documented fact:** Smith accepted a well-paid customs office and enforced regulations after attacking much of the tariff and monopoly system.

**Critical interpretation:** The position compromised his principles and made him financially dependent on government restrictions.

**Counterevidence:** Smith never argued that every customs duty was illegitimate. His official work was compatible with gradual reform, revenue tariffs, and legal enforcement. The tension is real but does not by itself establish corruption.

### Plagiarism and priority disputes

Smith drew on a wide intellectual field: Aristotle, Stoicism, natural-law writers, Hutcheson, Hume, Montesquieu, Mandeville, Locke, French economists, travel literature, and contemporaneous commercial debate.

**Allegation or theory:** Some later critics have accused Smith of insufficiently acknowledging predecessors, including Sir James Steuart, whose *Inquiry into the Principles of Political Oeconomy* appeared in 1767 and was not named in *The Wealth of Nations*.

**Qualification:** Smith’s book engaged broad systems rather than supplying modern footnotes. Silence about Steuart can reflect rivalry or strategic avoidance, but it does not demonstrate literal copying.

**Reported dispute with Adam Ferguson:** Smith complained that Ferguson had borrowed ideas on division of labor without acknowledgment. Ferguson reportedly answered that both had drawn on French sources. Ferguson later referred readers to Smith’s forthcoming political economy.

The surviving evidence supports a priority quarrel, not a judicial or institutional finding of plagiarism against either man.

### Treatment of women

Women appear in Smith’s discussions of family, education, dependency, labor, and social custom, but political and economic actors are overwhelmingly treated as male.

**Scholarly criticism:** His system insufficiently analyzes unpaid domestic labor, legal subordination, and gendered access to property and education.

**Qualification:** This is a limitation of analytical scope, not evidence of a specific personal abuse. No reliable accusation of sexual misconduct was found.

### Economic and historical errors

Later economics rejected or heavily revised several propositions associated with Smith:

- labor-commanded and cost-of-production approaches to value;
- the sharp distinction between productive and unproductive labor;
- some accounts of money’s origin;
- parts of his stage theory and conjectural history;
- expectations concerning joint-stock companies;
- empirical generalizations drawn from travel literature.

These are scholarly criticisms, not scandals or fraud. Smith openly presented political economy as historical and institutional inquiry rather than as a complete mathematical system.

### Destruction of manuscripts

**Documented fact:** Smith asked Joseph Black and James Hutton to burn most unpublished papers. They complied shortly before his death.

**Speculation:** The destroyed writings may have included a major history of law and government, a work on literature, or material revealing heterodox religious opinions.

**Unresolved:** Titles and lecture announcements show that Smith planned further projects, but the contents and reasons for destruction cannot be reconstructed. Claims that the burning concealed a scandal, secret doctrine, or plagiarism are unsupported.

### Political appropriation

Smith has been selectively appropriated by free-market conservatives, classical liberals, social democrats, neoliberals, libertarians, critics of monopoly, and critics of capitalism.

Selective quotation often isolates the butcher-brewer-baker passage while neglecting moral sentiments, public education, labor degradation, employer power, and monopoly. The opposite simplification turns Smith into an opponent of markets or private property. Neither captures the documentary whole.

### Recent adversarial-search findings, 2024–2026

Searches combining Smith’s name with “controversy,” “scandal,” “allegations,” “lawsuit,” “fraud,” “accusations,” “conspiracy,” and “criticism” produced predominantly:

- disputes over whether he was a right-wing or laissez-faire thinker;
- reassessments of slavery, colonial trade, inequality, and corporations;
- criticisms of his economic history and theories of value;
- the Ferguson priority dispute;
- claims that his influence has been exaggerated or misunderstood.

No new evidence from 2024–2026 established personal criminality, financial fraud, slave ownership, a secret business empire, or a previously unknown judicial proceeding.

## Health, Appearance, Habits, and Personality

Dugald Stewart described Smith as sickly in infancy. Oxford-era correspondence and biography record recurrent illness.

Contemporaries repeatedly described:

- absent-mindedness;
- talking to himself;
- a peculiar gait;
- bodily tics or involuntary movements;
- absorption in thought;
- warmth and generosity in company;
- limited attention to dress;
- strong attachment to books.

Smith reportedly called himself “a beau in nothing but my books.”

### Anecdotal absent-mindedness

Stories include:

- putting bread and butter into a teapot;
- walking many miles while absorbed in thought;
- signing another customs officer’s name;
- falling into a tanning pit while conversing;
- conducting imaginary conversations in public.

Some episodes appear in early reminiscence and may preserve genuine traits. Others accumulated as folklore around the stock figure of the absent-minded philosopher. They should not be used to diagnose a neurological or psychiatric condition retrospectively.

### Appearance

James Tassie’s **1787 medallion**, inscribed as representing Smith in his sixty-fourth year, is the strongest surviving likeness. Dugald Stewart considered it accurate. Many familiar engraved portraits derive from it rather than from independent sittings.

## Later Life and Death

Smith’s mother died in 1788. Janet Douglas died not long afterward. Smith’s own health deteriorated during revisions to the sixth edition of *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*.

In 1790 he arranged for Black and Hutton to destroy unwanted manuscripts. The authorized remnants were later published as *Essays on Philosophical Subjects*.

Smith died at Panmure House or in his Edinburgh residence on **17 July 1790**. Joseph Black attended him. According to an often-repeated report, Smith apologized to assembled friends that he had “made no great progress” in their discussion or said that they would have to continue the meeting elsewhere. Exact wording varies.

He was buried in Canongate Kirkyard. His grave became a place of intellectual commemoration rather than a religious cult site.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The father of capitalism”

- **Story:** Smith invented capitalism and supplied its definitive moral justification.
- **Appearance:** Especially common from the nineteenth century onward and intensified during twentieth-century ideological conflict.
- **Evidence:** Smith systematized political economy and analyzed commercial society, but the word “capitalism” was not his organizing term, capitalist institutions predated him, and he criticized merchants, monopolies, rent-seeking, and domination.
- **Effect:** Made Smith a symbolic ancestor claimed or attacked by later political movements.

### The universal “invisible hand”

- **Story:** Every selfish act automatically benefits society.
- **Evidence:** Smith used “invisible hand” once in *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*, once in *The Wealth of Nations*, and once in the posthumous “History of Astronomy,” in distinct contexts. His work contains numerous failures of private interest to serve public welfare.
- **Effect:** The phrase became vastly more prominent in twentieth-century economics and political rhetoric than in Smith’s own exposition.

### Kidnapped by “gypsies”

- **Story:** Romani travelers abducted the child Smith, who was rapidly recovered.
- **Source history:** Preserved through family or local tradition and repeated by nineteenth-century biographers.
- **Evidence:** No known contemporary record; evidentiary status is **reported legend**.
- **Effect:** Supplied an irresistible biographical opening and reinforced stereotypes about Romani criminality.

### Smith as an isolated genius

- **Story:** *The Wealth of Nations* emerged almost wholly from one man’s insight.
- **Evidence:** His work developed through university lectures, Scottish debates, Hume’s friendship, French political economy, British commercial controversies, student interaction, travel literature, and government information.
- **Effect:** Encouraged “great man” narratives and minimized collaborative intellectual networks.

### Franklin as secret co-author

- **Story:** Benjamin Franklin read or corrected the entire manuscript and inserted its account of America.
- **Evidence:** Acquaintance is well supported; direct manuscript annotations, correspondence, or chapter-by-chapter records are absent.
- **Status:** **Theory based on later reminiscence**, not established fact.
- **Effect:** Enlarged the perceived connection between *The Wealth of Nations* and the American Revolution.

### Secret love or secret sexuality

- **Story:** Smith abandoned marriage after a lost love; alternatively, he concealed homosexual relations, sometimes specifically with Hume.
- **Evidence:** Early reports support at least one romantic attachment but lack names and documentation. No evidence establishes a sexual relationship with Hume or anyone else.
- **Status:** The lost-love account is **reported but unresolved**; more specific sexual claims are **rumor or speculation**.
- **Effect:** Attempts to fill one of the largest silences in a life whose papers were heavily destroyed.

### Smith as atheist

- **Story:** He secretly shared Hume’s unbelief and concealed it to preserve employment.
- **Evidence:** Friendship with Hume and naturalistic explanation support possible skepticism; Smith also retained providential and theological language.
- **Status:** **Interpretive theory**, unresolved.
- **Effect:** Made Smith alternatively a hero of secular modernity or a thinker reclaimed for natural theology.

### Deathbed remorse

- **Story:** Smith regretted *The Wealth of Nations*, repudiated free markets, or destroyed papers out of panic.
- **Evidence:** No reliable deathbed recantation exists. He revised both major books late in life and arranged selective destruction consistent with long-held literary standards.
- **Status:** **Unsupported myth**.

## Works by Adam Smith

### Works published under Smith’s authority

1. **“A Letter to the Authors of the Edinburgh Review”** (1756).  
   Anonymous contribution to the short-lived *Edinburgh Review*. Discussed continental literature and included translated passages from Rousseau’s *Discourse on Inequality*. Attribution to Smith is accepted.

2. **The Theory of Moral Sentiments** (London and Edinburgh, 1759).  
   Six lifetime editions:
   - 1st: 1759;
   - 2nd: 1761;
   - 3rd: 1767;
   - 4th: 1774;
   - 5th: 1781;
   - 6th: 1790.  
   The sixth edition substantially revises the work and adds Part VI, “Of the Character of Virtue.”

3. **Considerations Concerning the First Formation of Languages, and the Different Genius of Original and Compounded Languages** (first published 1761 in *The Philological Miscellany*; later appended to editions of *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*).  
   Genuine authorship.

4. **An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations** (London, 9 March 1776).  
   Lifetime editions:
   - 1st: 1776;
   - 2nd: 1778;
   - 3rd: 1784, significantly revised and expanded;
   - 4th: 1786;
   - 5th: 1789.

5. **Letter from Adam Smith, LL.D. to William Strahan, Esq.** (1777).  
   Account of David Hume’s last illness and character, published with Hume’s autobiographical *My Own Life*. Genuine authorship and a major contemporary religious controversy.

6. **Preface and editorial involvement in William Hamilton, *Poems on Several Occasions*** (1748 edition involvement is attributed in the scholarly record).  
   Smith’s precise editorial contribution requires qualification; it was not an independent authored book.

### Posthumously published manuscripts authorized to survive

7. **Essays on Philosophical Subjects** (1795), edited by Joseph Black and James Hutton. Contents include:

   - “The Principles Which Lead and Direct Philosophical Enquiries; Illustrated by the History of Astronomy”;
   - “The Principles Which Lead and Direct Philosophical Enquiries; Illustrated by the History of the Ancient Physics”;
   - “The Principles Which Lead and Direct Philosophical Enquiries; Illustrated by the History of the Ancient Logics and Metaphysics”;
   - “Of the Nature of that Imitation which takes place in what are called the Imitative Arts”;
   - “Of the Affinity between Music, Dancing, and Poetry”;
   - “Of the Affinity between certain English and Italian Verses”;
   - “Of the External Senses.”

These are genuine manuscripts, but the 1795 volume was edited and published after Smith’s death.

### Student notes and reconstructed lectures

8. **Lectures on Justice, Police, Revenue and Arms** (1896), edited by Edwin Cannan from student notes discovered in the nineteenth century.  
   Not a manuscript written for publication by Smith. It records a student’s version of Glasgow lectures.

9. **Lectures on Jurisprudence** (Glasgow Edition, 1978/1980 publication history).  
   Incorporates two bodies of student notes, conventionally identified as reports from 1762–1763 and 1766, plus an early draft related to *The Wealth of Nations*. Authentically derived from Smith’s teaching but not verbatim authorial transcripts.

10. **Lectures on Rhetoric and Belles Lettres** (Glasgow Edition, 1983).  
    Student notes from the 1762–1763 Glasgow course. Derived from Smith’s lectures; not an author-prepared text.

### Correspondence and administrative writing

11. **Correspondence of Adam Smith**, edited by Ernest Campbell Mossner and Ian Simpson Ross, Glasgow Edition, revised 1987.  
    Contains surviving letters by and to Smith and associated documents. This is an edited scholarly collection, not a book Smith prepared.

12. **Customs memoranda, official board documents, university letters, recommendations, and petitions.**  
    Scattered through archives and scholarly editions. Some are co-signed administrative instruments rather than individually composed essays.

### Lost or destroyed projects

Smith publicly or privately anticipated works concerning:

- a general theory and history of law and government;
- literature and rhetoric;
- the sciences and arts;
- further philosophical history.

Most associated manuscripts were destroyed at his direction. Their completeness and exact titles cannot be established.

### Standard collected edition

The **Glasgow Edition of the Works and Correspondence of Adam Smith** comprises:

- *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*;
- *An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations*;
- *Essays on Philosophical Subjects*;
- *Lectures on Rhetoric and Belles Lettres*;
- *Lectures on Jurisprudence*;
- *Correspondence of Adam Smith*;
- an index volume.

### Critical and revisionist lines of study

Important scholarship treats:

- Smith’s relation to slavery and slave-produced commerce;
- the Eurocentrism of four-stage theory;
- gender and household labor;
- empire and colonial independence;
- inequality and admiration of wealth;
- the “Adam Smith problem”;

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Immediate reception

*The Theory of Moral Sentiments* made Smith famous before *The Wealth of Nations*. The latter sold well, circulated across Europe, and entered British policy debate, although claims of instantaneous transformation are exaggerated.

William Pitt the Younger praised Smith publicly in Parliament after Smith’s death. French, German, Italian, Spanish, Russian, Japanese, and Chinese translations and adaptations produced distinct national “Smiths.”

### Classical political economy

David Ricardo, Thomas Malthus, Jean-Baptiste Say, and others accepted, rejected, or reformulated Smith’s categories. Smith became a canonical predecessor even where later theorists abandoned his explanations.

### Karl Marx

Marx treated Smith as a major scientific representative of classical political economy. He drew upon Smith’s analysis of labor, division of labor, accumulation, class revenues, and productive labor while criticizing his inconsistencies and limits.

This is a documented textual influence and critical engagement, not a personal connection.

### Twentieth-century free-market symbolism

Friedrich Hayek, Milton Friedman, George Stigler, and market-oriented institutions elevated Smith as a predecessor of spontaneous-order and free-market arguments. Their readings emphasized decentralized knowledge, exchange, and skepticism toward state direction.

Revisionist scholarship later stressed that Smith’s natural liberty operated within justice, education, taxation, public works, and controls on monopoly and financial risk.

### Social-democratic and egalitarian readings

Other readers emphasize Smith’s concern with worker degradation, unequal bargaining power, vanity, poverty, education, and the political influence of merchants. These readings do not erase his defense of commercial society; they contest the claim that he sanctioned unrestricted corporate power.

### Material commemoration

Smith appears on monuments, university buildings, banknotes, commemorative medals, and institutional names. The Bank of England’s 2007 paper £20 note used an image based on the Tassie medallion, making him the first Scot featured on a Bank of England note.

His Kirkcaldy birthplace, Glasgow university career, Panmure House, and Canongate grave form the principal geography of public commemoration.

### Continuing disputes

The principal unresolved interpretive questions concern:

- the unity of his moral and economic writings;
- the depth of Hume’s influence;
- his religious beliefs;
- the place of slavery and empire in his commercial analysis;
- whether “capitalism” is a useful retrospective category;
- whether later free-market traditions accurately preserve or selectively reconstruct his arguments.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted record are included. Shared chronology or broad intellectual similarity alone is excluded.

### Aristotle — documented intellectual influence

Smith studied and discussed ancient ethics, rhetoric, logic, politics, and theories of imitation. His accounts of virtue, propriety, rhetoric, and the imitative arts engage Aristotelian traditions, though he did not simply reproduce Aristotle’s system. No personal connection was possible.

### Benjamin Franklin — documented or strongly reported personal acquaintance

Smith and Franklin moved in overlapping London circles by approximately 1774. They discussed or had opportunity to discuss American colonial affairs, wages, population, and commerce. Later claims that Franklin read and corrected the whole of *The Wealth of Nations* are not documented by surviving correspondence.

### Jean-Jacques Rousseau — documented textual engagement; personal meeting unresolved

Smith reviewed and translated passages from Rousseau’s *Discourse on Inequality* in 1756. Rousseau’s diagnosis of inequality, dependence, vanity, and commercial corruption formed an important background to Smith’s moral and social thought. They probably never met; a Paris encounter in late 1765 or early 1766 remains possible but unsupported.

### John Locke — documented intellectual inheritance

Smith worked within natural-law and political-economic traditions shaped partly by Locke’s writings on property, government, money, education, and toleration. The connection is textual and mediated through eighteenth-century teaching, not personal.

### Sir Isaac Newton — documented methodological influence

Smith treated Newtonian astronomy as a leading example of philosophical explanation. His “History of Astronomy” analyzes how systems calm surprise and connect phenomena. Claims that Smith directly converted Newtonian mechanics into economic laws should be treated as interpretive analogy rather than literal methodological identity.

### Immanuel Kant — documented reception, no personal connection

Kant and Smith did not meet or correspond. German Enlightenment and later philosophical discussion placed Smith’s moral-sentiment theory in dialogue with Kantian ethics. Direct influence on particular Kantian doctrines is debated and mediated through translations and reviews.

### Thomas Jefferson — documented posthumous reception, no meeting

Jefferson wrote on 30 May 1790 that *The Wealth of Nations* was the best existing book on political economy and repeated this recommendation in later correspondence. No evidence establishes a personal meeting with Smith.

### Alexander Hamilton — reported intellectual reception, no personal meeting documented

Hamilton and American associates obtained or discussed *The Wealth of Nations*. Smithian ideas formed part of the wider political-economic literature available to American constitutional and fiscal debate. Precise borrowing must be demonstrated argument by argument; no correspondence or meeting with Smith is known.

### George Washington — indirect institutional and reception connection only

Smith analyzed the American conflict during Washington’s military leadership, and *The Wealth of Nations* circulated among American political elites. No direct meeting, correspondence, or personally acknowledged influence was established in the sources consulted.

### Karl Marx — documented critical intellectual influence

Marx read Smith extensively and treated him as one of the central classical political economists. He adopted and transformed Smithian problems involving labor, value, accumulation, class revenues, productive labor, and division of labor while criticizing Smith’s contradictions. The relationship is entirely posthumous and textual.

### Carl Menger — documented disciplinary succession and criticism

Menger’s marginalist theory of value displaced central elements of the Smithian-classical value tradition. This is a documented history-of-economic-thought relationship, not a personal connection.

### Eugen Böhm von Bawerk — documented critical reception

Böhm-Bawerk engaged the classical tradition descending from Smith, especially theories of capital, value, and interest. The relationship is posthumous and analytical.

### Ludwig von Mises — documented posthumous reception

Mises placed Smith within the liberal and market tradition while developing a substantially different theory of value, action, and money. No personal connection was possible.

### Friedrich August von Hayek — documented posthumous appropriation and influence

Hayek treated Smith as an important predecessor of spontaneous-order analysis and criticism of constructivist rationalism. His interpretation helped shape twentieth-century free-market reception.

### Milton Friedman — documented posthumous appropriation

Friedman repeatedly praised Smith’s continuing relevance and used him as a symbol of voluntary exchange and limited government. This reception sometimes narrows the institutional and moral range of Smith’s work.

### John Maynard Keynes — documented disciplinary relationship, limited direct allegiance

Keynes worked within a discipline for which Smith was canonical but did not treat him as a close theoretical master. Their connection is historical and disciplinary, not personal.

### Joseph Alois Schumpeter — documented critical historiography

Schumpeter assessed Smith critically in his history of economic analysis, questioning claims to absolute originality while recognizing his canonical importance.

### Max Weber — documented comparative intellectual history

Weber’s analysis of capitalism, rationalization, and religion addressed questions overlapping the commercial-society tradition in which Smith was foundational. A specific personal or direct acknowledged influence was not established; the connection should be treated as disciplinary comparison rather than a direct bond.

### Francis Bacon — documented place in Smith’s history of knowledge

Smith’s philosophical histories and lectures inherited the early modern project of organizing knowledge associated with Bacon. This is a textual-historical connection, not a personal one.

### David Hume

Hume would be the single most important Atlas-style connection, but **David Hume is not present in the supplied atlas roster**. He is nevertheless documented throughout the dossier because excluding him would distort Smith’s life.

### Absence as a finding

No supported personal connection was found between Smith and the overwhelming majority of the 896-name roster. In particular, mere later use of Smith by liberals, socialists, financiers, politicians, technologists, or conspiracy writers was not counted without a traceable textual acknowledgment or institutional relationship.
