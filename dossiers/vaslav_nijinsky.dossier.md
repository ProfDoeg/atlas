# Vaslav Nijinsky: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

This dossier reconstructs the life of the dancer and choreographer generally known as Vaslav Nijinsky. It distinguishes:

- **Documented fact:** supported by archival records, contemporary programs, manuscripts, medical records, or convergent first-hand evidence.
- **Reported fact:** stated in reputable scholarship or memoirs but not independently verified here from the original document.
- **Disputed claim:** credible sources give materially different versions.
- **Allegation:** an accusation or assertion lacking conclusive corroboration.
- **Rumor or myth:** a culturally influential story whose literal accuracy is doubtful or unestablished.
- **Later interpretation:** a retrospective psychological, sexual, political, or artistic reading rather than a contemporary finding.

Nijinsky’s life is unusually dependent on interested witnesses. His wife Romola, sister Bronislava Nijinska, employer and former lover Sergei Diaghilev, colleagues, doctors, critics, and later choreographic reconstructors all preserved essential evidence while also advancing conflicting interpretations.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

**Principal name:** Vaslav Nijinsky.

**Birth name:** Polish: **Wacław Niżyński**. Russian forms include **Вацлав Фомич Нижинский** and **Vatslav Fomich Nizhinsky**. “Fomich” is the Russian patronymic derived from Tomasz/Thomas.

**Alternative spellings:** Waslaw Nijinsky, Vaclav Nijinsky, Vatslav Nijinsky, Vaslav Nijinski, Vaclav Nižinskij, Nijinski, Nizhinsky, Niżyński.

**Family diminutives:** “Vatsa” or “Vaca” appears in family memoir literature.

**Honorific or publicity names:** “God of the Dance,” “le dieu de la danse,” and variants were journalistic and promotional epithets, not official titles.

**Nationality and identity:** Born in Kyiv in the Russian Empire to Polish Roman Catholic theatrical parents. He was administratively a Russian imperial subject, culturally attached to Polish family identity, trained in the Russian imperial ballet system, and internationally identified with the Ballets Russes. Later national labels—Russian, Polish, Ukrainian-born, or combinations of these—describe different dimensions of the same history.

**Occupation:** Ballet dancer, choreographer, occasional ballet director, choreographic theorist and dance-notation inventor, diarist, and visual artist.

**Birth:** The traditional date is **12 March 1890**, corresponding to **28 February 1890 Old Style**. Some sources and scholarship give **1889**, including 17 December 1889, and the surviving documentation has not resolved the discrepancy. The 1889–1890 uncertainty should be preserved rather than concealed.

**Birthplace:** Kyiv, Russian Empire, now Kyiv, Ukraine.

**Baptism:** Roman Catholic; reported as having taken place in Warsaw.

**Death:** **8 April 1950**, London, England.

**Recorded cause of death:** Uremia associated with chronic nephritis; medical summaries also mention hypertension and arteriosclerosis.

**Burial:** Initially St Marylebone Cemetery, East Finchley, London. His remains were reinterred in Montmartre Cemetery, Paris, in 1953, near the grave of dancer Gaétan Vestris.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

Nijinsky’s parents were Polish dancers working in touring theatrical companies:

- **Tomasz Niżyński**, also called Thomas or Foma Nijinsky, born in 1862. He was a dancer, ballet master, choreographer, and proprietor or leader of touring dance ensembles.
- **Eleonora Bereda**, also rendered Eleanora or Eleanor, born in 1856. She was a dancer and later the principal practical support of the children.

The family toured through cities of the Russian Empire. Vaslav’s childhood was therefore spent less in a settled Polish household than within the mobile economy of provincial theaters, hotels, rehearsals, and touring companies.

Tomasz and Eleonora separated during Vaslav’s childhood. According to the family histories used by Joan Acocella and other biographers, Tomasz left the household after becoming involved with another woman, who was pregnant. Eleonora subsequently moved with the children to St Petersburg.

### Siblings

- **Stanislav Niżyński**, called Stassik or Stasik, the elder brother. Family accounts say he suffered neurological or psychiatric impairment after falling from a window as a child. He later spent time in institutions. The episode became central to retrospective arguments for hereditary mental illness in the family, although the nature of Stanislav’s condition was never established to modern diagnostic standards.
- **Bronislava Nijinska** (1891–1972), younger sister, dancer, teacher, and major modernist choreographer. She trained at the Imperial Theatre School, danced with the Ballets Russes, assisted Vaslav with rehearsals and choreographic development, and left Diaghilev’s company in solidarity with him in 1914. Her *Early Memoirs* are among the most important first-hand sources for the family and Vaslav’s formation.

### Marriage and children

Nijinsky married **Romola de Pulszky** in Buenos Aires on **10 September 1913**. Romola was the daughter of Hungarian National Gallery director Károly Pulszky and celebrated actress Emilia Márkus. She had joined or attached herself to the Ballets Russes milieu after becoming fascinated with Nijinsky.

They had two daughters:

- **Kyra Nijinsky**, born in Vienna on 19 June 1914 according to most biographical sources, though one genealogical compilation gives 18 June. She became a dancer. She later married composer and conductor Igor Markevitch; they had a son, Vaslav Markevitch.
- **Tamara Nijinsky**, born 14 June 1920. She became a custodian of her parents’ papers and an executive of the Vaslav and Romola Nijinsky Foundation.

A persistent rumor held that Tamara was the child of a physician with whom Romola allegedly had an affair. Romola herself invoked the claim during a later inheritance dispute. **Evidentiary status: unproved allegation.** Nijinsky was at home on leave from Bellevue during the period in which Tamara could have been conceived, and no genetic or documentary proof establishing another father is known.

## Childhood and Early Life

### Touring childhood, approximately 1890–1897

Vaslav grew up among dancers. His parents trained the children informally, and he appeared in childhood performances. The itinerant environment exposed him to Polish popular dances, Russian theatrical ballet, mime, circus-like athletic performance, and the discipline of touring companies.

Family memoirs describe him as physically gifted, withdrawn, slow or reluctant in ordinary verbal communication, and intensely responsive to movement. Some teachers and relatives considered him intellectually limited because of his quietness and academic difficulties. Later interpretations have variously called these traits shyness, dyslexia, developmental difference, autism, prodromal mental illness, or simply the result of an unstable childhood. None can be established retrospectively.

After the parental separation, Eleonora took the children to St Petersburg. A family friend and Imperial School teacher, Stanisław Gillert, reportedly helped secure consideration for Vaslav’s admission.

---

## Education and Formation

### Imperial Theatre School

Nijinsky entered the ballet division of the Imperial Theatre School in St Petersburg in **1898**. Some institutional summaries give 1900, but family and major biographical chronologies generally favor 1898.

His teachers included:

- **Sergei Legat** and **Nikolai Legat**, leading exponents of the Imperial Ballet tradition.
- **Enrico Cecchetti**, the Italian virtuoso and pedagogue, from whom Nijinsky received advanced coaching.
- **Mikhail Fokine**, whose reforming choreography gave the young dancer some of his earliest important opportunities.
- Teachers in mime, music, academic studies, and the Stepanov system of dance notation.

Nijinsky’s general academic performance was weak. He was reportedly mocked by classmates for his Polish background, awkwardness, and limited conversational fluency. In dance he rapidly became exceptional, especially in elevation, beats, turns, balance, and the ability to transform posture and facial expression into character.

### Formative performances

In 1905 Fokine cast the schoolboy Nijinsky as the faun in the student ballet *Acis and Galatea*. Contemporary notices recognized his unusual promise.

Before graduation he appeared in Imperial productions and partnered established ballerinas. He graduated in **1907** and entered the Imperial Ballet as a **coryphée**, reportedly bypassing the ordinary corps rank.

### Artistic influences

His formation combined:

- The classical Petipa–Imperial repertory.
- Cecchetti’s technical discipline.
- Fokine’s belief that each ballet should possess an integrated movement language.
- Russian Symbolist theater and the visual art associated with the *Mir iskusstva* circle.
- Léon Bakst’s flattened, decorative, and archaizing visual designs.
- Greek, Egyptian, and archaic visual profiles, particularly important to *L’Après-midi d’un Faune*.
- Émile Jaques-Dalcroze’s eurhythmics, mediated especially through Marie Rambert during rehearsals for *Le Sacre du printemps*.
- Leo Tolstoy’s religious and ethical writings, which became increasingly important during World War I and dominate the 1919 diary.
- His own experiments with Stepanov-derived movement notation.

## Early Career: Imperial Ballet, 1907–1911

Nijinsky danced at the Mariinsky Theatre and other Imperial venues in ballets including *La Source*, *Don Quixote*, *Giselle*, *The Sleeping Beauty*, and Fokine works such as *Le Pavillon d’Armide*, *Chopiniana*, and *Egyptian Nights*.

Prominent partners included:

- **Anna Pavlova**
- **Tamara Karsavina**
- **Mathilde Kschessinska**
- **Olga Preobrajenska**

His short, muscular physique diverged from the long-line ideal later associated with male ballet stars. Photographs cannot show the timing, suspension, phrasing, and dramatic transformation for which contemporaries praised him. No authenticated motion-picture footage of him dancing is known.

### Prince Pavel Lvov

The unexpurgated diary and subsequent research identify **Prince Pavel Lvov** as an early lover and patron. Lvov provided material assistance and entrée into elite circles. Nijinsky’s own account presents the relationship as emotionally significant but embedded in an economy of patronage and sexual access.

**Documented through Nijinsky’s diary and biographical research:** Nijinsky had sexual relations with Lvov and with other men in this patronage milieu.

**Interpretive question:** Whether modern categories such as gay or bisexual adequately describe him remains unresolved. His diary records relationships with men, sexual desire for women, visits to prostitutes, masturbation, marriage, and strong guilt shaped by religious thought. Claims that he was exclusively heterosexual and merely coerced, or exclusively homosexual and “converted” by Romola, exceed the evidence.

### Sergei Diaghilev

Nijinsky met impresario **Sergei Pavlovich Diaghilev** by 1908–1909. They became lovers as well as employer and star performer. The relationship was professionally productive and structurally unequal: Diaghilev controlled contracts, repertory, travel, money, artistic access, and social networks.

Later accounts range from romantic partnership to exploitation. Nijinsky’s diary indicates both attachment and resentment. Material and career benefits accompanied the sexual relationship. No court ever adjudicated whether the relationship was coercive, and the evidence does not permit a simple legal characterization. The disparity in age, money, and authority is documented.

## Ballets Russes: International Stardom, 1909–1913

### Paris, 1909

Nijinsky and Bronislava joined Diaghilev’s first ballet season in Paris. The 1909 program included *Le Pavillon d’Armide*, *Les Sylphides*, *Cléopâtre*, *Le Festin*, and the Polovtsian dances from *Prince Igor*. Nijinsky’s elevation, dramatic intensity, and exoticized roles produced immediate celebrity.

### Seasons of 1910–1911

Important roles included:

- The Golden Slave in *Schéhérazade* (1910), with Ida Rubinstein.
- Albrecht in *Giselle*.
- The poet or youth in *Les Sylphides*.
- Harlequin in *Carnaval*.
- The title role in *Petrushka* (1911).
- The Rose in *Le Spectre de la rose* (1911), partnered by Tamara Karsavina.
- Roles in *Narcisse*, *Le Dieu bleu*, *Daphnis et Chloé*, and other Fokine ballets.

The roles emphasized sexual ambiguity, animality, tragic puppetry, and masculine virtuosity. Photographs by E. O. Hoppé and Baron Adolf de Meyer, and designs by Bakst, helped create an image as influential as the performances themselves.

### Dismissal from the Imperial Ballet

On 23 January 1911 Nijinsky danced Albrecht in *Giselle* in a costume designed without the customary modesty trunks or court-approved covering. Members of the imperial family were present. He was dismissed from the Imperial Theatre.

**Competing versions:**

- **Documented core:** The costume caused official offense and dismissal followed.
- **Diaghilev/Romola-centered version:** Diaghilev encouraged the breach to sever Nijinsky’s Russian employment and secure him exclusively.
- **Alternative version:** Nijinsky knowingly accepted an artistically appropriate costume and the bureaucracy reacted independently.
- **Unresolved:** The exact degree of Diaghilev’s orchestration. No conclusive documentary proof establishes a deliberate plot.

The dismissal made Nijinsky economically more dependent on Diaghilev’s newly permanent company.

## Nijinsky as Choreographer

Nijinsky created four completed ballets generally accepted as his original choreography.

### *L’Après-midi d’un Faune* — 1912

Music: Claude Debussy’s *Prélude à l’après-midi d’un faune*.  
Design: Léon Bakst.  
Premiere: Théâtre du Châtelet, Paris, 29 May 1912.

The ballet replaced rounded academic movement with side-on poses, parallel or turned-in legs, flattened procession, and gestures recalling Greek vase painting and archaic relief.

At the ending, the faun lowers himself onto a scarf left by a nymph. Critics interpreted the movement as masturbation or orgasmic consummation. Gaston Calmette attacked the work in *Le Figaro*; Auguste Rodin publicly defended Nijinsky’s art.

**Documented fact:** The ending provoked a major press scandal.

**Disputed detail:** Accounts differ over whether Nijinsky temporarily softened the gesture and then restored it, and over the number of rehearsals—estimates include approximately 60, 90, and 120.

Ida Rubinstein initially rehearsed the principal nymph but withdrew, reportedly saying the choreography would turn her into a dislocated marionette.

Nijinsky later recorded much of *Faune* in his own notation system. Ann Hutchinson Guest and Claudia Jeschke decoded the score, exposing differences between Nijinsky’s written choreography and versions transmitted through performance memory.

### *Jeux* — 1913

Music: Claude Debussy.  
Design: Léon Bakst.  
Premiere: Théâtre des Champs-Élysées, Paris, 15 May 1913.

The scenario involved a young man and two women searching for a tennis ball and entering an unstable erotic triangle. Its modern setting, fragmented gestures, flexed bodies, and refusal of conventional climax confused many critics. Debussy’s later comments about Nijinsky were often hostile, although the composer had worked directly on the production.

No complete authoritative movement score survives. Modern productions described as reconstructions depend on rehearsal evidence, drawings, photographs, musical annotations, and informed conjecture.

### *Le Sacre du printemps* — 1913

Music: Igor Stravinsky.  
Scenario and designs: Nicholas Roerich.  
Choreography: Nijinsky.  
Premiere: Théâtre des Champs-Élysées, Paris, 29 May 1913.

Nijinsky organized dancers into stamping, pigeon-toed, heavy, collectively patterned bodies rather than ethereal classical figures. Marie Rambert assisted with the difficult rhythms.

The premiere generated loud argument, laughter, shouting, and physical confrontations. Nijinsky reportedly called counts from the wings because dancers could not hear the orchestra; Diaghilev ordered the house lights flashed in an attempt to control the room.

**Documented fact:** The performance was seriously disrupted.

**Myth qualification:** The familiar story of a single spontaneous “riot caused by Stravinsky’s music” is an oversimplification. Contemporary testimony conflicts; audience factions, advance publicity, choreography, social antagonism, and musical novelty all contributed. Later retellings enlarged the event, and the word “riot” became part of modernist origin mythology.

Nijinsky’s choreography soon disappeared from repertory, while Stravinsky’s score achieved independent canonical status. Millicent Hodson and Kenneth Archer created a widely performed 1987 reconstruction after extensive archival work. It is a scholarly reconstruction, not an exact recovered film or complete original score.

### *Till Eulenspiegel* — 1916

Music: Richard Strauss.  
Libretto and choreography: Nijinsky, after Charles de Coster.  
Design: Robert Edmond Jones.  
Premiere: Manhattan Opera House, New York, 23 October 1916.

Nijinsky made the ballet under difficult touring conditions, with limited rehearsal time and conflict over company administration. It was his final completed choreography. Later reconstructions again depend on incomplete documentation.

### Unfinished projects and notation

Nijinsky considered additional ballets, including projects associated with Bach, the Bible, war, and religious subjects. He sought a notation system that could preserve movement as musical notation preserves sound. Manuscripts from 1915–1918 show multiple stages of this project.

He did not patent the system. It did not become a commercial enterprise, but the surviving *Faune* score later allowed a closer reconstruction of his choreographic language.

## Marriage, Rupture with Diaghilev, and Independent Failure, 1913–1914

Diaghilev did not accompany the 1913 South American voyage, reportedly because of severe fear of sea travel. Romola de Pulszky pursued contact with Nijinsky aboard the ship despite their lack of a fluent common language. They became engaged and married in Buenos Aires on 10 September 1913.

Nijinsky notified Diaghilev. Diaghilev dismissed him by telegram.

**Documented fact:** Marriage was followed immediately by professional dismissal.

**Interpretive dispute:**

- Romola portrayed the marriage as love and liberation.
- Diaghilev’s circle portrayed her as an obsessive fan who captured a naïve man.
- Some later accounts describe Diaghilev’s act as jealous revenge.
- Contractual explanations cite Nijinsky’s failure to notify management and the practical problems of retaining him.
- The emotional and professional motives were probably inseparable; no neutral adjudication exists.

Nijinsky attempted to form and manage his own troupe for a London season in 1914. It was artistically and financially unsuccessful. He lacked administrative experience, dependable capital, and sufficient repertory. Bronislava helped him; disputes emerged over contracts, rehearsal practices, and payment. The failure damaged his finances and demonstrated how much infrastructure Diaghilev had previously supplied.

Kyra was born in Vienna in June 1914. The assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand followed days later, and Europe entered war.

---

## World War I: Budapest Internment and Return to the Ballets Russes

As a Russian imperial subject in Austria-Hungary, Nijinsky was treated as an enemy alien. He, Romola, and Kyra remained under house arrest or restricted residence in Budapest, largely in the household of Romola’s mother Emilia Márkus.

He worked on choreography and dance notation with assistance from Romola’s cousin, pianist Lily de Markus. He read Tolstoy and developed increasingly pacifist and religious ideas.

Negotiations for his release involved Diaghilev, American presenters, diplomats, and eminent intermediaries. Later lists include King Alfonso XIII, Queen Alexandra, Dowager Empress Maria Feodorovna, Emperor Franz Joseph, Pope Benedict XV, and President Woodrow Wilson. The precise effectiveness of each intervention is difficult to separate from publicity.

Nijinsky was released in 1916 to join the Ballets Russes in the United States. Relations with Diaghilev remained damaged. Nijinsky was expected to dance, choreograph, rehearse, and at times direct touring operations.

### North American tour, 1916–1917

The company toured numerous American cities. Nijinsky supervised a second tour beginning in autumn 1916. Administrative confusion, exhaustion, artistic disagreements, delayed payments, and his increasing suspiciousness complicated the enterprise.

*Till Eulenspiegel* premiered in New York in October 1916. Contemporary responses were mixed.

### Final South American tour, 1917

Nijinsky rejoined the company for South America. He increasingly demanded payment in cash before appearing, reflecting both real contractual mistrust and possible emerging paranoia.

His last generally accepted public performance occurred in **Montevideo, Uruguay, at the end of September 1917**, usually dated 30 September. The Library of Congress chronology identifies it as his final public appearance.

---

## Switzerland and Final Performance, 1917–1919

Nijinsky returned to his family and settled at St Moritz, Switzerland, in December 1917. Europe remained at war. He was unemployed, professionally isolated, financially dependent, and increasingly absorbed in religion, vegetarianism, sexuality, guilt, war, and a sense of divine mission.

On **19 January 1919**, he gave a private performance in the ballroom of the Suvretta House hotel. It is conventionally called his last dance.

Witness narratives say he sat staring at the audience for an extended period, arranged fabric into a cross, spoke of war and suffering, and performed violent improvisations. He called the occasion his “wedding with God” or an equivalent phrase.

**Documented core:** A strange and disturbing performance occurred before invited spectators.

**Mythic development:** Later dance works present it as the exact instant at which genius became madness. Psychiatric deterioration had already been developing, and the event cannot medically date the onset of illness.

---

## The Diary and Psychiatric Crisis

Between January and early March 1919, Nijinsky filled notebooks in Russian. The writing combines:

- Daily observations.
- Religious declarations and identification with God.
- Pacifism and horror at war.
- Tolstoyan ethics, compassion for animals, vegetarianism, and criticism of wealth.
- Sexual memories and compulsions.
- Accounts of Diaghilev, Lvov, Romola, doctors, and family.
- Grandiose and persecutory ideas.
- Poems, letters, drawings, and plans.
- Acute awareness that others believed him insane.

The diary is simultaneously a literary work, historical testimony, and clinical document. Treating every sentence as either revelation or “mere madness” distorts it.

### Diagnosis

Nijinsky was examined in Zurich by psychiatrist **Eugen Bleuler**, associated with the development of the schizophrenia concept. He entered the Bellevue Sanatorium at Kreuzlingen under Ludwig Binswanger’s family institution and was described initially as suffering from catatonia; schizophrenia became the enduring diagnosis.

A modern review in *Brain* found the historical diagnosis broadly consistent with recorded grandiose and persecutory delusions, hallucination-like experiences, disorganization, withdrawal, and catatonic symptoms. However:

- No living clinician can examine him.
- “Schizophrenia” in 1919 did not map perfectly onto current diagnostic criteria.
- Retrospective proposals include bipolar disorder, schizoaffective disorder, trauma-related illness, autism spectrum condition, and organic disease.
- These alternatives remain later interpretations, not established replacements for the contemporary diagnosis.

---

## Institutionalization, Treatments, and Family Life, 1919–1945

Nijinsky spent much of the next three decades in sanatoria, hospitals, nursing facilities, or family care in Switzerland, France, Austria, and Hungary. He experienced periods of severe withdrawal, mutism, agitation, paranoia, and limited remission.

Institutions and physicians associated with his treatment include:

- Burghölzli psychiatric hospital, Zurich.
- Bellevue Sanatorium, Kreuzlingen.
- Ludwig and Kurt Binswanger’s clinical circle.
- Viennese and Central European psychiatric facilities.
- **Manfred Sakel**, associated with insulin-coma treatment.

Treatments available in this period included confinement, hydrotherapy, sedatives, occupational routines, and later insulin-shock therapy. Some caused substantial physical distress and carried serious risk. Reports that insulin treatment temporarily restored movement or communication should not be equated with recovery.

Romola alternated between placing him in institutions, bringing him home, pursuing new doctors, managing money and publicity, and preserving or controlling access to his papers. Descriptions of her conduct range from heroic caregiving to exploitation and manipulation. Both elements can coexist: she sustained his material care under extreme conditions and also constructed a commercial and mythic public narrative around him.

Their second daughter, Tamara, was born in 1920.

### Meeting with Diaghilev in later illness

Romola arranged meetings between Nijinsky and former colleagues, hoping to stimulate recognition. Diaghilev reportedly visited him before Diaghilev’s death in 1929. Accounts emphasize Nijinsky’s limited or absent response. These encounters became emotionally charged scenes in later biographies and films; exact dialogue is not reliably documented.

### Nazi-era danger

During World War II the family was in Hungary and Austria amid German occupation, the Arrow Cross regime, Soviet advance, and the Nazi murder of psychiatric patients.

A modern medical-historical account reports that in May 1945 orders threatened psychiatric patients with extermination and that Nijinsky hid in nearby caves. The episode is plausible within the documented Nazi killing of disabled patients, but details rely substantially on family and later clinical narratives rather than a located order naming Nijinsky personally.

After the Red Army arrived, Russian soldiers reportedly recognized him. Witnesses said he became temporarily sociable and danced or whirled with them.

**Reported fact:** A transient improvement and movement in the presence of Soviet soldiers was recorded by family or attendants.

**Legendary elaboration:** The story is often told as the miraculous return of the “God of Dance.” There is no film and no evidence of a sustained clinical recovery.

---

## Final Years and Death

After the war, Nijinsky and Romola moved through Austria. Physicians identified hypertension and renal or vascular disease. They settled in England in 1948.

He apparently required no prolonged British psychiatric admission. His physical health deteriorated, and he died in London on 8 April 1950 from uremia with chronic nephritis. He was approximately sixty, depending on the unresolved birth year.

He was initially buried in London. In 1953 his remains were transferred to Montmartre Cemetery in Paris. The Paris grave became a place of dance-world commemoration, often decorated with ballet shoes.

## Employment, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Principal institutions

- Touring companies led by or employing his parents.
- Imperial Theatre School, St Petersburg.
- Imperial Ballet/Mariinsky Theatre.
- Sergei Diaghilev’s Ballets Russes.
- Nijinsky’s short-lived independent company or London season.
- European and American theatrical presenters during the 1916–1917 tours.
- Bellevue and other psychiatric institutions.
- Posthumously, the Vaslav and Romola Nijinsky Foundation.

### Finances

Nijinsky became highly paid by ballet standards but did not create durable independent wealth. His finances were inseparable from patrons, Diaghilev’s contracts, touring receipts, and Romola’s management.

Relevant episodes include:

- Prince Lvov’s material patronage.
- Economic dependence on Diaghilev.
- Loss of Imperial employment in 1911.
- The costly failure of the 1914 independent venture.
- Wartime inability to earn.
- Disputes and insistence upon cash payment during the 1917 tour.
- Long-term medical and institutional costs.
- Romola’s income from lectures, biographies, diary publication, permissions, and the Nijinsky name.
- Sale of the unexpurgated diary manuscripts at Sotheby’s in 1979 for a reported $104,400.

No evidence located in the dedicated adversarial search indicates fraud, hidden corporations, money laundering, patents, corrupt political financing, or criminal conviction by Nijinsky.

## Political, Religious, Philosophical, and Artistic Development

Nijinsky was not a party politician. His diary contains a personal moral politics centered on:

- Opposition to war and killing.
- Sympathy for poor people, workers, animals, and social outsiders.
- Suspicion of governments, elites, doctors, critics, and commercial art.
- Hostility to accumulated wealth.
- Tolstoyan Christian ethics.
- Vegetarianism.
- Identification of love, feeling, and God.
- Rejection of purely intellectual or mechanistic accounts of life.

His religious language became inseparable from psychosis: compassion and ethical criticism appear beside declarations that he was God or acted directly under divine command. The political and theological content should therefore neither be dismissed wholesale nor accepted as a systematic doctrine.

Artistically, he moved from virtuoso embodiment of Fokine’s reforms to a choreography that challenged turnout, verticality, seamless line, gender coding, narrative climax, and the separation between dance and visual composition.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Sergei Diaghilev

Lover, patron, employer, impresario, collaborator, and eventual antagonist. Their relationship enabled Nijinsky’s international career and left him vulnerable to dismissal when the personal bond ruptured.

### Bronislava Nijinska

Sister, fellow dancer, rehearsal assistant, confidante, and later major choreographer. She helped embody and transmit his difficult movement ideas. Her memoirs are indispensable but terminate before much of his later illness.

### Romola de Pulszky Nijinsky

Admirer, wife, mother of his children, manager, caregiver, biographer, editor, and controller of his papers. She preserved Nijinsky’s legacy while censoring and rearranging his diary in its first published form.

### Mikhail Fokine

Teacher and choreographer of most roles that established Nijinsky’s dancing reputation. Nijinsky’s promotion as choreographer contributed to Fokine’s alienation from Diaghilev’s company, making them artistic successors and rivals.

### Tamara Karsavina

Frequent partner, notably in *Le Spectre de la rose* and *Petrushka*. Her memoirs preserve impressions of his stage presence and rehearsal behavior.

### Igor Stravinsky

Collaborator on *Le Sacre du printemps*. Their working relationship was strained by rhythmic difficulty and later arguments over credit, competence, and the premiere. Stravinsky’s later accounts changed over time and helped center the music rather than Nijinsky’s lost choreography.

### Claude Debussy

Composer of the music used for *Faune* and commissioned for *Jeux*. Debussy expressed reservations about Nijinsky’s choreography and later caricatured his working method, but the collaborations produced two core modernist works.

### Léon Bakst

Designer and visual collaborator on *Faune*, *Jeux*, and several roles danced by Nijinsky. Bakst’s costumes and stage images were central to Nijinsky’s public persona.

### Nicholas Roerich

Scenario and visual-design collaborator on *Le Sacre du printemps*. The archaic Russian setting and ritual conception were shared components, although later histories dispute the balance of creative credit among Roerich, Stravinsky, Nijinsky, and Diaghilev.

### Marie Rambert

Rehearsal assistant for *Le Sacre*, recruited partly because of her Dalcroze rhythmic training. Her testimony became important to later reconstruction.

### Auguste Rodin

Artist and public defender after the *Faune* scandal. Rodin praised Nijinsky’s embodiment of antique beauty and made studies associated with him.

### Enrico Cecchetti and the Legat brothers

Major technical teachers. Cecchetti also worked in the Ballets Russes and appeared alongside Nijinsky in the company.

### Eugen Bleuler and the Binswangers

Psychiatrists who assessed or treated him in 1919 and afterward. Their records anchor the clinical history more firmly than later popular speculation.

## Controversies, Allegations, and Evidentiary Findings

### Sexual patronage and exploitation

**Documented fact:** Nijinsky had sexual relations with male patrons, including Lvov and Diaghilev, while receiving material or professional benefits.

**Unresolved question:** The balance of desire, affection, necessity, and coercion. Nijinsky’s limited economic independence and Diaghilev’s authority created structural pressure, but surviving records do not support reducing the relationship to either pure romance or a formally proved abuse case.

### Romola’s pursuit and marriage

Some witnesses depicted Romola as a stalker-like admirer who maneuvered herself onto the South American tour and pursued a man with whom she could barely converse. Romola represented herself as responding to mutual love.

**Documented core:** Her pursuit was deliberate; communication was difficult; marriage followed rapidly.

**Disputed interpretation:** Rescue, seduction, manipulation, or mutual attachment.

### *Faune* obscenity scandal

The culminating movement was read as masturbation and condemned as indecent.

**Documented fact:** The press controversy was real.

**Interpretation:** Whether the choreography literally depicts ejaculation or uses stylized erotic symbolism remains a performance question. Later retellings often make the gesture more explicit than photographs or notation can prove.

### The “Rite riot”

**Documented fact:** Loud disorder and confrontations occurred.

**Myth:** That an unsuspecting audience instantly rioted solely because of Stravinsky’s notes. Evidence instead indicates prior factionalism, theatrical publicity, hostility to Nijinsky’s choreography, social performance by the audience, and musical shock.

### Professional incompetence

Hostile contemporaries called Nijinsky unintelligent, inarticulate, rhythmically confused, or incapable of management.

**Supported in part:** His independent company and tour management encountered serious failures.

**Counterevidence:** The internally complex choreography, notation manuscripts, sustained rehearsal systems, and surviving diary show a specialized intelligence poorly captured by conventional academic or conversational measures.

### Romola’s diary censorship

**Documented fact:** The 1936–1937 edition edited by Romola was heavily bowdlerized, rearranged, and normalized. It removed or modified sexual, scatological, repetitive, accusatory, and psychologically disturbing material.

The complete notebooks were not published in a reliable unexpurgated English edition until 1999, following earlier French publication of much of the material. The first published *Diary* should not be cited as a transparent transcription of Nijinsky’s text.

### Commercial exploitation by Romola

Critics accused Romola of making a living from Nijinsky’s illness and celebrity through books, lectures, permissions, and controlled access.

**Documented fact:** His name and papers became sources of income, and Romola shaped the public narrative.

**Qualification:** She also bore medical, travel, and family expenses and preserved materials that might otherwise have disappeared. “Exploitation” is an interpretation rather than a judicial finding.

### Tamara’s paternity

**Rumor/allegation:** Tamara was fathered by one of Romola’s doctors or associates.

**Evidence against treating it as fact:** No proof is known; timing allowed Nijinsky to be the father. Romola’s later assertion arose during a family property or inheritance conflict.

### Criminal, financial, and political allegations

The adversarial search combined Nijinsky’s name with “controversy,” “scandal,” “allegations,” “lawsuit,” “fraud,” “accusations,” “conspiracy,” and “criticism,” including 2024–2026 material.

**Finding:** No credible record was located of Nijinsky being investigated, indicted, convicted, or civilly adjudged for fraud, corruption, money laundering, political violence, or other serious crime. The recurring controversies concern sexuality, patronage, alleged indecency, marital and contractual conflict, psychiatric treatment, authorship, and posthumous mythmaking.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### “He could remain suspended in the air”

The story says Nijinsky’s leap was so high and sustained that he appeared to pause in midair.

**Origin and circulation:** Contemporary critics, dancers, publicity writers, and later biographies.

**Evidence:** Numerous witnesses attest extraordinary elevation and landing control. Physics does not permit literal suspension. The perceived “pause” likely arose from timing, elevation, pose at the apex, and exceptionally controlled descent.

**Reputational effect:** It made the leap the central emblem of Nijinsky, sometimes eclipsing his mime and choreography.

### Pavlova searched his shoes for springs

A popular anecdote says Anna Pavlova examined Nijinsky’s shoes to discover the secret of his jumps.

**Status:** Legend or unverified anecdote. It expresses contemporary amazement but should not be treated as a documented incident without a traceable first-hand source.

### The single catastrophic night of madness

The story treats the 19 January 1919 Suvretta performance as the precise moment Nijinsky became insane.

**Evidence:** His deterioration was progressive, and unusual thought and conduct preceded the performance.

**Effect:** The dramatic compression supports plays and ballets structured around “the last dance.”

### Diaghilev or Romola “caused” the schizophrenia

Competing hostile myths blame Diaghilev’s seduction and abandonment or Romola’s marriage and sexual control.

**Evidence:** Emotional stress, war, isolation, career collapse, and family vulnerability may all have affected him. None establishes a single cause of schizophrenia. These narratives often serve later sexual or moral polemics.

### Madness created the choreography

A romantic myth reads turned-in feet, angular bodies, or ritual violence as symptoms of illness.

**Evidence against:** The principal choreographies were deliberate, laboriously rehearsed, collaboratively produced works made years before the acute 1919 crisis. Innovation is not clinical evidence.

### Nijinsky as an uneducated idiot-genius

Diaghilev’s circle and later writers sometimes described him as barely articulate offstage but divine in motion.

**Qualification:** He struggled with ordinary schooling and social communication, but developed choreography, a notation system, religious-political arguments, and extensive writing. The binary was useful publicity and caricature.

### A miraculous dance for Soviet soldiers

The story says Nijinsky, mute for decades, suddenly danced when recognized by Russian soldiers in 1945.

**Reported basis:** Family and medical narratives describe temporary sociability and dancing or whirling.

**Mythic form:** Later versions present a full restoration of his former virtuosity. No evidence supports that embellishment.

### No film exists because Diaghilev prohibited it

It is often asserted that Diaghilev refused to let the Ballets Russes be filmed, explaining the absence of Nijinsky footage.

**Status:** Plausible reported tradition, but absolute versions are difficult to prove. No authenticated moving footage of Nijinsky dancing is presently recognized; misidentified clips and reenactments circulate periodically.

## Works by Nijinsky

### Choreographic works

1. **L’Après-midi d’un Faune** (1912). Choreography and scenario by Nijinsky; music by Claude Debussy; design by Léon Bakst. Genuine work. A substantial notation score survives.
2. **Jeux** (1913). Choreography and scenario by Nijinsky; music by Debussy; design by Bakst. Genuine work; original choreography incompletely preserved.
3. **Le Sacre du printemps** (1913). Choreography by Nijinsky; music by Igor Stravinsky; scenario/design by Nicholas Roerich under Diaghilev’s production. Genuine collaborative work; original choreography lost in complete authoritative form.
4. **Till Eulenspiegel** (1916). Choreography and libretto by Nijinsky after Charles de Coster; music by Richard Strauss; design by Robert Edmond Jones. Genuine work; incompletely preserved.

### Dance notation and visual material

- **Notation score for *L’Après-midi d’un Faune***, chiefly dated 1915. Genuine manuscript.
- Other choreographic notation notebooks and theoretical experiments, approximately 1915–1918. Genuine manuscripts, partly unfinished and not a fully published standardized system.
- Drawings and abstract geometric works, especially from the late 1910s. Genuine works where provenance is secure; frequently interpreted through the illness but also connected with notation, bodily geometry, eyes, circles, and repeated pattern.

### Diary

- Russian manuscript notebooks, written January–March 1919. Genuine, not prepared by Nijinsky for conventional publication and not supervised by him in print.
- *The Diary of Vaslav Nijinsky*, edited by Romola Nijinsky, London: Victor Gollancz, 1937; related editions appeared from 1936 onward. **Heavily edited and bowdlerized posthumous edition.**
- *Cahiers*, Actes Sud, 1995. Major unexpurgated French publication authorized after Tamara Nijinsky opened access.
- *The Diary of Vaslav Nijinsky: Unexpurgated Edition*, edited by Joan Acocella, translated from Russian by Kyril FitzLyon, 1999; later University of Illinois Press edition, 2006. **Best-known complete English edition**, restoring material removed or altered by Romola.

### Letters, poems, and statements

Letters and poems occur within the fourth diary notebook and archival collections. Some public statements or interviews attributed to Nijinsky were mediated by translators, publicity departments, Romola, or Diaghilev’s organization and should not automatically be treated as verbatim authored prose.

Nijinsky wrote no authenticated patents, technical papers, commercial treatises, or independently published political manifesto.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Nijinsky’s reputation developed along several partly independent lines.

### The vanished performer

Because no authenticated performance film survives, his dancing exists through criticism, memoir, photographs, drawings, sculptures, and roles transmitted to other dancers. This absence encouraged superlatives that cannot be tested against film.

### Founder of choreographic modernism

His four ballets rejected important conventions of classical ballet. *Faune*, *Jeux*, and *Sacre* became reference points for modern dance, postmodern reconstruction, queer readings of performance, and debates about the body as image, ritual, machine, or sexual sign.

### The artist identified with madness

Publication of the diary made Nijinsky an emblem of creativity and psychosis. Some artists found in it a direct language of suffering; clinicians used it as a rare longitudinal document. Romantic representations often erase the thirty years of disability by turning illness into artistic martyrdom.

### Textual recovery

The unexpurgated diary materially changed Nijinsky studies. It restored sexual history, repetition, obscenity, political anger, accusations against Romola and doctors, and the full extent of disorganized and visionary writing. It also exposed how thoroughly Romola had created the earlier public text.

### Reconstruction controversies

Restagings by Guest and Jeschke, Hodson and Archer, and others returned approximations of Nijinsky’s choreography to the theater.

**Documented value:** Reconstructions synthesize notation, scores, photographs, designs, reviews, and witness testimony.

**Limitation:** Except for much of *Faune*, they are not exact recoveries. Billing them without qualification as “Nijinsky’s original choreography” can obscure conjectural choices.

### Influence

His influence can be traced in work by Bronislava Nijinska, Marie Rambert, Frederick Ashton, Maurice Béjart, John Neumeier, Pina Bausch, Martha Graham’s broader modernist environment, and choreographers concerned with turned-in movement, mass pattern, male vulnerability, ritual, and fragmented gesture. The strength of influence varies: in some cases it is direct repertorial transmission; in others, acknowledged historical inspiration.

## Atlas Connections

Only roster members for whom a supported connection was found are included.

- **Sergei Pavlovich Diaghilev — documented fact.** Lover, patron, employer, producer, and professional antagonist, approximately 1908/1909–1913, with later attempted reconciliation. Principal locations included St Petersburg, Paris, Monte Carlo, London, and Ballets Russes touring centers.

- **Igor Fyodorovich Stravinsky — documented fact.** Composer and choreographer collaborated on *Le Sacre du printemps*, principally in Paris and during rehearsals in 1912–1913. Their later accounts disputed choreographic competence and creative credit.

- **Achille-Claude Debussy — documented fact.** Debussy’s *Prélude à l’après-midi d’un faune* supplied the score for Nijinsky’s 1912 ballet; Debussy composed *Jeux* for the 1913 production. He attended rehearsals and later criticized aspects of Nijinsky’s choreography.

- **Anna Matveyevna Pavlova — documented fact.** Fellow Imperial Ballet and early Ballets Russes dancer; partnered or shared repertory and the institutional scene with Nijinsky in St Petersburg and the early international seasons. The shoe-inspection story is a rumor or myth.

- **Auguste Rodin — documented fact.** Rodin defended Nijinsky publicly during the 1912 *Faune* scandal and praised his embodiment of antique form; he also made artistic studies associated with the dancer in Paris.

- **Nicholas Konstantinovich Roerich — documented fact.** Scenario and design collaborator on *Le Sacre du printemps*, 1912–1913.

- **Émile Jaques-Dalcroze — documented influence, primarily indirect.** Nijinsky’s *Sacre* rehearsals used rhythmic expertise associated with Dalcroze eurhythmics through Marie Rambert. No independently established personal meeting between Nijinsky and Dalcroze was found.

- **George Balanchine — reported institutional and artistic connection, not a documented close personal relationship.** Balanchine belonged to the later Ballets Russes tradition and treated Nijinsky as a predecessor. Their active Ballets Russes periods did not substantially overlap; no well-supported personal meeting was located.

- **Gabrielle Bonheur “Coco” Chanel — no direct connection established in the consulted evidence.** Chanel later patronized Diaghilev and *The Rite of Spring*, but the documented patronage postdated Nijinsky’s active participation. Shared association with the Ballets Russes is insufficient to claim a personal connection.

- **Pablo Ruiz Picasso — no direct connection established.** Picasso designed *Parade* for the Ballets Russes in 1917, but Nijinsky was then associated with the separate South American tour. No supported meeting or collaboration was found.

- **Isadora Duncan — reported artistic influence, not a documented direct encounter.** Nijinsky belonged to the Russian ballet world affected by Duncan's performances, and the two were later paired in art-historical studies, especially Antoine Bourdelle's imagery. No securely documented personal meeting was found; this is weaker than Duncan's documented ties to Diaghilev and Fokine. (Cross-checked against isadora_duncan.dossier.md's own Nijinsky entry, which states the connection in matching terms.)

- **Martha Graham — documented later influence rather than personal connection.** Graham engaged with the modern dance world transformed partly by the Ballets Russes, but no supported Nijinsky–Graham meeting was found.

The remainder of the supplied roster produced no substantiated personal, institutional, or explicitly acknowledged connection in the sources consulted. Absence from this list is therefore a finding, not evidence that a connection was impossible.
