## Works by Stanley Jeyaraja Tambiah

Tambiah's bibliography traces a single career making two major turns, not a flat shelf of equally weighted titles. He began as a close ethnographer of Thai Buddhist village religion, widened into comparative historical theory about Buddhist kingship, then — after Sri Lanka's civil war broke out in 1983, his own country of birth — turned the same analytic tools on ethnic violence itself. A few titles mark those turns and are landmark; others are refinements, lectures, or occasional pieces that matter less on their own than as way-stations.

### Thai Buddhism and ritual

**Buddhism and the Spirit Cults in North-east Thailand** (Cambridge University Press, 1970) is the foundational fieldwork book and the one that made his name: a village-level study showing that Thai Buddhism is not a pure philosophical system layered over "superstition," but a single working religious field in which monks, spirit mediums, and amulets all have defined, interlocking roles. It set the template — take local religious practice seriously on its own terms rather than measure it against a textbook version of the Pali canon — that runs through everything he wrote afterward. (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Jeyaraja_Tambiah)

**The Buddhist Saints of the Forest and the Cult of Amulets** (Cambridge, 1984) returns to the same Thai material a decade later but shifts the lens to charisma and relics: how forest-dwelling meditation monks become objects of mass veneration, and how their amulets circulate as both religious and economic objects. It is a mature, second look at the world of the 1970 book rather than a new departure. (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Jeyaraja_Tambiah)

**A Performative Approach to Ritual** (the 1979 Radcliffe-Brown Lecture, published by the British Academy) is short but influential out of proportion to its length: Tambiah argued, drawing on J.L. Austin's speech-act theory, that ritual works less by what it says about the world than by what it *does* — it is performative, not merely descriptive or symbolic. The essay became a standard reference point in the anthropology of ritual well beyond his own Thai specialism.

**Magic, Science, Religion, and the Scope of Rationality** (Cambridge, 1990, from his 1984 Lewis Henry Morgan Lectures) is the theoretical companion to the ritual essay: a sustained critique of the Western assumption that "magic" is simply failed science, arguing instead that different cultures operate with different, internally coherent notions of causality and efficacy. It is one of his most cited theoretical statements, read in the philosophy of social science as much as in anthropology. (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Jeyaraja_Tambiah)

### Kingship and political theory

**World Conqueror and World Renouncer: A Study of Buddhism and Polity in Thailand against a Historical Background** (Cambridge, 1976) is the pivot of his career and arguably his single most important book. It asks how a religion built around renunciation of the world (the monk, the "world renouncer") coexists with, and historically legitimates, kingship and state power (the "world conqueror"). Out of this study Tambiah developed the idea of the **galactic polity** — a model of traditional Southeast Asian kingdoms as loosely bound constellations of satellite centers orbiting a sacred royal center, their borders fluid and their authority radiating outward rather than being fixed by the hard, bounded-territory logic of the modern nation-state. That model became widely used well outside Thai studies, by historians and political scientists describing pre-modern polities generally. (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Jeyaraja_Tambiah)

### Sri Lanka and ethnic violence

After 1983 Tambiah redirected his anthropology toward the civil war convulsing the country of his own birth, and this body of work is where he became known to a much wider audience than specialists in Buddhist studies.

**Sri Lanka: Ethnic Fratricide and the Dismantling of Democracy** (University of Chicago Press, 1986) was the first major scholarly account of how Sri Lanka's postcolonial democracy curdled into Sinhala-Tamil communal war, written early enough in the conflict to shape how outsiders understood it. (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Jeyaraja_Tambiah)

**Buddhism Betrayed? Religion, Politics, and Violence in Sri Lanka** (University of Chicago Press, 1992) is the most pointed and, for some Sinhala Buddhist readers, the most controversial of his books: it argues that politically militant monks and Buddhist nationalist organizations helped drive the ethnic violence, a direct challenge to the self-image of Buddhism as a religion of nonviolence. The title's question mark is deliberate — he is asking whether Buddhism as practiced had betrayed its own stated ideals. (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Jeyaraja_Tambiah)

**Leveling Crowds: Ethnonationalist Conflicts and Collective Violence in South Asia** (University of California Press, 1996) is his broadest and most theoretically ambitious work on violence: a comparative study of riots and communal violence across Sri Lanka and India that treats the crowd itself — its rituals, its spread, its apparent spontaneity masking real organization — as an object of anthropological analysis, not just a political or criminological one. It is the capstone of his second career, generalizing from Sri Lanka to a theory of ethnic riot as a social form. (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Jeyaraja_Tambiah)

### Essays and shorter writings

**Edmund Leach: An Anthropological Life** (Cambridge, 2002) is a biography of his Cambridge mentor and one of the dominant figures of British structuralist anthropology; it is personal as well as scholarly, written by a former student who became Leach's intellectual peer, and it doubles as an inside history of mid-century British social anthropology. (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Jeyaraja_Tambiah)

His many journal articles on Thai kinship, Sinhalese land tenure, and dowry across South Asia (collected in part in *Culture, Thought, and Social Action*, Harvard University Press, 1985) are workmanlike rather than landmark — useful evidence of the range of his fieldwork interests, but not texts that shifted a field the way the 1970, 1976, 1986, 1990, or 1996 books did.
