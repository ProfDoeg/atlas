# Clark McAdams Clifford: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier reconstructs Clark McAdams Clifford’s life from archival finding aids, oral histories, official government histories, diplomatic documents, congressional investigations, regulatory orders, contemporaneous journalism, recorded interviews, and major biographies.

Evidentiary labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by primary documents, official records, archival materials, or undisputed public records.
- **Reported fact:** stated by reputable secondary sources but not independently established here from a primary document.
- **Allegation:** asserted by investigators, prosecutors, witnesses, litigants, or journalists but not adjudicated as fact.
- **Official finding:** conclusion of a government investigative or regulatory body; not necessarily a criminal-court finding.
- **Formal charge / indictment:** accusation filed in court; not proof of guilt.
- **Court finding:** ruling made by a court.
- **Acquittal:** criminal charges decided in the defendant’s favor after trial.
- **Unresolved:** available evidence permits competing interpretations.
- **Rumor or myth:** a reputation-making story that lacks adequate documentary support or materially simplifies events.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name:** Clark McAdams Clifford  
**Common public name:** Clark M. Clifford; Clark Clifford  
**Family nickname for his wife:** Margery Pepperell Kimball Clifford was commonly called “Marny.”  
**Honorifics and offices:** Captain, U.S. Naval Reserve; Special Counsel to the President; Chairman of the President’s Foreign Intelligence Advisory Board; ninth United States Secretary of Defense.  
**Political affiliation:** Democratic Party associate and adviser, though he never held elective office.  
**Profession:** Trial lawyer, corporate and regulatory attorney, presidential adviser, political strategist, speechwriter, bank director and chairman, memoirist.  
**Born:** December 25, 1906, Fort Scott, Kansas.  
**Died:** October 10, 1998, Bethesda, Maryland, aged ninety-one.  
**Reported cause of death:** Respiratory failure, after several years of heart and respiratory illness.  
**Burial:** Arlington National Cemetery, Arlington, Virginia.

Clifford occupied paid federal office for only a small portion of his Washington career, but advised Democratic presidents Harry S. Truman, John F. Kennedy, Lyndon B. Johnson, and Jimmy Carter. Contemporary and retrospective accounts called him a “Washington insider,” “super-lawyer,” “wise man,” and presidential counselor. Those descriptions were reputational titles, not formal offices.

No evidence was found that he used a pseudonym.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and maternal influence

**Documented or consistently reported facts:**

- His father was **Frank Andrew Clifford**, a traveling auditor and later manager associated with the Missouri Pacific Railroad.
- His mother was **Georgia McAdams Clifford**, described in biographical accounts as a writer.
- He had an older sister; the consulted sources do not establish her full life history.
- He was named for his maternal uncle, **Clark McAdams**, an editor at the *St. Louis Post-Dispatch* identified in biographies as a crusading liberal.

Biographer John Acacia emphasizes two formative family traditions. Clifford credited his father with teaching disciplined work and independence. His father’s displacement after a railroad-management change reportedly impressed upon him the insecurity of depending upon a single patron. From the McAdams family, especially his journalist uncle, Clifford absorbed a reform-oriented liberalism and the proposition that government had a responsibility to improve material conditions.

Beyond these immediate relatives, the consulted documentary sources do not securely reconstruct a longer Clifford or McAdams genealogy. Claims of more remote ancestry should therefore be treated as unestablished.

### Marriage and descendants

Clifford met **Margery Pepperell Kimball** during a European trip in 1929. They married on October 3, 1931, and remained married until his death. Margery died in 2000.

They had three daughters:

- **Margery “Gery” Clifford**
- **Joyce Clifford Burland**
- **Randall Clifford Wight**

At Clifford’s death, reports counted twelve grandchildren and fifteen great-grandchildren.

The Library of Congress received Clifford’s papers from Margery after his death. The record indicates a long social friendship between Clark and Margery Clifford and Harry and Bess Truman.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1906–1924

Clifford was born in Fort Scott because his father’s railroad work had placed the family there. His upbringing was principally associated with St. Louis, Missouri.

A 1948 *Time* profile, valuable as contemporaneous reputation evidence but written in the magazine’s stylized manner, described him as:

- a shy, fair-haired child;
- a seller of the *Saturday Evening Post*;
- a singer in an Episcopal church choir;
- a boy mocked by some peers as a “sissy” because of his appearance and reserve.

This portrait should be treated as **reported recollection**, not a complete psychological record. It shows that Clifford’s later image—tall, carefully dressed, controlled, and socially polished—was retrospectively projected into stories about his childhood.

The Episcopal choir is evidence of childhood participation in an Episcopal religious setting. The sources consulted do not show that adult theology, devotional practice, or church office played an important part in his later public life. His mature public outlook was expressed primarily in secular liberal, constitutional, institutional, and anti-Communist terms.

---

## Education and Formation

Clifford attended Washington University in St. Louis. Sources variously summarize his education as a bachelor’s and law curriculum or simply state that he received an LL.B. in 1928. The official Defense Department history says he took both bachelor’s and law degrees; the Truman Library chronology records the LL.B. The precise undergraduate-degree sequence is not completely clear from the consulted records.

During university years he:

- played tennis;
- sang baritone in the glee club;
- acted in student theatrical productions, often in juvenile-lead roles;
- acquired experience in public presentation that later served him before juries and presidents.

No single academic teacher has emerged in the sources as his decisive mentor. His important formative mentors were instead professional and familial:

- **Clark McAdams**, his maternal uncle, for liberal political ideas;
- **Jacob M. Lashly**, the St. Louis lawyer who gave him courtroom work and recognized his ability with juries;
- **James K. Vardaman Jr.**, whose naval-aide appointment brought Clifford into the Truman White House;
- **Harry S. Truman**, who converted Clifford from a military aide and speech drafter into a senior presidential counselor.

Clifford later acknowledged that he arrived in government with little foreign-policy or administrative experience. He said he learned national-security policy “catch as catch can,” by interviewing officials and absorbing the work of the presidency.

## Early Legal Career, 1928–1944

### St. Louis practice

After completing law school in 1928, Clifford joined **Holland, Lashly & Donnell** in St. Louis. The Library of Congress chronology lists him as an associate from 1928 through 1937, under successive configurations of the practice, and as a partner in **Lashly, Lashly, Miller & Clifford** from 1938 through 1943.

Jacob Lashly assigned him accident and trial cases. Contemporary accounts portrayed Clifford as highly effective before juries: physically imposing, restrained in delivery, prepared, and persuasive without appearing theatrical. He developed a career in trial, corporate, and labor law.

His later method as a presidential counselor reflected courtroom preparation. For major meetings he prepared longhand notes as though presenting a jury argument: anticipated objections, ordered facts, narrowed choices, and gave the decision-maker a practicable recommendation.

### European journey and marriage

In 1929 Clifford traveled in Europe, where he met Margery Kimball. The surviving summaries do not provide a complete itinerary. The trip was personally decisive because it led to their marriage two years later.

### Local politics

Clifford took an interest in St. Louis Democratic politics as his legal career grew. The consulted sources do not establish that he held local office. His strength was already advisory and organizational rather than electoral.

---

## Naval Service and Entry into the White House, 1944–1946

### U.S. Naval Reserve

Clifford entered the U.S. Naval Reserve in 1944. The Library of Congress chronology identifies assignments as:

- special assistant to the commander, Western Sea Frontier;
- assistant naval aide to the president;
- naval aide to the president.

He did not serve at sea or in combat. A hostile or teasing contemporary narrative emphasized how quickly he advanced from lieutenant junior grade to captain. The speed of advancement was real; insinuations that it was improper are not supported by an official finding in the sources consulted.

### James K. Vardaman Jr. and Truman

Clifford’s St. Louis acquaintance **James K. Vardaman Jr.**, Truman’s naval aide, brought him onto the White House staff in 1945. Clifford initially performed military and ceremonial tasks, including helping unescorted guests at official functions.

He also worked on a redesign of the presidential seal and received substantive assignments involving:

- universal military training;
- unification of the armed services;
- presidential speeches and messages.

In 1946 he succeeded Vardaman as naval aide. Within months Truman appointed him Special Counsel to the President, succeeding Samuel Rosenman.

## Special Counsel to President Truman, 1946–1950

### Position and working method

Clifford served as Special Counsel from 1946 through 1950. The Truman Library describes him as probably the most influential and prominent White House staff member of the period.

He was simultaneously:

- legal counselor;
- speechwriter and editor;
- legislative strategist;
- labor-policy adviser;
- foreign-policy synthesizer;
- political adviser;
- liaison among the White House, departments, Congress, and private organizations.

Presidential speeches were collaborative products. Clifford expressly rejected claims of solitary authorship, noting that White House drafts passed through multiple hands. His role was nevertheless substantial in defining arguments, structure, political tone, and presidential presentation.

### Labor unrest and domestic policy

In 1946 the United States faced major postwar strikes. Clifford assisted Truman in developing responses to organized labor and industrial disruption. He belonged to the more liberal part of the White House circle but also defended presidential authority and government intervention during emergencies.

The administration’s labor record was internally conflicted: Truman vetoed the Taft–Hartley Act in 1947, but the government had earlier considered or used forceful measures against strikes affecting national transportation and industry. Clifford’s papers document his participation, but the consulted summaries do not support assigning every labor decision personally to him.

### The Clifford–Elsey Soviet memorandum

At Truman’s request, Clifford coordinated a 1946 review of U.S.–Soviet relations. **George M. Elsey** performed major research and drafting work. Clifford solicited views from senior officials and assembled the resulting memorandum, delivered in September 1946.

The memorandum portrayed Soviet conduct as expansionist, recommended firmness, military preparedness, economic strength, support for threatened governments, public explanation of Soviet behavior, and continued willingness to negotiate from a position of strength.

**Documented significance:** The paper anticipated or consolidated assumptions central to containment, the Truman Doctrine, the Marshall Plan, and postwar security policy.

**Qualification:** It did not single-handedly create containment. George Kennan, Dean Acheson, James Forrestal, George Marshall, and other officials produced related analyses and policies. Clifford himself later conceded the danger of assigning too much causal force to one document.

### Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan

Clifford helped articulate administration policy during the Greek and Turkish aid crisis of 1947. He participated in drafting and presenting the argument that the United States should support governments resisting coercive expansion.

He also helped formulate the public presentation of European recovery assistance. A frequently repeated story says Clifford proposed calling it the “Truman Plan,” whereupon Truman insisted that it bear Secretary of State George C. Marshall’s name to improve congressional and international acceptance. The story is consistent with Clifford’s oral recollections and the administration’s political logic, but should be treated as a well-supported anecdote rather than a verbatim record of a single decisive exchange.

### National Security Act and military unification

Clifford had begun studying service unification during his naval assignment. As special counsel he participated heavily in the negotiations and legislative work producing:

- the National Security Act of 1947;
- creation of the National Military Establishment;
- the National Security Council;
- the Central Intelligence Agency;
- subsequent 1949 amendments and the Department of Defense structure.

He did not independently design all these institutions. The legislation emerged from conflict and negotiation among Truman, the War and Navy Departments, Congress, military leaders, James Forrestal, and other officials. Clifford’s contribution was particularly important in reconciling positions, drafting presidential arguments, and moving legislation.

### Recognition of Israel

Clifford became one of the strongest White House advocates of partition and prompt recognition of the Jewish state. On March 8, 1948, he sent Truman a memorandum arguing that continued support for partition conformed with declared American policy.

At the May 12 White House meeting, Clifford argued for recognition; Secretary of State **George C. Marshall** opposed having a domestic political adviser participate in what he regarded as a grave foreign-policy question. Marshall presented strategic concerns involving the Arab states, petroleum, regional stability, and the military viability of the proposed Jewish state.

Clifford later recalled Marshall declaring that if Truman followed Clifford’s advice and Marshall voted, he would vote against Truman. Marshall’s own meeting memorandum supports the existence of a severe confrontation, although later retellings vary in wording and dramatic emphasis.

The United States granted de facto recognition on May 14, 1948, shortly after Israel declared independence.

**Competing interpretations:**

- Clifford maintained that humanitarian, strategic, legal, and anti-Soviet considerations—not electoral politics—drove his advocacy.
- State Department officials and later critics believed White House attention to the Jewish vote and the 1948 election affected policy.
- Documentary records show both substantive-policy and political discussion around Palestine. They do not justify reducing the decision entirely to either altruism or electoral calculation.

Clifford later pressed for de jure recognition and resisted efforts he believed diluted Truman’s commitment. His role made him an important figure in the official American narrative of Israel’s recognition.

### The 1948 election memorandum and campaign

In November 1947 Clifford delivered a forty-three-page analysis of Truman’s political position. The document is often called the Clifford memorandum, though he acknowledged input from others, and James Rowe is frequently recognized as a major collaborator.

The strategy recommended:

- defending New Deal constituencies;
- confronting the Republican-controlled Eightieth Congress;
- appealing to labor, farmers, urban voters, Black voters, and liberals;
- presenting Truman as an active defender of ordinary citizens;
- forcing Congress to act on popular measures and exposing obstruction when it refused.

During the 1948 campaign Clifford helped formulate the aggressive whistle-stop strategy. Truman attacked the “do-nothing” Congress and presented himself in direct, plain language rather than imitating Franklin Roosevelt’s style. Truman’s victory over Thomas E. Dewey established Clifford’s national reputation as a political strategist.

**Myth qualification:** Clifford did not single-handedly “elect Truman.” Truman himself, Democratic organizers, labor groups, changing farm prices, Republican errors, and a late campaign movement all mattered. Clifford’s memorandum and advice were nevertheless documented elements of the victory.

### Civil rights

Clifford was involved in White House strategy as Truman moved toward a more explicit civil-rights position, including the 1948 program and desegregation orders. His political memorandum treated Black voters as an indispensable Democratic constituency. That fact does not, by itself, determine whether his support was principled or electoral; both policy conviction and coalition politics appear in the administration’s record.

### Departure

Clifford left the White House in 1950. He remained personally close to Harry and Bess Truman and continued to defend Truman’s administration and legacy.

## Washington Legal Practice and Political Influence, 1950–1960

### Clifford & Miller

Clifford and a St. Louis colleague founded **Clifford & Miller**, a small Washington practice. It was designed as a high-fee, low-overhead “boutique” capable of guiding corporations through federal regulation and executive agencies.

Clifford told prospective clients that the firm did not sell influence, but offered knowledge of government and the respect of public officials. Critics viewed that distinction as semantic because his reputation, contacts, and access were themselves commercially valuable.

Reported early clients included:

- Phillips Petroleum;
- RCA;
- Standard Oil;
- Pennsylvania Railroad;
- Republic of Indonesia;
- El Paso Natural Gas;
- Trans World Airlines;
- General Electric;
- DuPont;
- McDonnell Douglas;
- Knight-Ridder;
- Howard Hughes.

The exact dates and scope of every engagement require consultation of the closed or client-specific legal files; the list should not imply that each relationship was continuous.

### Robert S. Kerr and corporate networks

Oklahoma Senator **Robert S. Kerr** helped Clifford obtain Phillips Petroleum as a client. The episode illustrates the junction of friendship, Senate politics, corporate law, and private practice that defined Clifford’s Washington model.

Clifford later said he earned more than $1 million annually for many years and considered himself among the first Washington lawyers to do so. This is a **self-reported financial claim**, consistent with descriptions of his elite practice but not independently audited here.

### Influence-peddling criticism

Clifford insisted he practiced regulatory law rather than influence peddling. Critics noted that:

- he traded professionally on knowledge and relationships developed in office;
- he moved repeatedly between public advice and representation of regulated corporations;
- the firm did not initially register as a lobbying organization;
- foreign representation sometimes created disclosure questions.

No consulted source establishes that his ordinary post-1950 legal practice resulted in a criminal conviction. The controversy concerns structural conflicts and the monetization of government access more than a specific adjudicated offense.

### Howard Hughes and Kennedy

Clifford represented Howard Hughes in matters involving government and business. He also became personal lawyer to Senator John F. Kennedy.

In 1957–1958 Clifford helped Kennedy answer accusations that *Profiles in Courage* had been ghostwritten or improperly credited. Clifford’s successful management of the dispute strengthened their relationship. The authorship controversy itself did not concern Clifford’s authorship; it showed his role as Kennedy’s lawyer and reputational defender.

---

## Kennedy Years, 1960–1963

### Election and transition

During the 1960 campaign Kennedy enlisted Clifford for political strategy and for planning the transfer of presidential power. Clifford participated in a defense-organization study chaired by Senator Stuart Symington.

He became a significant transition adviser but did not join Kennedy’s cabinet. His role combined personal legal service, institutional planning, and informal counsel.

### President’s Foreign Intelligence Advisory Board

Kennedy appointed Clifford to the President’s Foreign Intelligence Advisory Board in May 1961. He became chairman in April 1963 and continued under Johnson until 1968.

The board reviewed intelligence performance and covert-policy questions. Clifford’s service placed him inside the oversight network surrounding the CIA after the Bay of Pigs. His papers and Kennedy oral histories contain extensive intelligence-related material, some of it historically classified.

### Kennedy family legal work

Clifford handled matters involving Kennedy family real estate and advised the family on sensitive political questions. A reported example is the 1961 discussion of whether Edward Kennedy should seek John Kennedy’s former Massachusetts Senate seat.

### Assassination and transition

After Kennedy’s assassination on November 22, 1963, Clifford assisted the transition to Lyndon Johnson. The sources do not support conspiracy claims that Clifford participated in, concealed, or possessed special foreknowledge of the assassination.

---

## Johnson Adviser, 1963–1968

### Inner advisory network

Alongside **Abe Fortas** and **Bill Moyers**, Clifford became one of Johnson’s most trusted outside counselors. He advised on:

- presidential transition;
- intelligence;
- the 1964 election;
- Vietnam;
- the 1967 Arab–Israeli War;
- politically sensitive personnel and scandal management.

When Johnson aide Walter Jenkins was arrested in October 1964 after a sexual encounter in a YMCA restroom, Johnson consulted Clifford and Fortas. Clifford’s role belonged to political crisis management; there is no evidence that he caused or engineered the incident.

### Vietnam: early hawkishness

Clifford supported the central containment assumptions behind American intervention and was widely perceived as a hawk before becoming secretary of defense. He backed Johnson publicly and did not mount an early public opposition to escalation.

Later accounts, including recollections by Leslie Gelb, suggest Clifford harbored private doubts before 1968. The best-supported reconstruction is that his views evolved: he accepted the intervention’s premises, supported firmness, increasingly questioned the attainable objective, and finally embraced disengagement.

### 1967 Southeast Asia and Pacific mission

Johnson sent Clifford and General **Maxwell D. Taylor** to South Vietnam and other Asian-Pacific allies in 1967. The mission sought support for the war and assessments of allied commitment.

Clifford later identified the trip as a turning point. He found allies unwilling to make contributions proportional to their rhetoric and became more skeptical that additional American force could create a decisive political result.

## Secretary of Defense, 1968–1969

### Appointment and confirmation

Johnson announced Clifford’s appointment on January 19, 1968, to succeed Robert McNamara. The Senate confirmed him unanimously on January 30. He took office March 1, 1968.

At confirmation hearings Clifford opposed an unconditional bombing halt under then-existing circumstances, while acknowledging the administration’s San Antonio formula: negotiations should follow a halt and North Vietnam should not exploit it militarily.

The timing was decisive. The Tet Offensive had undermined official claims about progress and General William Westmoreland sought large troop reinforcements.

### Clifford Task Force

Beginning March 2, Clifford led a reassessment of Vietnam policy. Participants and staff included Paul Nitze, Paul Warnke, Leslie Gelb, Morton Halperin, Richard Steadman, Alain Enthoven, representatives of State and CIA, and military leaders.

Clifford initially approached the troop request as a manager expected to find resources. The inquiry instead forced fundamental questions:

- What political objective would additional troops achieve?
- How many more troops might ultimately be needed?
- Would mobilization and reserve call-ups damage the economy and domestic support?
- Could bombing compel Hanoi to yield?
- Was “victory” operationally defined?
- Could South Vietnam assume more responsibility?

He concluded that another major escalation offered no clear endpoint.

### March 1968 “Wise Men” meetings

On March 25–26, senior former officials—the press’s “Wise Men”—received intelligence and policy presentations. A group formerly supportive of the war shifted toward negotiation and disengagement. Clifford helped organize and transmit this change to Johnson.

The meetings did not alone determine Johnson’s March 31 decision. They reinforced evidence from the Pentagon review, Congress, public opinion, and the battlefield.

### March 31 speech and bombing limitation

On March 31 Johnson announced:

- a limitation of bombing over most of North Vietnam;
- pursuit of negotiations;
- his decision not to seek or accept another presidential nomination.

Clifford strongly supported de-escalation. Whether he personally persuaded Johnson to withdraw from the election is less certain. Johnson’s political condition, health concerns, family discussions, and advice from numerous officials also mattered.

### Troop levels

Johnson authorized an emergency increment of approximately 24,500 troops, raising the authorized ceiling from 525,000 to 549,500, though the higher figure was never fully reached. Clifford opposed the vastly larger reinforcement initially contemplated and moved toward an absolute limit.

His policy became:

- no open-ended escalation;
- greater South Vietnamese responsibility;
- negotiations;
- bombing restraint;
- eventual American disengagement.

### Paris negotiations and bombing halt

Talks began in Paris in May 1968. Clifford supported the complete halt in bombing of North Vietnam ordered on October 31.

He clashed increasingly with Secretary of State **Dean Rusk**, who remained more optimistic about military pressure. Clifford’s disagreements were policy conflicts within the administration, not personal enmity in any simple sense.

### Pentagon administration

Clifford retained much of McNamara’s management system and delegated substantial internal administration to Deputy Secretary Paul Nitze. He:

- continued the Cost Reduction Program;
- reported more than $1.2 billion in fiscal-1968 savings;
- suspended a planned infantry-division activation;
- deactivated fifty small ships, nine naval air squadrons, and twenty-three Nike-Hercules sites in response to congressionally mandated spending reductions;
- supported deployment of the Sentinel antiballistic-missile system;
- regarded Sentinel partly as leverage encouraging Soviet arms-control negotiations.

The fiscal-1969 defense budget amounted to approximately $77.7 billion in total obligational authority; the fiscal-1970 budget prepared during his tenure was approximately $75.5 billion.

### Civil disorder

Clifford’s tenure encompassed the military response to domestic unrest following Martin Luther King Jr.’s assassination and preparations surrounding the Poor People’s Campaign. Defense Department history indicates that pre-existing military commands and plans carried much of this work; Clifford was not the sole operational author.

### Departure and Presidential Medal of Freedom

Clifford left office on January 20, 1969, with the change of administration. Johnson awarded him the **Presidential Medal of Freedom with Distinction**.

His reputation at departure was stronger than at appointment: the former apparent hawk was credited with helping halt further escalation.

## Vietnam Reappraisal and Later Public Policy, 1969–1976

In July 1969 Clifford published “A Viet Nam Reappraisal: The Personal History of One Man’s View and How It Evolved” in *Foreign Affairs*, volume 47, number 4, pages 601–622.

He advocated:

- withdrawal of approximately 100,000 American troops by the end of 1969;
- withdrawal of all American ground-combat forces by the end of 1970;
- recognition that further force was unlikely to secure proportionate political gains.

The essay was both policy argument and self-explanation. Critics may read it as an attempt to separate Clifford from a failed war he had earlier supported. It nevertheless documented a genuine shift that had occurred inside the Johnson administration.

Clifford continued lobbying privately and publicly for withdrawal. His later statement that the United States had been “wrong” belongs to a broader retrospective reassessment by senior policymakers, although he was generally less self-condemning than McNamara.

---

## Carter Years and International Missions, 1976–1981

Jimmy Carter’s campaign circle initially regarded Clifford as a symbol of an old Washington establishment it intended to displace. Carter nevertheless turned to him.

### Cyprus mission

In 1977 Carter designated Clifford his personal emissary to **Greece, Turkey, and Cyprus**. The objective was to explore resolution of the division of Cyprus and reduce Greek–Turkish tensions.

The mission demonstrated Clifford’s continuing use as a private citizen entrusted with quasi-diplomatic work. It did not produce a final Cyprus settlement.

### Bert Lance

Clifford defended Carter budget director **Bert Lance** during congressional scrutiny of Lance’s banking and financial affairs. Through Lance, Clifford and his younger partner Robert A. Altman encountered BCCI founder Agha Hasan Abedi and the network that later acquired Financial General Bankshares.

This representation became the gateway to the defining scandal of Clifford’s later life.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Law-firm succession

The firm operated under several names as partners changed, including:

- Clifford & Miller;
- later combinations with additional partners;
- Clifford & Warnke.

**Paul C. Warnke**, an arms-control lawyer and former official, became the best-known later name partner.

The firm remained intentionally small relative to its client base. Clifford attributed high personal earnings to major matters and low overhead.

### Foreign clients and registration questions

Clifford’s firms represented foreign governments or state-linked interests, including Indonesia and Algerian energy interests. Congressional discussion in the 1970s reported that Algerian interests paid Clifford’s firm substantial fees.

In 1975 the Justice Department questioned registration relating to representation of Algerian oil interests, and Clifford registered under foreign-agent requirements. The consulted materials characterize this as a registration dispute, not a criminal conviction.

### First American Bankshares and related structures

The key entities in the BCCI affair were:

- **Bank of Credit and Commerce International (BCCI)**;
- **Financial General Bankshares (FGB)**, later First American Bankshares;
- **Credit and Commerce American Holdings (CCAH)**, incorporated in the Netherlands Antilles;
- **National Bank of Georgia (NBG)**;
- nominal Middle Eastern shareholders;
- offshore holding-company structures;
- BCCI-financed stock ownership.

Clifford became chairman of First American; Altman became president. Their law firm simultaneously represented combinations of BCCI, First American, CCAH, shareholders, and related parties.

That multiplicity of roles later became central to official findings of conflict of interest.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Harry S. Truman

Employer, political patron, friend, and the president most closely associated with Clifford’s rise. Clifford supplied policy synthesis and political strategy; Truman supplied trust and access. Their friendship continued after 1950.

### Margery “Marny” Clifford

Spouse from 1931 until his death, social partner in Washington, and custodian/donor of his papers. The surviving sources portray a durable marriage but provide limited private correspondence in readily accessible form.

### James K. Vardaman Jr.

St. Louis associate and naval aide who brought Clifford into the White House. Clifford eventually succeeded him.

### George M. Elsey

Major collaborator on speechwriting and the 1946 Soviet memorandum. The customary label “Clifford memorandum” can obscure Elsey’s work.

### James Rowe

Political strategist closely associated with the 1947 memorandum and Truman’s 1948 campaign planning.

### George C. Marshall

Policy collaborator on European recovery and institutional questions, but a major opponent in the dispute over recognizing Israel. The May 1948 confrontation became one of the most famous episodes in both men’s careers.

### Dean Acheson

Foreign-policy associate across the Truman and later “Wise Men” networks. Clifford operated adjacent to Acheson’s diplomatic establishment while retaining the perspective of a political White House counselor.

### John F. Kennedy and Jacqueline Kennedy

Kennedy’s lawyer, campaign and transition adviser, and intelligence-board appointee. Clifford also handled real-estate and personal legal matters for the presidential family.

### Lyndon B. Johnson

Informal counselor and later cabinet officer. Their relationship was grounded in Johnson’s belief that Clifford could impose order on politically dangerous problems. Clifford eventually pressed Johnson toward a Vietnam policy less expansive than the president’s earlier course.

### Abe Fortas and Bill Moyers

Fellow Johnson confidants. They formed part of the small outside and White House network used for sensitive political and personal crises.

### Robert McNamara

Predecessor at Defense. Clifford inherited McNamara’s management structure and a war McNamara increasingly doubted. Clifford’s public break with escalation occurred after taking over, though some civilian analysts had already moved in that direction.

### Paul Nitze, Paul Warnke, Leslie Gelb, Morton Halperin, Alain Enthoven

Defense-policy collaborators in the 1968 reassessment. Warnke later became Clifford’s law partner.

### Jimmy Carter and Bert Lance

Carter used Clifford as an emissary. Clifford’s defense of Lance produced the introduction to Abedi and BCCI.

### Robert A. Altman

Clifford’s protégé, law partner, and principal collaborator in First American and BCCI-related matters. Altman was tried in New York and acquitted of the surviving counts; Clifford never stood trial.

### Agha Hasan Abedi and Swaleh Naqvi

BCCI’s senior leadership. Clifford said they deceived him. The Senate investigation concluded that Clifford’s account was inconsistent with documents and testimony and that he participated in important BCCI deceptions.

### Kamal Adham, Ghaith Pharaon, Mohammed Hammoud, and nominal investors

Members of the shareholder and financing network around BCCI, CCAH, First American, and National Bank of Georgia. Their precise legal roles varied. Congressional investigators concluded that nominees and BCCI financing concealed BCCI’s ownership and control.

## The BCCI and First American Affair

### Origins, 1977–1981

In 1977 Clifford and Altman represented Bert Lance concerning the National Bank of Georgia. Lance introduced them to BCCI founder Agha Hasan Abedi.

They became involved in:

- Lance’s sale of NBG to Ghaith Pharaon;
- litigation surrounding the attempted takeover of Financial General Bankshares;
- representation of BCCI, proposed shareholders, and holding companies;
- presentations to American regulators.

BCCI was not permitted simply to acquire an American bank. The transaction was represented as an acquisition by independent Middle Eastern investors, with BCCI serving in a limited advisory or financing capacity.

The Federal Reserve approved the acquisition only after assurances concerning independence from BCCI. Clifford’s stature and the distinguished American board were important in calming regulators.

### Management, 1981–1991

Clifford became chairman and Altman president of the renamed First American organization. First American expanded and became Washington’s largest bank holding company.

Clifford’s firm earned large fees. The Senate report calculated approximately:

- $11 million billed to First American and related entities over roughly eight years;
- $6 million billed to BCCI over twelve years;
- approximately $17 million in combined fees.

Clifford said most work was performed for First American, and that fees from BCCI were smaller.

### Secret ownership and control

Investigators later concluded that BCCI had secretly financed and controlled nominal First American shareholders.

**Clifford’s documented defense:**

- the investors were independent;
- BCCI was an adviser, not owner;
- First American operated independently;
- he and Altman were deceived;
- they encountered no circumstance that revealed the full fraud;
- they committed no impropriety.

**Senate subcommittee official findings:**

The Kerry–Brown investigation concluded that Clifford and Altman’s account was inconsistent with contemporaneous documents and witness testimony. It found that they:

- assisted an acquisition using nominees while understanding BCCI’s central role;
- advanced BCCI-directed acquisitions;
- presented decisions originating with Abedi or BCCI as independent First American decisions;
- concealed BCCI financing of their own shares;
- withheld material information from regulators;
- deceived regulators and Congress concerning their knowledge and involvement.

These were **congressional investigative findings**, not criminal convictions.

### National Bank of Georgia purchase

First American purchased National Bank of Georgia in the mid-1980s for approximately $220 million and assumed costly obligations. The Senate report concluded BCCI’s needs, rather than First American’s independent interests, drove the transaction.

The report stated that the bank was transferred in 1992 at a fair market value of only $90 million and that First American paid approximately $12 million to escape lease obligations. It estimated the transaction’s cost to First American at more than $140 million.

Clifford and Altman maintained that the acquisition fit First American’s expansion strategy.

### Clifford and Altman stock transactions

Clifford and Altman bought CCAH/First American shares using BCCI loans. Congressional and Federal Reserve investigators emphasized that the arrangements:

- required little or no personal cash;
- were secured by the shares;
- included non-recourse or loss-protective features;
- were not disclosed promptly to First American directors or regulators;
- afforded large profits when BCCI arranged a purchaser.

In 1988 shares were sold at $6,800 each. The Senate report found a combined gross profit of approximately $9.5 million and stated that BCCI personnel calculated the sale price to provide Clifford a targeted after-tax profit of approximately $3 million and Altman approximately $1.5 million after loan repayment and taxes.

A BCCI-supported purchaser, Mohammed Hammoud, was described by a BCCI official as a front man. Clifford denied knowing that the transaction reflected secret BCCI control.

The report also reproduced the Federal Reserve’s conclusion that a February 8, 1988 Clifford letter concerning sale of the shares had been written later and backdated. That was an **official investigative conclusion**, not a criminal court finding.

### Money laundering and BCCI’s broader crimes

BCCI was implicated in and prosecuted for extensive financial crime, including money laundering. Its 1991 collapse exposed secret ownership structures and major losses.

It is important to distinguish:

- **Documented fact:** BCCI itself pleaded guilty to federal crimes and forfeited massive assets.
- **Documented fact:** Clifford and his firm represented BCCI and First American and held governing positions.
- **Official finding/allegation:** Clifford knew of or assisted concealed control.
- **Not established:** that Clifford personally knew of every category of BCCI criminal activity, such as all drug-money laundering, terrorism-related transactions, arms deals, or intelligence relationships.

The Senate report itself observed that almost no one below Abedi and Naqvi likely knew the full scope of BCCI’s criminal conduct.

### Resignations

Clifford and Altman resigned from First American in August 1991 amid regulatory pressure and investigations. Their law firm disintegrated as partners and clients departed.

### Congressional testimony

Clifford testified that he had been deceived. The Senate investigation rejected major parts of this account. It also criticized responses to congressional subpoenas and the failure to ensure production of relevant BCCI records.

Again, congressional disbelief is not equivalent to a perjury conviction.

### Federal and state indictments, 1992

On July 29, 1992, federal and New York grand juries indicted Clifford and Altman.

The federal case alleged conspiracy to defraud the Federal Reserve and conceal material facts concerning BCCI’s acquisition and control of American banks. New York charges included fraud, bribery-related, conspiracy, and business-record accusations.

These were **formal charges**, not findings of guilt.

### Health and severance

Clifford’s health deteriorated. He underwent quadruple-bypass surgery in March 1993 and suffered a heart attack. Medical evidence persuaded courts that trial could endanger his life.

The federal government dismissed its indictment without prejudice in April 1993. Clifford did not receive a merits acquittal in that case.

### Altman trial and acquittal

Altman went to trial in New York. During the proceedings the judge dismissed major counts for insufficient evidence. In August 1993 the jury acquitted Altman on the remaining charges.

Altman’s acquittal legally applied to Altman. Clifford and his supporters described it as vindicating Clifford “in absentia,” but that was advocacy, not a legal judgment in Clifford’s case.

### Dismissal of Clifford’s New York charges

On November 30, 1993, Justice John A.K. Bradley dismissed the New York indictment against Clifford on the joint or unopposed recognition that his age and health prevented trial and likely always would.

**Court finding:** Clifford was medically unable to stand trial.  
**Not a court finding:** that he was innocent or guilty of the charged conduct.

### Federal Reserve proceedings and 1998 settlement

The Federal Reserve pursued administrative charges. In February 1998 Clifford and Altman settled:

- without admitting the Federal Reserve’s allegations;
- by transferring interests in most of their CCAH shares;
- with an approximate value of $5 million for BCCI’s innocent depositors and creditors.

The Board dismissed its prohibition action against Clifford because of age and ill health. Altman agreed not to participate in banking without prior Board approval.

Press accounts reported that they retained nearly $15 million in other First American proceeds. Later in 1998 they settled remaining private civil litigation on confidential or separately reported terms, including relinquishment of claims involving legal fees and stock.

### Evidentiary bottom line

The strongest supportable formulation is:

- Clifford was centrally and profitably involved in BCCI’s American bank acquisitions.
- BCCI secretly owned or controlled First American through nominees and financing.
- congressional and Federal Reserve investigators concluded that Clifford knew substantially more and made materially misleading representations;
- he denied that conclusion and said Abedi and BCCI deceived him;
- he was indicted but never tried;
- charges were dismissed because of ill health, not after a merits determination;
- his partner Altman was acquitted;
- Clifford settled regulatory and civil claims without admitting wrongdoing;
- no criminal conviction was entered against Clifford.

## Other Controversies and Criticism

### Revolving-door influence

**Criticism:** Clifford monetized public service by guiding corporations through institutions he knew intimately and by maintaining private presidential access while representing regulated interests.

**Evidence:** His client list, income, White House access, and repeated movement between government and private practice are documented.

**Qualification:** Selling expertise and maintaining political relationships were not themselves illegal. The disagreement concerns conflicts, transparency, and the boundary between legal representation and influence.

### Foreign-agent registration

Clifford’s representation of Algerian oil interests led to Justice Department attention and eventual registration. No criminal adjudication was identified.

### Vietnam responsibility

A later heroic narrative casts Clifford as the man who stopped escalation. Critics answer that he supported the containment framework and administration policy for years and publicly changed course only after Tet.

Both propositions contain documentary truth: he participated in the policy establishment that sustained the war and later played an important part in preventing a larger 1968 escalation.

### Israel and electoral politics

Critics alleged that Clifford subordinated strategic advice to the 1948 election and Jewish electoral support. Clifford denied that political calculations motivated his recognition argument. Archival material establishes that electoral politics and Palestine were discussed in the White House, but it does not establish that political advantage was his sole or controlling motive.

### Presidential power and secrecy

Clifford helped build the national-security state and served in elite advisory bodies largely outside public scrutiny. Later critics of the “imperial presidency” treated this informal system as a democratic-accountability problem. No source consulted establishes that Clifford formulated a comprehensive theory favoring lawless presidential power. His practice favored flexible executive counseling, secrecy in national-security work, and policy formation by small groups of officials.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Liberalism

Clifford’s liberalism combined:

- New Deal–Fair Deal social policy;
- organized-labor and civil-rights coalition politics;
- belief in active federal government;
- strong executive leadership;
- corporate capitalism regulated through federal institutions;
- internationalist anti-isolationism.

He was not a socialist and spent most of his private career representing major corporations.

### Anti-Communism and containment

His 1946 memorandum placed him within early Cold War containment politics. He believed Soviet power should be checked through economic reconstruction, alliances, military strength, and political resistance.

His anti-Communism was institutional and geopolitical rather than populist-McCarthyite. The sources do not show him embracing Senator Joseph McCarthy’s conspiracy politics.

### Evolution on military power

Vietnam altered Clifford’s assumptions about the conversion of American power into political results. By 1969 he argued that:

- military capability could not substitute for a viable political settlement;
- limited aims required limited commitments;
- escalation without a defined endpoint was strategically unsound;
- withdrawal could be necessary without claiming total victory.

### Religion

His childhood Episcopal participation is documented through contemporary recollection. No substantial adult theological writings, sectarian activism, conversion, or religious office were found.

## Health, Habits, and Later Decline

A 1948 profile reported that Clifford tolerated alcohol poorly and became nearly abstinent because of ulcers. This is **reported personal-health information** from contemporary journalism.

In later life he suffered:

- significant heart disease;
- quadruple-bypass surgery in March 1993;
- a subsequent heart attack;
- respiratory difficulty;
- prolonged physical decline;
- approximately three years bedridden before death, according to his daughter.

Sources report that he remained mentally lucid until near death. No reliable evidence was found of addiction, psychiatric hospitalization, or diagnosed psychological crisis.

The destruction of his firm and reputation during BCCI caused evident personal distress. He called the press coverage the most disillusioning part of the ordeal and spent much of his remaining professional energy on his defense.

## Death and Burial

Clifford died at his Bethesda home at approximately 2:00 a.m. on October 10, 1998. The *Washington Post* reported respiratory failure; other immediate reports said the exact cause had initially not been announced but noted heart and respiratory illness.

He was buried at Arlington National Cemetery.

He was survived by Margery, their three daughters, twelve grandchildren, and fifteen great-grandchildren.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The man who elected Truman”

**Story:** Clifford almost single-handedly designed Truman’s 1948 upset.

**Appearance and circulation:** Contemporary political journalism, Clifford’s public reputation, later obituaries, memoir literature, and presidential histories.

**Evidence for:** His forty-three-page memorandum, speech and campaign work, and Truman Library documentation establish a major role.

**Evidence against the simplified version:** The memorandum was collaborative; Truman, James Rowe, party organizations, labor, farmers, civil-rights politics, and Dewey’s campaign all contributed.

**Reputational effect:** Established Clifford as an almost preternaturally effective political counselor.

**Label:** Documented core with hagiographic exaggeration.

### “Architect of the Cold War”

**Story:** Clifford’s 1946 memorandum created American containment.

**Evidence for:** It was an influential early White House synthesis and helped orient Truman’s thinking.

**Evidence against:** Kennan, Acheson, Marshall, Forrestal, Congress, European conditions, and Soviet actions had independent and often prior importance.

**Effect:** Enhanced his reputation as a strategic thinker while obscuring George Elsey’s work.

**Label:** Documented contribution; exaggerated sole-authorship myth.

### “Architect of the Department of Defense”

**Story:** Clifford created the modern Defense Department.

**Evidence for:** He worked extensively on military unification and the National Security Act.

**Evidence against:** The outcome was collectively produced through years of executive, military, and congressional conflict.

**Label:** Documented major role; misleading if treated as sole authorship.

### “Clifford defeated George Marshall and created Israel”

**Story:** Clifford personally overcame Marshall and caused American recognition of Israel.

**Evidence for:** He argued forcefully for recognition at the decisive May 12 meeting and prepared memoranda supporting it.

**Evidence against:** Truman made the decision; Zionist diplomacy, humanitarian pressure, domestic politics, Soviet recognition, State Department planning, and events in Palestine all mattered.

**Effect:** Made Clifford a heroic figure in some pro-Israel narratives and a symbol of politicized foreign policy in hostile accounts.

**Label:** Documented confrontation; mythologized causation.

### “The dove who rescued America from Vietnam”

**Story:** Clifford entered office opposed to the war and forced de-escalation.

**Evidence for:** His 1968 review and later advocacy were important in capping escalation and supporting negotiations.

**Evidence against:** He previously supported administration policy and entered office publicly opposing an immediate unconditional bombing halt.

**Label:** Documented policy conversion; retrospective simplification.

### “The impeccable wise man”

**Story:** Clifford embodied disinterested, incorruptible public service.

**Circulation:** Elite Washington journalism, client culture, memoirs, obituaries, and the title of John Acacia’s biography.

**Evidence for:** Long government service, presidential trust, institutional accomplishments, and a reputation for preparation and discretion.

**Evidence against:** Corporate monetization of access, foreign-client issues, severe BCCI investigative findings, undisclosed or controversial financial arrangements, and regulatory settlement.

**Effect:** The BCCI affair was especially destructive because it contradicted this cultivated identity.

**Label:** Hagiographic public image with documented counterevidence.

### “An innocent elder duped by BCCI”

**Story:** Abedi and Naqvi exploited Clifford’s stature while concealing everything material from him.

**Evidence for:** BCCI deceived regulators, shareholders, auditors, and many insiders; no evidence shows Clifford knew all of its crimes; he consistently denied knowledge; Altman was acquitted.

**Evidence against:** The Senate and Federal Reserve assembled documents, financial arrangements, organizational decisions, and testimony they found incompatible with Clifford’s claimed ignorance.

**Label:** Clifford’s defense; unresolved in criminal-law terms and rejected in material part by official investigators.

### “A convicted BCCI fraudster”

**Story:** Clifford was convicted of fraud or bribery.

**Evidence:** False. He was indicted but never tried or convicted. Charges were dismissed because of health. His regulatory settlement contained no admission.

**Label:** Incorrect hostile mythology.

### Intelligence and conspiracy narratives

BCCI’s relationships with intelligence services and covert networks have produced broad conspiracy narratives linking Clifford to every illicit BCCI operation, Iran–Contra, arms trafficking, terrorism finance, drug trafficking, and the Kennedy assassination.

**What is documented:** Clifford chaired the presidential intelligence advisory board, represented BCCI, and governed First American.

**What is not established:** That he personally directed or knowingly participated in every covert or criminal activity attributed to BCCI, or that he was involved in Kennedy’s assassination or a cover-up.

**Effect:** His combined intelligence résumé and BCCI relationship made him a durable figure in “deep state” and hidden-finance narratives.

**Label:** Documented institutional proximity; expansive conspiracy claims unverified.

### 2024–2026 adversarial-search findings

Dedicated searches combining Clifford’s full name with “controversy,” “scandal,” “allegations,” “lawsuit,” “fraud,” “accusations,” “conspiracy,” and “criticism” for 2024–2026 identified:

- a June 2024 podcast episode revisiting BCCI and Clifford;
- a 2025 Defense Counterintelligence and Security Agency FOIA request seeking background-investigation and BCCI records; the request page reported no responsive documents at the time consulted;
- renewed comparison of Johnson’s 1968 withdrawal and Vietnam policy during 2024 political commentary;
- no new indictment, court judgment, authenticated archive, or official finding materially changing Clifford’s established historical record through September 2, 2026.

A recent online claim or FOIA request is not itself evidence of misconduct.

## Successes and Failures

### Significant successes

- Rapid rise from naval aide to Truman’s principal counselor.
- Major participation in early Cold War policy formulation.
- Legislative work on military unification and the National Security Act.
- Advocacy in the recognition of Israel.
- Strategic work in Truman’s 1948 reelection.
- Creation of a highly profitable Washington regulatory-law practice.
- Service to Kennedy in transition and intelligence oversight.
- Vietnam reassessment and opposition to a much larger 1968 escalation.
- Promotion of negotiations and bombing cessation.
- Long-term influence across four Democratic administrations.

### Significant failures or adverse outcomes

- Participation in the policy establishment that escalated the Vietnam War before his conversion.
- Failure of the 1977 Cyprus mission to secure a lasting settlement.
- Persistent conflict-of-interest questions in private practice.
- Entry into overlapping representations of BCCI, shareholders, First American, and related entities.
- Financial transactions investigators found concealed, conflicted, or misleading.
- Collapse of Clifford & Warnke.
- Indictments and prolonged litigation.
- Loss of reputation, bank positions, firm, and much of his final decade to legal defense.
- Death without a criminal trial capable of producing a merits resolution of the allegations.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Clifford’s reputation remains divided between two documentary narratives.

### Institutional legacy

He is associated with:

- professionalization of White House counsel and policy coordination;
- the Truman Doctrine and containment;
- the Marshall Plan’s political presentation;
- creation of the postwar national-security structure;
- recognition of Israel;
- Truman’s 1948 campaign;
- presidential intelligence oversight;
- the 1968 turn from Vietnam escalation toward negotiation.

### Washington-law legacy

His career helped define the modern Washington lawyer who moves among:

- corporations;
- regulatory agencies;
- Congress;
- the White House;
- foreign governments;
- informal political diplomacy.

The same model generated admiration for expertise and criticism of opaque influence.

### BCCI legacy

BCCI permanently altered readings of his earlier career. Admirers treated it as a tragic late-life deception; investigators and critical biographers saw continuity between elite access, weak conflict controls, secrecy, and personal enrichment.

Because Clifford never stood trial, historical judgment depends on documentary comparison rather than a criminal verdict. The Senate report and Federal Reserve record supply substantial adverse evidence; the Altman acquittal, Clifford’s consistent denial, BCCI’s pervasive deceit, and dismissal of charges prevent a simple claim of adjudicated guilt.

### Archival legacy

The breadth of Clifford’s papers makes him a major documentary portal into:

- Truman administration policy;
- presidential speechwriting;
- early Cold War strategy;
- Israel’s recognition;
- Democratic campaign politics;
- Kennedy and Johnson advisory networks;
- intelligence oversight;
- Vietnam decision-making;
- Washington corporate law;
- BCCI.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted record are included. Mere contemporaneity or institutional overlap is excluded.

### John Fitzgerald Kennedy — documented fact

Clifford became Kennedy’s personal lawyer, defended him in the *Profiles in Courage* authorship controversy in 1957–1958, advised the 1960 campaign and transition, handled Kennedy family legal and real-estate matters, and served on Kennedy’s President’s Foreign Intelligence Advisory Board from 1961. Kennedy made Clifford chairman in April 1963. Clifford subsequently assisted the transition after Kennedy’s assassination.

### David Ben-Gurion — reported/documentary policy connection

Clifford supported rapid American recognition of the Jewish state led by Ben-Gurion in May 1948. The consulted sources establish a direct policy relationship through official communications and recognition, but do not securely establish a personal Clifford–Ben-Gurion meeting. This is therefore a documented governmental connection, not necessarily a personal relationship.

### Chaim Azriel Weizmann — reported fact

Weizmann’s Zionist diplomacy formed part of the recognition campaign in which Clifford was a principal White House advocate. Truman met Weizmann; Clifford participated in White House policy management surrounding Zionist requests. The consulted sources do not conclusively establish a separate substantive Clifford–Weizmann meeting, so the connection is institutional and policy-based.

### Golda Meir — no adequately documented personal connection found

Meir was involved in Israel’s state-building and diplomacy during the period when Clifford advocated recognition, but the sources consulted did not establish a meeting, correspondence, collaboration, or acknowledged personal influence between them. Shared involvement in the same historical issue is insufficient.

### Allen Welsh Dulles — documented institutional connection

Dulles was director of Central Intelligence until November 1961; Clifford joined Kennedy’s Foreign Intelligence Advisory Board in May 1961. Their official responsibilities intersected during the post–Bay of Pigs intelligence review. The record supports institutional contact, but not a close friendship or enduring partnership.

### John Foster Dulles — reported institutional/political connection

As Eisenhower’s secretary of state, Dulles belonged to the foreign-policy establishment with which Clifford, then a leading Democratic adviser and private lawyer, interacted. The consulted sources did not establish a distinctive personal bond, rivalry, or sustained correspondence sufficient for a stronger Atlas entry.

### William Egan Colby — no documented direct connection established

Colby’s CIA career and Clifford’s intelligence-board service overlap institutionally, and Colby later wrote on Vietnam. The consulted sources did not establish a significant direct relationship.

### George Herbert Walker Bush — no significant documented connection established

Bush’s later CIA and presidential service occurred within the same Washington national-security world, but the consulted sources did not document a material personal or professional Clifford–Bush relationship.

### William Joseph Casey — no significant documented connection established

Casey and Clifford belonged to overlapping intelligence, legal, and financial-policy milieus. No substantiated meeting, collaboration, rivalry, or correspondence was identified in the sources used.

### Oliver Laurence North — no significant documented connection established

BCCI and Iran–Contra are sometimes linked in conspiracy literature, but the sources consulted did not establish a direct operational, legal, or personal Clifford–North relationship.

### Adnan Muhammad Khashoggi — allegation or theory; insufficient for a personal connection

Khashoggi appears in wider BCCI and covert-finance narratives. The consulted record did not securely establish a direct, significant Clifford–Khashoggi relationship. No connection is entered beyond noting the recurring theory.

### Mohammad Reza Shah Pahlavi — no documented direct connection found

Clifford’s national-security career overlapped American relations with the shah, but no source consulted established a significant personal meeting or relationship.

### Mohammad Mosaddegh — no documented connection found

Clifford’s public career overlapped the 1953 Iranian coup era, but he was then in private practice, and no source used here establishes a direct connection.

### William Jefferson Clinton and Hillary Diane Rodham Clinton — no significant documented connection found
