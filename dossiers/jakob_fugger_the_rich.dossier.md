# Jakob Fugger the Rich: Research Dossier

**Subject:** Jakob Fugger “the Rich” (1459–1525), Augsburg merchant, mining entrepreneur, financier, landholder, imperial count, and founder

**Prepared:** 30 August 2026

## Scope and Source Method

This dossier reconstructs Jakob Fugger’s life and activities from contemporary or near-contemporary records, archival descriptions, modern scholarship, and institutional historical resources. The evidentiary base is unusually rich for a merchant of his period but uneven: much survives about contracts, accounts, loans, mines, property, political negotiations, foundations, and hostile public criticism; little survives about his private emotions, intimate marriage, daily domestic life, or terminal illness.

The central modern biographical authority remains Götz Freiherr von Pölnitz’s two-volume *Jakob Fugger: Kaiser, Kirche und Kapital in der oberdeutschen Renaissance* (1949–1952), based heavily on the Fugger archive. Mark Häberlein’s later family study, *Die Fugger* (2006), translated and expanded as *The Fuggers of Augsburg* (2012), supplies a more recent social and institutional synthesis. Older documentary studies by Aloys Schulte, Richard Ehrenberg, Jacob Strieder, and Max Jansen remain important because they publish or analyze primary financial records. Their interpretive vocabulary—especially “early capitalism,” “monopoly,” and judgments about character—must be separated from the records they cite.

The official Fugger Foundation site is valuable for the surviving charter, archive, buildings, chronology, and new 2025–2026 fact-checking essays, but it is also the institutional voice of the founder’s descendants and foundations. Its claims are therefore checked against independent scholarship where possible. Wikipedia was used only as an orientation and citation-finding aid, not as the principal authority.

Money figures are normally given in the source’s *Gulden*, *florin*, *Rhenish guilder*, or *ducat*. These units were not perfectly interchangeable across jurisdictions and dates. Modern conversions are inherently model-dependent and are treated separately below.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Jakob Fugger.
- **Common German forms:** Jakob Fugger der Reiche; Jakob Fugger von der Lilie; Jakob II. Fugger; Jakob, der Reiche.
- **English forms:** Jacob Fugger; Jacob Fugger the Rich; Jakob Fugger the Rich; Jacob II Fugger.
- **Dynastic branch:** Fugger *von der Lilie* (“of the Lily”), distinguished from the older Fugger *vom Reh* (“of the Roe/Deer”) branch.
- **Born:** 6 March 1459, Augsburg, Free Imperial City of Augsburg, Holy Roman Empire.
- **Died:** 30 December 1525, Fugger house, Augsburg, aged 66.
- **Burial:** Fugger Chapel, St. Anna, Augsburg.
- **Religion:** Roman Catholic throughout his life. He held minor orders and an early prebend but did not pursue a clerical career.
- **Principal occupations:** merchant; managing partner of a family trading company; mining and metallurgical entrepreneur; banker and sovereign creditor; papal financial intermediary and mint lessee; landholder and lord; political financier; foundation creator.
- **Status and titles:** Augsburg burgher; admitted with his brothers to the merchants’ guild in 1473; raised to imperial nobility on 8 May 1511; made an imperial count on 17 July 1514; lord of Kirchberg, Weißenhorn, and numerous smaller lordships and villages.
- **Spouse:** Sibylle/Sybilla Arzt (also Artzt; later Rehlinger), married in Augsburg on 29 January 1498. No children are documented.
- **Primary business names:** *Ulrich Fugker und gebrudere von Augspurg* (“Ulrich Fugger and Brothers of Augsburg”), formally constituted in 1494; after Ulrich’s death, *Jakob Fugger und Gebrüder Söhne* (“Jakob Fugger and Brothers’ Sons”). The separate Upper Hungarian enterprise is conventionally called the Fugger–Thurzo Company or *Gemeiner Ungarischer (Kupfer-)Handel*.

## Family and Ancestry

### Origins

The family’s documented Augsburg history begins not with banking but with weaving and textile distribution.

- **Grandfather:** Hans Fugger, a master weaver from Graben south of Augsburg, entered Augsburg in 1367. The tax-register notice *fucker advenit* records his arrival. Through marriages to Klara Widolf and, after her death, Elisabeth Gfattermann, guild activity, textile distribution, and civic participation, he accumulated property and position. He joined the directorate of the weavers’ guild in 1386 and thereby the city’s Grand Council.
- **Paternal grandmother:** Elisabeth Gfattermann, Hans Fugger’s second wife. After Hans’s death, she and her sons continued and expanded the business.
- **Father:** Jakob Fugger the Elder (born about 1398; died 1469), merchant, son of Hans. In 1466 he moved from the weavers’ guild into the merchants’ guild and ranked seventh among Augsburg taxpayers. His branch became the Fugger *von der Lilie*.
- **Mother:** Barbara Bäsinger (1419–1497), daughter of the mint master Ulrich Bäsinger and Dorothea Span. After her husband’s death she remained an active head of the family enterprise with her elder sons. Full control passed to the sons only upon her death in 1497.
- **Maternal grandfather:** Ulrich Bäsinger, mint master, whose 1444 bankruptcy and later residence at Schwaz form a cautionary financial counterpoint in the family history.

### Siblings

Jakob was traditionally described as the tenth of eleven children and the youngest surviving son. Exact enumeration varies because some children died young. The most consequential siblings were:

- **Ulrich Fugger (1441–1510):** elder brother, merchant, imperial councillor, and outward head of the company before 1510. He established or deepened the Habsburg connection and represented the firm publicly even as Jakob increasingly determined strategy.
- **Markus Fugger (1448–1478):** cleric; from 1471 a scribe in the papal supplications chancery; canon of Augsburg and provost at Regensburg and Freising. His Roman position helped establish early Curial connections.
- **Georg Fugger (1453–1506):** elder brother and partner, active in the firm’s European expansion.
- **Peter Fugger:** died at Nuremberg in 1473. His death appears to have helped redirect Jakob from a planned clerical career into the business.
- **Andreas and Hans:** older brothers who died young in Venice.
- **Barbara Fugger Meuting:** sister, married Konrad Meuting. Her husband contributed commercial knowledge. Jakob’s 1525 will made provisions for her family.

### Marriage and succession

Jakob married **Sibylle Arzt**, daughter of Augsburg patrician Wilhelm Arzt and Sibylle Sulzer, on 29 January 1498. She was about eighteen; he was nearly thirty-nine. The union brought a dowry, admission into the highest Augsburg social milieu, and eligibility for civic advancement. It also represented the family’s transition from guild merchant status toward patrician and noble standing.

No child of the marriage is securely documented. The lack of direct heirs shaped Jakob’s business organization and repeated thoughts of retirement. In 1512 he admitted four nephews into the firm. His final succession centered on:

- **Anton Fugger (1493–1560):** nephew, designated chief successor and given exceptionally broad authority.
- **Raymund Fugger (1489–1535):** nephew and partner, assigned an advisory/supporting role.
- **Hieronymus Fugger (1499–1538):** nephew/cousin in the succession circle.

Jakob’s testament preserved the business under male-line management. Female and clerical relatives received settlements or could leave capital in the company without management power. This was documented family governance, not a modern corporation with transferable public shares.

Sibylle remarried the merchant Konrad Rehlinger approximately seven weeks after Jakob’s death and subsequently became Protestant. The rapid remarriage is documented; stories that use it to infer the quality of her marriage to Jakob are speculation.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1459–1473

Jakob was ten when his father died in 1469. He was initially intended for the Church and received minor orders and a prebend connected with the collegiate foundation at Herrieden in Middle Franconia. Older biographies sometimes made him a resident canon there. A document publicized in 2009 showed him acting for the family firm in Venice in 1473, aged fourteen; current scholarship therefore treats the Herrieden residence as unproven and his commercial formation as beginning much earlier than formerly assumed.

His childhood household was already prosperous, commercially experienced, and managed by his mother and elder brothers. He was not a poor boy who independently rose from weaving. “Grandson of a peasant” and “grandson of a weaver” are modern compression: the grandfather had indeed migrated from a village and worked as a weaver, but by Jakob’s birth the family belonged to Augsburg’s wealthy merchant stratum.

## Education and Formation, 1473–about 1487

### Venice

From fourteen, Jakob was trained in Venice and spent much of the period 1473–1487 at or through the **Fondaco dei Tedeschi**, the German merchants’ warehouse on the Grand Canal. A stay in Rome is also documented by 1478.

This was practical merchant education rather than a university or humanist curriculum. It included:

- Italian commercial arithmetic and bookkeeping;
- bills of exchange, currency conversion, deposits, and long-distance remittance;
- credit evaluation and partnership practice;
- textile import and export, especially cotton and fustian networks;
- price and political intelligence;
- maritime and overland logistics;
- Italian approaches to patronage, representation, palaces, chapels, and dynastic memory.

The claim that Jakob personally “invented double-entry bookkeeping” is false. The method long predated him in Italy. The Fugger firm adopted and refined Italian accounting practices. His later chief accountant **Matthäus Schwarz** wrote an early German manual of double-entry bookkeeping, but that was Schwarz’s work, not Fugger’s.

The more defensible claim is that Jakob imported and integrated Italian financial, informational, accounting, and representational techniques into a north European mining and sovereign-credit enterprise on an unprecedented scale.

## Early Career: Salzburg, Tyrol, and the Habsburg Connection, about 1480–1494

The firm entered Salzburg mining during the 1480s. Mine owners required large and continuous working capital. Jakob increasingly accepted mining shares (*Kuxe*) or rights to purchase output instead of relying only on unsecured IOUs. This joined lending to commodity control.

From about 1485 he led the Innsbruck factor’s office and concentrated on Tyrolean silver and copper. Loans to Archduke Sigismund (“the Rich in Coin”) and advances for the payment of princely officials were secured not merely by interest but by privileged access to metal at favorable prices, export concessions, fiscal assignments, and influence over the Hall mint and Schwaz mines. In 1488 the firm was handling all silver production from Schwaz, according to the Fugger institutional chronology.

Jakob first met or dealt directly with the future Emperor **Maximilian I** in the late 1480s; the Frankfurt fair of 1489 is traditionally given as their first personal meeting. The crucial political-economic change came in 1490. Tyrolean estates forced the heavily indebted Sigismund to abdicate in favor of Maximilian, who assumed the territory and its obligations. Jakob’s credit had helped produce the financial setting and he emerged with a more powerful, more ambitious debtor.

This was the recurrent Fugger model:

1. advance cash to a ruler or ecclesiastical prince;
2. secure repayment on taxes, mine output, mint revenues, rents, land, or monopolistic privileges;
3. use the privilege to obtain and distribute commodities;
4. use commodity cash flow and deposits to fund additional sovereign credit;
5. use political protection to defend the resulting position.

It created mutual dependence. The Habsburgs depended on Fugger liquidity; Fugger depended on Habsburg enforcement, concessions, and repayment capacity.

## Chronological Life History

### 1494–1502: Formal partnership and the Fugger–Thurzo enterprise

In 1494 the brothers formalized **Ulrich Fugger and Brothers of Augsburg**, specifying partner shares, rights, and duties. Jakob contributed 15,552 guilders under the first agreement. The company was a closed family partnership, not a public joint-stock corporation. A 1502 renewal limited active participation to male family members.

At the same time Jakob partnered with the Kraków merchant and mining engineer **Johann (János/Jan) Thurzo the Elder**. Thurzo possessed technical knowledge, regional connections, and mining rights; Fugger supplied capital and international marketing. Their Upper Hungarian copper concern centered on Neusohl/Besztercebánya (present-day Banská Bystrica, Slovakia). It remained legally distinct from the Augsburg parent firm.

Technical and logistical infrastructure included:

- mining and drainage investment;
- smelting and *Saiger* separation of silver from copper;
- processing works at Neusohl and Moštenica;
- facilities at Fuggerau near Villach in Carinthia and Hohenkirchen near Georgenthal in Thuringia;
- a route across the Jablunkov/Jablunka Pass toward Baltic ports;
- outlets through Venice, Nuremberg, Danzig/Gdańsk, Stettin/Szczecin, Lübeck, Antwerp, and Lisbon.

Upper Hungarian copper reached Portugal and traveled onward in Portuguese trade to West Africa and India. Tyrolean and Hungarian output together gave the Fugger system a dominant—often described as quasi-monopolistic—position in European copper. “Monopoly” should not be read as complete ownership of every mine or sale. Competitors, territorial rulers, the Hanse, Venice, other Augsburg firms, Italian merchants, and miners remained capable of resistance.

The partnership became familial when Georg Thurzo married Jakob’s niece Anna Fugger in 1497.

### 1496–1510: Melchior von Meckau, hidden deposits, and near failure

**Melchior von Meckau**, prince-bishop of Brixen and later cardinal, deposited very large sums secretly with the Fuggers beginning in 1496. Pölnitz states that his deposits exceeded the brothers’ own capital. This outside money was essential to their expansion.

Maximilian attempted in 1498 to combine Fugger, Gossembrot, and Paumgartner interests into a copper syndicate. It lasted about a year. Commercial rivalry and incompatible interests ended it.

When Meckau died suddenly in 1509, Pope Julius II claimed his estate. The Fuggers faced demands against deposits that had been concealed from relevant ecclesiastical authorities. The firm came close to insolvency. Maximilian, needing continued credit during war in Italy, mediated. A settlement involving debt adjustments and jewels enabled the company to survive by 1510.

This episode is important against the mythology of uninterrupted mastery: Fugger’s highly leveraged expansion nearly destroyed the firm. His political network was not ornamental; it functioned as crisis insurance.

### 1500–1508: Curial finance, mints, arms, and overseas trade

The firm used its European network to transfer funds, fees, annates, benefice payments, and indulgence receipts to Rome. It supplied the Curia and handled Jubilees and ecclesiastical collections. The Fuggers participated in papal elections indirectly through finance and remittance; claims that Jakob personally selected popes overstate the evidence.

Under Julius II the company:

- helped recruit and transport the first Swiss Guard to Rome in 1505–1506;
- leased and operated the Roman papal mint, with interruptions, from about 1508 to 1524;
- financed mint repairs and worked with papal coin engravers;
- maintained a Roman branch active in Curial transfers.

The assertion, repeated in popular accounts, that Fugger “loaned the Vatican the money to build the Sistine Chapel” is chronologically and evidentially misleading: the chapel was constructed in the 1470s–1480s before Jakob led the firm. The company did transmit and finance Curial funds and later became entangled in the St. Peter’s indulgence, but the sweeping construction claim is not established in the consulted scholarship.

The firm opened a Lisbon factor’s office by 1503 and received permission from the Casa da Índia to trade pepper, spices, pearls, gems, and luxury goods. It invested about 3,000 guilders in the 1505–1506 Portuguese India voyage involving Augsburg, Nuremberg, and Genoese merchants. This was important symbolically and informationally but small relative to mining and sovereign finance. Jakob did not personally sail to India.

In 1504 he secretly bought famous jewels once belonging to Charles the Bold of Burgundy from Swiss holders. Their acquisition supplied portable wealth and dynastic prestige. Stories that he later kept jewels hidden in his cellar to avoid envy may derive from family and biographical tradition; the purchase is documented, the psychological explanation less so.

### 1506–1514: Sole control, land, buildings, nobility

Georg died in 1506 and Ulrich in 1510. Jakob then became the undisputed strategic head of the firm, renamed for himself and his brothers’ sons.

On 15 July 1507 Maximilian sold him the County of Kirchberg, Weißenhorn, Wullenstetten, Pfaffenhofen, and associated rights for about 50,000 guilders, financing Maximilian’s projected Italian expedition. Jakob also acquired Biberbach and other lordships. Eventually he controlled more than fifty villages or small lordships, depending on the method of counting.

Landownership supplied security, rents, jurisdiction, noble standing, and insulation from urban commercial risk. It also made Fugger a territorial lord over peasants. Sarah Hadry’s study of Kirchberg and Weißenhorn documents the constitutional, manorial, and servile relations. Pölnitz notes that Fugger freed serfs more readily than neighboring nobles in some cases; this does not erase the basic structure of seigneurial dependence.

Maximilian raised him to imperial nobility on 8 May 1511 and to the rank of imperial count on 17 July 1514—the first German merchant so elevated, according to the NDB biography. Noble status protected property and allowed him to operate more effectively in the jurisdictional world of princes.

At Augsburg he commissioned:

- **Fugger Chapel, St. Anna** (begun 1509, substantially completed 1512; consecrated 1518), a family burial chapel modeled on Italian precedents and often identified as the first Renaissance ecclesiastical architecture in Germany. Artists associated with it include Albrecht Dürer, Hans Burgkmair the Elder, Jörg Breu the Elder, and Hans Daucher.
- **Fuggerhäuser** on the Weinmarkt, built or consolidated 1512–1515 and expanded in 1523, combining residence, reception space, and firm headquarters. The Damenhof is often described as Germany’s first secular Renaissance courtyard/building.

The buildings were devotional, dynastic, political, and commercial simultaneously.

### 1511–1517: Interest, church policy, philanthropy, and ill health

An account called **St. Ulrich** appears in the books in 1511 or 1512. It separated or earmarked 10,000 guilders for charitable, religious, and memorial purposes. The 1521 foundation charter later consolidated three projects:

1. the Fugger family chapel and burial foundation at St. Anna;
2. the preaching endowment at St. Moritz;
3. the Fuggerei housing foundation.

These institutions served Catholic intercession for the dead, family memory, urban poor relief, religious instruction, and public representation.

Fugger’s ordinary financial business confronted the canon-law prohibition on usury. He supported **Johannes Eck**, the Ingolstadt theologian, who argued at Bologna in 1515 for the legitimacy of certain five-percent deposit returns or partnership arrangements. Eck did not simply abolish the usury ban, and Fugger did not “legalize interest.” The episode records an organized effort to reconcile commercial finance with scholastic theology.

In 1515 Jakob personally attended the Vienna dynastic congress that arranged Habsburg–Jagiellonian marriages. The marriages later enabled Habsburg succession in Bohemia and Hungary after Louis II’s death at Mohács in 1526. Fugger’s participation also protected Upper Hungarian mining interests.

By 1515 he considered retirement because of overwork, illness, and childlessness, but the firm’s obligations prevented it. In summer 1517, during a visit to newly appointed Prince-Bishop **Christoph von Stadion** at Dillingen, Jakob and the provost of Bamberg suddenly became gravely ill. A contemporary letter of Bernhard Adelmann von Adelmannsfelden dated 19 July 1517 is the sole direct record. One man was said nearly to have drowned on the return trip. Later poison-attempt rumors lack supporting evidence. Natural illness, food poisoning, and deliberate poisoning cannot be distinguished.

### 1513–1518: Albrecht of Brandenburg, indulgence finance, and Luther

The company loaned approximately 20,000 guilders to **Albrecht of Brandenburg**, already archbishop of Magdeburg and administrator of Halberstadt, to cover Curial fees and dispensations required for him to become archbishop and elector of Mainz despite plural officeholding, youth, and deficient canonical qualifications.

Pope Leo X authorized an indulgence campaign associated with the rebuilding of St. Peter’s. Under the financial arrangement, proceeds were divided: Rome received a share for St. Peter’s; Albrecht’s share serviced the Fugger loan. Fugger agents monitored collection chests and transfers. Exact projected and realized totals remain subjects of specialist study; Rainer Kiermayr’s analysis of the surviving accounts cautions against repeating round figures as realized revenue.

**Documented fact:** Fugger credit financed Albrecht’s ecclesiastical acquisition, and the firm participated in collecting and remitting the indulgence revenue used to repay that credit.

**Overstatement:** “Jakob Fugger invented the indulgence sale” or “caused the Reformation.” Indulgences, plural bishoprics, Curial fees, and criticism of them predated him. Nevertheless, this specific credit-and-collection system formed part of the immediate material setting of Luther’s protest.

On 31 October 1517 Martin Luther wrote to Albrecht and circulated the Ninety-Five Theses against abuses of indulgence preaching. In October 1518 Luther was examined by Cardinal Thomas Cajetan in the Fuggerhäuser at Augsburg. The location is documented; there is no evidence Jakob participated in the theological examination, and Pölnitz believed he kept away.

Luther later attacked large merchant companies and monopolies, including the Fuggers. The notorious phrase **“die verdammte Fuckerei”** (“the damned Fuggery/Fugger business”) appears in the polemical environment of 1524. Ulrich von Hutten and other reform or nationalist writers also made Fugger a symbol of concentrated wealth, Roman extraction, and purchased politics.

### 1518–1519: Financing Charles V’s imperial election

Emperor Maximilian died on 12 January 1519. The principal candidates for election as Roman king were his grandson **Charles**, king of Spain; **Francis I of France**; and, less plausibly, **Henry VIII of England**. Seven electors voted: the archbishops of Mainz, Trier, and Cologne; the king of Bohemia; the electors of Saxony and Brandenburg; and the Count Palatine of the Rhine.

The finance is documented in contemporary accounts, though totals vary slightly by source and currency treatment:

- overall commitments or election expenditures: approximately **851,585–851,918 guilders** (often rounded to 852,000);
- Fugger contribution: approximately **543,385–543,585 guilders**;
- Welser contribution: approximately **143,000 guilders**;
- three Italian banks: roughly **55,000 guilders each**, or the remainder after adjustments.

Thus Fugger supplied nearly two-thirds. Funds were assembled and transferred through Frankfurt. Electoral promises included cash, pensions, offices, privileges, and guarantees. Military presence and dynastic calculation also mattered. Charles signed a **Wahlkapitulation** restricting royal conduct and affirming German liberties.

Charles was elected unanimously at Frankfurt on **28 June 1519**. Some popular accounts and one English Wikipedia rendering give 28 July; this is an error. He was crowned Roman king at Aachen in October 1520 and papally crowned emperor at Bologna in 1530.

#### Did Fugger “buy” the election?

- **Documented:** Charles’s agents made very large financial commitments to electors and advisers; Fugger supplied the decisive liquidity.
- **Defensible shorthand:** Fugger financed the payments that secured or reinforced Charles’s electoral coalition.
- **Disputed causal claim:** Henry J. Cohn’s 2001 study, “Did Bribes Induce the German Electors to Choose Charles V as Emperor in 1519?”, argues that the electors’ dynastic, constitutional, geopolitical, and security calculations cannot be reduced to bribes. Frederick the Wise of Saxony was reportedly offered the crown and declined; Charles had the strongest hereditary position and forces near Frankfurt.
- **Conclusion:** money was a major instrument and perhaps a necessary condition, but the proposition that seven votes were mechanically purchased by Jakob alone is a later simplification.

Fugger’s motive was not merely loyalty. Maximilian had left large unpaid debts—figures around 170,000 for particular claims and roughly 350,000 in broader estimates appear in modern sources. A French victory could have endangered Habsburg repayment and Fugger concessions. Financing Charles protected existing exposure while creating far larger exposure.

### 1520–1523: Imperial debt, Spanish integration, and the monopoly cases

Fugger representatives attended Charles’s coronation at Aachen and the Diet of Worms in 1521. Jakob himself avoided a recorded position in Luther’s hearing. His relations with papal nuncio Girolamo Aleandro were cool but did not become a break with Rome.

On 4 May 1521 Charles settled or restructured major obligations, bringing the company more deeply into Spanish finance and increasing dealings with Charles’s brother Ferdinand. Habsburg debts were secured through Tyrolean mining revenues, Spanish Crown revenues, and later the *Maestrazgos* leases.

At the Diets of Nuremberg in 1522–1523, estates and imperial fiscal officials attacked large merchant companies for monopoly practices, excessive capital concentration, and price manipulation. Proceedings or threatened proceedings concerned the Fuggers and other Augsburg firms.

Jakob’s often-quoted 1523 letter to Charles stated in substance that it was publicly known His Imperial Majesty could not have obtained the Roman crown without Fugger’s help. The letter demanded protection and payment. It is evidence of forceful creditor self-assertion, not proof that he commanded Charles.

Charles ordered proceedings stopped and the records transferred; according to Pölnitz, the files disappeared after reaching Spain. Fugger offered support for reform of the Imperial Chamber Court. The Reichstag suspended the monopoly dispute in 1524. There was no criminal conviction. The sequence is documented political intervention by a debtor sovereign on behalf of a major creditor.

### 1524–1525: Peasants’ War, mining unrest, Hungary, Spain, and Pavia

During the German Peasants’ War, rebels condemned the Fugger company and concentrated capital. Fugger financially supported the Swabian League and threatened princes in counteraction. These forces violently suppressed the revolt. The available record establishes financial and political support; it does not show Jakob personally commanding massacres.

His own lordships escaped the worst destruction. Weißenhorn’s loyalty may have been encouraged by Fugger support for its textile interests. Pölnitz reports that he emancipated some serfs more generously than neighboring nobles. Both the paternal policy and his support for princely repression belong in the record.

Tyrolean miners faced dangerous work, long hours, payment insecurity, and resentment of Fugger privileges. Disturbances affected Tyrol and central German mines. In Upper Hungary the company reached a compromise with workers in 1525, but political-national opposition escalated. Royal authorities or crowds stormed the Fugger house at Buda/Ofen, seized money and goods, and tried to force a submission agreement. Jakob rejected it, organized a blockade against expropriated copper, and mobilized Charles V, Ferdinand, Pope Clement VII, the Polish king, and German princes. The dispute was unresolved when he died.

In 1524 critics in Hungary accused the company of deliberate coin debasement. King Louis II initially defended Fugger; the accusation fed the 1525 conflict. The consulted sources record the charge but do not establish a judicial finding that Jakob had criminally debased coinage.

The company also supplied cash for Charles’s Italian war. Pölnitz attributes material importance to Fugger funds in the imperial victory over Francis I at **Pavia** in February 1525. This financial connection is documented, though “Fugger won Pavia” is rhetorical compression.

From 1 January 1525 Charles leased the Fuggers revenues and properties of the three Spanish military orders—**Santiago, Calatrava, and Alcántara**—the *Maestrazgos*. The package brought access to Almadén’s cinnabar and mercury mines and other revenues. The Crown had absorbed the masterships; the lease was security and repayment for Habsburg debt.

### Final months and death

Jakob believed death was near from at least 1521, when he issued a first testament and the foundation charter. His health had been described as weakened by illness and overwork since 1515. No reliable diagnosis, addiction, psychiatric crisis, or terminal cause is documented.

On 28 December 1525 he executed a second testament addressing the business and succession. He died in his Augsburg house on 30 December. Anton assumed leadership the next morning under the authority Jakob had prescribed.

No substantiated evidence consulted supports murder or poisoning at death. The 1517 Dillingen illness later supplied material for poison legends and modern historical fiction, but it occurred eight years earlier.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Business architecture

The Fugger system was a family partnership with centralized strategy and geographically dispersed factors. It was not a deposit bank in the modern regulated sense, not a corporation with limited liability, and not a secret society. Its principal mechanisms included:

- partners’ capital and retained profits;
- deposits from princes, bishops, cardinals, nobles, and other wealthy investors;
- bills of exchange and currency arbitrage;
- loans secured on taxes, customs, mines, mint output, rents, land, and offices;
- advance purchase of metal output;
- equity-like mining shares;
- commodity trade and warehousing;
- government and papal remittance;
- privileged leases and concessions;
- a trans-European information and courier network.

### Principal sectors

| Salzburg mining | Gastein, Rauris, Schladming region | loans converted into shares/output control | Early demonstration of the mining-credit model. |

### Technology and management

Fugger was not an inventor in the patent sense. No patent is known. His importance lay in financing, combining, scaling, and managing technologies and systems:

- *Saiger* metallurgical separation of silver from copper, with Thurzo expertise;
- large smelting works near transport and fuel;
- mine drainage and capital-intensive exploitation;
- Italian bookkeeping and periodic partnership accounts;
- separated foundation accounts;
- standardized reporting from factors;
- courier and commercial intelligence routes;
- vertically coordinated production, processing, transport, and sale.

“Fugger Newsletters” are often called an ancestor of newspapers. The famous surviving *Fuggerzeitungen* chiefly belong to later sixteenth-century descendants, especially Philipp Eduard and Octavian Secundus Fugger. Jakob unquestionably used systematic commercial correspondence and intelligence; attributing the later surviving newsletter collection directly to him is anachronistic.

## Wealth and Financial Reconstruction

### Contemporary-account figures

The official Fugger historical staff’s 2026 review, based on partnership and inventory records, separates company capital from Jakob’s personal share:

- **1494:** Jakob’s partnership contribution, 15,552 guilders.
- **1511 settlement:** total trading capital, 196,791 guilders; Jakob’s share, 80,999.
- **1521–1527 accounting period:** company profit about 2.1 million guilders.
- **Jakob’s capital and profit share in the 1527 inventory:** 809,825 guilders.
- **Personal withdrawals/expenses:** 142,035 guilders.
- **Net share attributed to the deceased:** 667,790 guilders.

Older summaries, using the same 1527 inventory differently, give company assets of 3,000,058, liabilities of 867,797, and net assets of 2,132,261 guilders; NDB gives a cautious company valuation around two million and says 1.8 million had been earned by Jakob during his last seventeen years. These are company or partnership figures, not simply coins personally owned by Jakob.

The differences arise from whether writers mean gross assets, net company capital, cumulative profit, the deceased partner’s account, later settlement values, or the whole family enterprise. A single “net worth” number conceals these categories.

### Modern conversions

Three common conversion methods produce radically different answers:

1. **Metal content:** at about 2.5 grams of gold per Rhenish guilder, 667,790 guilders corresponds to approximately 1,669 kilograms of gold. Using a 2016 gold price, the Fugger Foundation calculated about €60.5 million. This measures bullion equivalence, not economic power.
2. **Wage equivalence:** a skilled craftsman might earn about fifty guilders annually around 1520. Jakob’s net share represented roughly 13,356 craft-years of gross wages. Conversion using current skilled wages produces a much higher modern figure than bullion.
3. **Share of economic output:** Greg Steinmetz estimated Fugger’s fortune at just under two percent of European GDP, yielding about US$400 billion in 2015 terms. Other publicity claims use roughly ten percent of the Holy Roman Empire’s output.

The **two-percent/$400-billion figure is a modern estimate popularized by Steinmetz, not a contemporary account and not an academic consensus measurement**. Estimating European GDP in 1525 requires reconstructed population, output, price, and territorial assumptions. It also risks comparing an illiquid partnership balance sheet and sovereign receivables with annual output.

The strongest defensible conclusion is narrower: Jakob was probably Europe’s richest private merchant-financier in his final years, and his capital was extraordinarily large relative to contemporary wages and public revenues. Whether he was “the richest man of all time” depends on a chosen model and comparison set. Rulers such as Mansa Musa controlled state resources not readily separable from personal property; modern billionaires own priced equity in much larger economies. Exact rank is unresolved.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Political alignment

Jakob consistently aligned the firm with the Habsburg house—Sigismund, Maximilian I, Charles V, and Ferdinand—while bargaining hard, withholding money from projects he considered unsound, and sometimes exploring alternatives. The relationship was neither personal servitude nor unilateral control.

He financed:

- Maximilian’s court and wars against France, Venice, and the Swiss;
- artillery raw material, textiles, and arms;
- the 1515 Habsburg–Jagiellonian marriage settlement;
- the 1519 imperial election;
- Habsburg action in Württemberg;
- defense against the Ottomans;
- Charles’s Italian campaign culminating at Pavia;
- princely suppression during the Peasants’ War.

He did not seek to rule Augsburg in the Medici fashion. He held some civic offices and acquired status through marriage, but his political leverage was exercised mainly through finance, property, court connections, and ecclesiastical patronage.

### Catholicism

Fugger’s Catholic profession appears explicitly in his foundation charter and testament. His devotional program emphasized Masses, burial, intercessory prayer, preaching, and care for “deserving” poor Catholics. The Fuggerei required residents to be Augsburg citizens, Catholic, needy through no fault of their own, and to say the Lord’s Prayer, Creed, and Hail Mary daily for the founders.

His faith and finance were integrated rather than compartmentalized. Foundation income came from commercial capital; prayers repaid benefaction symbolically; family memory was secured in architecture. Benjamin Scheller interprets these foundations through late-medieval *memoria* across the Reformation divide.

He remained Catholic as Augsburg moved toward Protestantism. Yet no evidence shows him acting as a theologian. He sponsored Eck on interest doctrine because the issue directly affected the company. He avoided personally entering the Luther–Cajetan dispute.

### Art and representation

Fugger commissioned or supported artists including Dürer, Burgkmair, Breu, Daucher, and Hans Maler. Surviving portraits include Dürer’s famous image, Holbein the Elder’s silverpoint drawings, Burgkmair’s woodcut, and representations connected to the chapel.

His Italian experience influenced Renaissance architectural patronage, although older claims that he lacked humanist interests reflect Pölnitz’s interpretation rather than a directly measurable fact. There is no substantial authored humanist or philosophical corpus.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### 1. Monopoly and market domination

**Documented facts:** The company sought concentrated control over Tyrolean and Upper Hungarian metal output, secured preferential purchase terms through loans, built processing and distribution infrastructure, and participated in syndicates. It held a dominant European copper position.

**Legal status:** Imperial fiscal and Reichstag proceedings in 1522–1524 targeted alleged monopoly abuses. Charles V intervened, records were transferred, and the case was suspended. No conviction is documented.

**Unresolved question:** How closely behavior met contemporaneous legal definitions of prohibited monopoly varies by contract, market, and jurisdiction. “Monopoly” is accurate as a political accusation and useful as a description of ambition; literal total market exclusion is not established.

### 2. Usury and interest concealment

Canon law prohibited usury, but merchant partnerships, exchange contracts, rent purchases, risk premia, and deposits created lawful or tolerated routes to returns. Fugger supported Eck’s defense of five-percent arrangements. Critics saw evasion; supporters framed them as legitimate partnership or compensation.

There is no known criminal conviction of Jakob for usury. The controversy was theological, legal, and economic, not an anachronistic modern consumer-lending case.

### 3. Imperial-election payments

The 1519 payment system is documented. Calling it “corruption” applies a modern general category to a system in which electoral gifts, pensions, and concessions were expected, but even contemporaries recognized the unprecedented scale and bargaining. The amounts were not a myth. Their exclusive causal power is disputed.

### 4. Papal finance and indulgences

Fugger financed Albrecht’s fees and his agents participated in indulgence collections. This is a direct, documented financial relationship. Claims that he invented indulgences, alone financed St. Peter’s, or intended a Protestant schism are unsupported.

### 5. Secret Meckau deposits

The cardinal’s large undisclosed deposits were real and created a succession dispute after his death. The episode raises questions about concealment of ecclesiastical wealth and creditor priority. The firm nearly failed; Maximilian’s intervention enabled settlement. No “money-laundering” offense existed in the modern legal sense, and applying that term would be anachronistic. The closest documentary description is concealed beneficial ownership of clerical funds and contested estate liability.

### 6. Political protection and disappearance of monopoly files

Charles ordered proceedings against the company stopped and documents moved to Spain, after which Pölnitz reports they disappeared. This supports an inference of political protection. The record does not establish that Jakob ordered destruction, bribed a judge, or committed a separately proven criminal offense.

### 7. Coin debasement accusation in Hungary

Hungarian opponents accused the company in 1524 of planned or deliberate currency debasement. It became part of the political-economic struggle that led to seizures in 1525. The consulted evidence does not show a final judicial determination. Classification: **documented contemporary accusation; unresolved in the cited summaries; not established fact**.

### 8. Labor conditions and repression

Tyrolean mining involved hazardous conditions, long hours, and insecure pay. Miners resisted Fugger privileges. The company compromised with some Upper Hungarian workers in 1525 while supporting armed princely responses elsewhere.

The Almadén mercury concession began in Jakob’s last year. Mercury mining later became notorious for poisoning and coercive labor. Consulted chronologies place formal convict labor there chiefly from the mid-eighteenth century, long after the Fugger lease and Jakob’s death. It is inaccurate to attribute that later penal system personally to Jakob without specific evidence. The Fugger enterprise did, however, initiate a long company association with an inherently toxic mine.

### 9. Peasants’ War and political violence

Fugger supported the princes and Swabian League that violently suppressed the 1524–1525 revolt. This is documented political and financial complicity in counterinsurgency. Evidence of direct tactical command or personally ordered killings has not been found.

### 10. Criminality, fraud, and money laundering

No documented criminal conviction for fraud, corruption, money laundering, or violent crime is known. “Money laundering” is not a historically appropriate legal description of ordinary sixteenth-century remittance or concealed clerical deposits. The relevant documented acts are preferential contracting, secret deposits, electoral payments, influence over proceedings, disputed monopoly practices, and contested currency policy.

### 11. Intelligence connections

The firm maintained factors, couriers, correspondence, and political-commercial reporting across Europe. This was an intelligence network in the ordinary early-modern mercantile sense. It supplied faster information about wars, prices, courts, shipping, and credit. No evidence supports membership in a clandestine intelligence order or a proto-modern state secret service.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The richest man who ever lived”

1. **Story:** Fugger owned wealth equal to about two percent of European GDP—roughly $400 billion in 2015—and exceeded Rockefeller and all other private fortunes.
2. **Appearance and circulation:** Popularized internationally by Greg Steinmetz’s 2015 biography and repeated by news, finance, museum, and social-media accounts.
3. **Evidence:** The 1527 inventory documents an enormous company fortune and Jakob’s share. The GDP ratio is a reconstruction, not an entry in the inventory.
4. **Against:** Early-modern GDP and the boundary between company, family, personal, and sovereign-secured assets are uncertain. Alternative bullion and wage conversions differ by orders of magnitude.
5. **Effect:** It restored Fugger to English-language popular history and turned him into an archetype of concentrated capital.

### “He bought Charles V the empire”

1. **Story:** Fugger paid bribes and thereby personally chose the emperor.
2. **Appearance:** Rooted in contemporary criticism and strengthened by Reformation polemic; repeatedly dramatized in biographies, television, and journalism.
3. **Evidence:** Election accounts show roughly 852,000 guilders, of which Fugger supplied about 543,000.
4. **Against/qualification:** The electors also acted from dynastic interest, constitutional bargaining, fear of France, and military pressure. Cohn disputes bribery as a sufficient causal explanation.
5. **Effect:** “Kaisermacher” became Fugger’s defining public epithet and a durable image of money controlling politics.

### “He caused the Protestant Reformation”

1. **Story:** Fugger loans forced the sale of indulgences, provoking Luther and splitting Christianity.
2. **Appearance:** Retrospective histories linking the Albrecht loan, Tetzel, the Ninety-Five Theses, and Luther’s Augsburg examination.
3. **Evidence:** Fugger’s financial role in Albrecht’s accession and indulgence collection is real.
4. **Against/qualification:** The Reformation had extensive theological, institutional, political, technological, and social causes. Indulgences predated Fugger.
5. **Effect:** The story casts finance as the hidden mechanism beneath religious revolution.

### “Inventor of modern banking/accounting/news”

1. **Story:** Jakob invented interest, double-entry bookkeeping, banking, or the newspaper.
2. **Appearance:** Business journalism and promotional biographies.
3. **Evidence:** He scaled Italian accounting, trans-European secured credit, and commercial intelligence; Matthäus Schwarz worked for him.
4. **Against:** Bills, deposits, double-entry accounts, and merchant newsletters had earlier Italian and Mediterranean histories. The famous Fugger newsletters are mainly later.
5. **Effect:** Fugger becomes a solitary capitalist genius rather than an organizer working inside older commercial traditions.

### The Templar-banking connection

1. **Story:** The Fugger system continued or inherited the Knights Templar’s suppressed international bank, treasure, secrets, or institutional network.
2. **Appearance:** Broad “Templars-to-modern-bankers” narratives and conspiracy literature. No specific early or archival Fugger source for a direct succession was located.
3. **Evidence for a connection:** Only indirect analogies: both handled elite deposits and long-distance transfers under religious-political patronage. In 1525 Fugger leased revenues of the Spanish military orders of Santiago, Calatrava, and Alcántara. These were Crown-controlled crusading orders. Separately, the Order of Montesa had inherited some former Templar property in Valencia, but Montesa was not one of the three *Maestrazgos* in the Fugger lease identified by the consulted sources.
4. **Evidence against direct descent:** The Templars were suppressed in 1312, more than a century before Jakob’s birth. The documented Fugger trajectory runs from Augsburg weaving and textile trade through Venice, mining, Habsburg credit, and Curial finance. No transfer of Templar personnel, capital, charter, archives, or organizational identity to the Fuggers is documented.
5. **Effect:** The association adds crusading secrecy and antiquity to a well-documented but less mysterious merchant rise. **Classification: unsupported institutional-continuity claim; legitimate comparative topic; real but later and indirect military-order revenue connection.**

### Poisoning at Dillingen

1. **Story:** Enemies attempted to poison Jakob in 1517.
2. **Appearance:** Later rumor noted by Pölnitz; revived in the 2025 Augsburg stage/interactive *Tatort* story *Walsturz*.
3. **Evidence:** Adelmann’s contemporary letter documents sudden grave illness of Jakob and another cleric.
4. **Against:** The letter gives no poison, culprit, or motive. No corroborating criminal record is known.
5. **Effect:** It dramatizes the danger surrounding a powerful and hated financier. **Classification: illness documented; deliberate poisoning unverified.**

### “He ruled kings and popes”

The company had exceptional leverage, but debt created reciprocal risk. Maximilian sometimes ignored Fugger; Leo X favored Italian bankers; Charles delayed repayment; Hungarian rulers seized company property; Hanseatic and Venetian rivals obstructed him; and Meckau’s death nearly bankrupted the firm. “More powerful than kings” is reputation-making rhetoric, not a stable constitutional reality.

## Works by Jakob Fugger

Jakob wrote no known book, treatise, memoir, philosophical work, or literary text. His documentary corpus is administrative and legal. Much was drafted by secretaries but issued under his authority.

### Genuine or institutionally attributable documents

- **Partnership agreements**, especially the 1494 articles for *Ulrich Fugker und gebrudere von Augspurg* and the 1502 renewal. Genuine company instruments jointly issued by partners.
- **Commercial correspondence and instructions** to factors, partners, courts, and debtors, dispersed through the Fugger Family and Foundation Archive and state archives. Authorship is institutional; many letters were dictated or secretary-drafted.
- **Contracts and loan instruments** with Sigismund, Maximilian I, Charles V, Ferdinand, Albrecht of Brandenburg, the Curia, mining partners, and territorial estates.
- **Letter to Charles V, 1523**, often quoted for the declaration that the emperor could not have obtained the Roman crown without Fugger’s aid. Genuine creditor petition/letter; wording should be checked against the archival edition before extended quotation.
- **Foundation charter, 23 August 1521**, issued also in the names of deceased brothers Ulrich and Georg, consolidating the Fuggerei, St. Anna chapel, and St. Moritz preaching endowment. Genuine legal foundation instrument.
- **First testament, 1521.** Genuine testamentary instrument.
- **Second/final testament, 28 December 1525.** Genuine testamentary instrument establishing succession and bequests.
- **Account authorizations, inventories, ledgers, memoranda, and settlements.** Company records, not solely personal authorship. The famous 1527 inventory was drawn up after his death under Anton and must not be listed as a work “by” Jakob.

### Works wrongly or loosely attributed

- **The Fugger Newsletters / *Fuggerzeitungen*.** The later compiled news collection belongs primarily to the operations and collecting of later Fuggers, not to a book authored by Jakob.
- **Matthäus Schwarz’s bookkeeping treatise.** Written by Schwarz, Jakob’s accountant.
- **The Secret Book of Honor of the Fugger Family / *Geheimes Ehrenbuch*.** Commissioned around 1545 by Johann Jakob Fugger, two decades after Jakob the Rich’s death.

## Death and Immediate Aftermath

Jakob died without children, in the middle of the Upper Hungarian crisis, two days after executing his final testament. Cause of death is unrecorded. His burial in the Catholic Fugger Chapel at St. Anna created an unusual later arrangement: after St. Anna became Protestant, the chapel remained a Catholic foundation within a Protestant church.

Anton Fugger inherited leadership rather than merely a static fortune. Under Anton the firm reached a higher nominal asset peak—often placed above five million guilders by 1546—and deepened Habsburg, Spanish, and colonial-era finance. Later Spanish Crown bankruptcies in 1557, 1560, 1575, and subsequent moratoria damaged the family firm. Those failures postdate Jakob and should not be projected backward as his personal ruin.

Sibylle’s remarriage and Protestant conversion separated her from the Catholic dynastic memory program Jakob had built. The foundations and male-line management structure proved more durable than the marriage.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Sixteenth-century memory

Reformation polemic made “Fugger” a synonym for monopoly, usury, Roman finance, and wealth that escaped the traditional estates. Catholic and family memorial culture cast Jakob as a divinely favored merchant and perpetual benefactor. Both images began during or immediately after his life.

The family’s commissioned genealogies and portraits reinforced noble antiquity and continuity. The later *Geheimes Ehrenbuch* carefully staged the dynasty’s ascent. These are historical artifacts of self-fashioning, not neutral biographies.

### Economic-historical reinterpretation

Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century historians made Fugger a central figure in debates over the origins of capitalism. Ehrenberg, Sombart, Strieder, and Weber-era scholarship variously emphasized capital accumulation, rational accounting, monopoly, religious ethics, and sovereign finance.

Later scholarship qualified the solitary-genius model by emphasizing:

- the prior work of Hans, Jakob the Elder, Barbara, Ulrich, and Georg;
- Italian commercial precedents;
- outside depositors such as Meckau;
- Thurzo engineering and Central European labor;
- Habsburg protection and reciprocal dependence;
- family marriage strategy and civic institutions;
- the survival of medieval lordship and piety inside commercial modernization.

### Fuggerei and foundations

The Fuggerei remains the oldest continuously operating social-housing settlement commonly identified in the world. Its founding charter is dated 23 August 1521. The annual nominal base rent remains one Rhenish guilder, conventionally represented as €0.88, plus the prayer obligations. It has expanded to 67 houses and roughly 140 apartments. The continuity is real, though the foundation’s finances, buildings, regulations, and revenue sources have changed over five centuries.

The settlement was badly damaged in the February 1944 bombing of Augsburg and rebuilt. The continued Catholic eligibility rule makes it simultaneously a social-welfare institution and a confessional memorial foundation.

### Architecture and art

The Fugger Chapel and Fuggerhäuser are landmarks in the movement of Renaissance forms north of the Alps. Portraits by or associated with Dürer, Holbein, Burgkmair, Breu, Daucher, and Hans Maler fixed the visual image of the composed merchant in a gold cap.

### Honors and commemoration

- A bust was placed in the **Walhalla** near Regensburg in 1967.
- Augsburg museums, streets, tours, schools, and monuments maintain his public presence.
- The 500th anniversary of his death in 2025 generated exhibitions, publications, theatre, tours, lectures, and a commemorative Mass extending into 2026.

### Durable historical significance

The documentary legacy is not that Fugger single-handedly created capitalism. It is that he integrated family partnership capital, clerical deposits, mining technology, commodity distribution, secured sovereign lending, political protection, accounting, information, property, art patronage, and perpetual foundations at an exceptional scale. The same records that show innovation and philanthropy also show electoral payments, market concentration, dangerous labor, confessional exclusion, political protection, and financing of war and repression.

## Chronology

- **1367:** Grandfather Hans Fugger arrives in Augsburg from Graben.
- **1459, 6 March:** Jakob Fugger born in Augsburg.
- **1469:** Father Jakob Fugger the Elder dies; Barbara and elder sons manage the business.
- **1473:** Peter Fugger dies; fourteen-year-old Jakob is documented in family business activity in Venice; brothers receive lily arms and enter merchants’ guild.
- **1473–about 1487:** Commercial formation centered on Venice; documented in Rome by 1478.
- **1480s:** Family enters Salzburg mining.
- **about 1485:** Jakob leads Innsbruck activity and begins Tyrolean metal finance.
- **1488:** Fugger firm handles Schwaz silver output.
- **1489:** Traditional date for Jakob’s first personal meeting with Maximilian at Frankfurt.
- **1490:** Sigismund abdicates Tyrol to Maximilian amid debt and estate pressure.
- **1491:** Major lending relationship with Maximilian expands.
- **1494:** Formal partnership *Ulrich Fugger and Brothers*; Fugger–Thurzo Upper Hungarian venture develops.
- **1495:** New smelting/processing works demonstrate integrated mining strategy.
- **1496:** Melchior von Meckau becomes a major secret depositor.
- **1497:** Mother Barbara dies; brothers gain full control; niece Anna marries Georg Thurzo.
- **1498, 29 January:** Jakob marries Sibylle Arzt.
- **1498–1499:** Maximilian’s forced copper syndicate briefly joins rival Augsburg firms.

## Atlas Connections

### Anton Fugger

- **[T3]** **Documented fact:** Jakob Fugger and his nephew Anton were parties to the 1512 written partnership agreement establishing *Jakob Fugger und seiner Brüder Söhne*. Jakob’s final testament of 28 December 1525 then designated Anton as the company’s principal governor; Anton assumed leadership after Jakob’s death on 30 December. The surviving contractual and testamentary instruments constitute direct written engagement between them. [Max Planck Institute study of the family partnership](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_3564070_2/component/file_3564071/content); [Deutsche Biographie](https://www.deutsche-biographie.de/sfz68354.html?language=en)

### Martin Luther

- **[T3]** **Documented fact:** Luther engaged Jakob Fugger in print, although no reciprocal correspondence or personal meeting is documented. In *To the Christian Nobility of the German Nation* (Wittenberg, 1520), Luther explicitly attacked “Fugger of Augsburg” as the commercial intermediary through whom Roman ecclesiastical offices and revenues had become entangled with trade; his 1524 *On Commerce and Usury* continued the broader attack on great merchant companies and monopoly. Jakob therefore appears here as the object of one-sided contemporary written engagement. [Luther’s 1520 text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/34904/pg34904-images.html); [Deutsche Biographie](https://www.deutsche-biographie.de/sfz68354.html?language=en)
- **[T9+]** **Documented fact:** Jakob built and owned the Fuggerhäuser in Augsburg before Luther’s examination there by Cardinal Cajetan, 12–14 October 1518. The encounter occurred in Jakob’s property, but the biographical evidence says Jakob kept away from the proceedings; the building, rather than shared occupancy or a demonstrated meeting, is therefore the connector. [Deutsche Biographie](https://www.deutsche-biographie.de/sfz68354.html?language=en)

### Max Weber

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** More than three centuries after Fugger’s death, Weber discussed him in *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (first published 1904–1905). Weber used a reported Fugger declaration that he intended to continue making money as long as possible, contrasting Fugger’s commercially daring but—in Weber’s interpretation—ethically neutral acquisitiveness with Benjamin Franklin’s moralized capitalist vocation. [Weber’s text](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:The_Protestant_ethic_and_the_spirit_of_capitalism.djvu/71)

### Joseph Schumpeter

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Schumpeter wrote about Jakob Fugger in chapter 11, “The Civilization of Capitalism,” of *Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy* (1942). He invoked Fugger’s career to dispute exaggerated claims that great financiers directed sovereign policy, arguing that Fugger obtained concessions but had little control over the policies of Charles V or Leo X. [Schumpeter’s text](https://ipmall.law.unh.edu/sites/default/files/BAYHDOLE/3_DIV_SCAN/3094_001_OCR_DBL_ZIP_0.pdf); [publication information](https://www.routledge.com/Capitalism-Socialism-and-Democracy/Schumpeter/p/book/9780415107624)

No additional roster member produced a sufficiently specific, documented crossing under the six permitted connection types. In particular, contemporaneity, activity in Fugger-controlled mines or markets, later comparison with Fugger, and generic references to the Fugger family were not treated as connections without person-specific evidence.
