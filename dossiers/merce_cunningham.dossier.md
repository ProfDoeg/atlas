# Merce Cunningham: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier reconstructs the documented life, career, relationships, institutions, methods, disputes, and posthumous history of Merce Cunningham. It distinguishes contemporaneous or archival evidence from later recollection, criticism, allegation, and popular mythology.

The surviving record is exceptionally rich concerning Cunningham’s choreography, professional organizations, collaborators, performances, and artistic methods. It is less complete concerning his private finances, medical history, military status during World War II, and the detailed distribution of his cremated remains. No substantiated record was found of criminal proceedings, fraud, corruption, money laundering, intelligence activity, political violence, patent ownership, or personal business investments.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Professional name:** Merce Cunningham.
- **Full name:** Usually given as **Mercier Philip Cunningham** in regional historical sources; some reference works give **Merce Philip Cunningham**. “Merce” was his lifelong familiar and professional name.
- **Born:** April 16, 1919.
- **Birthplace:** Centralia, Lewis County, Washington, United States.
- **Died:** July 26, 2009, aged ninety.
- **Place of death:** His home in New York City.
- **Occupation:** Dancer, choreographer, teacher, company director, author, filmmaker/video collaborator, and artistic director.
- **Principal institution:** Merce Cunningham Dance Company, initially styled “Merce Cunningham and Dance Company.”
- **Partner:** Composer John Milton Cage Jr., Cunningham’s artistic collaborator and life partner until Cage’s death in 1992.
- **Children:** None documented.
- **Principal residences and working bases:** Centralia; Seattle; New York City; recurring summer residencies at Black Mountain College, North Carolina; Westbeth Artists Housing in Greenwich Village from 1971.
- **Known personal nicknames or pseudonyms:** No documented pseudonym. “Merce” functioned as both nickname and professional name.
- **Output:** Approximately 180–190 choreographed dances, depending on cataloguing rules, and roughly 700–800 adaptable performance “Events.” The [Merce Cunningham Trust](https://www.mercecunningham.org/about/merce-cunningham/) describes 180 dances and nearly 800 Events; earlier biographies sometimes give 190 dances and more than 700 Events.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

Cunningham was the son of:

- **Clifford D. Cunningham** (1883–1963), a Centralia lawyer.
- **Mayme Joach Cunningham** (1887–1976), described in secondary sources as a former teacher and as a woman of pronounced independence who periodically traveled abroad without her husband.

Regional historian Paula Becker identifies Cunningham as one of three sons. Both brothers followed their father into law; their names and full vital details are inconsistently reported in readily accessible institutional sources and are therefore not asserted here.

The family was financially stable enough to support theatrical and dance training, though Cunningham did not come from an artistic dynasty. His father reportedly chose small-town practice because it permitted him to handle many kinds of law. He supported Merce’s decision to study theater and dance, including tuition at Cornish.

### The Centralia Massacre connection

**Documented fact:** Clifford Cunningham acted as a special prosecutor in the trial arising from the violent November 1919 confrontation between American Legion marchers and members of the Industrial Workers of the World, commonly called the Centralia Massacre. Merce was an infant and had no role in the case. The episode is relevant to his family environment, not evidence of his own political beliefs or activities. [HistoryLink](https://historylink.org/file/9042) records the connection.

### Ancestry and religion

Reference works describe Clifford Cunningham as of Irish ancestry. A complete genealogical reconstruction was not located in the consulted sources. The Cunningham family attended church in Centralia; first dance teacher Maude Barrett was a neighbor and fellow church member. No evidence was found that adult Cunningham maintained a formal religious affiliation.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1919–1937

Cunningham grew up in Centralia, a lumber and railway town between Seattle and Portland. His mother’s travel and independence and his father’s settled legal practice offered contrasting models of movement and stability.

At approximately age ten or eleven, Cunningham began studying with **Maude M. Barrett**, a retired vaudeville and circus performer who operated Barrett’s School of the Dance. Cunningham later regarded Barrett’s theatrical energy as a permanent influence. His recollection emphasized dance as an immediate event—“as accurate and impermanent as breathing”—rather than as a vehicle for social or psychological narrative.

Barrett trained him in:

- tap;
- soft shoe;
- exhibition ballroom dancing;
- theatrical presentation;
- rhythmic precision.

He was partnered with Barrett’s daughter **Marjorie Barrett**. They performed at schools, Grange halls, Elks clubs, American Legion functions, and other venues around Washington State. His later avant-garde practice therefore grew partly from popular entertainment and touring showmanship, not only from concert modern dance.

Cunningham’s own 1978 oral-history interviews describe these early acts, including a specialty solo and ballroom routines. The interviews also confirm that his father supported his theatrical ambitions. See the [New York Public Library oral-history catalogue](https://digitalcollections.nypl.org/items/7ab76ee0-b8f8-0133-ed1c-60f81dd2b63c).

### George Washington University

**Reported fact:** Cunningham attended George Washington University in Washington, D.C., for approximately one year before returning west. Sources do not agree on a detailed curriculum, and no degree is documented.

## Education and Formation, 1937–1939

### Cornish School, Seattle

In 1937 Cunningham entered the Cornish School, now Cornish College of the Arts. He initially studied drama. He found spoken text and conventional miming restrictive and moved increasingly toward dance.

Important Cornish figures included:

- **Nellie Cornish**, founder and institutional director;
- **Bonnie Bird**, head of dance and a former Martha Graham dancer;
- **Alexander Koriansky**, theater teacher;
- **John Cage**, accompanist and teacher of rhythmic composition;
- fellow students **Dorothy Herrmann**, **Joyce Wike**, **Syvilla Fort**, and others.

Bonnie Bird taught Graham-derived technique and composition. She recognized an unusual performing quality in Cunningham and helped redirect him from theater toward dance. A [Cornish historical account](https://www.cornish.edu/news/history-of-a-photo/) quotes Bird as remembering his “magical quality.”

Cunningham’s earliest known student choreographies included:

- *Unbalanced March*;
- *Jazz Epigram*;
- *Skinny Structures*.

At Cornish, Cunningham also played percussion in Cage’s experimental ensemble. This exposed him to nontraditional instruments, rhythmic structures, and a conception of sound independent of conventional melody.

### Mills College and the Bennington summer program

During summer programs at Mills College, Cunningham encountered major modern-dance figures including:

- Martha Graham;
- Doris Humphrey;
- Charles Weidman;
- Hanya Holm;
- Lester Horton.

He danced in works by Horton and Weidman. In 1939 Martha Graham saw him and invited him to join her company in New York. Cunningham accepted and left Cornish before completing a conventional degree.

---

## Early Career: Martha Graham Company, 1939–1945

Cunningham moved to New York in autumn 1939. He began performing with the Martha Graham Dance Company in December and remained a soloist for six years.

He appeared in Graham works including:

- *American Document*;
- *Every Soul Is a Circus*;
- *El Penitente*;
- *Letter to the World*;
- *Punch and the Judy*;
- *Deaths and Entrances*;
- *Appalachian Spring*.

His athleticism, height, stage intensity, and jumping ability made him a prominent male dancer. He nevertheless became dissatisfied with Graham’s dramatic and psychologically symbolic use of movement. The disagreement was artistic rather than a documented personal feud. He continued to speak respectfully of Graham’s abilities and enjoyed dancing with her even while rejecting aspects of her theatrical system.

Cunningham supplemented his training at the School of American Ballet. He encountered Lincoln Kirstein and took at least one class from George Balanchine. Ballet’s precision, uprightness, footwork, and leg articulation became part of the technical synthesis later called Cunningham Technique.

He also worked outside Graham’s company:

- performed in or around Ballet Russe and Ballet Caravan productions;
- appeared in *One Touch of Venus*, choreographed by Agnes de Mille;
- served as dance director for Paul Bowles’s *The Wind Remains* at the Museum of Modern Art in 1943.

### Wartime status

No reliable source consulted establishes that Cunningham served in the armed forces, was imprisoned, or was a conscientious objector during World War II. His documented activity throughout the war years was in American dance and theater. In the absence of a draft file or military record, the reason he did not enter military service should be treated as unresolved.

## John Cage: Partnership in Work and Life

Cunningham met John Cage at Cornish in the late 1930s. Cage was then married to Xenia Kashevaroff. By the early 1940s Cunningham and Cage were corresponding and collaborating; Cage’s surviving letters reveal an increasingly intimate relationship. Their romantic partnership appears to have consolidated around 1942–1944.

The exact beginning is difficult to date because:

- homosexuality carried legal and professional risks;
- neither man publicly narrated the relationship in conventional autobiographical terms;
- earlier profiles often called them “friends” or “lifelong collaborators”;
- Cage was married until the mid-1940s.

**Documented fact:** The Merce Cunningham Trust now identifies Cage as Cunningham’s life partner. Their domestic and professional relationship lasted until Cage’s death in 1992.

The surviving Cage letters contain explicit affection and sexual longing, contradicting older depictions of the men as merely professional associates. The relationship’s concealment was partly produced by mid-century publishing conventions and by the men’s own guardedness.

Their collaboration rested on a division that became foundational:

- Cunningham developed dance;
- Cage or other composers developed sound;
- designers developed décor and costume;
- the components could share duration and space without illustrating or governing one another.

A 1981 [Walker Art Center interview](https://www.walkerart.org/reader/chance-conversations-an-interview-with-merce/) documents their explanation of independence, chance procedures, and Cage’s Zen-derived effort to escape personal preference.

They shared apartments, artistic networks, travel, food gathering, and a collection of works received from artist friends. The simplifying formula “Cage was the brains and Cunningham the body,” attributed to some contemporaries, circulated widely. It obscures Cunningham’s conceptual authorship and Cage’s embodied theatrical practice, but records a real perception that Cage was the more verbal public theorist.

## Chronological Life History

## 1942–1949: Independent Choreography

In 1942 Cunningham and Jean Erdman presented work at Bennington; Cage composed *Credo in Us* for the program. Cunningham began making choreography while still dancing for Graham.

On April 5, 1944, Cunningham and Cage gave their first New York concert devoted entirely to Cunningham dances. Six solos included:

- *Totem Ancestor*;
- *Root of an Unfocus*;
- *Triple-Paced*;
- *Tossed as It Is Untroubled*;
- *The Unavailable Memory Of*;
- *Spontaneous Earth*.

Cage supplied prepared-piano music. The billing “Dances by Merce Cunningham; Music by John Cage” already suggested equal, independent components.

Cunningham left the Graham company in 1945. Thereafter he presented solo concerts, small-group work, and teaching engagements without a permanent company.

### Black Mountain College

Beginning in 1948, Cunningham and Cage took part in summer sessions at Black Mountain College in North Carolina. In 1948 Cunningham participated in Erik Satie’s *Le Piège de Méduse* alongside a community that included R. Buckminster Fuller and other artists.

In summer 1952, Cunningham performed in Cage’s untitled, multi-part theater event, later commonly called *Theatre Piece No. 1*. Participants included Robert Rauschenberg, David Tudor, M. C. Richards, Charles Olson, and others. Activities occurred around an audience seated in an arrangement that disrupted a single frontal viewpoint.

### “First Happening” claim

**Later historical designation, disputed:** The 1952 Black Mountain event is frequently called the first “Happening.” The term itself was coined later by Allan Kaprow. Contemporary documentation is incomplete, no definitive script survives, and eyewitness accounts conflict about the sequence, duration, paintings, ladder lectures, film, phonograph records, and the famous dog said to have crossed Cunningham’s dancing.

The strongest formulation is that it was an important precursor to Happenings and intermedia performance, not an event universally or contemporaneously recognized as the first of its genre.

## 1950–1960: Chance Operations and Formation of the Company

### Chance

Cage’s engagement with the *I Ching* helped establish chance operations in their circle. Cunningham first used chance structurally in *Sixteen Dances for Soloist and Company of Three* (1951). The work addressed nine emotional states from Indian aesthetic theory; chance determined their ordering and aspects of the final choreography.

In *Suite by Chance* (1953), he extended chance to movement sequences, timing, and spatial directions. Chance did not mean improvisation or absence of authorship. Cunningham:

1. invented the possible movements;
2. framed the questions;
3. constructed charts or gamuts;
4. used coins, dice, imperfections in paper, the *I Ching*, or later computer procedures to select combinations;
5. taught the resulting fixed choreography to dancers.

A recent scholarly reconsideration, [“Queer Thoughts on Merce Cunningham”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-drama-review/article/queer-thoughts-on-merce-cunningham/30AC3EC835C9BE7D7089630A5A72ED51), stresses that this was labor-intensive decision-making, not simply “letting the dance happen.”

### Founding of the company, 1953

During the 1953 Black Mountain summer, Cunningham assembled the group that became Merce Cunningham and Dance Company, later the Merce Cunningham Dance Company.

Early members included:

- Carolyn Brown;
- Viola Farber;
- Remy Charlip;
- Marianne Preger-Simon;
- Jo Anne Melsher;
- Anita Dencks;
- Paul Taylor.

John Cage served as music director, and David Tudor was the principal pianist and electronic musician.

Important works of the period included:

- *Sixteen Dances for Soloist and Company of Three* (1951);
- *Les Noces* (1952), commissioned by Leonard Bernstein and using Stravinsky’s score;
- *Suite by Chance* (1953);
- *Solo Suite in Space and Time* (1953);
- *Dime a Dance* (1953);
- *Septet* (1953);
- *Minutiae* (1954);
- *Banjo* (1955);
- *Springweather and People* (1955);
- *Suite for Five* (1956);
- *Changeling* (1957);
- *Antic Meet* (1958);
- *Summerspace* (1958);
- *Rune* (1959);
- *Crises* (1960).

### Robert Rauschenberg

Rauschenberg designed *Minutiae* in 1954 and became the company’s resident designer through the 1964 world tour. He supplied sets, costumes, lighting, found objects, and flexible solutions for a company with little money. His freestanding scenic object for *Minutiae* refused the traditional painted backdrop and occupied the dancers’ space as an independent object.

Cunningham received Guggenheim Fellowships in 1954 and 1959. Despite these recognitions, the company remained financially precarious. Performers frequently worked for little or deferred compensation. Engagements were sporadic, and hostile or indifferent audiences were common.

## 1960–1970: Recognition, World Tour, Foundation, and “Events”

### Early 1960s

Notable dances included:

- *Crises* (1960);
- *Hands Birds* (1960);
- *Aeon* (1961);
- *Suite de Danses* (1961);
- *Field Dances* (1963);
- *Story* (1963);
- *Winterbranch* (1964);
- *Museum Event No. 1* (1964);
- *How to Pass, Kick, Fall and Run* (1965);
- *Variations V* (1965);
- *Place* (1966);
- *RainForest* (1968);
- *Walkaround Time* (1968).

*Story* allowed Rauschenberg and company personnel to construct changing scenery from whatever could be found locally. Dancers recalled that Cunningham could nevertheless become angry about particular realizations, illustrating a tension between proclaimed indeterminacy and authorial expectations.

### 1964 world tour

The company’s six-month tour of Europe and Asia in 1964 was decisive. Participants included Cunningham, Cage, Tudor, Rauschenberg, Alex Hay, Carolyn Brown, Viola Farber, Steve Paxton, Deborah Hay, Barbara Lloyd, Sandra Neels, and others. David Vaughan helped coordinate the itinerary.

The tour included performances in Western and Eastern Europe, India, Thailand, and Japan. The company achieved a major London success at Sadler’s Wells. It also confronted:

- debt;
- exhaustion;
- injuries;
- uneven reception;
- internal resentment;
- difficult logistics;
- artistic and personal disagreements.

The tour contributed to Rauschenberg’s departure as resident designer and to personnel changes. An academic reconstruction describes accumulated “financial stressors, artistic differences, physical injuries, and hurt feelings.” See the [Northwestern project on the tour](https://sites.northwestern.edu/cageanddance/mcdc-tour/) and Rauschenberg Foundation [chronology](https://www.rauschenbergfoundation.org/artist/chronology).

### Cunningham Dance Foundation

The Cunningham Dance Foundation was incorporated in 1964 to operate the company, studio, school, archive, and related programs. It eventually became the principal nonprofit business structure around Cunningham’s work.

### Events

The first formally catalogued Event, *Museum Event No. 1*, took place at Vienna’s Museum des 20. Jahrhunderts on June 24, 1964. Events assembled excerpts, transitions, and recombinations of existing material for nontraditional spaces. They permitted Cunningham to adapt work to museums, gymnasiums, public squares, and unconventional stages.

### American recognition

Cunningham’s earlier European success helped change American reception. In 1966 George Balanchine invited him to restage *Summerspace* for New York City Ballet. This was institutionally important but reportedly difficult for ballet dancers unused to the independent torso and directional demands of Cunningham movement.

## 1970–1980: Westbeth, Video, and Sounddance

In 1971 the company became an original tenant at **Westbeth Artists Housing**, the converted Bell Laboratories complex in Greenwich Village. Cunningham and Cage lived and worked there. The studio remained the company’s base until institutional dissolution.

Important dances included:

- *Second Hand* (1970);
- *Canfield* (1969–70);
- *Landrover* (1972);
- *Changing Steps* (1973);
- *Un jour ou deux* (1973), made for the Paris Opera Ballet;
- *Sounddance* (1975);
- *Torse* (1976);
- *Squaregame* (1976);
- *Travelogue* (1977);
- *Exchange* (1978);
- *Roaratorio* (1983, developed from work beginning around Cage’s 1979 composition);
- numerous Events.

### Video and Charles Atlas

Cunningham recognized that film could do more than preserve a stage dance. With filmmaker **Charles Atlas**, he developed choreography specifically for the camera:

- *Westbeth* (1974);
- *Blue Studio: Five Segments* (1975–76);
- *Fractions* (1978);
- *Locale* (1979);
- *Channels/Inserts* (1981).

Camera position, framing, editing, and screen space became choreographic variables. Cunningham’s 1983 essay [“Collaborating with Visual Artists”](https://www.mercecunningham.org/the-work/writings/collaborating-with-visual-artists/) discusses these experiments.

### Jasper Johns

After Rauschenberg, Jasper Johns served as artistic adviser from 1967 into the 1980s. Johns brought in designers and artists including Frank Stella, Andy Warhol, Bruce Nauman, Robert Morris, and others.

For *Walkaround Time*, Johns created transparent set pieces based on Marcel Duchamp’s *The Large Glass*. Warhol supplied silver helium-filled pillows for *RainForest*. The dancers had to negotiate objects whose movements could not be fully controlled.

### *Sounddance*

*Sounddance* (1975), with music by David Tudor and décor by Mark Lancaster, became one of Cunningham’s most forceful ensemble works. It begins and ends through an opening in a gold curtain. Its density and speed complicate the stereotype of Cunningham work as emotionally blank or cool.

## 1980–1990: Mature Institutional Period

Major works included:

- *Channels/Inserts* (1981);
- *Pictures* (1984);
- *Doubles* (1984);
- *Points in Space* (1986);
- *Fabrications* (1987);
- *Five Stone Wind* (1988);
- *August Pace* (1989);
- *Polarity* (1990).

Cunningham continued to dance, adapting roles as his body aged. His later solos often made age and physical limitation visible without turning them into narrative subjects.

The decade brought major honors:

- Commandeur de l’Ordre des Arts et des Lettres, 1982;
- Samuel H. Scripps/American Dance Festival Award, 1982;
- New York Mayor’s Award of Honor for Arts and Culture, 1983;
- honorary membership in the American Academy and Institute of Arts and Letters, 1984;
- MacArthur Fellowship, 1985;
- Kennedy Center Honors, 1985;
- Laurence Olivier Award, associated with the company’s London performances, 1985;
- Dance/USA National Honor, 1988;
- Chevalier of the Légion d’honneur, 1989;
- National Medal of Arts, 1990.

The Trust’s [honors register](https://www.mercecunningham.org/about/honors-and-awards/) provides a fuller institutional list.

## 1990–1994: Computers, Cage’s Death, and *Ocean*

### LifeForms / DanceForms

Cunningham began working with **LifeForms**, a computer choreography program developed at Simon Fraser University by Thomas Calvert and collaborators including Thecla Schiphorst and Iris Garland. Schiphorst worked with Cunningham from approximately 1990 to 2005.

The program allowed him to:

- pose a three-dimensional digital figure;
- construct and store movement sequences;
- view figures from changing directions;
- layer multiple bodies in a virtual stage space;
- invent coordinations difficult to generate through habitual bodily memory.

Cunningham did not invent or patent LifeForms. He was an influential user and collaborator whose requirements helped its development. The program was later called DanceForms. Simon Fraser University documents [the development team](https://www.sfu.ca/sca/events---news/news/merce-cunningham---danceforms/) and [Schiphorst’s collaboration](https://www.sfu.ca/siat/people/research-faculty/thecla-schiphorst.html).

Works associated with computer assistance included:

- *Trackers* (1991);
- *Beach Birds* (1991);
- *Change of Address* (1992);
- *Enter* (1992);
- *CRWDSPCR* (1993);
- *Ocean* (1994);
- later *Scenario*, *BIPED*, *Split Sides*, and *eyeSpace*.

Cunningham’s first-person article [“High-Wired Act”](https://www.inc.com/magazine/19960915/2011.html) explains his use of the software.

### Death of John Cage

Cage died on August 12, 1992. Cunningham lost his partner of approximately half a century and the company lost its founding music director. David Tudor and later Takehisa Kosugi assumed major musical responsibilities.

Cunningham continued working. He did not attempt to replace Cage as a personal or theoretical counterpart.

### *Ocean*

Cage and Cunningham had discussed a circular work inspired partly by James Joyce and architect Philip Johnson. Cunningham completed *Ocean* in 1994 after Cage’s death, with orchestral music by Andrew Culver derived from Cage’s plans and electronic music by David Tudor.

The musicians surrounded the audience, which in turn surrounded a circular dancing space. No single seat offered an authoritative view. The work is often treated as the culmination of Cage and Cunningham’s spatial thinking, although Cage did not live to see it.

## 1995–2009: Late Work, Digital Media, Disability, and Legacy Planning

Major late dances included:

- *Ground Level Overlay* (1995);
- *Installations* (1996);
- *Scenario* (1997);
- *Pond Way* (1998);
- *BIPED* (1999);
- *Interscape* (2000);
- *Way Station* (2001);
- *Loose Time* (2002);
- *Split Sides* (2003);
- *Views on Stage* (2004);
- *eyeSpace* (2006);
- *XOVER* (2007);
- *Nearly Ninety* (2009).

### Motion capture and virtual bodies

In 1997–98 Cunningham collaborated with digital artists **Paul Kaiser** and **Shelley Eshkar** on *Hand-drawn Spaces*. Dancers’ movements were optically captured, recombined, and mapped onto drawn virtual figures.

For *BIPED* (1999), Cunningham used DanceForms for choreography while Kaiser and Eshkar created projected motion-capture animation. Virtual figures crossed a scrim in front of live dancers. The [Trust’s production record](https://www.mercecunningham.org/the-work/choreography/biped/) documents the premiere on April 23, 1999, at Zellerbach Hall in Berkeley.

These artists were collaborators, not invisible technicians. Kaiser later described how digitally recorded phrases could be combined or mapped onto nonhuman-looking figures in [his technical account](https://openendedgroup.com/artworks/biped_essay.html).

### *Loops* and open data

Cunningham’s hand choreography *Loops* was motion-captured. In 2008 underlying data were released under a Creative Commons license, permitting further artistic and software reinterpretation. This was unusual in an art form traditionally controlled through personal transmission and licensing.

### Physical decline

Cunningham developed severe arthritis and increasingly used a wheelchair. He nevertheless continued attending rehearsals, teaching through demonstration and verbal or digital instruction, and making choreography. Public obituaries reported the arthritis but did not identify a specific terminal illness.

### *Nearly Ninety*

Cunningham premiered *Nearly Ninety* at the Brooklyn Academy of Music on April 16, 2009, his ninetieth birthday. Collaborators included musicians and designers from younger generations. The title acknowledged age without making the dance a conventional autobiography.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

## Merce Cunningham Dance Company

- **Founded:** 1953 at Black Mountain College.
- **Continuous operation:** 1953–2011.
- **Initial form:** Cunningham and approximately five dancers, with Cage and Tudor as musicians.
- **Final form:** Fourteen dancers under Robert Swinston as director of choreography after Cunningham’s death, with a music committee.
- **Reach:** Nearly 3,000 performances in more than forty countries, according to the Trust.
- **Artistic director:** Cunningham until his death.

## Cunningham Dance Foundation

Incorporated in 1964, the nonprofit foundation operated:

- MCDC;
- the Cunningham Studio and School;
- archives and preservation;
- touring;
- fundraising;
- licensing and educational activity.

The company’s archival records—contracts, correspondence, grants, financial materials, production records, photographs, and programs—are held by the New York Public Library. The [foundation finding aid](https://archives.nypl.org/dan/19852) notes forty-three linear feet of material.

## Finances and precarity

The early company repeatedly operated close to insolvency. Dancers tolerated low or absent pay, shared travel hardships, and taught to survive. European recognition and nonprofit funding improved conditions, but touring remained expensive.

No credible evidence was found that Cunningham accumulated wealth through ordinary commercial businesses, securities, real estate ventures, or patents. His significant financial assets late in life included art given by collaborators and friends.

### Art collection and estate

Cunningham and Cage possessed works by Jasper Johns, Robert Rauschenberg, Philip Guston, and others. Cunningham left the bulk of his estate to the Merce Cunningham Trust. Christie’s announced that six major works from the collection would be sold for the Trust’s benefit. The sale ultimately generated approximately **$8.9 million**, according to the Legacy Plan case study and reporting.

This capital formed an endowment; it was not ordinary operating revenue for the dance company. Confusion among some donors about this restriction became a posthumous financial issue.

The contemporaneous [Christie’s announcement](https://www.christies.com/presscenter/pdf/09222009/13384.pdf) identifies the property and beneficiary.

## Legacy Plan

Planning for a post-Cunningham future began intermittently around 1990–91. Cunningham wanted rights to his works placed in a trust but reportedly had little interest in administrative details. Staff and board members developed the final structure.

The announced plan provided for:

- a two-year international Legacy Tour;
- documentation and preservation;
- dissolution of the company and foundation;
- transition payments and employment assistance for dancers and staff;
- transfer of intellectual property and assets to the Trust;
- continued licensing rather than maintenance of a successor repertory company.

The 2008 financial crisis forced the 2009 operating budget to be cut by about **23 percent**, including layoffs and furloughs. The proposed Legacy Plan budget was reduced from approximately **$12 million to $8.3 million**. Preservation ambitions were initially reduced, although eighty-six digital Dance Capsules were ultimately completed.

The company gave its final performance at the Park Avenue Armory on December 31, 2011. The Foundation closed June 30, 2012. The studio’s closure was not originally part of the plan; it was reportedly losing approximately $200,000 annually. Open classes ended March 31, 2012, and Trust programs began at New York City Center.

The Foundation’s own [Legacy Plan case study](https://www.mercecunningham.org/themes/default/db_images/documents/Merce_Legacy_Plan.pdf) presents the plan as successful. A [Dance/USA analysis](https://www.danceusa.org/ejournal/2013/04/26/parsing-the-merce-cunningham-dance-company-legacy-plan-a-special-report) records its contested aspects, budget cuts, and institutional consequences.

## Merce Cunningham Trust

The Trust was formed in 2000 and became the rights-holding successor. Its mission includes:

- licensing dances;
- protecting artistic integrity;
- training authorized stagers;
- supporting repertory workshops;
- maintaining Cunningham Technique;
- enabling scholarly and public access.

“Merce Cunningham” is a federally registered trademark of the Trust. The Trust does not reproduce the former company as a permanent ensemble.

## Intellectual property and patents

- Cunningham owned or controlled copyright in choreographic works and associated writings.
- Rights passed to or are administered by the Trust.
- No patent authored or co-authored by Cunningham was located.
- He did not invent LifeForms, DanceForms, Character Studio, or motion-capture equipment.
- His importance to technology lies in artistic use, testing, collaboration, and public demonstration.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

## Political activity

No sustained party membership, candidacy, government office, intelligence relationship, or organized political campaign by Cunningham was located.

His art was sometimes described as nonpolitical because he refused explicit narrative or social-message choreography. That is an aesthetic position, not proof of political indifference. His rejection of hierarchical stage centers, gendered partnering conventions, and music’s dominance has received political interpretations, but Cunningham generally discussed these questions as artistic and perceptual problems.

His father’s role in the Centralia Massacre prosecution should not be transferred to him as a political affiliation.

## Religion and philosophy

Cage studied Zen Buddhism with D. T. Suzuki and communicated Zen-influenced ideas to Cunningham’s circle. Cunningham used the *I Ching* as a compositional instrument but is not documented as having converted formally to Buddhism or Taoism.

His mature philosophy emphasized:

- events as themselves rather than symbols for something else;
- present-tense attention;
- decentralization of stage space;
- coexistence rather than synthesis of arts;
- acceptance of unpredictable conjunctions;
- discovery beyond habit and taste;
- the individuality of dancers;
- dance as materially embodied and impermanent.

## Independence of music and dance

Cunningham rejected the standard assumption that dance must illustrate musical rhythm, emotion, or story. Dance and music commonly shared only a total duration. Dancers often rehearsed without hearing the final music and encountered it at performance.

This did not mean Cunningham disliked music. He sometimes choreographed in more direct relation to Satie and used pre-existing scores. The separation was a method, not an absolute ban.

## Space

Cunningham treated every stage location as potentially important. There was no necessary privileged center. Multiple events could occur simultaneously, leaving spectators responsible for choosing where to look.

## The body and technique

Cunningham Technique combined:

- a mobile, articulated torso;
- ballet-derived foot and leg clarity;
- rapid directional changes;
- rhythmic independence;
- balance under unstable conditions;
- strength and flexibility;
- the capacity to perform complex phrases without musical cues.

Male and female dancers were often given comparable technical burdens. Cunningham nevertheless worked within a predominantly Western concert-dance institution, and later scholars have examined how its ballet inheritance, bodily ideals, and historically majority-white company complicate claims of formal neutrality.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### John Cage

**Documented fact:** Life partner, principal intellectual interlocutor, music director, composer, touring companion, and co-architect of the separation between sound and movement.

### Martha Graham

**Documented fact:** Employer, teacher, and central formative influence, 1939–45. Cunningham retained Graham’s seriousness and technical intensity while rejecting her dramatic-symbolic model.

### Bonnie Bird

**Documented fact:** Principal Cornish dance teacher who recognized and cultivated Cunningham’s abilities and brought him into the environment in which he met Cage.

### Maude and Marjorie Barrett

**Documented fact:** First teacher and teenage dance partner. Barrett’s vaudeville theatricality shaped Cunningham’s conception of performance as immediate action.

### Robert Rauschenberg

**Documented fact:** Designer from 1954 through the 1964 tour, friend, and intermittent later collaborator. Their relationship survived professional strain; a 2007 photograph records them jokingly staging a wheelchair race.

### Jasper Johns

**Documented fact:** Friend, artistic adviser, designer, donor of valuable artworks, and mediator between Cunningham and other visual artists.

### David Tudor

**Documented fact:** Pianist, electronic composer, sound engineer, touring musician, and music director after Cage. Tudor’s live electronics made unpredictability audible.

### Carolyn Brown

**Documented fact:** Founding dancer and principal performer, 1953–72. Brown’s memoir is the most substantial insider account of the early company. Her relationship with Cunningham combined devotion, frustration, and artistic dependence.

### Viola Farber

**Documented fact:** Founding dancer, 1953–65 and briefly 1970; later choreographer and teacher. She embodied many early Cunningham roles before creating her own company.

### Remy Charlip

**Documented fact:** Early dancer and designer. Cunningham’s abrupt dismissal or break with Charlip became an example cited by colleagues of his poor personal communication.

### David Vaughan

**Documented fact:** Friend, tour administrator, company archivist from 1976, and author of the principal documentary chronology *Merce Cunningham: Fifty Years*.

### Charles Atlas and Elliot Caplan

**Documented fact:** Filmmakers who helped Cunningham translate choreography into camera space rather than merely record stage works.

### Paul Kaiser, Shelley Eshkar, Thomas Calvert, Thecla Schiphorst

**Documented fact:** Digital and software collaborators central to Cunningham’s computer-assisted choreography and motion-capture works.

### Robert Swinston

**Documented fact:** Dancer from 1980, assistant to Cunningham, director of choreography after 2009, and trustee involved in preservation and staging.

## Successes and Failures

### Documented successes

- Established an enduring alternative to expressive modern dance.
- Created a technique and repertory capable of transmission across companies.
- Maintained MCDC for fifty-eight years.
- Integrated experimental music, visual art, film, computers, and motion capture into dance practice.
- Achieved sustained international touring.
- Influenced Judson Dance Theater, postmodern dance, ballet, film/video dance, and digital performance.
- Developed an unusually explicit post-founder preservation plan.
- Received major American, British, French, Italian, and Japanese honors.

### Documented failures or abandoned outcomes

- Many early works disappeared because of limited documentation.
- Some chance-generated dances were dropped when Cunningham considered them unsuccessful.
- Early tours incurred debt and strained relationships.
- The 1964 tour fractured the company’s personnel and ended Rauschenberg’s resident role.
- Cunningham’s personal reserve sometimes damaged relations with dancers.
- A permanent company could not or was not intended to survive him.
- Financial contraction reduced parts of the original preservation plan.
- The Westbeth studio and school proved financially unsustainable after institutional transition.

## Controversies, Criticism, Allegations, and Financial Questions

## Chance as abdication of authorship

**Critical claim:** Some critics alleged that chance procedures surrendered artistic responsibility or produced arbitrary, emotionally empty work.

**Evidence:** Chance selected among possibilities Cunningham had created and framed. Choreography was rehearsed and usually fixed. Chance did not erase selection, revision, technique, or Cunningham’s ability to withdraw unsuccessful work.

**Status:** Documented critical interpretation; the claim that the dances were literally uncontrolled is false or materially misleading.

## “Emotionless,” “cerebral,” or inaccessible dance

Contemporary critics sometimes called the work dry, esoteric, or empty. Doris Hering described early chance composition as resembling tired utterances in an “emotionless void”; John Martin found little content or formal value. Other observers saw intense lyricism, violence, comedy, eroticism, and grief.

**Status:** Documented disagreement in reception, not a question resolvable as factual or false.

## Personal aloofness and control

Carolyn Brown and other dancers described Cunningham as reserved, difficult to read, and poor at explaining decisions. The company’s apparent freedom coexisted with exacting technical demands and a strong hierarchy centered on Cunningham.

Brown’s memoir recounts:

- chronic financial insecurity;
- anxiety among dancers;
- abrupt personnel decisions;
- Cunningham’s limited personal reassurance;
- devotion to the work despite those conditions.

A [New Yorker review](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2007/06/04/chance-and-circumstance) summarizes the paradox: hostile audiences and Cunningham’s inability to communicate personally generated both pain and collective defiance.

**Status:** Reported fact based on named participant testimony. It does not establish abuse in a legal sense.

## Dancer compensation and labor

Early dancers frequently worked for little money. Economic precarity was structural to the company and to mid-century experimental dance. During the 2009 recession, staff were laid off or furloughed and budgets cut.

No consulted source documented a wage-theft judgment, union action, or successful employment lawsuit against Cunningham personally.

**Status:** Low compensation and financial hardship are documented; criminal or civil illegality is not established.

## Legacy Plan and company closure

The deliberate dissolution of MCDC was controversial. Critics and dancers questioned whether a living ensemble—the medium through which the dances had developed—was being sacrificed to an archive-and-licensing model. Supporters argued that a successor company without Cunningham would inevitably decline or falsify the repertory.

**Documented fact:** Cunningham wanted a trust to hold the work and approved the planned closing structure. Administrative details were developed mainly by staff and the board.

**Unresolved interpretive question:** Whether the closure best preserved the dances or destroyed their essential institutional culture.

## Sexuality, privacy, and the closet

Older coverage often concealed Cunningham and Cage’s partnership behind terms such as “friends” and “collaborators.” Later archival publication and Trust language identify them as life partners.

Recent queer scholarship asks whether Cunningham’s claims to impersonality and abstraction can be understood partly within the conditions imposed on gay men in twentieth-century America. It also warns against turning every formal device into coded autobiography.

**Status:** Their partnership is documented fact. Claims that particular dances secretly narrate homosexual experience are interpretations, not established biographical facts.

## Gender and race

Cunningham gave women and men comparably difficult movement and weakened conventional male-lifts-female partnering. This has been regarded as an egalitarian formal innovation.

Critical scholarship nevertheless argues that:

- “neutral” bodies can conceal inherited ballet ideals;
- the company’s institutional history was predominantly white;
- abstraction does not automatically eliminate racial or gender coding;
- modern dance’s borrowings from Asian philosophy and aesthetics require historical scrutiny.

No evidence was found that Cunningham faced a formal discrimination proceeding. These are scholarly critiques of repertoire, casting history, and universalist rhetoric, not findings of intentional racial misconduct.

## Asian philosophy and appropriation

Cunningham used the Chinese *I Ching* and referred in *Sixteen Dances* to Indian rasa theory. Some contemporary scholarship treats these as part of a wider American avant-garde pattern of selectively extracting Asian systems.

Evidence shows serious and sustained compositional use, especially through Cage, but not formal scholarly mastery of Chinese religion or classical Indian aesthetics.

**Status:** Documented borrowing; whether it constitutes appropriation is a critical interpretation.

## Authorship in collaborative works

Because music, design, film, and dance were created independently, determining authorship can be complicated. Rauschenberg, Johns, Tudor, Atlas, Kaiser, Eshkar, and others were substantive artistic collaborators. Public shorthand that attributes the total theatrical object solely to Cunningham understates their work.

Conversely, the claim that Cage was the true intellectual author of Cunningham’s choreography is unsupported. Cunningham selected, constructed, taught, and revised the dances.

## Criminal, legal, or financial misconduct

A dedicated search using Cunningham’s name with “scandal,” “allegations,” “lawsuit,” “fraud,” “accusations,” “conspiracy,” and related terms located no credible evidence of:

- criminal charge or conviction;
- fraud adjudication;
- corruption;
- money laundering;
- sexual-assault allegation;
- intelligence relationship;
- political violence;
- lawsuit establishing personal wrongdoing.

This negative finding applies to sources consulted through September 2, 2026 and should not be read as proof that no private dispute ever occurred.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

## “The first Happening”

- **Story:** Cage, Cunningham, Rauschenberg, and colleagues created the world’s first Happening at Black Mountain in 1952.
- **Appearance and circulation:** The claim became common in later histories of performance art and Black Mountain College.
- **Evidence for:** Simultaneous independent actions, disrupted spectatorship, mixed media, and incomplete control anticipate later Happenings.
- **Evidence against:** The term did not yet exist; accounts conflict; other precursors are identifiable; some celebrated details rest on late recollection.
- **Effect:** The story placed Cunningham in the genealogy of performance art beyond dance.
- **Label:** Later historical designation; disputed priority claim.

## The dog in the Black Mountain performance

- **Story:** A dog entered or chased Cunningham during the 1952 event.
- **Evidence:** Appears in retrospective eyewitness narratives; no film or contemporaneous comprehensive record confirms the exact action.
- **Effect:** Became a compact symbol of accident entering art.
- **Label:** Reported anecdote, not independently verified.

## “Everything was decided by chance”

- **Story:** Cunningham rolled dice and allowed performances to unfold without choreographic control.
- **Evidence against:** Surviving notes, rehearsal records, testimony, and Cunningham’s writings show that he constructed movement vocabularies and fixed difficult sequences.
- **Effect:** Made him famous but also encouraged the accusation that his work was arbitrary.
- **Label:** Popular myth based on a distorted version of documented procedure.

## “Cage was the brains; Cunningham was the body”

- **Story:** Cage supplied theory while Cunningham merely danced.
- **Circulation:** Reportedly voiced by associates and reproduced in profiles.
- **Evidence against:** Cunningham’s notebooks, essays, software experiments, and choreographic systems demonstrate independent conceptual work.
- **Effect:** Both publicized the partnership and diminished Cunningham’s intellectual agency.
- **Label:** Hostile or reductive mythology.

## The isolated genius

- **Story:** Cunningham single-handedly invented postmodern dance.
- **Evidence against:** His work depended on Graham, Bird, Cage, Tudor, dancers, designers, funders, Black Mountain, and other experimental communities.
- **Effect:** Supports a conventional “great man” account that his own collaborative practices complicate.
- **Label:** Hagiographic simplification.

## “No emotion” and “no meaning”

- **Story:** Cunningham deliberately removed all feeling and meaning from dance.
- **Evidence:** He rejected prescribed narrative meaning, not spectators’ feelings or dancers’ human presence. He repeatedly emphasized dancing’s vitality and allowed viewers to make associations.
- **Effect:** Defined both hostile and admiring accounts of his abstraction.
- **Label:** Critical myth derived from a partial truth.

## “Merce is in the purse”: the ashes story

In 2024 *The Real Housewives of Beverly Hills* featured former Cunningham organization employee **Sutton Stracke** carrying material she identified as part of Cunningham’s ashes in a plastic inner bag and later scattering it in Spain. The phrase “Merce is in the purse” circulated as a reality-television catchphrase.

Stracke stated that Cunningham had been her friend and mentor and that she had held the ashes for more than a decade. She had worked as studio manager and later in development for the Cunningham organization. Bravo’s [account of her statement](https://www.bravotv.com/the-daily-dish/sutton-stracke-opens-up-on-merce-and-ex-husband-christian) confirms what she publicly claimed.

No probate document, executor’s statement, chain-of-custody record, or independent forensic evidence was located establishing:

- what proportion of Cunningham’s remains she received;
- who gave them to her;
- whether the transfer was directed by Cunningham’s will;
- whether all or only part was scattered;
- whether Sitges or another Spanish location had prior significance designated by Cunningham.

The statement that Cunningham “left his ashes to her” appears in entertainment reporting but was not independently established in the consulted estate sources.

- **Underlying relationship:** Reported and institutionally plausible.
- **Possession and scattering:** Publicly performed and stated by Stracke.
- **Formal provenance:** Unverified.
- **Label:** Reported fact as to Stracke’s possession claim and televised act; unresolved as to legal provenance and authenticity.
- **Historical effect:** Introduced Cunningham’s name to a mass reality-television audience and generated a new comic popular mythology around an artist previously associated with elite avant-garde culture.

## Health, Accidents, and Cause of Death

Cunningham’s profession exposed him to normal accumulated dance injuries, but no comprehensive medical record is public.

In later life:

- severe arthritis impaired mobility;
- he used a wheelchair;
- he continued attending the studio and choreographing;
- friends described continuing alertness and creative activity.

Cunningham died peacefully at home on July 26, 2009. The Foundation did not initially announce a precise medical cause. Contemporary reporting said no cause was disclosed; later popular accounts commonly say “natural causes.”

There is no substantiated evidence of addiction, suicide attempt, psychiatric hospitalization, or a terminal scandal involving his care.

His body was cremated. The complete disposition of the ashes remains incompletely documented publicly.

## Death and Immediate Aftermath

Cunningham died at home on July 26, 2009. Robert Swinston became director of choreography. The company proceeded with its planned Legacy Tour rather than appointing a replacement artistic director.

The final tour presented major repertory internationally and ended on December 31, 2011, at the Park Avenue Armory. Tickets for the final event were priced at $10, reportedly at Cunningham’s insistence.

The company closed immediately afterward. The Foundation closed in 2012. The Trust, NYPL, Walker Art Center, former dancers, and licensed companies divided responsibility for preservation, material objects, archives, and performance transmission.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Cunningham’s reputation rests on several interconnected changes to twentieth- and twenty-first-century performance:

- separating choreography from musical illustration;
- using chance as a disciplined compositional tool;
- decentralizing theatrical space;
- preserving dancer individuality within exacting choreography;
- treating décor as an autonomous art;
- developing dance for camera rather than simply filming stage works;
- adopting computer animation and motion capture early;
- creating adaptable Events for nontraditional sites;
- institutionalizing posthumous licensing and digital preservation.

His influence is visible in postmodern dance, Judson Dance Theater, contemporary ballet, multimedia installation, generative choreography, dance film, and digital-humanities preservation.

The posthumous situation remains paradoxical:

- Cunningham’s works are widely licensed and reconstructed;
- the originating company no longer exists;
- detailed archives make revival possible;
- archival accuracy cannot reproduce the social conditions or individual performers of the original ensemble;
- his methods invite reinterpretation, while the Trust protects defined standards and trademarks.

The 2017 Walker/Museum of Contemporary Art Chicago exhibition *Merce Cunningham: Common Time* situated his work among artists including Rauschenberg, Johns, Warhol, Nam June Paik, Bruce Nauman, Frank Stella, Yoko Ono, and others.

The centennial year, 2019, included *Night of 100 Solos*, performed simultaneously in London, New York, and Los Angeles, as well as international revivals and exhibitions.

Recent scholarship increasingly considers:

- Cunningham and Cage as a queer couple rather than euphemistic “friends”;
- dancers as co-producers of the repertory’s physical knowledge;
- the limits of claims to neutrality or universality;
- the role of technological collaborators;
- financial and labor realities beneath avant-garde mythology;
- preservation as both transmission and institutional control.

## Compact Chronology

- **1919, April 16:** Born in Centralia, Washington.
- **Circa 1929–30:** Begins dance study with Maude Barrett.
- **1930s:** Performs tap and ballroom routines with Marjorie Barrett around Washington.
- **Circa 1936–37:** Attends George Washington University for roughly one year.
- **1937:** Enters Cornish School in Seattle.
- **1938–39:** Studies with Bonnie Bird; meets John Cage; plays in Cage’s percussion ensemble.
- **1939:** Encounters Martha Graham at Mills; moves to New York and joins her company.
- **1942:** Begins independent choreography and important collaboration with Cage.
- **1944, April 5:** First New York concert devoted entirely to Cunningham dances.
- **1945:** Leaves the Martha Graham Dance Company.
- **1948:** First major Black Mountain College summer; participates in Satie production.
- **1951:** Uses chance in *Sixteen Dances*.
- **1952:** Choreographs *Les Noces*; participates in Cage’s Black Mountain theater event.
- **1953:** Creates *Suite by Chance*; forms his company at Black Mountain College.
- **1954:** Begins major collaboration with Robert Rauschenberg; receives Guggenheim Fellowship.
- **1956:** Premieres *Suite for Five*.
- **1958:** Premieres *Antic Meet* and *Summerspace*.
- **1959:** Receives second Guggenheim Fellowship.
- **1963:** Creates *Story*; company begins important Walker Art Center relationship.
- **1964:** Six-month Europe-and-Asia tour; first formal Event in Vienna; Cunningham Dance Foundation incorporated.
- **1965:** *Variations V* integrates electronic sensing and live performance.
- **1966:** Restages *Summerspace* for New York City Ballet.
- **1968:** Publishes *Changes*; premieres *RainForest* and *Walkaround Time*.
- **1971:** Company and household move to Westbeth.
- **1973:** Creates *Un jour ou deux* for Paris Opera Ballet.
- **1974:** Makes *Westbeth* for video.
- **1975:** Premieres *Sounddance*.
- **1976:** Creates *Torse* and *Squaregame*.
- **1977:** Reunites with Rauschenberg for *Travelogue*.
- **1979:** Makes *Locale* with Charles Atlas.
- **1982:** Receives Scripps Award and French Arts and Letters honor.
- **1983:** *Roaratorio* enters the repertory.
- **1985:** Receives MacArthur Fellowship and Kennedy Center Honors.
- **1986:** Makes *Points in Space*.
- **1989:** Named Chevalier of the Légion d’honneur.
- **1990:** Receives National Medal of Arts; begins sustained computer-assisted work.
- **1991:** Premieres *Trackers* and *Beach Birds*.
- **1992, August 12:** John Cage dies.
- **1993:** Premieres *CRWDSPCR*.
- **1994:** Premieres *Ocean*.
- **1997:** Creates *Scenario* with Rei Kawakubo’s body-altering costumes.
- **1998:** Collaborates on *Hand-drawn Spaces*.
- **1999:** Premieres *BIPED*; celebrates eightieth birthday.
- **2000:** Receives Gish Prize and Library of Congress “Living Legend” designation.
- **2003:** Premieres *Split Sides*, using chance to combine alternative choreographic, musical, and design components.
- **2004:** Promoted to Officier of the Légion d’honneur.
- **2005:** Receives Praemium Imperiale.
- **2008:** *Loops* data released under a Creative Commons license.
- **2009, April 16:** Premieres *Nearly Ninety* on his ninetieth birthday.
- **2009, July 26:** Dies at home in New York.
- **2009–11:** MCDC completes Legacy Tour.
- **2011, December 31:** Final company performance at Park Avenue Armory.
- **2012:** Cunningham Dance Foundation and Westbeth studio close; Trust assumes continuing functions.
- **2017:** *Merce Cunningham: Common Time* retrospective.
- **2019:** Cunningham centennial; *Night of 100 Solos* and international programs.
- **2024:** Sutton Stracke’s televised account of possessing and scattering part of his ashes produces the “Merce is in the purse” popular narrative.
- **2025–26:** Major repertory revivals and new queer-historical and technological scholarship continue.
