# Diego Armando Maradona: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

This dossier reconstructs Diego Armando Maradona’s life through 3 September 2026.

Labels used for contested material:

- **Documented fact:** supported by official records, court decisions, audiovisual evidence, or direct contemporaneous documentation.
- **Reported fact:** consistently reported by reputable sources but not independently verified here from a primary record.
- **Allegation:** asserted by an identified person, prosecutor, litigant, witness, or investigator and not established by a final judgment.
- **Court finding / conviction:** determined by a court, with procedural status specified where available.
- **Disputed claim:** supported and contested by identifiable evidence or parties.
- **Rumor or myth:** culturally significant but unproved.
- **Unresolved question:** evidence remains incomplete or proceedings remain pending.

Football appearance totals vary because some sources count league matches only and others include cups and unofficial fixtures.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Diego Armando Maradona.
- **Born:** 30 October 1960.
- **Birthplace:** Policlínico Evita, Lanús, Buenos Aires Province, Argentina. He was raised in Villa Fiorito, Lomas de Zamora, on the southern edge of Greater Buenos Aires.
- **Died:** 25 November 2020, aged 60.
- **Place of death:** a rented residence in the San Andrés/Villanueva area of Dique Luján, Tigre Partido, Buenos Aires Province.
- **Nationality:** Argentine.
- **Occupation:** professional footballer; football coach and national-team manager; television host; commercial endorser and public personality.
- **Position:** principally attacking midfielder or withdrawn forward; left-footed.
- **Height:** generally listed at approximately 1.65 metres.
- **Nicknames and public names:**
  - **Diego**
  - **Pelusa** (“Fluff”), a childhood family nickname
  - **El Pibe de Oro** (“the Golden Boy”)
  - **El Diez / D10S**, combining his number 10 with *Dios*, “God”
  - **Barrilete cósmico** (“cosmic kite”), from Víctor Hugo Morales’s celebrated commentary on his second goal against England in 1986
  - **El Diego**, denoting the private, contradictory person as distinct from the global “Maradona” persona
  - In Naples, he was frequently represented as a secular saint or quasi-religious protector.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and social background

Maradona was the fifth of eight children and the first son of:

- **Diego Maradona Sr.**, usually called **Don Diego** or **Chitoro** (1927–2015), a labourer who moved from Corrientes Province to Greater Buenos Aires and worked, among other jobs, in a chemicals factory.
- **Dalma Salvadora Franco**, **Doña Tota** (approximately 1929/1930–2011), the dominant emotional presence in Maradona’s accounts of childhood.

Published genealogical descriptions attribute Guaraní or other Indigenous, Spanish, Italian and Croatian elements to the family. These claims are unevenly documented; no comprehensive genealogical record was located. The secure social history is that both parental lines came from northeastern Argentina and that the household lived in severe material poverty after migrating to the Buenos Aires periphery.

### Siblings

The children of Don Diego and Doña Tota included Ana María, Rita Mabel (“Kitty”), Elsa, María Rosa, Diego, Raúl (“Lalo”), Hugo (“El Turco”) and Claudia Nora (“Cali”). Brothers Raúl and Hugo also played professional football, though neither approached Diego’s career. Hugo played in Argentina, Italy, Austria, Spain and Japan and died in 2021.

### Marriage and long partnership

Maradona met **Claudia Rosana Villafañe** while young in Villa Fiorito. Their relationship predated his international fame. They married on 7 November 1989 in a highly publicized ceremony at Luna Park, Buenos Aires. Their daughters were:

- **Dalma Nerea Maradona**, born 1987.
- **Gianinna Dinorah Maradona**, born 1989.

Villafañe sought divorce in 1998, alleging abandonment of the marital home; the divorce became final in 2004. Despite separation, they remained linked through their children, business affairs, litigation and public events.

Gianinna later had a son, Benjamín, with footballer Sergio Agüero, making Maradona Agüero’s former father-in-law and Benjamín’s maternal grandfather.

### Legally recognized children

At his death, five children had established or acknowledged legal status:

1. **Diego Armando Maradona Sinagra / Diego Junior**, born in Naples in 1986 to **Cristiana Sinagra**. Maradona denied or avoided the claim for years. A Naples court declared paternity after he did not submit to testing and ordered maintenance. Father and son eventually reconciled publicly in 2016. [Contemporaneous 1992 court report](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1992/05/06/Italian-court-rules-Maradona-the-father-of-five-year-old-child/2691705124800/)
2. **Dalma Nerea**, daughter of Claudia Villafañe.
3. **Gianinna Dinorah**, daughter of Claudia Villafañe.
4. **Jana Maradona**, born in 1996 to **Valeria Sabalain**. A court established paternity after Maradona repeatedly failed to submit to DNA testing; he developed a public relationship with Jana in 2014–2015.
5. **Diego Fernando Maradona**, born in 2013 to **Verónica Ojeda**, Maradona’s partner during much of 2005–2012.

Maradona’s belated recognition of Diego Junior and Jana is a documented component of his family history. Claims that he had six, nine, eleven or more children remain unproved. Claimants have included **Santiago Lara**, **Magalí Gil** and **Eugenia Laprovíttola**. Their claims should not be treated as established paternity without conclusive DNA evidence or final adjudication.

### Other partners and relationships

Publicly documented or reported relationships included:

- Claudia Villafañe
- Cristiana Sinagra
- Valeria Sabalain
- Verónica Ojeda
- **Rocío Oliva**, a former footballer, whose relationship with him began in the 2010s and ended before his death
- **Mavys Álvarez**, a Cuban woman who says their relationship began when she was sixteen; her allegations are treated separately below.

Maradona’s sexual life attracted a large rumor economy. Assertions about anonymous partners, prostitutes or children should not be converted into fact merely because they were repeated by members of his entourage or entertainment media.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1960–1976

Maradona grew up at Azamor 523 in Villa Fiorito, in a small house without reliable running water. His autobiographical narrative emphasized hunger, flooded dirt roads, the open cesspit near the house and Doña Tota pretending to have stomach trouble so her children could eat her portion. Some details acquired hagiographic repetition, but the family’s poverty is independently documented.

He received his first football at about three. By childhood he played constantly in streets and improvised grounds. A neighborhood friend, **Goyo Carrizo**, brought him to a trial connected with Argentinos Juniors. Scout and youth coach **Francisco “Pancho” Cornejo** became his foundational football mentor.

Maradona joined **Los Cebollitas**, the Argentinos Juniors youth side. The team became famous for a purported 136- or 141-match unbeaten run. The exact number varies by account and incomplete youth records make it difficult to audit, but the team’s sustained dominance and Maradona’s exceptional prominence are well documented. He appeared on television demonstrating ball control and reportedly said that his dreams were to play in the First Division and win a World Cup.

Formal education receded as football became his work. No evidence was found of completed secondary or university education. His principal formation came through the household, neighborhood football and Argentinos Juniors, especially Cornejo.

## Early Professional Career, 1976–1982

### Argentinos Juniors

Maradona made his first-division debut for Argentinos Juniors on **20 October 1976**, ten days before his sixteenth birthday, against Talleres de Córdoba. He entered as a substitute and nutmegged an opponent in an early display that entered his personal mythology.

His first senior goals came in November 1976 against San Lorenzo de Mar del Plata. Between 1978 and 1980 he repeatedly led the Argentine league scoring charts. Argentinos was not a dominant club, but his individual production turned it into a major attraction.

He debuted for the senior national team against Hungary on **27 February 1977**, aged sixteen. National coach **César Luis Menotti** omitted him from Argentina’s 1978 World Cup squad, concluding that he was still too young. Maradona described the omission as a profound injury and formative grievance.

### 1979 World Youth Championship

Menotti made him captain and central figure of Argentina’s team at the 1979 FIFA World Youth Championship in Japan. Argentina defeated the Soviet Union 3–1 in the final; Maradona scored and received the tournament’s Golden Ball. The side also included **Ramón Díaz**, with whom Maradona later had a complicated competitive relationship.

### Boca Juniors

After competing offers and complex negotiations, Maradona joined Boca Juniors in 1981, initially through a loan structure because Boca could not straightforwardly finance his acquisition. He scored twice on his debut against Talleres and starred in a 3–0 victory over River Plate, including a famous goal after leaving goalkeeper Ubaldo Fillol and defender Alberto Tarantini off balance.

Boca won the **1981 Metropolitano championship** under Silvio Marzolini. Maradona’s affection for Boca became a lasting element of his identity, although his boyhood testimony about club loyalties has been variously retold.

## Chronological Life History

## Barcelona and the 1982 World Cup, 1982–1984

### 1982 World Cup

Maradona entered the World Cup in Spain carrying enormous expectations. Argentina lost its opening match to Belgium but advanced from its group. He scored twice against Hungary. Against Italy he was aggressively marked, notably by Claudio Gentile. Argentina was eliminated by Brazil, and Maradona was sent off for kicking João Batista.

### Transfer to Barcelona

Barcelona acquired him in 1982 for a then-world-record fee, usually reported around £5 million or 1.2 billion pesetas. The transfer joined sporting ambition to substantial commercial exploitation of his image.

His Barcelona period was disrupted by:

- hepatitis;
- conflict with club president **Josep Lluís Núñez**;
- nightlife and the beginnings—or acceleration—of cocaine use;
- tactical and personal tensions;
- a severe ankle fracture after a September 1983 tackle by Athletic Bilbao defender **Andoni Goikoetxea**.

Maradona returned unusually quickly from the fracture and helped Barcelona win the 1983 Copa del Rey, Spanish League Cup and Super Cup during his two seasons.

The final rupture came in the **1984 Copa del Rey final** against Athletic Bilbao. After Barcelona lost, Maradona initiated or participated centrally in a mass brawl, kicking and kneeing opponents while players, staff and police fought on the field. The violence occurred before King Juan Carlos and a national television audience. It helped make his departure unavoidable.

## Napoli, 1984–1991

### Arrival and transfer

Napoli bought Maradona in July 1984 for another world-record fee, commonly reported as approximately £6.9 million or 13.5 billion lire. Tens of thousands attended his presentation at the Stadio San Paolo.

Naples was burdened by economic inequality, anti-southern prejudice and a football hierarchy dominated by northern clubs. Maradona’s identification with the city’s marginalized status became central to his appeal. His reported declaration that he wanted to become the idol of Naples’s poor children echoed his Villa Fiorito origin.

### Sporting transformation

Under coaches including **Ottavio Bianchi** and later **Alberto Bigon**, and alongside players such as Careca, Bruno Giordano, Ciro Ferrara, Fernando De Napoli and Salvatore Bagni, Maradona led Napoli through its greatest period:

- **1986–87:** first Serie A championship in club history; Coppa Italia.
- **1987–88:** Serie A runner-up after surrendering a late lead.
- **1988–89:** UEFA Cup, defeating Stuttgart in the final; Serie A runner-up.
- **1989–90:** second Serie A championship.
- **1990:** Italian Super Cup.

The 1987–88 collapse generated rumors of match-fixing, Camorra pressure or deliberate defeat. A cooperating criminal later made accusations surrounding Napoli and organized crime. No final judicial finding established that Maradona or Napoli deliberately lost the championship. It remains a **rumor/allegation**, not proven sporting corruption.

### Cocaine and nocturnal life

Maradona later acknowledged long-term cocaine addiction. Accounts disagree over whether first use occurred at Boca in 1981, Barcelona in 1982–83 or Naples. His own versions changed. By the later Napoli years, addiction, missed training, weight fluctuation and a nightlife structured by intermediaries had become documented problems.

### Contacts with the Giuliano clan

**Documented fact:** photographs showed Maradona socializing with members of the Giuliano family, a powerful Camorra clan based in Forcella, including images at a clan-associated residence with its shell-shaped bathtub. Police and prosecutors noted these contacts, and contemporary reporting connected the clan to illegal gambling and cocaine networks. [Contemporaneous report](https://elpais.com/diario/1989/09/23/deportes/622504811_850215.html) [Italian retrospective citing police documentation](https://www.repubblica.it/sport/calcio/2010/01/22/news/maradona_giuliano_procura-2040831/)

**Allegation:** informants and journalists asserted that the clan supplied drugs, arranged women, recovered stolen property or exploited Maradona’s image and gambling importance.

**Counterclaim:** former clan member Nunzio Giuliano reportedly denied that the family introduced or supplied Maradona with cocaine, attributing his drug access to affluent social circles.

**Assessment:** social contact with identified Camorra members is documented. Knowing participation in drug trafficking, match manipulation or a formal criminal partnership was never established by a conviction.

### 1990 World Cup in Italy

Maradona captained an injury-plagued Argentina. The team lost to Cameroon in the opening match but advanced, eliminating Brazil and Yugoslavia.

Argentina faced Italy in the semi-final at Naples. Maradona appealed to Neapolitans to support him and consider how the rest of Italy treated their city. Argentina won on penalties after a 1–1 draw. The event deepened hostility toward him elsewhere in Italy without erasing his Neapolitan following.

West Germany defeated Argentina 1–0 in the Rome final through an Andreas Brehme penalty. Maradona wept during the presentation ceremony and criticized the refereeing and Italian crowd.

### Positive test and departure

In March 1991 Maradona tested positive for cocaine in Italy and received a fifteen-month suspension. He left Naples without a conventional farewell.

On 26–27 April 1991 Argentine police arrested him in a Buenos Aires apartment and reported finding cocaine. The case ended in a suspended sentence conditioned on treatment rather than imprisonment. The arrest is a **documented criminal proceeding**, distinct from later rumors that he trafficked drugs.

## Return to Football, 1992–1997

### Sevilla

After his suspension, Napoli retained contractual rights while Maradona sought to leave. He joined Sevilla in 1992, reuniting with Argentina coach **Carlos Salvador Bilardo**. His fitness and relations with club officials deteriorated. A dispute after he was substituted against Burgos helped end the episode in 1993.

### Newell’s Old Boys

He joined Newell’s Old Boys in Rosario in September 1993. His presentation drew a large crowd, but he played only a handful of official matches. His departure followed injury, inadequate conditioning and conflict with coach Jorge Castelli.

### First coaching attempts

While unable or unwilling to sustain regular play, Maradona and former teammate **Carlos Fren** coached:

- Deportivo Mandiyú of Corrientes in 1994.
- Racing Club in 1995.

Both appointments were brief and unsuccessful. Neither produced a title.

### 1994 World Cup comeback

Bilardo’s successor **Alfio Basile** recalled Maradona for qualification. He helped Argentina defeat Australia in the intercontinental playoff.

At the 1994 World Cup in the United States he scored against Greece and assisted goals against Nigeria. A post-match test found a prohibited cocktail of stimulants associated with ephedrine. FIFA removed him from the tournament and imposed another fifteen-month suspension.

**Documented fact:** prohibited substances were detected and the suspension followed.

**Maradona’s account:** he said his trainer gave him an American weight-loss supplement without understanding that its formula differed from an Argentine product.

**Disputed implication:** whether he intentionally sought performance enhancement. The sanction did not require proof of intent. His phrase “they cut off my legs” became part of his mythology of persecution by football authorities.

### Return to Boca and retirement

Maradona returned to Boca Juniors in 1995. Performances were intermittent, and he missed several penalties in succession. In 1997 another drug-test controversy followed a positive result for a cocaine metabolite; litigation temporarily allowed him to play.

He made his last professional appearance on **25 October 1997**, a Boca victory over River Plate, and announced retirement around his thirty-seventh birthday.

## Later Public Life, 1998–2007

### Farewell match and “the ball is not stained”

Boca organized Maradona’s testimonial at La Bombonera on 10 November 2001. In an emotional speech he acknowledged personal errors but declared: **“La pelota no se mancha”**—“the ball is not stained.” The phrase became his most durable attempt to separate his football from his life outside it.

### Uruguay medical crisis and Cuba

On 4 January 2000, while in Punta del Este, Uruguay, Maradona suffered a severe hypertensive and cardiac episode. Cocaine was reportedly detected. He entered treatment in Cuba later that month and spent extended periods there.

Cuba provided distance from Argentine media and access to addiction treatment. It also deepened his friendship with **Fidel Castro** and his identification with the Cuban Revolution. He sometimes resided in Havana and received treatment at La Pradera and other facilities.

### Health crisis of 2004

In April 2004 he was admitted in Buenos Aires with severe cardiac and respiratory problems and required mechanical ventilation. Physicians publicly warned of the danger of sudden death. Reports of cocaine in his blood circulated, though family representatives contested some details.

He entered psychiatric/addiction treatment after stabilization. Disputes among relatives, physicians and Maradona himself arose over where and how he should be treated.

### Bariatric surgery and television

On 6 March 2005 he underwent gastric-bypass surgery in Cartagena, Colombia, after his weight had reportedly reached approximately 120 kilograms. He lost substantial weight and returned to public life.

From August to November 2005 he hosted **La noche del 10** on Argentina’s Canal 13. Guests included Pelé, Lionel Messi, Mike Tyson, Charly García, Roberto Gómez Bolaños and Fidel Castro. The program was a ratings success and won Argentine television awards.

In 2007 he was hospitalized with **acute toxic hepatitis** associated with heavy alcohol consumption and later received psychiatric care. Reports that he had died circulated during this crisis; they were false.

---

## Argentina Manager, 2008–2010

The Argentine Football Association appointed Maradona national-team coach in October 2008, succeeding Alfio Basile. His staff included Carlos Bilardo in an administrative role and Alejandro Mancuso as an assistant.

Argentina’s qualifying campaign was unstable:

- a historic 6–1 defeat at high altitude against Bolivia;
- losses to Ecuador, Brazil and Paraguay;
- late qualification through wins over Peru and Uruguay.

After qualification, Maradona delivered a sexually explicit attack on journalists. FIFA suspended him for two months and fined him.

At the 2010 World Cup in South Africa, Argentina defeated Nigeria, South Korea, Greece and Mexico but lost **4–0 to Germany** in the quarter-final. The AFA did not renew his contract after disagreement about retaining his staff.

His management of Lionel Messi became a lasting comparison point. Maradona treated Messi as the central creative figure but did not build a defensively balanced tournament side.

## Coaching, Employment and Travel, 2011–2020

### United Arab Emirates

- **Al Wasl, Dubai, 2011–2012:** Maradona signed a lucrative coaching contract but was dismissed after one season without a major trophy.
- **Dubai sports ambassador, from 2012:** he accepted a promotional role connected with the Dubai Sports Council.
- **Fujairah, 2017–2018:** he coached in the UAE second tier and left after failing to secure automatic promotion.

His years in Dubai included luxury living, sponsorship activity, legal disputes and his relationship with Rocío Oliva.

### Belarus

In 2018 Maradona was announced as chairman or strategic president of **Dynamo Brest**. The title generated international publicity, but his sustained operational involvement was limited.

### Dorados de Sinaloa

Maradona coached second-division Mexican club **Dorados** from September 2018 to June 2019. The appointment was symbolically charged because Sinaloa is associated with the drug trade, but there is no evidence that Maradona’s employment involved cartel activity. He led Dorados to two promotion-playoff finals, losing both to Atlético San Luis, and resigned citing health reasons.

### Gimnasia y Esgrima La Plata

He became coach of Gimnasia in September 2019. Crowds greeted him at away grounds, turning relegation fixtures into public tributes. He briefly resigned amid a club presidential dispute, then returned.

Relegation was suspended when Argentine football was curtailed during the COVID-19 pandemic. His last public appearance came at Gimnasia’s ground on his sixtieth birthday, 30 October 2020. He appeared physically unwell and left soon after a ceremony.

## Footballing Method and Artistic Development

Maradona developed in informal street football and small-sided youth play. His physical and technical profile included:

- exceptional low center of gravity;
- very close left-footed control at speed;
- capacity to absorb physical contact;
- sudden acceleration and directional change;
- disguised passing;
- free-kick and penalty skill;
- improvisation in congested areas;
- control of match tempo from an advanced midfield position;
- psychological command of teammates and crowds.

Principal formative figures included Francisco Cornejo, César Luis Menotti and later Carlos Bilardo. Menotti represented expressive, possession-oriented football; Bilardo emphasized tactical detail and competitive pragmatism. Maradona maintained relationships with both despite their symbolic rivalry in Argentine football culture.

His career coincided with violent defensive challenges, permissive refereeing and poor pitches. This context is material to assessments of his achievement but does not resolve comparisons with Pelé, Lionel Messi or later players.

## Companies, Commercial Projects and Financial Interests

### Earnings and endorsements

Maradona’s playing transfers twice broke the world record. He earned from club salaries, bonuses, testimonials, television appearances, endorsements, image rights and later coaching contracts.

Brands and commercial uses of his name included sportswear, beverages, video games, clothing, perfumes and audiovisual productions. His commercial affairs were repeatedly managed through relatives, agents, lawyers and corporate vehicles rather than a transparent single structure.

### Agents and business managers

Important representatives included:

- **Jorge Cyterszpiler**, childhood friend and early manager.
- **Guillermo Cóppola**, the most famous manager of his peak and post-peak years.
- **Matías Morla**, lawyer and commercial representative in his later years.

Relationships with each eventually produced conflict, recrimination or disputes about money and control.

### Sattvica S.A. and the Maradona trademarks

Morla formed **Sattvica S.A.** in 2015 to administer trademarks and other commercial rights. Proceedings after Maradona’s death concerned approximately 246 marks associated with his name and image.

As of April 2026, an Argentine court had ordered Morla, Maradona’s sisters Rita Mabel and Claudia Nora, assistants Maximiliano Pomargo and Sergio Garmendia, and notary Sandra Iampolsky to stand trial over alleged fraudulent administration. Prosecutors allege that the heirs were improperly deprived of the economic use of Maradona’s brand. The accused had not been convicted as of the cutoff date. [2026 proceedings](https://english.elpais.com/international/2026-04-29/maradonas-lawyer-and-two-of-his-sisters-to-stand-trial-over-undue-profit-from-his-trademarks.html)

### Estate and net worth

Published estimates of Maradona’s net worth at death range from negligible liquid wealth to tens or hundreds of millions of dollars in gross brand value. These estimates are not reliable accounts.

His estate included or potentially included:

- Argentine real property and vehicles;
- accounts and personal goods;
- continuing royalty and licensing claims;
- the Maradona name, signatures and associated trademarks;
- claims against or by former managers and family members;
- foreign contractual rights;
- tax and litigation exposure.

The five legally recognized children became principal heirs. The economic value of the estate cannot be established simply from celebrity-net-worth websites because ownership of trademarks and receivables remained contested.

### Italian tax litigation

Italian authorities pursued Maradona for decades over taxes connected with Napoli compensation in 1985–1990. The publicly quoted demand grew to approximately €37–40 million, much of it interest and penalties. Authorities seized jewelry and watches during visits to Italy.

The procedural history was frequently misreported as a single final “tax-evasion conviction.” The dispute concerned withholding treatment, notification, Napoli’s tax amnesty and whether settlement benefiting the club extended to the player.

In 2021 Italy’s Court of Cassation revived or accepted Maradona’s position on the effect of Napoli’s settlement. A January 2024 Cassation ruling was widely described as definitively eliminating the residual claim. Thus earlier statements that Maradona permanently owed €37–40 million became outdated. The litigation was real; the final procedural outcome favored his estate. [2021 legal analysis](https://www.gpblex.it/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/GPBL_Alerts_01-1.pdf) [2024 decision text reproduced with docket information](https://www.antoniochicoli.it/blog/sentenze-leggi/223/maradona-vince-in-cassazione-corte-suprema-di-cassazione-sezione-tributaria-numero-registro-generale-6318-2019-numero-sezionale-6314-2023-numero-di-raccolta-generale-161-2024-data-pubblicazione-03-01-2024)

## Political, Religious and Ideological Development

### Football politics

Maradona saw himself as an advocate for footballers against governing institutions. He attacked FIFA presidents João Havelange, Sepp Blatter and other officials, alleging that administrators enriched themselves while players bore the risks.

In 1995 he helped promote an international players’ union project with figures including Eric Cantona and George Weah. It did not displace existing institutions but reflected his anti-establishment position.

### Latin American left politics

His politics became increasingly explicit after his Cuban treatment:

- admiration for **Fidel Castro** and the Cuban Revolution;
- a Che Guevara tattoo on his arm and a Castro portrait on his leg;
- support for Hugo Chávez, Evo Morales, Nicolás Maduro and the Kirchner governments;
- opposition to US foreign policy and George W. Bush;
- participation in the 2005 “People’s Summit” protests at Mar del Plata.

On 4 November 2005 he traveled on the **ALBA Express** and appeared with Chávez at an anti-Bush rally. This was a documented political intervention, not merely a private friendship. [Contemporary account](https://www.theguardian.com/football/2013/jun/26/the-knowledge-maradona-george-bush)

His commitments were personalist and performative rather than a systematic written ideology. He supported governments whose records were themselves contested but did not produce a developed political program.

### Catholicism and popular religion

Maradona used Catholic language, wore religious objects, invoked God and later met Pope Francis. At the same time, fans sacralized Maradona himself.

The **Iglesia Maradoniana** was founded in Rosario in 1998, initially as an affectionate joke among supporters including Hernán Amez, Héctor Campomar and Alejandro Verón. It developed commandments, mock liturgy and a calendar dated from Maradona’s birth. Claims of 40,000 or 100,000 formal members are promotional and not independently audited. [Université Laval religious-studies profile](https://croir.ulaval.ca/fiches/e/eglise-maradonienne-l/)

Maradona did not found or administer the church. It is best understood as fandom, parody religion and genuine vernacular devotion existing simultaneously.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Teachers and coaches

- **Francisco Cornejo:** discovered and developed him at Argentinos Juniors.
- **César Luis Menotti:** senior national-team debut, 1979 youth title and Barcelona coach; also omitted him from the 1978 World Cup.
- **Carlos Bilardo:** built the 1986 team around him, coached the 1990 finalists and later reunited with him at Sevilla.
- **Ottavio Bianchi:** coach of Napoli’s first championship and UEFA Cup sides.
- **Alberto Bigon:** coach during Napoli’s second league title.

### Teammates and collaborators

- Jorge Valdano, Jorge Burruchaga, Oscar Ruggeri, Sergio Batista and Héctor Enrique in 1986.
- Claudio Caniggia, with whom he formed an especially visible partnership and friendship.
- Careca and Bruno Giordano in Napoli’s “Ma-Gi-Ca” attack.
- Carlos Fren and Alejandro Mancuso as coaching collaborators.
- Guillermo Cóppola as manager, companion and later adversarial former associate.

### Rivals and antagonists

- **Pelé:** a fluctuating symbolic rivalry over the identity of football’s greatest player. They alternated between public insult and reconciliation.
- **Lothar Matthäus:** principal elite opponent in the 1986 and 1990 World Cup finals, but with mutual respect.
- **Andoni Goikoetxea:** inflicted the 1983 ankle injury.
- **João Havelange and Sepp Blatter:** targets of Maradona’s criticism of FIFA.
- Italian football and media institutions became antagonistic after the 1990 World Cup and 1991 drug case.
- Relations with Claudia Villafañe, Dalma and Gianinna alternated between family solidarity and public litigation.

## Controversies, Criminal Proceedings and Contested Conduct

## Drug use and doping

### Cocaine addiction

**Documented fact:** Maradona acknowledged cocaine addiction extending across much of the 1980s, 1990s and early 2000s. It damaged his health, training, family life and career.

### 1991 Italian doping case

**Documented fact:** a sample tested positive for cocaine metabolites; he received a fifteen-month football suspension.

### 1991 Argentine possession case

**Documented fact:** police arrested him and seized cocaine. He received a suspended judicial disposition requiring treatment. He was not imprisoned for a long custodial term.

### 1994 World Cup case

**Documented fact:** testing found several ephedrine-related stimulants. FIFA expelled him from the tournament and suspended him.

**Disputed issue:** intentional doping versus negligent supplement use. The violation was established; intent was not conclusively established.

### 1997 test

A positive test during his Boca comeback produced judicial challenges and conflicting analyses. It accelerated the end of his playing career but is less procedurally clear than the 1991 and 1994 cases.

## Violence and weapons

### 1984 Copa del Rey brawl

The televised record shows Maradona participating in serious on-field violence. This is not an allegation.

### Shooting at journalists

In February 1994 Maradona fired an air rifle at reporters outside his Moreno property, injuring several. An Argentine court later imposed a suspended sentence of two years and ten months. This was a **conviction**, although it did not send him to prison.

### Rocío Oliva video

A 2014 video appears to show Maradona approaching and striking Oliva during an argument over a telephone. She can be heard telling him to stop hitting her. Maradona acknowledged the confrontation but denied striking her, saying he knocked the phone away. The audiovisual evidence supports describing an apparent physical assault, though no final criminal conviction resulted. [Contemporary video report](https://www.itv.com/news/2014-10-28/diego-maradona-secretly-filmed-beating-his-girlfriend-after-row-over-mobile-phone)

In Madrid in 2017 police responded to another suspected domestic incident. Oliva denied an assault and did not file charges; the investigation was closed. This remains a **reported allegation without charge or conviction**.

## Mavys Álvarez allegations

Álvarez says she met Maradona in Cuba when she was sixteen and he was about forty. She alleges that:

- the relationship involved coercive control and violence;
- he induced or forced her to use drugs;
- she was subjected to sexual abuse or rape;
- she underwent breast surgery without valid parental consent;
- members of his entourage brought her to Argentina and restricted her freedom.

Photographs and acquaintances corroborate that a relationship existed while she was a teenager. They do not by themselves prove each alleged offense.

A 2021 Argentine complaint directed principally at members of Maradona’s entourage alleged human trafficking, servitude, unlawful confinement, drug initiation and injuries. Proceedings were initially rejected or held time-barred. In September 2024 the Federal Cassation Chamber ordered renewed examination of the trafficking complaint concerning her entry into Argentina at sixteen. [Report on reopening](https://www.infobae.com/sociedad/policiales/2024/09/03/la-justicia-ordeno-que-se-investigue-la-denuncia-de-la-mujer-cubana-que-acuso-de-trata-de-personas-a-maradona-y-a-su-entorno/?outputType=amp-type)

As Maradona is dead, he cannot be tried. As of the cutoff date, the allegations against him were not established by a criminal conviction. They should neither be erased nor stated as adjudicated fact.

## Paternity avoidance

Courts established the paternity of Diego Junior and Jana after Maradona declined testing or participation. His prolonged public denials and absence from their childhoods are documented. Later reconciliation did not erase the earlier legal history.

## Russian journalist allegation

Journalist Yekaterina Nadólskaya alleged that Maradona attempted to remove her clothes or sexually harass her during an interview in Russia in 2017. Maradona’s associates disputed her account. No court finding was located; it remains an **allegation**.

## Money-laundering investigation

In 2007 Argentina’s Central Bank reportedly examined Maradona’s account movements from 2005 for suspected money laundering. [Contemporaneous report](https://elpais.com/deportes/2007/03/07/actualidad/1173255723_850215.html)

No sourced final conviction or finding of laundering was located. The correct status is **investigation reported; outcome not established**, not proven laundering.

## Litigation against Claudia Villafañe

Beginning in 2015 Maradona accused Villafañe of misappropriating money and acquiring Miami properties with his funds. Proceedings spread between Argentina and the United States. Villafañe denied stealing from him and argued that transactions reflected her rights and prior arrangements.

Some rulings revived portions of the dispute; others favored Villafañe. No simple, comprehensive final judgment establishing that she stole the amounts Maradona alleged was located. The affair is best classified as prolonged civil and criminal litigation with conflicting outcomes.

## Tax questions

The Italian tax case was genuine and involved enormous assessments, but headlines frequently blurred tax assessment, alleged evasion, notification defects and criminal conviction. The final Cassation outcome materially favored Maradona’s estate; descriptions of him as still owing the full €37–40 million after 2024 are inaccurate.

## Camorra association

The photographs and meetings are established. Drug supply, deliberate match loss, trafficking participation and formal membership are allegations or rumors. No evidence located supports calling Maradona a convicted Camorra associate.

## Illnesses, Injuries, Addictions and Psychological Crises

Documented major health events included:

- severe 1983 ankle fracture;
- hepatitis while at Barcelona;
- recurrent knee and ankle problems;
- cocaine addiction;
- obesity after retirement;
- hypertension and cardiomyopathy;
- January 2000 cardiac crisis in Uruguay;
- April 2004 cardiopulmonary collapse requiring intensive care;
- psychiatric and addiction treatment in Argentina and Cuba;
- 2005 gastric bypass;
- 2007 toxic hepatitis associated with alcohol;
- later knee arthritis and impaired mobility;
- anxiety, depression and alcohol dependence treated with psychotropic drugs;
- October–November 2020 anemia, dehydration, depression or withdrawal concerns and a subdural hematoma requiring surgery.

His public mood could alternate between euphoria, aggression, grandiosity, tearfulness and withdrawal. Retrospective psychiatric diagnoses beyond those documented by treating clinicians would be speculative.

## Final Illness and Death

Maradona entered a La Plata clinic on 2 November 2020. Neurosurgeon **Leopoldo Luque** operated on a subdural hematoma on 3 November. He was discharged on 11 November to a rented house for what was described as home hospitalization.

On 25 November he was found unresponsive. Resuscitation failed.

### Medical cause

The autopsy attributed death to **acute pulmonary edema secondary to exacerbated chronic heart failure**, in the context of dilated cardiomyopathy. Toxicology detected prescribed psychotropic medication but no alcohol or illegal drugs. [Trial medical summary](https://apnews.com/article/fa75d5af559411da0809f8ad21de2974)

### Competing medical interpretations

A court-appointed multidisciplinary panel reported in 2021 that:

- the home-care arrangement was deficient and reckless;
- warning signs of heart failure were not adequately monitored;
- the residence lacked appropriate equipment;
- Maradona might have survived with proper hospital care;
- deterioration may have continued for many hours.

A defense-commissioned 2024 report disputed the duration and avoidability of the terminal cardiac event, arguing that a fatal arrhythmia could have developed quickly. Prosecutors criticized that review as incomplete. [Competing report](https://apnews.com/article/48d67441eb69a0fd367d3534d61c3666)

### Criminal proceedings over the death

Eight health professionals were charged in separate procedural tracks. Seven—including Luque, psychiatrist **Agustina Cosachov**, psychologist **Carlos Díaz**, physician **Pedro Di Spagna**, medical coordinator **Nancy Forlini**, nursing coordinator **Mariano Perroni** and nurse **Ricardo Almirón**—were tried on accusations generally translated as homicide with eventual intent or culpable homicide. Nurse **Gisela Madrid** elected a separate jury process.

The defendants deny criminal responsibility.

The first trial opened in March 2025. It collapsed in May when judge **Julieta Makintach** was found to have participated in the production of an unauthorized documentary centered partly on herself. The court declared a mistrial; no determination of the defendants’ guilt resulted. [Mistrial report](https://apnews.com/article/ad44fc5d1e871224663bd27408be8a04)

A new trial began in March–April 2026. By August it was further complicated by discovery that Swiss Medical had supplied records belonging to Maradona’s father, rather than Maradona, among materials used by the medical panel. Prosecutors called for investigation of the error; defendants argued that it compromised the case. This did not itself establish either medical innocence or guilt. As of 3 September 2026, the retrial remained unresolved. [2026 retrial](https://apnews.com/article/f89775668926225a61fc7c13c303f7c2) [wrong-record dispute](https://efe.com/mundo/2026-08-20/nos-enganaron-y-ahora-quieren-voltear-el-juicio-dice-el-fiscal-del-juicio-por-la-muerte-de-diego-maradona/)

Claims that “Maradona was murdered” are therefore advocacy or theory, not a court finding. The documented cause was cardiac failure; the unresolved legal question is whether deficient care created criminal responsibility.

## Funeral, Burial and Remains

President Alberto Fernández declared **three days of national mourning** by Decree 936/2020. [Official decree](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/normativa/nacional/decreto-936-2020-344573/texto)

Maradona lay in state at the Casa Rosada on 26 November 2020. Enormous crowds gathered, and the ceremony ended early amid clashes and security concerns.

He was buried that day at **Jardín Bella Vista cemetery**, near his parents.

In 2024 a court authorized relocation of his remains to the proposed **Memorial del Diez** in Puerto Madero, designed for public access. Authorization did not mean that the transfer had already occurred. As of the cutoff date, reporting on completion remained less definite than reporting on judicial permission. ## Myths, Legends, Rumors and Disputed Narratives

### “Born kicking”

- **Story:** Maradona entered the world kicking like a footballer.
- **Type:** family folklore/hagiography.
- **Evidence:** no evidentiary value as literal history.
- **Effect:** makes talent appear predestined.

### The Cebollitas’ 136 or 141 unbeaten matches

- **Story:** Maradona’s youth team went undefeated in 136, 141 or another very large number of consecutive matches.
- **Evidence:** sustained success is documented; exact totals vary and youth records are incomplete.
- **Effect:** established the child-prodigy narrative before his professional debut.

### “Hand of God”

- **Story:** his first goal against England on 22 June 1986 was scored partly by Maradona’s head and partly by “the hand of God.”
- **Documented fact:** video shows him punch or handle the ball past goalkeeper Peter Shilton; referee Ali Bin Nasser allowed the goal.
- **Mythic dimension:** the phrase transformed an undetected handball into folk cunning or *viveza criolla*.
- **Reputational effect:** celebrated in Argentina and condemned as cheating in England; inseparable from the second goal four minutes later. [FIFA account](https://www.fifa.com/en/articles/diego-maradona-argentina-england-hand-of-god-1986)

### Revenge for the Falklands/Malvinas War

- **Story:** victory over England avenged Argentina’s 1982 military defeat.
- **Evidence:** Maradona later explicitly connected the emotional meaning of the match to Argentine soldiers killed in the war, while also saying football was not literally war.
- **Qualification:** this was retrospective symbolic interpretation, not proof that the team played on government orders or that the match had military significance.
- **Effect:** turned the quarter-final into a national political myth.

### “Goal of the Century”

Maradona’s second goal against England—carrying the ball from his own half past multiple defenders and Shilton—was later chosen in a FIFA poll as the greatest World Cup goal. Víctor Hugo Morales’s “cosmic kite” commentary amplified its mythic status.

### Napoli’s 1988 collapse was fixed

- **Story:** Camorra interests or illicit betting required Napoli to lose the title; Maradona and teammates complied.
- **Evidence for:** later criminal-source allegations, the collapse from a commanding lead and documented contacts between Maradona and organized-crime figures.
- **Evidence against:** no final judicial proof, no reliable match-by-match documentary chain and ordinary sporting explanations including fatigue and dressing-room conflict.
- **Status:** unresolved allegation/legend, not established match-fixing.

### Maradona as literal deity or saint

Murals, votive offerings, relic-like shirts, the Iglesia Maradoniana and Neapolitan shrines cast him as “D10S.” Most participants understand the language as metaphor, parody or fan devotion; some practices resemble vernacular religion. He was not canonized by any recognized church.

### “FIFA conspiracy” in 1994

- **Story:** FIFA removed him because officials feared his politics, charisma or criticism.
- **Evidence:** his sample contained banned stimulants.
- **Possible supporting context:** he had publicly antagonized football administrators.
- **Evidence lacking:** proof that the laboratory result was fabricated or that FIFA secretly arranged the violation.
- **Status:** self-created persecution narrative built around a real doping result.

### The missing 1986 Golden Ball

Maradona’s 1986 World Cup best-player trophy disappeared for decades. Stories claimed that he:

- lost it in a poker game;
- sold it to pay debts;
- placed it in a Naples bank vault that was robbed;
- had it taken by Camorra figures and melted down.

The trophy resurfaced in France after being bought unknowingly in a mixed auction lot. His heirs claimed theft and attempted to block a 2024 auction, but a French court found insufficient proof that the current holder lacked good faith. No contemporaneous police complaint establishing theft was produced. [Court dispute](https://apnews.com/article/77cb0603c616b7c0fd26146f057cb32a)

### The divided self: “Diego” and “Maradona”

Asif Kapadia’s documentary popularized a formulation attributed to trainer Fernando Signorini: vulnerable “Diego” was the person; “Maradona” was the constructed survivor and celebrity. This is an interpretive framework, not a clinical diagnosis, but it has strongly shaped posthumous biography.

## Works by or Under Maradona’s Name

Maradona was primarily an oral, visual and athletic author. His books were dictated or produced with journalists and editors.

### Books

1. **_Yo soy el Diego (de la gente)_** (2000).
   - Autobiography/memoir.
   - Produced with journalists **Daniel Arcucci** and **Ernesto Cherquis Bialo**.
   - Best classified as an authorized, collaboratively written or ghost-assisted autobiography rather than a wholly self-written manuscript.
   - Contains his accounts of Villa Fiorito, football, cocaine, FIFA, Napoli, family and the 1986 World Cup.
   - English edition: **_El Diego: The Autobiography of the World’s Greatest Footballer_** (2004), translated/edited for the English-language market.
   - [Publisher record](https://www.planetadelibros.com/libro-yo-soy-el-diego/11202)

2. **_México 86: Así ganamos la Copa / Mi Mundial, mi verdad_** (2016).
   - First-person reconstruction of the 1986 World Cup.
   - Written with or ghostwritten by Daniel Arcucci.
   - Authorized testimony, not independently authored prose.
   - [Publisher catalogue](https://www.penguinlibros.com/ar/module/penguinlibros/productosautor?id=997801)

### Television and interviews

- **_La noche del 10_** (Canal 13, 2005): presenter, interviewer and central creative personality.
- Interview with Fidel Castro for *La noche del 10*, recorded in Cuba in 2005. [Archival record](https://cesalc.net.ar/repositorio/items/show/26)
- Numerous long-form interviews with Argentine, Italian, Spanish, Cuban and international media.
- His farewell speech at La Bombonera, 10 November 2001, is a major recorded public text.
- Press conferences at the 1986, 1990, 1994 and 2010 World Cups form an extensive audiovisual corpus.

### Attributed sayings and performance texts

Famous phrases—“La pelota no se mancha,” “me cortaron las piernas,” “la mano de Dios”—are authentically associated with documented speeches or interviews. Many other quotations circulated on posters and social media lack reliable provenance.

Maradona did not write the song **“La mano de Dios.”** It was composed by Alejandro Romero and popularized by Rodrigo Bueno. Nor was Fito Páez’s “Y dale alegría a mi corazón” originally composed for him, although it became associated with his public mythology.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Maradona’s death produced immediate mass mourning in Argentina and Naples. Argentina’s state funeral and three-day national mourning recognized him as a national figure beyond sport.

Naples renamed the **Stadio San Paolo** as the **Stadio Diego Armando Maradona** in December 2020. [Official prefectural authorization](https://www1.prefettura.it/napoli/contenuti/Il_prefetto_autorizza_l_intitolazione_dello_stadio_san_paolo_a_diego_armando_maradona-10016576.htm)

Argentinos Juniors had already named its ground Estadio Diego Armando Maradona. Murals, shrines and memorabilia proliferated in Buenos Aires and Naples. Shirts, the 1986 match ball and other artifacts became exceptionally valuable at auction.

His sporting legacy rests especially on:

- the 1986 World Cup;
- making Napoli champion against wealthier northern clubs;
- the synthesis of playmaking and goalscoring;
- symbolic identification with poor neighborhoods and peripheral regions;
- influence on Argentine number 10s, including Pablo Aimar, Juan Román Riquelme and Lionel Messi.

His posthumous reputation remains divided by:

- admitted addiction and doping;
- apparent or alleged violence against women;
- treatment of children he initially refused to recognize;
- Camorra contacts;
- financial opacity and entourage politics;
- the unresolved criminal case concerning his medical care;
- battles over ownership of his image.

The mythology was not destroyed by these records. In many communities, contradiction is integral to the Maradona figure: genius and self-destruction, political rebel and global commodity, protective family idol and absent father, victim of exploitation and active participant in destructive conduct.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the researched record are included. Mere contemporaneity is excluded.

### Ernesto “Che” Guevara

- **Nature:** explicitly acknowledged political and symbolic influence; Maradona tattooed Guevara’s portrait on his arm and repeatedly invoked him as a revolutionary model.
- **Dates/places:** visible especially from the late 1990s onward; referenced in Cuba, Argentina and international appearances.
- **Label:** **documented influence**, not a personal meeting—Guevara died when Maradona was six.

### Fidel Alejandro Castro Ruz

- **Nature:** friendship, political identification, correspondence, addiction-treatment context and televised interview.
- **Dates/places:** Maradona first visited Cuba in the 1980s; resided there for treatment from January 2000; interviewed Castro in Havana in 2005; called him a “second father.”
- **Label:** **documented fact**.
- Their deaths fell on the same calendar date, 25 November, four years apart—a coincidence later incorporated into hagiography.

### Hugo Rafael Chávez Frías

- **Nature:** political friendship and joint public activism.
- **Dates/places:** prominently the anti-Bush People’s Summit, Mar del Plata, 4 November 2005; later visits and expressions of support in Venezuela.
- **Label:** **documented fact**.

### George Walker Bush

- **Nature:** political antagonism. Maradona helped lead protests against Bush’s presence at the Summit of the Americas.
- **Date/place:** Mar del Plata, Argentina, November 2005.
- **Label:** **documented fact**. There is no evidence of a personal meeting.

### Cristina Elisabet Fernández de Kirchner

- **Nature:** public political support and ceremonial contact. Maradona supported the Kirchner political current and embraced Fernández at Néstor Kirchner’s funeral.
- **Date/place:** Buenos Aires, October 2010, with later public endorsements.
- **Label:** **documented/reported fact**.

### Nicolás Maduro Moros

- **Nature:** public political support and appearances associated with the Venezuelan government.
- **Dates/places:** particularly during the 2010s in Venezuela and public broadcasts.
- **Label:** **reported fact**.

### Charly García

- **Nature:** Argentine celebrity friendship/shared cultural scene; García appeared on *La noche del 10*.
- **Date/place:** Buenos Aires, 2005.
- **Label:** **documented fact**.

### Silvio Berlusconi

- **Nature:** institutional football rivalry rather than a demonstrated close personal relationship. Berlusconi owned AC Milan during Maradona’s Napoli years, when Milan and Napoli competed for Serie A championships.
- **Dates/places:** Italy, principally 1987–1990.
- **Label:** **documented shared football arena**; allegations that political or club interests involving Milan engineered Napoli’s 1988 collapse remain **unproved theory**.

### Diego Armando Maradona

- The subject appears in the atlas roster himself; this is not a separate connection.

No substantiated direct connection was identified with the overwhelming majority of the remaining roster. Shared nationality, political orientation, religious symbolism or residence alone was not treated as evidence of contact.
