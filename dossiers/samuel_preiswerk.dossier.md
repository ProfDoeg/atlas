# Samuel Preiswerk: Research Dossier

## Scope and Identification

This dossier concerns **Samuel Preiswerk (1799–1871)**, the Swiss Reformed pastor, Hebraist, hymn writer, editor, university lecturer, and Antistes of Basel—not his son, the pastor Samuel Gottlob Preiswerk-Stähelin (1825–1912), or later namesakes.

The documentary record is uneven. Preiswerk’s offices, publications, marriages, and institutional career are comparatively well established. His private life is known chiefly through genealogical material, family reminiscence, and later literature about his grandson Carl Gustav Jung. Stories about visions and conversations with spirits therefore require different evidentiary treatment from the official and bibliographical record.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Samuel Preiswerk.
- **Catalog variants:** Samuel Preiswerck; Samuel I Preiswerk; Samuel Preiswerk-Hopf; Samuel Preiswerk-Faber; Sam. Preiswerk.
- **Titles and offices:** Reformed pastor; teacher; professor or lecturer in Hebrew, biblical philology, Old Testament exegesis, and Oriental languages; **Antistes** of the Basel church.
- **Honorific:** Dr. theol. h.c., University of Basel.
- **Born:** 19 September 1799.
- **Birthplace:** Rümlingen, then in the territory of Basel, now Basel-Landschaft, Switzerland.
- **Died:** 13 January 1871.
- **Place of death:** Basel, Switzerland.
- **Confession:** Swiss Reformed Protestant.
- **Municipal affiliation or place of origin:** Basel.
- **Principal residences and workplaces:** Rümlingen; Basel; Arisdorf; Biel-Benken/Benken; Muttenz; Geneva; Basel again.
- **Primary occupations:** Clergyman, educator, Hebraist, exegete, editor, hymn writer.
- **Portrait:** A mid-nineteenth-century portrait reproduced by the Personenlexikon des Kantons Basel-Landschaft is identified as Preiswerk circa 1850.

The description of him as “Lutheran,” occasionally found in English-language summaries, is misleading. He belonged to the Swiss Reformed ecclesiastical tradition.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and siblings

Samuel was the son of:

- **Alexander Preiswerk**, a teacher and Reformed pastor at Rümlingen.
- **Anna Maria Burckhardt**.

The Basel-Landschaft biographical dictionary identifies Samuel as the **second of three children**. His father personally prepared or taught him before university.

Later Jung-family accounts describe visionary or eccentric traits among Samuel’s siblings, including a brother and sister. Those descriptions derive from a disguised family history in Carl Gustav Jung’s 1902 medical dissertation and later family recollections. They do not supply an independently verified clinical or biographical record.

### First marriage

In **1824**, Preiswerk married **Charlotte Hopf** (approximately 1800–1825).

- Charlotte died in 1825, shortly after the marriage.
- The marriage produced one son:
  - **Samuel Gottlob Preiswerk**, also called Samuel Preiswerk-Stähelin (25 August 1825–27 August 1912), later a Reformed pastor.

The repeated name has caused catalog and internet confusion. Records for the son sometimes appear in searches for the elder Samuel.

### Second marriage

In **1826**, Preiswerk married **Caroline Louise Augusta Faber**, generally called **Augusta Faber**, a pastor’s daughter from Nürtingen or Oberensingen in Württemberg.

Her life dates are inconsistently reported:

- Genealogical and German National Library-derived material commonly gives **1805–1862**.
- Some Jung literature gives **1805–1865**.

The stronger authority-linked genealogy supports 1862, but the discrepancy should not be silently erased.

The Basel-Landschaft reference work states that Samuel and Augusta had **eight sons and four daughters**, making twelve children from the second marriage and thirteen children in all. Later accounts sometimes say “thirteen children with Augusta,” apparently conflating the total number with the issue of the second marriage.

Children associated with the second marriage in genealogical and Jung-family sources include:

- **Lucas Preiswerk** (1829–1908).
- **Rudolf Preiswerk**, a pastor and father of Helene “Helly” Preiswerk.
- **Theophil Preiswerk** (1846–1919), later known as a landscape and genre painter.
- **Emilie Preiswerk** (1848–1923), who married the pastor Paul Achilles Jung and became the mother of Carl Gustav Jung.
- Other sons and daughters whose identities appear in extended family trees include members named Eduard, Wilhelm, Anna, Rosina, Marie Louise, and Emma. The accessible genealogical chart is graphically compressed, and the precise allocation of every displayed name to Samuel’s immediate children cannot be established safely from its extracted text alone.

A frequently repeated statement that eight of Carl Jung’s Preiswerk uncles were pastors reflects the strongly clerical character of this family branch, although accessible institutional sources do not individually document all eight careers.

### Descendants of historical importance

Preiswerk’s best-known descendant was his maternal grandson:

- **Carl Gustav Jung** (1875–1961), psychiatrist and founder of analytical psychology.

Preiswerk died more than four years before Jung’s birth and therefore never met him. His importance in Jung’s biography was transmitted through family structure, theological culture, stories about visionary experiences, and the later séances of Preiswerk descendants.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1799–1817

Samuel grew up in a Reformed clerical household in rural Basel territory. His father served as both teacher and pastor and supervised Samuel’s early education.

In **1817**, at approximately seventeen, Samuel passed the **Magister examination**. The exact institutional meaning of the examination in his case is not fully explained in the surviving short biographies, but it marked his readiness for advanced university study.

The evidence does not establish:

- A childhood residence outside Rümlingen.
- Details of household finances.
- Serious childhood illness.
- A surviving childhood diary.
- Specific youthful friendships or named tutors apart from his father.

## Education and Formation, 1817–1822

Preiswerk studied theology at the universities of:

- **Basel**
- **Tübingen**
- **Erlangen**

The regional biography places the course of study through **1821**. A later biographical dictionary of German student fraternities records that he joined the **Erlanger Burschenschaft** during the winter semester of **1821–22**.

Membership in a Burschenschaft placed him within the associational culture of post-Napoleonic German university students. The surviving material does not establish that Preiswerk became a revolutionary nationalist or participated in illegal political activity. His later conflict in Basel-Landschaft, in fact, arose from opposition to prayers demanded by a revolutionary government.

His principal intellectual formation combined:

- Reformed theology.
- Biblical languages, especially Hebrew.
- Old Testament exegesis.
- Evangelical and revivalist Protestantism.
- A growing interest in missionary activity.
- An eschatological understanding of the Jewish people and Palestine.
- Later openness to charismatic currents associated with Edward Irving and the Catholic Apostolic movement.

No complete list of his university teachers has been recovered from the consulted sources.

## Early Ministry and Teaching, 1820–1830

Authority records do not agree perfectly on his earliest assignments:

- The University Library Basel’s authority record gives service as a **vicar at Arisdorf in 1820**.
- It then gives **1821–1824** as vicar at Benken.
- Narrative biographies usually simplify this to a vicariate at **Biel-Benken beginning in 1822**.

These variations probably reflect successive temporary appointments rather than a genuine contradiction.

### Basel Orphanage, 1824–1828

In **1824**, Preiswerk became pastor or preacher at the Basel orphanage.

The position joined pastoral care with the disciplined educational world of a nineteenth-century charitable institution. During this period he began writing poems and hymns, often for missionary meetings or other evangelical gatherings.

The same year he married Charlotte Hopf. Her death in 1825 left him with an infant son. He remarried Augusta Faber in 1826.

### Basel Mission House, 1828/29–1830

In **1828 or 1829**, Preiswerk became a teacher of Hebrew at the **Basel Mission House**. Sources divide between 1828 and 1829, perhaps distinguishing appointment from active service.

A nineteenth-century English-language biblical encyclopedia says he succeeded the theologian and hymn writer **Rudolf Stier**. This is plausible within the mission-house teaching succession but is not independently confirmed by the regional summary.

His work there placed Hebrew study at the service of biblical interpretation and missionary formation. It also joined him to the dense Basel network of pastors, missionary organizers, educators, printers, and evangelical patrons.

## Muttenz, Revolution, Threats, and Dismissal, 1830–1834

In **1830**, Preiswerk became pastor at **Muttenz**.

His ministry coincided with the political conflict that separated the rural districts from the city of Basel. The Basel-Landschaft revolution and ensuing civil confrontation culminated in the creation of the canton of Basel-Landschaft in 1832–33.

### Refusal to read revolutionary prayers

**Documented reported fact:** Preiswerk refused to read or lead prayers supportive of the revolutionary cause or government.

The surviving concise sources do not reproduce the required prayer or a dismissal decree. They agree, however, that:

- His refusal was understood politically.
- He received death threats.
- He was driven from, removed from, or formally dismissed from his Muttenz pastorate.
- The dismissal is dated **1832** in the nineteenth-century *Allgemeine Deutsche Biographie* and **1833** in the Basel-Landschaft and University of Basel honor records.

The difference likely separates his effective expulsion during the disturbances from the later administrative termination by the new Basel-Landschaft government.

### Political position

Preiswerk should not automatically be classified through later left-right terminology. The evidence supports:

- Loyalty to the existing Basel ecclesiastical order.
- Refusal to lend liturgical authority to the rural revolutionary movement.
- Identification with the conservative or orthodox religious camp.
- Personal exposure to political intimidation and loss of employment.

There is no evidence in the consulted material that he organized violence, participated in armed operations, or was criminally prosecuted.

### Economic hardship

After losing the Muttenz office, he supported his large and growing family through private instruction, including work described as history teaching.

The *Allgemeine Deutsche Biographie* says he maintained the family **“kümmerlich”**—meagerly or with difficulty. No salary figures, debts, insolvency filings, real-estate records, or estate inventory were located. His period of unemployment appears to have been the clearest documented financial crisis of his life.

### The *Christliche Volksbote*

A University Library Basel account of digitized newspapers identifies Preiswerk as one of several displaced Basel-Landschaft pastors who founded the **Christliche Volksbote aus Basel**. The other named founders include Adolf Sarasin-Forcart, Johannes Linder, and Eduard Bernoulli.

This newspaper connection belongs to the institutional aftermath of the cantonal separation. The consulted source does not define Preiswerk’s exact editorial ownership, financial stake, or duration of involvement.

## Geneva, 1834–1837

In **1834**, Preiswerk was appointed to teach at the **École de Théologie of the Société Évangélique de Genève**, an institution independent of the established Genevan church.

His subjects included:

- Old Testament exegesis.
- Hebrew.
- Oriental languages.

Some sources call him an extraordinary professor; others simply call him a teacher or professor. The apparent variation reflects institutional terminology rather than a demonstrable change of rank.

### Hebrew grammar

During this period he prepared his principal scholarly book:

**Grammaire hébraïque, précédée d’un précis historique sur la langue hébraïque**

The first edition appeared at Geneva in **1838**, published by Ch. Gruaz, shortly after or around his departure. The first edition contained fifty-nine preliminary pages and 280 pages. It went through multiple editions; biographical sources report four editions, while the third edition was current by the time of his death.

Separate pedagogical tables appeared under the title:

**Paradigmes du verbe parfait, des verbes imparfaits et des noms de la langue hébraïque, extraits de la grammaire hébraïque**

The grammar’s publication in French reflects his Genevan teaching environment and broadened its potential readership beyond German-speaking theological schools.

### Break over Irvingian contacts

Preiswerk left Geneva in **1837**.

- The nineteenth-century ADB says he resigned for “confessional reasons.”
- Later biographies specify that the Société Évangélique required him to sever contact with adherents of **Edward Irving** or the developing Catholic Apostolic movement, and that he refused.
- The available summaries do not identify every Irvingite contact, reproduce institutional minutes, or prove that Preiswerk formally joined the Catholic Apostolic Church.

**Best-supported interpretation:** Preiswerk was sufficiently sympathetic to, or unwilling to denounce, charismatic and restorationist Christians influenced by Irving that continued employment became impossible.

**Not established:** formal Irvingite ordination, membership in an Irvingite apostolic hierarchy, or repudiation of the Reformed Church.

This episode is significant because it shows that his supernatural and eschatological interests were not solely inventions of later Jung-family mythology. His documented religious network did extend toward a controversial prophetic movement.

## Return to Basel and Scholarly-Editorial Work, 1837–1845

Preiswerk returned to Basel in **1837** and initially lived without a permanent pastoral appointment.

### University career

He habilitated in **1839** and became a **Privatdozent in biblical philology**, with teaching associated particularly with Hebrew language and literature and Old Testament exegesis.

Later accounts sometimes compress this into a university “chair.” The evidence more securely supports an unpaid or fee-dependent private lectureship rather than a continuously salaried full professorship.

The University of Basel later granted him:

- A theological licentiate honoris causa, reported in the university honor-roll as **1851**.
- The degree **Doctor of Theology honoris causa on 25 December 1860**, explicitly for his services to Hebrew instruction at the university.

### *Das Morgenland*, 1838–1843

From June **1838 through 1843**, Preiswerk edited and contributed to:

**Das Morgenland. Altes und Neues für Freunde der heiligen Schrift. Eine Monatsschrift**

Six annual volumes appeared, initially through Spittler and later Bahnmaier.

The periodical combined:

- Biblical antiquities.
- Biblical geography.
- Contemporary reports from Palestine and the wider Ottoman and Middle Eastern world.
- Missionary correspondence.
- Accounts of Jews, Christians, Druze, and other communities.
- Political and technological news.
- Maps and illustrations.
- Prophetic and eschatological interpretation.

The first volume’s contents included material on the descendants of Abraham, names of the Promised Land, the contemporary state of the “Orient,” the four monarchies in Daniel, the Druze, Muhammad Ali of Egypt, letters from Jerusalem, steamships and railways, the Ark of the Covenant, the road to Jericho, and Daniel’s seventy weeks.

A 2022 University of Basel study by Noemi Schürmann treats the journal as an important source for nineteenth-century Basel representations of Palestine. It argues that the publication contained a differentiated but unmistakably European Protestant “Orient” discourse and documents a network connecting Basel missions and research with people and information in the region.

### Jewish restoration and Palestine

Preiswerk used *Das Morgenland* to support the return or settlement of Jews in Palestine. A passage discussed in later scholarship spoke of a **“Reconstituierung der jüdischen Nation”** and recognized a Jewish national “right” or “claim” to the land and inheritance of its ancestors.

This position was:

- Based primarily on Protestant biblical prophecy and restoration theology.
- Articulated decades before Theodor Herzl’s political Zionism.
- Connected to missionary and conversionist expectations.
- Not equivalent in institutional or political form to the Zionist movement founded in the 1890s.

Thus:

- **Documented fact:** Preiswerk advocated Jewish return or national restoration in Palestine.
- **Reasonable historical characterization:** he was a Christian restorationist and a Basel precursor of Zionism.
- **Potentially misleading shorthand:** calling him simply a “Zionist,” without noting that the organized political movement and even the term in its later sense postdated him.

The 2022 Basel study also places his journalism within Orientalism: it transmitted European descriptions, classifications, and biblical projections of Palestine while participating in actual missionary and scholarly information networks.

### Basel ecclesiastical appointments

Institutional records distinguish several stages:

- **1839:** *Gemeiner Helfer*, a general assistant or substitute minister.
- **1840:** second assistant or deacon at St. Leonhard.
- **1843 or 1845:** full pastor at St. Leonhard.

The ADB gives institution as pastor in 1843; the Basel-Landschaft biography and library authority record give **1845**. The likely sequence is an assistant appointment followed by installation as full pastor.

A printed **Einsegnungsrede**—consecration or installation address—for Preiswerk was delivered by **Jacob Burckhardt**, the senior Basel clergyman of that name, not the younger cultural historian with whom he is easily confused.

## Hymnody and Worship

Preiswerk wrote hymns for mission meetings and other occasions rather than constructing a literary career independent of the church.

In **1844**, two friends anonymously edited the **Evangelischer Liederkranz aus älterer und neuerer Zeit**, a collection of 271 hymns. Sixteen texts by Preiswerk were included. He subsequently participated in or controlled the second edition.

Nine of his hymns entered Albert Knapp’s **Evangelischer Liederschatz**, and several passed into congregational hymnals.

He also helped edit the **Basel Gesangbuch of 1854**.

### Principal surviving hymns

#### “Die Sach’ ist dein, Herr Jesu Christ”

- First two stanzas by Preiswerk, dated **1829**.
- A third stanza was later supplied by **Felician Martin von Zaremba**, dated 1869.
- It became a durable Protestant missionary hymn and circulated in German and translated hymnals.
- Later attributions sometimes assign the whole composite text to Preiswerk; the authorship should be divided between Preiswerk and Zaremba.

#### “Das ist der Gemeine Stärke”

- Published in the 1844 *Evangelischer Liederkranz*.
- A mission hymn of five four-line stanzas.
- Translated by Catherine Winkworth as **“Hark! the Church proclaims her honour”** in the second series of *Lyra Germanica* (1858).
- Winkworth’s citation reportedly altered or misremembered the first German line as “Dies ist der Gemeinde Stärke.”
- It subsequently appeared in English and American hymnals.

#### “Einer nur ist’s ewig wert”

- Published or documented in **1844**.
- A five-stanza Christological hymn organized around Christ’s incarnation, crucifixion, resurrection, ascension, and promised return.

#### Other catalogued German texts

- “Wir treten in das neue Jahr”
- “Wohlan, wir schlagen Hand in Hand”

International hymn databases also record English, Portuguese, Spanish, Polish, Dutch, French, and Swahili translations or adaptations of Preiswerk’s texts. These are translations, not separate original works by him.

## Pastor of St. Leonhard, 1845–1859

As pastor of St. Leonhard, Preiswerk combined parish ministry, preaching, Hebrew instruction, writing, and participation in Basel’s evangelical institutions.

Digitized manuscripts or catalog records preserve several funeral addresses:

- **Rede bei der Beerdigung des Herrn Karl Rudolf Preiswerk V.D.M.**, delivered 9 January 1851.
- **Leichenrede bei der Beerdigung von Friedrich Bruder-Bürgin**, delivered 15 December 1853.
- **Leichenrede zur Beerdigung von Martin Schneider-Baltz**, associated with 11 April 1859.
- **Leichen-Rede bei der Beerdigung von J. J. Merian-Burckhardt**, delivered 30 July 1859.

These records show routine pastoral activity beyond the better-known grammar, journal, and hymns.

An archival file in the Staatsarchiv Basel-Stadt specifically concerning Preiswerk survives for **1849**. Its online description supplies no subject detail; conclusions about its contents require physical archival consultation.

### Frey-Grynaeisches Institut and educational institutions

Preiswerk served on the governing board of the **Frey-Grynaeisches Institut** from **1859 to 1871**, in his capacity as Antistes.

An institutional history also associates him or his St. Leonhard office with the Basel **Alumneum**, a theological student residence and support institution, over the period 1844–1871. The exact division between personal service and ex-officio representation is not fully stated in the accessible excerpt.

### Hebrew antiquarian activity

A history of the Jews in Basel mentions a copy, made by Antistes Samuel Preiswerk, of a Hebrew funerary inscription. This is a small but concrete example of his continued work with Hebrew texts and local Jewish antiquities.

## Antistes of Basel, 1859–1871

In **1859**, the Basel Grand Council elected Preiswerk **Antistes**, the senior ecclesiastical officer of the Basel Reformed church. He served at the Basel Münster until his death.

The office involved leadership of the clergy and responsibilities in church and school administration. It was not a Catholic episcopate, although some English accounts loosely describe it as a superintendent or highest ecclesiastical dignity.

As Antistes he succeeded **Jacob Burckhardt-Schorndorff**, another Basel pastor who should not be confused with the art historian Jacob Burckhardt.

Preiswerk’s election demonstrates that his earlier political dismissal and Genevan confessional dispute did not permanently marginalize him. By 1859 he had attained the highest formal position in Basel’s Reformed establishment.

### Academic honors

- **1851:** Licentiate of Theology honoris causa, according to the University of Basel honor register.
- **25 December 1860:** Doctor of Theology honoris causa.
- **Reason stated for the doctorate:** services to instruction in Hebrew at the University of Basel.

### Later theological publication

His essay **“Der Heilige Geist und die christliche Kirche”** appeared in *Zur Verantwortung des christlichen Glaubens*, second edition, Basel, 1862, pages 214–233.

The subject is consistent with his career-long interest in the activity of the Holy Spirit, ecclesiology, and charismatic or eschatological Christianity.

## Religious, Intellectual, and Political Development

Preiswerk’s intellectual position joined several currents that are sometimes treated separately:

1. **Confessional Reformed Christianity:** He remained a Basel Reformed minister and ultimately its Antistes.
2. **Evangelical revivalism:** His work at the Mission House, hymnody, and missionary networks placed him in the nineteenth-century awakening movement.
3. **Hebraism:** He treated Hebrew as essential to scripture and produced a substantial French grammar.
4. **Biblical eschatology:** His journal repeatedly interpreted contemporary geography and politics through Daniel, Revelation, and prophetic restoration.
5. **Christian restorationism concerning the Jews:** He expected a collective Jewish return to Palestine.
6. **Charismatic openness:** His refusal to abandon contacts with Irvingian Christians shows resistance to strict institutional boundaries around prophecy and spiritual gifts.
7. **Political conservatism in Basel’s cantonal conflict:** He refused liturgical cooperation with the Basel-Landschaft revolution.

These positions were not necessarily contradictory within nineteenth-century evangelical Protestantism. Political conservatism, missionary activism, millenarian expectation, biblical scholarship, and interest in supernatural gifts could coexist.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Alexander Preiswerk

**Documented fact:** Father, early teacher, Reformed pastor, and first formative theological influence.

### Charlotte Hopf

**Documented fact:** First wife, married in 1824; died in 1825; mother of Samuel Gottlob Preiswerk.

### Augusta Faber

**Documented fact:** Second wife from 1826; mother of twelve children according to the Basel-Landschaft reference work.

**Family report:** Augusta experienced fainting, trance, “second sight,” and prophetic somnambulism after an illness in youth. The primary published source behind this tradition is Jung’s disguised clinical genealogy, supplemented by later family accounts. No contemporary medical record was located.

### Rudolf Stier

**Reported fact:** Preiswerk succeeded Stier as Hebrew teacher at the Basel Mission House.

### Edward Irving and Irving’s followers

**Documented at secondary level:** Preiswerk maintained contacts with representatives of Irving’s charismatic movement and would not abandon them when pressured by his Genevan employer.

**Not established:** a personal meeting with Irving himself, who died in 1834, or Preiswerk’s formal membership in the Catholic Apostolic Church.

### Basel missionary and evangelical network

Preiswerk worked in or with:

- Basel Mission House.
- Société Évangélique de Genève.
- *Christliche Volksbote*.
- *Das Morgenland*.
- St. Leonhard congregation.
- University of Basel.
- Frey-Grynaeisches Institut.
- Basel Münster and church administration.

### Adolf Sarasin-Forcart, Johannes Linder, and Eduard Bernoulli

**Reported fact:** Fellow displaced pastors and co-founders of the *Christliche Volksbote*.

### Jacob Burckhardt-Schorndorff

**Documented institutional relationship:** Senior Basel clergyman, predecessor as Antistes, and participant in Preiswerk’s ecclesiastical installation.

### Catherine Winkworth

**Documented literary relationship without demonstrated personal contact:** English translator of “Das ist der Gemeine Stärke.”

### Carl Gustav Jung

**Documented family relationship:** Maternal grandson, born after Preiswerk’s death.

**Documented intellectual and mythic influence:** Jung repeatedly invoked the Preiswerk family’s visionary reputation. His dissertation used a disguised family psychiatric history that appears to describe Samuel as an intelligent clergyman subject to waking hallucinations. Jung also transmitted stories that Preiswerk conversed with spirits.

### Helene “Helly” Preiswerk

**Documented family relationship:** Granddaughter of Samuel and cousin of Jung.

**Reported séance role:** During Jung-led séances in the 1890s, Helly’s purported spirit guide identified himself as “Grandfather Preiswerk.” These events occurred decades after Samuel’s death and do not constitute evidence that Samuel communicated with the group.

## Finances, Property, and Institutional Interests

No evidence was found that Preiswerk:

- Owned a commercial company.
- Held patents.
- Operated an investment enterprise.
- Accumulated major financial holdings.
- Participated in banking or securities structures.
- Was investigated for debt, embezzlement, or financial misconduct.

His identifiable economic relationships were occupational:

- Clerical salaries.
- Teaching and lecture income.
- Private lessons after his expulsion from Muttenz.
- Editorial and publishing activity.
- Institutional office.

The only explicit financial characterization in the consulted biographical record is the statement that he supported his family with difficulty after his political displacement.

No verified real-estate inventory, probate value, will, or estate settlement was located.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, or Financial Questions

### Political removal from Muttenz

**Status: documented event; exact administrative chronology varies.**

Preiswerk was displaced or dismissed after refusing to read prayers favoring the Basel-Landschaft revolution. He reportedly received death threats.

- No criminal conviction is reported.
- No evidence was found that he participated in political violence.
- Descriptions such as “driven out” reflect the dangerous revolutionary setting; the 1833 Basel-Landschaft dismissal was also an official political act.

### Geneva confessional dispute

**Status: documented at biographical-summary level.**

He left the Evangelical Society’s theological school after refusing to end contacts with Irvingian Christians.

- This was an employment and confessional controversy.
- No lawsuit or criminal proceeding was located.
- His exact personal theological commitments remain incompletely documented.

### Spirit visions and possible hallucinations

**Status: retrospective family report, later clinical characterization—not a contemporaneous diagnosis.**

Jung’s 1902 dissertation describes the grandfather in a disguised family history as a very intelligent clergyman who frequently experienced “waking hallucinations,” including visionary phenomena. Jung later reportedly applied that term directly to Samuel while also regarding it as an inadequate label.

No nineteenth-century medical file, institutional competency proceeding, or contemporary allegation that Preiswerk was mentally unfit was located. His long pastoral, teaching, and administrative career weighs against any inference that such experiences incapacitated him.

### Orientalism and Christian restorationism

**Status: documented intellectual position; modern critical interpretation.**

Preiswerk’s journal framed Palestine and its peoples through European Protestant biblical, missionary, ethnographic, and prophetic categories. The 2022 Basel study examines this as nineteenth-century Orientalism while emphasizing that the journal’s contents were not uniform and included real cross-cultural research contacts.

His advocacy of Jewish restoration was not a secular doctrine of national self-determination. It existed inside a Christian eschatological framework that commonly anticipated Jewish conversion. Calling it simply modern Zionism can obscure those motives.

### Alleged Zionist political role

**Status: retrospective characterization; some later details unverified.**

Later writers call Preiswerk a “forerunner of Zionism.” This is defensible if narrowly defined as advocacy of Jewish return and national reconstitution in Palestine. It does not prove:

- Membership in a Zionist organization.
- Contact with Herzl.
- Influence on the Basel Program.
- Participation in a Zionist congress.

He died in 1871, twenty-six years before the First Zionist Congress.

### Adversarial search findings

Dedicated searches combined Preiswerk’s name with German and English terms for controversy, scandal, accusation, fraud, lawsuit, conspiracy, criticism, corruption, and criminality, including searches limited conceptually to 2024–26.

No evidence was located of:

- Fraud.
- Corruption.
- Money laundering.
- Sexual scandal.
- Criminal charge or conviction.
- Civil lawsuit.
- Intelligence activity.
- Ownership misconduct.
- Academic plagiarism.
- Abuse investigation.

Most apparently adverse results concerned:

- His political removal.
- The Irvingian dispute.
- Retrospective claims of hallucinations or spirit communication.
- Criticism of Christian Zionism and Orientalism.
- Other people with the same name.
- Controversies involving Jung or Helly Preiswerk rather than Samuel himself.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### The empty chair for Charlotte Hopf

**Story:** Preiswerk supposedly kept a special chair in his study for the spirit of his first wife, Charlotte, who visited him once each week.

**Appearance and circulation:** The story is prominent in Jung biographies and literature on Jung’s supernatural background. Its traceable basis is family memory recorded through Jung, especially reminiscences given to Aniela Jaffé in 1957, long after all direct participants were dead.

**Evidence for:** Persistent transmission within the Preiswerk-Jung family; compatibility with reports that Samuel believed himself surrounded by spirits.

**Evidence against or limiting:** No contemporary diary, letter, household account, or eyewitness statement from Charlotte, Augusta, or Samuel was found. The story was recorded approximately eighty years after Samuel’s death.

**Classification:** Family legend or reported family memory; unverified as a literal event.

**Reputational effect:** It turned Preiswerk from an obscure Hebraist into a symbolic ancestor of Jungian engagement with spirits, visions, and the autonomous psyche.

### Emilie guarding her father from spirits

**Story:** While Preiswerk composed sermons, his daughter Emilie sat behind him so spirits could not disturb him or read over his shoulder.

**Appearance:** Jung-family tradition; parapsychological and biographical writings repeat it.

**Evidence for:** Multiple later retellings derived from the family.

**Limitations:** No contemporary corroboration; Emilie was a child in the household and her later narration may have been shaped by family interpretation.

**Classification:** Reported family memory, not independently verified.

### Preiswerk as a habitual “spiritualist”

**Story:** He actively practiced Spiritualism in the later séance-based sense.

**Evidence:** Claims of visions, conversations with spirits, and Irvingian contacts.

**Counterevidence and qualification:** Modern Spiritualism conventionally dates from the late 1840s. Preiswerk’s religious framework was older Protestant visionary, prophetic, and charismatic Christianity. The evidence does not show that he joined a Spiritualist society or regularly conducted table-turning séances.

**Classification:** Partly anachronistic label. “Visionary” or “charismatically inclined Reformed clergyman” is better supported.

### Hebrew as the language of heaven

**Story:** Preiswerk learned Hebrew because he believed it was spoken in heaven and wanted to read the celestial newspapers after death.

**Appearance:** Repeated by Jung and modern biographers.

**Evidence:** Family anecdote only.

**Assessment:** The line has the form of family humor or self-created mythology. It may reflect Preiswerk’s wit, literal piety, or a later caricature of his Hebraism.

**Classification:** Family anecdote; wording and literal belief unverified.

### Augusta’s apparent death and revival

**Story:** Augusta Faber suffered a severe fever at approximately twenty, remained in a deathlike trance for thirty-six hours or three days, and revived only after a red-hot iron was applied to her head. She thereafter displayed clairvoyance or prophetic somnambulism.

**Appearance:** Jung’s dissertation and later family biographies.

**Evidence for:** A repeated family narrative and Jung’s early clinical account.

**Evidence against or limiting:** No medical or civil record was located; duration varies between versions; interpretations such as typhoid, catalepsy, somnambulism, or paranormal “second sight” remain speculative.

**Classification:** Family medical legend with an uncertain historical core.

### “Grandfather Preiswerk” at Helly’s séances

**Story:** In séances organized by the young Jung, Helly Preiswerk spoke in a bass voice as the deceased Samuel and delivered religious messages, including a mission involving the Jewish people.

**Evidence:** Jung’s dissertation, later family reconstruction by Stefanie Zumstein-Preiswerk, and scholarship on mediumship.

**Complication:** Jung disguised identities and shifted the séance chronology. His 1902 account placed sessions around 1899–1900; family research places their beginning in 1895, interruption in 1896–97, and resumption afterward.

**Assessment:** It is documented that participants represented or understood an alleged communicating personality as Samuel Preiswerk. That does not establish survival after death or spirit communication.

**Classification:** Documented séance claim; supernatural explanation unresolved and unverifiable.

### Herzl honoring Preiswerk

**Story:** Theodor Herzl supposedly commemorated Preiswerk in an address as a precursor of Zionism.

**Evidence:** The claim appears in later Jung-related religious biographies.

**Problems:** The consulted records of Preiswerk and accessible histories of the Zionist congresses did not provide the speech, date, or exact wording. Preiswerk had died before Herzl’s movement began.

**Classification:** Unverified secondary claim. Preiswerk’s restorationist writing is real, but a specific Herzl tribute requires confirmation from congress proceedings, Herzl’s speeches, or diaries.

### “The Chair Kept Empty”

The atlas journey title encapsulates three distinct layers:

1. **Documented:** Preiswerk was a Basel Hebraist and editor of *Das Morgenland*.
2. **Reported family memory:** he maintained an empty chair for Charlotte’s spirit.
3. **Documented later reception:** his descendants held séances in which an entity claiming to be Preiswerk spoke.

The title is therefore historically apt as reception history but should not be treated as a single verified biographical fact.

## Death and Burial

Samuel Preiswerk died in Basel on **13 January 1871**, aged seventy-one.

No consulted source specifies:

- The terminal illness.
- A precipitating accident.
- A physician’s diagnosis.
- An autopsy.
- A suicide, addiction, or psychiatric crisis.
- The immediate medical cause of death.

A memorial volume appeared in Basel in 1871 with a funeral sermon by **J. R. Respinger** and a selection of Preiswerk’s hymns.

The family genealogy cites gravestones at Basel’s **Wolfgottesacker**, indicating a burial or surviving memorial there, but a cemetery register was not independently consulted.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Preiswerk’s reputation separated into several strands.

### Ecclesiastical memory

He was remembered as:

- A distinguished preacher.
- A senior leader of the Basel Reformed church.
- A representative of orthodox and evangelical Basel Protestantism.
- A clergyman whose career survived political expulsion and confessional controversy.

### Hebrew scholarship

His French Hebrew grammar went through several editions and earned formal recognition from the University of Basel. It was later superseded by newer philological grammars, but it remains evidence of Hebrew pedagogy in francophone Protestant theological education.

### Hymnody

His most durable direct cultural legacy is hymnological. “Die Sach’ ist dein, Herr Jesu Christ” and “Das ist der Gemeine Stärke” crossed linguistic and national borders through mission and revivalist hymnals.

Authorship frequently became blurred as translators, later stanza writers, editors, and hymnal compilers modified the texts.

### Christian restorationism and Zionist prehistory

Modern Basel scholarship presents him as an early Christian advocate of Jewish return to Palestine. His importance lies less in direct institutional continuity with Herzl than in documenting a nineteenth-century Protestant restorationist environment that preceded political Zionism.

The label remains contested because his aims were theological and missionary and because political Zionism was later organized principally by Jewish activists under different historical pressures.

### Jungian genealogy

Preiswerk achieved much wider posthumous fame as Carl Jung’s visionary maternal grandfather.

The family stories supplied later writers with an ancestral narrative for Jung’s interest in:

- Spirits.
- Mediumship.
- Dreams.
- Apparitions.
- Autonomous psychic personalities.
- Religious experience.
- The relation between psychopathology and visionary meaning.

This influence was indirect: Jung never met Preiswerk. The psychologically significant object was the family memory and symbolic image of Preiswerk, not a personal teacher-student relationship.

### Mythic afterlife

In later retellings Preiswerk became:

- The pastor who reserved a chair for a dead wife.
- The Hebraist preparing to read newspapers in heaven.
- The stern Basel Antistes surrounded by spirits.
- A proto-Zionist honored by Herzl.
- The deceased spirit guide of Helly Preiswerk.
- An occult ancestor of analytical psychology.

Some of these images rest on documented writings or family practices; others survive only as late recollection. Together they have eclipsed much of his ordinary work as parish pastor, language teacher, funeral preacher, and institutional administrator.

## Atlas Connections

### Carl Gustav Jung

**Evidentiary label: documented family relationship and documented posthumous influence.**

Preiswerk was Jung’s maternal grandfather through Emilie Preiswerk Jung. They never met: Preiswerk died on 13 January 1871, and Jung was born on 26 July 1875.

Jung’s 1902 dissertation included a disguised psychiatric genealogy describing visionary traits in the Preiswerk family. In later recollections he identified Samuel as a visionary who conversed with spirits and kept a chair for his first wife. The alleged spirit of “Grandfather Preiswerk” also appeared in the séances Jung conducted with Helly Preiswerk. The connection is therefore genealogical, intellectual, and mythological, but entirely posthumous from Jung’s side.

### Theodor Herzl

**Evidentiary label: reported posthumous reputational connection; direct connection unverified.**

Preiswerk advocated Jewish national restoration in Palestine decades before Herzl organized political Zionism. Later writers therefore call him a precursor of Zionism.

A secondary claim says Herzl commemorated Preiswerk in a speech. The consulted sources did not establish the exact speech, date, wording, or primary document. No meeting or correspondence was possible because Preiswerk died in 1871, before Herzl’s Zionist career.

### Abraham and Moses

**Evidentiary label: documented subjects of religious and editorial work, not biographical encounters.**

Abraham, his descendants, the Promised Land, Mosaic scripture, and Hebrew tradition were recurring subjects in Preiswerk’s biblical scholarship and *Das Morgenland*. These are textual and theological connections, not historical meetings, and consequently are not counted as personal atlas crossings.

### Remaining roster

No documented meeting, correspondence, collaboration, rivalry, patronage, family tie, shared institutional activity, or explicitly acknowledged personal influence was established between Preiswerk and the other named atlas figures.

Shared geography, religious subject matter, or mere contemporaneity was not treated as evidence of connection.
