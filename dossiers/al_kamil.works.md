## Works by Al-Malik al-Kamil

### Architectural obras and urban projects

- **1207–08–1238:** *Residential and administrative complex of the Cairo Citadel*. An architectural project through which al-Kamil made the previously military citadel a royal residence, adding palatial and administrative buildings; almost nothing survives intact, and the precise division between his work and that of al-ʿAdil remains disputed. ([Behrens-Abouseif](https://doi.org/10.3406/anisl.1989.1117); [Rabbat](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=0dc79a7d67236591adafcbb32c0d9b08e1c9b3bb&repid=rep1&type=pdf))

- **1207–08–early thirteenth century:** *Completion and enlargement of the Cairo Citadel fortifications*. This obra comprises gates, curtain walls, and massive towers rather than a single newly founded building: Saladin initiated the citadel, al-ʿAdil continued it, and al-Kamil completed or substantially transformed parts of it. Individual masonry campaigns cannot always be securely separated, so attribution of the whole citadel to al-Kamil would be misleading. ([Creswell](https://www.persee.fr/doc/bifao_0255-0962_1924_num_23_1_1811); [Discover Islamic Art](https://islamicart.museumwnf.org/database_item.php?id=monument%3Bisl%3Beg%3Bmon01%3B8%3Ben); [Speiser](https://www.ifao.egnet.net/anisl/38/4))

- **Probably early thirteenth century:** *Ayyubid aqueduct to the Cairo Citadel*. A hydraulic project carrying Nile water toward the citadel along the earlier city wall; Creswell and later scholars provisionally associate its construction or completion with al-Kamil, but the textual and archaeological evidence does not permit a secure date or exclusive attribution. ([Rabbat](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=0dc79a7d67236591adafcbb32c0d9b08e1c9b3bb&repid=rep1&type=pdf); [Confluent Waters, Fragmented Powers](https://backoffice.biblio.ugent.be/download/01KF0A3AEWWSR8Q510X7XGJ8NF/01KF0ANCWJ667QMT3MKEH60CRB))

- **1211:** *Mausoleum of Imam al-Shāfiʿī*. A domed funerary and pilgrimage monument commissioned by al-Kamil over the jurist’s existing tomb, near the graves of al-Kamil’s mother and subsequently al-Kamil himself. Its fabric has undergone extensive later rebuilding and restoration—especially the dome—so the present monument is a layered manifestation rather than an untouched obra of 1211. ([MIT, “Transforming the Religious Landscape of Cairo”](https://www.mit.edu/course/4/4.615/OldFiles/www/handout08.htm); [Archnet bibliography and monument study](https://s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/media.archnet.org/system/publications/contents/2704/original/DPC0001.PDF?1384766939=))

- **1219:** *Al-Manṣūra*. Initially a fortified military camp founded by al-Kamil during the Fifth Crusade, it became a permanent city. It therefore resists classification as a single architectural work: it is simultaneously a strategic project, an urban foundation, and the beginning of a settlement subsequently made by many inhabitants. ([Laurence W. Marvin, *The Damietta Crusade, 1217–1221*](https://academic.oup.com/book/57913); [Encyclopaedia Italiana, “Al-Manṣūrah”](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/al-mansurah_%28Enciclopedia-Italiana%29/))

- **1225 (622 AH):** *al-Madrasa al-Kāmiliyya* (also *Dār al-Ḥadīth al-Kāmiliyya*). A college devoted particularly to the study of hadith and an early Cairene example of the two-iwan madrasa plan; only fragmentary remains survive, and reconstruction of its original arrangement depends substantially on archaeological and comparative work. ([Behrens-Abouseif, *Islamic Architecture in Cairo*](https://ghayb.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/ISLAMIC_ARCHITECTURE_IN_CAIRO.pdf); [Comité de conservation des monuments de l’art arabe](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ccmaa_1110-6824_1934_num_1927_35_12131))

### Diplomatic protocols

- **30 August 1221:** *Capitulation and truce of Damietta*. A jointly made diplomatic protocol ending the Fifth Crusade: it provided for the return of Damietta, an exchange of hostages, evacuation of the crusading army, and a truce. It survives as an event and reconstructed set of terms rather than as a securely preserved authorial text by al-Kamil. ([Marvin, “The Failed Campaign, Negotiations, and the End of the Crusade”](https://academic.oup.com/book/57913/chapter-abstract/474658520); [James M. Powell, *Anatomy of a Crusade, 1213–1221*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt3fhpgt))

- **18 February 1229:** *Treaty of Jaffa and Tall al-ʿAjūl*. A jointly negotiated protocol between al-Kamil and Frederick II that placed Jerusalem and a connecting corridor under Christian political control while reserving the Ḥaram al-Sharīf and specified religious rights to Muslims. No complete treaty text survives; its provisions must be reconstructed from mutually inconsistent Muslim and Christian narratives and letters, so the protocol is real but its exact wording and full scope are disputed. ([Cambridge University Press, “Too Big to be Owned”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/review-of-middle-east-studies/article/too-big-to-be-owned-reflections-on-jerusalem-in-islamic-history/4F1E56BF8F7081B74B274910DE983C1D/share/3537df2708390298bc7af0ed715a05f8afc63fd6c); [Frederick II’s contemporary report](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/source/fred2cdelets.asp))

### Monetary manifestations of sovereignty

- **1218–1238 (615–635 AH):** *Coinage in the name of al-Malik al-Kamil*. A corpus of hammered gold dinars, silver dirhams, and copper fulūs issued in multiple years, denominations, mints, and inscriptional varieties; these are administrative manifestations of his sovereignty, not objects personally designed or fabricated by him. Paul Balog’s standard corpus assigns separate catalogue numbers to the varieties, while later discoveries and corrections prevent the series from being treated as a permanently closed œuvre. ([Balog, *The Coinage of the Ayyūbids*](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Coinage_of_the_Ayy%C5%ABbids.html?id=XFRmAAAAMAAJ); [Nicol, “Paul Balog’s *The Coinage of the Ayyubids*: Additions and Corrections”](https://www.stevealbum.com/pdfs/academic/Nicol1986.pdf); [British Museum specimen, Balog no. 420](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/C_1853-0406-133))
