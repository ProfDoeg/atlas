# Afif al-Din Ali ibn Adlan al-Mawsili: Research Dossier

## Scope, method, and evidentiary limits

ʿAfīf al-Dīn ʿAlī ibn ʿAdlān survives in a thin but unusually varied record: medieval biographical notices, quotations attributed to him by near-contemporaries, poems preserved in later compilations, an extant cryptanalytic manual, manuscript catalogues, and modern studies of Arabic cryptology and grammar.

No continuous medieval biography, family archive, correspondence collection, financial record, or official appointment document has been identified. Consequently:

- **Documented fact** denotes information in Ibn ʿAdlān’s surviving work, an identifiable manuscript, or a near-contemporary report.
- **Reported fact** denotes information transmitted by medieval biographers or later bibliographers whose underlying source is not always recoverable.
- **Scholarly inference** denotes a modern reconstruction from textual or historical evidence.
- **Disputed attribution** identifies works or biographical details assigned to Ibn ʿAdlān by some scholars but rejected by others.
- **Unknown** means that the consulted record supplies no defensible answer.

Dates are given in both Hijri and Common Era form where possible. Medieval Islamic-to-Julian/Gregorian conversions are approximate because the historical calendar depended on lunar observation.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name:** أبو الحسن عفيف الدين علي بن عدلان بن حماد بن علي الربعي الموصلي  
**Transliteration:** Abū al-Ḥasan ʿAfīf al-Dīn ʿAlī ibn ʿAdlān ibn Ḥammād ibn ʿAlī al-Rabaʿī al-Mawṣilī.

A late chronicle prints the grandfather’s name as **Ḥamdān**, but the form **Ḥammād** is better represented in biographical and bibliographical notices. The discrepancy may be scribal or typographical.

**Common short forms:**

- Ibn ʿAdlān
- ʿAlī ibn ʿAdlān
- ʿAfīf al-Dīn ibn ʿAdlān
- Ibn Adlan, Ibn ʿAdlan, Ibn ʿAdlān
- Ali ben Adlan in some European-language literature

**Kunya:** Abū al-Ḥasan.

**Honorific:** ʿAfīf al-Dīn, “Chastity/Purity of the Faith.”

**Nisbas and occupational descriptions:**

- **al-Rabaʿī:** a genealogical or tribal affiliation to Rabīʿa. Medieval and modern summaries consequently describe him as Arab, but the precise lineage is not preserved.
- **al-Mawṣilī:** “of Mosul.”
- **al-Naḥwī:** grammarian.
- **al-Adīb:** man of letters.
- **al-Shāʿir:** poet.
- **al-Mutarjim:** in this context, an interpreter or solver of ciphered writing, not merely a translator between languages.
- Modern descriptions include **cryptanalyst**, **cryptologist**, and sometimes **linguist**.

**Birth:** 583 AH, conventionally 1187 CE, at Mosul. The hadith scholar al-Dimyāṭī reportedly qualified this as “583 or earlier,” so the year is not absolutely secure.

**Death:** Cairo or “Egypt,” 666 AH. Sources disagree between Friday, 9 Shawwāl and Friday after the afternoon prayer, 10 Shawwāl. The latter corresponds approximately to 29 June 1268 under a modern calculated conversion. Some catalogues give 1267 because 666 AH began in 1267.

**Occupations:** grammarian, teacher of Arabic and literature, poet, solver and composer of riddles, cryptanalyst, and sometime court-connected intellectual.

**Religious affiliation:** Muslim. A precise legal school is not securely stated in the consulted sources. His principal Baghdad teacher Abū al-Baqāʾ al-ʿUkbarī was a Ḥanbalī jurist and grammarian, but the pupil’s study with him does not prove Ibn ʿAdlān’s own madhhab.

## Family and Ancestry

The name preserves three ascending generations: his father **ʿAdlān**, grandfather **Ḥammād**, and great-grandfather **ʿAlī**. The nisba al-Rabaʿī has been understood as Arab tribal affiliation.

Nothing reliable was located concerning:

- his mother;
- his father’s or ancestors’ occupations;
- siblings;
- marriage;
- spouses or partners;
- children or later descendants;
- inherited wealth or family property.

The absence of this information is a limitation of the surviving biographical genre, not evidence that he never married or had children.

## Childhood and Early Life

Ibn ʿAdlān was born and spent at least his early youth in **Mosul**, then an important northern Mesopotamian center of Arabic letters, mathematics, administration, and court culture.

A modern Arabic discussion drawing on biographical literature says that he “reached the end of his boyhood” in Mosul and studied literature there with **Abū al-Ḥaram Makkī ibn Rayyān al-Māksīnī**, from whom he read the poetry of al-Mutanabbī. This detail is entangled with the disputed authorship of a commentary on al-Mutanabbī and should therefore be treated as **reported, not independently demonstrated**.

His surviving writings show a formation combining:

- Arabic grammar and syntax;
- literary prose;
- poetic meter and rhyme;
- lexical and orthographic analysis;
- riddles and verbal puzzles;
- quantitative observation of language;
- knowledge of contemporary cipher practices;
- practical experience in solving intercepted or deliberately challenging messages.

No source establishes his childhood household, childhood income, or the age at which he left Mosul.

## Education and Formation

### Mosul

**Reported teacher:** Abū al-Ḥaram Makkī ibn Rayyān al-Māksīnī, a grammarian and literary teacher. Some later scholarship assigns to Ibn ʿAdlān a formative study of al-Mutanabbī under him. Because the same evidence is used in a contested authorship argument, it cannot be accepted without qualification.

### Baghdad

Ibn ʿAdlān studied in Baghdad, where his best-attested teacher was:

- **Abū al-Baqāʾ ʿAbd Allāh al-ʿUkbarī** (d. 616 AH/1219), grammarian, Qurʾanic exegete, and Ḥanbalī jurist.

Biographical summaries additionally name:

- **Ibn al-Akhḍar**;
- **Ibn Mīnā**;
- unnamed or incompletely identified teachers.

He reportedly heard transmitted learning from these men, suggesting that his education was not confined to grammar. No surviving certificate of audition or complete teacher list has been located.

A controversial modern reconstruction also assigns him study in Egypt under **ʿAbd al-Munʿim ibn Ṣāliḥ al-Taymī al-Iskandarānī**. That claim largely comes from identifying Ibn ʿAdlān as the author of *al-Tibyān fī sharḥ al-dīwān*. Since that identification is disputed, the Egyptian teacher relationship is likewise **disputed**.

### Intellectual formation

The cryptanalytic manual reveals a practical, language-centered method rather than a purely mathematical theory. Ibn ʿAdlān expected a competent cryptanalyst to possess:

- intelligence and practiced judgment;
- mastery of the language being attacked;
- grammar and vocabulary;
- prosody and rhyme;
- knowledge of letter and word frequencies;
- familiarity with encipherment procedures;
- repeated practical experience.

His approach belonged to an Arabic cryptanalytic tradition extending back to al-Kindī. Modern comparison finds that some of Ibn ʿAdlān’s letter-frequency data agree exactly with data transmitted in al-Kindī’s treatise, although Ibn ʿAdlān does not explicitly credit al-Kindī.

## Chronological Life History

### 583 AH / approximately 1187: birth in Mosul

The conventional birth year is 583 AH. Al-Dimyāṭī reportedly wrote “583 or before,” leaving open a slightly earlier birth. Mosul is consistently given as the birthplace.

### Late twelfth or early thirteenth century: education in Mosul and Baghdad

He received his initial literary formation in Mosul and later studied grammar at Baghdad with Abū al-Baqāʾ al-ʿUkbarī. His Baghdad period must fall before al-ʿUkbarī’s death in 616 AH/1219.

Later accounts portray him as extraordinarily intelligent. Al-Ṣafadī’s *al-Wāfī bi-l-wafayāt* calls him one of “the most intelligent of the sons of Adam” and says that he was singularly accomplished in solving ciphered writings and riddles. This is a medieval evaluative report, not a measurable biographical fact.

### Early thirteenth century: Syrian circuit

Ibn ʿAdlān lived or worked in **Damascus** and appears to have moved through Ayyubid Syria. A disputed reconstruction of the author of *al-Tibyān* supplies a more elaborate itinerary—Mosul, Samarra, Baghdad, Kufa, Damascus, Aleppo, and finally Cairo—but only the broader Mosul–Baghdad–Damascus–Cairo trajectory is securely supported across independent notices.

### Work for al-Muʿaẓẓam ʿĪsā

In his cryptanalytic treatise, Ibn ʿAdlān says that the first significant cryptogram he solved was for **al-Malik al-Muʿaẓẓam ʿĪsā ibn al-ʿĀdil**, ruler of Damascus from 1218 to 1227.

This is the strongest evidence that Ibn ʿAdlān’s cryptanalysis was not merely recreational. The ruler’s administration gave him access to enciphered material and an institutional setting in which decipherment had political and potentially military value.

The text does not establish a formal bureaucratic title, salary, permanent appointment, or intelligence office. Descriptions of him as an official “court cryptanalyst” should therefore be treated as a reasonable modern shorthand, not a documented office.

### Work for al-Nāṣir Dāwūd

Ibn ʿAdlān states that he solved another cryptogram for al-Muʿaẓẓam’s son, **al-Malik al-Nāṣir Dāwūd**. The message had reportedly been intercepted “from some frontier regions.”

**Documented core:** Ibn ʿAdlān claimed to have solved an intercepted communication for al-Nāṣir.

**Unresolved questions:** the sender, recipient, frontier, date, military context, and practical consequences are not identified. No independent state-paper copy or chronicle of the particular interception has been located.

### 1229–1237: relationship with al-Ashraf Mūsā

Ibn ʿAdlān dedicated his surviving manual to **al-Malik al-Ashraf Muẓaffar al-Dīn Mūsā**, Ayyubid ruler of Damascus between 1229 and 1237. Its extended title is usually reconstructed as *al-Muʾallaf lil-Malik al-Ashraf fī ḥall al-tarājim*.

The dedication fixes composition or presentation most plausibly within al-Ashraf’s Damascene reign. Modern scholarship interprets the preface and references to prior court service as a bid for patronage or an appointment at al-Ashraf’s court. That is a **scholarly inference**; no appointment decree or evidence of payment survives.

### Literary and riddle-solving reputation

Medieval anecdotes portray Ibn ʿAdlān as a public solver of difficult verbal puzzles.

One story transmitted by al-Ṣafadī through **al-Ḥusayn ibn ʿAbd al-Salām** concerns two encoded verses sent by **Muḥammad ibn al-Jahm**. Ibn ʿAdlān reportedly solved them, with difficulty, in two hours. The repeated letters represented *ʿarabiyya* (grammar), *ʿarūḍ* (prosody), and *ʿibāra* (expression). The anecdote is evidence of his reputation and of puzzle culture, though the exact wording may have changed in transmission.

A second story, told in Ibn ʿAdlān’s voice, says that **Ismāʿīl al-Masmūl**, an associate or dependent of **Ṣalāḥ ibn Shaʿbān al-Irbilī**, presented him with enigmatic verses. Ibn ʿAdlān identified the object as a bird-catching net. The questioner initially reviled him and stopped only when Ibn ʿAdlān said he had already written down the answer. The reason for the hostility is unexplained. Modern interpretation suggests that solving riddles was competitive and reputational rather than a harmless pastime.

### Connection with Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn al-Irbilī and Ibn Khallikān

In *Wafayāt al-aʿyān*, Ibn Khallikān calls Ibn ʿAdlān **ṣāḥibunā**, “our companion/associate.” Ibn ʿAdlān told him that a letter written by the Damascene poet **Sharaf al-Dīn Ibn ʿUnayn** to Egypt had passed through Ibn ʿAdlān’s hands and included a recommendation concerning Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn al-Irbilī.

This is direct evidence that Ibn ʿAdlān participated in the circulation of elite literary correspondence between Damascus and Egypt. It does not prove that he held a formal postal or chancery office.

### Cairo: teaching and court-related movement

Ibn ʿAdlān eventually settled in Cairo and taught at **Jāmiʿ al-Ṣāliḥ**, described in modern scholarship as the mosque of al-Ṣāliḥ outside Cairo. The precise identification and duration of the post require caution, but medieval notices agree that he “sat at the head” or taught there.

His poetry records the physical burden of repeated ascents to the **Citadel of the Mountain** in Cairo. He complains of riding there every morning through dust, comparing himself to a projectile aimed at the fortress and punning that it was literally a “citadel” grinding down his sandals and body.

This poem establishes repeated travel to the Citadel but does not reveal the purpose. Possible explanations include teaching, petitioning, seeking patronage, administrative or cryptanalytic work, or attendance on officials. Claims that it proves a specific office are unsupported.

### Teaching and students

Reported students or transmitters who heard from him include:

- **Ibn al-Ẓāhirī**;
- **al-Abywardī**;
- **Sharaf al-Dīn al-Dimyāṭī**.

Al-Dimyāṭī preserved a poem in which Ibn ʿAdlān remembered Baghdad—*Dār al-Salām*—and its nights of pleasure. This is useful autobiographical evidence for residence in Baghdad and for a social as well as scholastic life there.

### Final years

He remained in Cairo as a teacher, grammarian, poet, and recognized authority on riddles and decipherment. No late-life dismissal, imprisonment, exile, major illness, or withdrawal from public activity is documented.

### 666 AH / 1268: death

Ibn Taghrībirdī’s chronicle reports that Ibn ʿAdlān died in Egypt on **Friday, 9 Shawwāl 666 AH**. A report attributed to his student al-Dimyāṭī gives **Friday after the afternoon prayer, 10 Shawwāl 666 AH**.

Calculated calendars place 10 Shawwāl near **29 June 1268**, and that calculated day is Friday; 9 Shawwāl falls on Thursday in the same modern calculation. This supports, but does not conclusively prove, al-Dimyāṭī’s 10 Shawwāl reading because medieval lunar observation could differ by a day.

His burial place and cause of death are unknown.

## Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Ayyubid courts

He had documented working or dedicatory relationships with al-Muʿaẓẓam ʿĪsā, al-Nāṣir Dāwūd, and al-Ashraf Mūsā. His court-connected activity combined linguistic virtuosity, cipher-solving, and literary patronage.

No evidence establishes:

- formal rank;
- wages;
- land grants or iqṭāʿ;
- tax privileges;
- state property;
- commercial investments;
- an inherited or accumulated fortune.

### Jāmiʿ al-Ṣāliḥ in Cairo

Medieval notices report that he taught or presided there. His subject was Arabic grammar and literature. The date of appointment and institutional status are unknown.

### Cryptanalytic technology

Ibn ʿAdlān did not invent cryptography or frequency analysis. His importance lies in systematizing and extending Arabic cryptanalytic practice.

His surviving manual contains a preface, twenty rules grouped thematically, and a concluding worked example. Its techniques include:

- counting distinct cipher symbols;
- ranking letters by frequency;
- classifying the twenty-eight Arabic letters into seven abundant, eleven common/intermediate, and ten scarce letters;
- analyzing frequent digrams, trigrams, and four-letter combinations;
- identifying likely two- and three-letter words;
- exploiting stock openings and closings;
- recognizing the definite article *al-*;
- testing which letters may occur beside one another;
- using repeated letters and positional constraints;
- identifying a cipher symbol for spaces;
- attacking texts with omitted spaces, called *al-mudmaj*;
- handling systems that use multiple possible symbols for spacing;
- using poetic meter and rhyme to solve verse;
- revising hypotheses when the emerging plaintext ceases to make sense.

His best-known statistical judgment is that frequency analysis normally requires a ciphertext of approximately **ninety letters**, about three rotations through the twenty-eight-letter Arabic alphabet, while allowing that shorter texts can sometimes be solved. This is an early explicit discussion of **minimum sample size** in statistical cryptanalysis.

The concluding demonstration attacks a cipher in which bird names substitute for letters. He counts nineteen distinct signs, determines their frequencies, tests the suspected separator, reconstructs common combinations and words, abandons false starts, and gradually obtains a coherent plaintext.

### Adaptation to a cryptographic “arms race”

Ibn ʿAdlān says that encipherers claimed that ciphers written without word spaces could not be broken. His rules directly answer that technique. His work therefore documents interaction between cipher-makers and cipher-breakers: defensive innovations produced new analytic methods.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Political context

His adult career unfolded during the division and recombination of Ayyubid territories, the Fifth and Sixth Crusades, Mongol expansion, the end of Ayyubid rule in Egypt, and the rise of the Mamluk sultanate.

He served rulers or solved communications for their courts, but the record does not reveal:

- membership in a political faction;
- participation in military operations;
- his views on Crusaders, Mongols, Ayyubid succession, or Mamluk government;
- whether deciphered messages led to arrests, warfare, or diplomatic decisions.

The intercepted frontier communication mentioned in his own treatise indicates at least one connection to state security.

### Intellectual profile

Ibn ʿAdlān treated decipherment as applied philology. Grammar, morphology, vocabulary, prosody, formulaic writing, and statistics formed one method. His work shows no modern separation between humanistic and quantitative disciplines.

Unlike more encyclopedic Arabic cryptological works, the surviving manual concentrates on **cryptanalysis**, not on an exhaustive taxonomy of cipher construction. It has been described as a practitioner’s handbook.

### Religion and philosophy

His manual uses conventional Islamic invocations and courtly dedication. No theological, philosophical, sectarian, or mystical treatise securely attributed to him was located.

A modern biographical reconstruction says that he inclined toward asceticism and copied sayings of spiritual masters for himself. Because this detail enters through the disputed *al-Tibyān* authorship reconstruction, it should be labeled **reported and uncertain**.

No accusation of heresy, unbelief, sectarian deviation, or religious prosecution concerning Ibn ʿAdlān was found.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, or Financial Questions

### Dedicated adversarial search result

Searches were conducted in English and Arabic combining his name with terms corresponding to controversy, scandal, allegation, accusation, fraud, conspiracy, criticism, lawsuit, forgery, and corruption, including searches weighted toward material published during the most recent two years.

**Finding:** no historical evidence was located that Ibn ʿAdlān was accused, investigated, indicted, tried, convicted, imprisoned, exiled, or implicated in fraud, corruption, homicide, sexual misconduct, financial crime, or religious prosecution.

That negative finding should not be confused with proof of an unblemished private life. The surviving documentation is too sparse to support such a conclusion.

### State-intelligence implications

He admittedly deciphered an intercepted frontier message for a ruler. This is documented professional activity involving intelligence material, not evidence of a crime. Neither the identities of the correspondents nor the consequences of the interception are known.

### Boasting and self-presentation

Ibn ʿAdlān says that cipher-makers claimed their systems could defy analysis and that he had broken several of them. His manual presents prior successes to an intended patron.

**Evidentiary assessment:** the claim is partly self-authored professional promotion. Its plausibility is strengthened by the manual’s sophisticated worked example and by his independent medieval reputation, but the individual cryptograms are not externally documented.

### Uncredited use of al-Kindī’s data

Modern scholars observe that Ibn ʿAdlān’s letter-frequency figures coincide with those in the earlier treatise of al-Kindī, whom he does not name.

**Status:** documented textual similarity; the mechanism is unresolved. It may represent direct borrowing, use of a shared source, or inherited professional knowledge. Calling it “plagiarism” would impose a modern authorship norm without evidence about medieval expectations or his immediate source.

### The *al-Tibyān* authorship controversy

A large commentary on al-Mutanabbī’s poetry, *al-Tibyān fī sharḥ al-dīwān*, circulated in manuscripts and many printings under the name of Abū al-Baqāʾ al-ʿUkbarī.

In the twentieth century, Muṣṭafā Jawād argued that it was actually by Ibn ʿAdlān. Among his evidence was a passage in which the commentator discusses a reading attributed to “Abū al-Ḥasan ʿAfīf al-Dīn ʿAlī ibn ʿAdlān.” Jawād also used apparent biographical clues about teachers and travels.

Later researcher ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn Ibrāhīm al-Hulayl rejected both al-ʿUkbarī and Ibn ʿAdlān as authors and argued for **Abū ʿAlī al-Ḥasan ibn ʿAlī ibn Aḥmad al-Saʿdī/al-Khazrajī**, or a similarly identified grammarian.

Arguments against Ibn ʿAdlān’s authorship include:

- the commentator mentions Ibn ʿAdlān in the third person;
- no clear authorial colophon names Ibn ʿAdlān;
- the works mentioned in the commentary do not match secure medieval lists of his writings;
- the commentator’s grammatical affiliations and teacher references allegedly fit another scholar better;
- biographies of Ibn ʿAdlān do not credit him with a monumental commentary on al-Mutanabbī.

**Current dossier classification:** **disputed attribution, with substantial arguments against Ibn ʿAdlān’s authorship**. Details derived only from identifying him as the commentator—expanded travel itinerary, Egyptian teachers, asceticism, and certain readings—must remain provisional.

### Title instability

His riddle book appears as both *ʿUqlat al-mujtāz fī ḥall al-alghāz* and *ʿUqlat al-majāz fī ḥall al-alghāz* in catalogues and secondary notices. This may reflect manuscript variation, misreading, or bibliographical corruption. The original wording has not been conclusively established here.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### “Founder of cryptanalysis”

**Story:** popular summaries sometimes imply that Ibn ʿAdlān invented frequency analysis or founded cryptanalysis.

**Evidence against:** al-Kindī’s ninth-century *Risāla fī istikhrāj al-muʿammā* predates him and contains a systematic account of frequency analysis; Arabic reports attribute an even earlier, now-lost cryptographic work to al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad.

**Historical significance:** the simplified claim makes Ibn ʿAdlān easier to present as an isolated inventor but obscures the cumulative Arabic tradition.

**Assessment:** myth or exaggeration. Ibn ʿAdlān was an important innovator and practitioner, not the first cryptanalyst.

### “Inventor of the ninety-letter rule”

**Story:** Ibn ʿAdlān discovered a universal minimum of exactly ninety characters.

**Evidence:** he gave approximately ninety letters as a practical rule of thumb and expressly admitted that shorter texts might sometimes be solved.

**Assessment:** the attribution is broadly correct if stated as an early heuristic about sample size, not a mathematically universal threshold.

### “Court spy” or formal intelligence chief

**Story:** his work for rulers and intercepted messages is sometimes expanded into a formal career as a spy or intelligence official.

**Evidence:** his own text proves cryptanalytic service and exposure to intercepted correspondence. No title, office, espionage mission, salary, or command responsibility is documented.

**Assessment:** unverified extrapolation.

### The angry riddle challengers

**Story:** questioners responded to his correct solutions with anger or abuse.

**Appearance:** medieval anecdotes preserved by al-Ṣafadī.

**Evidence:** literary-biographical transmission rather than an independently corroborated event record.

**Reputational effect:** the stories construct Ibn ʿAdlān as an almost unbeatable solver whose skill humiliated competitors. They also preserve the social stakes of riddling in elite literary culture.

**Assessment:** reported anecdote with possible stylization.

### “Most intelligent of mankind”

**Story:** medieval biographers describe him as one of the cleverest “sons of Adam.”

**Evidence:** conventional biographical praise supported by a genuine technical reputation.

**Assessment:** hagiographic or encomiastic characterization, not a factual ranking.

### The forgotten universal scholar

Modern crypto-history occasionally presents Ibn ʿAdlān as a “forgotten genius” whose discoveries were independently reinvented in Europe. His treatment of no-space ciphers and sample length does precede comparable surviving European discussions, but sweeping claims of direct anticipation require care because the Arabic and Latin traditions had different genres, transmission routes, and surviving corpora.

## Works by Ibn ʿAdlān

### 1. *Al-Muʾallaf lil-Malik al-Ashraf fī ḥall al-tarājim*

**Arabic:** المؤلف للملك الأشرف في حل التراجم  
**Alternative titles:** *Fī ḥall al-mutarjam*; *Risāla fī ḥall al-tarājim*; *Ḥall al-tarjumān* in some notices.  
**English titles:** *The Composition for al-Malik al-Ashraf on the Solution of Ciphers*; *A Manual for King al-Ashraf*; *On Cryptanalysis*.  
**Date:** probably 1229–1237.  
**Status:** genuine and extant.

A copy is preserved in Istanbul’s Süleymaniye Library, manuscript no. **5359**, in a codex containing important Arabic cryptological texts. The work was edited in Arabic by Muḥammad Mrayātī, Yaḥyā Mīr ʿAlam, and Ḥassān al-Ṭayyān and later published in English translation as volume 2 of the *Series on Arabic Origins of Cryptology*.

### 2. *Al-Muʿlam*

**Arabic reconstruction:** المعلم  
**English:** approximately *The Instructive/Told Book*.  
**Status:** genuine reference in Ibn ʿAdlān’s surviving manual, but lost.

He refers readers to it for fuller algorithms or procedures. No securely identified manuscript was located.

### 3. *Al-Intikhāb li-kashf al-abyāt al-mushkilāt al-iʿrāb*

**Arabic:** الانتخاب لكشف الأبيات المشكلة الإعراب  
**Subject:** grammatical analysis of syntactically difficult poetic verses.  
**Status:** genuine or strongly attributed; extant.

A manuscript copied in 720 AH is reported in the Egyptian National Library as a reproduction from Cambridge, formerly cited under shelf notation 996/81. A modern edition was prepared by **Ḥātim Ṣāliḥ al-Ḍāmin**, published by Muʾassasat al-Risāla in Beirut; a second edition appeared in 1405 AH/1985.

The text selects difficult verses and explains possible syntactic analyses, often weighing rival readings before stating the author’s preference.

### 4. *ʿUqlat al-mujtāz fī ḥall al-alghāz*

**Arabic variants:** عقلة المجتاز في حل الألغاز; عقلة المجاز في حل الألغاز  
**English:** approximately *The Traveler’s Restraint/Resource for Solving Riddles*.  
**Status:** attributed in medieval or later bibliographies; present survival uncertain.

The unstable title may reflect copying errors. No modern critical edition was identified.

### 5. Poetry

No independent collected *Dīwān* has been identified. Poems and isolated verses survive in:

- al-Dimyāṭī’s transmission;
- *al-Wāfī bi-l-wafayāt*;
- *Fawāt al-wafayāt*;
- *Masālik al-abṣār*;
- *ʿUqūd al-jumān*;
- *ʿUyūn al-tawārīkh*;
- later historical and literary anthologies.

Preserved subjects include:

- nostalgia for Baghdad and youthful pleasure;
- poverty and frustrated ambition;
- courtly and social complaint;
- the exhausting daily ascent to Cairo’s Citadel;
- riddles;
- literary and grammatical observations.

### 6. *Al-Tibyān fī sharḥ dīwān al-Mutanabbī*

**Arabic:** التبيان في شرح ديوان المتنبي  
**Status:** disputed attribution; not to be included among secure works.

The commentary was printed repeatedly under al-ʿUkbarī’s name in Calcutta, Būlāq, Cairo, and Delhi. Muṣṭafā Jawād reassigned it to Ibn ʿAdlān, but later study argued that it belonged to neither scholar. The Paris manuscript cited in the debate is Bibliothèque nationale manuscript 3105, approximately 480 folios.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Medieval reputation

Medieval biographers remembered Ibn ʿAdlān principally as:

- an outstanding grammarian and man of letters;
- a clever poet;
- an exceptional solver of riddles;
- a master of *ḥall al-tarājim*, deciphering encoded messages.

His technical work influenced the later Arabic cryptological tradition. The fourteenth-century Mosuli polymath **Tāj al-Dīn Ibn al-Durayhim** drew upon Ibn ʿAdlān’s manual, transmitting and extending methods within Mamluk scholarly and administrative culture.

### Manuscript survival and obscurity

His cryptanalytic reputation remained largely confined to manuscript and biographical traditions. Modern histories of cryptography long privileged Greek and European developments. The recovery and publication of Arabic cryptological manuscripts in the twentieth century restored al-Kindī, Ibn ʿAdlān, Ibn Dunaynīr, and Ibn al-Durayhim to the technical history of the discipline.

### Modern significance

Ibn ʿAdlān is now cited especially for:

- treating cryptanalysis as a teachable algorithm;
- attacking monoalphabetic substitutions without spaces;
- combining letter, digram, trigram, and word-pattern analysis;
- identifying approximate sample-size requirements;
- integrating grammar, prosody, and quantitative analysis;
- supplying a worked example that exposes false starts and iterative correction.

James L. Massey described the worked decipherment as conveying the authentic experience of a skilled cryptanalyst. His ninety-letter heuristic has also attracted historians of statistics because it recognizes that frequency distributions become unreliable in small samples.

### Limits of influence claims

No evidence establishes direct transmission from Ibn ʿAdlān to Renaissance European cryptographers. Similarities between later European methods and Arabic precedents establish chronological priority, not necessarily a demonstrable transmission chain.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are listed. Mere contemporaneity, residence in the same region, or participation in a broad intellectual tradition has not been counted.

### Abu Yusuf Yaʿqub ibn Ishaq al-Kindi

**Nature:** earlier intellectual predecessor in Arabic cryptanalysis.  
**Date/place:** al-Kindī worked in ninth-century Baghdad; Ibn ʿAdlān studied in Baghdad and wrote in the early thirteenth century.  
**Evidence:** modern textual comparison finds that Ibn ʿAdlān’s letter-frequency data coincide with al-Kindī’s, while Ibn ʿAdlān does not name him.  
**Label:** **documented textual relationship; exact transmission route unresolved**.

This is not a personal connection: the men lived approximately four centuries apart.

### Al-Khalil ibn Ahmad al-Farahidi

**Nature:** remote predecessor in the Arabic study of cryptographic writing and linguistic pattern.  
**Evidence:** later Arabic tradition credits al-Khalīl with a lost work on cryptographic messages. No source consulted says that Ibn ʿAdlān read or explicitly cited it.  
**Label:** **reported intellectual background, not a documented direct influence**.

### Al-Malik al-Kamil

**Nature:** possible shared Ayyubid court environment, but no direct personal relationship established.  
**Evidence:** Ibn ʿAdlān worked for al-Kāmil’s brother al-Muʿaẓẓam and nephew al-Nāṣir and dedicated a book to another brother, al-Ashraf. A letter involving Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn al-Irbilī also passed through Ibn ʿAdlān’s hands; Ṣalāḥ served al-Kāmil. None of this by itself proves a meeting or patronage relationship between Ibn ʿAdlān and al-Kāmil.  
**Label:** **unresolved; no qualifying documented Atlas connection**.

### Taj al-Din Ali ibn Muhammad Ibn al-Durayhim al-Mawsili

**Nature:** later Mosuli cryptologist who used and extended Ibn ʿAdlān’s work.  
**Date/place:** Ibn al-Durayhim was born in Mosul in 712 AH/1312, forty-four years after Ibn ʿAdlān’s death, and later worked between Syria and Egypt.  
**Evidence:** modern manuscript study identifies reliance on Ibn ʿAdlān’s manual and treats the two as links in an Ayyubid–Mamluk cryptological tradition.  
**Label:** **documented posthumous intellectual influence**.

### Ahmad al-Qalqashandi

**Nature:** later Mamluk chancery encyclopedist in the same transmission history.  
**Evidence:** al-Qalqashandī preserves cryptological material transmitted through Ibn al-Durayhim, including discussion of prose and poetic ciphers. The consulted evidence does not establish that he directly read or named Ibn ʿAdlān.  
**Label:** **indirect intellectual transmission; direct connection unproved**.
