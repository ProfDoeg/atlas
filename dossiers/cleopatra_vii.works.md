## Works by Cleopatra VII Thea Philopator

### Royal protocols and documentary texts

- **50 BC:** *Royal decree concerning grain purchases* (Select Papyri II.209). This administrative protocol, issued jointly with Ptolemy XIII, regulated compulsory grain purchasing during famine; it is a royal act composed and transmitted through the chancery, not necessarily Cleopatra’s personal wording or handwriting. ([Attalus, after Hunt and Edgar](https://www.attalus.org/docs/select2/p209.html))

- **Possibly 50–47 BC:** *Amnesty decree concerning fugitives* (P.Kent G 60291). Fragmentary royal ordinances survive on papyrus; palaeography permits attribution to the joint reign of Cleopatra and Ptolemy XIII, but neither the date nor Cleopatra’s authorship is certain. ([University of Kent Digital Papyri](https://blogs.kent.ac.uk/digital-papyri/g-60291-recto1_li/))

- **After 44 BC:** *Renewal of asylum for a Jewish proseuchē* (OGIS 129; TM 6400). An inscription records that “the queen and king”—probably Cleopatra VII and Ptolemy XV Caesarion—ordered renewal of an earlier grant; the identification is probable rather than explicit, and the text is a sovereign protocol rather than a personally composed work. ([Attalus, OGIS 129](https://www.attalus.org/docs/ogis/s129.html))

- **23 February 33 BC:** *Royal ordinance granting fiscal privileges to Publius Canidius* (P.Bingen 45; Berlin P. 25239; TM 78025). This chancery protocol exempts Canidius from specified taxes and requisitions; its closing authorization, *ginesthōi* (“let it be done”), has been identified as Cleopatra’s only surviving autograph, but that identification remains disputed and may instead represent a senior official’s subscription. ([Berlin Papyrus Database](https://berlpap.smb.museum/record/?Alle=25239+&lang=en&result=0); [Papyrus Stories, with scholarly bibliography](https://papyrus-stories.com/2020/07/20/on-a-document-signed-by-cleopatra/))

- **51–30 BC:** *Royal decrees, correspondence, and subscriptions now lost*. Cleopatra’s government necessarily produced many such documents, probably by dictation and scribal drafting, but only scattered inscriptions and papyri survive; this is therefore a lost administrative body of work rather than a reconstructable list of individually authored texts. ([Peter van Minnen, “Cleopatra VII in Teos?”](https://teos.ankara.edu.tr/wp-content/uploads/sites/295/2025/05/Cleopatra_VII_in_Teos_Chiron_41_2011_41.pdf))

### Technical protocols attributed to her

- **No secure date:** *Kosmētikon*. Later medical tradition assigns Cleopatra a protocol-like manual on bodily care, pharmacology, hair treatments, and related subjects, surviving only through fragments quoted by later authors. Modern analysis indicates that at least the transmitted material belongs to a physician named Cleopatra who wrote after AD 64, not securely to Cleopatra VII; it therefore resists inclusion as her work and is retained here only as a disputed ancient attribution. ([Rebecca Flemming, “Women, Writing and Medicine in the Classical World”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/F79B07D54BE6BF0820C703248BE10C3B/S0009838807000225a.pdf/women-writing-and-medicine-in-the-classical-world.pdf); [Véronique Boudon-Millot, dossier and edition project](https://orbi.uliege.be/handle/2268/78022))

- **No secure date:** *On Weights and Measures*. A metrological text or section circulated under the name Cleopatra and may have formed part of the *Kosmētikon* rather than an independent work. Its attribution to Cleopatra VII is disputed for the same chronological and prosopographical reasons, so neither its independence nor her authorship should be treated as established. ([Rebecca Flemming](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/F79B07D54BE6BF0820C703248BE10C3B/S0009838807000225a.pdf/women-writing-and-medicine-in-the-classical-world.pdf); [National Endowment for the Humanities interview with Duane W. Roller](https://www.neh.gov/humanities/2010/julyaugust/iq/impertinent-questions-duane-w-roller))

### Monumental projects

- **51–30 BC:** *Temple of Hathor at Dendera: continuation and exterior program*. Cleopatra continued a temple begun under Ptolemy XII and, with Caesarion, appears in the monumental relief and inscription program on its rear exterior wall. This is collaborative royal patronage and image-making, not a building designed or physically made by Cleopatra alone. ([Oxford chronology of Egyptian temples](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3Ad6bee2aa-49a5-42db-9617-394ea1f73cf5/files/m3b458b3100d086c15cb5601b368c3678); [University of Chicago, *Cleopatra’s Egypt*](https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/saoc40.pdf))

- **51–30 BC:** *Bark chapel of the Temple of Geb at Coptos*. The architectural chronology assigns this chapel to Cleopatra’s reign; as a royal commission it is an obra of patronage, while the individual architects, sculptors, and scribes remain unnamed. ([Oxford chronology of Egyptian temples](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3Ad6bee2aa-49a5-42db-9617-394ea1f73cf5/files/m3b458b3100d086c15cb5601b368c3678))

- **51–30 BC:** *Chapel of Cleopatra and Caesarion at Coptos*. A jointly dynastic cult building associated with mother and son; its conventional modern designation describes its patrons rather than preserving a securely attested ancient title. ([Oxford chronology of Egyptian temples](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3Ad6bee2aa-49a5-42db-9617-394ea1f73cf5/files/m3b458b3100d086c15cb5601b368c3678))

- **After 47–before 30 BC:** *Birth house of Caesarion at Armant (Hermonthis)*. Cleopatra commissioned this mammisi beside the temple of Montu to monumentalize Caesarion’s divine birth and legitimacy; it was demolished in 1861–63 and is known from surviving blocks, descriptions, plans, drawings, and photographs. ([“Cleopatra’s Temple at Armant,” *Nature*](https://www.nature.com/articles/136798a0); [UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology, “Birth House”](https://escholarship.org/content/qt8xj4k0ww/qt8xj4k0ww.pdf))

- **Before 30 BC:** *Caesareum of Alexandria*. Cleopatra initiated or substantially developed this harbor-facing sanctuary, conventionally understood as honoring Julius Caesar and perhaps subsequently Mark Antony; earlier groundwork complicates the claim that she created it wholly anew, and Augustus completed and rededicated the unfinished project after her death. ([Centre d’Études Alexandrines](https://www.cealex.org/research/excavations-in-the-process-of-being-published/caesareum/))

- **Before 30 BC:** *Altar for the cult of Mark Antony in the Caesareum*. This cult installation is specifically attributed to Cleopatra, but survives archaeologically only through the difficult reconstruction of the larger sanctuary. ([Centre d’Études Alexandrines](https://www.cealex.org/research/excavations-in-the-process-of-being-published/caesareum/))

### Coinage and reproducible royal images

- **51–30 BC:** *Royal coinage of Cleopatra VII*. This is a distributed body of monetary objects—silver and bronze issues from Egypt and other eastern Mediterranean mints, including sole and joint issues, portrait and inherited dynastic types—not one authored “text.” Mint officials and die engravers physically made the coins, while Cleopatra supplied the issuing authority and a changing program of names, titles, denominations, and royal images; individual attributions and mint assignments remain contested. ([Catharine C. Lorber, *Coins of the Ptolemaic Empire, Part II: Ptolemy V through Cleopatra VII*](https://www.brepols.net/products/978-0-89722-402-4); [Roman Provincial Coinage Online, Cleopatra VII](https://rpc.ashmus.ox.ac.uk/search/browse?q=Cleopatra+VII))

- **From approximately 47/46 BC:** *Alexandrian portrait coin type of Cleopatra VII*. A reproducible numismatic manifestation presents the living queen in a diadem and became a durable instrument of royal self-presentation; variation among dies and regions means it is a type-family rather than a single designed object. ([Adriana Rose Dunn, *Numismatic Portraits of Cleopatra VII*](https://digitalcommons.memphis.edu/etd/2335/))

- **Approximately 38–32 BC:** *Joint coinages of Mark Antony and Cleopatra VII*. These issues combine the rulers’ portraits, names, or titles and operate as political manifestos in metal, asserting alliance and reciprocal sovereignty. Responsibility is inherently joint, and the precise mint and date of some series remain disputed. ([Art Institute of Chicago, catalogue no. 22](https://publications.artic.edu/roman/api/epub/480/510/print_view); [Roman Provincial Coinage I.4096](https://rpc.ashmus.ox.ac.uk/coins/1/4096))

### Dynastic spectacle

- **34 BC:** *The Donations of Alexandria*. This staged ceremony distributed kingdoms and royal titles among Cleopatra, Caesarion, and her children with Antony; known through hostile or retrospective literary accounts, it is best understood as a political Gesamtkunstwerk joining costume, bodies, throne imagery, proclamation, and territorial project rather than as a surviving object or fixed text. Its details—and even the formal character of the “donations”—remain contested. ([Duane W. Roller, *Cleopatra: A Biography*, publisher’s description and source basis](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/cleopatra-9780199829965); [Cassius Dio, *Roman History* 49.40–41](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cassius_Dio/49%2A.html))
