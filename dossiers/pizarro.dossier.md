# Francisco Pizarro González: Research Dossier

## Scope and Identification Note

This dossier concerns the Spanish conquistador generally known as **Francisco Pizarro**, not another person of the same name. “Francisco Pizarro González” is a modern expanded form derived from his father’s surname, Pizarro, and his mother’s, González. Sixteenth-century documents generally call him **Francisco Pizarro**, later **Governor Francisco Pizarro**, or **the Marquis**.

Dates and numerical claims differ among primary and secondary sources. Such disagreements are identified below rather than silently harmonized.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Usual name:** Francisco Pizarro.
- **Expanded name forms:** Francisco Pizarro González; Francisco Pizarro y González.
- **Documentary form:** Francisco Pizarro.
- **Titles and offices:**
  - Captain.
  - Governor and Captain-General of New Castile.
  - Adelantado.
  - Alguacil mayor.
  - Knight of the Order of Santiago.
  - Marquis, commonly styled **Marqués Gobernador** or “Marquis of the Conquest.” The Crown granted him a marquisate without a securely established territorial designation; “Marquis of the Conquest” became customary rather than being the original formal title.
- **Reported Indigenous appellations:** later accounts give forms such as **Apu**—a Quechua title for a lord or authority—and **Machu Capitán**, “Old Captain.” Their precise contemporary range and frequency are not securely documented.
- **Birth:** in Trujillo, Extremadura, Crown of Castile, probably during the 1470s.
- **Competing birth years:** approximately **1471, 1475, or 1478**. No contemporary baptismal certificate has been found. Archival authorities commonly use “ca. 1478”; major twentieth-century biographers have preferred 1475 or a broader 1470s range.
- **Alleged exact date:** 16 March 1478 appears frequently in popular material but is not supported by a known contemporary birth record.
- **Death:** **26 June 1541**, Lima, Government of New Castile.
- **Cause of death:** assassination by an armed group associated with the followers of the deceased Diego de Almagro.
- **Religion:** Roman Catholic by documented public profession and institutional activity.
- **Literacy:** probably unable to write sustained prose and dependent on secretaries and notaries. He used a rubric or signature formula. Calling him simply “illiterate” is broadly defensible but can obscure his ability to participate in documentary government through dictation, oral instruction, scribal mediation, and recognition of legal instruments.
- **Principal occupations:** soldier, colonial settler, expedition organizer, conquistador, encomendero, governor, town founder, allocator of offices, lands, Indigenous tribute, and labor.
- **Principal residences:** Trujillo; Hispaniola; Urabá and Darién; Panama; San Miguel de Piura; Jauja; Cusco intermittently; Lima.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Father — documented identification**

Pizarro’s father was **Gonzalo Pizarro Rodríguez de Aguilar**, known in later genealogical writing as **Gonzalo Pizarro el Largo** or **el Romano**. He was a Trujillo hidalgo and military man associated with campaigns in Italy. He had children by several women.

**Mother — documented identification, uncertain social description**

His mother was **Francisca González Mateos**. Older biographies often describe her as a poor servant or attendant connected with the convent of San Francisco el Real near Trujillo’s Puerta de Coria. Later genealogical reconstructions describe her more specifically as a woman attached to the household of Beatriz Pizarro. Her exact status cannot be recovered with confidence.

Francisco was born outside marriage. The claim that his father never acknowledged him is repeated in biographies, but the family’s later integration and his association with paternal relatives make the practical history more complicated than a complete absence of recognition.

### Siblings and Half-Siblings

The extensive Pizarro kin group included:

- **Hernando Pizarro**, the father’s legitimate son and Francisco’s paternal half-brother. He became Francisco’s principal representative at court, military subordinate, political defender, and later husband of Francisco’s daughter Francisca.
- **Gonzalo Pizarro**, paternal half-brother; conquistador and later leader of the rebellion against the New Laws. He survived Francisco but was executed in 1548.
- **Juan Pizarro**, paternal half-brother; killed during the siege of Cusco in 1536.
- **Francisco Martín de Alcántara**, maternal half-brother and close associate; killed beside Francisco in 1541.
- Numerous additional paternal and maternal relations appear in genealogies and testamentary dispositions. Exact sibling lists differ because Gonzalo Pizarro Rodríguez de Aguilar had several extramarital relationships.

### Partners and Children

Pizarro never contracted a securely documented canonical marriage.

#### Quispe Sisa / Inés Huaylas Yupanqui

**Documented relationship:** Pizarro formed a sexual and domestic union with **Quispe Sisa**, baptized **Inés Huaylas Yupanqui**, a daughter of Huayna Cápac and the Andean noblewoman Contarhuacho. She was therefore a member of the Inca royal kin network and a half-sister of Atahualpa.

Their children were:

- **Francisca Pizarro Yupanqui**, born in 1534, later legitimized by royal provision. She was sent to Spain and in 1552 married her uncle Hernando Pizarro. After Hernando’s death she married Pedro Arias Portocarrero. She died in 1598. Her descent and estate became a durable Pizarro-Inca lineage.
- **Gonzalo Pizarro Yupanqui**, also legitimized in 1537; he died young, generally reported around age fourteen.

Inés later married Francisco de Ampuero. The transition from Pizarro’s household to that marriage is documented, but sources do not permit a modern consensual-romantic reconstruction of her relationship with Pizarro. It occurred within conquest, captivity, unequal colonial power, and dynastic politics.

#### Cuxirimay Ocllo / Angelina Yupanqui

Pizarro later had a relationship with **Cuxirimay Ocllo**, baptized **Angelina Yupanqui**, an Inca noblewoman commonly described as a sister or close relative of Atahualpa and as having been attached to his household.

Children attributed to this union include:

- **Francisco Pizarro Yupanqui**, who died young after being taken to Spain.
- **Juan Pizarro Yupanqui**, whose place in older child lists is inconsistent but who is supported by later genealogical research summarized in publications on Pizarro’s mestizo descendants.

Some older references list only four children and omit Juan or substitute an unidentified child. The safest conclusion is that the record of Pizarro’s children was incompletely preserved and has been revised through later archival discoveries.

### Dynastic Strategy

Pizarro’s unions with Inca royal women and his provisions for their children created a kinship structure joining the Pizarro family to the descendants of Huayna Cápac. Whether Pizarro consciously intended a generalized ideological “union of the races” is **an interpretive theory**, not a statement documented in his own surviving words. The concrete dynastic and property consequences, however, are documented.

## Childhood and Early Life

No continuous contemporary account of Pizarro’s childhood survives.

### Documented or strongly supported

- He was born in Trujillo.
- He was illegitimate.
- His formal education was slight or nonexistent.
- He entered the military-colonial world as a relatively obscure adult and acquired practical experience through campaigning, settlement, command, negotiation, and patronage.

### Reported traditions

- He grew up in poverty.
- He tended pigs.
- He was abandoned at a church door.
- He was sent to live with rural relatives.
- A herd of pigs under his care was lost, prompting him to flee.

These details are repeated in later biographies but lack contemporary childhood documentation. The “swineherd” tradition became important because admirers used it as a rise-from-nothing narrative, while hostile writers used it to represent him as ignorant and socially coarse.

### Claimed European military service

Some biographies state that Pizarro fought in the Italian wars before going to America, sometimes with his father. Evidence for Francisco’s own Italian service is weak and disputed. His father’s military activity in Italy is much better established.

---

## Education and Formation

No school, university, or identified academic teacher is known.

His formative experience came instead from:

- Extremaduran military and kinship culture.
- Caribbean colonization.
- The armed expeditions of Alonso de Ojeda.
- Frontier survival in Urabá and Darién.
- The leadership and exploration methods of Vasco Núñez de Balboa.
- The patronage and coercive politics of Pedrarias Dávila.
- News of Hernán Cortés’s conquest of Mexico, which provided an obvious strategic precedent: seek royal authorization, exploit political division, seize a ruler, recruit Indigenous enemies of an empire, and translate conquest into governorship and encomiendas.

The last point is a **documented historical influence at the level of model and context**, not evidence that Pizarro and Cortés met.

## Early Career: Caribbean, Urabá, Darién, and Panama

### Arrival in America

The traditional date for Pizarro’s arrival in the Indies is **1502**, with the fleet of Nicolás de Ovando to Hispaniola. Details of his earliest Caribbean years are sparse.

### Ojeda’s expedition, 1509–1510

Pizarro joined **Alonso de Ojeda’s** venture to the Gulf of Urabá. The settlement of San Sebastián de Urabá failed amid hunger, illness, conflict, and Indigenous resistance. Ojeda left Pizarro temporarily responsible for survivors under instructions to wait before abandoning the settlement.

Pizarro then joined the survivors associated with **Martín Fernández de Enciso**, eventually moving into Darién.

### Balboa and the Pacific, 1513

Pizarro accompanied **Vasco Núñez de Balboa** across the Isthmus of Panama in 1513. Balboa’s force, supported by large numbers of Indigenous carriers and fighters, sighted the Pacific, then called the South Sea. Pizarro participated in reconnaissance and military operations but was not the expedition’s commander.

### Pedrarias and Balboa’s arrest

After Pedrarias Dávila displaced Balboa politically, Pizarro became one of Pedrarias’s dependable captains. In 1518–1519 he arrested Balboa on Pedrarias’s orders. Balboa was tried and beheaded.

**Documented fact:** Pizarro carried out the arrest.

**Unresolved responsibility:** Pizarro did not pronounce the death sentence, but his participation placed him within the machinery that destroyed his former commander.

### Panama

Pizarro became a resident and officeholder in Panama, receiving land and Indigenous labor. He served as alcalde of Panama City and accumulated enough standing to organize new ventures. He was already a colonial beneficiary before the Peruvian expeditions, though not yet exceptionally wealthy.

## The Company for the Southern Venture

### Partners

- **Francisco Pizarro:** field commander.
- **Diego de Almagro:** recruiter, supplier, ship organizer, and eventually field commander.
- **Hernando de Luque:** clerical and financial representative.
- **Gaspar de Espinosa:** the major concealed or indirect financial backer in much of the scholarship.
- **Pedrarias Dávila:** governor whose consent was initially necessary and whose interest was later bought out or relinquished.

A notarized 1526 agreement declared that Pizarro, Almagro, and Luque would share the enterprise equally. The ceremonial story that Luque divided a consecrated host among the three partners is a **later tradition**; the written contract is the secure evidence.

## Chronological Life History

## 1524–1525: First Southern Expedition

Pizarro sailed from Panama in November 1524 with roughly eighty men and a few horses. Numbers vary by source.

The expedition followed the Pacific coast through regions of modern Colombia. Hunger, storms, disease, difficult coastal terrain, and armed resistance reduced the party. At **Punta Quemada**, an Indigenous attack wounded Pizarro and helped force withdrawal. Almagro, following with reinforcements, lost an eye in fighting.

The expedition was a material failure but confirmed that inhabited territory extended southward.

## 1526–1528: Second Expedition

A renewed expedition began in 1526. The pilot **Bartolomé Ruiz** sailed farther south and encountered a large balsa trading raft carrying textiles, ornaments, and metal goods. Captives or passengers from this encounter supplied linguistic and geographical intelligence; among the interpreters later known to the Spanish was **Felipillo**.

Pizarro’s party suffered prolonged privation at places remembered as the **Isle of the Cock** and **Gorgona Island**.

### “The Thirteen of Fame”

The famous story says that when Governor Pedro de los Ríos ordered the expedition home, Pizarro drew a line in the sand with his sword, invited those willing to pursue wealth and danger to cross it, and thirteen men joined him.

- **Core documented fact:** a small group remained with Pizarro rather than return immediately.
- **Embellished or disputed element:** the precise sword-line scene and its speech come through later narrative elaboration.
- **Reputational function:** the episode became a foundational heroic tableau in Spanish and Peruvian historical art.

After relief arrived, Pizarro explored as far as **Tumbes**, whose buildings, political organization, textiles, and metalwork demonstrated the existence of a wealthy state. He returned to Panama and then traveled to Spain to obtain direct royal authority.

## 1528–1530: Spain and the Capitulación de Toledo

Pizarro reached Spain with Indigenous people, camelids, textiles, and metal objects as evidence of the southern lands.

On **26 July 1529**, Queen Joanna and Emperor Charles’s government issued the **Capitulación de Toledo**. It authorized Pizarro to conquer and settle **New Castile**, granted him the governorship and captaincy-general, and distributed lesser rewards to Almagro, Luque, and others.

### Structural source of the Pizarro–Almagro conflict

The capitulation elevated Pizarro far above Almagro despite their earlier equality agreement. Pizarro may have been unable to obtain more from the Crown, as his defenders argued; Almagro and his supporters saw a deliberate betrayal. The documentary inequality is certain, while Pizarro’s private intention is not.

Pizarro recruited relatives and Extremaduran associates, including Hernando, Juan, and Gonzalo Pizarro and Francisco Martín de Alcántara. He departed Spain in January 1530.

## 1531–1532: Third Expedition and Entry into Tawantinsuyu

Pizarro sailed from Panama late in 1530 or early in 1531. Contemporary counts differ, but his invading force ultimately numbered roughly **160–180 Spaniards**, including cavalry, infantry, firearms, and artillery.

He advanced through:

- San Mateo Bay.
- Coaque, where Spaniards acquired gold and emeralds and suffered illness.
- Puná Island, where relations became violent.
- Tumbes, found altered or damaged amid regional conflict.
- The Piura valley.

In 1532 Pizarro founded **San Miguel**, usually identified with the origins of Piura, the first durable Spanish town in Peru. He left a garrison and marched inland.

### Political setting

The Inca polity had recently undergone:

- The death of Huayna Cápac and his designated successor, probably during epidemic disease.
- A civil war between **Huáscar** and **Atahualpa**.
- Atahualpa’s victory.
- Resentment among subject peoples and factions within the Inca elite.

Spanish success cannot be reconstructed as a contest between fewer than two hundred Europeans and a politically unified empire. The Spaniards combined surprise, steel weapons, horses, firearms, literacy-based administration, hostage seizure, and ruthless risk-taking with extensive Indigenous intelligence, porters, auxiliaries, and alliances. Cañari, Chachapoya, Huanca, and other groups pursued their own objectives against Cusco or rival factions.

## 16 November 1532: Cajamarca

Pizarro entered Cajamarca and concealed troops around its central plaza. Atahualpa arrived in a large ceremonial procession. His main army remained nearby.

The Dominican **Vicente de Valverde**, accompanied by an interpreter, presented Christian and royal claims. Accounts disagree about the exact words, the book offered to Atahualpa, whether it was a Bible, breviary, or other liturgical book, and whether he threw or merely dropped it.

At a signal, Spanish cavalry, infantry, artillery, and firearms attacked the densely packed attendants.

### Evidentiary findings

- **Documented fact:** the meeting was prepared as an ambush and Atahualpa was seized.
- **Documented fact:** very large numbers of Indigenous attendants were killed; the Spanish suffered few or no combat deaths.
- **Disputed number:** chroniclers give casualty totals ranging from approximately 2,000 to 7,000 or more. Such precision cannot be established.
- **Misleading later label:** “Battle of Cajamarca” can suggest combat between comparable armies. Several accounts describe the people inside the plaza as substantially unarmed and unable to organize effective resistance.
- **Documented act:** Pizarro personally helped secure Atahualpa and received a hand injury in the crush, reportedly while preventing Spaniards from killing the captive.

## 1532–1533: Captivity and Ransom of Atahualpa

Atahualpa offered to fill a room with gold and additional space with silver in exchange for release. Objects arrived from across his territories and were melted down, destroying large quantities of Andean metalwork.

The notarized distribution recorded approximately:

- **1,326,539 pesos of gold**, with slight variations in transcriptions.
- **51,610 marks of silver**.

Modern weight and currency conversions vary and should not be treated as exact equivalents. Pedro Sancho’s distribution record assigned Pizarro approximately **31,800 gold pesos and 2,350 silver marks**, plus the golden throne or litter and additional command-related allocations. The Crown received its fifth.

### Execution of Atahualpa

Atahualpa was subjected to a summary proceeding and accused of offenses including idolatry, usurpation, fratricide, polygamy, conspiracy, and organizing an attack.

- **Documented fact:** the proceeding did not provide safeguards resembling either a full Castilian criminal trial or an independent adjudication.
- **Documented fact:** Pizarro authorized the execution after accepting the ransom.
- **Disputed date:** commonly 26 July 1533; some sources give 29 August.
- **Documented form:** after baptism, Atahualpa was garroted rather than burned.
- **Reported baptismal name:** Juan, though names differ in later accounts.
- **Contested explanation:** Pizarro’s defenders emphasize fear of an approaching army and pressure from Almagro’s newly arrived men; critics emphasize premeditated removal of a captive ruler after extraction of his treasure.
- **Later eyewitness claim:** Pedro Pizarro wrote that he saw the marquis weep because he could not save Atahualpa. This is testimony written decades later and does not remove Pizarro’s command responsibility.
- **Felipillo theory:** later narratives blame the interpreter for falsifying evidence out of hatred or sexual jealousy. His participation is documented; decisive responsibility for the sentence cannot be transferred to him on secure evidence.

## 1533–1534: March to Cusco and Puppet Incas

The Spaniards advanced toward Cusco. At Jauja, Pizarro established a provisional Spanish settlement. He installed **Túpac Huallpa** as Inca, but Túpac Huallpa soon died. Accusations that the Inca was poisoned by the Inca general Chalcuchímac are unresolved.

Pizarro had **Chalcuchímac burned alive**, accusing him of conspiracy and responsibility for Túpac Huallpa’s death.

The Spaniards entered **Cusco in November 1533**, accompanied by Indigenous allies. Temples, palaces, storehouses, and elite residences were seized and searched; precious-metal cult objects were appropriated and melted. Sexual coercion, seizure of women, requisitions, and violence accompanied the occupation.

Pizarro recognized **Manco Inca Yupanqui** as ruler under Spanish supremacy. The arrangement initially served both sides: Pizarro needed a legitimate Inca intermediary, while Manco sought advantage against Atahualpa’s faction. It later collapsed under Spanish demands and abuse.

## 1534–1535: Governance and Foundations

Pizarro distributed encomiendas and municipal offices, rewarding followers and creating a dependent political network.

Foundations or refoundations made under his authority included:

- **Jauja**, initially intended as a capital.
- **Cusco** as a Spanish municipality.
- **Quito**, through subordinate captains and in competition with Pedro de Alvarado’s incursion.
- **Trujillo**, named for his birthplace.
- **Lima / Ciudad de los Reyes**, founded **18 January 1535**.
- Later foundations by his lieutenants included Arequipa and settlements connected with expansion toward Chile and the Amazon.

Lima’s coastal location, port access, agricultural valley, and easier communication with Panama made it preferable to highland Jauja. Pizarro received a central house site beside the main plaza and cathedral site—the origin of the later “Casa de Pizarro” designation for Peru’s Government Palace.

## 1535–1536: Almagro’s Chile Expedition and Breakdown with Manco Inca

Almagro departed for Chile after receiving authority over **New Toledo**, south of Pizarro’s New Castile. The boundary’s relationship to Cusco was ambiguous and politically explosive.

Meanwhile, Manco Inca experienced detention, extortion, humiliation, and abuse by members of the Pizarro faction, especially Juan and Gonzalo Pizarro. Sources report the seizure or rape of women connected with Manco, including his principal wife Cura Ocllo.

In 1536 Manco escaped and organized a massive uprising.

## 1536–1537: Great Inca Rebellion

Manco’s forces besieged Cusco and attacked Lima.

- Juan Pizarro died from a head wound during fighting at Sacsayhuamán.
- Indigenous auxiliaries were decisive in preserving Spanish control.
- Inés Huaylas’s mother, Contarhuacho, is reported to have sent Huaylas forces to assist Lima.
- Pizarro directed defenses and sought reinforcements from the Caribbean and Central America.

Manco failed to expel the Spaniards but established resistance from Vilcabamba. The invasion had therefore not “ended the Inca Empire” in one afternoon at Cajamarca; a Neo-Inca polity survived until 1572.

## 1537–1538: Return of Almagro and Civil War

Almagro returned from the disastrous Chile expedition, occupied Cusco, and captured Hernando and Gonzalo Pizarro. Negotiations alternated with military preparation. Pizarro and Almagro met at Mala, but no durable settlement followed.

At the **Battle of Las Salinas**, 6 April 1538, Hernando Pizarro’s forces defeated the Almagrist faction. Diego de Almagro was tried and executed by strangulation and decapitation on **8 July 1538**.

### Responsibility

- **Documented fact:** Hernando controlled the proceeding and execution.
- **Political responsibility:** Francisco was governor and principal beneficiary.
- **Disputed direct responsibility:** accounts differ over whether Francisco desired, permitted, failed to prevent, or later regretted Almagro’s death.
- **Documented consequence:** Almagro’s son and impoverished adherents regarded the Pizarros as having unlawfully destroyed their leader and confiscated their position.

## 1539–1541: Apex of the Pizarro Regime

Pizarro continued allocating encomiendas, lands, governorships, and expeditions.

Important commissions included:

- Pedro de Valdivia’s conquest of Chile.
- Gonzalo Pizarro’s expedition eastward in search of the “Land of Cinnamon,” from which Francisco de Orellana eventually descended the Amazon.
- Suppression campaigns against Indigenous resistance.

Pizarro’s government combined municipal construction and imperial administration with coercive extraction, forced service, armed punishment, and factional patronage.

### Conchucos child-killing allegation

A royal document dated 25 December 1551 stated that Pizarro had sent **Francisco de Chávez** to punish Indigenous people in Conchucos and alleged that Chávez killed approximately **600 boys and girls aged three and upward** after adult opponents withdrew into the mountains.

Evidentiary classification:

- **Documented allegation in a royal instrument:** the Crown had received and acted upon the report.
- **Not a court conviction of Pizarro:** the surviving reference postdates his death and does not establish that he ordered the killing of children specifically.
- **Command relationship:** Chávez was described as Pizarro’s captain and had been sent on the punitive operation.
- **Unresolved question:** what Pizarro knew, authorized, or later learned cannot presently be established from the consulted material.

## 26 June 1541: Assassination

Followers of Diego de Almagro’s son, often called **the Men of Chile**, conspired against Pizarro. Warnings reached him, but he did not take effective precautions.

An armed party stormed his Lima residence. Those killed included:

- Francisco Pizarro.
- Francisco Martín de Alcántara.
- Several household defenders.

Later accounts dramatize Pizarro, despite advanced age, fighting strongly before being overwhelmed. A frequently repeated story says that after receiving mortal wounds he traced a cross with his own blood and kissed it before a killer smashed a vessel over him.

- **Documented fact:** he died violently from multiple edged-weapon wounds.
- **Forensic support:** examination of the remains attributed to him found cranial, neck, arm, and hand injuries consistent with attack and defense.
- **Blood-cross episode:** later death-scene tradition, not independently verifiable.
- **Immediate political result:** the conspirators proclaimed Diego de Almagro the Younger governor. The revolt was later defeated by Cristóbal Vaca de Castro.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

## Company of the Levant

The 1526 Panama contract formally established equal sharing among Pizarro, Almagro, and Luque. Gaspar de Espinosa supplied much of the capital behind Luque’s public role.

The enterprise was a private-public conquest structure:

1. Private investors financed ships, arms, provisions, and recruitment.
2. The Crown licensed conquest and reserved sovereignty and the royal fifth.
3. Conquerors expected offices, booty, land, tribute, and labor.
4. Indigenous societies bore the extraction, military, demographic, and political costs.

## Encomiendas

Pizarro both granted encomiendas and held major ones, including the valuable **Yucay** estate or encomienda associated with Inca royal lands.

An encomienda did not formally transfer ownership of Indigenous people. It granted rights to tribute and labor while imposing nominal duties of protection and Christian instruction. In practice it created severe coercive power and was repeatedly abused.

## Property and Wealth

His wealth derived from:

- His leadership share of Atahualpa’s ransom.
- Additional precious-metal objects, including the Inca’s golden seat or litter.
- Encomienda income.
- Agricultural and urban property.
- Official emoluments and command privileges.
- The political capacity to allocate resources to relatives and clients.

His 1537 testament and later amendments provided for:

- His children, particularly Francisca and Gonzalo.
- A proposed family entail or succession.
- Religious foundations and masses.
- Relatives and servants.
- Settlement of partnership accounts.
- At one stage, conciliatory obligations toward Almagro and requests that Almagro protect his children.

Some modern monetary conversions of his ransom share or total estate reach spectacular dollar amounts. They are **speculative analogies**, not documentary valuations, because bullion content, purchasing power, labor relations, and status wealth are not directly convertible.

## Technology and Military Advantage

Pizarro did not invent technologies or hold patents. His forces employed:

- Steel swords, lances, armor, and crossbows.
- Arquebuses.
- Small artillery.
- Horses.
- Ocean-going ships and coastal navigation.
- Writing, notarization, contracts, and royal seals as technologies of command and ownership.
- Indigenous roads, bridges, food stores, porters, interpreters, and intelligence.

Firearms were psychologically and tactically significant but slow and few. Horses, steel, surprise, hostage seizure, Indigenous alliances, and existing political fragmentation were at least as important.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

Pizarro was not a systematic political writer. His politics can be reconstructed from actions and dictated documents.

### Monarchical allegiance

He consistently grounded authority in service to Charles I/V and the Crown. He sought royal titles rather than formal independence. His family’s later conflicts with royal reform do not prove that Francisco himself planned secession.

### Personal and factional rule

His administration rested upon:

- Kinship.
- Fellow Extremadurans.
- Distribution of offices and encomiendas.
- Military loyalty.
- Control of municipal councils.
- Alliances with selected Inca and provincial elites.

### Religion

He invoked Catholic mission and royal-Christian jurisdiction, founded churches, supported clergy, and made conventional testamentary provisions for his soul.

The religious justification for conquest coexisted with material extraction and warfare. The Requerimiento and conversion ceremonies cannot be treated as evidence that Indigenous parties understood or freely accepted Spanish sovereignty.

### Ideology of conquest

His documents use the standard vocabulary of discovery, pacification, settlement, service to God, and service to the Crown. Indigenous resistance was commonly categorized as rebellion even when directed against newly imposed foreign rule.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Diego de Almagro

Partner, co-investor, collaborator, rival, and ultimately political enemy. Their unequal royal rewards, geographical boundary dispute, control of Cusco, and competition between client networks produced civil war.

### Hernando de Luque and Gaspar de Espinosa

Luque was the clerical face of the enterprise; Espinosa was a crucial financier. Their involvement demonstrates that the conquest was not solely an act of personal bravery but also a capitalized venture.

### Hernando Pizarro

Half-brother, military commander, court representative, and protector of the family estate. His execution of Almagro intensified the factional crisis. He was imprisoned for years in Spain and later married Francisca Pizarro Yupanqui.

### Gonzalo and Juan Pizarro

Half-brothers and lieutenants. Their conduct toward Manco Inca contributed to the 1536 rebellion. Juan died during the siege; Gonzalo later rebelled against royal authority.

### Francisco Martín de Alcántara

Maternal half-brother and household defender, killed with Pizarro.

### Atahualpa

Captive ruler, political intelligence source, ransom provider, and victim of an execution authorized by Pizarro. Reports that the men ate and played games together may preserve real interaction but come through later narrative testimony. Descriptions of a genuine friendship are interpretive.

### Manco Inca Yupanqui

Initially an allied or client ruler; later leader of the anti-Spanish rebellion. Their relationship moved from tactical cooperation to war.

### Inés Huaylas Yupanqui and Angelina Yupanqui

Partners within an unequal conquest setting, mothers of his children, and links to Inca royal legitimacy. Neither should be reduced to a passive genealogical bridge: Inés’s family network played an active political role.

### Felipillo and other interpreters

Essential intermediaries in negotiation, intelligence, and legal-religious performance. Later chroniclers frequently turned Felipillo into a convenient villain; the surviving evidence cannot make him the sole cause of Atahualpa’s death.

### Indigenous Allies

Cañari, Chachapoya, Huanca, Huaylas, and other forces collaborated at different times for their own political and military objectives. Their participation was essential, especially during the sieges of Cusco and Lima.

### Pedro de Candía, Bartolomé Ruiz, Nicolás de Ribera, and the “Thirteen”

Explorers, pilots, soldiers, and early adherents who sustained the second expedition and supplied evidence used to obtain royal authorization.

### Pedro de Valdivia and Francisco de Orellana

Subordinates or expeditionary figures whose later careers in Chile and the Amazon began under the expanding Pizarro regime.

### Pedro de Alvarado

Rival conquistador whose southward movement from Guatemala threatened Pizarro’s claims in Quito. A negotiated purchase or settlement absorbed Alvarado’s expedition and removed the immediate competition.

### Cristóbal Vaca de Castro

Royal official sent to investigate and impose order. He arrived after Pizarro’s death and defeated the younger Almagro.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, and Financial Questions

## Cajamarca massacre

**Documented fact:** Pizarro planned the seizure of Atahualpa and unleashed an attack in a confined plaza.

**Documented fact:** thousands or at least a very large number of attendants were killed while Spanish losses were negligible.

**Disputed detail:** casualty figures and the degree to which attendants were armed.

## Hostage-taking and ransom

**Documented fact:** Atahualpa was held captive; enormous quantities of gold and silver were delivered; Pizarro received a major share; Atahualpa was then executed.

Calling this “the largest kidnapping ransom in history” is a modern rhetorical classification, not a conclusion found in the notarial record. The underlying seizure, promised exchange, transfer, and killing are documented.

## Atahualpa’s proceeding and execution

**Documented fact:** Pizarro authorized an extraordinary summary proceeding and death sentence.

**Contested defense:** imminent military necessity.

**Contested accusation:** that Pizarro always intended to kill Atahualpa. No surviving private instruction proves that initial intent.

## Looting and destruction of sacred objects

**Documented fact:** Spanish forces seized and melted royal and sacred metalwork at Cajamarca and Cusco. The ransom distribution itself is notarized.

The broader destruction of Andean religious institutions occurred through soldiers, clerics, settlers, and later colonial extirpation campaigns. Not every later act can be assigned personally to Pizarro.

## Forced labor and encomienda abuse

**Documented structure:** Pizarro distributed and benefited from encomiendas giving colonists claims to tribute and service.

**Documented systemic controversy:** abuses of Indigenous labor contributed to royal reform culminating in the New Laws of 1542.

**Caution:** assigning every later demographic death directly to Pizarro would be historically unsound. Warfare, displacement, food-system disruption, coerced labor, social breakdown, and Old World epidemics interacted.

## Sexual violence and captive women

Conquest forces appropriated Indigenous women as servants, sexual partners, hostages, translators, and dynastic intermediaries.

Pizarro’s relationships with Inés and Angelina are documented, but sources do not record their consent in terms adequate to modern evaluation. Presenting them simply as ordinary spouses sanitizes the coercive environment; labeling every interaction with a precise modern criminal category exceeds the surviving evidence.

## Treatment of Manco Inca

The detention, extortion, and abuse of Manco by the Pizarro faction are well attested. Responsibility for particular assaults lies especially with Francisco’s brothers, but Francisco’s patronage system and government supplied the setting.

## Burning of Chalcuchímac

**Documented act:** the Inca general was executed by burning under Pizarro’s authority.

**Disputed basis:** accusations of poisoning and conspiracy were not independently proven.

## Death of Diego de Almagro

**Documented fact:** Almagro was executed under Hernando Pizarro after defeat.

**Unresolved issue:** Francisco’s precise instructions and whether he genuinely sought to prevent the execution.

## Almagro estate and succession

The 1541 murder-process material states that Pizarro denied or reduced the younger Almagro’s expected inheritance, offices, and political position.

- **Documented grievance and administrative action.**
- **Contested legality:** the competing governorships, testamentary claims, and royal grants were legally tangled.
- **Political effect:** Almagrist dispossession directly fed the assassination conspiracy.

## Conchucos children

As noted above, a 1551 royal document reports the killing of approximately 600 children by Pizarro’s captain Francisco de Chávez during a punitive campaign.

- **Evidentiary label:** documented royal report/allegation.
- **Not established:** a personal order from Pizarro to kill children.
- **Established:** Pizarro commissioned the punitive expedition and Chávez operated within his command system.

## Fraud, corruption, and money laundering

No evidence was found of “money laundering” in the modern legal sense.

Financial controversies instead involved:

- Whether Pizarro concealed or unfairly divided profits.
- Whether he betrayed the equal partnership with Almagro.
- Allocation and reassignment of encomiendas.
- Confiscation or diminution of Almagrist property.
- Patronage favoring relatives and clients.
- Accounting for royal treasure and the Crown’s fifth.

These were political, contractual, and imperial-administrative disputes. The consulted record does not yield a modern criminal conviction of Pizarro for fraud or corruption.

## Genocide classification

“Genocide” is widely used in present political debate about Pizarro and the conquest.

- **Documented history:** mass killing at Cajamarca, executions, punitive terror, dispossession, forced labor, cultural and religious destruction, and demographic catastrophe.
- **Legal caution:** genocide is a twentieth-century legal category requiring specific intent to destroy a protected group. The consulted sources do not establish a judicial finding concerning Pizarro.
- **Historiographical status:** scholars debate terms including conquest, invasion, colonial war, massacre, ethnocide, demographic catastrophe, and genocide. The empirical acts should be evaluated separately from the retrospective legal label.

## Recent adversarial findings, 2024–2026

A dedicated search combining Pizarro’s name with controversy, scandal, allegations, lawsuits, fraud, accusations, conspiracy, criticism, genocide, massacre, and Indigenous abuse found:

- Renewed controversy over the Lima equestrian statue in January 2025.
- Accusations by Indigenous representatives and critics that honoring him celebrates massacre, colonial domination, or genocide.
- Defenses by municipal and Spanish conservative officials presenting him as Lima’s founder and a symbol of mestizo Peru.
- Continuing criticism of heroic school narratives that minimize Indigenous agency and colonial violence.
- Renewed circulation of unsupported claims that he was secretly Jewish, that the Lima statue depicts Cortés, that he was worshipped as a god, and that he consciously designed a harmonious racial union.
- No credible recent evidence of a newly discovered lawsuit, fraud conviction, secret intelligence connection, or modern financial scandal involving the historical Pizarro.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## The abandoned swineherd

**Story:** the illegitimate child was abandoned, raised in misery, and worked as a pig keeper before losing the herd and fleeing.

**Appearance and circulation:** post-conquest biographical tradition, amplified by nineteenth- and twentieth-century popular histories.

**Evidence:** his illegitimacy, limited education, and modest beginnings are plausible; the detailed pig episode lacks contemporary proof.

**Effect:** heroic “self-made conqueror” narrative for admirers; symbol of uncultivated brutality for detractors.

## The sword-line on Gallo Island

**Story:** Pizarro drew a line in the sand and said that on one side lay Panama and poverty, on the other Peru and riches; thirteen crossed.

**Evidence:** a small remnant remained with him. The staged line and polished speech are later literary elaborations.

**Effect:** became a canonical image of resolution, reproduced in painting, monuments, textbooks, and political rhetoric.

## Pizarro as a god

**Story:** Andeans believed Pizarro or the Spaniards were gods.

**Evidence against a simple version:** Indigenous observers tested whether Spaniards ate, slept, had sex, tired, bled, and died. Sixteenth-century sources contain supernatural interpretations, but they do not support a uniform belief that Pizarro was divine.

**Later circulation:** conquest apologetics and simplified school narratives.

**Effect:** obscures Indigenous intelligence, diplomacy, experimentation, and resistance.

## The speaking Bible at Cajamarca

**Story:** Valverde gave Atahualpa a Bible; the Inca held it to his ear, heard nothing, and threw it down, provoking attack.

**Evidence:** accounts disagree over the object, gestures, translation, and sequence. The ambush had already been militarily prepared.

**Effect:** furnished a religious justification for violence and a compact allegory of failed communication.

## Felipillo betrayed Atahualpa for a woman

**Story:** the interpreter deliberately mistranslated accusations because he desired one of Atahualpa’s women.

**Evidence:** Felipillo served as interpreter and later shifted allegiances. The romantic-jealous motive is inseparable from hostile later characterization and is not enough to explain the command decision.

**Effect:** displaced responsibility from Spanish commanders onto an Indigenous intermediary.

## Pizarro promised freedom in good faith

**Story:** Pizarro sincerely planned to release Atahualpa but was overruled by frightened or greedy followers.

**Evidence for:** later reports that he opposed execution or wept afterward.

**Evidence against:** he presided over the ransom distribution, controlled the prisoner, accepted the summary proceeding, and authorized the sentence.

**Classification:** disputed interpretation of intention.

## Pizarro intended to create mestizo Peru

**Story:** his relationships and children show a conscious program to unite Spaniards and Incas into a new people.

**Evidence:** he fathered children with Inca royal women, legitimized some of them, and provided dynastic wealth.

**Evidence against:** no surviving manifesto or statement presents a general project of racial synthesis.

**Effect:** central to modern Hispanist rehabilitation and “father of Peruvian mestizaje” rhetoric.

## The Lima statue is really Hernán Cortés

**Story:** Charles Cary Rumsey’s equestrian statue was intended for Mexico as Cortés; Mexico rejected it and Peru received it under another name.

**Evidence:** replicas or versions were installed in Trujillo, Lima, and Buffalo. The generic armor and horse encouraged the legend. No consulted documentation establishes that it was secretly a Cortés statue.

**Effect:** used to question the monument’s authenticity and legitimacy.

## Crypto-Jewish ancestry

**Story:** symbols associated with Pizarro’s tomb or speculative genealogy prove Jewish or converso ancestry.

**Evidence:** no reliable genealogical or inquisitorial documentation consulted establishes this. Tomb symbols admit other interpretations.

**Classification:** unverified theory.

## “A handful of Spaniards conquered an empire”

**Story:** 168 Spaniards independently destroyed Tawantinsuyu through courage or technological superiority.

**Evidence against:** Indigenous civil war, epidemic disruption, provincial rivalries, local allies, porters, informants, and client rulers were indispensable.

**Effect:** served both imperial hero worship and inverted narratives of inexplicable Indigenous passivity.

## Works by Francisco Pizarro

Pizarro wrote no known book, memoir, chronicle, philosophical work, technical paper, or literary composition. Most documents under his name were dictated, drafted by secretaries, formally issued in his authority, witnessed by notaries, or signed with a rubric.

### Principal documentary corpus

- **Letters of Francisco Pizarro**, 1533–1541, addressed to the Crown, royal officials, clergy, municipalities, and associates.
- **Letter to Tomás de Berlanga**, 28 August 1537.
- **Letter to the King recommending or requesting reward for Cristóbal de Burgos**, 28 February 1539.
- **Letters from Los Reyes**, including one dated 4 June 1540.
- **Administrative commissions and orders** governing expeditions, settlements, offices, and encomiendas.
- **Encomienda grants**, including the grant to Diego Maldonado, Cusco, 15 April 1539.
- **Instructions and powers of attorney** issued as governor.
- **Foundation acts and municipal instruments** associated with San Miguel, Jauja, Cusco, Lima, Trujillo, and other settlements. These are institutional-notarial documents, not sole-authored literary texts.
- **Testament of Francisco Pizarro**, originally executed in 1537, with later alterations or supplementary provisions. Dictated/notarial authorship.
- **Obligation of Francisco Pizarro**, 17 August 1529, connected with the Capitulación de Toledo.
- **Official Requerimiento used in the Peruvian conquest**, an institutional legal formula proclaimed under his authority, not composed personally by him.
- **Petitions, memorials, and reports submitted to the Crown**, normally scribally produced.

### Major modern documentary editions

- Raúl Porras Barrenechea, ed., *Cedulario del Perú, siglos XVI, XVII y XVIII*, especially the early volumes covering 1529–1534.
- Raúl Porras Barrenechea, *Cartas del Perú, 1524–1543*.
- Guillermo Lohmann Villena, ed., *Francisco Pizarro: testimonio: documentos oficiales, cartas y escritos varios* (Madrid, 1986).
- *Cartas del Marqués Don Francisco Pizarro, 1533–1541*, published in archival and historical compilations.
- Raúl Porras Barrenechea, “El testamento de Francisco Pizarro, conquistador del Perú,” *Boletín de la Real Academia de la Historia* 108 (1936).

### Works incorrectly or loosely attributed

- Francisco de Jerez’s *Verdadera relación de la conquista del Perú* was produced by Pizarro’s secretary and promoted the expedition, but it is not authored by Pizarro.
- Pedro Sancho de la Hoz’s report and ransom certificate were official eyewitness documents, not Pizarro’s personal narrative.
- Pedro Pizarro’s *Relación del descubrimiento y conquista de los reinos del Perú* is by a younger relative and participant, completed in 1571.
- The Sámano–Xerez relation concerns Pizarro’s expeditions but is not his authorship.

### Historiographical positions

- **Heroic-national tradition:** emphasizes courage, endurance, city foundations, Christianity, Spanish institutions, and mestizaje.

- **Revisionist imperial-network scholarship:** studies finance, kinship, Indigenous alliances, translation, litigation, encomienda, and factionalism rather than treating Pizarro as a solitary hero or uniquely omnipotent villain.
- **Recent public-history debate:** focuses on whether statues commemorate a founder, preserve difficult history, or confer public honor upon a conqueror.

## Death, Burial, and Remains

Pizarro was initially buried in Lima’s cathedral precinct under circumstances shaped by the immediate political crisis.

His head and body were at some stage kept separately. For centuries, remains publicly displayed as Pizarro’s were accepted as authentic.

In 1977 workers discovered a lead box and skeletal material in a cathedral crypt. The box bore an inscription identifying the head of the Marquis Francisco Pizarro. Anthropological and forensic investigation concluded that the newly recovered skeleton was more consistent with:

- An older male.
- Multiple sword or edged-weapon injuries.
- Defensive wounds.
- The documented manner of Pizarro’s death.

In 1985 the attributed remains were reinterred or placed for public commemoration in Lima Cathedral.

**Evidentiary classification:** strongly supported forensic-historical identification, though not a modern DNA identification against an unquestioned reference sample.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Sixteenth and seventeenth centuries

Pizarro’s image divided quickly:

- Pizarrist narratives defended his service and courage.
- Almagrist narratives stressed betrayal and tyranny.
- Royal officials regarded the family’s concentration of power as dangerous.
- Indigenous and mestizo chroniclers recorded violence, religious conflict, betrayal, and social transformation.
- His daughter Francisca and Hernando consolidated an aristocratic family memory in Spain.

### Nineteenth century

Romantic histories turned Cajamarca and the conquest into scenes of courage, fatalism, greed, and civilizational conflict. Prescott’s work became internationally influential.

Independence-era and republican narratives faced a recurring problem: whether Pizarro was a founder of Hispanic Peru or the destroyer of an Indigenous state.

### Twentieth-century rehabilitation and criticism

Raúl Porras Barrenechea and José Antonio del Busto produced major documentary and sympathetic studies. Indigenist scholarship and public culture foregrounded Atahualpa, Manco Inca, Indigenous suffering, and resistance.

Pizarro’s historical reputation became entangled with competing definitions of Peru:

- Indigenous continuity.
- Hispanic foundation.
- Mestizo synthesis.
- Colonial domination.
- Multiethnic conflict and alliance.

### The equestrian statue

Charles Cary Rumsey designed the monumental equestrian Pizarro. Versions stand or have stood in Trujillo, Lima, and Buffalo.

In Lima:

- Installed near the cathedral in 1935.
- Moved in 1952 to an adjacent plaza.
- Removed from its privileged location in April 2003.
- Reinstalled at Parque de la Muralla in late 2004.
- Returned to the historic center, near the Plaza Mayor, on **18 January 2025**, Lima’s 490th anniversary.

The 2025 ceremony involved Lima mayor Rafael López Aliaga and Madrid regional president Isabel Díaz Ayuso. Protesters and Indigenous representatives described the restoration as honoring invasion or genocide; supporters described it as restoration of history and recognition of Lima’s founder.

This dispute concerns public honor, not whether Pizarro existed or founded the Spanish city.

### Continuing influence

Pizarro’s durable institutional legacy includes:

- The Spanish foundation and capital status of Lima.
- Urban settlements and municipal structures.
- The initial colonial distribution of land, tribute, and labor.
- The creation of a Pizarro-Inca aristocratic lineage.
- A central role in narratives of conquest, colonialism, Indigenous alliance, resistance, and mestizaje.
- A recurring symbolic place in debates over monuments, education, national identity, and historical responsibility.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted evidence are included. Mere shared period or geography is excluded.

### Atahualpa — documented fact

Pizarro met Atahualpa at Cajamarca on 16 November 1532, arranged his capture, held him through the collection and distribution of an enormous ransom, and authorized his execution in 1533. Their relationship included repeated personal contact during captivity. Claims of close friendship are interpretive.

### Hernán Cortés — documented influence; no documented meeting found

Cortés’s conquest of Mexico supplied a strategic and political precedent for Pizarro’s enterprise, especially the seizure of a sovereign, exploitation of internal divisions, recruitment of Indigenous allies, acquisition of royal authority, and conversion of conquest into governorship and encomiendas. No reliable evidence was found that the two men personally met or corresponded.

### Christopher Columbus — documented institutional-historical influence; no personal meeting established

Columbus’s voyages created the transatlantic colonial system through which Pizarro reached Hispaniola and pursued conquest. Pizarro reportedly sailed with Nicolás de Ovando’s fleet in 1502, while Columbus undertook his separate fourth voyage that year. No documented personal meeting was found.

### El Inca Garcilaso de la Vega — documented posthumous narrative connection

Garcilaso was born in Cusco in 1539 while Pizarro was still alive, but no personal meeting is documented. Garcilaso later narrated Pizarro and the conquest in the *Comentarios reales* and *Historia general del Perú*. The connection is therefore posthumous historiographical treatment, not demonstrated personal association.

### Felipe Guamán Poma de Ayala — documented posthumous narrative connection

Guamán Poma’s *Nueva corónica y buen gobierno* depicted and discussed Pizarro, Atahualpa, and the conquest. No personal meeting is documented; Guamán Poma’s birth date is itself uncertain and his chronicle belongs to a later generation.

### Blas Valera — documented posthumous intellectual connection

The Jesuit and mestizo chronicler Blas Valera wrote about Inca history and the conquest after Pizarro’s death. His work influenced later accounts, especially Garcilaso. No personal contact with Pizarro is possible on the generally accepted chronology.

### Francisco de Orellana — documented organizational connection

Orellana operated within the expanding Pizarro network. He joined or was associated with Gonzalo Pizarro’s eastern expedition, commissioned during Francisco Pizarro’s government, and then descended the Amazon. Direct day-to-day collaboration with Francisco is less completely recorded than the institutional connection.

### Pedro de Mendoza — reported shared imperial milieu; no direct connection established

Both were Spanish adelantados and conquest organizers, but no documented meeting, correspondence, partnership, or rivalry was found.

### Francisco Pizarro González — identity

The subject is himself included in the atlas roster.

### Remaining roster

No defensible personal or documentary life-crossing was established with the other listed atlas figures. Many later writers, artists, political figures, or filmmakers may have referred broadly to the conquest, but reference without a sourced, specific influence or relationship was not treated as an Atlas connection. Absence of evidence here is a finding, not proof that no undiscovered document exists.

- **1510:** Moves with survivors into Darién.

- **17 August 1529:** Executes related financial/legal obligation.
- **January 1530:** Leaves Spain with brothers and recruits.
- **1531:** Launches final invasion from Panama; advances through Coaque and Puná.
