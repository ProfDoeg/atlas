## Works by Haile Selassie I

### Autobiographical obra

- **1936–1937; published 1973:** *My Life and Ethiopia’s Progress, Volume I: 1892–1937*. An Amharic political autobiography begun during Selassie’s exile and organized as a history of both his life and Ethiopia’s modernization. Edward Ullendorff’s standard scholarly edition treats Selassie as author, but the surviving work is an official, translated, and editorially mediated account rather than an unproblematic private memoir.[^1][^2]

- **Published 1974:** *My Life and Ethiopia’s Progress, Volume II: Addis Abeba, 1966 E.C.* A continuation concerned principally with the Italian occupation, exile, resistance, and restoration. The scholarly English edition attributes the work to Selassie while identifying Harold G. Marcus, Ezekiel Gebissa, and Tibebe Eshete as editors and Ezekiel Gebissa and Guluma Gemeda as translators; claims that officials assembled some of its documentary material make its precise degree of personal authorship uncertain.[^1][^3]

### Public utterances as a body of work

- **1918–1967:** *Selected Speeches of His Imperial Majesty Haile Selassie First, 1918–1967*. An official 1967 compilation of approximately 190 speeches, proclamations, messages, toasts, commemorations, and ceremonial addresses on education, diplomacy, African unity, war, law, development, religion, and public welfare. It is an editorially selected body of utterances—not a complete collected-works edition—and many texts were institutionally drafted, translated, or issued in the sovereign’s name, so delivery and authorization are firmer than sole literary authorship.[^1][^4][^5]

### Manifestos and protocols

- **16 July 1931:** *Decree Granting the Constitution*. An imperial manifesto that performs the act it announces: Selassie grants Ethiopia’s first written constitution and defines it as an exercise of his sovereign will. The decree is attributable to him as an official act, but the associated constitution is a state document whose drafting should not be reduced to individual authorship.[^4][^6]

- **3 October 1935:** *Mobilization Proclamation*. A call to arms issued and publicly read when Italian forces invaded Ethiopia; it is simultaneously a manifesto, military protocol, and performed sovereign act. Its place in the official speech collection establishes Selassie’s authorization and delivery, not necessarily unaided composition.[^4][^7]

- **30 June 1936:** *Appeal to the League of Nations*. A diplomatic speech delivered in Geneva against Italy’s conquest and chemical warfare: an address that seeks international action and therefore behaves as a manifesto rather than merely describing events. Contemporary cataloguing attributes the speech to Selassie, although its movement among Amharic, French, and published translations complicates the idea of a single definitive verbal text.[^4][^8]

- **24 July 1940:** *The Mercy Proclamation*. A wartime proclamation offering pardon and calling Ethiopians—including former opponents or collaborators—to join resistance against Italian rule. It is a protocol because it prescribes conduct and a manifesto because its public issuance attempts to reorganize political allegiance; some later tables of contents misdate it simply “July 1940,” while the contemporary text records 24 July.[^4][^9]

- **5 May 1941:** *Triumphant Entry—Victory Day Address*. A restoration speech delivered upon Selassie’s return to Addis Ababa, joining commemoration, political reconciliation, and the public re-establishment of imperial authority. It is an utterance whose occasion is inseparable from its force as a sovereign act.[^4]

- **3 November 1955:** *Proclamation Promulgating the Revised Constitution of the Empire of Ethiopia*. A manifesto and legal protocol placing the revised constitution into force on the silver jubilee of Selassie’s coronation. The proclamation speaks in his name and claims that “we” prepared the revision, but parliamentary examination and governmental drafting make the constitution itself a collaborative state obra, not securely a personally authored text.[^4][^10][^11]

- **4 October 1963:** *Address to the United Nations General Assembly*. A diplomatic manifesto connecting collective security, racial equality, decolonization, and the institutional responsibilities of the United Nations. The official UN record identifies Selassie as speaker and notes that the English version was supplied by the Ethiopian delegation, marking the published wording as an authorized manifestation rather than necessarily his unaided composition.[^12]

### Patronage and commissioned manifestations

- **1961–1962:** *Revised Amharic Bible*. A revised Amharic biblical translation produced under Selassie’s authority and publicly associated with him; his collected speeches include an address marking its publication. It resists classification as his “work”: he sponsored and authorized the project but neither originated the biblical texts nor can be credited as their sole translator.[^4][^5]

- **1931 and 1955:** *The Constitution of the Empire of Ethiopia* and *The Revised Constitution of the Empire of Ethiopia*. These are legal creations of Selassie’s reign and were granted or promulgated by him, but they resist inclusion as individually authored works: advisers, ministers, legal draftsmen, and, in 1955, Parliament participated in their production. They belong here as imperial-state obra for which Selassie supplied intention, authorization, and performative force—not as books he simply “wrote.”[^6][^10][^11]
