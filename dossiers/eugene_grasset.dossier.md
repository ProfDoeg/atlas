# Eugène Samuel Grasset: Research Dossier

## Scope, identity, and evidentiary note

This dossier concerns the Swiss-born, naturalized French decorative artist Eugène Samuel Grasset, born at Lausanne on 25 May 1845 and deceased at Sceaux on 23 October 1917. He must not be confused with:

- Bernard Grasset, the twentieth-century French publisher.
- Bernard Grasset’s father, another Eugène Grasset, a lawyer from Chambéry/Montpellier.
- The French parliamentarian Eugène de Grasset.
- Other nineteenth- and twentieth-century bearers of the surname.

A persistent cataloguing error gives the artist’s birth year as **1841**. That date remains in some museum records, older books, commercial catalogues, and derivative databases. French national-library authority data, the Institut national d’histoire de l’art, the Musée d’Orsay, and the Lausanne scholarship associated with the 2011 retrospective agree on **25 May 1845**. The 1845 date is therefore used throughout this dossier.

Several parts of Grasset’s private life remain poorly documented in accessible published sources. In particular, no reliable evidence located for this dossier establishes children, substantial property ownership, a political party affiliation, criminal conduct, addiction, or a diagnosed chronic illness. Such absences are reported rather than filled by inference.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full civil name:** Eugène Samuel Grasset.
- **Usual professional name:** Eugène Grasset.
- **Alternative catalogue forms:** Eugène-Samuel Grasset; Grasset, Eugène Samuel; E. Grasset; “EG” as a printed monogram.
- **Pseudonyms or nicknames:** None securely documented.
- **Birth:** 25 May 1845, Lausanne, Canton of Vaud, Switzerland.
- **Death:** 23 October 1917, Sceaux, then in the département of Seine, France; now Hauts-de-Seine.
- **Burial:** Sceaux municipal cemetery, probably 25 or 26 October 1917. The temporary concession was apparently not perpetuated, and the remains were probably later transferred to the cemetery ossuary.
- **Nationalities:** Swiss by birth; naturalized French in 1891.
- **Languages:** French is the documented language of his writings and professional life.
- **Principal occupations:** Decorative artist, draftsman, painter, sculptor, illustrator, poster designer, typographer/type designer, furniture designer, designer of stained glass, mosaics, jewelry, textiles, wallpaper and metalwork; teacher and theorist of ornament.
- **Principal geographical centers:** Lausanne; Zürich; Egypt; Paris; Sceaux.
- **Principal institutional affiliations:** École Guérin; École d’Art graphique, rue Madame; Académie de la Grande Chaumière; École Estienne; Société nationale des beaux-arts; Union centrale des arts décoratifs; Salon des Cent.
- **Honors:** Chevalier of the Légion d’honneur, 1895; promoted officier, 1911.

The Bibliothèque nationale de France describes him as a “peintre, dessinateur, décorateur, architecte, illustrateur et affichiste,” born in Lausanne on 25 May 1845, deceased in Sceaux on 23 October 1917, and naturalized in 1891. The Musée d’Orsay similarly records both Swiss and French nationality and holds an archival fonds relating to him. The INHA’s AGORHA authority record identifies archival materials at the Musée d’Orsay, the Bibliothèque de l’INHA–Collections Jacques Doucet, and the Bibliothèque littéraire Jacques Doucet.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Documented fact:** Grasset was the son of:

- **Samuel Joseph Grasset**, an ébéniste—usually described as a cabinetmaker, furniture maker, decorator, and sculptor.
- **Jeanne Louise Marguerite Burnens**, named in later biographical and genealogical scholarship.

The father’s workshop supplied Grasset’s earliest practical artistic environment. The standard biographical account says that the elder Grasset accepted his son’s artistic inclination but encouraged architectural training because he feared that painting alone would not provide a living. This story appears in early twentieth-century biographical literature and is plausible, but the exact dialogue is not independently recoverable.

### Siblings

**Reported fact:** A sister, **Louise Grasset**, remained in Lausanne throughout her life. Detailed documentation of her dates and occupation was not located in the consulted institutional sources.

No other sibling has been securely identified.

### Marriage and household

**Documented or strongly reported fact:** Grasset lived with and married **Euphrasie Charlotte Charrotton**, born at Gibraltar. Published accounts indicate that she accompanied or lived with him from his early Paris years. The precise marriage date is not consistently reported in accessible sources.

She survived him and died at Sceaux in 1926. The absence of a readily accessible marriage record leaves open whether the marriage occurred before or after their settlement in Paris.

### Children

No documented children were found in the sources consulted. This should be stated as an archival gap, not as proof that the couple had none.

### Extended ancestry

No securely documented multigenerational ancestry was located beyond the names of his parents. Grasset did not belong to the French aristocratic de Grasset family, and no relationship to publisher Bernard Grasset is established.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1845–1861

Grasset grew up in Lausanne in an artisan household. His father’s cabinetmaking and sculptural work exposed him to drawing, carving, three-dimensional construction, and the relationship between ornament and useful objects. That workshop experience later became central to his rejection of a rigid division between “fine” and “applied” arts.

A Spanish doctoral study based on the modern Grasset literature places him at Lausanne’s **École Moyenne in 1857**. There he encountered the painter **François-Louis David Bocion**. Museum chronologies generally date his formal drawing study with Bocion to approximately **1859–1863**.

A youthful statement preserved in later scholarship describes his time as divided among drawing, walking, ploughing, and modeling busts in clay. The exact manuscript context should be checked against the Lausanne archival material before treating the wording as a definitive autobiographical quotation.

### François Bocion

Bocion, a painter closely associated with Lake Geneva landscapes, was Grasset’s first important named teacher. His instruction provided training in observation and draftsmanship. Later accounts say Bocion expected Grasset to become a painter. Grasset instead moved toward architecture and then toward an extraordinarily broad practice in decorative design.

### Early visual influences

Grasset reportedly encountered the illustrated work of **Gustave Doré** while young. This was a formative model for the integration of narrative and image, although Grasset’s mature linear organization differed markedly from Doré’s dramatic tonal manner.

## Education and Formation, 1861–1871

### Zürich Polytechnicum

In 1861 Grasset went to Zürich to study architecture at the **Eidgenössisches Polytechnikum**, now ETH Zürich. The common date range is **1861–1863**. Some accounts say that he entered after success in a drawing competition.

The German-language tradition identifies **Gottfried Semper** as a professor under whom he studied. The extent of direct personal teaching is less firmly documented than Grasset’s enrollment in architectural studies. It is safest to distinguish institutional presence from a proved close mentorship.

The studies were either interrupted or not completed in the form of a professional architectural qualification. Nonetheless, architectural training remained fundamental to his later theories: structure was to govern ornament, and decoration should grow from an object’s construction and use.

### Lausanne architectural and theatrical work

After Zürich, Grasset worked in or around a Lausanne architectural office. Early biographers relate that he met a young French sculptor there and traveled with him to Egypt. The companion is not consistently identified.

In **1869–1870**, Grasset worked as a painter and sculptor on the decoration of the **Théâtre de Lausanne**. This constitutes his first substantial documented professional project. His exact share in the surviving decorative program requires archival reconstruction.

### Journey to Egypt

The most reliable museum chronology dates his Egyptian journey to **1866**. Some older secondary sources place it in 1869. The 1866 date is better supported by the Lausanne exhibition chronology, but the discrepancy should remain visible.

The journey exposed him to Egyptian antiquity, monumental architecture, flat pattern, symbolic forms, and non-naturalistic systems of ornament. Egyptian references later appeared in his graphic and decorative work. No detailed itinerary, duration, travel diary, or financial sponsor for the journey was located.

### Viollet-le-Duc and the medieval revival

Grasset became an intense reader and admirer of **Eugène-Emmanuel Viollet-le-Duc**, especially his analysis of Gothic structure and ornament. “Disciple” is frequently used in the intellectual sense; no evidence establishes that Grasset was formally enrolled as Viollet-le-Duc’s student.

From Viollet-le-Duc he drew several durable principles:

- structure and decoration should be coherent;
- materials should be treated according to their properties;
- medieval art offered a model of integrated production rather than a repertory to copy mechanically;
- an artist could design an entire environment, from architecture to furniture and lettering.

These principles later combined with Japanese, Islamic, Egyptian, Celtic, and Renaissance sources.

### Franco-Prussian War and departure for Paris

Grasset remained in Switzerland during the Franco-Prussian War and the Paris Commune. He settled permanently in Paris near the end of **1871**, when he was twenty-six. No evidence was found that this migration was an exile or the result of political persecution. It appears to have been a professional move toward the largest French market for illustration and decorative manufacture.

## Early Paris Career, 1871–1883

### Initial employment

At Paris, Grasset initially undertook what museum sources call **travaux alimentaires**—work accepted for income. One early position was with a manufacturer of furnishing fabrics. He supplied designs or models for textiles, wallpapers, ceramics, tapestry, jewelry, and other manufactured decoration.

These commercial jobs were important training. They taught him to design for repetition, translation into industrial processes, limited color, and collaboration with fabricators. They also established the pattern of his mature career: Grasset usually provided the design or cartoon, while specialist firms executed the final object.

### Japanese and Asian collection

After settling in Paris, Grasset became deeply interested in Japanese prints, illustrated books, Asian objects, and ornamental sources from the Near and Far East. He assembled a private study collection including Japanese prints and other antiquities.

His japonisme was not simply a matter of adopting isolated motifs. He studied:

- asymmetrical placement;
- flat colored areas;
- silhouette;
- the active use of empty space;
- cropped or oblique compositions;
- the transformation of plants and animals into repeatable ornament.

Later Cooper Hewitt scholarship notes that Grasset, like René Lalique, Josef Hoffmann and other European designers, used Japanese **katagami** textile stencils as ornamental source material.

### Charles Gillot

The decisive early Paris relationship was with printer, inventor, and collector **Charles Gillot**. Gillot was the son of Firmin Gillot, associated with the photomechanical zinc-relief process known as gillotage. Charles developed color-printing processes capable of integrating illustration with letterpress production.

Grasset’s access to Gillot’s Japanese collection deepened his study of Japanese graphic art. Gillot also became a patron and collaborator.

### Gillot residence, rue Madame

Beginning around **1877–1880**, Gillot commissioned Grasset to design the decoration and principal furniture for his hôtel particulier on rue Madame. An ébéniste named **Fulgraff** executed at least some pieces under Grasset’s supervision.

The ensemble included a monumental buffet and reportedly a bed, fireplace, and other furnishings. Parts of the dining-room furniture survive at the Musée des Arts décoratifs in Paris. The work fused medieval, Renaissance, Asian, and imaginative zoomorphic forms. It is one of the earliest French attempts to apply a unified artistic conception to an entire modern domestic interior.

### Le Chat Noir

Around **1881–1883**, Grasset participated in decorating **Le Chat Noir**, the Montmartre cabaret associated with Rodolphe Salis and the Symbolist milieu. A design for a lantern is reproduced in the contemporary volume *Eugène Grasset et son œuvre*. The surviving evidence confirms participation but does not support treating him as the sole designer of the establishment.

### *Les Fêtes chrétiennes*

For Abbé Drioux’s *Les Fêtes chrétiennes*, published around **1880**, Grasset drew ornamental letters, page headings, tailpieces, and other typographic decoration. These were republished in 1898 as *Ornements typographiques*. The work established his sustained interest in the page as a unified architectural surface.

### *Le Petit Nab*

René Delorme, writing as Saint-Juirs, produced *Le Petit Nab*, illustrated by Grasset and printed through Gillot’s processes around **1882–1883**. This children’s narrative allowed Grasset to explore imagined antiquity and medievalism.

### *Histoire des Quatre Fils Aymon*

Grasset’s breakthrough was *Histoire des Quatre Fils Aymon, très nobles et très vaillans chevaliers*, published by H. Launette in **1883**, with introduction and notes by Charles Marcilly and printing/engraving by Charles Gillot.

Grasset supplied extensive color compositions integrated with borders, initials, text blocks, and page ornament. The Morgan Library describes the illustrations as color-printed photo-relief or “chromotypogravure.” The project is regularly identified as a landmark in the renewal of French illustrated-book design.

The volume was collaborative:

- anonymous or traditional medieval narrative;
- Charles Marcilly, editor/introduction/notes;
- Eugène Grasset, compositions and page design;
- Charles Gillot, engraving and printing;
- H. Launette, publisher.

It is therefore inaccurate to list Grasset as author of the narrative. His responsibility was visual and ornamental.

## Chronological Life History: Maturity, 1883–1900

### Expansion into posters and periodicals

Grasset became a major contributor to the revival of the illustrated color poster. His first major poster is usually identified as **Les Fêtes de Paris** in **1885**. He subsequently designed advertising and theatrical posters for:

- *Encre L. Marquet*;
- Chocolat Mexicain Masson;
- À la Place Clichy;
- Librairie romantique;
- Théâtre de l’Odéon;
- exhibitions in France and Britain;
- Harper’s Magazine;
- The Century Magazine;
- La Belle Jardinière;
- bicycles and automobiles manufactured by Georges Richard;
- his own Salon des Cent exhibition.

His poster method favored thick contour, controlled color, patterned surface, historical costume, floral framing, and women posed as allegorical or commercial figures.

### *Paris illustré* and *La Parade*

Grasset worked for *Paris illustré* during the 1880s. His color composition **La Parade**, published 1 February 1887, depicts the popular spectacle or sideshow. The Metropolitan Museum of Art has used it in scholarship concerning the visual culture surrounding Georges Seurat’s *Circus Sideshow*.

This does not prove collaboration between Grasset and Seurat, but it demonstrates that Grasset’s mass-produced imagery belonged to the same Parisian visual field.

### Jules Massenet’s *Esclarmonde*

For the 1889 première of Jules Massenet’s opera **Esclarmonde** at the Opéra-Comique, Grasset designed the poster, illustrated opening pages of the score, and prepared designs connected with scenery. BnF records preserve projected décor images attributed to him.

This was an important synthesis of publishing, theatrical publicity, medieval fantasy, and stage design.

### Sarah Bernhardt and *Jeanne d’Arc*

In **1889–1890**, Grasset designed a poster for Sarah Bernhardt in Jules Barbier’s *Jeanne d’Arc* at the Théâtre de la Porte-Saint-Martin.

**Documented dispute:** The Musée des Arts décoratifs records the first state as undiffused. University and museum accounts report that Bernhardt disliked or objected to aspects of the design. In the revised version:

- Jeanne’s head no longer tilts toward the sky but faces the viewer;
- the short, crimped or reddish hair resembling Bernhardt’s was changed to longer brown hair;
- the skirt was closed more modestly.

The composition retained the figure’s expansive posture and surrounding weapons, flames, and sacred-national imagery.

This was a professional disagreement over a commissioned image, not a lawsuit. The poster nevertheless became one of Grasset’s most reproduced works. It also preceded Alphonse Mucha’s celebrated poster relationship with Bernhardt.

### Teaching at the École Guérin

From approximately **1890 to 1903**, Grasset taught decorative composition at the **École Guérin**, also called the École normale d’enseignement du dessin. His teaching addressed geometric construction, stylization of plants and animals, lettering, color, and the relation of decoration to function.

Students associated with his teaching include:

- Paul Berthon;
- Maurice Pillard Verneuil;
- Paul Follot;
- Augusto Giacometti;
- Mathurin Méheut;
- Eliseu Visconti;
- Philippe Wolfers;
- Pierre and Tony Selmersheim;
- Henri Rapin;
- Marcelle Gaudin;
- Juliette Milési;
- Emma and Ada Herwegh;
- Georges Bourgeot;
- Camille Gabriel Schlumberger;
- Jean Gaudin;
- Louise-Marcelle Gillot-Seure;
- Marthe Jeanne Giacomini-Picard.

Not every name is equally well documented in institutional registers. The list partly derives from later biographical compilations and should be checked student by student against school archives.

### French naturalization

Grasset was naturalized French in **1891**. No evidence located suggests that naturalization followed political pressure or the loss of Swiss protection; it accompanied his permanent professional and domestic establishment in France.

### Salon des Cent

In **1894**, the Salon des Cent at the offices of *La Plume*, 31 rue Bonaparte, devoted an exhibition to Grasset. He designed its poster: a flower-bearing woman reading or studying amid an ornamental field.

The BnF records the work as a chromotypographic print produced by the firm G. de Malherbe. A special number of *La Plume* and the later volume *Eugène Grasset et son œuvre* assembled critical appreciations by Camille Lemonnier, Gustave Kahn, Arsène Alexandre, Thiébault-Sisson, Charles Saunier, and others.

These essays are contemporaneous evidence of reputation but are not neutral biography. They frequently employ celebratory language and helped construct Grasset’s image as a “master” of modern decorative art.

### Société nationale des beaux-arts

Grasset exhibited with and became a member of the **Société nationale des beaux-arts**. Later accounts state that he served on juries associated with the Union centrale des arts décoratifs.

His tapestry or stained-glass-related composition **Le Printemps** was presented during the mid-1890s. Contemporary criticism often treated the period around 1894–1900 as the summit of his public career.

### Church of Saint-Étienne, Briare

One of Grasset’s largest permanent commissions was the decorative program for the new **church of Saint-Étienne at Briare**, inaugurated in 1895.

The Département du Loiret identifies its mosaics and stained glass as designed by Grasset. The program included:

- the façade’s angel of Saint Stephen’s martyrdom;
- extensive floral ornament;
- the river of life;
- the four ages of man;
- the five senses;
- the four elements;
- virtues and vices;
- a rose window incorporating zodiac signs around the Creator.

The mosaics were made using the products of the Briare enamel works. **Félix Gaudin** executed stained glass from Grasset’s designs. Some intended motifs were never installed and survive in museum holdings.

The zodiac, senses, elements, and life-cycle imagery made the program unusually encyclopedic. Its symbolism should not automatically be read as proof of occult or esoteric membership; no evidence of such membership was located.

### Légion d’honneur

Grasset was appointed **chevalier de la Légion d’honneur in 1895**. He was promoted to **officier in 1911**. These dates are supported by French authority records, although some compressed accounts mention only the later promotion.

### La Semeuse for Larousse

Grasset created the female “Sower” emblem for Larousse: a woman blowing the seeds of a dandelion under the motto **“Je sème à tout vent.”**

The design is variously dated **1890** or **1895**:

- The Musée des Arts décoratifs chronology gives 1890.
- The 2025 Maison Claude Augé account associates the commission with Claude Augé in 1895.
- Publication history may explain the discrepancy between design, commission, and appearance in Larousse products.

The image became a corporate emblem rather than an independent authored text.

#### “La Semeuse nue” or “la Scandaleuse”

**Reported controversy:** A recent Maison Claude Augé account says that the original Sower’s body was interpreted as nude or as exposing the contour of a breast, provoking complaints because dictionaries circulated among children. Around 1905, a drape or stole was added.

The episode is supported as a piece of Larousse institutional memory. The consulted source does not reproduce the complete contemporaneous press controversy or identify each complainant. It should therefore be described as a reported design controversy, not as proof of a nationwide scandal.

### *La Plante et ses applications ornementales*

Published in **1896**, *La Plante et ses applications ornementales* appeared “under the direction” of Grasset. Plates were produced by Grasset and students or collaborators including **Maurice Pillard Verneuil, Emma Herwegh, and Juliette Milési**.

The book studies plants first observationally and then as sources for ornamental transformation. Subjects include iris, poppy, water lily, columbine, cyclamen, lily of the valley, dandelion, wisteria, lilac, chestnut, thistle, periwinkle, buttercup, wild rose, and chrysanthemum.

Grasset’s introduction rejects servile copying of historic artifacts. It proposes structural necessity and nature as bases for new composition. Because multiple named students contributed plates, the volume should not be described as wholly drawn by Grasset.

### La Belle Jardinière calendars

In **1896**, the Paris department store **La Belle Jardinière** commissioned or used Grasset’s twelve monthly compositions. The BnF identifies them as a suite of twelve color typogravures. Each combines a female figure, seasonal flora, space for calendar information, and advertising.

The series circulated as both commercial ephemera and decorative prints and became one of his best-known bodies of graphic work.

### International reception

By the late 1890s, Grasset’s work circulated outside France:

- American magazines commissioned or reproduced his designs.
- He designed material for *Harper’s* and *The Century*.
- He exhibited decorative prints at **La Libre Esthétique** in Brussels between 1896 and 1898.
- Six lithographs were reportedly shown at the **first Vienna Secession exhibition in 1898**.
- British and American publishers issued or reproduced his ornamental work.

This circulation made him an important transmitter of French decorative modernism before “Art Nouveau” became fixed as a historical label.

## The Grasset Typeface and the Peignot Litigation

### Creation and commercialization

In **1897**, typefounder **Georges Peignot** approached Grasset about translating his distinctive poster lettering into a printing type. Grasset drew an alphabet with a broad pen or calamus. The foundry G. Peignot et Fils developed punches, matrices, and multiple sizes from the drawings.

The alphabet was deposited at the French Ministry of the Interior on **16 October 1897**. The resulting **caractère Grasset** appeared from approximately 1898 and received major publicity at the 1900 Exposition universelle. Related italic and decorative variants followed.

The division of labor was:

- Grasset: design of the alphabet;
- Peignot firm: technical adaptation, cutting, matrices, casting, marketing, and asserted ownership of reproduction rights.

### Earlier-type allegation

**Litigated claim:** Competitors argued that the Grasset type was not wholly original but was a lightly modified copy of types associated with the sixteenth-century Lyon printer **Sébastien Gryphe**.

That was a legal defense and originality claim, not a settled historical conclusion about plagiarism by Grasset. His lettering visibly drew on historical forms, consistent with his entire method of recombining medieval and Renaissance sources.

### Peignot et Fils v. Société Paul Dupont

The *Droit d’auteur* review reproduces the substance of litigation before the Tribunal de commerce de la Seine. Peignot accused the **Société Paul Dupont** of making molds from purchased Grasset type and casting copies for its own printing office.

The defendants argued:

- Peignot lacked exclusive ownership;
- the face derived from older Gryphe types;
- purchasing type conferred a right to reproduce it for internal use;
- no unfair competition occurred because copied type was not sold to third parties.

A prior judgment had reportedly refused to characterize reproduction of Grasset type as infringement. On **11 August 1909**, the Commercial Court distinguished copyright infringement from unfair competition. The litigation continued on appeal and was reported internationally in 1912.

This is the most substantial documented legal controversy connected with Grasset’s work. The parties were principally the foundry and printing company; the evidence located does not show Grasset personally prosecuted, accused of fraud, or required to repay income.

## Exposition Universelle of 1900

Grasset showed work in several media at the **Paris Exposition universelle of 1900**, including jewelry, stained glass, tapestry, chromolithography, and decorative designs.

### Vever jewelry

The jewelers **Henri and Paul Vever** commissioned Grasset to design a line of artistic jewelry. Vever craftsmen translated his gouaches into precious objects using gold, enamel, pearls, ivory, and stones.

The Cooper Hewitt records the **Nymph Brooch** as a Grasset design made by Vever and exhibited in 1900. Musée d’Orsay collections include or document works such as **Apparitions**, a brooch in embossed gold, cloisonné enamel, ivory, and topaz.

Grasset was the designer, not necessarily the bench jeweler. The Vever house supplied technical execution, materials, retail organization, and commercial identity.

### Tapestries and decorative panels

Works associated with 1900 include **La Marseillaise**, **Fête de printemps**, and decorative printed panels such as **Le Parasol**. A later decoration in Lausanne adapted *Fête de printemps*, but that reuse should not be confused with an original installation personally executed by Grasset.

## Later Career, 1900–1917

### Residence at Sceaux

Around **1900**, Grasset and Euphrasie lived at Sceaux in a house formerly occupied by **Dr. Eugène Curie**, father of Pierre Curie. The address was then rue des Sablons; it became rue Pierre-Curie in 1907. Local research gives the historical number as 13 and the present number as 9.

The house appears to have been rented, not owned. Grasset continued to maintain or use his Paris studio at the **Cité fleurie, boulevard Arago**, where he worked among other artists.

### The Cité fleurie network

Artists in or near this studio environment included ceramist-sculptor **Jean Carriès** and Danish sculptor-ceramist **Niels Hansen Jacobsen**. Proximity enabled exchanges among painters, sculptors, ceramicists, and decorative artists.

### The unrealized Porte de Parsifal

The American sewing-machine heiress **Winnaretta Singer**, later Princesse de Polignac, commissioned Jean Carriès to develop a monumental glazed-stoneware **Porte de Parsifal** for a projected room or structure associated with Wagner’s *Parsifal* manuscript.

Carriès sought Grasset’s help with the design. Musée d’Orsay archival material preserves Grasset’s architectural and ornamental studies. The project was abandoned because of technical difficulty, escalating expense, and the patron’s withdrawal.

The origin and intended final location of the commission remain partly uncertain. One account says Singer wanted a room for the *Parsifal* manuscript. The surviving drawings document collaboration but not completion.

This was a significant professional failure in the sense of a major unrealized project. It was not evidence of deception by Grasset.

### Teaching institutions

Grasset’s later teaching sequence is generally given as:

- **École Guérin:** approximately 1890–1903.
- **École d’Art graphique, rue Madame:** approximately 1903–1904.
- **Académie de la Grande Chaumière:** approximately 1904–1913.
- **École Estienne:** from 1905 until his death in 1917.

At École Estienne he taught the **history and drawing of the letter**. A 1916 report by Grasset discussed the establishment or organization of this subject in the school’s program.

### *Méthode de composition ornementale*

Grasset’s principal theoretical work, **Méthode de composition ornementale**, appeared in volumes or installments dated variously **1905–1907**.

It systematized:

- rectilinear elements;
- geometric divisions;
- symmetry and repetition;
- rhythmic construction;
- adaptation of ornament to surfaces and objects;
- the development of plant, animal, and abstract forms.

Modern scholarship treats the work as an outgrowth of his courses. Its conceptual importance lies in moving from historical ornament toward a method of generating form.

### Metalwork and ceramics

Grasset published or directed pattern books including **Ouvrages de ferronnerie moderne** in 1906. He also wrote on ceramic decoration; a 1912 article titled **“La décoration céramique: essais de rénovation dans le décor des céramiques de table”** appeared in *Art et Décoration*.

### Furniture for Marcelle Seure

In **1905**, he designed a walnut dining-room ensemble for **Marcelle Seure**, daughter of Charles Gillot. It reportedly comprised:

- a large table;
- six chairs;
- a buffet;
- a serving table;
- two wall consoles.

The commission demonstrates the continuation of the Gillot family relationship across generations.

### Public standing

By the last decade of his life, Grasset was an established teacher and theorist rather than the leading public face of the newest style. Younger designers had extended or simplified his methods. His work could appear historicizing beside more geometric currents, yet his courses continued to shape decorative education.

### Technologies and inventions

Grasset was a designer working closely with technological innovators, but no personal mechanical patent was found.

Technologies central to his work included:

- gillotage and color photomechanical relief printing;
- chromotypography/chromotypogravure;
- chromolithography;
- industrial typecasting;
- stained-glass manufacture;
- enamel mosaic production;
- cloisonné and painted enamel jewelry;
- repeated textile and wallpaper patterns.

The description of *Quatre Fils Aymon* as “the first book printed by chromotypography” appears in later accounts. The Morgan Library securely describes its photo-relief color printing but does not establish an uncontested universal priority claim. The “first” formulation should therefore be treated as a repeated historical claim, not a conclusively demonstrated world record.

### Finances and property

No comprehensive account books, investment portfolio, bank holdings, or estate valuation were located. The available pattern suggests a diversified professional income from:

- commissions;
- book and magazine illustration;
- poster design;
- teaching salaries;
- licensing or sale of typeface rights;
- designs supplied to manufacturers;
- exhibition and print sales.

The Sceaux house was rented. No major owned real estate has been documented.

His 1904 testament appointed Félix Gaudin executor and directed the later handling of his collections and studio holdings. After his death, Japanese prints, Greek vases, Tanagra figurines, medieval antiquities, and workshop materials were dispersed in four Hôtel Drouot sales in 1918.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Political activity

No evidence found establishes Grasset as a party member, officeholder, revolutionary, intelligence asset, propagandist, or participant in political violence.

He contributed to **Aux victimes de la guerre russo-japonaise** in 1904–1905, a charitable publication involving Anatole France and numerous artists. Participation indicates humanitarian cultural activity but not a complete political program.

### Religious position

Grasset designed extensive Christian imagery, including work for *Les Fêtes chrétiennes* and the church at Briare. No reliable evidence located identifies his personal denomination, degree of observance, conversion, anticlericalism, or membership in an esoteric order.

His religious designs combine Christian iconography with zodiacal, natural, and encyclopedic symbols. Those elements reflect nineteenth-century decorative historicism and Symbolism; they do not by themselves demonstrate occult initiation.

### Artistic doctrine

Grasset’s mature doctrine joined several sources:

1. **Architectural structure:** derived from formal training and Viollet-le-Duc.
2. **Nature:** plants and animals as generative systems, not objects for literal copying.
3. **Medieval integration:** unity of architecture, image, lettering, furniture, and craft.
4. **Japanese design:** flatness, asymmetry, contour, pattern, and active negative space.
5. **Industrial production:** acceptance of reproducible media when design and technique remained coherent.
6. **Arts and Crafts principles:** restoration of artistic intelligence to useful objects.
7. **Symbolism:** allegorical women, dream imagery, medieval fantasy, and emotional states.

### “L’Art nouveau” lecture

On **11 April 1897**, Grasset delivered a lecture titled **“L’Art nouveau”** to the Union centrale des arts décoratifs.

He criticized industries that merely reproduced past styles for commercially conservative buyers. He called for new models grounded in construction, materials, and study of nature. His position was not a rejection of historical study; it was a rejection of unthinking historical reproduction.

### Representations of women

Women dominate Grasset’s posters, calendars, decorative panels, and jewelry. They appear as:

- seasons and months;
- personifications of knowledge, art, or commerce;
- medieval heroines;
- flower-associated figures;
- anxious, jealous, cold, dangerous, morphine-dependent, or violent types.

Modern design scholarship interprets these figures within the contradictory fin-de-siècle construction of women as decorative ideals, consumers, erotic objects, allegories, and symbols of social anxiety. The works do not document Grasset’s private conduct toward women, but they are valid subjects for gender-historical criticism.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### François Bocion

Teacher of drawing in Lausanne and the first named professional artistic mentor.

### Samuel Joseph Grasset

Father and first workshop instructor; introduced the material and craft basis of decorative production.

### Charles Gillot

Patron, friend, printer, collector, and technological collaborator. Gillot’s household commission and printing resources made Grasset’s early breakthrough possible.

### Charles Marcilly

Editor and annotator of *Histoire des Quatre Fils Aymon*; helped translate a traditional narrative into a modern luxury publication.

### G. de Malherbe

Printer and publisher responsible for many posters and chromotypographic works.

### Georges Peignot

Typefounder who converted Grasset’s alphabet into commercially cast type. Their partnership was central to the history of French display typography.

### Félix Gaudin

Master glassmaker who executed Grasset’s stained-glass designs. Grasset named him executor of his 1904 testament, evidence of substantial trust.

### Henri and Paul Vever

Jewelers who executed Grasset’s designs in costly materials and exhibited them at the 1900 Exposition universelle.

### Jean Carriès

Sculptor and ceramicist; studio neighbor and collaborator on the unfinished Porte de Parsifal.

Patron of the Porte de Parsifal project. Her withdrawal terminated the commission.

Commissioning theatrical celebrity. She reportedly rejected aspects of Grasset’s first *Jeanne d’Arc* poster and required revision.

### Maurice Pillard Verneuil, Paul Berthon, Paul Follot, and others

Students who transmitted elements of his decorative method into posters, textiles, interiors, books, and industrial design.

### Critics of *La Plume*

Camille Lemonnier, Gustave Kahn, Arsène Alexandre, Thiébault-Sisson, and others promoted Grasset’s reputation through a special issue and the 1900–1901 book *Eugène Grasset et son œuvre*. Their texts form part criticism, part reputation-making campaign.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Disputed Questions

### Dedicated adversarial-search result

Searches were run in French and English using Grasset’s full name with terms corresponding to controversy, scandal, accusation, lawsuit, fraud, conspiracy, plagiarism, criticism, antisemitism, colonialism, and misogyny, including searches restricted to 2024–2026 material.

No reliable evidence was found of:

- fraud prosecution;
- corruption or money laundering;
- sexual or violent abuse allegation;
- intelligence service connection;
- political extremism;
- imprisonment or exile;
- addiction;
- personal bankruptcy;
- a documented antisemitic campaign;
- a documented colonial administrative role.

This negative finding should not be converted into a claim of exemplary conduct. It means the accessible documentary record and dedicated search did not produce such cases.

### 1. Typeface infringement litigation

- **Status:** Documented litigation involving Grasset’s design rights as acquired by G. Peignot et Fils.
- **Issue:** Société Paul Dupont molded and recast Grasset type acquired from Peignot.
- **Defense allegation:** The design lacked originality and copied Sébastien Gryphe types.
- **Outcome:** A prior judgment had rejected an infringement theory; the 1909 Commercial Court considered unfair competition separately. Appellate reporting continued in 1912.
- **Caution:** This was not a criminal case against Grasset and does not establish that he committed plagiarism.

### 2. Sarah Bernhardt’s rejection of the first *Jeanne d’Arc* poster

- **Status:** Documented or strongly reported commissioning dispute.
- **Issue:** Bernhardt objected to visual details and required a revised state.
- **Evidence:** Museum records identify an undiffused state; academic and museum accounts describe the changes.
- **Reputational effect:** Both versions became evidence of Bernhardt’s management of her public image and of Grasset’s role in modern theatrical advertising.

### 3. “Scandalous” Larousse Sower

- **Status:** Reported historical controversy; underlying full press dossier not located.
- **Story:** The Sower appeared nude or showed the contour of a breast, provoking objections concerning children’s dictionaries.
- **Reported response:** A drape was added around 1905.
- **Reputational effect:** The story survives as part of Larousse institutional folklore and the history of advertising censorship.

### 4. Historical borrowing and originality

Contemporary and later critics repeatedly noted Grasset’s debts to medieval art, Viollet-le-Duc, Japan, Celtic ornament, the Renaissance, and Islamic or Egyptian sources.

- **Documented fact:** Grasset deliberately studied and recombined historical and non-European ornament.
- **Contested interpretation:** Admirers called the synthesis original; critics could see archaeological reconstruction, eclecticism, or dependence on earlier forms.
- **Grasset’s position:** Historical knowledge was necessary, but direct commercial copying was artistically sterile.

### 5. Orientalism and cultural appropriation

- **Status:** Modern interpretive framework, not a documented lifetime accusation.
- **Basis:** Grasset collected and adapted Japanese, Egyptian, Islamic, and other ornamental forms within a European commercial system.
- **Evidence limits:** No source located documents consultation with source communities, claims of cultural ownership, or a colonial governmental role.
- **Historical significance:** His work belongs to the larger nineteenth-century Western appropriation and commodification of Asian and Middle Eastern design.

### 6. Gender criticism

- **Status:** Scholarly interpretation.
- **Basis:** Frequent use of idealized, eroticized, endangered, drug-dependent, jealous, or destructive women, including *La Morphinomane* and *La Vitrioleuse*.
- **Evidence limits:** These images document fin-de-siècle visual types; they are not evidence of crimes or private misogynistic acts by Grasset.

### 7. Date and identity errors

Many reputable and commercial records still give **1841** rather than 1845 and sometimes Paris rather than Sceaux as the place of death. These errors have produced false biographical chains. The strongest authority records support 1845 and Sceaux.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### Myth 1: Grasset “invented Art Nouveau”

- **Story:** Grasset is sometimes presented as the inventor, father, or first complete practitioner of Art Nouveau.
- **Appearance:** Exhibition publicity, popular biographies, auction descriptions, and histories of poster design.
- **Evidence for:** His integrated Gillot interior, *Quatre Fils Aymon*, posters, teaching, and 1897 lecture all preceded or accompanied the naming of the movement.
- **Evidence against literal priority:** Art Nouveau developed through overlapping Belgian, French, British, Swiss, Czech, Austrian, and Catalan networks. No single inventor can be documented.
- **Reputational effect:** The formula elevated Grasset from commercial designer to movement founder.

### Myth 2: *Quatre Fils Aymon* was the first chromotypographic book

- **Story:** It was the first book ever printed using chromotypography.
- **Circulation:** Graphic-design histories and popular reference sites.
- **Evidence for:** Gillot’s unusual photo-relief color process and the unprecedented integration of color ornament and text.
- **Evidence against certainty:** “Chromotypography” was used inconsistently, and universal priority would require comparison with other experimental color-relief publications.
- **Assessment:** Landmark production is documented; absolute world priority remains insufficiently demonstrated.

### Myth 3: Direct pupil of Viollet-le-Duc

- **Story:** Grasset personally studied under Viollet-le-Duc.
- **Evidence:** Strong intellectual dependence on Viollet-le-Duc’s writings.
- **Counterevidence:** No consulted enrollment or correspondence record proves a formal teacher-student relationship.
- **Assessment:** “Disciple” is secure intellectually, uncertain institutionally.

### Myth 4: The artist and publisher Grasset were family

- **Story:** Eugène Grasset was related to Bernard Grasset or the publishing house.
- **Cause:** Shared surname and the publisher’s father also being named Eugène.
- **Evidence against:** The families and professions are distinct.
- **Effect:** Repeated misattribution in general web searches, especially around political controversies concerning Bernard Grasset.

### Myth 5: Occult symbolism at Briare proves esoteric membership

- **Story:** Zodiac, elements, senses, and initiatory movement through the church show that Grasset belonged to an occult or secret society.
- **Evidence for:** The decorative program indisputably contains zodiacal and encyclopedic symbols.
- **Evidence against:** No membership record, correspondence, or contemporaneous testimony located connects him to Freemasonry, Theosophy, Rosicrucianism, or another occult organization.
- **Assessment:** Symbolic content is documented; secret-society conclusions are unverified theory.

### Myth 6: Two deaths

A 2017 Swiss newspaper observed that Grasset effectively “died twice” in the press: the civil date was 23 October 1917, while some Vaudois obituaries gave a date twelve days later.

- **Documented fact:** Civil and authority records support 23 October.
- **Legendary element:** The “second death” is a modern rhetorical description of obituary error, not a claim that he literally survived or was misidentified.

## Death and Estate

Grasset died on **Tuesday, 23 October 1917**, aged seventy-two.

A later Swiss account says his death was sudden and occurred in the street while he was traveling from Sceaux toward his Paris studio. The civil location is Sceaux. No medical certificate or precise physiological cause was located, so terms such as heart attack or stroke should not be supplied without further evidence.

Some Vaudois newspapers printed a death date twelve days later. Civil and national authority documentation supports 23 October.

He was buried at Sceaux:

- the burial register reportedly gives **25 October 1917**;
- the funeral-convoy register reportedly gives **26 October at 3 p.m.**

The concession was for ten years. Local research considers it probable that, after its expiration, the grave was reclaimed and his remains placed in the ossuary for temporary concessions. No surviving individual monument has been securely identified.

### Testament and posthumous sales

A testament dated **1904** named Félix Gaudin executor. Grasset’s collection contained:

- Japanese prints;
- Asian art and illustrated material;
- Greek vases;
- Tanagra figurines;
- medieval antiquities;
- workshop designs and other study objects.

Four sales at Hôtel Drouot in **1918** dispersed the collection and studio estate. The sale itself is documentary evidence of his working sources and collecting habits. A complete financial accounting of the estate was not located.

His widow Euphrasie remained at Sceaux and died in 1926.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Immediate reputation

Obituaries remembered Grasset as a leading decorative artist, poster designer, illustrator, and teacher. The confusion over his death date did not diminish contemporary recognition.

The 1918 dispersal of his collection separated working materials from the artist’s environment. Museums later reconstructed his practice through purchases, donations, archival deposits, and retrospective exhibitions.

### Influence on graphic design

His principal graphic legacy lies in:

- integration of image, border, initial, ornament, and text;
- revival of the poster as an authored artistic surface;
- development of reproducible decorative alphabets;
- use of flat color and heavy contour;
- demonstration that commercial work could receive museum and Salon attention.

### Influence through teaching

Students carried aspects of his method into French, Belgian, Swiss, and Brazilian design. Especially important were Paul Berthon, Maurice Pillard Verneuil, Paul Follot, Mathurin Méheut, Eliseu Visconti, and Philippe Wolfers.

His teaching bridged nineteenth-century historical ornament and twentieth-century systematic design education. Marie-Ève Celio-Scheurer’s research emphasizes that his theory remained rooted in nineteenth-century sources while projecting toward the treatment of “pure form” as an autonomous artistic object.

### Art Nouveau classification

Grasset is now consistently classified as an Art Nouveau pioneer, but his style was more archaeological, heraldic, and structurally medieval than the highly fluid work commonly associated with Alphonse Mucha or Hector Guimard.

His career complicates a simple fine-art/commercial-art division: the same method operated in church mosaics, type, department-store calendars, jewelry, furniture, and book pages.

### Institutional preservation

Important holdings survive at:

- Musée d’Orsay;
- Musée des Arts décoratifs;
- Musée cantonal des beaux-arts de Lausanne;
- BnF;
- INHA;
- Cooper Hewitt;
- Metropolitan Museum of Art;
- MoMA;
- V&A;
- Morgan Library;
- Museum für Gestaltung Zürich.

The Lausanne retrospective **Eugène Grasset: L’Art et l’ornement** in 2011 was central to renewed scholarly attention. The Musée d’Orsay’s continuing cataloguing of his archive and recent presentations of Art Nouveau jewelry have further restored his position beyond poster history.

### Type history

The Grasset face became a characteristic fin-de-siècle display type. Its legal history illustrates the difficulty of protecting type designs under French copyright and design law. The face remains historically influential even when modern revivals alter proportions or spacing.

### Modern reassessment

Recent reassessment concentrates on:

- the collaborative labor hidden behind the single designer’s name;
- the contribution of students to *La Plante*;
- the execution of designs by industrial workers, printers, glassmakers, mosaicists, and jewelers;
- gendered commercial imagery;
- European appropriation of Japanese and other non-European design;
- mistaken dates and inflated “first” claims;
- Grasset’s teaching as a precursor to systematic modern design education.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are included. Mere contemporaneity, residence in Paris, participation in the same broad movement, or appearance in the same later exhibition is excluded.

### Alphonse Mucha

- **Evidentiary label:** Documented institutional association; reported artistic influence.
- **Nature:** Grasset and Mucha are named among the founders or participants of the **Société internationale de l’art populaire**, together with Henri Cazalis/Jean Lahor, René Lalique, Émile Gallé, and Victor Horta. Both were leading Salon des Cent poster artists.
- **Influence:** Modern studies repeatedly describe Grasset as an influence on Mucha’s early decorative and poster language. No consulted document establishes a formal teacher-student relationship.
- **Dates/places:** Paris, principally the 1890s.
- **Caution:** Both designed images for Sarah Bernhardt, but at different moments; this does not itself prove direct collaboration.

### Sarah Bernhardt

- **Evidentiary label:** Documented professional relationship and reported commissioning dispute.
- **Nature:** Bernhardt or the Théâtre de la Porte-Saint-Martin commissioned Grasset’s poster for her 1890 performance in Jules Barbier’s *Jeanne d’Arc*.
- **Dates/places:** Paris, 1889–1890; Théâtre de la Porte-Saint-Martin.
- **Dispute:** She reportedly rejected details of the first state and required a revised face, hair, and costume.

### Paul Berthon

- **Evidentiary label:** Documented teacher-student relationship.
- **Nature:** Berthon studied decorative art with Grasset at the École Guérin. His early work shows strong dependence on Grasset’s contour, floral ornament, and female figures.
- **Dates/places:** Paris, principally the 1890s.
- **Later reception:** Victor Arwas treated them together in *Berthon & Grasset*.

### Luc-Olivier Merson

- **Evidentiary label:** Documented shared educational network; no direct personal collaboration established.
- **Nature:** Paul Berthon studied painting under Merson and decorative art under Grasset. Merson and Grasset also worked within overlapping French decorative-art and stained-glass circles.
- **Assessment:** The shared student is documented; a close Grasset–Merson relationship was not established.

### Auguste Rodin

- **Evidentiary label:** Documented shared institution, not a demonstrated intimate relationship.
- **Nature:** Both were associated with the Société nationale des beaux-arts and its exhibitions. The contemporary *La Plume* publishing world covered both artists.
- **Dates/places:** Paris, 1890s.
- **Assessment:** No correspondence, collaboration, or close friendship was located; the connection should not be overstated.

### Camille Claudel

- **Evidentiary label:** No supported direct connection found.

### Achille-Claude Debussy

- **Evidentiary label:** No supported direct connection found.
- **Note:** Debussy and Grasset occupied overlapping Symbolist Parisian cultural circles, but no documentary crossing was located.

### Erik Satie

- **Evidentiary label:** No supported direct connection found.
- **Note:** Both had associations with Montmartre culture, and Grasset contributed to Le Chat Noir, but the consulted sources do not establish a meeting or collaboration.

### Loïe Fuller

- **Evidentiary label:** No direct connection established.
- **Note:** Grasset’s work and posters relating to Fuller’s theatre appear together in later poster histories and museum collections. No Grasset commission for Fuller was documented in the sources used.

### Élisabeth Sonrel

- **Evidentiary label:** Unverified claimed educational association.
- **Nature of claim:** Some derivative artist biographies list Sonrel among artists influenced by or taught by Grasset.
- **Evidence:** No school register or strong institutional biography confirming formal study was located.
- **Assessment:** Not treated as established.

### William Morris and John Ruskin

- **Evidentiary label:** Reported intellectual affinity; no direct personal contact found.
- **Nature:** Grasset’s effort to reunite design, craft, construction, and useful objects parallels Arts and Crafts principles associated with Morris and Ruskin.
- **Assessment:** Modern scholars place Grasset within this intellectual current, but no meeting or correspondence with either man was found.

### Charles Rennie Mackintosh and Gustav Klimt

- **Evidentiary label:** No documented personal connection found.
- **Note:** Their works appear with Grasset’s in international Art Nouveau histories and exhibitions. This is reception history, not biographical contact.

### Atlas-connection finding

Despite the exceptionally large roster, the securely reconstructable direct connections are concentrated among **Sarah Bernhardt, Paul Berthon, and Alphonse Mucha’s institutional network**. The remaining names are principally parallel figures in the international Art Nouveau, Symbolist, theatrical, or decorative-art world. Absence of a documented crossing is the appropriate finding for most of the roster.

## Chronology

|---|---|
| **25 May 1845** | Born Eugène Samuel Grasset at Lausanne. |
| **1857** | Reportedly enters Lausanne’s École Moyenne. |
| **c. 1859–1863** | Studies drawing with François Bocion. |
| **1861–1863** | Studies architecture at the Zürich Polytechnicum. |
| **1866** | Travels to Egypt; some older sources give a later date. |
| **1869–1870** | Works as painter and sculptor on the Théâtre de Lausanne decoration. |
| **Late 1871** | Settles permanently in Paris. |
| **1870s** | Works for furnishing and decorative manufacturers; studies Japanese art. |
| **c. 1877** | Relationship with Charles Gillot becomes professionally decisive. |
| **1880** | Designs Gillot residence furniture; supplies ornament for *Les Fêtes chrétiennes*. |
| **1881–1883** | Participates in decoration of Le Chat Noir. |
| **1882–1883** | Illustrates *Le Petit Nab*. |
| **1883** | Publication of *Histoire des Quatre Fils Aymon*. |
| **1885** | Designs *Les Fêtes de Paris*, generally identified as his first major poster. |
| **1 February 1887** | *La Parade* appears in *Paris illustré*. |
| **1889** | Designs for Massenet’s *Esclarmonde*; produces *Les douze mois*. |
| **1889–1890** | Creates two states of the *Jeanne d’Arc—Sarah Bernhardt* poster. |
| **c. 1890** | Begins teaching at École Guérin. |
| **1890 or 1895** | Designs Larousse Sower emblem; sources differ between commission/design/publication dates. |
| **1891** | Naturalized French. |
| **1892** | *Encre L. Marquet* poster; work on Porte de Parsifal period. |
| **1893** | *Le Siège d’Orléans* stained-glass project. |
| **April–May 1894** | Major Salon des Cent exhibition and poster. |
| **1895** | Briare church inaugurated with mosaics and stained glass from his designs; appointed chevalier of the Légion d’honneur. |
| **1896** | Publishes/directs *La Plante et ses applications ornementales*; produces *Les Mois* and La Belle Jardinière calendar series. |
| **11 April 1897** | Delivers “L’Art nouveau” lecture. |
| **16 October 1897** | Grasset alphabet deposited for rights purposes. |
| **1897–1898** | Grasset typeface developed and issued by G. Peignot et Fils. |
| **1898** | Exhibits lithographs at the first Vienna Secession; publishes *Ornements typographiques*. |
| **1900** | Major presence at Paris Exposition universelle; Vever jewelry shown; *Apparitions*, *Fête de printemps*, and related work. |
| **c. 1900** | Establishes residence at Sceaux while retaining Paris studio. |
| **1902** | Illustrates Anatole France’s *Le Procurateur de Judée*. |
| **1903–1904** | Teaches at École d’Art graphique, rue Madame. |
| **1904** | Names Félix Gaudin executor in testament; contributes to Russo-Japanese War relief publication. |
| **1904–1913** | Teaches at Académie de la Grande Chaumière. |
| **1905** | Begins teaching at École Estienne; publishes first form of *Méthode de composition ornementale*; designs Marcelle Seure dining room. |
| **1906** | *Ouvrages de ferronnerie moderne*. |
| **11 August 1909** | Seine Commercial Court rules in dispute over recasting of Grasset type. |
| **1911** | Promoted officier of the Légion d’honneur; major Lausanne retrospective scholarship later commemorates this period. |
| **1912** | Publishes article on ceramic decoration; international report discusses typeface litigation. |
| **1916** | Report on teaching the history and drawing of letters at École Estienne. |
| **23 October 1917** | Dies suddenly at Sceaux, aged seventy-two. |
| **25 or 26 October 1917** | Burial/funeral at Sceaux; registers differ by one day. |
| **1918** | Collection and studio dispersed in four Hôtel Drouot sales. |
| **1926** | Death of widow Euphrasie Charlotte Charrotton at Sceaux. |
| **1978–1981** | Major modern books by Arwas, Plantin/Weill, and Murray-Robertson restore scholarly attention. |
| **1998–2001** | Exhibitions emphasize his female imagery and Salon des Cent work. |
| **2011** | Major Lausanne exhibition, *Eugène Grasset: L’Art et l’ornement*. |
| **2017** | Centenary renews Swiss and French attention to his death, collections, and conflicting obituary dates. |
| **2024–2026** | Institutional cataloguing and recent scholarship continue to correct the 1841 birth error and contextualize his archive, jewelry, posters, and decorative programs. |
