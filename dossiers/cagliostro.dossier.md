# Giuseppe Balsamo: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

Giuseppe Balsamo, better known as Count Alessandro di Cagliostro, generated unusually polarized documentation. Three bodies of evidence must be kept separate:

1. **Official and near-contemporaneous records:** baptismal and marriage evidence; police and court papers from London and Paris; proceedings in the Diamond Necklace affair; Masonic certificates and manuscripts; correspondence; the Roman Holy Office prosecution; and administrative reports from San Leo.
2. **Partisan contemporary narratives:** Cagliostro’s legal defenses and autobiographical declarations; Théveneau de Morande’s newspaper campaign; Eliza von der Recke’s hostile exposé; the Holy Office’s published *Compendio*; and accounts by admirers.
3. **Later reconstruction and mythology:** nineteenth- and twentieth-century biographies, occultist apologetics, novels, films, conspiracy theories, and claims that “Cagliostro” and Balsamo were different people.

The identification of Cagliostro with Giuseppe Balsamo rests on converging documentary evidence—not solely on the Holy Office biography—including matching information about his wife, handwriting comparisons made for French authorities, testimony and correspondence from Palermo, the use of the Cagliostro surname within Balsamo’s maternal family, and Johann Wolfgang von Goethe’s 1787 inquiries among Balsamo’s relatives. Some details of the Palermo years and the travels before 1776 nevertheless remain dependent on hostile or retrospective testimony.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Principal birth name:** Giuseppe Balsamo.
- **Baptismal names:** commonly transcribed as Giuseppe Giovanni Battista Vincenzo Pietro Antonio Matteo Balsamo; some secondary transcriptions add *Franco*.
- **French form:** Joseph Balsamo.
- **Best-known assumed identity:** Alessandro, conte di Cagliostro; Alexandre, comte de Cagliostro.
- **Other recorded or reported names and titles:** Count Fenix or Phoenix; Count Harat/Acharat; Marquis Pellegrini; Marquis d’Anna; Marquis Balsamo; Prince of Santa Croce; Tito; Belmonte; Mélissa; Colonel or lieutenant-colonel in alleged Prussian service; “Joseph Cagliostro, colonel of the Third Brandenburg Regiment.”
- **Ritual titles:** *Grand Cophte*, *Gran Cofto* or Grand Cophta of Egyptian Masonry. Lorenza was presented within the rite as Serafina and sometimes as the “Queen of Sheba.”
- **Birth:** 2 June 1743, Palermo, Kingdom of Sicily. Baptized 8 June 1743.
- **Birthplace within Palermo:** the Albergheria/Ballarò district, traditionally Via della Perciata, later called Vicolo Conte di Cagliostro.
- **Death:** 26 August 1795, fortress of San Leo, then in the Papal States.
- **Reported cause:** an apoplectic or cerebral attack, according to the San Leo administrative tradition and later biographical synthesis. The surviving evidence does not permit a modern medical diagnosis.
- **Burial:** reportedly in unconsecrated ground near San Leo. No securely identified remains survive.
- **Primary occupations and activities:** itinerant adventurer; draftsman and picture restorer or falsifier; seller and compounder of medicines; unlicensed healer; occultist; alchemist; diviner; organizer of a Masonic-derived Egyptian Rite; lecturer and ritual leader.
- **Legal status of the title:** no evidence establishes that he possessed the hereditary title Count of Cagliostro. His marriage record reportedly names Giuseppe Balsamo without a noble title.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and siblings

**Documented or strongly supported:**

- His father was **Pietro Balsamo**, a Palermo trader, usually described as a cloth, ribbon, or small-goods merchant. Pietro reportedly became bankrupt and died at approximately forty-four or forty-five, shortly after Giuseppe’s birth.
- His mother was **Felicita or Felice Bracconieri**. She survived into 1787, when Goethe encountered the family in Palermo.
- Giuseppe had an elder sister, generally identified as **Maria or Giovanna Maria Balsamo**, who married **Giovanni Battista Capitummino** and had children. Different secondary accounts vary over her complete baptismal name.

The family lived modestly. Assertions that both parents belonged to impoverished nobility occur in apologetic literature but are not demonstrated by the surviving evidence summarized by Carlo Francovich in the *Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani*.

### Maternal ancestry and the name Cagliostro

The genealogy reconstructed for French investigators and later examined by Goethe traced the maternal line to **Matteo Martello**, whose daughters were said to include:

- Maria, who married Giuseppe Bracconieri; and
- Vincenza, who married a **Giuseppe Cagliostro** of Messina, reportedly an administrator or steward connected with the prince of Villafranca.

Felicita Bracconieri was descended from the first branch. The Cagliostro relative therefore belonged to the extended maternal family and provides a plausible source for Balsamo’s later surname.

The precise kinship terminology varies across accounts: Giuseppe Cagliostro is called a great-uncle, an uncle by marriage, or the husband of Balsamo’s maternal great-aunt. The underlying point—that the surname existed in his maternal network before he adopted it—is much better supported than claims that he invented it from nothing.

### Claims of Jewish, Moorish, Greek, Portuguese, or noble ancestry

- **Unverified historical claim:** some nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century writers described Pietro Balsamo or his ancestry as Jewish, Moorish, or Levantine. No cited baptismal, conversion, or communal record establishes this.
- **Inference rather than documentation:** the Greek derivation sometimes assigned to the surname Balsamo and the ethnically mixed history of the Albergheria do not prove Greek ancestry.
- **Self-created mythology:** Cagliostro implied or asserted noble, eastern, Maltese, or cosmopolitan origins and sometimes suggested descent associated with Charles Martel. These stories conflict with the Palermo documentation.
- **Modern revisionist theory:** Raffaele and Tommaso De Chirico and some occultist writers argue that Cagliostro was a Portuguese or noble traveler distinct from Giuseppe Balsamo. No comparably strong independent documentary chain has displaced the identification accepted by most historians.

### Marriage, spouse, and children

On **21 April 1768**, at San Salvatore in Campo, Rome, Giuseppe Balsamo married **Lorenza Feliciani**, born in Rome on 8 April 1751, daughter of the bronze-founder or metalworker Giuseppe Feliciani. She was about seventeen, although older literature frequently calls her fourteen. The surviving marriage information identifies the groom as Giuseppe Balsamo, son of Pietro, from Palermo.

Lorenza was illiterate or possessed very limited literacy. Under her husband’s later identity she became **Serafina Feliciani**, Countess Cagliostro. She participated in his travels, social presentation, and Masonic enterprises, presiding over women’s Egyptian Rite assemblies.

The relationship was unstable:

- Paris police material from 1773 concerns Balsamo’s complaint after Lorenza left him for an attorney named Duplessis and her subsequent confinement in Sainte-Pélagie.
- Hostile sources accused Balsamo of coercing her into sexual relationships or prostitution to obtain money and patronage.
- Lorenza periodically accused him of violence, exploitation, irreligion, and Masonic activity, then withdrew or contradicted some declarations.
- In London in 1786–87, Morande obtained statements from her and encouraged separation. At Bienne she formally retracted at least part of this material and rejoined her husband.
- In Rome in 1789, amid renewed marital conflict and pressure from her family and clergy, her disclosures contributed to the Holy Office investigation.

No securely documented children of the marriage are known.

After Cagliostro’s conviction, Lorenza was formally acquitted but confined as a disciplinary measure in the convent of Sant’Apollonia in Trastevere. A report that she died there in 1794 is frequently repeated but not firmly established. Her ultimate fate remains uncertain.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1743–1768

### Palermo and schooling

After Pietro Balsamo’s death, Felicita relied on her brothers **Matteo** and **Antonio Bracconieri**. Matteo was reportedly a senior clerk in the Palermo post office, while Antonio worked as a bookkeeper. Giuseppe attended the institution or seminary of **San Rocco**, commonly described as an establishment for poor or orphaned boys under Piarist influence.

He reportedly ran away repeatedly. In **1756**, aged thirteen, he was sent as a novice or lay trainee to the **Fatebenefratelli**, the Brothers Hospitallers of Saint John of God, at Caltagirone. The house served the sick, and Balsamo was placed with the convent apothecary.

This is the most plausible foundation for his later familiarity with herbs, pharmaceutical preparations, elementary chemistry, and bedside practice. No evidence shows that he received a medical degree or formal license.

### Expulsion or flight from Caltagirone

The Holy Office *Compendio* says he behaved irreverently and was expelled. The famous story has him replace saints’ names with those of Palermo prostitutes while reading aloud in the refectory. It is vivid but comes through prosecutorial and literary retellings; it should be classified as a **hostile anecdote, possibly based on institutional memory but not independently verified**.

Whether he was formally expelled or escaped is uncertain. He returned to Palermo.

### Alleged Palermo offenses

The *Compendio* and later biographies accuse the young Balsamo of:

- forging theater tickets;
- altering or forging documents, including a will;
- theft;
- street violence;
- procuring;
- falsifying drawings and antiquities;
- magical treasure-hunting schemes.

The best-known episode concerns a goldsmith or jeweler named **Marano**. According to the hostile narrative, Balsamo persuaded Marano that buried treasure could be obtained through magic, collected money or valuables for the operation, and arranged for accomplices disguised as spirits or demons to beat the victim while Balsamo escaped.

This is a **reported accusation**, not a surviving conviction. Later accounts give incompatible sums and embellishments. It may preserve the memory of a real fraud complaint, but its theatrical details cannot be treated as proven.

Balsamo apparently attracted police attention and may have experienced short detentions. No complete sequence of Palermo judgments has been established.

### The undocumented interval

Between 1756 and his securely evidenced presence in Rome in 1768, the record is poor. Two incompatible traditions dominate:

- **Prosecutorial version:** he remained a petty criminal, forger, treasure hunter, and fugitive.
- **Cagliostro’s version:** he was raised or instructed by the mysterious **Althotas**, traveled through Arabia, Mecca, Medina, Egypt and Rhodes, learned languages, botany, medicine and sacred wisdom, and assisted Grand Master Manuel Pinto da Fonseca with alchemy in Malta.

Malta is geographically and historically plausible, and Balsamo may have acquired practical pharmaceutical knowledge there. Nevertheless, no secure Order of Malta record cited in the consulted sources proves his service as a *donato*, initiation by Pinto, or the existence of Althotas. Althotas belongs at present to **self-authored mythology or an unresolved possibility**, not established biography.

## Marriage and Early Wanderings, 1768–1776

### Rome, 1768

In Rome, Balsamo supported himself through drawing, copying, touching up, or selling pictures and designs. Hostile sources accuse him of passing altered images as originals and forging documents. He lodged in modest quarters and married Lorenza Feliciani.

He associated with a self-styled **Count Agliata**, described as a professional impostor and forger. The three allegedly traveled together until Agliata disappeared with money or property. The precise chronology and the claim of a sexual ménage à trois come chiefly from hostile accounts.

### Iberian Peninsula and first London stay

The couple’s route is reconstructed, with uneven certainty, as:

- Bergamo;
- Barcelona;
- Madrid;
- Lisbon;
- London, arriving in or about summer 1771.

Accounts accuse the couple of living from drawings, forged papers, fortune-telling, sexual patronage, and schemes involving lottery numbers or alchemy. Because the published Roman biography sought to demonstrate a continuous criminal disposition, each allegation requires separate caution.

In London Balsamo became involved in debts and litigation and spent time in a debtors’ prison. He reportedly worked as a decorative artist. Claims that he repeatedly duped gamblers by predicting lottery numbers are plausible in context but survive largely through opponents’ narratives and later testimony.

### Paris, 1772–1773

The couple went to Paris late in 1772. Lorenza left Balsamo for **Duplessis**, an attorney or lawyer. Balsamo appealed to the authorities. Police arrested Lorenza, and in early 1773 she was confined at Sainte-Pélagie, an institution used for women accused of sexual misconduct.

The surviving administrative references are important evidence linking the earlier Balsamo couple to the later Count and Countess Cagliostro:

- both wives were Roman-born Felicianis;
- both were described as unable to write;
- the husband’s signatures were judged comparable;
- the same marital history reappeared in later testimony.

They reconciled and traveled through the Low Countries and German territories.

### Return to Italy and renewed travel, 1774–1776

The couple returned to Palermo and Naples around 1775. Lorenza’s brother accompanied them for part of their travels.

Balsamo, now styling himself **Marquis Pellegrini**, obtained the patronage of a wealthy Sicilian aristocrat apparently attracted to Lorenza. When Marano renewed his complaint, the patron reportedly intimidated Marano’s lawyer and secured Balsamo’s release. Goethe later recorded the story from Palermo informants. The intervention is a **reported event based on local testimony**, while the patron’s precise identity varies in the literature.

At Marseille, Balsamo began appearing more distinctly as a healer and occult adept. One story says he promised to restore an elderly man’s sexual vigor through prolonged ritual treatment and fled when the result failed. This is **hostile reportage**, not a documented medical proceeding.

Further reported movements include Spain, Venice, Alicante and Cádiz. By 1776 Lorenza’s brother had separated from the couple, allegedly after cheating them.

---

## London Transformation and Freemasonry, 1776–1777

During his second London period, Balsamo adopted the identity under which he became famous: **Count Alessandro di Cagliostro**. Lorenza became **Serafina**.

### Litigation and financial schemes

Cagliostro was involved with gamblers, moneylenders, attorneys and persons hoping to obtain winning lottery numbers. The complicated case involving **Miss Fry**, her associates, jewelry, money, arbitration, alleged deception, and competing accusations ended with Cagliostro himself imprisoned for debt in the King’s Bench prison.

The record indicates a mixed picture:

- he made extraordinary claims about occult or numerical knowledge;
- people gave him money or valuables;
- he was also exploited by associates and by the highly predatory civil-debt system;
- later pamphleteers simplified the cases into a one-directional story of fraud.

There is no basis for describing every London accusation as a criminal conviction.

### Masonic initiation

On **12 April 1777**, Cagliostro and Lorenza were initiated or received at the **Lodge of Esperance**, meeting at the King’s Head, Gerard Street, Soho. The lodge served a largely French-speaking, foreign and artisanal membership. Cagliostro reportedly entered as “Joseph Cagliostro, Colonel of the Third Brandenburg Regiment.”

A rediscovered Grand Lodge certificate strengthens the documentary basis for the 1777 initiation. The supposed Prussian military rank remains unsubstantiated.

Freemasonry supplied:

- an international network;
- institutional forms and titles;
- access to noble and bourgeois patrons;
- a language of initiation and universal fraternity;
- a structure Cagliostro could transform into his own system.

He left England late in 1777, reportedly with only fifty guineas.

## Northern and Central European Ascendancy, 1777–1780

### Route and documentary uncertainty

Cagliostro later named Brussels as his next major stop. His route probably included some combination of Brussels, The Hague, Nuremberg, Leipzig, Berlin, Königsberg, Mitau in Courland, Saint Petersburg and Warsaw. Exact arrival and departure dates often come from memoirs written by adherents or opponents.

### The Hague and German territories

Masonic lodges received him with growing ceremony. His claims now combined:

- alchemy and transmutation;
- clairvoyance;
- spirit or angelic communication;
- medical cures;
- knowledge of concealed events;
- moral and physical regeneration;
- an “Egyptian” source of wisdom.

He often used children—described in the ritual vocabulary as “doves” or “pupils”—as clairvoyant mediums in ceremonies involving a globe, carafe, water, mirrors, or other reflective surfaces.

### Mitau, Courland, 1779

At Mitau he entered the circle of the Medem family and the Courland court. **Elisa von der Recke**, initially impressed, later concluded that his séances and revelations were fraudulent. Her *Nachricht von des berüchtigten Cagliostro Aufenthalt in Mitau* became one of the most influential contemporary exposés.

Von der Recke alleged:

- concealed observation and prior intelligence;
- manipulation of children used as mediums;
- failed treasure or spirit operations;
- false claims of supernatural authority;
- efforts to gain power over patrons.

Her book is a **named eyewitness accusation by a disillusioned participant**. It is highly important but not disinterested: it was written to expose someone she believed had deceived her family.

### Saint Petersburg, 1779–1780

Cagliostro attempted to establish himself as healer and wonder-worker in Russia. Stories include a supposed revival or cure of a gravely ill child and claims that a substituted child was returned to its parents. Catherine II’s court did not grant him lasting favor, and hostile satire followed his visit.

No evidence establishes that he literally raised a dead child. The event belongs to **contemporary miracle claim and hostile counter-allegation**.

### Warsaw, May–June 1780

In Warsaw he was hosted by aristocrats interested in alchemy, including **Prince Adam Poniński** and **August Fryderyk Moszyński**. He offered alchemical demonstrations and promoted Egyptian Masonry.

The anonymous or pseudonymous eyewitness diary published as *Cagliostro in Warschau* describes investigators secretly substituting or marking materials and thereby exposing the manipulation of a metal-transmutation experiment. Cagliostro left Warsaw abruptly on or about **26 June 1780**.

The Warsaw exposure is a **contemporaneous reported experiment**, stronger than later generic charges but still transmitted through a hostile publication.

## Strasbourg, Switzerland, Bordeaux and Lyon, 1780–1785

### Strasbourg, 1780–1783

Cagliostro arrived in Strasbourg on **19 September 1780** according to one detailed chronology; other accounts place the beginning of his residence in January 1781. He achieved his greatest reputation there as a charitable healer.

He treated crowds, frequently without charging fees, distributed medicines and alms, and received both testimonials and opposition. His preparations included:

- “refreshing powders,” apparently herbal;
- “Egyptian wine,” described as stimulating or aphrodisiac;
- digestive or purgative compounds;
- an “elixir of long life.”

Some patients reportedly improved. This does not establish miraculous powers: spontaneous recovery, herbal effects, suggestion, selective reporting, and ordinary bedside care remain sufficient possibilities. He was attacked by licensed physicians for practicing without recognized qualifications.

### Cardinal de Rohan and elite patronage

At Strasbourg he formed his decisive relationship with **Louis René Édouard de Rohan**, cardinal and prince-bishop of Strasbourg. Rohan became admirer, patient, patron, and intimate associate. Cagliostro performed experiments and consultations in Rohan’s circle and gained access to aristocratic networks.

Other associates included:

- the Swiss banker and manufacturer **Jacques or Jean-Jacques Sarasin/Sarrasin** and his family;
- the physiognomist and pastor **Johann Kaspar Lavater**;
- Count Lamberg;
- various military officers, nobles, merchants, and Masons.

Lavater asked the basis of his medicine; Cagliostro’s famous answer was “in words, herbs, and stones.” This is a reported contemporary saying.

### Finances and household

His finances were deliberately opaque. He declared that Providence supplied what he needed. Reconstructable sources included:

- aristocratic hospitality;
- gifts;
- donations;
- fees or indirect rewards;
- support from patients;
- Masonic subscriptions;
- money entrusted for ritual or alchemical work;
- Lorenza’s social relationships.

He sometimes treated the poor free of charge and spent conspicuously on clothing, servants, carriages, jewelry, lodgings and ceremonial spaces. No complete accounts, balance sheets, stable landed estate, bank portfolio, or inheritable business organization are known.

### Bordeaux and Lyon

After movements through Switzerland and a possible return toward Naples, the couple lived in **Bordeaux from November 1783 to October 1784**, hosted in part by the Marquis de Canolle. An initial Egyptian Rite organization was attempted there.

They moved to **Lyon**, then a major center of mystical and high-degree Masonry. On **24 December 1784**, Cagliostro established the mother lodge **La Sagesse Triomphante**.

The surviving manuscript ritual, now catalogued as Lyon municipal manuscript Ms 6871, was copied by the lodge’s venerable officer Saincostard during Cagliostro’s residence. It is material evidence that the rite was an organized system, not merely a later legend.

### Egyptian Masonry

Cagliostro’s system retained the ordinary three-degree framework while promising purification or regeneration. Its features included:

- admission of both men and women through parallel structures;
- invocations, prayer, fasting and moral preparation;
- angelic or spiritual operations using young mediums;
- alchemical and cabalistic imagery;
- a forty-day “moral regeneration” and a forty-day “physical regeneration” in some ritual texts;
- Cagliostro as Grand Cophte;
- Serafina as presiding female dignitary.

The ritual’s originality is disputed. It drew on existing Freemasonry, Rosicrucianism, ceremonial magic, high-degree systems, biblical symbolism and contemporary fascination with Egypt. Assertions that Cagliostro found the entire system in an English bookseller’s manuscript come from later testimony and cannot be verified.

## Paris and the Diamond Necklace Affair, 1785–1786

### Arrival and public prominence

Cagliostro reached Paris on **30 January 1785**. His residence became a fashionable gathering place for nobles, clerics, military officers, Masons and seekers of cures or occult knowledge.

The **Philalèthes**, an important Masonic research circle, invited him to participate in its convention. He demanded recognition of the superiority of his Egyptian Rite and acts of submission the organizers would not accept. The negotiations collapsed and damaged his standing with some established Masons.

In August 1785, he inaugurated or promoted the mixed-gender Egyptian lodge **Isis**. Serafina achieved substantial independent celebrity.

### Structure of the necklace fraud

The central fraud was organized by **Jeanne de Valois-Saint-Rémy, comtesse de La Motte**, with accomplices including her husband and Rétaux de Villette. She persuaded Cardinal de Rohan that she could reconcile him with Marie Antoinette and that the queen secretly wished him to arrange the purchase of an enormous diamond necklace from Boehmer and Bassenge. Forged letters and a nocturnal impersonation of the queen supported the deception. The necklace was delivered to La Motte’s circle, dismantled, and sold.

### Cagliostro’s role

Jeanne de La Motte accused Cagliostro of involvement or direction. Because he was Rohan’s intimate, police arrested him and Serafina in August 1785. Cagliostro was sent to the Bastille; Serafina was detained separately and released earlier.

**Court finding:** the Parlement of Paris acquitted Cagliostro on **31 May 1786**. No evidence presented at trial established that he planned the fraud, received diamonds, forged the queen’s letters, or profited from the necklace. The strongest reconstructable conclusion is that he was imprisoned because of association with Rohan and La Motte’s accusation, not proven participation.

Cagliostro’s advice to Rohan after discovery—reportedly that he should disclose everything to the king—was inconsistent with being the scheme’s organizer.

His advocate was **Jean-Charles Thilorier**. The published *Mémoire pour le comte de Cagliostro, accusé, contre M. le procureur général, accusateur* presented both a legal defense and Cagliostro’s mythic account of his origins.

### Release and expulsion

A large crowd celebrated his release. Images, medallions, fans, prints and busts circulated, some bearing *Divo Cagliostro*. His acquittal became an opposition symbol against arbitrary imprisonment and the monarchy’s use of *lettres de cachet*.

Despite acquittal, royal authority ordered him to leave France. He departed on **21 June 1786** for England. This distinction is essential:

- **Documented fact:** acquitted by the Parlement.
- **Documented administrative act:** expelled from France.
- **Unsupported allegation:** mastermind or beneficiary of the necklace fraud.

## London Polemic, Exposure and Decline, 1786–1787

From London, Cagliostro published or authorized the **Letter to the French People**, dated 20 June 1786. It denounced Baron de Breteuil, arbitrary detention and the Bastille, anticipated the destruction of the Bastille and abolition of *lettres de cachet*, and referred to the summoning of representative institutions.

Some editions bear 1789 publication dates, although the text circulated earlier. Whether the famous “prophecy” was preserved exactly in its original 1786 form requires bibliographical caution.

The French government’s interests aligned with the campaign conducted by journalist and blackmailer **Charles Théveneau de Morande** in the *Courrier de l’Europe*. Morande investigated and publicized the claim that Cagliostro was Giuseppe Balsamo.

### Evidence for the identification

The investigative chain included:

- an anonymous 1786 letter from Palermo;
- information from Balsamo’s uncle Antonio Bracconieri;
- evidence that Balsamo had written under the Cagliostro name;
- the shared Feliciani identity and biography of the wife;
- Paris police records from 1773;
- expert comparison of Balsamo and Cagliostro signatures;
- information about the maternal Cagliostro relatives.

Morande was a compromised operator who used threats, paid informants and sensational journalism. His character does not invalidate the independently corroborated records, but it makes his unsupported accusations unsafe.

### Separation crisis and alchemical exposure

Morande and his associates encouraged Lorenza to separate from her husband and obtained incriminating statements. Cagliostro later brought her to Bienne, where she retracted claims before a notary.

An oft-repeated story says an alchemical student secretly substituted tobacco for the metal Cagliostro was supposed to transmute and that the operation nevertheless “succeeded,” exposing a prepared trick. The episode is a **reported hostile anecdote**, not a judicial finding.

Members of La Sagesse Triomphante also complained that lodge funds were being used for Cagliostro’s personal expenses. This is a **contemporary financial accusation**; no complete audit or court judgment is known.

---

## Switzerland, Savoy and Italy, 1787–1789

Cagliostro sought refuge with associates including Lavater and Sarasin. He and Lorenza then moved through:

- Bienne and Basel;
- Aix-les-Bains in Savoy;
- Turin;
- Genoa;
- Rovereto;
- Trento.

Authorities in several jurisdictions monitored or excluded him. He alternated healing, alchemical instruction, Masonic organization, and appeals to patrons.

At Rovereto, writer **Clementino Vannetti** observed him and composed the satirical Latin *Liber memorialis de Caleostro cum esset Roboreti*, later called the “Gospel of Cagliostro.” Vannetti described a heavyset, agile man with dark hair, brilliant deep-set eyes, animated gestures, and a persuasive voice.

At Trento in autumn 1788, Cagliostro obtained the favor of Prince-Bishop **Pietro Vigilio Thun**, who helped him seek safe conduct for Rome. Whether Thun’s assistance resulted from personal conviction, medical gratitude, or a Masonic link is unresolved.

---

## Rome, Arrest and Holy Office Proceedings, 1789–1791

### Return to Rome

Cagliostro and Lorenza arrived in Rome in **May 1789**. The decision was hazardous:

- Papal constitutions of 1738 and 1751 condemned Freemasonry.
- News of the French Revolution heightened fear of secret societies.
- Cagliostro’s anti-Bastille declarations made him politically suspect.
- His finances and reputation were declining.
- Lorenza wanted contact with her family.

He approached the Roman lodge **Amici Sinceri**, associated with the French painter **Augustin Belle**, but established Masons reportedly distrusted him.

Patrons or contacts included:

- Cardinal François-Joachim de Pierre de Bernis, French ambassador;
- Princess Lambertini;
- a knight or bailiff de Loras of the Order of Malta;
- Marquis Vivaldi;
- Father Francesco Giuseppe da San Maurizio, born Giacinto Antonio Roulier, a Swiss Capuchin.

On **16 September 1789**, at Villa Malta, Cagliostro presented Egyptian Masonry to a gathering of clerics and nobles. Few formally joined.

He also petitioned the French National Assembly for permission to return to France and offered his services to the new order. No effective response is recorded.

### Surveillance and denunciations

Lorenza, her relatives, clerics, informants, and associates supplied information. She accused Cagliostro of Masonic activity, magical practices, irreligion, coercion and abuse. Although she reportedly refused at one point to sign a denunciation, it reached the authorities alongside statements from her father and the informer Carlo Antonini.

The Holy Office was already investigating Roman Freemasonry. Lorenza’s evidence was therefore a catalyst, not the sole cause.

### Arrest

On **27 December 1789**, papal authorities arrested Cagliostro and searched associated premises. He was imprisoned in Castel Sant’Angelo. Lorenza was confined at Sant’Apollonia; Father Francesco Giuseppe was sent to the Ara Coeli prison.

The commission included Cardinal Francesco Saverio de Zelada and Cardinal Leonardo Antonelli. Monsignor **Giuseppe Barberi**, fiscal official and secretary of the judging congregation, later compiled the published *Compendio*.

The first formal interrogation cited by Francovich took place on **8 May 1790**.

### Charges and evidentiary character

The prosecution treated him as:

- a Freemason;
- founder of an illicit Masonic sect;
- heretic or formal apostate;
- practitioner of magic and superstition;
- author or promoter of blasphemous ritual;
- political subversive linked to the Illuminati;
- impostor and habitual fraudster.

The first four categories correspond to acts or doctrines the papal legal system criminalized. The allegations of an organized revolutionary conspiracy were much weaker.

During interrogation, Cagliostro reportedly claimed contact with or agency for the **Bavarian Illuminati**, describing a blood oath and a conspiracy against monarchies and Rome. Historians including Francovich regard the story as false or produced under pressure. A letter of **Friedrich Münter**, dated 26 January 1790, warned Cardinal Stefano Borgia that legitimate lodges considered Cagliostro a dangerous impostor while also criticizing the Holy See for prosecuting Freemasons generally.

Thus:

- **Documented fact:** Cagliostro organized Egyptian Rite Masonry.
- **Court finding under papal law:** heresy, magic and Freemasonry.
- **Unproven confession or claim:** formal Illuminati agency.
- **Unsupported conspiracy narrative:** command of an international revolutionary treasury or plot.

### Defense and sentence

Court-appointed ecclesiastical advocates defended him. He alternated denials, admissions, recantations and demonstrations of Catholic repentance.

On **7 April 1791**, judgment declared that he had incurred the penalties imposed on formal heretics, magicians and Freemasons. The prescribed exemplary death sentence was commuted by **Pope Pius VI** to perpetual imprisonment without hope of pardon.

Father Francesco Giuseppe received ten years. Lorenza was acquitted but administratively confined.

On **4 May 1791**, books, ritual objects and Masonic instruments seized from Cagliostro were publicly burned in Piazza della Minerva.

The trial was legally real under the Papal States’ law. It was also structured as a public prosecution of Freemasonry during a period of revolutionary panic. Barberi’s *Compendio* is therefore both a source extracted from proceedings and an official justification of the verdict.

## Imprisonment and Death at San Leo, 1791–1795

Cagliostro entered the fortress of **San Leo on 21 April 1791**. He was first held under extraordinary security in a cell commonly identified as the *Tesoretto*. Later tradition associates him with the even harsher *Pozzetto*, entered from above and deprived of normal light, movement and sanitation. Accounts sometimes confuse the sequence or architecture of the cells.

The governor sent reports to the papal legation at Urbino and the Secretariat of State. These papers, preserved in the Biblioteca Oliveriana at Pesaro, are the principal documentary foundation for his last years.

Reported conditions included:

- isolation;
- continuous surveillance;
- restricted communication;
- poor light and ventilation;
- deliberate prevention of escape;
- recurrent physical restraint or beatings;
- deterioration in mental and physical health.

Francovich’s synthesis states that he exhibited progressive mental disturbance and was periodically beaten. Some older accounts dramatize his screams being heard in the town below. The administrative correspondence should be preferred over later picturesque narration.

Cagliostro died on **26 August 1795**, probably following an apoplectic attack. The death entry portrayed him as impenitent. He was reportedly buried without consecrated rites.

His exact grave was lost. The claim that he died in 1791 is erroneous; the best-supported date is 1795.

## Education, Knowledge and Techniques

Cagliostro had no documented university education. His knowledge was heterogeneous:

- rudimentary pharmacy and materia medica, probably learned at Caltagirone and through practice;
- practical preparation of herbal powders, wines, purgatives and tonics;
- drawing, copying, design and possibly engraving;
- enough reading ability to use Masonic, biblical, magical and alchemical material;
- theatrical presentation and improvisation;
- multilingual conversational competence acquired through travel, though his writing was often assisted;
- techniques of suggestion, intelligence gathering, cold reading and ceremonial staging.

Claims that he practiced modern hypnotism are anachronistic unless used descriptively. He operated during the rise of **Franz Anton Mesmer’s** animal magnetism, and contemporaries compared their effects, but direct borrowing or a meeting has not been securely established.

His polished French publications were almost certainly prepared with legal or literary assistance. Even the Holy Office biography conceded that the ideas of the Egyptian ritual may have been his while its elegant French exceeded his unaided writing ability.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects and Financial Interests

Cagliostro did not create a modern company and possessed no known patents.

His principal institutional project was the **High Egyptian Rite of Freemasonry**, with proposed or functioning circles in the Netherlands, German states, Courland, Poland, Strasbourg, Bordeaux, Lyon, Paris, Switzerland, northern Italy and Rome.

Important organizations and institutions included:

- San Rocco institution, Palermo;
- Fatebenefratelli at Caltagirone;
- Lodge of Esperance, London;
- Masonic and occult salons in Mitau, Warsaw and Strasbourg;
- La Sagesse Triomphante, Lyon;
- Isis lodge, Paris;
- proposed Egyptian Rite group at Villa Malta, Rome;
- the Philalèthes, with whom negotiations failed;
- the Parlement of Paris;
- the Bastille;
- Roman Holy Office;
- Castel Sant’Angelo;
- fortress of San Leo.

### Medicines and “technologies”

No patented invention is documented. His operational repertoire included:

- herbal preparations and compound remedies;
- elixirs and stimulant wines;
- ritual mirrors, globes and carafes;
- child clairvoyants;
- alchemical furnaces and crucibles;
- coded passwords, seals, lodge certificates and initiation scripts;
- ceremonial environments intended to induce expectation and altered attention.

Claims of transmutation, resurrection, physical regeneration, clairvoyance and extreme longevity were never demonstrated under reliable controls.

### Wealth and financial structure

No trustworthy total for his fortune exists. He alternated between debt, imprisonment, patron-supported luxury and conspicuous generosity.

His economic model appears to have combined:

- gifts from wealthy admirers;
- lodging and hospitality;
- medical or occult gratuities;
- Masonic dues and contributions;
- entrusted funds;
- jewelry or valuables;
- occasional artistic work;
- Lorenza’s role in attracting patrons;
- lottery and alchemical ventures.

He publicly claimed to give treatment to the poor without payment. Both charity and financial manipulation can be documented or credibly reported; neither cancels the other.

## Political, Religious and Intellectual Development

### Religion

Born and baptized Catholic, Balsamo entered a Catholic charitable order as a youth. His mature system mixed Catholic prayer, biblical symbolism, esoteric Christianity, ceremonial magic, alchemy, Kabbalah-like motifs and Masonic forms.

He did not present a systematic atheistic philosophy. The Holy Office nevertheless found ritual propositions heretical, superstitious and blasphemous.

### Politics

Before 1786, his politics were secondary to healing and initiation. The Bastille imprisonment radicalized his public rhetoric. His *Letter to the French People* attacked arbitrary royal authority and made predictions later interpreted as revolutionary.

He was neither shown to be an operational leader of the French Revolution nor a proven Bavarian Illuminatus. His effort in 1789 to attach himself to the National Assembly shows political opportunism or genuine sympathy with the new order, but the petition went unanswered.

### Intellectual and artistic significance

Cagliostro occupied the border between Enlightenment experimental culture and illuminist mysticism. His practice drew energy from the same environment that produced:

- electrical and chemical demonstrations;
- Mesmerism;
- high-degree Freemasonry;
- Swedenborgian spirit communication;
- Rosicrucian alchemy;
- fascination with ancient Egypt;
- public distrust of licensed medical monopolies;
- an expanding European print market.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Lorenza/Serafina Feliciani

Wife, traveling partner, ritual dignitary, intermediary, occasional accuser and ultimately a source for the Holy Office. Their relationship combined dependency, collaboration, coercion allegations, separation and reconciliation.

### Cardinal de Rohan

Patient, patron, intimate associate and gateway to aristocratic France. The association caused Cagliostro’s arrest in the necklace affair but also enabled his acquittal to become a major public spectacle.

### Johann Kaspar Lavater

Swiss pastor, physiognomist and mystic; admirer and correspondent. He accepted or seriously entertained Cagliostro’s medical and spiritual claims and assisted him during later difficulties.

### Jacques Sarasin/Sarrasin and family

Swiss banking and mercantile patrons who provided hospitality, social access and correspondence networks. Their papers are important for reconstructing Cagliostro’s movements and reputation.

### Elisa von der Recke

Initially fascinated participant in Courland; later one of his most effective critics. Her exposé helped define the “charlatan” interpretation.

### Charles Théveneau de Morande

Journalist, pamphleteer, police-connected investigator and adversary. His campaign exposed Balsamo’s identity but also employed sensationalism and coercive tactics.

### Jean-Charles Thilorier

Defense lawyer in the necklace case. He helped transform Cagliostro’s defense into a successful public intervention.

### Clementino Vannetti

Rovereto writer who observed and satirized him in the pseudo-biblical *Liber memorialis*.

### Father Francesco Giuseppe da San Maurizio

Capuchin follower and secretary, possibly Lorenza’s lover according to the prosecution narrative. Arrested and convicted alongside Cagliostro.

### Prince-Bishop Pietro Vigilio Thun

Trento patron who facilitated Cagliostro’s return to Rome by obtaining or supporting safe-conduct negotiations.

### Augustin Belle, Marquis Vivaldi and Roman Masons

Contacts in the unsuccessful effort to establish Egyptian Masonry in Rome. Some were informants, suspects, or fugitives when arrests began.

Searches in English, Italian and French for combinations of “Giuseppe Balsamo” or “Cagliostro” with fraud, scandal, accusation, criticism, conspiracy and related terms—including material indexed or published in 2024–2026—produced no newly discovered court evidence altering the established eighteenth-century record.

Recent pieces mainly recycle:

- the claim that the Inquisition manufactured Balsamo’s identity;

- the necklace accusation despite his acquittal;

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda and Disputed Narratives

### Noble orphan raised in Mecca

**Story:** Cagliostro was born to unknown Christian nobles, abandoned or entrusted to guardians, raised in Medina or Mecca, and educated by Althotas.

**Appearance and circulation:** prominently in his 1786 Paris defense and later occultist biographies.

**Evidence:** inconsistent with Palermo baptismal, marital, family and handwriting evidence.

**Effect:** supplied an eastern sacred origin and detached the Grand Cophte from Balsamo’s criminal reputation.

**Classification:** self-created mythology.

### Althotas

**Story:** an immensely learned Greek, Arab, Armenian or Levantine adept taught him languages, botany, medicine and occult science.

**Evidence:** no independent contemporary record securely identifies Althotas. The figure may combine one or more real traveling companions with a literary master-archetype.

**Classification:** unresolved person embedded in myth.

### Disciple or continuation of the Comte de Saint-Germain

**Story:** Cagliostro studied under the immortal Saint-Germain, inherited his mission, or was Saint-Germain in another identity.

**Evidence:** parallels in longevity claims, alchemy and courtly self-presentation do not prove a meeting. The genealogy is chiefly occultist tradition.

**Effect:** placed both men in a lineage of immortal European adepts.

**Classification:** later occult legend.

### Immortality and ancient lives

Cagliostro encouraged claims that he had lived for centuries or millennia. Alexandre Dumas later amplified this into a figure thousands of years old who had known antiquity.

**Classification:** self-dramatization transformed into literary myth.

### Resurrection of the Russian child

**Story:** he restored a dead child to life; the hostile version says he substituted another child.

**Evidence:** mutually polemical reports, with no adequate medical or identity documentation.

**Classification:** miracle claim and counter-rumor.

### Prophecy of the French Revolution

**Story:** from the Bastille or London he precisely predicted the fall of the Bastille, Estates-General and abolition of *lettres de cachet*.

**Evidence:** the *Letter to the French People* contains striking anti-absolutist predictions, but edition dates and possible post-event textual handling require caution. Political unrest was already visible.

**Effect:** made him a prophet or secret architect of revolution.

**Classification:** partly documented political prediction, later magnified into prophecy.

### Grand Master of the Illuminati

**Story:** Cagliostro controlled or represented an international order planning the overthrow of monarchies and the papacy.

**Evidence:** based largely on dubious inquisitorial statements, hostile pamphlets and later conspiracy literature. Illuminati-associated testimony quoted by Francovich rejected him as an impostor.

**Classification:** unsupported conspiracy narrative.

### Inquisition substitution theory

**Story:** the real noble Cagliostro was replaced by the criminal Giuseppe Balsamo, whom enemies paid to impersonate him and discredit Masonry.

**Appearance:** occult apologetics and modern books, recently revived online.

**Evidence against:** spouse identity, 1773 police documents, signatures, family correspondence, the maternal Cagliostro name, Palermo testimony and Goethe’s investigation converge.

**Classification:** revisionist theory with insufficient supporting evidence.

### Escape from San Leo

**Story:** he escaped, survived beyond 1795, appeared in Russia, and once produced fresh strawberries in winter for a sick person.

**Circulation:** notably Helena Petrovna Blavatsky and Theosophical tradition.

**Evidence:** contradicts the San Leo administrative and death documentation.

**Classification:** posthumous occult legend.

### Revolutionary soldiers drinking from his skull

**Story:** when Polish or French republican troops reached San Leo in 1797, they opened his grave and drank wine from his skull in his honor.

**Evidence:** late anecdotal tradition; grave and remains have never been securely identified.

**Classification:** revolutionary folklore.

### Prediction written on his cell wall

Accounts claim that inscriptions survived in his cell, sometimes assigning the last one to 6 March 1795. The authenticity and authorship of individual inscriptions are uncertain.

**Classification:** local prison tradition.

## Works by or Published Under Cagliostro’s Name

Cagliostro’s limited formal education and dependence on lawyers, secretaries and lodge officers make “authorship” a spectrum.

### Contemporary publications and manuscripts

1. **_*Mémoire pour le comte de Cagliostro, accusé, contre M. le procureur général, accusateur*_** (Paris, 1786).  
   Legal defense in the Diamond Necklace affair, prepared with advocate Jean-Charles Thilorier. It contains Cagliostro’s autobiographical declaration but is not safely treated as unaided composition.

2. **_*Requête à joindre au mémoire du comte de Cagliostro*_** (Paris, 1786).  
   Supplementary legal pleading. Collaborative legal authorship.

3. **_*Mémoire pour le comte de Cagliostro, demandeur, contre Me Chesnon … et le sieur de Launay … gouverneur de la Bastille*_** (1786).  
   Action concerning the search, seizure and conditions of detention. Lawyer-mediated.

4. **_*Lettre au peuple français, écrite par M. le comte de Cagliostro, de Londres, le 20 juin 1786*_**.  
   Circulated after his expulsion; editions or reprints are sometimes dated 1789. Attributed or dictated authorship with likely editorial assistance.

5. **Letters and declarations in the *Courrier de l’Europe* and other newspapers** (1786–1787).  
   Polemical responses to Morande. Individual texts require bibliographical verification because newspapers paraphrased, edited or forged correspondence.

6. **_*Statuts et règlements de la R.L. de La Sagesse Triomphante*_** (Lyon, 24 December 1784).  
   Institutional statutes issued under Cagliostro’s authority; likely drafted or copied by lodge officers.

7. **_*Rituel de la haute maçonnerie égyptienne* / *Rituel de maçonnerie égyptienne pour la loge de la Sagesse Triomphante*_** (1784–1785 manuscript).  
   The surviving Lyon manuscript is in Saincostard’s hand. Cagliostro is best treated as originator, ritual director or source rather than physical scribe.

8. **Egyptian Rite catechisms, instructions and the “two quarantines.”**  
   Survive in variant manuscripts and later editions. Attribution ranges from direct dictation to lodge redaction.

9. **Petition or memorial to the French National Assembly** (Rome, 1789).  
   Requested permission to return and offered services. Apparently unanswered; mediated authorship likely.

10. **Medical recipes and prescriptions.**  
    Scattered through correspondence and manuscript collections. These are not a single securely authored book.

### Works wrongly attributed to him or about him

- **_*Compendio della vita e delle gesta di Giuseppe Balsamo denominato il Conte Cagliostro*_** (Rome, 1791) was not written by Cagliostro. It was an official Holy Office publication conventionally attributed to Giuseppe Barberi.
- **_*Liber memorialis de Caleostro cum esset Roboreti*_** was written by Clementino Vannetti, not Cagliostro.
- Many later “secret rituals,” memoirs and magical manuals marketed under his name are edited compilations or derivative occult publications rather than authenticated authorial texts.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Cagliostro’s reputation divided almost immediately into incompatible traditions:

- papal authorities: heretic, magician, Mason and fraud;
- French opposition culture: victim of arbitrary imprisonment;
- former believers: manipulative charlatan;
- continuing disciples: healer and initiated master;
- nineteenth-century novelists: immortal conspirator and revolutionary puppet-master;
- Theosophists and occultists: persecuted adept;
- skeptics: archetypal quack and confidence man;
- Masonic historians: irregular but historically important ritual innovator.

His concrete institutional legacy lies chiefly in:

- the creation or codification of an “Egyptian” Masonic system;
- mixed or parallel female participation;
- the fusion of healing, theurgy and Masonic initiation;
- influence—sometimes direct, sometimes genealogically overstated—on later Egyptianizing rites such as Misraïm and Memphis-Misraïm.

Direct organizational continuity between his lodge and every later “Egyptian Rite” cannot be assumed. Later bodies frequently appropriated his authority retrospectively.

His literary afterlife, especially through Goethe and Dumas, helped detach the name “Cagliostro” from the historical Balsamo and turn it into a reusable cultural type: the mysterious foreigner, hypnotist, conspirator, alchemist, revolutionary and immortal impostor.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are included. Mere shared interests or contemporaneity are excluded.

### Johann Wolfgang von Goethe — documented fact and posthumous artistic connection

In Palermo in April 1787, Goethe investigated whether Cagliostro was Giuseppe Balsamo. He consulted a local lawyer or dossier, traced the maternal genealogy, and visited Balsamo’s mother, sister and nephews. He recorded the inquiry in *Italienische Reise*. He later dramatized the Cagliostro milieu in *Der Groß-Cophta* (1792).

### Alexandre Dumas — documented posthumous literary connection

Dumas, working with Auguste Maquet, transformed Cagliostro into the central immortal conspirator of *Joseph Balsamo* and related novels beginning in 1846. There was no lifetime meeting; Dumas was born in 1802. The connection is documented influence on literary reputation.

### Franz Anton Mesmer — reported intellectual parallel; no documented meeting found

Cagliostro’s healing, suggestion and ritual phenomena were repeatedly compared with Mesmer’s animal magnetism. Consulted sources did not establish a personal meeting, apprenticeship or collaboration. The link is contextual rather than biographical.

### Emanuel Swedenborg — reported intellectual influence; no personal connection found

Mystical Freemasonry in Cagliostro’s environment drew on Swedenborgian spirit communication. No source consulted documents a meeting or correspondence with Swedenborg.

### The Comte de Saint-Germain — rumor or myth

Cagliostro was compared with Saint-Germain and later described as his disciple, successor, or even another manifestation of the same immortal adept. No consulted source establishes a meeting or teacher-student relationship.

### Antoine Court de Gébelin — shared Masonic milieu; direct connection not established

Both belonged to the French world of Masonic antiquarianism and Egyptian symbolism. The consulted sources did not demonstrate a meeting, correspondence or collaboration. No atlas connection should be inferred merely from overlapping Parisian and Masonic settings.

### Louis XVI — documented institutional connection

Louis XVI’s government arrested and imprisoned Cagliostro during the Diamond Necklace affair and, after his judicial acquittal, expelled him from France. No personal meeting was established.

### Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart — no documented connection found

Both became associated in later writing with eighteenth-century Freemasonry and Egyptian symbolism, but no meeting, correspondence or acknowledged direct influence was found.

### Helena Petrovna Blavatsky — documented posthumous interpretive connection

Blavatsky defended Cagliostro against the charlatan tradition and circulated the legend that he survived San Leo and later appeared in Russia. She did not meet him; the connection is posthumous hagiography and myth transmission.

### Giuseppe Maria Garibaldi — indirect institutional legacy only

Garibaldi later became associated with the united Memphis-Misraïm tradition. This does not demonstrate personal or direct organizational contact with Cagliostro. The connection is a later claimed lineage within Egyptianizing Masonry.

### Giuseppe Balsamo — identity, not a separate connection

The roster includes the dossier’s own subject. Claims that Giuseppe Balsamo and Alessandro Cagliostro were separate persons remain a minority theory contradicted by the strongest converging documentation.

No reliable connections were found with the remaining roster names. Their absence is preferable to constructing links from shared geography, Freemasonry, occult interests, or later analogy.

## Chronology

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 2 June 1743 | Giuseppe Balsamo born in the Albergheria district of Palermo. |
| 8 June 1743 | Baptized in Palermo. |
| 1743–1744 | Father Pietro dies shortly after his birth; mother relies on Bracconieri relatives. |
| 1750s | Attends San Rocco institution; repeatedly runs away according to later accounts. |
| 1756 | Sent to the Fatebenefratelli at Caltagirone; exposed to pharmacy and care of the sick. |
| Late 1750s–early 1760s | Leaves or is expelled; returns to Palermo. Alleged forgeries, thefts and treasure frauds belong to this poorly documented period. |
| c. 1760s | Alleged travels with Althotas to the eastern Mediterranean and Malta; not securely documented. |
| 1768 | In Rome as artist or copyist. |
| 21 April 1768 | Marries Lorenza Feliciani at San Salvatore in Campo. |
| 1768–1771 | Travels through northern Italy and the Iberian Peninsula with Lorenza and, for a time, “Count Agliata.” |
| Summer 1771 | First known London stay; debts, lottery schemes and imprisonment reported. |
| Late 1772–1773 | Paris; Lorenza leaves with Duplessis, is arrested and confined at Sainte-Pélagie, then reconciles with Balsamo. |
| 1773–1775 | Travels in the Low Countries and Germany; returns to Palermo and Naples. |
| 1775 | Marano complaint revived in Palermo; aristocratic intervention reportedly secures Balsamo’s release. |
| 1775–1776 | Marseille, Spain, Venice, Alicante and Cádiz reported. |
| 1776 | Returns to London and assumes the identity Count Alessandro di Cagliostro; Lorenza becomes Serafina. |
| 12 April 1777 | Cagliostro and Lorenza initiated at the Lodge of Esperance in Soho. |
| 1777 | King’s Bench imprisonment and civil litigation; leaves England late in the year. |
| 1778–1779 | Travels through the Netherlands and German states; grows prominent in occult-Masonic circles. |
| 1779 | At Mitau, Courland; impresses and then alienates Elisa von der Recke. |
| 1779–1780 | Saint Petersburg; disputed cures and miracle stories. |
| May–26 June 1780 | Warsaw; hosted by Poniński and Moszyński; alchemical demonstrations challenged; abrupt departure. |
| 19 September 1780 | Detailed chronology places his arrival in Strasbourg on this date. |
| 1780/1781–1783 | Strasbourg healer; friendship and patronage of Cardinal de Rohan, Lavater and Sarasin. |
| November 1783–October 1784 | Residence in Bordeaux. |
| October 1784–January 1785 | Lyon; organizes La Sagesse Triomphante. |
| 24 December 1784 | Statutes or formal establishment of the Lyon mother lodge. |
| 30 January 1785 | Arrives in Paris. |
| 1785 | Negotiations with the Philalèthes fail; Egyptian lodge Isis established. |
| August 1785 | Arrested in connection with the Diamond Necklace affair and imprisoned in the Bastille. |
| 31 May 1786 | Acquitted by the Parlement of Paris. |
| 21 June 1786 | Leaves France under royal expulsion order. |
| 1786–1787 | London polemic with Morande; Balsamo identity publicized; Lorenza separation crisis. |
| April 1787 | Goethe investigates Balsamo’s identity and family in Palermo. |
| 1787–1788 | Switzerland, Savoy, Turin, Genoa, Rovereto and Trento. |
| Autumn 1788 | At Trento under Prince-Bishop Thun’s protection. |
| May 1789 | Returns to Rome. |
| 16 September 1789 | Presents Egyptian Masonry at Villa Malta. |
| Late 1789 | Petitions French National Assembly; marital denunciations and police surveillance intensify. |
| 27 December 1789 | Arrested by papal authorities; imprisoned in Castel Sant’Angelo. |
| 8 May 1790 | First formal interrogation cited in the Roman proceedings. |
| 7 April 1791 | Convicted of heresy, magic and Freemasonry; death sentence commuted to perpetual imprisonment. |
| 21 April 1791 | Confined in San Leo fortress. |
| 4 May 1791 | Seized books and Masonic implements publicly burned in Rome. |
| 1791–1795 | Severe isolation and physical and psychological deterioration at San Leo. |
| 26 August 1795 | Dies at San Leo, reportedly following an apoplectic attack. |
| 1797 | Revolutionary forces reach San Leo too late to liberate him; grave-opening story enters folklore. |
| 1792 onward | Goethe, Dumas, occultists, dramatists and filmmakers transform him into a major legendary figure. |
