# Doris Humphrey: Research Dossier

**Prepared:** 24 August 2026  
**Scope:** A documentary, chronological reconstruction of Doris Humphrey’s life, family, training, career, institutions, artistic ideas, health, finances, controversies, writings, repertory, archives, and posthumous legacy.  
**Evidence note:** Exact premiere dates and venues are drawn principally from the New York Public Library (NYPL) finding aid to the Doris Humphrey collection, whose approximately 7,000 items include family and professional correspondence, manuscripts, contracts, itineraries, tax returns, and financial statements. Published biographies and institutional sources supplement it. Where those sources conflict, the conflict is stated.

## Family and Ancestry

Humphrey was the only child of **Horace Buckingham Humphrey** and **Julia Ellen Wells Humphrey**. Horace worked as a journalist and hotel or boarding-house manager. A frequently repeated detail, supported by institutional biographies, is that he ran the Palace Hotel, a Chicago residence used by vaudeville performers. Julia (“Nell” or “Nelly” in family correspondence) was a trained pianist, music teacher, accompanist, and later the business and musical support of her daughter’s first school.

Both parents came from New England clerical families. The NYPL family papers trace antecedent correspondence back to 1811. Horace and Julia were distant cousins; their courtship began by correspondence in 1891 after Horace sought the approval of Julia’s father, the Reverend **Moses H. Wells**. Her maternal grandmother was **Emily M. Taylor Wells**. Humphrey’s paternal line included the Reverend **Simon James Humphrey**. Published genealogies describe her as a descendant of Mayflower passenger **William Brewster**. That descent is plausible and repeated in dance reference works, but a complete generational proof was not reconstructed here; it should be treated as a genealogical claim rather than a central biographical fact.

The parents expressed belief in Providence but, according to the NYPL’s synthesis of their letters, remained outside conventional churchgoing life. Music, literature, wit, and social observation shaped the household. Financial insecurity also shaped it. In adulthood Humphrey repeatedly sent money to her parents; her father suffered a stroke in 1930 and died on 17 March 1934. Julia died in October 1945.

Humphrey met Charles Francis Woodford aboard the *S.S. Dominica* during a West Indies voyage in May–June 1931. Her letters initially rejected marriage as an institution—she told her parents she was “not thinking of marrying him” and did not believe in it—but the couple wanted a child and married on 10 June 1932. She announced the event almost as an afterthought in a postscript to her parents. Their only son was born the following year. Woodford participated intellectually in her work: from 1933 they attempted a book on modern dance, surviving in unfinished chapter drafts. Her professional and domestic worlds nevertheless pulled against each other, particularly during chronic illness and heavy touring and teaching schedules.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1895–1913

Humphrey was born in Oak Park but grew up chiefly in Chicago. In 1900 she entered the progressive **Francis W. Parker School**, where she remained through graduation in 1913. Around 1903, aged about eight, she began folk and gymnastic dance with **Mary Wood Hinman**, the school’s influential movement teacher. Her education was eclectic: ballet, folk and social dance, “gymnastic” or expressive movement, Delsartean ideas, Dalcroze-related rhythmic training, piano and concert music, clog dancing, and the vaudeville world visible through her father’s work.

Hinman was the decisive early mentor. She treated dance as an educative and creative activity rather than merely an ornamental accomplishment. Humphrey’s preserved school notebooks, sketches, essays, and contributions to the Parker *Recorder* show early verbal as well as movement ability. By her teens she was teaching and composing dances. She also toured western towns in a Santa Fe Railway-sponsored entertainment group, with Julia accompanying at the piano. Accounts differ on her exact age and itinerary; the episode belongs to the years before her 1913 graduation and gave her experience in touring, self-presentation, and popular entertainment.

## Education and Formation

Humphrey did not attend a university. Her formation came through Parker’s progressive curriculum, private and school dance instruction, professional touring, and apprenticeship.

- **Mary Wood Hinman** supplied early technique, folk repertoire, educational philosophy, and the recommendation that eventually sent Humphrey west.
- **Ruth St. Denis** gave her theatrical polish, solo roles, choreographic opportunity, and a model of the female dance artist as company leader.
- **Ted Shawn** supplied disciplined professional training, ensemble practice, and institutional experience.
- **Denishawn** exposed her to ballet, music visualization, Delsartean expressivity, theatrical spectacle, vaudeville, touring logistics, and the period’s Orientalist repertory.
- **Charles Weidman** became the complementary partner whose weight, humor, dramatic attack, and interest in kinetic action helped crystallize a shared technique.
- **Pauline Lawrence**, a Denishawn colleague, became administrator, designer, adviser, intimate friend, and practical stabilizer.

Humphrey’s mature theory was not a simple extension of Denishawn. It emerged through rejection: she objected to what she saw as decorative exoticism, music-bound choreography, autocratic management, and insufficiently American subject matter. Her independent work increasingly treated gravity, breath, rhythm, spatial design, and group relations as primary compositional materials.

## Early Career: School Owner and Denishawn, 1913–1928

### Oak Park and Chicago, 1913–1917

After graduating in 1913, Humphrey opened her own dance school in Oak Park and surrounding communities. Julia managed it and played piano. The school offered classical, gymnastic, interpretive, ballroom, and children’s dance. It succeeded sufficiently to support the family, but Humphrey found the work artistically limiting. In 1914 she taught at the Outdoor Players Camp in Peterborough, New Hampshire. Hinman encouraged her to seek professional development in California.

### Denishawn, 1917–1920

In 1917 Humphrey moved to Los Angeles and enrolled in the **Denishawn School of Dancing and Related Arts**. St. Denis and Shawn quickly accepted her into the company. She met Pauline Lawrence and Charles Weidman there. She became a leading dancer, teacher, rehearsal director, and choreographer—an unusually rapid rise reflecting musical intelligence, reliability, and compositional skill.

Her early choreographic record includes:

- *Valse Caprice* (also called *Scarf Dance*), premiered 7 January 1920, Egan Little Theatre, Los Angeles.
- *Bourrée*, first danced as a solo on 5 April 1920, Potter Theatre, Santa Barbara.
- *Soaring* and *Sonata Pathétique*, co-choreographed with Ruth St. Denis and first performed 20 September 1920, Spreckels Theatre, San Diego.

These works remain associated with Denishawn’s music-visualization practice, but *Soaring* in particular survived as an important bridge between Denishawn lyricism and Humphrey’s later phrasing.

### Independent vaudeville group, 1921–1922

From January 1921 to September 1922 Humphrey formed and toured her own group on the vaudeville circuit. This was an early experiment in independence rather than a permanent break. She rejoined Denishawn in September 1922.

### Denishawn maturity and Asian tour, 1922–1927

She created and danced *Sonata Tragica* (15 October 1923, Apollo Theatre, Atlantic City) and *Hoop Dance/Scherzo Waltz* (6 October 1924, Academy of Music, Newburgh). In August 1925 the Denishawn company sailed for an extensive Asian tour. The principal NYPL chronology calls it a “two-year tour,” but the company returned to the United States on 26 November 1926; it was roughly fifteen months abroad. Stops included Singapore and Kobe, with performances and observation elsewhere in East and Southeast Asia. Humphrey co-created *A Burmese Yein Pwe* with St. Denis (Singapore, 15 July 1926) and made *At the Spring* (Kobe, 23 October 1926). Her and Julia’s long letters—113 and 264 pages respectively—form a major contemporaneous record of the tour.

The tour broadened her visual and kinetic vocabulary but also placed her within Denishawn’s Orientalism: Western theatrical constructions presented as “Indian,” “Egyptian,” “Javanese,” “Burmese,” or other Asian forms, often without the training or cultural accountability now expected. Humphrey later defined her independence partly by turning away from imported exoticism toward American history, social organization, and abstract movement. That turn did not erase her participation in the earlier practice.

Back in the United States, *Whims* premiered in Los Angeles on 6 December 1926. In 1927 she ran Denishawn House in New York, assisted by Weidman and Lawrence. On 24 March 1928 her student group presented a substantial Brooklyn program including *Air for the G String*, *Color Harmony*, *Pavane for the Sleeping Beauty*, and *Pathetic Study*. *The Banshee* followed on 15 April and *Rigaudon* on 14 May.

### Departure from Denishawn

Humphrey, Weidman, and Lawrence split from Denishawn in July–August 1928. Retrospective shorthand sometimes portrays this as a single heroic revolt. Humphrey’s own unfinished reminiscences describe accumulated causes: artistic dissatisfaction, poor or uncertain remuneration, the demand that she reproduce St. Denis’s style, limited control, and management conflicts. The break was a labor and business separation as well as an aesthetic declaration.

## Chronological Life History: Independent Modernism, 1928–1945

### Founding the Humphrey–Weidman enterprise

Humphrey and Weidman opened a New York school and performing group, with Pauline Lawrence performing essential administrative and design work. The names “Humphrey–Weidman Group,” “Humphrey–Weidman Company,” “Humphrey–Weidman School,” and later “Studio Theatre” refer to connected but evolving entities, not a modern corporation with a continuously documented legal form.

Their first independent concert, 28 October 1928 at Eva Le Gallienne’s Civic Repertory Theatre, included *Sarabande* and *Water Study*. The latter was danced without conventional musical accompaniment, using breath and bodily rhythm to evoke waves. It became an emblem of autonomy from music and of the developing “fall and recovery” technique.

The 31 March 1929 Guild Theatre program added *Air on a Ground Bass*, *Gigue*, *Concerto in A Minor*, *Speed*, and *Life of the Bee*. At Lake Placid on 2 August came *The Call*, *Quasi Waltz*, *Courante*, and *Mazurka to Imaginary Music*.

### Cooperative modern dance and the early 1930s

Humphrey, Weidman, **Martha Graham**, and **Helen Tamiris** founded the short-lived **Dance Repertory Theatre**, whose first season ran 5–9 January 1930 at Maxine Elliott’s Theatre. It was an attempt by competing modernists to pool audiences and costs. Humphrey premiered *A Salutation to the Depths*, *Breath of Fire*, *Drama of Motion*, *La Valse*, and *Descent into a Dangerous Place*. The cooperative did not become permanent, but it established a second generation of American modern dance outside Denishawn.

Humphrey also entered opera and theatre: choreography for Schoenberg’s *Die glückliche Hand* (Philadelphia, 11 April 1930); with Weidman, dances for *Lysistrata* (28 April); *Les Romanesques* interludes (18 July); and other concert appearances. **José Limón** joined the group in 1930 and became its most consequential student.

*The Shakers (Dance of the Chosen)* premiered during the Dance Repertory Theatre’s second season, 1–4 February 1931. Based on the celibate American religious community and researched through historical material, it converted shaking worship, segregation of the sexes, suppression and release into formal group structure. The work’s durable status has generated a simplified myth that it is ethnographic documentation. It is better classified as a modernist theatrical interpretation of Shaker worship, not a literal reconstruction.

The Library of Congress presented her *Bloch String Quartet* on 23 April 1931. After a knee injury and exhaustion, she took a West Indies voyage, meeting Woodford. On 31 October 1931 she premiered *Three Mazurkas*, *Variations on a Theme of Handel*, and *Two Ecstatic Themes*. The latter stages opposed impulses—circular descent and pointed ascent—and has often been read through her simultaneous experience of romantic attachment and artistic independence.

### Marriage, maternity, Broadway, and Bennington, 1932–1934

*The Pleasures of Counterpoint* and *Dionysiaques* premiered 13 March 1932. Humphrey married Woodford in June, taught at Perry-Mansfield Camp in Colorado in July, and worked with Weidman and Eleanor Frampton on stadium productions of *Carmen* and *Aïda* in Cleveland.

In 1933 she choreographed Hall Johnson’s Black folk opera *Run Little Chillun* (Lyric Theatre, 1 March). Humphrey later said material observed among Bahamian performers during her West Indies voyage informed a ritual or mating dance. The production was historically important—it was a major African American musical drama—but the use of a white choreographer to mediate Black and Bahamian movement raises authorship and appropriation questions. Accessible evidence establishes collaboration and borrowing; it does not establish that Humphrey claimed sole invention of the source movement or that a legal dispute followed.

Her son was born on 8 July. She returned to performance quickly, appearing in *Suite in E* on 8 August and creating the ballet interlude “The Dream of Sganarelle” for the Theatre Guild’s *The School for Husbands* (16 October). She and Woodford also began their never-completed theoretical book.

Her father died in March 1934. In April she premiered *Rudepoema*, two further *Pleasures of Counterpoint*, and *Exhibition Piece*. In July she joined the founding faculty of **Martha Hill’s Bennington School of the Dance** at Bennington College. The summer school became a laboratory where Humphrey, Graham, Weidman, and Hanya Holm could make work, teach, and exchange ideas while retaining distinct companies.

### The New Dance Trilogy and social vision, 1935–1937

The 1935 Humphrey–Weidman national tour, from Toronto to Texas, was among the first modern-dance tours of that scale. Artistically ambitious, it was financially disappointing. Humphrey created dances for Gluck’s *Iphigenia in Aulis* in Philadelphia, then premiered *New Dance* at Bennington on 3 August.

The **New Dance Trilogy** comprised:

1. *New Dance* (1935), an idealized social order in which diverse individuals achieve cooperative unity;
2. *Theatre Piece* (1936), a critique of theatrical competition and egotism;
3. *With My Red Fires* (1936), a dark drama of possessive authority, family pressure, and thwarted love.

Wallingford Riegger supplied the musical framework. The three parts were not performed as one complete evening in Humphrey’s lifetime. *Theatre Piece* is generally treated as lost; later reconstructions preserved or restored sections of the other two. Commentators in the Popular Front era saw *New Dance* as socially progressive or collectivist. The evidence supports a democratic, anti-authoritarian social philosophy and engagement with left-leaning dance networks, but not a documented Communist Party membership.

*To the Dance* won a Dance Magazine group-choreography prize and premiered 23 February 1937 in Bloomington, Indiana. Award dates vary in references (1937 or 1938, depending on competition, announcement, or presentation); the NYPL chronology attaches the prize to the 1937 premiere.

### Touring, Passacaglia, and institutional organizing, 1938–1940

The company made transcontinental tours in January–April 1938 and February–May 1939. *American Holiday* and *The Race of Life* premiered in January 1938. Humphrey’s *Passacaglia in C Minor* premiered at Bennington on 5 August 1938. Structured to Bach, it is widely considered her large-ensemble masterpiece: a sustained architecture of individual and community, resistance and surrender, assembled without plot.

In 1939 she met **Helen Mary Robinson**, who became a close friend and correspondent, at Perry-Mansfield, and taught with the Bennington faculty at Mills College in California. *Square Dances* premiered 25 November. She participated in efforts to organize dancers through the **Dancers’ Association**, later the American Dance Association, in 1936–37. The surviving business papers include financial statements and organizational notes. She also sought subsidies and a permanent New York home through correspondence with the William C. Whitney Foundation and Anna Bogue.

The larger New Deal dance environment matters, but claims that Humphrey was straightforwardly employed by the Federal Dance Project require care. She worked within organizations and venues touched by federal arts support and benefited from the period’s dance infrastructure; the accessible NYPL chronology does not list a sustained Federal Dance Project appointment in the way it lists her formal teaching posts.

In 1940, worsening health kept her from Bennington. She made a film of *The Shakers* with Tom Bouchard. The first sections of *Song of the West* premiered on 8 November. The **Humphrey–Weidman Studio Theatre** opened in New York on 26 December, giving the group a home but also creating large fixed costs at a precarious moment.

### War years, illness, and end of the performing company, 1941–1945

Humphrey premiered *Dance“ings”* (1941), *Decade* (1941), “Rivers” from *Song of the West* (1942), *Four Chorale Preludes* and *Partita in G Major* (1942), and *El Salón México* (1943). *Inquest*, premiered 5 March 1944, was her last work as a performer. With Weidman she staged dances for *Sing Out, Sweet Land* later that year.

The company’s dissolution is sometimes represented as a single 1944 event caused solely by arthritis. It was a convergence: chronic hip and arthritic disability, wartime disruption, finances, shifting ambitions, and the strain of the Studio Theatre. Humphrey last danced in 1944 and formally retired as a dancer in 1945. She did not retire from dance.

## Later Career: Director, Teacher, and Choreographer, 1945–1958

### Limón company and 92nd Street Y

In 1946 Humphrey became Director of Dance in the education department of New York’s **YM-YWHA** (92nd Street Y) and artistic director of the newly established **José Limón Dance Company**. The latter arrangement was structurally unusual: Limón was founder, star, and chief choreographer, while Humphrey selected repertory, coached, shaped programs, and created works. It enabled her to choreograph beyond the limits of her own body. Pauline Lawrence, by then Limón’s wife, connected the old and new enterprises.

For Limón she made *The Story of Mankind* and *Lament for Ignacio Sánchez Mejías* (both premiered 11 July 1946 at Bennington). The latter, after Federico García Lorca’s elegy, became one of Limón’s great dramatic vehicles. *Day on Earth* followed on 10 May 1947. In 1947 she also created programs at the Green Mansions resort in New York.

She joined the faculty of the **Connecticut College School of the Dance** in 1948 and made *Corybantic*. On 31 March 1949 she received a **John Simon Guggenheim Fellowship** to write a book on choreography; *Invention* premiered that summer. Limón traveled to Mexico and Paris without her in 1950, illustrating both her indispensable directorial role at home and the limits illness placed on travel.

### Juilliard, Mexico, children’s work, and final dances

In fall 1951 Humphrey joined the dance faculty of the **Juilliard School of Music**, again under Martha Hill. She accompanied Limón to Mexico in December and mounted *Passacaglia* at the Instituto Nacional de Bellas Artes. *Night Spell* premiered in August 1951. At Connecticut College in 1952 she made *Fantasy*, *Fugue in C Major*, and *Fugue in C Minor*. She also directed a children’s dance enterprise, the **Merry-Go-Rounders**, beginning around 1952.

In 1953 she choreographed dances for *Poor Eddy*, premiered *Ritmo Jondo*, and created *Ruins and Visions* to Britten. She received the **Capezio Dance Award** on 9 March 1954 and made *Felipe el Loco*. In 1955 she organized **Juilliard Dance Theatre** and joined the advisory panel of the American National Theatre and Academy’s International Exchange Program. *The Rock and the Spring* and *Airs and Graces* premiered that year. A brief hospitalization in June is documented; accessible sources do not establish its precise diagnosis.

The last phase brought *Theatre Piece No. 2* and *Dawn in New York* (1956), *Descent into the Dream* and dances for *The Child and the Apparitions* (1957), and her last completed work, *Dance Overture* (15 August 1957). Despite illness she toured Europe with the Limón company from August to December 1957. In 1958 she received the **Dance Magazine Award**, finished *The Art of Making Dances*, began *Brandenburg No. 4* (completed after her death by Ruth Currier), and began unfinished reminiscences.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Business structures and money

Humphrey’s enterprises were small performing-arts organizations dependent on tuition, touring fees, theatre engagements, patronage, awards, and uncompensated or undercompensated labor. No evidence was found of patents, industrial inventions, investments, substantial real-estate holdings, or personal wealth.

The documentary record instead emphasizes scarcity:

- her first school helped support her parents;
- she continued remittances after moving to New York;
- the Dance Repertory Theatre sought pooled economies but lacked durable funding;
- the 1935 tour failed financially despite artistic success;
- Pauline Lawrence handled much company management;
- contracts, tax returns, itineraries, financial notes, and statements survive particularly for 1940–42;
- Humphrey petitioned the William C. Whitney Foundation for subsidy and a permanent home;
- the Studio Theatre imposed costs just as war and illness weakened the company;
- the 1949 Guggenheim supported the choreography book.

Exact income, debt, asset, and subsidy totals cannot be responsibly reconstructed from online finding aids; they require box-level archival research. No bankruptcy, securities scheme, hidden business empire, or contested estate surfaced in the consulted evidence.

### Preservation technology

Humphrey understood dance’s ephemerality and used photography, film, notes, and later **Labanotation**. The Dance Notation Bureau reports a substantial notated Humphrey repertory; secondary summaries often give **35 dances**. Notation preserved structure but not every performance nuance. Films can restore dynamics and gesture but contain gaps, edits, and misleading camera angles. Later reconstructions therefore combine notation, films, photographs, music, designs, written notes, and dancers’ memories.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Fall and recovery

Humphrey’s signature principle examines the body between balance and surrender to gravity—her “arc between two deaths,” the two limiting states being static equilibrium and complete collapse. “Fall and recovery” is not merely a stunt of dropping and rising. It organizes weight, suspension, succession, breath, rebound, and the psychological drama of risk and restored control.

### Breath rhythm, natural movement, and musical form

She treated breath as phrasing and dynamic impulse. *Water Study* made this audible and visible without a score. She was highly musical—Bach, Beethoven, Brahms, Bloch, Riegger, Copland, Britten—but rejected servile illustration. Her choreography could counterpoint, resist, or structurally converse with music.

### Group and individual

Where Martha Graham’s public image centered the heroic or anguished individual, Humphrey frequently composed social fields: groups divide, pressure, shelter, absorb, or release individuals. Her preferred large forms—fugue, passacaglia, counterpoint—made plurality visible. Her democratic ideal was neither unstructured individualism nor total group submission; it sought differentiated unity.

### American themes and religion

After Denishawn, she looked to American sources: Shaker worship, social competition, the Depression-era city, the West, Mexican music, Black musical theatre, and democratic imagery. Religious subjects were primarily choreographic and historical rather than evidence of personal conversion. The family’s nonsectarian Providence and her humanistic belief in communal possibility are better documented than formal adherence to a church.

### Politics

Her 1930s works were received amid labor organizing, antifascism, Popular Front aesthetics, and debates over federally supported art. *New Dance* offered a collective democratic utopia; *Theatre Piece* attacked competitive vanity; *With My Red Fires* dramatized authoritarian possession; *Inquest* addressed social judgment. She worked with politically varied modern dancers and belonged to professional organizing bodies. No consulted primary or scholarly source establishes Communist Party membership, intelligence work, political violence, or government prosecution.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### Documented matters

1. **Denishawn Orientalism.** Humphrey performed and helped create dances presented through generalized Asian identities. This is documented repertory, not rumor. Current scholarship interprets much Denishawn work as Orientalist and appropriative. Her later Americanism was partly a reaction against it, not evidence she had never participated.
2. **Black and Bahamian source material in *Run Little Chillun*.** Humphrey described the movement route from African-derived Bahamian ritual into the Broadway choreography. The production’s Black collaborators and performers are essential to attribution. The evidence supports cultural borrowing and mediated authorship; it does not support a known lawsuit or finding of plagiarism.
3. **Labor and managerial conflict at Denishawn.** Humphrey’s departure involved money, control, credit, and aesthetic authority. It was not merely a serene stylistic evolution.
4. **Company finances.** Archival descriptions document chronic underfunding, unsuccessful touring, subsidy appeals, and incomplete business records. There is no evidence of fraud, embezzlement, money laundering, or corruption.
5. **Attribution and reconstruction.** Several Denishawn pieces were co-choreographed with St. Denis; stage works were co-created with Weidman or other collaborators; *Brandenburg No. 4* was completed by Ruth Currier. Later stagings are reconstructions, sometimes substantially interpreted. Programs that list only Humphrey can conceal these layers.

### Matters not supported by the consulted record

No documented criminal case, civil lawsuit, government investigation, intelligence connection, addiction, corruption charge, abuse allegation, or political-violence allegation was found. Absence from the consulted record is not proof that every private conflict is known; it means such claims should not be invented.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “She invented modern dance”

- **Story:** Humphrey is called a founder or mother of modern dance, sometimes implying sole invention.
- **Appearance/circulation:** Institutional tributes, educational summaries, and promotional language, especially after her death.
- **Evidence:** She did create a distinct technique and one of the first systematic modern choreographic theories. But Duncan, Fuller, St. Denis, Shawn, Graham, Holm, Tamiris, Dunham, Weidman, and others were independent founders.
- **Effect:** The formula secured canonical status but flattens a crowded and contested field.

### “Fall and recovery” as a complete technique invented in one instant

- **Story:** Humphrey suddenly discovered a universal law of movement.
- **Evidence:** Her theory developed across years from breath, gravity, Delsartean oppositions, ensemble experimentation, and collaboration with Weidman. It was codified progressively, not revealed in a single documented epiphany.
- **Effect:** The simplified phrase made the technique memorable while obscuring its wider rhythmic, spatial, and social system.

### The amicable Denishawn emancipation

- **Story:** Humphrey and Weidman simply matured and peacefully founded a new school in 1928.
- **Evidence:** Humphrey’s recollections and archival summaries identify disillusionment, management and pay issues, and artistic constraint.
- **Effect:** The cleaner origin myth supports a heroic genealogy from Denishawn to modern dance but suppresses labor conflict.

### The 1959 death date

- **Story:** One NYPL papers finding aid says she died 29 December 1959.
- **Evidence against:** The main NYPL chronology, gravestone, obituary-derived references, and standard biographies give 29 December **1958**.
- **Effect:** The error propagates easily in database copying; it has no substantive biographical basis.

### “Two-year Oriental tour”

- **Story:** Denishawn toured Asia for two full years.
- **Evidence:** Departure in August 1925 and return on 26 November 1926 indicate about fifteen months. “Two-year” may mean portions of two calendar years or reflect an intended itinerary.
- **Effect:** It exaggerates duration but not the tour’s formative importance.

### The isolated genius and the lost repertory

- **Story:** Humphrey alone created a self-contained system, then her dances simply disappeared.
- **Evidence:** Lawrence, Weidman, Limón, Hill, dancers, composers, notators, filmmakers, and reconstructors were integral. Some works are lost; many survive in notation, film, memory, or reconstruction.
- **Effect:** The tragic-genius story both magnifies Humphrey and undercredits the network that made her legacy possible.

## Works by Doris Humphrey

### Books and prose

1. **The Art of Making Dances** (New York: Rinehart, 1959). Completed in 1958 and published posthumously. Humphrey is the author; Barbara Pollack edited the manuscript for publication. It combines theory, practical compositional analysis, and autobiographical observation.
2. **“New Dance”** (*Dance Perspectives*, no. 25, 1966). Posthumous publication of her unfinished autobiographical reminiscences, not the 1935 choreography of the same title.
3. **“Dance Drama.”** Essay published in Walter Sorell, ed., *The Dance Has Many Faces* (first edition 1951; later revised editions). Genuine authored essay.
4. **Doris Humphrey: An Artist First** (ed. Selma Jeanne Cohen, 1972). Posthumous compilation incorporating Humphrey’s autobiographical “New Dance” and writings; edited collection, not a book assembled by Humphrey herself.
5. **Unpublished modern-dance book with Charles Francis Woodford** (drafts, 1930s). Joint planned project; never completed or published as such.

### Authorship cautions

- Denishawn works may have variant titles and shared St. Denis authorship.
- Theatre and opera assignments often involved Weidman, directors, composers, and performers.
- Humphrey’s choreography is not equivalent to the musical composition named in a title.
- Revivals reconstructed from Labanotation or film should identify the reconstructor and edition.
- *Theatre Piece* from the 1936 trilogy is lost; *Theatre Piece No. 2* (1956) is a different work.

## Illness, Later Life, Death, and Burial

Humphrey suffered a knee injury by 1931 and progressively disabling hip trouble and arthritis through the 1930s and 1940s. The NYPL describes “crippling arthritis,” premature performing retirement, and a continuous battle with debilitating illness. She last performed in *Inquest* in March 1944 and formally retired as a dancer in 1945. She continued to teach, direct, choreograph, write, and travel.

She was hospitalized briefly in June 1955, toured Europe in 1957, and completed her choreography book in 1958. Cancer caused her final decline. She died at Flower and Fifth Avenue Hospital on 29 December 1958. No reliable evidence of addiction, suicide attempt, psychiatric institutionalization, or accidental death was found. She was buried in the family region at Forest Home Cemetery, Forest Park, Illinois.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

*The Art of Making Dances* appeared in 1959 and became a foundational choreography manual. Limón and his company institutionalized her approach to weight, breath, succession, and dramatic group form, though “Limón technique” is not identical to Humphrey’s. Juilliard, Connecticut College, Bennington alumni, the Dance Notation Bureau, Repertory Dance Theatre, and later Humphrey foundations preserved parts of the repertory.

Her son donated the core personal collection to the NYPL in 1967. The **Doris Humphrey Foundation for Dance**, established in 2008, promotes preservation, reconstruction, teaching, and performance. A related UK foundation and reconstruction projects have extended the repertory internationally.

Humphrey entered the National Museum of Dance Hall of Fame in 1987. The centenary in 1995 generated reconstructions, festivals, and scholarship. Yet her stage repertory has remained less continuously visible than Graham’s or Limón’s. Reasons documented by preservation scholarship include early death, the collapse of her own company, lost works, lack of a single permanent Humphrey company, the cost of large casts, dependence on notation and reconstruction, and the transfer of public recognition to Limón.

Historical reinterpretation has broadened. Earlier accounts centered formal genius and humanism. Later scholarship examines labor, institutional finance, New Deal politics, gendered authorship, Orientalism, Black source material, and the authority of reconstructors. These approaches do not negate the dances’ formal history; they document the conditions under which they were made and remembered.

## Research Limitations and Evidence Conflicts

- The online finding aids describe but do not reproduce most letters, contracts, tax returns, medical records, or financial statements. Exact financial reconstruction requires on-site archival work.
- “Complete works” lists differ because classroom studies, variants, shared choreography, theatre dances, lost works, and reconstructions are counted differently.
- Cancer is consistently given as cause of death, but accessible authoritative sources do not name a primary site.
- Award references disagree over whether a Dance Magazine prize belongs to 1937 or 1938 and whether the Guggenheim is described as 1948 or 1949. The principal NYPL chronology gives 31 March 1949 for the Guggenheim and a 1937 premiere/prize association for *To the Dance*.
- The papers finding aid’s 1959 death date conflicts with the overwhelming 1958 evidence and is treated as an error.
- No evidence was found for criminality, intelligence connections, addiction, or litigation. This is a bounded finding from consulted records, not proof about every unprocessed private event.

## Atlas Connections

### Ruth St. Denis

- **[T5] Documented fact:** Humphrey met St. Denis at Denishawn in Los Angeles in 1917, became her student and company member, and later co-choreographed *Soaring*, *Sonata Pathétique*, and *A Burmese Yein Pwe* with her. Their relationship deteriorated during Humphrey’s 1928 departure from Denishawn; surviving records include correspondence between them. [NYPL Doris Humphrey collection](https://archives.nypl.org/dan/19673)

### Ted Shawn

- **[T5] Documented fact:** Humphrey met Shawn at Denishawn in Los Angeles in 1917 and worked under him as dancer, teacher, rehearsal director, and choreographer. Shawn encouraged her choreography during Denishawn’s 1925–26 Asian tour, but managerial and artistic disputes culminated in a complete break at a heated board meeting in June 1928. [NYPL Doris Humphrey collection](https://archives.nypl.org/dan/19673)

### Martha Graham

- **[T5] Documented fact:** Humphrey and Graham were Denishawn contemporaries and later jointly founded the Dance Repertory Theatre with Charles Weidman and Helen Tamiris. Its inaugural season ran at Maxine Elliott’s Theatre in New York in January 1930; they subsequently worked in the same Bennington and Juilliard institutional networks. [NYPL chronology](https://archives.nypl.org/dan/19673), [Library of Congress program](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/public/music/musihas-200154284/musihas-200154284.pdf)

### Hanya Holm

- **[T4] Documented fact:** Holm and Humphrey belonged to the founding artistic milieu of the Bennington School of the Dance at Bennington College in summer 1934, alongside Martha Graham and Charles Weidman. The sources establish shared institutional presence, but not a close friendship. [Bennington College](https://www.bennington.edu/news-and-features/how-modern-dance-took-root-vermont)

### José Limón

- **[T5] Documented fact:** Limón entered the Humphrey–Weidman group in New York in 1930 as Humphrey’s student and dancer. In 1946 he and Humphrey founded the Limón Dance Company, with Humphrey serving as artistic director, choreographer, coach, and repertory shaper; she created such works for him as *Lament for Ignacio Sánchez Mejías* and *Day on Earth*. [NYPL Doris Humphrey collection](https://archives.nypl.org/dan/19673), [Limón Dance Company](https://www.limon.nyc/purpose)

### Federico García Lorca

- **[T3] Documented fact:** This was a one-sided, posthumous engagement with Lorca’s writing, not a personal meeting. Humphrey based *Lament for Ignacio Sánchez Mejías*, created for José Limón in 1946, on Lorca’s elegy for the bullfighter; Lorca had died in 1936. [Larousse](https://www.larousse.fr/encyclopedie/oeuvre/Lament_For_Ignacio_S%C3%A1nchez_Mej%C3%ADas/128574), [NYPL chronology](https://archives.nypl.org/dan/19673)

### Rudolf Laban

- **[T9-] Documented fact:** Laban originated the movement-notation system later used to preserve Humphrey’s choreography. Humphrey valued the system, and numerous dances—including *New Dance*, *Ritmo Jondo*, *Day on Earth*, and *Night Spell*—were recorded in Labanotation. This is a connection through a transmitted technical instrument, not evidence that Humphrey and Laban met. [Library of Congress Dance Notation Collection](https://findingaids.loc.gov/agents/people/24131), [Dance Notation Bureau](https://www.dancenotation.org/theory-bulletin-board/resources-and-riches-dance-notation-bureau/)

No additional roster crossings were included where the evidence showed only broad influence, parallel careers, shared tradition, or unverified contemporaneity.
