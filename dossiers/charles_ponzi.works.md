## Works by Charles Ponzi

### Autobiography

- **1935–1937?:** *The Rise of Mr. Ponzi*. A self-published autobiography and self-justifying account of Ponzi’s life through the collapse of his Boston enterprise. The dating is unsettled: the surviving first edition has been described as 1935, bibliographies often give 1936, and copies bearing a Rome imprint are dated 1937; later subtitles such as *The Autobiography of a Financial Genius* should not be mistaken for securely established parts of the original title.

### Publishing and utility projects

- **1912–1913?:** *Ponzi Power, Light & Water Company of Blocton, Alabama*. An unrealized utility project for which Ponzi obtained a municipal franchise and commissioned engineers to design and cost a generating and water plant. No plant was built, so the obra here is the project—the intention, franchise, and planning—rather than a completed utility.

- **1919:** *The Trader’s Guide*. A projected multilingual, loose-leaf international trade publication, promoted through Ponzi’s Bostonian Advertising & Publishing Company. It was designed as a continuously updated combination of reference work and geographically targeted advertising, but financing failed before regular publication; it therefore resists classification as a book because it chiefly survives as an abandoned publishing system described by Ponzi himself.

### Financial constructions and fraudulent instruments

- **1908:** *Forged Canadian Warehousing cheque*. A counterfeit financial instrument for 423.58 Canadian dollars, made by Ponzi from a company cheque and bearing the forged signature of director Damien Fournier. It is an authored object only in the forensic sense, not an obra Ponzi publicly claimed; the conviction establishes the forgery, while some narrative particulars come from later biographical reconstruction.

- **1919–1920:** *Securities Exchange Company international-reply-coupon plan*. A financial project combining a nominal postal-coupon arbitrage strategy, a one-man business registered under additional names without their authorization, promissory notes offering 50 percent in ninety days, and a commissioned-agent network. Limited coupon arbitrage was possible and Ponzi reported testing it, but the promised scale was impossible; new subscriptions were consequently used to redeem earlier notes, making the realized thing a fraudulent payment system rather than the international-trading business it described itself as.

- **1920:** *Securities Exchange Company branch network*. A manifestation of the preceding plan as approximately thirty-five New England collection offices supported by agents, advertising, stationery, bank accounts, and standardized promissory notes. It is difficult to separate this network into autonomous “works”: it was the operational and persuasive apparatus of the larger fraud.

- **1920 onward:** *“Ponzi scheme”*. The name designates the financial structure manifested by Ponzi’s Securities Exchange Company—returns to earlier participants paid chiefly from later participants’ principal—but not an invention securely attributable to him. Earlier schemes used the same structure, so this is an eponymous category created by public memory and journalism, not a title or an obra made by Ponzi alone.

- **1925–1926:** *Charpon Land Syndicate*. A Florida land-sales and investment project organized with Rose Ponzi and Calcedonio and Maria Alviati. It subdivided roughly one hundred acres in rural Columbia County into small lots marketed as being near Jacksonville and promised investors 200 percent returns in about sixty days; because the land existed but its location, value, and return were misrepresented, the thing made was simultaneously a real subdivision scheme and a fraud.

### Sources

- Mitchell Zuckoff, *Ponzi’s Scheme: The True Story of a Financial Legend* (Random House, 2005), the principal modern research biography, based on court, newspaper, bankruptcy, and government records; its contents and selected bibliography are documented by [Penguin Random House](https://penguinrandomhouselibrary.com/book/?isbn=9780767918121) and [Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/Ponzi_s_Scheme.html?id=RshbAA3v1kUC).
- Charles Ponzi, [*The Rise of Mr. Ponzi*](https://www.charlesponzi.com/charles-ponzi-autobiography), consulted as the primary source for the Blocton utility, *The Trader’s Guide*, the Securities Exchange Company’s organization, its notes, and its branch system. Ponzi’s retrospective and self-exculpatory claims were not treated as independent verification.
- U.S. National Archives, [“When Ponzi’s Bubble Burst”](https://www.archives.gov/publications/prologue/2010/summer/ponzi-inmate-case-file), for the federal inmate file, the Canadian forgery conviction, the 1910 immigrant-smuggling conviction, and the documented operation of the 1920 fraud.
- CUNY, [“Of Skulduggery”](https://www.cuny.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/4/page-assets/publications/salute-to-scholars/sts-winter10.pdf), for the rare first edition described as Ponzi’s self-published 1935 autobiography.
- James Cummins Bookseller, [*Americana*, January 2021](https://jamescumminsbookseller.cdn.bibliopolis.com/images/upload/americana-january-2021-3.pdf), for a bibliographical description of a Rome copy of *The Rise of Mr. Ponzi* dated 1937.
- Smithsonian Magazine, [“In Ponzi We Trust”](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/in-ponzi-we-trust-64016168/), drawing on Donald Dunn’s extensive biographical research, for corroboration of Ponzi’s autobiography and later career.
- The Jaxson, [“Jacksonville’s Notorious Con Artist: Charles Ponzi”](https://www.thejaxsonmag.com/article/jacksonvilles-notorious-con-artist-charles-ponzi/), for the organization, collaborators, acreage, subdivision method, location, and advertised returns of the Charpon Land Syndicate.
- *Time*, [“The History of Ponzi Schemes Goes Deeper Than the Man Who Gave Them His Name”](https://time.com/5877434/first-ponzi-scheme/), for the distinction between Ponzi’s historically famous manifestation and earlier schemes using the same payment structure.
