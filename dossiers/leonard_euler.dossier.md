# Leonhard Euler: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

This dossier reconstructs Leonhard Euler’s life from documentary catalogues, surviving correspondence, academy records, contemporary memorial notices, modern historical scholarship, and specialist studies of his mathematics and institutional career. “Documented fact” denotes information supported by contemporary records, correspondence, publications, or institutional archives. “Reported fact” denotes a claim repeated by reputable secondary scholarship but not independently verified here from an original document. Legends and disputed stories are labeled separately.

Euler produced too much work for every item to be reproduced bibliographically in a practical dossier: Gustaf Eneström’s standard catalogue enumerates **866 books, articles, memoirs, and selected letters**, while the *Opera Omnia* and correspondence series encompass more material still. The “Works” section therefore identifies the comprehensive catalogues and gives a chronological bibliography of the principal independent books and most historically consequential papers.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Leonhard Euler.
- **Latinized publication name:** Leonhardus Eulerus; genitive title-page form, *Leonhardi Euleri*.
- **French form:** Léonard Euler, sometimes encountered in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century editions.
- **Russian:** Леонард Эйлер.
- **Pronunciation:** In German, approximately “Oiler,” not the English spelling pronunciation “You-ler.”
- **Titles and institutional descriptions:** Professor of physics and later mathematics at the Imperial Academy of Sciences in Saint Petersburg; director of the mathematical class of the Prussian/Brandenburg Academy in Berlin; foreign member of several learned societies.
- **Later honorific descriptions:** *Mathematicorum princeps* (“prince of mathematicians”) and “the master of us all.” These are commemorative judgments, not offices held by Euler.
- **Birth:** **15 April 1707**, Basel, Swiss Confederacy.
- **Death:** **18 September 1783**, Saint Petersburg, Russian Empire.
- **Calendar issue:** Some older sources give 7 September Old Style or, erroneously, 19 September or 18 October. The accepted modern Gregorian date is 18 September 1783.
- **Citizenship and identity:** A Basel-born Swiss subject who spent most of his adult life in Russian and Prussian state service. Calling him “Russian,” “Prussian,” or “German” without qualification confuses employment, language, and nationality.
- **Primary professions:** Mathematician, physicist, astronomer, geographer, engineer, scientific administrator, teacher, textbook author, and consultant on navigation, shipbuilding, hydraulics, optics, artillery, and cartography.
- **Languages:** German was his family language; he wrote extensively in Latin and French and also functioned in the multilingual environments of Basel, Berlin, and Saint Petersburg.
- **Religion:** Reformed Protestant; active in the Reformed community and a published defender of Christian revelation.
- **Principal residences:** Basel and Riehen, 1707–1727; Saint Petersburg, 1727–1741; Berlin and Charlottenburg, 1741–1766; Saint Petersburg, 1766–1783.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and early household

**Documented fact:** Euler was the eldest son of **Paul III Euler** (1670–1745), a Reformed pastor, and **Margaretha or Marguerite Brucker**. Paul Euler had studied theology at the University of Basel and attended lectures by Jakob Bernoulli. He knew members of the Bernoulli family, a connection that later proved decisive for Leonhard.

Margaretha Brucker was herself a minister’s daughter. Claims that her ancestry included several classical scholars appear in modern genealogical accounts, but this dossier has not independently reconstructed every line from parish registers.

Soon after Leonhard’s birth the family moved from Basel to **Riehen**, where Paul became pastor. The children grew up in a clerical household in which religious instruction and elementary mathematics were both present.

Leonhard’s known siblings included:

- **Anna Maria Euler**, later associated with the family household.
- **Maria Magdalena Euler**.
- A younger brother, often identified as **Johann Heinrich Euler**, who became a painter; sources do not always present the siblings consistently.

The precise sibling list and dates should be checked against Basel parish registers before being treated as complete.

### First marriage

**Documented fact:** On **7 January 1734**, in Saint Petersburg, Euler married **Katharina Gsell** (5 April 1707–10 November 1773). She was the daughter of the Swiss painter and art dealer **Georg Gsell**, employed in the Academy milieu.

Katharina’s family connected Euler indirectly with a substantial artistic and scientific household. Georg Gsell’s later wife was the artist and natural-history illustrator **Dorothea Maria Graff**, daughter of naturalist and illustrator **Maria Sibylla Merian**. Katharina was therefore Salome Abigail Gsell’s half-sister, not ordinarily described as Merian’s biological granddaughter.

The couple had **thirteen children**, of whom five are generally said to have reached adulthood. Sources occasionally compress this to “three surviving sons,” meaning sons who survived the parents, and thereby omit daughters who reached adulthood.

A modern reconstruction gives:

1. **Johann Albrecht Euler** (27 November 1734–18 September 1800), mathematician and physicist; professor and secretary at the Saint Petersburg Academy.
2. **Anna Margaretha Euler** (28 May–2 July 1736), died in infancy.
3. **Maria Gertrude Euler** (28 April 1737–3 March 1739).
4. **Anna Elisabeth Euler** (25 October–8 November 1739).
5. **Karl Johann Euler** (15 July 1740–16 March 1790).
6. **Katharina Helena Euler** (15 November 1741–4 May 1781).
7. **Johann Christoph Euler** (1 May 1743–24 February 1808), later a lieutenant-general in Russian service.
8. **Charlotte Euler** (12 July 1744–13 February 1780).
9. An unnamed child, apparently stillborn or dying shortly after birth, around 1745.
10. **Hermann Friedrich Euler** (8 May 1747–12 December 1750).
11. **Erthmuth Louise Euler** (13 April–9 August 1749), twin.
12. **Helene Eleonora Euler** (13 April–11 August 1749), twin.
13. **August Friedrich Euler** (20 March–10 August 1750).

The exact identity of all thirteen children is a genealogical reconstruction rather than a list Euler himself published.

Euler’s household was large and multigenerational. His mother lived with him in Berlin from about 1750. Children, relatives, assistants, pupils, and servants shared his residences. A recollection attributed to Euler says that some discoveries were made while he held a baby with other children playing around him. It is plausible as household description but survives as anecdote.

### Second marriage

**Documented fact:** After Katharina’s death in 1773, Euler married her half-sister **Salome Abigail Gsell** in **1776**. She cared for him during his blindness and survived him. No children are known from the second marriage.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1707–1720

Euler was born in Basel but spent most of his childhood at Riehen, a village just outside the city. Paul Euler taught him elementary subjects, including mathematics, while intending him for the ministry.

In **1713**, according to a University of Würzburg chronology, Euler began attending the Basel Gymnasium. The young Euler also lodged in Basel with his maternal grandmother when schooling required him to be in the city.

There is little reliable evidence for the highly dramatized childhood-genius episodes common in popular biographies. What can be established is that he entered university unusually young and possessed enough mathematical ability to attract Johann Bernoulli’s attention.

---

## Education and Formation, 1720–1727

### University of Basel

**Documented fact:** Euler matriculated at the **University of Basel in 1720**, aged thirteen. His formal curriculum included philosophy, theology, Greek, and Hebrew. In **1723**, aged sixteen, he received the degree of **Master of Philosophy**. His dissertation compared the natural philosophies of **René Descartes** and **Isaac Newton**.

Paul Euler expected his son to enter the Reformed ministry. Leonhard accordingly began theological studies in 1723. Johann Bernoulli persuaded Paul that Leonhard’s strongest vocation was mathematics.

### Johann Bernoulli’s instruction

Euler attended **Johann I Bernoulli’s** lectures. Because Bernoulli was unwilling to provide daily private tuition, an arrangement developed in which Euler studied difficult works independently during the week and visited Bernoulli on Saturdays with unresolved questions. This was an important pedagogical method: intensive self-study with periodic expert correction.

Bernoulli’s sons **Daniel** and **Niklaus II Bernoulli** became Euler’s friends and professional intermediaries. Daniel’s work in mechanics, fluids, probability, and mathematical physics was especially formative.

Other important early influences included:

- **Jakob Bernoulli**, through his published work and through Paul and Johann Bernoulli.
- **Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz**, whose differential notation and analytic methods Euler extended.
- **Isaac Newton**, whose mechanics and gravitational astronomy Euler translated into increasingly systematic analytical form.
- **René Descartes**, both as a subject of Euler’s student dissertation and as a foundational figure in analytical geometry.
- **Jakob Hermann**, a relative and mathematician later encountered at Saint Petersburg.

### First publications and failed Basel appointment

In **1726**, Euler’s short paper on isochronous curves in a resisting medium appeared in print. He also worked on reciprocal trajectories and acoustics.

In **1727**, he entered the Paris Academy prize competition on the arrangement of masts aboard ships. Though he had little or no practical experience at sea, his mathematical essay received **second place**. Pierre Bouguer won.

Euler applied for the vacant physics professorship at Basel. Candidates who reached the final stage were selected by lot, but Euler was not advanced to that stage. His age—nineteen—was probably a major disadvantage. This was the most consequential early professional failure of his life.

## First Saint Petersburg Period, 1727–1741

### Journey to Russia

**Documented fact:** Euler left Basel on **5 April 1727**. He traveled down the Rhine by boat, crossed German territories by post wagon, and sailed from Lübeck. He reached **Saint Petersburg on 17 May 1727**.

Niklaus II Bernoulli had died in Saint Petersburg in July 1726, producing an opening at the new Imperial Academy. Daniel Bernoulli and Jakob Hermann helped secure Euler an appointment. The contemplated position concerned physiology and the applications of mathematics to medicine, so Euler studied relevant subjects before leaving Basel. On arrival he was instead placed in the mathematical-physical division.

### Academy and naval service

The Saint Petersburg Academy had been planned by Peter the Great and opened under Catherine I. It offered salaries, housing, printing facilities, and time for research, but was vulnerable to court politics, administrative instability, and the deaths of imperial patrons.

Euler’s early colleagues included:

- **Daniel Bernoulli**, friend, collaborator, correspondent, and occasional scientific competitor.
- **Jakob Hermann**, mathematician and relative.
- **Christian Goldbach**, academy secretary and Euler’s most important correspondent on number theory.
- **Joseph-Nicolas Delisle**, astronomer and organizer of Russian mapping projects.
- **Friedrich Christoph Maier**, associated with trigonometry.
- Academy administrator **Johann Daniel Schumacher**, with whom Euler maintained an important administrative correspondence.

From **1727 to 1730**, Euler also held the rank or post of **medical lieutenant in the Russian navy**. This was salaried state service rather than evidence that he practiced as a shipboard physician.

In **1730**, he became professor of physics and a full Academy member. When Daniel Bernoulli returned to Basel in **1733**, Euler succeeded him as senior professor of mathematics. The higher salary enabled his marriage to Katharina Gsell.

### Scientific work

This period established Euler internationally.

- He solved the **Basel problem**, showing in 1735 that  
  \[
  \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{6}.
  \]
- He developed infinite products, continued fractions, differential equations, number theory, mechanics, and astronomical computation.
- His two-volume *Mechanica* (1736) recast mechanics through analysis rather than relying mainly on synthetic geometry.
- His *Tentamen novae theoriae musicae* (1739) applied mathematical ratios to musical consonance.
- He contributed to geodesy and the construction of a general map of the Russian Empire.
- His 1736 solution of the Königsberg bridges problem, published in 1741, is conventionally treated as a foundation of graph theory and topology.

### Health crisis and loss of the right eye

In **1735**, Euler suffered a severe illness, often described as fever. Some accounts link it to extreme exertion while completing an urgent astronomical calculation; that causal explanation is not medically demonstrable.

By **1738**, he had lost most or all sight in his **right eye**. Later portraits show a damaged or closed right eye. Sources have variously attributed the loss to fever, an abscess, overwork, or inflammation. The precise diagnosis is unresolved.

**Legend versus evidence:** The statement that Euler “worked himself blind” is an attractive moral anecdote but not an established medical finding.

### Political and institutional pressures

Following Empress Anna’s death in 1740, Russian court politics became unstable. Foreign academy members faced uncertainty, and Euler had a growing family. Frederick II of Prussia offered him a position in Berlin. Euler departed Saint Petersburg in June 1741 but retained connections to the Russian Academy and continued to receive part of his Russian salary.

## Berlin and Charlottenburg, 1741–1766

### Appointment and Academy building

Euler arrived in **Berlin in July 1741**. Frederick II was reorganizing the old Brandenburg Society of Sciences into the **Royal Prussian Academy of Sciences**. Euler became director of its mathematical class and played a central role in recruiting members, editing publications, judging prize submissions, managing finances, and advising the crown.

The reorganized Academy formally opened in **1746**. **Pierre-Louis Moreau de Maupertuis** became president. Euler carried much of the routine scientific and administrative labor without receiving the presidency.

From roughly **1753 to 1765**, especially after Maupertuis withdrew and then died in 1759, Euler functioned as the Academy’s administrative leader with royal approval, although Frederick did not make him president.

### Residences and property

Euler initially lived in Berlin. By the early 1750s he was financially secure enough to buy a **country estate in Charlottenburg**, then outside the central city. His widowed mother, sister-in-law, children, and other household members lived there.

During the **Seven Years’ War**, Russian forces entering the Berlin region in **1760** plundered or damaged Euler’s Charlottenburg property. When the Russian commander learned whose estate it was, compensation was reportedly arranged; Empress Elizabeth is said to have added a further payment. The broad episode is reported in serious biographies, although exact losses and compensation figures vary.

Euler had stable academic salaries, book income, prize money, and property but was not a commercial entrepreneur. No evidence was found of speculative companies, hidden business structures, patents, or private financial scandals.

### Service to the Prussian state

Euler’s Berlin work was not confined to abstract mathematics. Projects included:

- Hydraulic and canal problems.
- Pumps, fountains, and water systems.
- Cartography and geodesy.
- Ship theory and naval architecture.
- Telescopes, achromatic lenses, and optical instruments.
- Artillery and exterior and interior ballistics.
- Actuarial and demographic questions.
- Academy calendars and almanacs.
- Educational texts.

A fountain system at **Sanssouci** became an institutional embarrassment. Euler warned that the contractors’ machinery and piping were inadequate; Frederick later mocked the failed fountain and “vanity of geometry.” Accounts sometimes turn the episode into proof that Euler himself designed a useless system. The documentary position is more complicated: he advised on parts of the scheme and analyzed its defects, while practical construction involved other personnel.

### Major Berlin publications

The Berlin period produced the core of Euler’s mature synthesis:

- *Methodus inveniendi lineas curvas maximi minimive proprietate gaudentes* (1744), a foundational work in the calculus of variations.
- *Neue Grundsätze der Artillerie* (1745), a German translation and extensive expansion of Benjamin Robins’s *New Principles of Gunnery*.
- *Rettung der göttlichen Offenbarung gegen die Einwürfe der Freygeister* (1747), defending divine revelation.
- *Introductio in analysin infinitorum* (1748), two volumes.
- *Scientia navalis* (1749), two volumes on ship theory.
- *Theoria motus lunae* (1753), lunar theory.
- *Institutiones calculi differentialis* (1755).
- Studies of rigid-body mechanics, fluid motion, optics, astronomy, number theory, elastic curves, and acoustics.

### Euler and the “German princess”

From **1760 to 1762**, Euler wrote a series of pedagogical letters in French for **Friederike Charlotte of Brandenburg-Schwedt** and, according to some accounts, her younger sister. They covered mechanics, optics, sound, astronomy, logic, philosophy, and religion. Published from 1768 as *Lettres à une princesse d’Allemagne*, they became one of the century’s most widely read works of scientific popularization.

The princess was not Euler’s romantic partner. Modern internet retellings sometimes emphasize that she was an adolescent and Euler wrote 234 letters to her, implying an unusual private intimacy. The documentary context is court-sponsored education through formal instructional letters.

### Relations with Frederick II

Euler and Frederick differed in temperament and intellectual allegiance. Frederick favored French literary culture and philosophical wit; Euler was a devout, methodical mathematician with limited interest in court performance. Frederick mocked Euler’s damaged eye with the nickname **“Cyclops”** and apparently considered him insufficiently polished for the presidency.

Their relationship deteriorated over:

- Academy administration.
- The handling of the Maupertuis–König dispute.
- Frederick’s intervention in appointments and finances.
- Technical failures attributed to Academy advice.
- Frederick’s efforts to recruit **Jean le Rond d’Alembert** as president in 1763.
- The king’s refusal to place Euler formally at the Academy’s head.

D’Alembert declined the presidency and reportedly indicated that Euler was the appropriate choice. Frederick still did not appoint Euler.

### Maupertuis–König controversy

In the early 1750s **Samuel König** claimed that Leibniz had anticipated Maupertuis’s principle of least action and accused Maupertuis of plagiarism. König could not produce the original Leibniz letter, only a copy or extract whose authenticity was challenged.

Euler was asked to investigate. He sided with Maupertuis and supported the Academy judgment accusing König of falsification or fraud. **Voltaire**, aligned with König and hostile to Maupertuis, satirized the affair in *Diatribe du docteur Akakia*. Euler was not the principal target but was included in the ridicule.

Evidentiary classification:

- **Documented fact:** Euler participated institutionally and supported Maupertuis.
- **Documented accusation:** The Academy accused König of producing an inauthentic document.
- **Unresolved historical question:** The lost Leibniz document and the exact priority of formulations related to least action remain subjects of historical interpretation.
- **Not established:** That Euler knowingly suppressed authentic evidence or committed fraud.

### Departure

Catherine II offered Euler highly favorable terms to return to Saint Petersburg: commonly reported as **3,000 rubles annually**, a pension for his wife, and positions for his sons. Euler sought Frederick’s permission. The king reacted angrily and delayed or obstructed aspects of the departure, but Euler left Berlin in **1766**.

## Second Saint Petersburg Period, 1766–1783

### Return and family appointments

Euler returned to Saint Petersburg with much of his household in 1766. His eldest son **Johann Albrecht Euler** became professor of physics at the Academy and its conference secretary in 1769. **Johann Christoph Euler**, after Prussian military service, entered the Russian army and eventually became a general.

Catherine II’s patronage gave the family a high level of financial security. This was patronage through imperial academic service, not evidence of partisan political office.

### Blindness

Soon after his return, a cataract developed in Euler’s remaining left eye. By the late 1760s he was nearly blind.

In **1771**, cataract surgery—usually described as couching—temporarily restored some sight. Postoperative complications then left him effectively or completely blind. Accounts blaming Euler for failing to obey medical instructions derive from later biographical reporting and should not be treated as a secure diagnosis of personal fault.

Euler adapted by dictating calculations and prose. Assistants read papers aloud, recorded formulas, and checked manuscripts. Important helpers included:

- **Johann Albrecht Euler**.
- **Christoph Euler**.
- **Wolfgang Ludwig Krafft**.
- **Anders Johan Lexell**.
- **Nicolas Fuss**, invited from Basel in 1772 and later Euler’s assistant and grandson-in-law.

Euler’s memory and ability to calculate mentally were exceptional, but the image of a solitary blind genius understates the household and academy labor that converted dictation into publishable mathematics.

### Fire of 1771

On **29 May 1771** Old Style/9 June New Style, a major Saint Petersburg fire destroyed Euler’s home and many possessions. A servant, usually named **Peter Grimm**, carried or led the blind Euler to safety. Euler’s manuscripts were reportedly saved in substantial part, although property and library losses were serious.

Catherine II financed replacement housing. The often-repeated claim that Euler saved “only himself and his manuscripts” is a compressed anecdotal form; servants and neighbors materially enabled the rescue.

### Later productivity

Euler produced almost half of his surviving work after returning to Russia. In 1775 he was reportedly averaging approximately one mathematical paper per week. Blindness altered his working procedure but did not end his research.

Major late projects included:

- *Vollständige Anleitung zur Algebra* (1768–1770 editions; often dated 1770 as a two-volume work).
- *Institutiones calculi integralis* (1768–1770).
- *Dioptrica* (1769–1771).
- *Theoria motuum lunae, nova methodo pertractata* (1772).
- Studies of planetary perturbations, lunar motion, optics, continued fractions, partitions, number theory, fluid mechanics, and geometry.
- Extensive dictated papers published posthumously.

### Family losses

Katharina died in **1773** after nearly forty years of marriage. Euler married Salome Abigail Gsell in 1776.

Several adult children died before him: Charlotte in 1780 and Katharina Helena in 1781. Eight children had already died in infancy or early childhood. The cumulative impact is evident from the family chronology, although no reliable evidence supports later attempts to diagnose Euler with a psychological disorder or prolonged breakdown.

## Education, Employment, Offices, and Honors

### Education

- Basel Gymnasium, from about 1713.
- University of Basel, matriculated 1720.
- Master of Philosophy, 1723.
- Theological, Greek, and Hebrew studies after 1723.
- Independent mathematical study under Johann Bernoulli’s weekly supervision.

### Employment and appointments

- 1727: Adjunct or associate in the mathematical-physical division, Saint Petersburg Academy.
- 1727–1730: Medical lieutenant or medical officer in the Russian navy.
- 1730: Professor of physics and full Academy member.
- 1733: Professor of mathematics, succeeding Daniel Bernoulli.
- 1741: Member and mathematical-class director in Berlin.
- 1746: Director of the mathematical class in the reorganized Prussian Academy.
- 1753/1759–1765: De facto leader of Academy administration, without the presidency.
- 1766–1783: Senior member of the Saint Petersburg Academy.

### Memberships and honors

Euler was elected to, or associated with:

- Imperial Academy of Sciences, Saint Petersburg.
- Prussian/Brandenburg Academy of Sciences.
- Paris Academy of Sciences as a foreign associate.
- Royal Society of London as a foreign fellow.
- Other European academies and learned bodies.

He won or shared **twelve Paris Academy prizes**, according to modern astronomical scholarship; six concerned astronomical subjects. Prize counts can differ depending on whether shared awards and later competitions are classified separately.

No Nobel Prize or comparable modern award existed in his lifetime.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

Euler formed no known private company and held no patents in the modern sense. His practical work occurred within imperial academies, state ministries, military institutions, and publishing systems.

### Russian cartography

Euler helped Joseph-Nicolas Delisle’s geographical department calculate and organize materials for a general map of the Russian Empire. He became involved in quality control and complained about administrative weaknesses. The Russian atlas completed in 1745 drew on a large institutional project rather than Euler alone.

### Navigation and shipbuilding

His prize essay on mast placement led to deeper work on naval architecture. *Scientia navalis* treated equilibrium, stability, resistance, propulsion, and ship motion analytically. Practical shipwrights sometimes found his assumptions too idealized, but the work became part of the mathematization of naval engineering.

### Artillery and military technology

Euler translated Benjamin Robins’s ballistics work and added extensive calculus-based commentary. His models affected artillery education in Britain, France, Prussia, and later the United States.

This work served military states and improved understanding of projectile motion, gun pressure, and resistance. That is a documented military application, not evidence that Euler personally directed combat or political violence.

Specialist histories identify technical errors:

- An extra factor in a pressure-vessel calculation.
- Rejection of the “Robins effect” in projectile deflection.
- Dependence on incomplete experimental data.
- Idealized fluid assumptions with limited direct engineering applicability.

### Hydraulics and turbines

Euler developed general equations of fluid motion and analyzed turbines and hydraulic machines. The modern Euler turbine equation bears his name. There is no evidence that he owned or commercially exploited a turbine business.

### Optics

Euler investigated lens construction, chromatic aberration, telescopes, microscopes, and physiological vision. He disputed Newton’s conclusion that chromatic aberration made effective achromatic refracting lenses impossible. Correspondence involving James Short and John Dollond shows how theory, experiment, priority, and patent-era instrument making intersected.

### Actuarial and demographic work

Euler analyzed annuities, mortality, population growth, and pension calculations. These were mathematical studies useful to states and insurance systems, not documented personal investments.

### Publishing and finances

Euler received salaries from the Saint Petersburg and Berlin academies, sometimes simultaneously; prize payments; royalties or honoraria; state compensation; and property income. He bought a Charlottenburg estate and later received a replacement Saint Petersburg house after the fire.

No reliable evidence was found for insolvency, embezzlement, tax evasion, secret partnerships, money laundering, or corrupt enrichment.

## Political, Religious, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

### Political position

Euler was not a party politician or public political theorist. His career depended on monarchical patronage under Catherine I, Anna, Elizabeth, Frederick II, and Catherine II. He negotiated salaries and appointments skillfully and sought protection for his family.

His work nevertheless served state priorities: mapping imperial territory, military ballistics, navigation, hydraulic construction, astronomical prediction, and academy prestige. His institutional accommodation should not be converted either into a claim of political neutrality or into unsupported allegations of ideological complicity.

### Religion

Euler remained a Reformed Christian throughout his life. His *Rettung der göttlichen Offenbarung gegen die Einwürfe der Freygeister* argued for the divine inspiration of Scripture against freethinking objections.

In *Letters to a German Princess*, he addressed God, spirit, matter, freedom, causation, and revelation. His Christianity coexisted with mathematical natural philosophy; there is no documented conversion crisis or secret atheism.

### Philosophy

Euler opposed aspects of **Christian Wolff’s** system and **Leibnizian monadology**, which he regarded as insufficiently grounded in quantitative natural laws. His criticism of Leibnizian metaphysics did not prevent him from using and developing Leibniz’s mathematical notation.

Euler’s philosophical physics combined analytical technique, mechanical explanation, and theological commitment. His views on space, matter, force, and causation influenced younger thinkers, including Immanuel Kant, although Kant did not simply adopt Euler’s system.

### Mathematical style

Euler treated functions, equations, series, and infinitesimals as productive calculational objects, sometimes before nineteenth-century definitions of limit and convergence existed. Later mathematicians supplied stricter foundations. Describing all Eulerian infinite-series reasoning as “invalid” imposes later standards too bluntly; describing it all as rigorous by modern standards is equally inaccurate.

## Scientific and Mathematical Contributions

### Analysis and notation

Euler consolidated or popularized:

- \(f(x)\) for a function.
- \(e\) for the base of natural logarithms.
- \(i\) for \(\sqrt{-1}\).
- Extensive use of \(\pi\), although he did not invent the symbol.
- Trigonometric functions in forms close to modern usage.
- The exponential relation  
  \[
  e^{ix}=\cos x+i\sin x,
  \]
  with the special case \(e^{i\pi}+1=0\).

Many notation claims require the word **popularized**, not “invented,” because symbols had prior sporadic users.

### Number theory

Euler:

- Solved the Basel problem.
- Developed the Euler product connecting the zeta function with prime numbers.
- Proved Fermat’s little theorem in generalized form.
- Introduced the totient function.
- Worked on quadratic reciprocity, partitions, perfect numbers, amicable numbers, and Diophantine equations.
- Corresponded with Christian Goldbach about the conjecture now bearing Goldbach’s name.

### Mechanics and rigid bodies

Euler formulated equations for rotating rigid bodies, developed the calculus of variations with Lagrange, analyzed buckling and elastic columns, and recast Newtonian mechanics in systematic analytic language.

### Fluid mechanics

The Euler equations describe inviscid fluid flow. Euler’s ideal-fluid model cannot by itself represent viscosity and boundary-layer effects; this limitation produced results such as zero theoretical drag in circumstances where real bodies experience resistance.

### Astronomy

He worked on:

- Lunar motion and the three-body problem.
- Perturbations of Jupiter and Saturn.
- Comets and planetary orbits.
- Solar and lunar tables.
- Precession and celestial mechanics.

Euler, Clairaut, and d’Alembert initially considered whether discrepancies in lunar apsidal motion required modifying Newton’s inverse-square law. Improved approximations ultimately removed the apparent need.

### Geometry and topology

The Königsberg bridges paper abstracted a problem from distances and shapes to incidence and connectivity. Euler’s polyhedron formula \(V-E+F=2\) became foundational, though its valid domain required later clarification.

### Logic

Euler used circles to depict relations among classes in the *Letters to a German Princess*. These became known as **Euler diagrams**. Similar diagrammatic ideas had predecessors; Euler’s importance lies in his influential presentation and transmission.

### Music

The *Tentamen novae theoriae musicae* sought a mathematical measure of musical agreeableness. Its reputation as “too mathematical for musicians and too musical for mathematicians” is a later aphoristic summary, not a securely sourced contemporary verdict.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Johann I Bernoulli

Teacher and advocate. Bernoulli recognized Euler’s ability, persuaded Paul Euler to permit a mathematical career, and maintained correspondence with him.

### Daniel Bernoulli

Friend, host, colleague, collaborator, correspondent, and scientific competitor. They shared interests in fluid mechanics, elasticity, probability, and astronomy. Euler often defended Daniel’s scientific priority, including in the family conflict over *Hydrodynamica*.

### Niklaus II Bernoulli

Friend and intermediary in Euler’s appointment at Saint Petersburg. Niklaus died before Euler arrived.

### Christian Goldbach

Academy official and principal number-theory correspondent. Their exchange comprised approximately 196 surviving letters over thirty-five years. Goldbach’s 1742 letter stated the conjecture now associated with his name.

### Pierre-Louis Moreau de Maupertuis

Academy president, colleague, and recipient of Euler’s institutional support. Euler defended him during the König priority controversy.

### Jean le Rond d’Alembert

Correspondent, rival, and scientific critic. Disputes concerned priority, vibrating strings, fluids, lunar theory, and Academy affairs. Euler acknowledged d’Alembert’s priority for two disputed papers in an Academy notice.

### Alexis-Claude Clairaut

Correspondent and competitor in lunar theory, gravitation, and Earth-shape research. Their disputes combined real technical disagreement with priority management.

### Joseph-Louis Lagrange

Younger correspondent and beneficiary of Euler’s patronage. When Lagrange sent work on isoperimetric problems, Euler recognized its importance, delayed publication of related work, and helped secure Lagrange’s election to the Berlin Academy. Calling Euler Lagrange’s formal doctoral adviser is anachronistic.

### Frederick II

Royal patron, employer, critic, and eventual institutional antagonist. Their relationship combined salary and protection with ridicule, technical disagreement, and conflict over Academy control.

### Catherine II

Imperial patron who induced Euler’s return with salary, family provisions, and housing support after the fire.

### Nicolas Fuss

Assistant, pupil, later grandson-in-law, academy secretary, and author of the principal contemporary eulogy.

### Anders Johan Lexell

Mathematician and astronomer, assistant and colleague in Euler’s last years. He was with Euler on the day of his death.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Technical Failures

### Dedicated adversarial-search finding

Searches combining Euler’s name with *controversy, scandal, allegations, lawsuit, fraud, accusations, conspiracy,* and *criticism*, including results from **2024–2026**, found no credible evidence that Euler was investigated or charged for financial crime, sexual misconduct, corruption, espionage, or ordinary criminal activity. Recent results chiefly recycle blindness anecdotes, mathematical memes, the Diderot legend, false-attribution theories, and historical priority controversies.

### Priority disputes with d’Alembert

**Documented fact:** D’Alembert objected that Euler’s 1749 papers used results for which d’Alembert deserved priority. Euler printed an acknowledgment in the Berlin Academy’s journal.

This is evidence of a genuine priority dispute and a corrective acknowledgment, not proof of deliberate plagiarism.

### Lunar-theory controversy

Euler, Clairaut, and d’Alembert competed over lunar apsidal motion. They circulated sealed and date-stamped papers to protect priority and criticized one another in correspondence and reference works.

- **Documented fact:** Priority claims and mutual criticism occurred.
- **Not established:** Fraudulent appropriation by Euler.
- **Historical assessment:** Their successive approximations contributed collectively to resolving the anomaly within Newtonian gravitation.

### Maupertuis–König affair

Euler participated in an Academy process that declared König’s evidence fraudulent. Voltaire depicted the proceeding as authoritarian protection of Maupertuis.

- The proceeding and Euler’s alignment are documented.
- König’s missing original document prevents a fully conclusive modern judgment.
- There is no evidence Euler fabricated documents himself.

### Ballistics and engineering errors

Specialist historians identify incorrect calculations and idealizations in Euler’s artillery work. These include the pressure-cylinder factor and denial of a projectile-deflection effect reported by Robins.

These are documented scientific errors, not fraud. Euler published criticism and extensions using the incomplete experimental knowledge available.

### Infinite series and modern rigor

Euler assigned values or transformations to divergent series in ways that later analysts rejected or reinterpreted. Some manipulations can be justified through modern summability or analytic continuation; others cannot be read literally under standard convergence.

This is a major technical criticism of Euler’s work but not evidence of intellectual dishonesty.

### Euler’s sum-of-powers conjecture

Euler conjectured that at least \(n\) positive \(n\)th powers were required to sum to another \(n\)th power. Counterexamples were found in the twentieth century:

- L. J. Lander and T. R. Parkin refuted the fifth-power case in 1966.
- Noam Elkies and Roger Frye later produced fourth-power counterexamples.

The conjecture’s falsity is established. It was proposed as a conjecture, not represented as a proved theorem.

### Military applications

Euler’s artillery studies improved military calculation and training. Modern ethical criticism may examine his service to Prussian and Russian military states, but no evidence was found that Euler commanded forces, selected targets, or participated directly in violence.

### Court patronage and imperial institutions

Euler benefited financially from autocratic states and helped their academies, military science, mapping, and administration. This is documented patronage. No evidence was found that he held intelligence responsibilities or participated in political repression.

## Illnesses, Injuries, and Death

### Documented or strongly reported conditions

- Severe febrile illness in 1735.
- Destruction or atrophy of the right eye by about 1738.
- Cataract in the left eye during the 1760s.
- Near-total or total blindness following complications after cataract surgery in 1771.
- No documented addiction.
- No well-supported psychiatric diagnosis.
- No documented imprisonment or exile.

### Final day

On **18 September 1783**, Euler spent part of the day working and discussing the orbit of **Uranus**, discovered by William Herschel in 1781, with Anders Johan Lexell. After dining with his family, he suffered a sudden cerebral event and died within hours.

Contemporary and near-contemporary accounts describe the cause as **apoplexy** or a **brain hemorrhage**. Without an autopsy report, the exact modern diagnosis cannot be established.

Condorcet’s memorial phrasing—that Euler “ceased to calculate and to live”—became the canonical literary description of his death.

## Burial and Posthumous Treatment of the Remains

Euler was buried beside Katharina in the **Smolensk Lutheran Cemetery** on Vasilievsky Island.

In **1837**, the Russian Academy erected a new monument after the original marker deteriorated.

In **1957**, during commemorations of the 250th anniversary of his birth, his remains or tomb were transferred to the **Lazarev Cemetery at the Alexander Nevsky Monastery** in Leningrad/Saint Petersburg.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### The Euler–Diderot “proof of God”

**Story:** Catherine II, worried that Denis Diderot was spreading atheism, arranged a confrontation. Euler approached Diderot and declared an algebraic expression—commonly rendered as  
\[
\frac{a+b^n}{n}=x,\quad \text{therefore God exists; reply!}
\]
Diderot, allegedly ignorant of mathematics, was humiliated and fled Russia.

**Appearance and circulation:** The story appears in Dieudonné Thiébault’s recollections of Berlin, published around 1801–1804, decades after the supposed event. Augustus De Morgan later embellished it, and popular histories repeatedly reproduced it.

**Evidence against:**

- No contemporary court record or Euler–Diderot account substantiates the confrontation.
- Diderot was not mathematically illiterate; he published mathematical work.
- The formula varies across retellings.
- Thiébault’s version was retrospective and anecdotal.

**Classification:** **Myth/apocryphal anecdote.**

**Reputational effect:** It constructed Euler as the pious mathematical genius defeating Enlightenment atheism, while depicting Diderot as an arrogant but innumerate philosophe.

### “He worked himself blind”

**Story:** Euler lost his sight because he solved an urgent astronomical problem in three days without sleep or because relentless calculation destroyed his eyes.

**Evidence:** He suffered serious illness before loss of the right eye, while the remaining eye later developed a cataract. No medical record establishes calculation as the cause.

**Classification:** **Unverified causal legend built around documented illness and blindness.**

### The failed Sanssouci fountain

**Story:** Euler’s abstract mathematics produced a fountain system that would not work, whereupon Frederick mocked the uselessness of geometry.

**Evidence:** The fountain failed; Euler advised on hydraulics and warned of inadequate equipment. Construction responsibility was distributed among engineers and contractors.

**Classification:** **Documented project failure with disputed assignment of personal responsibility.**

### “Everything is named after Euler”

**Story:** Euler discovered so many results that theorems are named after the first person to rediscover them after him.

**Origin:** Often called “Stigler’s law” or an informal joke among mathematicians.

**Evidence:** Euler did anticipate numerous later results, but no systematic naming rule exists.

**Classification:** **Humorous professional folklore expressing real breadth.**

### “One paper every week while blind”

Euler’s 1775 output is estimated at roughly one paper per week, but publication dates do not always equal composition dates, and assistants were indispensable.

**Classification:** **Broadly supported statistical summary, often simplified into heroic legend.**

### “Half his work was written after total blindness”

Approximately half his output belongs to the second Saint Petersburg period, when he was severely visually impaired and later blind. He did not spend that entire period totally blind.

**Classification:** **Substantially true but chronologically compressed.**

### The servant who saved Euler

A servant named Peter Grimm reportedly rescued Euler during the 1771 fire. Retellings range from carrying him on his back to leading him through smoke.

**Classification:** **Reported fact with dramatized variants.**

### “Euler’s hoax” conspiracy literature

A fringe publication encountered in the adversarial search alleges that Euler participated in a long-running ideological fraud concerning mathematics or scientific history. It provides no credible archival chain establishing fabrication by Euler and belongs to polemical conspiracy literature.

**Classification:** **Unsupported conspiracy allegation.**

### Internet sexualized fabrications

Recent meme and social-media material includes invented vulgar stories and jokes about Euler. No eighteenth-century evidence supports them.

**Classification:** **Modern fabricated humor, not biography.**

## Works by Leonhard Euler

### Comprehensive bibliography

The authoritative practical census is the **Eneström Index**, *Die Schriften Eulers chronologisch nach den Jahren geordnet, in denen sie verfasst worden sind* (1910–1913). It assigns identifiers **E1–E866**. The University of the Pacific’s Euler Archive provides searchable records, scans, publication dates, translations, and Eneström numbers.

The *Leonhardi Euleri Opera Omnia*, initiated by the Swiss Euler Commission in 1907 and first published in 1911, is organized as:

- **Series I:** Mathematics.
- **Series II:** Mechanics and astronomy.
- **Series III:** Physics and miscellaneous subjects.
- **Series IVA:** Scientific correspondence.
- Additional planned or digital materials: notebooks, manuscripts, and general correspondence.

A complete item-level bibliography should cite the Eneström catalogue rather than reproduce a selective list as though it were exhaustive.

### Principal books and independent works

- **1736 — *Mechanica sive motus scientia analytice exposita***, 2 vols., E15–E16. Analytical mechanics.
- **1738, 1740 — *Einleitung zur Rechen-Kunst zum Gebrauch des Gymnasii bey der Kayserlichen Academie der Wissenschaften in St. Petersburg***, 2 vols. Arithmetic textbook.
- **1739 — *Tentamen novae theoriae musicae ex certissimis harmoniae principiis dilucide expositae***, E33. Mathematical music theory.
- **1744 — *Methodus inveniendi lineas curvas maximi minimive proprietate gaudentes, sive solutio problematis isoperimetrici latissimo sensu accepti***, E65. Calculus of variations.
- **1745 — *Neue Grundsätze der Artillerie, enthaltend die Bestimmung der Gewalt des Pulvers…***, E77. Translation and extensive commentary on Benjamin Robins.
- **1747 — *Rettung der göttlichen Offenbarung gegen die Einwürfe der Freygeister***. Theology and apologetics.
- **1748 — *Introductio in analysin infinitorum***, 2 vols., E101–E102. Functions, infinite series, exponentials, logarithms, trigonometry, and analytic geometry.
- **1749 — *Scientia navalis, seu tractatus de construendis ac dirigendis navibus***, 2 vols., E110–E111.
- **1753 — *Theoria motus lunae exhibens omnes eius inaequalitates***. First major lunar theory.
- **1755 — *Institutiones calculi differentialis cum eius usu in analysi finitorum ac doctrina serierum***, E212.
- **1762 — *Constructio lentium objectivarum ex duplici vitro***. Achromatic lens theory.
- **1765 — *Theoria motus corporum solidorum seu rigidorum***, E289.
- **1768–1772 — *Lettres à une princesse d’Allemagne sur divers sujets de physique et de philosophie***, three published volumes, E343, E344, E417. Based on letters of 1760–1762.
- **1768–1770 — *Institutiones calculi integralis***, 3 vols., E342, E366, E385.
- **1769–1771 — *Dioptrica***, 3 vols. Optics and instrument theory.
- **1770 — *Vollständige Anleitung zur Algebra***, 2 vols., E387–E388. Dictated with assistance; later translated widely.
- **1772 — *Theoria motuum lunae, nova methodo pertractata***. Revised lunar theory.
- **1783–1785 — *Opuscula analytica***, 2 vols.; publication straddled his death.
- **1785 onward — *Mémoires posthumes* and Academy publications.** Genuine Euler manuscripts edited and printed after his death.
- **1862 — *Opera postuma mathematica et physica***, edited posthumously by Paul-Heinrich Fuss and Nicolas Fuss; genuine writings in later compilation.

### Selected consequential papers

- **1726 —** Paper on isochronous curves in a resisting medium, Euler’s first printed article.
- **1736/1741 — *Solutio problematis ad geometriam situs pertinentis***, E53. Königsberg bridges.
- **1737 — *Variae observationes circa series infinitas***. Includes the Euler product conception.
- **1744 —** Papers on elastic curves and the calculus of variations.
- **1748 — *Introductio* treatment of \(e^{ix}\), trigonometric functions, and complex exponentials.
- **1752 — *Découverte d’un nouveau principe de mécanique***, E177.
- **1755–1757 —** Foundational papers on inviscid fluid equations.
- **1758 — *Elementa doctrinae solidorum***. Polyhedral relation.
- **1761 — *Solutio facilis problematum quorundam geometricorum difficillimorum***. Geometric results.
- **1763 — *Theoremata arithmetica nova methodo demonstrata*** and related number-theory memoirs.
- **1765–1770 —** Studies of rigid rotation, buckling, lunar motion, lenses, and integral calculus.
- **Posthumously published —** Numerous papers on partitions, Diophantine equations, continued fractions, and special functions.

## Posthumous Publication, Reputation, and Legacy

### Immediate reputation

Euler was already regarded as Europe’s leading analyst before his death. Condorcet’s and Fuss’s memorials presented him as extraordinarily productive, personally modest, religious, domestically attached, and intellectually tireless. These accounts are indispensable but partly hagiographic.

The Saint Petersburg Academy possessed such a backlog of manuscripts that it continued publishing Euler’s papers for decades. Claims that he “outpublished the living from the grave” are rhetorical, but the long posthumous publication program is factual.

### Nineteenth-century reception

Lagrange, Laplace, Gauss, Cauchy, Poisson, Jacobi, and later analysts built upon Euler’s methods while revising foundations. Nineteenth-century rigor exposed weaknesses in the treatment of divergent series, infinitesimals, and unrestricted formulas, yet Euler’s results repeatedly survived in reformulated form.

His textbooks helped standardize the language of analysis, algebra, mechanics, and trigonometry.

### Opera Omnia

The Swiss Academy of Sciences established the Euler Commission in **1907**. The first *Opera Omnia* volume appeared in **1911**. The project expanded as new manuscripts were identified. By the early twenty-first century approximately eighty volumes had appeared; the remaining correspondence and manuscript work increasingly moved to digital publication.

### Named concepts

Euler’s name is attached to hundreds of objects, including:

- Euler’s formula and identity.
- Euler characteristic.
- Euler line.
- Euler angles.
- Euler equations in fluid dynamics and rigid-body motion.
- Euler–Lagrange equation.
- Euler–Bernoulli beam theory.
- Euler buckling.
- Euler product.
- Euler totient function.
- Euler numbers and polynomials.
- Euler–Maclaurin formula.
- Euler–Mascheroni constant.
- Euler method for differential equations.
- Euler diagrams.
- Eulerian paths and circuits.
- Euler spiral.
- Euler force and Euler turbine equation.

Not every eponym denotes sole discovery; many encode later historical naming conventions.

### Public commemoration

Euler has appeared on Swiss banknotes and postage stamps. Statues, plaques, schools, competitions, astronomical objects, and mathematical societies bear his name. His grave remains a site of scientific commemoration in Saint Petersburg.

### Historical reinterpretation

Modern scholarship has complicated several older images:

- Euler was not an isolated calculator but a central node in a vast correspondence network.
- His blindness-era output depended on assistants, sons, editors, and institutional printers.
- His work was deeply embedded in imperial mapping, military science, court patronage, and academy politics.
- His religious commitment was neither a late legend nor reducible to the Diderot anecdote.
- His mathematics combined remarkable discovery with historically conditioned standards of proof and occasional error.
- The label “Swiss mathematician” remains correct but does not capture his long service to Russia and Prussia.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are included. Mere chronological overlap has been excluded.

### Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz — documented intellectual connection

Euler adopted and substantially developed Leibnizian differential notation and infinitesimal analysis. He opposed Leibniz’s monadology and some Leibniz–Wolff metaphysics. During the Maupertuis–König affair, Euler evaluated König’s claim that a lost Leibniz letter anticipated the principle of least action. There was no personal meeting: Leibniz died in 1716, when Euler was nine.

### Sir Isaac Newton — documented intellectual connection

Euler’s 1723 master’s dissertation compared Newtonian and Cartesian philosophy. His mechanics and celestial astronomy analytically developed Newtonian laws, while his lunar work tested whether apparent anomalies required modifying inverse-square gravitation. There was no documented meeting; Newton died in March 1727, before Euler left Basel.

### René Descartes — documented intellectual connection

Descartes was the other principal subject of Euler’s master’s dissertation. Euler inherited analytical geometry from the Cartesian tradition while participating in its eighteenth-century transformation. No personal meeting was possible.

### Christiaan Huygens — documented intellectual influence

Euler’s mechanics, pendulum theory, optics, and mathematical physics operated within fields shaped by Huygens. This is a documented intellectual lineage, not a personal relationship; Huygens died in 1695.

### Johannes Kepler — documented intellectual influence

Euler’s celestial mechanics worked with planetary laws originating in Kepler’s astronomy and transformed them through Newtonian dynamics and perturbation theory. No personal connection existed.

### Nicolaus Copernicus — documented intellectual influence

Euler’s astronomy presupposed heliocentric planetary theory. This is a historical intellectual connection only.

### Galileo Galilei — documented intellectual influence

Euler’s mechanics and projectile studies developed a mathematical-physical tradition in which Galileo’s work was foundational. No direct relationship existed.

### Archimedes of Syracuse — documented intellectual influence

Euler’s work on mechanics, hydrostatics, centers of gravity, and geometry belonged to an explicitly classical mathematical tradition descending from Archimedes. No direct connection was possible.

### Joseph-Louis Lagrange — documented correspondence, patronage, and collaboration

Lagrange sent Euler mathematical work from Turin beginning in the 1750s. Euler recognized his ability, corresponded on the calculus of variations, deferred publication of related work, and supported Lagrange’s election and eventual appointment in Berlin. Their connection was direct and documented, although describing Euler as Lagrange’s formal doctoral adviser is misleading.

### Immanuel Kant — documented transmission and intellectual influence

Kant sent Euler his first publication. Kant later cited Euler’s natural philosophy and used or adapted Eulerian logical diagrams. The consulted scholarship says Kant “knew Euler well,” but it does not establish a face-to-face meeting; the safest description is documented exchange and influence.

### Carl Friedrich Gauss — documented posthumous influence

Gauss was six when Euler died. Euler’s number theory, infinite series, analysis, and astronomical computation provided major foundations for Gauss’s later work. No personal relationship existed.

### Pierre-Simon Laplace — documented posthumous/disciplinary influence

Laplace extended the analytical celestial mechanics developed by Euler, Clairaut, d’Alembert, and Lagrange. Popular tradition attributes to Laplace high praise of Euler as a master of mathematics, but the exact wording and source of several such quotations require caution. No direct meeting was established in the consulted material.

### Augustin-Louis Cauchy — documented posthumous influence and correction

Cauchy’s rigorous analysis reformulated subjects Euler had developed, especially infinite series, functions, and calculus. Cauchy was born in 1789 and could not have met Euler.

### Siméon Denis Poisson — documented posthumous disciplinary influence

Poisson’s mathematical physics followed the Euler–Lagrange–Laplace analytical tradition. He was born in 1781, so no meaningful personal relationship is documented.

### Jean-Baptiste Joseph Fourier — documented disciplinary influence

Fourier’s analysis of heat built on the mathematical analysis, trigonometric expansions, and differential equations developed in the Eulerian tradition. No documented personal relationship was found.

### Johann Elert Bode — no direct connection established

Both worked in astronomy, and Bode was active during Euler’s final decades, but the consulted sources did not establish a meeting, correspondence, collaboration, or explicit personal influence. Mere disciplinary overlap is insufficient.

### Benjamin Franklin — no supported direct connection found

Franklin moved through European scientific and diplomatic circles during Euler’s lifetime, but no meeting or correspondence with Euler was established in the consulted sources.

### Emanuel Swedenborg — no supported direct connection found

Swedenborg and Euler both had connections to eighteenth-century scientific institutions and Saint Petersburg-era networks, but no documented personal crossing was established here.

### Other roster members

The atlas contains numerous mathematicians, physicists, philosophers, and astronomers whose later work ultimately depended on Euler, including James Clerk Maxwell, William Rowan Hamilton, Arthur Cayley, Felix Klein, Amalie Emmy Noether, Albert Einstein, Richard Feynman, Alan Turing, John von Neumann, and Edward Witten. The sources consulted for this dossier do not document personal crossings, and a general chain of disciplinary inheritance is too diffuse for individual atlas entries. Absence of an entry is therefore a finding, not an assertion that Euler had no long-term influence on them.

- **1760–1762:** Writes the future *Lettres à une princesse d’Allemagne*.
- **1763:** Frederick offers Academy presidency to d’Alembert, who declines.
- **1765:** Publishes rigid-body mechanics.
- **1766:** Leaves Berlin and returns to Saint Petersburg; vision in left eye deteriorates.
- **1768:** First volumes of *Lettres* and integral calculus appear.
- **1769–1771:** *Dioptrica* published.
- **1770:** *Vollständige Anleitung zur Algebra* published.
- **1771:** Saint Petersburg fire destroys home; cataract surgery briefly improves sight, followed by effective total blindness.
- **1772:** Nicolas Fuss arrives; new lunar theory published.
- **1773, 10 November:** Katharina Euler dies.
- **1775:** Reported production reaches approximately one paper weekly.
- **1776:** Marries Salome Abigail Gsell.
- **1780:** Daughter Charlotte dies.
- **1781:** Daughter Katharina Helena dies; Uranus discovered, later studied by Euler and Lexell.
- **1783, 18 September:** Suffers apoplexy/brain hemorrhage and dies in Saint Petersburg.
- **1783–1830s:** Saint Petersburg Academy continues publishing his manuscript backlog.
- **1837:** Academy installs a replacement grave monument.
- **1843:** Paul-Heinrich Fuss publishes major correspondence collection.
- **1862:** *Opera postuma* published.
- **1907:** Swiss Academy establishes Euler Commission.
- **1911:** First *Opera Omnia* volume appears.
- **1910–1913:** Eneström catalogue establishes E1–E866 numbering.
- **1957:** Grave transferred to the Lazarev Cemetery at Alexander Nevsky Monastery.
- **2007:** International tricentenary commemorations and major scholarly publications.
- **2022:** Remaining portions of the *Opera Omnia* correspondence project move toward digital-first publication.
- **2024:** Bernoulli–Euler Society transfers a major collection of Euler manuscript and letter copies to the University Library Basel.
- **2024–2026:** Adversarial search reveals no new credible criminal or personal scandal; contemporary online material chiefly recirculates legends and memes.
