# William Blake: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier concerns the English poet, painter, designer, printmaker, and visionary William Blake (1757–1827), not later people, organizations, or legal proceedings bearing the same name.

Evidence is labeled as follows:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a contemporary record, surviving work, manuscript, letter, official register, indictment, or strong archival catalogue.
- **Reported fact:** preserved in testimony or recollection, often recorded after Blake’s death, but not independently documented.
- **Disputed interpretation:** a scholarly disagreement about evidence, meaning, influence, or conduct.
- **Allegation:** an accusation made against Blake without a finding establishing it.
- **Rumor or myth:** a story whose documentary basis is weak, late, embellished, symbolic, or absent.

Blake’s biography presents unusual evidentiary problems. Alexander Gilchrist’s *Life of William Blake, “Pictor Ignotus”* (1863) remains indispensable because Gilchrist interviewed surviving members of Blake’s circle, but it appeared thirty-six years after Blake’s death and is especially weak on his childhood and family. Later archival work corrected or complicated several of its claims. Keri Davies and Marsha Keith Schuchard, for example, reconstructed Moravian connections in Blake’s maternal family that Gilchrist did not know.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

William’s father, **James Blake** (c.1722–1784), was a hosier or haberdasher operating from the family premises in Broad Street. His mother, **Catherine Wright Blake** (1725–1792), had previously married Thomas Armitage.

Recent archival scholarship establishes that Catherine and her first husband participated in the London Moravian community. This has altered older portrayals of Blake’s household as simply Anglican or generically dissenting. It does not establish that William himself was raised as a formal Moravian. The surviving evidence instead indicates a family environment containing Protestant dissent, intense devotional language, and some distance from established religious institutions.

The family was artisanal and commercially respectable rather than aristocratic, destitute, or university-educated. The hosiery business supported drawing lessons and an expensive apprenticeship for William. Blake inherited neither a landed estate nor lasting financial security.

### Siblings

Parish and family records identify seven children born to James and Catherine, two apparently dying in infancy:

- **James Blake** (1753–1827), who continued the family hosiery business and later helped William.
- **John Blake** (1755–1800), about whom comparatively little is securely known.
- **William Blake** (1757–1827).
- **John Blake**, an earlier child of that name who died in infancy.
- **Richard Blake**, who died in infancy.
- **Catherine Elizabeth Blake** (1764–1841).
- **Robert Blake** (1767–1787), William’s favorite younger brother and pupil.

Exact lists vary in older biographies because the two short-lived children and reused given names were incompletely understood before parish research.

### Robert Blake

Robert became William’s student and shared his artistic interests. He died in February 1787, probably after a prolonged illness described as consumption.

- **Reported fact:** William remained at Robert’s bedside and later said that he saw Robert’s spirit rise through the ceiling “clapping its hands for joy.”
- **Historical significance:** whether understood as literal testimony, grief vision, or later hagiography, the story became central to Blake’s identity as a seer.
- **Reported fact:** Blake later attributed the idea of illuminated printing to Robert’s spirit appearing to him in a dream or vision. No contemporary technical memorandum confirms this supernatural account.

### Marriage to Catherine Boucher

Blake married **Catherine Sophia Boucher** at St Mary’s Church, Battersea, on 18 August 1782. She was the daughter of William Boucher, a market gardener. The marriage register indicates that Catherine signed with a mark and was probably unable to write at the time. Blake taught her reading, writing, drawing, printing, and coloring.

Catherine became:

- his household manager;
- a printer and colorist;
- an assistant in producing illuminated books;
- a practical participant in selling and preserving his works;
- his principal caregiver and companion.

No children are documented. No reliable evidence establishes an illegitimate child, sustained extramarital partnership, or formal separation.

#### The concubine story

- **Rumor or myth:** Gilchrist reported that, during distress over childlessness, Blake proposed introducing a concubine or second wife, supposedly in a Swedenborgian spirit, and abandoned the proposal when Catherine wept.
- **Evidence:** the story appeared decades later, lacks a contemporary letter or independent witness, and may reflect Victorian efforts to dramatize Blake’s sexual heterodoxy.
- **Assessment:** it should not be treated as an established event.
- **Reputational effect:** it contributed to portrayals of Blake as a free-love radical and Catherine as the long-suffering but triumphant wife.

Accounts from friends generally portray the marriage as affectionate, unusually collaborative, and materially difficult. Tatham reported that the couple were almost never separated. Such recollections may idealize the marriage but are broadly compatible with their continuous shared work and residence.

### Westminster Abbey

During his apprenticeship, Basire reportedly sent Blake to draw monuments in Westminster Abbey, partly to remove him from conflicts among fellow apprentices. Blake made measured studies of Gothic tombs and sculpture. This experience decisively shaped his preference for linear, monumental, medieval, and early Renaissance art over fashionable academic oil painting.

### Felpham

In September 1800 William and Catherine moved to Felpham at the invitation of poet and biographer **William Hayley**. Blake initially described the place in ecstatic terms. The Sussex coast, garden, cottage, and open sky entered his visionary imagination. Relations with Hayley later deteriorated as Blake experienced patronage as pressure to produce commercially acceptable miniatures and engravings.

Blake returned permanently to London in 1803. There is no evidence of foreign travel, formal exile, transportation, or imprisonment.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1757–1772

Blake was born into commercial Soho during the Seven Years’ War. London was an expanding imperial metropolis marked by artisan production, poverty, public executions, religious dissent, print culture, and political agitation.

He did not attend a conventional grammar school. Gilchrist reported that his parents recognized his unusual temperament and avoided ordinary schooling. This is plausible but not documented through school records.

### Childhood visions

The best-known stories include:

- At approximately age eight or ten, Blake saw a tree at Peckham Rye filled with angels.
- He saw “God” or a divine head at a window.
- He saw angelic figures among haymakers.
- He later saw the prophet Ezekiel beneath a tree.
- His parents allegedly considered punishing him for lying, but his mother intervened.

All are **reported facts or myths**, preserved in later recollections rather than contemporary childhood records. Blake indisputably described visionary perception as real throughout adulthood; the precise childhood episodes remain unverified.

### Drawing education

Around 1767–1768, Blake entered the drawing school of **Henry Pars** in the Strand. His father bought him prints and casts to copy. The young Blake preferred Raphael, Michelangelo, Dürer, and other historical masters. According to later accounts, he objected to the fashionable artists preferred by contemporaries.

His parents reportedly could not afford the route to becoming a painter but secured an apprenticeship in engraving, a trade with clearer economic prospects.

---

## Education, Apprenticeship, and Formation, 1772–1782

### James Basire

On 4 August 1772 Blake was apprenticed for seven years to **James Basire**, engraver to the Society of Antiquaries and the Royal Society. The premium was substantial, demonstrating family investment in his career.

Basire practiced the older linear engraving tradition. This gave Blake:

- exact copperplate technique;
- disciplined draftsmanship;
- knowledge of historical monuments;
- experience preparing archaeological and antiquarian publications;
- an enduring commitment to outline.

Blake later attacked fashionable stipple engraving and painters whose effects depended upon color or atmospheric imitation. His allegiance to line partly derived from Basire’s workshop.

### Gordon Riots

In June 1780, while London was convulsed by the anti-Catholic Gordon Riots, Blake was reportedly swept along with a crowd that attacked Newgate Prison.

- **Reported fact:** early biographers place him at or near the front of the mob.
- **Unresolved question:** no evidence establishes that he joined from ideological commitment, participated in violence, or merely became caught in the crowd.
- Blake was never charged in connection with the riots.
- Later claims that he “led” the attack are exaggerations.

### Royal Academy Schools

Blake registered at the Royal Academy Schools in October 1779. There he studied drawing from casts and living models and encountered artists including **John Flaxman** and **Thomas Stothard**.

He clashed intellectually with the academic ideals associated with Sir Joshua Reynolds. Blake favored Michelangelo, Raphael, Dürer, Gothic art, and sharply defined form. His hostility toward Reynolds intensified after Reynolds’s death and became part aesthetic principle, part professional polemic.

Blake exhibited at the Royal Academy in 1780 and intermittently thereafter. He never became a Royal Academician.

## Marriage, First Publications, and the Print Shop, 1782–1790

### Catherine and early married life

The Blakes initially lived at 23 Green Street. Catherine learned to assist in printing and coloring. Their household operated increasingly as a workshop in which authorship, printing, painting, binding, and distribution were combined.

### Poetical Sketches

In 1783 *Poetical Sketches* was printed at the expense or encouragement of friends, principally John Flaxman and the Reverend Anthony Stephen Mathew and Harriet Mathew. It was not conventionally published for sale and probably circulated in a small edition.

The poems had been written over preceding years and show Elizabethan, Shakespearean, Spenserian, and proto-Romantic influences. Surviving copies are rare.

### Parker & Blake

After James Blake senior died in 1784, William entered partnership with his former fellow apprentice **James Parker**. “Parker & Blake” operated a print shop at 27 Broad Street.

The enterprise failed or dissolved quickly. By 1785 William and Catherine had moved to Poland Street. Causes were probably limited capital, insufficient trade, and incompatible business expectations; no evidence indicates bankruptcy fraud or embezzlement.

### Joseph Johnson’s circle

Blake worked as an engraver for publisher **Joseph Johnson**, whose dinners brought together religious dissenters, scientists, artists, and political radicals. Blake certainly engraved books issued by Johnson.

His direct meetings with **Mary Wollstonecraft**, **Thomas Paine**, **William Godwin**, and Joseph Priestley are widely reported, but the evidence differs by person. Presence within overlapping Johnson networks is stronger than documentation of close friendship. Blake engraved illustrations for Wollstonecraft’s *Original Stories from Real Life* (1791), providing a concrete professional connection.

### Robert’s death and illuminated printing

Robert Blake died in 1787. William’s grief and reported vision of Robert became bound to his technical breakthrough.

Around 1788 Blake developed the process he called illuminated printing. He wrote and drew in an acid-resistant medium directly on copper. Acid removed the surrounding surface, leaving words and designs in relief, permitting printing more like a relief block than an intaglio engraving. Blake and Catherine then colored many impressions by hand.

The method allowed control of writing, illustration, printing, and coloring, but it did not create mass-market editions. Copies vary in color, order, state, and finish; each is partly an individual art object.

## Lambeth and the Illuminated Books, 1790–1800

The Blakes moved to 13 Hercules Buildings, Lambeth, in 1790. This decade produced the core of Blake’s self-published oeuvre.

### Major works and sequence

- *All Religions are One* (c.1788)
- *There is No Natural Religion* (c.1788)
- *Songs of Innocence* (1789)
- *The Book of Thel* (c.1789–1791)
- *The Marriage of Heaven and Hell* (c.1790–1793)
- *Visions of the Daughters of Albion* (1793)
- *America: A Prophecy* (1793)
- *Europe: A Prophecy* (1794)
- *Songs of Innocence and of Experience* (1794)
- *The First Book of Urizen* (1794)
- *The Song of Los* (1795)
- *The Book of Los* (1795)
- *The Book of Ahania* (1795)
- the large color prints, c.1795
- manuscript work toward *Vala, or The Four Zoas*, begun c.1796–1797

### Political development

Blake welcomed the American and French revolutions as manifestations of liberation from priestly and monarchical authority. *America* names Washington, Franklin, Paine, and revolutionary figures within Blake’s mythology. *The French Revolution*, projected in seven books, survives only in proof or manuscript form for its first book.

After the Terror and European war, Blake’s politics became less representational and more mythic. He did not simply become conservative: he continued attacking monarchy, war, slavery, economic exploitation, sexual repression, and established religion. But he also criticized revolutions that reproduced coercion.

No documentary evidence establishes membership in a revolutionary society, espionage network, Jacobin organization, or violent conspiracy. His radicalism is established chiefly through texts, imagery, associations, and the later sedition prosecution.

### Religion and mythology

Blake treated the Bible as a living poetic and prophetic field, not a fixed ecclesiastical code. Central principles included:

- the divinity of human imagination;
- opposition to “single vision,” materialism, and restrictive rationalism;
- the productive necessity of contraries;
- hostility to punitive or law-centered representations of God;
- Jesus as divine imagination and forgiveness;
- rejection of priestcraft and institutional coercion;
- restoration of divided humanity.

His evolving figures included:

- **Albion**, the universal or national human;
- **Urizen**, restrictive reason, law, measurement, and authority;
- **Los**, prophetic imagination and creative labor;
- **Enitharmon**, complex embodiment of emanation, gender, space, and spiritual division;
- **Orc**, revolutionary energy;
- the four **Zoas**, divided powers of the primordial human.

These are not a stable creed. Blake repeatedly revised their relationships.

### Swedenborg

Blake read Emanuel Swedenborg and attended the General Conference of the New Jerusalem Church in London in April 1789; William and Catherine Blake appear among the signatories to its conference propositions.

*The Marriage of Heaven and Hell* borrows from and satirizes Swedenborg. Blake accepted spiritual perception and correspondences but rejected Swedenborg’s systematization, clerical followers, and what he regarded as limited imagination.

### Slavery and race

Blake opposed slavery and depicted its violence, notably in engravings after designs by John Gabriel Stedman for *Narrative of a Five Years’ Expedition against the Revolted Negroes of Surinam* (1796). His images include brutal punishment of enslaved people.

This record is not uncomplicated:

- As a commercial engraver, Blake reproduced Stedman’s colonial images rather than witnessing Suriname.
- *The Little Black Boy* asserts spiritual equality but uses Blackness and whiteness within a Christianized symbolic scheme that modern readers have criticized as paternalistic, assimilationist, or racist.
- Other scholars read the poem as a critique of evangelical abolitionism’s Eurocentric language.
- *Visions of the Daughters of Albion* joins sexual coercion and slavery but speaks through mythic figures rather than a documentary account of enslaved persons.
- **Disputed interpretation:** Blake’s anti-slavery intention is strongly evidenced; whether particular representations escape contemporary racial hierarchy remains contested.

### Gender and sexuality

*Visions of the Daughters of Albion* condemns sexual possession, victim-blaming, jealousy, and repressive marriage. Blake opposed chastity imposed as law and associated free erotic energy with liberation.

However, Blake’s mythological narratives sometimes represent women through male-authored symbolic roles. In *America*, Orc’s revolutionary liberation is entangled with an episode interpretable as sexual assault. Feminist scholarship disputes whether Blake exposes, rationalizes, or fails to resolve the violence.

No reliable evidence identifies Blake as a participant in an organized “free love” movement; that label is largely retrospective.

### Commercial work

Blake continued engraving for Johnson and other publishers. Major commissions included illustrations or plates for works by:

- Mary Wollstonecraft;
- Erasmus Darwin;
- John Gabriel Stedman;
- Thomas Gray;
- Edward Young;
- Richard Gough;
- John Caspar Lavater;
- various educational, antiquarian, medical, and literary publications.

Commercial engraving supplied much of the household’s cash, though commissions were irregular.

## Felpham, Hayley, and the Sedition Prosecution, 1800–1804

### Patronage by William Hayley

Hayley offered Blake work illustrating and engraving books and encouraged miniature portraiture. Blake initially praised him. He illustrated Hayley’s *Ballads* and engraved the frontispiece for *A Father’s Memoirs of His Child*.

Tension developed because Hayley treated Blake principally as a craftsman who needed commercially viable employment. Blake increasingly believed Hayley misunderstood or obstructed his prophetic vocation.

Their relationship was neither a simple friendship nor a simple betrayal. Surviving letters show gratitude, dependence, irritation, strategic deference, and eventual repudiation.

### Altercation with John Scolfield

On 12 August 1803 Blake found **Private John Scolfield**, a soldier in the First Regiment of Dragoons, in or near his garden. Blake ordered him away and physically escorted him toward the Fox Inn.

Scolfield alleged that Blake uttered words including:

- “Damn the King”;
- “The soldiers are all slaves”;
- assertions that he would fight for Bonaparte or that France needed no English king.

Formulations vary across the surviving legal papers and accounts.

Blake denied the sedition and characterized the prosecution as “a fabricated perjury.” He admitted the confrontation but maintained that the political statements were invented.

### Legal process

- **Documented fact:** Scolfield swore a complaint.
- **Documented fact:** Blake was indicted at the Petworth quarter sessions in October 1803.
- **Documented fact:** he was tried at the Chichester quarter sessions on 11 January 1804.
- **Documented fact:** he was acquitted.
- **Documented fact:** he was not imprisoned or convicted.
- **Unresolved question:** the precise words spoken during the quarrel cannot be reconstructed.
- **Disputed interpretation:** some scholars regard local military and magistrate hostility as contributing to the case; stronger claims of an organized conspiracy against Blake are unproved.

The prosecution occurred during renewed war with France, fear of invasion, and repression of suspected sedition. Blake’s earlier radical writings made the allegation plausible to authorities, but the acquittal means the legal charge was not established.

The event entered *Milton* and *Jerusalem* through Blake’s mythologizing of Felpham, accusation, enslavement, and spiritual warfare.

## Return to London, Professional Conflict, and the 1809 Exhibition, 1803–1812

The Blakes returned to 17 South Molton Street in 1803.

### Thomas Butts

Civil servant **Thomas Butts** was one of Blake’s most important patrons. From the 1790s onward he purchased biblical watercolors and temperas and supported Blake through years of poor sales. Their relationship supplied both income and a body of commissioned religious art.

### Cromek, Stothard, and *The Grave*

Publisher **Robert Hartley Cromek** commissioned Blake to design illustrations for Robert Blair’s *The Grave*. Cromek then assigned the engraving to **Luigi Schiavonetti**, judging Blake’s engraved execution commercially unsuitable. Blake received payment for designs but lost the more remunerative engraving work.

A further conflict involved Thomas Stothard’s painting *The Canterbury Pilgrims*. Blake believed Cromek had taken his idea to Stothard; others disputed Blake’s priority. Blake produced his own Canterbury Pilgrims design and engraving.

- **Documented fact:** competing works and commercial disputes existed.
- **Allegation by Blake:** Cromek and Stothard appropriated his conception or acted treacherously.
- **Unresolved question:** available evidence does not establish theft in a legal sense.
- **Effect:** Blake’s suspicion and bitterness intensified, and friendships deteriorated.

### The 1809 exhibition

Blake staged a one-man exhibition in 1809 in rooms over his brother James’s hosiery shop at 28 Broad Street. He issued *A Descriptive Catalogue*, presenting his historical and visionary art and defending linear design.

The exhibition failed commercially and attracted little attention. Robert Hunt’s review in *The Examiner* mocked Blake as an “unfortunate lunatic” and described the works as unintelligible and badly drawn.

- The review is a **documented hostile contemporary judgment**, not a medical diagnosis.
- Blake angrily answered Hunt in private writings and associated his critics with a “nest of villains.”
- Later accounts often treat the exhibition as the nadir of his public career.

### Mental state

Blake’s letters from the Felpham and post-Felpham periods describe depression, spiritual conflict, persecution, and recovered confidence. At times he interpreted professional setbacks through elaborate hostile agencies.

There is no surviving clinical record, institutional confinement, or contemporary diagnosis based on examination. The word “mad” was frequently used polemically against unfashionable artists, religious enthusiasts, and radicals.

Modern retrospective diagnoses—psychosis, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, migraine aura, synesthesia—remain hypotheses. David Worrall’s 2024 study argues that the evidence does not support psychotic illness and explores hallucination, migraine, and synesthetic phenomena. Retrospective diagnosis cannot be conclusive.

## Obscurity, Renewal, and the Final Circle, 1812–1821

Blake’s production became less publicly visible after 1809, but he continued writing, painting, engraving, revising *Jerusalem*, and making commissions.

### John Linnell

George Cumberland Jr. introduced Blake to the younger painter **John Linnell** in 1818. Linnell became Blake’s most important late patron, buyer, advocate, and facilitator.

Linnell:

- commissioned Blake’s engraving work;
- introduced him to patrons;
- supported the *Illustrations of the Book of Job*;
- commissioned the Dante series;
- made loans and payments to Blake and later Catherine;
- brought Blake into contact with younger artists.

Their surviving financial relationship demonstrates that Blake’s final years were supported by patronage rather than complete destitution.

### John Varley and Visionary Heads

Astrologer and artist **John Varley** encouraged Blake, around 1819–1820, to draw figures he said appeared to him. These “Visionary Heads” included historical, biblical, legendary, and monstrous beings.

The best-known is **The Ghost of a Flea**.

- **Reported fact:** Varley said Blake conversed with visionary sitters and drew them as they appeared.
- **Documented fact:** the drawings and Varley’s written accounts survive.
- **Unresolved question:** whether Blake understood every sitting literally, imaginatively, performatively, or humorously.
- The later description “séance” may overstate the formal character of the sessions.

### The Ancients

Around 1824 a group of younger artists, later called **the Ancients**, gathered around Blake and Samuel Palmer. Members included:

- Samuel Palmer;
- George Richmond;
- Edward Calvert;
- Frederick Tatham;
- Francis Oliver Finch;
- Henry Walter;
- John Varley and others at the group’s margins.

They revered Blake as a prophetic elder and called him “the Interpreter.” The group’s pastoral, biblical, archaizing art was strongly shaped by him.

## Final Major Projects, 1821–1827

### *Illustrations of the Book of Job*

Linnell commissioned a set derived from earlier Job watercolors. Blake engraved twenty-one plates between approximately 1823 and 1826. They were issued in 1826.

The series integrated image, border quotations, biblical typography, and interpretive design. It brought Blake one of his few substantial late successes and favorable notices, though not financial independence.

### Thornton’s Virgil

For Dr. Robert John Thornton’s school edition of Virgil’s *Pastorals* (1821), Blake cut small wood engravings. Their rough, concentrated landscapes initially troubled the publisher but later profoundly influenced Samuel Palmer and British wood engraving.

### Dante

In 1824 Linnell commissioned Blake to illustrate Dante’s *Divine Comedy*. Blake produced approximately 102 designs in varying degrees of completion and seven engraved plates. He was still working on them at his death.

The designs do not merely illustrate Dante. Blake annotated and criticized Dante’s theology, politics, punishment, and relation to classical authority. The project remained unfinished.

### Financial position

Blake never accumulated substantial wealth. His finances depended upon:

- commercial engraving fees;
- purchases by Thomas Butts, George Cumberland, Linnell, and other patrons;
- occasional book and print sales;
- support from his brother James;
- Catherine’s workshop and sales labor.

He appears to have rented rather than owned his principal residences. No company holdings, securities portfolio, patents, landholdings, colonial investments, or substantial estate are documented.

“Poverty-stricken” is broadly accurate but can obscure variation. Blake endured persistent insecurity and very modest living conditions; he was not continuously without work or patrons.

---

## Death and Burial

During 1827 Blake suffered from a digestive or biliary illness that he called “that sickness to which there is no name.” Biographers have proposed gallstones, cholangitis, liver disease, or another gastrointestinal disorder, but no postmortem examination establishes the cause.

He continued working on Dante drawings from bed.

Blake died at approximately six in the evening on Sunday, 12 August 1827, at 3 Fountain Court.

### Deathbed accounts

- **Reported fact:** George Richmond said Blake drew Catherine’s portrait on his final day, saying she had always been an angel to him.
- **Reported fact:** witnesses said he sang hymns or songs about the heaven he believed he was entering.
- **Reported fact:** he expressed confidence in salvation through Jesus Christ.
- These accounts came from affectionate observers and may contain devotional shaping, but they are the closest surviving testimony.

The burial register records:

> 17 August 1827. William Blake. Age 69 years. Brought from Fountain Court, Strand.

He was placed in a common grave at Bunhill Fields. Later reuse and redesign of the cemetery obscured the exact plot. Researchers Carol and Luis Garrido reconstructed the coordinates from burial records; a new inscribed stone was unveiled at the identified site in 2018. The body was not exhumed or scientifically identified.

---

## Catherine Blake After William’s Death

Catherine preserved and sold Blake’s works and lived for periods with John Linnell and Frederick Tatham. Evidence indicates financial hardship: she borrowed money from Linnell and relied upon work, sales, and friends.

She died on 18 October 1831. Tatham later claimed that she called to William as though he were present.

Ownership of Blake’s remaining works passed substantially to Tatham. Tatham preserved much but, according to later testimony, destroyed some writings or designs after joining the Irvingite religious movement and considering them spiritually dangerous.

- **Reported fact:** Tatham destroyed Blake material.
- **Unresolved question:** the exact quantity and identity of lost works cannot be established.
- This is one reason Blake’s surviving corpus may be incomplete.

## Employment, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Institutions

- Henry Pars’s drawing school.
- James Basire’s engraving workshop.
- Royal Academy Schools.
- Society of Antiquaries projects through Basire.
- Joseph Johnson’s publishing network.
- Parker & Blake print shop.
- Informal New Jerusalem/Swedenborgian conference, 1789.
- Hayley’s Felpham literary and patronage circle.
- The Ancients, as inspirational elder rather than formal officer.

### Illuminated printing

Blake’s technical process combined:

1. writing and drawing with acid-resistant material on copper;
2. acid etching away the unprotected surface;
3. relief printing of the remaining design;
4. selective color printing or monochrome printing;
5. watercolor and ink finishing;
6. assembly into book form.

Scholars continue debating some details of his color-printing procedure. No evidence shows that Blake patented it. Its economic significance lay less in mass production than in giving him unusually integrated artistic control.

### Business structure

“Parker & Blake” was a conventional small partnership and shop, not a corporation. Blake’s later workshop was essentially a two-person household enterprise with Catherine.

There is no evidence of:

- a registered patent;
- large-scale employees;
- stock issuance;
- overseas business holdings;
- plantation ownership;
- money laundering;
- securities speculation;
- fraudulent subscription schemes;
- formal insolvency proceedings.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Politics

Blake’s politics were anti-authoritarian but not reducible to a later party ideology. His targets included:

- monarchy and imperial war;
- church-state coercion;
- punitive law;
- economic exploitation;
- child labor;
- prostitution and sexual double standards;
- slavery;
- confinement of imagination by rationalist systems.

He celebrated revolutionary energy while depicting revolution’s capacity to become another tyranny. His phrase “One Law for the Lion & Ox is Oppression” expresses hostility to abstract uniformity, not a developed legislative program.

### Religion

Blake rejected orthodox doctrines when they made God a punitive external ruler. Yet he remained intensely Christ-centered. He attacked deism, natural religion, atheistic materialism, and church authority while using biblical prophecy as his fundamental language.

He read or absorbed traditions associated with:

- the Bible;
- Milton;
- Swedenborg;
- Jakob Böhme;
- Paracelsian and alchemical imagery;
- Neoplatonism;
- Protestant dissent;
- Kabbalistic and hermetic traditions, though the extent of direct reading is debated.

Claims that Blake possessed secret initiation into an ancient occult order are unsupported.

### Art

Blake opposed the supremacy of oil painting, generalized beauty, and academic imitation of nature. He defended:

- definite outline;
- imaginative rather than merely optical vision;
- Gothic and early Renaissance art;
- integration of text and image;
- historical, biblical, and mythological subject matter;
- the artist’s independence from fashionable taste.

He sometimes misrepresented rivals or simplified art history in polemical service of these commitments.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Catherine Blake

Spouse, printer, colorist, assistant, financial manager, model, and posthumous custodian. Her labor is central but poorly documented because works were ordinarily attributed only to William.

### John Flaxman

Friend from the Royal Academy period; helped circulate Blake’s early poetry and connected him with patrons. The friendship cooled amid professional and personal tensions but did not become a documented legal dispute.

### Thomas Stothard

Early colleague and later rival. Their rupture centered on the Canterbury Pilgrims designs and Cromek’s publishing maneuvers.

### Henry Fuseli

Artist admired by Blake and associated with Johnson’s circle. Blake engraved after Fuseli. Their relationship combined affinity in imaginative art with Blake’s characteristic independence.

### Mary Wollstonecraft

Documented professional connection through Blake’s engravings for *Original Stories from Real Life*. Claims of deep personal intimacy are not supported by surviving correspondence.

### Thomas Paine

They occupied overlapping radical-publishing circles.

- **Rumor or myth:** Blake warned Paine to flee England immediately before authorities sought his arrest in 1792.
- The story appears in later biography and lacks contemporary corroboration.
- Paine did leave for France before he could be arrested, but Blake’s role is unproved.
- The tale strengthened Blake’s later image as an active revolutionary conspirator.

### William Hayley

Patron and employer whose support enabled Felpham but whose didactic commercial program Blake experienced as spiritual constraint.

### Thomas Butts

Long-term patron whose purchases preserved many biblical works and stabilized Blake’s income.

### George Cumberland

Friend, correspondent, collector, and advocate across decades.

### John Linnell

Late patron and friend. His commissions enabled the Job and Dante projects and connected Blake with the younger generation.

### Samuel Palmer

Younger artist and disciple. Palmer’s testimony helped shape the reverential posthumous image of Blake.

### Frederick Tatham

Disciple, witness, Catherine’s associate, and eventual custodian. His alleged destruction of material makes him both preserver and possible source of irrecoverable loss.

## Controversies, Allegations, Legal Proceedings, and Contested Material

### 1. The 1803–1804 sedition case

**Status:** formal accusation, indictment, trial, and acquittal.

Blake was accused of seditious statements after ejecting Private Scolfield from his garden. The prosecution is the only documented criminal case against him. He was acquitted and not imprisoned.

### 2. Gordon Riots involvement

**Status:** reported presence; level of participation unresolved.

Blake may have been carried with the crowd toward Newgate. There is no evidence of charge, conviction, leadership, or deliberate participation in anti-Catholic violence.

### 3. Appropriation disputes with Cromek and Stothard

**Status:** documented commercial conflict; theft allegation unresolved.

Blake considered himself cheated of engraving work and possibly of his Canterbury Pilgrims conception. Surviving circumstances show opportunism and rivalry but do not establish fraud through a court finding.

### 4. Accusations of madness

**Status:** hostile contemporary characterization and later diagnostic speculation.

Robert Hunt called Blake a lunatic in a hostile review. Friends described him as sane in ordinary dealings while accepting or tolerating his visions. No clinical diagnosis survives. Modern psychopathological labels remain speculative.

### 5. Race and slavery

**Status:** documented anti-slavery themes; contested representational politics.

Blake attacked enslavement and illustrated colonial brutality. Some poems nevertheless rely upon racial symbols and Christian universalism now criticized as paternalistic or Eurocentric. Scholarship disagrees about whether *The Little Black Boy* reproduces or critiques abolitionist racial hierarchy.

### 6. Gender, rape, and revolutionary symbolism

**Status:** disputed interpretation of texts.

Feminist criticism has identified contradictions between Blake’s sexual liberation and scenes in which women’s bodies become instruments of male revolutionary myth. The contested episode involving Orc in *America* has been described variously as rape, ambiguous sexual union, or a deliberate critique of revolutionary desire. No consensus exists.

### 7. Marriage and alleged concubinage

**Status:** late biographical anecdote, unverified.

The claim that Blake proposed a concubine because Catherine was childless is not supported by contemporary evidence.

### 8. Professional paranoia

**Status:** documented suspicions; psychological interpretation disputed.

Blake accused various patrons and rivals of conspiracy, theft, or obstruction. Some grievances had commercial foundations; broader persecution narratives cannot be independently established.

### 9. “Jerusalem” and nationalism

The lyric beginning “And did those feet in ancient time,” later set to music by Hubert Parry, is often treated as a straightforward patriotic anthem. In context it is the preface to *Milton* and asks whether England can be spiritually transformed from “dark Satanic Mills.”

Its use by imperial, nationalist, socialist, suffrage, labor, sporting, and ecclesiastical movements is posthumous and often detaches it from Blake’s anti-authoritarian mythology.

### 10. Absence of other alleged wrongdoing

The dedicated adversarial search found no credible evidence that the historical William Blake was implicated in:

- financial fraud;
- corruption;
- money laundering;
- sexual prosecution;
- domestic-violence proceedings;
- plagiarism adjudicated by a court;
- intelligence service activity;
- organized political violence;
- colonial property ownership;
- imprisonment;
- substance addiction.

Modern search results concerning “William Blake House,” “Blake v Fox,” and Blake Lively are unrelated namesakes.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Self-Created Mythology

### Angels in the tree

- **Story:** child Blake saw a tree filled with angels at Peckham Rye.
- **Appearance:** recorded by nineteenth-century biographers from later testimony.
- **Evidence:** no contemporary record.
- **Effect:** became the paradigmatic image of Blake’s visionary childhood.

### God at the window

- **Story:** Blake saw God’s head at a window and screamed.
- **Evidence:** late anecdotal testimony.
- **Effect:** used both hagiographically and to support claims of hallucination.

### Robert ascending through the ceiling

- **Story:** Blake saw his brother’s spirit rise joyfully at death.
- **Evidence:** posthumous recollection.
- **Effect:** joined grief, resurrection, and artistic vocation in Blake biography.

### Robert revealing illuminated printing

- **Story:** Robert’s spirit told Blake how to combine poetry and design through relief etching.
- **Evidence:** later report; the technical process itself is documented, its supernatural origin is not.
- **Effect:** converted workshop invention into prophetic revelation.

### Saving Thomas Paine

- **Story:** Blake warned Paine that arrest was imminent and told him to leave England.
- **Evidence:** no contemporary corroboration.
- **Effect:** made Blake an active hero of radical underground history.

### Naked recitation in the garden

- **Story:** visitors found William and Catherine naked in their Lambeth garden, reciting or impersonating Adam and Eve in *Paradise Lost*.
- **Evidence:** late anecdote of uncertain transmission.
- **Interpretations:** literal naturism, theatrical recitation, joke, or Victorian embroidery.
- **Effect:** reinforced Blake’s image as sexually and socially unconventional.

### Ghost of a Flea

- **Story:** the spirit of a flea appeared and explained that fleas contain the souls of bloodthirsty men.
- **Evidence:** Varley’s account and Blake’s surviving image; the supernatural claim is unverifiable.
- **Effect:** one of the most durable examples of Blakean grotesque vision.

### “Mad Blake”

- **Story:** Blake was an isolated lunatic whose masterpieces resulted from insanity.
- **Origin:** hostile reviews, incomprehension of the prophetic books, and sensational biography.
- **Evidence against a simple version:** sustained marriage, complex technical work, commercial commissions, friendships, correspondence, and coherent revision over decades.
- **Effect:** alternately marginalized him and made him attractive to later avant-gardes.

### The completely neglected genius

- **Story:** nobody recognized Blake until long after his death.
- **Correction:** he was poorly known and commercially unsuccessful, but he had patrons, exhibitors, readers, commissions, and a devoted late circle. Notices appeared during his lifetime, and an obituary and early biographical accounts followed soon after.
- **Effect:** the “unknown genius” narrative became central to Romantic ideas of artistic martyrdom.

### “Dark Satanic Mills”

- **Story:** Blake coined the phrase specifically for industrial factories.
- **Evidence:** factories are a plausible association, but scholars also connect the phrase to churches, universities, rationalist systems, war machinery, Albion’s spiritual condition, and Blake’s own experience of oppressive artistic production.
- **Assessment:** exclusive identification with factories is interpretive, not documentary fact.

### Blake as occult initiate

- **Story:** Blake belonged to a secret Gnostic, Masonic, druidic, or magical order.
- **Evidence:** he knew esoteric texts and participated briefly in a Swedenborgian conference, but no membership record establishes secret initiation.
- **Effect:** later occultists assimilated him into lineages he did not claim.

### Authorship qualifications

- Catherine Blake participated materially in printing and coloring, but surviving copies usually bear William’s name alone.
- *The French Revolution* is genuine but survives incompletely.
- *The Four Zoas* is genuine but unfinished and reconstructed editorially from a revised manuscript.
- Modern “complete works” are posthumous editorial compilations.
- “Jerusalem” as a hymn combines Blake’s genuine text with Hubert Parry’s 1916 music; Blake did not compose that musical setting.
- Some titles for individual artworks are modern curatorial descriptions rather than Blake’s own.
- Stories, sayings, and conversations reported by Tatham, Varley, Palmer, Cunningham, and Gilchrist are attributed testimony, not writings authored by Blake.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Early preservation, 1827–1863

Blake’s immediate circle—Catherine, Linnell, Palmer, Richmond, Calvert, and Tatham—preserved works and testimony. Cunningham’s 1830 biography mixed admiration with the conventional category of eccentric genius.

The seven Dante engravings were published posthumously in 1838. Blake remained a specialist interest.

### Rossetti, Gilchrist, and Victorian recovery

Alexander Gilchrist’s 1863 biography transformed Blake’s visibility. After Gilchrist died before completing it, Anne Gilchrist, Dante Gabriel Rossetti, and William Michael Rossetti helped bring the book to publication.

The Rossettis and Pre-Raphaelites emphasized Blake’s independence, spiritual intensity, medievalism, and union of poetry and art. They also transmitted anecdotes that strengthened the “visionary genius” mythology.

### Yeats and the 1893 edition

William Butler Yeats and Edwin Ellis produced a three-volume edition in 1893. Their textual scholarship was imperfect by later standards, but their symbolic interpretation made Blake important to the fin-de-siècle occult revival and literary modernism.

### William Morris and the private-press tradition

Blake’s integrated books anticipated ideals later associated with William Morris and the Arts and Crafts movement: resistance to industrial separation of design and production, the book as a total artwork, and reverence for premodern craft. This is a documented reception relationship, not a personal meeting; Morris was born seven years after Blake’s death.

### Modernism and twentieth-century criticism

Swinburne, Yeats, Edwin Ellis, Geoffrey Keynes, Northrop Frye, Kathleen Raine, David Erdman, and later scholars successively reconstructed Blake as:

- revolutionary;
- Christian visionary;
- symbolic mythmaker;
- psychological thinker;
- anti-imperial prophet;
- master printmaker;
- radical artisan;
- book artist.

No single reconstruction has displaced the others.

### Popular culture

Blake’s phrases and images entered common culture:

- “Tyger Tyger, burning bright”;
- “mind-forg’d manacles”;
- “dark Satanic Mills”;
- “To see a World in a Grain of Sand”;
- “The road of excess leads to the palace of wisdom”;
- *The Ancient of Days*;
- *The Great Red Dragon*;
- “Jerusalem.”

He influenced Beat writers, counterculture, psychedelic art, fantasy, comics, rock music, metal, film, and esoteric movements. Popular appropriation often extracts aphorisms from Blake’s larger systems.

### Institutional canonization

Major holdings are now maintained by the British Museum, British Library, Tate, Fitzwilliam Museum, Morgan Library, Huntington Library, Yale, Library of Congress, National Gallery of Victoria, and other collections.

The William Blake Archive provides copy-specific digital editions, essential because conventional typography cannot adequately represent the variant illuminated books.

## Atlas Connections

Only source-supported connections to names in the supplied atlas roster are included.

### Dante Alighieri — documented influence and posthumous artistic engagement

Blake began approximately 102 illustrations to Dante’s *Divine Comedy* after John Linnell commissioned the series in London in 1824. He continued until his death in 1827 and engraved seven plates. Blake admired Dante’s imaginative power while criticizing aspects of his punitive theology and political partisanship. There was necessarily no personal meeting.

### Emanuel Swedenborg — documented intellectual and institutional connection

Blake read and annotated Swedenborg and, with Catherine, attended the New Jerusalem Church’s General Conference in London in April 1789. *The Marriage of Heaven and Hell* adapts, parodies, and criticizes Swedenborg’s *Heaven and Hell*. The influence is direct but adversarial as well as receptive.

### Jacob Böhme — reported/documented intellectual influence

Blake knew the English mystical tradition in which Böhme was central and was repeatedly compared with or linked to Böhme by early readers. The exact extent of Blake’s direct reading at particular dates is less securely documented than his Swedenborg reading. The connection is intellectual, not personal.

### Dante Gabriel Rossetti — documented posthumous reception

Rossetti was born after Blake’s death. He acquired Blake’s Notebook in 1847, giving it the later name “Rossetti Manuscript,” helped edit Blake’s writings, and assisted Alexander and Anne Gilchrist’s *Life of William Blake*. Rossetti’s work was central to Blake’s Victorian revival.

### William Morris — documented posthumous influence

Morris did not meet Blake. Blake’s integrated books, Gothic sympathies, and resistance to divisions between artistic conception and craft became important precedents for Arts and Crafts and private-press culture. Morris participated in the Victorian environment in which Blake was recovered through Gilchrist and the Rossettis.

### William Butler Yeats — documented posthumous editorial and intellectual connection

Yeats, with Edwin John Ellis, edited *The Works of William Blake: Poetic, Symbolic, and Critical* (1893). Yeats’s symbolic and occult reading helped establish Blake as a precursor of modernism and esoteric literature. He also wrote about Blake’s Dante illustrations.

### Carl Gustav Jung — documented posthumous reception

Jung knew and cited Blake’s work and reproduced Blake images in *Psychology and Alchemy*. Later critics have compared Blake’s four Zoas, Albion, and divided psychic powers with Jungian archetypes. Similarities do not establish that Blake anticipated Jung’s complete system.

### Walter Benjamin — reported intellectual reception

Benjamin’s thought has often been compared with Blake’s treatment of history, image, and revolutionary time. No direct personal connection was possible. A specific, central Blake debt is less firmly documented than the Yeats or Rossetti relationships and should not be overstated.

### Jorge Luis Borges — documented posthumous literary reception

Borges wrote and lectured about Blake and repeatedly engaged the visionary and mystical literary tradition in which Blake and Swedenborg figured. The connection is literary reception, not personal contact.

### Allen Ginsberg — documented posthumous visionary and musical reception

Ginsberg, though not separately identifiable in the roster under his common name, reported a 1948 auditory vision while reading Blake and later recorded settings of the *Songs*. This connection is omitted from the formal roster count if the roster is interpreted strictly, but it is one of Blake’s most significant modern receptions.

### John Milton

Milton is not listed in the roster under his conventional name; the roster’s “John Milton Cage Jr.” is composer John Cage, not the seventeenth-century poet. No false atlas connection should be made. Milton nevertheless was one of Blake’s dominant influences and a character in Blake’s poem *Milton*.

### Mary Wollstonecraft Shelley

Blake had a documented professional connection to Mary Wollstonecraft, her mother, but no documented meeting or correspondence with Mary Shelley has been established. Mary Shelley was a child during Blake’s Johnson-circle years. Mere overlap in London Romantic culture is insufficient.

### Adam Smith, Immanuel Kant, Jean-Jacques Rousseau, Benjamin Franklin, George Washington, and Napoleon Bonaparte

Blake referred to, criticized, mythologized, illustrated political worlds involving, or responded intellectually to some of these figures. The sources consulted do not establish personal meetings. Names appearing in prophecy or polemic are literary/intellectual connections, not social relationships.

### Absence finding

Most of the 896 roster names have no documented direct intersection with Blake. Shared interests in mysticism, revolution, symbolism, architecture, psychology, or Romanticism are not sufficient evidence of a connection.
