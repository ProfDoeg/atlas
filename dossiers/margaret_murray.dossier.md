# Margaret Alice Murray: Research Dossier

## Research scope and evidentiary note

This dossier reconstructs Margaret Alice Murray’s life from institutional records, archival catalogues, her autobiography, archaeological publications, contemporary notices, later biographies, and scholarship on witchcraft historiography and modern Paganism.

Murray’s autobiography, *My First Hundred Years* (1963), is indispensable but selective. It minimizes her suffrage activity, occasionally compresses chronology, and was written at nearly one hundred years of age. Kathleen L. Sheppard’s archival biography and Ruth Whitehouse’s UCL study supply important corrections and institutional context.

Evidentiary labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by institutional, archival, contemporary, or multiple scholarly sources.
- **Reported fact:** credible secondary reporting, but the underlying record was not directly inspected.
- **Claim by Murray:** an autobiographical or scholarly assertion made by Murray that requires separate evaluation.
- **Disputed interpretation:** an interpretation for which credible scholars give materially different accounts.
- **Rejected historical thesis:** a claim once advanced as scholarship but no longer accepted by the relevant specialist field.
- **Rumor or myth:** a culturally significant story unsupported as literal history.
- **Unresolved:** surviving evidence does not permit a firm conclusion.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Margaret Alice Murray.
- **Common published forms:** Margaret A. Murray; M. A. Murray; Margaret Murray.
- **Titles and postnominals:** Dr. Margaret Murray; Assistant Professor of Egyptology at University College London; Fellow of University College London; honorary D.Litt.; Fellow of the Society of Antiquaries of Scotland; Fellow of the Royal Anthropological Institute.
- **Informal title:** “The Grand Old Woman of Egyptology,” a retrospective institutional and journalistic epithet.
- **Birth:** 13 July 1863.
- **Birthplace:** Calcutta, Bengal Presidency, British India; now Kolkata, India.
- **Death:** 13 November 1963, aged 100.
- **Place of death:** Queen Victoria Memorial Hospital, Welwyn, Hertfordshire, England.
- **Disposition:** Cremation.
- **Nationality and social background:** British, born into the English commercial and missionary community in colonial India.
- **Professions:** Egyptologist, archaeologist, lecturer, museum cataloguer, linguistic instructor, anthropologist, folklorist, author, public educator, and suffrage activist.
- **Principal institutional affiliation:** University College London, as student from 1894 and teacher from the late 1890s until retirement in 1935.
- **Marriage and children:** Murray never married and had no documented children.
- **Pseudonyms:** No substantiated pseudonym has been identified.
- **Religious position:** Raised as a committed Protestant Christian; later rejected organized Christianity and was remembered as a rationalist or sceptic, while retaining belief in an impersonal ordering power she described as what science calls Nature and religion calls God.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**James Charles Murray**, Murray’s father, was an English or Anglo-Irish businessman born in India. He managed the Serampore paper mills and was elected president of the Calcutta Chamber of Commerce three times. His position placed the family in the prosperous British commercial middle class of colonial Bengal.

**Margaret Murray, née Carr**, her mother, went to India in 1857 as a Protestant missionary. Her work included Christian instruction and the education of Indian women, probably including zenana mission work, in which European missionary women entered gender-segregated Indian households.

The family’s position combined commerce, evangelical religion, imperial privilege, and unusually direct—though still colonial and unequal—contact with Indian domestic society.

### Sibling

Murray had an elder sister, **Mary Murray**. The sisters repeatedly moved between India and Europe during childhood and shared the experience of being treated as belonging to Britain while having been born and partly raised in India.

No reliable evidence identifies additional surviving siblings.

### Extended family

A formative figure was Murray’s paternal uncle, **the Reverend John Murray of Lambourn**, Berkshire. Margaret and Mary spent periods in his household. John Murray reinforced strict Christian teaching and conventional Victorian ideas about women. Margaret later rejected both his view of female inferiority and much of his theology. At the same time, visits he arranged to churches, antiquities, and local monuments contributed to her archaeological curiosity.

Murray’s childhood Calcutta household reportedly included her paternal grandmother and maternal great-grandmother at various times, as well as numerous Indian servants. Exact household composition changed over time.

### Ancestry and identity

Murray has sometimes been called “Anglo-Indian.” In nineteenth- and early twentieth-century usage this could mean a Briton born or resident in India; it should not automatically be read as evidence of mixed South Asian and European ancestry. Available biographical sources describe her family as British or Anglo-Irish. No substantiated evidence of South Asian ancestry was located.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1863–1894

### Calcutta and colonial India

Murray was born in Calcutta on 13 July 1863, six years after the Indian Rebellion and the transfer of government from the East India Company to the British Crown. Her family lived primarily within Calcutta’s European social world.

Her upbringing nevertheless exposed her to Bengali and North Indian domestic life through servants, her mother’s missionary activities, and family travel. Later recollections included Indian household customs, servants, bazaars, religious observances, and holidays in hill stations. These memories are valuable testimony about colonial childhood but reflect the assumptions and vocabulary of a British imperial household.

### Britain, Lambourn, and Germany

Margaret and Mary were repeatedly sent or taken to Britain. Periods at Lambourn with the Reverend John Murray exposed Margaret to parish life, ancient churches, monuments, and local history.

In 1873 their mother took the girls to Bonn, Germany. They remained there long enough to acquire strong German, later an important professional advantage: Murray could read and review German Egyptological scholarship that her mentor Flinders Petrie could not easily use.

The sisters returned to Calcutta in 1875 and remained there until approximately 1877. Further movement between India and Britain followed. The precise sequence of every childhood residence is difficult to reconstruct from the online documentary record.

### Education

Murray did not receive a continuous elite institutional education comparable to that available to men who later became her colleagues. Her education was assembled from family instruction, schools in Britain and Germany, language learning, religious education, and self-study.

Her early interests included language, antiquity, religion, and the comparative observation of customs. Her later intellectual habits—wide comparison across cultures, confidence in large historical syntheses, and insufficient caution about differences in time and place—can all be traced in part to this formation.

### Nursing and social work

Murray initially sought a useful occupation rather than an academic career. She worked or trained at Calcutta General Hospital under an Anglican nursing sisterhood and assisted during an epidemic, described in later accounts as cholera. One account states that at about twenty-one she temporarily acted as sister-in-charge.

She subsequently tried to qualify as a nurse in Britain. According to Murray and later biographers, she was rejected because she was considered too short or physically slight for the profession. That explanation is plausible but derives largely from autobiographical testimony.

She also undertook social or missionary work. Her contact with women confined within zenana households contributed to her later opposition to restrictive gender systems, although she initially participated in the evangelical framework through which such work was organized.

### Oxford English Dictionary contribution

Murray later recalled responding in 1881 to James A. H. Murray’s public appeal for readers to submit quotations for the developing *New English Dictionary*. The lexicographer was not a relative. This is a reported episode rather than a central part of her career, but it illustrates her early interest in words and documentary collecting.

### Break with an expected Victorian life

By her early thirties, Murray had neither married nor entered a permanent nursing or missionary vocation. In 1894, aided by family resources and the comparatively open admissions policy of University College London, she began formal Egyptological study. The decision initiated her principal career at an age when many academic biographies already record completed university training.

## Education and Formation at University College London, 1894–1902

### Entry into UCL

Murray entered University College London in January 1894. UCL admitted women and non-Anglicans at a time when many British institutions restricted them, making it one of the few viable routes into university study.

She studied:

- Egyptian hieroglyphic language and archaeology under **William Matthew Flinders Petrie**;
- language with **Francis Llewellyn Griffith**;
- additional linguistic and archaeological subjects with **J. H. Walker** and other members of the developing Egyptology department.

### Flinders Petrie

Petrie became Murray’s principal mentor, employer, collaborator, and institutional patron. Their relationship lasted until Petrie’s death in 1942.

Murray learned Petrie’s emphasis on:

- systematic excavation;
- typological comparison;
- pottery sequences;
- careful object registration;
- the value of small everyday artifacts, rather than only monumental art;
- publication of excavation results;
- training students through direct handling of material.

She was not simply Petrie’s assistant. She became a major organizer of his UCL teaching system and trained a generation of students who later conducted independent work. Nonetheless, her position was structurally subordinate, and Petrie received much of the public and institutional credit associated with the department.

### First publications

Murray’s first scholarly article appeared in the *Proceedings of the Society of Biblical Archaeology* in 1895. Her early publications concerned Egyptian language, religion, inscriptions, and women’s status.

Her competence in German made her particularly useful in reviewing Continental scholarship. She wrote numerous reviews and supplied linguistic, copying, indexing, and editorial labor that was essential to the department but less visible than directing excavations.

### Academic appointments

Institutional summaries vary slightly over the titles and dates:

- **1898 or 1899:** appointed junior lecturer or junior assistant in Egyptology.
- **1909:** listed as assistant.
- **1921:** lecturer.
- **1922:** senior lecturer and Fellow of University College.
- **1924–1935:** Assistant Professor of Egyptology.

The variation reflects changing UCL titles and differences between appointment, promotion, and formal recognition. She is regularly described as the first woman in Britain to hold a full-time university lectureship in archaeology or Egyptology.

## Chronological Life History

## Field Archaeology in Egypt, 1902–1904

### Abydos, 1902–1903

Murray joined Petrie’s excavation at Abydos during the 1902–03 season. Women were increasingly present in British archaeological expeditions but still faced restricted professional authority and assumptions that they should perform illustration, cataloguing, or domestic work.

At Abydos, Murray participated in excavation and recording and became closely associated with the **Osireion**, a monumental structure adjoining the temple of Seti I. Petrie’s team recognized it as an unusual subterranean complex connected with the cult of Osiris. Murray prepared the principal report, *The Osireion at Abydos* (1904), with contributions by J. G. Milne and W. E. Crum.

Later popular accounts sometimes say Murray “discovered” the Osireion. A more exact formulation is that she worked in Petrie’s expedition, supervised or conducted significant excavation and recording there, and authored the major publication. Discovery was collective and institutional rather than exclusively hers.

### Saqqara, 1903–1904

During the next season Murray worked at Saqqara, recording Old Kingdom mastaba tombs. Her responsibilities included copying inscriptions, analyzing names and titles, preparing plates, and organizing publication.

*Saqqara Mastabas, Part I* appeared in 1905. A second part did not appear until 1937. Her *Index of Names and Titles of the Old Kingdom* (1908) grew from this work and offered a reference tool for philologists and historians.

### Fieldwork and gender

Murray later remembered excavation camps as places where women could behave with greater freedom than in Britain. Fieldwork temporarily loosened conventions of clothing, mobility, professional hierarchy, and social supervision. This did not eliminate inequality: women still depended heavily on male-controlled funding, permits, professorships, and publication series.

## Teaching, Museums, and Public Egyptology, 1904–1914

### UCL teaching

Murray taught hieroglyphs, Coptic, Egyptian history, religion, and archaeology. She helped transform Petrie’s loosely structured instruction into a repeatable curriculum.

Her students included people who later became prominent archaeologists and Egyptologists, among them:

- **Gertrude Caton-Thompson**;
- **Veronica Seton-Williams**;
- **Winifred Brunton**;
- **Olga Tufnell**;
- other women who found in Murray a teacher and practical professional model.

She promoted formal certification, and a certificate in Egyptian archaeology was introduced around 1910.

### Museum work

Because her UCL salary was limited, Murray supplemented it through lectures, cataloguing, and museum work. She advised or worked on Egyptian collections at:

- Manchester Museum;
- the British Museum, principally as a researcher and public lecturer rather than a permanent curator;
- the National Museum of Ireland in Dublin;
- the Royal Scottish Museum or Edinburgh Museum of Science and Art;
- the Society of Antiquaries of Scotland;
- the Ashmolean Museum and other regional collections.

The precise title “first curator of Egyptology at Manchester Museum” appears in Manchester institutional commemoration. Her relationship there included cataloguing, lecturing, research, and temporary curatorial responsibility rather than a conventional lifelong curatorship.

### The Two Brothers, 1907–1910

Petrie’s excavations at Deir Rifeh in 1907 uncovered the intact Middle Kingdom tomb group of **Nakht-ankh and Khnum-nakht**, usually called the “Two Brothers.” The assemblage went to Manchester Museum after purchase through public subscription and private donations.

On **6 May 1908**, Murray directed the public unwrapping of Khnum-nakht in the University of Manchester’s Chemical Theatre before approximately 500 spectators. Her team included anatomists, chemists, and other specialists. The procedure lasted about ninety minutes.

The resulting study was important because it treated a mummy as a multidisciplinary archaeological and medical object rather than simply a public curiosity. Murray published *The Tomb of the Two Brothers* in 1910.

There are important qualifications:

- Public mummy unwrappings had a long history as spectacles, and the 1908 event was advertised in sensational terms.
- Contemporary critics protested that disturbing and displaying human remains was desecration.
- Murray rejected such objections as superstition incompatible with science.
- Current museum ethics place greater weight on consent, colonial acquisition, dignity, and the treatment of human remains.
- A 2023 study demonstrated that the famous sequence of photographs was mislabeled in Murray’s 1910 report: images of Khnum-nakht were captioned as Nakht-ankh, and the error was repeated for decades.
- The date and circumstances of Nakht-ankh’s unwrapping are less certain; it appears to have occurred privately after the public event.

The mistake is a documented publication error, not evidence of fraud. It does show how an authoritative caption can manufacture a persistent false “fact.”

### Popularization

Murray deliberately addressed non-specialists through public courses, affordable manuals, museum demonstrations, and books on Egyptian art, architecture, religion, and legend.

Her *Elementary Egyptian Grammar* and *Elementary Coptic Grammar* were designed for beginners. Her public method treated access to archaeological knowledge as a form of education rather than an exclusively professional possession.

## Suffrage and Institutional Feminism

### Suffrage activity

Archival research establishes that Murray supported women’s suffrage and belonged to the **Women’s Social and Political Union**, the organization associated with Emmeline and Christabel Pankhurst. She marched in suffrage demonstrations and participated in political organizing.

Her exact involvement in militant actions is unclear. No evidence located for this dossier shows that she committed arson, assault, bombing, or property destruction; nor was evidence found of arrest, imprisonment, hunger strike, or forced feeding.

Her autobiography largely omitted this activism. The omission may have reflected the book’s selective structure, changing public memory, or a wish not to center political controversy. It should not be taken as evidence that the activism did not occur.

### Women at UCL

Murray’s most sustained feminism was institutional:

- mentoring women students;
- defending women’s access to archaeological careers;
- serving on committees;
- pressing for a women’s common room;
- challenging sex-segregated faculty facilities;
- serving for many years on the Refectory Committee and advocating nutritious, affordable meals.

She campaigned for a common room shared on equal terms. Full desegregation was reportedly achieved only in 1969, six years after her death.

Her feminism did not always use later feminist vocabulary. It operated through appointments, teaching, access to rooms and meals, professional credentials, and the normalization of women in field research.

## First World War and Turn Toward Folklore, 1914–1921

### Wartime interruption

The First World War disrupted UCL’s Egyptology department and travel to Egypt. Murray’s archaeological work contracted, though teaching and public education continued where possible.

After an illness she recuperated at **Glastonbury**, probably in 1915, and became interested in the abbey, Arthurian traditions, Joseph of Arimathea, and Grail legends.

### Grail study

Her article “The Egyptian Elements in the Grail Romance” appeared in two parts in *Ancient Egypt* in 1916. Murray proposed connections between Egyptian religious motifs and medieval Grail literature.

This was a **disputed interpretation** from publication onward. Jessie Weston and others considered Murray’s parallels insufficient to establish historical transmission. The episode foreshadowed her later tendency to treat resemblances across distant periods as evidence of survival or continuity.

### Formation of the witch-cult hypothesis

Murray later said she could not remember who first directed her attention to witches. Glastonbury conversations and wartime reading are plausible contexts, but the exact initiating event is unresolved.

She read printed witch-trial records, demonological texts, antiquarian collections, and folklore. In April 1917 she presented “Organisations of Witches in Great Britain” to the Folklore Society.

Her developing thesis held that:

1. many people prosecuted as witches were members of an actual religion;
2. that religion preserved pre-Christian fertility worship;
3. its deity was interpreted by Christians as the Devil;
4. members met in organized covens, commonly of thirteen;
5. sabbaths and seasonal festivals preserved ancient rites;
6. fairy traditions sometimes recorded memories of this population or religion;
7. certain political deaths might represent ritual sacrifices.

In *The Witch-Cult in Western Europe* (1921), she presented this as a reconstruction from trial evidence rather than as speculation.

## The Witch-Cult Thesis: Reception and Expansion

### Initial reception

The book attracted wide public attention. Its promise was powerful: witch-trial testimony, stripped of theological language and supernatural elements, supposedly disclosed the organization of an underground religion.

The thesis was not wholly original. Nineteenth-century writers including Jules Michelet, Karl Ernst Jarcke, Franz-Josef Mone, and folklorists influenced by survivalist anthropology had proposed pagan continuities. Murray’s distinctive contribution was to impose an apparently precise system—coven size, hierarchy, ritual calendar, initiation, deity, sacrifice—on a large body of printed trial material.

### Contemporary criticism

Specialists raised objections early:

- she used geographically and chronologically scattered cases as evidence for a uniform European organization;
- she relied heavily on printed editions rather than manuscripts and local archival series;
- she often treated testimony produced under coercion, leading questions, or torture as straightforward description;
- she removed or discounted supernatural elements when inconvenient but accepted organizational details from the same testimony;
- she assumed that repeated answers demonstrated shared practice rather than standardized interrogation;
- she normalized exceptional cases;
- she sometimes silently shortened quotations in ways that changed their implications;
- she counted covens or participants selectively to produce the number thirteen.

George Lincoln Burr’s reviews and later critiques identified serious documentary problems. W. B. Halliday’s 1922 *Folklore* review questioned the system. Elliot Rose’s *A Razor for a Goat* (1962), Norman Cohn’s *Europe’s Inner Demons* (1975), Keith Thomas, Alan Macfarlane, and later regional historians supplied increasingly detailed rebuttals.

### Why it gained authority

Despite early criticism, several circumstances increased its reach:

- Murray possessed legitimate academic authority as an Egyptologist.
- Witchcraft history was neglected by many mainstream historians.
- Her theory supplied a coherent secular explanation in place of either literal Satanism or mass irrationality.
- It fit James Frazer’s influential model of fertility religion, sacred kingship, and ritual survival.
- It appealed to readers interested in folklore, feminism, religious alternatives, and suppressed popular culture.
- Murray wrote the influential **“Witchcraft” entry for the 1929 *Encyclopaedia Britannica*** and presented her theory with little indication that it was contested. Revisions of the entry remained influential for decades.
- General historians sometimes repeated it without examining the trial records.

### *The God of the Witches* and *The Divine King in England*

In *The God of the Witches* (1931; often dated by later editions as 1933), Murray made the thesis more accessible and emphasized a prehistoric or ancient Horned God.

In *The Divine King in England* (1954), she extended Frazerian sacrificial-kingship theory to English history. She interpreted the deaths of William II, Thomas Becket, Joan of Arc, Gilles de Rais, and others as elements in a secret religious structure or substituted royal sacrifice.

These reconstructions received little support even from some readers sympathetic to her earlier work. No independent documentary evidence establishes that William II or Becket was ritually killed by a surviving pagan organization.

### Present scholarly status

The organized pan-European witch religion described by Murray is a **rejected historical thesis**.

Local magical practitioners, visionary traditions, folk healers, benandanti, cunning people, and remnants or adaptations of pre-Christian custom certainly existed. Their existence does not establish Murray’s centralized or standardized religion.

Carlo Ginzburg’s work on the Friulian benandanti demonstrated that some trial testimony incorporated vernacular visionary traditions. Ginzburg’s findings are sometimes claimed as a partial vindication of Murray, but his historically specific argument differs substantially from her claim of a physically meeting, uniformly organized, pan-European cult.

## Malta, Menorca, Petra, and Palestine, 1921–1939

### Malta

Between approximately 1921 and 1927, Murray directed or participated in excavations at Maltese prehistoric sites, especially **Borġ in-Nadur**. Reports appeared as *Excavations in Malta*, Parts I–III, between 1923 and 1929.

Her work helped define stratigraphy, pottery sequences, and the relationship between temple and Bronze Age occupation. A contemporary *Nature* review praised the care and scientific character of the excavation.

Murray later collaborated with **Themistocles Zammit** on *Corpus of the Bronze-Age Pottery of Malta* (1934).

### Cambridge and Menorca

In 1930–31 she investigated megalithic sites in Menorca, particularly **Sa Torreta** and **Trapucó**. Reports appeared in 1934 and 1938.

These excavations broadened her work from Egyptology to Mediterranean prehistory. They also reinforced her interest in comparative religion and long-duration cultural forms. Her archaeological reports are methodologically distinct from her witch-cult work and have not undergone the same wholesale rejection.

### Petra

Murray worked at Petra in Transjordan in 1937, studying Nabataean remains. She produced the popular *Petra: The Rock City of Edom* (1939) and the more technical *A Street in Petra* (1940), associated with the British School of Archaeology in Egypt.

### Tell el-Ajjul

In 1938, despite being in her mid-seventies, she joined Petrie and Ernest Mackay at Tell el-Ajjul in British Mandatory Palestine. The expedition formed part of Petrie’s final Near Eastern field program.

## Retirement and Later Life, 1935–1963

### Retirement from UCL

Murray retired formally in 1935 at seventy-two. UCL established the **Margaret Murray Prize**, awarded for distinguished Egyptological work, commonly a dissertation. The prize continued into the twenty-first century.

Retirement did not end her work. She continued to:

- use the British Museum library;
- teach adult education courses at the City Literary Institute;
- lecture publicly;
- publish Egyptology and folklore;
- travel for fieldwork;
- advise students and colleagues;
- work with Petrie until his death in 1942.

She later recommended her former pupil Veronica Seton-Williams as her successor in adult education teaching.

### Second World War

During the Second World War Murray remained intellectually active. Exact wartime residences and movements are not fully recoverable from the consulted online sources. She continued writing and maintaining professional correspondence amid the disruption of London institutions.

### Popular Egyptology

*The Splendour That Was Egypt* (1949) became her best-known general book on Egypt. It synthesized political history, society, religion, literature, architecture, and daily life for non-specialists.

Her work increasingly emphasized women in Egyptian society, religious poetry, and the accessibility of ancient culture. Some of its historical formulations are now dated, but it belongs to the history of serious public Egyptology rather than occult writing.

### Folklore Society presidency

Murray had published in the Folklore Society’s journal since 1917 and became one of its best-known members. After Allan Gomme’s resignation and another candidate’s refusal, she served as president from **1953 to 1955**.

Her presidential addresses urged systematic collection of English traditions and criticized scholars who pursued exotic folklore while neglecting everyday customs at home.

The presidency testified to personal esteem and longevity, but it also gave her contested witchcraft views additional institutional prestige. Later folklorists argued that her prominence damaged the discipline’s reputation among historians.

### Relationship to Gerald Gardner and Wicca

Gerald B. Gardner used Murray’s books as historical validation for the religion he publicized as surviving witchcraft and later called Wicca. Murray wrote the preface to Gardner’s *Witchcraft Today* (1954).

What this establishes:

- **Documented fact:** Murray endorsed Gardner’s book sufficiently to supply a preface.
- **Documented intellectual influence:** Gardner adopted her terminology, ritual calendar, coven model, and survival narrative.
- **Not established:** that Murray joined Gardner’s coven, underwent Wiccan initiation, or practiced Gardnerian ritual.
- **Misleading popular claim:** Murray “invented Wicca.” Gardner and collaborators constructed the ritual religion; Murray supplied an influential historical charter.
- **Unresolved:** how much Murray privately believed Gardner represented literal survival as opposed to a contemporary manifestation supporting her theory.

Murray should not automatically be described as Wiccan. Her own religious statements were rationalist, anti-clerical, and broadly theistic rather than confessional Pagan.

### Fairies, ghosts, and supernatural reports

Murray collected accounts of fairies, apparitions, seasonal customs, sacred sites, and rural beliefs. The surviving Murray Collection includes correspondence from informants describing personal encounters.

She often interpreted fairy traditions as memories of an earlier population or concealed religious group. This “pygmy race” or hidden-population theory is not accepted as a general historical explanation of European fairy lore.

Her attitude to ghosts was complex. She collected testimony seriously but attempted naturalistic or anthropological explanations. She was neither a conventional spiritualist apologist nor a simple dismissive sceptic.

### Wandlebury figures

In 1957 Murray publicly supported T. C. Lethbridge’s claim that large pre-Christian hill figures lay beneath turf at Wandlebury in Cambridgeshire. She privately expressed doubts about aspects of the evidence.

The supposed figures did not gain archaeological acceptance. The episode illustrates her willingness in old age to endorse survivalist interpretations on limited evidence.

### Final illness and centenary

Arthritis increasingly restricted Murray’s movement. She lived for a period in North Finchley with a retired nursing couple who cared for her. She continued taking taxis to central London and using the UCL or British Museum libraries when able.

In 1962 she moved to Queen Victoria Memorial Hospital at Welwyn for continuous care.

On 13 July 1963, her hundredth birthday was marked by a gathering of friends, doctors, former students, and colleagues at Ayot St Lawrence. Two days later she attended a UCL celebration and received an illuminated address from the Professorial Board. This was her final visit to the college.

In 1963 she published:

- *The Genesis of Religion*, arguing for the priority of female deities in early religion;
- *My First Hundred Years*, her autobiography.

Murray died at Welwyn on 13 November 1963. No more specific terminal diagnosis than age-related frailty and severe arthritis was found in the consulted sources. She was cremated.

- **Childhood periods:** Lambourn and other British family residences.

- **1875–1877:** Calcutta.

- **1915:** convalescence at Glastonbury.
- **1920s:** repeated Maltese field seasons.
- **1930–1931:** Menorca.
- **1937:** Petra, Transjordan.
- **1938:** Tell el-Ajjul, Palestine.
- **Later life:** North Finchley.
- **1962–1963:** Welwyn, Hertfordshire.

There is no evidence that Murray experienced political exile or imprisonment.

### Technologies and methods

Murray did not patent inventions. Her technical work included:

- hieroglyphic transcription;
- archaeological illustration and epigraphy;
- ceramic typology;
- stratigraphic excavation;
- museum cataloguing;
- multidisciplinary mummy examination;
- elementary-language teaching systems;
- photographic documentation.

The 1908 mummy investigation joined anatomy, chemistry, dentistry, pathology, textiles, and archaeology. Its continuing importance lies partly in this multidisciplinary design, although the destructive physical unwrapping would be ethically and technically disfavored today in comparison with non-invasive imaging.

### Businesses, investments, and property

No evidence was located that Murray owned or controlled substantial businesses, investment partnerships, archaeological companies, or patents.

Her father’s commercial success gave the family a comfortable base. Murray’s own professional income appears to have consisted of a modest UCL salary supplemented by teaching, lectures, cataloguing, and royalties.

No verified account of her estate value, securities, real property portfolio, debts, tax disputes, or probate distribution was found in the consulted sources. Claims about her personal wealth should therefore be treated as unresolved.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Politics

Murray’s documented political activity centered on women’s suffrage and equality within higher education. No evidence was found that she held elected office, belonged to a parliamentary party, or exercised formal colonial administrative power.

Her life was made possible by British imperial networks, and her archaeology operated within systems that removed antiquities and human remains from colonized territories to British museums. This is documented institutional context, not evidence of an individual criminal offense under the laws then governing excavation and division of finds.

### Religion

Her development can be summarized as:

1. evangelical Protestant childhood;
2. Sunday-school and missionary participation;
3. rejection of doctrines of female submission and organized Christianity;
4. comparative study of Egyptian and other religions;
5. rationalist interpretation of witchcraft;
6. continuing belief in an impersonal divine or natural ordering power;
7. sympathy toward religious alternatives without documented conversion to Wicca.

### Anthropology and survivalism

Murray’s comparative framework came from nineteenth-century evolutionary anthropology and especially Frazerian “survivals.” Similar customs separated by centuries were treated as remnants of a common earlier religious stage.

This method produced suggestive comparative questions but frequently substituted formal resemblance for evidence of transmission. In archaeology she was constrained by objects, strata, and inscriptions; in witchcraft history she applied a much freer synthetic method.

### Views of women

Murray challenged restrictions on women’s work and education and studied women’s roles in ancient Egypt. Her last book’s argument for primordial goddesses belongs to a larger twentieth-century search for female-centered prehistory.

No evidence supports presenting her as a systematic theorist of matriarchy equivalent to later feminist archaeology. Her thought combined practical institutional feminism with speculative religious history.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### William Matthew Flinders Petrie

Mentor, employer, excavation director, colleague, and long-term intellectual influence, 1894–1942. Murray organized much of the teaching associated with his UCL department and produced reports within his publication system.

### Hilda Petrie

Petrie’s wife and archaeological collaborator. Murray worked within the Petries’ excavation and publication network. The surviving record supports professional collaboration, though no complete account of their private relationship was located.

### Francis Llewellyn Griffith

Teacher of Egyptian language and a significant member of Murray’s early scholarly formation.

### J. H. Walker

Language instructor during Murray’s UCL studies.

### J. G. Milne and W. E. Crum

Contributors to *The Osireion at Abydos* and members of her Egyptological publication network.

### Kurt Sethe

Contributed specialized chapters or readings to Murray’s Saqqara publication. This was scholarly collaboration rather than a close personal partnership.

### Themistocles Zammit

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, and Financial Questions

### No known criminal or civil case

No substantiated evidence was found that Murray was:

- arrested or imprisoned;
- criminally charged or convicted;
- sued for professional misconduct;
- formally investigated for fraud;
- implicated in corruption, money laundering, espionage, or political violence;
- dismissed for misconduct.

This absence should not be converted into a claim that every aspect of her career was uncontroversial.

### Manipulation of witch-trial evidence

**Documented scholarly criticism:** Murray selectively quoted, silently omitted inconvenient words, combined cases separated by centuries and countries, treated coerced confessions literally, and counted evidence inconsistently.

**Evidentiary status:** Serious methodological failure is well demonstrated. Some critics use terms such as “dishonesty” or “manipulation.” Evidence that she knowingly fabricated documents or pursued financial fraud is lacking. The safest conclusion is that she used sources tendentiously in the service of a theory to which she became strongly committed.

### Encyclopaedia Britannica entry

**Documented fact:** Murray’s 1929 entry presented the witch-cult interpretation with inadequate disclosure of its contested status.

**Significance:** Britannica’s authority allowed a minority hypothesis to circulate as settled knowledge.

**Not established:** that editors or Murray conspired to deceive readers. The controversy concerns editorial and scholarly standards, not a proven fraudulent agreement.

### Human remains and colonial archaeology

**Documented fact:** Murray directed the destructive unwrapping and public display of an Egyptian mummy acquired within the colonial antiquities system.

**Contemporary controversy:** Newspapers published objections describing the procedure as desecration.

**Present assessment:** Modern professional ethics would question the spectacle, lack of consent, physical destruction, and removal of the dead from Egypt. These standards do not make the 1908 procedure a documented crime, but they materially alter its interpretation.

### Photographic misidentification

**Documented fact:** Murray’s 1910 report mislabeled photographs of Khnum-nakht as Nakht-ankh.

**Evidentiary status:** publication error with long-lived consequences. No proof of intentional deception.

### Colonial attitudes

Murray’s autobiography and popular writing contain language and assumptions characteristic of elite British colonial society. She participated in institutions that treated Egyptian and Mediterranean antiquities as objects for European extraction, classification, and display.

At the same time, she trained women and expanded public access to archaeological knowledge. These facts coexist; neither cancels the other.

### Professional marginalization

Later accounts sometimes portray Murray as wholly suppressed by male academics. That is exaggerated:

- she held a UCL post for decades;
- received promotions and an honorary doctorate;
- published through respected presses;
- led excavations;
- became president of the Folklore Society.

She nevertheless worked within unequal structures, earned less authority than male leaders, and spent substantial time on teaching, cataloguing, and administration that could limit recognition.

### Adversarial search, 2024–2026

A dedicated search combined Murray’s name with *controversy, scandal, allegation, lawsuit, fraud, accusation, conspiracy,* and *criticism*, including material published or indexed during 2024–2026.

Results continued to center on:

- her influence on Wicca and popular witch imagery;
- ethical reinterpretation of the Manchester mummy unwrapping;
- colonial structures of archaeology.

No credible recent evidence was found of previously unknown lawsuits, financial fraud, sexual scandal, criminal conduct, intelligence activity, or political violence.

Recent popular descriptions calling her a “flake,” fraud, or inventor of Wicca are rhetorical judgments, not new documentary findings.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Murray proved that the witches were Pagans”

- **Story:** Early modern witch-trial victims belonged to a surviving prehistoric religion.
- **Origin and circulation:** Murray’s 1917 paper, 1921 book, Britannica entry, later books, popular histories, and Wiccan literature.
- **Evidence for:** scattered evidence of folk magic, visionary traditions, seasonal customs, local magical specialists, and some group practices.
- **Evidence against:** no documentary trace of Murray’s uniform international organization; trial testimony was coercively generated; supposed structural uniformities often result from selective reading.
- **Status:** Rejected historical thesis.
- **Reputational effect:** Made Murray famous beyond Egyptology and later overshadowed her archaeological work.

### “Covens have always contained thirteen members”

- **Story:** Thirteen was the standard organizational unit of the historic witch religion.
- **Origin:** Murray’s reconstruction from selected trials.
- **Evidence:** certain cases contain groups or lists approximating thirteen.
- **Counterevidence:** group sizes vary; Murray’s counting was selective; interrogators and Christian symbolism could generate the number.
- **Status:** Unsupported generalization that became influential modern ritual folklore.

### “The Horned God survived continuously from prehistory”

- **Story:** A single horned male deity was worshipped from Paleolithic times through ancient religions, medieval witchcraft, and modern survivals.
- **Origin:** Comparative religious speculation, consolidated in *The God of the Witches*.
- **Evidence:** horned deities occur in multiple cultures.
- **Counterevidence:** iconographic similarity does not establish identity, organizational continuity, or uninterrupted transmission.
- **Status:** Mythic synthesis, not demonstrated history.
- **Effect:** Major symbolic contribution to modern Paganism and fantasy culture.

### “Joan of Arc and Gilles de Rais were leaders or sacrifices of the witch-cult”

- **Story:** Their deaths reflected participation in, or ritual functions within, an underground pagan religion.
- **Origin:** Murray’s later witch-cult publications.
- **Evidence:** Murray reinterpreted trial and political records.
- **Counterevidence:** contemporary documentation supplies political, military, ecclesiastical, and criminal contexts without independent evidence of her proposed cult.
- **Status:** Rejected interpretation.

### “William II and Thomas Becket were ritual royal sacrifices”

- **Story:** Their violent deaths continued an ancient system of sacred kingship or substitution.
- **Origin:** *The Divine King in England*.
- **Evidence:** analogical use of Frazerian sacrificial-kingship theory.
- **Counterevidence:** no supporting organizational or ritual documentation.
- **Status:** Rejected speculative theory.

### “Murray was herself a witch or Wiccan high priestess”

- **Story:** Her knowledge came from initiation into a surviving coven.
- **Circulation:** Pagan folklore, sensational biographies, and internet retellings encouraged by her Gardner association.
- **Evidence for:** she wrote Gardner’s preface and defended the reality of the old religion.
- **Evidence against:** no initiation record or credible first-person testimony; her personal religious statements were rationalist and non-sectarian.
- **Status:** Rumor or myth.

### “Murray invented Wicca”

- **Story:** Murray created the modern religion.
- **Evidence:** Gardner and other early Wiccans used her historical framework.
- **Counterevidence:** she did not author Gardnerian ritual texts or found its covens.
- **Status:** Misleading compression. She was a major intellectual precursor, not sole founder.

### “Murray discovered the Osireion alone”

- **Story:** Murray independently discovered the monument.
- **Evidence:** she had major excavation and publication responsibility.
- **Counterevidence:** work occurred within Petrie’s expedition and was collaborative.
- **Status:** Hagiographic simplification.

### “The Two Brothers were literally brothers”

Murray’s publication treated Nakht-ankh and Khnum-nakht as the occupants of a shared tomb and discussed their kinship. Modern DNA and osteological study has complicated the traditional label, suggesting different fathers and a common mother or otherwise more complex kinship, depending on the test and interpretation.

This later scientific question does not represent deception by Murray: “brothers” followed the tomb’s documentary and curatorial interpretation available to her.

### Fictional and cultural afterlives

Murray’s survival narrative entered:

- Wiccan origin stories;
- occult publishing;
- folk horror;
- historical novels;
- fantasy fiction;
- feminist accounts of a suppressed goddess religion;
- conspiracy narratives about secret pagan elites.

The framework’s cultural success outlasted its academic credibility.

## Death and Immediate Commemoration

Murray’s death on 13 November 1963 was marked in archaeological, anthropological, and folklore publications.

The immediate commemorative image was that of an exceptionally durable teacher and scholar who had connected Victorian colonial India, Petrie’s formative archaeology, women’s suffrage, two world wars, modern university Egyptology, and the beginnings of Wicca.

The Folklore Society’s 1961 festschrift for her ninety-eighth birthday included archaeology, folklore, fairy traditions, Near Eastern symbolism, and Greek song, but reportedly no defense of her witch-cult thesis. That absence foreshadowed its rapid disciplinary collapse.

A 1964 Folklore Society lecture by Rossell Hope Robbins attacked “Murrayism.” Accounts of the occasion describe Sybil Leek’s pet jackdaw and broomsticks left outside the hall—an episode that became minor folklore in its own right.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Egyptology and archaeology

Murray is now reassessed as:

- one of the first professionally trained British women Egyptologists;
- the first woman to hold a full-time British university archaeology or Egyptology lectureship;
- a major teacher in Petrie’s UCL department;
- an advocate for women students;
- an early practitioner of public and multidisciplinary mummy research;
- a field archaeologist in Egypt, Malta, Menorca, Petra, and Palestine;
- a prolific popularizer.

Her reputation was long distorted because scholarship focused either on Petrie or on her failed witchcraft theory.

### Witchcraft historiography

Her direct historical thesis is rejected, but it remains important as an object of historiography:

- it shows the power of comparative “survival” models;
- it demonstrates how selective quotation can create apparent systems;
- it illustrates how encyclopedias confer authority;
- it shaped decades of general historical writing;
- its failure helped stimulate more rigorous regional archive-based witchcraft studies.

### Modern Paganism and Wicca

Murray supplied one of Wicca’s foundational historical narratives. Gardner’s religion could present itself not as a new religious synthesis but as a persecuted ancient survival.

Many contemporary Wiccans no longer accept that literal origin story. Murray’s categories nevertheless remain embedded in Pagan symbolism:

- the Horned God;
- covens of thirteen;
- seasonal sabbaths;
- the “Old Religion”;
- the Burning Times as religious persecution;
- witches as guardians of pre-Christian nature religion.

### Literature and popular culture

Murray influenced:

- Sylvia Townsend Warner’s *Lolly Willowes*;
- H. P. Lovecraft’s depictions of secret, ancient cults;
- occult thrillers and historical novels;
- folk horror;
- goddess and matriarchy literature;
- popular images of witchcraft as organized religious resistance.

Her cultural legacy is therefore not reducible to whether her historical claims were correct.

### Institutional legacy

- The UCL Margaret Murray Prize.
- Archival collections at UCL and the Folklore Society.
- Continuing display and reinterpretation at Manchester Museum.
- A model—qualified by colonial and ethical criticism—of public-facing scholarship.
- Renewed study of women’s administrative and teaching labor in archaeology.

| **1954** | Publishes *The Divine King in England* and writes preface to Gardner’s *Witchcraft Today*. |

| **1960** | Donates folklore papers and correspondence to the Folklore Society. |
| **1961** | *Folklore* publishes a festschrift for her ninety-eighth birthday. |

| **13 July 1963** | Celebrates her hundredth birthday. |

| **2013** | Sheppard publishes the first full-length scholarly biography; UCL marks Murray’s sesquicentennial. |
| **2023–2026** | Continued reassessment of her mummy research, archival legacy, colonial setting, and influence on popular witchcraft narratives. |
