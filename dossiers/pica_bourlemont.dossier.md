# Pica Bourlémont: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

“Pica Bourlémont” is the conventional modern name given to the mother of Francis of Assisi. The surviving evidence does not permit a continuous cradle-to-grave biography. It establishes substantially less than later genealogies and devotional biographies claim.

The dossier therefore distinguishes:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a surviving medieval document or by multiple early sources sufficiently close to the events.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a medieval narrative source, but not independently documented.
- **Later tradition:** first attested generations or centuries after Pica’s lifetime.
- **Allegation or theory:** a proposed reconstruction unsupported by conclusive documentary evidence.
- **Rumor or myth:** a story shaped primarily by hagiography, folklore, polemic, or genealogical invention.
- **Unresolved:** evidence is insufficient to decide among competing versions.

No surviving writing by Pica, authenticated marriage contract, birth record, death record, will, property instrument issued in her own name, portrait from life, or contemporary narrative biography has been identified.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

### Most defensible identity

- **Name securely attested:** Pica.
- **Medieval forms:** *domina Pica* (“Lady/Mistress Pica”); indirect family designations include *Angelus Picae* and *Iohannectus Angeli dominae Picae*.
- **Conventional modern forms:** Monna Pica; Madonna Pica; Donna Pica; Giovanna Pica; Joanna Pica; Pica di Bourlemont; Pica de Bourlémont; Giovanna/Joanna Pica de Bourlémont; Pica di Bernardone.
- **Status:** wife of Pietro di Bernardone and mother of Francis of Assisi.
- **Religion:** Latin Christian, inferred from the family’s participation in baptism and from the hagiographic sources; no independent account of her religious practices survives.
- **Birth:** unknown.
- **Birthplace:** unknown.
- **Death:** unknown.
- **Principal known residence:** Assisi, in the Duchy of Spoleto, probably in the Bernardone household near the site later occupied by the Chiesa Nuova.
- **Occupation:** none documented. She belonged to a prosperous merchant household and managed the household during Pietro’s absences.
- **Social title:** *domina* or *monna*, a conventional respectful designation for a married adult woman. It is not, by itself, evidence of titled nobility.

### Is “Giovanna” established?

The early sources say that Francis’s mother named her infant Giovanni. Some later accounts call the mother Giovanna or Iohanna. Treccani accepts “Giovanna, detta Pica,” but the surviving evidence most securely attests **Pica**, not a complete baptismal name.

Accordingly:

- **Pica:** documented.
- **Giovanna/Iohanna/Joanna:** reported or conventional identification; not as securely established as Pica.
- **Bourlémont:** a much later attribution, first traceable in the presently accessible debate to Claude Frassen in the seventeenth century.
- **Moriconi/Morosini:** applied in some late genealogies to Pietro’s family; not supported by reliable contemporary evidence.

### Meaning of “Pica”

Several explanations have been proposed:

1. **Personal name or familiar form:** Pica may simply have been her given or commonly used name.
2. **Piccarda/Picardy theory:** Pica has been interpreted as an abbreviation of Piccarda, or as indicating origin in Picardy.
3. **French *pique* theory:** a modern linguistic suggestion associating the name with northern France.
4. **Picene theory:** some Italian writers derive it from the Piceni or the Marche/Picene region.
5. **“Little one” theory:** another modern interpretation treats Pica as a diminutive.

None is conclusively demonstrated. The 1253 family designations show that “Pica” functioned as an identifying personal name in Assisi.

## Family and Ancestry

## Parents and ancestry

No securely authenticated medieval source identifies Pica’s parents, siblings, grandparents, or lineage.

### Bourlémont lineage claim

**Later tradition / disputed claim:** Claude Frassen, a Franciscan theologian and Sorbonne doctor, stated in a commentary on the Franciscan Third Order that Pica belonged to the noble Bourlémont family. According to later writers citing him, an old manuscript or marriage contract was preserved in that family’s archives, and was supposedly still there in 1703.

Problems with the claim:

- The alleged document has not been published.
- No reliable archival description, shelf mark, transcription, facsimile, chain of custody, or independent witness has been produced.
- The claim appears more than four centuries after Pica’s lifetime.
- Bourlémont is associated geographically with Lorraine, not Provence.
- Nineteenth-century critics noted difficulties in placing the later Bourlémont line in Pica’s period and region.
- A 1926 *Il Mattino* article reportedly argued that the relevant Bourlémont line arose only in 1468 from the Anglure/Anglusires des Stoges family.

The accessible evidence therefore does not justify presenting “de Bourlémont” as an authenticated surname.

### Modern online pedigrees

Public family trees assign Pica such parents as:

- Pierre de Bourlémont, allegedly born about 1130 and died about 1170;
- Ide de Froville;
- or members of various Morosini, Anglure, Provençal, Lorraine, or Picard families.

They commonly assign her a birth in 1155 and death in 1211. The examined trees either cite one another, cite user-submitted trees, or expressly acknowledge that their data lack primary sources. These claims are **unverified genealogical constructions**, not established medieval genealogy.

### Italian-origin theories

Several alternatives have been proposed:

- Belmonte Piceno;
- Monsampietro Morico;
- the Picene region generally;
- Monte Burella near Nocera Umbra;
- an Umbrian family of “Franco-Picene” cultural origin.

These theories attempt to explain “Pica,” Francis’s association with French speech, or “Bourlemont” through Italian place names. No surviving medieval record connects Pica to those places. They remain **theories**, with some resting principally on phonetic resemblance.

## Marriage

Pica married Pietro di Bernardone, a prosperous Assisi cloth merchant.

### Pietro’s identity

Early sources and later scholarship identify Francis’s father as Pietro di Bernardone. His commercial activity included cloth and dealings connected with France. The evidence does not securely establish the elaborate “dei Moriconi” or “Morosini della Sbarra” pedigrees frequently attached to him online.

### Alleged French courtship

**Later tradition:** Pietro supposedly met Pica during a commercial journey to Provence or France and brought her to Assisi as his wife.

**Evidence:** Pietro’s travel to France at the time of Francis’s birth is reported by the *Legend of the Three Companions*. Pica’s own French origin and a French marriage, however, are not stated by the earliest sources. The purported marriage contract survives only as a later claim.

## Children

### Francis of Assisi

- Born in Assisi in 1181 or 1182; an exact day is not securely known.
- Baptized Giovanni.
- Later called Francesco.
- Died on 3 October 1226.
- Canonized in 1228.

Pica’s maternity is established by the Franciscan narrative tradition and supported indirectly by later Assisi family records.

### Angelo

A brother of Francis named Angelo is supported by documentary family references. Treccani reports the designation *Angelus Picae*, “Angelo of Pica,” and identifies his son as *Iohannectus Angeli dominae Picae*. A 1253 Assisi property-division instrument reportedly involving Angelo’s descendants is an important source for the name Pica.

Angelo had descendants. Two sons commonly named in scholarship are Giovanetto and Piccardo, although a complete reconstruction of the line requires consultation of the Assisi archival instruments.

### Other alleged children

Some modern genealogies give Pica children named Michele Morosini della Sbarra, Marino Morosini, and Tomaso Morosini. These entries conflict with the established Assisi context and often omit Francis entirely. No reliable evidence was found for them as Pica’s children.

Early biographies sometimes describe Francis as one of several children, but only Angelo is consistently recoverable by name. Claims that Pica had exactly two children should be treated as **reported tradition**, not certainty.

## Childhood and Early Life

Nothing is securely known about Pica’s childhood.

No source establishes:

- her date of birth;
- her childhood residence;
- her parents;
- her language of upbringing;
- her education;
- her dowry;
- her social rank before marriage;
- when she entered Assisi;
- or the date of her marriage.

The frequently repeated date **1155** is an unsourced modern genealogical estimate. If she bore Francis in 1181–1182, a birth somewhere in the middle decades of the twelfth century is plausible, but cannot be narrowed responsibly.

---

## Education and Formation

No teacher, school, convent, tutor, mentor, or course of study is documented.

Later portraits make her:

- a cultured Provençal noblewoman;
- the transmitter of French language and troubadour culture to Francis;
- a deeply pious Christian mother;
- or, in modern revisionist accounts, an Umbrian or Picene woman retrospectively “Frenchified.”

The early texts praise the mother’s integrity and compare her prophetically with Saint Elizabeth. This is hagiographic characterization, not evidence of a particular education.

### Alleged linguistic influence

Francis was remembered as singing or speaking in French. Later biographers connected this with Pica’s supposed French origin. Other explanations include Pietro’s commercial relations with France and Francis’s exposure to French chivalric and mercantile culture.

No source records Pica teaching Francis French. This is a plausible but **unverified inference** if one first accepts her French origin.

---

## Early Married Life in Assisi

Pica became part of a wealthy urban merchant household. Pietro’s cloth commerce brought the family substantial means and required absences from Assisi.

The family’s economic position is reflected in accounts of Francis’s youth:

- Francis worked in his father’s business.
- He had access to money and expensive clothing.
- He participated in the social culture of prosperous Assisi youth.
- His parents were represented by Thomas of Celano as having indulged his youthful vanity.

These descriptions concern the household collectively. They do not permit a financial separation between Pica’s property, dowry, and Pietro’s commercial capital.

## Chronological Life History

## Before 1181–1182: uncertain origins and marriage

At an unknown date, Pica married Pietro di Bernardone and lived in Assisi.

The common reconstruction that she was a Provençal noblewoman whom Pietro met while buying cloth in France is not found in the earliest Franciscan biographies. It is a later synthesis of:

- Pietro’s documented or reported French commercial connections;
- Francis’s name;
- Francis’s use of French;
- and Frassen’s Bourlémont claim.

## 1181 or 1182: birth and naming of Francis

The *Legend of the Three Companions* reports that Francis was born in Assisi while his father was away in France. His mother first called him John/Giovanni; after Pietro returned, the father called him Francis/Francesco.

Thomas of Celano’s *Memorial of the Desire of a Soul*, written about 1246–1247, similarly states that Francis’s mother named him John at baptism. Celano portrays her as a woman “of complete integrity” who resembled Saint Elizabeth in naming and prophetic spirit.

Bonaventure’s *Minor Legend*, composed in the 1260s, repeats that his mother first called him John and his father later called him Francis.

### What can be concluded

- **Reported fact:** Pica arranged or approved the name Giovanni.
- **Reported fact:** Pietro was away in France when the child was born.
- **Reported fact:** Pietro subsequently imposed or favored Francesco.
- **Unresolved:** whether Francesco was a legal renaming, a household nickname, or a familiar name that displaced Giovanni.
- **Unresolved:** why Pietro chose it. Commercial affection for France is plausible; maternal French ancestry is possible but not demonstrated.
- **Unresolved:** the precise birth date. The proposed 26 September 1181 or 1182 is late and uncertain.

## Francis’s childhood and adolescence

Thomas of Celano presents Francis’s early life as worldly and vain, enabled by parents who indulged him. Bonaventure, writing a more controlled official hagiography, softened some of this material.

Pica’s individual conduct during these years is not described in detail. Later writers assign her tenderness, refinement, religious devotion, and sympathy with the poor. These traits mostly reflect retrospective interpretation of her later liberation of Francis and Celano’s comparison with Saint Elizabeth.

## 1202–1205: war, imprisonment, illness, and conversion

Francis fought in Assisi’s conflict with Perugia, was captured, spent approximately a year as a prisoner, and later experienced illness and a gradual religious conversion. No source provides a detailed account of Pica’s response.

Modern films frequently depict her nursing him through fever and psychological crisis. Such scenes are dramatizations grounded in Francis’s illness but are not independent evidence about Pica.

## About 1205–1206: conflict over San Damiano and family property

Francis sold cloth and apparently a horse, intending to give proceeds toward the rebuilding of San Damiano. Pietro treated the act as a misappropriation of family property.

According to the early biographical tradition:

1. Francis hid from his father.
2. He returned to Assisi.
3. Townspeople abused him as mad.
4. Pietro seized him, dragged him home, beat or mistreated him, and confined him in bonds.
5. Pietro then left Assisi on commercial business.
6. Francis’s mother disapproved of her husband’s treatment.
7. Seeing that Francis would not abandon his purpose, she freed him and allowed him to return to San Damiano.
8. Pietro returned, reproached his wife, and pursued the dispute against Francis.

Bonaventure states explicitly that the mother, during Pietro’s absence, freed Francis from his bonds and let him go wherever he wished.

### Evidentiary assessment

- **Reported fact with strong early support:** Pica freed Francis during Pietro’s absence.
- **Reported fact:** she disapproved of Pietro’s coercive treatment.
- **Reported fact:** Pietro subsequently reproached her.
- **Interpretive question:** whether this constituted full support for Francis’s vocation, maternal protection from violence, recognition that coercion was futile, or some combination. The sources emphasize both compassion and recognition of Francis’s unchangeable resolve.
- **Modern description as “domestic abuse”:** an interpretive label applied to a medieval narrative that expressly describes blows, bonds, forcible confinement, and verbal aggression. The physical conduct is reported in the sources; the modern legal and psychological classification was not available to the medieval authors.

## About 1206: renunciation before Bishop Guido

Pietro pursued recovery of his money and brought the dispute into public or episcopal jurisdiction. Francis renounced his property and paternal inheritance before Bishop Guido.

Pica is not given an active role in the surviving account of the hearing. Artistic and cinematic versions often place her there, grieving or silently supporting Francis, but this should not be confused with documentary evidence.

## After 1206

The early biographies cease to provide biographical information about Pica. Treccani observes that after the family rupture the ancient biographers no longer mention Pietro; Pica likewise disappears as a historical actor.

There is no reliable evidence concerning:

- whether she maintained contact with Francis;
- whether she supported the early Franciscan movement;
- whether she joined a penitential association;
- whether she met Clare of Assisi;
- whether she reconciled Pietro and Francis;
- where she lived in widowhood, if widowed;
- when Pietro died;
- whether Pica was alive at Francis’s death in 1226;
- or where she was buried.

## 1253: documentary afterlife of her name

A generation after Francis, Assisi documents reportedly used Pica’s name to identify descendants:

- Angelo as *Angelus Picae*;
- Giovanetto as *Iohannectus Angeli dominae Picae*.

This suggests that Pica was sufficiently distinctive within the family and local memory to serve as a genealogical identifier. It does not prove noble birth or a Bourlémont surname.

## Residences, Migrations, and Travel

### Documented or strongly reported

- **Assisi:** household residence and setting of all episodes securely connected with her.
- **Bernardone family house:** traditionally associated with the Chiesa Nuova area. Its exact medieval layout and the identification of particular rooms are matters of later local tradition.

### Claimed but unverified

- Provence;
- Picardy;
- Champagne;
- Lorraine/Bourlémont;
- Belmonte Piceno;
- Monsampietro Morico;
- Monte Burella near Nocera Umbra.

No journey by Pica herself is documented. The narrative that she migrated from France to Assisi following marriage to Pietro is a later reconstruction.

---

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

Pica is not independently documented as an owner, merchant, investor, inventor, or member of a corporation.

## Bernardone commercial household

Pietro was a cloth merchant associated with international or transalpine trade. The household possessed:

- cloth stock;
- commercial money;
- a house in Assisi;
- sufficient capital to support Francis’s expensive clothing and social ambitions;
- and commercial connections involving France.

Pica’s dowry, separate property, inheritance rights, or participation in bookkeeping are unknown.

## San Damiano dispute

The central financial crisis involving the family concerned Francis’s disposition of cloth or its proceeds for San Damiano. Pica did not publicly claim the money in the surviving narratives. Her documented narrative act was to release Francis from confinement.

## Usury allegation

A recent Basilica-related article characterizes references to Pietro’s “ill-gotten money” as suggesting usury, a practice condemned by the medieval Church but intertwined with commerce.

Evidentiary status:

- Francis’s conflict with his father over money and property is reported.
- Hagiographic language condemning avarice or “ill-gotten” wealth is not equivalent to an account ledger, prosecution, or court finding of usury.
- No identified surviving charge, trial, or conviction establishes that Pica or Pietro committed usury.
- No evidence connects Pica personally with fraud, money laundering, or criminal financial activity.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Cultural Development

## Political activity

No political office, factional allegiance, diplomatic action, or civic appointment is documented for Pica.

The family lived amid the social conflict of communal Assisi, including struggles between elites and the emerging commune and warfare with Perugia. No evidence assigns her an active political role.

## Religious orientation

Celano describes Francis’s mother as a woman of integrity and compares her with Saint Elizabeth. Bonaventure portrays her as compassionate toward Francis’s religious calling.

This supports a hagiographic image of a pious Christian woman. It does not establish:

- membership in the Franciscan Third Order;
- Cathar belief;
- affiliation with Waldensians or other dissenting movements;
- mystical experiences;
- theological writings;
- or formal ecclesiastical patronage.

## Alleged Cathar connection

Some modern speculative works place a Provençal Pica in a region associated with Catharism and imply that she may have transmitted heterodox or dualist ideas to Francis.

No contemporary or near-contemporary evidence identifies Pica as a Cathar. The argument generally depends on the unproven premises that she was Provençal and that regional origin implies religious affiliation. It should be classified as **allegation or theory without direct evidence**.

## Troubadour and courtly influence

Francis’s French songs, chivalric vocabulary, and courtly imagery are well known features of his cultural formation. Pica is often made their transmitter.

Possible channels include:

- maternal language, if she was French;
- Pietro’s French trade;
- merchant networks;
- aristocratic and popular song culture;
- Francis’s own contact with transalpine travelers.

No source records a specific song, book, lesson, or troubadour transmitted by Pica.

## Important Relationships and Networks

## Pietro di Bernardone

- **Nature:** husband.
- **Context:** prosperous Assisi merchant household.
- **Documented conflict:** Pietro’s coercive confinement of Francis and Pica’s decision to release him.
- **Marriage quality:** otherwise unknown.
- **Modern interpretation:** sometimes framed as a contrast between an authoritarian father and an empathetic mother. The contrast comes from hagiographic narrative selection and should not be expanded into a complete psychological portrait.

## Francis of Assisi

- **Nature:** son.
- **Documented or early reported acts:** she named him Giovanni; raised him in the Bernardone household; released him from confinement.
- **Claimed influence:** French language, courtliness, compassion, Marian spirituality, generosity, and religious sensibility.
- **Assessment:** only the naming and liberation episodes have strong early textual support.

## Angelo

- **Nature:** son, or at minimum Francis’s brother and a person identified through Pica.
- **Evidence:** medieval family designations and later genealogical reconstruction.
- **Details of relationship:** unknown.

## Giovanetto and Piccardo

- **Nature:** grandsons through Angelo, according to Assisi genealogical references.
- **Importance:** their legal identification helped preserve Pica’s name.
- **Personal contact:** unknown; Pica may have died before their adulthood.

## Clare of Assisi

Later artistic and devotional treatments often place Pica and Clare in a shared sympathetic network around Francis. No source consulted documents a meeting, correspondence, friendship, or patronage between them.

## Bishop Guido of Assisi

Pica’s family crisis culminated before Bishop Guido, but she is not securely recorded as present or as having a direct relationship with him.

Searches were conducted using Pica’s principal French and Italian name forms with terms equivalent to controversy, scandal, allegations, lawsuit, fraud, accusations, conspiracy, criticism, false genealogy, and polemic, including material published in 2024–2026.

No evidence was found of:

- ecclesiastical investigation;
- formal charge;
- conviction;
- political violence;
- corruption;
- fraud;
- money laundering;
- intelligence activity;
- sexual scandal;
- or personal excommunication.

The major contested material concerns **identity, ancestry, place of origin, family wealth, and retrospective religious interpretation**.

## The missing Bourlémont marriage contract

### Story

Claude Frassen reportedly stated that an old manuscript or marriage contract in the Bourlémont family archive proved that Pica belonged to that noble house.

### Circulation

- Frassen’s seventeenth-century Franciscan commentary;
- Nicolò Papini’s 1825–1827 history;
- nineteenth-century and early twentieth-century lives of Francis;
- modern devotional websites and genealogies.

### Evidence for

- Frassen’s assertion;
- later repetition of the assertion;
- compatibility with the established fact that Pietro traveled to France.

### Evidence against or limiting it

- no produced document;
- no archival reference;
- no independent examination;
- chronological and geographical objections concerning the Bourlémont line;
- nineteenth-century scholars explicitly demanded publication and verification.

### Assessment

**Disputed claim, presently unverified.** It may reflect a lost document, a misunderstanding, family prestige-making, or invention. The evidence currently accessible cannot determine which.

## Possible patronage motive behind the genealogy

A modern local-history argument notes that Louis d’Anglure de Bourlémont was a powerful ecclesiastical figure in France around Frassen’s time and proposes that connecting his family to Saint Francis’s mother might have been flattering or politically useful.

This is an **allegation or theory**. Chronological coincidence and potential benefit do not prove that Frassen fabricated evidence at a patron’s request.

## Moriconi/Morosini genealogy

Some later biographies and online trees transform Pietro di Bernardone into “Pietro Bernadone dei Moriconi,” “Morosini,” or a member of a Venetian line, then assign Pica children belonging to that genealogy.

Treccani states that no document survives expressly concerning Pietro and warns that noble-family reconstructions rest on late material. The Venetian-style pedigrees examined lack adequate sourcing.

Assessment: **unverified genealogical conflation**, with a substantial risk that unrelated names and families have been merged.

## Family violence and coercion

The Franciscan sources describe Pietro seizing, striking, binding, and confining Francis. Pica released him.

- The conduct is a **reported fact in early hagiographic sources**.
- It was not the subject of a known criminal prosecution.
- Modern descriptions of Pica and Francis as victims of domestic abuse are interpretive applications of present terminology.
- The narrative also had a theological function: contrasting earthly paternal authority with obedience to God.

## Parental indulgence and responsibility

Celano’s account places some responsibility on Francis’s parents for indulging his youthful extravagance. This is hagiographic moral analysis, not a judicial accusation. It cannot establish Pica’s individual spending decisions or parenting practices.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## Birth in a stable

### Story

Pica suffered difficult labor. A mysterious pilgrim advised that she be taken to a stable, where she gave birth beside an ox and ass, making Francis’s birth resemble Christ’s at Bethlehem.

### Appearance and circulation

The story is absent from:

- Thomas of Celano’s early lives;
- the *Legend of the Three Companions*;
- Bonaventure;
- the *Acts of Blessed Francis and His Companions*;
- the *Fioretti*;
- Arnald of Sarrant’s *Kinship of Saint Francis*;
- and Bartolomeo da Pisa’s *Book of Conformities*.

Franciscan scholarship dates its emergence to the late fourteenth or fifteenth century. It became attached to the oratory of San Francesco Piccolino in Assisi.

### Evidence

No early evidence supports it. Its structure transparently conforms Francis’s life to Christ’s nativity.

### Effect on reputation

It transformed Pica into a Marian figure and Francis into an *alter Christus* from birth. The legend shaped pilgrimage, local sacred geography, painting, and devotional biography.

### Assessment

**Late hagiographic myth.**

## The prophetic pilgrim

### Story

A pilgrim came to the Bernardone house after Francis’s birth, insisted on seeing the child, embraced him, and prophesied that he would become one of the best men in the world. Another child born nearby would become one of the worst.

### Sources

- *Book of Exemplary Stories*, compiled approximately 1280–1310, attributes the report through Brother Nicholas of Assisi to his mother, allegedly a neighbor.
- Arnald of Sarrant’s *Kinship of Saint Francis* (1365).
- Bartolomeo da Pisa’s *Book of Conformities* (1385–1399).
- Later Mariano da Firenze, who names the evil counterpart Azolino de Navata.

### Evidence

The story has a relatively early local-memory chain but is absent from the first biographies. Its strong conformity to the presentation of Jesus in the Temple and Simeon’s prophecy suggests literary shaping.

### Effect

It made Pica a receptive witness to divine foreknowledge and strengthened her Marian/Elizabethan symbolism.

### Assessment

**Late-medieval reported tradition with hagiographic structure; not independently verified.**

## Pica as Provençal noblewoman

### Story

Pica was a gentlewoman of a distinguished Provençal house, met by Pietro during a trading journey.

### Earliest clear circulation

Seventeenth-century Frassen, followed by Papini and numerous later biographies.

### Evidence for

Pietro’s French travel, Francis’s name, Francis’s use of French, and Frassen’s assertion of an archival manuscript.

### Evidence against or limiting it

The earliest sources do not name Bourlémont or Provence; the alleged archival document has not been produced.

### Effect

The story offered a maternal explanation for Francis’s courtliness, French songs, emotional refinement, and international cultural character.

### Assessment

**Later tradition; possible but unproven.**

## Pica as Picard

This version derives Pica from Picardy or treats her name as Piccarda. It attempts to align the name with northern French trade routes and fairs. No direct evidence proves it.

Assessment: **philological and geographical theory**.

## Pica as Picene or Umbrian

This family of theories relocates her to central Italy and explains “Bourlemont” as a French-style rendering of Belmonte or Monte Burella.

Evidence consists largely of names, regional symbolism, and commercial geography. No marriage, birth, or land record identifies her with these places.

Assessment: **modern regional-origin theory**.

## Pica as Cathar

The argument associates a supposedly Provençal mother with Cathar culture and then reads Francis’s spirituality through that connection.

No evidence identifies Pica as Cathar or records any heresy inquiry concerning her.

Assessment: **unsupported theory**.

## Pica as the source of Francis’s sanctity

Devotional biography often contrasts:

- Pietro as wealth, anger, commerce, and coercion;
- Pica as faith, tenderness, freedom, and spiritual intuition.

The contrast has a basis in the liberation episode but extends far beyond the evidence when used to explain Francis’s entire character.

Assessment: **hagiographic and psychological interpretation**.

No illness, injury, addiction, or psychological crisis of Pica is documented.

The difficult-childbirth story belongs to the stable-birth legend and should not be treated as medical history.

### Death

- **Date:** unknown.
- **Place:** unknown, conventionally assumed to be Assisi.
- **Cause:** unknown.
- **Burial:** unknown.

- the approval of his later rule;
- the stigmata;

## Awards, Honors, Offices, and Veneration

Pica was not canonized or beatified and held no known office.

Her informal honors include:

- respectful medieval designation *domina*;
- later “Monna Pica” or “Madonna Pica”;
- representation in Franciscan art;
- statues with Pietro near Assisi’s Chiesa Nuova;
- incorporation into pilgrimage narratives;
- literary and cinematic portrayal as Francis’s sympathetic mother.

She is sometimes treated devotionally as a model of maternal trust, but there is no universal Catholic liturgical cult attached to her.

## Books and Major Works About the Person

## Primary and medieval sources

1. **Thomas of Celano, *Vita beati Francisci* / *First Life of Saint Francis***, completed about 1228–1230.  
   Earliest full biography; provides the family and moral setting but not Pica’s name.

2. **Thomas of Celano, *Memoriale in desiderio animae* / *Memorial of the Desire of a Soul* or *Second Life***, about 1246–1247.  
   States that Francis’s mother named him John and compares her with Saint Elizabeth; narrates the family crisis.

3. **The *Legend of the Three Companions***, compiled from material associated with Leo, Rufino, and Angelo and transmitted in the mid-thirteenth century.  
   Reports that Francis’s mother named him John while Pietro was in France.

4. **Bonaventure, *Legenda maior sancti Francisci***, composed about 1260–1263.  
   Narrates Pica’s liberation of Francis, without supplying the later Bourlémont genealogy.

5. **Bonaventure, *Legenda minor sancti Francisci***, about 1260–1262.  
   Repeats the Giovanni/Francesco naming account.

6. ***Liber exemplorum fratrum minorum saeculi XIII* / *Book of Exemplary Stories***, approximately 1280–1310.  
   Contains the prophetic-pilgrim story and names *domina Pica*.

7. **Arnald of Sarrant, *De cognatione sancti Francisci* / *The Kinship of Saint Francis***, 1365.  
   Develops the prophetic-birth tradition.

8. **Bartolomeo da Pisa, *Liber de conformitate vitae beati Francisci ad vitam Domini Iesu***, 1385–1399.  
   Develops parallels between Francis and Christ; contains the pilgrim prophecy but apparently not the stable-birth story.

## Early modern and modern historical works

9. **Claude Frassen, commentary on the Rule of the Third Order**, seventeenth century; later editions include Paris, 1752.  
   Principal source for the Bourlémont-family claim and alleged archival manuscript.

10. **Nicolò Papini Tartagni, *Storia di San Francesco d’Assisi***, two volumes, Foligno, 1825–1827.  
    Repeats the Provençal Bourlémont claim and discusses the birthplace traditions.

11. **Stanislao Melchiorri, nineteenth-century Franciscan scholarship**, including criticism published around 1856.  
    Challenged the French noble genealogy after consulting French family histories.

12. **Michele Faloci Pulignani, writing in *Archivio storico per le Marche e l’Umbria***, 1885.  
    Criticized uncritical repetition of the alleged contract and called for its publication.

13. **Paul Sabatier, *Vie de S. François d’Assise***, first published 1893; numerous later editions.  
    Landmark critical biography of Francis; influential in modern reinterpretation.

14. **Arnaldo Fortini, *Nova vita di San Francesco***, twentieth century.  
    Major Assisi-based historical study; analyzes family tradition and the late stable legend.

15. **Girolamo Golubovich, *La storicità della casa paterna di San Francesco di Assisi, oggi Chiesa Nuova, e la popolare leggenda della stalletta***, Florence, 1941.  
    Rejects the stable-birth story as legend.

16. **L. Bracaloni, *La Chiesa Nuova di San Francesco converso, casa paterna del Santo di Assisi***, Todi, 1943.  
    Study of the family-house tradition.

17. **Omer Englebert, *La vie de Saint François d’Assise***, Paris, 1947.  
    Treats the Bourlémont archival assertion skeptically.

18. **Raoul Manselli, *San Francesco d’Assisi***, 1980s editions.  
    Critical historical biography important to modern Franciscan studies.

19. **André Vauchez, *François d’Assise: Entre histoire et mémoire***, 2009; English translation, *Francis of Assisi: The Life and Afterlife of a Medieval Saint*.  
    Major scholarly synthesis attentive to the difference between Francis’s history and later memory.

20. **Augustine Thompson, *Francis of Assisi: A New Biography***, Cornell University Press, 2012.  
    Critical reconstruction separating narrative life from detailed source analysis.

21. **Noel Muscat, “The Birth of Saint Francis of Assisi.”**  
    Focused study of the early sources, prophetic-pilgrim narrative, stable legend, and sacred geography.

22. **Medardo Arduino and Giuseppe Gentili, *San Francesco d’Assisi era di famiglia Picena?***  
    Modern argument for a Picene/Umbrian rather than Provençal maternal origin; speculative.

## Films, Television, Interviews, and Archival Material

Pica’s screen representations generally amplify the sympathetic-mother role beyond the sparse medieval record.

### Film and television portrayals

- **1927 – *Frate Francesco***  
  Monna Pica portrayed by Elena Droubetzkoy-Baranovitch. The historical-film catalogue consulted identifies the cast and production.

- **1961 – *Francis of Assisi***, directed by Michael Curtiz  
  Donna Pica portrayed by Edith Sharpe.

- **1966 – *Francesco di Assisi***, directed by Liliana Cavani  
  Pica portrayed by Maria Grazia Marescalchi. Cavani’s film presents Francis through a modern, socially oppositional interpretation influenced by Paul Sabatier.

- **1972 – *Fratello sole, sorella luna / Brother Sun, Sister Moon***, directed by Franco Zeffirelli  
  Pica portrayed by Valentina Cortese. The film depicts her nursing Francis and responding sympathetically to his transformation. The American Film Institute synopsis calls her a pious Frenchwoman and contrasts her acceptance with Pietro’s impatience.

- **2002 – *Francesco / St. Francis***, television film directed by Michele Soavi  
  Monna Pica portrayed by Erika Blanc.

- **2007 – *Chiara e Francesco / Clare and Francis***, television film directed by Fabrizio Costa  
  Pica portrayed by Ángela Molina.

These are dramatizations, not documentary sources for Pica’s speech, appearance, or private psychology.

### Artistic and pilgrimage material

- Chiesa Nuova and the traditional Bernardone house in Assisi;
- San Francesco Piccolino, associated with the stable-birth legend;
- sculptures of Pietro and Pica near Chiesa Nuova;
- Sacro Monte di Orta representations of Francis’s birth;
- paintings and devotional images portraying Pica as a Marian mother.

No authenticated likeness from life survives.

### Archival priorities for further research

The most promising repositories and document groups are:

- Archivio Storico Comunale di Assisi;

- Franciscan manuscript collections containing the early lives;
- editions of *Analecta Franciscana*;

- surviving Bourlémont and Anglure family archives;
- the 6 November 1926 issue of *Il Mattino*;
- nineteenth-century issues of *Archivio storico per le Marche e l’Umbria*.

## Later Life and Present Historical Status

Pica’s traceable life ends with the family crisis of approximately 1205–1206. All later-life reconstructions are conjectural.

Possible but unproven scenarios include:

- continued residence with Pietro in Assisi;
- continued private sympathy with Francis;
- widowhood in Assisi;
- survival into the early Franciscan movement;
- death before Francis’s public fame;
- or burial in a family or parish grave later lost.

The historical individual is deceased, but neither the year nor circumstances of death are known.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Pica’s reputation developed in distinct layers.

### Thirteenth century

She was:

- the unnamed virtuous mother who named Francis Giovanni;
- the mother who freed him from paternal confinement;
- and, by later thirteenth-century local tradition, *domina Pica*, witness to a prophecy over the newborn.

### Fourteenth and fifteenth centuries

Hagiography increasingly conformed Francis’s life to Christ’s. Pica consequently acquired Marian and Elizabethan characteristics. The stable-birth story placed her within an Assisian Bethlehem.

### Seventeenth through nineteenth centuries

Frassen’s Bourlémont claim gave her:

- a surname;
- French noble status;
- Provençal origins;
- and an alleged archival marriage contract.

Devotional biographies repeated these details as fact. Critical historians increasingly questioned the missing document and genealogical chronology.

### Twentieth century

Biographies, statues, cinema, and television established the familiar image:

- cultivated French mother;
- tender counterweight to a severe merchant father;
- defender of Francis’s vocation;
- and transmitter of French courtliness.

Regional revisionists contested the French account and claimed Picene or Umbrian origins.

### Twenty-first century

Institutional, religious, tourist, and popular sources still frequently state “Pica de Bourlemont, noblewoman from Provence” without explaining the late provenance of the claim. More recent local historical treatments emphasize the uncertainty and absence of the alleged contract.

Her enduring historical importance rests on one early narrative decision: freeing Francis when paternal force could not change his religious resolve.
