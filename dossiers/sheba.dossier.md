# The Queen of Sheba: Research Dossier

## Scope and Evidentiary Method

The Queen of Sheba cannot presently be identified with a securely documented historical individual. No contemporary inscription, administrative record, tomb, monument, or excavated object names a queen who can confidently be matched to the visitor described in 1 Kings 10.

Consequently, a continuous biography—from birth through death—cannot be reconstructed in the ordinary sense. What can be reconstructed is:

1. The earliest surviving literary account.
2. The historical and geographical setting in which that account places her.
3. Later Jewish, Christian, Ethiopian, and Islamic expansions.
4. The political, religious, racial, gendered, artistic, and dynastic uses of her story.
5. The limits of the archaeological evidence.

The following evidentiary labels are used:

- **Documented textual fact:** A claim demonstrably present in a cited source, without implying that the narrated event occurred historically.
- **Historically documented fact:** Supported by contemporary inscriptional, archaeological, administrative, or comparable evidence.
- **Probable historical context:** Supported by historical evidence but not directly attached to the queen.
- **Reported tradition:** A later source’s report, not independently verified.
- **Disputed historical hypothesis:** A scholarly reconstruction for which the evidence is inconclusive.
- **Myth or legend:** A culturally consequential narrative lacking contemporary verification or containing supernatural elements.
- **Unverified identification:** An attempt to attach the literary queen to a named historical person without adequate evidence.

## Historical Setting: Saba, South Arabia, and the Incense Trade

### The documented kingdom

Sabaʾ was a real South Arabian polity. Its heartland lay around the Marib oasis in present-day Yemen. Sabaean civilization is documented through monumental architecture, irrigation works, temples, and thousands of Ancient South Arabian inscriptions.

UNESCO describes the Marib complex as evidence of a centralized and technologically accomplished society whose prosperity was connected to the incense trade. Its protected archaeological components include:

- Ancient Marib.
- The Awām Temple.
- The Barrān Temple.
- The ancient Marib Dam.
- The ancient city of Sirwāḥ.
- Jabal al-Balaq.
- Associated irrigation works.

The documented florescence emphasized by UNESCO begins around the eighth century BCE, although settlement and the early formation of Sabaean culture may be older. This creates a chronological problem for a literal tenth-century-BCE meeting with Solomon: the kingdom is archaeologically real, but its best-documented monumental phase is later than Solomon’s conventional dates. [UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1700/)

The Awām Temple outside Marib was one of ancient Arabia’s major religious centers, associated with Almaqah, principal deity of Saba. Archaeological work there has revealed monumental structures and extensive inscriptions but no inscription naming Bilqīs, Makeda, Solomon, or a queen identifiable with the biblical visitor. [Smithsonian National Museum of Asian Art](https://asia-archive.si.edu/exhibition/awam-temple/)

### Government and the problem of a queen

Known Sabaean rulers generally bore titles such as *mukarrib* and later *malik*. The epigraphic record has not produced a securely attested sovereign queen corresponding to the story.

Female rulers were not impossible in ancient Arabia. Assyrian sources name several queens of North Arabian tribal confederations during the eighth and seventh centuries BCE. That evidence demonstrates that Arabian queenship existed, but it does not identify any of those women as the Queen of Sheba. Attempts to equate her with a known North Arabian queen remain speculative.

### Trade context

The biblical gifts—spices, gold, and precious stones—fit the literary image of South Arabia as a source or intermediary for luxury goods. Frankincense and myrrh moved from southern Arabia northward through caravan routes and maritime exchange. A diplomatic-commercial encounter between a South Arabian ruler and a Levantine court is therefore historically conceivable.

Conceivability is not documentation. No South Arabian or Levantine record independently confirms the meeting.

### Arabia or Africa?

The principal geographical alternatives are:

1. **South Arabia, especially Yemen:** The majority identification because Saba is epigraphically documented there and was associated with incense and luxury trade.
2. **Northeast Africa:** Josephus calls her ruler of Egypt and Ethiopia; Ethiopian tradition makes her an Ethiopian queen.
3. **A Red Sea realm spanning both shores:** Cultural exchange between South Arabia and the Horn of Africa is documented, but no evidence establishes a single tenth-century queen ruling both.
4. **North Arabia:** Proposed by some researchers because Assyrian records preserve Arabian queens and because early overland trade passed through northwestern Arabia. This remains a minority and disputed hypothesis.

Ancient South Arabian language and monumental culture crossed the Red Sea into the Horn of Africa during the early first millennium BCE. This helps explain later competition between Yemeni and Ethiopian traditions for the queen but does not establish her nationality.

## Family and Ancestry

### Historically recoverable family

Nothing is known. No primary or near-contemporary source gives:

- Her father or mother.
- A dynasty.
- Siblings.
- A husband.
- Children.
- An heir.
- A predecessor or successor.

### Ethiopian legendary genealogy

The *Kebra Nagast* supplies a retrospective dynastic genealogy:

- Makeda is Queen of Ethiopia or the South.
- She visits Solomon.
- Solomon has intercourse with her.
- She bears Bayna Ləḥkəm, later called Menelik.
- Menelik travels to Jerusalem as a young man and is recognized by Solomon.
- He returns with Israelite nobles and the Ark of the Covenant.
- Ethiopia’s kings descend from him.

This is **medieval dynastic tradition**, not contemporary genealogy. The extant *Kebra Nagast* is a medieval Geʽez compilation, conventionally associated with the thirteenth or fourteenth century, although it incorporates older materials. Its political purpose includes legitimating Ethiopia’s Solomonic dynasty. [*Kebra Nagast*, introduction](https://archive.sacred-texts.com/afr/kn/kn004.htm)

### The serpent-king tradition

Some Ethiopian oral and literary versions place Makeda after a ruler or serpent called Arwe or Wainaba. Variant narratives say that her father—or a hero who became her father or predecessor—killed the serpent that had oppressed the country. The versions are inconsistent and late. They belong to **foundation mythology**, not reconstructable family history.

### Islamic legendary genealogy

Muslim commentators supplied competing ancestries for Bilqīs. Some made her the daughter of a Yemeni king; some identified her father as al-Hadhād or Sharaḥbīl. Other accounts gave her a mother from the jinn, usually to explain stories about unusual feet, hairy legs, or supernatural ancestry. These are **post-Qur’anic legends**: the Qur’an itself names neither parent.

## Childhood, Early Life, Education, and Formation

No early source describes the queen’s birth, childhood, education, accession, teachers, or formative experiences.

The Hebrew narrative introduces her already reigning and already possessing:

- Independent resources.
- Intelligence and curiosity.
- Knowledge of Solomon’s reputation.
- The ability to organize a major caravan.
- Authority to negotiate and exchange royal gifts.

The Qur’anic narrative similarly introduces an established ruler with:

- A throne.
- A governing council.
- Military resources.
- Diplomatic judgment.
- A people engaged in solar worship.
- Power to dispatch emissaries and costly gifts.

These are features of her literary characterization, not an independently verified education or career.

Later traditions infer wisdom from her riddles. She consequently became an emblem of intellectual inquiry: the foreign ruler who does not accept hearsay but tests reputed wisdom personally.

## Chronological Life History

## Unrecorded Beginning

Birth, ancestry, childhood, accession, and early reign are unknown.

If a historical ruler lies behind the tradition, she probably lived somewhere within the network joining South Arabia, western Arabia, the Red Sea, northeast Africa, and the Levant. Her exact kingdom, capital, and dates cannot be established.

## The Hebrew Bible Narrative

### Text and date

The earliest surviving narrative is 1 Kings 10:1–13, repeated with limited alteration in 2 Chronicles 9:1–12. Kings reached its received form through a long process of composition and editing centuries after Solomon’s conventional reign. The account is therefore not a contemporary court record.

### Hearing of Solomon

**Documented textual fact:** The queen hears of Solomon’s fame, linked in the Hebrew text with the name of YHWH. She decides to test him with “hard questions” or riddles.

The narrative does not state:

- Who informed her.
- Whether she came for trade, diplomacy, religion, curiosity, or marriage.
- Her personal name.
- Her capital.
- Her route.
- Her age.

### Journey to Jerusalem

She travels to Jerusalem with a “very great caravan” or retinue. Her camels carry:

- Spices.
- Very much gold.
- Precious stones.

The use of camels and South Arabian luxury goods evokes long-distance Arabian trade. The precise route is unstated. A journey from Marib to Jerusalem by caravan would have crossed approximately 2,000 kilometers of western Arabia, though that distance is a modern reconstruction, not given in the text.

### Examination of Solomon

She speaks with Solomon about “all that was on her heart.” Solomon answers every question. No biblical source preserves the questions.

She then inspects:

- Solomon’s wisdom.
- His palace.
- Food and table arrangements.
- The seating or residence of officials.
- Servants and their clothing.
- Cupbearers.
- His ascent or burnt offerings at the Temple—the Hebrew wording is textually debated.

She is overwhelmed or “breathless.” She declares that reports heard in her homeland understated his wisdom and prosperity.

### Political and religious speech

The queen blesses YHWH for placing Solomon on Israel’s throne and associates his kingship with justice and righteousness. The narrative does not explicitly say that she converts.

### Gifts

She gives Solomon:

- 120 talents of gold.
- A very great quantity of spices.
- Precious stones.

Depending on the assumed weight of a biblical talent, 120 talents could represent roughly four metric tons of gold. The figure is part of a literary description of royal magnificence; it should not be treated as an audited transaction.

Solomon gives her “all her desire” and whatever she asks, in addition to royal gifts. The nature and value of his gifts are not specified.

### Departure

She returns “to her own land” with her servants. That is the end of her biblical life history. There is:

- No marriage.
- No sexual encounter.
- No child.
- No annexation of Sheba.
- No later correspondence.
- No death account.

The full biblical episode is available in [1 Kings 10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+Kings+10&version=CSB%3BNET%3BNIV%3BKJV%3BNKJV).

## Second Chronicles

2 Chronicles 9:1–12 reproduces the visit as part of its presentation of Solomon’s divinely granted wisdom, Temple-centered kingship, and international prestige.

The Chronicler does not independently identify the queen, add a romance, or narrate descendants. The repetition shows the story’s canonical importance but is not a second independent witness to the event.

## Josephus: First-Century Reinterpretation

In *Jewish Antiquities* 8.165–175, written in the 90s CE, Flavius Josephus retells the encounter.

He adds or emphasizes that:

- She was queen of Egypt and Ethiopia.
- She was highly intelligent and devoted to philosophy.
- She wanted empirical proof rather than hearsay.
- She brought balsam or a balsam-related plant to the region.

Josephus’s geographical identification differs from the usual South Arabian reading. It may reflect Greco-Roman geographical traditions in which “Ethiopia,” Arabia, and the lands south of Egypt were imperfectly differentiated.

Josephus wrote roughly a millennium after Solomon’s conventional period and does not cite a contemporary inscription. His additions are **ancient reported tradition**, not direct evidence for her identity. [Josephus, *Antiquities* 8.165](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0146%3Abook%3D8%3Asection%3D165)

## New Testament Reception

Matthew 12:42 and Luke 11:31 call her the “Queen of the South.” Jesus says she will rise at the judgment and condemn a generation that failed to respond, because she came “from the ends of the earth” to hear Solomon’s wisdom.

This is not a new biography. The queen functions typologically as:

- A foreign witness.
- A seeker of wisdom.
- A contrast to unreceptive hearers.
- A participant in the future judgment.

The passage greatly strengthened her status in Christian exegesis. It also created a documented literary connection between her, Jesus, and Solomon.

## Rabbinic and Jewish Legendary Development

### The skeptical rabbinic reading

A tradition in *Bava Batra* 15b questions whether the expression should be read as an individual “Queen of Sheba,” proposing instead a “kingdom” or sovereignty of Sheba. This is an exegetical position, not proof that the biblical author meant no woman.

### Riddles

Jewish legend supplied the missing “hard questions.” Collections vary, but commonly attributed tests concern:

- Distinguishing boys from girls dressed identically.
- Distinguishing natural from artificial flowers.
- Threading a perforated or twisted object.
- Water that does not originate from heaven or earth.
- Things that live or move in paradoxical ways.
- Legal or theological puzzles.

There is no stable ancient list. The riddles are **later literary elaborations** expressing the queen’s intelligence and Solomon’s superior discernment.

### Targum Sheni to Esther

The *Targum Sheni* embeds an extensive Solomon–Sheba episode into its elaboration of Esther. In this version:

- Solomon rules animals, birds, demons, spirits, and liliths.
- The hoopoe discovers that Sheba has not submitted to him.
- The queen worships the sun.
- Solomon sends a threatening letter by bird.
- The queen tears her clothing when the birds darken the sun.
- Although Sheba is described as a seven-year journey away, she reaches Solomon much faster.
- She brings ships or treasures and poses riddles.

The text’s dating is disputed, with proposals ranging across late antiquity and the early medieval period; its Queen of Sheba material is plainly much later than Kings. [*Targum Sheni* on Esther 1:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Targum_Sheni_on_Esther.1.2)

### Gender and demonization

Some Jewish and Islamic expansions focus on whether the queen is fully human, sexually dangerous, hairy, demonic, or related to Lilith. Jacob Lassner’s study treats these narratives as a cross-cultural negotiation of gender, foreign sovereignty, religious boundaries, and Solomon’s authority. [University of Chicago Press](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/D/bo3634130.html)

These stories do not preserve verifiable anatomy. They document how later interpreters managed the unsettling image of an autonomous foreign woman testing a paradigmatic male king.

## The Qur’anic Narrative

### Text

The queen appears, unnamed, in Sūrat al-Naml 27:20–44. “Bilqīs” comes from later commentary, not the Qur’an.

### The hoopoe’s report

Solomon notices the hoopoe’s absence. The bird returns with news from Saba:

- A woman rules them.
- She has been given “of everything.”
- She possesses a magnificent throne.
- She and her people prostrate themselves to the sun rather than God.

The queen is not initially mocked as incompetent. She is depicted as powerful, informed, and accustomed to consultation.

### Solomon’s letter

Solomon sends a letter through the hoopoe. The queen reads it to her council:

- It is from Solomon.
- It begins “In the name of God, the Compassionate, the Merciful.”
- It commands them not to exalt themselves against him and to come in submission.

### Consultation and diplomacy

Her advisers say they possess strength and military power but leave the decision to her. She warns that kings, when entering a city, ruin it and humiliate its nobility. She chooses to send a gift and observe the response.

This is a distinctive Qur’anic characterization: she favors reconnaissance and diplomacy before war.

### Rejection of the gift

Solomon rejects the gift, declaring what God has given him better than material tribute. He threatens military action.

### The throne

Before she arrives, Solomon asks his assembly who can bring her throne. An *ifrit* offers to do so; a figure “with knowledge of the Book” brings it in an instant. Solomon orders the throne disguised to test whether she recognizes it.

When asked whether it resembles her throne, she cautiously answers, in effect, “It is as though it were the same.”

### The glass floor

She enters a palace or court with a floor of glass. Mistaking it for water, she bares her legs. Solomon explains that it is a smooth surface of glass.

The Qur’an does not say her legs are hairy, goatlike, or deformed. Those details belong to later exegesis.

### Submission

She declares that she has wronged herself and submits with Solomon to God, Lord of the worlds. The text does not state that she marries Solomon or bears his child. [Qur’anic passage and commentary](https://quran.com/en/27%3A27/tafsirs/tazkirul-quran-en)

## Post-Qur’anic Islamic Traditions

Muslim historians, storytellers, and commentators developed the queen into Bilqīs.

Variants include:

- Bilqīs as queen of Yemen.
- Her father as a Himyarite or Yemeni ruler.
- Her mother as a jinni.
- Her accession after political conflict.
- Her people’s worship of the sun.
- The hoopoe’s discovery of her kingdom.
- Solomon’s advisers suspecting that her legs are hairy or hoofed.
- Solomon constructing the glass floor to see her legs.
- The invention or use of depilatory substances.
- Marriage to Solomon.
- Marriage to a Yemeni or Hamdanite ruler at Solomon’s direction.
- Continued independent rule.
- Her death and burial.

The versions conflict. Classical Qur’anic commentary preserves reports without producing a single canonical biography. Al-Ṭabarī’s tafsir is one major repository of such material. [Arabic text of al-Ṭabarī’s commentary](https://quran.ksu.edu.sa/tafseer/tabary/sura27-aya37.html)

### The hairy-leg narrative

**Myth or legend:** Solomon’s court suspects Bilqīs has nonhuman legs because of alleged jinn ancestry. The glass floor causes her to lift her clothing, revealing her legs. Some versions say Solomon orders a depilatory.

**Evidence:** The Qur’an mentions only that she uncovered her legs after mistaking glass for water. It says nothing about hair, hooves, deformity, or jinn parentage.

**Effect:** The expansion sexualized and domesticated a politically independent queen while generating stories about cosmetics, bodily difference, and the boundary between human and demon.

## Ethiopian Life Narrative: Makeda

### Textual source

The *Kebra Nagast*—“Glory of Kings”—contains the most extensive continuous life narrative. Its surviving form is medieval, not Solomonic-era. The work states or implies transmission through Coptic, Arabic, and Geʽez stages, but its textual history remains disputed.

### Makeda’s reign

Makeda rules a wealthy southern realm. A merchant named Tamrin, who trades with Solomon, reports the king’s wisdom and magnificence. Unlike the biblical account, this provides a named intermediary and a motivation for her journey.

### Journey and instruction

Makeda travels to Jerusalem with valuable goods. Solomon instructs her in:

- Government.
- Wisdom.
- The God of Israel.
- Israelite religious teaching.

She rejects solar worship and acknowledges the Creator.

### The oath and sexual encounter

The *Kebra Nagast* creates a carefully structured but coercive encounter:

- Makeda asks Solomon to swear that he will not take her by force.
- Solomon asks her to swear that she will not take anything from his house.
- He serves her heavily seasoned food.
- During the night she becomes thirsty and takes water.
- Solomon declares that she has broken her oath.
- They have intercourse.

The text presents the act through the logic of reciprocal oaths. By modern categories, her agreement occurs after thirst has been deliberately engineered and under a claim that she forfeited protection; the narrative does not supply evidence for enthusiastic consent. This is a **medieval literary episode**, not documented conduct by a historical Solomon or queen.

### Conception and departure

Solomon gives Makeda a ring or token for a possible child. She leaves Jerusalem and later gives birth to a son:

- Bayna Ləḥkəm, “son of the wise man.”
- Walda-Tabbib, another “son of the wise” formulation.
- Menelik in later usage.

### Menelik and the Ark

As a young adult, Menelik visits Solomon. Solomon recognizes him, wishes to keep him, and ultimately sends him home with the firstborn sons of Israelite nobles.

In the epic, the Ark of the Covenant leaves Jerusalem with Menelik’s company—whether through theft, divine will, or a combination of both in different readings. It comes to Ethiopia, transferring sacred election from Israel to Ethiopia.

Makeda then establishes or accepts dynastic rules favoring descent through Menelik. Some passages say that women will no longer occupy the Ethiopian throne.

### End of life

The *Kebra Nagast* does not provide a historically verifiable date or cause of Makeda’s death. Later Ethiopian king lists assign her reign and succession various dates, but these lists do not constitute contemporary evidence.

The English translation associated with E. A. Wallis Budge is available as a [complete digital text](https://www.sacred-texts.com/chr/kn/kn000-0.htm) and [PDF](https://www.yorku.ca/inpar/kebra_budge.pdf).

## Government, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Royal office

The queen is consistently represented as a sovereign, but only in texts composed long after the proposed event.

The Hebrew Bible emphasizes control over:

- A royal treasury.
- A large caravan.
- Luxury commodities.
- Servants and emissaries.

The Qur’an adds:

- A council of chiefs.
- Military capacity.
- Diplomatic messengers.
- A monumental throne.
- A settled polity whose elite could be endangered by invasion.

### Wealth

The biblical amount of 120 talents of gold is a narrative claim. No corresponding treasury record survives.

Spices and gemstones fit South Arabia’s trade networks, but some products may have originated farther away and passed through Arabian intermediaries. “Gold of Sheba” need not imply gold mining inside Saba itself.

### Business structures and property

No source identifies:

- Private estates.
- Commercial companies.
- Partnerships.
- Debts.
- Taxes.
- Investments.
- Patents or inventions.
- Palaces securely belonging to her.
- A personal tomb.

Modern sites called “Bilqīs’s throne,” “Bilqīs’s temple,” or “Bilqīs’s palace” carry folkloric names. The Barrān Temple near Marib is commonly called *ʿArsh Bilqīs*, “Throne of Bilqīs,” and the Awām Temple *Maḥram Bilqīs*. These names attest later memory, not ownership by an identified queen.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Biblical profile

The biblical queen begins as an independent foreign monarch. She tests Solomon, acknowledges his wisdom, blesses YHWH, exchanges gifts, and leaves. The text does not unequivocally narrate conversion or political submission.

### Qur’anic profile

The Qur’anic queen progresses through:

1. Solar worship.
2. Consultation.
3. Diplomatic testing.
4. Recognition of Solomon’s divinely supported power.
5. Recognition of her throne.
6. Encounter with the glass palace.
7. Submission to God with Solomon.

Her submission is religious, though it follows the pressure of Solomon’s military threat.

### Ethiopian profile

Makeda’s ideological transformation is from worship of the sun to the God of Israel. Her encounter becomes the origin of:

- Ethiopian Israelite identity.
- The Solomonic dynasty.
- Ethiopian possession of the Ark.
- A theology of transferred election.

### Political uses

The Solomonic descent claim became a charter of Ethiopian monarchy. The 1931 Ethiopian Constitution described Haile Selassie’s line as descending from Menelik I, son of Solomon and the Queen of Ethiopia known as the Queen of Sheba. The claim was dynastically and constitutionally consequential even though it is not historically verified.

In modern Black, Ethiopianist, and Rastafari reception, Makeda and Solomon form part of a sacred genealogy culminating in Haile Selassie. The Queen of Sheba thereby became:

- An emblem of African antiquity.
- A figure of Black sovereignty.
- A link between Ethiopia and biblical Israel.
- A female model of statecraft and wisdom.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### King Solomon

**Status:** Central literary relationship; historical meeting unverified.

Versions range from:

- Intellectual and diplomatic exchange in Kings.
- Political-religious submission in the Qur’an.
- A riddling contest in Jewish legend.
- Sexual union and co-parenthood in Ethiopian tradition.
- Romance and geopolitical conspiracy in modern film.

### Royal entourage

Kings mentions servants accompanying her but gives no names. Their number, offices, and later lives are unknown.

### Council and commanders

The Qur’an depicts a council of chiefs capable of war. No member is named.

### Tamrin

In the *Kebra Nagast*, Tamrin is a merchant and intermediary whose report of Solomon inspires Makeda’s journey. **Mythic-literary relationship; no contemporary documentation.**

### Menelik

Makeda’s son by Solomon in Ethiopian tradition. He has no role in the Hebrew Bible, Chronicles, Josephus, or Qur’an. **Medieval dynastic tradition.**

### Hiram of Tyre

Kings places the queen’s visit amid material about Solomon’s commerce and Hiram’s maritime activity. The text does not say that she met, corresponded with, or partnered with Hiram. Mere narrative proximity is not a documented relationship.

---

## Enemies, Rivals, Violence, and Coercion

No early source names a personal enemy or rival of the queen.

Potential conflict appears in the Qur’an:

- Solomon threatens invasion if her polity does not submit.
- Her council claims military strength.
- She chooses diplomacy because conquering kings devastate cities and humiliate elites.
- No battle occurs.

The *Targum Sheni* intensifies Solomon’s demand with threats involving human, animal, and supernatural armies.

The *Kebra Nagast* sexual encounter has generated controversy because Solomon manipulates the conditions of an oath. This is a feature of a medieval text, not evidence usable for a criminal accusation against a demonstrably historical person.

## Controversies, Allegations, and Unresolved Questions

## Did she exist?

**Unresolved question.**

Evidence favoring a possible historical core:

- Saba was real.
- Arabian female rulers existed.
- Long-distance Arabian–Levantine trade was historically possible.
- The episode contains plausible diplomatic gift exchange.

Evidence limiting confidence:

- No contemporary record names her.
- The biblical narrative was written or edited substantially later.
- Solomon’s own historical scale remains disputed.
- Secure monumental Sabaean evidence is strongest from a later period.
- Later traditions disagree radically about her name, territory, religion, family, and fate.

The defensible conclusion is neither “proved historical” nor “proved fictional,” but **historically unverified literary ruler, possibly preserving a memory or type of ancient diplomatic contact**.

## Was she Yemeni or Ethiopian?

**Disputed historical-geographical claim.**

- South Arabian identification has the strongest connection to the documented kingdom of Saba.
- Ethiopian identification has immense medieval and modern cultural importance but lacks tenth-century-BCE documentation.
- Cross-Red-Sea interaction helps explain overlapping claims.
- Josephus’s “Egypt and Ethiopia” is ancient evidence for reception, not contemporary proof.

## Was her name Bilqīs?

**Reported post-Qur’anic tradition.** Neither the Bible nor Qur’an names her. The etymology of Bilqīs remains uncertain.

## Was her name Makeda?

**Reported Ethiopian tradition.** The name is preserved by the *Kebra Nagast* but is not present in the earliest accounts.

## Did she marry Solomon?

- Bible: no.
- Josephus: no.
- Qur’an: no.
- Later Islamic reports: inconsistent.
- *Kebra Nagast*: sexual union, but not a conventional marriage narrative.
- Modern retellings: frequently romanticize the meeting.

Therefore, marriage is **legend**, not established fact.

## Was Menelik her son?

**Ethiopian dynastic legend.** No pre-medieval contemporary evidence establishes Menelik I as a historical son of Solomon and Makeda.

## Did she bring or cause the Ark to reach Ethiopia?

The *Kebra Nagast* attributes the Ark’s transfer to Menelik and his companions. No contemporary Levantine, South Arabian, or Ethiopian record verifies this. The claimed Ark at Aksum has not been subjected to an open scholarly examination capable of establishing its age or identity.

## Was she Black?

The ancient sources do not provide a physical description. Her later location in Ethiopia, Josephus’s identification, the “Queen of the South” title, and interpretations of “black and beautiful” in the Song of Songs encouraged African and Black representations. European artists alternately depicted her as white, Middle Eastern, exoticized “Oriental,” or African.

Claims about complexion are reception history, not recoverable physical biography.

## Was she a demon or Lilith?

**Myth.** Later interpretive traditions associated her with hairy legs, hooves, jinn ancestry, or Lilith. These associations have no basis in Kings and only indirect narrative prompts in the Qur’an’s glass-floor scene.

## Alleged tombs

Sites in Yemen, Ethiopia, Eritrea, Syria, and elsewhere have been associated in popular tradition with Bilqīs or Makeda. None has yielded an inscription and archaeological context sufficient to identify the occupant as the Queen of Sheba.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## The riddle contest

**Story:** The queen tests Solomon using verbal riddles, disguised children, artificial flowers, or objects requiring impossible manipulation.

**Appearance:** Later Jewish, Arabic, and European retellings expanding the biblical “hard questions.”

**Evidence:** Kings confirms questions but preserves none. Specific riddles are later inventions or transmitted folklore.

**Reputational effect:** Established the queen as intelligent and Solomon as intellectually supreme.

## The sun-worshipping kingdom

**Story:** Sheba worships the sun until confronted by Solomon.

**Appearance:** Qur’an 27 and expanded Targumic and Islamic narratives.

**Evidence:** Solar cults existed in ancient societies, but the religion of historical Saba centered on a pantheon including Almaqah and ʿAthtar. No inscription ties a queen Bilqīs to a particular conversion.

**Effect:** Turned diplomacy into a monotheistic conversion narrative.

## The hoopoe

**Story:** A hoopoe discovers Sheba and carries Solomon’s letter.

**Appearance:** Qur’an and Targum Sheni, with related differences.

**Evidence:** Supernatural-literary motif.

**Effect:** The hoopoe became a symbol of inspired messengership in Islamic and Persian literature, including later mystical works.

## The miraculous throne

**Story:** Her throne is transported to Solomon before she arrives and is disguised.

**Appearance:** Qur’an 27:38–42 and commentary.

**Evidence:** Miracle narrative, not historically verifiable.

**Effect:** Demonstrates Solomon’s divinely granted knowledge and power while allowing the queen to display perceptiveness.

## The glass floor

**Story:** She mistakes transparent glass over water for a pool and uncovers her legs.

**Appearance:** Qur’an 27:44.

**Later additions:** Hairy legs, hooves, jinn descent, depilation.

**Evidence:** Only the basic glass-floor episode is Qur’anic; bodily abnormalities are later commentary.

**Effect:** Produced extensive erotic, gendered, cosmetic, and demonological interpretation.

## Makeda and the oath

**Story:** Solomon promises not to take Makeda by force if she takes nothing from his house; after engineered thirst causes her to take water, he claims release from his promise.

**Appearance:** *Kebra Nagast*.

**Evidence:** Medieval literary tradition.

**Effect:** Supplies the conception of Menelik and thus the claimed Davidic ancestry of Ethiopian emperors.

## The Ark’s transfer to Ethiopia

**Story:** Menelik’s companions remove the Ark; divine signs indicate that its departure is providential.

**Appearance:** *Kebra Nagast*.

**Evidence:** No independent contemporary confirmation.

**Effect:** Made Aksum a sacred counterpart or successor to Jerusalem and formed a central Ethiopian national-religious narrative.

## The True Cross

Medieval Christian legend connected the Queen of Sheba to the wood later used for Christ’s cross. In variants, she recognizes or venerates the wood during her journey and predicts its future significance. Piero della Francesca’s fresco cycle at Arezzo integrates her with the Legend of the True Cross.

The story appears in medieval compilations including Jacobus de Voragine’s *Golden Legend*. It is Christian typology, not an incident in the biblical narrative. [*Golden Legend*, volume 3](https://ccel.org/ccel/v/voragine/goldleg3/cache/goldleg3.pdf)

## Freemasonry and esoteric reception

Solomon’s Temple became central to Freemasonic symbolism. Because the Queen of Sheba belongs to the Solomon cycle, she entered some Masonic, occult, Rosicrucian, and romantic retellings. No evidence connects a historical queen to Freemasonry, Hiram Abiff, or later initiatory organizations. Such claims are retrospective symbolic constructions.

## Works by or Attributed to the Queen

No securely authentic writing, inscription, speech transcript, letter, decree, artwork, or composition by the Queen of Sheba survives.

### Speeches attributed in ancient scripture

These are literary speeches placed in her mouth, not autograph documents:

- Questions to Solomon, unpreserved, in 1 Kings 10.
- Praise of Solomon and blessing of YHWH in 1 Kings 10:6–9.
- Consultation with her council in Qur’an 27:29–35.
- Warning about conquering kings in Qur’an 27:34.
- Recognition of the throne in Qur’an 27:42.
- Declaration of submission in Qur’an 27:44.

### Attributed letter

The *Targum Sheni* and later narratives attribute correspondence to the queen. No physical manuscript or contemporary inscription survives. These are literary compositions.

### Riddles

Numerous riddles circulate under her name. None can be authenticated as her composition.

### The *Kebra Nagast*

The work is about Makeda and her descendants, not authored by her. It must not be catalogued as her book.

## Books and Major Works About the Queen

### Primary and formative texts

- **1 Kings 10:1–13** — earliest canonical narrative.
- **2 Chronicles 9:1–12** — later biblical repetition.
- **Matthew 12:42; Luke 11:31** — “Queen of the South.”
- **Flavius Josephus, *Jewish Antiquities*, Book VIII, 8.165–175** — Egypt-and-Ethiopia identification.
- **Babylonian Talmud, *Bava Batra* 15b** — skeptical grammatical interpretation.
- ***Targum Sheni* to Esther 1** — hoopoe, sun worship, threats, riddling material.
- **Qur’an, Sūrat al-Naml 27:20–44** — unnamed queen, council, throne, glass court, submission.
- **Al-Ṭabarī, *Jāmiʿ al-bayān*, commentary on Qur’an 27** — variant Islamic reports.
- ***Kebra Nagast* / *Kəbrä Nägäśt*** — Makeda, Solomon, Menelik, and the Ark.
- **Jacobus de Voragine, *Legenda aurea*** — True Cross tradition.

### Major modern studies

- E. A. Wallis Budge, trans., *The Queen of Sheba and Her Only Son Menyelek (I): The Kebra Nagast* (1922). Important translation; its philology and historical assumptions require comparison with later scholarship.
- Enno Littmann, *The Legend of the Queen of Sheba in the Tradition of Axum* (1904).
- David Allen Hubbard, *The Literary Sources of the Kebra Nagast* (1956).
- Edward Ullendorff, “Hebraic-Jewish Elements in Abyssinian (Monophysite) Christianity” (1956).
- Jacob Lassner, *Demonizing the Queen of Sheba: Boundaries of Gender and Culture in Postbiblical Judaism and Medieval Islam* (1993).
- Kenneth A. Kitchen, *Documentation for Ancient Arabia*, multi-volume reference work (1994–2000).
- Nicholas Clapp, *Sheba: Through the Desert in Search of the Legendary Queen* (2001). Popular archaeological travel narrative.
- St John Simpson, ed., *Queen of Sheba: Treasures from Ancient Yemen* (British Museum Press, 2002).
- Vickie C. Byrd, ed., *Queen of Sheba: Legend and Reality* (2004).
- Stuart Munro-Hay, *The Quest for the Ark of the Covenant* (2005).
- Bernard Leeman, *Queen of Sheba and Biblical Scholarship* (2005). Advocates an early historical interpretation; several conclusions remain disputed.
- Marjorie Susan Bacon, *The Queen of Sheba’s Visit to King Solomon*.
- Deborah M. Coulter-Harris, *The Queen of Sheba: Legend, Literature and Lore* (2013).
- Michael Wood, *In Search of Myths and Heroes*, including treatment of the Queen of Sheba.
- Stanley M. Burstein, works on ancient northeast Africa and the geographical traditions surrounding Ethiopia.
- Yigal Levin, “The Queen of Sheba: Sabean or Ethiopian?”—a study of competing geographic interpretations.

The standard reference literature does not produce a scholarly consensus identifying her with a named ruler.

## Artistic, Literary, and Musical Reception

### Visual art

Major representations include:

- Medieval biblical manuscripts and cathedral sculpture.
- Ethiopian church paintings and manuscript cycles showing Makeda, Solomon, Menelik, and the Ark.
- Piero della Francesca’s *Legend of the True Cross* frescoes at San Francesco, Arezzo, including the queen’s recognition of the sacred wood.
- Tintoretto and other European treatments of the visit.
- Edward Poynter, *The Visit of the Queen of Sheba to King Solomon* (approximately 1890).
- Persian and Mughal manuscript illustrations of Bilqīs before Solomon.
- Twentieth-century African and diasporic images emphasizing Makeda’s African sovereignty.

The British Museum holds studies and prints related to European depictions and has exhibited ancient Yemeni material under the Queen of Sheba rubric. [British Museum biographical collection record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/term/BIOG78480)

### Music

- George Frideric Handel, *Solomon* (1749), whose instrumental “Arrival of the Queen of Sheba” became one of the best-known musical evocations of the episode.
- Charles Gounod, *La reine de Saba* (1862), opera with libretto by Jules Barbier and Michel Carré.
- Karl Goldmark, *Die Königin von Saba* (1875), opera with libretto by Salomon Hermann Mosenthal.
- Ottorino Respighi, *Belkis, regina di Saba* (1931), ballet score.
- Numerous popular songs, reggae recordings, Ethiopian works, and jazz compositions use “Sheba,” “Makeda,” or “Bilqīs” symbolically.

These compositions are adaptations, not biographical evidence.

## Films, Television, Documentaries, and Recordings

### Dramatic films

- ***The Queen of Sheba* (1921)** — American silent film directed by J. Gordon Edwards, starring Betty Blythe and Fritz Leiber. The production turned the story into an eroticized royal romance. Only fragments and stills are known to survive. The American Film Institute documents its production history. [AFI Catalog](https://catalog.afi.com/Film/11493-THE-QUEEN-OF-SHEBA)
- ***La regina di Saba / The Queen of Sheba* (1952)** — Italian film directed by Pietro Francisci, starring Leonora Ruffo; invents a romance involving Rehoboam.
- ***Solomon and Sheba* (1959)** — directed by King Vidor, starring Yul Brynner and Gina Lollobrigida. It makes Sheba an Egyptian-aligned political actor and Solomon’s lover. Tyrone Power originally played Solomon but died during production; Brynner replaced him.
- ***Solomon & Sheba* (1995)** — television film starring Jimmy Smits and Halle Berry.
- ***Solomon* (1997)** — television miniseries featuring the queen as part of Solomon’s dramatized reign.
- Numerous Bible, Qur’an-story, Ethiopian-history, and animated productions incorporate the episode.

### Documentary and educational treatments

- British Museum programming associated with *Queen of Sheba: Treasures from Ancient Yemen*.
- Michael Wood’s television and book project *In Search of Myths and Heroes*.
- Documentaries on Marib, ancient Yemen, Aksum, the Ark of the Covenant, and Haile Selassie regularly treat the tradition.
- Museum archives documenting the Wendell Phillips excavations at Timna and Marib supply archaeological context but no personal archive of the queen. [Smithsonian exhibition record](https://www.si.edu/exhibitions/unearthing-arabia-archaeological-adventures-wendell-phillips%3Aevent-exhib-5311)

## Death, Burial, and End of Life

No early source records her death.

Unknown details include:

- Date.
- Age.
- Location.
- Illness.
- Injury.
- Assassination or natural death.
- Successor.
- Funeral.
- Burial place.

Later Islamic and Ethiopian accounts sometimes supply reign lengths, successors, or burial traditions, but the versions are inconsistent and lack contemporary confirmation.

There is no authenticated skeletal remain, tomb inscription, funerary inventory, or ancient DNA sample associated with her.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Jewish tradition

She became an examiner of Solomon’s wisdom and, in some texts, a potentially disordering foreign woman whose sovereignty had to be subordinated. Other traditions retained her as a perceptive and wealthy monarch.

### Christianity

She became:

- The “Queen of the South” who recognized wisdom.
- A witness invoked by Jesus.
- A typological precursor of the Gentile Church.
- A visitor connected to the True Cross.
- A model of homage to sacred kingship.

### Islam

Bilqīs became one of the Qur’an’s most important women rulers. Her consultation with advisers and attempt to avert war have supported later discussions of:

- Female political authority.
- Diplomatic prudence.
- Conversion.
- The relationship between worldly sovereignty and prophetic authority.

The Qur’an does not condemn her for being a woman ruler. Later commentators variously admired her intelligence or added narratives that diminished, sexualized, married, or physiologically othered her.

### Ethiopia

Makeda became foundational to:

- Ethiopian Christian sacred history.
- The Solomonic dynasty.
- The Ark tradition at Aksum.
- Claims of descent from David and Solomon.
- State legitimacy through 1974.
- Ethiopian visual culture and national memory.

### Yemen

Bilqīs is associated with the monumental ruins of Marib. Temples popularly carrying her name have reinforced Yemen’s identification as the land of Sheba. The association supports cultural memory and heritage tourism but should not be confused with an excavated personal palace.

The Marib monuments were inscribed on UNESCO’s World Heritage List in Danger in 2023 because of conflict-related threats. Their survival preserves the historical civilization onto which the queen’s memory has been mapped. [UNESCO nomination and evaluation](https://whc.unesco.org/archive/2023/whc23-18extcom-INF.5-en.pdf)

### Africa and the diaspora

The queen has served as:

- An emblem of ancient African kingship and queenship.
- A counter-image to colonial narratives denying complex African states.
- An ancestor in Ethiopianist and Rastafari sacred history.
- A symbol of Black female intelligence, beauty, wealth, and autonomy.

### Modern gender politics

Modern writers have treated her as:

- A successful woman head of state.
- A negotiator who consulted her council.
- A ruler pressured by a more powerful monarch.
- An exoticized sexual object.
- A figure whose autonomy later traditions sought to control.

These readings are reception history. They should not be projected backward as facts about an unverified individual.

## Atlas Connections

Only source-supported connections from the supplied roster are included. Most are literary, genealogical, or reception-historical rather than verified personal meetings.

### King Solomon

- **Nature:** Central meeting, intellectual testing, diplomatic gift exchange, and—only in later traditions—conversion, sexual union, or marriage.
- **Date and place:** Traditionally tenth century BCE, Jerusalem; exact date unknown.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Documented textual fact; historical meeting unverified.**
- **Sources:** 1 Kings 10; 2 Chronicles 9; Qur’an 27; Josephus; *Kebra Nagast*.

### David ben Jesse

- **Nature:** Solomon’s father and, in Ethiopian dynastic tradition, remote ancestor of Makeda’s son Menelik and the Ethiopian royal line.
- **Date and place:** No meeting with the queen is narrated.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Literary/dynastic connection, not a documented personal relationship.**

### Bethsabée

- **Nature:** Solomon’s mother in the biblical royal genealogy. No source says that Bathsheba met the Queen of Sheba.
- **Date and place:** None recoverable.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Indirect literary family-network connection; no documented meeting.**

### Flavius Josephus

- **Nature:** Retold her story in *Jewish Antiquities* and identified her as queen of Egypt and Ethiopia.
- **Date and place:** Josephus wrote in the 90s CE, roughly a millennium after the supposed visit.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Documented literary reception; no personal contact.**

### Jesus of Nazareth

- **Nature:** Invoked her as the “Queen of the South” who came to hear Solomon and would rise at judgment.
- **Date and place:** Reported in first-century Galilean/Judean teaching traditions; Matthew 12:42 and Luke 11:31.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Documented New Testament literary connection; no personal meeting.**

### Muhammad ibn Abdullah

- **Nature:** The Qur’an, proclaimed in Muhammad’s prophetic career, retells the story of the unnamed ruler of Saba and Solomon.
- **Date and place:** Sūrat al-Naml is generally classified as Meccan, before the 622 CE migration.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Documented scriptural reception; no personal contact.**

### Haile Selassie I

- **Nature:** Claimed dynastic descent through Menelik I from Solomon and the Queen of Sheba; the genealogy formed part of Ethiopian monarchical legitimacy.
- **Dates and places:** Haile Selassie reigned in Ethiopia, 1930–1974, excluding the Italian occupation and exile period.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Documented dynastic claim; genealogy historically unverified.**

### Jacobus de Voragine

- **Nature:** His *Golden Legend* transmitted the medieval True Cross complex in which the queen recognizes wood connected to Christ’s crucifixion.
- **Date and place:** Thirteenth-century Latin Christendom.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Documented literary reception; mythic narrative.**

### Piero della Francesca

- **Nature:** Although not present on the roster, he is relevant to Jacobus’s connection; painted the queen in the *Legend of the True Cross* fresco cycle at Arezzo.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Documented artistic reception.**

### Hiram Abiff

- **Nature:** The queen and Hiram Abiff coexist in some modern Masonic, occult, and fictional Solomon cycles.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Later mythic association only.** No ancient source documents a meeting or relationship.

### Hiram of Tyre

Hiram of Tyre is not separately listed in the atlas roster. Kings places his commercial relations with Solomon near the Sheba episode, but does not document contact between Hiram and the queen.

### Abraham, Moses, Nabucodonosor II, Cleopatra VII, Dido, Alexander the Great, Aristotle, and other ancient roster figures

No consulted source establishes a personal meeting, correspondence, kinship, or historically acknowledged direct influence between these individuals and the Queen of Sheba. Similarity of legendary status, royal title, geography, or inclusion in later universal histories is insufficient.

**Atlas finding:** The only central first-order connection is Solomon. Connections to David and Bathsheba are indirect through Solomon; Josephus, Jesus, Muhammad, Jacobus de Voragine, and Haile Selassie belong to her reception and afterlife. No other roster connection should be inferred from contemporaneity or shared geography.
