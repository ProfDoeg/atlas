# Saint Augustine of Hippo: Research Dossier

**Prepared:** 24 August 2026  
**Scope:** Reconstructable life, writings, institutional activity, controversies, legends, reception, and source record.  
**Evidence convention:** “Documented” means supported by Augustine’s works, Possidius, surviving legal/ecclesiastical records, or modern critical scholarship. “Augustine’s account” identifies retrospective self-testimony. “Tradition/legend” identifies claims whose documentary basis is late, devotional, contested, or absent.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Principal Latin name:** *Aurelius Augustinus*.
- **Common English names:** Augustine of Hippo; Saint Augustine; St Augustine; Augustine.
- **Other forms:** Augustinus Hipponensis; Aurelius Augustine; French *Augustin d’Hippone*; Italian *Agostino d’Ippona*; Spanish *Agustín de Hipona*; Arabic forms include أغسطينوس/أوغسطينوس.
- **Ecclesiastical titles:** presbyter of Hippo Regius (ordained 391); coadjutor bishop, probably 395; bishop of Hippo Regius, normally dated 395/396–430; Church Father; one of the four traditional “great Latin Doctors”; “Doctor of Grace” in later Catholic usage.
- **Birth:** 13 November 354, Thagaste in Roman Numidia (modern Souk Ahras, Algeria).
- **Death:** 28 August 430, Hippo Regius (modern Annaba, Algeria), during the Vandal siege; about seventy-five years old.
- **Ethno-political setting:** a Roman citizen and Latin-speaking provincial of North Africa. Ancient evidence does not permit a modern racial label with precision. His family belonged to the municipally respectable but financially limited property-owning class; the names and cultural environment were Romanized, while Numidian/Punic background and spoken Punic in his region formed part of his African setting.
- **Languages:** Latin was his literary and professional language. He learned Greek poorly by his own account. He knew some Punic or at least recognized its pastoral importance, but the degree of fluency is disputed.
- **Feast:** 28 August in Western calendars; commemorated in multiple Christian traditions. Monica’s feast is 27 August in the modern Roman calendar.
- **Principal residences:** Thagaste; Madauros; Carthage; Rome; Milan; Cassiciacum (location disputed, traditionally Cassago Brianza); Ostia; back to Rome; Thagaste; Hippo Regius. His travel was concentrated in North Africa and Italy; no evidence supports medieval stories of extensive European hermit-foundations.

## Source Problems and Historiography

The early life is unusually visible because *Confessiones* (written roughly 397–400) narrates it, but that work is a prayer, theological construction, memory-work, and defense of providence—not a neutral diary. Names and details are selected for rhetorical purpose. Augustine never names his long-term partner, minimizes some chronology, and turns episodes into scriptural patterns. Possidius of Calama’s *Vita Augustini*, written soon after Augustine’s death, is near-contemporary and eyewitness testimony for the episcopal years, but it is also hagiography by a disciple and ally. Augustine’s *Retractationes* (426–427) reviews much of his own corpus, sometimes correcting positions; Possidius’s *Indiculus* catalogs works, letters, and sermons. These controls make the authentic corpus better mapped than most ancient authors, yet hundreds of pseudo-Augustinian texts entered medieval transmission.

Exact dates remain disputed for the garden conversion (usually late summer 386), episcopal consecration (395 or 396), composition spans of long works, Adeodatus’s birth and death, and several journeys. This dossier gives standard scholarly ranges rather than false precision.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

- **Patricius (Patrick):** Augustine’s father, a minor landholder and local municipal figure at Thagaste. He remained a non-Christian for most of Augustine’s youth, was hot-tempered and sexually unfaithful according to Augustine, accepted baptism late, and died around 371. Augustine represents him as ambitious for his son’s secular advancement. The family was not destitute but could not continuously finance elite education.
- **Monnica/Monica (c. 331–387):** Augustine’s mother, a committed Catholic Christian, probably of Berber/North-African ancestry though precise genealogy is unrecoverable. Augustine depicts her as disciplined, prayerful, persistent, socially capable, and influential; he also records her youthful habit of secretly drinking wine until a servant’s rebuke stopped it, her endurance of Patricius’s temper and infidelity, and her pressure upon Augustine’s domestic and marital arrangements. She followed him to Italy and died at Ostia in 387. Her own cult and the literary portrait in *Confessions* shaped Christian ideals of maternal intercession.

### Siblings and extended family

- **Navigius:** a brother, present at Ostia and later connected with the Cassiciacum circle. Details are sparse.
- **Unnamed sister:** widowed and later superior of a community of women; Augustine addressed a rule/letter to a female religious community after conflict following her death. Some traditions name her Perpetua, but the name is not securely ancient.
- Nieces, nephews, and cousins appear indirectly. The evidence does not sustain a full genealogy.

### Partner and child

- Around 371/372 Augustine entered a long-term, socially unequal but apparently monogamous concubinage with an unnamed woman. Roman class and marriage law likely mattered: a formal marriage could impede status strategy. They lived together about fourteen or fifteen years in Carthage, Thagaste, Rome, and Milan.
- Their son **Adeodatus** (“gift of God”) was born about 372/373. He was baptized with Augustine in Milan in 387, appears as an intellectually gifted interlocutor in *De magistro*, and died young, probably around 389–391. Exact dates are not secure.
- In Milan, Monica and Augustine arranged a socially advantageous marriage to a girl who was under legal marriage age. Augustine sent his partner back to Africa around 385; he says she vowed sexual continence and that his heart was “torn” by the separation. Adeodatus remained with him. While waiting to marry, Augustine took another sexual partner. The projected marriage never occurred after his conversion and adoption of continence.
- The unnamed woman’s perspective, status, later life, and even name are unrecoverable. Calling her merely a prostitute or casual mistress is unsupported; calling the union a modern marriage also overstates the legal evidence.

## Childhood and Early Life, 354–370

Augustine was born into a household crossing religious worlds: Monica’s Catholic Christianity, Patricius’s traditional religion, and a late Roman civic culture in which education could raise family status. He was enrolled as a Christian catechumen and, when gravely ill as a boy, sought baptism; recovery led to its postponement, then a common practice.

He attended elementary school at Thagaste and grammar school at **Madauros** (modern M’Daourouch). He later remembered beatings, fear, games, petty theft, and intense aversion to Greek. He mastered Latin grammar and literature, especially Virgil. His famous story of stealing pears with companions—valuable chiefly because the theft was gratuitous—became a meditation on socialized wrongdoing and disordered desire. It is self-testimony shaped decades later; no independent record exists.

Around 369/370, finances forced a year at home in Thagaste. Augustine retrospectively portrayed this as a sexually restless adolescence while his father was pleased by signs of virility and Monica feared moral danger. **Romanianus**, a wealthy Thagastan patron and family friend, helped finance further study.

## Education and Formation

### Carthage, rhetoric, and philosophy, c. 371–383

Augustine went to Carthage for advanced rhetoric. The city offered education, theaters, public ambition, religious competition, and sexual relationships. He associated with a student group called the *eversores* (“wreckers/subverters”), though he says he disliked their hazing violence. His partner and Adeodatus formed his household.

Reading Cicero’s lost **Hortensius** around 373 awakened a programmatic “love of wisdom.” He tried Scripture but found its style inferior to classical prose and its anthropomorphic passages intellectually difficult. He then joined **Manichaeism** as an *auditor* (“hearer”), not one of the ascetic *elect*. The religion offered an account of evil as conflict between Light and Darkness, criticism of Catholic Scripture, an elite rationalist self-image, cosmology, and a disciplined transregional network. Augustine recruited or influenced friends, defended the movement, and remained attached for roughly nine years. Modern scholarship cautions against accepting his later anti-Manichaean caricature as a transparent description of the religion.

He taught grammar briefly at Thagaste, probably c. 374–375, then rhetoric at Carthage. A beloved unnamed friend’s death produced the psychological crisis narrated in *Confessions* IV. Students’ disorder and fee avoidance frustrated him. He wrote an early, lost work **De pulchro et apto** (*On the Beautiful and the Fitting*), dedicated to the Roman orator Hierius.

### Faustus and departure from Manichaeism

Augustine had expected the Manichaean bishop **Faustus of Milevis** to resolve astronomical and doctrinal problems. Their meeting around 382/383 disappointed him: Faustus was eloquent and personally agreeable but, Augustine says, unable to answer technical questions. Augustine’s commitment weakened; he did not instantly become Catholic, instead moving through skepticism while retaining Manichaean connections.

## Early Career and Migration, 383–386

### Rome, 383–384

Augustine left Africa for Rome, deceiving Monica about the sailing so she could not prevent it. Manichaean contacts helped him find lodging and clientele. He suffered a severe illness. Roman students behaved more quietly than Carthaginian students but evaded tuition by transferring teachers before payment.

### Milan, 384–386

Through the recommendation of **Symmachus**, pagan prefect of Rome and a major senatorial figure, Augustine won the salaried imperial chair of rhetoric at Milan. This was a politically exposed post near the court of the young Valentinian II and his mother Justina. It did not make Augustine a secret official or intelligence agent; it did make him a court-adjacent professional expected to compose ceremonial praise.

He heard Bishop **Ambrose**, initially for rhetorical technique. Ambrose’s allegorical interpretation showed him that Catholic readings need not be crudely literal. Monica, Adeodatus, Alypius, Navigius, and friends joined the household. Augustine became a catechumen. He explored Academic skepticism; translations of “books of the Platonists,” probably Plotinus and Porphyry through Marius Victorinus, gave him a non-material account of God, evil as privation, and inward ascent. The priest **Simplicianus** told him of Victorinus’s public conversion.

Professional ambition remained strong. Augustine delivered a panegyric for the emperor and participated in the competitive patronage system. His marriage negotiations and removal of his partner show the interaction of class advancement, family strategy, and gender inequality.

## Conversion, Cassiciacum, Baptism, 386–388

### Anthony the Great / “san Antonio Abad” link

The relationship with **Anthony of Egypt (Anthony the Great/Abbot)** was indirect but important. In *Confessions* VIII, the imperial official **Ponticianus** found Augustine and Alypius with Paul’s letters and told them about Anthony and monastic communities. He related how two court officials at Trier had encountered a *Life of Antony*—normally identified with Athanasius’s *Vita Antonii*—and abandoned careers and intended marriages for ascetic life. The story confronted Augustine with people acting immediately on an ideal he had delayed.

Anthony was not Augustine’s teacher, correspondent, or personal acquaintance; Anthony died in 356, when Augustine was an infant. The link is literary and exemplary: Athanasius → Latin monastic circulation → Trier officials → Ponticianus → Augustine’s crisis. Later art and preaching often compress this mediation into “Anthony converted Augustine.”

### Garden episode

Augustine describes an acute crisis in a Milan garden, probably late summer 386. Hearing a childlike voice chant **“tolle lege, tolle lege”** (“take up and read”), he opened Paul and read Romans 13:13–14, a command to abandon revelry and sexual provision. Alypius read the next verse and joined the decision. Augustine renounced marriage and the rhetoric career.

The experience is documented only through Augustine’s later theological narrative. Competing readings treat it as literal auditory experience, a nearby children’s rhyme, providential coincidence, literary construction modeled on conversion stories, or a remembered event reshaped by scripture. No evidence establishes psychosis, fraud, or invention. His intellectual and moral conversion had unfolded over months; the garden scene functions as the decisive act of will.

### Cassiciacum and baptism

Augustine withdrew with family and friends to the villa of **Verecundus** at Cassiciacum. Dialogues from this retreat—*Contra Academicos*, *De beata vita*, *De ordine*, and *Soliloquia*—combine Christian commitment, Platonism, classical dialogue, and philosophical therapy. He formally resigned his chair, citing health problems and the vacation calendar rather than staging a public rupture.

Ambrose baptized Augustine, Adeodatus, and Alypius at Milan during the Easter vigil, 24–25 April 387. The later legend that Ambrose and Augustine improvised the **Te Deum** antiphonally at the baptism appears centuries later and lacks early support.

The group traveled toward Africa. At Ostia, Augustine and Monica shared the contemplative experience later called the “vision at Ostia.” Monica developed fever and died, traditionally aged fifty-six, in 387. Augustine records grief despite initially restraining tears. She was buried at Ostia; her relic history became a separate medieval cult.

Augustine spent months in Rome, studying monasteries and writing against Manichaeism, then returned to Africa in 388.

## Thagaste Community and Ordination, 388–395/396

At Thagaste, Augustine converted family property into a lay ascetic community organized around prayer, study, common possessions, and philosophical-theological work. Sources variously say he sold or distributed patrimony while retaining/repurposing the house; exact finances are unknown. This was not a registered corporation in the modern sense, and no accounts survive.

Early works included *De moribus ecclesiae catholicae et de moribus Manichaeorum*, *De vera religione*, *De Genesi contra Manichaeos*, *De musica*, and *De magistro*. Adeodatus died during this period or soon after; Augustine gives little detail.

In 391 Augustine visited Hippo, reportedly seeking a prospective member or site for a monastery. Bishop **Valerius**, an elderly Greek-speaking bishop, needed a Latin-speaking priest. During worship the congregation seized upon Augustine as a candidate; he wept and was ordained. “Forced ordination” was a recognized late-antique trope and social practice, but the crowd’s exact pressure cannot be reconstructed. It was not a judicial arrest or imprisonment.

Valerius gave him a garden for a clerical monastery and unusually allowed him to preach while still a presbyter. Augustine’s preaching, biblical exposition, pastoral adjudication, catechesis, poor relief, and polemics rapidly made him prominent. In 393 he addressed an African council at Hippo on faith and creed. Around 395 Valerius arranged his consecration as coadjutor, possibly without knowing a Nicene canon disfavored a bishop being consecrated in another living bishop’s see. Augustine succeeded after Valerius’s death, conventionally in 396.

## Episcopal Career: Institutions, Work, and Daily Administration

Augustine’s episcopal complex combined church, residence, library, guesthouse, charitable distribution, court, and male religious household. Clergy lived under common discipline. He trained bishops—including Alypius and Possidius—creating a network of allied African sees. The later **Rule of Saint Augustine** grew from authentic prescriptions/letters associated with these communities, though the exact textual form, addressees, and priority of male and female versions remain debated.

His income was tied to the see’s property, offerings, and patrimonial structures, not personal business enterprise. Possidius depicts modest food and dress, hospitality, careful stewardship, avoidance of expensive silver, and periodic sale of church vessels for captives or the poor. This is favorable testimony from a disciple. Augustine complained about administrative burdens and property disputes. He heard civil cases as *episcopalis audientia*, interceded with officials, sought clemency for offenders, arranged ransoms, supervised clergy, corresponded across the empire, and preached frequently.

No evidence connects him to patents, commercial companies, investments, money laundering, espionage, or personal enrichment. His “technology” was institutional and textual: trained stenographers, manuscript copying, letter networks, libraries, council machinery, and serialized polemical publication.

## Major Controversies and Intellectual Development

### Anti-Manichaean campaign

From the late 380s onward Augustine attacked his former religion in dialogues, treatises, and public controversy. He debated the Manichaean presbyter **Fortunatus** at Hippo in 392; the surviving record presents Fortunatus as unable to answer and departing. He wrote against **Adimantus**, **Faustus**, **Felix**, and **Secundinus**, and developed accounts of evil as privation, responsible will, scriptural unity, and creation.

Documented tension: Augustine was an exceptionally informed apostate, but his works are partisan controversy. He sometimes preserved Manichaean material otherwise lost; he also framed opponents through Catholic categories and retrospective embarrassment. Claims that he remained secretly Manichaean are unsupported; claims that Manichaean habits left no trace are equally implausible. Scholars debate continuities in his dualistic rhetoric, sexuality, will, and predestination.

### Donatist schism, debate, and coercion

North African Christianity had been divided since the early fourth century over legitimacy, martyrdom, episcopal succession, and the treatment of clergy accused of surrendering scriptures during persecution. Donatists were not a small foreign sect; in many regions they were the locally dominant African church. Augustine argued that sacramental validity rests in Christ, not the minister’s purity, and that the worldwide Catholic communion marked the true church.

He first favored conference and persuasion and participated in sustained letter campaigns. Violence was real and multi-sided: some militant **Circumcellions** associated with Donatism attacked clergy and property; imperial/Catholic enforcement also fined, dispossessed, exiled, and physically punished opponents. Attribution of every rural violent act to a centralized Donatist policy is unsafe.

By the 400s Augustine endorsed imperial coercion. In **Letter 93** (408) and **Letter 185** he argued that fear and legal pressure could break destructive habit and enable instruction, citing “compel them to come in” (Luke 14:23). He opposed execution in particular cases and asked officials for restraint, yet supported laws that deprived Donatists of meeting places and imposed penalties. His distinction between corrective discipline and persecution served Catholic power. This is documented, not merely a hostile allegation.

At the imperial **Conference of Carthage (411)**, hundreds of Catholic and Donatist bishops argued before Marcellinus; the judgment favored Catholics and suppression intensified. The proceeding had formal legal architecture but occurred under an imperial regime already defining Donatism as unlawful. Augustine’s role became a foundational precedent cited both for pastoral correction and for later Christian religious coercion.

### Paganism, Rome, and *City of God*

After Alaric’s Visigoths sacked Rome in 410, some pagans blamed abandonment of traditional gods. Augustine began *De civitate Dei* in 413 and completed it about 426. Books I–X answer pagan criticism and attack Roman religious-philosophical claims; XI–XXII narrate two “cities” constituted by opposed loves across sacred and secular history. The earthly city is not simply the state, nor the City of God simply the institutional church.

He accepted state authority, defensive war, punishment, and slavery as effects/remedies of sin while denying that domination belonged to original human nature. Later “just war” systems drew on him, though he never wrote a standalone just-war treatise. His defense of coercion and providential history influenced medieval church-state structures; modern appropriation often detaches formulas from late-Roman context.

### Pelagius, Caelestius, grace, sin, and predestination

The British ascetic **Pelagius** emphasized moral responsibility and the capacity to obey divine commands. **Caelestius** provoked controversy in Africa over Adam’s sin, infant baptism, and human ability. Augustine argued that humanity inherited a damaged condition from Adam; grace precedes and enables every saving movement, and infant baptism remits original sin. African councils, Roman bishops, imperial authorities, and eastern proceedings produced shifting decisions; Pelagius was acquitted at Diospolis (415), later condemned in the West, and imperial rescripts expelled his adherents.

Augustine’s position developed toward stronger doctrines of gratuitous election, perseverance as a gift, and predestination. Late works against Julian of Eclanum became exceptionally sharp and sexually explicit in debating concupiscence. Whether Augustine taught “double predestination” in the later Calvinist sense remains disputed. His system preserved voluntary action but denied autonomous saving initiative.

The controversy institutionalized doctrines of original sin and grace in Latin Christianity, while critics—ancient and modern—argue that Augustine intensified inherited guilt, pessimism about sexuality, and divine selectivity beyond earlier consensus. Those are interpretive judgments grounded in real textual disputes, not criminal findings.

### Jews and Judaism

Augustine opposed Judaism theologically but developed the influential “witness doctrine”: Jews should survive, dispersed and subordinated, as bearers of scriptures that testify to Christian claims. This could restrain forced elimination, yet it encoded religious inequality and supersession. Later rulers and theologians used Augustinian ideas both to protect Jewish communities from killing and to justify inferior civic status. It is inaccurate either to call his position modern toleration or to equate it without qualification with later racial antisemitism.

### Sexuality, women, marriage, and the body

Augustine treated marriage as good and procreation/fidelity/sacramental bond as goods, while ranking consecrated virginity higher. He located postlapsarian disorder in involuntary sexual arousal and developed concupiscence as a central sign of divided will. His abandonment of his unnamed partner, arranged child-marriage prospect, and later polemic have become central evidence in criticism of his gender and sexual ethics. His works also depict women as rational images of God while reproducing patriarchal hierarchy. Claims that he “invented” original sin or Christian sexual repression oversimplify earlier traditions; his synthesis nevertheless gave these doctrines unprecedented Latin authority.

### Slavery and social order

Augustine regarded slavery as contrary to created nature but a historical result of sin and war within fallen order. He did not campaign for abolition. He supported ransom and manumission, denounced kidnapping into slavery, and described mass trafficking after raids. His acceptance of the institution’s continued legality and household hierarchy belongs in the record alongside charitable interventions.

### Apuleius, magic, miracles, and demons

As an African intellectual he knew the prestige of Apuleius of Madauros but treated magic and pagan theurgy as demonic deception. In *City of God* XXII he collected contemporary healing and relic miracle reports, stating that written memoranda were used at Hippo. Modern historians treat these as evidence for late-antique belief and local cult practice; whether supernatural causation occurred is outside what documentary method can establish.

## Ratramnus Connection

**Ratramnus of Corbie** (fl. ninth century) lived roughly four centuries after Augustine. No meeting, correspondence, lineage of direct discipleship, or biographical interaction existed. The defensible connection is reception history:

1. Carolingian theological vocabulary on grace and predestination was deeply Augustinian. Ratramnus wrote in the controversy prompted by Gottschalk of Orbais, whose teaching appealed to Augustine; Ratramnus defended a strong predestinarian position.
2. In *De corpore et sanguine Domini*, Ratramnus distinguished sacramental appearance/sign from spiritual reality in a way later readers compared with Augustine’s sign theory. The exact balance between Ratramnus, Paschasius Radbertus, and Augustine is debated; neither ninth-century author maps neatly onto later Catholic/Protestant categories.
3. Sixteenth-century Protestant publication and use of Ratramnus made him part of a contested “Augustinian” genealogy of Eucharistic thought. This is a history of selective reception, not proof that Augustine held Ratramnus’s or a Reformation confession’s complete doctrine.

## Political Activity, Law, Violence, and Abuse Questions

- Augustine never held elected civil office. His rhetoric chair was an imperial appointment; his episcopate exercised substantial public power.
- He lobbied governors, commanders, imperial commissioners, and Roman bishops; influenced councils; requested legislation or enforcement; adjudicated cases; and interceded for clemency.
- **Documented coercion:** support for state penalties against Donatists and theological justification of compulsory pressure. Penalties could entail confiscation, fines, exile, and corporal mistreatment in the enforcement system. Augustine preferred correction to killing and protested some brutality, but cannot be separated from the coercive policy he legitimized.
- **Political violence:** he condemned Circumcellion attacks and appealed against capital punishment even for offenders who attacked Catholics. His record is therefore neither simple pacifism nor unqualified persecution.
- **Fraud/corruption:** no credible evidence of personal fraud, embezzlement, money laundering, or corrupt enrichment. Polemical accusations between religious factions cannot be converted into convictions.
- **Criminal activity:** none documented in the modern sense. His youthful pear theft is autobiographical moral illustration, not a preserved legal case.
- **Intelligence connections:** none documented. Court, episcopal, courier, and official correspondence was political networking, not evidence of espionage.
- **Abuse:** modern readers identify coercion, patriarchal treatment of his partner, and stigmatizing polemic as abuses of power. The underlying acts/texts are documented; applying modern legal categories requires qualification.

## Health, Psychology, and Death

Augustine reports childhood illness, a severe fever in Rome, chest/lung or voice trouble around resignation from teaching, toothache, hemorrhoids or other recurrent ailments in later correspondence, exhaustion, grief, sexual compulsion, ambition, and an acute conversion crisis. Retrospective diagnoses—depression, addiction, bipolar disorder, psychosis, sex addiction—are speculative and cannot be clinically established from literary texts.

During the Vandal invasion under **Geiseric/Gaiseric**, refugees crowded Hippo. The city was besieged in 430. Possidius says Augustine fell ill with fever, requested the penitential psalms be placed where he could read them, wept and prayed, and died on 28 August. A dying man’s healing after Augustine prayed for him is hagiographic testimony by Possidius; natural documentation cannot verify miraculous causation. The specific pathogen is unknown. Augustine was buried at Hippo.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Tolle lege”

1. **Story:** a childlike voice ordered “take and read”; Augustine opened Paul at Romans 13:13–14 and converted.
2. **Appearance:** *Confessions* VIII, c. 397–400, about eleven to fourteen years after the event.
3. **Circulation:** Augustine himself; then biographers, liturgy, art, and preaching.
4. **Evidence:** primary self-testimony; no external witness, though Alypius is placed at the scene. Literary parallels and theological shaping are clear but do not disprove an event.
5. **Effect:** canonical image of conversion through scripture; central to autobiography, bibliomancy debates, and iconography.

### “Make me chaste—but not yet”

1. **Story:** Augustine prayed for chastity and continence but not immediately.
2. **Appearance:** *Confessions* VIII.7.
3. **Circulation:** Augustine, later moral literature and popular culture.
4. **Evidence:** authentic self-report, but a crafted retrospective summary, not a recorded contemporaneous quotation.
5. **Effect:** fixed the image of Augustine as libertine-turned-saint; popular retellings often exaggerate his sex life beyond the documented long monogamous concubinage plus a later temporary partner.

### The unnamed concubine as prostitute, saint, or secret wife

No ancient source gives her name. Later fiction supplies identities and motives. She was not documented as a prostitute. Her long household union and vow on separation are in Augustine; “secret legal wife” and later canonization stories lack evidence. Her anonymity has become a symbol of women erased by male autobiography.

### Te Deum improvised with Ambrose

The hymn was later said to have been spontaneously composed in alternating verses by Ambrose and Augustine at the 387 baptism. The attribution is late and rejected by most modern hymn scholarship. The story enhanced the pairing of the two Latin doctors and gave the hymn an apostolic-style origin.

### Child and seashell

A child on a beach tries to pour the sea into a small hole; when Augustine says this is impossible, the child replies that comprehending the Trinity is still more impossible, then disappears. The story is medieval, attaches to other theologians in variants, and is not in Augustine or Possidius. It became one of his most recognizable artistic attributes.

### Founder of the Order of Saint Augustine and European hermit houses

Augustine founded/organized African ascetic communities and supplied texts later used as a rule. The mendicant Order of Hermits of Saint Augustine was formed through papal unions in the thirteenth century. Claims that Augustine personally founded medieval Tuscan houses or the later order are anachronistic; pseudo-Augustinian *Sermones ad fratres in eremo* helped authorize the institutional legend.

### Miraculous preservation of Hippo’s library

Possidius says the city was later burned while the church and its library escaped. Retellings sometimes turn this into a supernatural miracle. The preservation claim is near-contemporary but the cause is not documented; much of the corpus survived through copying elsewhere.

### Relic translations

Tradition says bishops expelled by Vandal king Huneric moved Augustine’s body from Hippo to Sardinia, and Lombard king Liutprand transferred/redeemed it to Pavia around 720. Bede is a key witness for the Pavia tradition, centuries after Augustine. Bones found at San Pietro in Ciel d’Oro in 1695 prompted dispute between canons and Augustinian hermits; the bishop of Pavia recognized them after investigation. The identity cannot be demonstrated by an unbroken modern forensic chain. A portion of an arm was returned to Annaba in 1842. The contested relics affected pilgrimage, institutional property, and Pavia’s prestige.

### “First autobiography” and “inventor of the self”

*Confessions* is not the first autobiographical writing; Greco-Roman, Jewish, and Christian precedents exist. Its sustained inward analysis, addressed to God, profoundly changed Latin self-writing. “First modern man” is a historiographical slogan, useful for reception but not a literal fact.

### African identity claims

Augustine has been represented as European/Latin, Berber, Black African, proto-Algerian, or a symbol of French colonial Algeria and postcolonial reclamation. He was indisputably born and worked mostly in Roman Africa. Exact ancestry, phenotype, and modern national/racial identity are not recoverable. Competing images reveal later political stakes more than secure genealogy.

## Works by Augustine

Dates are approximate; many works were revised or serialized. Latin titles are supplied. The corpus includes more than five million surviving words, roughly one hundred substantial titled works, more than 250 letters (including received letters), and hundreds of sermons. A literal itemization of every sermon and letter would require a separate prosopographical catalog; the principal and titled works follow, with corpus guides linked in Sources.

- **Epistulae:** a large correspondence with bishops, officials, ascetics, laypeople, Jerome, Paulinus, Boniface, and opponents; numbering differs by edition and includes letters received.

### Lost, disputed, and pseudo-Augustinian material

Augustine identifies lost works in *Retractationes*; Possidius’s catalog provides another ancient control. Major pseudo-Augustinian texts include the *Sermones ad fratres in eremo*, *Meditationes*, *Soliloquia animae ad Deum* (not the authentic early *Soliloquia*), some “Manuals,” and the maxim **“Roma locuta, causa finita”** in that exact form. The aphorism “Love God and do what you will” paraphrases an authentic Johannine homily but is often stripped of context. Every unattributed internet quotation requires checking against Latin text and critical edition.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Late antiquity and Middle Ages

Augustine’s writings became foundational for Latin doctrines of grace, Trinity, church, sacraments, signs, biblical exegesis, time, memory, will, history, and political order. Prosper of Aquitaine defended his grace theology. Critics in southern Gaul resisted aspects of predestination. Caesarius of Arles, Gregory the Great, Isidore, Bede, Anselm, Victorines, scholastics, and mystics selectively appropriated him.

There was no modern canonization trial. Cult by acclamation and ecclesiastical reception preceded centralized procedures. Boniface VIII’s 1298 decree placed Ambrose, Jerome, Augustine, and Gregory among the four great Latin Doctors. The Augustinian mendicant order, canons regular, and many congregations adopted his rule or identity, though he did not found their medieval institutional forms.

### Reformation and confessional conflict

Luther was an Augustinian friar; Luther, Calvin, Jansenists, and Catholic adversaries all claimed Augustinian authority on grace and will. The Council of Trent accepted original sin and prevenient grace while rejecting readings it judged incompatible with cooperation and sacramental teaching. **Jansenism** became a major early-modern struggle over whether Augustine’s mature grace doctrine had been faithfully preserved. Ratramnus was printed and recruited into Eucharistic arguments, demonstrating how “Augustinian” genealogies could diverge.

### Philosophy, literature, and modern theory

Descartes was compared with Augustine on inward certainty; Malebranche was deeply Augustinian. Pascal, Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, Freud, Heidegger, Hannah Arendt, Wittgenstein, Ricoeur, Derrida, and many others engaged his analyses of memory, time, language, desire, and selfhood. Arendt’s dissertation treated love in Augustine. The opening of Wittgenstein’s *Philosophical Investigations* quotes Augustine’s childhood account to stage a theory of language acquisition.

Political theorists debate whether *City of God* supports political realism, two-spheres separation, Christian empire, critique of empire, or pilgrim non-identification with any state. The text’s distinction of cities by love prevents simple mapping, but Augustine’s actual episcopal lobbying and coercion complicate idealized readings.

### Colonial and postcolonial North Africa

French colonial institutions promoted Augustine as a Latin-Christian ancestor of European Algeria; Algerian, Amazigh, African, and postcolonial readers have reclaimed or contested him. The Basilica of Saint Augustine at Annaba and return of an arm relic in 1842 materialized rival geographies of belonging. Claims that he was simply “European” erase Roman Africa; claims of a precise modern ethnic nationality exceed evidence.

### Continuing controversies

- **Positive receptions** emphasize psychological depth, grace, anti-imperial critique, communal religious life, care for captives and poor, and intellectual synthesis.
- **Critical receptions** focus on religious coercion, inherited guilt, predestination, sexual pessimism, patriarchal domestic choices, supersessionism, and accommodation to slavery/state violence.
- These are documented reception streams. Neither canonization nor modern criticism substitutes for historical proof about particular acts.

## Atlas Connections

### Abraham

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Augustine repeatedly wrote about the biblical patriarch Abraham, notably in *City of God* XVI, where Abraham’s family, covenant, and chronology form part of Augustine’s sacred history. The connection is textual and theological, not personal.

### Aeneas

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Augustine wrote about the literary hero Aeneas, recalling in *Confessions* I.13 that his North-African schooling made him study Aeneas’s wanderings while weeping over Dido. He later invoked Aeneas and Rome’s Trojan origin traditions in *City of God*. [Primary text](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/augustine/confess.ii.xiii.html)

### Alexander the Great

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Augustine discussed Alexander as a historical conqueror in *City of God* IV.4, reproducing the famous exchange in which a captured pirate compares Alexander’s imperial warfare with piracy. Augustine used the anecdote to argue that kingdoms without justice are enlarged bands of robbers.

### Anthony the Great / San Antonio Abad

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Augustine wrote in *Confessions* VIII.6–7 that Ponticianus told him at Milan in 386 about Anthony of Egypt and about imperial officials transformed by reading a *Life of Antony*. The report helped precipitate Augustine’s conversion crisis. Anthony, who died in 356, never met Augustine; the connection was mediated through biography and oral narration. [Primary text](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/110108.htm)

### Apostle Paul

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Augustine wrote extensively about Paul in biblical commentaries, sermons, anti-Pelagian works, and *Confessions*. Most decisively, he reports that during the Milan garden episode of 386 he opened Paul’s epistles and read Romans 13:13–14, treating the passage as the scriptural resolution of his crisis. [Primary text and commentary](https://faculty.georgetown.edu/jod/conf/comm8.html)

### Apostle Peter

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Augustine repeatedly interpreted Peter’s place among the apostles, his confession of Christ, denial, restoration, and symbolic representation of the Church. Sermon 26, for example, discusses both Peter and Paul while explaining Matthew 16 and ecclesial unity. [Primary text](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/160326.htm)

### Aristotle

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Augustine named and evaluated Aristotle and the Aristotelian tradition, although his access was largely through Latin school texts and doxography rather than sustained reading of the Greek corpus. In *Confessions* IV.16 he recalls independently studying Aristotle’s *Categories* as a young man; later works place Aristotle within accounts of pagan philosophy.

### Cicero

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Augustine wrote repeatedly about Cicero. In *Confessions* III.4 he says that reading Cicero’s now-lost *Hortensius* at Carthage around 373 redirected his aspirations toward philosophy; *City of God* later quotes and disputes Cicero’s definitions of a republic, justice, religion, and divination. Cicero and Virgil were the dominant classical authors in Augustine’s education. [Scholarly overview](https://faculty.georgetown.edu/jod/augustine/civ.html)

### Constantine the Great

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Augustine wrote about Constantine in *City of God* V.25–26, presenting his imperial success, Christian worship, donations, and clemency as evidence that temporal prosperity can be granted to a Christian ruler without constituting Christianity’s ultimate reward. Augustine was born seventeen years after Constantine’s death, so no personal crossing occurred.

### Dante Alighieri

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Dante placed Augustine among the blessed in *Paradiso* XXXII and engaged Augustinian themes of conversion, ordered love, signs, memory, and pilgrimage throughout the *Commedia*. Scholars particularly identify *Confessions* and *De doctrina christiana* as important textual precedents, while cautioning that some apparent parallels may derive from broader Christian traditions. [Scholarly study](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/24457/1/Reading%20Dante%27s%20Commedia%20through%20Augustine%27s%20Hermeneutics%20of%20caritas--RD%20Sellgren%202019%20Final.pdf)

### Dido

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Augustine wrote about Virgil’s Dido in *Confessions* I.13, contrasting the tears he shed over her literary death with his indifference to his own perceived spiritual condition. He also revisited Dido and Carthaginian-Roman foundation narratives in *City of God*. [Primary text](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/augustine/confess.ii.xiii.html)

### Jacques Derrida

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Derrida closely and explicitly engaged Augustine’s *Confessions* in *Circonfession* (1990–1991), borrowing its confessional form, Latin phrases, and questions about truth, prayer, memory, the mother, and self-writing. This is documented reception, not agreement with Augustine’s theology. [Scholarly analysis](https://openurl.ebsco.com/contentitem/doi%3A10.5325/complitstudies.52.4.0729?id=ebsco%3Adoi%3A10.5325%2Fcomplitstudies.52.4.0729&sid=ebsco%3Aplink%3Acrawler)

### René Descartes

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** After correspondents noticed the resemblance between the Cartesian *cogito* and Augustine’s certainty of self-existence, Descartes discussed Augustine explicitly in letters to Andreas Colvius and Marin Mersenne in November–December 1640, including *City of God* XI.26. Whether Augustine directly generated Descartes’s argument remains disputed; Descartes’s written engagement with the comparison is certain. [Historical study](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9780192517203_A37214561/preview-9780192517203_A37214561.pdf)

### Michel Foucault

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Foucault analyzed Augustine at length in *Les aveux de la chair* (*Confessions of the Flesh*), especially Augustine’s writings on marriage, concupiscence, sexual subjectivity, and the government of desire. Scholars dispute whether Foucault projects later psychoanalytic categories onto Augustine, but the textual engagement itself is explicit. [Scholarly analysis](https://revistas.usp.br/discurso/en/article/view/193763)

### Martin Heidegger

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Heidegger devoted his 1921 Freiburg lecture course “Augustine and Neoplatonism” to a sustained interpretation of *Confessions* X, particularly temptation, care, memory, and factical life. Augustine also remained important to Heidegger’s later treatment of temporality. [Scholarly account](https://doi.org/10.1163/156916409X448193)

### Jacobus de Voragine

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Jacobus included Augustine in the thirteenth-century *Golden Legend*, retelling his conversion, episcopate, teachings, death, miracles, and relic traditions. This is a medieval hagiographic reception: it combines material ultimately traceable to Augustine and Possidius with later legendary accretions.

### Jesus of Nazareth

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Augustine’s surviving corpus is pervasively centered on Jesus Christ. Dedicated examples include the *Tractates on the Gospel of John*, *Harmony of the Gospels*, sermons on Christ’s life and teaching, and doctrinal treatments of incarnation, crucifixion, resurrection, grace, and Christ as mediator. The connection is scriptural and theological; Jesus lived more than three centuries before Augustine.

### King David

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Augustine wrote extensively about David as king, sinner, psalmist, prophet, and ancestor or type of Christ. His long-running *Expositions of the Psalms* treated the Psalter traditionally attributed to David as simultaneously Davidic, ecclesial, and Christological; *Confessions* is itself densely shaped by the Psalms. [Scholarly analysis](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/harvard-theological-review/article/abs/augustines-mixed-feelings-vergils-aeneid-and-the-psalms-of-david-in-the-confessions/55CFA16D0A7D458BAAE3725C766C75B4)

### Martin Luther

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Luther intensively studied and cited Augustine, especially during his lectures on Romans in 1515–1516 and in disputes over sin, grace, free will, and justification. His 1545 preface to his Latin writings explicitly compares his own intellectual development with Augustine’s practice of progressing through writing. Agreement was selective, so “Augustinian” does not mean that Luther adopted Augustine’s complete theology. [Primary text](https://www.bluffton.edu/courses/humanities/2/ml-1545.htm) [Scholarly overview](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/62249/chapter-abstract/551355794)

### Moses

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Augustine repeatedly wrote about Moses as lawgiver, prophet, and the traditionally received author of the Pentateuch. Genesis and Exodus are central to *Confessions* XI–XIII, *The Literal Meaning of Genesis*, *Against the Manichaeans on Genesis*, and the *Questions on the Heptateuch*. Augustine’s canon list explicitly calls the Pentateuch the five books of Moses. [Primary text](https://biblehub.com/library/augustine/on_christian_doctrine_in_four_books/chapter_13_now_the_whole.htm)

### Friedrich Nietzsche

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Nietzsche referred critically to Augustine in his published writings—most conspicuously *Beyond Good and Evil* §50—and in notebooks after reading the *Confessions*. His comments treat Augustine as an exemplary Christian psychologist and religious type, usually with hostility or irony. Claims of deeper structural influence are scholarly interpretations; the explicit references are documented.

### Origen of Alexandria

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Augustine wrote about Origen’s exegetical and theological positions, sometimes through Latin translations and intermediary reports. He criticized Origenist teachings on souls, universal restoration, demons, and resurrection in works including *City of God* and *On Heresies*, while also inheriting elements of the broader allegorical exegetical tradition. [Source overview](https://academic.oup.com/book/33448/chapter-abstract/287719445)

### Plato

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Augustine repeatedly wrote about Plato and “the Platonists,” praising Plato as pre-eminent among Socrates’ disciples while subordinating Platonist philosophy to Christian revelation. His knowledge came principally through Latin translations, later Platonists, and doxographical sources; *City of God* VIII–X contains his fullest sustained assessment. [Primary text](https://biblehub.com/library/augustine/city_of_god/chapter_4_concerning_plato_the_chief.htm) [Scholarly overview](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/augustine/)

### Ratramnus of Corbie

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Ratramnus, writing in the ninth century, worked within an Augustinian textual tradition when addressing predestination and the Eucharist. His positions were later compared with Augustine’s accounts of grace and sacramental signs, although the extent and doctrinal meaning of the dependence remain debated. No personal contact was chronologically possible.

### Solomon

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Augustine discussed Solomon as biblical king, wisdom figure, temple builder, and traditionally received author of Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and the Song of Songs. He included these works among “the books of Solomon” in *On Christian Doctrine* II.8 and interpreted Solomon typologically in *City of God*. [Primary text](https://biblehub.com/library/augustine/on_christian_doctrine_in_four_books/chapter_13_now_the_whole.htm)

### Tertullian

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Augustine named and evaluated his North-African Christian predecessor Tertullian, particularly when discussing the later Tertullianist sect in *On Heresies* 86 and questions of soul, marriage, discipline, and ecclesiology elsewhere. There was no personal contact: Tertullian died roughly a century before Augustine’s birth.

### Thomas Aquinas

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact:** Aquinas cited and interpreted Augustine throughout the *Summa theologiae*, biblical commentaries, disputed questions, and other works, treating him as a major patristic authority on grace, Trinity, knowledge, will, evil, and creation. Aquinas nevertheless reorganized Augustinian material within an increasingly Aristotelian framework, so the connection is extensive reception rather than simple doctrinal identity. [Scholarly overview](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/augustine/)

### Virgil

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Augustine quoted, interpreted, and criticized Virgil throughout *Confessions* and *City of God*. He recalled learning the *Aeneid* in school, weeping for Dido, and later used Virgilian language and Roman myth while challenging the moral and religious authority of classical poetry. [Primary text](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/augustine/confess.ii.xiii.html) [Scholarly analysis](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/harvard-theological-review/article/abs/augustines-mixed-feelings-vergils-aeneid-and-the-psalms-of-david-in-the-confessions/55CFA16D0A7D458BAAE3725C766C75B4)
