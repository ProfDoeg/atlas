# Winfried Georg Sebald: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier concerns the German writer and academic generally published as **W. G. Sebald** and privately known as **Max Sebald**. It reconstructs his life from documentary chronologies, university and archive records, contemporary interviews and profiles, obituaries, bibliographies, and subsequent biographical research.

The following labels are used where evidence requires qualification:

- **Documented fact:** supported by institutional records, publications, archival catalogues, or multiple independent sources.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a named witness, biographer, interviewer, or contemporary source but not independently documented here.
- **Allegation or disputed claim:** asserted by an identifiable source and contested, inferential, or incompletely documented.
- **Rumor or myth:** recurrent reputation-making material for which proof is unavailable.
- **Unresolved question:** evidence is insufficient to choose conclusively among competing accounts.

Sebald deliberately mixed biography, autobiography, documentary evidence, fiction, photographs, unattributed borrowing, and invented material. Events narrated in his literary books therefore are not treated as biographical facts unless independently supported.

## Names and Self-Presentation

Sebald disliked **Winfried** and **Georg**, regarding them as ponderously German and implicated in the nationalist atmosphere into which he had been born. In Britain he increasingly used “Max.” Some interviews and reference works expanded this to **Winfried Georg Maximilian Sebald**, but biographical research found no evidence that Maximilian was registered at birth.

This discrepancy is not merely a cataloguing error. It belongs to Sebald’s habit of creating small documentary uncertainties around himself. The fictive expansion supplied a retrospective origin for “Max,” while his legal name did not. It is therefore best classified as:

- **Documented fact:** his legal name was Winfried Georg Sebald.
- **Documented fact:** he used W. G. Sebald professionally and Max socially.
- **Disputed/self-created claim:** Maximilian was his third legal given name.
- **Interpretation:** the claim functioned as modest personal mythology, consistent with the unstable relations between records and narration in his books.

He disliked the label “novelist” for his major prose works. They have variously been classified as novels, documentary fictions, prose narratives, travel writing, fictional memoir, essayistic fiction, and hybrid literature.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

Sebald’s father was **Georg Sebald**, the son of a railway worker from the Bavarian Forest. Georg trained as a locksmith and entered the **Reichswehr in 1929**. He remained in the military under the Third Reich and eventually attained the rank of captain in the Wehrmacht.

Georg met **Rosa (“Rosi”) Egelhofer** in Wertach. They married in 1936. During the Second World War Rosa lived for a period in Bamberg; in 1943 she returned to her parents’ home in Wertach to escape the air war. She was there when Winfried was born in May 1944.

Georg was absent during his son’s infancy, serving in the war and then being held in French captivity. He returned in 1947. He subsequently worked in or alongside the police and, from the mid-1950s until 1971, served in the Bundeswehr, eventually reaching lieutenant-colonel.

No evidence located establishes that Georg Sebald committed a war crime, belonged to the Nazi Party, or served in a particular criminal formation. His military service under the Nazi state is documented; broader characterizations of him as “a Nazi” were principally his son’s moral and generational judgment. Sebald repeatedly described his father’s refusal or inability to discuss the war. “I don’t remember” was reportedly Georg’s characteristic response.

Rosa Sebald later described her son as having been born “without a skin,” meaning unusually vulnerable to experience and other people’s suffering. This is a maternal recollection reported by Carole Angier, not a clinical finding.

### Siblings

Sebald was the middle of three children:

- **Gertrud Sebald**, born 1941, later Gertrud Sebald-Aebischer.
- **Winfried Georg Sebald**, born 1944.
- **Beate Sebald**, born 1951.

Gertrud later lived in Fribourg, Switzerland. Sebald joined her household there in September 1965, sharing a small apartment at Lausannegasse 11 with Gertrud, her husband, and their infant daughter, Sebald’s goddaughter.

Both sisters supplied extensive childhood information to Carole Angier. His widow and daughter, by contrast, declined substantial participation in Angier’s unauthorized biography and sought to protect the family’s privacy.

### Maternal grandparents

Sebald’s most important early attachment was to his maternal grandfather, **Josef Egelhofer**, Wertach’s village policeman or gendarme for approximately forty years. Josef reportedly:

- taught Sebald to read and write;
- told humorous tall stories;
- took him on walks and nature excursions;
- encouraged his attention to plants, animals, weather, and landscape;
- provided the sense of protection absent from Sebald’s relationship with his father.

Sebald said he thought of his grandfather daily. Josef’s death in **1956**, when Sebald was twelve, was one of the decisive losses of his life. Walking, natural observation, country lore, melancholy memory, and the figure of a benign older companion all recur in Sebald’s writing.

Detailed genealogical information beyond the parents and maternal grandfather is sparse in accessible documentary sources. No aristocratic lineage, inherited business fortune, or politically prominent ancestry is documented.

### Marriage and child

Sebald married **Ute Rosenbauer**, described as Austrian-born, in **1967**. A local biographical page elsewhere gives 1969, but the more detailed chronology and later biographies favor 1967. The couple had one daughter, **Anna**, born in 1972.

Ute and Anna remained largely outside his public literary identity. Because they withheld cooperation from Angier’s biography and restricted quotation from private correspondence, the inner history of the marriage cannot be fully reconstructed from public evidence.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1944–1963

### Wartime birth and absent father

Sebald was born during the final year of the European war in the alpine village of Wertach. The area escaped the physical destruction suffered by major German cities. His earliest environment therefore combined apparent pastoral safety with concealed national catastrophe.

His father was absent until 1947. Sebald was about three when Georg returned from French captivity. He later said that the returning stranger displaced the close relationship he had formed with his grandfather. The difficult father-son relationship became entangled with his later recognition of the Wehrmacht’s role and the silence of the parental generation.

### Wertach and Sonthofen

The family initially lived in Wertach, including a period in rented rooms above the Steinlehner wine tavern. Accounts differ about precisely when the family relocated permanently:

- some summaries describe Sebald as living in Sonthofen from 1948;
- the detailed local chronology dates the family move to **1952**.

The strongest reconstruction is that the family’s residence shifted within the Wertach–Sonthofen area after Georg’s return, with the definitive move to Sonthofen in 1952.

Sonthofen was symbolically important. Its military complex included a former National Socialist **Ordensburg**, later used by the Bundeswehr. Sebald grew up amid institutions and adults carried over from the Third Reich into the Federal Republic.

### Religion

He was raised Roman Catholic, attended Catholic religious observances, and was educated in an environment marked by crucifixes and clerical authority. Around the early 1960s he rejected organized religion and reportedly left the church in **1962**.

His later work retained Catholic visual and emotional residues—pilgrimage, relics, saints, penance, apocalypse, burial, commemoration—without a return to institutional faith. Scholarship accordingly distinguishes:

- **Documented fact:** Catholic baptism and upbringing.
- **Reported fact:** formal departure from the church in 1962.
- **Interpretation:** later literature retained a Catholic or quasi-theological imagination despite his rejection of organized religion.

### Schooling

From 1954 he attended the **Realgymnasium Maria Stern in Immenstadt**, then, from 1955, the **Oberrealschule in Oberstdorf**, where he completed the Abitur in 1963.

A formative teacher was **Armin Müller**, later transformed into **Paul Bereyter** in *Die Ausgewanderten*. Müller had been disadvantaged under National Socialism because of partial Jewish ancestry. Sebald reportedly did not learn this history while at school; it emerged later. The real teacher’s biography, altered and combined with fiction, became one of Sebald’s best-known acts of literary restitution and one of the earliest examples of his appropriation of another person’s life.

### Encounter with Holocaust evidence

According to Angier’s reconstruction, a school showing of documentary footage from concentration camps in the early 1960s was a psychic shock. Sebald connected the images with the refusal of his family and teachers to explain the recent past. He argued with his father, questioned Catholic and national authority, and experienced depression or a breakdown.

The severity and clinical nature of this episode cannot be independently established. It is best characterized as a **reported psychological crisis**, derived from Sebald’s retrospective statements and Angier’s interviews.

### Health and military exemption

Sebald was exempted from West German military service because of a reported **heart defect**. The precise diagnosis is not public. This early cardiac history later became relevant to the coroner’s interpretation of his fatal accident.

## Education and Intellectual Formation, 1963–1973

### Freiburg im Breisgau, 1963–1965

In 1963 Sebald began studying **German and English literature** at the Albert Ludwig University of Freiburg.

He became alienated from West German Germanistik. He considered its hierarchy authoritarian and believed many senior academics had concealed or inadequately confronted their careers under National Socialism. Freiburg bore the particular historical association of **Martin Heidegger’s 1933 rectorship** and support for the Nazi regime.

Sebald later said that he could not accept the prospect of advancing under the supervision of such professors. This dissatisfaction was intellectual, political, personal, and professional. He did not become a conventional activist of the West German New Left, however. Available scholarship finds no sustained party membership or allegiance to Marxist, Maoist, or Marxist-Leninist factions.

### Fribourg, Switzerland, 1965–1966

In September 1965 he moved to **Fribourg, Switzerland**, living with his sister Gertrud’s family at Lausannegasse 11. He studied at the University of Fribourg and completed a **licence ès lettres** in 1966, reportedly summa cum laude, with work on the playwright and satirist **Carl Sternheim**.

Sebald contrasted Switzerland’s older liberal-conservative traditions with what he regarded as the shallow, newly learned liberalism of postwar West Germany. This judgment was his retrospective political characterization rather than a neutral description of either country.

### Manchester, 1966–1969

In 1966 Sebald emigrated to Britain to become a **Lektor** at the University of Manchester. Manchester represented a radical geographical and social break from the Bavarian Alps. After an initial cultural shock, he developed a strong attachment to the city and to Britain.

Manchester also brought him into direct contact with Jewish refugees and émigrés. He later said that there, for the first time, he understood that Nazi persecution had happened to identifiable people rather than existing only as abstract history.

His Manchester landlord, **Peter Jordan**, became a major source for “Max Aurach/Max Ferber” in *The Emigrants*. Jordan’s family stories, photographs, and a female relative’s writings were absorbed into Sebald’s literary construction.

Sebald received an **MA from Manchester in 1969** for a thesis on Sternheim. The resulting monograph, *Carl Sternheim: Kritiker und Opfer der Wilhelminischen Ära*, appeared that year.

### St Gallen interlude

The chronologies differ slightly on the placement of Sebald’s year at the private boarding school **Institut auf dem Rosenberg** in St Gallen, Switzerland. The most likely sequence is that it occurred between the Manchester appointment and his move to Norwich, or partly overlapped the 1966–69 period summarized as “Manchester.”

He taught German and English but did not settle there. By 1970 he had returned to Britain.

### University of East Anglia and doctorate

In 1970 Sebald joined the **University of East Anglia (UEA)** in Norwich as a lecturer in German literature. He remained associated with UEA for the rest of his life.

He completed a doctorate in 1973 on **Alfred Döblin**, generally cited in English as *The Revival of Myth: A Study of Alfred Döblin’s Novels*. A revised German book, *Der Mythus der Zerstörung im Werk Döblins*, appeared in 1980.

## Chronological Life History and Career

### 1970–1976: Establishing an English life

Sebald taught modern German literature at the young, comparatively less hierarchical UEA. His daughter Anna was born in 1972.

His early scholarship was often polemical. Rather than follow a single theoretical school, he combined textual criticism, psychoanalytic suggestion, biography, ideological criticism, and moral judgment. He distrusted method-driven academic writing and sometimes preferred visiting places associated with an author over surveying the latest scholarship.

In 1975–76 he trained as a German teacher at the **Goethe-Institut in Munich**, an episode described in one chronology as another attempted return followed by renewed “emigration.” He ultimately chose Britain.

In **1976**, Sebald, Ute, and Anna moved into a Victorian former rectory—usually identified as **The Old Rectory**—in **Poringland**, south of Norwich. The house and its large garden became his permanent domestic base. He cultivated an extensive East Anglian garden from what had been rough ground.

### 1976–1988: Academic production

During the late 1970s and 1980s Sebald published studies of German and Austrian literature, including Döblin, Adalbert Stifter, Arthur Schnitzler, Elias Canetti, Thomas Bernhard, Peter Handke, Hermann Broch, and others.

In 1985 he published *Die Beschreibung des Unglücks: Zur österreichischen Literatur von Stifter bis Handke*. The book later served as the basis for his **Habilitation at the University of Hamburg in 1986**.

UEA’s internal career chronology places his advancement as follows:

- Senior Lecturer, October 1985;
- Reader, October 1987;
- Professor of European Literature, October 1988.

Some summaries state that he obtained a chair in 1987. The more detailed institutional study supports the 1988 professorial date, with 1987 marking promotion to Reader.

### Emergence as a literary writer

Sebald wrote poetry from at least the 1960s, but his public literary career developed late.

In 1988 he published *Nach der Natur: Ein Elementargedicht*, three long interlinked poetic narratives involving the painter Matthias Grünewald, the naturalist Georg Wilhelm Steller, and an autobiographical figure. The work established themes that remained central:

- natural and human destruction;
- exile and travel;
- historical recurrence;
- the limits of representation;
- melancholic observation;
- the transformation of archival facts into literature.

In 1990 came *Schwindel. Gefühle.* The four-part work moves among Stendhal, Kafka, northern Italy, Vienna, Verona, and a partly fictional return to Sebald’s native village. Its documentary appearance masks numerous transformations and inventions.

### British Centre for Literary Translation

In **1989** Sebald founded the **British Centre for Literary Translation (BCLT)** at UEA, drawing on continental European translator-house models.

Its purposes were to:

- provide practical and financial support to literary translators;
- increase the professional visibility of translation;
- host writers, translators, workshops, conferences, and the St Jerome Lecture;
- compile a directory of literary translators;
- deepen exchange between British and European literary cultures.

Sebald secured recurrent support from the Arts Council of Great Britain and other sources. He served as founding director until **1994**, stepping back as his literary career expanded but remaining connected to the centre.

### 1991–1995: Austrian criticism and the major prose works

In 1991 Sebald published *Unheimliche Heimat: Essays zur österreichischen Literatur*. The title’s contradiction—an “uncanny” or “unhomely homeland”—encapsulated his approach to German-speaking identity.

*Die Ausgewanderten: Vier lange Erzählungen* followed in 1992. Its subjects—Henry Selwyn, Paul Bereyter, Ambros Adelwarth, and Max Aurach—were constructed from identifiable lives, family testimony, photographs, published accounts, and invention.

The book’s German reception was initially limited. International recognition grew after Michael Hulse’s English translation, *The Emigrants*, appeared in 1996.

In August 1992 Sebald undertook the walking journey through Suffolk later reconstructed in *Die Ringe des Saturn*. The itinerary included Norwich and the eastern Suffolk coast, with such places as Lowestoft, Southwold, Dunwich, Middleton, Woodbridge, and the surrounding estates and heathlands. The narration expands far beyond a literal travelogue into accounts of herring fisheries, colonial violence, silk cultivation, Joseph Conrad, Roger Casement, Thomas Browne, and the destruction of landscapes and peoples.

The book’s narrator begins in hospital after a collapse. Later accounts have sometimes treated every detail of that frame as literal autobiography. Some illness and depression were real, but the precise relationship between the narrated paralysis and Sebald’s clinical history remains uncertain.

*Die Ringe des Saturn: Eine englische Wallfahrt* appeared in 1995 and became his defining literary synthesis of walking, archive, photograph, memory, and catastrophe.

### 1996–1999: International recognition

Sebald’s admission to the **Deutsche Akademie für Sprache und Dichtung** in 1996 marked increasing German recognition. The English *Emigrants* was strongly received in Britain and the United States and won the Jewish Quarterly/Wingate Prize.

Michael Hulse translated *The Emigrants*, *The Rings of Saturn*, and *Vertigo*. Sebald subjected translations to extensive revision. Angier’s research found that he spent roughly 350 hours working through *The Rings of Saturn* with its translator. This made him, in Jo Catling’s formulation, at least a co-translator of his English books in practice, although the title pages identify the translators.

The process produced friction. Sebald’s intervention could be minute and controlling; his relationship with Hulse deteriorated. **Anthea Bell** subsequently translated *Austerlitz*, *On the Natural History of Destruction*, *Campo Santo*, and other material.

In 1998 Sebald published *Logis in einem Landhaus*, portraits of Gottfried Keller, Johann Peter Hebel, Robert Walser, Jean-Jacques Rousseau, Eduard Mörike, and the painter Jan Peter Tripp. The work is both criticism and indirect autobiography.

He was elected to the **Bavarian Academy of Fine Arts** in 1998.

### The air-war controversy

Sebald delivered lectures in Zürich in 1997 on the failure, as he saw it, of postwar German literature to represent the Allied destruction of German cities. Revised with an essay attacking Alfred Andersch’s self-presentation, they appeared in 1999 as *Luftkrieg und Literatur: Mit einem Essay zu Alfred Andersch*.

The intervention generated extensive debate. Sebald argued that:

- the destruction of German cities and civilian experience had been insufficiently rendered in serious postwar literature;
- collective silence and rapid reconstruction concealed the dead;
- acknowledging German civilian suffering did not erase German responsibility for the Holocaust and aggressive war.

Critics objected that he understated existing air-war literature, generalized too broadly, or risked feeding German victim narratives. Others credited him with opening a neglected field. The hostile letters he received included antisemitic and conspiratorial responses; these do not demonstrate that his argument itself was antisemitic.

His essay on Andersch alleged that the prominent postwar writer manipulated autobiographical facts, particularly concerning his first wife’s Jewish ancestry and his own conduct under the Nazi regime. The criticism was influential but was also regarded as prosecutorial and insufficiently attentive to context.

### 2000–2001: Final period

Sebald received major German literary prizes in 2000, including the Joseph Breitbach Prize and the Heinrich Heine Prize.

*Austerlitz* appeared in German in 2001. Its title character, transported from Prague to Britain as a Jewish child, gradually reconstructs his erased childhood, his mother’s deportation to Theresienstadt, and his parents’ fate. The book became Sebald’s most internationally visible work.

Its construction drew on multiple lives and texts, among them:

- Kindertransport survivor **Susi Bechhöfer**;
- architectural historians and acquaintances;
- Ludwig Wittgenstein’s image and biography;
- documentary programs and published memoir;
- Sebald’s own journeys to Prague, Theresienstadt, London, Paris, and Marienbad.

Also in 2001 he published *For Years Now*, short poetic texts with images by **Tess Jaray**.

His final years brought publicity, readings, interviews, travel, prizes, and widespread speculation that he might eventually receive the Nobel Prize. The pace aggravated longstanding headaches, back pain, depression, and reported breathlessness.

## Residences, Migrations, and Journeys

### Principal residences

1. **Wertach, Bavaria**, 1944 to childhood; maternal grandparents’ environment.
2. **Sonthofen and the Oberallgäu**, principally 1952–1963.
3. **Freiburg im Breisgau**, 1963–1965.
4. **Lausannegasse 11, Fribourg, Switzerland**, 1965–1966.
5. **Manchester**, from 1966 during his university appointment.
6. **St Gallen, Switzerland**, approximately one academic year.
7. **Norwich/Wymondham area**, after joining UEA in 1970.
8. **The Old Rectory, Poringland, Norfolk**, from 1976 until death.

### Significant travel

Sebald traveled repeatedly through:

- Switzerland;
- Austria and Germany;
- Belgium;
- France, especially Paris and Corsica;
- northern Italy, including Venice, Verona, Riva, and other sites used in *Vertigo*;
- Prague, Marienbad/Mariánské Lázně, and Theresienstadt/Terezín;
- East Anglia and the Suffolk coast;
- the United States for teaching, readings, interviews, and literary events.

His journeys were research methods as much as travel. He photographed buildings, railway stations, cemeteries, landscapes, museum objects, documents, and people; collected postcards and found images; and retraced earlier writers’ movements.

## Employment, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Employment

Sebald’s documented livelihood before literary success came primarily from salaried teaching:

- University of Manchester, German lector;
- Institut auf dem Rosenberg, language teacher;
- University of East Anglia, lecturer through professor;
- Goethe-Institut teacher training in Munich.

He spent more than thirty years at UEA. Former students described his seminars as conversational, associative, demanding, subversive, and humorous.

### British Centre for Literary Translation

The BCLT was his principal institutional creation. It was not a private business. It operated within UEA with public and cultural funding, including Arts Council support.

### Publishing relationships

His principal German literary publishers included Greno, Eichborn, Hanser, Residenz, Klett, Kohlhammer, and later S. Fischer. Major English-language publishers included Harvill, Hamish Hamilton, and Penguin-related imprints.

His increasingly international readership presumably produced royalties, but no reliable public accounting of advances, royalty income, estate value, bank holdings, investments, or tax arrangements was located.

### Property

The Sebald family owned or occupied the Old Rectory in Poringland from 1976. Public evidence consulted does not establish purchase price, mortgage structure, later estate valuation, or additional property.

### Businesses, investments, patents, and technologies

No evidence was found that Sebald:

- founded a commercial company;
- held significant corporate investments relevant to his public life;
- developed a patented invention;
- owned industrial technology;
- maintained a complex business structure.

He reportedly avoided computers and composed through handwriting and typewriting, accompanied by extensive paper files, photographs, photocopies, maps, and clippings.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Political position

Sebald’s politics were anti-authoritarian, anti-nationalist, suspicious of German institutions, and preoccupied with the consequences of National Socialism, colonialism, capitalist extraction, and technological destruction.

No evidence was found of:

- political party membership;
- elected office;
- government appointment;
- intelligence employment;
- participation in political violence;
- imprisonment or exile imposed by a state.

His emigration was voluntary, although he described it in morally charged terms as an escape from postwar Germany.

He did not fit comfortably within orthodox Marxism or the organized 1968 left. His social criticism was closer to negative critique, historical melancholy, and distrust of progress than to a program of political action.

### Frankfurt School and Walter Benjamin

**Theodor W. Adorno** and **Walter Benjamin** were important early intellectual reference points. Sebald’s attention to ruins, fragments, constellations, quotation, natural history, failed progress, photography, and the recovery of the defeated has repeatedly been connected to Benjamin. Adorno informed his sense that culture after catastrophe could neither continue innocently nor simply fall silent.

Sebald did not merely reproduce Frankfurt School doctrine. He combined it with literary and antiquarian traditions, psychoanalysis, travel writing, local history, and natural observation.

### Literary influences and affinities

Frequently documented influences or “elective affinities” include:

- Franz Kafka;
- Robert Walser;
- Thomas Bernhard;
- Adalbert Stifter;
- Gottfried Keller;
- Johann Peter Hebel;
- Vladimir Nabokov;
- Jorge Luis Borges;
- Marcel Proust;
- Joseph Conrad;
- Thomas Browne;
- Elias Canetti;
- Alfred Döblin;
- Peter Handke;
- Jean-Jacques Rousseau;
- Stendhal;
- Chateaubriand.

He explicitly described aspects of his syntax as a tribute to Bernhard’s “periscopic” writing. Nabokov’s *Speak, Memory* supplied both material and method. Benjamin and Borges offered models for arranging fragments and remote correspondences.

### Aesthetic principles

Sebald’s characteristic practice included:

- exceptionally long, recursive sentences;
- indirect, nested testimony—“Austerlitz said,” reported by another narrator;
- uncaptioned or ambiguously captioned photographs;
- altered photographs and reproductions;
- archival research alongside invented documentation;
- real names adjacent to fictional characters;
- recurrent walking and railway journeys;
- a narrator resembling but not identical to Sebald;
- transformations of source language without ordinary citation;
- preservation of uncertainty rather than final historical explanation.

He understood literature as capable of a limited **restitution**: not repairing historical injury, but attending to what had been ignored. Critics dispute whether his uncredited appropriation of victims’ narratives contradicted that ethic.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Ute Rosenbauer Sebald

Wife from 1967 until his death. She shared his migrations and Norfolk household but did not participate in his public mythology. Public material is insufficient for a detailed account of the marriage.

### Anna Sebald

Only child, born 1972. She was in the car during the fatal 2001 collision and survived, reportedly with serious injuries. Later press accounts vary in describing the extent of injury.

### Josef Egelhofer

Maternal grandfather and central childhood attachment; taught Sebald reading, nature observation, storytelling, and walking.

### Georg Sebald

Father and principal figure of generational conflict. His Wehrmacht and Bundeswehr careers, emotional remoteness, and silence about the war became crucial to Sebald’s intellectual development.

### Armin Müller

Schoolteacher and model for Paul Bereyter. The relationship links Sebald’s childhood to his later recovery and transformation of persecuted lives.

### Peter Jordan

Manchester landlord, friend, and source for Max Aurach/Ferber. Jordan supplied autobiographical stories, photographs, and family documents. Angier’s research suggests that Sebald used more of this material than Jordan or relatives expected.

### Michael Hamburger

German-born British poet and translator; friend, intellectual companion, and supporter of the BCLT. Sebald nominated him for an honorary doctorate at UEA. Hamburger translated *After Nature* and *Unrecounted*.

### Michael Hulse

English translator of *The Emigrants*, *The Rings of Saturn*, and *Vertigo*. Their intensive collaboration shaped the English Sebald style, but the relationship became strained by Sebald’s extensive intervention.

### Anthea Bell

Translator of *Austerlitz*, *On the Natural History of Destruction*, *Campo Santo*, and other later work. Her translation of *Austerlitz* received the Helen and Kurt Wolff Translator’s Prize; the novel also won the Independent Foreign Fiction Prize.

### Jan Peter Tripp

Painter and etcher; longstanding friend and collaborator. Their joint *Unerzählt* paired thirty-three brief texts with thirty-three images of eyes.

### Tess Jaray

Artist and collaborator on *For Years Now*.

### Uwe Schütte

Sebald’s student and doctoral supervisee at UEA, later a major scholar, memoirist, critic, and biographer of Sebald. Schütte has challenged both the reverential “Saint Sebald” reputation and parts of Angier’s biography.

### Carole Angier

Interviewed Sebald in 1996 and later wrote the unauthorized biography *Speak, Silence*. Her attempt to separate documentary lives from Sebald’s versions transformed discussion of his methods.

### Publishers and editors

Important professional associates included publisher/editor **Michael Krüger**, posthumous editor **Sven Meyer**, and the German and British publishing teams who negotiated Sebald’s images, translations, and rights.

### Late relationship with “Marie”

Angier reports a renewed relationship late in Sebald’s life with a woman from his youth identified as **Marie**. Reviewers describe it as emotionally intense and possibly an affair.

Evidence and limits:

- **Reported fact:** Sebald renewed contact and exchanged intimate communications with a woman from his past.
- **Unresolved question:** the exact physical or sexual nature of the relationship.
- His widow’s non-participation and restrictions on private correspondence prevent a balanced reconstruction.

No evidence located supports describing Marie as a public partner or treating the marriage as formally separated.

## Health, Psychological Crises, and Accidents

Sebald had longstanding health complaints, including:

- a childhood or young-adult cardiac defect sufficient for military exemption;
- severe migraines;
- chronic back pain;
- recurrent depression;
- anxiety and episodes he described as approaching the boundary between melancholy and madness;
- later breathlessness suggestive, in retrospect, of cardiac trouble.

Friends reportedly urged medical attention, which he resisted.

He also had several minor automobile accidents and was described as distractible while driving. This history is relevant but does not establish negligence in the fatal crash.

The “paralysis” framing *The Rings of Saturn* should not automatically be read as a literal medical record. A real collapse or depressive illness may underlie it, but the published account is literary construction.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Disputed Conduct

### 1. Use of real lives without permission or acknowledgment

This is the most substantial documented controversy.

Sebald’s prose repeatedly transformed identifiable people and their documents. Some models or surviving relatives objected that he:

- changed ethnicity, religion, family history, or motive;
- fused several people into one character;
- reproduced or paraphrased private writing without attribution;
- used photographs in ways implying false identity;
- allowed readers to mistake constructed fiction for testimony;
- did not respond adequately when sources requested acknowledgment.

No criminal charge or judicial finding resulted. The issue concerns copyright, privacy, consent, historiography, and literary ethics.

### 2. Frank Auerbach and Max Aurach/Ferber

The artist in the final section of *Die Ausgewanderten* was originally called **Max Aurach**, transparently echoing painter **Frank Auerbach**. The character combined material from Auerbach, Peter Jordan, Jordan’s family, and published art criticism.

Documented or strongly reported consequences:

- Auerbach objected to the portrayal.
- He refused permission for his images to be reproduced in the English edition.
- The character was renamed **Max Ferber**.
- images closely associated with Auerbach were removed from the English edition.
- later German editions also adopted “Ferber.”

Some accounts say Auerbach threatened litigation against the German publisher. No filed lawsuit or adjudicated case was identified; “threatened to sue” should therefore remain a **reported allegation**, not a court fact.

Auerbach reportedly called the book “repellent.”

### 3. Peter Jordan, Thea Gebhardt, and family materials

Sebald drew on stories and photographs given by Peter Jordan and on writings associated with Jordan’s family, including **Thea Gebhardt**. Angier and subsequent critics argue that he incorporated passages without conventional acknowledgment and altered the family’s Jewish and refugee histories.

The documentary issue is not simply that fiction changes reality. Sebald publicly suggested that some characters corresponded more directly to actual Jewish émigrés than his sources support.

### 4. Henry Selwyn and Rhoades Buckton

The model for Dr Henry Selwyn has been identified as **Rhoades Buckton**, an English country doctor. Sebald represented Selwyn as a Lithuanian Jewish émigré and associated his suicide with exile and Holocaust memory.

Angier’s investigation concluded that Buckton was not Jewish and was born in Cheshire. His suicide was real. Family members reportedly objected not principally to fictionalizing his ancestry, but to using his suicide as symbolic material.

This supports a distinction:

- **Documented fact:** a real suicide and acquaintance contributed to the story.
- **Fictional construction:** Jewish-Lithuanian origins and the narrated historical significance.
- **Disputed conduct:** presenting later interview accounts as though he had discovered Selwyn’s concealed Jewish identity.

### 5. Susi Bechhöfer and *Austerlitz*

Kindertransport survivor **Susi Bechhöfer** and her twin sister Lotte were sent from Germany to Britain in 1939. Susi later learned that their mother, Rosa Bechhöfer, was Jewish and had been murdered at Auschwitz. Her history appeared in the BBC documentary *Whatever Happened to Susi?* and in memoir.

Sebald used conspicuous elements of this history in *Austerlitz*. Bechhöfer recognized them and complained that her tragic past had been taken by a bestselling author. She sought public acknowledgment. Sebald corresponded or communicated about addressing the matter but died before a satisfactory acknowledgment appeared.

Evidence:

- **Documented fact:** strong correspondences exist between her documentary/memoir and the novel.
- **Reported fact:** she contacted Sebald and felt cheated or robbed of her history.
- **Documented absence:** the original novel did not credit her.
- **Disputed interpretation:** whether this constituted plagiarism, ethically permissible transformation, or an abuse intensified by Sebald’s position as a non-Jewish German writer.
- **No court finding:** no plagiarism judgment or damages award was located.

### 6. Invented citations and academic practice

Angier found or reported examples of Sebald inventing footnotes, references, or documentary details in academic prose. His first book on Sternheim reportedly included the false claim that **Theodor Adorno had written to him approvingly**.

If correctly reconstructed, this is more serious in scholarship than in avowed fiction:

- **Reported/document-supported claim:** Sebald invented at least some citations or claims of authority.
- **No known disciplinary proceeding:** no university investigation, dismissal, retraction process, or fraud judgment was located.
- **Disputed significance:** defenders treat the practice as continuous with his provocative, literary criticism; critics regard it as academic dishonesty.

### 7. “Steal as much as you can”

Former students recall Sebald advising writers to take or “steal” useful material because no one would notice. The line circulates in several formulations.

It should be treated as a **reported recollection**, not a verified verbatim transcript. Its consistency with his working methods has made it central to later criticism.

### 8. Translation conflicts

Sebald’s exceptionally close rewriting of English translations blurred authorship. He sometimes restored material, altered syntax, or rewrote passages. Relations with Michael Hulse became difficult.

There is no evidence of litigation or formal misconduct. The dispute concerns credit, control, and the translator’s autonomy. Published translations remain attributed to their named translators.

### 9. The Holocaust and “singularity”

Sebald stated that the German catastrophe, however dreadful, was not an isolated event outside history but developed from European history and remained embedded within it.

This has been paraphrased as “denying the singularity of the Holocaust.” Evidence does **not** show Holocaust denial: Sebald repeatedly treated the extermination of European Jews as historical fact and as a central rupture. The genuine interpretive dispute is whether placing it within a longer continuum of colonial, technological, and state violence clarifies or relativizes it.

### 10. German civilian suffering

*Luftkrieg und Literatur* was criticized for centering Germans as victims of Allied bombing and for undercounting earlier literary treatments. Conversely, Sebald explicitly situated bombing within the moral situation created by Nazi aggression and genocide.

The controversy remains literary-historical and political. No evidence supports conspiracy claims that his intervention was directed or financed by a political organization.

### 11. Polemics against Alfred Andersch and Jurek Becker

Sebald accused Alfred Andersch of self-exculpatory manipulation. His unpublished or posthumously published criticism of Holocaust survivor **Jurek Becker** dismissed aspects of Becker’s work in terms some scholars considered arrogant and aesthetically dogmatic.

These episodes complicate the later image of Sebald as an automatically reverent “Holocaust author.” They show a critic willing to apply harsh standards to persecuted writers as well as to members of the German majority.

### 12. Criminality, fraud, intelligence, and financial allegations

The dedicated adversarial search found no credible evidence that Sebald was involved in:

- criminal prosecution or conviction;
- fraud for financial gain;
- money laundering;
- corruption;
- intelligence service activity;
- political violence;
- abuse investigations;
- unlawful imprisonment;
- a litigated plagiarism judgment.

Recent 2024–2026 scholarship continues examining appropriation, intertextual copying, source manipulation, photography, and restitution. It has not produced a new criminal, financial, or sexual-abuse case.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Self-Created Narratives

### “Maximilian” as the missing third name

**Story:** Max was short for a registered third name, Maximilian.

**Circulation:** Sebald interviews, catalogues, reference works, and early biographies.

**Evidence against:** later examination of the legal name found only Winfried Georg.

**Effect:** reinforced Sebald’s elusive self-presentation and caused persistent bibliographic error.

**Classification:** self-created or self-assisted myth.

### Imminent Nobel laureate

**Story:** Sebald was about to win, or had effectively been selected for, the Nobel Prize in Literature when he died.

**Circulation:** obituaries, publisher publicity, profiles, and later reader mythology.

**Evidence:** he was widely discussed by critics as a plausible future candidate. Nobel nominations and deliberations remain secret for fifty years; the public archive cannot verify nominations from 2001.

**Evidence against certainty:** no accessible Swedish Academy record establishes selection or imminent award.

**Effect:** intensified the sense of a career cut off at its apex.

**Classification:** plausible reputation claim; unverified as a formal nomination and mythic when stated as certainty.

### “Saint Sebald”

**Story:** Sebald was a solitary, morally pure custodian of Holocaust memory who sacrificed ordinary life to restitution.

**Circulation:** admiring criticism after his death, strengthened by early profiles and the austerity of his authorial image.

**Counterevidence:** testimony depicts a funny, sociable, sharp, controlling, sometimes ruthless writer who transformed other people’s lives without consent; he had professional ambitions, conflicts, and a possibly romantic late relationship.

**Effect:** contributed to rapid canonization while provoking corrective biographies by Angier and Schütte.

**Classification:** posthumous hagiographic mythology.

### The wholly autobiographical narrator

**Story:** the first-person narrator in *Vertigo*, *The Rings of Saturn*, and other books is Sebald giving a factual account.

**Evidence against:** drafts and source research reveal composites, altered dates, relocated events, staged images, fictional encounters, and borrowed testimony.

**Effect:** gave the books documentary authority while exposing Sebald to charges of deception.

**Classification:** deliberately cultivated generic illusion.

### The absolutely humorless melancholic

**Story:** Sebald was personally as grave and mournful as his prose.

**Evidence against:** family, students, colleagues, interviewers, and recordings repeatedly describe deadpan humor, mimicry, teasing, and comic exaggeration.

**Effect:** solemn photographs and the themes of catastrophe encouraged a flattened public persona.

**Classification:** hostile or reverential caricature.

### A single literal Suffolk pilgrimage

**Story:** *The Rings of Saturn* directly transcribes one continuous walk.

**Evidence:** a 1992 Suffolk journey underlies the book, but the final itinerary and chronology were artistically assembled from multiple journeys, research, and composition.

**Effect:** encouraged literary tourism along a reconstructed “Sebald Way.”

**Classification:** fact-based literary myth.

### Prophetic death image

Sebald recalled seeing, at about age five, a photograph taken by his father of a beautiful young soldier killed in a car accident. He told an interviewer in 2001 that he sensed in the photograph the beginning of an unknown catastrophe. Four months later he died in a car collision.

The photograph and recollection are reported; the prophetic relation is retrospective symbolic association, not evidence of foreknowledge.

## Death

On **14 December 2001**, Sebald was driving near his Poringland home with his daughter Anna as passenger. His car crossed or turned into the opposing lane and collided head-on with a lorry.

Sebald died at the scene. Anna survived; accounts describe her injuries variously as limited or serious. The coroner reportedly concluded that Sebald probably suffered a **heart attack immediately before or during the loss of control**.

The evidentiary distinction is important:

- **Documented fact:** death occurred in the collision.
- **Officially reported probability:** cardiac event precipitated it.
- **Unresolved medical detail:** accessible accounts do not provide a complete public postmortem report or exact pathological sequence.

He was fifty-seven. He was buried at **St Andrew’s Church, Framingham Earl**, close to Poringland.

## Posthumous Archive, Reputation, and Legacy

### Archives

In 2004 Sebald’s literary estate was transferred to the **Deutsches Literaturarchiv Marbach**. It includes manuscripts, drafts, source files, annotated books, images, correspondence, and working materials. The archive has enabled scholars to reconstruct his copying, cutting, photographic selection, and compositional methods.

UEA holds the **W. G. Sebald Audio-Visual Collection**, covering 1975–2001, in eight boxes and a ring binder. It includes recordings collected by Gordon Turner and UEA Archives and is partly digitized.

### Editorial afterlife

Posthumous publications include:

- *Campo Santo*, edited by Sven Meyer;
- *Unerzählt*, with Jan Peter Tripp;
- *Über das Land und das Wasser*, edited by Sven Meyer;
- interview collections;
- lectures, reminiscences, and previously uncollected essays.

These are not new books completed and authorized in final form by Sebald. Their editors selected and arranged material from publications and the estate.

### Literary influence

Sebald’s combination of image, travel, archive, testimony, and fiction became a major international model. His influence is visible in:

- documentary and archival fiction;
- psychogeography;
- essayistic memoir;
- image-text books;
- climate and extinction writing;
- postmemory and Holocaust literature;
- hybrid criticism and autofiction.

Writers, artists, filmmakers, critics, and musicians have adapted or responded to his methods. At the same time, “Sebaldian” became a sometimes dismissive label for melancholy walking narratives furnished with grainy photographs and historical digressions.

### Memorials

At UEA, Sebald’s family planted a copper beech in 2003. A circular bench, recalling Saturn’s rings, surrounds it and bears a line from *Unerzählt* concerning the unrecorded history of averted faces.

The **Deutsche Sebald Gesellschaft** promotes research and administers or supports the W. G. Sebald Literature Prize in association with Kempten, Sonthofen, and Wertach.

Literary walking routes in East Anglia, including projects commonly called the “Sebald Way,” have turned the landscape of *The Rings of Saturn* into a site of pilgrimage.

### Reassessment after 2021

Carole Angier’s *Speak, Silence* shifted attention from Sebald’s canonization to:

- his family history;
- depression and bodily illness;
- his invented autobiographical details;
- the real people behind his characters;
- unacknowledged sources;
- anger among models and families;
- the ethical cost of his literary method.

Critics of Angier argue that she sometimes treats fiction as failed biography, psychologizes incomplete evidence, underplays politics, or creates a new hagiography even while exposing Sebald’s deceptions.

Recent scholarship increasingly treats the conflict between restitution and appropriation as structural to Sebald’s work rather than a problem resolvable by declaring him either fraud or saint.

- The four principal prose books are genuine Sebald works, but incorporate uncredited documentary and literary sources.

- No evidence was found of ghostwritten books, patents, political manifestos, diaries published intact, or works issued commercially under his name by an unidentified ghostwriter.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the researched sources are included. Mere appearance of a rostered person inside a historical digression is not treated as a personal connection.

### Walter Benjamin — documented intellectual influence

Sebald identified Walter Benjamin, with Adorno, as an important early intellectual influence. Benjamin’s methods—constellation, montage, quotation, natural history, ruin, the recovery of defeated lives, and distrust of progress—became central reference points in Sebald scholarship. There was no meeting: Benjamin died in 1940, four years before Sebald’s birth.

**Label:** documented influence; no personal contact.

### Theodor W. Adorno — documented intellectual influence; disputed personal-contact mythology

Sebald described Adorno as an early influence. His literature of catastrophe and postwar criticism were shaped by arguments associated with Adorno and the Frankfurt School.

Sebald also claimed or implied in early scholarly work that Adorno had sent him an approving letter concerning his Sternheim research. Angier’s reconstruction treats that letter as invented.

**Label:** documented intellectual influence; disputed or apparently fabricated correspondence claim.

### Jorge Luis Borges — documented literary affinity/influence

Interviews and profiles name Borges among Sebald’s elective affinities. Their shared devices include labyrinthine research, imaginary documentary authority, remote correspondences, and blurred boundaries between essay and fiction. No meeting or correspondence was found.

**Label:** documented reported influence; no personal contact established.

### Marcel Proust — documented literary influence

Proust supplied a major model for involuntary memory, recursive narration, and the reconstruction of vanished worlds. Sebald’s work and contemporary criticism repeatedly place him in a Proustian lineage. Proust died before Sebald’s birth.

**Label:** documented literary influence; no personal contact.

### Jean-Jacques Rousseau — documented subject of Sebald’s criticism

Sebald wrote an extended literary portrait of Rousseau in *Logis in einem Landhaus*. The connection is one of critical engagement, not personal contact.

**Label:** documented intellectual/literary engagement.

### Sigmund Freud — documented intertextual/intellectual presence

Scholarship has identified Freud and psychoanalytic narrative among Sebald’s sources, particularly in relation to trauma, repression, repetition, and screen memory. No direct personal connection was possible.

**Label:** documented intellectual/intertextual connection.

### Martin Heidegger — documented institutional-historical and critical connection

Sebald studied at Freiburg, where Heidegger’s 1933 rectorship and Nazi affiliation formed part of the compromised history of German academic life that repelled him. Sebald also discussed Heidegger critically in relation to Johann Peter Hebel and “high origins.” No meeting is documented.

**Label:** documented institutional-historical and critical connection; no personal contact established.

### Jean Baudrillard, Jacques Derrida, Paul-Michel Foucault, Gilles Deleuze, Jürgen Habermas, and other rostered contemporaries

No source consulted establishes a meeting, correspondence, collaboration, rivalry, or explicitly acknowledged formative influence between Sebald and these figures. Some occupy the broader intellectual field through which later critics interpret him, but that does not constitute a biographical connection.

**Label:** no documented connection found.

### Remaining atlas roster

The search found no reliable personal or intellectual connection meeting the atlas standard for the overwhelming majority of the roster. The fact that Sebald wrote about European history, war, travel, colonialism, art, or philosophy does not by itself establish a connection to every historical figure appearing in those subjects.
