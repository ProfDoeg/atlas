## Works by Plato

### Philosophical dialogues generally accepted as Plato’s

- **Fourth century BC; exact date disputed:** *Euthyphro*. A dramatic philosophical dialogue on piety, set immediately before Socrates’ trial.[^1][^2]
- **After 399 BC; exact date disputed:** *Apology of Socrates*. A literary courtroom speech presenting Socrates’ defence; it resembles forensic oratory more than dialogue and is not a trial transcript.[^1][^2]
- **Fourth century BC; exact date disputed:** *Crito*. A prison dialogue on justice, obligation, and Socrates’ refusal to escape.[^1][^2]
- **Fourth century BC; exact date disputed:** *Phaedo*. A narrated dialogue on Socrates’ final day, the soul, and death, combining philosophical argument with memorial drama.[^1][^2]
- **Fourth century BC; exact date disputed:** *Cratylus*. A dialogue on names, language, and whether words are correct by nature or convention.[^1][^2]
- **Fourth century BC; exact date disputed:** *Theaetetus*. A dialogue testing definitions of knowledge; its inconclusive form resists treatment as a doctrinal treatise.[^1][^2]
- **Probably late fourth-century work; exact date disputed:** *Sophist*. A dialectical dialogue attempting to define the sophist and examining being, non-being, and false statement.[^1][^3]
- **Probably late fourth-century work; exact date disputed:** *Statesman* (*Politicus*). A dialectical dialogue defining the true statesman through division, myth, and comparison with rival arts.[^1][^3]
- **Fourth century BC; exact date disputed:** *Parmenides*. A dialogue in which objections to forms lead into an extended dialectical exercise; its place in Plato’s development remains contested.[^1][^2]
- **Probably late fourth-century work; exact date disputed:** *Philebus*. A dialogue about pleasure, knowledge, measure, and the constitution of a good human life.[^1][^3]
- **Fourth century BC; exact date disputed:** *Symposium*. A sequence of competitive speeches and dramatic episodes concerning erōs; it is simultaneously philosophical dialogue, banquet literature, and literary performance.[^1][^2]
- **Fourth century BC; exact date disputed:** *Phaedrus*. A dialogue combining speeches on love with an inquiry into rhetoric, dialectic, memory, and writing.[^1][^2]
- **Fourth century BC; exact date disputed:** *Charmides*. An aporetic dialogue testing definitions of temperance or self-control.[^1][^2]
- **Fourth century BC; exact date disputed:** *Laches*. An aporetic dialogue about courage and the education of young men.[^1][^2]
- **Fourth century BC; exact date disputed:** *Lysis*. A dramatic and inconclusive inquiry into friendship and desire.[^1][^2]
- **Fourth century BC; exact date disputed:** *Euthydemus*. A dialogue staging eristic verbal combat against Socratic philosophical exhortation.[^1][^2]
- **Fourth century BC; exact date disputed:** *Protagoras*. A dialogue-drama about virtue, education, and whether virtue can be taught.[^1][^2]
- **Fourth century BC; exact date disputed:** *Gorgias*. A dialogue on rhetoric, justice, punishment, and political life.[^1][^2]
- **Fourth century BC; exact date disputed:** *Meno*. A dialogue on virtue, inquiry, recollection, and the difference between knowledge and true belief.[^1][^2]
- **Fourth century BC; exact date disputed:** *Hippias Minor* (*Lesser Hippias*). A short dialogue whose paradoxical argument concerns voluntary and involuntary wrongdoing.[^1][^2]
- **Fourth century BC; exact date disputed:** *Ion*. A short dialogue about poetic performance, expertise, and inspiration.[^1][^2]
- **Fourth century BC; exact date disputed:** *Menexenus*. A dialogue enclosing a parodic or philosophically charged funeral oration; its mixture of dialogue and civic speech makes its purpose disputed.[^1][^2]
- **Fourth century BC; composed and revised over an unknown period:** *Republic*. A ten-book dialogue and large-scale political-philosophical obra concerning justice, education, knowledge, psychology, poetry, and the ideal city.[^1][^2]
- **Probably late fourth-century work; exact date disputed:** *Timaeus*. A cosmological dialogue dominated by Timaeus’ long likely account of the cosmos, living beings, and embodiment.[^1][^3]
- **Probably late fourth-century work; unfinished or deliberately incomplete:** *Critias*. The surviving beginning of a dialogue about prehistoric Athens and Atlantis; it breaks off in mid-narrative, and whether Plato abandoned it cannot be established.[^1][^3]
- **Late fourth century BC; left in an uncertain editorial state at Plato’s death:** *Laws*. A twelve-book dialogue presenting a legislative project for Magnesia. Ancient testimony says Philip of Opus transcribed it from wax tablets, so the surviving text may embody posthumous editorial work.[^1][^4][^5]

### Dialogues of genuinely disputed Platonic authorship

- **Fourth century BC; authorship and date disputed:** *Alcibiades* (*First Alcibiades*). A dialogue on self-knowledge and preparation for political rule; Cooper marks its attribution as plausibly questioned, although it continues to have defenders.[^1][^2]
- **Fourth century BC; authorship and date disputed:** *Hippias Major* (*Greater Hippias*). A dialogue repeatedly testing proposed definitions of beauty; scholars remain divided over whether it is Plato’s work.[^1][^2]
- **Fourth century BC; authorship and date disputed:** *Clitophon*. A brief dialogue criticizing the merely exhortative effect of Socratic speech; its abrupt ending and anomalous stance have encouraged doubts about attribution.[^1][^2]

### Dialogues transmitted in the ancient canon but generally rejected as Plato’s

- **Date unknown; probably post-Platonic:** *Second Alcibiades*. A Socratic dialogue about prayer and knowledge; it belongs to Plato’s transmitted corpus but is generally regarded as pseudonymous.[^1][^6]
- **Date unknown; probably post-Platonic:** *Hipparchus*. A dialogue concerning love of gain and containing a narrative about the Athenian Hipparchus; modern scholarship generally rejects Plato’s authorship.[^1][^6]
- **Date unknown; probably post-Platonic:** *Rival Lovers* (*Lovers*). A short dialogue about philosophy and the proper range of philosophical competence; generally judged pseudonymous.[^1][^6]
- **Date unknown; probably post-Platonic:** *Theages*. A dialogue about political ambition and Socrates’ divine sign; generally judged pseudonymous.[^1][^6]
- **Date unknown; probably post-Platonic:** *Minos*. A dialogue seeking a definition of law; transmitted in the Thrasyllan canon but generally rejected as Plato’s.[^1][^6]
- **After Plato’s death, probably fourth century BC:** *Epinomis*. A proposed continuation or supplement to *Laws* concerning astronomy and wisdom. Ancient tradition assigned it to Philip of Opus, although attribution has never been entirely unanimous.[^1][^4][^6]

### Letters: documentary forms with disputed or rejected authorship

- **Date and authorship disputed:** *Letter I*. A political letter addressed to Dionysius II; its Platonic authorship is generally rejected.[^1][^2]
- **Date and authorship disputed:** *Letter II*. A letter to Dionysius II mixing political counsel with deliberately guarded philosophical material; generally regarded as pseudonymous.[^1][^2]
- **Date and authorship disputed:** *Letter III*. A letter to Dionysius II defending the writer’s conduct in Sicily; generally regarded as pseudonymous.[^1][^2]
- **Date and authorship disputed:** *Letter IV*. A letter of encouragement to Dion; generally regarded as pseudonymous.[^1][^2]
- **Date and authorship disputed:** *Letter V*. A short advisory letter to Perdiccas III of Macedon; generally regarded as pseudonymous.[^1][^2]
- **Date and authorship disputed:** *Letter VI*. A letter commending cooperative philosophical friendship to Hermias, Erastus, and Coriscus; generally regarded as pseudonymous.[^1][^2]
- **After 354 BC if authentic; authorship intensely disputed:** *Letter VII*. An open letter to Dion’s associates combining political intervention, autobiography, and reflection on philosophical writing. It remains the strongest candidate for authenticity among the letters, but prominent scholars reject even this attribution.[^1][^2]
- **After 354 BC if authentic; authorship disputed:** *Letter VIII*. A political intervention addressed to Dion’s associates; sometimes defended alongside Letter VII, but widely treated as pseudonymous.[^1][^2]
- **Date and authorship disputed:** *Letter IX*. A brief letter to Archytas concerning public duty and private interests; generally regarded as pseudonymous.[^1][^2]
- **Date and authorship disputed:** *Letter X*. A very short letter to Aristodorus praising steadfast character; generally regarded as pseudonymous.[^1][^2]
- **Date and authorship disputed:** *Letter XI*. A letter to Laodamas concerning legislation for a colony; generally regarded as pseudonymous.[^1][^2]
- **Date and authorship disputed:** *Letter XII*. A brief letter to Archytas concerning exchanged writings; generally regarded as pseudonymous.[^1][^2]
- **Date and authorship disputed:** *Letter XIII*. A letter to Dionysius II combining personal, financial, and philosophical business; generally regarded as pseudonymous.[^1][^2]

### Ancient Platonic appendix: works already treated as spurious in antiquity

- **Date unknown; not Plato’s:** *Definitions*. A handbook-like sequence of concise philosophical definitions rather than a dialogue. It may preserve Academic material, but it resists classification as an individual Platonic work and is better understood as a school compilation.[^1][^6]
- **Date unknown; not Plato’s:** *On Justice*. A brief Socratic dialogue about justice, knowledge, and education, listed among the ancient spuria.[^1][^6]
- **Date unknown; not Plato’s:** *On Virtue*. A short dialogue asking whether virtue can be taught; it reworks material familiar from *Meno* and was already excluded from Plato’s genuine works in antiquity.[^1][^6]
- **Date unknown; not Plato’s:** *Demodocus*. A composite text concerning deliberation, testimony, and trust; its seams make it resist classification as a single unified dialogue.[^1][^6]
- **Date unknown; not Plato’s:** *Sisyphus*. A dialogue about deliberation and expertise, transmitted as part of the Platonic appendix.[^1][^6]
- **Date unknown; not Plato’s:** *Halcyon*. A miniature dialogue on metamorphosis and divine power; some manuscript traditions instead associate it with Lucian.[^1][^6]
- **Probably Hellenistic; not Plato’s:** *Eryxias*. A Socratic dialogue about wealth and its use, transmitted under Plato’s name but treated as spurious.[^1][^6]
- **Probably Hellenistic, possibly first century BC; not Plato’s:** *Axiochus*. A consolatory dialogue in which Socrates argues against fear of death; its philosophical mixture and later vocabulary place it outside Plato’s lifetime.[^1][^6]

### Epigrams and lost poetic projects

- **Dates unknown; attribution disputed or rejected:** *Epigrams I–XXXII*. A transmitted collection of short poems attributed to Plato rather than one securely authored obra. Cooper prints thirty-two numbered epigrams, but the collection’s contents and authorship are unstable, and it should not be silently converted into thirty-two certain works by Plato.[^1]
- **Allegedly before Plato’s philosophical career; no surviving text:** *Tragedies*. Later biographical tradition says Plato composed tragedies and burned them after encountering Socrates. The story is not securely historical, no titles or texts survive, and the supposed project therefore resists inclusion as an established work.[^4]
- **Allegedly in Plato’s youth; no securely identifiable surviving corpus:** *Dithyrambs, lyric poems, and hexameter poetry*. Ancient biography credits Plato with several kinds of verse, but the testimony is late and cannot securely connect these projects with the transmitted epigrams.[^4]

### Spoken philosophical manifestation

- **During Plato’s mature teaching career; exact date unknown:** *On the Good*. An attested public lecture, not a surviving text, reportedly concerned mathematics, number, geometry, astronomy, and the identification of the good with unity. Accounts derive from listeners and later testimony, so it is a performed philosophical manifestation rather than a recoverable written work.[^7]

### Institutional obra

- **Late 390s–380s BC; conventionally “founded” c. 387 or 383 BC:** *The Academy*. Plato created or consolidated a long-lived philosophical community associated with property near the Athenian Academy. It resists the model of a formally founded school: the evidence instead suggests that an informal circle gradually became an institution.[^7]
