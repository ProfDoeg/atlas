# Marco Polo: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier concerns Marco Polo, the Venetian merchant and traveler associated with the *Devisement dou monde*, commonly called *Il Milione* or *The Travels of Marco Polo*.

Evidence falls into three distinct classes:

1. **Independent documentary evidence:** Venetian legal and commercial records, Marco’s will, his postmortem property inventory, family litigation, and surviving manuscripts.
2. **Reported autobiographical evidence:** statements in the *Devisement dou monde*, a collaborative work transmitted through divergent manuscripts and shaped by Rustichello da Pisa.
3. **Later tradition:** stories recorded decades or centuries afterward, including claims from Giovanni Battista Ramusio, popular legends, nationalist claims, and modern fictional treatments.

The original dictated manuscript is lost. Consequently, exact wording and some episodes cannot be recovered with certainty. Assertions about Marco’s Asian career are labeled accordingly rather than treated as equivalent to Venetian archival facts.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name:** Marco Polo.

**Contemporary forms:** Marcus Paulo; Marco Paulo; Marcho Polo; Marc Pol; Marc Polle; Marc Polos; *Marc Pol, citoyen de Venise*, depending on language and manuscript.

**Titles and forms of address:**

- *Messer Marco Polo* or “Ser Marco Polo,” denoting a man of recognized standing.
- “Marco Polo the Venetian.”
- “Marco Milioni” or “Marco Polo Milion,” connected with the Polo family branch-name or byname *Emilione/Milione*. The popular explanation that “Milione” was bestowed because Marco constantly spoke of millions is a later and disputed story.
- “Marco Polo of the parish of San Giovanni Grisostomo,” in Venetian documentary identification.
- “Marco Polo of the Corte del Milion,” retrospectively associated with the Polo family’s Venetian property.

**Occupation:** Merchant; long-distance traveler; commercial investor and property holder; co-originator and principal experiential source of the *Devisement dou monde*. His frequently repeated classification as an “explorer” is modern and can obscure the fact that he traveled through functioning Eurasian commercial, diplomatic, and Mongol imperial networks.

**Reported occupation in Asia:** Envoy or trusted agent of Kublai Khan. The book describes assignments undertaken for the khan, but Marco’s precise formal rank is unknown.

**Birth:** Conventionally 1254.

**Birthplace:** Probably Venice. No contemporary birth certificate survives. Korčula, Constantinople, and other places have been proposed, but the Venetian identification is supported by the book’s prologue, Marco’s family’s established Venetian presence, and his later legal identity. The Korčula claim is treated separately below.

**Death:** 8 or 9 January 1324 by modern dating. His will was drawn up on 9 January according to the Venetian calendar, which began the year on 1 March and therefore recorded the year as 1323 *more veneto*.

**Place of death:** Venice, probably in the Polo family residence near San Giovanni Grisostomo.

**Burial:** Marco directed burial at the church of San Lorenzo in Venice, where his father Niccolò was reportedly buried. The grave no longer survives in an identifiable state.

**Religion:** Latin Christian. His will contains conventional Catholic invocations, charitable bequests, payments for masses, and gifts to religious institutions. His book is strongly interested in Christianity outside Latin Europe, especially the Church of the East, and in the possibility of Mongol conversion, while also describing Buddhism, Islam, Hindu traditions, and local religious practices.

**Language:** Marco’s native speech was Venetian. He likely acquired practical knowledge of one or more trade and administrative languages in Asia, plausibly Persian and some form of Mongolian or Turkic. Claims that he was fluent in four Asian languages derive from the book and cannot be independently specified. No evidence establishes fluency in written Chinese.

## Family and Ancestry

### The Polo commercial family

The Polos belonged to a Venetian merchant family rather than to the highest stratum of Venice’s old ruling nobility. Family branches were associated with San Geremia and San Giovanni Grisostomo. The branch to which Marco belonged used the byname *Milion* or *Emilione*.

Older biographies sometimes present a tidy genealogy, but the archival record contains several men with the same recurring names—Marco, Niccolò, Maffeo, and Stefano—and identification is not always secure.

### Parents

**Father:** Niccolò Polo, also Nicolò or Nicola Polo.

- **Documented/reported fact:** Venetian merchant and brother of Maffeo Polo.
- **Reported fact from the book:** Niccolò left Marco’s mother pregnant before departing eastward. On returning to Venice around 1269, he learned that she had died and met his approximately fifteen-year-old son.
- **Death:** Before Marco’s own death, probably by the early fourteenth century. An exact date is uncertain.
- **Burial:** Later tradition and Marco’s burial request associate Niccolò with San Lorenzo.

**Mother:** Name unknown.

- She reportedly died while Niccolò was absent.
- The frequent absence of her name is not evidence that Marco was illegitimate; medieval travel narratives and commercial records often omitted women unless relevant to a transaction.
- Claims assigning her a specific name are generally unsupported.

### Paternal uncle and principal collaborator

**Maffeo Polo**, also Matteo Polo.

- Brother and commercial partner of Niccolò.
- Participated in the first Polo journey east and the later journey with Marco.
- His will, dated 1309, and related family records are important evidence for the family’s property.
- Maffeo apparently had no legitimate surviving son and left significant property within the extended Polo family.

### Siblings and half-siblings

The standard biography gives Marco no securely documented full siblings. Some reconstructed genealogies suggest half-brothers or other close male relatives, but confusion among identically named Polos prevents certainty.

A newly studied 1319 testament made by an **Agnese, wife of Nicolò Calbo**, raises the possibility that she was a daughter of Marco born before his marriage to Donata Badoer. This remains a scholarly hypothesis, not an established family fact. It would make Agnese Marco’s daughter, not his sister.

### Marriage

**Wife:** Donata Badoer.

- Member of the Badoer family and daughter of Vitale Badoer.
- The marriage probably occurred around 1300, after Marco’s return from Genoese captivity.
- Donata first appears securely in surviving documents in 1312, when a dowry settlement transferred property in the parish of San Salvador to Marco.
- She survived Marco and served as an executor of his will.
- Her death appears to have occurred between approximately 1333 and 1336, though the exact date is unsettled.

### Children

Marco’s will identifies three legitimate daughters:

1. **Fantina Polo**
   - Married Marco Bragadin.
   - Became involved in prolonged litigation over property inherited from her father.
   - A successful claim in 1366 recovered assets or their value from administrators of her deceased husband’s estate.
   - Her litigation helped preserve a detailed list of Marco’s goods.

2. **Bellela Polo**, also Belella
   - Married Bertuccio Querini.
   - Apparently died in 1326.
   - Her early death complicated the division of the inheritance.

3. **Moreta Polo**
   - Married Ranuzzo Dolfin.
   - Participated with her mother and sister in family property disputes.

**Possible additional child:** Agnese, wife of Nicolò Calbo, has recently been proposed as a premarital daughter. The identification rests on circumstantial family and testamentary relationships and remains unresolved.

No legitimate son is documented. Marco therefore made his daughters principal heirs.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1254–1269

Marco’s childhood is almost entirely undocumented.

He was born around 1254 while his father participated in Mediterranean and Black Sea commerce. According to the book, Niccolò and Maffeo left Constantinople around 1260, traded in Soldaia—modern Sudak in Crimea—and traveled into the territories of the Golden Horde. Political conflict prevented an easy westward return, so they continued through Central Asia.

Marco’s mother died during Niccolò’s absence. Later claims that an aunt or uncle raised Marco are plausible reconstructions but are not securely documented.

His likely formation was practical and mercantile:

- Venetian dialect and commercial literacy;
- arithmetic, weights, currencies, and valuation;
- contracts and maritime practice;
- familiarity with commodities, ports, and political jurisdictions;
- Latin Christian instruction.

There is no evidence that he attended a university or received formal training in classical geography. His later book’s strongest recurring interests—currencies, customs dues, production, trade goods, distances, road security, urban provisioning, and political authority—accord with merchant formation.

## Niccolò and Maffeo Polo’s First Asian Journey, c. 1260–1269

This journey preceded Marco’s own travels but determined his future.

### Constantinople and Crimea

According to the prologue:

- Niccolò and Maffeo traded from Constantinople.
- They converted assets into portable jewels before traveling to Soldaia.
- They entered the dominions of Berke Khan of the Golden Horde.
- War between Berke and Hülegü or his Ilkhanid successors obstructed their return.

### Bukhara and the Mongol court

The brothers reportedly remained around three years in Bukhara. An envoy traveling to Kublai Khan’s court encouraged them to accompany him, partly because Kublai had not met Latin Christians.

At the court, Kublai allegedly questioned them about Europe, the papacy, Christianity, and political conditions. He sent them west with requests for:

- learned Christian teachers;
- information about Western religion and knowledge;
- oil from the lamp at the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem;
- letters or credentials for the pope.

The Mongols’ use of merchants and religious intermediaries as envoys is historically well documented, making the framework plausible. No surviving Yuan document names the brothers.

### Return to the West

The brothers reached Acre around 1269 and encountered papal legate Tebaldo Visconti. Because Pope Clement IV had died in 1268 and the papal election remained unresolved, they could not immediately fulfill Kublai’s request. They returned to Venice, where Niccolò met Marco.

---

## Education and Formation

No named teacher is documented. Marco’s identifiable formative influences were institutional and experiential:

- his father Niccolò and uncle Maffeo;
- Venetian mercantile culture;
- the Mongol imperial transport and passport system;
- Persian-speaking commercial networks;
- Kublai Khan’s multinational court;
- oral geographical and marvel traditions;
- Rustichello da Pisa’s Franco-Italian chivalric prose.

Marco’s book repeatedly evaluates societies through questions useful to merchants and administrators: What is produced? What circulates as money? How are roads guarded? What revenue does a ruler obtain? What commodities can be exchanged? How are posts, envoys, and armies supplied?

## Chronological Life History

## Departure from Venice and the eastern Mediterranean, 1271–1272

Niccolò, Maffeo, and the approximately seventeen-year-old Marco left Venice in 1271.

At Acre they again consulted Tebaldo Visconti. When he was elected Pope Gregory X, he supplied letters for Kublai and appointed two Dominican friars—Niccolò da Vicenza and Guglielmo da Tripoli—to accompany the Polos. The friars soon turned back, reportedly frightened by war in the region.

The Polos obtained oil from the Holy Sepulchre and continued through the Armenian and Anatolian commercial world. The most likely route included:

- Acre;
- Ayas or Laiazzo in Cilician Armenia;
- Anatolia, possibly through Sivas or Erzurum;
- Tabriz;
- Yazd;
- Kerman;
- Hormuz.

The exact route cannot be reconstructed at every stage because the book often describes regions thematically rather than as a continuous diary.

## Persia and the abandoned sea passage

At Hormuz the Polos considered traveling to China by sea. The book says they rejected the vessels as dangerously constructed, using cord rather than iron nails in ways the narrator considered insecure.

They then turned north and east by land. The narrative describes Persia’s cities, deserts, caravan dangers, precious stones, textiles, horses, and stories associated with the Magi.

## Afghanistan, Badakhshan, the Pamirs, and Central Asia, c. 1272–1274

The Polos probably moved through:

- northern Persia;
- Balkh and the Oxus region;
- Badakhshan;
- the Wakhan corridor;
- the Pamir plateau;
- Kashgar;
- Yarkand;
- Khotan;
- the fringes of the Taklamakan;
- Lop;
- the Gobi.

The identification of every medieval place-name is disputed. Some route reconstructions place the party farther north or south.

Marco’s description of high-altitude travel in the Pamirs—cold, scarcity of birds, difficulty maintaining fire, and physical effects of altitude—is frequently cited as experiential evidence. The “Marco Polo sheep,” *Ovis ammon polii*, was later named after him because his book described enormous wild sheep with great horns. He did not scientifically classify the animal.

The narrative’s desert voices and uncanny sounds belong to a widespread Central Asian travel tradition. They may preserve travelers’ interpretations of wind, shifting sand, disorientation, and caravan deaths, but the book presents them as dangerous supernatural phenomena.

## Arrival at Kublai Khan’s court, c. 1274–1275

The journey supposedly took approximately three and a half years. The Polos reached Kublai’s summer capital, Shangdu—Xanadu—or met him in its region around 1275.

**Reported fact:** Kublai received Marco favorably, valued his intelligence, and employed him on missions because he returned with observations about peoples and customs rather than only delivering official business.

**Independent context:** Kublai’s court routinely employed Central Asians, Persians, Turks, Christians, Muslims, and other non-Chinese personnel. Foreign merchants could serve as intermediaries, revenue specialists, or envoys.

**Unresolved:** Marco is not unambiguously named in surviving Yuan sources. A court figure called Boluo has sometimes been proposed as Marco, but phonetic and contextual objections make the identification uncertain.

## Life under Kublai Khan, c. 1275–1291

The book depicts Marco as spending approximately seventeen years in Kublai’s dominions. It attributes to him wide travel within or near:

- northern China;
- Cathay and Manzi;
- Shanxi and Shaanxi;
- Sichuan;
- Yunnan;
- Tibet’s borderlands;
- possibly Burma’s frontier;
- Yangzhou;
- Hangzhou;
- Fujian;
- ports of southern China.

Not every descriptive chapter records personal travel. Marco distinguishes imperfectly among what he saw, what he heard, information obtained from officials or merchants, and literary or oral traditions incorporated by Rustichello.

### Kublai Khan and the Yuan state

The book provides extensive descriptions of:

- Kublai’s palaces at Shangdu and Khanbaliq;
- seasonal migration between capitals;
- court ceremonies and banquets;
- hunting;
- imperial marriages and households;
- the postal relay system;
- official passports or tablets of authority;
- grain storage and famine relief;
- salt revenue;
- customs duties;
- paper currency;
- urban administration;
- religious pluralism.

Some of the strongest evidence for a genuine Yuan-era information base lies in the book’s precise monetary and fiscal details. Hans Ulrich Vogel’s comparative work argues that Polo gives accurate, unusual information about:

- manufacture and validation of paper money;
- denominations and regional currency zones;
- exchange of precious metals;
- use of cowries in Yunnan;
- salt production and taxation;
- variations not easily obtainable from a single Persian or European written source.

These details do not prove every autobiographical claim, but they weigh strongly against the hypothesis that the entire China account was invented in a Black Sea trading post.

### Alleged governorship of Yangzhou

**Traditional claim:** Marco governed Yangzhou for three years.

**Evidence:** Some manuscript versions say he exercised authority or resided there for three years; translation of the relevant administrative term is uncertain.

**Problems:** No Yuan appointment record clearly names him. The title “governor” may inflate a more limited fiscal, inspection, or special-assignment role.

**Assessment:** Possible service in or around Yangzhou is not independently proven. “Governor of Yangzhou” should not be stated as documentary fact.

### Missions and languages

The book says Marco was sent on missions and knew several languages and scripts. It does not specify them consistently. Persian was the most important transregional commercial language across much of Mongol Asia. Mongolian, Turkic languages, and lingua-franca forms are possible. His command of Chinese is unknown, and many Chinese names in the book came through Persian or Mongolian forms.

### Hangzhou

Marco’s celebrated description of Quinsai—Hangzhou—emphasizes its size, waterways, bridges, markets, manufactures, courtesans, baths, wealth, and tax revenue. Numerical claims are sometimes hyperbolic or corrupted in transmission, but significant structural details correspond to Chinese accounts of the former Southern Song capital.

### Religion and cultural observation

Marco classified religious communities through Latin Christian categories:

- Church of the East Christians are usually called “Nestorians.”
- Muslims are often described polemically as “Saracens.”
- Buddhist and other image-venerating traditions may be grouped as “idolaters.”
- Religious practices are sometimes filtered through marvel literature or imperfect translation.

He nevertheless recorded pluralism at Kublai’s court and presented the khan as treating Christianity, Islam, Judaism, and Buddhism pragmatically. The book’s suggestion that Kublai was close to Christian conversion reflects Christian missionary hope and may exaggerate his interest.

### The Mongol conquest and warfare

Marco reports campaigns, commanders, elephants, siege warfare, and political conflicts. Some accounts are valuable; others contain chronological or attributional problems.

The most famous is the siege of Xiangyang.

- **Book claim in some recensions:** The Polos suggested or helped construct powerful trebuchets that enabled the Mongols to take the city.
- **Documented chronology:** Xiangyang surrendered in 1273, probably before Marco reached China.
- **Chinese and Persian evidence:** Muslim engineers from the western Mongol world built counterweight trebuchets for the siege.
- **Possible explanation:** A story about western engineers was attached to the Polos, perhaps through family narration, scribal expansion, or confusion over chronology.
- **Assessment:** Marco’s personal participation is chronologically untenable. This is a demonstrable error or interpolation, though not proof that the entire journey was fraudulent.

## Negotiating departure

The book says Kublai repeatedly refused to let the Polos leave because he valued their service and feared losing useful agents.

An opportunity arose when envoys from the Ilkhan Arghun sought a Mongol princess to replace his deceased wife, Bulughan Khatun. Kublai selected **Kökejin**, called Cocachin or Kokachin in European versions.

Because overland routes were insecure, the Polos were attached to the maritime escort, reportedly because they possessed relevant navigational experience or because returning envoys recommended them.

---

## Maritime Return, c. 1291–1295

The expedition probably left a southern Chinese port in 1291 or 1292. The fleet’s route included:

- the South China Sea;
- Champa, in present-day Vietnam;
- islands and ports of the Malay world;
- Sumatra, where the party reportedly waited about five months for seasonal winds;
- the Nicobar and Andaman region;
- Sri Lanka or waters near it;
- the Indian coast;
- Hormuz.

The book describes heavy mortality. Different recensions give different starting and survivor numbers, often around 600 passengers with only eighteen surviving. Exact figures cannot be verified.

### Independent corroboration

Chinese and Persian sources do not name the Polos as escorts, but independent records corroborate the underlying diplomatic marriage mission:

- a Mongol princess of the appropriate lineage was sent from Kublai’s court;
- the mission departed around the period described;
- Persian sources record Kökejin’s arrival and marriage after Arghun’s death;
- the names or fates of envoys in Persian evidence correspond significantly with the book.

This is among the strongest external supports for the return narrative.

### South and Southeast Asian observations

The book provides major early European descriptions of:

- Sumatra’s kingdoms and products;
- sago-like foodstuffs;
- rhinoceroses, though described through the conceptual category of the unicorn;
- pearl fisheries;
- Indian Ocean monsoons;
- Hindu customs;
- ascetics;
- diamonds;
- spices;
- horses shipped to India;
- ports and merchant communities.

Some passages mix observation with inherited marvels, including dog-headed islanders and monstrous peoples. Others preserve valuable commercial information.

### Persia and final westward journey

By the time the party reached Persia, Arghun had died. Kökejin was ultimately married to Ghazan, his son, or otherwise incorporated into the Ilkhanid dynastic arrangement; textual formulations vary.

The Polos apparently received tablets or letters of safe conduct from Mongol rulers. They traveled through Tabriz and Trebizond, crossed the Black Sea, passed through Constantinople, and returned to Venice in 1295.

Kublai Khan died in 1294, before or near the time the Polos reached home.

---

## Return to Venice, 1295

Later tradition says relatives initially failed to recognize the travelers because of their foreign clothing, altered appearance, and long absence. At a banquet the Polos supposedly cut open worn garments and released jewels sewn into them, establishing their identity and wealth.

**Evidentiary label:** Later legend, chiefly associated with Ramusio’s sixteenth-century account. Portable jewels were a normal mercantile means of moving capital, so the story is plausible in general structure but not contemporaneously documented.

Marco returned to a Venice transformed by more than two decades of political and commercial change. Documentary evidence confirms that he became a prosperous, though not uniquely wealthy, merchant and property holder.

---

## War with Genoa, Capture, and Imprisonment, c. 1298–1299

Venice and Genoa fought a major war from 1294 to 1299. Marco became a Genoese prisoner by 1298.

### Place and circumstances of capture

Several versions compete:

- **Traditional version:** Marco commanded or served aboard a Venetian galley at the Battle of Curzola/Korčula on 7 September 1298 and was captured.
- **Alternative version:** He was captured in an earlier engagement near Laiazzo in 1296.
- **Problem:** Secure contemporary evidence does not establish him as a galley commander. The Korčula connection appears in later sources and remains plausible but not conclusively proved.

The title “captain” is therefore unsafe unless qualified as tradition.

### Genoese imprisonment

In Genoa, Marco encountered **Rustichello da Pisa**, a writer associated with French-language Arthurian and chivalric compilations.

The prologue of the *Devisement* identifies Rustichello as the writer who set down Marco’s account. Their respective contributions cannot be precisely divided:

- Marco supplied travel experience, commercial information, geographical description, stories, and possibly notes.
- Rustichello supplied literary form, prologue, organization, conventional rhetoric, and romance motifs.
- Some manuscript families contain later additions, omissions, reinterpretations, and Christianizing alterations.

The work was composed around 1298, probably in Franco-Italian or Franco-Venetian literary prose. “Dictated in prison” is broadly supported by the textual tradition, but it need not mean word-for-word stenographic dictation from memory. Notes, maps, earlier memoranda, other informants, and continuing revision are possible.

Marco was released after the Venetian-Genoese peace of 1299 and returned to Venice.

## Marriage, Commerce, and Property, c. 1300–1324

Marco married Donata Badoer around 1300. Their daughters were probably born in the opening years of the fourteenth century.

Surviving contracts show that Marco resumed ordinary Venetian mercantile and family life. He engaged in investments, loans, purchasing, property management, and kinship-based business.

### Financial standing

The legend of fabulous treasure should be distinguished from the records.

**Documented:**

- He possessed real property.
- His household contained valuable textiles, garments, jewelry, trade goods, silver, and Asian-associated objects.
- He made loans and maintained claims against debtors.
- He could make charitable bequests.
- His estate was substantial enough to generate litigation among heirs and sons-in-law.

**Not documented:**

- A modern net worth.
- Ownership of a vast international corporation.
- Continued direct management of an Asian trade network.
- Treasure on the scale suggested by popular retellings.

He was affluent by ordinary standards but was not among Venice’s supreme magnates.

### Copy given to Thibault de Cepoy, 1307

A note in the manuscript tradition records that Marco gave a copy of his book to **Thibault de Cepoy**, representative of Charles of Valois, in August 1307. This establishes that Marco remained involved with the text after its prison composition.

Whether the Cepoy copy incorporated authorial revision is debated. Its descendants constitute one branch of the manuscript tradition.

### Maffeo’s estate and family disputes

After Maffeo’s death, Marco participated in the administration or inheritance of family assets. Archival scholarship indicates increasing concentration of Polo property in Marco’s hands.

His will excluded some wider relatives in favor of Donata and his daughters. Later lawsuits show that family and marital alliances did not prevent conflict over inheritance.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

No modern “company” associated with Marco existed. His economic world operated through:

- household partnerships;
- family capital;
- temporary merchant ventures;
- maritime shipping arrangements;
- loans;
- property and dowry transfers;
- personal claims enforceable before Venetian courts.

### Known or probable commercial interests

- Precious fabrics and silk.
- Jewelry and gemstones.
- Spices, musk, rhubarb, aloe wood, and other high-value portable goods.
- Property in Venice.
- Loans or outstanding commercial credits.
- Family investment partnerships.

### Household inventory

The inventory made after Marco’s death, apparently by his son-in-law Marco Bragadin, listed fabrics, jewelry, stock goods, clothing, and household valuables. Items included:

- silk cocoons or spun silk;
- musk;
- rhubarb;
- aloe wood;
- cloth of gold;
- white and yellow textiles associated with Cathay;
- fabrics with unusual animals;
- Tartar-style blankets or garments;
- yak hair;
- jewelry with pearls and gemstones.

A gold object has been interpreted as a **paiza**, an official Mongol tablet of authority. This attractive identification is disputed; the damaged or ambiguous wording does not conclusively establish that Marco possessed such a tablet.

Another term once read as a Mongol aristocratic headdress, *bokhtakh*, may instead denote an ordinary Venetian jeweled dress ornament. Recent archival work warns that expectations about exotic souvenirs can produce overinterpretation.

### Technologies described, not invented

Marco is not documented as inventing or patenting anything. His book introduced many European readers to detailed accounts of:

- paper currency;
- coal as fuel;
- imperial postal relays;
- salt manufacture and tax administration;
- large-scale urban provisioning;
- Chinese and Mongol siege technologies;
- Indian Ocean navigation;
- porcelain-like ceramics;
- asbestos and fire-resistant mineral fiber.

Claims that he introduced pasta, ice cream, gunpowder, printing, or spectacles to Europe are unsupported.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Political position

Marco held no documented Venetian elected office. His political importance rests on his purported service to Kublai and his book’s presentation of Mongol imperial governance.

He depicts Kublai as:

- powerful and administratively capable;
- tolerant toward multiple religions;
- generous but commanding;
- supported by a communications and revenue system of extraordinary reach.

This portrayal is neither neutral ethnography nor straightforward propaganda. It reflects Marco’s interest in secure rule, trade, fiscal capacity, and magnificence, while Rustichello’s literary conventions cast Kublai as a romance sovereign.

### Christianity and the Mongols

The prologue organizes the Polo family’s journeys partly as an unfulfilled missionary opportunity. Kublai’s request for learned Christians and the pope’s inadequate response became a recurring European story about a lost chance to convert Asia.

Kublai’s actual religious policy was pluralist and politically pragmatic. There is no evidence that he was on the verge of becoming a Latin Christian.

### Islam

The book frequently reproduces medieval Christian polemic against Muslims. At other points it depends heavily on Persian and Muslim commercial knowledge. This contradiction reflects the difference between ideological vocabulary and practical Eurasian interaction.

### Buddhism and Asian religions

The work’s term “idolaters” collapses several distinct religious systems. Descriptions of reincarnation, monasteries, ritual practices, cremation, astrologers, and ascetics range from perceptive to confused.

### Intellectual significance

Marco did not construct a systematic philosophy. His importance lies in turning Eurasia into a describable network of routes, resources, jurisdictions, and markets. His world is connected through commerce and imperial infrastructure rather than divided into isolated civilizations.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Niccolò Polo

Father, teacher, business partner, and travel companion. The relationship is central but emotionally undocumented.

### Maffeo Polo

Uncle, commercial partner, and travel companion. His prior Mongol contacts made Marco’s journey possible.

### Kublai Khan

**Reported relationship:** Patron and sovereign who employed Marco.

**Documentary status:** Kublai’s court and foreign-service system are independently established; Marco’s personal relationship is known only from the Polo narrative.

### Rustichello da Pisa

Collaborator in producing the book. Rustichello’s influence complicates every attempt to isolate Marco’s unmediated voice.

### Kökejin

Mongol princess escorted toward Persia. The diplomatic mission is externally corroborated, but a personal friendship with Marco is not documented.

### Thibault de Cepoy

Recipient of a copy of the book in 1307; a documented point in its transmission.

### Donata Badoer

Wife, executor, and economic partner within Venetian marriage and dowry structures.

### Fantina, Bellela, and Moreta

Daughters and heirs. Their later lawsuits preserved evidence about Marco’s property.

### Marco Bragadin

Fantina’s husband and compiler of the inventory. Later found to have diverted or withheld property belonging to Fantina; the dispute was adjudicated after his death.

### Peter, the “Tartar” servant

Marco’s will ordered the liberation of a man named Peter, described as being of the Tartar people, provided he was not already free. Marco also forgave obligations and made a bequest to him.

**Documented fact:** Peter appears in the will.

**Unresolved:** Whether Peter accompanied Marco from Asia, when he entered Marco’s household, and whether his status would today be described as slavery, servitude, or bonded dependency. The will’s language demonstrates that Marco possessed legal power over his freedom.

## The Book: Composition, Titles, and Manuscript Problem

### Original title

The earliest recoverable title is usually rendered:

- *Le Devisement dou monde*;
- *Divisament dou monde*;
- “Description of the World.”

Other titles include:

- *Livre des merveilles du monde*;
- *Livres du Grant Caam*;
- *Il Milione*;
- *De consuetudinibus et conditionibus orientalium regionum* in Latin transmission;
- *The Travels of Marco Polo*.

### Meaning of *Milione*

Two explanations compete:

1. The name developed from the Polo branch-name *Emilione/Milione*.
2. Venetians mockingly associated Marco with the millions or enormous numbers in his stories.

The family-name explanation has stronger documentary and linguistic support. The boasting story contributed substantially to his later reputation as an exaggerator.

### Authorship

**Genuine collaborative authorship:** Marco Polo and Rustichello da Pisa.

The common description “written by Marco Polo” is convenient but incomplete. Marco did not leave an autograph manuscript. Rustichello’s literary labor was fundamental.

### Manuscripts

The original is lost. More than one hundred manuscripts and fragments survive in substantially divergent forms. Major textual families include:

- **F:** Franco-Italian text, Paris, Bibliothèque nationale de France, MS français 1116; generally considered the closest surviving witness to the earliest form.
- **FG or French redactions:** including the line associated with Thibault de Cepoy.
- **TA:** early Tuscan translation.
- **VA/VB:** Venetian versions.
- **P:** Latin translation by the Dominican Francesco Pipino.
- **Z:** Latin recension represented by the Toledo “Zelada” manuscript, containing significant material absent from F.
- **R:** Giovanni Battista Ramusio’s sixteenth-century Italian edition, which preserves otherwise lost material but also reflects editorial intervention.

No single surviving manuscript is “the original Marco Polo.”

### Editorial consequences

Popular translations often combine readings from incompatible manuscript branches. Such editions can produce a fuller narrative but create a text that never existed in any medieval codex.

Francesco Pipino Christianized and moralized aspects of the work. Ramusio regularized and expanded the prose. Modern editors including Yule, Benedetto, Moule, Pelliot, Latham, and Kinoshita made different choices about reconstruction versus fidelity to one witness.

## Controversies, Accusations, Errors, and Unresolved Questions

## Did Marco Polo go to China?

### The skeptical case

Most fully developed by Frances Wood in *Did Marco Polo Go to China?* (1995), though doubts date to the nineteenth century.

Arguments include:

- no explicit Marco Polo in surviving Chinese sources;
- no discussion of tea drinking;
- no description of footbinding;
- no clear description of Chinese writing;
- no mention of chopsticks;
- silence about the Great Wall;
- errors concerning Japan and Chinese campaigns;
- the impossible Xiangyang siege claim;
- apparent dependence on Persian place-names;
- the possibility that information was gathered from merchants around the Black Sea or Persia.

Wood suggested that Marco may not have gone much beyond family trading stations in Constantinople or the Black Sea region.

### The case for substantial authenticity

Igor de Rachewiltz, Stephen Haw, Hans Ulrich Vogel, David Morgan, and other specialists have answered that:

- omission is not proof of absence;
- the Great Wall familiar today is substantially a Ming construction, while Yuan-period northern walls were neglected and politically irrelevant;
- footbinding was not universal and Marco’s descriptions of women’s restricted steps may indirectly reflect it;
- Persian was a normal lingua franca of Mongol administration and trade;
- foreign minor officials often disappear from Yuan records;
- Chinese sources do not preserve complete personnel lists;
- the book contains precise and regionally differentiated information about currencies, salt, fiscal practice, roads, cities, and commodities;
- the maritime princess mission corresponds with independent Chinese and Persian evidence;
- many details would have been exceptionally difficult to assemble from then-available European texts.

### Assessment

**Scholarly balance:** Marco’s presence in Yuan China is highly probable, though not confirmed by a surviving Chinese document naming him beyond dispute. The book is neither a modern travel diary nor a reliable record in every chapter. It contains first-hand observation, second-hand intelligence, literary conventions, chronological errors, and later textual changes.

“Marco went to China” and “everything in the book is true” are separate propositions.

## Absence from Chinese records

This remains a genuine evidentiary gap.

The proposed identification with a Yuan official named **Boluo** is speculative. *Boluo* was not rare, and no decisive biographical match has been demonstrated.

Conversely, expecting a recognizable transcription of “Marco Polo” may be anachronistic. Marco could have used a Mongolian, Persian, or administrative name, and many court personnel were omitted from surviving chronicles.

**Status:** Unresolved question, not disproof.

## Xiangyang trebuchets

Marco could not personally have directed the decisive siege engineering if he arrived around 1275 because Xiangyang surrendered in 1273.

**Status:** Documented chronological contradiction.

Possible explanations include:

- a claim originally made for Niccolò and Maffeo;
- appropriation of deeds performed by Muslim engineers;
- a story added by Rustichello;
- textual interpolation;
- Marco’s own exaggeration.

## Yangzhou governorship

The full governorship claim lacks independent proof and may arise from an expansive translation of administrative service.

**Status:** Reported claim, disputed.

## Japan invasions

The book’s treatment of Kublai’s invasions of Japan appears to combine events from 1274 and 1281 and includes legendary elements.

**Status:** Mixed historical report and confused transmission.

## Exaggerated numbers

Numbers of bridges, households, troops, revenues, cities, and sexual partners are frequently enormous. Causes may include:

- scribal corruption;
- differing meanings of administrative units;
- rhetorical magnificence;
- oral memory;
- Rustichello’s romance style;
- Marco’s own exaggeration.

The numerical problem is real but does not uniformly invalidate qualitative descriptions.

## Ethnographic bias and hostile reporting

Marco’s account contains:

- religious polemic;
- broad categories such as “idolaters” and “Saracens”;
- claims about sexual hospitality;
- cannibalism;
- sorcery;
- monstrous peoples;
- judgments about beauty, cleanliness, courage, and political obedience.

Some passages may describe real local customs imperfectly. Others derive from travelers’ tales or established literary motifs. They should not be treated as transparent ethnography.

## Slavery

Marco lived within and benefited from societies in which slavery and servitude were normal. His will’s treatment of Peter is direct documentary evidence that a “Tartar” man’s freedom depended on Marco’s legal act.

The book describes slave markets, captives, concubines, and forced movement without consistently questioning the institutions.

**Status:** Documented historical context and documented household relationship.

## Inheritance litigation

After Marco’s death, his widow and daughters became involved in disputes over the estate. Fantina’s husband, Marco Bragadin, inventoried goods but later misappropriated or failed to deliver her share. Fantina pursued recovery against the administrators of his estate and prevailed in 1366.

**Status:** Documented civil property conflict; not evidence that Marco himself committed fraud.

## Dedicated adversarial search: 2024–2026

Searches pairing the historical Marco Polo with *controversy*, *scandal*, *allegations*, *lawsuit*, *fraud*, *accusations*, *conspiracy*, and *criticism*, including 2024–2026, produced no new criminal or financial allegation concerning the medieval individual.

Results bearing his name commonly concerned unrelated modern businesses, ships, persons, the Marco Polo argali, or fictional adaptations. These were excluded.

Relevant recent scholarship instead continued controversies over:

- Yangzhou service;
- identification with Boluo;
- Xiangyang;
- anachronistic expectations about the Great Wall and Chinese institutions;
- manuscript instability;
- overinterpretation of allegedly Asian objects in Marco’s inventory.

Stephen Haw’s 2024 study argues that several skeptical tests impose later categories on Yuan China while reopening the possibility of some form of Yangzhou responsibility. The 2024 Tübingen collection edited by Hans Ulrich Vogel and Ulrich Theobald presents competing analyses rather than resolving every question.

No evidence was found of lawsuits, formal charges, convictions, corruption investigations, money laundering, or intelligence activity involving Marco in the modern senses of those terms.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## “I have not told half of what I saw”

**Story:** On his deathbed, friends or a priest urged Marco to retract his incredible stories. He replied that he had not told half of what he had seen.

**Appearance:** Recorded in later tradition, not in Marco’s will or a contemporary deathbed account.

**Evidence:** No contemporary witness statement survives.

**Reputation:** The story serves both hagiographic and skeptical readings: for admirers it proves that Asia exceeded European imagination; for critics it dramatizes refusal to confess exaggeration.

**Label:** Later legend.

## Jewels sewn into clothing

**Story:** The returning Polos invited relatives to a feast, cut open shabby Tartar garments, and revealed gemstones.

**Source:** Most famously Ramusio, writing more than two centuries later.

**Evidence for:** Portable jewels were plausible merchant capital.

**Evidence against:** No contemporary record of the banquet.

**Effect:** Established the image of the unrecognized traveler returning with fabulous Eastern wealth.

**Label:** Plausible later legend.

## “Marco Millions”

**Story:** Venetians called him *Marco Milioni* because he constantly spoke in millions.

**Evidence:** The family branch-name *Milion/Emilione* predates or independently explains the usage.

**Effect:** Helped turn his surname and book title into evidence of boastfulness.

**Label:** Popular etymological legend built on a real byname.

## Marco Polo brought pasta from China

**Story:** Marco introduced noodles or pasta to Italy in 1295.

**Circulation:** Widely disseminated in twentieth-century popular food history and commercial promotion.

**Evidence against:** Pasta and related preparations are documented in Italy before Marco’s return. The book does not say he introduced pasta.

**Effect:** Became one of the most durable myths of culinary globalization.

**Label:** Disproved modern myth.

## Ice cream, gunpowder, printing, and other inventions

Marco is variously credited with bringing ice cream, gunpowder, coal technology, paper money, printing, spectacles, and kites to Europe.

He described several technologies but did not document their introduction, invent them, or secure patents.

**Label:** Unsupported popular mythology.

## Chinese wife Hao Dong

**Story:** Marco married Hao Dong, sometimes described as Kublai’s daughter. She followed him to Venice and killed herself after being falsely told that he had died in Genoese captivity.

**Appearance and circulation:** Modern Venetian and Italian popular tradition; repeated in tourism and romantic retellings.

**Evidence against:** No contemporary Chinese, Persian, Venetian, testamentary, or property record identifies her. Marco’s documented wife was Donata Badoer. Kublai’s daughters and dynastic marriages were matters of political importance unlikely to vanish without trace.

**Effect:** Supplies a romantic East-West tragedy absent from the historical record.

**Label:** Legend without documentary support.

## Birth in Korčula

**Story:** Marco was born in Korčula, now Croatia, or belonged to a Dalmatian De Polo family.

**Development:** Claims draw on later genealogies, the presence of Polo/Depolo surnames, the island’s Venetian history, and the tradition that Marco was captured near Korčula in 1298. Croatian cultural and tourism institutions expanded the claim in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries.

**Evidence for:** Possible wider Adriatic family connections; later documentary assertions of Dalmatian origins; capture traditionally associated with Korčula.

**Evidence against:** No thirteenth-century record names Korčula as his birthplace. The family was already established in Venice, and Marco’s book and legal identity consistently present him as Venetian.

**Reputation:** Marco has become part of competing Italian, Venetian, Croatian, Dalmatian, and trans-Adriatic heritage claims. Academic analysis describes the Korčula birthplace as an “invented tradition,” though Korčulan opinion is not uniform.

**Label:** Disputed heritage claim; Venice remains the best-supported birthplace.

## Marco as the first European in China

Europeans reached Mongol territories and China before Marco, including papal envoys and merchants. Giovanni da Pian del Carpine and William of Rubruck preceded him, as probably did members of other merchant families.

Marco’s distinction lies in the scale and circulation of his geographical account, not demonstrable priority.

**Label:** False popular simplification.

## Marco as a lone explorer

He traveled with his father, uncle, servants, merchants, envoys, guides, crews, and Mongol-protected caravans. The image of a solitary European discovering an unknown Asia is a modern heroic construction.

**Label:** Modern myth.

## The swimming-pool game

The blindfolded calling game “Marco Polo” has no demonstrated historical connection to him. Proposed explanations—that players are “exploring,” that Marco was lost, or that he called to companions—are retrospective.

**Label:** Unresolved naming folklore.

## Works by Marco Polo

Marco Polo’s authorial corpus consists essentially of one collaborative work and a small documentary residue.

### 1. *Le Devisement dou monde*

**Original composition:** c. 1298–1299.

**Language:** Franco-Italian or Franco-Venetian literary French.

**Authorship:** Marco Polo as narrator, informant, and experiential authority; Rustichello da Pisa as writer, compiler, and literary collaborator.

**Original manuscript:** Lost.

**Alternative titles:**

- *Divisament dou monde*
- *Le Livre de Marco Polo*
- *Livres des merveilles du monde*
- *Livres du Grant Caam*
- *Il Milione*
- *De consuetudinibus et conditionibus orientalium regionum*
- *The Description of the World*
- *The Travels of Marco Polo*

### Major medieval versions

- **F manuscript:** BnF français 1116, early fourteenth century; closest surviving Franco-Italian witness.
- **Early Tuscan *Milione*:** c. 1300s; highly influential in Italian prose history.
- **Francesco Pipino, Latin translation:** c. 1310s; genuine medieval translation but altered, moralized, and Christianized.
- **Z recension:** Latin; preserves material absent from F and may reflect later authorial information or an independent branch.
- **French *Livre des merveilles* tradition:** richly illuminated courtly manuscripts, including BnF français 2810.
- **Ramusio version:** published 1559 in volume II of *Navigationi et viaggi*; posthumous edited reconstruction using sources no longer fully extant.

### Major scholarly and modern editions

- Société de Géographie, *Recueil de voyages et de mémoires*, 1824: publication of the Franco-Italian text.
- G. B. Baldelli Boni, *Il Milione di Marco Polo*, 1827.
- Henry Yule, *The Book of Ser Marco Polo the Venetian*, 1871; revised by Henri Cordier, 1903.
- Luigi Foscolo Benedetto, *Marco Polo: Il Milione*, 1928.
- Aldo Ricci, *The Travels of Marco Polo*, 1931, based on Benedetto.
- A. C. Moule and Paul Pelliot, *Marco Polo: The Description of the World*, 1938.
- Ronald Latham, *The Travels*, 1958: readable composite English text.
- Gabriella Ronchi, *Milione: Le divisament dou monde*, 1982.
- Alvaro Barbieri, *Milione: redazione latina del manoscritto Z*, 1998.
- Philippe Ménard, *Le Devisement du monde*, multivolume critical work.
- Sharon Kinoshita, *The Description of the World*, 2016: English translation centered on manuscript F rather than a synthetic “complete” text.
- Eugenio Burgio, Mario Eusebi, and associated Venice/Padua philological projects: digital and critical study of the textual tradition.

### 2. Testament

**Date:** 9 January 1324, recorded as 1323 *more veneto*.

**Authorship:** Dictated legal instrument, not literary autograph.

**Contents:** Executors; wife and daughters as heirs; religious gifts; debts and credits; liberation and bequest for Peter the Tartar; burial request.

### 3. Documentary acts and statements

Marco appears in commercial contracts, property instruments, dowry records, and family transactions. These are not “works” in the literary sense and were drafted by notaries.

### 4. The 1307 Cepoy presentation note

The note concerning Marco’s gift of a copy to Thibault de Cepoy is associated with the manuscript tradition. It is evidence of dissemination and possible revision, not a separate book.

### Works falsely attributed or overstated

- No authenticated diary from the journey survives.
- No autograph map securely attributable to Marco survives.
- No letters written by him from China are known.
- No patents, technical treatises, or separate memoirs survive.
- Later illustrated “Books of Marvels” are manuscript presentations of the travel text, not artworks by Marco.
- The *Secret History of the Mongols* and other Asian chronicles are unrelated works sometimes confused in popular treatments.

## Death and Estate

By January 1324 Marco was seriously ill. The disease is not identified. Statements that a particular infection or chronic illness killed him are speculative.

His will was prepared by the priest-notary Giovanni Giustiniani. Marco described himself in standard testamentary language as ill in body but sound in mind.

Principal provisions included:

- Donata and the three daughters as executors or principal beneficiaries;
- payment of debts;
- recovery or disposition of sums owed to him;
- gifts to churches, monasteries, guilds, and charitable causes;
- money for masses;
- liberation of Peter the Tartar and cancellation of obligations;
- burial at San Lorenzo.

Marco probably died later on 8 January or during 9 January 1324. Differences result from medieval day-reckoning, sunset conventions, and the act’s dating.

### Burial and lost tomb

Marco was buried at San Lorenzo. The church underwent repeated reconstruction, and the grave’s location was lost. Stories about the destruction, transfer, or rediscovery of his remains remain unverified.

### Postmortem property conflict

Marco’s daughters inherited the estate, but settlement took years. Bellela’s death and conflicts involving sons-in-law complicated distribution. The litigation culminating in Fantina’s 1366 recovery demonstrates both the estate’s value and the vulnerability of married women’s inherited property.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Medieval reception

The book circulated rapidly in French, Italian, Latin, German, and other languages. It was read by:

- merchants;
- friars and missionaries;
- cartographers;
- rulers and court audiences;
- compilers of geographical and marvel literature.

Readers did not uniformly treat it as either factual geography or fantasy. Medieval categories allowed marvels, commercial intelligence, missionary information, and political description to coexist.

### Cartography

Polo’s place-names and descriptions entered late medieval mapping. His influence is associated with:

- the Catalan Atlas;
- Fra Mauro’s world map;
- later printed cosmographies;
- European mapping of Cathay, Cipangu, Java, and the Indian Ocean.

The claim that Marco personally brought back a specific map later used by Fra Mauro rests on late testimony and is disputed.

### Columbus

Christopher Columbus owned an annotated printed copy of Francesco Pipino’s Latin recension. More than 800 marginal annotations are associated with the volume, though not all are necessarily by Columbus and many appear to postdate his first voyage.

Columbus used Polo’s geography of Cathay, Cipangu, gold, spices, and the Great Khan while interpreting Atlantic discoveries. The influence is documented, but the simplistic claim that Polo’s book alone caused the 1492 voyage overstates a larger geographical and commercial context.

### Reputation for truth and falsehood

Marco became both:

- the exemplary witness to Asian wealth and Mongol power;
- “Marco Millions,” archetype of the extravagant traveler.

Modern authenticity debate intensified because his book occupies several genres simultaneously. Its factual errors are genuine; so are its unusually accurate fiscal and geographical observations.

### Colonial and Eurocentric appropriations

Later European narratives cast Marco as a solitary Western “discoverer” of Asia. This framing marginalizes:

- Asian hosts, guides, officials, merchants, and sailors;
- existing Eurasian trade;
- earlier Franciscan envoys;
- Muslim, Armenian, Jewish, Persian, Turkic, and Mongol intermediaries;
- east-to-west travelers such as Rabban Bar Sauma.

Conversely, modern official commemorations sometimes turn him into an uncomplicated symbol of intercultural dialogue. That diplomatic image also selects from a text containing religious polemic, hierarchy, slavery, warfare, and marvel traditions.

### Modern commemoration

His name has been applied to:

- Venice Marco Polo Airport;
- ships, hotels, geographic features, commercial products, and expeditions;
- the Marco Polo sheep;
- European Union educational and cultural programs;
- an enormous body of fiction, television, games, and tourism;
- sites in Venice and Korčula.

The 700th anniversary of his death in 2024 produced major exhibitions and conferences in Venice, China, and elsewhere. These events renewed attention to the documentary Marco—the merchant, testator, husband, father, and property holder—alongside the iconic traveler.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the sources are included. Mere contemporaneity is excluded.

### Kublai Khan

**Nature:** Reported patron-sovereign relationship. Marco’s book says he met Kublai around 1275, served him on missions, and remained within his dominions until the maritime embassy of c. 1291–1292.

**Dates and places:** c. 1275–1291; Shangdu, Khanbaliq, and other Yuan territories.

**Evidence label:** Reported fact grounded in Marco’s narrative and compatible with Yuan institutional context; no independent document naming Marco at Kublai’s court has been identified.

### Genghis Khan

**Nature:** Historical and narrative influence, not a personal meeting. Genghis, Kublai’s grandfather, died in 1227, before Marco’s birth. Marco’s book recounts his rise, wars, laws, successors, and conflict with the ruler identified with Prester John.

**Evidence label:** Documented textual connection; no lifetime contact possible.

### Giovanni da Pian del Carpine

**Nature:** Predecessor in Latin-European travel to the Mongol world. Giovanni’s papal mission of 1245–1247 preceded Marco and helped establish a European textual tradition about the Mongols.

**Evidence label:** Documented historical and comparative connection; no evidence Marco personally met him or explicitly cited his book.

### Odoric of Pordenone

**Nature:** Later Franciscan traveler in Asia whose fourteenth-century account overlaps geographically and textually with regions described by Marco. Odoric traveled after Marco’s return and may have encountered a world already interpreted in Europe through Polo’s book.

**Evidence label:** Documented shared travel-literature tradition; no documented meeting.

### Rabban Bar Sauma

**Nature:** Near-contemporary east-to-west traveler and Mongol diplomat. His route reversed the general direction associated with Marco and shows the same Mongol diplomatic system connecting China, Persia, and Europe.

**Dates:** Bar Sauma’s western embassy occurred mainly in 1287–1288 while Marco was reportedly in Yuan territory.

**Evidence label:** Documented parallel network; no documented meeting.

### Abu Abdallah Muhammad ibn Battuta

**Nature:** Later comparative traveler. Ibn Battuta began his travels in 1325, after Marco’s death, and dictated a separate travel account. Modern scholarship frequently compares their methods, routes, and author-scribe collaborations.

**Evidence label:** Documented comparative and historiographical connection; no personal contact or proven direct influence.

### Christopher Columbus

**Nature:** Documented reader and later user of the Polo textual tradition. Columbus owned and annotated a Latin printed version and employed Polo’s Asian geography when interpreting his voyages.

**Dates and places:** Printed volume acquired or annotated in the late fifteenth century; preserved in Seville.

**Evidence label:** Documented posthumous influence. Qualification: many annotations probably postdate Columbus’s first voyage, and not every mark is securely autograph.

### Alexander the Great

**Nature:** Repeated figure in the *Devisement*’s geography and legends. Marco’s route descriptions use places, boundaries, and stories associated with Alexander, including Central Asian and Near Eastern traditions.

**Evidence label:** Documented textual and legendary influence; no personal connection.

### Aristotle

**Nature:** Indirect intellectual background through medieval traditions concerning Alexander and world geography. No source consulted establishes Marco’s direct reading of Aristotle.

**Evidence label:** No secure direct Atlas connection; excluded beyond this negative finding.

### Prester John

Prester John is not separately named in the roster, but the legend intersects with **Genghis Khan**: Marco identifies the historical Ong Khan/Toghrul of the Keraites with the Western Christian monarch Prester John.

**Evidence label:** Documented textual identification; historically inaccurate legendary synthesis.

### Absence finding

No documentary connection was found between Marco Polo and the overwhelming majority of the atlas roster. In particular, no personal meeting with Ibn Battuta, Odoric, Rabban Bar Sauma, Giovanni da Pian del Carpine, or Christopher Columbus was possible or documented. Their connections are limited to predecessor, successor, parallel network, comparison, or posthumous influence.
