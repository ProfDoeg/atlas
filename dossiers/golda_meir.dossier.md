# Golda Meir: Research Dossier

**Prepared:** 24 August 2026  
**Subject:** Golda Meir (1898–1978)  
**Scope:** Chronological life history; family, institutions, policy, networks, writings, controversies, myths, health, death, and posthumous reputation.  

## Research and evidence note

This dossier distinguishes documented events from retrospective testimony, allegation, interpretation, and legend. For the most consequential disputes—Meir's contacts with King Abdullah, missed peace opportunities before the 1973 war, responsibility for the intelligence surprise, the reported nuclear alert, covert retaliation after Munich, and disputed quotations—the competing accounts are presented rather than collapsed into a verdict. Official records are indispensable but not treated as neutral: the Agranat Commission had a defined and contested mandate; memoirs are retrospective and self-protective; state archives reflect what governments recorded and preserved; and partisan institutes are used only where their claims can be identified and, where possible, checked against underlying records.

No substantiated evidence was found that Meir personally operated a private business empire, accumulated unexplained wealth, laundered money, was criminally charged, convicted, or formally found corrupt. That negative finding does not erase state violence, covert action, discriminatory policies, property disputes, or political responsibility discussed below.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and household

- **Father:** Moshe Yitzhak Mabovitch (c. 1873–1944), a carpenter/cabinetmaker. Economic failure and insecurity marked the family's years in the Russian Empire. He left in 1903 for the United States, worked first in New York and then in Milwaukee railroad shops, and brought his wife and surviving daughters over in 1906.
- **Mother:** Blume Neiditch Mabovitch (d. 1951). In Milwaukee she ran a small grocery store; Golda frequently minded it as a child. Blume favored an early, conventional marriage for Golda and opposed prolonged schooling, producing the adolescent break that sent Golda to Denver.
- **Siblings:** Eight children were reportedly born to Moshe and Blume; five died in infancy or early childhood. The three surviving daughters were **Sheyna/Shana Korngold** (1889–1972), **Golda**, and **Tzipke/Czipke**, later **Clara** (1902–1981). Newly publicized Kyiv metrical material also identifies records associated with siblings Aaron-Zelik and Tsipa, illustrating how names and infant deaths complicate simple family lists. Golda's own recollection of “three of us children” means the surviving household, not necessarily all live births; see the [Jewish Museum Milwaukee oral-history presentation](https://virtual.jewishmuseummilwaukee.org/golda-meir/).
- The family was Ashkenazi Jewish, Yiddish-speaking, poor, and exposed to the legal disabilities and antisemitic violence of late-imperial Russia. Meir later made her father's boarding of the door against a rumored pogrom her earliest defining memory. The event is plausible and consistent with the period, but the precise incident is autobiographical memory, not independently documented violence at that door.

### Marriage, partners, and children

- **Husband:** Morris Meyerson/Myerson (17 December 1893–25 May 1951), Chicago-born sign painter, socialist, reader, and amateur musician. They met in Denver around 1913 and married at the Mabovitch home in Milwaukee on 24 December 1917. Golda made migration to Palestine a condition of marriage.
- The marriage remained legally intact until Morris's death but broke down emotionally and domestically. He disliked collective kibbutz life and the sacrifices demanded by Golda's political vocation. They separated in practice for long periods, especially after her rise in labor politics. There is no record of divorce or remarriage.
- **Children:** **Menachem Meyerson/Meir** (1924–2014), a cellist who studied at the Israel Conservatory and Manhattan School of Music, later wrote *My Mother Golda Meir* (1983); and **Sarah Meyerson/Revivim** (17 May 1926–30 January 2010), who lived at Kibbutz Revivim. Sarah's serious kidney illness partly occasioned Golda's 1932–34 American stay.
- Biographies differ in how strongly they describe extramarital emotional or sexual relationships. Pnina Lahav and other biographers discuss intense attachments within the labor movement; older authorized accounts are reticent. No partner replaced Morris in public or legal life. Claims should therefore be described as biographical interpretation based on letters and testimony, not a documented second household.
- Her public image as national mother coexisted with criticism from family members and biographers that politics took precedence over domestic parenting. Menachem's memoir is affectionate but records the cost. “Mother of a nation, not much of a mother” is a retrospective journalistic formulation, not an established clinical judgment.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1898–1912

Golda was born in Kyiv, spent part of early childhood in **Pinsk** (now Belarus), and lived amid poverty, hunger, infant mortality, and antisemitic fear. Moshe's departure for America split the family for about three years. In 1906 Blume and the daughters traveled via Quebec and train to **Milwaukee, Wisconsin**.

The family settled on Milwaukee's north side. Golda attended **Fourth Street School** from 1906 to 1912, worked in or watched her mother's store, and used the public library. In 1908 she and Regina Hamburger organized the **American Young Sisters Society** to raise money for classmates' textbooks, rented a hall, and called a public meeting. It became a favorite origin story of her political talent. Local archival material confirms her schooling and library employment, although later retellings polish the event into a miniature political campaign; see [Milwaukee Public Library](https://mpl.org/blog/now/goldie-mabowehz-golda-meir-from-the-milwaukee-public-library-to-prime-minister-of-israel) and the [American Jewish Archives](https://www.americanjewisharchives.org/snapshots/golda-meir-in-america/).

She graduated near or at the head of her class. In 1912 she entered **North Division High School** and worked part-time, including at Schuster's department store and the library. Conflict with Blume over leaving school for marriage precipitated her flight west.

## Education and Formation, 1912–1921

### Denver interlude

In 1913, aged about fifteen, she traveled without parental permission to **Denver**, joining Sheyna and brother-in-law Shamai/Sam Korngold. She enrolled at North High School and worked in the family laundry. The Korngold home at West Colfax hosted discussions among immigrants, convalescents, socialists, Zionists, suffragists, and readers. Meir later said these “talk-filled nights” gave form to her convictions. The restored [Golda Meir House history](https://aurariacampus.edu/auraria/golda-meir-house/golda-meir-house-history/) documents this setting.

There she met Morris Meyerson. She also encountered ideological arguments separating philanthropic or diplomatic Zionism from **Labor Zionism**, which sought Jewish national reconstruction through Hebrew labor, collective settlement, union institutions, and socialist nation-building.

### Milwaukee return and teaching

After reconciliation with her parents, Golda returned to Milwaukee in 1914, completed North Division High School in 1915, joined **Young Poale Zion**, and became a street-corner speaker and fundraiser. She attended the **Milwaukee State Normal School** (predecessor of the University of Wisconsin–Milwaukee) in 1916–17 but did not complete a conventional four-year degree. She taught at a Yiddish-speaking *Folks Schule* and worked at Milwaukee Public Library; a library personnel record shows her final resignation on 13 May 1918.

Her mentors were less individual professors than a movement network: Sheyna and the Korngold salon; Poale Zion emissaries; socialist writers; Zionist organizers; and the wider intellectual lineage of **Theodor Herzl**. Herzl died when Golda was six, so there was no personal relationship. His political Zionism supplied the sovereign-state horizon; Golda's decisive formation came through the socialist, practical, settlement-oriented branch of Zionism. The [National Library of Israel's Zionism history](https://www.nli.org.il/en/discover/israel/zionism/zionism-history) situates that distinction.

Marriage on 24 December 1917 did not alter her stipulated goal of *aliyah*. World War I shipping conditions delayed departure. She toured the United States for Poale Zion, converting American fluency and fundraising talent into her first sustained political profession.

## Chronological Life History

### 1921–1924: Migration and Kibbutz Merhavia

In 1921 Golda and Morris traveled with Sheyna's family and Golda's friend Regina on the **SS *Pocahontas*** to Europe, then onward through Naples and by train/ship routes to Mandatory Palestine. Their group was initially rejected by **Kibbutz Merhavia** in the Jezreel Valley, reportedly because members doubted the Americans' endurance. Accepted later, Golda picked almonds, planted trees, worked with poultry, and reorganized the kitchen. She represented Merhavia at the Histadrut and impressed movement leaders.

The kibbutz years also exposed the marriage's incompatibility. Morris disliked deprivation, communal regimentation, malaria risk, and lack of private life. In 1924 they left for Tel Aviv and then Jerusalem. Golda regarded departure from the kibbutz as a defeat; Morris regarded it as necessary.

### 1924–1928: Jerusalem, children, economic strain

Menachem was born in 1924 and Sarah in 1926. Morris worked intermittently; Golda taught and took organizational work. Domestic poverty and her political absences intensified marital strain. She briefly returned to Merhavia in 1925 but the family did not reconstitute permanent kibbutz life.

### 1928–1934: Women workers' leadership and American mission

In 1928 Meir became secretary of **Mo'etzet Ha-Po'alot**, the Histadrut's Working Women's Council. The position combined welfare, training, childcare, agricultural placement, and political organization. It placed her inside the labor establishment while also exposing the contradiction between formal socialist equality and male control of senior institutions.

From 1932 to 1934 she served in the United States as an emissary for Pioneer Women/Mo'etzet Ha-Po'alot and obtained treatment for Sarah's kidney illness. Morris remained in Jerusalem for much of this period. Her American English, familiar cultural manner, and capacity to translate socialist-Zionist projects into donor language became durable political assets.

### 1934–1945: Histadrut and Jewish Agency ascent

On return, she joined the Histadrut executive and eventually led its political department. She worked alongside **David Remez**, **Berl Katznelson**, **David Ben-Gurion**, **Moshe Sharett**, **Levi Eshkol**, and other Mapai leaders. She entered the executive of the Jewish Agency and participated in the diplomacy and institution-building of the Yishuv.

At the **Évian Conference** in July 1938, convened to address refugees from Nazi persecution, she attended as the Jewish observer from Palestine. Delegates expressed sympathy but offered few admissions. Her often-cited response—hoping to live until Jews no longer needed expressions of sympathy—captures the lesson she drew: sovereign capacity mattered more than humanitarian rhetoric. The line is reported contemporaneously and in her memoir but often appears in polished variants.

During World War II she supported Ben-Gurion's formula of fighting Hitler as though there were no British White Paper and fighting the White Paper as though there were no war. She helped recruit support for the British war effort while opposing restrictions on Jewish immigration, especially after the Holocaust became known.

### 1946–1947: British repression, Jewish Agency Political Department

On **29 June 1946**, British forces arrested Jewish Agency leaders during Operation Agatha/“Black Sabbath.” With Sharett detained, Meir became acting head of the Agency's Political Department in Jerusalem and a principal liaison with Mandatory authorities. Her elevation was functional, not merely symbolic: she handled negotiation, immigration questions, detainees, and the movement's response to British repression.

In 1947 she went to the Cyprus detention camps to negotiate allocation of limited immigration certificates, persuading detainees to prioritize families with children. After the UN partition vote, she became one of the Yishuv's senior envoys to neighboring Arab leadership.

### 1947–1948: King Abdullah, war finance, independence

#### Meetings with Abdullah I

Meir met **King Abdullah I of Transjordan** at Naharayim on **17 November 1947**, and again clandestinely in Amman on **10 May 1948**, traveling disguised as an Arab woman for the latter journey. The documentary core—meetings occurred, both sides opposed the Mufti's camp, and the Jewish Agency sought to keep the Arab Legion out of the prospective Jewish state—is secure.

The interpretation is disputed:

- **Avi Shlaim's “collusion” thesis:** Zionist and Hashemite leaders reached an unwritten understanding to avoid fighting one another and divide Arab Palestine, frustrating a sovereign Palestinian Arab state.
- **Efraim Karsh and other critics:** the reports do not show a binding partition agreement; Abdullah's intentions shifted, and the May meeting ended without accord.
- **Strongest bounded conclusion:** November produced mutual probing and a provisional convergence, not a treaty. By May, Abdullah would not stand aside from the Arab intervention, and Meir returned convinced war was imminent. The subsequent Jordanian-Israeli battle for Jerusalem demonstrates that no comprehensive nonaggression arrangement controlled events. The dispute is usefully framed by [Karsh's critique](https://www.meforum.org/middle-east-quarterly/historical-fictions) and later scholarship on the [Hashemite–Zionist unwritten relationship](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00263206.2021.1898383).

#### American fundraising

In January–March 1948, sent by Ben-Gurion to North America, Meir delivered an emergency appeal beginning in Chicago and toured major Jewish communities. Contemporary and retrospective totals differ: roughly **$30 million was secured in the first drive**, while the combined 1948 campaigns are often summarized as **$50 million** or, in some accounts, up to **$90 million** in pledges/receipts. Different figures combine different drives and distinguish cash from commitments inconsistently. The safe claim is that she raised tens of millions urgently used to procure arms and supplies. Her surviving [Chicago appeal](https://awpc.cattcenter.iastate.edu/communication/speech-made-possible-jewish-state-jan-2-1948) and the [JWA reconstruction](https://jwa.org/thisweek/jan/21/1948/golda-meir) support the episode. Ben-Gurion's diary later credited “a Jewish woman” with money that made the state possible; this is both documentary praise and a foundational legend built around a real achievement.

On **14 May 1948**, she was one of two women among the 37 signatories of the **Declaration of the Establishment of the State of Israel**. The [Israel State Archives exhibition](https://catalog.archives.gov.il/site/en/chapter/the-declaration-of-independence/) and [Knesset declaration page](https://main.knesset.gov.il/en/about/pages/declaration.aspx) document her signature. She was not, however, appointed to the thirteen-member provisional cabinet, a setback revealing the limits of her position in a male hierarchy.

She returned to the United States for another fundraising mission after independence. During that journey she was injured in a car accident, tearing a ligament and fracturing a bone; the leg troubled her later. She received Israel's first passport/travel document, according to official and archival histories, although “passport no. 1” is sometimes presented ceremonially rather than with the terminological caveat that early documents preceded the 1952 nationality law; see the [history of early Israeli travel documents](https://www.jpost.com/israel-news/article-800864).

### 1948–1949: First envoy to the Soviet Union

Meir served as Israel's first minister plenipotentiary in Moscow from September 1948 to early 1949. The Soviet Union had recognized Israel and enabled Czechoslovak arms deliveries, but Stalin's regime was turning against autonomous Jewish organization and Zionism.

On Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur 1948, huge crowds of Soviet Jews greeted her at Moscow's Choral Synagogue—“Nasha Golda” (“our Golda”) in later memory. The image became an Israeli national icon and appeared on a 10,000-shekel banknote. The encounter was real; crowd estimates range widely and its conversion into a symbol of Soviet Jewry's suppressed Zionism is part of later state memory.

Her mission achieved little durable diplomatic improvement. She did not speak Russian fluently, disliked protocol, and felt isolated from decisions in Israel. Scholarship describes the post as structurally “doomed to fail” as Soviet policy hardened.

### 1949–1956: Knesset and Minister of Labor and National Insurance

Elected to the First Knesset for Mapai, Meir remained an MK until 1974. She declined an ill-defined deputy-premier position and became **Minister of Labor and National Insurance** on 10 March 1949.

Her “seven beautiful years” coincided with mass immigration, austerity, transit camps, housing shortage, and state construction. Her ministry:

- directed large public-works and road-building programs;
- built large volumes of low-cost housing and helped convert tent/transit-camp populations into permanent settlements and development towns;
- advanced the **National Insurance Law (1953/54)** and benefits covering maternity, work injury, old age, survivors/widows and orphans, and burial;
- promoted employment and vocational absorption of immigrants;
- defended borrowing and American fundraising to finance development.

The achievement and criticism concern the same system. Rapid housing and social insurance absorbed an extraordinary population; standardized peripheral development towns, paternalistic administration, and unequal allocation also entrenched disadvantages for Mizrahi and North African immigrants. Meir worked inside the Ashkenazi Mapai establishment and shared some of its civilizational assumptions.

In 1955 Mapai proposed her as mayor of Tel Aviv. City-council votes from religious representatives denied her the office, reportedly because she was a woman. She was hospitalized that August with chest pain and diagnosed with arrhythmia.

During the **Lavon Affair**—the political crisis following the failed 1954 Israeli military-intelligence false-flag bombings in Egypt—she initially sided with Ben-Gurion against Defense Minister Pinhas Lavon. Later rounds of the scandal split Mapai and ultimately ruptured her relationship with Ben-Gurion. Meir was not shown to have planned Operation Susannah; her relevance is political alignment and the party struggle over responsibility and inquiry.

### 1956–1966: Foreign Minister

Meir replaced Moshe Sharett in June 1956. Ben-Gurion preferred her loyalty and hard security outlook to Sharett's more diplomatic style. She Hebraized Meyerson to Meir.

#### Suez/Sinai War

She participated in secret coordination with France before Israel's October 1956 invasion of Egypt, part of the British-French-Israeli scheme in which Israel attacked Sinai and the European powers issued a prearranged ultimatum before attacking Egypt. She was not a signatory of the Protocol of Sèvres—Ben-Gurion, Dayan, and Peres were the core Israeli participants—but she helped prepare French political and military cooperation and defended Israel at the UN. The operation obtained temporary military gains but ended under U.S., Soviet, and UN pressure with Israeli withdrawal. The [U.S. intelligence assessment](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v16/d637) and [legal-historical study of Suez](https://scholarship.law.bu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1199&context=faculty_scholarship) document the collusion context.

#### Africa, Asia, and MASHAV

Meir made decolonizing Africa a diplomatic priority. She visited Ghana, Liberia, Nigeria, Senegal, Côte d'Ivoire, and other new states; promoted agricultural, medical, cooperative, labor, and women's training; and helped establish **MASHAV**, Israel's Agency for International Development Cooperation, in 1958. She described Israel and African states as postcolonial societies solving dryland agriculture and institution-building problems. Strategic motives accompanied solidarity: Israel sought diplomatic votes, trade, access, and partners against Arab isolation. MASHAV's own [institutional history](https://govextra.gov.il/foreign-affairs/mashav/home/about-us/) identifies Meir's role.

She supported the Mount Carmel International Training Center for women in Haifa (opened 1961), Hawk missile acquisition from the United States (approved 1962), and closer relations with France and emerging states. After 1967 and especially 1973, many African governments severed ties under Arab and Non-Aligned pressure, limiting this legacy without erasing the training networks.

#### Immigration-selection controversy

A 1958 letter to ambassador Katriel Katz in Warsaw asked whether Poland could be told Israel wanted selection because it could not continue accepting “sick and handicapped people.” This is a documented proposal, not rumor, and reflects fiscal/medical pressures expressed through discriminatory exclusion. The available report does not establish that Poland accepted a categorical ban or that every proposed exclusion was implemented. See the [contemporary archival report](https://www.jta.org/2009/12/09/israel/golda-meir-wanted-to-keep-sick-poles-from-making-aliyah) and [Polish Radio quotation](https://www2.polskieradio.pl/eo/print.aspx?iid=121629).

#### Weizmann, Herzl, and Ben-Gurion in her Zionist genealogy

- **Herzl:** ideological ancestor, never a personal acquaintance. Meir converted political Zionism into a Labor-Zionist program of migration, labor, and state institutions.
- **Chaim Weizmann:** senior diplomatic Zionist and first president of Israel. Meir operated within institutions he helped build but rose as his influence waned after 1945. Their relationship was institutional and generational, not a close partnership. Weizmann favored high diplomacy and British connections; Meir belonged to the Palestinian labor executive increasingly led by Ben-Gurion.
- **Ben-Gurion:** patron, ally, superior, and eventually estranged elder. He recognized her fundraising and administrative talent, appointed her labor and foreign minister, and urged her Hebrew surname. The Lavon Affair and his campaign against Levi Eshkol fractured the relationship; she helped unify the anti-Ben-Gurion labor camp. Late-life gestures produced partial personal reconciliation without undoing the political breach. The [National Library of Israel account](https://blog.nli.org.il/en/golda-meir-and-david-ben-gurion/) traces that arc.

#### Illness and retirement

In late 1965 doctors diagnosed lymphoma. She left the Foreign Ministry in January 1966, publicly citing exhaustion and health. Treatment continued secretly. She remained in the Knesset and became Mapai secretary-general, helping merge Mapai, Ahdut HaAvoda, and later Rafi into the Israel Labor Party/Alignment. Thus “retirement” was from ministerial office, not politics.

### 1966–1969: Party unifier and return to power

Meir used organizational authority to manage Labor's factional feuds. After Prime Minister Levi Eshkol died on 26 February 1969, Dayan and Yigal Allon were rival successors. Party elders selected the seventy-year-old Meir as a compromise with broad legitimacy. Labor's central committee chose her; the Knesset invested her government on 17 March. It was not a direct popular election, though her Alignment subsequently won the October 1969 election.

### 1969–1970: Early premiership and War of Attrition

Meir retained the national-unity coalition, with Dayan at Defense, Abba Eban at Foreign Affairs, Yigal Allon as deputy premier, and Pinhas Sapir at Finance. The Alignment won 56 seats in the October 1969 election; her coalition reached 102 members after negotiations, an exceptional parliamentary majority.

The War of Attrition along the Suez Canal escalated. Israel conducted deep-penetration air raids; Egypt received direct Soviet air-defense and pilot support. Meir opposed the December 1969 Rogers settlement proposals as requiring unacceptable withdrawal, but accepted the more limited 1970 Rogers ceasefire/Jarring framework after U.S. assurances. Menachem Begin's Gahal left the coalition in August 1970. Egypt moved surface-to-air missiles into the standstill zone in violation of the ceasefire understanding, reinforcing Meir's distrust. See the [U.S. State Department overview](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1969-1976/arab-israeli-war-1973) and [Israeli government document introduction](https://www.gov.il/en/pages/the-war-of-attrition-and-the-cease-fire-introduction).

Her June 1969 *Sunday Times* interview included the statement that “there was no such thing as Palestinians,” treated separately under controversies and myths.

### 1971: Social protest and diplomatic rigidity

Young Mizrahi activists in Jerusalem's Musrara neighborhood formed the Israeli **Black Panthers** to protest poverty, policing, ethnic discrimination, and Ashkenazi establishment power. Meir met leaders on 13 April 1971. Minutes and reports show a confrontational meeting; the phrase **“they are not nice”** attached to her afterward. The precise referent—leaders she considered violent/lawbreaking versus the whole Mizrahi protest—was contested, but the remark became a symbol of establishment contempt. The protests pushed government social spending upward while exposing Meir's limited grasp of structural ethnic inequality. See the [historical meeting summary](https://israeled.org/israeli-black-panthers/) and [scholarly contextualization](https://www.972mag.com/israeli-black-panthers-book-mizrahim/).

Egyptian president **Anwar Sadat** signaled willingness in 1971 to an interim Suez arrangement and, under UN mediator Gunnar Jarring, to forms of peace tied to Israeli withdrawal. Meir insisted on direct negotiation and rejected full return to the pre-1967 lines. Whether a genuine peace opportunity was missed remains disputed: Mordechai Gazit argues the parties' terms did not actually meet; Uri Bar-Joseph and other historians argue Sadat's initiatives went further than Meir admitted. What is documented is that no direct negotiation occurred and Israeli policy favored retaining strategic positions and settlements absent a signed peace.

### 1972–1973: Munich, Committee X, and covert killings

On 5–6 September 1972, Black September militants took Israeli athletes hostage at the Munich Olympics; eleven Israelis, five attackers, and a West German policeman died after the failed German rescue. Meir's government rejected prisoner exchange and demanded continuation of the Games only after memorial observance.

Afterward Meir chaired or authorized a small ministerial body commonly called **Committee X** and approved covert operations against individuals Israeli intelligence linked to Black September and the wider attack network. The campaign is popularly named **Wrath of God** or **Bayonet**. New scholarship based on European archives shows Western intelligence services shared tracking information through covert networks. Meir reportedly required evidence tying targets to terrorism; nevertheless, the program involved extrajudicial killings outside declared battlefields, targets whose precise connection to Munich remains disputed, collateral deaths, and no judicial process. See the [FAS study](https://irp.fas.org/eprint/calahan.htm), [European intelligence scholarship](https://academic.oup.com/ehr/article-abstract/140/604-605/777/8140798), and [2025 archival reporting](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2025/may/14/western-countries-gave-mossad-information-used-to-track-and-killi-palestinian-terrorists-in-1970s).

On 21 July 1973 Mossad agents in Lillehammer, Norway, killed **Ahmed Bouchikhi**, an innocent Moroccan waiter, after mistaking him for Ali Hassan Salameh. Norwegian arrests exposed Israeli networks. Meir suspended the campaign. She was not charged in Norway; operational agents and commanders bore direct responsibility, while political authorization and oversight ran to her government.

### The road to the Yom Kippur War: diplomacy and warning

#### “Missed peace” dispute

In February 1973 Sadat's adviser Hafez Ismail transmitted proposals to Henry Kissinger. Historian Uri Bar-Joseph argues they met most Israeli requirements except Meir's refusal to relinquish the occupied territories and that rejection closed the last chance to avoid war. Moshe Gat and Shaul Rahavi reply that Sadat refused direct negotiation and that Meir accepted exploration of Egyptian sovereignty with Israeli security positions. The record supports neither a simple “Sadat offered the 1979 treaty and Meir said no” nor “there was no offer”: there were substantive indirect proposals, incompatible positions on sovereignty/security/withdrawal, and Israeli overconfidence that reduced urgency. See [Bar-Joseph's article abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022009406064668), [Gat's counterargument](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13537121.2020.1720110), and [Gazit on 1971](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/002200949703200107).

#### Warnings and the “concept”

Military Intelligence under Eli Zeira held that Egypt would not attack without air capability to strike Israel deeply and Syria would not attack alone. This *conceptzia* survived exercises, Soviet evacuations, Syrian deployments, warnings from King Hussein, and other signals. Mossad source **Ashraf Marwan** warned director Zvi Zamir in London on the night of 5–6 October, but the timing was late and earlier alarms had proved false. The [Israel State Archives' document collection](https://catalog.archives.gov.il/site/en/publication/the-yom-kippur-war-october-1973/) and [National Security Archive briefing](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB98/index.htm) show the sequence.

Meir accepted professional intelligence estimates too long. On the morning of 6 October she approved Chief of Staff David Elazar's larger reserve mobilization over Dayan's smaller proposal, but rejected a preemptive air strike, principally to preserve U.S. diplomatic and resupply support and avoid appearing the aggressor. That decision likely improved U.S. backing; it also left Israel to absorb the opening attack.

### 6–25 October 1973: Yom Kippur/October War

Egyptian and Syrian forces attacked at 2:00 p.m. on 6 October. Israel suffered severe initial reverses in Sinai and on the Golan. Meir's war cabinet managed mobilization, U.S. resupply appeals, military disagreements, ceasefire diplomacy, and public communication. Dayan briefly spoke in catastrophic terms; Meir did not accept wholesale retreat. Israeli forces stabilized the Golan, crossed the Suez Canal, and encircled Egypt's Third Army, while the superpowers approached direct confrontation.

U.S. records show intense negotiations with Nixon and Kissinger over airlift, ceasefire, prisoners, and postwar disengagement. The [FRUS memoranda of 1 November](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v25/d306) and [17 December](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v25/d401) record Meir's direct bargaining.

#### Nuclear-alert claim

The strongest modern reconstruction does **not** support the popular story that Meir ordered thirteen nuclear bombs armed and threatened Nixon with them. Seymour Hersh and later dramatizations circulated variants; a 1976 *Time* story popularized “13 bombs.” Declassified and interview evidence indicates that on 7 October, amid fear of Syrian breakthrough, Dayan or nuclear officials raised demonstrative/alert options. Accounts differ on whether warheads were assembled or delivery systems merely readied. Several researchers conclude Meir rejected a nuclear demonstration. The [CNA study](https://www.cna.org/analyses/2013/israeli-nuclear-alert-of-1973) treats some alert/signaling as plausible but finds the evidence incomplete; the [National Security Archive](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB98/index.htm) reports that she rejected military advice for nuclear use. **Status:** a limited nuclear readiness episode is plausible; a personal nuclear ultimatum by Meir is unverified and heavily mythologized.

Israel lost roughly 2,600 soldiers (official counts vary by inclusion), with many more wounded and captured. Egypt and Syria suffered larger military losses but shattered the post-1967 assumption of effortless Israeli superiority. Arab oil producers' embargo amplified the war's global economic impact.

### 1973–1974: Agranat Commission, election, resignation

Meir's government established the **Agranat Commission** on 21 November 1973. Its mandate concentrated on intelligence, military readiness, and the opening days. The interim report of 1 April 1974 blamed senior military officers, especially Chief of Staff Elazar and intelligence leaders, while not assigning personal blame to Meir or Dayan. Critics argued the commission applied a doctrine that civilians could rely on military advisers and insulated the political echelon. The [Defense Ministry's Agranat archive](https://archives.mod.gov.il/sites/English/docs/agranat/Pages/default.aspx) and [Israel State Archives publication](https://catalog.archives.gov.il/site/en/chapter/golda-meirs-government-agranat-report-april-1974/) provide the official record.

The Alignment still won the delayed 31 December 1973 election with 51 seats but was weakened. Meir formed a new government on 10 March 1974. Public protest led by reservists, coalition strain, and the report's political insufficiency made continued rule untenable. She announced resignation on 11 April; Rabin formed a government and succeeded her on 3 June. The claim that Agranat “exonerated” her is legally narrow: it made no adverse personal finding within its mandate. Political and historical responsibility remained contested, and Meir herself later accepted that a head of government could not escape the burden.

### 1974–1978: Retirement, memoir, Sadat, final illness

Meir withdrew from daily office but remained an international speaker and Labor elder. She received the Israel Prize in 1975 and published *My Life*, a bestseller. On 21 November 1977 she addressed Sadat in the Knesset during his unprecedented visit, praised his courage, and expressed hope for the next generation to avoid war. The irony—Sadat and Meir had failed to meet before 1973—became central to reassessments of missed diplomacy.

Her lymphoma, diagnosed in 1965, was kept from the public through years of treatment. She was a heavy cigarette smoker and coffee drinker, had arrhythmia and leg problems, and underwent chemotherapy even during high office. Smoking contributed to poor health but the documented cause of death was lymphoma, not lung cancer. Hadassah officials disclosed the long illness only at death; see the contemporaneous [Washington Post obituary](https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1978/12/09/golda-meir-dies-at-80/395441fe-7d51-448b-b1ba-662cf8fc06a1/).

She entered a coma on 7 December 1978 and died the next day. Her funeral was deliberately simple by state standards; she was buried on Mount Herzl.

### Projects and technologies

Meir was not an inventor and held no known patents. Her projects were institutional: social insurance, mass housing, roads, vocational absorption, international technical aid, and the diplomatic acquisition of military technology. Relevant technologies included irrigation and dryland agriculture exported through MASHAV, U.S. Hawk surface-to-air missiles obtained during her foreign ministry, and—at the level of supreme political oversight—Israel's opaque nuclear capability. Evidence does not show that she designed these systems.

### Money, property, and financial relationships

- **Fundraising:** Her major financial power was public/movement fundraising, especially the 1948 American campaign. Figures should not be treated as personal assets.
- **Salary and lifestyle:** Accounts consistently describe a modest official lifestyle, simple apartment/household, cigarettes, coffee, and kitchen diplomacy. The “kitchen cabinet” literally met around her table but was also an informal decision network.
- **Villa Harun al-Rashid:** In the 1960s she occupied an upper apartment in a large Talbiya house built by Palestinian businessman Hanna Bisharat and transferred to Israeli control after the Bisharat family's wartime displacement. Grandson George Bisharat says the family lost title under the Absentees' Property Law and alleges Meir had Arabic-name tiles sandblasted before a diplomatic visit. Her occupancy is documented; the tile-removal attribution relies on family testimony and is disputed by later residents. Meir did not personally seize the house in 1948 or prove private title. The controversy concerns her benefit from and symbolic place in the post-1948 property regime. See [Bisharat's account](https://www.historynewsnetwork.org/article/how-my-family-lost-their-home-when-israel-took-ove) and [Haaretz's report](https://www.haaretz.com/2004-01-02/ty-article/the-family-never-lived-here/0000017f-dc8c-df9c-a17f-fe9cfa6c0000).
- **Corruption:** No criminal indictment, court finding, or well-supported personal enrichment scheme was located. Informal party patronage and concentrated Labor institutions were characteristic of the era, but should not be relabeled as Meir's personal fraud absent evidence.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Labor Zionism

Meir's core ideology remained unusually stable: Jewish national sovereignty, mass immigration, Hebrew labor, social-democratic welfare, collective responsibility, and military self-reliance. She preferred organization and material capacity to abstract theory. Herzl supplied the goal; Poale Zion and the Histadrut supplied the method; the Holocaust and Évian hardened the conviction that international sympathy could not substitute for state power.

### Socialism and pragmatism

Her socialism was institution-building rather than revolutionary Marxism. She defended public works, social insurance, labor organization, and kibbutzim, yet accepted American capital, diaspora philanthropy, state borrowing, military procurement, and a mixed economy. She was anti-communist in practice after the Soviet repression of Zionism, despite lifelong socialist language.

### Judaism and belief

Meir was culturally and nationally Jewish but not conventionally observant. The line “I believe in the Jewish people, and the Jewish people believe in God” expresses her secular-national posture, though its exact original setting is elusive. She used biblical covenant language more often in later public rhetoric. It is more accurate to call her secular/nonobservant than to assign a fully documented philosophical atheism.

### Gender

She rejected or resisted the feminist label and often presented sex as irrelevant to competence. Yet she experienced explicit discrimination, led a women's labor organization, advanced maternity protections, and became a global symbol of women's political leadership. Scholars disagree whether she subverted patriarchy by achievement or accommodated it by distancing herself from organized feminism. Pnina Lahav's [feminist biography description](https://scholarship.law.bu.edu/books/336/) and the study of [Meir's “reluctant feminism”](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.2979/israelstudies.19.3.108) frame the tension.

### Arabs, Palestinians, borders, and security

Meir recognized Arab individuals, refugees, and neighboring states but long denied that Palestinian Arabs constituted a distinct nation requiring a separate state. She treated Jordan as the principal locus for Palestinian political expression. Her security doctrine favored defensible borders, settlements, deterrent force, and direct negotiation rather than imposed withdrawal. Declassified 1970 minutes reportedly show she was willing privately to consider some form of Palestinian state, which complicates—but does not erase—her public denial; see the [archival report](https://www.haaretz.com/israel-news/2023-08-01/ty-article/.premium/golda-meir-was-open-to-a-palestinian-state-1970-minutes-reveal/00000189-b054-d083-a999-fc5ff4350000).

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Political Violence, and Abuses

### Documentary status key

- **Documented:** supported by official records, contemporaneous material, or strong corroboration.
- **Disputed interpretation:** facts are established but meaning/responsibility is contested.
- **Allegation/unverified:** circulated but lacking adequate proof.
- **Formal finding:** conclusion of a named legal or official body, limited to its mandate.

### 1. 1948 war, Palestinian displacement, and property

Meir was a senior Jewish Agency leader, fundraiser, diplomatic emissary, and state founder during a war in which roughly 700,000 Palestinians became refugees through a combination of flight, fear, expulsions, military assaults, and prevention of return; mechanisms differed by locality. She was not the operational commander of expulsions, but she served the leadership that decided war and postwar policy and later defended the refusal of mass return. Her later statement that Jews did not arrive and “throw them out” is incompatible with the documented occurrence of expulsions and permanent dispossession in significant places, even though no single mechanism explains the whole exodus. **Status:** displacement and Israeli prevention of return are documented; degree of centralized prior master plan remains historiographically disputed; Meir's personal operational authorship is not established.

### 2. Abdullah “collusion”

Meetings are documented. A binding secret treaty to partition Palestine is not. Shlaim's interpretation finds an unwritten convergence; critics argue contingency and incompatible reports. **Status:** disputed interpretation, not proven conspiracy treaty.

### 3. Lavon Affair

Operation Susannah was a real false-flag sabotage operation. Meir participated in later political struggles, sided first with Ben-Gurion, then with the Eshkol camp, and helped isolate Ben-Gurion. There is no evidence she ordered the bombings. **Status:** documented state covert crime; her role political, not operational.

### 4. Suez collusion and war

The 1956 tripartite deception is documented. Meir helped coordinate with France and publicly defended the invasion. **Status:** documented secret interstate collusion and use of force; no personal criminal proceeding.

### 5. Immigration selection and Mizrahi hierarchy

The 1958 disability-selection letter is documented. Statements attributed to her about immigrants from Morocco, Libya, Iran, and Egypt having a “sixteenth-century level” circulate with a 1964 Zionist Federation setting; the quotation should be used cautiously unless the original speech is produced. Her government's confrontation with the Black Panthers and the “not nice” phrase are documented in substance. **Status:** policy and paternalistic attitudes documented; some viral quotations require original-source verification.

### 6. Settlements, Aqraba, and occupied land

Meir's governments expanded civilian settlement in territories occupied in 1967 and treated the Jordan Valley, Golan, and parts of Sinai as strategic. International legal bodies generally regard West Bank settlements as contrary to the Fourth Geneva Convention; Israel disputes that interpretation.

Archival documents reported in 2023 describe the military declaring land near **Aqraba** a training area, damaging tools and crops, and aerially spraying herbicide in 1972 to stop Palestinian cultivation before establishment of **Gitit**. The surviving chain shows state and military action under Meir's government; currently available reporting does not show a personal written order from Meir to spray poison. **Status:** underlying state operation documented in released records; personal direction by Meir unproved. See the [archival investigation](https://www.haaretz.com/israel-news/2023-06-23/ty-article-magazine/.highlight/israel-poisoned-palestinian-land-to-build-west-bank-settlement-in-1970s-documents-reveal/00000188-e8aa-df52-a79d-fcabdd200000).

### 7. Munich retaliation and extrajudicial killing

Meir authorized a covert campaign. Some targets were linked to militant networks; direct involvement in Munich varied and is disputed. Bouchikhi was unquestionably an innocent victim of mistaken identity. **Status:** authorization and killings documented; individual target culpability variable; legality and oversight deeply contested.

### 8. Rejection of negotiation and responsibility for 1973 war

Meir did not “cause” Egypt and Syria to initiate war in the narrow operational sense. Her government did, however, maintain territorial positions, reject full withdrawal, discount indirect initiatives, and tolerate an intelligence system built on overconfidence. Historians disagree whether available proposals could have produced peace. **Status:** policy record documented; counterfactual avoidability unresolved.

### 9. Intelligence surprise and Agranat

The commission formally criticized military officials, not Meir. Its mandate and civil-military doctrine were criticized as shielding politicians. Meir approved mobilization on the final morning but had accepted low-risk assessments earlier. **Status:** formal non-blame within commission mandate; broader political responsibility remains a historical judgment, not a court finding.

### 10. Nuclear threat

No declassified document proves Meir threatened Nixon or ordered nuclear attacks. Some readiness/signaling may have occurred. **Status:** unresolved limited alert; dramatic ultimatum is unverified.

### 11. Yemenite children affair

The disappearances and families' mistreatment occurred mainly while Meir was labor minister and part of the governing elite. Multiple commissions found most investigated children died amid chaotic medical systems, some were adopted/fostered, and dozens remained unresolved; no documentary proof establishes a centrally ordered kidnapping program by Meir or her ministry. Families' testimony and unresolved files remain historically significant. **Status:** deaths, administrative cruelty, poor notification, and unresolved cases documented; personal Meir kidnapping conspiracy unsupported. A balanced survey is in the [Association for Israel Studies volume](https://aisisraelstudies.org/the-yemenite-children-affair-ethnic-tensions-immigration-and-public-records-in-israel/).

### 12. Fraud, corruption, money laundering, criminal charges

No credible evidence found of personal fraud, money laundering, criminal indictment, or conviction. Her controversies concern public policy, war, secrecy, covert force, discrimination, and accountability—not documented personal financial crime.

## Awards, Honors, Offices, Appointments, and Dismissals

### Offices and appointments

- Secretary, Mo'etzet Ha-Po'alot, 1928.
- Histadrut executive/political leadership, 1930s–40s.
- Acting head, Jewish Agency Political Department, 1946–48.
- Member, People's Council/Provisional State Council and Declaration signer, 1948.
- Minister plenipotentiary to the Soviet Union, 1948–49.
- Member of Knesset, 1949–1974.
- Minister of Labor and National Insurance, 1949–56.
- Foreign Minister, 1956–66.
- Mapai secretary-general and Labor-unification leader, 1966–68.
- Prime Minister and Labor Party leader, 1969–74.
- Acting/temporary Interior Minister, July–September 1970.

She was not formally dismissed from the premiership. She resigned under political pressure after forming a postwar government. Agranat recommended removal of military officials, not her.

### Honors

- Honorary degrees included the University of Wisconsin (1960) and other American institutions.
- James Madison Award for Distinguished Public Service, Princeton's American Whig-Cliosophic Society, 1974.
- “World Mother”/National Mother honor from American Mothers, 1974.
- **Israel Prize**, 1975, for special contribution to society and the state.
- Posthumous induction into the Colorado Women's Hall of Fame, 1985.
- *Time* retroactively named her its representative Woman of the Year for 1956 in the 2020 “100 Women of the Year” project.

## Illness, Habits, Accidents, and Psychological Strain

- Childhood exposure to hunger, infant death, antisemitic threat, and family separation became recurring autobiographical reference points.
- 1948 U.S. automobile accident: leg fracture/ligament injury, chronic later pain.
- 1955 chest pain and diagnosed arrhythmia.
- Lymphoma diagnosed late 1965; treatment concealed throughout her return to high office.
- Heavy cigarette smoking and coffee consumption; she was frequently photographed smoking. No documented alcoholism or illicit drug addiction.
- 1973 war records and recollections describe sleeplessness, despair, crying, and acute strain. These are crisis reactions, not evidence for a retrospectively diagnosable psychiatric disorder.
- Final hospitalization in 1978, coma on 7 December, death from lymphoma on 8 December.

## Death, Funeral, and Burial

Hospital officials announced her death on 8 December 1978 and disclosed the long-secret malignant lymphoma. International leaders attended or sent delegations. She was buried four days later on Mount Herzl. Her requested simplicity and the mass public response became the final extension of the austere “Golda” image. Her death came before the Egypt–Israel peace treaty of March 1979 but after Sadat's visit and Camp David framework, allowing admirers and critics alike to frame her as having glimpsed the peace that earlier eluded her.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Israel

Meir's domestic reputation split sharply after 1973. The founder, fundraiser, labor minister, Moscow icon, and blunt grandmotherly diplomat survived in institutional commemoration; the prime minister associated with complacency, “the concept,” mass casualties, Mizrahi alienation, and denial of Palestinian identity became a negative symbol. Younger Israelis often encountered her through banknotes, school names, “Golda shoes,” an ice-cream chain sharing the name, and Yom Kippur War culture rather than policy history.

The 2019 documentary, Lahav and Lipstadt biographies, and the 2023 Mirren film produced a revisionist wave. Some argue gender made her an easier vessel for blame than Dayan or male intelligence chiefs; others answer that feminist rehabilitation cannot erase executive responsibility. Both can be true: gender shaped reception, and she held supreme office.

### Palestinian and Arab memory

Her 1969 sentence functions as a concise symbol of national erasure. Her government's settlement policy and assassination campaign reinforce a hostile image of Labor Zionism as expansionist despite socialist language. The Bouchikhi killing and post-Munich operations remain central to criticism of Israeli extrajudicial violence. More nuanced readings note her 1976 recognition that Palestinian national aspirations had developed, without confusing that recognition with endorsement of an independent West Bank/Gaza state.

### United States, Milwaukee, and women's leadership

In the United States she remains an immigrant-success and women's-leadership icon, especially in Milwaukee and Denver. Fourth Street School became **Golda Meir School** in 1979; UWM's library was renamed for her; Denver preserved the Korngold house. These memorials foreground education, migration, and civic courage more than Middle Eastern controversy.

### Ukraine and Zelenskyy

Independent Ukraine increasingly claims Kyiv-born Meir as part of the city's Jewish and transnational history. In his **20 March 2022 Knesset address**, President **Volodymyr Zelenskyy**, himself a Kyiv-born Jewish Ukrainian leader, invoked words attributed to Meir—“We want to live, but our neighbors want to see us dead”—to press Israel for stronger support against Russia. The official [Ukrainian presidential transcript](https://www.president.gov.ua/en/news/promova-prezidenta-ukrayini-volodimira-zelenskogo-v-kneseti-73701) documents the invocation; the [Knesset release](https://m.knesset.gov.il/EN/News/PressReleases/Pages/press20322y.aspx) records its Israeli setting. The analogy is political rhetoric, not a direct lineage: Meir left Kyiv as a child, did not develop a Ukrainian national identity, and lived most of her formative life in the United States and Palestine/Israel.

### Institutional legacy

Her most durable nonmilitary legacies are the National Insurance and public-works state, mass immigrant housing (with its inequalities), MASHAV, the Mount Carmel training center, and an American-Israeli political relationship personalized at the head-of-government level. Her worst-known legacies are the 1973 preparedness failure, public denial of Palestinian nationhood, tolerated settlement expansion, and authorization of extrajudicial retaliation.

## Atlas Connections

### David Ben-Gurion

- **[T5] Documented fact:** Meir first saw Ben-Gurion speak in Milwaukee in 1917 and met him personally in Tel Aviv after both returned to Palestine in 1921. They subsequently worked together for decades in the Histadrut, Jewish Agency, Mapai, and Israeli government; both signed the Declaration of Independence on 14 May 1948. Their alliance later fractured over the Lavon Affair and Mapai split, followed by a partial personal reconciliation at Kibbutz Revivim in 1971. [National Library of Israel](https://blog.nli.org.il/en/golda-meir-and-david-ben-gurion/)
- **[T3] Documented fact:** They corresponded privately and officially throughout their political association. Surviving examples include Ben-Gurion’s affectionate sixtieth-birthday letter to Meir in 1958; their written exchanges also addressed government, party, and security matters. [National Library of Israel](https://blog.nli.org.il/en/golda-meir-and-david-ben-gurion/)

### Chaim Weizmann

- **[T4] Documented fact:** Meir and Weizmann occupied the same senior Zionist institutional milieu, including the Jewish Agency Executive’s 1946 deliberations over testimony before the Anglo-American Committee: Weizmann was selected to present the political case and Meir to testify for the Histadrut. This establishes participation in the same organized leadership process, but the located record does not support describing them as close associates or friends. [Meron Medzini, *Golda Meir: A Political Biography*](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/master/gdc/gdcebookspublic/20/19/66/78/09/2019667809/2019667809.pdf)

### John F. Kennedy

- **[T5] Documented fact:** Kennedy met Israeli foreign minister Meir at Palm Beach, Florida, on 27 December 1962. The Kennedy Library preserves the White House photographic record, while the diplomatic papers identify the meeting as addressing Arab–Israeli security, refugees, and Israel’s nuclear program. [JFK Library](https://www.jfklibrary.org/asset-viewer/archives/jfkwhp-1962-12-27-a), [U.S. Department of State](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v18/d118)

### John Foster Dulles

- **[T5] Documented fact:** As Israeli foreign minister, Meir met U.S. secretary of state Dulles in Washington on 3 October 1958 for a forty-minute discussion of Middle Eastern policy and Israeli security. Abba Eban accompanied her. [Jewish Telegraphic Agency](https://www.jta.org/archive/golda-meir-meets-with-dulles-discuss-present-middle-east-situation)
- **[T3] Documented fact:** Dulles subsequently sent Meir a secret diplomatic message dated 31 October 1958 concerning the United Arab Republic’s response to an interview and broader regional policy. [U.S. Department of State](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v13/d45)

### Haile Selassie I

- **[T5] Documented fact:** Meir met Emperor Haile Selassie at his palace during her 1962 visit to Ethiopia and introduced him to her daughter Sarah. In her memoir she explicitly distinguished the relationship from friendship, writing that they did not become “fast friends.” [Meir memoir excerpt](https://www.ethiopiaobserver.com/2017/01/13/prime-minister-golda-meir-emperor-haile-selassie/), [Meron Medzini, *Golda Meir: A Political Biography*](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/master/gdc/gdcebookspublic/20/19/66/78/09/2019667809/2019667809.pdf)

### David Rockefeller

- **[T5] Documented fact:** A published Council on Foreign Relations archival inventory identifies a photograph of Rockefeller with Prime Minister Meir at a 1973 black-tie function at the Harold Pratt House in New York. The photograph documents an in-person encounter but, by itself, does not establish friendship or a substantive bilateral discussion. [Council on Foreign Relations archival history](https://cdn.cfr.org/sites/default/files/book_pdf/Continuing_The_Inquiry.pdf)

### Volodymyr Zelenskyy

- **[T1+] Documented fact:** In his remote address to the Knesset on 20 March 2022, Zelenskyy invoked and quoted Kyiv-born Meir while arguing for stronger Israeli support for Ukraine following Russia’s invasion. Meir is the earlier, written-about figure and therefore carries the positive sign. [President of Ukraine](https://www.president.gov.ua/en/news/promova-prezidenta-ukrayini-volodimira-zelenskogo-v-kneseti-73701), [Knesset](https://m.knesset.gov.il/EN/News/PressReleases/Pages/press20322y.aspx)
