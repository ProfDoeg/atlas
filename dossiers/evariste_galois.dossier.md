# Évariste Galois — Research Dossier

**Subject:** Évariste Galois (1811–1832)  
**Nationality:** French  
**Fields:** Mathematics; republican political activism  
**Principal historical significance:** Founder of the body of ideas now called Galois theory; major early contributor to the theory of permutation groups and finite fields; radical republican activist under the July Monarchy.  
**Dossier scope:** Birth to death, documentary afterlife, mathematical publication history, political and legal record, surviving personal relationships, disputed circumstances of the fatal duel, and the subsequent manufacture of the “Galois legend.”

## Evidentiary Note

The biographical record for Galois is unusually substantial for a person who died at twenty. Important evidence includes his own surviving manuscripts and letters; school records; École préparatoire/École normale records; Académie des sciences registers and the Lacroix–Poisson report; contemporary newspaper coverage of his political trials and funeral; prison registers; hospital and autopsy records; and testimony collected from relatives and contemporaries. At the same time, several of the stories most often repeated about him entered circulation decades after his death.

The principal documentary-biographical foundation remains Paul Dupuy’s **“La vie d’Évariste Galois”** (1896). Dupuy consulted archives of the École normale, Ministry of Public Instruction, Académie de Paris, Archives nationales, Bourg-la-Reine, Seine prefecture, Sainte-Pélagie and other institutions, as well as newspapers and living family connections. Some police and judicial material had been destroyed in the fires of 1871, leaving genuine gaps. Dupuy also repeated family and oral traditions, which have to be distinguished from the records he transcribed.

René Taton’s archival work, especially his 1971 study of Galois and Cauchy, corrected a major part of the older Academy narrative. Caroline Ehrhardt’s studies have reconstructed the institutional context of Galois’s schooling and Academy submissions and have challenged the simple image of a genius uniquely persecuted by incompetent institutions. Peter M. Neumann’s critical bilingual edition of the mathematical writings (2011; corrected printing 2013) is especially useful for the chronology, manuscripts, publication history, mathematical content, and later reception. Carlos Alberto Infantozzi and, more recently, Olivier Courcelle have examined the documents surrounding the duel and the possible identity of “Stéphanie.”

Dates below are therefore labeled where necessary as **documented**, **later testimony**, **probable**, **disputed**, or **legendary**. The dossier does not treat later repetition as independent corroboration.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Évariste Galois.
- **Alternative spellings in contemporary records and press:** *Galois*; occasionally *Gallois*. An Académie record rendered the name “Le Gallois,” apparently a clerical error rather than an alias.
- **Pseudonyms:** No stable pseudonym is known. His December 1830 attack on the director of the École normale was published anonymously under the description **“Un élève de l’École Normale.”**
- **Birth:** **25 October 1811**, Bourg-la-Reine, then in the département de la Seine, south of Paris. The birth act was declared on 26 October. Older biographical traditions sometimes place him loosely “in Paris”; the municipal birth record fixes Bourg-la-Reine.
- **Birth address:** The Galois family pension/house on the principal street of Bourg-la-Reine, later identified by Dupuy as approximately **20 Grand’Rue** under nineteenth-century numbering.
- **Death:** **31 May 1832, about 10 a.m.**, Hôpital Cochin, Paris, from peritonitis following a pistol wound received in a duel the previous morning.
- **Age at death:** **20 years, 7 months.** Some contemporary administrative documents called him twenty-one; this is an age error in the record.
- **Burial:** **2 June 1832**, Montparnasse Cemetery, Paris. Contemporary and early sources place him in a common grave; no securely identifiable individual grave survives.
- **Marital status:** Unmarried. No children are known.
- **Occupations/statuses appearing in records:** student; mathematician; *répétiteur* (tutor/repeater) in a prison register; former pupil of the École normale; former artilleryman of the Paris National Guard; member or activist associated with the Société des Amis du Peuple.
- **Political identification:** radical republican opposed to the July Monarchy of Louis-Philippe.
- **Religious status:** Raised under a mother with a classical and Christian education. Later evidence suggests estrangement from conventional belief; Dupuy reports that Galois refused a priest while dying. There is no adequate basis for assigning him a formal philosophical or denominational label beyond that.

### Physical likenesses

A portrait made from life when Galois was approximately fifteen or sixteen survived in the family and was reproduced by Dupuy. A second highly familiar image was drawn from memory by his younger brother **Alfred Galois** for an 1848 biographical notice, sixteen years after Évariste’s death. The existence of a life portrait is important because many later popular illustrations are imaginative reconstructions rather than eyewitness likenesses.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal family

Galois’s father was **Nicolas-Gabriel Galois** (3 December 1775–2 July 1829). The Galois family operated a private educational establishment at Bourg-la-Reine. The upheavals of the Revolution and reorganization of education helped the family pension prosper after older religious schools had been suppressed or transformed. Nicolas-Gabriel became a teacher and director of the pension; Évariste’s birth act in 1811 describes him as director of a pension connected with the Imperial University.

Nicolas-Gabriel was remembered as sociable, witty, literary, and politically liberal. He composed verses and occasional theatrical pieces for local festivities. During the Hundred Days of 1815 he became **mayor of Bourg-la-Reine** and, unusually, remained mayor through the Second Restoration until his death in 1829. His political position is generally described as liberal rather than as a doctrinaire revolutionary one.

A paternal uncle, **Théodore-Michel Galois**, served as an officer during the Napoleonic era. The broader paternal family belonged to the educated provincial bourgeoisie rather than to the aristocracy or great financial elite.

### Maternal family

Galois’s mother was **Adélaïde-Marie Demante** (born **9 July 1788**). She came from a family of jurists and magistrates. Her father, **Thomas-François Demante** (1752–1823), had been a *docteur agrégé* in law in the old University of Paris and later a magistrate, serving as president of the civil tribunal at Louviers. He appears as a witness in Évariste’s birth record. Members of the Demante family continued in legal and academic careers; Adélaïde’s brother **Antoine-Marie Demante** became a law professor.

Adélaïde received a substantial classical education unusual enough to matter for her son’s formation: Latin, Greek, French literary culture, moral philosophy, and Christian instruction. She educated Évariste at home before he entered a lycée.

There is a minor source conflict over her death. Dupuy and the Institut Henri Poincaré’s bicentenary material give **1788–1872**. Some genealogical and MacTutor material gives **1 August 1871**. The discrepancy is later than Évariste’s lifetime and has no bearing on his biography, but it should not be silently harmonized. A fresh civil-status verification would be needed to settle it definitively.

### Parents’ marriage and children

Nicolas-Gabriel Galois and Adélaïde-Marie Demante married at Bourg-la-Reine on **24 February 1808**. Their known children were:

1. **Nathalie-Théodore Galois**, born 27 September 1808; later married and known in some sources as Mme Chantelot. Portions of a diary associated with her are valuable for Évariste’s prison period and health.
2. **Évariste Galois**, born 25 October 1811.
3. **Alfred Galois**, born 17 December 1814; later an important guardian of his brother’s manuscripts and memory.

The given name **Évariste** has been connected with the feast of Saint Evaristus/Evariste around 26 October, immediately after his birth. This is plausible naming context rather than evidence of any later devotion by Galois himself.

### Family finances and property

The family’s economic base was educational and professional: the Bourg-la-Reine pension, the father’s directorship, and the Demante legal-professional network. The household was respectable and educated but not documented as possessing major wealth. No evidence has emerged of substantial assets, investments, securities, commercial ventures, or inherited fortune belonging personally to Évariste. After Nicolas-Gabriel’s death, Adélaïde maintained links with the family’s Bourg-la-Reine and Paris properties. Later records cited by MacTutor state that she sold the family house in 1851.

## Childhood and Early Life

### 1811–1824: Bourg-la-Reine and home education

Évariste spent his earliest years in Bourg-la-Reine in a household where education was also the family business. His mother was his principal teacher. The standard old biography says that she educated him until age twelve. Modern reconstruction of his Louis-le-Grand entry date slightly lengthens that period.

The curriculum appears to have been predominantly classical: French, Latin, Greek, classical literature, moral texts, and religion. Later family testimony presented him as serious, affectionate, verbally precocious, and inclined to compose dialogues and verses for family amusement. A cousin, Mme Bénard, supplied Dupuy with recollections of these early performances. Because these memories were recorded many decades later, they are best treated as family testimony rather than as contemporaneous observation.

There is no evidence that Galois was a child prodigy in mathematics. The famous mathematical transformation came in adolescence. His early formation was humanistic, not technical.

### Political atmosphere of the household

His father’s long tenure as liberal mayor gave Évariste early contact with municipal politics, conflicts between Restoration liberals and clerical conservatives, and the symbolic importance of 1789, the Empire, and the competing regimes that followed. It would be reductive to derive the son’s republicanism automatically from the father; nevertheless, political argument was not foreign to the household.

## Education and Formation

## Louis-le-Grand

### Entry date: traditional account versus newer archival correction

Dupuy and most twentieth-century biographies said that Galois entered the **Collège royal Louis-le-Grand** in **1823** as a boarder in the *quatrième*. Caroline Ehrhardt’s later archival work found an entry/exit register indicating registration as a boarder in **February 1824** and entry into *quatrième* on **1 April 1824**. The older 1823 date therefore remains important as a historiographical tradition, but 1824 is the stronger modern archival reconstruction.

Louis-le-Grand was one of the premier secondary institutions in France, operating under the Restoration’s centralized educational system. Its curriculum emphasized classical languages and rhetoric, with strict boarding-school discipline.

### 1824 school unrest

The school experienced serious disciplinary conflict in 1824. A large number of pupils were expelled after resistance to school authority. Galois was not among the principal leaders and was not expelled. Older romantic biographies sometimes cast this episode as the first eruption of his revolutionary personality. The records instead show that he was still a successful conventional pupil.

### Classical success

In his early years at Louis-le-Grand Galois won prizes and accessits. He performed strongly in Latin verse and Greek translation, including distinction in the *Concours général*. This matters because the later portrait of a student incapable of ordinary schoolwork is false. His pattern was not general intellectual incompetence but increasing concentration on a narrow domain at the expense of subjects he came to regard as secondary.

### 1825–1827: academic unevenness

His results declined in *seconde*. The headmaster considered him immature and recommended repeating the year. The family initially resisted; Galois entered rhetoric in 1826 but was returned to *seconde* after the first term.

School comments reproduced by Dupuy include phrases such as **“bon, mais singulier”** and **“original et bizarre.”** These are disciplinary judgments made within a Restoration school culture, not clinical diagnoses. They nevertheless document how teachers perceived an increasingly independent and difficult pupil.

### 1827: encounter with advanced mathematics

The decisive turn came with mathematics instruction under **M. Vernier**. A later recollection by schoolmate Ludovic Lalanne says Galois read **Adrien-Marie Legendre’s Éléments de géométrie** with the intensity of a novel. He then moved rapidly into works intended for far more advanced students, especially Joseph-Louis **Lagrange**:

- *Résolution des équations numériques*
- *Théorie des fonctions analytiques*
- *Leçons sur le calcul des fonctions*

His reading widened to the mathematical theory of equations, analysis, number theory, and eventually work associated with Gauss, Abel, Jacobi, and Cauchy. The exact chronology of every book is not recoverable. Neumann cautions against turning plausible intellectual availability into proof that Galois read a particular work at a particular date.

Vernier recognized ability but criticized insufficient method and presentation. Galois won a mathematics prize at the *Concours général*. By this point he was increasingly impatient with elementary exercises and with the educational demand to display every intermediate step.

### First attempt at the École polytechnique, 1828

Galois attempted the entrance examination for the **École polytechnique** and failed. Sources disagree on the exact month—June or August 1828 appear in modern discussions—and the oral examination record that would explain the failure has not survived.

The failure became central to later legend. It cannot safely be reconstructed as a simple case of examiners too stupid to understand him. His own later writings show hostility toward examination culture, while school records show a real tendency toward compressed exposition. Both factors could matter.

### 1828–1829: Mathématiques spéciales and Louis-Paul-Émile Richard

In autumn 1828 Galois entered the *mathématiques spéciales* class under **Louis-Paul-Émile Richard**, who immediately recognized exceptional ability. Richard’s school reports are among the strongest contemporary testimony to Galois’s mathematical standing. He described him as showing a **“supériorité marquée sur tous ses condisciples”** and noted that Galois worked only on the higher parts of mathematics.

Richard preserved a number of Galois’s school exercises, which later passed through Charles Hermite and Émile Picard to the Institut de France. These manuscripts complicate the legend that Galois was constitutionally incapable of orderly school solutions: surviving work can be careful and legible, even when his preferred reasoning diverges from prescribed methods.

At the same time, his neglect of other subjects became extreme. A physics/chemistry report by Thillaye recorded **“travail nul.”** The contrast—brilliant in advanced mathematics, nearly absent intellectually elsewhere—is well documented.

### 1829: first publication

Galois’s first published paper was:

**“Démonstration d’un théorème sur les fractions continues périodiques,”** *Annales de mathématiques pures et appliquées* (Gergonne), vol. 19, 1828–1829, pp. 294–301; published in 1829.

It dealt with continued fractions rather than the theory now bearing his name. Its importance is biographical: by seventeen he had entered the published mathematical literature through a respected specialist journal.

## Early Career

Galois never had time to establish a conventional professional career. His “career” consisted of student work, research, a brief attempt at public mathematical teaching, possible private tutoring, and political activism.

### First Académie des sciences submissions, May–June 1829

Academy registers show that Galois submitted two papers on equations in **May and June 1829**. They were associated with **Augustin-Louis Cauchy** and **Louis Poinsot** as referees. The manuscripts are lost.

This episode became the basis of the story that Cauchy simply lost or suppressed Galois’s work. René Taton’s reconstruction of Academy records substantially weakens that story. Cauchy appears to have taken the work seriously enough to schedule a report and may have advised Galois to recast it into a stronger submission. The most plausible modern reconstruction is that the manuscripts were withdrawn or superseded in early 1830 when Galois prepared an entry for the Academy’s Grand Prix. The exact sequence is not completely documented.

### Father’s suicide, 2 July 1829

Near the end of that school year, Galois’s family suffered its defining catastrophe. In Bourg-la-Reine, Nicolas-Gabriel Galois had become the target of a political-clerical campaign involving a newly arrived priest and local opponents. Scurrilous or obscene verses were circulated and attributed to the mayor. Later tellings say that his signature was forged; Dupuy’s account securely establishes the defamatory attribution but is less explicit about the mechanism.

Nicolas-Gabriel became convinced he was ruined by the campaign. On **2 July 1829**, while his wife was away, he killed himself by asphyxiation in a Paris apartment on **rue Jean-de-Beauvais**, close to Louis-le-Grand.

His funeral procession to Bourg-la-Reine became politically charged. Townspeople carried the coffin. Near the church a confrontation occurred and the priest was reportedly struck by a stone. Évariste, seventeen, was in the principal family mourning party. Later biographers have often treated the father’s suicide as the psychological cause of the son’s subsequent political radicalization. The temporal connection is obvious, but a direct monocausal explanation cannot be documented.

### Second École polytechnique failure, summer 1829

Galois failed the École polytechnique entrance examination a second and final time in summer 1829. The identity of the examiner in the later projectile story is itself uncertain; Dupuy could not decide between Binet and Lefébure de Fourcy. The famous account that Galois, infuriated by an examiner, threw a blackboard eraser, chalk rag, or similar object at him is **oral tradition recorded later**, not a contemporary documented fact.

### Admission to the École préparatoire

Galois instead competed for the **École préparatoire**, the institution created to train secondary-school teachers and later renamed the École normale. His written entrance papers survive in the Archives nationales. Ehrhardt’s study of the 1829 competition shows that his mathematics work was careful and legible and that he ranked very strongly among the science candidates.

He was admitted in autumn 1829, generally dated **25 October 1829** in administrative summaries, though some modern chronologies say November. His mathematics oral examiner Leroy gave him **8/10**, noting intelligence, originality, and some obscurity of expression. His physics oral went much worse; Péclet doubted his command of the subject and teaching readiness. The final decision nevertheless admitted him near the top of the science intake.

He had not yet completed the required baccalaureates. He failed the *baccalauréat ès lettres* on **2 December 1829**, then passed on **14 December**, performing weakly in literary components but strongly in mathematics and physics. Shortly afterward he passed the *baccalauréat ès sciences* before examiners including Francœur, Hachette, and Lefébure de Fourcy.

On **20 February 1830** he signed the customary long-term commitment to serve the University system, generally described as a ten-year engagement. His later expulsion raised the theoretical question of repayment or enforcement; Dupuy reports that the minister ultimately assured the family that no punitive claim would be pursued.

## Chronological Life History

### 1830: the Grand Prix manuscript and the genuinely lost Academy paper

Galois reworked his research on algebraic equations for the Académie des sciences’ Grand Prix in mathematics. The submission was made in early 1830, probably late February. The Academy’s perpetual secretary, **Joseph Fourier**, received the manuscript. Fourier died on **16 May 1830** before the prize process was completed. Galois’s manuscript was not subsequently found among the submissions.

This loss is much better documented than the famous Cauchy-loss story. It does not follow that Fourier intentionally mishandled the paper. The surviving facts are simply that Fourier had the submission, died, and the manuscript was absent when the prize was adjudicated. The Grand Prix ultimately recognized work of **Niels Henrik Abel** posthumously and **Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi** on elliptic functions.

Galois experienced this as another institutional disappearance of his work.

### 1830 publications

Despite the image of total rejection, 1830 was a productive publication year. Three short pieces appeared in Férussac’s *Bulletin des sciences mathématiques, physiques et chimiques*:

- **“Analyse d’un mémoire sur la résolution algébrique des équations”** — April 1830.
- **“Note sur la résolution des équations numériques”** — June 1830.
- **“Sur la théorie des nombres”** — June 1830.

The number-theory paper introduced constructions later understood as finite fields, long known in some traditions as **Galois fields**. A further note, **“Notes sur quelques points d’analyse,”** appeared in Gergonne’s *Annales* in December 1830.

Thus five mathematical papers were published in his lifetime. The claim that the mathematical establishment prevented him from publishing anything is incorrect.

### July Revolution, 27–29 July 1830

The July Revolution overthrew Charles X and eventually installed Louis-Philippe. Galois was confined with other École préparatoire students while fighting occurred in Paris. Director of studies **Joseph-Daniel Guigniault** attempted to maintain institutional order and to keep students inside.

On 28 July Guigniault reportedly sought assurances from pupils that they would not leave. Galois and a future relative, Bénard, declined to give the desired pledge. Galois later said that he attempted unsuccessfully to climb the wall during the night. He learned that students of the École polytechnique had taken part openly in the street fighting and that the former Normalien **Farcy** had been killed. The contrast became a lasting grievance: Polytechnique appeared heroic and republican while the teacher-training school had been immobilized.

Galois did not therefore play a documented combat role in the Three Glorious Days, despite later shorthand saying that he “fought in the July Revolution.” His documented role is frustrated attempted participation.

### From liberal disappointment to militant republicanism

The July settlement replaced one Bourbon monarch with another constitutional king. For radical republicans this was a betrayal of the revolution. Galois gravitated toward the republican societies, particularly the **Société des Amis du Peuple**. The exact date of formal membership is uncertain. Dupuy inferred involvement by late 1830; by the time of his death contemporary republican notices explicitly treated him as a member.

His closest school friend **Auguste Chevalier**, and Chevalier’s brother Michel, moved toward Saint-Simonianism. Galois did not follow them. Dupuy’s reconstruction indicates that he rejected the Saint-Simonian hierarchy and its political distance from revolutionary republicanism. His friendship with Auguste survived the ideological divergence.

### December 1830: attack on Guigniault and expulsion

The *Gazette des Écoles* was engaged in a press war over educational administration. On **5 December 1830** it printed an anonymous letter signed only **“Un élève de l’École Normale.”** The author attacked Guigniault’s conduct during the July days and criticized the regime of the school. Galois was identified as the author.

Guigniault expelled him on **9 December 1830**. The expulsion was confirmed administratively in early January 1831; sources give **3 January** or **4 January** depending on whether one dates the council decision or formal act. Guigniault’s own report conceded Galois’s “incontestable talent” while characterizing his conduct and character as unacceptable and his ordinary work as irregular.

The affair entered the press. Galois responded publicly and resented the degree to which classmates distanced themselves from his attack. The event ended his institutional route toward a teaching career.

### January 1831: public course and renewed Academy submission

After leaving the school, Galois tried to teach higher algebra publicly. An advertisement announced a course beginning **13 January 1831** at the bookseller Caillot, **5 rue de Sorbonne**, on Thursdays in the early afternoon. The advertised topics were remarkably close to his research frontier: a new theory of imaginary quantities, algebraic solvability of equations, number theory, and elliptic functions treated algebraically. Dupuy says roughly forty listeners were initially attracted, but the course did not develop into a stable livelihood.

At the same time Galois prepared a new Academy manuscript. **Siméon-Denis Poisson** had shown interest and encouraged a more explicit presentation. On **17 January 1831** Galois submitted **“Mémoire sur les conditions de résolubilité des équations par radicaux.”** The Academy assigned **Sylvestre-François Lacroix** and Poisson as referees.

### National Guard artillery and republican networks

Galois associated himself with the artillery of the Paris National Guard, a unit with a strong republican reputation. The artillery was dissolved by royal authority in the aftermath of political conflict. Former artillerymen remained a recognizable political network and were prominent in republican demonstrations and prosecutions.

He also mixed with activists around the Société des Amis du Peuple. This network included figures such as François-Vincent Raspail, Étienne Arago, Godefroy Cavaignac, and others whose political careers and ideologies later diverged. Galois’s politics were not an abstract salon affiliation; he carried weapons, marched in uniform, attended oppositional banquets, and accepted incarceration.

### 9 May 1831: the Vendanges de Bourgogne banquet

On **9 May 1831**, approximately two hundred opponents of the government gathered at the restaurant **Les Vendanges de Bourgogne** to celebrate the acquittal of republican defendants associated with the former National Guard artillery. **Alexandre Dumas**, who attended, later described the banquet in his memoirs.

During the increasingly heated meal Galois rose with a glass and an open dagger or knife in the same hand and toasted **“À Louis-Philippe!”** The gesture was understood by many present as a threat against the king. Some later testimony said he added words equivalent to **“s’il trahit”** (“if he betrays [us/the nation]”), which would have made the toast conditional. Dumas said the combination of the king’s name and the open knife was enough for him to leave quickly through the garden to avoid being implicated.

Galois was arrested the next day, **10 May 1831**, at his mother’s residence. A later report stated that he had commissioned the dagger from a cutler on 6 May, saying he needed it urgently for a journey.

### 15 June 1831: first political trial and acquittal

Galois was tried before the Seine assize court on **15 June 1831** for publicly provoking an attack on the king. The prosecution relied on the banquet gesture and toast. Galois did not deny the essential conduct; the dispute concerned the meaning and whether conditional words had followed the toast.

He was represented by counsel, identified in Dupuy’s reconstruction as **Maître Dupont**. The defense emphasized ambiguity and the possible qualifying phrase. The jury acquitted him after a short deliberation.

The case is important because it shows both his willingness to use regicidal symbolism and the limits of the evidence: **he was formally charged and tried, but not convicted.** Later biographical statements calling this a “conviction for threatening the king” are wrong.

### 4 July 1831: Academy report rejects the mathematical memoir

Lacroix and Poisson delivered their report on **4 July 1831**. They did not declare the subject worthless. Their complaint was that the argument was insufficiently clear and developed for them to verify the general criterion Galois claimed. They recommended that the author make the exposition more complete before the Academy could judge it.

The memoir was therefore rejected/returned rather than accepted for publication. Galois regarded this as another instance of the Academy’s inability or unwillingness to understand him. His surviving preface from late 1831 speaks bitterly about repeated lost or rejected manuscripts.

Modern historians disagree over how to weigh the institutional episode. One line, descending from Chevalier and Dupuy, emphasizes catastrophic misrecognition of a great mathematical discovery. Ehrhardt’s social history points out that the Academy handled many unsolicited memoirs, that detailed referee attention was itself nontrivial, and that Galois’s trajectory was not uniquely anomalous among young aspirants. These positions are not mutually exclusive: the paper contained profound mathematics, while its presentation also posed genuine difficulties for contemporary evaluation.

### 14 July 1831: armed demonstration and second arrest

The republican movement attempted to mark Bastille Day. Police went to a residence on rue des Bernardins looking for Galois, but he had already gone out. Around midday he and his friend **Ernest Duchâtelet**, a law student and republican activist, were arrested on or near the **Pont-Neuf**.

They were wearing the uniform of the dissolved National Guard artillery and carrying loaded carbines. Galois also had pistols and a dagger. Duchâtelet drew a caricature or emblem hostile to Louis-Philippe in custody, showing the king in association with a guillotine.

The arrest is documented evidence of armed political militancy. It does not by itself establish an intended assassination or insurrectionary plot. Authorities ultimately pursued the more readily provable offenses involving prohibited uniform and weapons.

### Sainte-Pélagie and conviction

Galois was held for months before trial. On **23 October 1831**, the correctional court convicted him of illegally wearing the artillery uniform and associated weapons offenses; he received **six months’ imprisonment**. Duchâtelet received a shorter sentence. On **3 December 1831** the appeal was rejected/affirmed.

The prison register describes Galois as a **“répétiteur”** and gives an address at **16 rue des Bernardins**. This is one of the few documentary hints about how he may have described his occupation after expulsion from the École normale.

### Prison life: Sainte-Pélagie

Sainte-Pélagie held many political prisoners. The political wing allowed more sociability than a modern high-security prison, and republican prisoners created a subculture of speeches, drinking, argument, songs, and political organization.

**François-Vincent Raspail** later published a vivid memoir portrait of Galois in prison, calling him **“Zanetto.”** Raspail describes fellow prisoners encouraging the inexperienced Galois to drink heavily, followed by an extreme emotional and physical reaction. In the memoir Galois speaks with anguish about his father, love, and death. Dupuy himself cautioned that Raspail’s account seems to have been artistically arranged after the fact. It should not be used as a psychiatric case report.

Galois’s sister Nathalie worried about his appearance and health. Later recollections describe hollow eyes and physical deterioration. There is no adequate evidence for a specific chronic diagnosis or addiction.

During one prison incident, a shot fired from a neighboring building wounded another prisoner. Galois accused a prison employee or authority of responsibility and was placed in solitary confinement. Political prisoners protested until the punishment ended. The episode fed later narratives of political persecution, but there is no secure evidence that the shot was an assassination attempt on Galois personally.

### January 1832: temporary transfer to La Force

Prison records show a temporary transfer from Sainte-Pélagie to **La Force** on **22 January 1832**, with return on **31 January**. The reason is not central in the surviving biography and should not be embellished.

### March 1832: cholera and Maison de santé Faultrier

Paris was struck by the great cholera epidemic of 1832. On **16 March 1832**, Galois was transferred from Sainte-Pélagie to the private **Maison de santé Faultrier**, at **86 rue de l’Oursine** (street naming varies in later sources). The move was associated with health concerns and the epidemic; it is not evidence that Galois himself had cholera.

His formal prison term was approaching its end. Dupuy calculates expiration around **29 April 1832**. Some later biographical summaries loosely describe him as released in May. The distinction between being transferred on parole/health grounds and being wholly free of the sentence has produced inconsistent shorthand.

### “Stéphanie” and the failed love affair

Galois’s papers preserve copied fragments of letters involving a woman named **Stéphanie**, along with his own crossings-out and emotional annotations. A letter to Auguste Chevalier on **25 May 1832** makes clear that Galois had undergone an intense romantic disappointment over approximately the preceding month. He wrote of violent emotions and of feeling that his heart had revolted against his head.

In 1968 **Carlos Alberto Infantozzi** proposed identifying her as **Stéphanie-Félicie (or Félicité) Poterin du Motel**, a young woman connected with the Faultrier establishment. Later archival work has supplied civil-status and family connections that make the identification plausible, but **Robert Bourgne challenged the reading and chronology**, and Olivier Courcelle has emphasized the evidentiary steps involved. The common formula “Stéphanie, the doctor’s daughter” is therefore too confident.

The surviving material establishes an unhappy attachment to a Stéphanie. It does **not** establish with equal certainty her full identity, the nature or duration of the relationship, or whether she deliberately engineered the duel.

### 25 May 1832: evidence against the idea that death was already inevitable

Galois’s letter to Chevalier on 25 May is crucial. Although despairing about love and politics, he also discussed ordinary future plans: he expected to see Chevalier around **1 June** and contemplated travel to the **Dauphiné** around mid-June. This is evidence that five days before the duel he was not writing as a person who had already arranged a certain death.

### 29 May 1832: the pre-duel letters

On 29 May Galois wrote several extraordinary documents.

In a letter **“à tous les républicains,”** he said he was dying the victim of an **“infâme coquette”** and two dupes, that an “odious cancan” had forced the situation, and that he had tried to obtain reconciliation before accepting the provocation. The hostile phrase about the woman is Galois’s own emotionally charged characterization; it is evidence of his state of mind, not an objective finding about her conduct.

In another letter addressed to republican friends, conventionally identified by initials and later as **Napoléon Lebon** and **Vincent Delaunay**, he said he had been **“provoqué par deux patriotes”**—provoked by two patriots—and asked friends to make known that he had fought despite himself after exhausting means of accommodation. He explicitly said of his adversaries that they were acting **“de bonne foi.”**

Those statements weigh heavily against the later claim that he knowingly went to meet royalist police assassins.

The same day he wrote his celebrated scientific testament to **Auguste Chevalier**, summarizing results in the theory of equations and elliptic functions and asking that mathematicians such as Jacobi or Gauss be asked to judge their importance. He also corrected and annotated existing manuscripts.

### What he actually did on the “last night”

The popular story says Galois invented or wrote down all of Galois theory from scratch in one night because he knew he would die at dawn. The manuscripts disprove that version. His central work had been developing since at least 1829, had already been submitted to the Academy in multiple forms, and portions had been published.

What happened on 29 May is still remarkable: he created a compact scientific testament, cross-referenced manuscripts, added corrections and new indications, and tried to organize his intellectual estate under severe time pressure. The famous marginal phrase **“Je n’ai pas le temps”** (“I do not have time”) is genuine in the manuscript tradition, but it belongs to the correction of an existing argument, not the birth of the entire theory in a single nocturnal revelation.

### 30 May 1832: the duel

The duel took place early on **30 May 1832**, near the **Étang de la Glacière**, on the outskirts of Paris in the Gentilly/Glacière area. The exact choreography is not recoverable.

The autopsy evidence indicates a pistol shot from approximately **twenty-five paces**. The ball entered the right side of the abdomen, passed through the intestines multiple times, and lodged toward the left buttock. The trajectory caused severe internal injury and ultimately peritonitis.

Galois was found wounded and transported by a passerby or local man with a cart to **Hôpital Cochin**, arriving around 9:30 a.m. Popular versions say that both the opponent and Galois’s seconds abandoned him to die. The fact that he was found and transported by an outsider is well established; the motives and immediate movements of the participants are not. Dupuy suggested the seconds may have gone in search of help. “Abandoned by everyone” is therefore stronger than the evidence.

### Who shot Galois?

The shooter’s identity remains unresolved.

**Alexandre Dumas**, writing decades later, named **Pescheux (or Pécheux) d’Herbinville**, a republican associated with the former artillery and the political circles surrounding the 1831 prosecutions. Dumas had personally seen Galois at the May 1831 banquet but was not an eyewitness to the duel. A later-discovered handwritten annotation by a person closer in time to the events also reportedly names d’Herbinville, strengthening—but not proving—the identification.

Another line of speculation, associated with **André Dalmas** and later **Tony Rothman**, proposed Galois’s friend **Ernest Duchâtelet**, partly from initials printed in a newspaper. This is problematic because the surviving initials do not fit Ernest cleanly. The Duchâtelet identification remains a hypothesis.

Galois’s own letter says he was confronted or provoked by **two patriots**, without naming them. His cousin **Gabriel Demante**, speaking much later to Dupuy, remembered a family story involving a supposed uncle and supposed fiancé of the woman. That testimony supports a romantic/social trigger but is too late to identify the shooter definitively.

### 31 May 1832: death at Cochin

Galois remained conscious for much of the interval after the duel. His younger brother Alfred came to him. Dupuy reports that he refused the services of a priest. The frequently repeated final admonition to his brother not to cry and that he needed courage to die at twenty is based on later testimony rather than a stenographic deathbed record.

He died at approximately **10 a.m. on 31 May 1832**. The medical cause was acute peritonitis resulting from the gunshot wound and intestinal perforations. The death certificate identifies him as a mathematician and unmarried but incorrectly gives his age as twenty-one.

### 2 June 1832: funeral

The funeral left Cochin around late morning on **2 June 1832** and proceeded to Montparnasse Cemetery. Contemporary republican reporting described Galois as a former artilleryman and member of the Société des Amis du Peuple. Approximately **2,000–3,000** people are said to have attended, including students, former artillerymen, and republican delegations. Police monitored the procession closely because an uprising was feared.

The funeral itself remained essentially calm. General **Jean Maximilien Lamarque** had died on 1 June, and it was **Lamarque’s funeral on 5 June** that became the immediate mass occasion for the June Insurrection of 5–6 June. Later narratives sometimes compress the sequence and claim that riots erupted at Galois’s own funeral. That is inaccurate.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Institutions

Galois’s important institutional affiliations were educational, scientific, military-political, and carceral:

- Family pension at Bourg-la-Reine — family business/environment, not his own company.
- Collège royal Louis-le-Grand — pupil/boarder.
- École polytechnique — unsuccessful candidate twice; never enrolled.
- École préparatoire / École normale — science student, 1829–1830; expelled.
- Académie des sciences — repeated outside submitter, never a member.
- Journals of Gergonne and Férussac — publishing venues.
- Paris National Guard artillery — participant/former artilleryman in the republican milieu.
- Société des Amis du Peuple — radical republican association; membership or active association documented by the end of his life.
- Sainte-Pélagie and La Force — prisoner.
- Maison de santé Faultrier — health/parole transfer residence.
- Hôpital Cochin — place of death.

### Technologies, inventions, patents

No patents, machines, commercial technologies, or engineering inventions are known. His contribution was conceptual mathematics. Modern uses of finite fields in coding theory, cryptography, communications, and computer science are descendants of mathematical structures related to his work, not technologies created or patented by Galois himself.

### Business, finance, investments, property

No company ownership, securities, investments, substantial personal property, financial trusts, or business partnerships are documented for Galois. He briefly advertised a public mathematics course and may have worked as a *répétiteur*, but surviving sources do not establish income figures or a stable commercial practice.

The absence of such records is unsurprising given his age, student status, and repeated institutional/political disruption.

## Political / Religious / Intellectual / Artistic Development

### Political development

#### Family liberalism

Nicolas-Gabriel Galois’s liberal municipal politics formed the background of Évariste’s childhood. The father survived regime changes as mayor, suggesting practical local legitimacy rather than a purely conspiratorial political role.

#### July 1830 radicalization

The decisive political experience was the July Revolution and its aftermath. Galois’s resentment at being prevented from joining the street fighting, combined with the installation of Louis-Philippe rather than a republic, placed him among those who regarded the July settlement as an appropriation of the revolution by the bourgeois monarchy.

#### Republican organization

Galois associated with the Société des Amis du Peuple and the former National Guard artillery. He attended political banquets, appeared armed and in prohibited uniform, and used explicitly anti-monarchical language. His pre-duel letters still identify his social world through the term **“patriotes.”**

#### Attitude toward political violence

The evidence should not be softened. At the May 1831 banquet he combined the king’s name with a knife in a gesture widely read as threatening. In July he was arrested carrying loaded weapons in a politically demonstrative context. In the 25 May 1832 letter to Chevalier he wrote that even if violence were not necessary in his political conviction, it had become necessary in his heart, and spoke of hatred of the present and revenge for suffering.

At the same time, there is no documentary basis for attributing to him a completed assassination attempt, bombing, secret-police operation, or participation in a killing. The May charge of provoking an attack on the king ended in acquittal. His criminal conviction was for the later uniform/weapons episode.

### Saint-Simonian connections but not conversion

Auguste Chevalier became a Saint-Simonian. Saint-Simonian publications and personalities were therefore part of Galois’s immediate intellectual environment. Galois appears to have rejected the movement’s hierarchical social doctrine and its reluctance to embrace revolutionary republicanism. He remained Chevalier’s friend without becoming his political disciple.

### Religious development

His mother supplied a Christian as well as classical education. School records place him in a Catholic institutional culture. By late adolescence Dupuy believed conventional faith had disappeared. At death Galois reportedly refused a priest. No surviving systematic writing by Galois develops an atheistic, deistic, or anti-religious philosophy, so stronger labels go beyond the evidence.

### Educational and intellectual politics

Galois’s anger at educational institutions was not confined to personal grievance. In **“Sur l’enseignement des sciences. Des professeurs, des ouvrages, des examinateurs”** he attacked rote instruction, bad textbooks, examination systems, and the power of examiners to determine intellectual careers. Other manuscript fragments—later editorially titled **“Science, hiérarchie, écoles”** and related texts—show him thinking about the organization of scientific authority itself.

This critique has contributed greatly to the image of Galois as an intellectual rebel. It also reflects a real institutional problem he experienced: a style of research reasoning that was rewarded by Richard in one context and penalized when compressed or underexplained in another.

### Mathematical formation and development

The most visible intellectual influences were **Legendre** and **Lagrange**. The larger technical environment included Gauss’s number theory, Cauchy’s analysis and permutations, Abel’s theory of algebraic equations and elliptic functions, and Jacobi’s elliptic-function research.

Galois’s central innovation was to link the possibility of expressing the roots of an algebraic equation by radicals to structural properties of permutations of the roots. In modern language the resulting object is a **Galois group**, and solvability by radicals corresponds to a group-theoretic solvability condition. His original vocabulary and conceptual organization were not identical to the polished twentieth-century theory taught under his name.

His paper **“Sur la théorie des nombres”** developed a systematic treatment of imaginary roots modulo a prime and constructions now interpreted through finite fields. His later work also addressed primitive equations, permutations, elliptic functions and modular equations, and aspects of analysis.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Nicolas-Gabriel Galois — father

Teacher, pension director, mayor, liberal local politician. His suicide in July 1829 was the largest documented family trauma of Évariste’s adolescence. Later narratives often make it the emotional hinge of Galois’s radical politics; the connection is plausible but cannot be reduced to a single causal mechanism.

### Adélaïde-Marie Demante Galois — mother

First teacher and principal early intellectual influence. Her classical education gave Évariste Latin and Greek before formal school. He was arrested at or near her residence after the May 1831 banquet. She survived him by roughly four decades.

### Nathalie Galois — sister

Her diary or later family papers preserve evidence of concern about Évariste’s prison health and appearance. She belongs to the small circle of familial witnesses whose memories Dupuy used.

### Alfred Galois — younger brother

Visited Évariste at Cochin and became a key posthumous custodian of his memory and papers. Alfred supplied material for the 1848 *Magasin pittoresque* notice and drew a portrait of Évariste from memory. Family stories about political murder entered later biography partly through this channel.

### Louis-Paul-Émile Richard — mathematics teacher and advocate

Richard was the most important school mentor. He recognized Galois’s superiority, encouraged research, preserved his papers, and constitutes direct evidence against the claim that every teacher failed to recognize him.

### Augustin-Louis Cauchy — senior mathematician

Cauchy was not a mentor in the ordinary personal sense, but he was an early senior reader of Galois’s work. The older myth made him the careless or hostile official who lost a memoir. Taton’s archival reconstruction instead suggests serious engagement and probable advice to rework the papers for a larger prize submission.

### Joseph Fourier — Academy secretary

Fourier received the Grand Prix submission and died before adjudication. The paper disappeared. There is no evidence of hostile intent.

### Siméon-Denis Poisson and Sylvestre-François Lacroix — Academy referees

They evaluated the January 1831 memoir and returned a negative report in July. They found the exposition insufficiently complete and clear to verify the general criterion. Their report became central to Galois’s sense of scientific rejection.

### Auguste Chevalier — closest intellectual friend and posthumous executor

Chevalier was a fellow student and probably the most important friendship in Galois’s surviving correspondence. Politically he moved toward Saint-Simonianism while Galois moved toward militant republicanism. Galois nevertheless entrusted him with the 29 May 1832 scientific testament and asked him to circulate the work. Chevalier published the letter in September 1832, wrote the first substantial obituary, preserved manuscripts, and later helped bring them to Joseph Liouville.

### Joseph Liouville — posthumous mathematical mediator

Liouville was not a major relationship during Galois’s life. Posthumously he became decisive. Through papers transmitted by Chevalier and Alfred Galois, Liouville studied the manuscripts, announced their significance in the 1840s, and published the core mathematical works in 1846.

### Ernest Duchâtelet — republican comrade

Law student, companion at the 14 July 1831 demonstration, co-arrested and imprisoned. Later historians proposed him as the duel opponent, but this identification remains unproven and is weakened by the mismatch between his name and initials used in some contemporary reporting.

### François-Vincent Raspail — republican activist and prison memoirist

Raspail knew Galois in the republican milieu and at Sainte-Pélagie. His later memoir is indispensable but literary. It produced some of the most psychologically vivid scenes in Galois biography and therefore must be used with source criticism.

### Alexandre Dumas — witness to one political episode, later biographer-by-memoir

Dumas personally witnessed the 9 May 1831 banquet and described Galois’s knife-and-glass toast. Decades later he also named Pescheux d’Herbinville as the man who killed Galois in the duel. The first statement rests on personal observation; the second does not.

### “Stéphanie” — romantic attachment, identity disputed

A real Stéphanie appears in Galois’s papers. Infantozzi identified her as Stéphanie-Félicie Poterin du Motel, connected to the Faultrier household. The identification is plausible but disputed. Her role in the duel is unknown.

### Pescheux/Pécheux d’Herbinville — probable but unproven duel candidate

Dumas later named him as the shooter. Additional later archival annotation reportedly gives the same name. He was a republican, which fits Galois’s own reference to patriotic adversaries. No surviving contemporary judicial finding establishes the identity.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

### Institutional expulsion — documented, not criminal

Galois was expelled from the École normale in December 1830 after his anonymous newspaper attack on Guigniault. This was an administrative/disciplinary act, not a criminal judgment.

### May 1831 royal-threat prosecution — charge and acquittal

- **Documented conduct:** toast to Louis-Philippe while displaying an open dagger/knife at a republican banquet.
- **Formal legal status:** arrested and indicted/tried for public provocation to an attack on the king.
- **Outcome:** **acquitted 15 June 1831.**
- **Disputed detail:** whether he audibly added a conditional qualification such as “if he betrays.”

### July 1831 weapons/uniform case — conviction

- **Documented conduct:** public appearance in uniform of the dissolved artillery; loaded carbine; additional weapons found on Galois.
- **Outcome:** conviction in October 1831; six-month sentence; appeal unsuccessful in December.
- **Interpretive caution:** political policing was clearly involved, but the conviction itself was not fabricated from nothing; the prohibited uniform and weapons were real.

### Violence in political writing

Galois’s May 1832 letter contains explicit rhetoric of hatred, revenge, and violence. This is documentary evidence of his emotional-political state. It is not equivalent to proof that he had committed clandestine political violence.

### Duel — private violence and unresolved criminal dimension

Dueling was illegal or legally actionable under the period’s public-order regime, though socially common. Galois died from a pistol duel. Surviving sources do not provide a completed prosecution or judicial determination of the opponent, motive, or responsibility. The destruction of Paris police/judicial archives in 1871 limits later reconstruction.

### Fraud, corruption, money laundering, business crime

No evidence located in the documentary record connects Galois with fraud, corruption, embezzlement, money laundering, financial deception, or organized business crime. Such categories are largely anachronistic to his documented activities.

### Intelligence connections / police manipulation

Police watched republican activists, arrested Galois, and monitored his funeral. A later **family allegation** held that royal police manipulated or arranged the duel through hired provocateurs or swordsmen. No surviving document proves that allegation. Galois’s own last letters, which describe his adversaries as patriots and acting in good faith, are evidence against the simplest police-assassination version. Because relevant police archives were later destroyed, the question cannot be resolved by a complete surviving case file.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

The Galois legend formed early and grew because several elements were genuinely dramatic: a brilliant adolescent mathematician, political arrests, lost manuscripts, imprisonment, mysterious love affair, fatal duel, last-night letters, and delayed recognition. Later writers often connected these true elements with invented causal links.

### 1. “Cauchy lost or suppressed Galois’s first memoir”

**What the story says:** Galois gave a revolutionary manuscript to Cauchy, who either negligently lost it or deliberately suppressed it, depriving the young genius of recognition.

**When and where it appears:** The grievance appears in early memorial traditions, including Chevalier’s posthumous narrative, and was amplified by Dupuy and twentieth-century popular biographies, especially accounts built around the theme of institutional stupidity.

**Who circulated it:** Friends and family memory, later biographers, and popular histories of mathematics.

**Evidence:** Academy registers demonstrate two 1829 submissions and Cauchy’s involvement. René Taton found evidence that Cauchy was preparing to report on the work and that Galois probably withdrew or replaced it while preparing the Grand Prix submission. There is no documentary evidence of malicious suppression. The manuscripts themselves are lost.

**Effect on reputation:** The story became one of the canonical examples of the “misunderstood genius versus establishment.” Modern scholarship has substantially revised it.

### 2. “Fourier lost Galois’s Grand Prix memoir”

**What the story says:** Galois submitted a major memoir for the Academy prize; Fourier took it home, died, and the paper disappeared.

**Evidence status:** **Substantially documented.** The paper was submitted; Fourier was Academy secretary and had it; Fourier died before adjudication; the manuscript was not in the competition dossier afterward. What is not documented is negligence, theft, or deliberate suppression by Fourier.

**Effect:** Because this episode is real, it has often been merged with the weaker Cauchy story into a single narrative in which “the Academy lost Galois’s work twice.”

### 3. The Polytechnique examiner and the thrown eraser/rag

**What the story says:** During his second failed Polytechnique examination, Galois became exasperated by an examiner’s stupidity and threw a blackboard eraser, rag, or other object at him.

**Appearance/circulation:** Dupuy records it in 1896 explicitly as **tradition**. Even the examiner’s identity was uncertain. Twentieth-century biographies often retell it as fact.

**Evidence:** No contemporary examination report confirming the projectile has been located. Galois certainly failed twice and certainly disliked examiners. Those facts make the anecdote psychologically plausible but do not authenticate it.

**Effect:** It condenses his career into an irresistible image: genius literally throwing an object at incomprehension.

### 4. “He wrote all of Galois theory the night before he died”

**What the story says:** Knowing he would be killed at dawn, Galois stayed awake all night inventing and writing the entire theory that now bears his name, repeatedly scribbling “I have no time.”

**Appearance/circulation:** The story grew from the genuine 29 May scientific testament and manuscript annotations. Romantic biographies, especially E. T. Bell’s *Men of Mathematics*, transformed it into a dramatic creation myth. Film and popular articles further stabilized the scene.

**Evidence:** False in its strong form. Galois had developed the theory over several years, submitted versions in 1829, 1830, and 1831, and published related results. On 29 May 1832 he summarized, revised, and annotated already-existing work. The phrase **“Je n’ai pas le temps”** is genuine, but its context is a local incompleteness in an argument rather than proof of a single-night invention.

**Effect:** Probably the most influential Galois myth. It transformed manuscript revision into a secular deathbed revelation and helped make him an archetype of doomed youthful genius.

### 5. “The duel was a royal-police assassination”

**What the story says:** The love affair was a cover; agents of Louis-Philippe or police provocateurs arranged the duel to eliminate a dangerous republican.

**Appearance/circulation:** A family version was transmitted through Alfred Galois and later republican or romantic biographies. Twentieth-century political retellings sometimes presented it with certainty.

**Evidence for:** Galois was a known radical under police surveillance; he had twice been arrested; political provocation was real in Restoration/July Monarchy policing; important police archives later disappeared in 1871.

**Evidence against:** Galois’s own letters say he was provoked by **two patriots**, that he tried to reconcile, and that the men acted **in good faith**. The immediate trigger is described by Galois himself as involving a woman and gossip. No surviving police order, payment, agent file, or confession supports assassination.

**Status:** **Unverified conspiracy narrative; not established.** Complete disproval is impossible because the evidence base is incomplete, but the strongest surviving first-person evidence points elsewhere.

**Effect:** It turned Galois from a republican killed in a murky private duel into a political martyr consciously murdered by the state.

### 6. “The opponent was definitely Pescheux d’Herbinville”

**What the story says:** Republican **Pescheux/Pécheux d’Herbinville** shot Galois.

**Appearance:** Alexandre Dumas names him in memoir material published decades after the events. Dumas had known the political milieu and had witnessed the 1831 banquet. Later scholarship found another handwritten attribution of the same name in archival material.

**Evidence:** Multiple later attributions make d’Herbinville a serious candidate. No known contemporary legal record conclusively identifies him.

**Status:** **Probable in some modern reconstructions, but unresolved.**

### 7. “The opponent was definitely Ernest Duchâtelet”

**What the story says:** Galois’s friend and prison comrade Duchâtelet fought him over Stéphanie.

**Appearance:** Advanced in twentieth-century reconstruction, especially associated with André Dalmas and later Tony Rothman.

**Evidence:** The theory drew partly on initials in a press notice and on the known closeness of Duchâtelet to Galois. Later scrutiny notes that Duchâtelet’s first name was Ernest, which does not fit the relevant initial as cleanly as the theory requires. Galois also wrote of two patriots, not a uniquely identified former co-defendant.

**Status:** **Hypothesis, not established fact.**

### 8. “Stéphanie was certainly Stéphanie-Félicie Poterin du Motel”

**What the story says:** The woman was the daughter or relative of the physician/manager at the Faultrier health establishment and can be fully identified as Stéphanie-Félicie Poterin du Motel.

**Appearance:** Proposed by Carlos Alberto Infantozzi in 1968 after examining crossed-out manuscript material and civil/family records.

**Evidence for:** Name correspondence, connection with the establishment, and civil-status evidence make the identification plausible.

**Evidence against/limits:** Robert Bourgne contested the reading; the chronology and exact family relationship to the institution have been misunderstood in later retellings. Olivier Courcelle’s research supports taking the proposal seriously without converting it into certainty.

**Status:** **Plausible but disputed identification.**

### 9. “Stéphanie deliberately lured him into a fatal trap”

**What the story says:** A calculating woman seduced or deceived Galois and engineered the duel.

**Origin:** Galois’s own bitter phrase **“infâme coquette”**, combined with later attempts to make the duel a coherent love triangle.

**Evidence:** We know he felt rejected and blamed a woman and gossip. We do not possess her full account, and the copied letter fragments do not prove an assassination scheme or deliberate trap.

**Status:** **Galois’s accusation/interpretation, not an established finding.**

**Effect:** It created a misogynistic romantic archetype—the fatal woman destroying the genius—that later fiction readily exploited.

### 10. “Galois deliberately fired into the air”

**What the story says:** Galois refused to shoot his friend/opponent and intentionally discharged away from him, accepting death.

**Evidence:** No reliable eyewitness record establishes the direction of Galois’s shot. Versions of self-sacrifice fit later family/political mythology and some reconstructions of an unequal duel.

**Status:** **Unverified legend.**

### 11. “He was simply abandoned by both sides after the duel”

**What the story says:** Opponent and seconds left Galois bleeding alone until a peasant happened by.

**Evidence:** An outsider found him and transported him to Cochin. The movements of the seconds are unclear; they may have sought transport or medical help. A later family account implies continued contact by at least one participant.

**Status:** The finding-by-outsider is documented; total malicious abandonment is **unproven**.

### 12. “Galois’s funeral caused the June Insurrection”

**What the story says:** His funeral became a riot or immediately triggered the republican uprising.

**Evidence:** His 2 June funeral was a large, heavily policed republican gathering but remained comparatively calm. The major uprising began around **General Lamarque’s funeral on 5 June**.

**Status:** **Chronological conflation.**

### 13. “Every scientific authority rejected or ignored him”

**What the story says:** No teacher or senior mathematician recognized Galois until after his death.

**Evidence against:** Richard recognized his superiority; journals published five mathematical papers; Cauchy dealt with his submissions; Poisson encouraged a new version; Lacroix and Poisson produced a formal report; his École préparatoire mathematics examiner ranked him highly. He nevertheless suffered repeated failures, a lost prize manuscript, and final rejection of his central memoir.

**Status:** **Mythical simplification of a more contradictory record.**

### 14. “Galois invented group theory in the full modern sense”

**What the story says:** The abstract theory of groups was created complete by Galois.

**Evidence:** Galois made groups of substitutions central to the theory of algebraic equations and gave extraordinarily fertile structural insights. But permutation theory also developed through Cauchy and others; abstract group theory was systematized later through Cayley, Jordan, Dedekind, Weber, and others. Modern Galois theory is the result of later reformulation as well as Galois’s own manuscripts.

**Status:** **Foundational attribution that becomes anachronistic when stated too strongly.** “Founder of Galois theory” is accurate; “sole inventor of modern abstract group theory” is not.

### 15. “Galois proved the simplicity of all alternating groups in the modern theorem”

Peter Neumann specifically warns against a textbook legend that credits Galois with the modern theorem on the simplicity of alternating groups in the form later standard. Galois handled particular substitution-group structures crucial to equations, but the later theorem should not be retroactively inserted into his text.

## Works by the Person

The surviving corpus is small in page count but unusually complicated bibliographically because important manuscripts were rejected, lost, revised, published posthumously, and re-edited from originals.

### A. Mathematical works published during Galois’s lifetime

1. **“Démonstration d’un théorème sur les fractions continues périodiques.”**  
   *Annales de mathématiques pures et appliquées* (Gergonne), vol. 19 (1828–1829), pp. 294–301. Published 1829.  
   **Status:** genuine authored publication. Topic: periodic continued fractions.

2. **“Analyse d’un mémoire sur la résolution algébrique des équations.”**  
   *Bulletin des sciences mathématiques, physiques et chimiques* (Férussac), vol. 13 (April 1830), pp. 271–272.  
   **Status:** genuine authored publication/abstract. Announces work on algebraic solvability.

3. **“Note sur la résolution des équations numériques.”**  
   Férussac’s *Bulletin*, vol. 13 (June 1830), pp. 413–414.  
   **Status:** genuine authored publication.

4. **“Sur la théorie des nombres.”**  
   Férussac’s *Bulletin*, vol. 13 (June 1830), pp. 428–436.  
   **Status:** genuine authored publication. Important for the arithmetic structures later called finite/Galois fields.

5. **“Notes sur quelques points d’analyse.”**  
   *Annales de mathématiques pures et appliquées*, vol. 21 (December 1830), pp. 182–184.  
   **Status:** genuine authored publication.

### B. Educational/political prose published during his lifetime

6. **Anonymous letter signed “Un élève de l’École Normale.”**  
   *Gazette des Écoles*, 5 December 1830.  
   **Status:** accepted attribution to Galois; political-institutional polemic against Guigniault; catalyst for expulsion.

7. **“Sur l’enseignement des sciences. Des professeurs, des ouvrages, des examinateurs”** / commonly cited as **“Lettre sur l’enseignement des sciences.”**  
   *Gazette des Écoles*, no. 110, 2 January 1831.  
   **Status:** genuine authored polemic. Critique of scientific pedagogy, textbooks, and examinations.

### C. Academy submissions and major lost works

8. **Two algebraic memoirs submitted in May and June 1829.**  
   **Status:** genuine submissions documented in Académie registers; manuscripts lost. Exact relationship between their texts and later memoirs cannot be reconstructed with certainty.

9. **Grand Prix memoir on equations, submitted early 1830.**  
   **Status:** genuine submission; manuscript lost after Joseph Fourier’s death before prize adjudication. Later papers reuse or reconstruct related ideas, but no surviving copy allows exact comparison.

### D. The principal posthumously published mathematical memoirs

10. **“Mémoire sur les conditions de résolubilité des équations par radicaux.”**  
    Dated/submitted January 1831; Academy report 4 July 1831; first major publication by Joseph Liouville in 1846.  
    **Status:** genuine Galois manuscript, rejected during his lifetime, posthumously edited. This is the principal “First Mémoire” of Galois theory.

11. **“Des équations primitives qui sont solubles par radicaux.”**  
    Usually called the **Second Mémoire**; published by Liouville in 1846.  
    **Status:** genuine posthumous mathematical manuscript.

### E. Testamentary scientific letter

12. **Letter to Auguste Chevalier, 29 May 1832** (“Lettre testamentaire” / “Lettre-testament”).  
    First published at Galois’s explicit request in the *Revue encyclopédique*, September 1832, pp. 568–576; republished by Liouville and later editors.  
    **Status:** genuine letter and scientific summary. It points Chevalier toward results in equations, groups/permutations, and elliptic functions and asks for the judgment of leading mathematicians.

### F. Political/personal letters of 29 May 1832

13. **Letter “à tous les républicains.”**  
    **Status:** genuine pre-duel letter, known through the manuscript/posthumous transmission. Contains his account of the romantic/social circumstances of the duel.

14. **Letter to republican friends (“à N. L… et V. D…”).**  
    Later identifications commonly give Napoléon Lebon and Vincent Delaunay.  
    **Status:** genuine pre-duel letter. Says he was provoked by two patriots and had attempted reconciliation.

### G. Other surviving letters

The critical editions contain a small corpus—roughly nine known letters in traditional counts, depending on editorial definition—including letters to Auguste Chevalier, institutional authorities, family/friends, and the president of the Académie des sciences. Important among them are:

- letter to Chevalier from Sainte-Pélagie, December 1831;
- letter to Chevalier, **25 May 1832**, on love, suffering, violence, friendship, and future plans;
- letter to the president of the Académie des sciences, **31 March 1831**, pressing for attention to his submitted memoir;
- the pre-duel letters of 29 May.

### H. Manuscript fragments and texts first systematically published by Jules Tannery

Jules Tannery’s 1906–1907 work brought a large group of manuscripts and fragments into print. Titles in later critical editions include:

- **“Discours préliminaire”**
- **“Projet de publication”**
- **“Note sur Abel”**
- **“Préface”** / preface to proposed mathematical memoirs
- **“Discussion sur les progrès de l’analyse pure”**
- **“Fragments”**
- **“Science, hiérarchie, écoles”**
- **“Catalogue, note sur la théorie des équations”**
- **“Recherches sur la théorie des permutations et des équations algébriques”**
- texts conventionally titled **“Comment la théorie des équations algébriques…”** and **“Comment la théorie des équations dépend de celle des permutations”**
- **“Additions au second mémoire”**
- **“Mémoire sur la division des fonctions elliptiques de première espèce”**
- **“Note sur l’intégration des équations linéaires”**
- **“Recherches sur les équations du second degré”**

Some of these labels are editorial descriptions of unfinished manuscripts rather than titles Galois formally prepared for publication. The critical edition by Bourgne and Azra is the safest place to distinguish autograph title, inferred date, fragment, draft, and finished memoir.

### I. Major collected editions and translations

- **Joseph Liouville (ed.), “Œuvres mathématiques d’Évariste Galois,”** *Journal de mathématiques pures et appliquées*, vol. 11, 1846, pp. 381–444 (bibliographies differ slightly on final pagination depending on what is counted). First decisive collected publication.
- **Émile Picard / Société Mathématique de France, *Œuvres mathématiques d’Évariste Galois*, 1897.** Reprint of the Liouville corpus with historical framing.
- **Jules Tannery, “Manuscrits et papiers inédits de Galois,”** *Bulletin des sciences mathématiques*, 1906–1907, followed by broader 1908 publication work.
- **Robert Bourgne and Jean-Pierre Azra (eds.), *Écrits et mémoires mathématiques d’Évariste Galois*, 1962; revised/second edition and later reprint.** First integral modern critical edition of manuscripts and publications.
- **Peter M. Neumann, *The Mathematical Writings of Évariste Galois*, 2011; corrected second printing 2013.** French texts with English translation, introductions, notes, and reception history.
- **H. M. Edwards, *Galois Theory* (1984).** Not a collected edition, but historically oriented and influential for English-language access to the First Mémoire.
- **Eugen Netto / Heinrich Weber-era German reception and earlier German translations:** important in internationalizing Galois’s algebra in the late nineteenth century. The 1889 German collection/translation by H. Maser is a notable bibliographic milestone.

## Films, Documentaries, Interviews, and Archival Material

### Alexandre Astruc film

**Évariste Galois / Évariste Galois (L’éloge des mathématiques)**, directed and written by **Alexandre Astruc**, French short/medium-length film produced 1964 and catalogued/released in 1965 in several databases; French television/public release chronology is also given as 1967 in some catalogues. **José Varela** plays Galois. Music by **Antoine Duhamel**; cinematography by **Jacques Mercanton** (with Emmanuel Machuel also credited by Unifrance); narration by **Jean Négroni**.

The film focuses on Galois’s final night and duel rather than offering a complete biography. Contemporary *Le Monde* coverage explicitly framed it as a meditation on the final hours, amplifying the mythic image of the mathematician racing death. It is therefore valuable not merely as a “biopic” but as a document in the twentieth-century manufacture of the Galois legend.

### Audio/podcast treatments

**Travels Through Time**, “The Revolutions of Évariste Galois (1831),” with **Marcus du Sautoy** (2020), uses three scenes from the political and mathematical life to situate him in revolutionary Paris. It is a modern popular-history treatment rather than a primary source.

French radio and television have produced numerous Galois programs, but catalog records are unevenly accessible. Popular programs should be separated from archival holdings and from scholarly source editions.

## Death / Later Life / Present Status

Galois had no “later life”: he died on **31 May 1832**, less than a year after his twentieth birthday.

### Medical sequence

- 30 May: pistol wound to abdomen in duel.
- Transported to Hôpital Cochin later that morning.
- Severe intestinal perforation and internal trauma.
- Progressive peritonitis.
- 31 May, about 10 a.m.: death.

The autopsy record is one of the strongest pieces of evidence about the duel itself because the wound trajectory constrains but does not fully reconstruct the position of the participants.

### Priest and last words

Dupuy says Galois refused a priest. Reports of his final words to Alfred are moving but transmitted later. They should be cited as family/biographical testimony, not as a verbatim medical record.

### Burial and grave

The funeral took place 2 June at Montparnasse Cemetery. Early accounts describe burial in a common grave. The precise burial place is not now a reliably identifiable individual grave, which has itself contributed to the symbolic rather than monumental character of his posthumous cult.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### 1832: Chevalier begins the posthumous rescue

Galois’s first posthumous advocate was Auguste Chevalier. In September 1832 the *Revue encyclopédique* published the scientific testament as Galois had requested and Chevalier’s substantial obituary. The publication preserved both a mathematical program and a political-romantic biography almost immediately after death.

The letter did not produce immediate broad mathematical recognition.

### 1840s: Liouville and validation

Chevalier and Alfred Galois preserved and transmitted the papers. **Joseph Liouville** eventually studied them. In the 1840s he publicly announced that Galois’s work contained profound results on algebraic equations. In **1846** Liouville published the core memoirs in his *Journal de mathématiques pures et appliquées*.

This was the decisive transition from private/political memory to durable mathematical canon. Liouville also intervened editorially, clarifying presentation while preserving the substance of the manuscripts.

### 1848 and early heroic biography

The anonymous *Magasin pittoresque* article of 1848, informed by Alfred Galois and others, gave the public a compact life narrative and a memorable portrait. This was a crucial step in converting the dead mathematician into a cultural figure.

### 1850s–1870s: theory enters mathematics

Galois’s ideas circulated through a network rather than in a straight line. Important mediators included **Enrico Betti**, **Joseph-Alfred Serret**, **Leopold Kronecker**, and others. **Camille Jordan** made permutation groups central in his monumental **Traité des substitutions et des équations algébriques** (1870), explicitly placing his work in Galois’s lineage. Jordan’s synthesis helped connect the Galois and Cauchy streams of permutation theory.

The eventual theory was not simply Galois’s manuscript reproduced unchanged. It was translated, expanded, reorganized, and taught using concepts developed later.

### Dedekind, Weber, Netto, and structural reformulation

In German mathematics, **Richard Dedekind**, **Heinrich Weber**, and **Eugen Netto** contributed to the reconstruction and abstraction of equation theory and groups. By the late nineteenth century, a form recognizable as modern Galois theory was becoming teachable and textbook-standard.

British reception was slower. **Arthur Cayley** knew the language of groups and cited Galois, but the route by which abstract group theory developed in Britain differed from the French/German equation tradition.

### Sophus Lie and the 1895–1897 canonization

At the centenary of the École normale in **1895**, **Sophus Lie** spoke on the influence of Galois on the development of mathematics. The event symbolically brought the once-expelled pupil into the institutional pantheon.

Dupuy’s 1896 archival biography and the Société Mathématique de France/Picard edition of 1897 reinforced the canonization. Mathematical immortality and revolutionary biography now supported each other.

### Tannery and the manuscripts

Jules Tannery’s 1906–1907 publications exposed parts of the archive that Liouville had not printed. This changed the object of study: “Galois” no longer meant only the polished algebraic memoirs but also notes on analysis, Abel, education, hierarchy, elliptic functions, and unfinished projects.

### Twentieth-century romantic cult

E. T. Bell’s *Men of Mathematics* transformed the Galois life into one of the most famous legends in scientific biography: misunderstood schoolboy, obtuse examiners, lost papers, revolutionary passion, fatal woman, duel, and one feverish final night of mathematical creation. Leopold Infeld and later novelists extended the pattern.

This mythology was not created from nothing. Nearly every component has a documentary kernel. Its distortion came from converting uncertain or multistage events into single dramatic scenes and making every institutional encounter a morality play.

### Political cult

Galois also became a republican and left-wing martyr figure. Schools, streets, and institutions—especially in municipalities with strong republican or later left traditions—adopted his name. The political cult emphasized the former artilleryman and prisoner; the mathematical cult emphasized the creator rejected by academies. In public memory the two identities often fused.

### Modern historiographical revision

From Taton through Rothman, Ehrhardt, Brechenmacher, Albrecht, Weber, Neumann, Courcelle and others, historians have reconstructed how the legend was produced. The revision does not reduce his mathematical originality. Instead it replaces a binary “genius versus fools” story with a more historically specific picture:

- teachers could recognize him while exam systems still failed him;
- journals could publish him while the Academy still rejected a central memoir;
- Cauchy could take him seriously while the 1829 manuscripts still disappeared from the surviving record;
- his own exposition could be genuinely compressed while the underlying ideas were extraordinarily deep;
- republican surveillance could be real while a police murder conspiracy remains unproved;
- a real romantic crisis could precede the duel while the woman’s identity and shooter remain uncertain.

### Mathematical legacy in modern terms

Direct or lineal legacies include:

- **Galois theory** — relation between field extensions and symmetry groups, historically rooted in the solvability of polynomial equations by radicals.
- **Galois group** — modern term for groups attached to algebraic/field extensions.
- **Finite fields / Galois fields** — finite algebraic fields, often denoted GF(q), central in coding theory, cryptography, combinatorics, and digital communications.
- **Permutation-group theory** — Galois’s substitution groups were fundamental to the nineteenth-century development of group theory.
- **Solvable groups and solvability by radicals** — modern structural formulation descended from the criterion he developed.
- Later eponymic extensions—differential Galois theory, Grothendieck-style Galois categories, inverse Galois theory, Galois cohomology, etc.—belong to later mathematics inspired by the conceptual lineage, not to Galois’s own corpus.

### Historical significance

The extraordinary feature is not merely that Galois “had ideas before his time.” His surviving work participated in a live nineteenth-century problem—the algebraic solution of equations—and was gradually transformed by other mathematicians into a language of structure and symmetry. His fame is therefore the product of **both an original mathematical intervention and a long process of editorial, pedagogical, institutional, political, and literary reconstruction**.

## Chronology

### 1811
- **25 Oct:** Évariste Galois born at Bourg-la-Reine to Nicolas-Gabriel Galois and Adélaïde-Marie Demante.
- **26 Oct:** Birth formally declared/registered.

### 1811–1824
- Educated at home principally by his mother in French, Latin, Greek, classical literature, moral/religious subjects.

### 1824
- **Feb / 1 Apr:** Modern archival reconstruction places registration and entry as boarder in *quatrième* at Louis-le-Grand. Older biographies date entry to 1823.
- School disciplinary crisis; Galois is not among the major expelled pupils.

### 1824–1826
- Strong classical results; prizes/accessits in Latin and Greek, including Concours général recognition.

### 1826–1827
- Difficulty in *seconde*/rhetoric; repeats or is returned to *seconde*.
- Begins serious mathematics with Vernier.

### 1827
- Reads Legendre and Lagrange intensively; mathematics becomes dominant interest.
- Wins high school/Concours général mathematics distinction.

### 1828
- First unsuccessful École polytechnique entrance attempt.
- Autumn: enters *mathématiques spéciales* under Louis-Paul-Émile Richard, who recognizes exceptional talent.

### 1829
- Early year: first paper on periodic continued fractions appears in Gergonne’s *Annales*.
- **25 May and 1 Jun:** algebraic papers presented to Académie des sciences; Cauchy/Poinsot associated as referees. Manuscripts later lost; modern evidence suggests withdrawal/reworking rather than simple Cauchy negligence.
- **2 Jul:** father Nicolas-Gabriel commits suicide in Paris after local political-clerical defamation campaign.
- Summer: second École polytechnique failure; projectile-at-examiner story remains legend.
- Aug/autumn: competes successfully for École préparatoire.
- **25 Oct / Nov:** admitted/enters École préparatoire; date varies by administrative chronology.
- **2 Dec:** fails first *baccalauréat ès lettres* attempt.
- **14 Dec:** passes letters baccalaureate.
- Late Dec: passes science baccalaureate.

### 1830
- **20 Feb:** signs University service commitment.
- Late Feb (probable): submits revised equations memoir for Académie Grand Prix.
- **Apr:** “Analyse d’un mémoire sur la résolution algébrique des équations” published.
- **16 May:** Joseph Fourier dies; Galois’s Grand Prix manuscript disappears from the competition dossier.
- **Jun:** “Note sur la résolution des équations numériques” and “Sur la théorie des nombres” published.
- **27–29 Jul:** July Revolution. Galois confined at school; tries unsuccessfully to join events; deeply resentful afterward.
- **6 Aug:** École préparatoire restored/renamed École normale.
- Autumn: increasing association with radical republicans/Société des Amis du Peuple milieu.
- **5 Dec:** anonymous anti-Guigniault letter published in *Gazette des Écoles*.
- **9 Dec:** Galois expelled from École normale.
- **Dec:** “Notes sur quelques points d’analyse” published.

### 1831
- **3/4 Jan:** expulsion formally confirmed.
- **2 Jan:** educational polemic “Sur l’enseignement des sciences…” published (bibliographic calendars and the surrounding expulsion chronology can make the sequencing look counterintuitive; issue date is 2 Jan 1831).
- **13 Jan:** advertised public course begins at Caillot, 5 rue de Sorbonne.
- **17 Jan:** submits “Mémoire sur les conditions de résolubilité des équations par radicaux” to Académie; Lacroix and Poisson referees.
- **31 Mar:** writes to Academy president seeking action on memoir.
- **9 May:** republican banquet at Vendanges de Bourgogne; knife-and-glass toast to Louis-Philippe.
- **10 May:** arrested.
- **15 Jun:** tried for provocation against king; acquitted.
- **4 Jul:** Lacroix–Poisson report rejects/returns mathematical memoir as insufficiently clear/developed.
- **14 Jul:** arrested with Ernest Duchâtelet while in dissolved artillery uniform and carrying loaded weapons.
- **23 Oct:** convicted; Galois receives six months’ imprisonment.
- **3 Dec:** appeal unsuccessful.
- Late 1831: writes mathematical/political fragments and prefaces in prison.

### 1832
- **22 Jan:** transferred from Sainte-Pélagie to La Force.
- **31 Jan:** returned to Sainte-Pélagie.
- **16 Mar:** transferred to Maison de santé Faultrier during cholera epidemic/health concerns.
- **29 Apr (Dupuy’s calculation):** nominal expiration of prison term; later summaries sometimes say release in May.
- Spring: romantic involvement/disappointment with a woman named Stéphanie; full identity disputed.
- **25 May:** writes Auguste Chevalier about emotional crisis and also future plans for early/mid-June.
- **29 May:** writes letters to republicans and scientific testament to Chevalier; annotates mathematical manuscripts.
- **30 May:** pistol duel near Étang de la Glacière; shot through abdomen; transported to Hôpital Cochin.
- **31 May, ~10 a.m.:** dies of peritonitis.
- **2 Jun:** funeral to Montparnasse Cemetery; roughly 2,000–3,000 republican/student mourners under police surveillance.
- **5–6 Jun:** June Insurrection in Paris associated chiefly with General Lamarque’s funeral, not Galois’s funeral.
- **Sep:** Chevalier publishes Galois’s scientific testament and obituary in *Revue encyclopédique*.

### 1840s
- Chevalier and Alfred Galois transmit manuscripts to Joseph Liouville.
- Liouville recognizes their mathematical importance.

### 1846
- Liouville publishes the central mathematical corpus in *Journal de mathématiques pures et appliquées*.

### 1848
- *Magasin pittoresque* publishes influential biographical notice and Alfred Galois’s posthumous portrait.

### 1870
- Camille Jordan’s *Traité des substitutions et des équations algébriques* makes Galois’s substitution-group approach central to a major systematic theory.

### 1895
- Sophus Lie lectures on Galois’s influence during École normale centenary commemorations.

### 1896
- Paul Dupuy publishes the foundational archival biography.

### 1897
- Société Mathématique de France / Émile Picard issues Galois’s mathematical works in book form.

### 1906–1908
- Jules Tannery publishes large portions of the remaining manuscripts and papers.

### 1937 onward
- E. T. Bell’s *Men of Mathematics* enormously amplifies the romantic “last night / misunderstood genius” legend.

### 1962
- Robert Bourgne and Jean-Pierre Azra publish a modern integral critical edition of the writings and manuscripts.

### 1964–1965/1967
- Alexandre Astruc makes *Évariste Galois (L’éloge des mathématiques)*, a film centered on the final night and duel; catalogues vary between production/festival/television release dates.

### 1968
- Carlos Alberto Infantozzi publishes a major reconsideration of the death and proposes the identification of Stéphanie as Poterin du Motel.

### 1971
- René Taton’s Cauchy–Galois archival study substantially revises the “Cauchy lost the memoir” story.

### 1982
- Tony Rothman publishes “Genius and Biographers,” influential in separating biography from legend while proposing some disputed reconstructions of his own.

### 2008–2011
- Caroline Ehrhardt publishes major archival and social-historical studies of Galois’s schooling, Academy career, and later icon-making.

### 2011
- Bicentenary commemorations across France and abroad.
- Peter M. Neumann publishes the major bilingual critical edition *The Mathematical Writings of Évariste Galois*.
- New historiography examines Galois as both mathematical actor and manufactured public icon.
