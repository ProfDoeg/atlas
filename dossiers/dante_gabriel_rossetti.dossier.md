# Dante Gabriel Rossetti: Research Dossier

## Scope and Evidentiary Note

Dante Gabriel Rossetti left several overlapping documentary records: approximately 5,800 surviving letters to more than 330 recipients; manuscripts, proofs, and first editions; paintings, drawings, designs, and photographs; business records; recollections by relatives and associates; and posthumous biographies. The principal digital documentary resource is Jerome J. McGann’s *Rossetti Archive*, completed in 2008.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact**: supported by contemporary records, surviving correspondence, manuscripts, official records, or securely catalogued objects.
- **Reported fact**: stated by a generally reliable later witness or scholarly reconstruction but not fully proved by surviving primary documentation.
- **Disputed interpretation**: the evidence is accepted, but its meaning remains contested.
- **Allegation**: a claim made by an identifiable person without adequate independent proof.
- **Rumor or myth**: a circulated story that is unsupported or contradicted by available evidence.

No criminal conviction, formal prosecution, substantiated fraud, intelligence connection, political violence, patent, or independently incorporated business controlled by Rossetti was identified.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal line

Rossetti’s paternal grandfather, **Nicola Rossetti** (born 1740), was a blacksmith of Vasto in Abruzzo Citeriore, then in the Kingdom of Naples.

His father, **Gabriele Pasquale Giuseppe Rossetti** (1783–1854), was a poet, Dante scholar, political activist, and member or associate of the Carbonari. After the Bourbon restoration suppressed the Neapolitan constitutional movement of 1820–21, Gabriele was proscribed. He escaped to Malta with British assistance and arrived in London in 1824. He taught Italian and in 1831 became professor of Italian language and literature at King’s College London.

Gabriele’s political exile and idiosyncratic interpretation of Dante—he read the poet as encoding an anti-papal, initiatory political religion—gave his children a household culture combining Italian nationalism, medieval literature, biblical symbolism, anticlerical politics, and intense textual interpretation.

### Maternal line

Rossetti’s mother was **Frances Mary Lavinia Polidori** (1800–1886), daughter of the Italian-born writer, translator, and printer **Gaetano Polidori** and Anna Maria Pierce.

Frances’s brother, **John William Polidori** (1795–1821), had been Lord Byron’s physician and was the author of *The Vampyre* (1819), an important early literary-vampire narrative. He died before Rossetti’s birth. The family connection later contributed to Gothic mythology surrounding the Rossettis, although it provides no evidence for supernatural claims.

### Parents’ marriage and children

Gabriele Rossetti and Frances Polidori married on 10 April 1826. Their surviving children were:

1. **Maria Francesca Rossetti** (1827–1876), writer and later an Anglican nun.
2. **Gabriel Charles Dante—Dante Gabriel—Rossetti** (1828–1882).
3. **William Michael Rossetti** (1829–1919), civil servant, critic, editor, memoirist, and documentary custodian of the family.
4. **Christina Georgina Rossetti** (1830–1894), poet.

The household was multilingual and literary. Frances was a devout Anglican; Gabriele was politically radical and hostile to Roman clerical power. Their children therefore grew up amid both High Anglican devotional practice and Italian revolutionary memory.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1828–1841

Rossetti was born at 38 Charlotte Street. In 1836 the family moved to 50 Charlotte Street, Portland Place. That September he entered the Reverend Mr. Paul’s school in Foley Street. From 1837 to 1842 he attended the junior department of King’s College School.

He showed early interests in drawing, storytelling, medieval subjects, Shakespeare, Walter Scott, and illustrated books. In 1831 he experienced a weakness of the legs, apparently temporary; it should not be conflated with his late-life paralysis.

The family lived respectably but without durable wealth. Gabriele’s worsening eyesight and declining health reduced his earning capacity. William Michael later obtained a civil-service post partly to support the household.

Rossetti wrote the juvenile romance **“Sir Hugh the Heron”** around 1841. It was not a mature publication but demonstrates that his literary and visual ambitions developed together.

## Education and Formation, 1841–1848

### Formal training

- **King’s College School**, 1837–1842.
- **Sass’s Drawing Academy**, later run by Francis Stephen Cary, approximately 1841/42–1846.
- **Royal Academy Antique School**, admitted in 1846; Rossetti attended irregularly and left without completing the conventional curriculum.
- Brief private study with **Ford Madox Brown** in 1848.

Rossetti disliked systematic academic drilling and remained technically uneven. He was exceptionally inventive as a designer and colorist but struggled with anatomical construction, perspective, large-scale oil technique, and durable mural methods. Brown became a lifelong friend, adviser, collaborator, and occasional corrective influence.

### Intellectual and artistic sources

His early reading included:

- Dante Alighieri, especially *Vita nuova* and the *Commedia*.
- Early Italian lyric poets.
- Shakespeare.
- Sir Walter Scott.
- John Keats.
- Percy Bysshe Shelley.
- Robert and Elizabeth Barrett Browning.
- Thomas Malory.
- German ballads, including Bürger’s “Lenore.”
- William Blake.

In 1847, at approximately eighteen, Rossetti and William Michael acquired Blake’s manuscript notebook, subsequently called the **Rossetti Manuscript**. Rossetti copied and studied its poems. He later helped Alexander and Anne Gilchrist with *Life of William Blake, “Pictor Ignotus”* (1863). The direct documentary connection substantially aided Blake’s nineteenth-century recovery.

### Early literary work

Rossetti began translations from Dante and other early Italian poets in the mid-1840s. He wrote the first version of **“The Blessed Damozel”** in 1847, partly under Keatsian and Blakean influence. He was already seeking a form in which medieval subject matter, sensuous detail, supernatural vision, and modern psychological desire could coexist.

## Founding of the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood, 1848–1850

Rossetti met **William Holman Hunt** at the Royal Academy and sought him out after seeing Hunt’s *The Eve of St Agnes*. Through Hunt he became close to **John Everett Millais**. During August–September 1848, the three, meeting at Millais’s family home in Gower Street, initiated the **Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood**.

The seven original members were:

- Dante Gabriel Rossetti
- William Holman Hunt
- John Everett Millais
- William Michael Rossetti
- Thomas Woolner
- Frederic George Stephens
- James Collinson

Their program was never a fully coherent manifesto. Shared commitments included close observation, brilliant color, rejection of formulaic academic idealization, admiration for medieval and early Renaissance art, and the effort to treat serious religious and literary subjects with material specificity. The initials “PRB” were initially kept secret.

### First exhibited paintings

Rossetti’s first major oil, **The Girlhood of Mary Virgin** (1848–49), used his mother and Christina as models. It sold to Louisa, Marchioness of Bath. Its paired sonnets and symbolic objects established Rossetti’s practice of treating poem and picture as mutually interpretive “double works.”

His **Ecce Ancilla Domini!** (1849–50) presented the Annunciation in a compressed, white interior. Its unconventional perspective and bodily treatment of Mary drew adverse criticism. Rossetti was deeply affected and thereafter largely avoided open public exhibition, selling directly to patrons instead.

### *The Germ*

The Brotherhood published four issues of its journal in 1850—first as *The Germ*, then under an expanded title. Rossetti contributed:

- “The Blessed Damozel”
- “My Sister’s Sleep”
- “Hand and Soul”
- other verse and visual material

William Michael served as editor. The periodical failed commercially but later became a central document of Pre-Raphaelite literary theory.

### Journey to France and Belgium

In autumn 1849 or 1850, depending on how particular visits are counted, Rossetti travelled with Hunt to Paris and Belgium. He studied the Louvre and admired Van Eyck, Memling, and Flemish art. The trip strengthened his interest in compact narrative pictures, precise accessories, saturated color, and northern medievalism.

## Elizabeth Siddal, 1850–1862

### Meeting and artistic partnership

**Elizabeth Eleanor Siddall** (1829–1862), later spelling her surname Siddal, was working in or associated with a millinery establishment when Walter Deverell introduced her to the Pre-Raphaelite circle around 1849–50. She modelled for Deverell, Hunt, and Millais, most famously for Millais’s *Ophelia*.

Rossetti met her around 1850 and increasingly claimed her as his principal model. Their association became romantic, pedagogical, and artistic. Rossetti taught or encouraged her drawing and painting; she developed an independent body of medievalizing pictures and poetry. Modern scholarship rejects older accounts that reduce her exclusively to a passive muse.

### Courtship and difficulties

The engagement lasted for years. Factors delaying marriage probably included money, Siddal’s recurrent ill health, family anxiety, Rossetti’s unreliable conduct, and his attachments to other models. There is no secure evidence that a single cause explains the delay.

John Ruskin met Rossetti in 1854 and soon became a purchaser, advocate, and intermediary. He also supported Siddal, purchasing her work and arranging an allowance. Ruskin and Rossetti shared interests but later diverged over artistic practice, professionalism, and values; their relationship cannot be reduced simply to reluctant artist and useful patron.

### Other models and relationships

During the 1850s Rossetti drew or painted Annie Miller, Ruth Herbert, Fanny Cornforth, and others. His relations with Siddal were therefore neither socially nor artistically exclusive.

**Fanny Cornforth**, born Sarah Cox in 1835, probably met Rossetti in 1856 or 1857. The evidence supports that she became his model and sexual partner. Claims that she was working as a prostitute when they met remain plausible but are not conclusively established. She represented a more overtly physical, contemporary type in paintings such as *Bocca Baciata*.

### Marriage

Rossetti and Siddal married at St Clement’s Church, Hastings, on **23 May 1860**. She was seriously ill at the time. They honeymooned in France and lived chiefly at 14 Chatham Place, Blackfriars.

Siddal became pregnant but delivered a stillborn daughter in 1861. Correspondence and later testimony indicate profound grief. A subsequent pregnancy has often been reported but is not as securely documented.

### Siddal’s death

On 10 February 1862 Siddal took a large quantity of laudanum. She died early on **11 February 1862**.

**Documented fact:** the immediate mechanism was an opiate overdose.

**Reported fact:** Rossetti found her unconscious, summoned physicians, and attempted unsuccessfully to revive her.

**Disputed question:** accident or suicide. The coroner’s verdict enabled Christian burial and did not formally establish suicide. Ford Madox Brown later said Rossetti had found and destroyed a suicide note. If Brown’s account is accurate, the death was intentional; the note itself does not survive. Modern biographers frequently describe the death as probable suicide, not judicially established fact.

**Unsupported accusation:** Rossetti murdered Siddal. No credible documentary evidence supports homicide.

Siddal was buried in the Rossetti family grave in Highgate Cemetery on 17 February. Rossetti placed a manuscript book of poems in her coffin, an act understood as renunciation, grief ritual, or self-dramatization.

## Early and Middle Career, 1851–1865

Rossetti increasingly abandoned painstaking early-PRB oil technique for watercolors and dense, jewel-like literary subjects. Important works included:

- *Paolo and Francesca da Rimini*
- *Dante’s Dream at the Time of the Death of Beatrice*
- *The First Anniversary of the Death of Beatrice*
- *The Wedding of St George and Princess Sabra*
- *How Sir Galahad, Sir Bors and Sir Percival Were Fed with the Sanc Grael*
- *The Tune of Seven Towers*
- *The Blue Closet*
- *Found*, begun in the 1850s but never completed

### Oxford Union murals, 1857

In 1857 Rossetti organized an unpaid decorative campaign in the Oxford Union’s debating hall. Participants included William Morris, Edward Burne-Jones, Arthur Hughes, Val Prinsep, John Hungerford Pollen, and Roddam Spencer Stanhope.

The Arthurian murals were painted directly on improperly prepared walls. Their rapid deterioration was a significant technical failure. The project nonetheless formed the social nucleus of a “second generation” of Pre-Raphaelitism.

At Oxford, Rossetti and Burne-Jones encountered **Jane Burden**, daughter of a stableman. She modelled as Guinevere and later married William Morris in 1859. Rossetti’s later attachment to her became one of the central relationships of his life.

### Working Men’s College

Rossetti taught drawing briefly at the Working Men’s College in London, an institution associated with Christian Socialist educational reform. The work was limited and did not become a sustained academic career.

### Book illustration

Rossetti contributed influential wood-engraving designs to Edward Moxon’s 1857 edition of Tennyson’s poems. His images did not merely illustrate narrative events; they produced independent symbolic and psychological readings of the texts. He later illustrated Christina Rossetti’s *Goblin Market and Other Poems* (1862) and *The Prince’s Progress* (1866).

### Translations

In 1861 Smith, Elder published **The Early Italian Poets from Ciullo d’Alcamo to Dante Alighieri (1100–1200–1300)**. It included Rossetti’s translation of Dante’s *Vita nuova* and poems by Dante’s circle and predecessors. Revised editions appeared as **Dante and His Circle** in 1874 and thereafter.

These translations materially shaped English access to medieval Italian lyric poetry. They were literary recreations rather than strictly philological editions.

## Morris, Marshall, Faulkner & Co.

In April 1861 Rossetti became one of seven partners in **Morris, Marshall, Faulkner & Co.**, with:

- William Morris
- Edward Burne-Jones
- Ford Madox Brown
- Philip Webb
- Peter Paul Marshall
- Charles Faulkner
- Rossetti

Each nominally subscribed £1; Morris’s mother supplied a £100 loan. The firm produced stained glass, furniture, wall decoration, embroidery, metalwork, tiles, and other decorative arts. Rossetti supplied cartoons for stained glass, painted furniture panels, and designs.

The business was not Rossetti’s primary income source. In 1874–75 Morris restructured it as his sole proprietorship, **Morris & Co.** Brown, Rossetti, and Marshall resisted the proposed terms and demanded compensation. The dispute involved sharp correspondence and damaged friendships, but no discovered judicial ruling indicates a lawsuit carried to judgment. It was a contentious dissolution and buyout, not proved fraud.

## Cheyne Walk and the Later Painting Career, 1862–1868

Following Siddal’s death, Rossetti moved to **16 Cheyne Walk, Chelsea**, also called Tudor House. Algernon Charles Swinburne, George Meredith, and William Michael Rossetti initially shared or used parts of the property.

Rossetti kept an eccentric menagerie—including owls, peacocks, dogs, kangaroos or wallabies, and a much-loved wombat. The wombat “Top” became part of Rossetti-circle folklore and appears in caricatures. The menagerie was expensive, disorderly, and a conspicuous element of his self-presentation.

### Fanny Cornforth

Cornforth became housekeeper at Cheyne Walk and resumed or continued an intimate relationship with Rossetti. His friends sometimes treated her with class prejudice because of her accent, education, and sexuality. Rossetti also relied on her for domestic management, companionship, modelling, and care.

Their relationship became less consistently sexual as his attachment to Jane Morris deepened, but Cornforth remained connected to him. During his later illness his family and socially respectable friends repeatedly tried to remove her from the household. Surviving letters show enduring affection and financial concern.

### Alexa Wilding

Rossetti encountered **Alexa Wilding**, a dressmaker or aspiring actress, around 1865 and employed her as a regular model, reportedly paying a retainer so that other artists would not monopolize her. She appears in *Veronica Veronese*, *La Ghirlandata*, *The Blessed Damozel*, *A Sea-Spell*, and other works.

No reliable evidence establishes a sexual relationship between Rossetti and Wilding.

### Major patrons and market structure

Rossetti avoided juried exhibitions and sold through a private network. Major patrons included:

- **Frederick Richards Leyland**, shipowner
- **William Graham**, merchant and member of Parliament
- **George Rae**, banker
- **Constantine Alexander Ionides**, merchant and collector
- **George Boyce**, architect and watercolorist
- **James Leathart**, collector
- **Ellen Heaton**, patron
- **John Ruskin**, especially earlier

The market for large, richly colored images of single female figures brought Rossetti substantial prices. Works were commissioned, replicated, altered, or sold through intermediaries such as Charles Augustus Howell. Rossetti could earn well but spent heavily on the Cheyne Walk establishment, animals, antiquities, assistants, medicines, and irregular domestic arrangements. He accumulated neither an industrial fortune nor a stable corporate estate.

### Photography

In 1865 Rossetti directed photographic sessions with Fanny Cornforth and Jane Morris, using John Robert Parsons as photographer. The Jane Morris series was staged in Rossetti’s garden and used as source material for later drawings and paintings. Authorship is therefore collaborative: Parsons operated the camera; Rossetti selected, posed, and directed the images.

## Jane Morris and Kelmscott, 1865–1876

Rossetti’s sustained artistic fascination with **Jane Morris** intensified during the 1860s. She became the model for *Proserpine*, *Astarte Syriaca*, *Mariana*, *The Day Dream*, *Water Willow*, *La Pia de’ Tolomei*, and many drawings.

### Nature of the relationship

**Documented fact:** Rossetti and Jane exchanged affectionate and intimate correspondence; she repeatedly modelled for him; they spent extended periods together; and some surviving letters were restricted from publication until the twentieth century.

**Strongly reported fact:** they were lovers.

**Unresolved detail:** the dates and physical extent of the sexual relationship cannot be reconstructed with certainty. Surviving correspondence is incomplete, and participants had reasons for discretion. Claims of a precisely defined “ménage à trois” simplify an unstable and painful arrangement.

William Morris knew of their intimacy at least in broad outline. His departures for Iceland in 1871 and 1873 created periods in which Jane and Rossetti were together at Kelmscott, but it is speculative to say Morris travelled solely to facilitate or escape the affair.

### Kelmscott Manor

In 1871 Morris and Rossetti took a joint lease of **Kelmscott Manor**, Oxfordshire. Rossetti spent long periods there, frequently with Jane and her daughters Jenny and May. The manor entered his paintings as an identifiable landscape and architectural setting.

Rossetti left abruptly in July 1874 and never returned. His worsening dependency, paranoia, emotional volatility, and the deterioration of his relationship with Jane made continued residence impractical. Jane remained solicitous but increasingly distanced herself, especially after discovering the scale of his drug use.

## Recovery and Publication of the Buried Poems, 1868–1870

By the late 1860s friends urged Rossetti to publish a substantial poetry collection. Several important poems existed only, or supposedly only, in the manuscript buried with Siddal.

Surviving letters between December 1868 and October 1869 document Rossetti’s deliberations. Legal permission was obtained. On the night of **5 October 1869**, Highgate Cemetery workers opened the grave under the supervision or agency of **Charles Augustus Howell**. Rossetti was not present.

The manuscript was recovered, disinfected or treated, transcribed, proofed, revised, and combined with later work in **Poems** (1870). Surviving “Exhumation Proofs” show that the recovered texts underwent further editorial transformation.

### Ethical and historical status

Opening the grave was legally authorized. It was nevertheless controversial then and has remained the most notorious episode in Rossetti’s biography. Whether the act should be understood principally as desecration, literary recovery, self-betrayal, or Victorian mortuary practice is interpretation, not documentary fact.

### Hair-growth legend

**Myth:** Siddal’s red hair had continued growing after death and filled the coffin; her body remained perfectly preserved and beautiful.

**Probable origin:** stories circulated by Howell or later intermediaries after the 1869 exhumation.

**Evidence against:** hair does not continue biological growth after death; skin retraction can create that appearance. No reliable contemporary exhumation report verifies the coffin-filling hair. The account became a Gothic embellishment that softened, sensationalized, or supernaturalized the recovery of the poems.

## *Poems* and the “Fleshly School” Controversy, 1870–1872

**Poems** appeared in 1870 and initially received substantial attention, respectable sales, and many favorable reviews. It included “Jenny,” “The Blessed Damozel,” “Sister Helen,” “Eden Bower,” and an early arrangement of **The House of Life**.

In October 1871 **Robert Buchanan**, writing as “Thomas Maitland” in the *Contemporary Review*, attacked Rossetti and associated poets as the **“Fleshly School of Poetry.”** He alleged sensuality, morbidity, affectation, indecency, effeminacy, and self-advertising cliquishness. The use of a pseudonym while referring to Rossetti’s private circulation of poems made the attack seem personally covert.

Rossetti replied in the *Athenaeum* with **“The Stealthy School of Criticism”** on 16 December 1871. Buchanan acknowledged authorship and expanded his argument into the 1872 pamphlet *The Fleshly School of Poetry and Other Phenomena of the Day*.

### Evidentiary assessment

- Buchanan’s accusation of aesthetic and sexual impropriety was **criticism**, not a legal charge or finding.
- Rossetti’s poetry does deliberately integrate erotic and spiritual love. “Nuptial Sleep” depicts post-coital lovers; Buchanan did not invent its erotic content.
- Buchanan’s portrayal of Rossetti as morally corrupt was polemical and not an independently proved biographical finding.
- Buchanan later qualified or apologized for aspects of the attack.
- Rossetti’s circle subsequently portrayed Buchanan as the man who destroyed Rossetti. That causal claim is overstated: the controversy aggravated an already unstable condition but was one of several factors.

## Mental and Physical Collapse, 1872

By 1872 Rossetti suffered severe insomnia, eye complaints, depression, obsessive fear of criticism, and persecutory ideas. He had begun using **chloral hydrate**, then a medically prescribed sedative. He combined it with alcohol and progressively increased consumption.

In June 1872, at the house of Dr. William Hake in Roehampton, Rossetti swallowed laudanum and became critically ill.

**Reported fact:** the overdose was medically life-threatening.

**Disputed interpretation:** William Michael Rossetti and several witnesses treated it as a suicide attempt. Some later accounts allowed the possibility of accident or impulsive overmedication. Given the surrounding despair, farewell-like conduct, and family testimony, deliberate self-harm is the stronger reconstruction, but no judicial finding exists.

Afterward Rossetti experienced hallucinations or delusions, especially beliefs that organized enemies were attacking him through reviews and rumors. Friends removed him to Scotland, including Urrard and later Penkill Castle. He eventually joined Jane Morris at Kelmscott.

### Competing explanations

- **Documented contributing factors:** chloral use, alcohol, chronic insomnia, physical pain, public controversy, emotional conflict, and professional anxiety.
- **Possible contributing factors:** guilt over Siddal, the exhumation, hereditary vulnerability, and fear of blindness.
- **Mythologized version:** Buchanan’s article alone caused the collapse.
- **Unsupported simplification:** Rossetti was merely malingering or theatrically eccentric. His impairment was repeatedly observed and functionally serious.

## Last Decade, 1872–1882

Rossetti resumed painting after periods of improvement. Important late works included:

- *Proserpine*
- *Astarte Syriaca*
- *La Ghirlandata*
- *The Blessed Damozel*
- *The Day Dream*
- *La Pia de’ Tolomei*
- later versions of *Beata Beatrix*
- replicas and revisions of earlier subjects

Assistants and friends helped prepare canvases, manage commissions, read aloud, and negotiate with patrons. **Henry Treffry Dunn** served as studio assistant and left important recollections. **Theodore Watts-Dunton** became a close adviser. **Hall Caine**, a young admirer, entered the circle late and helped care for Rossetti.

### Health

Rossetti’s late illnesses included:

- dependency on chloral hydrate
- heavy alcohol consumption
- insomnia
- depression and paranoia
- hydrocele and complications associated with treatment or surgery
- impaired eyesight
- kidney disease or chronic renal dysfunction
- recurrent weakness
- a paralytic or quasi-paralytic attack affecting his legs in December 1881
- terminal uraemia

Modern retrospective diagnoses such as “alcohol psychosis,” specific mood disorders, or particular neurological syndromes remain hypotheses unless tied closely to contemporary clinical evidence.

### Finances and working methods

Rossetti continued receiving substantial payments but was frequently short of liquid cash. He negotiated advances, produced replicas, revised already sold works, and sometimes promised pictures before completion. Patrons could become frustrated by delay, reworking, or non-delivery.

No adequately documented evidence supports fraud in the legal sense. The pattern was one of erratic production, informal credit, dependency, and a private art market centered on personal relationships.

### Social withdrawal

Rossetti became increasingly reclusive at Cheyne Walk. He continued receiving a smaller circle—Watts-Dunton, Caine, Dunn, Shields, Leyland, Brown, William Michael, and others—but old relationships with Morris, Swinburne, and some former associates weakened.

In 1881 he published **Ballads and Sonnets**, containing the expanded 101-sonnet *House of Life*, and a revised edition of *Poems*. He also sold his large *Dante’s Dream* to Liverpool’s Walker Art Gallery, with Hall Caine assisting negotiations.

---

## Death

In February 1882 Rossetti moved to a bungalow at Birchington-on-Sea offered by architect **John Pollard Seddon**. Hall Caine, a nurse, relatives, and friends attended him at various times.

He attempted to work on *Proserpine*, *Joan of Arc*, and the comic ballad “Jan Van Hunks.” His renal illness worsened.

Rossetti died on **Easter Sunday, 9 April 1882**. William Michael’s near-contemporary account identified the terminal stage as **uraemia**. “Bright’s disease,” chronic kidney disease, and uraemic poisoning are common historical descriptions. Chloral and alcohol had seriously damaged his general health, but the documentary record does not justify calling either the sole immediate cause.

He was buried on 14 April in All Saints churchyard, Birchington. Ford Madox Brown designed the Celtic-style memorial cross. His mother, Christina, William Michael, Watts-Dunton, Hall Caine, and Frederic Shields were closely involved in his final period or memorialization.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Politics

Rossetti inherited the memory of Italian revolutionary exile but was not a sustained party activist. His early milieu included republican nationalism, Chartism, Christian Socialism, and reformist education. He taught at the Working Men’s College and associated with politically active artists, but he held no political office and produced no systematic political program.

His art occasionally addressed prostitution, poverty, labor, and class, most explicitly in the unfinished *Found* and the poem “Jenny.” These works render social contradiction but do not establish a consistent reform platform.

### Religion

His early pictures use intense Christian symbolism. Rossetti was immersed in Anglican biblical culture and medieval Catholic imagery without becoming an orthodox Roman Catholic. His mature work increasingly transferred sacramental, annunciatory, and devotional forms to erotic love, memory, art, and female beauty.

Religious imagery remained central even as doctrinal certainty weakened. His position is best described as culturally Christian, heterodox, and symbolist rather than straightforwardly devout or atheist.

### Artistic phases

1. **Juvenile medievalism and literary illustration**, before 1848.
2. **Early Pre-Raphaelite naturalism and Christian symbolism**, 1848–1851.
3. **Small-scale watercolor medievalism**, roughly 1851–1860.
4. **Decorative collaboration and book illustration**, 1857–1860s.
5. **Sensuous female “half-length” portraits and paired poems**, 1860s.
6. **Late symbolist mythologies and obsessive replicas**, 1870s–1882.

Rossetti helped redirect Pre-Raphaelitism from literal naturalism toward an art of memory, desire, dream, decorative surface, and psychologically charged female figures. This became an important source for Aestheticism and European Symbolism.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Family

- **Gabriele Rossetti:** father, Dante scholar and revolutionary exile; primary Italian and Dantesque influence.
- **Frances Polidori Rossetti:** mother, household anchor and Anglican influence.
- **William Michael Rossetti:** brother, PRB colleague, editor, financial and medical intermediary, later memoirist.
- **Christina Rossetti:** sister, poet; reciprocal literary and visual collaboration.
- **Maria Rossetti:** sister, Dante commentator and religious writer.

### Artistic colleagues

- **William Holman Hunt:** co-founder of the PRB; early studio companion; later ideological divergence.
- **John Everett Millais:** co-founder; technically accomplished early collaborator; moved toward the Royal Academy establishment.
- **Ford Madox Brown:** mentor, friend, business partner, and memorial designer.
- **Edward Burne-Jones:** younger associate, Oxford collaborator, designer, and friend.
- **William Morris:** poet, designer, business partner, Kelmscott co-lessee, and husband of Jane Morris; friendship damaged by business and intimate conflict.
- **Algernon Charles Swinburne:** poet, Cheyne Walk housemate and literary ally; later estranged.
- **Frederic George Stephens, Thomas Woolner, James Collinson:** original PRB colleagues.
- **Arthur Hughes, Val Prinsep, Spencer Stanhope, John Hungerford Pollen:** Oxford mural collaborators.
- **Frederic Shields and Henry Treffry Dunn:** late collaborators and carers.

### Models, partners, and muses

- **Elizabeth Siddal:** model, pupil, partner, wife, and artist.
- **Fanny Cornforth:** model, lover, housekeeper, companion.
- **Jane Morris:** model, intimate correspondent, probable lover.
- **Alexa Wilding:** paid model; no proved sexual relationship.
- **Annie Miller, Ruth Herbert, Ellen Smith, Marie Spartali Stillman:** significant models or artistic associates.

### Critics, patrons, and adversaries

- **John Ruskin:** critic, purchaser, patron, and intellectual interlocutor.
- **Frederick Leyland, William Graham, George Rae, Constantine Ionides:** important collectors.
- **Charles Augustus Howell:** agent and intermediary; resourceful but widely distrusted; supervised the manuscript recovery.
- **Robert Buchanan:** hostile critic in the Fleshly School controversy.
- **Theodore Watts-Dunton:** friend, literary adviser, and late-life supporter.
- **Hall Caine:** admirer, companion, business intermediary, and late-life carer.

## Controversies, Allegations, and Adversarial Findings

A dedicated search was conducted using combinations of Rossetti’s name with *controversy, scandal, allegations, lawsuit, fraud, accusations, conspiracy,* and *criticism*, including results published in 2024–2026.

### Siddal’s death

- **Documented:** death followed laudanum overdose.
- **Probable but not legally established:** suicide.
- **Reported:** Rossetti destroyed a suicide note.
- **Unverified/unsupported:** Rossetti murdered her or deliberately withheld aid.
- **Reputational effect:** created the enduring narrative of the destructive male genius and sacrificed muse.

### Grave opening

- **Documented:** legally authorized opening on 5 October 1869 and recovery of manuscripts.
- **Disputed:** moral characterization as necessary retrieval, desecration, or self-mythologizing betrayal.
- **Myth:** miraculous preservation and coffin-filling hair.
- **Reputational effect:** fused Rossetti’s life with Gothic themes already present in his art.

### Fanny Cornforth and class

- **Documented:** model, lover, housekeeper, and companion.
- **Probable, not certain:** she had engaged in sex work before meeting Rossetti.
- **Documented social context:** members of Rossetti’s circle disparaged her manners and working-class speech.
- **Interpretive controversy:** later biographies sometimes treated her as a corrupting sexual presence; revisionist scholarship emphasizes her labor and care.

### Jane and William Morris

- **Documented:** intimate correspondence, repeated stays, joint Kelmscott arrangement, extensive modelling.
- **Strongly reported:** long affair.
- **Unresolved:** precise sexual chronology and William Morris’s full knowledge.
- **Mythologized form:** a stable and mutually accepted ménage à trois. Evidence instead indicates concealment, pain, avoidance, and changing arrangements.

### Artistic treatment of women

Recent gender scholarship argues that Rossetti transformed living working women into controlled aesthetic types, and that Siddal’s work and Cornforth’s domestic labor were subordinated to his legend. This is a **scholarly interpretation grounded in documented power differences**, not a criminal allegation. Counter-evidence includes Rossetti’s encouragement of Siddal’s work and continuing financial concern for Cornforth; neither fact eliminates the asymmetry.

### Race and *The Beloved*

Recent scholarship has examined the unnamed Black model in *The Beloved* and Rossetti’s search for racialized visual contrast. His correspondence uses objectifying language concerning complexion and treats the model as a pictorial element. Scholars disagree over whether this represents personal racial ideology, aestheticized indifference, or the broader racial economy of Victorian painting.

- **Documented:** the painting employs a Black attendant as a compositional and racial contrast.
- **Documented:** Rossetti participated in an imperial metropolitan art market.
- **Disputed interpretation:** the nature and extent of his personal racism.
- **No evidence found:** political membership in an organized racial-supremacist movement.

### Fleshly School

- **Documented:** Buchanan accused Rossetti of indecency and cliquish self-promotion under a pseudonym.
- **Documented:** Rossetti answered publicly.
- **Criticism, not finding:** that the poems were morally corrupt.
- **Disputed causation:** how much the attack contributed to the 1872 collapse.

### Drugs, alcohol, and self-harm

- **Documented:** prolonged chloral use, alcohol use, dependence, and serious functional deterioration.
- **Strongly reported:** deliberate laudanum overdose in 1872.
- **Unresolved:** exact retrospective diagnosis.
- **No substantiated criminality:** chloral was a prescribed nineteenth-century sedative, though use became compulsive and dangerous.

### Business conduct

Rossetti accepted advances, missed deadlines, repeatedly revised works, and participated in bitter dissolution negotiations with Morris. No reliable evidence of a formal fraud finding, lawsuit judgment against him, corrupt business organization, money laundering, or forged-art enterprise was found.

### Recent two-year findings

The 2024 study of Rossetti’s exhumation correspondence by Carl Plasa does not uncover a new crime; it shows how Rossetti constructed and rationalized the recovery through his letters and how the “Willowwood” sonnets engage themes of burial and retrieval. Recent institutional publications continue reassessing Siddal, Cornforth, Jane Morris, and the Black model in *The Beloved*, chiefly by restoring agency or examining class, gender, and race. No credible newly reported lawsuit, indictment, fraud discovery, or conspiracy finding emerged in the 2024–2026 search.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Self-Created Mythology

### The hair filling Siddal’s coffin

- **Story:** Siddal remained beautiful in death, while her red hair grew until it filled the coffin.
- **Appearance and circulation:** associated with Howell’s account and expanded in later biographies and popular retellings.
- **Evidence:** no secure eyewitness document confirms it.
- **Assessment:** **rumor or myth**.
- **Effect:** made Rossetti’s biography resemble his supernatural ballads and images of fatal women.

### Rossetti killed Siddal through neglect or murder

- **Story:** he drove her to suicide, abandoned her while overdosing, or killed her.
- **Evidence:** the marriage was troubled and he had other attachments; he summoned medical help. No evidence establishes homicide.
- **Assessment:** emotional neglect is interpretive; murder is **unsupported allegation**.
- **Effect:** central to hostile “muse destroyed by genius” narratives and several dramatizations.

### The doomed Pre-Raphaelite brotherhood

- **Story:** the PRB was a secret, quasi-occult fraternity destroyed by scandal.
- **Evidence:** it used initials, ritualized fellowship, and a list of “Immortals,” but operated principally as an artistic association.
- **Assessment:** secrecy is documented; occult-conspiracy versions are **myth**.
- **Effect:** heightened the group’s countercultural aura.

### Buchanan as Rossetti’s murderer

- **Story:** the Fleshly School attack directly killed Rossetti.
- **Evidence:** it preceded his collapse, but death came ten years later amid renal disease, chloral dependence, alcohol, pain, and paralysis.
- **Assessment:** **hostile counter-myth** circulated by Rossetti’s defenders.
- **Effect:** cast Rossetti as a martyr to criticism and Buchanan as persecutor.

### Rossetti as a supernatural or vampiric figure

His uncle Polidori’s *The Vampyre*, Siddal’s exhumation, nocturnal habits, fatal-woman imagery, and red-haired models encouraged later occult and vampire fiction. Tim Powers’s *Hide Me Among the Graves* elaborates this association as fantasy. There is no historical evidence for supernatural activity.

### The wombat cult

Rossetti’s enthusiasm for wombats is documented. He frequented the animal at the London Zoo, owned one called Top, drew it, and commemorated its death. Later popular culture turned the wombat into a comic emblem of the Pre-Raphaelite circle. The animal itself is factual; the “cult” is affectionate retrospective mythology.

### The solitary genius

Rossetti cultivated privacy and avoided exhibitions, helping construct an image of the secluded master at Cheyne Walk. In fact, his production depended on models, assistants, photographers, engravers, patrons, relatives, publishers, business partners, and carers. The solitary-genius image conceals a large collaborative network.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Immediately after Rossetti’s death, relatives and loyal friends constructed a memorial image of the generous, persecuted genius. William Michael’s editing preserved enormous quantities of evidence while also defending family reputation.

Late nineteenth-century Aesthetic and Decadent writers drew on Rossetti’s fusion of art, eroticism, medievalism, and self-conscious style. His influence is documented in discussions of **Oscar Wilde**, **Arthur Symons**, **Walter Pater**, and **William Butler Yeats**. European Symbolism likewise found precedents in his dreamlike women, floral and musical symbolism, and treatment of the image as an autonomous imaginative world.

Modernism initially reduced his reputation. Critics attacked the lushness, repetitive female type, sentiment, and verbal ornament. Mid- and late-twentieth-century scholarship restored the structural sophistication of *The House of Life*, his intermedial practice, translation, manuscript revision, and importance to book and decorative design.

Feminist reassessment altered the field by recovering Siddal, Cornforth, Jane Morris, Alexa Wilding, and Christina Rossetti as participants rather than interchangeable “muses.” Another line of scholarship critiques Rossetti’s aesthetic control of women’s bodies and the class hierarchy of studio practice.

Race-conscious scholarship has examined his Black sitters, Orientalizing accessories, imperial commodities, and the racialized composition of *The Beloved*. Digital scholarship has made Rossetti unusually accessible: the *Rossetti Archive* was a foundational large-scale humanities computing project and treats every documentary state as part of the work.

His standing now rests on several intertwined legacies:

- co-founder and major theorist-practitioner of Pre-Raphaelitism;
- pivotal link from Romantic medievalism to Aestheticism and Symbolism;
- major Victorian sonneteer and ballad writer;
- influential translator of Dante and early Italian lyric;
- innovator in poem-picture relations and revision across media;
- important book and decorative designer;
- subject of an unusually durable Gothic biographical mythology;
- center of continuing debates about artistic collaboration, gender, class, addiction, race, and the construction of genius.

| **1850** | Contributes to *The Germ*; meets Elizabeth Siddal around this period; travels in France and Belgium with Hunt. |
| **1851–56** | Produces medievalist watercolors and drawings; relationship with Siddal deepens. |
| **24 April 1854** | Important early meeting with John Ruskin at Denmark Hill. |
| **1854** | Father Gabriele Rossetti dies. |
| **1856/57** | Meets Fanny Cornforth, who becomes model and lover. |
| **1857** | Organizes Oxford Union murals; meets William Morris, Burne-Jones, and Jane Burden; contributes to Moxon Tennyson. |
| **23 May 1860** | Marries Elizabeth Siddal at Hastings. |
| **1861** | Probable stillbirth of daughter; publishes *The Early Italian Poets*; becomes partner in Morris, Marshall, Faulkner & Co. |
| **11 February 1862** | Siddal dies after laudanum overdose. |
| **17 February 1862** | Siddal buried at Highgate with Rossetti’s manuscript poems. |
| **1862** | Rossetti moves to 16 Cheyne Walk; illustrates Christina’s *Goblin Market*. |
| **1863** | Assists with Gilchrist’s *Life of William Blake*. |
| **1865** | Photographic sessions with Fanny Cornforth and Jane Morris; Alexa Wilding becomes regular model. |
| **1868–69** | Relationship with Jane Morris becomes increasingly intimate; considers recovering buried manuscript. |
| **5 October 1869** | Siddal’s grave opened under legal authority; manuscript recovered. |
| **1870** | Publishes *Poems*. |
| **1871** | Jointly leases Kelmscott Manor with William Morris; Buchanan publishes “The Fleshly School of Poetry.” |
| **16 December 1871** | Rossetti publishes “The Stealthy School of Criticism.” |
| **June 1872** | Severe mental collapse and probable suicide attempt by laudanum overdose. |
| **1872–73** | Recuperates in Scotland and at Kelmscott; dependence on chloral and alcohol continues. |
| **1874** | Publishes *Dante and His Circle*; leaves Kelmscott permanently in July; conflict over Morris firm restructuring. |
| **25 March 1875** | Reconstituted Morris & Co. replaces the former partnership after contentious negotiations. |
| **1876** | Sister Maria dies; Jane Morris increasingly distances herself. |
| **Late 1870s** | Increasing reclusion, paranoia, ill health, and reliance on assistants and carers. |
| **1881** | Publishes *Ballads and Sonnets* and revised *Poems*; sells *Dante’s Dream* to Liverpool; suffers paralytic attack in December. |
| **4 February 1882** | Moves to Birchington-on-Sea. |
| **9 April 1882** | Dies from terminal uraemic illness associated with kidney disease. |
| **14 April 1882** | Buried at All Saints, Birchington. |
| **1886** | William Michael publishes *Collected Works*. |
| **1895** | William Michael publishes family letters and memoir. |
| **1967** | Ken Russell’s *Dante’s Inferno* broadcasts on BBC1. |
| **1971** | Virginia Surtees publishes the standard catalogue raisonné. |
| **1993–2008** | Rossetti Archive conceived, developed, and completed under Jerome McGann. |
| **2002–15** | Ten-volume *Correspondence of Dante Gabriel Rossetti* published. |
| **2023** | Tate’s *The Rossettis* exhibition reframes the family as a collaborative artistic constellation. |
| **2024** | New scholarship closely analyzes the exhumation correspondence and its literary construction. |
