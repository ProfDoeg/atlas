## Works by Isadora Duncan

### Choreographic obra

The entries below follow the Isadora Duncan Archive’s repertory rather than treating later performances, reconstructed stagings, or variant titles as new works. The catalogue is explicitly unfinished: Duncan created hundreds of dances, dances made in silence are lost, and bodily transmission produced divergent versions whose exact authorship and form cannot always be fixed ([repertory principles](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/dances/)).

- *Bach studies*. A pedagogical repertory comprising walk, waltz-turn, skip-and-polka, step-swing, sauté, and related studies set to Bach’s suites and Brandenburg concertos; the catalogue also records a choreography to the *St Matthew Passion* ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Seventh Symphony*. A four-movement choreographic cycle to Beethoven’s Symphony No. 7 ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Moonlight Sonata*. A three-movement choreographic cycle to Beethoven’s Piano Sonata No. 14 ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Beethoven dances and studies*. Choreographies to Piano Sonata No. 5, the *Pathétique* adagio, the *Turkish March*, a minuet, selections from *King Stephen*, and the *Pastoral Symphony*; some survive chiefly as class or children’s studies ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *The Childhood of Christ*. A dance to Berlioz’s *L’enfance du Christ* ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Adagietto from L’Arlésienne*. A dance to Bizet’s orchestral movement ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *The Many Faces of Love*. A cycle made from Brahms’s sixteen Op. 39 waltzes; transmitted component titles include *Greeting*, *Lullaby*, *Flamenca*, *Lovers*, *Seagull*, *Diamond*, *Cymbals*, and *Rose Petals*, but these names and versions are not entirely stable ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Slow Arm Movements*. A class study to Brahms’s Intermezzo in A major, Op. 118, no. 2; its descriptive title makes it closer to a pedagogical protocol embodied in movement than to a conventional stage work ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *The King’s Hunting Jig*. A dance to music attributed in the repertory to Ole Bull ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Preludes*. A cycle of dances to all twenty-four Chopin Op. 28 preludes ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Funeral March*. A dramatic choreography joining the third and fourth movements of Chopin’s Piano Sonata No. 2; its transmitted form is associated with mourning after the deaths of Duncan’s children ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Chopin mazurkas*. Dances to mazurkas Op. 7 no. 1, Op. 17 nos. 1 and 4, Op. 24 no. 2, Op. 33 nos. 2–4, Op. 67 no. 3, and Op. 68 no. 2. Transmitted titles include *Water Study*, *Death and the Maiden*, *Peasant Mazurka*, and *Orientale*; the Op. 24 dance is recorded as lost ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Chopin nocturnes*. Dances to nocturnes Op. 9 no. 2, Op. 15 no. 2, Op. 27 nos. 1–2, Op. 32 no. 2, and Op. 72 no. 1; *Angel and Spirit Rising* and *Tanagras* are among their variant titles ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Chopin études*. Dances to the “Revolutionary,” “Aeolian Harp,” and “Butterfly” études ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Chopin polonaises*. Dances to Op. 26 no. 1 and Op. 40 nos. 1–2; the first is recorded as lost, while the “Military Polonaise” became part of Duncan’s heroic repertory ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Chopin waltzes and Berceuse*. Dances to the Op. 34, Op. 64, and Op. 70 waltzes and the Op. 57 *Berceuse*. Variant titles include *Ballspiel*, *Narcissus*, *Tanagra*, and *Twirling* ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Tanagra Figures*. A dance to Corelli’s *La Follia*; the surviving title describes an imagined antique embodiment rather than a narrative ballet ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Reflections in the Water*. A dance to Debussy’s *Reflets dans l’eau* ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Slavonic Dances*. A choreographic group to Dvořák’s music; the catalogue does not specify a securely complete set ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Panis Angelicus*. A dance to César Franck ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- **1915–1916:** *Rédemption*. A wartime choreography to Franck’s *Morceau symphonique*; scholarly reconstruction places it among the works Duncan developed during her incompletely documented 1915–16 period ([June Layson’s choreochronicle](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/26100/1/405155.pdf)).
- *Orfeo ed Euridice*. A suite of dances to Gluck, including *Dance of Joy*, *Dance for Eurydice*, *Lament*, *Morning Star*, *Dance of the Furies*, *Dance of the Blessed Spirits*, *Cherubim*, *Feather Dance*, and *Garland*. The archive distinguishes multiple inherited versions and alternative labels ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Iphigénie*. A large suite drawn from Gluck’s *Iphigénie en Aulide* and *Iphigénie en Tauride*, including dances for maidens, slaves, priestesses, Scythians, harvesters, and caryatids; individual numbers sometimes function independently ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *War Dance*. A Gluck-based choreography whose musical source and fixed title remain uncertain; the parenthetical archival designation appropriately resists treatment as a settled title ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Alceste: Andante*. A dance to Gluck’s opera ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Pan*. A dance to music by Benjamin Godard ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Overture to Mireille*. A dance to Gounod’s overture ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Kinderlieder*. Five children’s dances to Gretchaninoff’s Op. 31 songs, including *The Call of Spring*, *Birdstory*, *Cradle Song*, and *The Bells of Moscow* ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Gretchaninoff children’s dances*. A group comprising *Lullaby*, *Ding Dang Dong*, and *The Lone Oak Tree*; the music and titles of the latter two are incompletely identified ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Lullaby*. A dance to Grieg’s Lyric Piece, Op. 38, no. 1 ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Norwegian Dance*. A children’s dance to Grieg’s Op. 35, no. 2 ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Incidental Pieces from Königskinder*. A choreography to Humperdinck whose precise extent is not fixed by the present catalogue ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Le Secret d’Amour*. A dance to music attributed to Klein ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Die Werber*. A dance to Lanner’s waltz, Op. 103 ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *After Reading Dante*. A dance to Liszt’s *Après une lecture de Dante* ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *The Blessing of God in Solitude*. A dance to Liszt’s *Bénédiction de Dieu dans la solitude* ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Funérailles*. A dance to Liszt’s composition of that name ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *The Legend of St Francis Preaching to the Birds*. A dance to Liszt’s first *Légende* ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Liebestraum*. A lost choreography to Liszt’s S. 541 no. 3; its existence is catalogued, but the dancing itself no longer survives ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Rákóczi March*. A dance to the Liszt–Berlioz march ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Marche Lorraine*. A patriotic or processional dance to the French song ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Spring Song*. A dance to Mendelssohn’s *Song without Words*, Op. 62, no. 6 ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Spanish Dances*. A five-part cycle to Moszkowski’s Op. 12 ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Turkish March*. A class and children’s dance to the finale of Mozart’s Piano Sonata K. 331 ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Les petits riens*. A choreography to Mozart’s ballet music ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Water Scenes*. Dances to Ethelbert Nevin’s *Ophelia*, *Water Nymphs*, and *Narcissus* ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *A Shepherd’s Tale*. A dance to Nevin’s Op. 16, no. 1 ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Dances inspired by Renaissance paintings*. Choreographies associated with Picchi but preserved without a securely identified musical title; they resist reduction to a single fixed work ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Der Asra*. A dance to Rubinstein’s song, Op. 32, no. 6 ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Reflections of Germany*. A suite to Florent Schmitt’s *Reflets d’Allemagne*, represented by the *Lübeck*, *Dresden*, and *Nuremberg* sections ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Great Symphony*. A three-part cycle to movements II–IV of Schubert’s Ninth Symphony, transmitted as *Ode to Apollo*, *Ode to Dionysus*, and *Ode to Artemis* ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Schubert Dances for Children*. A repertory of waltzes, écossaises, and German dances bearing transmitted titles such as *Three Graces*, *Petal Waltz*, *Four Winds*, *Butterfly*, *Around the Linden Tree*, *Under the Scarf*, *Gallop*, *Romp*, *Parting*, *Lullaby*, and *Classical Duet* ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Ballspiel*. A lyrical dance transmitted in both Schubert and Chopin settings; whether these are versions, distinct choreographies, or later repertorial conflations should not be silently decided ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Water Study*. A lyrical dance likewise transmitted with both Schubert and Chopin musical settings, making it an unstable work-identity rather than a single recoverable original ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Schubert studies and dances*. A group to the *Marche militaire*, Moments musicaux nos. 3 and 5, the *Grande Marche*, *Ave Maria*, *Irish Jig*, and *Rosamunde*; several are class exercises or children’s dances ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Unfinished Symphony*. A choreography to Schubert’s Eighth Symphony; “Unfinished” is the music’s conventional name, not evidence that Duncan left the dance unfinished ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Scenes from Childhood*. Dances to Schumann’s *Of Foreign Lands and People*, *A Curious Story*, and *Blind Man’s Bluff* ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Sicilienne*. A dance to Schumann’s *Album for the Young*, Op. 68, no. 11 ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- **After 1913:** *Mother*. A grief-charged dramatic dance to Scriabin’s Étude, Op. 2, no. 1, created in the aftermath of her children’s deaths; its survival depends on embodied lineage rather than Duncan’s notation ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Revolutionary*. A dramatic dance to Scriabin’s Étude, Op. 8, no. 12; it is distinct from Duncan’s dance to Chopin’s “Revolutionary” Étude despite the shared repertorial name ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *The Crossing*. A dramatic dance to Scriabin’s Étude, Op. 42, no. 5 ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Poem of Ecstasy*. A choreography to Scriabin’s orchestral work ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Work Trilogy: Labor / Famine / Labor Triumphant*. A political triptych to music attributed to Sheyne; its three-part social argument makes it both choreography and manifesto in movement ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Fantasie Pastorale*. A dance to Singelée’s Op. 56 ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *On the Beautiful Blue Danube*. A lyrical dance to Johann Strauss II ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Artist’s Life*. A children’s waltz study to Strauss’s Op. 316 ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Roses from the South*. A lyrical dance to Strauss’s Op. 388 ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Chanson d’Amour*. A dance to music by Taubert ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- **1915–1916:** *Pathétique*. A three-movement cycle to movements II–IV of Tchaikovsky’s Sixth Symphony, developed during Duncan’s wartime period; it joins personal lament to a symphonic, multimedia theatrical conception ([Layson choreochronicle](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/26100/1/405155.pdf)).
- **1917:** *Marche Slave*. A dramatic national dance to Tchaikovsky, first presented at the Metropolitan Opera on 26 April 1917 according to the documented performance chronology ([Deutsches Tanzarchiv chronology](https://www.deutsches-tanzarchiv.de/archiv/nachlaesse-sammlungen/d/isadora-duncan)).
- *Dubinushka*. A heroic dance to the Russian work song ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *The Internationale*. A revolutionary choreography to the socialist anthem; it operates as danced political manifestation as much as autonomous stage work ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- *Russian children’s and folk dances*. A transmitted group titled *Giants Tall*, *My Pigeon House*, *One, Two, Three*, *Subotu/Farewell Song*, *The Blacksmith*, *There Stands a High Mountain*, and *We Wander* ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- **1924:** *Varshavianka*. A heroic revolutionary choreography to the Polish-Russian workers’ song; the archive preserves it as repertory, while accounts date its composition to 1924 ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- **1904:** *Tannhäuser Bacchanale*. A choreography to Wagner made at Cosima Wagner’s invitation in Bayreuth ([Deutsches Tanzarchiv chronology](https://www.deutsches-tanzarchiv.de/archiv/nachlaesse-sammlungen/d/isadora-duncan)).
- *Wagner repertory*. Dances to *Die Meistersinger*, the *Ride of the Valkyries*, four sections of *Parsifal*, and the *Liebestod* from *Tristan und Isolde*. These are separate musical manifestations but survive through an unsettled performance tradition rather than an authoritative score ([repertory](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/repertory/all/)).
- **1915–1916:** *La Marseillaise*. A wartime political dance to Rouget de Lisle’s anthem; it became a standard repertory work, but its force as a patriotic manifestation complicates the notion of an autonomous artwork ([Layson choreochronicle](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/26100/1/405155.pdf)).

### Pedagogical and institutional projects

- **1904–1909:** *Free-Dance School, Berlin-Grunewald*. A tuition-free residential school founded with Elizabeth Duncan for twenty girls: simultaneously an educational institution, communal-life experiment, and project for transmitting Duncan’s dance beyond her own body. Elizabeth directed much of its daily work, so sole authorship by Isadora would be misleading ([Deutsches Tanzarchiv](https://www.deutsches-tanzarchiv.de/archiv/nachlaesse-sammlungen/d/isadora-duncan)).
- **1908–1911:** *Neuilly and itinerant continuations of the school*. An unstable continuation rather than a single institution: Duncan acquired a Neuilly studio, while Elizabeth’s pupils also worked in Switzerland, France, Frankfurt, and Dresden before the related Darmstadt school opened ([Deutsches Tanzarchiv](https://www.deutsches-tanzarchiv.de/archiv/nachlaesse-sammlungen/d/isadora-duncan)).
- **1914–1920:** *Dionysion/Bellevue school*. A second Paris-area school established in the Bellevue property supplied by Paris Singer. It was a pedagogical Gesamtkunstwerk—building, community, visual environment, bodily curriculum, and performance project—and the building had to be sold in 1919–20 ([Deutsches Tanzarchiv](https://www.deutsches-tanzarchiv.de/archiv/nachlaesse-sammlungen/d/isadora-duncan)).
- **1915:** *Century Theatre experiment*. A short-lived New York communal production project combining drama, music, dance, teaching, and shared residence in an attempted renewal of Greek theatre; it ended after a fire-department eviction and resists classification as a completed work ([Layson choreochronicle](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/26100/1/405155.pdf)).
- **1921–1924:** *Moscow school*. Duncan’s project for a publicly supported school for working-class children in revolutionary Russia. She left amid failed state-financing promises, while Irma Duncan continued the institution; it is therefore both an unfinished Isadora project and a collaborative institution with an afterlife beyond her control ([Amsterdam University Press](https://www.aup.nl/nl/book/9789633869277/a-revolutionary-dancer-in-revolutionary-russia)).

### Manifestos, lectures, essays, and programmatic texts

The posthumous *Art of the Dance* was assembled from published essays, program notes, open letters, manuscripts, and sometimes several translated or retranslated versions. Its editor called the collection as complete as then possible but acknowledged variant texts and the likelihood of undiscovered manuscripts, so its contents should not be mistaken for a definitive authorial book planned by Duncan ([1928 edition](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b1/The_art_of_the_dance_%28IA_artofdancebyisad00dunc%29.pdf)).

- **1903:** *The Dance of the Future*. A lecture-manifesto first issued in German as *Der Tanz der Zukunft*; it acts by announcing a new bodily, educational, and social order rather than merely describing dance ([works bibliography](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/reference/isadora)).
- **1904:** *The Parthenon*. An essay or programmatic meditation joining ancient architecture, sculpture, and bodily movement ([1928 contents](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b1/The_art_of_the_dance_%28IA_artofdancebyisad00dunc%29.pdf)).
- **1905:** *The Dancer and Nature*. An essay-manifesto grounding dance in natural movement and the emancipation of the body ([1928 contents](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b1/The_art_of_the_dance_%28IA_artofdancebyisad00dunc%29.pdf)).
- **1906:** *What Dancing Should Be*. A prescriptive essay: part aesthetic manifesto, part protocol for a different dance education ([1928 contents](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b1/The_art_of_the_dance_%28IA_artofdancebyisad00dunc%29.pdf)).
- **1906:** *A Child Dancing*. An essay on childhood movement and education, later reprinted in *The Dancing Times* in 1925 ([works bibliography](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/reference/isadora)).
- **1909:** *Movement Is Life*. A programmatic essay identifying natural movement with life and criticizing imposed bodily discipline ([1928 contents](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b1/The_art_of_the_dance_%28IA_artofdancebyisad00dunc%29.pdf)).
- **1909:** *The Dance*. An authorized booklet distinct from *The Dance of the Future*, despite their recurrent bibliographic conflation ([NYPL collection bibliography](https://archives.nypl.org/dan/19640)).
- **1914:** *Beauty and Exercise*. An essay linking physical practice to Duncan’s aesthetic and educational program ([1928 contents](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b1/The_art_of_the_dance_%28IA_artofdancebyisad00dunc%29.pdf)).
- **1915:** *The Dance in Relation to Tragedy*. An essay on dance and tragic theatre, published again posthumously in *Theatre Arts* in 1927 ([works bibliography](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/reference/isadora)).
- **1915:** *The Greek Theatre*. An essay treating theatre as a synthesis of bodily movement, music, architecture, and communal ceremony—a textual argument for a Gesamtkunstwerk ([1928 contents](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b1/The_art_of_the_dance_%28IA_artofdancebyisad00dunc%29.pdf)).
- **1915:** *Dionysion*, vol. 1, no. 1. A performance pamphlet or short-lived magazine containing Duncan’s writing alongside Nietzsche, Whitman, Robert Henri, Percy MacKaye, and others, with drawings by Van Deering Perrine, Henri, and Bourdelle. It is not simply “a book by Duncan”: it is a collaborative print manifestation assembled to frame her Metropolitan Opera performances ([archive object record](https://isadoraduncanarchive.org/collection/archives/268)).
- **1917:** *The Dance*. An article in *Theatre Arts*; its relation to the 1909 booklet and other similarly titled texts requires textual comparison rather than an assumption of identity ([works bibliography](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/reference/isadora)).
- **1920:** *The Philosopher’s Stone of Dancing*. A theoretical essay presenting dance as a transformative principle ([1928 contents](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b1/The_art_of_the_dance_%28IA_artofdancebyisad00dunc%29.pdf)).
- **1927:** *I See America Dancing*. A national and social dance manifesto, subsequently anthologized under this title ([works bibliography](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/reference/isadora)).
- **1927:** *Dancing in Relation to Religion and Love*. A late essay published in *Theatre Arts* shortly before Duncan’s death ([works bibliography](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/reference/isadora)).
- *Education and the Dance*. An undated pedagogical essay collected posthumously; no date should be inferred from its placement in the volume ([1928 contents](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b1/The_art_of_the_dance_%28IA_artofdancebyisad00dunc%29.pdf)).
- *Terpsichore*. An undated essay or fragment collected posthumously ([1928 contents](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b1/The_art_of_the_dance_%28IA_artofdancebyisad00dunc%29.pdf)).
- *The Dance of the Greeks*. An undated essay on Duncan’s reconstructed—not archaeologically copied—idea of Greek movement ([1928 contents](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b1/The_art_of_the_dance_%28IA_artofdancebyisad00dunc%29.pdf)).
- *Youth and the Dance*. An undated essay collected posthumously ([1928 contents](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b1/The_art_of_the_dance_%28IA_artofdancebyisad00dunc%29.pdf)).
- *Depth*. An undated short text or fragment collected posthumously; its brevity makes “work” a less secure description than textual remainder ([1928 contents](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b1/The_art_of_the_dance_%28IA_artofdancebyisad00dunc%29.pdf)).
- *The Great Source*. An undated theoretical text collected posthumously ([1928 contents](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b1/The_art_of_the_dance_%28IA_artofdancebyisad00dunc%29.pdf)).
- *Richard Wagner*. An undated essay on Wagner and the relation between music and dance ([works bibliography](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/reference/isadora)).
- *A Letter to the Pupils*. An undated pedagogical letter turned into a public text by posthumous collection; it lies between correspondence, manifesto, and protocol ([1928 contents](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b1/The_art_of_the_dance_%28IA_artofdancebyisad00dunc%29.pdf)).
- *Moscow Impressions*. An undated account of revolutionary Russia collected posthumously ([1928 contents](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b1/The_art_of_the_dance_%28IA_artofdancebyisad00dunc%29.pdf)).
- *Reflections after Moscow*. An undated retrospective text collected posthumously ([1928 contents](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b1/The_art_of_the_dance_%28IA_artofdancebyisad00dunc%29.pdf)).
- *Fragments and Thoughts*. Editorially gathered textual remnants rather than a securely bounded work made and titled as such by Duncan ([1928 contents](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b1/The_art_of_the_dance_%28IA_artofdancebyisad00dunc%29.pdf)).
- *L’Envoi*. A closing text in the posthumous collection; its status as an independent authorial work is uncertain because the volume itself was editor-made ([1928 contents](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b1/The_art_of_the_dance_%28IA_artofdancebyisad00dunc%29.pdf)).

### Autobiographical and archival bodies of writing

- **1927:** *My Life*. Duncan’s autobiography, published during her lifetime but ending before her final years; later “restored” editions recover material omitted or altered in earlier publication, so the work has no entirely neutral definitive text ([works bibliography](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/reference/isadora)).
- **1928:** *The Art of the Dance*. A posthumous collected-works construction edited by Sheldon Cheney from Duncan’s manuscripts, fugitive publications, program notes, letters, and variant translations. It is an indispensable corpus of Duncan’s writing, but the made object as a book belongs partly to its editor and memorializing collaborators ([1928 edition](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b1/The_art_of_the_dance_%28IA_artofdancebyisad00dunc%29.pdf)).
- **1981:** *Isadora Speaks: Writings and Speeches of Isadora Duncan*. A posthumous scholarly recovery edited by Franklin Rosemont, grouping previously scattered writings and speeches on childhood, education, women, revolutionary Russia, Sergei Esenin, love, and America. The collection is not itself a lifetime Duncan work, but it manifests a broader textual corpus omitted from Cheney’s dance-centered volume ([bibliographic record](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL3781597M/Isadora_speaks)).
- **Published posthumously:** *What Love Means to Different Men*. A text attributed to Duncan and printed in George Seldes’s “What Love Meant to Isadora Duncan” in 1930; its mediated, posthumous presentation makes its independent title and textual completeness uncertain ([works bibliography](https://www.isadoraduncanarchive.org/reference/isadora)).
- **1904–1927:** *Letters*. Duncan produced a substantial correspondence, including letters to Gordon Craig, pupils, patrons, officials, and collaborators. These are made texts but not a single obra; later selections impose editorial boundaries on a dispersed archival body ([NYPL Irma Duncan collection](https://archives.nypl.org/dan/19640)).
