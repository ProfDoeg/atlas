## Works by Abraham Lincoln

### Documentary obra: the surviving corpus

- **1824–1865:** *The writings and utterances of Abraham Lincoln*. This is the closest defensible answer to “everything Lincoln made”: thousands of letters, speeches, memoranda, legislative drafts, presidential messages, proclamations, orders, legal papers, surveys, calculations, fragments, and signed instruments. No closed item-level catalogue exists: Basler’s eight-volume *Collected Works* and two supplements remain the standard baseline, while the continuing *Papers of Abraham Lincoln* includes newly located material and distinguishes documents written by Lincoln from those merely signed by or addressed to him. ([Collected Works](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/); [Papers of Abraham Lincoln: editorial scope](https://papersofabrahamlincoln.org/about))

### Oratory as manifestation

- **1832:** *Address to the People of Sangamon County*. A printed campaign address announcing Lincoln’s first candidacy and political program; it is his earliest substantial surviving political text. ([Collected Works, vol. 1](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/l/lincoln/lincoln1))

- **1838-01-27:** *Address before the Young Men’s Lyceum of Springfield, Illinois (“The Perpetuation of Our Political Institutions”)*. A formally prepared civic oration about law, violence, and the survival of republican government. ([Collected Works, vol. 1](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/l/lincoln/lincoln1); [Library of Congress chronology](https://www.loc.gov/collections/abraham-lincoln-papers/articles-and-essays/abraham-lincoln-timeline/))

- **1842-02-22:** *Address before the Springfield Washingtonian Temperance Society*. A reform oration advocating persuasion rather than denunciation; it is neither legislation nor manifesto, but public moral argument. ([Selected speeches based on the Collected Works](https://www.abrahamlincolnonline.org/lincoln/speeches/speech.htm))

- **1844–1858:** *Campaign speeches and stump addresses*. A large, incompletely preserved oral obra: reports range from summaries and fragments to texts Lincoln prepared or corrected, so a newspaper report is often a manifestation of a speech rather than an authorial manuscript. ([Collected Works, vols. 1–2](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/); [Papers of Abraham Lincoln: editorial scope](https://papersofabrahamlincoln.org/about))

- **1848-01-12:** *Speech in the United States House of Representatives on the Mexican War*. A congressional argument developing Lincoln’s “spot resolutions” attack on President Polk’s account of the war’s beginning. ([Library of Congress resource guide](https://guides.loc.gov/abraham-lincoln-guide/digital-collections); [Collected Works, vol. 1](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/l/lincoln/lincoln1))

- **1848-07-27:** *Speech in the United States House of Representatives on the Presidential Question*. A partisan campaign speech supporting Zachary Taylor and attacking Lewis Cass. ([Collected Works, vol. 1](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/l/lincoln/lincoln1))

- **1852-07-06:** *Eulogy on Henry Clay*. A memorial address and political portrait of the statesman Lincoln called his political ideal. ([Selected speeches based on the Collected Works](https://www.abrahamlincolnonline.org/lincoln/speeches/speech.htm))

- **1854-10-16:** *Speech at Peoria, Illinois*. A long antislavery political argument against the Kansas–Nebraska Act; surviving texts derive from newspaper publication rather than a complete autograph manuscript. ([National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/liho/learn/historyculture/speeches.htm); [Library of Congress chronology](https://www.loc.gov/collections/abraham-lincoln-papers/articles-and-essays/abraham-lincoln-timeline/))

- **1856-05-29:** *Speech at the Bloomington Convention (“Lost Speech”)*. An oral manifestation whose authoritative text was not preserved; purported reconstructions are disputed, so the project of the speech survives more securely than its words. ([Collected Works, vol. 2](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1857-06-26:** *Speech at Springfield on the Dred Scott Decision*. A political and constitutional address answering Stephen Douglas’s defense of the Supreme Court decision. ([Collected Works, vol. 2](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1858-06-16:** *Address to the Illinois Republican State Convention (“House Divided” Speech)*. A prepared campaign manifesto in the broad sense: it interprets recent political events as a coordinated movement toward nationalizing slavery and announces resistance to it. ([National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/liho/learn/historyculture/speeches.htm); [Library of Congress chronology](https://www.loc.gov/collections/abraham-lincoln-papers/articles-and-essays/abraham-lincoln-timeline/))

- **1858-08-21–1858-10-15:** *The Lincoln–Douglas Debates*. Seven jointly produced public events, not a single authored book: Lincoln made his own openings, replies, and rejoinders, while the familiar textual obra was assembled from rival partisan newspaper transcriptions whose accuracy differs. ([Library of Congress chronology](https://www.loc.gov/collections/abraham-lincoln-papers/articles-and-essays/abraham-lincoln-timeline/); [Collected Works, vol. 3](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1858-07–1858-10:** *Additional speeches in the senatorial campaign*. Addresses at Chicago, Springfield, Clinton, Edwardsville, Winchester, Paris, Carlinville, and other places form an incompletely recorded companion series to the formal debates. Their surviving forms include manuscripts, newspaper reports, and editorial composites. ([Collected Works, vol. 2](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/); [Collected Works, vol. 3](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1858–1860:** *Lecture on Discoveries and Inventions*. A lecture project surviving in two principal manuscript versions; Lincoln delivered versions of it on the lecture circuit, but it never became a finished book. ([Selected speeches based on the Collected Works](https://www.abrahamlincolnonline.org/lincoln/speeches/speech.htm))

- **1859-09-30:** *Address before the Wisconsin State Agricultural Society*. An occasional address on labor, cultivation, education, and technological improvement. ([Selected speeches based on the Collected Works](https://www.abrahamlincolnonline.org/lincoln/speeches/speech.htm))

- **1859-09-17:** *Speech at Cincinnati, Ohio*. A campaign address directed partly to Kentuckians and other Southerners, explaining what a Republican administration would and would not do. The surviving text depends substantially on newspaper reporting. ([*Political Writings of Abraham Lincoln*, contents](https://assets.cambridge.org/97810095/35915/frontmatter/9781009535915_frontmatter.pdf))

- **1860-02-27:** *Address at Cooper Institute (“Cooper Union Address”)*. A documented historical argument about the Founders’ treatment of slavery in the territories; its delivery and subsequent pamphlet and newspaper publication made it both an event and a reproducible campaign text. ([National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/liho/learn/historyculture/speeches.htm); [Collected Works, vol. 3](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1861-02-11:** *Farewell Address at Springfield, Illinois*. An extemporaneous railway-platform address; Lincoln wrote down a version after speaking, so the surviving manuscript is a retrospective textual manifestation rather than a verbatim record of the event. ([National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/liho/learn/historyculture/speeches.htm); [Collected Works, vol. 4](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1861-02-11–1861-02-23:** *Addresses on the inaugural journey*. A serial, partly improvised obra comprising speeches and remarks at railway stops from Springfield to Washington; newspaper reports, prepared texts, and later recollections preserve the events unevenly. ([Collected Works, vol. 4](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1861-02-22:** *Address in Independence Hall*. A brief declaration connecting preservation of the Union to the principles of the Declaration of Independence. ([National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/liho/learn/historyculture/speeches.htm))

- **1861-03-04:** *First Inaugural Address*. A constitutional and political manifesto intended to avert disunion while declaring that federal law would be maintained; William H. Seward suggested revisions, but Lincoln controlled the final composition. ([National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/liho/learn/historyculture/speeches.htm); [Library of Congress: Abraham Lincoln Papers](https://www.loc.gov/collections/abraham-lincoln-papers/about-this-collection/))

- **1863-11-19:** *Address at the Dedication of the Gettysburg National Cemetery (“Gettysburg Address”)*. A ceremonial speech and compact act of national rededication. Five manuscript versions in Lincoln’s hand survive, differing in wording and occasion; no single manuscript can simply be called the unique original delivered text. ([Library of Congress: Gettysburg Address exhibition](https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/gettysburg-address/); [Library of Congress collection overview](https://www.loc.gov/collections/abraham-lincoln-papers/about-this-collection/))

- **1864-03-18:** *Address at the Sanitary Fair, Washington, D.C.*. A brief public reflection on the meaning of “liberty,” known through contemporary reporting. ([Collected Works, vol. 7](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1864-04-18:** *Address at the Baltimore Sanitary Fair*. An occasional wartime speech discussing liberty and reporting the massacre at Fort Pillow; the surviving text is a newspaper manifestation. ([Collected Works, vol. 7](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1864-11-10:** *Response to a Serenade following Reelection*. A brief victory address interpreting the election as evidence that a republic could conduct an election during civil war. ([Collected Works, vol. 8](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1865-03-04:** *Second Inaugural Address*. A presidential address that resists ordinary policy classification: it is simultaneously civic theology, historical interpretation, confession, and program for reconciliation. ([National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/liho/learn/historyculture/speeches.htm); [Library of Congress collection overview](https://www.loc.gov/collections/abraham-lincoln-papers/about-this-collection/))

- **1865-04-11:** *Last Public Address*. A speech on reconstruction, Louisiana, and limited Black suffrage; it was Lincoln’s final public speech, but survives through press reports rather than a complete authorial manuscript. ([Library of America](https://www.loa.org/writers/187-abraham-lincoln/); [Library of Congress chronology](https://www.loc.gov/collections/abraham-lincoln-papers/articles-and-essays/abraham-lincoln-timeline/))

### Legislative and electoral texts

- **1834–1842:** *Illinois General Assembly bills, resolutions, reports, protests, and voting instruments*. This is collaborative legislative production, often drafted, amended, copied, or signed with other legislators; authorship therefore belongs at different levels to Lincoln, committees, clerks, and the assembly. ([Collected Works, vol. 1](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/l/lincoln/lincoln1); [Papers of Abraham Lincoln: editorial scope](https://papersofabrahamlincoln.org/about))

- **1837-03-03:** *Protest in the Illinois Legislature on the Subject of Slavery*. A formally entered legislative protest by Lincoln and Dan Stone; it is a jointly signed political declaration, not solely Lincoln’s private composition. ([Collected Works, vol. 1](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/l/lincoln/lincoln1))

- **1847-12-22:** *Spot Resolutions*. A sequence of interrogatory resolutions asking President Polk to identify the precise place where blood had first been shed in the Mexican War; they are legislative protocol designed to compel an account. ([Collected Works, vol. 1](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/l/lincoln/lincoln1))

- **1849-01-10:** *Bill to Abolish Slavery in the District of Columbia*. A legislative project for compensated, gradual abolition with a referendum provision. Lincoln prepared it but did not formally introduce it, so its unfinished political status is essential to what it is. ([Collected Works, vol. 2](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1859–1860:** *Autobiographical campaign sketches*. Two short third-person autobiographies supplied for political use, including the longer 1860 sketch prepared for John L. Scripps; they are campaign materials rather than autonomous memoirs. ([Collected Works, vol. 3](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1860-05-23:** *Acceptance of the Republican presidential nomination*. A formal letter accepting nomination; its conventional brevity makes it an electoral instrument as much as literary correspondence. ([Collected Works, vol. 4](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/); [*Political Writings*, contents](https://assets.cambridge.org/97810095/35915/frontmatter/9781009535915_frontmatter.pdf))

### Presidential manifestos and state papers

- **1861-04-15:** *Proclamation Calling Militia and Convening Congress*. A performative state text: it summoned 75,000 militia and called a special session of Congress after the attack on Fort Sumter. Its efficacy lay in issuance, not description. ([Collected Works, vol. 4](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1861-04-19 and 1861-04-27:** *Proclamations of Blockade*. Executive manifestos announcing a blockade of seceded-state ports, first for seven states and then for Virginia and North Carolina. These are related but distinct legal acts. ([Collected Works, vol. 4](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1861-07-04:** *Message to Congress in Special Session*. A major state paper explaining the administration’s response to secession and presenting the conflict as a test of popular government. ([Collected Works, vol. 4](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1861-12-03:** *First Annual Message to Congress*. A state paper reporting on the rebellion, foreign affairs, administration, and colonization. It was transmitted in writing rather than delivered as a modern spoken State of the Union address. ([Library of Congress resource guide](https://guides.loc.gov/abraham-lincoln-guide/digital-collections); [Collected Works, vol. 5](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1862-03-06:** *Message to Congress Recommending Compensated Emancipation*. A presidential proposal asking Congress to support states that voluntarily abolished slavery; its surviving drafts show the policy as a developing project. ([Library of Congress emancipation guide](https://guides.loc.gov/emancipation-proclamation/digital-resources); [Collected Works, vol. 5](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1862-04-16:** *Act for the Release of Certain Persons Held to Service or Labor in the District of Columbia*. A statute signed by Lincoln abolishing slavery in the District with compensation. The statute is a collective congressional obra that Lincoln enacted by signature, not a text attributable to him alone. ([U.S. Senate history](https://www.senate.gov/artandhistory/history/common/generic/DCEmancipationAct.htm))

- **1862-05-19:** *Proclamation Revoking General David Hunter’s Emancipation Order*. A presidential act voiding Hunter’s proclamation while renewing Lincoln’s appeal for compensated state emancipation. ([Collected Works, vol. 5](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1862-09-22:** *Preliminary Emancipation Proclamation*. A conditional manifesto announcing that enslaved people in areas still in rebellion on 1863-01-01 would be declared free. It is both a military warning and the first stage of a two-part executive project. ([National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/exhibits/featured-documents/emancipation-proclamation); [Library of Congress emancipation guide](https://guides.loc.gov/emancipation-proclamation/digital-resources))

- **1862-12-01:** *Second Annual Message to Congress*. A wide-ranging written state paper culminating in a proposed constitutional plan for gradual compensated emancipation. ([Collected Works, vol. 5](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1863-01-01:** *Final Emancipation Proclamation*. A legal-military manifesto declaring enslaved people in designated rebellious areas free and authorizing their reception into United States armed service. Secretary of State William H. Seward and the cabinet participated in revision, while Lincoln issued and signed the final presidential instrument. ([National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/exhibits/featured-documents/emancipation-proclamation); [Library of Congress collection overview](https://www.loc.gov/collections/abraham-lincoln-papers/about-this-collection/))

- **1863-03-30:** *Proclamation Appointing a National Fast Day*. A presidential proclamation establishing a day of national humiliation, fasting, and prayer; Secretary of the Treasury Salmon P. Chase submitted a draft, making sole compositional authorship inappropriate even though Lincoln issued the act. ([Collected Works, vol. 6](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1863-09-15:** *Proclamation Suspending the Writ of Habeas Corpus*. A nationwide legal instrument implementing congressional authorization for suspension during the rebellion. ([Collected Works, vol. 6](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1863-10-03:** *Thanksgiving Proclamation*. A manifesto fixing the last Thursday of November as a national day of thanksgiving; William H. Seward drafted the text, so it is properly a Lincoln-issued presidential act, not an exclusively Lincoln-authored composition. ([National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/legislative/features/thanksgiving); [Collected Works, vol. 6](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1863-12-08:** *Third Annual Message to Congress*. A written state paper reviewing the war and emancipation and transmitting the accompanying proclamation of amnesty and reconstruction. ([Collected Works, vol. 7](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1863-12-08:** *Proclamation of Amnesty and Reconstruction*. A protocol-manifesto offering pardons under an oath and describing how loyal governments might be reestablished when participants equaled ten percent of a state’s 1860 electorate. It proposed a route rather than claiming it was the exclusive constitutional route. ([Collected Works, vol. 7](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1864-07-08:** *Proclamation concerning the Wade–Davis Bill*. A presidential statement withholding signature from the bill while declining to bind the executive to one reconstruction plan; often called a pocket-veto proclamation. ([Collected Works, vol. 7](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1864-09-24:** *Proclamation Declaring Martial Law in Kentucky*. A performative order extending enforcement of habeas-corpus suspension and establishing martial law in Kentucky. ([Collected Works, vol. 8](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1864-12-06:** *Fourth Annual Message to Congress*. Lincoln’s last annual written report, asserting that emancipation and the presidential election had made reversal of the Union war policy impossible. ([Collected Works, vol. 8](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1865-02-03:** *Joint Resolution Submitting the Thirteenth Amendment*. A congressional text approved by Lincoln although presidential approval was constitutionally unnecessary; his signature is a ceremonial and political intervention, not authorship of the amendment. ([National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/13th-amendment))

- **1861–1865:** *Presidential proclamations, executive orders, pardons, commissions, appointments, permits, passes, endorsements, and warrants*. Hundreds of instruments survive. Many resist treatment as individually authored “works”: cabinet officers, attorneys, clerks, or printed forms supplied wording, while Lincoln’s handwriting or signature converted them into executive acts. ([Collected Works, vols. 4–8](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/); [Papers of Abraham Lincoln: editorial scope](https://papersofabrahamlincoln.org/about))

### Military protocols and command texts

- **1861-04-01:** *Memorandum to William H. Seward (“Some Thoughts for the President’s Consideration”)*. A private answer to Seward’s proposal for reorganizing the administration; it defines presidential responsibility without becoming a public state paper. ([Collected Works, vol. 4](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1862-01-27:** *President’s General War Order No. 1*. A command protocol ordering a general advance of United States land and naval forces by February 22. Its failure as an operational timetable is part of its status as a made but unrealized plan. ([Collected Works, vol. 5](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1862-01-31:** *President’s Special War Order No. 1*. A more specific order directing the Army of the Potomac toward Manassas; General George B. McClellan disputed the plan and obtained permission to pursue a different approach. ([Collected Works, vol. 5](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1862-10-21:** *Meditation on the Divine Will*. An undated private memorandum conventionally assigned to September or October 1862. It was not published or addressed to anyone and resists classification as policy, prayer, or essay; even its familiar title is editorial. ([Collected Works, vol. 5](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1863-01-26:** *Letter to Major General Joseph Hooker*. A commission-like letter appointing and admonishing the new commander of the Army of the Potomac; it combines command, character judgment, and protocol. ([Collected Works, vol. 6](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1863-07-14:** *Unsent letter to Major General George G. Meade*. A private draft criticizing Meade for allowing Robert E. Lee’s army to escape after Gettysburg. Lincoln did not send it, so it remains an emotional and strategic writing project rather than an executed command. ([Collected Works, vol. 6](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1864-08-23:** *Memorandum on Probable Failure of Re-election (“Blind Memorandum”)*. A sealed political contingency protocol signed on the back by cabinet members who were not shown its contents; it planned cooperation with the president-elect to save the Union before inauguration. ([Library of Congress collection overview](https://www.loc.gov/collections/abraham-lincoln-papers/about-this-collection/); [Collected Works, vol. 7](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1865-02-05:** *Instructions to William H. Seward concerning a proposed peace agreement*. A cabinet memorandum limiting any negotiation to restored national authority and cessation of hostilities; it records a rejected peace project rather than an agreement. ([Collected Works, vol. 8](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1861–1865:** *Telegrams and military endorsements*. A vast serial command practice in which Lincoln requested information, moved personnel, suspended punishments, and intervened in operations. Many pieces are jointly mediated by telegraph operators or written as annotations on other people’s papers, so their boundaries as autonomous works are porous. ([Collected Works, vols. 4–8](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/); [Abraham Lincoln Papers finding aid](https://findingaids.loc.gov/repositories/19/resources/3823))

### Correspondence

- **1836–1865:** *Personal, political, professional, and presidential correspondence*. Thousands of outgoing letters and notes constitute a social obra rather than a designed book. The scholarly record remains open because documents continue to surface; incoming letters belong to Lincoln’s papers but are not things he made. ([Papers of Abraham Lincoln: editorial scope](https://papersofabrahamlincoln.org/about); [Collected Works and supplements](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1841-01-23:** *Letter to John T. Stuart*. A private report of Lincoln’s emotional collapse and broken engagement; it survives as correspondence, not as a deliberately public confessional work. ([Collected Works, vol. 1](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/l/lincoln/lincoln1))

- **1841-01-27:** *Letter to John F. Speed*. One of a sequence of intensely personal letters about Lincoln’s depression and Speed’s impending marriage; the sequence is an epistolary relationship, not a retrospectively authored cycle. ([Collected Works, vol. 1](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/l/lincoln/lincoln1))

- **1848-02-15:** *Letter to William H. Herndon on the Mexican War*. A political-legal explanation defending Lincoln’s challenge to President Polk’s territorial claim. ([Collected Works, vol. 1](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/l/lincoln/lincoln1))

- **1855-08-24:** *Letter to Joshua F. Speed*. A private political essay on slavery, nativism, and the erosion of the Declaration’s principles. ([Collected Works, vol. 2](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1859-04-06:** *Letter to Henry L. Pierce and others*. A refusal to attend a Jefferson birthday celebration that develops Lincoln’s interpretation of the Declaration of Independence. ([Collected Works, vol. 3](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1862-07-13:** *Letter to Edwin M. Stanton and Gideon Welles*. A short written proposal to employ Black workers and organize armed Black forces; it records an emancipation-related policy project before public adoption. ([Collected Works, vol. 5](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1862-08-22:** *Letter to Horace Greeley*. A public letter answering Greeley’s “Prayer of Twenty Millions” and defining Lincoln’s official object as saving the Union; because it was composed for newspaper publication, it functions as a policy manifesto in epistolary form. ([Collected Works, vol. 5](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1863-06-12:** *Letter to Erastus Corning and others*. A public defense of military arrests and suspension of habeas corpus, written to be read to a Democratic meeting. It is a state paper disguised only superficially as private correspondence. ([Collected Works, vol. 6](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1863-08-26:** *Letter to James C. Conkling*. A public letter defending emancipation and Black military service; Lincoln instructed that it be read at a Union rally, making its oral presentation part of the work. ([Collected Works, vol. 6](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1864-04-04:** *Letter to Albert G. Hodges*. A retrospective explanation of how military necessity altered Lincoln’s action on slavery. It was a private letter subsequently copied and published with his consent. ([Library of Congress emancipation guide](https://guides.loc.gov/emancipation-proclamation/digital-resources); [Collected Works, vol. 7](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1864-11-21:** *Letter to Mrs. Lydia Bixby*. A condolence letter whose text survives through printed and facsimile manifestations because the original is lost. Its authorship has long been disputed, with some scholars attributing composition to John Hay; it should not be silently treated as certainly Lincoln’s. ([Collected Works, vol. 8](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/); [Library of Congress discussion](https://www.loc.gov/loc/lcib/0403-4/bixby.html))

- **1865-03-13:** *Letter to Thurlow Weed on the Second Inaugural Address*. A brief authorial reflection anticipating that the address would not be immediately popular but might endure. ([Collected Works, vol. 8](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

### Legal obra

- **1836–1861:** *The law practice of Abraham Lincoln*. More than five thousand cases and roughly 96,000 associated documents have been identified in the complete documentary edition. The archive includes pleadings, declarations, pleas, briefs, affidavits, agreements, fee records, docket entries, and notes, but many were made by partners, clerks, courts, clients, or opposing counsel; “Lincoln’s case” does not mean “Lincoln authored every paper.” ([National Archives project description](https://www.archives.gov/nhprc/projects/catalog/abraham-lincoln); [Law Practice of Abraham Lincoln](https://www.lawpracticeofabrahamlincoln.org/))

- **1839–1861:** *Lincoln partnership papers*. Documents from Lincoln’s successive practices with John T. Stuart, Stephen T. Logan, and William H. Herndon form collaborative professional corpora whose individual handwriting and authorship must be established document by document. ([Law Practice of Abraham Lincoln](https://www.lawpracticeofabrahamlincoln.org/))

- **1855:** *Notes and arguments for McCormick v. Manny (“The Reaper Case”)*. A patent-litigation project for which Lincoln researched and prepared but was excluded from presenting the defense; the unperformed argument is therefore the work’s defining frustration. ([Library of Congress chronology](https://www.loc.gov/collections/abraham-lincoln-papers/articles-and-essays/abraham-lincoln-timeline/); [Law Practice of Abraham Lincoln](https://www.lawpracticeofabrahamlincoln.org/))

- **1857:** *Argument in Hurd et al. v. Railroad Bridge Company (“Effie Afton” case)*. A courtroom argument defending the first railroad bridge across the Mississippi against claims that it obstructed navigation. Its authoritative form is reconstructed from notes and reports, not a finished publication by Lincoln. ([Library of Congress chronology](https://www.loc.gov/collections/abraham-lincoln-papers/articles-and-essays/abraham-lincoln-timeline/); [Law Practice of Abraham Lincoln](https://www.lawpracticeofabrahamlincoln.org/))

- **1858:** *Defense in People v. Armstrong*. A trial performance, not a freestanding authored text: Lincoln’s cross-examination and use of an almanac helped secure acquittal, but later retellings have embellished the episode. ([Library of Congress chronology](https://www.loc.gov/collections/abraham-lincoln-papers/articles-and-essays/abraham-lincoln-timeline/); [Law Practice of Abraham Lincoln](https://www.lawpracticeofabrahamlincoln.org/))

### Technical and graphic works

- **1824–1826:** *Sum book*. A manuscript arithmetic notebook containing calculations, rules, and copied exercises. It is a school artifact and learning protocol rather than an authored mathematical treatise; surviving leaves may represent only part of the original book. ([Collected Works, vol. 1](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/l/lincoln/lincoln1))

- **1833–1836:** *Survey plats, field notes, and calculations*. As deputy surveyor in Sangamon County, Lincoln made measured graphic-textual representations of roads, towns, and property. Surviving examples are functional technical objects, and the corpus is incomplete. ([Papers of Abraham Lincoln: editorial scope](https://papersofabrahamlincoln.org/about); [Collected Works, vol. 1](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/l/lincoln/lincoln1))

- **1849-05-22:** *Patent No. 6,469, “Manner of Buoying Vessels”*. An invention for lifting vessels over shoals by expandable buoyant chambers, represented by specification, drawings, and a scale model. It remained an unrealized project—there is no evidence it was commercially built—and Lincoln is the only United States president to have received a patent. ([Smithsonian National Museum of American History](https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_213141); [United States Patent and Trademark Office](https://www.uspto.gov/learning-and-resources/journeys-innovation/historical-stories/abraham-lincoln-patent-model-improvement))

### Literary experiments, satires, and fragments

- **1834:** *The “Sampson’s Ghost” burlesque*. A satirical poem associated with local Illinois political conflict; the attribution to Lincoln rests on recollection rather than a surviving autograph and should be treated as uncertain. ([Collected Works, vol. 1](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/l/lincoln/lincoln1))

- **1837:** *The “Trailor Murder Case” poem*. A comic verse narrative attributed to Lincoln; its textual history and title are editorial, and evidence of authorship is less secure than for writings surviving in his hand. ([Collected Works, vol. 1](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/l/lincoln/lincoln1))

- **1842:** *The “Rebecca” letters*. Anonymous satirical newspaper letters attacking Illinois state auditor James Shields, produced within a circle that included Mary Todd and Julia Jayne. Lincoln accepted responsibility, but the division of composition is disputed; they nearly precipitated a duel and resist secure treatment as solely his work. ([Library of America](https://www.loa.org/books/74-speeches-and-writings-1832-1858/); [Collected Works, vol. 1](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/l/lincoln/lincoln1))

- **1844–1846:** *Poems on Indiana and childhood (“My Childhood-Home I See Again” and related pieces)*. A group of autobiographical poems prompted by Lincoln’s return to Indiana; manuscript and letter versions preserve a project rather than a poetry collection prepared for publication. ([Collected Works, vol. 1](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/l/lincoln/lincoln1))

- **1846:** *The Bear Hunt*. A comic poem sent to Andrew Johnston as part of Lincoln’s verse correspondence; it belongs to an unfinished literary exchange, not an independent published volume. ([Collected Works, vol. 1](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/l/lincoln/lincoln1))

- **c. 1846:** *The Suicide’s Soliloquy*. A poem published anonymously in 1838 and later proposed as Lincoln’s work. The attribution is controversial and absent from Basler’s original *Collected Works*; it must remain an attributed possibility, not a settled Lincoln title. ([Abraham Lincoln Association discussion](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/for-the-people/))

- **c. 1850:** *Notes for a Law Lecture*. An unfinished professional essay or lecture manuscript advising young lawyers. Its title and approximate date are editorial, and there is no proof that Lincoln delivered it publicly. ([Collected Works, vol. 2](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **c. 1854:** *Fragment on Slavery*. An undated argumentative manuscript conventionally placed in 1854; it is working thought rather than a completed address, and its familiar title is editorial. ([Collected Works, vol. 2](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **c. 1858:** *Fragment on the Constitution and Union*. An unfinished manuscript distinguishing the Declaration’s principle from constitutional means; the date and title are editorial approximations. ([Collected Works, vol. 3](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **c. 1858:** *Fragment on Government*. A brief political-philosophical note defining legitimate government by the tasks people cannot perform individually; it was not made into a public essay. ([Collected Works, vol. 3](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **c. 1858–1860:** *Fragments on free labor, capital, and slavery*. A cluster of working notes later developed in speeches and presidential messages. Editorial grouping helps reveal a project, but Lincoln did not constitute them as a titled work. ([Collected Works, vol. 3](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

### Physical making and collaborative constructions

- **1828 and 1831:** *Flatboats to New Orleans*. Lincoln participated in building or preparing flatboats and transporting cargo down the Mississippi. These were collaborative utilitarian objects, now lost, and the evidence does not permit individual design authorship or fixed titles. They resist inclusion in a textual “works” catalogue but belong in an atlas of things he helped make. ([National Park Service chronology](https://home.nps.gov/liho/learn/historyculture/lincolnchronology.htm))

- **1830:** *The Lincoln family cabin in Macon County, Illinois*. Lincoln helped his father’s household clear land and construct a log dwelling. The original structure does not survive securely as an attributable architectural work, and later “Lincoln cabin” reconstructions are commemorative manifestations, not the object he made. ([National Park Service chronology](https://home.nps.gov/liho/learn/historyculture/lincolnchronology.htm))

- **1830–1831:** *Fence rails and related farm work*. Lincoln split rails and made fencing as hired and household labor. No catalogue of individual objects exists and none can be securely identified today; “the rails Lincoln split” became a campaign symbol in 1860, which is a later political manifestation rather than a recoverable oeuvre of artifacts. ([Library of Congress chronology](https://www.loc.gov/collections/abraham-lincoln-papers/articles-and-essays/abraham-lincoln-timeline/))

### Things often mistaken for works by Lincoln

- **1858–1860:** *Political newspaper transcriptions and campaign biographies*. These helped construct Lincoln’s public persona but were generally made by reporters, editors, and biographers; Lincoln’s corrections or supplied autobiographical information do not make every resulting publication his obra. ([Collected Works, vols. 2–4](https://abrahamlincolnassociation.org/abraham-lincoln-collected-works/))

- **1861–1865:** *Federal statutes and cabinet-drafted presidential papers bearing Lincoln’s signature*. Signature may enact, approve, authenticate, or witness a text without establishing verbal authorship. Such instruments belong to Lincoln’s presidential acts, but claims that he “wrote” them require manuscript or editorial evidence. ([Papers of Abraham Lincoln: editorial scope](https://papersofabrahamlincoln.org/about))

- **1861–1865:** *Documents addressed to Lincoln*. Incoming correspondence is indispensable evidence about his administration but is not his creation merely because it survives among his papers. The *Papers of Abraham Lincoln* intentionally includes documents written to and addressed to him, whereas an inventory of his works must keep that broader archival scope distinct from authorship. ([Papers of Abraham Lincoln: editorial scope](https://papersofabrahamlincoln.org/about); [Abraham Lincoln Papers finding aid](https://findingaids.loc.gov/repositories/19/resources/3823))
