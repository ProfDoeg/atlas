# Xul Solar: Research Dossier

## Research Scope and Evidentiary Note

This dossier concerns the Argentine artist, writer, linguist, musician, astrologer, and inventor born **Oscar Agustín Alejandro Schulz Solari** (1887–1963).

The surviving record is uneven. His European movements can be reconstructed from correspondence, catalogues, personal papers, and later archival research, but some popular biographies conflate distinct journeys or convert possibilities into facts. His occult experiences present an additional problem: the underlying documents demonstrate that Xul recorded visions and corresponded with Aleister Crowley, but they cannot establish the supernatural interpretations he assigned to them.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by archival material, institutional records, contemporaneous publications, objects, or multiple strong sources.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a credible secondary source but not independently established here.
- **Disputed claim:** incompatible versions exist.
- **Unverified claim:** repeated without sufficient supporting documentation.
- **Myth or legend:** a story important to Xul’s reputation but not demonstrable as literal history.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full birth name:** Oscar Agustín Alejandro Schulz Solari.
- **Usual professional name:** Xul Solar.
- **Other signatures and forms:** Alejandro Schulz Solari; Alessandro Xul Solari; Xul; occasionally variant spellings of Schulz or Solari in catalogues.
- **Name construction:** “Xul” reverses the sound or letters of *lux* (“light”) and approximates the Spanish pronunciation of Schulz; “Solar” condenses Solari while invoking the sun. The combined name consequently suggested “solar light.” Its adoption was gradual, beginning during his European period rather than through one securely dated legal change.
- **Born:** **14 December 1887**, San Fernando, Province of Buenos Aires, Argentina.
- **Died:** **9 April 1963**, Tigre district, Province of Buenos Aires, Argentina, aged seventy-five.
- **Nationality:** Argentine.
- **Principal occupations:** painter and draftsman; writer; illustrator; translator; astrologer; designer of constructed languages, games, writing systems, musical instruments, puppets, and architectural projects.
- **Principal media:** watercolor, tempera, gouache, ink, graphite, occasional oil, painted objects, cards, game boards, sculptural constructions, altered pianos, and what he called *grafías plastiútiles*.
- **Languages:** Spanish, German, Italian, French, and English from childhood or schooling; he subsequently studied Latin, Greek, Sanskrit or Sanskrit-derived terminology, Guaraní, and other languages to varying degrees. Claims that he “spoke twenty languages” should be treated as reputation rather than a demonstrated measure of proficiency.
- **Religion and worldview:** raised in a Christian milieu; later developed an eclectic esotericism drawing on Christianity, astrology, Theosophy, Rosicrucian material, Kabbalah, Buddhism, Hindu traditions, the *I Ching*, ceremonial magic, numerology, and beliefs in reincarnation.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

Xul was the son of:

- **Emilio Schulz Riga**, of German or Baltic-German ancestry, described in biographical records as an engineer connected with naval or riverine work.
- **Agustina Solari**, from an Italian, specifically Ligurian, family.

The maternal family’s house at **Zoagli**, on the Ligurian coast near Genoa, became Xul’s most stable European base between 1912 and 1924.

### Siblings and childhood household

A younger sister, generally identified as **Sara**, died in infancy. The early death appears to have left Xul as the surviving child of the immediate household. His maternal aunt **Clorinda Solari** was a significant family figure and kept a notebook recording his childhood schooling, illnesses, violin instruction, a broken arm, early employment, and architectural studies. Patricia Artundo emphasizes that this notebook is one of the few substantial sources for his pre-1912 life.

### Marriage and children

Xul met **Micaela Cadenas**, known as **Lita**, during the 1930s. Sources disagree on whether the initial meeting occurred in 1936 or slightly earlier. They married on **13 August 1946**. He called her his *cuidra*, a Neo-Creole formation meaning caretaker or guardian.

Lita participated in his astrological and intellectual world, read or interpreted cards, protected his papers, and became the central custodian of his legacy. The marriage produced **no children**.

A frequently repeated story, attributed to the poet Olga Orozco, says that Xul believed his horoscope normally forbade marriage but revealed a forty-eight-hour opening in which he could propose to Lita. This is best classified as a **reported reminiscence**, not an independently verifiable event.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1887–1905

Xul was born in San Fernando, north of Buenos Aires, in a multilingual immigrant household. His German and Italian family background was reinforced by French- and English-language schooling. Museum educational material records childhood interests in trains, books, music, and mechanical objects.

He learned violin and encountered other instruments at home, including his father’s zither. Music remained structurally important to his later painting and invention. His surviving library and instruments make clear that this was more than a decorative biographical anecdote.

The family moved to Buenos Aires around **1901**, after several earlier changes of address. No sustained evidence has emerged of formal childhood art training. Later descriptions of Xul as “self-taught” are therefore broadly accurate for painting, though they can obscure his schooling in architecture, drawing, languages, and music.

---

## Education and Formation, 1905–1912

Xul enrolled in architectural studies at the University of Buenos Aires, usually dated to **1905 or 1906**. He remained for approximately two years and did not graduate. Sources differ about the exact enrollment and withdrawal years.

Architecture nevertheless remained central to his work. Imagined cities, temples, ladders, floating dwellings, façades, communal buildings, and urban reform recur throughout his paintings and designs.

Before leaving Argentina, he held several short-lived jobs, reportedly including school teaching and municipal administrative work. Exact posts and dates remain insufficiently documented in readily accessible sources.

There is no evidence that Xul patented an invention, founded a conventional business, or accumulated professional credentials during this period.

## The European Years, 1912–1924

### Departure and disputed destination

On **5 April 1912**, Xul departed aboard the *England Carrier* or similarly rendered vessel name. A popular story says that he signed on intending to reach **Hong Kong**, possibly to become a Tibetan monk, but abandoned that plan after reaching Europe.

- The voyage and European arrival are **documented facts**.
- Hong Kong as the ticketed or announced destination is widely reported.
- The intention “to become a Tibetan monk” is a **reported or romanticized claim** whose documentary basis is less secure.

He reached London, then traveled through Paris and Turin. Over the next twelve years, his movements included repeated stays in **London, Paris, Turin, Milan, Florence, Munich, Hamburg, and Zoagli**, with other Italian and German locations appearing in his papers.

### First artistic development

A 1912 letter to his father states that he believed he had independently begun working in the direction of the future “higher art.” The letter is important evidence of both artistic ambition and a self-created prophetic identity.

During these years he studied modern European art through exhibitions, books, reproductions, and contacts rather than through enrollment in an academy. Relevant currents included:

- Expressionism and *Der Blaue Reiter*;
- Futurism;
- Cubism;
- Symbolism;
- abstraction and Neo-Plasticism;
- the work of Wassily Kandinsky and, increasingly, Paul Klee.

Claims that he formally “joined” *Der Blaue Reiter* are unsupported. He acquired or consulted its publications and absorbed its ideas; the group itself had effectively dispersed during the First World War.

### Occult networks

Archival research by Patricia Artundo substantially revised the chronology of Xul’s occult contacts.

Xul reported that the English artist and occultist **Austin Osman Spare** introduced him in **1913** to the teachings of **A∴A∴**, Aleister Crowley’s initiatory organization. The report is evidence of what Xul said; the precise circumstances remain uncertain.

During a London stay in **1919–1920**, his papers permit researchers to trace visits or contacts involving Theosophical Society lodges, Buddhist circles, and related publications. He acquired Jiddu Krishnamurti’s *The Messenger of the Star*. A possible meeting with the Buddhist teacher Allan Bennett has been proposed but is not securely established.

Popular accounts sometimes say Xul met Crowley in Paris in 1914. The strongest archival reconstruction instead treats an early encounter as uncertain and establishes an actual meeting in **1924**.

### First World War

Xul remained in Europe throughout the First World War. Museum teaching material says that he and Emilio Pettoruti lived cheaply, at times selling painted postcards and subsisting on polenta and milk. This is best treated as a **reported reminiscence**, probably based on Pettoruti and family testimony.

Paintings such as *Ruinas* and *Rua Ruini* have been interpreted in relation to wartime destruction, although they are not literal documentary records of combat.

### Emilio Pettoruti

Xul met Argentine painter **Emilio Pettoruti** in Italy, usually dated to **1916** in Florence or Milan. They became friends, collaborators, traveling companions, and advocates for artistic renewal in Argentina.

Pettoruti later claimed a role in encouraging Schulz Solari to adopt a memorable artistic name. Because Xul experimented with signatures over time, the name’s invention should be understood as a process rather than one conclusively documented conversation.

### Exhibitions in Europe

- **1920:** Xul exhibited with sculptor Arturo Martini at Galleria Arte, Milan, from 27 November to 16 December. The catalogue called him Alessandro Xul Solari.
- **1924:** He participated in the *Exposition d’Art Américain-Latin* at the Musée Galliera, Paris, 15 March–15 April.

These exhibitions brought limited commercial success. Available market research indicates that he sold little art in Europe and never established a secure income from painting.

### Crowley and the *San Signos*

In 1924 Xul met **Aleister Crowley**, apparently in Paris or during the final stages of his European stay; surviving Crowley papers and Leah Hirsig’s diary confirm contact.

Crowley instructed him in a method of controlled visionary work derived from Golden Dawn practices: meditate upon symbols, particularly the sixty-four hexagrams of the *I Ching*, and record the resulting visions. Crowley regarded him as an unusually able visionary.

Xul’s notebooks became the foundation for the work later titled **Los San Signos** or **San Signos**. The title is associated with Jorge Luis Borges. Xul revised and reorganized the material, notably in 1937–1938. The complete Spanish translation from Neo-Creole appeared only in 2012.

Claims that the visions reveal objectively existing supernatural realms belong to religious interpretation, not demonstrable historical fact.

### Return

Xul and Pettoruti sailed from Hamburg and reached Buenos Aires on or about **31 July 1924**, carrying works, books, and reproductions. The return ended twelve years of European residence and travel.

## Buenos Aires Avant-Garde, 1924–1932

Xul entered the circle around the magazine **Martín Fierro**, whose members included Jorge Luis Borges, Norah Borges, Oliverio Girondo, Macedonio Fernández, Leopoldo Marechal, and others associated with the Florida avant-garde.

He contributed drawings, commentary, and a defense of Pettoruti. His 1924 article on Pettoruti presented the painter as a father of the future “criollo” art and argued that cultural independence in the Americas remained unfinished.

### 1924 Witcomb disturbance

The October 1924 presentation of Pettoruti’s modernist work at Galería Witcomb provoked hostile reactions. Later accounts describe shouting, insults, spitting, and physical confrontation between traditionalist spectators and avant-garde defenders, including members of the *Martín Fierro* circle.

The general scandal is **documented through contemporary reception**; picturesque details vary among later retellings. Xul participated in the same modernist campaign but was not shown to have initiated violence.

### Exhibitions

- **1924:** Primer Salón Libre, Galería Witcomb.
- **1925:** Salón de los Independientes.
- **1926:** Exposición de Pintores Modernos, Amigos del Arte, with Pettoruti and Norah Borges.
- **1926:** exhibition at La Peña, Café Tortoni.
- **1927–1928:** several collective presentations.
- **1929:** first securely documented solo Buenos Aires exhibition at Amigos del Arte. Sixty-two works were catalogued, though fewer than twenty have since been identified with certainty.
- **1929–1931:** participation in Alfredo Guttero’s Nuevo Salón initiatives.
- **1932:** exhibition connected with La Plata’s fiftieth anniversary.

The phrase “first Buenos Aires exhibition” is sometimes attached incorrectly to 1924. That was a collective salon; his first individual Buenos Aires show was in 1929.

Prices in the 1929 catalogue ranged approximately from **50 to 150 Argentine pesos**. Evidence suggests few sales, often to friends rather than established collectors.

## Languages, Writing Systems, Games, and Technologies

### Neo-Creole (*neocriollo*)

Xul began developing Neo-Creole during the European period. Initially it fused Spanish and Portuguese; later versions incorporated English, German, French, Italian, Latin, Greek, and Guaraní elements.

Its purposes shifted:

- continental communication and Latin American unity;
- compression and simplification;
- poetic experimentation;
- a private or initiatory language;
- integration with his paintings and visionary records.

It was not adopted as a public language. Descriptions of it as a completed Esperanto-like system overstate its stability: Xul continually revised vocabulary and grammar.

### Panlengua

Panlengua was a more universal and systematic project. It attempted to connect phonetics, grammar, numeration, astrology, color, geometry, and musical structure. Its words were intended to be combinable and analytically transparent.

No definitive, finished grammar was published during Xul’s lifetime. Surviving notes support a substantial project, but claims that it was a fully operational universal language should be qualified.

### Panajedrez or Panjuego

Xul enlarged chess into an astrological, linguistic, numerical, and cosmological game, generally called **panajedrez**, **panjuego**, or “universal chess.” The board and pieces could encode letters, planets, signs, colors, musical relationships, and word formation.

Borges played it with him and later joked that Xul changed the rules each time he explained them. That remark contributed to the mythology of an unplayable infinite game. Surviving boards and rule materials demonstrate a genuine designed system, but also one that remained mutable.

### Duodecimal arithmetic

Xul promoted base-twelve calculation, treating twelve as more divisible and cosmologically appropriate than ten. He designed notations and related them to astrology, music, and Panlengua. There is no evidence that the system entered institutional mathematical use.

### Modified piano

He redesigned the piano keyboard, using colored and sometimes multiple rows of keys to facilitate musical learning and associate notes with visual or astrological correspondences. Surviving instruments establish the project as more than a paper proposal.

No patent located in the consulted record can be attributed to him. Calling these devices “inventions” is reasonable in an artistic sense but should not imply patented engineering.

### Puppets, tarot, and ritual objects

His surviving constructions include:

- tarot decks from approximately 1918 and 1954;
- *Marioneta I Ching*;
- the *Pan Altar Mundi*;
- carved crosses and rosaries;
- Panjuego boards and pieces;
- architectural models and household designs.

The 1954 tarot combined traditional imagery, astrology, numeration, and Neo-Creole. It was a working divinatory object as well as an artwork.

## The 1930s and 1940s

### Artistic and intellectual development

During the 1930s Xul increasingly combined Americanist imagery, flags, invented nations, pre-Columbian references, Christianity, astrology, and plans for spiritual or social reorganization.

His continental program should not be reduced either to conventional nationalism or to apolitical occultism. Neo-Creole sought a Latin American synthesis, while Panlengua moved toward universalism.

No strong evidence located in the adversarial search establishes membership in a political party, government intelligence service, violent organization, or fascist, communist, or Peronist movement.

### Astrology and spiritual teaching

From the late 1930s through the 1940s he drew natal charts for friends and cultural figures and offered lectures or courses on astrology and spiritual subjects. Charts survive for Jorge Luis Borges and Emilio Pettoruti.

Astrology provided an organizational matrix for colors, characters, buildings, games, language, and interpersonal interpretations. It was not merely a commercial sideline.

### Plastic writing

Around the late 1930s and 1940s Xul developed stenographic and pictographic systems in which text became the image. At his 1940 Amigos del Arte exhibition, critics Julio E. Payró and Jorge Romero Brest questioned whether eleven “all-written” pieces were paintings at all.

This was a documented critical dispute about artistic category, not an allegation of deception. Later historiography treats these pieces as an important fusion of language and image.

### Marriage and Laprida house

The Schulz Solari family acquired the property at **Laprida 1212/1214**, Buenos Aires, in the 1920s. Address numbering varies in sources. Xul lived and worked there with Lita after their 1946 marriage.

The building, his library, documents, paintings, games, and instruments eventually formed the basis of the Museo Xul Solar.

### Borges and literary collaborations

Xul and Borges maintained a long friendship centered on books, metaphysics, language, German literature, Swedenborg, Blake, Buddhism, the *I Ching*, astrology, and games.

Documented intersections include:

- Xul’s illustration of Borges’s **El idioma de los argentinos** (1928).
- Xul’s appearance by name in Borges’s **“Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius”** (1940).
- Illustrations for **Un modelo para la muerte** (1946 edition commonly dated or prepared from a 1944 collaboration), by Borges and Adolfo Bioy Casares under the pseudonym B. Suárez Lynch.
- Borges’s talks and writings about Xul, including the remembered characterization of him as a mystic in the lineage of Blake and Swedenborg.

Leopoldo Marechal transformed him into the astrologer **Schultze** in *Adán Buenosayres* (1948), one of the principal literary sources of the “magician of Buenos Aires” image.

## Later Life, 1949–1963

### Exhibitions and market position

Solo exhibitions included:

- **1949:** Galería Samos, Buenos Aires.
- **1951:** Galería Guión.
- **1953:** Galería Van Riel.

He also participated in the 1952 *Pintura y Escultura Argentina de Este Siglo* at the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes.

Research on his market shows that he remained financially marginal as an artist. He exhibited frequently but sold relatively few works; purchases often came from acquaintances. Assertions that he was wealthy from his art are unsupported. Family property and household support mattered more than commercial painting income.

### Tigre and “Li-Tao”

In **1954**, Xul acquired or established a house and studio on the **Río Luján** in the Tigre delta, often called **Li-Tao**. He spent increasingly long periods there and eventually made it his principal residence.

The house embodied his interest in small-scale architecture, waterways, isolation, contemplation, and the union of life with designed environment. His late paintings include more schematic architectural works, spiritual mountains, signs, and *grafías plastiútiles*.

### Music-paintings

Works such as:

- *Impromptu de Chopin* (1949),
- *Cinco melodías* (1949),
- *Coral Bach* (1950)

translated musical structures into visual patterns. They demonstrate the long continuity of his childhood musical training and total-art ambitions.

### Final work and death

His last years were devoted to Panlengua, plastic writing, astrological systems, and small tempera works such as *Mi pray per to min guardianjo* (1962).

Xul died at his Tigre home on **9 April 1963**. Accessible institutional sources state the date and place but do not give a medically documented cause. Therefore, a specific cause of death cannot be responsibly supplied.

A story says that he died holding a rosary he had carved. This is a **reported deathbed tradition**, possibly based on family testimony, not a medically or independently documented fact.

No reliable documentation located here establishes addiction, psychiatric hospitalization, imprisonment, exile, criminal prosecution, or a disabling accident in adulthood. His aunt’s family notebook reportedly mentions childhood illnesses and a broken arm.

The significant documented properties are the family building on Laprida Street and the Tigre house/studio. No large landholdings, corporate investments, or substantial securities portfolio emerged in the consulted research.

### Market and authentication authority

The Fundación Pan Klub–Museo Xul Solar identifies itself as the holder of intellectual-property rights and the principal issuer of authenticity certificates. That position has made it central to the modern Xul Solar market.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### Authentication dispute over *Gonfa 18*

**Documented controversy, unresolved authenticity in the consulted record.**

In May 2007, Buenos Aires auction house Arroyo offered the watercolor **Gonfa 18** (dated 1923). Natalio Povarché, then president of the Fundación Xul Solar and an expert in the artist’s work, called it doubtful, citing an atypical fluorescent-looking palette and the questionable title “Gonfa.”

Curator Patricia Artundo said she had previously rejected the piece after finding no documentary reference to it in a well-recorded period of Xul’s production.

The auction house relied on a 1995 certificate signed by members of the Asociación Argentina de Galerías de Arte. It proceeded with the sale, which reportedly reached **291,400 Argentine pesos**.

Povarché initially also questioned a drawing titled *Tronco* but withdrew that objection when shown a certificate bearing his own signature.

The episode demonstrates conflicting expert judgments and weaknesses in certificate-based authentication. The sources consulted do not establish a judicial finding that *Gonfa 18* was either genuine or forged.

### Broader forgery problem

Argentine art-market specialists have stated that false works attributed to Xul circulate. This is a **general professional allegation**, not proof against every disputed object. No evidence was found that Xul himself committed forgery or fraud.

### Critical rejection and market neglect

Contemporary critics sometimes treated his work as eccentric, incomprehensible, insufficiently painterly, or outside conventional art. His 1940 text-pictures prompted the explicit question “Are they works of art?”

Market studies indicate that he sold little during life and that his present high valuation developed posthumously. This creates incentives for misattribution and retrospective mythmaking but does not itself demonstrate wrongdoing by the estate or foundation.

### Adversarial search findings, including 2024–2026

Dedicated searches combined his name with controversy, scandal, allegations, lawsuit, fraud, accusations, conspiracy, criticism, forgery, and corresponding Spanish terms. The principal substantiated negative matters found were:

1. hostility surrounding the 1924 modern-art exhibitions;
2. critical dismissal or incomprehension during his lifetime;
3. later-market authentication disputes and alleged forgeries;
4. exaggeration in popular biographies about his occult contacts, linguistic fluency, and completed inventions.

No credible recent reporting from 2024–2026 located in this search alleged that Xul committed fraud, corruption, money laundering, political violence, sexual abuse, or other serious crime. Recent material instead concerns exhibitions and commemoration.

Absence from search results is not proof that no undiscovered dispute exists, but no responsible basis was found for adding such accusations.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “He sailed for Tibet to become a monk”

- **Story:** Xul left Argentina bound for Hong Kong, planning to continue to Tibet and become a monk.
- **Circulation:** museum education, biographies, popular articles.
- **Evidence:** the departure and eastward announced destination are reported; a literal monastic plan is less securely documented.
- **Assessment:** **reported claim with legendary elaboration**.
- **Effect:** casts the European years as an interrupted spiritual quest rather than an artistic migration.

### Secret initiate of Crowley from 1913

- **Story:** Crowley personally initiated Xul in 1913 or 1914.
- **Evidence:** Xul said Spare introduced him to A∴A∴ doctrine in 1913; an actual Crowley meeting is securely documented only in 1924.
- **Assessment:** early personal meeting **disputed/unverified**; 1924 instruction **documented**.
- **Effect:** strengthened the image of Xul as an occult adept and Crowley disciple.

### Supernatural visions

- **Story:** Xul entered objectively real astral worlds through the *I Ching*.
- **Evidence:** the notebooks and Crowley-directed practice are documented; supernatural reality is not historically testable.
- **Assessment:** visionary experiences are **documented as reports by Xul**; metaphysical interpretation is a matter of belief.
- **Effect:** central to later esoteric readings of his paintings.

### Panjuego had no fixed rules

- **Story:** every explanation produced new rules, making the game impossible to play.
- **Source:** chiefly Borges’s humorous recollections and later commentary.
- **Evidence:** material boards and rules survive, but the system did evolve.
- **Assessment:** **humorous report built on a mutable design**, not proof that no game existed.
- **Effect:** made Xul a figure of inexhaustible invention rather than a conventional game designer.

### Universal polyglot

- **Story:** he spoke or mastered twenty or more languages.
- **Evidence:** strong multilingual competence and extensive study are documented; exact mastery counts are not.
- **Assessment:** **reputational exaggeration**.
- **Effect:** supports the myth of the omniscient Buenos Aires magus.

### Astrologically permitted marriage

- **Story:** a forty-eight-hour planetary window enabled him to marry Lita.
- **Source:** Olga Orozco’s recollection.
- **Assessment:** **reported anecdote**, unverifiable as motive and astrological fact.
- **Effect:** integrates domestic life into his astrological mythology.

### Death holding his carved rosary

- **Story:** Xul died with a self-carved rosary in his hands.
- **Assessment:** **family or commemorative tradition**, not independently confirmed.
- **Effect:** frames his death as a deliberate mystical tableau.

### Borges’s “magician” and Marechal’s Schultze

Borges’s essays and Marechal’s fictional astrologer converted a private, technically obsessive experimenter into a cultural archetype: the benevolent hermetic magician of Buenos Aires. These are literary transformations, but they are historically consequential because much later biography has passed through them.

## Works by Xul Solar

A complete catalogue raisonné exceeds this dossier. His visual output consists of hundreds of watercolors, temperas, drawings, painted writings, objects, instruments, and designs. The following bibliography distinguishes authored texts and designed objects from later editions.

### Authored articles, texts, and manuscripts

- **“Pettoruti y obras”** (1924), *Martín Fierro*. Genuine signed criticism.
- Contributions and illustrations to ***Martín Fierro*** (1924–1927). Genuine but dispersed periodical work.
- **“Poema”** (1931), published in *Imán*, in Neo-Creole. Genuine authored work.
- Articles or interviews concerning Neo-Creole, Panlengua, astrology, and future art, published in periodicals including *Azul* and *Él*. Genuine, though later anthologies normalize titles.
- **“Apuntes de neocriollo.”** Manuscript notes, later edited and translated.
- ***Los San Signos / San Signos: Xul Solar y el I Ching*.** Sixty-four visionary records originating in 1924 and subsequently revised, particularly in 1937–1938. Genuine manuscript; the 2012 edition is a posthumous scholarly transcription and translation by Daniel E. Nelson.
- Notes and drafts for **Panlengua** and **Neocriollo**. Genuine archival material, never issued by Xul as a final comprehensive grammar.
- Astrological charts, correspondence, diaries, language tables, musical diagrams, and game instructions. Genuine archival materials, mostly unpublished or selectively reproduced.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

A tribute exhibition opened at the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes in 1963. Subsequent milestones included retrospectives in 1965, a 1966 Córdoba biennial tribute, later Buenos Aires exhibitions, a 1977 Paris presentation, a 1994 Courtauld exhibition on his architectures, the 2005 MALBA/Pinacoteca retrospective, and the 2013 *Xul Solar and Jorge Luis Borges: The Art of Friendship* exhibition.

The **Museo Xul Solar opened in 1993** in the Laprida property. Architect Pablo Tomás Beitía inserted a modern museum structure behind the preserved façade and residence. Lita’s preservation of the collection made this institutional afterlife possible.

Interpretations of Xul have shifted:

1. eccentric or marginal modernist;
2. Borges’s mystical friend;
3. Argentine and Latin American avant-garde pioneer;
4. precursor of invented-language art, conceptual writing, game art, and multimedia practice;
5. esoteric modernist whose occultism was central rather than incidental;
6. increasingly valuable international-market artist.

His influence is strongest where art, architecture, language, spirituality, music, and play overlap. Later artists and scholars have treated his Neo-Creole as a precursor to border languages and hybrid Latin American identity, while language scholars emphasize that it remained a changing personal system rather than a socially adopted language.

His posthumous market success is accompanied by authentication risk. The foundation’s archive and certification role therefore affect both scholarship and financial value.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are included.

- **Jorge Luis Borges — documented fact.** Close friendship from the *Martín Fierro* period after Xul’s 1924 return. They discussed metaphysics, language, astrology, mathematics, Swedenborg, Blake, Buddhism, and the *I Ching* and played Panjuego. Xul illustrated *El idioma de los argentinos* and *Un modelo para la muerte*. Borges included him in “Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius,” named or helped name *San Signos*, and repeatedly interpreted his legacy.

- **Adolfo Bioy Casares — documented fact.** Co-author with Borges, as B. Suárez Lynch, of *Un modelo para la muerte*, illustrated by Xul. The connection is a concrete publishing collaboration; the consulted evidence does not establish intimacy comparable to Xul’s friendship with Borges.

- **Silvina Ocampo — reported fact.** Shared Buenos Aires artistic-literary networks around Borges, Bioy Casares, Norah Borges, and *Sur*. Sources report Silvina exhibiting in artistic settings involving Xul and Norah Borges, but the exact depth of a personal relationship is not established here.

- **Victoria Ocampo — reported fact.** Belonged to the overlapping Buenos Aires cultural network around *Sur*, Borges, Bioy Casares, and Silvina Ocampo. No strong evidence located here establishes a close bilateral friendship, so mere shared milieu should not be overstated.

- **Wassily Kandinsky — documented influence, not a documented meeting.** Xul encountered *Der Blaue Reiter* publications and Kandinsky’s theory of spiritual art. No evidence establishes personal contact.

- **Paul Klee — documented influence, not a documented meeting.** Xul owned or consulted several early Klee monographs. Formal relationships between Klee’s works and paintings such as *Casa colonial* are documented by MALBA research. No personal meeting was established.

- **Helena Petrovna Blavatsky — documented intellectual influence, not a meeting.** Theosophical ideas and institutions informed Xul’s European formation. Blavatsky died before his European journey.

- **Rudolf Steiner — documented intellectual influence; personal contact unverified.** Xul studied anthroposophical and Theosophical materials. General claims that he “met Steiner” require stronger documentation than located here.

- **Jiddu Krishnamurti — documented textual influence, not a meeting.** Xul acquired *The Messenger of the Star* during his London occult investigations. No personal meeting was established.

- **Emanuel Swedenborg — documented intellectual influence, not a meeting.** Swedenborg’s visionary theology was a shared interest of Xul and Borges and informed later comparisons of Xul with visionary artists.

- **William Blake — documented artistic and intellectual influence, not a meeting.** Borges explicitly placed Xul within a visionary lineage including Blake; Xul’s interests in image-text synthesis and spiritual vision support the comparison.

- **Hermann Hesse — reported comparative connection, not a documented meeting.** Scholars have compared Panlengua and Panjuego with the totalizing symbolic play of Hesse’s *Glass Bead Game*. No evidence establishes correspondence or personal contact.

- **Carl Gustav Jung — possible intellectual affinity only.** Astrology, archetypal symbols, alchemy, and the *I Ching* create evident thematic overlap, but no source consulted here established correspondence, meeting, or explicit acknowledged influence. It is therefore excluded as a biographical connection.

- **Athanasius Kircher, Ramon Llull, Giordano Bruno, Paracelsus, Jacob Boehme, and other rostered esoteric figures — possible background sources only.** Xul’s library and combinatorial interests belong to traditions associated with these figures, but this research did not establish sufficiently precise, explicitly acknowledged individual influence to claim atlas connections.

No documented connection was found to the overwhelming majority of the supplied atlas roster. Shared nationality, city, era, political context, or interest in esotericism was not treated as evidence of personal connection.
