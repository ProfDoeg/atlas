# Friedrich Max Müller: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier reconstructs Friedrich Max Müller’s life from his own autobiographical writings and correspondence; Georgina Max Müller’s *Life and Letters*; Oxford, Gifford, Darwin Correspondence Project, and nineteenth-century institutional records; contemporary obituaries; bibliographical catalogues; and later historical scholarship.

Evidentiary labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by primary documents, institutional records, contemporaneous correspondence, or mutually consistent authoritative sources.
- **Reported fact:** stated in a generally reliable secondary source but not independently established here from a primary record.
- **Interpretation:** a scholar’s analysis rather than a directly recorded fact.
- **Allegation or theory:** asserted but not established by the available evidence.
- **Rumor or myth:** a reputational narrative circulated without adequate documentary proof.
- **Unresolved:** evidence is incomplete or conflicting.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name:** Friedrich Max Müller.

**Common forms:** Max Müller; F. Max Müller; Friedrich Max Mueller; Professor Max Müller. In English-language publications he increasingly treated “Max Müller” as a combined surname, partly because “Müller” alone was, as he remarked, no more distinctive than “Smith.”

**Occasional expanded form:** Friedrich Maximilian Müller appears in some later reference works, but his own regular name was Friedrich Max Müller.

**Titles and postnominal descriptions:**

- Professor of Modern European Languages and Literatures, Oxford.
- First Professor of Comparative Philology, Oxford.
- Fellow of All Souls College.
- Member of the United Kingdom Privy Council, hence “The Right Honourable,” from 1896.
- Knight of the Prussian civil Pour le Mérite.
- Recipient of the Bavarian Maximilian Order for Science and Art.
- Corresponding, foreign, or honorary member of numerous academies and learned societies.

**Occupations:** Sanskritist, Vedic philologist, comparative linguist, editor, translator, historian of religion, comparative mythologist, lecturer, essayist, university professor, librarian, and public intellectual.

**Nationality:** Born a subject of the Duchy of Anhalt-Dessau in the German Confederation; naturalized as a British subject in October 1855.

**Religion:** Baptized and raised Lutheran. His mature theology was liberal, comparative, and strongly influenced by German idealism, historical criticism, and his study of Indian religion. He continued to identify as Christian.

**Birth:** 6 December 1823, Dessau, Duchy of Anhalt-Dessau, German Confederation.

**Death:** 28 October 1900, Oxford, England, aged seventy-six.

**Burial:** 1 November 1900, Holywell Cemetery, Oxford.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal family

Müller’s father was **Johann Ludwig Wilhelm Müller** (1794–1827), normally known as Wilhelm Müller, a poet, classicist, teacher, and ducal librarian at Dessau. He had fought in the Wars of Liberation against Napoleon and studied history and philology in Berlin.

Wilhelm Müller is remembered particularly for the poems set by Franz Schubert in the song cycles *Die schöne Müllerin* and *Winterreise*. Schubert and Max Müller did not meet: Schubert died when Müller was four.

Wilhelm Müller died unexpectedly in 1827, leaving his widow with two young children. Max retained only limited direct memories of him, but his father’s literary reputation, books, friendships, and cultivated milieu became a major inherited identity.

### Maternal family

His mother was **Adelheid Müller, née von Basedow**, eldest daughter of the president or chief minister of Anhalt-Dessau. Georgina Max Müller described her as multilingual, musically gifted, intellectually cultivated, emotionally intense, and sometimes severe with her children.

Müller’s maternal great-grandfather was the educational reformer **Johann Bernhard Basedow** (1724–1790), founder of the Philanthropinum at Dessau and an important precursor of Pestalozzi and Fröbel. This ancestry connected Müller to Enlightenment educational reform as well as to the governing elite of the small duchy.

### Sibling

Müller had one sibling, an elder sister, **Auguste**, approximately two years older. She married a man surnamed **Krug** and lived principally at Chemnitz. Two of her children died within weeks of one another in 1849. Auguste never fully recovered from these losses and died in the 1860s. Müller’s correspondence records intense grief: he called her his oldest friend and companion.

### Childhood finances and patronage

After Wilhelm Müller’s death, Adelheid received only a small civil-service widow’s pension. The Duke of Anhalt-Dessau granted her an additional **100 thalers annually until Max reached twenty-one**, expressly to assist with the children’s education. This was an early and important act of princely patronage.

### Marriage

On **3 August 1859**, Müller married **Georgina Adelaide Grenfell** (1831–1916 in the *Deutsche Biographie*; some later sources incorrectly give 1919) at Bray Church, Berkshire.

Georgina was the elder daughter of **Riversdale Grenfell** of Ray Lodge near Maidenhead and **Charlotte Elliot Grenfell**. The courtship lasted several years and encountered opposition from members of her family, apparently involving Müller’s foreign birth, uncertain early finances, and academic position.

The couple spent a first week at Eversley Rectory, lent by Charles and Fanny Kingsley. They then traveled through Heidelberg, Dresden, Chemnitz, Dessau, Prague, and Saxon Switzerland. On the Papststein they were caught in a violent electrical storm which they believed might kill them.

Georgina became a translator, editor, household manager, social intermediary, and guardian of Müller’s posthumous archive. She edited the two-volume *Life and Letters of the Right Honourable Friedrich Max Müller* (1902) and thematic posthumous collections such as *Thoughts on Life and Religion*.

### Children

The marriage produced four children:

1. **Ada Müller**, born 30 December 1860. Georgina nearly died following this delivery.
2. **Mary Müller**, born in February 1862; the OCR text of *Life and Letters* produces the impossible date “February 31,” so the exact day requires verification against the original printed page or civil register.
3. **Beatrice Stanley Müller**, born 1 August 1864; Dean Arthur Penrhyn Stanley was asked to be her godfather. She married Sir Thomas Colyer Colyer-Fergusson in 1890 and died on 17 June 1902.
4. **Wilhelm Grenfell Max Müller** (1867–1919), born 9 June 1867. Usually called William in Britain, he became an Egyptologist and Orientalist and worked in the United States.

Two daughters predeceased both parents, but the accessible sources consulted here do not consistently identify both or supply reliable dates. Beatrice survived her father but died before Georgina.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1823–1841

Müller was born in the ducal capital of Dessau. His father served both as librarian to the duke and as a teacher at the Gymnasium. The family’s circle included poets, musicians, officials, and educators.

He was named Friedrich after a maternal uncle and Max after the protagonist of Carl Maria von Weber’s opera *Der Freischütz*. Weber was a close friend of his parents and Müller’s godfather. Later retellings sometimes exaggerate this association into a musical apprenticeship; the documented relationship was one of family friendship and godparenthood.

After Wilhelm Müller died, Adelheid and her children temporarily moved into her father’s household, then established their own home in Dessau. Müller later remembered frequent visits to his father’s grave and his mother’s enduring grief. Family recollections also describe him as energetic and mischievous; an old servant called him *dieser infame Junge*, approximately “that dreadful boy.”

His earliest formal education was in Dessau. He studied piano and showed enough musical ability to consider music professionally. He encountered Felix Mendelssohn in Leipzig and later recalled that Mendelssohn discouraged him from choosing music as a career. The precise force of this advice is autobiographical and cannot be independently measured, but Müller’s lifelong musical knowledge is well documented.

In 1836 he went to Leipzig and lived in the household of Dr. Carus while attending the **Nicolaischule**. His curriculum emphasized Greek and Latin classics. To qualify for an Anhalt government scholarship, he sat his final examination at Zerbst rather than Leipzig.

## Education and Formation, 1841–1846

### Leipzig University

In 1841 Müller entered the University of Leipzig with an Anhalt-Dessau scholarship. He initially intended to concentrate on Greek and Latin philology.

His surviving lecture record shows unusually broad study: forty-nine courses in five semesters. Teachers and subjects included:

- **Johann Gottfried Hermann:** Greek and Latin philology.
- **Moritz Haupt:** classical and Old German studies.
- **Hermann Brockhaus:** Sanskrit; the decisive Leipzig teacher in Müller’s turn toward Indian philology.
- **Heinrich Leberecht Fleischer:** Hebrew, Arabic, and Persian.
- **Christian Hermann Weisse:** aesthetics, metaphysics, and Hegelian philosophy.
- **Hermann Lotze:** anthropology.
- **Ernst Wilhelm Gottfried Wachsmuth:** history of civilization.
- Becker, Stallbaum, and Heinroth in classical, psychological, and related fields.

He joined the Leipzig student association known as the **Kochei** in 1841.

On **1 September 1843**, aged nineteen, he received his doctorate. His dissertation concerned the third book of Spinoza’s *Ethics*, *De Affectibus*. His first major Sanskrit publication, a German translation of the *Hitopadeśa*, appeared in 1844.

### Berlin

In spring 1844 Müller moved to Berlin. He studied:

- Comparative linguistics with **Franz Bopp**.
- Philosophy with **Friedrich Wilhelm Joseph Schelling**.
- Persian with **Friedrich Rückert**, alongside Paul de Lagarde.
- Sanskrit manuscripts in the Chambers collection.

Bopp supplied methodological grounding in Indo-European comparison. Schelling encouraged Müller to connect the history of language with the history of religion and asked him to translate passages from the Upanishads. Müller’s later “science of religion” combined this idealist formation with textual philology.

### Paris

Early in 1845 Müller moved to Paris to work with **Eugène Burnouf**, then a leading authority on Sanskrit, Pali, Buddhism, and Iranian languages. Fellow students included **Rudolf Roth** and **Theodor Goldstücker**.

Burnouf urged Müller to prepare a complete edition of the *Rig-Veda* with the medieval commentary of Sāyaṇa. This became the central project of Müller’s early career.

Müller supported himself through scholarly assistance, copying, collating, and related literary work. He was not independently wealthy.

## Migration to Britain and the Rig-Veda Project, 1846–1849

Müller traveled to England in June 1846 to consult Sanskrit manuscripts in London and Oxford. He brought introductions to **Christian Karl Josias von Bunsen**, the Prussian ambassador in London.

Bunsen became friend, patron, theological influence, and intermediary. With assistance from Bunsen and **Horace Hayman Wilson**, Boden Professor of Sanskrit and librarian of the East India Company, Müller obtained East India Company support for his projected edition.

The Company’s directors agreed to bear the printing expense and support Müller while he prepared the work. Their minute described the project as especially deserving patronage because it concerned the early religion, history, and language of Britain’s Indian subjects.

This relationship was a documented financial and institutional connection to the colonial state. It was scholarly patronage, not evidence that Müller held an intelligence or covert appointment.

In June 1847 he visited Oxford for the British Association meeting and spoke on Bengali and its relationship to the Aryan languages.

In early 1848 he returned to Paris to collate manuscripts. When the February Revolution began, he hurried back to London with the manuscripts. A later anecdote says he was the first to inform Lord Palmerston that Louis-Philippe had fled Paris; this appears in the contemporary biographical tradition but is not independently verified here.

Because the *Rig-Veda* was being printed by Oxford University Press, Müller moved to Oxford in 1848. He remained based there for the rest of his life. The first volume appeared in 1849.

### Nature of the edition

The work was not a complete English translation of the *Rig-Veda*. It was a printed Sanskrit edition, in Devanāgarī, accompanied by Sāyaṇa’s commentary, established through comparison of manuscripts. Müller separately translated selected hymns.

Six volumes appeared between 1849 and 1873/74; a revised four-volume edition followed in 1890–1892. The long project depended upon Oxford printing technology, Indian manuscripts, earlier collections assembled with Indian scholarly labor, and East India Company finance.

Müller acquired about eighty Vedic manuscripts. His edition helped shift a text maintained primarily through highly regulated oral and manuscript traditions into European print circulation.

## Oxford Career, 1850–1860

In 1850 Müller became **Deputy Taylorian Professor of Modern European Languages**. In 1851 Oxford awarded him an honorary M.A. and attached him to Christ Church. He succeeded to the full Taylorian professorship in 1854.

He lectured mainly on German and French literature, literary history, comparative grammar, and related subjects rather than holding an Oxford Sanskrit chair.

He became a naturalized British subject in October 1855. His correspondence shows strong attachment to Britain but continued identification with German language and culture.

Oxford appointed him a curator of the Bodleian Library in 1856. He was elected to a life fellowship at All Souls College in 1858. The fellowship strengthened his income, status, and scholarly independence.

### Early public and scholarly work

His publications in the 1850s included:

- A proposed uniform missionary alphabet.
- Practical linguistic guides for British officers during the Crimean War.
- Work on “Turanian” language classification.
- Studies of Indian logic and ritual.
- *Comparative Mythology*.
- *The German Classics*.
- *A History of Ancient Sanskrit Literature*.

The missionary alphabet and wartime language manuals reveal that his philology was not isolated from imperial administration and missionary activity. He advocated practical training in Asian languages for officers and merchants and proposed an Oriental institute serving British commerce.

### Income and household

Müller’s early finances were insecure and depended on scholarships, commissioned editing, teaching, publishers, and patrons. By the late 1850s he possessed a professorial salary, an All Souls fellowship, royalties, and continuing scholarly commissions.

His later correspondence and Georgina’s account describe systematic charitable giving. He aimed to give at least one-tenth of his income and assisted relatives, students, and writers. Georgina also reported that he temporarily gave up expensive cigars after marriage to preserve the household and charity budgets. These claims originate in a sympathetic family-edited source but are consistent with surviving letters.

## The Boden Sanskrit Professorship Election, 1860

After Horace Hayman Wilson died in May 1860, Müller sought Oxford’s Boden Professorship of Sanskrit. His principal opponent was **Monier Williams**, later Monier Monier-Williams.

### Structure of the contest

The chair was elected by Oxford Convocation, a large electorate of M.A. graduates, many of them Anglican clergymen. The campaign resembled a parliamentary election: candidates circulated testimonials, advertised in newspapers, corresponded with thousands of voters, and formed committees.

Müller wrote that he had to contact approximately 4,000 electors. Williams reportedly spent more than £1,000 on his campaign.

### Scholarly and ideological differences

Müller emphasized early Vedic texts, comparative philology, intellectual history, and religion. Williams emphasized practical Sanskrit and the preparation of British administrators and missionaries.

Colonel Boden’s bequest had explicitly connected Sanskrit study to Christian conversion. Müller therefore argued that his *Rig-Veda* edition served the founder’s purpose by making Brahmanical doctrine accessible. His election statement cited missionary testimony and said the work could assist efforts to overthrow “idolatry” and establish Christianity.

This was a documented campaign position. It should not be confused with proof that every textual decision in his edition was dictated by missionaries.

### Religion and nationality

Opponents attacked Müller as a foreigner and questioned his theological orthodoxy. Evangelical newspapers supported Williams as the safer Christian candidate. The *Times*, by contrast, called Müller the world’s leading Sanskrit scholar.

On **7 December 1860**, Williams won **833 votes to 610**, a majority of 223.

### Consequences

Müller regarded the defeat as bitter and attributed it to xenophobia, religious suspicion, and electioneering. Williams privately acknowledged that circumstances and his conservative reputation favored him.

Modern historians differ over the relative weight of nationality, theology, Oxford connections, teaching experience, and missionary politics. It is documented that all entered the campaign.

The defeat redirected Müller toward public lectures, comparative language, mythology, and religion. A. A. Macdonell later judged that victory might have kept him more closely engaged in specialist Sanskrit research and produced work of greater permanent technical value, though perhaps less public fame. That is a counterfactual interpretation, not a fact.

## Public Lecturer and Comparative Philologist, 1861–1868

Müller delivered Royal Institution lectures on the “science of language” in 1861 and 1863. Published as *Lectures on the Science of Language*, they became international bestsellers. The first series reached three editions in under a year; the work eventually passed through numerous editions and translations.

He was an unusually effective English prose stylist and public lecturer. His lectures made Indo-European philology intelligible to Victorian nonspecialists.

### Language theory

Müller argued that language distinguished human beings qualitatively from other animals. He called language humanity’s “Rubicon” and denied that natural selection had yet explained the passage from animal cries to conceptual speech.

He nonetheless sometimes borrowed Darwinian vocabulary, including selection, to describe linguistic change. This produced tensions in his system: he could use evolutionary analogies while rejecting an evolutionary origin of genuinely human language.

His proposed theories of early word formation were caricatured as the “bow-wow,” “pooh-pooh,” and “ding-dong” theories. “Ding-dong” became particularly associated with Müller’s suggestion of a natural correspondence between sound and conceptual impressions. Later linguistics rejected these speculative origin theories.

### Mythology

Müller interpreted many Indo-European myths as transformations of poetic language about natural phenomena, especially the sun, dawn, storm, and sky. When metaphors ceased to be understood as metaphors, words became gods and narratives.

His formulation of myth as a **“disease of language”** became famous. It was influenced by German comparative philology and nature mythology, especially Adalbert Kuhn.

Critics objected that the method forced myths into solar or meteorological patterns and relied on etymologies later invalidated by stricter sound laws. By 1901 Macdonell observed that few specialists still accepted central identifications such as the equation of Greek Erinys with Sanskrit Saraṇyū.

### Personal and domestic events

Ada was born at the end of 1860, followed by Mary in 1862 and Beatrice in 1864. Müller’s second lecture series sold rapidly; *Life and Letters* reports 1,000 copies sold on the first day.

In 1865 he became Oriental sub-librarian at the Bodleian. He found the routine uncongenial and resigned in 1867.

In 1867 he purchased **Parks End, 7 Norham Gardens**, from historian Goldwin Smith and enlarged it. It became the family residence, workplace, salon, and destination for visiting scholars and Indian travelers.

William Grenfell Max Müller was born there or during the family’s final period at 64 High Street on 9 June 1867; the surviving letter is headed 64 High Street, and the family’s exact moving date within 1867 requires property or census confirmation.

### Comparative Philology chair

In 1868 Oxford created a Professorship of Comparative Philology and appointed Müller its first holder. The chair was effectively designed around his established work and supplied reduced teaching obligations and improved income.

## Mature Career, 1868–1875

Müller continued completing the *Rig-Veda* while publishing essays and lectures on language, mythology, literature, and religion.

His four-volume *Chips from a German Workshop* gathered a large portion of his periodical work. The title presented him as a German craftsman working in British intellectual culture.

### Strasbourg

In 1872 the newly reorganized University of Strasbourg offered him a Sanskrit professorship. He declined permanent appointment but lectured there during the summer term.

He returned his honorarium to the university, which used it to create a triennial **Max Müller Stipendium** for Sanskrit scholarship.

### Franco-Prussian War and German politics

During the Franco-Prussian War of 1870–1871, Müller publicly defended the German position in letters to *The Times* and in *Letters on the War between Germany and France*. This drew criticism in Britain but did not end his British institutional career.

His politics did not fit a single stable party label. *Life and Letters* describes him as a Unionist in Oxford borough politics late in life, voting “blue,” while retaining admiration for William Gladstone’s intellect and personality.

### Religion as a comparative science

Royal Institution lectures in 1870, published as *Introduction to the Science of Religion*, proposed that religions should be compared historically and philologically without reducing all traditions to one.

In December 1873, at Dean Stanley’s invitation, he lectured in Westminster Abbey on the religions of the world. The setting gave comparative religion unusual public and ecclesiastical visibility.

### Completion of the Rig-Veda

The final volume of the first edition appeared in 1873/74. By then the undertaking had occupied Müller for approximately twenty-five years.

### Retirement from active teaching

In 1875 he retired from the active duties of his comparative-philology chair but retained the title and Oxford connection. This allowed him to undertake his largest editorial enterprise.

## The Sacred Books of the East, 1875–1900

Müller conceived and served as general editor of the **Sacred Books of the East**, published by Oxford University Press.

The series assembled English translations of Hindu, Buddhist, Jain, Zoroastrian, Islamic, Confucian, and Daoist texts by an international network of scholars. The Christian Bible was not included, partly because it already possessed abundant European translations and scholarly apparatus.

Fifty principal volumes were ultimately published, with a general index appearing posthumously. Forty-eight were available by Müller’s death.

Müller personally translated or contributed substantially to volumes containing:

- The Upanishads.
- The *Dhammapada* and related Buddhist texts.
- Vedic hymns.
- Buddhist Sanskrit texts including the *Prajñāpāramitā-hṛdaya* materials.

### Scholarly network

Contributors or closely connected scholars included James Legge, T. W. Rhys Davids, Hermann Oldenberg, Julius Jolly, George Bühler, Julius Eggeling, Georg Thibaut, E. B. Cowell, and others.

Müller’s role included choosing texts, recruiting translators, negotiating with the Press, editing manuscripts, standardizing presentation, resolving conflicts, raising support, and marketing the series. Arie Molendijk consequently characterizes him as both scholar and successful academic entrepreneur.

### Limits and criticisms

The series depended heavily on male European textual specialists and presented Asian religions primarily through canonical books. It often separated texts from living ritual, oral transmission, social institutions, and vernacular practice.

The title “Sacred Books of the East” constructed “the East” as a large comparative category. The series was nevertheless foundational infrastructure for comparative religion and made many works widely available for the first time in English.

## Later Intellectual Development, 1878–1900

### Hibbert Lectures

Müller inaugurated the Hibbert Lectures in 1878 with *Lectures on the Origin and Growth of Religion as Illustrated by the Religions of India*.

He argued that religion developed from human apprehension of the infinite through finite phenomena. His concept of **henotheism**—the temporary worship of one god as supreme without denying others—was developed to describe portions of Vedic religion.

### India lectures

His Cambridge lectures of 1882 became *India: What Can It Teach Us?* (1883). He argued for the intellectual importance of Sanskrit and ancient Indian literature to British students and colonial administrators.

His associated “Renaissance theory,” proposing a long interruption followed by a sixth-century revival in Indian literary activity, was later disproved by inscriptional evidence. The theory remains part of the history of Indology rather than accepted chronology.

### Kant and philosophy

In 1881 Müller published an English translation of Immanuel Kant’s *Critique of Pure Reason*, with historical and philosophical introductions. Kant’s distinction between the conditions of knowledge and ultimate reality was important to Müller’s attempts to discuss the infinite and the limits of religious language.

*The Science of Thought* (1887) maintained the inseparability of language and conceptual thought. Contemporary and later critics found it rich in suggestive ideas but insufficiently systematic.

### Gifford Lectures

From 1888 through 1892 he delivered four successive Gifford series at the University of Glasgow:

1. *Natural Religion*.
2. *Physical Religion*.
3. *Anthropological Religion*.
4. *Theosophy, or Psychological Religion*.

The first lecture drew an audience reported as approximately 1,400. Across four courses he delivered sixty-two lectures—an exceptional sequence.

He used “theosophy” in the older sense of knowledge of the divine or the relation between human spirit and God, not as endorsement of Helena Blavatsky’s occult movement.

### Indian philosophy

Late works included *Three Lectures on the Vedanta Philosophy* and *The Six Systems of Indian Philosophy*. Müller treated Vedanta, especially the Upanishads, as a major philosophical achievement while attempting to reconcile its insights with his Christian belief in the Logos and personal religious development.

### Ramakrishna and Vivekananda

Müller learned about Ramakrishna through Indian periodicals, Brahmo reform circles, Pratap Chandra Mozoomdar, and correspondence.

On **28 May 1896**, Swami Vivekananda—born Narendranath Datta—visited Müller at his Oxford home. Müller and his wife received him for lunch, discussed Ramakrishna, and accompanied him to the railway station.

Vivekananda later wrote admiringly that Müller’s reverence for Ramakrishna was profound. He circulated the symbolic claim that Sāyaṇa had been reborn as Müller to restore his own Vedic commentary. This was devotional metaphor or hagiographic myth, not Müller’s own claim.

Müller published *Râmakrishna: His Life and Sayings* in 1898, one of the first substantial Western-language biographical treatments. Its sources were mediated through disciples and reformers, so it is both an important reception document and a limited historical biography.

## Travel and Geographical Movements

Müller never visited India. Claims that describe him as having worked there are false.

Documented principal movements include:

- **1823–1836:** Dessau.
- **1836–1841:** Leipzig, with final examination at Zerbst.
- **1841–1844:** Leipzig University.
- **1844–1845:** Berlin.
- **1845–1846:** Paris.
- **1846 onward:** London and Oxford manuscript work.
- **1848:** Paris during the February Revolution; return to London.
- **1848–1900:** Oxford as principal residence.
- Repeated visits to Dessau, Dresden, Chemnitz, Leipzig, Heidelberg, Paris, and other European centers.
- **1859:** Eversley, Heidelberg, Dresden, Chemnitz, Dessau, Prague, and Saxon Switzerland after marriage.
- **1863:** Paris, Germany, northern Italy, and Edinburgh.
- **1864:** Osborne House and other royal venues for lectures.
- **1872:** Strasbourg for a summer lecture course.
- **1875:** Italy and British lecture travel.
- **1888–1892:** repeated or extended stays in Glasgow for the Gifford Lectures.
- **1899:** Germany, where he suffered a dangerous illness.

His correspondence records additional stays at Windsor, the Isle of Wight, Ray Lodge, Weymouth, the Mumbles, and numerous country houses.

## Employment, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Positions

- East India Company-supported editor of the *Rig-Veda*.
- Deputy Taylorian Professor, Oxford, 1850–1854.
- Taylorian Professor of Modern European Languages and Literatures, 1854–1868.
- Bodleian curator from 1856, with interruptions or differing dates in institutional summaries.
- Oriental sub-librarian, 1865–1867.
- Life Fellow of All Souls College from 1858.
- Professor of Comparative Philology, 1868–1900; retired from active duties in 1875.
- General editor of the *Sacred Books of the East*.
- Gifford Lecturer, Glasgow, 1888–1892.
- Member of the Privy Council from 1896.

### Publishers and economic structure

Principal publishing partners included Oxford University Press, Longmans, Trübner, Williams and Norgate, Macmillan, and German firms including Brockhaus.

His economic base developed from precarious scholarships and commissioned copying into a combination of salary, fellowship income, commissioned editions, lecture honoraria, and book royalties.

*Deutsche Liebe* sold repeatedly in Germany; the *Lectures on the Science of Language* became a commercial success in Britain and abroad. An unauthorized American translation of *Deutsche Liebe*, titled *Memories*, circulated very widely.

No company ownership, industrial investments, patents, or inventions have been found. His “technologies” were principally scholarly: manuscript collation, standardized transliteration, comparative linguistic classification, and coordinated print publication.

### Property

He rented or occupied Oxford premises including rooms at 64 High Street before purchasing **7 Norham Gardens/Parks End** in 1867. He enlarged the house according to its earlier architectural plan. It became a valuable North Oxford property and a center of intellectual sociability.

No reliable probate valuation was located in the sources consulted. Exact estate value is therefore unresolved.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Political positions

Müller’s political interventions were intermittent rather than those of a party organizer:

- Advocacy of language training for military officers during the Crimean War.
- Advocacy of an Oriental institute for imperial administration and trade.
- Support for the German cause in 1870–1871.
- Interest in Schleswig-Holstein and German unity.
- Late identification as an Oxford Unionist voter.
- Defense of Britain’s position in the South African War against criticism by Theodor Mommsen.

He held no elected political office.

### Christianity and comparative religion

Müller’s Christianity combined Lutheran upbringing, historical criticism, Bunsen’s liberal theology, Schelling, Kant, and comparative study.

He rejected biblical literalism: his letters approvingly noted historical-critical challenges to Mosaic authorship. He nevertheless believed in divine providence, Christ, immortality, and a universal human capacity for religion.

He expected comparison to reveal both historical difference and an underlying divine education of humanity. Critics accused him either of covert Christian apologetics or of undermining Christianity. Both reactions reflect real tensions in his work.

### Evolution

Müller opposed the extension of Darwinian evolution to the origin of conceptual language, believing language marked a discontinuity between humans and animals. He corresponded courteously with Darwin in 1873 and 1875 while maintaining disagreement.

George Darwin and other critics attacked Müller’s reasoning. By the late nineteenth century, evolutionary anthropology and linguistics had marginalized several of his positions.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Teachers and mentors

- Hermann Brockhaus: Sanskrit teacher and decisive early influence.
- Franz Bopp: comparative philology.
- Friedrich Schelling: philosophy and the language–religion relationship.
- Eugène Burnouf: Vedic and Buddhist scholarship; proposed the full *Rig-Veda* edition.
- Christian von Bunsen: patron, friend, theologian, and political intermediary.
- Horace Hayman Wilson: institutional advocate for the East India Company commission.
- Alexander von Humboldt: used court influence in Prussia to assist proposed publication.

### Friends and collaborators

- Charles and Fanny Kingsley: close family friends; lent Eversley Rectory after Müller’s wedding.
- Arthur Penrhyn Stanley: dean, godparent, religious ally, and host of Müller’s Westminster Abbey lecture.
- William Howard Russell: journalist and friend.
- Francis Turner Palgrave and William Gifford Palgrave.
- George Biddell Airy, Benjamin Jowett, George Rawlinson, William Gladstone, Queen Victoria’s family, and numerous Oxford scholars.
- Indian correspondents including Pratap Chandra Mozoomdar, Keshub Chandra Sen’s circle, and Ramakrishna’s disciples.
- Japanese students Bunyiu Nanjio, Kenjiu Kasawara, and Junjirō Takakusu, whom Müller advised in Sanskrit and Buddhist studies.

### Rivals and critics

- Monier Monier-Williams: electoral rival in 1860.
- William Dwight Whitney: major American critic and rival in linguistics.
- George Darwin and Darwinian language theorists.
- Andrew Lang, E. B. Tylor, and anthropological mythologists who rejected the solar-linguistic school.
- Theodor Mommsen in late political controversy over the South African War.
- Theosophists, whom he criticized for representing occult doctrines as authentic Buddhism or ancient Eastern wisdom.

## Controversies and Disputed Questions

### 1. Colonial patronage and missionary utility

**Documented fact:** The East India Company financed Müller’s *Rig-Veda* edition because it considered knowledge of Indian religion, history, and language valuable for governing Indian subjects.

**Documented fact:** In his 1860 Boden campaign Müller argued that the edition helped missionaries understand and oppose Brahmanical religion.

**Documented fact:** He wrote privately in 1866 that presenting the root of Indian religion could “uproot” what had grown from it over three millennia.

**Interpretation:** Historians debate whether this makes the project fundamentally missionary, primarily philological within a colonial funding structure, or both.

**Unsupported extension:** The frequently circulated claim that Müller was a secret British “agent” hired to falsify the Vedas is not supported by evidence of a covert appointment, intelligence relationship, or instructions to mistranslate particular passages.

### 2. “Aryan race”

Müller was central to popularizing “Aryan” as a philological and historical category.

In earlier work he sometimes wrote of Aryan ancestry, blood, brethren, conquerors, or race in ways that blurred language, culture, genealogy, and physical descent. He used imagined kinship between Indians and Europeans to challenge claims of absolute racial separation, but also placed the relationship within a paternalistic vision of British civilizing rule.

Later he repeatedly insisted that “Aryan” properly designated language rather than biological race. His famous formulation compared “Aryan blood” or “Aryan eyes and hair” to a “dolichocephalic dictionary” or “brachycephalic grammar.”

**Documented tension:** His early rhetoric helped naturalize racial readings even though his later explicit doctrine rejected a biological “Aryan race.”

**Historical consequence:** Racial nationalists and later Nazi ideologues appropriated “Aryan” in ways Müller did not endorse. It is inaccurate either to make him the sole inventor of Aryan racialism or to treat his work as entirely separate from its nineteenth-century formation.

### 3. Aryan invasion and Vedic chronology

**Rumor or myth:** Müller “invented the Aryan invasion theory” in a single deliberate act to degrade India.

**Evidence:** Indo-European migration theories developed collectively among eighteenth- and nineteenth-century linguists, historians, and ethnologists. Müller contributed influential formulations, including descriptions of Aryan-speaking migrants entering India and conflict with *dasyus*.

He proposed a rough Vedic chronology by working backward from Buddhism and allotting conventional blocks of approximately two centuries to stages of Vedic literature, placing the earliest hymns around 1200 BCE.

He later emphasized that such dates were provisional and that no power could determine the exact date of Vedic composition.

Modern scholarship dates much of the *Rig-Veda* broadly within the second millennium BCE using linguistic, archaeological, and comparative evidence independent of Müller’s arithmetic. His 1200 BCE estimate was not a fixed discovery, but neither is modern chronology merely an unexamined repetition of it.

### 4. Did he deliberately mistranslate Hindu scripture?

**Allegation:** Müller intentionally produced obscene, degrading, or false translations under British instruction.

**Evidence against the strong form:** His principal *Rig-Veda* achievement was an edition of the Sanskrit text with Sāyaṇa’s Sanskrit commentary, not the wholesale replacement of an Indian text by an English fabrication. His readings were publicly inspectable and criticized by other Sanskritists. No documentary instruction to falsify the text has been found.

**Documented qualification:** His translations and interpretations were shaped by nineteenth-century philology, Protestant concepts of scripture, colonial institutions, and his own theological aims. Some readings are obsolete or incorrect. Bias and error are demonstrable; an organized falsification scheme is not.

### 5. Myth as “disease of language”

Critics charged that Müller reduced religion to faulty wordplay and interpreted almost every deity as the sun.

The caricature overstates his complete theory but has a factual basis: he did regard the forgetting of metaphorical origins as a major generator of mythology and privileged nature imagery.

The anthropological school associated with Tylor, Lang, and Frazer displaced this approach. Later structuralist and historical linguistics retained comparative interests while rejecting most of Müller’s etymological reconstructions.

### 6. The Boden election

Supporters presented Müller as the victim of clerical prejudice and xenophobia; opponents presented Williams as the practical Christian candidate suited to India.

The recorded vote and inflammatory press campaign are facts. Claims that Müller lost solely because he was German, solely because he was religiously liberal, or solely because Williams was better connected simplify a multi-causal election.

### 7. No evidence of criminal proceedings

No documented lawsuits, criminal charges, imprisonment, fraud investigation, corruption case, money laundering, political violence, or intelligence service connection was found.

His controversies were scholarly, religious, electoral, imperial, and political.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Symbolic Narratives

### Müller as Sāyaṇa reborn

**Story:** Swami Vivekananda suggested that the medieval Vedic commentator Sāyaṇa had been reborn as Max Müller to publish his own commentary.

**Appearance and circulation:** Vivekananda voiced this in 1897 after meeting Müller and it entered Ramakrishna-Vivekananda literature.

**Evidence:** It is explicitly a devotional reincarnation claim, not empirically verifiable and not professed by Müller.

**Effect:** It transformed a European editor into a figure within Hindu sacred history and expressed Indian admiration for the scale of his Vedic labor.

### Müller as destroyer of Hinduism

**Story:** Müller was a paid British or missionary conspirator whose exclusive objective was to uproot Hinduism, fabricate the Aryan invasion, and impose biblical chronology.

**Origin:** The story draws upon authentic passages in his letters, his East India Company financing, Boden election statement, and Christian convictions. It expanded in twentieth- and twenty-first-century nationalist polemic and online circulation.

**Evidence for the underlying core:** Colonial finance, missionary utility, Christian purpose, and the “uprooting” letter are authentic.

**Evidence against the total narrative:** His private and public writings also praised Indian philosophy, opposed crude missionary contempt, cultivated Indian reformers, and insisted upon studying traditions in their own documents. There is no evidence of secret orders to falsify texts.

**Reputational effect:** The narrative changed him in some Indian public discourse from admired “Moksha Mula” or Vedic benefactor into an emblem of colonial distortion.

### Müller as founder or “father” of comparative religion

**Story:** Müller founded the academic science of religion single-handedly.

**Evidence:** He was a highly influential pioneer, gave the field public visibility, coined or popularized programmatic formulations, and edited essential source translations. Comparative work also depended on earlier and contemporary scholars including Burnouf, Bunsen, Tylor, Renan, Pfleiderer, and many others.

**Label:** Hagiographic simplification of a substantial documented role.

### Müller as a scholar of India who lived in India

**Story:** Popular biographies sometimes imply that he traveled through or worked in India.

**Evidence:** He never visited India. His knowledge came through texts, manuscripts, correspondence, Indian visitors, colonial institutions, and European libraries.

**Effect:** The error obscures the “armchair” and textual character of his Indology.

### The “Max Müller Bhavan” memory

In India the Goethe-Institut branches have been known since 1957 as **Max Mueller Bhavan**. This institutional naming helped preserve a positive image of Müller as a German mediator of Indian culture even as postcolonial criticism grew.

### Harry Smith’s surreal Max Müller

Experimental filmmaker Harry Smith’s intentionally nonsensical account of *Heaven and Earth Magic* says its heroine returns to earth after being “eaten by Max Müller” on the day Edward VII dedicated the Great Sewer of London. This has no biographical content; it is a twentieth-century surrealist appropriation of his name.

## Illness, Habits, and Death

Müller was a heavy cigar smoker in early adulthood, stopped after marriage for financial reasons, and resumed limited cigarette smoking in his last two decades. No evidence of addiction treatment or substance-related illness was found.

He experienced periods of grief, exhaustion, and professional disappointment but no documented psychiatric diagnosis or hospitalization.

His health deteriorated from approximately 1898. During a visit to Germany in June 1899 he became dangerously ill, then recovered unexpectedly. A relapse occurred in 1900.

He continued working until approximately ten days before death. He dictated corrections to his unfinished autobiography to his son.

Müller died at his Oxford home on **28 October 1900**. The accessible contemporary notices do not specify a precise medical cause. Assigning one would therefore be speculative.

He was buried at Holywell Cemetery on 1 November. His grave identifies him as a Fellow of All Souls and as a professor at Oxford for fifty years. Georgina, William, and William’s wife Wanda Maria were later buried with him.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Immediate memorialization

Georgina arranged and bound Müller’s papers and correspondence and edited the two-volume *Life and Letters* in 1902. Her account is indispensable but selective, familial, and designed partly to preserve his reputation.

Oxford established the **Max Müller Memorial Fund** to support research on the history, archaeology, languages, literatures, and religions of ancient India.

### Scholarly legacy

Enduring contributions include:

- The first major printed edition of the *Rig-Veda* with Sāyaṇa’s commentary.
- Formation of Vedic studies as a European academic specialization.
- Popularization of Indo-European comparative linguistics in Britain.
- Institutionalization of comparative religion.
- The *Sacred Books of the East* as a large-scale translation infrastructure.
- Mentorship of European, Indian, and Japanese scholars.
- Expansion of public interest in Sanskrit, Buddhism, Vedanta, and comparative mythology.

Superseded or heavily revised elements include:

- Many individual etymologies.
- Solar and meteorological myth interpretation.
- The “disease of language” as a general explanation of mythology.
- Speculative accounts of language origins.
- Rigid linguistic-family schemes, especially “Turanian.”
- The Indian literary “Renaissance theory.”
- His proposed method of Vedic dating.

### Indian reception

Reception in India has never been uniform. Reformers and religious intellectuals read him with admiration, criticism, or strategic appropriation. Vivekananda praised his dedication. Other Indian writers considered his editions invaluable while rejecting his chronology, theology, or racial vocabulary.

Postcolonial and nationalist discussions emphasize colonial finance and the missionary language in his correspondence. Some accounts investigate these contexts carefully; others expand them into unsupported conspiracy narratives.

### Historiography

Nirad C. Chaudhuri’s *Scholar Extraordinary* offered a substantial, sympathetic but independent biography and received India’s Sahitya Akademi Award for English.

Lourens van den Bosch reconstructed Müller as a Victorian humanist and scholar of religion. Arie Molendijk emphasized the organization, economics, networks, and canonical politics of the *Sacred Books of the East*. John R. Davis and Angus Nicholls situated him within German philosophy, British institutional life, and Victorian debates over language and science.

His present reputation is divided among several fields: a foundational Indologist; an architect of comparative religion; a gifted popularizer; a representative of colonial Orientalism; and a source of concepts subsequently appropriated by racial ideologies.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are listed. Mere shared subject matter, geography, or later disciplinary succession is excluded.

### Alexander von Humboldt — documented fact

While Müller was working in Paris, Alexander von Humboldt used his influence with King Friedrich Wilhelm IV of Prussia to assist efforts to publish Müller’s *Rig-Veda* edition in Germany. The German publication plan did not become the final institutional arrangement; East India Company and Oxford support ultimately sustained the edition.

### Friedrich Wilhelm Joseph Schelling — documented fact

Müller studied with Schelling in Berlin in 1844–1845. Schelling asked him to translate Upanishadic passages and influenced his attempt to connect the history of language with the history of religion.

### Immanuel Kant — documented intellectual connection

Müller studied Kant extensively and published an English translation of *Kritik der reinen Vernunft* in 1881 with historical and philosophical introductions. Kant’s epistemology informed Müller’s discussions of the infinite, reason, and religious knowledge.

### Charles Robert Darwin — documented correspondence and intellectual dispute

Darwin and Müller corresponded in 1873 and 1875 about language and evolution. Müller rejected a demonstrated evolutionary continuity between animal cries and conceptual human language; Darwin considered descent from animals to imply development from inarticulate sounds. Their exchange remained courteous.

### Narendranath Datta / Swami Vivekananda — documented meeting and intellectual relationship

Vivekananda visited Müller at 7 Norham Gardens, Oxford, on 28 May 1896. They discussed Ramakrishna and the materials needed for a fuller biography. Müller subsequently published *Râmakrishna: His Life and Sayings*. Vivekananda publicly praised Müller and circulated the devotional comparison of him to Sāyaṇa reborn.

### Helena Petrovna Blavatsky — documented intellectual controversy; direct meeting unestablished

Blavatsky cited Müller, while Müller criticized Theosophical representations of “Esoteric Buddhism” and distinguished his use of *theosophy* from the Theosophical Society’s occult claims. The consulted sources do not establish a personal meeting between Müller and Blavatsky; the connection should therefore be treated as textual and polemical.

### Johann Wolfgang von Goethe — documented literary influence, not personal contact

Müller edited and taught German literature in a tradition in which Goethe was central and published Goethe-related biographical and literary commentary. Goethe died in 1832, when Müller was eight; no meeting is documented.

### Friedrich Schiller — documented editorial connection, not personal contact

Müller edited Schiller’s correspondence with Duke Friedrich Christian of Schleswig-Holstein in 1875. Schiller died before Müller’s birth.

### Jacob Ludwig Carl Grimm and Wilhelm Carl Grimm — documented disciplinary influence

Müller’s comparative philology and mythology developed from the German tradition in which the Grimms were foundational. He discussed their linguistic and mythological work. No personal meeting with Jacob Grimm has been established in the sources consulted; Wilhelm Grimm died in 1859, and a meeting likewise remains unverified.

### Friedrich Nietzsche — no supported direct connection found

Both worked within nineteenth-century classical and philological culture, but the consulted sources did not document a meeting, correspondence, collaboration, or explicitly acknowledged personal influence between them.

### Rabindranath Tagore — no supported direct connection found

Müller had extensive Indian correspondence and visitors, but no documented meeting or correspondence with Tagore was found in the consulted material.

### J. R. R. Tolkien — no direct connection

Tolkien’s later Oxford philology belongs to an institutional world Müller helped establish, but Tolkien was eight when Müller died. No meeting or explicit personal relationship was found.

### Claude Lévi-Strauss, Carl Gustav Jung, Sigmund Freud, Mircea Eliade, William Butler Yeats, Annie Besant, Rudolf Steiner, and later atlas figures

Later mythologists, psychologists, esotericists, and historians of religion worked in fields affected directly or indirectly by nineteenth-century comparative studies. The consulted sources did not establish the specific documented meeting, correspondence, or explicitly acknowledged influence required for an Atlas entry. Their omission is therefore deliberate.
