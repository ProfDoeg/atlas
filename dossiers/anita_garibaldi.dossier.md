# Anita Garibaldi: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

Anita Garibaldi left a very small first-person documentary record. Much of the familiar narrative derives from Giuseppe Garibaldi’s memoirs, composed and revised after her death, and from later patriotic biographies. The dossier therefore distinguishes among:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary civil, ecclesiastical, military, police, judicial, or archival records.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a generally reliable scholarly source but not independently verified here against the original record.
- **Memoir claim:** principally derived from Giuseppe Garibaldi’s retrospective account.
- **Allegation or theory:** proposed but not established.
- **Rumor or myth:** unsupported, embellished, folkloric, propagandistic, or demonstrably shaped as heroic narrative.
- **Unresolved question:** evidence remains incomplete or contradictory.

The most substantial modern biographical synthesis consulted was Roberto Balzani’s 2016 entry in the *Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani*. Silvia Cavicchioli’s *Anita: Storia e mito di Anita Garibaldi* provides the principal modern study of her posthumous construction. The records concerning her death are represented by the Archivio di Stato di Bologna’s presentation of the surviving 1849 police documents.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name:** Ana Maria de Jesus Ribeiro. Sources also give Ana Maria de Jesus Ribeiro da Silva, Anna Maria Ribeiro da Silva, and Ana Maria Ribeiro da Silva.

**Known name:** Anita Garibaldi.

**Alternative spellings:** Anna, Annita, Ana; Ribeiro/Ribero; Duarte de Aguiar sometimes appears as Duarte de Aguiaz.

**“Anita”:** Conventionally understood as the Spanish or Italian diminutive by which Giuseppe Garibaldi called Ana Maria. Precisely when it became her usual name is not firmly documented.

**Titles and epithets:**

- “Heroína dos Dois Mundos” / “Heroine of the Two Worlds.”
- “Eroina dei Due Mondi.”
- “Brazilian heroine.”
- “Heroine of the Risorgimento.”
- Giuseppe Guerzoni called her a “martyr of love” in his 1882 biography of Garibaldi.

These are commemorative titles, not offices she held.

**Birth:** Conventionally 30 August 1821.

**Birthplace:** Traditionally Morrinhos, then in or near Laguna, Santa Catarina, Brazil. Modern administrative boundaries complicate the designation; Morrinhos is associated today with the municipality of Tubarão.

**Birthplace status:** **Unresolved question.** No contemporary baptismal or civil birth record securely identifiable as hers has been found. Laguna and Lages have both claimed her. A Brazilian court ordered a late civil registration in 1998 identifying Laguna as her birthplace, but that ruling created a modern legal record from historical evidence; it did not uncover a missing contemporary birth certificate.

**Death:** 4 August 1849, probably in the late afternoon or evening.

**Place of death:** Guiccioli farm, operated by Stefano Ravaglia, at Mandriole near Ravenna, in the Papal Legations then under Austrian military control.

**Age at death:** Twenty-seven if the conventional birth date is correct.

**Cause of death:** Most likely a severe febrile illness, usually identified as malaria or “pernicious fever,” aggravated by pregnancy, exhaustion, exposure, hunger, and the retreat from Rome. A contemporary surgeon initially suspected strangulation from marks on the decomposed body, but retracted that conclusion. No murder finding resulted.

**Nationality and political identity:** Born a subject of the United Kingdom of Portugal, Brazil and the Algarves shortly before Brazilian independence; subsequently Brazilian. Closely associated with the Rio-Grandense and Julian republican movements, Uruguay’s Colorado-aligned war effort, and the Roman Republic of 1849. Statements that she was formally “naturalized Italian” are anachronistic or require qualification: the Kingdom of Italy did not exist during her lifetime.

**Occupations and roles:** No documented salaried profession. Household worker and mother; political and military companion; rider, transporter of ammunition and supplies, attendant to wounded men, fugitive, and participant in revolutionary campaigns. Later representations call her a soldier or guerrilla, although no surviving commission, rank, enlistment, or payroll record establishes formal military status.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Father:** Bento Ribeiro da Silva, also called Bentão. Modern accounts variously describe him as a cattle drover, herdsman, transporter, small trader, or rural laborer. The 1998 judicial registration identified him as a native of São José dos Pinhais, Paraná.

**Mother:** Maria Antônia de Jesus Antunes, associated with Lages, Santa Catarina.

The late registration named Anita’s paternal grandparents as Manuel Collaço and Ângela Maria da Silva, and her maternal grandparents as Salvador Antunes and Quitéria Maria de Sousa. These particulars come through the 1998 proceeding rather than a contemporary record of Anita’s birth.

Claims of specifically Azorean ancestry on her mother’s side are common in Brazilian local history. They are plausible given regional settlement patterns, but the complete genealogical chain was not established in the sources consulted.

A recent internet claim of “ memories, however, explicitly acknowledge that Ana Maria was married and describe their relationship as injuring an “innocent man.” The marriage itself should therefore be treated as **documented fact**; competing explanations concern the separation, not whether the marriage existed.

### Siblings

Balzani reports that Ana Maria belonged to a family containing five sisters and four brothers and that her father and some brothers died of typhus before her marriage. Popular biographies often call her the third of ten children—six girls and four boys. Complete sibling lists vary, and the exact birth order was not securely reconstructed here.

### Partner and second husband

**Giuseppe Maria Garibaldi** (1807–1882), sailor, republican revolutionary, military commander, and later central figure of Italian unification.

They met in Laguna in 1839 and lived together thereafter. They married in the Church of San Francisco de Asís, Montevideo, on **26 March 1842**. Some secondary sources give 16 June, but the 26 March date is the one adopted by Balzani’s *Dizionario Biografico* entry and modern scholarship.

Because Manuel Duarte’s legal status was uncertain, Ana Maria and Giuseppe apparently represented him as dead or disappeared. The surviving Montevideo marriage act reportedly does not explain the matter. The canonical validity of the second marriage has consequently generated later controversy.

### Children with Giuseppe Garibaldi

1. **Domenico Menotti Garibaldi**, born 16 September 1840 at São Luís de Mostardas, Rio Grande do Sul; died 22 August 1903. Named for Italian revolutionary Ciro Menotti. He became a soldier and politician.
2. **Rosa “Rosita” Garibaldi**, born in Montevideo in 1843; died there on 23 December 1845, reportedly of smallpox.
3. **Teresa “Teresita” Garibaldi**, born 22 February 1845 in Montevideo; died 5 January 1903. She married Garibaldian officer Stefano Canzio.
4. **Ricciotti Garibaldi**, born 24 February 1847 in Montevideo; died 17 July 1924. He became a soldier and father of an extensive political-military family.

Anita was approximately six months pregnant with a fifth child when she died. The fetus did not survive and was never independently named.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1821–1835

The secure documentary record is extremely thin. Ana Maria grew up in southern Brazil in a family dependent on cattle movement and irregular rural work. She probably spent part of her childhood at Morrinhos and moved with her family to Laguna around 1834.

Her father and some male siblings reportedly died in a typhus outbreak, leaving Maria Antônia and the surviving children in reduced circumstances. Later narratives say Ana helped support the household by domestic work.

No reliable evidence establishes formal schooling. One later biographical tradition says she learned rudimentary reading and writing only in Europe shortly before her death. Her surviving archival footprint includes a letter to her mother-in-law, but the extent to which Anita could compose rather than merely sign correspondence remains uncertain.

Stories of a fiercely independent childhood—riding horses, swimming naked, resisting men, carrying a knife, fighting off an assailant, and alarming conservative townspeople—appear principally in later biographies. They may preserve local memories but also conform closely to the retrospective archetype of the untamed future warrior.

---

## First Marriage and the Farroupilha Setting, 1835–1839

Ana Maria married Manuel Duarte de Aguiar on 30 August 1835, conventionally her fourteenth birthday, at the parish church of Santo Antônio dos Anjos in Laguna.

The marriage coincided with the beginning of the Farroupilha or Ragamuffin War. Rio Grande do Sul landowners, merchants, military officers, and republican intellectuals rebelled against Brazil’s imperial government over taxation, trade, regional autonomy, and political power. In 1836 the rebels proclaimed the Rio-Grandense Republic.

Italian republican exiles, including Livio Zambeccari and Luigi Rossetti, brought connections with Giuseppe Mazzini’s Young Italy movement. Giuseppe Garibaldi entered Rio-Grandense service and became a naval commander.

Later accounts often make the teenage Ana Maria an established republican before she met Garibaldi. **This is possible but not documented.** Balzani cautions that the claim fits later revolutionary biography and rests on little contemporary evidence.

---

## Meeting Giuseppe Garibaldi and the Julian Republic, 1839

Rebel forces under Davi Canabarro, Garibaldi, and their associates captured Laguna in July 1839 and proclaimed the short-lived Julian Republic. Garibaldi commanded improvised naval operations based there.

### The meeting

**Documented core:** Ana Maria and Giuseppe Garibaldi met at Laguna during the rebel occupation in 1839 and began a relationship.

**Memoir version:** Garibaldi wrote that, after losing companions and feeling desolate aboard the *Itaparica*, he looked through a telescope toward shore, saw a young woman, had himself rowed toward her, entered a house, and told her in Italian: “You must be mine.” He described the encounter as instantaneous recognition and destiny.

**Assessment:** The relationship is certain; the telescope, exact house, first words, and mystical mutual recognition depend on Garibaldi’s retrospective memoir and became foundational episodes of Garibaldian romantic mythology.

On 23 October 1839 Anita reportedly embarked with Garibaldi aboard the *Rio Pardo*. As loyalist pressure increased, the Julian Republic collapsed in November. She left Laguna with Garibaldi and permanently separated from Manuel Duarte.

Garibaldi later accepted responsibility for violating the first marriage, writing that if a wrong had been committed, the fault was his. That passage is unusually important because it contradicts later sanitized accounts in which Anita was already unquestionably free to remarry.

---

## Campaigns in Southern Brazil, 1839–1841

Anita accompanied the retreating republicans through Santa Catarina and Rio Grande do Sul. Her exact role varied by episode and is difficult to separate from later embellishment.

### Naval fighting near Laguna

Popular biographies place her under fire aboard rebel vessels, carrying ammunition between the powder magazine and guns during fighting at Imbituba or near Laguna. Imperial admiral Frederico Mariath later credited a woman associated with Garibaldi’s force with bravery in combat, evidence used by historians of her early reputation.

No formal service record survives. Her participation as an armed or logistical auxiliary is strongly reported but the precise actions described in heroic biographies are not all independently corroborated.

### Curitibanos

In 1840 republican forces were defeated near Curitibanos. Anita, pregnant with Menotti, was captured by imperial troops.

**Supported core:** She was captured and escaped on horseback, later rejoining Garibaldi.

**Memoir and heroic elaboration:** She persuaded the commander to let her search the dead for Garibaldi, stole a horse, was pursued, crossed the Canoas River, lost the horse, swam or struggled across, and rode for days without food before reaching the republican force.

The capture, escape, and lengthy ride are accepted by Balzani. Dialogue, precise mileage, river feats, and the dramatic search among corpses are less securely documented.

### Menotti’s birth and flight

Menotti was born at Mostardas on 16 September 1840. Garibaldi called the successful birth miraculous after Anita’s campaigning, deprivation, and a fall from a horse.

Soon afterward, an armed enemy party attacked the place where Anita and the infant were staying. She fled with Menotti and hid in the countryside or forest until reunited with Giuseppe. Later accounts specify four days without food; that duration should be treated as a memoir-derived or popular detail rather than independently documented fact.

By 1841 the Rio-Grandense military position had deteriorated. Garibaldi obtained permission to leave republican service. The family traveled overland with cattle toward Uruguay, a journey sometimes estimated at roughly 600 kilometers.

---

## Montevideo, 1841–1847

The family reached Montevideo on **17 June 1841** and eventually lived on what became Calle 25 de Mayo.

### Marriage and household

Anita and Giuseppe married on 26 March 1842. Their household was financially modest. Garibaldi attempted commerce and taught mathematics, geography, French, and handwriting before returning to military and naval employment.

No evidence identifies property, investments, corporate holdings, patents, or substantial personal wealth belonging to Anita. The household depended chiefly on Giuseppe’s irregular work, military service, supporters, and the solidarity networks of Italian exiles.

### War in Uruguay

Montevideo became the center of the “Great Siege,” in which the Colorado government resisted Manuel Oribe’s forces, backed by Argentine ruler Juan Manuel de Rosas. Garibaldi entered Uruguayan naval service and organized the Italian Legion.

Balzani emphasizes that Anita principally cared for the children and did **not** continuously participate in Giuseppe’s Uruguayan operations. Popular accounts that place her in every engagement overstate the evidence.

She did travel to Salto in 1845 after the death of Rosita, during Giuseppe’s campaigning. His surviving letters to Anita from September–December 1845 are brief and practical rather than an extensive reciprocal correspondence.

### Children and bereavement

Rosita’s death at about two years old was a major personal loss. Teresita and Ricciotti were born amid war and financial insecurity. The family nevertheless became part of an Italian republican exile community whose political culture connected Latin American conflicts with European nationalism.

---

## Migration to Europe, 1847–1848

News of liberal and national uprisings in Italy led the Garibaldis to plan a return.

Anita embarked from Montevideo with Menotti, Teresita, and Ricciotti on **27 December 1847**. She reached Genoa on **2 March 1848** and Nice on **8 March**, where she stayed with Giuseppe’s mother, Rosa Raimondi. Giuseppe followed separately and arrived in April; the family reunited on 21 June.

Accounts of a public welcome and speech by Anita at Genoa are reported in later sources but require stronger contemporary confirmation.

Life in Nice was difficult. Anita reportedly had a strained relationship with Rosa Raimondi and made journeys seeking Giuseppe while he participated in military operations after the First Italian War of Independence. She developed a closer relationship with Giuseppe and Adelaide Deideri, family friends who hosted or assisted her.

Garibaldi returned to Nice on 10 September 1848 after Piedmont’s defeat. Anita then traveled with him through Liguria and Tuscany before he sent her back while his legion moved into central Italy.

---

## Rome, Rieti, and the Roman Republic, 1849

After Pope Pius IX fled Rome, a constituent assembly proclaimed the Roman Republic in February 1849. Giuseppe Garibaldi became one of its principal military commanders.

Anita left Nice in mid-February, reached Rome on 23 February and joined Giuseppe at Rieti on 26 February. She remained there until early April and became pregnant for the fifth time.

She returned to Nice while Giuseppe moved toward besieged Rome. As military news worsened, she traveled back despite pregnancy and declining health, arriving without warning at Garibaldi’s headquarters at Villa Spada on **26 June 1849**.

She was present during the final resistance against French forces restoring papal government. Whether she personally fought in the Roman defenses is less certain than popular images suggest. The securely supported fact is that she joined the republican forces at their headquarters and remained with Garibaldi during the final days and retreat.

---

## Retreat from Rome and Final Journey, 2 July–4 August 1849

Garibaldi left Rome on 2 July with several thousand volunteers, hoping to continue resistance and possibly reach Venice. Anita accompanied him, visibly pregnant and increasingly ill.

The column passed through central Italy under pursuit by French, Austrian, Spanish, Neapolitan, and papal forces. It reached San Marino on 31 July. Garibaldi dissolved the main force there, but continued with approximately 250 loyalists.

On 2 August the remaining party seized fishing boats at Cesenatico and attempted to sail toward Venice. An Austrian warship intercepted the flotilla. Some boats surrendered; Garibaldi’s party landed near Magnavacca, now Porto Garibaldi.

Patriot Nino Bonnet and local helpers guided Anita, Giuseppe, and officer Giovan Battista Culiolo—called “Leggero”—through the Comacchio marshes toward Ravenna. A Comacchio military report dated 6 August stated that Garibaldi disguised his wife as a man and placed her on an animal for the journey toward Pomposa.

Anita’s condition deteriorated. Later diagnosis is necessarily retrospective. Reported symptoms and conditions support malaria or another acute febrile disease compounded by pregnancy and extreme physical exhaustion.

At the Guiccioli farm in Mandriole on 4 August, tenant farmer Stefano Ravaglia admitted the fugitives. Dr. Pietro Nannini of Sant’Alberto was summoned but could not save her. She died with Garibaldi present, probably during the late afternoon or evening.

Garibaldi and Leggero had to continue their escape immediately. Stefano and Giuseppe Ravaglia promised to arrange burial.

---

## Burial, Discovery, and Investigation

Fearing punishment for sheltering Garibaldi, the Ravaglia brothers buried Anita hurriedly in sandy ground at the Mota della Pastorara near the farm.

On 10 August two children saw a hand protruding from the sand. Police recovered the body on 11 August. A provincial police report of 12 August described a pregnant woman in a white cambric shift and petticoat, a purple-patterned cambric mantle, barefoot and without jewelry.

Professor Luigi Fuschini, chief surgeon at Ravenna’s hospital, examined the badly decomposed body. He initially interpreted marks on the neck as evidence of strangulation. The Ravaglia brothers were arrested on 14 August on suspicion of complicity in murder and for sheltering fugitives.

Fuschini subsequently withdrew the strangulation opinion, stating that decomposition had misled him. The investigation accepted natural death. The brothers were released on 7 September. The Austrian proclamation criminalizing assistance to Garibaldi had been issued on 5 August, after Anita died, and could not lawfully be applied to their conduct on 4 August.

Anita was buried in the parish cemetery on 11 August 1849.

---

## Death: Evidentiary Findings

### Natural death

**Best-supported conclusion:** Anita died naturally from a severe febrile illness, probably malaria, aggravated by pregnancy and exhaustion.

### Murder by strangulation

**Initial medical suspicion, later retracted:** Fuschini saw neck marks on a decomposed body and initially suspected strangulation. This prompted arrests, but he withdrew the opinion and no one was convicted.

### Killing ordered or performed by Garibaldi

**Rumor or conspiracy theory:** Later hostile narratives have alleged that Garibaldi killed Anita, had her killed, abandoned her alive, or ordered a hurried burial to conceal murder. No contemporary witness or judicial finding establishes this. The theory draws primarily upon the initial neck observation, Garibaldi’s rapid departure, and the crude burial. Those facts have alternative explanations documented in the record: decomposition, active pursuit, and the Ravaglias’ fear of Austrian punishment.

### Pregnancy

**Documented fact:** The recovered body contained a fetus estimated at approximately six months. Her pregnancy was noted in the investigation and is consistent with her movements earlier in 1849.

### Exact disease

**Unresolved question:** Malaria or “pernicious fever” is the dominant diagnosis. Typhoid fever and other infections appear in secondary accounts. No modern laboratory evidence exists; “malaria” should therefore be described as probable rather than certain.

---

## Posthumous Movements of the Remains

- **1849:** Buried first in sand near the Guiccioli farm, then in the Mandriole parish cemetery.
- **September 1859:** Giuseppe Garibaldi returned with Menotti and Teresita to recover the remains.
- **11 November 1859:** Reinterred in the chapel of the Garibaldi family castle or burial place in Nice, near Rosa Raimondi and Rosita.
- **1931:** Benito Mussolini’s government obtained the transfer of the remains from Nice.
- **1931–1932:** The remains passed through Genoa’s Staglieno Cemetery.
- **4 June 1932:** Placed beneath Mario Rutelli’s equestrian monument to Anita on Rome’s Janiculum, inaugurated during the fascist commemoration of the fiftieth anniversary of Giuseppe Garibaldi’s death.

The repeated reburials transformed the body into a national and political relic.

---

## Education and Formation

No teacher, school, or formal curriculum can be securely identified.

Her formative environments were:

- A working rural and maritime community in southern Brazil.
- A household affected by disease and economic insecurity.
- The Farroupilha conflict.
- Contact with Italian republican exiles and the republican leadership at Laguna.
- Years within Montevideo’s Italian exile community.
- Personal association with Giuseppe Garibaldi and, indirectly, the transatlantic democratic-nationalist culture associated with Young Italy.

Claims that she became a fully articulated Mazzinian ideologue are not supported by writings of her own. Her conduct demonstrates commitment to Giuseppe and repeated willingness to accompany republican movements, but the relative weight of romantic attachment, family loyalty, adventure, political conviction, and necessity cannot be measured.

Religious background was Roman Catholic: both documented marriages were ecclesiastical. Later reports describing her as fiercely anticlerical belong largely to Risorgimento memory and are not substantiated by an extensive first-person record.

---

## Political, Intellectual, and Ideological Development

### Republicanism

**Reported fact:** She became associated successively with the Rio-Grandense and Julian republics and later the Roman Republic.

**Qualification:** There is no manifesto, diary, political essay, or detailed confession of belief written by Anita. Later authors often infer a coherent republican ideology from her actions.

### Nationalism

Italian commemoration recast her as a participant in the Italian national cause. During her lifetime in Italy, however, she accompanied a republican internationalist movement that was not identical to the later Savoyard monarchy’s unification project.

### Gender and military identity

Her life challenged customary nineteenth-century divisions between domestic femininity and military-political action. Posthumous representations alternately emphasized:

- the armed Amazon;
- the faithful wife following her husband;
- the self-directed revolutionary;
- the suffering mother;
- the exotic Brazilian lover;
- the Catholic spouse;
- the secular or anticlerical republican;
- the sacrificial maternal symbol adopted by nationalism.

These images frequently reveal more about the period producing them than about Anita’s own recorded views.

---

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Giuseppe Garibaldi

Partner, military companion, second husband, father of her children, and principal creator of her written legend. Their relationship began while she remained legally married to Manuel Duarte. It included combat, migration, prolonged separations, poverty, bereavement, and political exile.

### Manuel Duarte de Aguiar

First husband. His disappearance, military allegiance, and date of death remain uncertain. His legal existence complicated the 1842 marriage. Later heroic narratives often minimized or erased him.

### Maria Antônia de Jesus Antunes

Mother. Traditionally represented as arranging Anita’s first marriage and disapproving of her conduct; the precise emotional relationship is poorly documented.

### Rosa Raimondi

Giuseppe’s mother and Anita’s mother-in-law. She sheltered Anita and the children in Nice. Modern scholarship reports difficult cohabitation.

### Giuseppe and Adelaide Deideri

Friends of the Garibaldi family in Nice. They hosted and assisted Anita; she reportedly formed a particularly close bond with them.

### Davi Canabarro

Farroupilha commander and one of the founders of the Julian Republic. Anita entered Garibaldi’s life within the military-political structure Canabarro helped command.

### Luigi Rossetti

Italian Mazzinian exile and secretary of the Julian Republic. Part of the political network connecting southern Brazilian separatism with Italian republicanism. No separately documented intimate relationship with Anita was found.

### Nino Bonnet

Roman-republican patriot who helped guide the fugitives after their landing near Magnavacca in August 1849.

### Giovan Battista Culiolo (“Leggero”)

Garibaldian officer who accompanied Giuseppe and Anita during the last stage of the flight and was present around the time of her death.

### Stefano and Giuseppe Ravaglia

Farm family who sheltered the fugitives, obtained medical help, buried Anita, and were arrested in the subsequent murder investigation.

### Dr. Pietro Nannini

Physician called to the Guiccioli farm during Anita’s final illness.

### Professor Luigi Fuschini

Surgeon who examined the decomposed body, initially suspected strangulation, and later retracted the opinion.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

No company ownership, patent, investment portfolio, financial disclosure, or independent business enterprise is documented for Anita.

Relevant institutions and projects include:

- Rio-Grandense Republic.
- Julian Republic.
- Farroupilha navy.
- Italian exile community in Montevideo.
- Uruguayan naval forces and Italian Legion, through Giuseppe.
- Roman Republic of 1849.
- Garibaldian retreat network in Romagna.
- Posthumous museums, commemorative committees, civic associations, and state-sponsored monuments.

The family’s material condition was often precarious. Garibaldi traded and taught in Montevideo before obtaining military work. There is no reliable evidence that Anita accumulated substantial property or wealth.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Legal Questions

### Place and date of birth

**Unresolved historical question / modern court determination.** No contemporary birth record has been recovered. Laguna civic organizations sought a delayed registration in 1998. On 5 December 1998 a judge ordered Ana Maria registered as born in Laguna on 30 August 1821. The record was issued in May 1999.

The ruling supports Laguna’s modern legal claim but cannot be treated as a newly discovered 1821 record. Lages advocates point to family residence and siblings’ records there and allege that Anita’s baptismal page disappeared. No evidence examined here establishes intentional removal.

### First marriage and possible bigamy

**Documented marriage; unresolved marital termination.** The 1835 marriage to Manuel Duarte is supported by a church record. Anita began living with Garibaldi before Manuel’s securely documented death. The 1842 Montevideo marriage may therefore have involved an unsupported declaration that Manuel was dead or missing.

This creates a plausible canonical or legal bigamy question. No prosecution or annulment involving Anita has been identified. Later sources disagree on whether Manuel died before 1842.

### Abandonment versus escape from Manuel Duarte

**Competing reports.** One version says Manuel abandoned Anita by joining imperial forces. Another says she left him for Garibaldi. Garibaldi’s memoir accepts that their union injured her first husband, favoring the conclusion that the prior relationship had not been formally dissolved.

### Military participation

**Documented core, embellished details.** Her presence in campaigns, capture, escape, riding, and retreat are strongly supported. Claims that she fought in every battle, formally commanded soldiers, or possessed an official military rank are not established.

### Death and murder investigation

**Documented investigation; no murder finding.** The Ravaglias were arrested after Fuschini’s preliminary observation suggested strangulation. He retracted it, and they were freed. Modern murder narratives should not be presented as proven history.

### Garibaldi’s control over Anita

The famous “You must be mine” passage has been interpreted as romantic destiny, possessiveness, abduction, coercion, or later literary self-dramatization. The exact words are a **memoir claim**. No contemporary account from Anita survives to establish her experience of the first encounter.

### Slavery and racial politics

Anita lived in a slaveholding Brazilian empire and participated in a rebellion led partly by regional elites whose relationships to slavery were complex and inconsistent. No evidence consulted shows that Anita owned enslaved people. Nor is there a surviving text in which she explains a position on abolition. Later universalist language attached to her—liberty, equality, humanity—should not be substituted for a documented personal statement on slavery.

### Recent adversarial search, 2024–2026

Dedicated searches combined her name with controversy, scandal, accusations, fraud, lawsuits, conspiracy, criticism, and related terms in Portuguese, Italian, and English. Most recent results concerned the Brazilian municipality of Anita Garibaldi, the Ponte Anita Garibaldi, or unrelated living people—not the historical person.

No credible newly discovered allegation, financial scandal, criminal case, intelligence connection, or abuse attributable to Ana Maria herself appeared in the 2024–2026 results. The continuing historical disputes remain her birthplace, first marriage, degree of military agency, literacy, and cause and circumstances of death.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### The telescope and predestined recognition

**Story:** Garibaldi saw Anita through a telescope from the *Itaparica*, immediately sought her, and declared, “You must be mine.”

**Origin and circulation:** Giuseppe Garibaldi’s memoirs; subsequently repeated by biographies, schoolbooks, films, museums, and civic commemorations.

**Evidence:** Their meeting at Laguna is certain. The telescope, words, and feeling of prior mystical recognition have no independent contemporary corroboration.

**Effect:** Established their relationship as romantic destiny and obscured Anita’s existing marriage and personal agency.

### The Amazon warrior

**Story:** Anita was a naturally fearless horsewoman who personally fought through cannon fire, fired weapons, transported ammunition, tended the wounded, escaped captivity, swam rivers, and crossed wilderness while pregnant.

**Evidence:** Several core episodes—campaign participation, exposure to fire, care of wounded men, capture, horseback escape, pregnancy, and flight with Menotti—are credible or well reported. Exact feats and dialogue accumulated through Garibaldi’s memoir and later popular biography.

**Effect:** Made her an international archetype of the female revolutionary while sometimes reducing her to a supporting figure in Giuseppe’s legend.

### Four days in the forest with newborn Menotti

**Story:** Anita escaped an attack carrying her twelve-day-old child and survived four days without food until Garibaldi found her.

**Evidence:** The attack and flight are accepted in major biographies. Precise duration and hardships largely depend on retrospective narrative.

**Effect:** Fused warrior, fugitive, and sacrificial-mother imagery.

### Murder and concealed burial

**Story:** Garibaldi, Leggero, or the Ravaglias strangled Anita, abandoned her alive, or concealed a crime through secret burial.

**First basis:** The corpse’s hurried burial and Luigi Fuschini’s initial observation of neck marks.

**Evidence against:** Fuschini withdrew the strangulation diagnosis; the body was decomposed; a physician had attended Anita before death; the fugitives and their helpers faced immediate danger; the investigation produced no murder finding.

**Effect:** Supplied anti-Garibaldian and conspiratorial literature with a counter-myth to patriotic martyrdom.

### The “heroine of two worlds”

The phrase transfers Giuseppe Garibaldi’s celebrated title to Anita. It is historically meaningful as commemoration but not a contemporary rank. It positions her Brazilian and Italian experience as a unified freedom struggle, smoothing over distinct local conflicts and political contradictions.

### Fascist appropriation

Mussolini’s government transferred her remains to Rome and inaugurated Rutelli’s Janiculum monument in 1932. The mounted woman carrying a pistol and infant allowed the regime to combine martial courage, motherhood, sacrifice, and national destiny.

This was not evidence of Anita’s fascism; she died more than seventy years before fascism emerged. It was an appropriation of a nineteenth-century republican figure for twentieth-century state symbolism.

### Brazilian reclamation

From the late nineteenth century onward—and especially around anniversaries in 1949, 1999, and 2021—Brazilian institutions increasingly claimed Anita as a national and regional heroine. Museums, municipalities, roads, schools, bridges, monuments, plays, and civic festivals shifted attention from “Garibaldi’s wife” to “a Brazilian heroine.”

### Feminist reinterpretation

Late twentieth- and twenty-first-century scholarship has sought to recover Anita as an actor rather than an appendage. Some versions risk reversing the older simplification by attributing a fully documented autonomous political program where her own written evidence remains sparse.

## Honors, Memorials, and Named Places

Anita received no documented formal award or state office during her lifetime.

Posthumous commemorations include:

- Mario Rutelli’s equestrian monument on the Janiculum in Rome, inaugurated 4 June 1932; her remains lie beneath it.
- Museu Histórico Anita Garibaldi in Laguna, located in the former civic building associated with the proclamation of the Julian Republic.
- Casa de Anita in Laguna.
- The municipality of Anita Garibaldi, Santa Catarina.
- Anitaópolis, Santa Catarina.
- Ponte Anita Garibaldi at Laguna.
- Numerous streets, schools, squares, associations, and women’s organizations in Brazil and Italy.

## Works by Anita Garibaldi

No book, memoir, manifesto, article, speech transcript, diary, patent, artwork, or formal political document authored by Anita is securely known.

### Surviving correspondence

- **Letter from Anita Garibaldi to Rosa Garibaldi** — autograph or attributed manuscript held in the Fondo Garibaldi, Museo Centrale del Risorgimento/Istituto per la storia del Risorgimento italiano, Rome, box B.926. The online catalogue confirms the archival unit but does not supply a full transcription or date in the displayed record.

### Correspondence received

The same archive catalogues:

- Autograph letter from Giuseppe Matteo Antonini to Anita Garibaldi, B.924.
- Autograph letter from Ugo Bassi to Anita Garibaldi, B.924.
- Letters and a copied letter from Giuseppe Garibaldi to Anita, B.931.

These are not works authored by Anita but are important components of her documentary record.

### Material falsely liable to confusion

Numerous twentieth-century publications appear under the name “Anita Garibaldi” but were written by descendants, particularly Annita Garibaldi Jallet and Anita Italia Garibaldi. They must not be attributed to Ana Maria de Jesus Ribeiro.

Garibaldi’s *Memorie autobiografiche* are Giuseppe’s memoirs, not a jointly authored work, even though Anita occupies a central place in them. Alexandre Dumas edited or mediated an early version of Garibaldi’s memoirs; Anita did not co-author it.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Anita’s reputation developed in identifiable stages.

### 1849–1880s: grief and Garibaldian memory

Giuseppe’s accounts made her the courageous companion whose death sealed the emotional epic of 1849. Early opposition narratives focused on the suspicious burial and possible strangulation.

### Late nineteenth century: patriotic heroine

Biographers such as Guerzoni and Bandi integrated her into the Risorgimento’s romantic canon. Italian national culture could honor a republican outsider while emphasizing loyalty, marriage, sacrifice, and motherhood.

### Brazilian republican period

After Brazil became a republic in 1889, writers including Henrique Boiteux reclaimed her as a Brazilian precursor of republican patriotism. Her regional origins became politically valuable, intensifying the Laguna–Lages dispute.

### Fascist Italy

The 1932 transfer and monument constituted the largest state appropriation of her image. Fascist representation emphasized an armed mother whose fertility and violence served the nation. Her republican internationalism was subordinated to state nationalism.

### Postwar popular culture

Anna Magnani’s portrayal in *Camicie rosse* reinforced Anita as passionate, combative, and tragic for mass audiences.

### Late twentieth century to present

Brazilian historical writing, women’s history, and feminist interpretation increasingly examined her as an autonomous participant. Recent scholarship also studies how male memoir, nationalism, fascism, tourism, civic rivalry, and fiction successively remade her.

Her life remains unusually difficult to reconstruct because the archival record is asymmetrical: Giuseppe narrated; governments recorded her mainly when she crossed military or police systems; posterity supplied the interior life.

## Atlas Connections

### Giuseppe Maria Garibaldi

**Evidentiary label: documented fact.**

Anita met Giuseppe Garibaldi at Laguna in 1839 during the Julian Republic. They became partners, campaigned through southern Brazil, moved to Montevideo in 1841, married there on 26 March 1842, had four children, migrated to Europe, joined the Roman-republican struggle, and remained together through her death at Mandriole on 4 August 1849.

He was also the principal architect of her posthumous personal mythology through his memoirs and facilitated the recovery and reburial of her remains in 1859.

### Juan Manuel José Domingo Ortiz de Rozas y López de Osornio (Juan Manuel de Rosas)

**Evidentiary label: reported fact; indirect adversarial connection.**

During the family’s Montevideo years, Giuseppe fought for the Uruguayan government against Manuel Oribe and the forces supported by Argentine ruler Juan Manuel de Rosas. Anita belonged to the Garibaldi household and briefly traveled to Salto during these campaigns, but Balzani’s reconstruction states that she did not directly participate in most of Giuseppe’s Uruguayan military activity. No meeting or correspondence between Anita and Rosas is documented.

### Alexandre Dumas

**Evidentiary label: documented posthumous literary connection, not a lifetime meeting.**

Dumas prepared and published an early literary version of Giuseppe Garibaldi’s memoirs in 1860–1861, more than a decade after Anita’s death. His work helped internationalize the romantic account of her life. No meeting between Anita and Dumas is documented.

### Giuseppe Mazzini

Mazzini is not named in the supplied atlas roster as “Giuseppe Mazzini,” although several people associated with him are. Anita operated within networks strongly influenced by Young Italy, particularly through Garibaldi, Luigi Rossetti, and other Italian exiles. No direct meeting or correspondence between Anita and Mazzini was established in the sources consulted.

### Other roster names

No defensible direct connection was found with the remaining individuals in the atlas roster. Mere overlap with nineteenth-century republicanism, Italian unification, Brazil, Uruguay, Rome, or later commemorative movements has not been counted as a connection.
