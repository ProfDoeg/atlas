# Keiko Sofia Fujimori Higuchi: Research Dossier

**Research cutoff:** 3 September 2026  
**Status:** Living; president of Peru since 28 July 2026  
**Method:** Documentary synthesis drawing primarily on Peruvian electoral and judicial records, congressional material, international election-observation reports, academic research, interviews, and contemporaneous journalism. Legal and political claims are labeled by evidentiary status.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

Her father was **Alberto Fujimori**, president of Peru from 1990 until the collapse of his government in 2000. Born into a Japanese immigrant family, he built his early career as an agricultural engineer, professor, university administrator, and television presenter before entering politics.

His presidency combined several distinct historical records:

- economic stabilization after hyperinflation;
- market liberalization and privatization;
- the defeat or severe weakening of Sendero Luminoso and the MRTA;
- the April 1992 “self-coup,” dissolution of Congress, and restructuring of state institutions;
- rule through an increasingly authoritarian civilian–military and intelligence apparatus;
- corruption organized around intelligence chief Vladimiro Montesinos;
- state-linked human-rights crimes, including the Barrios Altos and La Cantuta killings.

Alberto Fujimori was later convicted in Peru of corruption and human-rights offenses. He received a 25-year sentence in the Barrios Altos–La Cantuta case, was controversially pardoned, returned to custody, and was released again in December 2023 following Constitutional Tribunal proceedings. He died in Lima on 11 September 2024. Keiko announced his death and played the leading family role at his wake and burial.

Her mother, **Susana Shizuko Higuchi Miyagawa**, was an engineer, businesswoman, first lady, government critic, and later member of Congress. She was also of Japanese-Peruvian ancestry.

### Siblings

Keiko is the eldest of four children:

- **Hiro Alberto Fujimori Higuchi**, born in December 1976;
- **Sachi Marcela Fujimori Higuchi**, born in March 1979;
- **Kenji Gerardo Fujimori Higuchi**, born in May 1980.

Kenji became a congressman and, for a period, a rival center of power inside Fujimorismo. Keiko and Kenji’s conflict became public during the struggle over whether President Pedro Pablo Kuczynski should be removed and whether their father should receive a pardon. Their relationship later partially normalized; both appeared together during Alberto Fujimori’s final illness and funeral.

### Parental rupture

In the early 1990s Susana Higuchi accused members of her husband’s circle of diverting donated clothing and other goods intended for poor Peruvians. The dispute became a public marital and political rupture.

Alberto Fujimori removed Higuchi from the conventional functions of first lady and from leadership of the Fundación por los Niños del Perú. In August 1994, he transferred the public first-lady role to their nineteen-year-old daughter Keiko.

Higuchi subsequently alleged that she had been confined, mistreated, and subjected to electric-shock torture by state agents. A post-Fujimori congressional investigation found reasonable indications supporting allegations that she had been unlawfully detained and physically and psychologically mistreated in military-intelligence facilities. These were congressional investigative findings and allegations concerning Alberto Fujimori and state personnel, not criminal findings against Keiko.

Keiko publicly said that she had not witnessed torture and initially aligned herself with her father during the family rupture. This produced the enduring hostile narrative that she “replaced” or “betrayed” her mother. The documented core is narrower: Keiko accepted the first-lady position after her mother’s removal, remained politically aligned with her father, and disputed or said she did not know about the alleged torture. Mother and daughter later reconciled; Higuchi supported Keiko’s presidential campaigns before her death in 2021.

### Marriage and children

While studying in New York, Fujimori met **Mark Vito Villanella**, a US citizen of Italian ancestry. They married in Lima on 3 July 2004 in a Catholic ceremony officiated by Cardinal Juan Luis Cipriani. Villanella later acquired Peruvian citizenship.

Their daughters are:

- **Kyara or Kiara Sofía Villanella Fujimori**, born in 2007;
- **Kaori Marcela Villanella Fujimori**, born in 2009.

On 21 June 2022, Fujimori announced that she and Villanella had decided to end their marriage. They described the separation publicly as amicable and continued presenting themselves as cooperating parents. Villanella had also been investigated in the broader campaign-finance proceedings associated with the Cócteles case; the mere fact of inclusion did not establish criminal liability.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1975–1990

Fujimori grew up in Lima in a professional, upwardly mobile Japanese-Peruvian household. Her father taught at the Universidad Nacional Agraria La Molina; her mother ran business ventures and raised the four children.

Keiko and her siblings attended **Colegio Sagrados Corazones Recoleta**, a private Catholic school in Lima. Accounts of the family describe Keiko as the sibling who tried to mediate between parents during their increasingly troubled marriage.

Her childhood changed decisively when Alberto Fujimori entered the 1990 presidential election as the little-known candidate of Cambio 90. His unexpected victory moved the family into the center of national political life. Keiko was fifteen when he became president.

The period was marked by Peru’s internal armed conflict, hyperinflation, state crisis, and political violence. Her formative political environment was therefore not an ordinary party apprenticeship but the presidential palace, the security-centered Fujimori government, the April 1992 self-coup, and the family’s exposure to intense public hostility and loyalty.

Later profiles report that she considered politics responsible for depriving her of an ordinary adolescence, friendships, and private relationships. She nevertheless came to derive her political identity and organizational base from the movement created around her father.

## Education and Formation

### Schooling

- Colegio Sagrados Corazones Recoleta, Lima.
- Brief university study at the State University of New York at Stony Brook is reported in biographical accounts.
- Transfer to **Boston University**.
- Bachelor’s degree in business administration, completed in 1997.
- Graduate study at **Columbia Business School**, New York.
- Master of Business Administration, generally reported as completed in 2006.
- JNE/Congressional biographical material also lists a diploma in Parliament, Democracy and Governance associated with George Washington University.

### Formative influences

No single academic mentor is prominent in the public record. Her major influences were institutional and familial:

- her father’s electoral organization and governing style;
- Japanese-Peruvian family discipline and identity;
- the social-assistance machinery associated with the first lady’s office;
- US business education;
- long-standing Fujimorista politicians such as Jaime Yoshiyama, Luz Salgado, Martha Chávez, José Chlimper, Luis Galarreta, Pier Figari, Ana Vega, and later Miguel Torres;
- Catholic social conservatism, alongside occasional tactical moderation on civil unions and therapeutic abortion;
- the direct-election, leader-centered structure of Peruvian politics.

### Financing of education: allegation and disposition

A major controversy concerned the cost of overseas education for the four Fujimori children.

**Documented fact:** Keiko and her siblings studied abroad during and after their father’s presidency, producing substantial tuition and living expenses.

**Allegation/testimony:** Vladimiro Montesinos and his former secretary Matilde Pinchi Pinchi alleged that money from the National Intelligence Service or funds managed by Montesinos helped pay those expenses. Reports also described a Panamanian foundation or financial channel allegedly connected to payments.

**Defense:** The Fujimori family said the education was financed through family resources, sale or refinancing of property, tuition arrangements, and loans.

**Legal status:** A prosecution was not sustained. The investigation was ultimately archived after authorities reported that they could not prove the alleged illicit funding. Thus the claim remains historically important and was supported by insider testimony, but it did not produce a conviction or judicial finding that Keiko knowingly received stolen state funds.

## First Lady of Peru, 1994–2000

### Appointment

On 23 August 1994, following the public break between her parents, Keiko became Peru’s de facto first lady. At nineteen, she was among the youngest people to hold such a role in Latin America and one of the unusual cases in which a president’s daughter replaced his estranged wife.

She interrupted her US studies periodically to fulfill official duties.

### Institutional roles

She headed or represented:

- **Fundación por los Niños del Perú**, traditionally associated with the first lady;
- social-assistance and children’s programs;
- **Fundación Peruana Cardioinfantil**, associated with treatment for children with congenital heart disease.

Her public activities included hospital visits, charitable events, ceremonies, foreign travel, and participation in meetings of first ladies and representatives of heads of government. Reported trips included:

- Brasília for Fernando Henrique Cardoso’s inauguration in January 1995;
- Cartagena de Indias for a regional childhood summit in 1998;
- Santiago for a Conference of First Ladies in 1998;
- Ottawa for a corresponding conference in 1999.

At Cardoso’s inauguration she was reported to have expressed an ambition eventually to become Peru’s first woman president.

### Political significance

Her position had three simultaneous meanings:

1. a genuine social-assistance office through which she gained administrative and representational experience;
2. a mechanism by which Alberto Fujimori displaced Susana Higuchi from the public household;
3. Keiko’s initiation into Fujimorismo as a political dynasty and mass-electoral identity.

### Relationship to abuses of the Fujimori government

There is no court finding that Keiko ordered or participated in the Barrios Altos or La Cantuta killings, forced sterilizations, bribery system, or other principal crimes for which her father and members of his government were investigated or convicted.

Her historical responsibility is instead debated in political terms: she served as first lady, defended her father, and represented the regime during years in which abuses and corruption occurred. Critics describe this as active political legitimation. Supporters argue that she was a young family member performing ceremonial and charitable duties and cannot inherit her father’s criminal responsibility.

The factual distinction is important: **association and political defense are documented; personal criminal responsibility for her father’s human-rights convictions has not been judicially established.**

## The Government’s Collapse and Movement Between Peru, Japan, and the United States, 2000–2005

In 2000, video evidence exposed Vladimiro Montesinos bribing opposition congressman Alberto Kouri. The ensuing “Vladivideos” scandal revealed a broad network involving politicians, judges, media owners, military officers, and business interests.

Alberto Fujimori traveled to Japan and attempted to resign the presidency by fax. Congress rejected the resignation and removed him on grounds of “permanent moral incapacity.”

Keiko initially urged her father to return to Peru and answer the accusations. She visited him in Tokyo in 2001 but continued defending the accomplishments of his government and disputing accusations she regarded as politically motivated.

In 2002 she moved to New York for graduate business studies. Accounts report some employment or practical experience connected with private business, including work associated with General Motors, although the duration and precise status are not consistently documented.

She was interviewed for Ellen Perry’s documentary *The Fall of Fujimori*, produced from material filmed during the early 2000s and released in 2005–2006. Her statements defended her father’s continuing popularity and portrayed prosecutions and attacks against him as products of political fear.

Her marriage to Mark Vito Villanella in 2004 anchored a period of residence between New York and Lima. By 2005–2006 she had returned to Peru and entered elective politics.

## Early Electoral Career and Congress, 2005–2011

### Entry into party politics

With Alberto Fujimori detained in Chile after leaving Japan in 2005, Keiko became the family’s most viable electoral representative. She joined the Fujimorista alliance **Alianza por el Futuro** and ran for Congress in 2006.

### Congressional election

She received the largest preferential vote total of the 2006 congressional election and served from 27 July 2006 to 26 July 2011.

Her prominence immediately exceeded that of an ordinary first-term legislator. She functioned as:

- head of the Fujimori political family;
- advocate for her father during his extradition and trials;
- national spokesperson for the movement;
- prospective presidential candidate.

### Legislative work

Her stated areas of interest included women, children, family policy, youth, and penal questions. She participated in congressional commissions and sponsored or co-sponsored bills, but evaluations of her record differ.

**Documented criticism:** Congressional attendance and leave records were used by opponents to allege roughly 500 absences from plenary, commission, and other parliamentary activities. Part of this total consisted of formally authorized leave, including approximately 94 days for her 2011 presidential campaign. Reporting also found a relatively modest number of individual legislative initiatives early in her term.

**Defense and qualification:** Aggregate “absence” claims often combine different types of session, leave, representation activity, and excused absence. They establish extensive nonattendance but not that every absence was unauthorized.

### Leadership succession

Alberto Fujimori’s convictions and imprisonment made a direct return to office progressively less plausible. Keiko consolidated her position over older figures from Cambio 90, Nueva Mayoría, and Sí Cumple.

On 13 January 2008 she announced plans for a new vehicle, **Fuerza 2011**. It was formally registered in March 2010 after the JNE accepted 145,058 valid signatures from 210,007 submitted. The organization was renamed **Fuerza Popular** in 2012.

## First Presidential Campaign, 2011

Fujimori ran with Rafael Rey and Jaime Yoshiyama on the Fuerza 2011 ticket.

Her campaign combined:

- defense of the economic and counterinsurgency results attributed to her father;
- acknowledgment that “errors” and “excesses” had occurred;
- social programs and security policy;
- advocacy of a humanitarian solution or pardon for her father;
- conservative family policy;
- an effort to present herself as a democratic successor rather than a restoration of the 1990s regime.

She reached the runoff against Ollanta Humala. The contest polarized voters between fear of a Humala government and fear of restored Fujimorismo. Mario Vargas Llosa, a roster-external figure who had lost to Alberto Fujimori in 1990, supported Humala.

Official results gave Humala approximately 51.45% and Fujimori 48.55%. She accepted the result. The narrow defeat established her as the permanent leader of the most organized personalist party in Peru.

---

## Party Consolidation, 2011–2016

After the election, Fujimori traveled extensively within Peru to preserve local committees and electoral networks.

In 2012 she and her siblings requested a humanitarian pardon for Alberto Fujimori. President Humala rejected it in 2013 after a medical evaluation and political controversy.

Fujimori attempted to distinguish her project from the most authoritarian features of the 1990s while retaining the brand’s core electorate. The repositioning included:

- stronger public commitments to constitutional government;
- a pledge not to use presidential power for family benefit;
- a promise, interpreted as applying to her father, not to issue a politically motivated pardon;
- limited moderation on social policy, including personal support expressed in 2016 for same-sex civil unions and abortion when necessary to save a pregnant woman’s life.

This moderation was inconsistent over time and coexisted with alliances involving conservative Catholic and evangelical sectors.

## Second Presidential Campaign, 2016

### Campaign

Fujimori entered the campaign as the best-organized candidate. Fuerza Popular combined a disciplined national network with the symbolic color orange and a highly personalized leadership structure.

She signed a democratic commitment at Harvard University and during the campaign apologized for “errors” committed under her father’s government. She promised not to repeat the 1992 self-coup and not to use power to benefit her family.

### Election

In the first round, she won 39.86%, far ahead of Pedro Pablo Kuczynski’s 21.05%. In the runoff, however, antifujimorista mobilization and late support for Kuczynski from sectors of the left produced another very narrow defeat.

The OAS recorded:

- Kuczynski ticket: 8,596,937 votes, 50.12%;
- Fujimori ticket: 8,555,880 votes, 49.88%.

The difference was 41,057 votes. Fujimori questioned aspects of the campaign and result but ultimately conceded.

### Joaquín Ramírez/DEA claim

During the campaign, Univision and *Cuarto Poder* reported that a DEA informant claimed to have recorded Fuerza Popular secretary-general **Joaquín Ramírez** saying that he had laundered US$15 million allegedly provided by Keiko Fujimori.

Evidentiary distinctions:

- **Documented fact:** the DEA had examined or was investigating matters involving Ramírez and an alleged recording.
- **Allegation:** an informant attributed the US$15-million statement to Ramírez.
- **Denial:** Ramírez denied it and temporarily stepped aside.
- **Official clarification:** the DEA stated that Keiko Fujimori herself was not and had not been under DEA investigation.
- **Unresolved evidentiary weakness:** the supposed audio was not publicly produced in a form allowing independent authentication.
- **No established finding:** no US or Peruvian conviction established that Fujimori gave Ramírez US$15 million to launder.

The episode damaged the campaign and became part of the broader mythology of hidden Fujimorista money, but it must not be conflated with the separately documented Credicorp or alleged Odebrecht contributions.

## Fuerza Popular’s Congressional Majority and the Kuczynski Crisis, 2016–2018

Although Fujimori lost the presidency, Fuerza Popular won 73 of 130 congressional seats. She did not hold a parliamentary seat, but exercised decisive party leadership from outside Congress.

The majority censured or pressured ministers, resisted parts of Kuczynski’s program, and initiated efforts to remove him. Critics described this as obstruction or revenge for the electoral defeat. Fujimori’s party characterized it as legitimate opposition and anti-corruption oversight.

### Pardon and sibling split

In December 2017, a group led by Kenji Fujimori abstained from the first attempt to remove Kuczynski. Days later Kuczynski granted Alberto Fujimori a humanitarian pardon. The sequence generated an allegation that congressional votes had been exchanged for the pardon. Kuczynski denied an illicit bargain; the pardon was publicly justified on humanitarian grounds.

Keiko opposed Kenji’s independent faction, producing a direct struggle between the siblings over control of Fujimorismo.

### “Mamanivideos”

Congressman Moisés Mamani secretly recorded conversations in which Kenji Fujimori and allies discussed political support, public works, and access to the executive in connection with a second impeachment vote.

Fuerza Popular released the recordings in March 2018. They accelerated Kuczynski’s resignation.

Kenji accused Keiko and her party of criminal or dishonorable tactics in recording and editing the material. No public evidence established that Keiko personally made the recordings; the documented fact is that her party used them politically.

The episode produced the label “Keikovideos,” deliberately evoking Montesinos’s Vladivideos. That analogy became powerful political mythology, although the recording circumstances, participants, and demonstrated conduct were not identical.

## The Cócteles Campaign-Finance Case

### Origins

The investigation grew from discrepancies and suspicions surrounding reported fundraising events—especially “cocktails”—and campaign contributions for 2011 and 2016. Prosecutors alleged that large donations had been concealed through false contributors and fragmented deposits.

### Documented contributions and testimony

Several underlying financial relationships became public:

- **Dionisio Romero Paoletti/Credicorp:** Romero testified that Credicorp provided **US$3.65 million** to Fujimori’s 2011 campaign, largely in cash and with confidentiality requested because of the political climate. He said money was delivered to Fujimori and Jaime Yoshiyama. Fujimori’s defense characterized this as lawful private campaign support, not criminal proceeds.
- **Odebrecht:** former Odebrecht executives Marcelo Odebrecht and Jorge Barata gave testimony concerning support for Fujimori’s 2011 campaign. Barata attributed approximately **US$1.2 million** to the campaign. Fujimori denied personally soliciting or receiving Odebrecht money.
- **Other private businesses:** prosecutorial material referred to contributions or alleged contributions from additional business groups and individuals.

The existence of substantial unreported or confidential business support is better documented than the prosecution’s full legal theory. Whether each payment was criminal property, whether Fujimori knew its origin and reporting treatment, and whether the conduct fit Peru’s then-existing money-laundering statute became the central disputed questions.

### Prosecution theory

Prosecutor José Domingo Pérez alleged that:

- Fuerza 2011/Fuerza Popular operated as a criminal organization within or through a political party;
- illicit or concealed corporate funds were introduced into campaigns;
- false donors were recruited to make the money appear to be small legitimate contributions;
- cocktail-event revenue was used as an accounting explanation;
- party officials obstructed the investigation and pressured witnesses.

The prosecution eventually sought **30 years and 10 months’ imprisonment** for Fujimori on charges that included money laundering, organized crime, obstruction of justice, and false declarations or related offenses.

These were prosecutorial allegations, not convictions.

### Defense

Fujimori and attorney Giulliana Loza argued that:

- Fujimori did not receive Odebrecht money;
- private political donations were not prohibited in the relevant period merely because they were undisclosed or confidential;
- forensic work did not trace Odebrecht money into Fujimori’s personal assets;
- no unjustified personal enrichment was found;
- later amendments to the money-laundering and organized-crime statutes could not be applied retroactively;
- the investigation became politically driven and unreasonably prolonged.

### Preventive detention

On 10 October 2018 Fujimori was initially detained during the investigation and released after an appellate ruling. On 31 October 2018 Judge Richard Concepción Carhuancho ordered 36 months’ preventive detention, accepting arguments about obstruction risk and influence over party witnesses.

She was held at the women’s prison in Chorrillos.

**Legal meaning:** preventive detention was not a conviction. It was a pretrial measure based on judicial findings about evidentiary probability and procedural risk.

In November 2019, Peru’s Constitutional Tribunal, by a divided vote, granted habeas relief and ordered her release. A new 15-month preventive-detention order was imposed on 28 January 2020. An appellate court revoked it in April, and she left prison on 4 May 2020 under restrictions and bail conditions. The second detention lasted roughly three months.

During incarceration she experienced a documented hypertensive health episode requiring clinical treatment in September 2019. No reliable evidence supports claims of chronic addiction or a major psychiatric disorder.

### Trial and annulment

The oral trial opened in July 2024 against Fujimori and numerous co-defendants.

On 13 January 2025, the Third National Collegiate Criminal Court annulled the trial and returned the case to the pretrial stage, applying a Constitutional Tribunal precedent involving co-defendant José Chlimper and defects in the accusation.

Prosecutors filed a new accusation in July 2025.

In October 2025, the Constitutional Tribunal decided Fujimori’s habeas-corpus case by a 5–2 majority. It nullified the rulings that had rejected her legal objections and set aside the preceding proceedings, including the July 2025 accusation.

The majority reasoned in substance that:

- the prosecution’s “reception of assets” theory relied on a form of money laundering introduced only in November 2016, after the campaign conduct;
- applying it to earlier conduct would violate the prohibition on retroactive criminal punishment;
- the charged political aim of winning elections did not satisfy the legally required criminal-purpose element of organized crime;
- repeated defective accusations and prolonged proceedings infringed the right to adjudication within a reasonable time.

Constitutional Tribunal president Luz Pacheco and magistrate Manuel Monteagudo dissented. The decision was therefore authoritative but contested inside the court itself.

The national criminal court subsequently ordered the **definitive dismissal** of the proceedings against Fujimori pursuant to the Constitutional Tribunal judgment.

### Final evidentiary classification

- **Documented facts:** major business figures provided or acknowledged large campaign contributions; the reported financing structure did not initially disclose the full reality of all support; prosecutors amassed witness and accounting evidence; Fujimori spent substantial time in preventive detention.
- **Allegations never adjudicated by conviction:** that Fujimori directed laundering through false donors and led a criminal organization.
- **Procedural/judicial outcome:** the prosecution and trial were nullified; the case against her was definitively dismissed.
- **Not an acquittal after full evidentiary trial:** the final outcome rested primarily on legality, statutory timing, accusatory defects, and constitutional due-process grounds.
- **Current legal status:** no Cócteles conviction; the dismissed allegations should not be presented as established crimes.

## Third Presidential Campaign and the 2021 Electoral Crisis

### First round

Fujimori entered the April 2021 election with high negative ratings and under continuing criminal investigation. She nevertheless reached the runoff with approximately 13.4% of valid votes, behind Pedro Castillo.

The geographical divide was pronounced:

- Fujimori performed strongly in Lima, the northern coast, Tumbes, Piura, Lambayeque, and Ucayali;
- Castillo dominated much of the rural Andes and southern Peru.

### Ideological repositioning

Against Castillo, Fujimori presented herself as the defender of:

- the 1993 Constitution;
- private investment and market economics;
- political pluralism against Marxism or “communism”;
- public order;
- continuity of macroeconomic institutions.

She received support from former opponents, including Mario Vargas Llosa, who had supported candidates against her in 2011 and 2016.

Fujimori also pledged to pardon her father if elected, reversing or qualifying the more restrictive position adopted during the 2016 campaign.

### Result

Official results gave:

- Pedro Castillo: **50.126%**;
- Keiko Fujimori: **49.874%**.

The difference was approximately 44,000 votes.

### Fraud allegations

Fujimori and Fuerza Popular alleged a “systematic fraud at the polling table,” including forged signatures, irregular tally sheets, and coordinated manipulation in rural districts. The party submitted 945 annulment requests seeking to invalidate roughly 200,000 votes; most were filed after the applicable deadline.

Evidentiary status:

- **Documented fact:** isolated electoral errors and disputed signatures were alleged and investigated.
- **Unsubstantiated broader claim:** no persuasive evidence demonstrated a coordinated national fraud capable of reversing the result.
- **Official and international finding:** the JNE confirmed Castillo’s victory. OAS and European Union observers described the process as legitimate and found no serious irregularities undermining the result.
- **Political effect:** the campaign delayed recognition, generated demonstrations, intensified urban–rural polarization, and exposed electoral officials and prosecutors to threats and intimidation.
- **Fujimori’s final position:** she said she would accept the authorities’ proclamation while continuing to describe the election as illegitimate or affected by irregularities.

The phrase “fraude en mesa” became both a partisan belief and a subject of academic study in electoral disinformation. It remains a major element of post-2021 Fujimorista mythology, but it is not supported by the official count or observation missions.

## Political Activity, 2021–2025

Fujimori retained control of Fuerza Popular, whose 24 seats made it the second-largest bloc in the 2021 Congress.

### Castillo’s presidency and fall

Fuerza Popular opposed Castillo and supported efforts to remove him. On 7 December 2022 Castillo announced the dissolution of Congress and emergency rule; state institutions rejected the measure, Congress removed him, and Vice-President Dina Boluarte assumed the presidency.

Fujimori supported the constitutional succession and called for order.

### Relationship with Dina Boluarte

Fujimori met Boluarte at Government Palace in February 2023. Fuerza Popular spokesperson Miguel Torres stated that the party would support the government so long as it did not make ideological “turns.”

The relationship was not a formal coalition. It nevertheless operated as a congressional support arrangement for much of Boluarte’s tenure. Academic and investigative accounts identify Fuerza Popular, Alianza para el Progreso, Renovación Popular, and at times Perú Libre as components of shifting legislative blocs that:

- preserved Boluarte in office;
- changed organized-crime and judicial rules;
- weakened or constrained prosecutors and oversight institutions;
- supported limitations on accountability for pre-2003 conflict-era crimes;
- negotiated control over public institutions.

Critics called this a “pact of impunity,” “fujicerronismo,” or an informal co-government. These are analytical and polemical descriptions, not the legal name of an organization.

Fuerza Popular argued that maintaining the constitutional presidential term prevented further instability and that many legal changes corrected prosecutorial overreach.

### Human-rights accountability legislation

In 2024, Congress passed legislation imposing limitation rules on crimes against humanity and war crimes committed before Peru adopted relevant international instruments. Fuerza Popular supported measures of this kind.

International human-rights bodies and UN experts argued that such measures violated Peru’s international obligations and promoted impunity. Supporters framed them as enforcement of legality and non-retroactivity. The legislation had direct symbolic relevance to Alberto Fujimori’s legacy and to military defendants from the internal conflict.

### Death of Alberto Fujimori

Alberto Fujimori died on 11 September 2024 after cancer and other health problems. Keiko, Hiro, Sachi, and Kenji jointly announced the death.

The government declared three days of mourning. A large public wake and burial demonstrated that Alberto retained both a mass following and intense opposition.

Keiko’s funeral address portrayed him as released from “hatred and vengeance.” Supporters celebrated him as the leader who defeated terrorism and restored the economy; opponents emphasized his convictions and authoritarian government. His death strengthened Keiko’s undisputed position as the principal custodian of the family movement.

### End of the Boluarte period

By 2025, escalating insecurity and political exhaustion weakened Boluarte. Fuerza Popular eventually withdrew its support as Congress removed her in October 2025. Fujimorismo’s earlier role in sustaining her remained a central criticism of Keiko’s 2026 campaign.

## Fourth Presidential Campaign and Election, 2025–2026

### Candidacy

Following the death of her father and the collapse of the Cócteles prosecution, Fujimori undertook a fourth presidential campaign.

Her ticket consisted of:

- Keiko Fujimori for president;
- **Luis Galarreta** for first vice-president;
- **Miguel Torres** for second vice-president.

### First round

On 12 April 2026, Peru held presidential and restored bicameral legislative elections amid logistical failures that required voting to continue in some places.

With the count complete:

- Fujimori led with **17.18%**;
- Roberto Sánchez of Juntos por el Perú received **12.03%**;
- Rafael López Aliaga finished narrowly behind Sánchez.

The OAS documented logistical problems and extensive disinformation but did not endorse allegations that the count itself was fraudulent.

### Campaign platform

Fujimori made crime and extortion the central issues. Proposals included:

- a national pacification plan;
- combined police and military security operations;
- military and police presence on metropolitan buses;
- financial disruption of extortion networks;
- expulsion of undocumented migrants who commit crimes;
- compulsory or expanded prison labor;
- construction of a high-security megaprison modeled partly on El Salvador’s CECOT;
- anonymous or identity-protected judges in dangerous organized-crime cases;
- infrastructure through public–private partnerships;
- preservation of market institutions;
- higher social-assistance benefits.

The campaign drew openly on Salvadoran President Nayib Bukele’s “mano dura” security model, while also presenting Fujimori as a predictable institutional conservative after a decade of presidential instability.

### Runoff and dispute

The runoff was held on 7 June 2026.

Final certified results were:

- **Keiko Fujimori/Fuerza Popular:** 9,223,396 votes;
- **Roberto Sánchez/Juntos por el Perú:** 9,173,755 votes.

Fujimori’s margin was 49,641 votes, approximately 50.135% to 49.865%.

Sánchez disputed overseas-voting procedures and said he would not recognize a government produced under what he called altered rules. Electoral authorities denied fraud. Unlike Fujimori’s failed 2021 challenge, the 2026 dispute was directed against the result benefiting her.

The JNE proclaimed Fujimori president-elect on 3 July 2026 and later issued her credentials. She became the first woman elected president of Peru.

## Presidency, 28 July 2026–Present

### Inauguration

Fujimori took office on 28 July 2026, swearing before Congress “by God, the fatherland and the Holy Gospels.” Demonstrations by supporters and opponents accompanied the inauguration.

Fuerza Popular and its principal ally Renovación Popular held half of the Senate’s seats, giving the new government a much stronger legislative position than most recent Peruvian administrations.

### Initial commitments

Her inaugural program included:

- reducing violent crime and extortion;
- temporarily placing military forces in leadership roles in the most crime-affected areas;
- creating or expanding high-security prison capacity;
- restructuring social services;
- addressing rural malnutrition;
- increasing the minimum wage by 15%;
- preparing for El Niño flooding;
- accelerating infrastructure investment;
- using artificial intelligence and intelligence analysis against organized crime;
- increasing Pensión 65 benefits.

### Cabinet and early governance

Luis Galarreta became prime minister. His presentation to the restored bicameral Congress emphasized security, prisons, military support, and state reorganization.

The government launched **Plan Escudo**, reportedly deploying more than 4,000 police and military personnel against transport-sector extortion. During the first month, continuing killings and extortion meant that no clear security turnaround had yet been demonstrated.

### Request for delegated legislative powers

At the end of August 2026, the government asked Congress for authority to legislate by decree for 120 days across 66 areas.

Proposed subjects included:

- treating some criminal gangs as terrorist organizations;
- expanding military participation in internal security and prisons;
- creating a presumption of legitimate defense in certain confrontations;
- restoring operational capacity to the national intelligence service;
- reducing administrative barriers;
- changing environmental regulation;
- removing caps on formal-sector interest rates;
- labor, tax, financial, and investment policy.

The breadth of the request generated criticism that powers advertised for emergency security policy extended deeply into unrelated economic and administrative fields. Congress had not completed its examination by the research cutoff.

### Memory policy and the LUM

Members of the government opened debate over the **Lugar de la Memoria, la Tolerancia y la Inclusión Social (LUM)**, the museum and memorial devoted to the 1980–2000 internal conflict.

Justice Minister Ernesto Álvarez and others proposed changing its administration or interpretation. Culture Minister Alberto Beingolea said it would not be closed but suggested changes because it had become a point of division.

Critics feared official revision of the record of state abuses and Alberto Fujimori’s government. Government-aligned voices argued that existing narratives insufficiently credited the armed forces and the defeat of terrorism. As of 3 September 2026, this remained a political and historiographical dispute rather than a completed institutional transformation.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Fujimorismo

Keiko’s ideology is best understood as an evolving form of Fujimorismo rather than a fixed doctrinal system.

Its recurring elements are:

- strong executive authority;
- social conservatism;
- market economics and private investment;
- targeted welfare programs;
- security-centered governance;
- support for police and armed forces;
- hostility to Sendero Luminoso, Marxism, and radical-left movements;
- personalist leadership and reliance on the Fujimori name;
- defense of the 1993 constitutional and economic settlement.

### Distancing and return

Her political rhetoric moved through several stages:

1. **2000s:** emphatic defense of Alberto Fujimori.
2. **2011:** selective defense combined with acknowledgment of errors.
3. **2016:** explicit democratic pledges, apology for excesses, and promise not to use office for family benefit.
4. **2021:** renewed defense of her father and promise to pardon him.
5. **2024–2026:** presentation of Alberto’s legacy as a model of order and pacification while promising reconciliation.
6. **Presidency:** emphasis on “iron fist” security, executive delegation, and reinterpretation of the internal-conflict narrative.

### Religion and social policy

Fujimori invokes God and Catholic forms in public ceremonies and political speech. Cardinal Juan Luis Cipriani officiated at her wedding, but evidence does not establish formal membership in Opus Dei.

Her party has collaborated with Catholic and evangelical conservative movements. Her personal statements have occasionally been more moderate than her party’s alliances, particularly her 2016 expressions of support for civil unions and therapeutic abortion. She has not become an advocate of same-sex marriage or elective abortion.

### Economics

Her economic outlook is conventionally market-oriented:

- defense of the autonomous central bank and private property;
- mining and infrastructure investment;
- public–private partnerships;
- targeted social transfers rather than wholesale nationalization;
- opposition to radical constitutional economic change.

Her 2026 government’s request to remove interest-rate caps and reduce environmental and administrative barriers illustrates the continuing liberal-business component.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Alberto Fujimori

Father, principal formative influence, political brand, and continuing object of her campaigns. Keiko was at different moments defender, dynastic successor, pardon advocate, caregiver, and curator of his legacy.

### Susana Higuchi

Mother and early source of family conflict. Keiko’s acceptance of the first-lady role after Higuchi’s expulsion became a defining reputational controversy. They later reconciled politically and personally.

### Kenji Fujimori

Brother, party colleague, rival, and later partial reconciled family ally. Their 2017–2018 conflict divided Fuerza Popular and revolved around Alberto Fujimori’s pardon and congressional control.

### Mark Vito Villanella

Husband from 2004 until their 2022 separation, father of her children, public campaign companion, and co-investigated figure in the campaign-finance proceedings.

### Jaime Yoshiyama

Former minister under Alberto Fujimori, campaign strategist and senior organizer. Prosecutors identified him as a key intermediary in 2011 financing. His relationship with Keiko became central to the Cócteles evidentiary narrative.

### José Chlimper

Businessman, 2016 vice-presidential candidate, party secretary-general, and co-defendant whose Constitutional Tribunal litigation contributed to annulment of the Cócteles trial.

### Dionisio Romero Paoletti

Banker and major campaign financier. His acknowledgment of US$3.65 million in support was one of the most consequential revelations of the financing investigation.

### Jorge Barata and Marcelo Odebrecht

Odebrecht executives whose testimony supplied the prosecution’s claim that the construction company financed the 2011 campaign.

### Giulliana Loza

Fujimori’s principal defense attorney in the Cócteles litigation and successful constitutional challenge.

### José Domingo Pérez

Lead prosecutor and one of Fujimori’s most consequential institutional adversaries. He constructed the organized-crime and money-laundering prosecution. Fujimoristas accused him of political persecution; supporters of the case portrayed attacks on him as efforts to disable anti-corruption enforcement.

### Pedro Pablo Kuczynski

Opponent in the 2016 runoff. His presidency was constrained by Fujimori’s congressional majority and ended during the Mamanivideos crisis.

### Pedro Castillo

Opponent in 2021. Their polarized runoff and the post-election fraud campaign shaped Peru’s subsequent democratic crisis.

### Dina Boluarte

Initially sustained by a congressional bloc in which Fuerza Popular was important; later abandoned by Fujimorismo before her removal. The relationship generated claims that Fujimori exercised power without formal executive responsibility.

### Luis Galarreta and Miguel Torres

Longtime party associates and members of the 2026 presidential ticket. Galarreta became prime minister; Torres became second vice-president.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “She tortured or ordered the torture of her mother”

- **Story:** Keiko personally participated in, ordered, or knowingly enabled Susana Higuchi’s alleged torture.
- **Origin/circulation:** anti-Fujimorista political rhetoric derived from Higuchi’s allegations and Keiko’s replacement of her as first lady.
- **Evidence:** congressional inquiries found indications that Higuchi was confined and abused by state agents and that Alberto Fujimori bore possible responsibility. No cited judicial or congressional record establishes that Keiko ordered or personally participated.
- **Assessment:** unsupported extension of a documented family and state-abuse controversy.
- **Reputational effect:** perhaps the most powerful hostile story about her character, symbolizing dynastic loyalty over maternal loyalty.

### “Her education was paid entirely by Montesinos with stolen intelligence money”

- **Story:** Montesinos directly financed Keiko’s US education from public intelligence funds.
- **Origin:** testimony by Montesinos, Matilde Pinchi Pinchi, and subsequent journalism.
- **Evidence for:** insider testimony, scale of expenses, allegations of cash delivery and offshore channels.
- **Evidence against/limitations:** contested family financial explanations; inability of prosecutors to prove the case; archival of the investigation.
- **Assessment:** serious but unproven allegation, not a court-established fact.

### “Keiko is only a puppet or placeholder for Alberto”

- **Story:** her candidacies existed solely to restore her father or pardon him.
- **Evidence for:** repeated defense of Alberto, pardon advocacy, common party personnel and symbols.
- **Evidence against:** she built an independent organization, defeated rival Fujimorista factions, clashed with Kenji over strategy, and maintained leadership after Alberto’s death.
- **Assessment:** a political metaphor that understates her independent organizational power.

### “Keiko never worked”

- **Story:** she has never held genuine employment outside inherited politics.
- **Evidence:** her record is overwhelmingly political and educational; critics correctly note limited sustained private employment.
- **Qualification:** she held formal first-lady institutional duties, served five years in Congress, led a national party, and has some reported US private-sector experience.
- **Assessment:** rhetorically effective but literally overstated.

### “Cócteles proved that she was a convicted money launderer”

- **Story:** the campaign-finance investigation established guilt.
- **Evidence:** donor testimony and suspected false-donor structures provided substantial grounds for investigation.
- **Against:** she was never convicted. The trial was annulled, and the case was definitively dismissed following a Constitutional Tribunal decision.
- **Assessment:** false if stated as a conviction or final judicial finding.

### “The Cócteles dismissal proved every donation was transparent and lawful”

- **Story:** constitutional dismissal established that all underlying financing was accurately reported and ethically unproblematic.
- **Evidence:** the court found decisive defects involving legality, retroactivity, statutory elements, and delay.
- **Limitation:** the proceeding did not conclude with a full merits trial resolving every disputed factual payment.
- **Assessment:** broader than the judgment supports.

### “Keiko won the 2021 election”

- **Story:** hidden fraud deprived her of victory.
- **Origin:** Fuerza Popular’s “fraude en mesa” campaign.
- **Evidence claimed:** disputed signatures, unusual rural vote patterns, and tally-sheet challenges.
- **Evidence against:** official count, rejection of annulment petitions, and OAS/EU observation finding no serious or outcome-changing irregularity.
- **Assessment:** partisan belief unsupported by the best available electoral evidence.

### “No one can beat antifujimorismo”

Before 2026, an electoral saying held that virtually any runoff candidate—or even an inanimate object—could defeat Keiko because antifujimorismo would unite against her. Her three narrow losses supported the narrative. Her 2026 victory disproved its absolute form but not the continued existence of a large anti-Fujimorista electorate: she won by fewer than 50,000 votes.

### “Savior’s daughter” versus “dictator’s daughter”

These paired myths reduce her biography to Alberto Fujimori’s polarized reputation.

- Supporters cast her as heir to economic rescue, the defeat of terrorism, rural infrastructure, and social order.
- Opponents cast her as heir to dictatorship, death squads, forced sterilization, intelligence corruption, and institutional capture.
- Both narratives are politically consequential.
- Neither substitutes for examining Keiko’s own acts, including party construction, congressional strategy, campaign financing, election challenges, and presidential decisions.

## Successes and Failures

### Significant successes

- Became first lady at nineteen and developed a national public profile.
- Won the largest preferential congressional vote in 2006.
- Reassembled fragmented Fujimorista groups into Fuerza 2011/Fuerza Popular.
- Built one of Peru’s few durable national party organizations.
- Reached four consecutive presidential runoffs.
- Won large congressional blocs in 2016 and 2021.
- Maintained party leadership through imprisonment, prosecution, family conflict, and three electoral defeats.
- Secured annulment and dismissal of the long-running Cócteles prosecution.
- Won the 2026 presidency and became Peru’s first woman elected head of state.

### Significant failures

- Lost the 2011 runoff despite a strong inherited movement.
- Lost the 2016 runoff after winning the first round by a large margin.
- Her party’s conduct during the 2016–2018 Congress helped produce public perceptions of obstruction and contributed to its later electoral decline.
- Lost control of part of her congressional bloc during the Kenji split.
- Spent approximately thirteen months in preventive detention across two periods.
- Lost the 2021 election and promoted a fraud narrative rejected by electoral authorities and international observers.
- Entered the presidency with nearly half the electorate opposed to her and with an unresolved national conflict over the meaning of her family’s rule.
- Her first month in office did not produce the rapid security improvement promised during the campaign.

## Present Status

Keiko Fujimori is alive and serving as president of Peru for the 2026–2031 constitutional term.

As of 3 September 2026:

- no final criminal conviction stands against her;
- the Cócteles case against her has been definitively dismissed following the Constitutional Tribunal’s 2025 judgment;
- her government is in its opening phase;
- crime and extortion are the principal stated policy priorities;
- Congress is considering an extensive request for delegated legislative powers;
- disputes over military power, intelligence, accountability, and the public memory of the 1980–2000 conflict remain active.

It is too early to assess the long-term results of her presidency.

## Reputation and Historical Legacy to Date

Fujimori occupies several overlapping historical identities:

- daughter and former first lady of an authoritarian president;
- leader who preserved Peru’s most durable post-2000 personalist party;
- three-time defeated presidential candidate who won on her fourth attempt;
- symbol of market continuity and public order for supporters;
- symbol of authoritarian inheritance and institutional obstruction for opponents;
- former preventive detainee whose prosecution ended through a constitutional legality ruling;
- first woman elected president of Peru.

Her reputation is inseparable from the dual memory of Alberto Fujimori: economic stabilization and defeat of insurgent organizations on one side; self-coup, corruption, and human-rights convictions on the other.

Her independent historical record includes the creation of Fuerza Popular, command of congressional majorities, participation in repeated presidential crises, the unsuccessful 2021 election challenge, survival of the Cócteles prosecution, and her own exercise of executive power beginning in 2026.
