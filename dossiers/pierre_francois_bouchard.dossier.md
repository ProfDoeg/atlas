# Pierre-François-Xavier Bouchard: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

This dossier reconstructs the life of Pierre-François-Xavier Bouchard from parish and civil-register evidence, military records as quoted or summarized by later scholars, his surviving military narratives, contemporary reports from the French expedition in Egypt, museum records, institutional histories, and modern research.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a contemporary document, official register, personnel record, or surviving work.
- **Reported fact:** asserted by a credible secondary source but not independently verified here in the underlying document.
- **Inference:** a reasoned reconstruction not directly stated in the surviving evidence.
- **Disputed claim:** materially conflicting versions exist.
- **Rumor or myth:** a circulating story unsupported by adequate contemporary evidence.

The surviving record is heavily weighted toward Bouchard’s military service. Little is documented about his private beliefs, domestic life, childhood personality, property, or intellectual interests outside engineering and military science.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name:** Pierre François Xavier Bouchard, conventionally hyphenated as **Pierre-François-Xavier Bouchard**.

**Common shorter forms:** Pierre Bouchard; Pierre-François Bouchard; François-Xavier Bouchard.

**Titles and ranks:** sergeant-major; lieutenant in the military aerostatic service; lieutenant of engineers, second and then first class; captain of engineers, second and then first class; chef de bataillon, approximately equivalent to major or commandant; chief military engineer at Givet.

**Profession:** French soldier, military engineer, mathematics teacher, aerostatic officer, fortifications specialist, and temporary member of the Commission des sciences et des arts attached to the Egyptian expedition.

**Known for:** recognizing, preserving, reporting, and transporting the inscribed fragment now called the Rosetta Stone after it was uncovered during engineering work at Fort Julien near Rashid in July 1799.

**Birth:** 29 April 1771, Orgelet, Jura, France.

**Death:** 5 August 1822, Givet, Ardennes, France, aged 51.

**Cause of death:** described in the documentary tradition as the result of a “long and painful illness.” Later biographers connect his chronically poor health to yellow fever contracted during the Saint-Domingue expedition, but the immediate medical cause is not specified in the consulted death-register references.

**Physical description:** Bouchard’s École polytechnique record describes him as approximately 1.78 metres tall, with brown eyes and hair, an uncovered or receding forehead, long nose, small mouth, round chin, and long face. No authenticated portrait is known. The Musée du Génie and local researchers have looked unsuccessfully for one.

### Correcting the dates 1772–1832

**Documented fact:** the Orgelet parish record supports 29 April **1771**, and the Givet civil register supports 5 August **1822**.

A significant body of older and derivative literature gives 1772, 1832, or both. Jean Leclant traced the 1772 date to École polytechnique matriculation file no. 490. He concluded that the age was probably reduced because Bouchard, actually 25 in 1796, exceeded the nominal admission limit of 20. Some older publications also carried an erroneous death year of 1832. Neither error should be treated as a viable alternative to the register evidence.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Father:** Pierre Bouchard, from a family of carpenters associated with Montureux-lès-Gray in Haute-Saône. His occupations are variously recorded as master carpenter, merchant or dealer, and later schoolteacher.

**Mother:** Pierrette Janet or Jeannet, from Cressia in the Jura.

The parents married at Orgelet on 25 November 1756.

### Siblings

Local parish-register research identifies seven children, four daughters and three sons, with Pierre-François-Xavier the youngest:

1. Marie-Claudine Bouchard, born 15 November 1756; later Pierre’s godmother.
2. Jean-Pierre-Alexis Bouchard, born 19 June 1759; died 31 August 1761.
3. Jeanne-Pierrette Bouchard, born 27 March 1762.
4. Marie-Jacqueline Bouchard, born 20 April 1764; died 7 May 1765.
5. Marie-Josèphe Bouchard, born 8 July 1766; died 29 November 1767.
6. Claude-François-Hyacinthe Bouchard, born 4 October 1768.
7. Pierre-François-Xavier Bouchard, born 29 April 1771.

The deaths of three siblings in infancy or early childhood belong to the documented family environment in which he grew up. No substantial correspondence or later relationship with his surviving siblings has been located in the consulted sources.

### Marriage

On 23 April 1798—four days after his assignment to the Egyptian expedition—Bouchard married **Marie-Élisabeth Bergère**, a Meudon woman approximately five years younger than he was.

Bergère reportedly managed or worked in the workshop that sewed the fabric envelopes used for balloons at the École nationale d’aérostatique. Their association therefore probably began in Bouchard’s Meudon aerostatic period.

She accompanied him, at the couple’s own expense, on the expedition to Saint-Domingue. Both reportedly contracted yellow fever.

### Children

The couple had two known children:

- A daughter, born in or about 1802. Her name has not been established from the sources consulted.
- A son, born during or shortly after the family’s residence at Napoléon-Vendée, approximately 1806–1807. He was considered academically promising but died aged about thirteen, shortly before his father’s death.

Only the widow and daughter were described as surviving Bouchard in the military pension documentation summarized by Leclant.

### Household finances after his death

Bouchard died after 29 years and one month of recognized service, eleven months short of the 30 years then required for his widow’s ordinary pension entitlement. Marie-Élisabeth petitioned on the basis of his “eminent services.” After intervention by supporters and review by the Conseil d’État and military authorities, she received a pension of 450 francs in April 1824.

## Childhood and Early Life

Bouchard grew up in Orgelet, a small Jurassian town, in a household rooted in skilled craftsmanship, small commerce, and elementary teaching. This was a modest background, but the claim occasionally made that he was born in outright poverty is not documented.

His father’s succession of trades suggests a household with artisanal skills and some educational capital. Bouchard’s attendance at secondary institutions also indicates access to more education than the poorest families normally possessed.

No childhood diaries, correspondence, medical records, descriptions of religious upbringing, or early personal recollections are known from the sources consulted.

## Education and Formation

### Orgelet and Besançon

Bouchard studied at the collège of Orgelet through the rhetoric class. He then spent two years at the collège of Besançon studying philosophy and mathematics.

This combination supplied the mathematical foundation required for aerostatic work, surveying, descriptive geometry, and military engineering.

### Revolutionary military formation

During the levée en masse, he entered military service in 1793 as a sergeant-major in a battalion of Paris grenadiers. He took part in the Champagne and Belgian campaigns.

On 12 August 1794, 25 Thermidor Year II, he was requisitioned for the newly organized second company of military aerostiers at Meudon. The requisition is referenced in the French National Archives’ executive-power series.

### École nationale d’aérostatique, Meudon

At Meudon, Bouchard served under inventor and engineer **Nicolas-Jacques Conté**. He became a lieutenant in 1795, deputy director, and professor of mathematics at the École nationale d’aérostatique at Chalais-Meudon.

Conté was a decisive mentor. His work combined practical mechanics, chemistry, manufacture, drawing, and military application. Bouchard’s later ability to identify a technologically and philologically important object within a construction site fits this training in attentive technical observation, although that connection is an inference rather than a documented self-assessment.

### Hydrogen explosion and eye injury

In June 1795, Bouchard assisted Conté in experiments intended to produce hydrogen for observation balloons. A large glass vessel containing hydrogen exploded.

- Conté lost an eye.
- Bouchard was seriously struck and retained substantially weakened vision in his right eye.

This is the most clearly documented major injury of Bouchard’s early life.

### École polytechnique

Bouchard entered the École polytechnique in October or November 1796, commonly assigned to the promotion of 1796. Leclant gives 22 October, while local accounts sometimes give 21 November.

He was ranked 54th at admission. He studied descriptive geometry under **Gaspard Monge** and learned fortification science.

Conté’s esteem for Bouchard, and Conté’s relationship with chemist and Polytechnique founder **Claude-Louis Berthollet**, probably assisted his admission. This is a strong scholarly inference, not an explicit statement in an admissions decision.

His recorded age appears to have been reduced by a year, probably because he exceeded the nominal age limit.

His education was interrupted on 20 April 1798 when the minister of war requisitioned him for the Egyptian expedition. He later completed his exit examination in Egypt before a board presided over by Monge and became a second-class lieutenant of engineers on 28 November 1798.

## Early Career: Soldier and Aerostier, 1793–1798

### 1793–1794: infantry service

Bouchard served as a sergeant-major with grenadiers in Champagne and Belgium during the First Coalition wars.

No evidence has been found that he held an independent political office or belonged to a Revolutionary faction. His participation in the mass levy and republican army documents military service, not a particular ideological program.

### 1794–1797: military aerostation

Military balloons were an experimental reconnaissance technology. Bouchard’s responsibilities at Meudon included mathematical instruction and assistance with technical experimentation. His period there placed him within a network connecting:

- Nicolas-Jacques Conté;
- Claude-Louis Berthollet;
- Gaspard Monge;
- the École polytechnique;
- the French military engineering establishment.

No patent or independent aerostatic invention by Bouchard has been identified. Descriptions calling him “the inventor of aerostation” are incorrect. He was an early military aerostier and instructor working within Conté’s program.

## Chronological Life History

## The Egyptian Expedition, 1798–1801

### Departure and scientific assignment

Bouchard embarked at Toulon aboard the *Franklin* on 19 May 1798. The expedition seized Malta in June and landed near Alexandria at the beginning of July.

He was attached to the Commission des sciences et des arts and placed under Conté in the group commonly called the *artistes mécaniciens*. His assigned work included examining Egyptian technologies, crafts, and trades.

Contemporary classifications were not entirely consistent: one later list placed him among unclassified Commission members; the notebooks of the expedition artist Henri-Joseph Redouté listed him among the surveyors or geometers.

### Alexandria, Cairo, and Lake Manzala

Bouchard remained around Alexandria for approximately two months. He left for Cairo on 7 September 1798, following Conté, but stayed only briefly.

On 3 October he joined General Antoine-François Andréossy’s approximately forty-day reconnaissance of Lake Manzala and its roadstead. The party included geographical engineers, a Ponts et Chaussées officer, and another Polytechnique student.

The mission combined military surveying, hydrography, geography, and reconnaissance. Bouchard’s later service statement reported fighting local forces associated with Hassan Toubar, an influential regional figure allied with Ibrahim Bey.

He returned for his Polytechnique examination and entered the Corps du génie on 28 November 1798.

### Assignment to Fort Julien

The exact date of Bouchard’s assignment to Fort Julien is not preserved clearly in his personnel file.

On 31 October 1798 Bonaparte had ordered the rehabilitation of an older fortification at Rashid, on the western branch of the Nile, as part of the defense of the river mouth. The fort was called Fort Julien or Jullien by the French.

**Documented framework:** engineering responsibility belonged to chef de bataillon Charles-Marie-Benjamin d’Hautpoul. Bouchard assisted him as the engineer lieutenant on site.

**Probable reconstruction:** Bouchard was sent there soon after joining the engineers. The language of later accounts that he personally designed or independently commanded the entire reconstruction overstates what can be demonstrated.

## The Rosetta Stone Discovery

### What was found

The object is a broken part of a granodiorite stela bearing a priestly decree issued in 196 BCE under Ptolemy V Epiphanes. The surviving fragment carries:

- 14 incomplete lines of hieroglyphic text;
- 32 lines of Demotic;
- 54 lines of Greek.

British Museum measurements are approximately 112.3 centimetres high, 75.7 centimetres wide, and 28.4 centimetres thick. Its mass is commonly estimated at about 760 kilograms.

The contemporary French report called it black granite. Other literature long called it basalt. Modern geological identification is granodiorite.

### Discovery date

**Disputed claim:** 15 July and 19 July 1799 are both widely repeated as exact dates.

**Best-supported conclusion:** the precise day is not securely established. Jean Leclant concluded that the find probably occurred between 14 and 25 July 1799. A later reconstruction favors approximately 15 July but does not supply a decisive contemporary discovery record for that particular day.

Confusion partly arose from incorrectly dated proceedings of the Institut d’Égypte. The institute heard Michel-Ange Lancret’s letter reporting Bouchard’s discovery at a meeting later reconstructed as 29 July.

### Who physically uncovered it?

The earliest published report, in *Courier de l’Égypte* no. 37, states impersonally that the stone “was found” during fortification excavations ordered by d’Hautpoul. It then identifies Bouchard as the officer directing the works and transporting the stone.

Three levels of attribution should therefore be distinguished:

1. **Documented fact:** laborers, soldiers, or workmen engaged in Bouchard’s engineering operation physically exposed the block during excavation or demolition.
2. **Documented fact:** Bouchard noticed or inspected the inscriptions, recognized the object’s potential importance, preserved it, reported it, and escorted it to Cairo.
3. **Conventional attribution:** museums and biographies consequently call Bouchard the “discoverer” or excavator.

The individual worker who first struck or saw the stone was not named in the earliest known account. Modern claims that an anonymous Egyptian laborer, rather than a French soldier, made the physical find are plausible but not established by an identified contemporary witness in the sources consulted.

### Bouchard’s recognition of its importance

The inscriptions appeared in three horizontal sections, one visibly Greek and another hieroglyphic. The middle script was initially misidentified by the French report as possibly Syriac; it was later recognized as Demotic.

Bouchard and his superiors understood that the parallel scripts might allow comparison. The contemporary report declared that the stone might finally provide “the key” to hieroglyphic writing.

This immediate recognition—not the mere act of striking buried masonry—is Bouchard’s securely documented historical contribution.

### D’Hautpoul, Jullien, Menou, and Lancret

The discovery was institutional rather than solitary:

- **D’Hautpoul** commanded the engineering work.
- **Bouchard** supervised on site, recognized the fragment, protected it, and transported it.
- **Adjudant-General Joseph-Louis-Victor Jullien**, then commanding Rashid, was probably the first senior local commander informed and may have ordered the transfer to Cairo.
- **Michel-Ange Lancret**, a Polytechnique-trained engineer and Institut d’Égypte member present at Rashid, wrote to the institute announcing the find.
- **General Jacques-François Menou** arranged or sponsored an early partial translation of the Greek and later possessed or controlled the stone at Alexandria.

Laurent Jullien’s modern reconstruction argues that later narratives improperly displaced Joseph-Louis-Victor Jullien with Menou in the earliest chain of custody. This is a source-based historical argument, not a court-like determination. The contemporary command structure makes Jullien’s early involvement probable.

### Transport and reproduction

Bouchard escorted the stone to Cairo, where it arrived at Boulaq around mid-August. The Institut d’Égypte made ink impressions, engravings, and sulfur casts. These reproductions enabled scholars in Europe to work on the text without possessing the original.

*Courier de l’Égypte* no. 37 publicly reported the discovery on 15 September 1799. French newspapers subsequently repeated the news. Bonaparte told the Institut national in Paris on 27 October that the stone would be brought to France.

### Did Bouchard decipher the stone?

No. He recognized its promise but did not decipher the scripts.

Silvestre de Sacy, Johan David Åkerblad, Thomas Young, Jean-Joseph Marcel, Antoine-Isaac Silvestre de Sacy, and ultimately Jean-François Champollion belong to the later history of analysis. Champollion announced his phonetic breakthrough in September 1822, several weeks after Bouchard’s death.

No evidence shows that Bouchard corresponded with Champollion, met him, or participated in the decipherment.

## El-Arish, Captivity, and the Cairo Fighting, 1799–1800

By December 1799 Bouchard was responsible for engineering works at El-Arish in northern Sinai.

The garrison became demoralized and mutinous under Ottoman pressure. According to Bouchard’s later service statement and narrative, soldiers demanded surrender, laid down their arms, and helped admit Ottoman forces.

Commander Louis-Joseph-Élisabeth Cazals sent Bouchard as a parlementaire to the grand vizier. Ottoman troops arrested, disarmed, robbed, and partly stripped him. He stated that Mustafa Pasha of Serbia rescued him from men who might otherwise have killed him.

When Ottoman forces entered the fort, many French defenders were killed. Bouchard was retained as a prisoner and spent approximately 42 days imprisoned at Damascus before release under the Convention of El-Arish.

### Evidentiary caution concerning his narrative

Bouchard’s detailed account is an indispensable primary source, but it was written by an officer required to explain his conduct after a humiliating collapse. The narrative assigns decisive responsibility to mutinous troops and emphasizes his own composure and involuntary captivity.

A modern reviewer plausibly identifies self-justification as one motive for the document. This does not prove the account false; it means its account of responsibility should be compared with Cazals, Feray, Villiers du Terrage, Ottoman materials, and other siege evidence.

### Return to Cairo

After release, Bouchard returned to Cairo. He joined the force defending headquarters in the Ezbekieh quarter during the uprising associated with the arrival of Nassif Pasha and Ibrahim Bey. He was then assigned to the defense of the Coptic quarter.

After the capitulation of Cairo on 21 April 1800, he went to the Damietta district and constructed two defensive works between Lake Manzala and the sea.

He was promoted to captain, second class, on 1 May 1800.

---

## Second Defense of Fort Julien and British Captivity, 1801

On 4 February 1801 Bouchard received orders to strengthen the western mouth of the Nile.

Fort Julien was subsequently attacked by British and Ottoman forces. Bouchard’s own later account described a tiny core of line troops and two companies of disabled veterans, many amputees, resisting a much larger allied force. He stated that a bastion had collapsed because of Nile flooding and that the defenders resisted for nine days before receiving honorable terms.

The fort capitulated in April 1801. Dates given in the secondary literature include 8 April and 19 April, reflecting differing references to the operational sequence. Bouchard became a British prisoner.

He returned to Marseille on 30 July 1801.

---

## Saint-Domingue, 1801–1804

### Voluntary participation

On 22 October 1801, at his own request, Bouchard joined the colonial expedition to Saint-Domingue. He embarked in December. His wife accompanied him.

The expedition, commanded initially by Charles Leclerc and later by Donatien de Rochambeau, sought to re-establish French control over the colony during the Haitian Revolution. The French government also restored slavery in the Caribbean colonial system in 1802.

### Bouchard’s role

The consulted sources document his presence and promotion but do not specify every fortification, combat action, or command he undertook. Rochambeau promoted him to captain first class on 20 June 1803, citing bravery, talent, and services to the colony. The French government confirmed the rank on 1 December 1804.

### Yellow fever

Both Bouchard and his wife reportedly contracted yellow fever. He survived but returned with permanently damaged health. A daughter was born in 1802, though it remains unclear whether she was born in Saint-Domingue or after Marie-Élisabeth had left the colony.

### Defeat and Jamaican detention

Following the French defeat and evacuation in late 1803, British forces captured Bouchard again. He spent approximately four months in Jamaica and returned to France on 29 June 1804 or during the summer of that year. Local accounts say he was released on parole in August; the discrepancy probably reflects differing dates for release, passage, or official return.

On 24 August 1804 he received three months’ paid convalescent leave, later extended because of continuing ill health.

### Political and ethical context

**Documented fact:** Bouchard voluntarily joined an expedition used to restore French metropolitan control and, in the broader colonial theater, slavery.

**Unestablished:** no consulted document records his views about slavery, Haitian independence, racial policy, Leclerc’s deportations, Rochambeau’s atrocities, or the expedition’s political aims. Participation establishes institutional involvement, but not a separately documented personal program.

---

## Napoléon-Vendée and Construction Work, 1805–1807

After convalescence, Bouchard was assigned in September 1805 to Napoléon-Vendée, now La Roche-sur-Yon.

The new administrative city was being built as the capital of the Vendée. Bouchard worked on military or public construction under prefect Jean-François Merlet and remained for about two years.

This was his principal documented period as a peacetime builder. His son was born during or around this residence.

No personal property development, private contracting business, architectural patent, or commercial investment by Bouchard has been identified.

---

## Iberian Wars, 1807–1814

### Portugal and Spain

After a brief stay at La Rochelle, Bouchard returned to campaigning. The record places him successively under Generals or Marshals Dupont, Soult, Masséna, Marmont, and Clausel.

He entered Portugal with the French force associated with Junot’s invasion and later served in Spain.

### Alcolea, Córdoba, and Bailén, 1808

Bouchard distinguished himself at the bridge of Alcolea on 7 June 1808. The French force then occupied Córdoba, where widespread pillage occurred.

**Documented institutional context:** Bouchard served in Dupont’s army during these operations.

**Unestablished individual conduct:** no consulted evidence specifically attributes killing, pillage, theft, or abuse at Córdoba to Bouchard personally.

Following Dupont’s capitulation at Bailén on 22 July 1808, Bouchard became a prisoner. His precise escape or release is unclear. He avoided the prolonged confinement suffered by many French captives on the Cádiz prison hulks and Cabrera and rejoined Soult’s army.

### Financial consequences

At Bailén or during the related retreat and captivity, Bouchard lost his equipment and two horses.

His wife, left in Paris with two young children, wrote to the minister of war on 19 April 1809 describing extreme deprivation. Because Bouchard’s pay was needed to replace equipment and horses, she obtained an advance of 500 francs.

On 24 September 1810 the ministry awarded Bouchard 1,117 francs compensation for his lost effects and horses.

These records indicate recurring liquidity problems and dependence on military pay. They do not indicate major wealth, investments, inherited property, or private business interests.

### Galicia, Porto, and Amarante, 1809

Bouchard served under Marshal Soult in Galicia and Portugal. He participated in operations around Coruña and Porto and commanded engineers associated with the first division.

At the fortified bridge of Amarante, Portuguese defenders had erected barricades and prepared or damaged the bridge approaches. On the night of 1–2 May, Bouchard and his sappers moved explosives against defensive works. On 3 May they helped force the passage under fire.

His service was described as courageous and technically capable. He received promotion to chef de bataillon on 24 November 1809.

### Portugal, 1810–1811

Bouchard served during Masséna’s failed invasion of Portugal, including the campaign that encountered the Lines of Torres Vedras and ended in retreat.

He became a chevalier of the Légion d’honneur on 6 August 1811.

### Astorga and English captivity, 1812–1814

Bouchard was assigned to the defense of Astorga. After a siege in the summer of 1812, he was captured for the fifth time and sent to England in September.

He remained a prisoner until after the 1814 Treaty of Paris and returned to France in July 1814.

---

## Restoration, Hundred Days, and Final Service, 1814–1822

### First Restoration

Bouchard was appointed to command engineering functions at Orléans. Under the restored Bourbon monarchy he was promoted to officer of the Légion d’honneur, with the date commonly given as 1 November 1814, and became a chevalier of the Royal and Military Order of Saint Louis.

These honors demonstrate that his Imperial service did not initially prevent Bourbon recognition.

### Hundred Days

On Napoleon’s return in 1815, Bouchard resumed service under him. He was assigned responsibility for the defenses of Laon, Soissons, and Château-Thierry and held Laon from 29 April until 1 August 1815.

### Political suspicion and half-pay

After the Second Restoration, officials accused him of excessive attachment to “the usurper” during the “interregnum.” A senior officer recommended removing him or placing him on half-pay.

Bouchard was sent back to Orléans on 16 January 1816 and placed on inactive status on 26 January. In seeking reconsideration, he minimized the political meaning of his Hundred Days service.

**Documented fact:** the administration investigated or reviewed his loyalty and employment.

**Not established:** no criminal prosecution, treason indictment, imprisonment, or conviction followed. This was an administrative and political loyalty matter.

His file was reviewed and he returned to active service at Bergues in October 1817.

### Final appointment

A fellow Polytechnique alumnus, Prévost de Vernois, described Bouchard as commendable for both morality and learning and proposed him for promotion to lieutenant-colonel. The promotion did not occur.

In 1821 or early 1822 Bouchard became chief engineer at the fortified town of Givet on the Meuse.

His son’s death at approximately thirteen occurred shortly before Bouchard’s own death. On 5 August 1822, Bouchard died at Givet after a long illness.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Institutions

Bouchard’s documented institutional affiliations were:

- Paris grenadier battalion;
- second company of military aerostiers;
- École nationale d’aérostatique at Chalais-Meudon;
- École polytechnique, promotion of 1796;
- Commission des sciences et des arts in Egypt;
- Institut d’Égypte network, although he was not a continuing institute scholar after entering the engineers;
- Corps du génie;
- French expeditionary forces in Egypt, Saint-Domingue, Portugal, and Spain;
- engineering commands at Napoléon-Vendée, Orléans, Bergues, and Givet.

### Technologies and engineering work

- Experimental hydrogen generation and observation balloons at Meudon.
- Military surveying and hydrographic reconnaissance at Lake Manzala.
- Fortification work at Fort Julien, El-Arish, Damietta, Rashid, Astorga, Laon, and other northern French posts.
- Combat engineering and demolition at Amarante.
- Military and public construction at Napoléon-Vendée.

### Inventions and patents

No patent, proprietary technology, independent invention, or commercial product by Bouchard has been identified.

The phrase “inventeur de la pierre de Rosette” found in older French literature uses *inventeur* in the archaeological sense of “finder,” not “inventor.”

### Wealth and property

No evidence of substantial landholdings, investment portfolios, commercial partnerships, colonial plantation ownership, enslaved-person ownership, or company interests was found.

Available records instead show:

- recurrent reliance on salary;
- equipment losses;
- his wife’s need for a 500-franc pay advance;
- a government indemnity of 1,117 francs;
- a widow initially left without pension eligibility;
- a later pension of 450 francs.

## Political, Religious, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

### Politics

Bouchard served successive regimes:

- the French Republic;
- Consulate;
- First Empire;
- First Bourbon Restoration;
- Napoleon during the Hundred Days;
- Second Bourbon Restoration.

His return to Napoleon in 1815 was treated by Bourbon officials as political disloyalty. Beyond that episode, the surviving evidence describes professional military adherence more clearly than ideological activism.

No party membership, elected office, political tract, or systematic statement of political belief is known.

### Colonial politics

His voluntary participation in the Saint-Domingue expedition placed him in the machinery of French colonial reconquest during the restoration of slavery. No personal statement explaining that decision has been located.

### Religion

No reliable evidence was found concerning Bouchard’s personal religious observance, conversion, anticlericalism, freemasonry, esotericism, or theological views.

His receipt of the Order of Saint Louis was a royal military honor and does not by itself establish personal religious commitment.

### Intellectual formation

His documented intellectual development was technical:

- mathematics and philosophy at Besançon;
- practical aerostation with Conté;
- descriptive geometry under Monge;
- fortification science;
- geographical and hydrographic reconnaissance;
- observation of Egyptian technologies.

His recognition of the Rosetta Stone’s importance shows educated awareness of Greek and hieroglyphic writing, but no evidence identifies him as a linguist or Egyptologist.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Nicolas-Jacques Conté

**Documented relationship:** superior, mentor, and collaborator at Meudon and in Egypt.

Conté valued Bouchard highly. Bouchard assisted him during the 1795 hydrogen experiment and followed him from Alexandria toward Cairo. Conté’s support probably helped Bouchard enter Polytechnique.

### Gaspard Monge

**Documented relationship:** teacher and examiner.

Bouchard studied descriptive geometry under Monge. Monge later presided over his exit examination in Egypt and co-signed a certificate confirming his aerostatic service.

### Claude-Louis Berthollet

**Documented institutional relationship:** Polytechnique founder and member of the Egyptian scientific network; he co-signed an official certificate concerning Bouchard. A closer personal friendship is not demonstrated.

### Charles-Marie-Benjamin d’Hautpoul

**Documented relationship:** commanding engineer at Fort Julien. Bouchard served under him when the Rosetta Stone was found.

### Joseph-Louis-Victor Jullien

**Probable and strongly documented command relationship:** commander at Rashid. He received or was likely informed of the discovery and probably authorized the stone’s transfer.

### Michel-Ange Lancret

**Documented collaboration:** Lancret reported Bouchard’s discovery by letter to the Institut d’Égypte.

### Jacques-François Menou

**Documented institutional relationship:** regional commander and later commander-in-chief. He arranged an early Greek translation and later claimed the stone as private property when British forces demanded French collections.

### Louis-Joseph-Élisabeth Cazals

**Documented relationship:** commanding officer at El-Arish. He selected Bouchard as parlementaire during the final crisis.

### Mustafa Pasha of Serbia

**Reported from Bouchard’s own service account:** intervened to protect him after his arrest at El-Arish.

### Jean-Baptiste Kléber

**Institutional relationship:** commander-in-chief after Bonaparte’s departure. No personal meeting with Bouchard is documented in the consulted material.

### Charles Leclerc and Donatien de Rochambeau

**Institutional relationships:** successive commanders in Saint-Domingue. Rochambeau promoted Bouchard to captain first class.

### Dupont, Soult, Masséna, Marmont, and Clausel

**Documented command relationships:** senior commanders under whom Bouchard served during the Iberian wars.

### Prévost de Vernois

**Documented professional supporter:** fellow Polytechnique alumnus who recommended Bouchard for lieutenant-colonel and praised his morality and instruction.

### Jean-François Champollion

**No documented personal relationship.** Bouchard found and helped preserve the document later used in Champollion’s decipherment, but no meeting, correspondence, friendship, or collaboration has been demonstrated.

## Illnesses, Injuries, and Psychological Evidence

### Documented or well-reported medical events

- Serious facial and right-eye injury in the 1795 hydrogen explosion.
- Yellow fever during the Saint-Domingue expedition.
- Prolonged convalescence after returning from the Caribbean.
- Chronic poor health thereafter.
- “Long and painful illness” preceding death.

### Not documented

No reliable evidence was found of addiction, alcoholism, psychiatric hospitalization, diagnosed depression, suicide attempt, or another psychological crisis.

Modern narratives sometimes attribute despair, obsession, adventure-seeking, or indifference to him. These are biographical interpretations, not documented diagnoses or first-person disclosures.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

### 1. Age alteration at Polytechnique

**Documented discrepancy; probable administrative falsification.**

Bouchard’s Polytechnique register gave 1772 rather than the parish-supported 1771. Leclant concluded that the age was “slightly falsified,” probably to mitigate his violation of the admissions age limit.

The evidence establishes a false age in the school record. It does not establish who altered it, whether Bouchard personally supplied it, or whether officials knowingly waived or disguised the problem.

### 2. Credit for the Rosetta Stone discovery

**Disputed attribution, not fraud.**

Bouchard is conventionally called the discoverer, but the earliest report implies that unnamed laborers or soldiers physically uncovered it. His defensible claim is that he supervised the work, recognized the inscriptions’ importance, preserved the block, reported it, and transported it.

There is no evidence that Bouchard fabricated the discovery or stole credit through a documented false statement. The simplified “lone discoverer” story developed largely through later commemorative retelling.

### 3. Roles of D’Hautpoul, Jullien, and Menou

**Historical dispute.**

- D’Hautpoul ordered or commanded the engineering project.
- Jullien commanded Rashid and probably handled the first administrative response.
- Menou arranged early translation and later held the stone.
- Bouchard performed the decisive on-site recognition and transport.

Accounts crediting only one of these men omit a collaborative chain.

### 4. El-Arish responsibility

**Documented military failure; contested apportionment of blame.**

Bouchard was an officer at a fort that collapsed amid mutiny, surrender efforts, and massacre. His account blamed the garrison’s revolt and Ottoman conduct while emphasizing his own mission under orders.

No evidence consulted shows a court conviction or formal finding of cowardice against him. His later promotions indicate that French authorities accepted or at least did not disqualify his explanation.

His narrative nevertheless had a self-exculpatory context and should not be treated as a neutral transcript of every event.

### 5. Participation in colonial reconquest and the restoration-of-slavery theater

**Documented institutional participation; individual conduct unresolved.**

Bouchard voluntarily joined the Saint-Domingue expedition and was promoted for his service. The expedition fought the Haitian Revolution within a policy environment that restored slavery and included mass violence.

No consulted source identifies Bouchard personally as the author of colonial policy or documents his participation in a particular atrocity. Conversely, absence of such a record does not remove him from the expedition’s military structure.

### 6. Córdoba and Iberian-war abuses

**Documented presence in an army associated with pillage; no individual accusation established.**

Bouchard served in Dupont’s force during the occupation and sack of Córdoba. No source consulted attributes a particular act of pillage or violence to him personally.

### 7. Bonapartist loyalty investigation

**Documented administrative accusation.**

After the Hundred Days, officials described his attachment to Napoleon as excessive and recommended removal or half-pay. He sought review and minimized his political role.

This resulted in temporary professional penalty, not a known criminal conviction.

### 8. Financial misconduct

No evidence was found of embezzlement, bribery, fraud, money laundering, illegal enrichment, or corrupt contracting.

His surviving financial story concerns loss, compensation, salary advances, and pension hardship.

### 9. Dedicated adversarial-search result, including 2024–2026

Searches combining his full name and variants with controversy, scandal, allegations, lawsuit, fraud, accusations, criticism, conspiracy, imposture, and related French terms produced no recent substantive allegation against Bouchard.

Recent material primarily repeats disputes over:

- who physically uncovered the Rosetta Stone;
- the precise discovery date;
- French versus British possession of the stone;
- its possible restitution to Egypt;
- mistakes in Bouchard’s birth and death years;
- whether modern biographies romanticize his life.

No lawsuit, indictment, formal charge, corruption inquiry, intelligence allegation, or newly surfaced criminal accusation concerning Bouchard was located for 2024–2026.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Bouchard alone dug up the stone”

**Story:** Bouchard personally struck the stone with a tool and lifted it from the ground.

**Origin and circulation:** simplified anniversary histories, educational texts, illustrations, and popular biographies.

**Evidence:** the contemporary *Courier de l’Égypte* uses an impersonal construction—“it was found”—during excavations directed by d’Hautpoul and led locally by Bouchard. The actual worker is unnamed.

**Assessment:** **mythic simplification**. Bouchard’s recognition and preservation are well supported; his being the first person physically to touch or see it is not.

### The anonymous French soldier

**Story:** an unnamed French soldier discovered the block and called Bouchard.

**Evidence:** consistent with a military work gang but not securely named in the earliest account.

**Assessment:** **plausible reconstruction**, not fully documented.

### The anonymous Egyptian laborer

**Story:** an Egyptian worker employed on the fort first exposed the stone.

**Circulation:** modern accounts attentive to colonial labor and erased local participation.

**Evidence:** Egyptian laborers may well have been present, but the consulted contemporary report does not identify the finder’s nationality.

**Assessment:** **plausible but unverified claim**.

### The horse stumbling over the stone

**Story:** Bouchard’s horse tripped over the Rosetta Stone, leading to its discovery.

**Evidence:** no adequate contemporary support located; it conflicts with the consistent fortification-excavation setting.

**Assessment:** **rumor or legend**.

### Exact discovery on 15 or 19 July 1799

**Story:** popular chronologies confidently assign either date.

**Evidence:** neither exact date is secured by a surviving contemporaneous discovery log. The best scholarly reconstruction gives 14–25 July.

**Assessment:** **unresolved chronological tradition**.

### “Friend of Champollion”

Some museum educational material and derivative narratives call Champollion Bouchard’s friend.

**Evidence:** none located. Champollion was eight years old in 1799, and Bouchard died before the public decipherment announcement.

**Assessment:** **unsupported commemorative fiction**.

### “Bouchard deciphered the Rosetta Stone”

**Evidence:** false. He recognized its likely comparative value; later scholars performed the decipherment.

**Assessment:** **popular misconception**.

### “The Rosetta Stone instantly translated hieroglyphs”

The stone was recognized immediately as promising, but decipherment required more than two decades, other inscriptions, knowledge of Coptic, and work by multiple scholars.

**Assessment:** **retrospective compression**.

### “The stone was basalt” or “black granite”

These descriptions reproduce historical visual or geological approximations. Modern museum identification is granodiorite.

**Assessment:** **outdated classification**, not fabrication.

### The forgotten or “unknown lieutenant”

Bouchard is frequently styled *le lieutenant inconnu*, the forgotten man displaced by Champollion.

The phrase has a factual basis: his later life attracted little notice, his portrait is unknown, and his correct vital dates were only firmly re-established in modern local research. Yet he was named in the original expedition press and never wholly absent from specialist literature.

**Assessment:** **commemorative framing grounded in relative neglect**, but exaggerated when it implies total historical erasure.

### “Christopher Columbus of Egyptology”

Ahmed Youssef used a comparison of Bouchard to Columbus: someone who opened access to a field without himself carrying out its later intellectual development.

**Assessment:** **modern biographical metaphor**, not a contemporary title.

### National possession narratives

French, British, and Egyptian narratives respectively emphasize French discovery and scholarship, British military acquisition and preservation, or Egyptian ownership and colonial dispossession.

The underlying facts are:

- the artifact was Egyptian and found at Rashid;
- it was recovered during a French military occupation;
- French scholars copied and studied it;
- Britain obtained it through the 1801 capitulation settlement after military victory;
- the British Museum received it in 1802;
- Egyptian restitution campaigns contest the legitimacy of that transfer.

These narratives concern the stone’s later ownership more than Bouchard personally, but they continually reshape his image as discoverer, occupier, savant, or agent of removal.

## Works by Pierre-François-Xavier Bouchard

Bouchard was not a prolific published author. His known corpus consists principally of military manuscripts and official correspondence.

### 1. *Journal historique de la défense d’El-Arich* / *Journal historique: La chute d’El-Arich (décembre 1799)*

- **Authorship:** genuine; written by Bouchard.
- **Original form:** manuscript military narrative, preserved in the French military archives, traditionally cited as SHAT MR 537.
- **Purpose:** account of the siege, garrison mutiny, fall of El-Arish, Bouchard’s mission as parlementaire, arrest, and captivity.
- **Publication:** serialized or reproduced by Gaston Wiet beginning in *La Revue du Caire* in the 1940s; issued as a book by Éditions de la Revue du Caire, Cairo, 1945, 176 pages.
- **Editorial status:** Gaston Wiet supplied the preface, historical study, and annotations.
- **Later reissue:** incorporated into Ahmed Youssef’s 2021 book.
- **Attribution caveat:** the 2021 subtitle calls it “inédit,” but it had already been published in 1945.

### 2. Manuscript account of the siege or defense of Fort Julien

- **Authorship:** attributed to Bouchard and accepted in the specialist literature.
- **Repository:** Service historique de la Défense, traditionally cited as SHAT MR 552.
- **Publication status:** apparently unpublished as an independent modern critical edition.
- **Content:** defense, allied attack, damaged fortifications, and capitulation of Fort Julien in 1801.

### 3. *État des affaires remarquables où s’est trouvé Pierre François Xavier Bouchard, capitaine de 1re classe au corps impérial du génie*

- **Date:** 17 Fructidor Year XIII, given as 4 or 5 September 1805 depending on calendar conversion.
- **Authorship/status:** official autobiographical service statement by Bouchard.
- **Repository:** military personnel file.
- **Importance:** principal first-person source for his experiences, including El-Arish.

### 4. Letter to the minister of war, General Berthier

- **Date:** 29 Fructidor Year IX, 16 September 1801.
- **Purpose:** requested confirmation of the captain’s rank Bouchard said he had earned during the Cairo fighting.
- **Status:** archival official correspondence.

### 5. Other service statements, petitions, and military correspondence

Leclant refers to several service records composed by Bouchard. Notably, he apparently did **not** list the Rosetta Stone discovery among the achievements in these later statements.

No diary covering his entire life, memoir of the Rosetta discovery, scientific paper, patent, philosophical work, literary text, or published book from his lifetime has been identified.

## Death and Immediate Aftermath

Bouchard died at Givet on 5 August 1822, shortly after the death of his son and after years of impaired health.

He did not live to hear Champollion’s September 1822 public presentation explaining the phonetic principles of hieroglyphic writing.

His widow and daughter were left without adequate resources. The initial pension obstacle arose because he had fallen just short of 30 years’ qualifying service. Marie-Élisabeth’s eventual 450-franc pension was granted in April 1824 after administrative appeals.

His grave or the subsequent disposition of his remains is not established in the consulted material.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Nineteenth century

Bouchard remained named in accounts of the Egyptian expedition and the discovery, but the intellectual drama of decipherment centered public attention on Champollion and Thomas Young.

Champollion acknowledged in an 1831 lecture that an engineering officer named Bouchard had found the inscribed stone, though he incorrectly placed the discovery in August rather than July 1799.

### Twentieth-century recovery

Gaston Wiet’s publication of the El-Arish journal in the 1940s made Bouchard visible as a military witness rather than merely the finder of an artifact.

Jacques Laurens’s 1991 article and the recovery or renewed examination of the Orgelet birth record corrected his chronology. Jean Leclant’s 1999 study integrated Bouchard into the institutional history of the Institut d’Égypte and the Rosetta Stone’s reproduction and circulation.

### Local commemoration

Orgelet has made Bouchard a prominent local historical figure through:

- municipal publications;
- anniversary lectures;
- historical exhibitions;
- a 2023 graphic biography;
- educational programming.

The absence of a verified portrait remains a significant limitation on memorial representation.

### Museum and global reputation

The British Museum catalog identifies Bouchard as the object’s excavator or field collector. The museum also increasingly describes the find collectively as the work of French soldiers, a formulation better aligned with the excavation context.

Bouchard’s name survives in three overlapping reputations:

1. the technically educated officer who recognized the Rosetta Stone;
2. the largely forgotten soldier whose later life consisted of repeated wars and captivities;
3. an agent within French imperial and colonial expeditions.

### Symbolic legacy

“Rosetta Stone” has become a global metaphor for a key that makes an unknown system intelligible. This symbolic expansion far exceeds Bouchard’s personal fame.

His historical contribution was not decipherment but mediation: he prevented an inscribed construction block from disappearing into fortification rubble and moved it into a scholarly network capable of copying, circulating, and studying it.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are recorded. Most of the names central to Bouchard’s life—Conté, Monge, Berthollet, Champollion, d’Hautpoul, Jullien, Menou, Cazals, Soult, Masséna, and Rochambeau—do not appear in the supplied atlas roster.

### Napoleon Bonaparte — documented institutional and command connection

- **Nature:** Bouchard served in the Egyptian expedition commanded by Bonaparte, joined its Commission des sciences et des arts, and carried out engineering work within Bonaparte’s fortification program. Bonaparte ordered rehabilitation of the fort at Rashid in October 1798. Bouchard later served the Napoleonic state at Napoléon-Vendée, in Iberia, and again during the Hundred Days.
- **Dates and places:** Egypt, 1798–1801; Napoléon-Vendée, 1805–1807; Iberian Peninsula, 1807–1812; Laon and northern France, 1815.
- **Evidence label:** **documented fact**.
- **Caution:** no consulted source proves a private friendship or a one-to-one meeting. The connection is command, government service, institutional participation, and political allegiance in 1815.

### Pierre-Simon Laplace — reported shared educational-scientific environment, not a demonstrated personal relationship

- A 2022 ASPHOR presentation places Laplace among the distinguished scientific figures encountered within Bouchard’s Meudon/Polytechnique environment.
- **Evidence label:** **reported fact**, insufficient to establish a specific meeting, lesson, correspondence, or collaboration.
- Because the atlas instruction excludes inference from mere shared institution, this is not treated as a firm personal connection.

### Joseph-Louis Lagrange / Jean-Baptiste Joseph Fourier

The same presentation lists Lagrange and Fourier among the scientific environment surrounding Bouchard. Fourier later belonged to the Egyptian expedition’s scholarly administration, but the sources consulted do not document a specific personal exchange with Bouchard.

- **Evidence label:** **reported shared institutional setting only**.
- These are not counted as established personal atlas crossings.

### Absence finding

No source-supported meeting, correspondence, collaboration, rivalry, family tie, or explicit acknowledged influence was found between Bouchard and any other person in the supplied atlas roster. In particular, mere participation in the French Revolutionary and Napoleonic world was not used to manufacture connections.
