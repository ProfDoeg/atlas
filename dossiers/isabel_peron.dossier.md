# María Estela Martínez Cartas de Perón: Research Dossier

## Scope, method, and evidentiary terminology

This dossier reconstructs María Estela Martínez Cartas de Perón’s life through official records, judicial and prosecutorial material, contemporaneous reporting, diplomatic documents, academic studies, archival catalogues, biographies, and later journalism. Spanish-language sources were prioritized.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by an official record, judgment, decree, archival document, or direct contemporary evidence.
- **Reported fact:** consistently reported by credible secondary sources but not independently confirmed here from a primary document.
- **Allegation:** asserted in a complaint, investigation, testimony, or publication without a final finding establishing it.
- **Court finding / conviction:** expressly established by a court.
- **Disputed claim:** materially conflicting accounts exist.
- **Rumor or myth:** culturally or politically significant story lacking adequate documentary proof.
- **Inference:** conclusion suggested by the evidence but not expressly established by it.

The research included a dedicated adversarial search using her name with terms including *controversia, escándalo, acusaciones, corrupción, fraude, conspiración, crítica, Triple A, extradición,* and *crímenes*, including material published or recirculated in 2024–2026.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full birth name:** María Estela Martínez Cartas.

**Married and political names:**

- María Estela Martínez de Perón.
- María Estela Martínez Cartas de Perón.
- Isabel Martínez de Perón.
- Isabel Perón.
- “Isabelita,” a diminutive widely used by supporters, opponents, and the press.
- “Isabel,” reportedly adopted from her Catholic confirmation name, associated in biographical accounts with Saint Elizabeth of Portugal.

**Birth:** 4 February 1931, La Rioja, capital of La Rioja Province, Argentina.

**Present status:** Living as of the latest reliable public evidence consulted. She reached age ninety-five on 4 February 2026.

**Nationality:** Argentine by birth. Biographical accounts report that she also obtained Spanish citizenship around 2000 without losing Argentine nationality.

**Religion:** Roman Catholic publicly and institutionally. Her private religious formation also intersected with spiritualism and, later, the occult and esoteric practices associated with José López Rega. The degree of her personal commitment to particular esoteric doctrines remains disputed.

**Professions and public roles:**

- Dancer and member of touring entertainment companies.
- Political emissary and personal delegate of Juan Domingo Perón.
- First lady of Argentina, 12 October 1973–1 July 1974.
- Vice-president of Argentina, 12 October 1973–1 July 1974.
- President of Argentina, 1 July 1974–24 March 1976, with a medical leave in September–October 1975.
- President or titular leader of the Partido Justicialista during portions of the post-Perón period.
- Former president and widow of Juan Domingo Perón after 1976.

She was the first woman to exercise Argentina’s presidency. She is commonly described as the first woman in the world to serve simultaneously as head of state and head of government of a presidential republic. The distinction requires qualification: earlier women had served as monarchs or prime ministers, and Sukarno’s daughter did not precede her; Isabel Perón’s specific historical priority concerns an elected presidential system and succession from the vice-presidency.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

Sources agree on her mother’s name as **María Josefa Cartas**, generally dated 1897–1966.

Her father’s identity presents a source conflict:

- A widely repeated account names him **Carmelo Martínez**, born about 1893 and deceased in 1938, an employee of the Banco Hipotecario Nacional transferred from Buenos Aires to La Rioja and reportedly sympathetic to the Unión Cívica Radical.
- Another biographical reference identifies him as **Marcelo Martínez Rosales**, also described as a branch manager or employee of the National Mortgage Bank.

The discrepancy is not satisfactorily resolved in the accessible sources. Carmelo Martínez is the form most frequently reproduced in Argentine biographical accounts, but the alternative should not be silently discarded.

### Siblings

The fullest reference located describes María Estela as the third of five children, with two older sisters and two younger brothers. Publicly accessible sources provide inconsistent or incomplete lists of their names. Later biographies emphasize her estrangement from much of her biological family.

### Substitute or adoptive household

After her father’s death in 1938, when she was approximately seven, she went to Buenos Aires and entered the household of **José Cresto** and **Isabel Zoila Gómez de Cresto**. They are variously described as guardians, foster or adoptive parents, and leaders or participants in a spiritualist school.

No adoption decree was located. “Adoptive parents” should therefore be understood as a biographical description unless civil records establish formal adoption.

This household was formative in three respects:

1. It separated her from her biological family.
2. It exposed her to spiritualism and non-orthodox religious practices.
3. Isabel Zoila’s name may have reinforced her use of “Isabel,” although other accounts derive the name entirely from Catholic confirmation.

### Marriage and children

She married **Juan Domingo Perón** in Spain on 15 November 1961. It was his third marriage, after Aurelia Tizón and Eva Duarte de Perón.

The marriage produced no children. Perón also had no legally recognized children.

Later claims that **Martha Holgado** was Perón’s biological daughter led to prolonged litigation and requests to exhume Perón for DNA testing. Isabel opposed exhumation for years. Subsequent genetic testing did not establish Holgado as Perón’s daughter. The episode nevertheless affected disputes over Perón’s inheritance and public mythology.

No credible evidence was found that Isabel had children, either before or during the marriage.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1931–early 1950s

María Estela was born into a lower-middle- or middle-class household connected to bank employment rather than the landowning elite. Her father’s early death destabilized the family.

Her transfer to Buenos Aires placed her in a household very different from her provincial birthplace. Biographies describe the Crestos as involved in spiritualism; later hostile narratives sometimes turn this early environment into a retrospective explanation for her attachment to López Rega. The basic connection to spiritualism is reported by multiple sources, but elaborate claims of occult initiation during childhood remain poorly documented.

### Education

Accounts differ slightly:

- Some say she left formal school after the fifth grade.
- Others say after the sixth grade.
- Several accounts say she continued some secondary-level studies at home.

She did not receive a documented university education or advanced professional credential.

She studied combinations of:

- Ballet.
- Spanish dance.
- Singing.
- Piano.
- English.
- French.

The precise schools, teachers, dates, and certificates are not consistently documented. Her artistic training was practical and performance-oriented.

### Name and identity formation

“Isabel” was used professionally and personally before she became a national political figure. The strongest conventional account is that it was her Catholic confirmation name. A second explanation links it to Isabel Zoila Gómez de Cresto. The two possibilities are not mutually exclusive.

“Isabelita” was initially affectionate but later acquired dismissive and gendered overtones, suggesting youth, dependence, or incapacity. In 2024, Vice-President Victoria Villarruel explicitly rejected the diminutive while promoting a political rehabilitation of her.

---

## Early Career as a Dancer

During the early 1950s Martínez became a professional or semi-professional dancer in nightclub and touring-company settings. Sources variously characterize her as:

- A cabaret dancer.
- A folkloric or Spanish dancer.
- A nightclub performer.
- A member of the **Joe Herald’s Ballet** or a similarly named troupe.

Sensational accounts sometimes imply sex work or describe her meeting with Perón as occurring “in a cabaret.” No reliable evidence consulted establishes that she was a sex worker. The documented core is that she worked as a dancer in an entertainment troupe whose itinerary included Latin American venues and nightclubs.

Her troupe reportedly traveled through Argentina, Colombia, Panama, and possibly other countries. One account says it had toured the United States and Colombia before reaching Panama.

---

## Meeting Juan Domingo Perón, 1955–1956

### Context

Juan Perón was overthrown in September 1955 and entered a long exile. After passing through Paraguay and other locations, he arrived in Panama.

### The meeting

The generally accepted account places their meeting in **Panama City in late 1955**, often around Christmas. Martínez was approximately twenty-four; Perón was sixty.

The exact venue and intermediary vary:

- A nightclub performance.
- A social introduction after a show.
- A meeting arranged through mutual acquaintances in the entertainment or expatriate community.

By mid-January 1956, according to later biographical reconstruction, they were living together.

### Nature of the early relationship

Martínez became Perón’s companion, domestic partner, assistant, and eventually political intermediary. The substantial age difference and Perón’s position as an exiled former president generated competing interpretations:

- Supporters describe loyalty, companionship, and practical devotion.
- Critics portray a dependent young woman absorbed into Perón’s household and political apparatus.
- Some biographers find that she gradually acquired practical political skills through managing visitors, messages, and personal relations in exile.

There is no documentary basis for reducing the relationship to only one of these interpretations.

---

## Exile and Migration with Perón, 1956–1973

Martínez followed Perón through stages of his exile, including periods associated with:

- Panama.
- Venezuela.
- The Dominican Republic.
- Spain.

Perón’s movements were affected by changes of government and pressure from Argentine authorities. In 1958 the fall of Venezuelan ruler Marcos Pérez Jiménez contributed to another displacement.

The couple eventually settled in **Madrid**, where Francisco Franco’s government permitted Perón to reside. Their principal home became the estate commonly called **Puerta de Hierro**, formally the Quinta 17 de Octubre.

### Marriage, 1961

They married in Madrid on 15 November 1961. The marriage also helped regularize a domestic situation that could be politically inconvenient in Francoist, Catholic Spain.

Perón was reconciled institutionally with the Catholic Church in the early 1960s after the conflicts and excommunication controversy of his second presidency.

### Role at Puerta de Hierro

Isabel handled domestic and political functions in a household that doubled as the headquarters of a transnational political movement. Peronist officials, union leaders, military officers, businessmen, intermediaries, journalists, and foreign political figures traveled there.

Her position gave her:

- Control over personal access at certain moments.
- Knowledge of correspondence and emissaries.
- Experience carrying oral and written instructions.
- A symbolic place as Perón’s loyal spouse and possible representative.

Her actual independent decision-making authority during this period remains debated.

---

## Political Formation and Missions to Argentina

### The Peronist problem in exile

Perón sought to control a movement divided among trade-union leaders, orthodox loyalists, revolutionary youth, conservative nationalists, provincial organizations, and personal rivals.

A principal conflict concerned **Augusto Timoteo Vandor**, the metalworkers’ leader associated with “Peronism without Perón.” Perón required emissaries whom he considered personally loyal.

### First major mission, 1965–1966

In 1965 Isabel traveled to Argentina as Perón’s personal delegate. The trip extended into a long tour—some accounts say roughly ten months—during which she met political and union figures and transmitted Perón’s directives.

Her activities included contacts with:

- Bernardo Alberte.
- Raúl Lastiri.
- José Alonso.
- Peronist provincial leaders.
- Trade-union factions opposed to or competing with Vandor.
- Groups later associated with the CGT de los Argentinos and left Peronism, although her later alignment was much further to the right.

This mission helped defeat efforts to detach organized Peronism from Perón’s personal leadership. It was one of the clearest instances in which Isabel performed consequential political work before holding office.

### Meeting José López Rega

During the 1965–1966 mission, she met **José López Rega**, a retired Federal Police corporal, printer, amateur writer, astrologer, and occultist. Accounts disagree on the introduction:

- Raúl Lastiri, López Rega’s son-in-law, introduced them.
- José Cresto or figures connected to the Anael lodge facilitated the meeting.
- They met through a Peronist political gathering.

What is documented is the rapid formation of a close relationship. Isabel brought López Rega back to Madrid, where he became Perón’s valet, secretary, gatekeeper, health adviser, and political operative.

### Second mission, 1971–1972

Isabel again served as Perón’s delegate amid disputes over electoral strategy and leadership. By this stage López Rega was deeply embedded in the household.

Her missions were later minimized by both anti-Peronist accounts and sectors of Peronism embarrassed by her presidency. The record nevertheless shows that Perón deliberately selected her as a trusted emissary.

---

## López Rega, Esotericism, and the Puerta de Hierro Circle

López Rega cultivated astrology, spiritualism, ceremonial ideas, and occult symbolism. He was known as **“El Brujo”**—the Sorcerer.

### Documented relationship

- Isabel trusted him and helped install him in Perón’s Madrid household.
- He exercised increasing control over access, correspondence, and health arrangements.
- He later became minister of Social Welfare and retained the portfolio under Héctor Cámpora, Raúl Lastiri, Juan Perón, and Isabel Perón.
- His relatives and associates entered important state positions.

### Claims of spiritual domination

Biographies and testimonies describe López Rega as Isabel’s spiritual adviser and assert that he convinced her she had a special connection with Eva Perón.

These accounts range from plausible psychological influence to sensational ritual narratives. López Rega’s occult interests and Isabel’s confidence in him are documented; claims that he literally controlled her through hypnosis, magic, or possession are not.

---

## Return of Perón and the 1973 Elections

After the military government opened an electoral path, Héctor Cámpora won the presidency in March 1973 as Perón’s delegate. Cámpora took office on 25 May.

Perón returned permanently on 20 June 1973. The mass reception at Ezeiza became the site of armed violence between Peronist factions.

Cámpora resigned in July. Raúl Lastiri, López Rega’s son-in-law, became interim president after a contested succession maneuver.

### Selection as vice-presidential candidate

For the September 1973 election, the formula was **Juan Domingo Perón–María Estela Martínez de Perón**.

Reasons reported for selecting her include:

- Absolute personal loyalty.
- The symbolic appeal of “Perón–Perón.”
- Lack of an alternative acceptable to all factions.
- Perón’s wish to avoid elevating a potential successor with an independent power base.
- Support from José Ignacio Rucci and orthodox union leaders.
- López Rega’s influence.
- The belief that Perón’s name, rather than the vice-presidential candidate, would decide the election.

The formula received approximately 62 percent of the vote on 23 September 1973.

She assumed office as vice-president and first lady on 12 October 1973.

---

## Vice-Presidency and First Ladyship, 1973–1974

Isabel presided over the Senate and represented the government ceremonially. Perón’s health deteriorated, compelling her to assume executive functions temporarily during his incapacity.

The administration sought a **Social Pact** among organized labor, business, and government, with José Ber Gelbard as economy minister. At the same time, Peronism’s internal war intensified.

Major events included:

- The assassination of CGT leader José Ignacio Rucci on 25 September 1973.
- Escalating attacks by the ERP and Montoneros.
- The development of the **Alianza Anticomunista Argentina**, or Triple A, from networks centered in the Ministry of Social Welfare and security forces.
- Perón’s definitive break with Montoneros in 1974.
- Political interventions against provincial administrations associated with the Peronist left.

### Assumption of presidential functions

Because of Perón’s illness, Isabel temporarily exercised executive authority in June 1974. She was therefore already acting constitutionally as chief executive before his death.

### Death of Juan Perón

Juan Domingo Perón died on 1 July 1974. Isabel announced his death publicly, describing him as an apostle of peace and nonviolence.

Under the Constitution she succeeded automatically to the presidency.

---

## Presidency, 1 July 1974–24 March 1976

## Initial succession

The succession itself was constitutional. No separate presidential election occurred after Perón’s death because she had been elected vice-president.

She inherited:

- A fragmented Peronist coalition.
- Armed insurgencies.
- State and para-state repression.
- Rapidly worsening inflation and external constraints.
- A powerful union movement.
- A military establishment increasingly prepared to resume political control.
- López Rega’s expanding personal network.

Her public formula was continuity: she frequently presented herself as Perón’s hand, disciple, or executor rather than as the founder of an independent program.

## Government structure and López Rega’s ascendancy

López Rega remained minister of Social Welfare and became the most influential figure around the president. His network included Raúl Lastiri, Carlos Villone, security personnel, police officers, and officials linked to the Triple A.

The characterization of Isabel as simply López Rega’s puppet is too categorical. Evidence demonstrates that he exerted extraordinary influence, but she made or ratified appointments, defended him, removed opponents, and continued policies after Perón’s death. Whether specific criminal operations were communicated to her remains unresolved.

## Political realignment

Her government moved against left-wing Peronism and aligned increasingly with:

- Orthodox Peronist unions.
- Conservative and nationalist Peronists.
- López Rega’s circle.
- Senior police and military institutions.
- Anti-communist regional governments.

Provincial interventions and dismissals weakened governors and officials associated with the left.

Montoneros returned to clandestinity in September 1974. The ERP continued armed operations outside Peronism.

## Triple A and political violence

### Established institutional facts

The Triple A carried out threats, bombings, kidnappings, and murders against leftists, lawyers, union dissidents, intellectuals, priests, artists, and political opponents.

A 2006 Argentine judicial ruling classified crimes committed in the framework of Triple A operations as crimes against humanity, emphasizing the use of state structures. Evidence tied the organization to the Ministry of Social Welfare, federal police personnel, López Rega, and associated officials.

Prominent victims attributed to Triple A or connected para-state networks included:

- Silvio Frondizi.
- Rodolfo Ortega Peña.
- Julio Troxler.
- Atilio López.
- Numerous activists, lawyers, students, and unionists.

Estimates of victims vary significantly. Contemporary and later reporting often cites hundreds of killings; claims of approximately 1,500 deaths sometimes combine murders, threats, and broader political violence and should not be treated as a settled count.

### Isabel’s legal position

**Documented fact:** the violence occurred during her vice-presidency and presidency, using personnel and resources connected to her government.

**Allegation/investigative theory:** she knew of, authorized, or bore command responsibility for Triple A operations.

**What was not established:** no final criminal judgment convicted her of Triple A murders or membership in the organization.

In 2007 Judge Norberto Oyarbide sought her arrest and extradition in connection with Triple A crimes. Spain rejected extradition in 2008. The Spanish court reasoned that the conduct attributed personally to her was not adequately shown to constitute crimes against humanity and treated the ordinary offenses as time-barred. This was an extradition ruling, not a factual exoneration after a trial on the merits.

## State of siege

Her government declared a nationwide state of siege in November 1974 after escalating political violence. The measure expanded detention powers and intensified political repression.

## Operativo Independencia

On 5 February 1975 Isabel signed secret Decree 261/1975. It ordered the Army to conduct operations in Tucumán necessary to “neutralize and/or annihilate” the actions of subversive elements.

The immediate target was the ERP’s rural guerrilla presence. Under the operation, security forces also created clandestine detention sites, including the school at Famaillá, and committed abductions, torture, disappearances, and killings.

Later decrees extending the “annihilation” directive nationwide were signed in October 1975 while Ítalo Luder was acting president during Isabel’s leave.

### Competing legal interpretations

- Prosecutors later sought to question or extradite Isabel concerning approximately 270 victims in Tucumán.
- One judicial approach argued that the military used the operation as part of its own plan and did not require the civilian government’s acquiescence.
- Prosecutors contended that signing the decree and the documented operation of clandestine repression required deeper investigation.
- Spanish and Argentine procedural decisions prevented a completed criminal trial of Isabel personally.

Thus:

- The decree and her signature are **documented facts**.
- Crimes committed during the operation are **documented facts and subjects of later convictions of military personnel**.
- Her individual criminal responsibility remains **unresolved and unadjudicated**, not established by conviction and not conclusively disproved.

## Economic policy before the Rodrigazo

José Ber Gelbard left the Economy Ministry in October 1974 as the Social Pact deteriorated. Alfredo Gómez Morales succeeded him.

The government faced:

- Inflationary pressure.
- Fiscal imbalance.
- Falling real wages.
- Shortages and black markets.
- External-sector pressure.
- Declining confidence.
- Employer and union resistance to wage-price controls.

## The Rodrigazo, June–July 1975

On López Rega’s recommendation, **Celestino Rodrigo** became economy minister on 2 June 1975. His program included:

- A very large devaluation, commonly summarized as roughly 100 percent or more depending on the exchange rate measured.
- Fuel increases reaching approximately 180 percent.
- Major rises in utility and transport charges.
- An attempted limit on wage increases well below the price shock.

The package became known as the **Rodrigazo**.

Consequences included:

- A sudden collapse in real wages.
- Accelerating inflation.
- Mass labor mobilization.
- The first general strikes called by the CGT against a Peronist national government.
- Rejection of government wage ceilings.
- The fall of Rodrigo and, soon afterward, López Rega.

The crisis marked a structural break in postwar Argentine economic and labor relations.

## Fall of López Rega

Under union, military, congressional, and public pressure, Isabel removed or accepted the resignation of López Rega in July 1975. He was sent abroad as ambassador-at-large or attached to the Spanish embassy, effectively removing him from the country.

His departure did not restore stable government. His associates remained in parts of the administration, and armed violence continued.

## Cruzada de Solidaridad scandal

The **Cruzada de Solidaridad Justicialista** was a charitable organization linked to the government and Social Welfare Ministry.

In August 1975, *La Prensa* and opposition figures raised allegations that funds had been diverted or deposited into Isabel’s personal accounts, including money connected to Perón’s succession and obligations to Eva Perón’s heirs.

The Chamber of Deputies investigated. A judicial proceeding initiated during or immediately around her government was reopened after the coup.

This controversy later produced a conviction, discussed below.

## Medical and psychological crisis; Luder’s interim presidency

In September 1975 Isabel took medical leave. Reports described exhaustion, nervous strain, depression, or related illness. **Ítalo Luder**, president of the Senate, exercised the presidency from 13 September until Isabel resumed office in October.

During Luder’s tenure, Decrees 2770, 2771, and 2772 created a national security structure and extended the order to “annihilate” subversive activity throughout Argentina.

Isabel’s return prevented plans to replace her permanently through succession. Political discussions included:

- Resignation.
- Impeachment.
- Permanent succession by Luder.
- A civilian-military arrangement modeled on Uruguay’s Juan María Bordaberry.
- Early elections.

No such constitutional transfer occurred.

## Final months

Economy ministers changed repeatedly:

- Pedro José Bonanni.
- Antonio Cafiero.
- Emilio Mondelli.

Inflation accelerated, and by March 1976 monthly price increases reached levels sometimes described as Argentina’s first hyperinflationary outbreak.

Political violence included:

- The Montoneros attack at Formosa in October 1975.
- The ERP assault on the Monte Chingolo arsenal in December 1975.
- State and para-state kidnappings and killings.
- Military pressure on the civilian government.
- An attempted Air Force uprising led by Brigadier Jesús Cappellini in December 1975.

The government advanced elections from 1977 to late 1976, but military commanders were already preparing a coup.

## Removal by coup, 24 March 1976

Shortly after midnight Isabel left the Casa Rosada by presidential helicopter, ostensibly for the Olivos residence. At approximately 3:00 a.m., military officers informed her that the armed forces had taken control and that she was under arrest.

A junta headed by Jorge Rafael Videla, Emilio Massera, and Orlando Agosti assumed power.

The coup was unconstitutional. The military cited disorder, corruption, economic collapse, and insurgency as justification. Subsequent evidence shows that the armed forces had planned systematic repression and disappearances beyond the already extensive illegal violence of the constitutional period.

## Imprisonment, Prosecution, and Release, 1976–1981

### Detention sites

Isabel was detained without ordinary freedom of movement for more than five years. Her places of confinement included:

- **El Messidor**, the presidential residence near Villa La Angostura, Neuquén.
- The **Azopardo naval facility** near Azul, Buenos Aires Province.
- The Perón estate at **San Vicente**, under guarded or house-arrest conditions.

Her confinement combined the junta’s state-of-siege powers with judicial proceedings.

### Health

Reports from confinement describe:

- Severe anxiety and panic.
- Depression or emotional instability.
- Irritable colon and back pain.
- Kinesiological treatment.
- A stomach-pumping emergency.
- A reported suicide attempt in June 1977 by ingesting Valium before a judicial interrogation.

The suicide-attempt account is repeated in biographies and later reporting, but complete medical records were not located. It should be treated as a strongly reported fact rather than independently verified clinical documentation.

### Judicial cases

#### Cruzada de Solidaridad

In May 1976 Judge Nino García Moritán processed her and other officials for alleged misuse of charitable funds.

On 19 March 1981 federal judge Norberto Giletta sentenced her to eight years’ imprisonment in proceedings concerning funds from the Cruzada. Contemporary reporting described the questioned amounts as approaching nine million U.S. dollars, although currency conversion and the composition of the total vary by account.

The prosecution involved, among other matters, an official or charitable cheque deposited into an account associated with her and payments connected to Perón’s inheritance.

**Evidentiary status:** conviction, not merely allegation.

Her defenders characterized the case as political justice under a dictatorship. The political context does not erase the conviction, but it is relevant to assessing due process and the military government’s selective prosecution.

#### Reserved funds

Accounts report that she was ultimately cleared or received a favorable disposition in a separate reserved-funds case.

#### Bemberg property

A separate proceeding concerned the transfer or donation to the Partido Justicialista of property previously confiscated from the Bemberg family. Sources describe another adverse judicial decision, although accessible summaries are less consistent about its final procedural status than for the Cruzada case.

### Release

She was released in July 1981 after serving approximately two-thirds of the adjusted sentence or period counted toward it. She then left Argentina for Spain.

---

## Later Political Role and Life in Spain, 1981–present

## Return to Madrid

After release, Isabel settled again in Spain. She lived for periods at or in connection with Puerta de Hierro and later in private residences in the Madrid region.

Reports that she considered entering a convent belong to recollections of acquaintances and were never realized publicly.

## Position in the Partido Justicialista

Despite exile, she remained formally important within the Justicialist Party. Before the restoration of democracy in 1983, Peronist leaders negotiated with her because she retained titular authority and the Perón name.

She issued or published **Las verdades del justicialismo** in 1983.

She returned to Argentina around the democratic transition and participated in meetings concerning party organization. She eventually relinquished active party leadership.

## Relationship with democratic governments

Raúl Alfonsín’s government restored property seized by the dictatorship and addressed her legal and patrimonial status. She attended official or political occasions intermittently.

She attended Carlos Menem’s presidential inaugurations in 1989 and 1995 according to biographical records. Another report states that her last visit to Argentina was in 1994; the exact dating of her final physical presence should therefore be treated cautiously.

Under Menem, she reached a settlement with the Argentine state for property or funds confiscated during military rule. Accounts place the payment above four million dollars. She also received the lifetime pension legally accorded to former presidents.

## Withdrawal from public life

From the 1990s onward she gave almost no substantive interviews. Her silence became one of the dominant features of her reputation.

In 1997 she testified in Madrid before Judge Baltasar Garzón in proceedings concerning Argentine disappearances. Contemporary reporting said she described herself as having been a “puppet” and denied knowledge of disappearances or paramilitary activity. That statement is a reported account of her testimony, not a judicial finding.

## 2007 arrest and extradition litigation

On 12 January 2007 Spanish authorities briefly detained her at the request of an Argentine judge investigating the disappearance of Héctor Aldo Fagetti Gallego and related pre-coup repression.

A second request concerned Triple A crimes. She was released provisionally while extradition proceedings continued.

In April 2008 the Spanish Audiencia Nacional rejected extradition. It concluded, in substance, that:

- The requests did not adequately demonstrate her personal participation in crimes against humanity.
- Offenses not qualifying as crimes against humanity were prescribed under the applicable extradition analysis.

The decision did not determine, after a full evidentiary trial, that she lacked all knowledge or political responsibility.

## Operativo Independencia proceedings

Argentine prosecutors later sought to question her concerning approximately 270 victims of Operativo Independencia.

The litigation did not produce her extradition or a criminal trial. In 2017 Argentina’s Supreme Court declined procedural petitions seeking to compel her interrogation as either witness or defendant.

Her legal status is therefore accurately summarized as:

- Convicted in 1981 in the Cruzada financial case.
- Investigated and sought for questioning or extradition in human-rights cases.
- Never convicted of Triple A or Operativo Independencia crimes.
- Never fully tried on the merits for those accusations.
- Protected in practice by failed extradition requests, prescription findings in Spain, procedural rulings, age, and residence abroad.

## Property and inheritance litigation

Isabel was Perón’s principal widow and heir, but Perón’s estate generated decades of litigation involving:

- Eva Perón’s sisters and heirs.
- Properties at Puerta de Hierro and San Vicente.
- Houses and accounts in Argentina and Spain.
- Funds restored after military confiscation.
- The Martha Holgado paternity claim.
- Later claims by Mario Rotundo concerning archival material and assets.

In 1986 reporting stated that Isabel was ordered to pay approximately two million dollars to Eva Perón’s sisters for inheritance rights.

In 2002 litigation in Madrid concerned approximately six million dollars remaining from Perón’s estate. Public reporting is insufficient to reconstruct a complete, audited balance sheet of Isabel’s present wealth.

Claims that she possesses hundreds of millions of dollars, extensive parking enterprises, or hidden international accounts circulate online but were not supported by reliable documentary evidence found in this research.

## Spanish citizenship

She reportedly obtained Spanish citizenship around 2000 while retaining Argentine citizenship. Spain’s rejection of extradition in 2008 did not rest solely on nationality; it involved the legal characterization and prescription of the alleged crimes.

## Recent reappearances

### Preserva award

In 2023–2024 photographs circulated showing her receiving a **Premio Hispanidad** from the Spanish association Preserva at her home. This was a private organizational distinction, not an award from the Spanish state.

### Victoria Villarruel meeting and Senate bust, 2024

In October 2024 Argentine Vice-President Victoria Villarruel disclosed that she had visited Isabel in Madrid. Photographs showed the two holding hands in Isabel’s residence.

On 17 October 2024 Villarruel inaugurated a bust of Isabel in the Argentine Senate’s Salón de las Provincias, calling it an act of historical reparation.

The bust was placed where a bust of Néstor Kirchner had previously stood. The event became part of a contemporary political dispute over:

- Whether Isabel had been erased from Peronist memory.
- Cristina Fernández de Kirchner’s symbolic status as the first woman elected directly to the presidency.
- The historical interpretation of 1970s political violence.
- Villarruel’s position on state terrorism.

Isabel did not travel to Argentina for the event.

### 2025–2026 status

No reliable source located for 2025–September 2026 reported her death, renewed prosecution, or a return to Argentina. Searches did not uncover a new court finding concerning her during those years.

Some search results dated in 2026 merely republished old 2007–2008 extradition news without clearly indicating their original date. These were not treated as new legal developments.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

No evidence was found that Isabel created or managed a conventional private corporation, patented inventions, or developed technology.

Her significant institutional and financial connections were:

- The touring dance company or companies of her early career.
- Juan Perón’s Madrid household and political secretariat.
- Partido Justicialista.
- Argentine national executive and Senate.
- Ministry of Social Welfare through López Rega’s network.
- Cruzada de Solidaridad Justicialista.
- Perón’s estate and associated properties.
- Former-presidential pension and compensation from the Argentine state.

### Perón estate

Known assets or disputed interests included:

- Quinta 17 de Octubre at Puerta de Hierro, Madrid.
- Quinta de San Vicente, Buenos Aires Province.
- Argentine houses and bank accounts.
- Political, documentary, and personal archives.
- Rights arising from Perón’s works, possessions, and restored assets.

No complete reliable public inventory of her present assets was located.

## Political, Religious, and Ideological Development

### Peronism

Isabel’s political identity was derivative by her own presentation: loyalty to Juan Perón, justicialist doctrine, vertical command, social justice rhetoric, organized labor, national sovereignty, and the “Third Position.”

Her speeches stressed:

- National unity.
- Christian and family values.
- Social justice.
- Fidelity to Perón.
- Opposition to violence.
- Anti-subversion.
- Latin American cooperation.
- The organized community.

### Rightward trajectory

Her 1960s missions sometimes involved cooperation with sectors later associated with left Peronism because their immediate purpose was defending Perón’s leadership against Vandor. By 1973–1975 she was aligned with orthodox, conservative, and anti-communist sectors.

This was not necessarily a fully articulated personal ideology. It was also a product of:

- Perón’s late political strategy.
- López Rega’s influence.
- Armed conflict inside Peronism.
- Police and military pressure.
- Cold War regional politics.

### Catholicism and esotericism

She publicly operated within Catholic ritual and institutions. The spiritualist Cresto household and López Rega’s occult practices formed a parallel private current.

Claims that her government was literally governed by astrology are exaggerated. López Rega’s esoteric worldview mattered to interpersonal trust and symbolism, but government decisions also had identifiable institutional, economic, military, and political causes.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Juan Domingo Perón

Husband, mentor, political principal, and source of her public authority. She served as companion, household administrator, emissary, vice-president, and constitutional successor.

### José López Rega

Secretary, spiritual adviser, minister, patronage broker, and dominant political associate. She facilitated his entry into Perón’s household and defended him until the 1975 crisis forced his departure.

### Raúl Lastiri

López Rega’s son-in-law; reportedly helped introduce her to López Rega. Interim president in 1973 and member of the governing network.

### José Ignacio Rucci

CGT leader and supporter of the Perón–Perón ticket. His murder in September 1973 accelerated Perónism’s internal war.

### Ítalo Luder

Senate president and constitutional acting president during her 1975 leave. Potential successor around whom plans for her permanent removal coalesced.

### Lorenzo Miguel and the CGT leadership

Initially essential pillars of the government. Organized labor’s strikes against the Rodrigazo helped remove Rodrigo and López Rega and demonstrated the limits of Isabel’s authority.

### José Ber Gelbard

Economy minister inherited from Cámpora and Perón. His Social Pact deteriorated, and Isabel removed him in 1974.

### Celestino Rodrigo

Economy minister responsible for the Rodrigazo. His brief tenure precipitated the government’s most severe labor and economic crisis.

### Armed-forces commanders

Her relations with Jorge Rafael Videla, Emilio Massera, Orlando Agosti, and other commanders evolved from constitutional command and delegation of anti-insurgency operations to open military pressure and, finally, her overthrow and imprisonment.

### Licio Gelli and P2-associated circles

Licio Gelli had documented access to Perón and Isabel through Italian intermediaries. Perón decorated him with the Order of the Liberator General San Martín in 1973. Gelli was associated with economic advisory activity around Argentina’s Rome embassy, while López Rega, Lastiri, Massera, and other Argentines appeared in the broader P2 network.

A personal meeting involving Isabel, Perón, Gelli, and Giulio Andreotti was later reported by Andreotti. This establishes contact, not Isabel’s membership in P2. No reliable membership record for Isabel herself was located.

### Augusto Pinochet

Her government developed relations with Pinochet’s Chile. Isabel met Pinochet at Morón Air Base in April 1975. Scholars have examined this meeting as part of emerging regional anti-subversive coordination.

Evidence supports political-security cooperation and mutual concern about armed opposition. It does not by itself establish that Isabel personally designed Operation Condor, formally created later in 1975 by security representatives.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Court Findings

## 1. Triple A

**Documented:** Triple A crimes; use of state-linked personnel and resources; López Rega’s central position; operation during her government.

**Judicial finding concerning the organization:** its crimes were characterized in Argentina as crimes against humanity.

**Allegation concerning Isabel:** knowledge, authorization, association, or command responsibility.

**Outcome:** extradition denied; no completed trial; no conviction or merits acquittal.

## 2. Operativo Independencia

**Documented:** she signed Decree 261/1975; illegal detention, torture, disappearances, and killings occurred during the operation.

**Allegation:** her decree and civilian command role made her criminally responsible.

**Counter-position:** military planners conducted illegal methods independently as part of a developing coup project.

**Outcome:** no trial or conviction of Isabel; military perpetrators were prosecuted separately.

## 3. Cruzada de Solidaridad

**Documented allegation followed by conviction:** diversion or improper handling of charitable funds.

**Outcome:** eight-year sentence in 1981, adjusted in relation to time served. Political motivation and dictatorship-era due-process concerns were asserted by her defenders.

## 4. Reserved funds

**Investigation:** questioned use of executive reserved funds.

**Outcome:** reported dismissal or acquittal in this separate matter.

## 5. Bemberg property

**Controversy/court proceeding:** donation or transfer to the Justicialist Party of confiscated property associated with the Bemberg family.

**Outcome:** sources report an adverse judgment, but accessible accounts differ on its precise finality and sentence.

## 6. Perón inheritance

**Documented litigation:** disputes with Eva Perón’s sisters, Martha Holgado, and Mario Rotundo.

**No reliable proof:** popular claims of a vast secret fortune or hundreds of millions of dollars.

## 7. Political incapacity

Opponents and later commentators frequently described her as unintelligent, mentally unstable, or wholly controlled by men.

The evidentiary record supports limited formal education, severe strain in office, illness, and López Rega’s influence. It does not support diagnosing permanent incapacity from political caricature alone. Her successful missions against Vandor and persistence against efforts to remove her show some capacity for tactical action.

## 8. Responsibility for the coup

Claims that she “caused” the 1976 dictatorship are political judgments, not documentary findings. Her government’s failures and pre-coup repression formed part of the context. The armed forces independently planned, executed, and administered the coup and subsequent systematic terror.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## “López Rega tried to place Evita’s spirit inside Isabel”

### Story

López Rega supposedly conducted rituals with Eva Perón’s embalmed body at Puerta de Hierro, attempting to transfer Evita’s spirit or political charisma into Isabel.

### Appearance and circulation

The story appears in biographies of López Rega, journalistic retellings, and accounts of the return of Eva’s body to Perón in Madrid in 1971. It was circulated by political insiders, hostile biographers, and later popular histories.

### Evidence

- López Rega’s occult interests are documented.
- Eva’s body was present at Puerta de Hierro.
- Isabel and López Rega had access to it.
- Accounts of grooming, combing, ritual gestures, or invocations rest mainly on testimony and retelling rather than contemporaneous photographs or formal records.

### Assessment

**Reported story with mythic elaboration.** Some form of private ritual behavior is plausible, but literal spirit transfer is a belief claim, not an empirically established event.

### Reputational effect

It fused Isabel with a counterfeit-Evita image: the dancer transformed into a political vessel by a sorcerer. Both anti-Peronists and Peronists hostile to López Rega used it to explain the government through occult pathology.

## “Isabel was a cabaret prostitute”

### Story

She was not merely a dancer but a prostitute who attached herself to Perón.

### Evidence

Entertainment work and nightclub dancing are documented. No reliable evidence consulted establishes prostitution.

### Assessment

**Hostile rumor.** It reflects gendered political defamation and the social ambiguity attached to nightclub performance.

## “She was only a puppet”

### Story

Perón, then López Rega, and finally the military controlled every decision.

### Evidence

She openly grounded her authority in Perón; López Rega had extraordinary influence; military pressure became overwhelming.

Counterevidence includes her independent travel missions, role in intra-Peronist organization, refusal to resign, return from medical leave, and participation in personnel decisions.

### Assessment

**Political metaphor containing a documented element of dependence but overstated as total incapacity.**

## “She founded or personally commanded the Triple A”

### Evidence

No located document identifies her as the organization’s operational founder or commander. López Rega and state-security personnel occupy that role in judicial and historical findings.

### Assessment

**Unproved allegation** if asserted as direct operational command. Political and possible command responsibility remain legitimate subjects of investigation.

## “She was completely innocent because Spain refused extradition”

Spain’s ruling addressed extradition standards, legal characterization, evidence of personal participation, and prescription. It was not a full criminal trial.

### Assessment

**Misleading legal myth.**

## “She was convicted of crimes against humanity”

She was convicted in the Cruzada financial case, not of Triple A killings or Operativo Independencia crimes.

### Assessment

**False conflation.**

## “Peronism erased her so Cristina Kirchner could be called the first woman president”

Some Peronist traditions minimized Isabel because her government was associated with López Rega, the Triple A, economic collapse, and the coup. Cristina Fernández de Kirchner was the first woman elected directly to Argentina’s presidency but not the first woman to hold the office.

The stronger conspiracy claim—that an organized erasure existed solely to benefit Cristina—lacks documentary proof. Isabel’s marginalization began decades before Cristina’s presidency.

### Assessment

**Partly grounded political narrative, with an unproved conspiratorial extension.**

## “She possessed Perón’s enormous hidden fortune”

Perón did leave disputed property and funds, and Isabel received state compensation and a pension. Exact present wealth is unknown.

Claims of hundreds of millions, secret business empires, or extensive concealed accounts were not substantiated.

### Assessment

**Rumor built around real inheritance litigation.**

## “She was the world’s first woman president”

The claim is broadly correct when carefully defined as the first woman to serve as both head of state and government in a republican presidential system. It becomes misleading if used to deny earlier female monarchs or prime ministers.

### Assessment

**Historically supportable with definitional qualification.**

## Successes and Failures

### Documentable successes

- Maintained Perón’s authority during important 1965–1966 factional struggles.
- Became the first woman elected vice-president of Argentina.
- Became Argentina’s first woman president through constitutional succession.
- Preserved formal constitutional institutions until overthrown; Congress and party politics continued despite emergency rule and repression.
- Survived prolonged imprisonment and later recovered property and civil status.
- Retained symbolic influence within parts of Peronism despite decades of silence.

### Documentable failures or adverse outcomes

- Failed to stabilize the governing coalition after Perón’s death.
- Protected López Rega until the Rodrigazo crisis made his position untenable.
- Presided over an expansion of para-state and state repression.
- Signed the decree initiating military “annihilation” operations in Tucumán.
- Failed to control inflation, fiscal dislocation, and wage collapse.
- Suffered repeated cabinet turnover and loss of authority to unions and armed forces.
- Was constitutionally overthrown before completing her term.
- Was convicted of financial misconduct in the Cruzada case.

## Works by María Estela Martínez de Perón

Her attributable corpus is small and consists principally of institutional speeches, messages, interviews, and one doctrinal book.

### Books

1. **Las verdades del justicialismo**. Buenos Aires: Ediciones Castañeda, 1983; approximately 132 pages; ISBN 950-9014-07-9 / 978-950-9014-07-7.  
   - Published under her name.
   - A commentary on Peronist doctrine and its “Twenty Truths.”

   - These are official utterances delivered or authorized by her, not necessarily privately written prose.

### Major official speeches and messages

Among the materials preserved or catalogued are:

- Address announcing Juan Perón’s death, 1 July 1974.
- Messages to Congress.

- Speeches invoking the Social Pact, National Reconstruction and Liberation, Latin American unity, the Third Position, and Perón’s legacy.

Her correspondence is dispersed among:

- Juan Perón archival collections.
- Partido Justicialista records.
- Argentine government and diplomatic archives.
- Private papers of Peronist intermediaries.

No complete critical edition of her correspondence was located.

## Later Reputation and Legacy

Her reputation divides into several partially overlapping constructions.

### Constitutional pioneer

She is recognized as Argentina’s first woman president and first woman vice-president. This status has been revived by those arguing that later histories improperly centered Cristina Fernández de Kirchner as the first female presidential figure.

### Failed and dependent ruler

A longstanding interpretation depicts her as unprepared, dependent on López Rega, and unable to manage the state. This reading draws support from cabinet instability, the Rodrigazo, medical leave, and military pressure but is often expressed through caricature rather than analysis.

### Civilian antecedent to state terror

Human-rights scholarship emphasizes that illegal state repression, clandestine detention, and para-state murder did not begin on 24 March 1976. Her government is therefore central to understanding continuities between constitutional rule and dictatorship.

This does not erase the dictatorship’s distinct responsibility for designing and expanding a systematic national program of disappearance.

### Victim of the dictatorship

She was herself overthrown, detained for more than five years, held under exceptional powers, and prosecuted by the regime. This status coexists with unresolved questions about abuses committed under her presidency.

### Marginalized Peronist

Most post-1983 Peronist traditions gave her little ceremonial prominence. Reasons include:

- The Triple A.
- López Rega.
- Economic collapse.
- Her financial conviction.
- The catastrophic end of the government.
- Her absence and silence.
- Later ideological realignments inside Peronism.

### Twenty-first-century rehabilitation

The 2024 Villarruel tribute recast her as:

- A constitutionally elected pioneer.
- A woman abandoned by her party.
- A victim of terrorism and military dictatorship.
- A symbol in contemporary conflict with Kirchnerism.

Critics answered that this rehabilitation minimized pre-coup state violence and the Triple A. The dispute demonstrates that Isabel functions less as an active political actor today than as a contested symbol of memory.

## Atlas Connections

Only roster members for whom a documented or credibly reported crossing was found are included. Shared nationality, chronology, or ideological resemblance alone was not counted.

### Juan Domingo Perón — documented fact

- Met in Panama in late 1955.
- Cohabited from approximately January 1956.
- Traveled with him through exile in Latin America and Spain.
- Married him in Madrid on 15 November 1961.
- Served as his political emissary, vice-president, and constitutional successor.
- Present in his Madrid household and final government until his death on 1 July 1974.

### Eva Duarte de Perón — documented posthumous relationship; ritual claims disputed

- Isabel never had a documented living meeting with Eva, who died in 1952.
- As Juan Perón’s third wife, Isabel lived in the political and symbolic shadow of Eva.
- Eva’s embalmed body was returned to Perón at Puerta de Hierro in 1971, where Isabel and López Rega had access to it.
- Stories that López Rega tried to transfer Eva’s spirit or charisma into Isabel are reported mythology with uncertain evidentiary foundations.
- Isabel later litigated inheritance questions involving Eva’s sisters and estate.

### José López Rega — documented fact

- Met during Isabel’s 1965–1966 Argentine mission.
- Returned with her to Madrid.
- Became secretary and adviser to the Perón household.
- Served as Social Welfare minister and dominant associate during her presidency.
- His ministry and security network were tied judicially and historically to the Triple A.
- Forced from effective power in July 1975.

### Augusto Pinochet Ugarte — documented fact

- Pinochet met Juan Perón at Morón Air Base on 16 May 1974; Isabel was part of the governing setting.
- Isabel personally met Pinochet at Morón in April 1975.
- Their governments discussed bilateral and security matters amid expanding regional anti-subversive coordination.
- Claims that this meeting alone proves her personal authorship of Operation Condor exceed the evidence.

### Licio Gelli — reported/documented contact; broader-control theories disputed

- Gelli had access to Juan and Isabel Perón through Italian and P2-associated intermediaries.
- Giulio Andreotti later described a meeting involving Perón, Isabel, and Gelli.
- Gelli received Argentina’s Gran Cruz de la Orden del Libertador General San Martín under Juan Perón.
- He was connected to economic advisory and embassy networks relevant to Isabel’s government.
- No documentary proof was found that Isabel was herself a formal P2 member.
- Claims that P2 secretly controlled her entire government remain a theory, not a court finding.

### Cristina Elisabet Fernández de Kirchner — documented symbolic/political connection, not a personal crossing

- No documented personal meeting was located.
- Isabel’s legacy became directly entangled with Fernández de Kirchner’s claim to be Argentina’s first woman elected president.
- In 2024 Villarruel used her tribute to Isabel as an explicit intervention against Kirchnerist historical memory.
- This is a documented relationship of political memory, not a personal association.

### Javier Gerardo Milei — reported institutional connection, no documented meeting

- Milei’s government invited or considered inviting living former presidents, including Isabel, to the 2024 May Pact.
- No reliable evidence was found that she attended or personally met Milei.
- Vice-President Villarruel’s later tribute occurred within the Milei administration but was strongly identified with Villarruel personally.

### Other roster names

No reliably supported direct crossing was found with Fidel Castro, Salvador Allende, Ernesto “Che” Guevara, Jorge Luis Borges, Gabriel García Márquez, Carlos Gardel, Diego Maradona, Charly García, Jorge Rafael Videla—who is not included in the supplied roster—or the many other roster members. Mere participation in the same international era or institutional field was excluded.
