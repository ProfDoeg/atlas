## Works by Publius Aelius Hadrianus

### Literary obra: books and sustained compositions

- **Late 130s?:** *Autobiography*. A self-authored life, now lost except for reported fragments, apparently circulated under the names of learned freedmen—especially Phlegon—and perhaps shaped as letters to Antoninus Pius; both its form and the claim of pseudonymous publication depend chiefly on the unreliable *Historia Augusta*. ([*Historia Augusta*, *Hadrian* 16](https://www.intratext.com/IXT/LAT0229/_P6.HTM); [Loeb commentary](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Historia_Augusta/Hadrian/1%2A.html))

- **Date unknown:** *Catacannae*. Lost, reputedly obscure books of verse written in imitation of Antimachus; neither their number nor even the exact force of the transmitted title is secure. ([*Historia Augusta*, *Hadrian* 16](https://www.intratext.com/IXT/LAT0229/_P6.HTM))

- **Date unknown:** *Works on grammar*. An attested but wholly lost body of grammatical writing, without recoverable titles or boundaries; it therefore resists division into individual works. ([Harold N. Fowler, *A History of Roman Literature*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/44975/44975-h/44975-h.htm))

- **Before 138:** *Speeches and declamations*. Hadrian delivered—and reportedly published—speeches and practised forensic controversiae, but no authoritative ancient catalogue separates them into stable titles. The category is a lost body of performance-texts rather than a reconstructible set of books. ([*Historia Augusta*, *Hadrian* 16](https://www.intratext.com/IXT/LAT0229/_P6.HTM); [Fowler](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/44975/44975-h/44975-h.htm))

- **Annually; final series 137–138:** *Astrological forecasts for his own year*. The *Historia Augusta* says Hadrian wrote on each New Year’s Day what would happen to him during the coming year, continuing in his last forecast to the hour of his death. These lost texts are closer to personal prognostic protocols than literature, and the sensational precision of the report is not independently verifiable. ([*Historia Augusta*, *Hadrian* 16](https://www.intratext.com/IXT/LAT0229/_P6.HTM))

### Poems and epigrams

- **Before 117:** *Dedication of Trajan’s Dacian spoils to Zeus Kasios* (Greek Anthology 6.332). A Greek dedicatory epigram praying that Trajan’s Dacian victory be followed by victory over Parthia; the anthology attributes it to Hadrian, while part of the manuscript tradition instead names Trajan. ([Perseus catalogue](https://catalog.perseus.tufts.edu/catalog/urn%3Acts%3AgreekLit%3Atlg0195.tlg001.opp-eng1); [scholarly discussion](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/372982884_A_Tripod_%27Worth_Seeing%27_in_the_Olympieion_at_Athens_Paus_1188))

- **Date unknown:** *Epitaph for Archilochus* (Greek Anthology 7.674). A Greek literary epitaph linking Archilochus’s furious iambics with Homer; it is a fictive commemorative text, not necessarily writing made for an actual tomb. ([Cambridge discussion](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/classical-quarterly/article/abs/o-matre-pvlchra-the-logical-iambist/85AB07D8982FC594EA11B1FB34050C2C))

- **Date unknown:** *Reply to the half-dead grammarian* (Greek Anthology 9.137). A two-line Greek epigram answering a grammarian’s verse petition with a mythological pun; its identity is an attributed poetic exchange rather than an independently transmitted authorial manuscript. ([Anthologia Graeca](https://anthologiagraeca.org/passages/urn%3Acts%3AgreekLit%3Atlg7000.tlg001.ag%3A9.137/))

- **Date unknown:** *Epitaph for Hector* (Greek Anthology 9.387). A Greek literary epitaph treating the Trojan hero as an ancestral figure; Hadrian’s attribution is expressly considered doubtful in modern discussion. ([Ewen Bowie discussion excerpt](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/315535609_Tiberius_and_Hellenistic_Poetry))

- **130:** *On Pompey’s mean tomb* (Greek Anthology 9.402). A one-line Greek epigram lamenting the inadequacy of Pompey’s Egyptian grave, associated with Hadrian’s visit and restoration of the monument; its anthology placement and the historical report together support, but do not prove, authorship. ([*Historia Augusta* commentary](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Historia_Augusta/Hadrian/1%2A.html); [Baldwin, “Literary and Artistic Patronage”](https://dokumen.pub/literary-and-artistic-patronage-in-ancient-rome-9780292765900.html))

- **Date unknown:** *Epigram for a slain bear* (IG VII 1828). An inscriptional Greek hunting epigram attributed in scholarship to Hadrian; the attribution is not as firmly transmitted as those attached to his name in the Greek Anthology. ([Bowie discussion excerpt](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/315535609_Tiberius_and_Hellenistic_Poetry))

- **Probably 122:** *Epitaph for Borysthenes* (CIL XII 1122; ILN Apt 33). A sixteen-line Latin funerary inscription for Hadrian’s hunting horse, physically preserved in fragments from Apt; Gascou and Janon regard Hadrian’s authorship as certain, although older editions of his literary remains did not include it. ([Gascou and Janon, “Les chevaux d’Hadrien”](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/272346673_Les_chevaux_d%27Hadrien))

- **Date unknown:** *Epitaph for Samis* (CIL XIV 3911). A Latin verse inscription from Aquae Albulae for another hunting horse; its resemblance to the Borysthenes poem places it near Hadrian’s oeuvre, but the surviving evidence does not establish his authorship securely. ([Gascou and Janon](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/272346673_Les_chevaux_d%27Hadrien))

- **Date unknown:** *Reply to Florus*. A four-line Latin verse response declining the poet Florus’s imagined life of taverns, shops, and mosquitoes; it survives only as a quoted exchange in the *Historia Augusta*, whose wording and attribution cannot be independently checked. ([*Historia Augusta*, *Hadrian* 16](https://www.intratext.com/IXT/LAT0229/_P6.HTM))

- **138:** *Animula vagula blandula*. A five-line Latin address to the departing soul, reportedly composed near death; it is complete as quoted but untitled in antiquity, and both its circumstances and authorship rest on later biographical transmission. ([study of Hadrian’s three Latin poems](https://avesis.istanbul.edu.tr/yayin/a1b8801e-9ca8-4604-97b4-c04a0b7e8a0b/sophron-guler-celgine-armagan-yazilar-arkeoloji-ve-sanat-yayinlari-istanbul))

- **Date unknown:** *Other Greek poems*. The *Historia Augusta* says Hadrian made further Greek poems but supplies neither titles nor an inventory. This is an attested body of work that cannot responsibly be decomposed into individual entries. ([*Historia Augusta*, *Hadrian* 16](https://www.intratext.com/IXT/LAT0229/_P6.HTM))

### Imperial acts: letters, laws, and protocols

- **121:** *Rescript on the Epicurean succession* (Oliver, *Constitutions* 73). A Latin legal response allowing the Athenian Epicurean scholarch to make a Greek will and choose either a Roman citizen or a non-citizen as successor; it survives inside a three-letter dossier with Plotina’s petition and announcement. As an imperial rescript it acts rather than merely describes. ([Attic Inscriptions Online](https://atticinscriptions.com/inscription/OliverConstitutions/73))

- **125:** *Letters on the Epicurean succession* (Oliver 74). The first letter securely restates the earlier privilege; authorship of the dossier’s longer second letter is disputed between Hadrian and Plotina, and extensive modern restorations remain conjectural. ([Attic Inscriptions Online](https://atticinscriptions.com/inscription/OliverConstitutions/74))

- **120s–130s:** *Letter banning the resale of allotted goods* (Oliver 77; IG II² 1103). An inscribed regulatory letter to Athens: a protocol backed by imperial authority, preserved incompletely rather than as a literary publication. ([Attic Inscriptions Online](https://atticinscriptions.com/inscription/OliverConstitutions/77))

- **126:** *Letter to the Achaian League* (Oliver 78). An imperial letter accepting some honours and refusing others as excessive; the text is fragmentary and survives through inscriptional copies connected with the League’s decree. ([Attic Inscriptions Online](https://www.atticinscriptions.com/inscription/OliverConstitutions/78))

- **After 128:** *Letter to the Athenians* (Oliver 83; IG II² 1101). A fragmentary civic letter preserved on stone; lost portions prevent a secure account of its entire purpose. ([Attic Inscriptions Online](https://atticinscriptions.com/inscription/OliverConstitutions/83))

- **132:** *Letter on gifts for the boys* (Oliver 85). An inscribed administrative letter governing benefactions for Athenian youths; its operative civic function makes it a protocol or enactment, not a “book.” ([Attic Inscriptions Online](https://atticinscriptions.com/inscription/OliverConstitutions/85))

- **125–138:** *Law on the Athenian olive harvest and export* (Oliver 92). A detailed imperial regulation, conventionally called “Hadrian’s law,” controlling the delivery and export of olive oil; it is a manifesto only in the literal sense that inscription made imperial policy public, but formally it is a legal protocol. ([Attic Inscriptions Online](https://atticinscriptions.com/inscription/OliverConstitutions/92))

- **134–138:** *Letter to the Artists of Dionysos* (Oliver 97; SEG 33.139). An inscribed imperial communication to a professional performers’ association; the surviving stone, not an authorial copy, defines the work available to us. ([Attic Inscriptions Online](https://atticinscriptions.com/inscription/OliverConstitutions/97))

- **128–138:** *Dossier concerning the musical Artists of the Dancer* (Oliver 98–104). A composite archive containing several imperial letters and associated documents, some fragmentary and not all written by Hadrian. It resists treatment as one authored work, but the dossier is the surviving manifestation through which his individual acts are known. ([Attic Inscriptions Online](https://atticinscriptions.com/inscription/OliverConstitutions/98-104))

- **Hadrian’s reign:** *Other imperial letters, edicts, and rescripts*. Scores of texts survive piecemeal in inscriptions, papyri, and later legal compilations; chancery production makes “authorship” institutional and sometimes scribal rather than personally Hadrianic. Oliver’s standard corpus contains 293 Greek imperial documents across several reigns, while Martín’s Hadrianic corpus and later discoveries show that no final closed list exists. ([review of Oliver’s corpus](https://www.persee.fr/doc/reg_0035-2039_1991_num_104_495_2512_t1_0275_0000_1); [Projet Volterra](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/social-historical-sciences/laws-database-projet-volterra); [survey of the dispersed corpus](https://etd.ohiolink.edu/acprod/odb_etd/ws/send_file/send?accession=osu1205903125&disposition=inline))

### Architectural projects and Gesamtkunstwerke

- **About 117–138:** *Hadrian’s Villa at Tibur*. A vast residential, ceremonial, hydraulic, sculptural, and landscape ensemble begun early in the reign and still developing near Hadrian’s death: properly a Gesamtkunstwerk and continuing project rather than a single building. Direct personal design is plausible for innovative portions but cannot be assigned uniformly across the estate. ([UNESCO dossier and chronology summarized here](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadrian%27s_Villa); [study of Hadrianic design](https://iris.uniroma1.it/handle/11573/1504838))

- **About 118–128:** *Pantheon reconstruction*. Hadrian’s government replaced Agrippa’s burned building with the surviving porch-and-rotunda complex while retaining Agrippa’s name on the façade. Calling it “made by Hadrian” means an imperial commission: the architect is unknown, and attribution of the design to Hadrian or Apollodorus remains disputed. ([Pantheon Museum](https://pantheon.museum/the-pantheon/history/); [Hadrianic architecture study](https://iris.uniroma1.it/handle/11573/1504838))

- **From 121; inaugurated 136/137; completed 141:** *Temple of Venus and Roma*. Rome’s enormous double-cella temple was begun from a design ancient tradition assigns to Hadrian, inaugurated by him, and completed under Antoninus Pius. It is consequently both Hadrian’s architectural project and an obra whose finished manifestation postdates its maker. ([Parco archeologico del Colosseo](https://colosseo.it/en/marvels/temple-of-venus-and-roma/))

- **About 123–139:** *Mausoleum of Hadrian*. A dynastic tomb and monumental riverfront complex begun by Hadrian and completed after his death under Antoninus Pius; its later identity as Castel Sant’Angelo is a succession of transformations, not Hadrian’s original work. ([Open University, *Hadrian’s Rome*](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/history-the-arts/hadrians-rome/content-section-2.2))

- **From 122:** *Hadrian’s Wall*. An approximately eighty-Roman-mile frontier system of wall, forts, milecastles, turrets, roads, and earthworks ordered after Hadrian’s visit to Britain. It is a state and military project rather than evidence that the emperor personally designed or built its components. ([*Historia Augusta*, *Hadrian* 11](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Historia_Augusta/Hadrian/1%2A.html))

- **About 122:** *Basilica in honour of Plotina at Nemausus*. An imperially sponsored basilica at modern Nîmes, praised by the ancient biography as exceptionally fine but not securely identified archaeologically. Its physical form and designer are unknown. ([*Historia Augusta*, *Hadrian* 12](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Historia_Augusta/Hadrian/1%2A.html))

- **122–123:** *Restoration of the Temple of Augustus at Tarraco*. Hadrian paid for the restoration during his stay at Tarragona; this was an act of architectural patronage, not a wholly new authored building. ([*Historia Augusta*, *Hadrian* 12](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Historia_Augusta/Hadrian/1%2A.html))

- **Hadrian’s reign:** *Temple of the Deified Trajan and Plotina*. A temple erected beside Trajan’s Forum as a dynastic and commemorative project; the precise architectural reconstruction is debated because little survives. ([Loeb commentary to *Historia Augusta*, *Hadrian*](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Historia_Augusta/Hadrian/1%2A.html))

- **About 125–132:** *Athenian building programme*. A coordinated urban project comprising the completion of the Olympieion, Hadrian’s Library, an aqueduct, sanctuaries, and other works. Individual architects are unknown, and the programme should not be mistaken for a personally drafted architectural oeuvre. ([Attic Inscriptions Online, IG II² 5185](https://www.atticinscriptions.com/inscription/IGII2/5185); [Oxford study of the programme](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3Aedb6cac4-ff85-4635-9e66-f92524b7226c/files/ma3ab6bbb9a170885bbe1273044f6ae27))

- **131–132:** *Completion and dedication of the Olympieion at Athens*. Hadrian completed and consecrated the centuries-old Temple of Olympian Zeus, so the obra is a completion-project rather than an original design; the occasion also anchored his new Panhellenic programme. ([*Historia Augusta* and commentary](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Historia_Augusta/Hadrian/1%2A.html); [Oxford study](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3Aedb6cac4-ff85-4635-9e66-f92524b7226c/files/ma3ab6bbb9a170885bbe1273044f6ae27))

- **About 132:** *Library of Hadrian at Athens*. A monumental cultural and administrative complex conventionally called a library, but incorporating courts and assembly spaces and therefore resisting reduction to a container for books. It belongs to the imperial building programme rather than to Hadrian’s demonstrable personal design. ([Attic Inscriptions Online](https://www.atticinscriptions.com/inscription/IGII2/5185))

- **131/132:** *Panhellenion and Panhellenia*. A league of selected Greek cities and its festival system, instituted with Athens as its centre. This is an institutional creation—a political-religious project made through rules, membership, ritual, and recurring performance—not a discrete physical object. ([Attic Inscriptions Online](https://www.atticinscriptions.com/inscription/IGII2/5185); [Willers, *Hadrians panhellenisches Programm*](https://books.google.com/books/about/Hadrians_panhellenisches_Programm.html?id=7T44AQAAIAAJ))

- **About 130:** *Antinoopolis*. A new Egyptian city founded near the place of Antinous’s death, together with a shrine and cultic programme in his honour. It is a city-making project and political memorial, not a building personally executed by Hadrian. ([*Historia Augusta* commentary](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Historia_Augusta/Hadrian/1%2A.html))

- **About 130:** *Aelia Capitolina*. A Roman colony founded on the site of Jerusalem, carrying Hadrian’s family name and an associated Capitoline religious programme. Its exact foundation date relative to the Bar Kokhba revolt remains disputed, and the “work” is an imposed colonial reconstitution of a city rather than a single monument. ([Oxford Bibliographies](https://academic.oup.com/reference/62341/reference-article-abstract/554104460); [Gray, “The Founding of Aelia Capitolina”](https://www.jstor.org/stable/528284))

- **130:** *Restoration of Pompey’s tomb at Pelusium*. Hadrian enlarged or refurbished Pompey’s neglected monument and reportedly attached a poetic lament to the act. The building intervention and epigram together approach a small multimedia memorial work, although the ancient account does not preserve its architectural form. ([*Historia Augusta* and commentary](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Historia_Augusta/Hadrian/1%2A.html))

- **117–138:** *Provincial buildings, restorations, roads, temples, and barriers*. Ancient and epigraphic sources attribute a much larger dispersed programme to Hadrian in Italy and the provinces, often without naming architects or distinguishing imperial funding from personal initiative. This body resists itemisation as Hadrian’s personal oeuvre: “Hadrianic” date, imperial authorization, patronage, design, and physical authorship are not synonyms. ([study of Italian construction](https://publications.dainst.org/journals/chiron/article/view/1147/5514); [*Historia Augusta*](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Historia_Augusta/Hadrian/1%2A.html))

### Images, cults, and serial state manifestations

- **After 130:** *Cult and image programme of Antinous*. Hadrian founded a city and shrine for Antinous and promoted his commemoration, from which an enormous sculptural and numismatic image-world emerged. The individual statues and coins were produced by many hands, so the programme is Hadrian’s project but its surviving images are manifestations, not securely his authored objects. ([*Historia Augusta* commentary](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Historia_Augusta/Hadrian/1%2A.html))

- **117–138:** *Imperial coinage of Hadrian*. The reign produced extensive named series—travel, province, army, restoration, and imperial-persona types—catalogued in *Roman Imperial Coinage* II.3. These coins are serial state manifestations issued under Hadrian, not objects he can be shown personally to have designed or engraved; accordingly, individual dies should not be silently converted into personal works. ([*Roman Imperial Coinage* II.3 publication notice](https://www.reddit.com/r/AncientCoins/comments/193gqn1/psabook_announcing_ric_v4_gallic_emperors_by/); [coin references in the *Historia Augusta* commentary](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Historia_Augusta/Hadrian/1%2A.html))
