## Works by Charles "Lucky" Luciano

### Organizational obras

- **1931:** *The Commission*. A collectively made governing arrangement among several Mafia families, intended to mediate disputes and coordinate joint decisions; Luciano was an influential founder, not its sole author. Claims that he simultaneously invented the American Mafia or a monolithic “National Crime Syndicate” are disputed: historical research describes Cosa Nostra as multiple organizations and identifies earlier Sicilian-American structures on which the Commission drew.[1][2]

- **Early 1930s–1936:** *New York prostitution combine*. A criminal enterprise that attempted to centralize control of numerous brothels and became the basis of Luciano’s 1936 conviction. It resists classification as his individual obra: contemporary witnesses and prosecutors attributed direction to him, but later historians dispute how directly he participated, and the surviving documentary corpus is principally a prosecution archive rather than an organizational record authored by Luciano.[3][4]

### Unfinished media project

- **c. 1960–1962:** *The Lucky Luciano Story* (working title). An unrealized motion-picture project for which producer Martin A. Gosch engaged Luciano as a consultant on a dramatization of his life; no completed film or securely attributable Luciano screenplay survives. Accounts differ over whether Luciano supplied a genuine autobiography, embellished anecdotes, or material for an avowedly fictional story, so this is best treated as an unfinished collaborative project rather than a film by Luciano.[5][6]

### Posthumous and disputed text

- **Conversations reportedly 1961–1962; published 1974/1975:** *The Last Testament of Lucky Luciano: The Mafia Story in His Own Words*. A posthumous first-person life narrative assembled by Martin A. Gosch and Richard Hammer from conversations or notes allegedly involving Luciano. Its status as Luciano’s memoir is sharply disputed: Gosch’s underlying notes were unavailable for independent examination, factual errors were identified upon publication, and historians have variously treated the book as a contaminated oral source, a purported memoir, or a substantially constructed work; it is therefore a manifestation made partly in Luciano’s name, not a securely authored book.[5][7][8]
