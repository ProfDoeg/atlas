# Paul Berthon: Research Dossier

## Scope, identity, and evidentiary note

This dossier concerns the French artist and magistrate **Paul Louis Joseph Berthon**, born at Villefranche-sur-Saône in March 1872 and deceased at Sceaux on 27 February 1934.

He must be distinguished from **Paul Émile Berton** (sometimes incorrectly written *Berthon*), the landscape painter born at Chartrettes in 1846 and deceased in Paris on 15 February 1909. Confusion between the two men has propagated false names, birthplaces, professions, and a false 1909 death date through museum databases, reference works, auction catalogues, and English-language biographies.

Evidentiary labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a civil record, official register, contemporaneous publication, signed work, or institutional object record.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a credible secondary or specialist source but not independently confirmed here from the underlying record.
- **Inference:** a reasoned interpretation, explicitly identified as such.
- **Disputed or erroneous attribution:** contradicted by stronger evidence or apparently produced through conflation.
- **Unverified claim:** repeated in the literature without adequate supporting documentation located.
- **No evidence located:** the targeted searches did not produce substantiation; this does not prove that an event never occurred.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full civil name:** Paul Louis Joseph Berthon.

**Usual professional name:** Paul Berthon.

**Variants and erroneous forms encountered:**

- Paul Louis Joseph Berthon
- Louis Joseph Paul Berthon
- Paul Louis Joseph Berthow
- Paul Émile Berthon
- Paul Emile Berton
- P. E. Berton

The final three forms principally reflect contamination from the record of landscape painter Paul Émile Berton rather than demonstrated pseudonyms used by the subject. Getty’s Union List of Artist Names illustrates the problem by clustering *Paul Berthon*, *Paul Louis Joseph Berthon*, *Paul Émile Berton*, and *Berthow* while still assigning the erroneous life span 1872–1909.

**Signature and monogram:** Works are signed in the stone or plate as **Paul Berthon**, and sometimes with the monogram **PB**. Surviving prints also carry the commercial designation **Atelier Paul Berthon**.

**Nationality:** French.

**Professions and occupations:**

- painter
- draughtsman
- lithographer and chromolithographic designer
- poster artist
- printmaker
- illustrator
- book-cover and binding designer
- furniture and decorative-arts designer
- ceramic designer
- lawyer at the Paris Court of Appeal
- magistrate and justice of the peace

**Artistic classification:** Art Nouveau and Symbolist decorative art, with strong medievalist, Pre-Raphaelite, Japanese-print, floral, and Italian Renaissance associations.

**Birth:** The civil birth register is indexed as **act no. 71, Villefranche-sur-Saône, 1872**. The best-established date in museum and civil-record-derived authorities is **15 March 1872**. The retrospective magistracy register gives **14 March 1872**. This may represent birth on 14 March and registration on 15 March, or a transcription error; the discrepancy remains to be resolved by direct paleographic inspection of the act.

**Death:** **27 February 1934**, at his home in Sceaux, then in the département de la Seine; recorded as **Sceaux death act no. 23**. He was sixty-one.

**False death:** **15 February 1909, Paris**, age thirty-six, is the death of Paul Émile Berton. It is not Paul Louis Joseph Berthon’s death.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

A biographical tradition traceable through art reference literature describes Berthon as the **son of an inspector of primary education**. A Paris social directory of 1900 lists a **“BERTHON père (Jean)” at 56 rue Denfert-Rochereau** and **“BERTHON fils (Paul), peintre,” at 37 quai d’Anjou**. This makes **Jean Berthon** a strong candidate for the artist’s father, but the relationship and full identity should be treated as **reported rather than conclusively established** until the birth and marriage acts are transcribed in full.

No dependable source consulted gave his mother’s name. The birth act should contain both parents’ names, occupations, ages, and domicile, but the archive viewer did not expose a searchable transcription.

### Siblings and extended ancestry

No reliably documented siblings, grandparents, or extended ancestral line were located. Online genealogy pages contain numerous Berthon families in the Rhône region, but none could be securely attached to Paul Louis Joseph Berthon without forcing a connection.

### Marriage

**Documented fact:** Berthon married in Paris’s 5th arrondissement in **1898**, recorded as **marriage act no. 641**. The exact date and bride’s identity were not recoverable from the searchable metadata consulted.

A recent specialist account states that he married in 1898 and then withdrew rapidly from the public poster scene. The chronological association is documented; the proposition that marriage caused his professional change is an **unverified interpretation**, not a fact established by the marriage record.

### Children

No documented children were located. Nothing in the consulted sources establishes either childlessness or descendants.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1872–1892

Berthon was born in **Villefranche-sur-Saône**, a commercial and administrative town north of Lyon. He reportedly received his first instruction in **painting, drawing, and sculpture** there.

His father’s reported employment as a primary-school inspector places the family within the educational civil service of the early Third Republic. Beyond that occupational fact, the sources consulted do not reconstruct the household’s wealth, religion, politics, property, or domestic arrangements.

No records were located for:

- his primary or secondary schools;
- childhood illnesses or accidents;
- military service;
- adolescent friendships;
- apprenticeship contracts;
- early legal education;
- the date or institution of any law degree.

The last omission is significant because Berthon later practiced as an advocate and entered the magistracy. He necessarily acquired legal qualifications, but the institution and chronology remain undocumented in the presently accessible biographical literature.

## Education and Formation in Paris

### Move to Paris

**Reported fact:** In **1893**, aged approximately twenty-one, Berthon moved to Paris.

He entered the institution commonly called the **École Guérin**, or École normale d’enseignement du dessin, then associated with premises in the rue Vavin. The school trained drawing teachers and decorative artists rather than operating solely as a conventional fine-arts academy.

### Teachers

**Documented or consistently reported teachers:**

- **Luc-Olivier Merson**, for painting;
- **Eugène Grasset**, for decorative composition and industrial art.

Some later biographies also name **Pierre Puvis de Chavannes** as a teacher. This is widely repeated but less securely tied to a particular class or enrollment record. Auction sources sometimes add Auguste Allongé and Jules-Élie Delaunay, apparently through confusion with Paul Émile Berton. Those additions should not be accepted without an independent enrollment or contemporary source.

### Eugène Grasset’s influence

Grasset was Berthon’s decisive artistic mentor. Early Berthon works employ:

- flattened color;
- forceful enclosing contours;
- stylized plants;
- medieval or Renaissance costume;
- hieratic female figures;
- decorative integration of type and image;
- visual rhythms suited to lithographic reproduction.

Berthon’s early resemblance to Grasset was sufficiently strong that later criticism often paired them. The 1978 monograph by Victor Arwas is titled *Berthon & Grasset*, and exhibitions have continued to present them together.

Berthon gradually differentiated himself through muted autumnal colors—olive, ochre, rust, gold, brown, dusty rose and green—and through quieter, dreamlike figures. His contours remained firm, but the composition often became less dense than Grasset’s.

### Other formative currents

The work itself and contemporary criticism support exposure to:

- Symbolist literature and theater;
- medieval and Gothic art;
- Italian primitives, especially Botticelli, Filippo Lippi, and Lorenzo di Credi;
- the English Pre-Raphaelites;
- Japanese woodblock-print principles;
- the late nineteenth-century Parisian revival of the artist-designed poster;
- decorative-art ideals that challenged the hierarchy between “fine” and “applied” art.

A later letter by Théophile-Alexandre Steinlen says Berthon used his free time as a magistrate to paint and study **Gothic art**, showing that medievalism remained a sustained intellectual interest rather than a superficial early motif.

## Early Literary and Artistic Career, 1894–1896

### *Rêve aux étoiles*

Berthon’s earliest identified authored publication is **_*Rêve aux étoiles*_**, issued by Edmond Girard in Paris in **1895** as a small eight-page work with a color plate. It had previously appeared in the periodical *L’Album des légendes* or *Le Livre des légendes*.

This is important because Berthon was not merely its illustrator: bibliographic notices place the text under his name. It is the strongest evidence that he briefly participated directly in Symbolist literary culture.

### First Salon appearance

**Reported fact:** Berthon first exhibited at the Paris Salon in **1895**. The works reportedly included:

- a decorative fresco or mural composition, *L’Aube*;
- color lithographs;
- bookbindings;
- furniture designs.

A contemporaneous illustrated notice described him as only twenty-three, identified Grasset and Olivier Merson as his masters, and discussed *L’Aube* as a six-figure decorative composition in which dawn, music, flowers, prayer, and awakening nature were allegorically combined. The critic saw Grasset’s influence but anticipated an emerging independent personality.

The appearance of bindings and furniture projects at the same exhibition confirms that Berthon understood himself as a decorative artist across media, not merely as an easel painter.

### Salon des Cent and La Plume

In **1895–1896**, Berthon entered the circle of the **Salon des Cent**, organized through Léon Deschamps’s journal *La Plume* at 31 rue Bonaparte. The Salon deliberately promoted posters, prints, illustration, and accessible original art outside traditional juried structures.

Berthon designed an advertisement for the **17th exhibition**. The work presents a stylized woman, floral ornament, and emphatic contour within the commercial framework of an exhibition poster. It became one of his signature images.

The Salon’s artists included Eugène Grasset, Alphonse Mucha, Pierre Bonnard, Georges de Feure, Henri-Gabriel Ibels, Adolphe Willette, and Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec. Shared inclusion does not prove friendship or personal collaboration, but it firmly locates Berthon within the same Parisian graphic-art institution.

### Entertainment posters

Among Berthon’s best-known commercial assignments is the approximately **1896 Folies-Bergère poster for Liane de Pougy**. He represented the dancer and courtesan as a seductive figure associated with a spiderweb. The image employs the femme-fatale vocabulary of fin-de-siècle commercial culture.

Other theater and entertainment designs include work for the **Eldorado**, **Bostock’s Hippodrome**, and performers such as **Jane Othello/Otello**. The Musée des Arts Décoratifs records a Jane Othello poster made about 1900, printed by Bourgerie & Cie, bearing Berthon’s monogram and the address **Atelier Paul Berthon, 37 quai d’Anjou**.

## Mature Graphic Career, 1897–1902

### *L’Ermitage*

In **1897**, Berthon created the celebrated color-lithographic poster and cover image for the literary journal **_*L’Ermitage: revue illustrée*_**.

The Musée d’art et d’histoire de Genève records the poster as a color lithograph, 615 × 430 mm, printed by **G. Enard & Cie of Étampes**. The image’s contemplative woman, abundant hair, flowers, and restrained typography became one of the clearest expressions of Berthon’s synthesis of Symbolism and Art Nouveau.

### *L’Image*

Berthon produced a wood-engraved cover for the July **1897** issue of **_*L’Image*_**. This placed his work within a journal specifically devoted to the arts of illustration and reproduction.

### *L’Estampe moderne*

His work **_*Source des Roches*_** appeared through *L’Estampe moderne* in 1897. This publication distributed original prints to subscribers, reinforcing the dual status of the modern poster image as both public communication and collectible art.

### Railway commission

A poster for the **Compagnie des chemins de fer de l’Ouest**, generally known as **_*Normandie-Bretagne*_**, advertised western French travel. An example is held by the Musée départemental breton at Quimper. The commission links Berthon’s decorative practice to tourism, railway marketing, and the commercial imaging of regional France.

### *Les Maîtres de l’affiche*

Jules Chéret’s Imprimerie Chaix published *Les Maîtres de l’affiche* monthly from December 1895 to November 1900. It reproduced selected posters by ninety-seven artists as collectible chromolithographic plates.

Berthon’s relationship to the enterprise had several forms:

- his **_*L’Ermitage*_** and **_*Leçons de violon*_** posters were reproduced;
- he supplied decorative supplemental plates;
- the bound volumes used a pictorial-cover design by him;
- a deluxe edition was issued on Japanese paper.

The first bound volume appeared in January 1897, with an introduction by Roger Marx and a binding manufactured by Jean Engel after Berthon’s design. Volume V contained four original supplemental designs by Berthon.

### Studio addresses

The surviving documentary trail gives at least two Paris addresses:

- **22 rue Denfert-Rochereau**, identified on 1898 prints as **Atelier Paul Berthon**;
- **37 quai d’Anjou**, listed for “Berthon fils (Paul), peintre” in a 1900 Paris directory and printed on the Jane Othello poster.

The rue Denfert-Rochereau address was part of a group of artists’ studios. Berthon’s **_*Leçons de violon, violoncelle et solfège*_** advertised lessons offered by a neighboring musician in the same atelier complex.

### Book and poetry projects

In **1898**, Berthon designed the large poster **_*Le Livre de Magda: Poésies par Armand Silvestre*_**. Surviving examples carry the Atelier Berthon and Imprimerie Chaix marks.

Other book-related work included covers, bindings, frontispieces, or promotional designs. The exact boundary between “illustrated book,” advertising poster, prospectus, and autonomous decorative print varies among catalogues and surviving states.

### Decorative panels

A large portion of Berthon’s production consisted not of advertising posters but of **panneaux décoratifs** intended for interiors. Repeatedly catalogued examples include:

- *La Fée aux étoiles*
- *La Lyre*
- *Les Pipeaux*
- *La Viole de gambe*
- *La Mandore*
- *Les Boules de neige*
- *Les Églantines*
- *Les Fils de la Vierge*
- *La Princesse au crapaud* / *La Jeune Fille et les crapauds*
- *Salomé*
- *Vision antique*
- *Sainte-Marie-des-Fleurs*
- *Harmonie médiévale*
- *Mai*
- *Femme de profil*
- *Portrait de jeune femme*

The fact that approximately two-thirds of the lithographic catalogue lacked conventional advertising text explains why calling all of them “posters” can be misleading.

### Landscape and travel imagery

Berthon also produced horizontally composed color lithographs depicting:

- Venice;
- the Lake Garda region;
- Notre-Dame and the Seine;
- the Pointe de Bretteville;
- the Breton coast;
- sardine fishing.

These works demonstrate a broader geography than the studio-bound female allegories. Whether all views record personal travel is unresolved. Their topographical specificity suggests firsthand or photographic sources, but this is not proof that Berthon visited every depicted location.

### Monaco commissions

A specialist study in *Annales monégasques* distinguishes Berthon from Paul Émile Berton and documents two Monte Carlo connections:

- Berthon designed **two book posters** for Prince Albert I’s *La Carrière d’un navigateur*.
- At **Princess Alice of Monaco’s request**, he prepared illustrations for programs of operas performed at the Théâtre de Monte-Carlo in **1900**.

These are documented patronage relationships. No evidence located shows that he held a salaried position at the court.

### Villeroy & Boch

Berthon is reported to have designed ceramic decoration for **Villeroy & Boch around 1898–1900**. The claim is repeated in standard art references and auction literature but was not tied in the consulted sources to factory ledgers, pattern numbers, or signed contracts. It should therefore be treated as **reported fact pending factory-archive confirmation**.

### Furniture and bindings

Furniture designs and bindings were reportedly included in his 1895 Salon presentation. The binding design for *Les Maîtres de l’affiche* is well documented. No surviving business organization or furniture workshop owned by Berthon was found.

## The 1899 Interview and Public Image

An interview titled **“An Interview with Paul Berthon”** appeared in the English-language journal **_*The Poster*_**, volume II, no. XI, in May **1899**, around page 203.

Its existence shows that Berthon’s reputation extended beyond France during his lifetime. The surviving references to it describe:

- his training under Grasset and Merson;
- the organization of his atelier;
- his integration of decorative art and lithography;
- his standing among Parisian poster artists.

A complete verified transcription was not available in the consulted online sources, so statements supposedly derived from the interview should be checked against the original before quotation.

## Portraiture and Important Sitters

### Sarah Bernhardt

In **1901**, Berthon produced a color lithograph of **Sarah Bernhardt as Mélissinde** in Edmond Rostand’s *La Princesse lointaine*. Institutional collections sometimes spell the character Mélisande or Melisande.

The National Gallery of Art records the work as a color lithograph and cites Victor Arwas’s catalogue raisonné, no. 62. Other impressions are held by the National Gallery of Victoria and the Victoria and Albert Museum.

This is a documented artistic connection—a named portrait of Bernhardt in a theatrical role—but no source consulted demonstrates a personal sitting, meeting, correspondence, contract, or friendship. The portrait could have derived from publicity photography or other visual sources.

### Queen Wilhelmina of the Netherlands

Berthon produced a color lithographic portrait of the young **Queen Wilhelmina** in **1901**. An impression is held by the Rijksmuseum. No personal sitting or meeting was established.

### Eugène Grasset and André des Gachons

Specialist literature records portrait prints of Berthon’s teacher Eugène Grasset and fellow Symbolist artist André des Gachons. Grasset’s portrait reinforces the teacher-pupil relationship; a personal friendship with des Gachons is plausible but not established merely by the portrait.

### Jane Othello and Liane de Pougy

The prints name their subjects and commercial contexts. Direct personal relationships beyond the commission were not documented.

## Withdrawal from Poster Art and Entry into Law

### Timing

Berthon married in 1898 and became less visible in the commercial-art world around **1900–1902**. His *Six estampes originales de Paul Berthon*, also called *Les Six Vierges*, appeared in **1902**, suggesting that the transition was gradual rather than an abrupt disappearance.

### Legal profession

A surviving letter by Théophile-Alexandre Steinlen identifies Berthon as:

- an **ancien avocat à la cour**;
- already functioning as a magistrate;
- a “conscientious magistrate” in Steinlen’s assessment.

The “court” was evidently the Paris Court of Appeal. The date of admission to the bar, legal school, thesis, chambers, and known cases were not located.

### Magistracy register

The retrospective French magistracy register lists **Paul Louis Joseph Berthon**, born at Villefranche-sur-Saône, as an active magistrate by **1 January 1904**. He does not appear under that identity in the 1902 register. His entry is present for 1905, 1906, 1907, 1913, 1914, and 1916.

Accordingly:

- entry into the magistracy occurred **after 1 January 1902 and no later than 1 January 1904**;
- he remained in the official magistracy through at least **1916**;
- the exact appointment decree and complete sequence of jurisdictions were not recovered.

The register gives his birth date as **14 March 1872**, one day earlier than the civil-record-derived date.

### Pontoise

By **1906**, contemporaneous press reports identified Berthon as **justice of the peace at Pontoise**.

The justice of the peace was a local magistrate dealing with relatively small civil disputes, conciliations, leases, neighborhood matters, and specified minor offenses. No surviving decision authored by Berthon was located in the searches.

## Patronage, Political Associations, and the Steinlen Letter

A letter from artist **Théophile-Alexandre Steinlen** to socialist journalist and politician **Alfred-Léon Gérault-Richard** asked him to support Berthon’s request for a justice-of-the-peace position in the Paris suburbs.

The auction-catalogue transcription describes Berthon as:

- “un ami de Crainquebille” who had proved this in office;
- “un disciple de Bergeret”;
- an artist known personally to Steinlen;
- a former advocate;
- a conscientious magistrate;
- a painter and student of Gothic art during the leisure allowed by his office.

Steinlen asked Gérault-Richard to solicit:

- “a word” from **Georges Clemenceau**;
- Gérault-Richard’s own recommendation to the justice minister.

### Meaning of the literary references

**Crainquebille** and **Bergeret** are figures created by Anatole France:

- Crainquebille is a street vendor victimized by arbitrary justice.
- Monsieur Bergeret is a skeptical, republican, Dreyfusard intellectual.

Calling Berthon their friend or disciple positioned him within a reformist, left-republican, socially sympathetic moral vocabulary. It was not a claim that these fictional figures literally existed.

### Evidentiary status

- **Documented fact:** Steinlen attempted to enlist political sponsorship for Berthon.
- **Documented fact:** the appeal invoked Clemenceau and the minister of justice.
- **Reported fact:** Berthon was close to socialist circles.
- **Inference:** his appointment may have been assisted by political patronage.
- **Not established:** that Clemenceau intervened, that the minister acted because of the letter, or that an improper quid pro quo occurred.
- **No evidence located:** party membership, elected office, political publications, bribery, or disciplinary proceedings.

This letter is the principal source for Berthon’s political network and the only substantial controversy-adjacent material discovered.

## The Palais-Salon, 1906

In 1906, Berthon exhibited **_*Les Musiciennes dans un parc*_**, an oil painting on panel, at the **Palais-Salon**, an exhibition of work by lawyers and magistrates organized with support from fine-arts undersecretary Étienne Dujardin-Beaumetz.

The painting depicts two richly dressed women playing a harp and lute in a flowered landscape. Its panel support, archaizing costumes, formal attitudes, and color recall Italian Renaissance and Pre-Raphaelite models.

Contemporary reception included:

- *L’Humanité* recalling that, before becoming a judge, Berthon had maintained a studio near place Denfert-Rochereau and issued successful posters and prints;
- Fernand Hauser in *Le Journal* jokingly asking whether the justice of the peace painted for relaxation or the painter administered justice for relaxation;
- Robert Tenax comparing the figures to Fra Filippo Lippi and Botticelli and the color prints to Henri Rivière;
- Marcel Mirtil in *La Grande Revue* judging it one of the exhibition’s outstanding works while acknowledging that detractors might call the manner derivative or “literary painting.”

This reception establishes that contemporaries recognized the artist and magistrate as the same living person in 1906. It independently refutes the later notion that his artistic identity had ended through death in 1909.

## Later Life, 1907–1934

After the Palais-Salon appearance, the accessible public record becomes thin.

### Documented framework

- He remained listed in the magistracy through at least 1916.
- He continued to be remembered as an artist who painted during his judicial career.
- He ultimately resided in Sceaux.
- He died at his home there on 27 February 1934.

### Unresolved matters

No reliable sources located gave:

- later judicial promotions or retirement date;
- wartime service during 1914–1918;
- political activity after the Steinlen appeal;
- household composition in Sceaux;
- property ownership;
- income, inheritance, investments, debts, or estate value;
- spouse’s later life;
- children or heirs;
- illnesses, disabilities, addictions, or psychological crises;
- the cause of death;
- burial place;
- obituary text.

The quiet archival record should not be converted into a story of failure or incapacity. It reflects the sharp division between a well-catalogued five-to-eight-year graphic career and an incompletely researched three-decade legal life.

## Artistic and Intellectual Development

### Symbolist phase

Berthon’s earliest literary and visual language was Symbolist: stars, music, twilight, allegorical women, medievalized costume, flowers, silence, reverie, and the fusion of the arts.

His female figures are seldom individualized psychological portraits. They operate as personifications of seasons, music, purity, danger, imagination, landscape, or poetic states.

### Art Nouveau method

His mature graphic method typically uses:

- strong outlines;
- compressed or flattened space;
- arabesque hair and vegetation;
- asymmetric plant forms;
- decorative borders;
- limited, harmonized color;
- comparatively subdued typography;
- integration of dress pattern and landscape.

### Relationship to commercial art

Berthon worked for theaters, journals, railways, literary publications, exhibition promoters, and printers. Yet much of his production was intentionally sold as autonomous household decoration. The distinction between commercial poster and fine-art print was therefore porous.

### Medievalism and Gothic study

Musical instruments—harp, lute, mandore, pipe, viol, violoncello—and archaic dress recur across his work. Steinlen’s later statement that Berthon studied Gothic art confirms an intellectual commitment extending into his judicial years.

### Politics and philosophy

Berthon left no located political manifesto, philosophical treatise, or religious profession. The Anatole France references in Steinlen’s letter and his proximity to socialist figures suggest republican and socially sympathetic associations, but a complete ideology cannot responsibly be reconstructed.

No evidence was located of formal religious office, occult society membership, Freemasonry, anarchist organization, or nationalist association.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Eugène Grasset

**Documented fact:** teacher and principal formative influence. Berthon produced a portrait of him, used similar decorative methods, and was repeatedly paired with him in scholarship and exhibitions.

### Luc-Olivier Merson

**Reported/documented educational relationship:** painting teacher at the École Guérin. Berthon acknowledged or was described as having studied under him.

### Théophile-Alexandre Steinlen

**Documented fact:** personal friend or professional acquaintance sufficiently close for Steinlen to lobby on his behalf. Steinlen wrote that he had first known Berthon as an artist.

### Alfred-Léon Gérault-Richard

**Documented fact:** intended recipient of Steinlen’s lobbying letter. No direct Berthon–Gérault-Richard correspondence was located.

### Georges Clemenceau

**Documented proposed connection only:** Steinlen wanted Clemenceau’s recommendation. No evidence was found that Clemenceau met Berthon, supplied the recommendation, or caused the appointment.

### Léon Deschamps and the Salon des Cent

**Documented institutional relationship:** Deschamps’s *La Plume* and Salon des Cent commissioned or exhibited Berthon’s work.

### Jules Chéret and Imprimerie Chaix

**Documented professional network:** Chaix printed Berthon’s lithographs and reproduced his posters through *Les Maîtres de l’affiche*. Chéret directed the publication. Personal friendship was not established.

### Bourgerie & Cie; G. Enard & Cie

**Documented commercial relationships:** printers of Berthon posters.

### Princess Alice and Prince Albert I of Monaco

**Documented patronage:** Alice commissioned program illustrations; Berthon created posters for Albert’s book.

### Armand Silvestre

**Documented professional association:** Berthon designed the poster for *Le Livre de Magda*. Co-authorship is not implied.

### Sarah Bernhardt, Queen Wilhelmina, Liane de Pougy, Jane Othello

**Documented visual representation:** all were subjects of Berthon prints. Meetings or sittings remain unproved.

### Maurice Pillard Verneuil

**Reported artistic parallel:** fellow Grasset pupil whose early work is frequently compared with Berthon’s. No direct collaboration was located.

### Alphonse Mucha

**Documented shared institutional field:** both designed Salon des Cent posters, appeared in *Les Maîtres de l’affiche*, and were marketed as leading Art Nouveau graphic artists. No direct correspondence, friendship, or rivalry was found.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Adversarial Findings

A dedicated adversarial search combined Paul Berthon’s name with French and English terms for controversy, scandal, accusation, lawsuit, fraud, corruption, conspiracy, criticism, dismissal, disciplinary action, and criminal proceedings, including searches restricted toward material published or indexed in 2024–2026.

### Political patronage in judicial appointment

**Documented fact:** Steinlen sought political intervention for Berthon’s desired suburban judicial appointment.

**Unresolved question:** whether Clemenceau or the justice minister acted on the request.

**No evidence located:** payment, corrupt bargain, falsified credentials, judicial misconduct, or unlawful appointment.

This is the only substantive patronage issue found. In the political culture of the Third Republic, recommendations for public office were common; the letter documents advocacy but does not establish corruption.

### Critical charge of derivativeness

Contemporary and later critics recognized Berthon’s heavy debt to Grasset and the Italian primitives. The 1906 discussion of *Les Musiciennes dans un parc* recorded a possible hostile view: that such archaizing work could be called false or merely imitative “literary painting.” This was an aesthetic criticism, not an allegation of forgery.

### Commercial and cataloguing misattribution

A major modern controversy concerns attribution rather than Berthon’s behavior:

- works by Paul Louis Joseph Berthon are repeatedly catalogued under **Paul Émile Berton**;
- Berthon is repeatedly said to have died in 1909;
- contradictory dates such as 1910 appear in commerce;
- places of birth are sometimes transferred between the men;
- auction catalogues inconsistently use “Paul Émile Berthon” for works signed Paul Berthon.

This confusion can materially affect provenance, cataloguing, biography, and market descriptions.

## Myths, Errors, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### Myth/error 1: Berthon died in 1909 at age thirty-six

**Story:** The artist died in Paris on 15 February 1909, ending a short career.

**Circulation:** English Wikipedia, Getty ULAN, the National Gallery of Art, Paris museum catalogues, auction houses, and commercial-art sites have reproduced the date.

**Origin:** The date belongs to the landscape painter **Paul Émile Berton**, born in 1846. Near-identical surnames and the later use of “Paul Émile Berthon” for the poster artist appear to have merged their records.

**Evidence against:**

- Sceaux death act no. 23 records Paul Louis Joseph Berthon’s death in 1934.
- The magistracy register lists him after 1909.
- Contemporary sources identify him as a living magistrate and artist in 1906.
- The *Annales monégasques* study explicitly separates the two men.

**Reputational effect:** The error transformed a lawyer-magistrate who lived to sixty-one into a romantic figure supposedly cut down at thirty-six. It also erased roughly thirty years of his judicial life.

### Myth/error 2: His birth date was 14 March 1872

The magistracy database uses 14 March; civil-record-derived art sources use 15 March. Neither version should be silently suppressed. Direct reading of act no. 71 is required to determine whether one is birth date and the other registration date.

### Myth/error 3: “Paul Émile Berthon” was his full name

No civil evidence consulted supports **Émile** in the poster artist’s name. His civil identity is Paul Louis Joseph Berthon. “Paul Émile” is most likely inherited from Paul Émile Berton.

### Myth/error 4: Marriage ended his art career

The chronology—marriage in 1898, reduced graphic production after about 1900—is real. Causal explanations involving a wife’s demand for stability, financial distress, or disillusionment with Art Nouveau are **unverified stories**.

He did not wholly cease creating: *Six estampes originales* appeared in 1902, and *Les Musiciennes dans un parc* was exhibited in 1906.

### Myth/error 5: Berthon invented or substantially developed chromolithography

He was an accomplished designer for chromolithographic reproduction. No patent or technical account demonstrates that he invented the process or introduced a fundamental technical innovation.

### Myth/error 6: Every woman in the prints was a real mistress, muse, or sitter

Most figures are symbolic or decorative types. Even identified portraits such as Sarah Bernhardt or Queen Wilhelmina need not have involved a sitting. No romantic relationship with a sitter was documented.

### Myth/error 7: He designed the Larousse “Sower” emblem

Some commercial websites credit Berthon with the Larousse female sower. No authoritative evidence located supported this attribution. The claim should remain **unverified** and may reflect confusion among fin-de-siècle designers.

### Self-created mythology

Berthon’s visual world repeatedly stages timeless women, enchanted vegetation, stars, archaic music, Salomé, princesses, and medieval reverie. This was a constructed artistic mythology rather than autobiography. It helped later collectors view him as a quintessential, mysterious Art Nouveau artist even as his prosaic legal career disappeared from common accounts.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Berthon’s best-known images remained within the international canon of Art Nouveau printmaking. His reputation rests particularly on:

- *L’Ermitage*;
- *Salon des Cent*;
- *Leçons de violon*;
- *Les Boules de neige*;
- *Le Livre de Magda*;
- the Liane de Pougy poster;
- Sarah Bernhardt as Mélissinde;
- decorative music-and-flower panels.

His prints are held by or documented in institutions including the BnF, Musée des Arts Décoratifs, Musée Carnavalet, Musée d’art et d’histoire de Genève, Musée départemental breton, National Gallery of Art, National Gallery of Victoria, Victoria and Albert Museum, Museum of Modern Art, and other international collections.

### Historical position

Berthon is generally placed in the generation after the first transformation of the Paris poster by Chéret and Toulouse-Lautrec. His importance lies in transferring Art Nouveau design from advertising into affordable decorative panels intended for domestic display.

### Effects of the identity confusion

Conflation with Paul Émile Berton has:

- shortened Berthon’s life by twenty-five years;
- erased his judicial career;
- assigned him Berton’s death place;
- attached “Émile” to his name;
- transferred training and exhibition histories between the two men;
- generated contradictory auction attributions;
- encouraged a false narrative of premature death.

The correction substantially changes his biography. Rather than dying at the end of the Belle Époque, Berthon deliberately or pragmatically shifted from graphic art into law and continued living through the First World War and into the interwar period.

### Market legacy

Original signed color lithographs and Salon des Cent or *Maîtres de l’affiche* states remain actively traded. Market descriptions require particular caution because:

- “Paul Émile Berthon” may mean this artist;
- “Paul Émile Berton” may be the landscape painter;
- 1909 and 1910 death dates recur;
- posthumous reproductions may be advertised as original lithographs;
- works “after Paul Berthon” may differ from artist-supervised impressions.

No comprehensive examination of forgery or market manipulation was found.

## Atlas Connections

Only source-supported connections with persons in the supplied atlas roster are included.

### Eugène Samuel Grasset

**Evidentiary label: documented fact.**

Grasset taught decorative art to Berthon at the École Guérin in Paris from approximately 1893. His influence is visible in Berthon’s contours, floral patterns, medievalism, and integrated decorative composition. Berthon also produced a portrait of Grasset. Their work was paired in Victor Arwas’s 1978 monograph and later exhibitions.

### Luc-Olivier Merson

**Evidentiary label: documented/reported fact.**

Merson taught Berthon painting at the École Guérin in Paris during the 1890s. A contemporary notice of *L’Aube* specifically identified Merson as one of Berthon’s masters.

### Sarah Bernhardt

**Evidentiary label: documented fact, limited to artistic representation.**

Berthon produced the 1901 color lithograph *Sarah Bernhardt as Mélissinde in La Princesse lointaine*. Institutional object records document the print. No meeting, sitting, correspondence, friendship, or contract was established.

### Alphonse Mucha

**Evidentiary label: documented shared institution; no direct personal relationship established.**

Mucha and Berthon both designed posters for the Salon des Cent and both appeared in *Les Maîtres de l’affiche*. They occupied the same Parisian poster and decorative-art field and were later exhibited and collected together. No direct collaboration, correspondence, friendship, or rivalry was located.

### Loïe Fuller

**Evidentiary label: no direct connection established.**

Fuller and Liane de Pougy both belonged to the Folies-Bergère milieu, and Berthon portrayed de Pougy. No source located showed that Berthon portrayed, met, or worked directly with Fuller. Shared association with the venue alone is insufficient.

### Achille-Claude Debussy

**Evidentiary label: no connection established.**

Berthon illustrated Monte Carlo opera programs in 1900, but the accessible summary did not identify the works performed. No documentary basis was found for connecting those commissions specifically to Debussy.

### Auguste Rodin

**Evidentiary label: documented shared exhibition network only.**

Rodin and Berthon were among artists represented through the Salon des Cent environment. No personal meeting or correspondence was found.

### Remaining atlas roster

No other connection in the supplied roster was documented strongly enough for inclusion. This negative result is material: broad contemporaneity in Paris, joint appearance in retrospective Art Nouveau catalogues, or mere inclusion in the same large publication does not by itself establish personal contact.
