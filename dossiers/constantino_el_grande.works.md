## Works by Constantine the Great

### Oration

- **c. 325–326, possibly later:** *Oration to the Assembly of the Saints* (*Oratio ad sanctorum coetum*). A Christian apologetic or manifesto transmitted in Greek after Eusebius’s *Life of Constantine* as a translation of a Latin speech; Constantine’s authorship, its date, audience, and whether it was ever delivered remain disputed.

### Imperial letters, edicts, and ecclesiastical protocols

These are acts of government rather than private literary compositions. Their language was normally prepared with secretarial help, so “by Constantine” means issued in his name and under his authority; many survive only as quotations, translations, or excerpts in later Christian histories.

- **313:** *Letter of Constantine and Licinius restoring Christian property* (“Edict of Milan”). A jointly issued administrative protocol preserved by Lactantius in Latin and Eusebius in Greek; it is conventionally called an edict, although the surviving text has the form of instructions to provincial officials.

- **313:** *First Letter to Anulinus*. A joint order of Constantine and Licinius directing the restoration of property to the Catholic Church.

- **313:** *Second Letter to Anulinus*. An administrative letter exempting Catholic clergy from compulsory public services.

- **313:** *Letter to Caecilian of Carthage*. An imperial grant assigning funds to African clergy and placing their distribution under episcopal supervision.

- **313:** *Letter to Miltiades, Bishop of Rome*. A protocol convening an episcopal tribunal in Rome to hear the dispute surrounding Caecilian and the emerging Donatist schism.

- **314:** *Letter to Aelafius (Ablabius)*. An order arranging the Council of Arles after the Roman hearing failed to end the African dispute.

- **314:** *Letter to Chrestus of Syracuse*. A summons and travel authorization for attendance at the Council of Arles.

- **314:** *Letter to the Bishops after the Council of Arles*. A denunciation of continued Donatist resistance and an exhortation to patience; older scholarship questioned its authenticity, while modern discussion generally treats the Constantinian documents in the Optatan dossier more favorably.

- **314:** *Letter of Constantine and Licinius to Probianus*. An order that the Donatist witness Ingentius be sent to the imperial court; the transmitted titulature varies.

- **314–315:** *Letter to the Donatist Bishops*. A summons requiring the disputants and Caecilian to appear before the emperor.

- **315:** *Letter to Celsus*. An administrative response to Donatist disturbances announcing Constantine’s intention to settle the matter in Africa.

- **315:** *Letter to Eumalius*. A fragmentary judgment declaring Caecilian innocent; only a brief excerpt survives in Augustine.

- **316–317:** *Letter to the Bishops and People of Africa*. A proclamation abandoning further coercive adjudication of the Donatist controversy and urging Catholics to endure their opponents.

- **c. 319–321:** *Letter to the People of Heliopolis*. A lost or unlocated imperial letter known only from Eusebius’s notice; its exact text and date cannot be recovered.

- **c. 321:** *Letter to Valerius concerning the Donatists*. A lost letter known through Augustine; even the recipient’s name is transmitted as Valerius, Valerianus, or Verinus.

- **c. 323:** *First Letter to Eusebius of Caesarea concerning churches*. An architectural commission authorizing the repair, enlargement, and construction of Christian places of worship.

- **324:** *Letter to the Eastern Provincials concerning restitution* (*Ad provinciales Palaestinae*). A theological proclamation and operative edict restoring property, status, and liberty to victims of persecution; it is preserved as a Greek translation in Eusebius.

- **324:** *Letter to the Eastern Provincials concerning religious concord* (*Ad provinciales Orientis*). A public manifesto against persecution and polytheistic error that nevertheless requests peaceful coexistence; it survives through Eusebius’s Greek version of a Latin original.

- **324:** *Letter to Alexander and Arius*. A failed peace-making intervention treating their theological dispute as unnecessarily divisive and ordering reconciliation.

- **324–325:** *Letter to Publilius Optatianus Porfyrius*. A response to the poet’s presentation volume for Constantine’s vicennalia; it is an act of literary patronage preserved with Optatian’s poems.

- **325:** *Summons to the Council of Nicaea*. A protocol convening bishops at Nicaea; the surviving Syriac witness is late, and the wording’s authenticity has been questioned.

- **325:** *Letter to the Churches after the Council of Nicaea*. A circular enforcing the council’s settlement of the Easter question and presenting its consensus as divinely achieved.

- **325:** *Letter to the Church of Alexandria*. A doctrinal proclamation announcing Arius’s condemnation and endorsing the Nicene bishops.

- **325:** *Letter to Arius and the Arians*. A polemical imperial address attacking Arius; its invective makes it as much a public manifesto as correspondence.

- **325:** *Order against the Writings of Arius*. A protocol requiring Arius’s books to be burned and threatening anyone who concealed them; it is sometimes transmitted as part of a broader letter to the churches.

- **325:** *Letter to the Nicomedians against Eusebius and Theognis*. A theological and political denunciation of the two bishops following Nicaea.

- **325:** *Letter to Theodotus of Laodicea*. An admonition instructing the bishop to avoid the errors attributed to Eusebius of Nicomedia and Theognis.

- **325–326:** *Letter to Macarius concerning the Church of the Holy Sepulchre*. An architectural protocol ordering an exceptionally magnificent sanctuary at Jerusalem and authorizing Macarius to obtain materials and public assistance.

- **c. 326:** *Letter to the Bishops of Palestine concerning Mamre*. An order to suppress cult activity at Mamre and construct a church there; its transmitted placement and addressees differ among witnesses.

- **c. 326–330:** *Edict against “heretics”*. A coercive protocol prohibiting named Christian groups from assembling and transferring their meeting places to the Catholic Church.

- **330:** *Letter to the Numidian Bishops*. A building and restitution order responding to Donatist occupation of a basilica and authorizing another church at imperial expense.

- **c. 330–332:** *Letter to the Antiochenes*. A circular directing the community not to remove Eusebius from Caesarea in order to make him bishop of Antioch.

- **c. 330–332:** *Letter to the Synod concerning Eusebius of Caesarea*. A related intervention opposing Eusebius’s transfer to Antioch.

- **c. 330–332:** *Second Letter to Eusebius of Caesarea*. A commendation of Eusebius for declining the see of Antioch.

- **c. 330–333:** *Letter to Shapur II*. A diplomatic confession of Christian faith commending Christians within the Sasanian realm to the Persian king; whether it was dispatched exactly as Eusebius transmits it cannot be demonstrated.

- **c. 333:** *Letter to Antony*. A lost communication, jointly associated with Constantine and his sons, known only from Athanasius’s account of Antony’s reply.

- **c. 333:** *Letter to Eusebius praising his work on Easter*. A request for further copies of Eusebius’s writing; the work praised and the precise date are uncertain.

- **c. 333:** *Letter to Eusebius commissioning fifty biblical codices*. A production protocol ordering fifty portable, professionally copied sets of scripture for Constantinople’s churches. The codices themselves are lost, and identifying Codex Sinaiticus or Codex Vaticanus with this commission is conjectural.

- **335:** *First Letter to Athanasius*. A fragmentary summons connected with the Council of Tyre; only a few lines survive.

- **335:** *Letter to the Alexandrian Church*. A public intervention in the accusations against Athanasius, expressing confidence in the bishop; its relation to Constantine’s subsequent treatment of Athanasius requires caution.

- **335:** *Second Letter to Athanasius*. A rejection of accusations brought by Meletian opponents, surviving in Athanasius’s apologetic dossier.

- **335:** *Letter to John the Meletian*. A congratulatory message concerning John’s reported reconciliation with Athanasius.

- **335:** *Letter to Arius*. A summons inviting Arius to court to present his profession of faith.

- **335:** *Letter to Dalmatius*. A lost judicial order requiring an investigation into the alleged murder of Arsenius; it is known only from Athanasius.

- **335:** *Letter to the Council of Tyre*. An instruction that the synod pursue ecclesiastical peace; the letter survives through Eusebius.

- **335:** *Letter to the Bishops assembled at Tyre*. A summons requiring the bishops to appear at Constantinople and account for their proceedings.

- **335:** *Letter urging the Council of Tyre to proceed to Jerusalem*. A lost or unquoted protocol known from narrative notices in Eusebius and Sozomen.

- **c. 336:** *Letter acknowledging receipt of the fifty biblical codices*. A lost letter mentioned by Eusebius; its existence is reported, but its words and exact date are unavailable.

### Legislation

- **306–337:** *Imperial Constitutions of Constantine*. A large body of edicts, letters, rescripts, judicial decisions, and speeches to the Senate rather than a single authored code. Hundreds of enactments or fragments survive chiefly because the fifth-century compilers of the *Codex Theodosianus* began their official collection with Constantine; they abbreviated, divided, retitled, and sometimes altered the texts, while other Constantinian laws survive in the *Codex Justinianus*, the *Fragmenta Vaticana*, the *Collatio*, the *Constitutiones Sirmondianae*, inscriptions, papyri, and literary quotations.

- **7 March 321:** *Law on the Venerable Day of the Sun*. A protocol ordering judges, urban populations, and workshops to rest on Sunday while allowing agricultural labor; it survives in an edited form in the *Codex Justinianus*.

- **3 July 321:** *Law permitting testamentary gifts to the Catholic Church*. A legal instrument giving ecclesiastical corporations capacity to receive bequests, preserved in the *Codex Theodosianus*.

- **c. 312–337:** *Fiscal and administrative reform enactments*. A distributed project—including measures affecting taxation, municipal obligations, the imperial bureaucracy, appeals, and official misconduct—rather than a freestanding text; individual constitutions survive unevenly and often only in later excerpts.

- **c. 312–337:** *Criminal, family, and status legislation*. A heterogeneous body of operative texts on marriage, sexual conduct, slavery, manumission, inheritance, abduction, exposure of children, and punishment. It resists treatment as a coherent authored “work,” and several enactments’ dates or Constantinian attribution are disputed because the legal compilations preserve damaged or contradictory subscriptions.

- **c. 313–337:** *Legislation concerning Christian institutions and religious practice*. A body of protocols granting privileges to clergy and churches, recognizing episcopal adjudication, restoring property, regulating dissident groups, and addressing sacrifice and divination. It is not a single programmatic code, and claims that Constantine enacted a comprehensive ban on pagan worship during his lifetime remain contested.

### Cities and urban Gesamtkunstwerke

- **324–330:** *Constantinople, or New Rome*. The refoundation and monumental enlargement of Byzantium as an imperial residence and capital, dedicated on 11 May 330. It is best understood as a continuing urban Gesamtkunstwerk—walls, streets, forums, palace, hippodrome, churches, temples, statuary, ceremonies, population policies, and legal privileges—made by an imperial administration and many laborers rather than personally designed by Constantine.

- **c. 313:** *Constantina, formerly Cirta*. The reconstruction and renaming of the Numidian capital after its destruction during the civil wars. The city is an imperial urban project, not a single object, and the extent of Constantine’s direct planning is unknown.

- **c. 324–330:** *Helenopolis, formerly Drepanum*. A civic refoundation or renaming in honor of Constantine’s mother Helena, accompanied by imperial attention to the martyr Lucian’s shrine; surviving evidence does not define a single construction campaign.

### Imperial and civic architecture

- **306–337:** *Constantinian Palace and Audience Hall at Trier*. A palace complex incorporating the monumental aula now called the Konstantinbasilika. Its several phases began before or continued beyond Constantine, so “Constantinian” describes imperial-period patronage rather than secure personal design.

- **c. 310:** *Baths of Constantine at Trier*. A vast imperial bathing project whose intended function may have shifted before completion; substantial remains survive, but construction history and completeness are debated.

- **312–315:** *Basilica of Maxentius and Constantine*. Constantine completed and reoriented the civic basilica begun by Maxentius in the Roman Forum, adding a new entrance, apse, and colossal imperial image. It is therefore an appropriated and rewritten predecessor’s project, not wholly his creation.

- **312–315:** *Colossus of Constantine*. A monumental seated portrait installed in the Roman basilica, surviving as marble fragments. Whether it was made entirely for Constantine or recut from an earlier statue remains disputed.

- **312–315:** *Arch of Constantine*. A senatorial triumphal monument commemorating Constantine’s defeat of Maxentius, assembled extensively from earlier imperial sculpture alongside newly carved reliefs. It bears Constantine’s program but was formally dedicated by the Senate, making sole authorship inappropriate.

- **c. 315–316:** *Baths of Constantine on the Quirinal*. An imperial bath complex in Rome known from late-antique notices and later remains; repeated rebuilding and eventual destruction obscure the original design.

- **c. 324–330:** *Constantinian Land Walls of Constantinople*. Fortifications enclosing a city much larger than Severan Byzantium. They were superseded by the Theodosian walls, and their course is reconstructed largely from texts and topography.

- **c. 324–330:** *Mese and monumental street system of Constantinople*. The capital’s principal processional avenue and associated colonnaded routes formed an urban and ceremonial project rather than a discrete building.

- **c. 324–330:** *Forum of Constantine*. An oval or circular forum on Constantinople’s principal avenue, framed by porticoes and ceremonial architecture. Later rebuilding and textual legend make its original contents difficult to separate from subsequent additions.

- **dedicated 330:** *Column of Constantine*. A porphyry honorific column carrying an imperial statue, probably fashioned in solar imagery; later Christian relic traditions and restorations complicate reconstruction of its first state.

- **c. 324–330:** *Great Palace of Constantinople*. The initial imperial residential and administrative complex beside the Hippodrome. Almost everything known about its Constantinian form is reconstructed from later sources, and many buildings later attributed to Constantine were actually later additions.

- **c. 324–330:** *Hippodrome of Constantinople*. Constantine enlarged, completed, and decorated an arena begun under Septimius Severus, integrating it with the palace through the imperial box. Like the Roman basilica, it is an appropriation and transformation rather than a wholly new work.

- **c. 324–330:** *Capitolium of Constantinople*. A civic and religious complex traditionally assigned to Constantine’s foundation program; its precise form, date, and cultic use remain uncertain.

- **c. 324–330:** *Constantinian Senate Houses at Constantinople*. Assembly buildings associated with the Augustaeum and the Forum of Constantine. The sources disagree about their early history, and later rebuilding prevents confident reconstruction.

### Christian foundations and sacred landscapes

- **c. 313–324:** *Lateran Basilica* (*Basilica Constantiniana*, now San Giovanni in Laterano). Rome’s episcopal cathedral, built over or beside the former barracks of the imperial horse guard and endowed by Constantine. The emperor was patron rather than architect, and the present church preserves little of the original fabric.

- **c. 315–325:** *Lateran Baptistery*. A monumental baptismal building associated with the cathedral; its earliest phase is Constantinian, but later rebuilding makes the original plan uncertain.

- **c. 315–325:** *Basilica of Saints Marcellinus and Peter and Helena’s Mausoleum*. A funerary basilica and imperial mausoleum on the Via Labicana, now fragmentary. It joins congregational, commemorative, dynastic, and mortuary media and thus approaches a sacred Gesamtkunstwerk.

- **c. 315–325:** *Basilica Apostolorum on the Via Appia* (later San Sebastiano fuori le mura). A covered-cemetery or “circus” basilica associated with apostolic commemoration; Constantinian-family patronage is accepted broadly, but the precise donor and chronology remain debated.

- **c. 315–330:** *Basilica of Saint Lawrence on the Via Tiburtina*. A funerary basilica established near Lawrence’s tomb. Its Constantinian structure was distinct from the present church and survives only archaeologically and textually.

- **c. 315–330:** *Basilica on the Via Praenestina*. A now-lost or archaeologically fragmentary covered-cemetery basilica assigned to the Constantinian patronage system; its dedication and exact patron remain uncertain.

- **c. 315–330:** *Basilica on the Via Ardeatina*. A funerary basilica traditionally connected with Pope Mark and the Constantinian period; archaeological identification and imperial patronage are less secure than for the Lateran or Saint Peter’s.

- **c. 320–327:** *Old Saint Peter’s Basilica*. A five-aisled pilgrimage and funerary basilica placed over the cemetery believed to contain Peter’s tomb. Constantine’s patronage is secure, but construction probably involved several years and institutional collaborators; the building was replaced during the Renaissance.

- **c. 320s:** *Santa Croce in Gerusalemme and the Sessorian complex*. A hall in the imperial Sessorian palace was adapted for Christian worship and associated with Helena and relics of the Cross. Constantine’s precise contribution is difficult to distinguish from Helena’s and from later alterations.

- **c. 325–335:** *Church of the Holy Sepulchre complex*. A multi-part sanctuary at Jerusalem comprising the Martyrium basilica, courtyard around Golgotha, and Anastasis over the tomb. Ordered and funded by Constantine but designed and executed by bishops, officials, architects, and laborers, it is a liturgical-architectural Gesamtkunstwerk rather than a personally authored building.

- **c. 326–333:** *Church of the Nativity at Bethlehem*. A basilica and octagonal memorial above the cave identified as Christ’s birthplace, sponsored by Constantine and associated in the literary tradition with Helena. Its original building was replaced under Justinian.

- **c. 326–333:** *Eleona Basilica on the Mount of Olives*. A Constantinian sanctuary above a cave associated with Christ’s teaching and Ascension. Attribution is shared in the sources between Constantine’s patronage and Helena’s sacred-site activity.

- **c. 326–333:** *Basilica at Mamre*. A church and enclosed sacred precinct ordered at the traditional site of Abraham’s oak, partly to displace existing ritual practices. Archaeology and Eusebius confirm the project, although its exact architectural sequence remains debated.

- **c. 327–341:** *Golden Octagon at Antioch* (*Domus Aurea*). A centrally planned cathedral begun under Constantine and completed after his death under Constantius II. It is therefore a Constantinian project but not a completed Constantinian object.

- **c. 324–337:** *Church of the Holy Apostles and Constantine’s Mausoleum*. A dynastic and apostolic memorial planned at Constantinople as Constantine’s burial place. Eusebius describes the emperor’s mausoleum, but whether Constantine also completed an adjoining church—or whether the church proper belonged chiefly to Constantius II—is disputed.

- **before 337:** *Churches of Hagia Eirene and Hagia Sophia at Constantinople*. Later tradition connects both foundations with Constantine, but the first securely attested Hagia Sophia was completed under Constantius II, while the early history of Hagia Eirene is obscure. They should therefore be treated as uncertain Constantinian projects, not securely finished works.

- **306–337:** *Empire-wide church-building program*. Letters and narrative sources attest numerous repairs, enlargements, new churches, and grants whose buildings cannot all be individually identified. This is a distributed patronage project rather than a catalogue of objects personally designed by Constantine.

### Reproducible objects and visual manifestations

- **306–337:** *Coinage in Constantine’s name*. A vast, catalogued body of gold, silver, and base-metal denominations struck at multiple imperial mints, including dynastic portraits, military and solar types, Christian signs, vows, victories, camp gates, and the commemoratives for Rome and Constantinople. These are official mass-produced manifestations authorized by the regime, not objects engraved or struck by Constantine personally; mint, officina, date, legend, and bust variations are catalogued separately in *Roman Imperial Coinage* volumes VI and VII.

- **c. 318–324:** *Coin types bearing the chi-rho on the helmet or military equipment*. Small-scale visual manifestations linking imperial victory with the Christian sign. Their interpretation remains contested because the symbol appears within a changing repertoire rather than as an unambiguous declaration on every issue.

- **330–337:** *VRBS ROMA and CONSTANTINOPOLIS commemorative coinage*. Paired civic personifications celebrating old and new Rome, struck across the empire in many mint and officina variants. The series is a reproducible political manifesto rather than one singular coin.

- **c. 317–337:** *Dynastic coin and medallion programs*. Issues representing Constantine’s sons, wife, mother, and deceased relatives constructed succession and household legitimacy. Design and production were collective products of the court and mints.

- **c. 312–337:** *Imperial portrait type of Constantine*. A distributed visual creation encompassing colossal sculpture, relief, cameos, medallions, coins, and painted or metal images. It has no single fixed original and resists being called one work, but its upward gaze, youthful face, and increasingly diademed presentation formed a deliberate public identity.

- **c. 312:** *Labarum*. An imperial military standard incorporating the Christian christogram, reportedly made after Constantine’s visionary experience. Eusebius supplies the fullest description only later, and neither an original object nor an independently contemporary technical record survives, so its first form and date remain disputed.

- **c. 324–330:** *Spoliation and statuary program of Constantinople*. The collection, relocation, and recontextualization of celebrated statues, columns, and cult objects from across the empire made the new capital into an assemblage of earlier works. It is creation by selection and arrangement—an urban manifestation whose individual components were not made by Constantine’s regime.

### Commissions and unfinished projects

- **c. 333:** *Fifty Copies of the Scriptures for Constantinople*. A commission to Eusebius for fifty professionally copied codices, probably intended for newly founded churches. The books are not extant as an identifiable set, their contents are unspecified, and associating surviving fourth-century biblical manuscripts with them is speculative.

- **c. 337:** *Persian campaign*. A military-religious project prepared with an army, bishops, and a portable church before Constantine’s death. It was never carried out by him, so the made thing here is the unfinished apparatus and intention, not a completed conquest.

- **before 337:** *Proposed church in honor of Lucian at Helenopolis*. An imperial sacred-building project reported in connection with Constantine’s reverence for the martyr; its precise realization and architectural form cannot be securely established.

### Misattributions and resistant “works”

- **eighth century:** *Donation of Constantine*. A papal-imperial privilege falsely presented as Constantine’s grant to Pope Sylvester. It is not Constantine’s work; its long attribution to him is itself an important political creation of the early medieval period.

- **late or legendary:** *Correspondence between Constantine and Helena on Judaism and Christianity*. Letters transmitted under their names but rejected as spurious; they belong to Constantine’s later textual afterlife, not his surviving oeuvre.

- **late or legendary:** *Treaty of Constantine, Sylvester, and Tiridates*. A purported agreement lacking credible Constantinian provenance. It resists inclusion as a work except as a record of later authors manufacturing a Constantinian past.

- **various dates:** *Unspecified “multitude of letters,” sermons, prayers, and discourses*. Eusebius reports extensive imperial writing and speechmaking beyond the documents he reproduces. These cannot be assigned invented titles or dates and survive only as testimony to a lost body of court-mediated utterance.
