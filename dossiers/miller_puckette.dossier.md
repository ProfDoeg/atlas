This dossier was prepared from Puckette’s own curriculum vitae, publication catalogue, technical and autobiographical writings, institutional biographies, patent records, interviews, conference materials, contemporary project documentation, and later historical accounts.

A dedicated adversarial search combined his full name and common name with terms including *controversy, criticism, scandal, allegation, accusations, lawsuit, fraud,* and *conspiracy*, with additional searches covering 2024–2026. A Georgia automobile case returned by name-based searching concerned another person and has therefore been excluded.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1959–1976

Puckette was born in Chattanooga in 1959 and grew up in Tennessee. He attended **Sewanee Academy**, an institutional predecessor of today’s St. Andrew’s-Sewanee School, in Sewanee. Later summaries sometimes call him an alumnus of St. Andrew’s-Sewanee, but the contemporary mathematical record identifies the school by its historical name.

His unusually strong mathematical ability was publicly visible while he was still in secondary school:

- In **1975**, representing the United States at the seventeenth International Mathematical Olympiad in Bulgaria, he scored 39 points, ranked seventh overall, and received a **gold medal**.
- In **1976**, at the eighteenth Olympiad in Lienz, Austria, he scored 26 points, ranked twenty-third, and received a **silver medal**.
- A history of the American mathematical olympiads identifies him as the second-listed United States Mathematical Olympiad student in 1975 and associates him with Sewanee Academy.

These trips to Bulgaria and Austria are the earliest firmly documented international journeys in his life.

No reliable source consulted gives detailed childhood anecdotes, early musical training, religious upbringing, illnesses, family finances, or the exact circumstances by which he first learned programming. His later account says that music and computers both interested him before his career choice, but that formal exposure at MIT brought those interests together.

## Education and Formation, 1976–1986

### Massachusetts Institute of Technology

Puckette studied mathematics at MIT and received a **B.S. in Mathematics in 1980**.

In **1979**, he became a William Lowell Putnam Mathematical Competition Fellow, meaning that he was one of the competition’s five highest-scoring individuals. His CV describes the honor as first place in the 1979–80 competition. He subsequently held:

- a **National Science Foundation graduate fellowship**, 1980–1983;
- a **William Lowell Putnam graduate fellowship**, 1983–1984.

The decisive formative relationship at MIT was with composer and computer-music researcher **Barry Vercoe**. Puckette later dated his entry into computer music to **1979**, while studying under Vercoe and encountering the Music 11 synthesis system at MIT’s Experimental Music Studio.

Three bodies of work shaped his technical thinking:

1. **Music-N and Music 11.** These systems represented sound synthesis as networks of “unit generators” controlled by a score.
2. **Max Mathews’s RTSKED and GROOVE.** These suggested real-time scheduling through concurrent processes and the separation of event control from audio synthesis.
3. **Graphical patching.** In 1980 Puckette saw Richard Steiger and Roger Hale’s unpublished **Oedit**, which graphically represented a Music 11 orchestra. He later emphasized that visual patch languages predated Max and that Max’s graphical idea was not wholly original.

Puckette began trying to construct a real-time computer-music system in the summer of **1980**. His earliest documented collaborators were Vercoe and **John Stautner**. Their work produced a real-time Music 11 emulator and a four-channel reverberation network. Puckette and Stautner’s 1982 article on multichannel reverberators became an enduring signal-processing reference.

Around **1982**, Puckette began developing **Music 500**, a real-time synthesis system without a graphical front end. He later described it as the earliest recognizable implementation in the lineage that became Max. Hardware limitations repeatedly frustrated attempts to combine flexible event scheduling, real-time synthesis, and an accessible interface.

Max Mathews spent a summer at MIT as a visiting researcher in approximately **1983 or 1984**. Puckette got to know him then. Mathews was both an intellectual influence and the eventual namesake of Max.

### Harvard University

Puckette pursued graduate mathematics at Harvard while continuing to work in computer music. He received a **Ph.D. in Mathematics in 1986**. His dissertation, *Shannon Entropy and the Central Limit Theorem*, was a 63-page work in probability/information theory.

The available sources do not identify his dissertation adviser. His movement from pure mathematics into computer music was therefore not a formal abandonment of mathematics: signal processing, probability, spectral analysis, and real-time scheduling remained central to his later research and artistic practice.

### MIT employment during graduate study

His CV records:

- **1982–1986:** research specialist, MIT Experimental Music Studio/MIT Media Lab;
- **1986–1987:** research scientist, MIT Media Lab.

The Media Lab formally opened in 1985. Institutional biographies consequently describe Puckette as a member “from its inception” until 1987.

## Early Career and the Development of Max, 1985–1994

### Synthetic Performer and score following

Puckette worked with Barry Vercoe on the **Synthetic Performer**, a system that listened to a live instrumentalist and adjusted a computer-generated part to the performer’s changing tempo. Vercoe and Puckette presented “Synthetic Rehearsal: Training the Synthetic Performer” in 1985.

Real-time score following was also being developed independently by Roger Dannenberg. Puckette later distinguished the approaches: Dannenberg concentrated on estimating tempo for a sequencer, whereas Vercoe’s system also had to control changing audio processes.

### Move to IRCAM

Puckette moved into research at **IRCAM**—the Institut de Recherche et Coordination Acoustique/Musique—in Paris:

- **1987–1993:** research staff member;
- **1993–1994:** head of the Real-Time Applications Group.

IRCAM had been founded by composer-conductor **Pierre Boulez** and possessed specialized real-time hardware, most notably Giuseppe di Giugno’s 4X system. The institution supplied the technical and artistic environment within which Puckette’s earlier experiments became a usable system.

### “The Patcher” and Max

Puckette began writing the graphical “patching” interface in **1987** and presented **“The Patcher”** at the 1988 International Computer Music Conference. The interface used boxes connected by patch cords, allowing musicians to assemble processes visually.

He later explained that the graphical interface arose because increasingly elaborate text descriptions were too difficult to visualize. The graphical component became a larger programming project than the scheduler, although Puckette considered the real-time scheduler the more original contribution.

The first major artistic use was **Philippe Manoury’s *Pluton***, for piano and computer, part of Manoury’s *Sonus ex Machina* cycle. Max analyzed aspects of the pianist’s performance—attacks, dynamics, and tempo—and controlled the 4X system accordingly. Manoury developed the concept of a **“virtual score”** around this capacity: the electronic part was specified but responded to the contingencies of live performance.

The cycle also included *Jupiter* for flute and computer, *Neptune* for percussion and computer, and *La Partition du ciel et de l’enfer* for orchestra and computer. Puckette’s technical work made these forms of live interaction practicable.

### Commercial Max

IRCAM owned the code written by its employee. It licensed a Macintosh version to **Opcode Systems**, which released Max commercially in **1990**. **David Zicarelli** became the principal commercial developer and extended the program. Puckette and Zicarelli co-authored the 1991 *MAX Development Package Manual*.

At IRCAM, Puckette also created:

- **FTS (“Faster Than Sound”)**, a multiprocessor real-time monitor;
- a Max/FTS architecture for the IRCAM Signal Processing Workstation, using a NeXT computer and dedicated DSP boards;
- score-following, analysis, synthesis, sequencing, and performance tools;
- work later associated with a nonlinear-distortion method of voice and musical-sound synthesis.

Joseph Francis and Paul Foley ported Max/FTS to Unix and X Windows in the early 1990s. A later IRCAM team led by François Déchelle extensively reworked it as **jMax**.

Max received *Keyboard* magazine’s **1990 Software Innovation of the Year** award.

## UC San Diego and Pure Data, 1994–2004

### Appointment at UCSD

Puckette left IRCAM and joined UC San Diego’s Department of Music in **1994**:

- **1994–1996:** assistant professor;
- **from 1996:** professor;
- later Distinguished Professor;
- **2000–2011:** associate director of the Center for Research in Computing and the Arts (CRCA).

UCSD’s music department joined experimental composition, performance, and computer research. Puckette taught computer music, electronic-music theory, sound analysis and synthesis, and related seminars.

### The intellectual-property break with IRCAM

Puckette’s departure led to the most significant documented controversy of his career. His account is direct but partisan and must be labeled accordingly.

**Documented fact:** the code Puckette wrote at IRCAM belonged, or “almost certainly” belonged, to IRCAM as his employer. IRCAM licensed Max commercially.

**Puckette’s documented claim:** after leaving, he wanted to continue and disseminate work on Max, but IRCAM would not permit that. He described himself as having been “burned” by the proprietary model and accused IRCAM of excessive protectionism. He argued that its policies:

- reduced its standing among researchers;
- discouraged employees from remaining;
- isolated it from outside innovation;
- constrained composers whose electronic realizations depended on IRCAM-controlled systems.

**Institutional outcome:** IRCAM continued Max/FTS as jMax and ultimately released jMax as free software. The commercial Macintosh line moved through Opcode and later Cycling ’74. No consulted record shows that Puckette sued IRCAM, that IRCAM sued him, or that a court determined ownership between them.

### Creation of Pure Data

After moving to UCSD, Puckette began a clean, redesigned system, **Pure Data** or **Pd**. Dating differs by source:

- Puckette wrote in *Max at Seventeen* that he began Pd after moving to UCSD in 1994.
- Early Pd papers appeared in 1996 and 1997.
- His CV lists published/open-source Pd from 1997 onward.
- The community commonly treats **1996** as its birth year.

The uncertainty reflects incremental development, not a disputed act of authorship.

“Pure Data” referred to the redesign of data representation and, in one retrospective explanation, “Pd” could also mean **“Public Domain.”** Pd was open source from the beginning and is now distributed under a BSD-style license.

Pd retained the boxes-and-patch-cords paradigm but reconsidered Max’s handling of:

- data structures;
- audio processing;
- timing;
- portability;
- source availability;
- interaction with graphics and video.

Puckette wrote DSP “tilde” objects for Pd. Zicarelli used that work as a starting point for **MSP**, introduced into commercial Max when general-purpose personal computers became fast enough for native real-time sound processing. Thus **Max/MSP** is not simply a single program written entirely by Puckette: Max originated with Puckette, the commercial line was extensively developed by Zicarelli and others, and MSP built upon Pd’s signal-processing design.

### Global Visual Music

Beginning in **1997**, Puckette collaborated with visual artist **Vibeke Sorensen** and composer **Rand Steiger** on the **Global Visual Music** project.

Its first performance experiment, *Lemma 1*, took place at the International Computer Music Conference on **27 September 1997**. Steven Schick performed on drums and George Lewis on trombone, with cameras attached to the musicians’ hands. Puckette, Sorensen, and Steiger operated the audio, video, and computer systems. The Intel Research Council sponsored the project.

*Lemma 2* was performed simultaneously at Columbia University’s Miller Theatre in New York and an Intel conference facility in Oregon during the 1999 Columbia Interactive Arts Festival. The work explored distributed, improvised audiovisual performance.

### Pure Data Repertory Project

Around **2001–2002**, Puckette began the **Pure Data Repertory Project (PDRP)**. It sought open, maintainable realizations of canonical works for instruments and live electronics. The project addressed a recurrent preservation problem: a composition could survive on paper while its electronic realization became unperformable when proprietary hardware or software disappeared.

## Mature Career, Performance, and Research, 2004–2016

### Convolution Brothers

From **2004**, Puckette performed with the **Convolution Brothers**, a computer-music group associated with live, often improvisatory electronic performance. His increasing activity as a performer accompanied a longstanding preference for music made in real time rather than studio-centered production.

### Intellectual and artistic position

Puckette’s writings and interviews articulate several consistent principles:

- electronic music is most interesting as live practice;
- software inevitably channels users toward what it makes easy;
- both Max and Pd are powerful yet inadequate;
- tools should expose basic operations rather than automate artistic decisions;
- the lasting object is musical practice and transmissible knowledge, not necessarily the original executable;
- composers, performers, and listeners increasingly overlap in electronic music;
- access to inexpensive computers could decentralize electronic music from elite institutions.

In 2016 he said that Max’s polished, production-oriented ecology and Pd’s do-it-yourself community had become distinct, although overlapping, cultures. He believed both programs acquired recognizable aesthetic tendencies despite his aspiration toward neutral software.

### Book

Puckette published *The Theory and Technique of Electronic Music* with World Scientific in **2007**. It treats audio synthesis, spectra, filters, modulation, sampling, Fourier analysis, and related subjects, with practical Pd examples. An authorized version remains freely available online.

The book is not a memoir or history of Max; it is a technical textbook intended to give musicians direct command of the mathematical and signal-processing foundations behind electronic sound.

### Installations and media work

His CV identifies:

- ***Lipsody* (2013):** sound installation, Avatar gallery, Quebec City.
- ***Illud Etiam* (2016):** fifteen-minute video; Puckette supplied sound design and other production work. First shown at the Festival Berlioz, La Côte-Saint-André, France, in August 2016.
- ***Four Sound Portraits* (2016):** sound installation at the third Kochi-Muziris Biennale, Kochi, India. It portrayed or invoked four composers Puckette admired: John Cage, Iannis Xenakis, Charles Dodge, and Philippe Manoury.

### Collaborators and students

Documented collaborators include Philippe Manoury, Barry Vercoe, John Stautner, David Zicarelli, Jean-Claude Risset, Cort Lippe, Zack Settel, François Déchelle, Rand Steiger, Vibeke Sorensen, Juliana Snapper, Kerry Hagan, Jaime E. Oliver La Rosa, Irwin, Roger Reynolds, George Lewis, Steven Schick, and numerous UCSD students.

Kerry Hagan was a graduate student of Puckette’s and later became a recurring artistic collaborator. She described their exchange as Puckette contributing programming and mathematics while she shaped the artistic application. Their later papers addressed spatial disturbance, presence and absence, and hand-gesture-to-timbre conversion.

Puckette and former student Jaime Oliver performed together at the 2016 Pure Data Convention using gesture-sensing controllers.

## Later Career, Networked Music, Preservation, and Retirement, 2017–Present

### Research after 2016

Puckette continued publishing on oscillators, stochastic sequences, gesture mapping, preservation of live electronics, and the reconstruction of historically important works.

A major late-career direction has been the preservation and “future-proofing” of live electronic realizations. His **Reality Check** framework separates the artistic specification of a work from particular obsolete software and hardware, permitting electronic parts to be tested and reimplemented.

### Puckette–Reynolds project

In **2019**, Puckette and composer **Roger Reynolds** received a joint UCSD Committee on Research award. Their project investigated real-time tracking of two continuously changing pitches produced together on a cello. Graduate researchers Jacob Sundstrom and Peter Ko participated, with cellist Alexis Descharmes also planned as a collaborator.

COVID-19 interrupted ordinary access to UCSD facilities. Early recordings were consequently made in Reynolds’s garage in Del Mar. The research fed a proposed approximately twenty-five-minute composition involving a live cellist and computer musician.

### Quacktrip and pandemic-era network music

Puckette developed **Quacktrip**, a low-latency, peer-to-peer network-audio system implemented in Pd, together with related tools such as Netty McNetface and Music 101. During the COVID-19 period these tools supported remote rehearsal and performance.

With the performer **Irwin**, Puckette developed Pd-based audio tools and plug-ins. Their first “not-quite-live” performance for the digitIZMir festival in Turkey occurred on **30 October 2020**: it was performed in real time but prerecorded for later transmission. They presented an IRCAM Forum keynote in March 2021.

### Retirement and continuing activity

A UCSD concert program states that Puckette and pianist Aleck Karis retired from UCSD in **2023**. Puckette remains Distinguished Professor emeritus and continues research, performance, publication, and collaboration.

His official publication list records new work through **2025**, including:

- preservation of real-time electronic realizations;
- the four-decade evolution of Manoury’s *Jupiter*;
- future-proofing a large electronic realization.

IRCAM’s current team page lists Puckette among affiliated researchers, indicating a later professional reconnection with the institution despite his earlier criticism of its intellectual-property policies.

## Political, Religious, Philosophical, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

No source consulted documents party membership, candidacy for public office, political donations, governmental employment, intelligence work, organized religious affiliation, or religious conversion.

His best-documented ideological position concerns **software freedom and artistic attribution**. After the IRCAM experience, he came to favor open-source dissemination. His argument was not that authorship disappears, but that digital artistic practice depends more reliably on attribution and shared technique than on exclusive possession of code.

Artistically, his trajectory ran from mathematical systems and signal processing toward live performance, improvisation, gesture, installation, and preservation. He resisted a rigid separation between technician and artist but also said that a programmer collaborating with a composer should not usurp decisions of musical form. In his description, the programmer may materially influence realization and sound while the composer retains responsibility for the work’s musical structure.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Barry Vercoe — teacher, mentor, employer, collaborator

Vercoe introduced Puckette to Music 11 and the MIT computer-music environment. They collaborated on real-time performance and score following. This was Puckette’s principal documented teacher-student relationship.

### Max Mathews — mentor and namesake

Puckette met Mathews during the latter’s MIT visit around 1983–84. Mathews’s Music-N, GROOVE, and RTSKED concepts influenced Puckette’s systems. Puckette named Max in his honor.

### Philippe Manoury — composer and formative collaborator

Manoury’s *Pluton* supplied the artistic problem around which the graphical Max environment achieved an early mature form. Their relationship continued through preservation and revision of *Jupiter* and other electronic realizations.

### David Zicarelli — developer and commercial successor

Zicarelli transformed and extended commercial Max, co-authored documentation with Puckette, and built MSP from ideas and code pathways associated with Pd’s audio engine. Their roles are complementary but frequently conflated in popular summaries.

Boulez founded the institution that employed Puckette and enabled Max’s development. No source consulted establishes a close personal friendship. Their relationship is best described institutionally.

### Rand Steiger and Vibeke Sorensen

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### 1. Ownership of Max and the break with IRCAM

**Status: documented institutional dispute; no located litigation or adjudication.**

Puckette acknowledges that code written on IRCAM salary belonged to IRCAM. He nevertheless argued that Max also embodied earlier MIT research, public ideas, other researchers’ contributions, and knowledge he personally carried away. IRCAM’s refusal, as Puckette described it, to permit him to disseminate further Max work led him to abandon that code base and create Pd.

The record supports a genuine conflict over intellectual-property control. It does not support claims that Puckette stole Max, that IRCAM judicially found him in breach, or that Pd was adjudicated an infringing copy.

### 2. Attribution of Max/MSP

**Status: recurrent oversimplification, not established misconduct.**

Popular accounts sometimes say Puckette “invented Max/MSP.” More precisely:

- Puckette created the Max lineage and its original graphical/scheduling architecture.
- Zicarelli and other Opcode/Cycling ’74 developers created and maintained the commercial product.
- Pd’s DSP design influenced MSP.
- Jitter and many other extensions were developed by still other programmers.

Attributing the entire modern Max platform exclusively to Puckette erases collaborators; denying his foundational authorship is equally misleading.

### 3. Technical and aesthetic criticism

**Status: documented criticism, much of it voiced by Puckette himself.**

Critics have noted Max/Pd’s unconventional data handling, hidden object internals, execution-order difficulties, and tendency to produce visually tangled patches. Puckette’s *Max at Seventeen* explicitly catalogued weaknesses, especially Max’s strength in representing process and weakness in representing data.

He also argued that software shapes musical style by steering users toward convenient operations. His ideal of “neutral” software was therefore an acknowledged aspiration rather than an achieved state.

### 4. Governance of Pure Data

**Status: reported community characterization, not a scandal.**

Academic writing has described Puckette as Pd’s “benevolent dictator,” a conventional open-source term for a founding maintainer with unusually strong influence. No credible evidence located alleged financial diversion, hidden ownership, code theft, discriminatory governance, or other misconduct in the project.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### “Puckette single-handedly created modern Max/MSP”

- **Story:** Modern Max/MSP is solely Puckette’s invention.
- **Circulation:** Brief biographies, software histories, and promotional descriptions.
- **Evidence:** He unquestionably created Max’s foundational architecture and early implementations. Zicarelli and many others developed the commercial system, while MSP drew from Pd and was integrated by Cycling ’74.
- **Assessment:** **Simplifying origin myth.**
- **Effect:** It strengthens the image of Puckette as a solitary inventor while obscuring the distributed history of the software.

### “Max was invented in one moment in 1988”

- **Story:** Puckette created Max in 1988.
- **Evidence:** “The Patcher” appeared publicly in 1988, but Puckette traces the lineage through work begun in 1980, Music 500 around 1982, a redesigned system in the mid-1980s, and graphical work begun in 1987.
- **Assessment:** **Conventional milestone date, not a literal account of invention.**

### “Max’s visual language was unprecedented”

- **Story:** Puckette invented graphical, node-and-wire programming.
- **Evidence against:** Puckette himself cited Oedit, analog synthesizer patching, and 1960s graphical-programming research as antecedents.
- **Assessment:** **Unsupported priority legend.** Max’s importance lies in its particular integration and dissemination, not in inventing every graphical principle it uses.

### “MSP stands for Miller Smith Puckette”

- **Story:** The Max audio extension was secretly or playfully named after Puckette’s initials.
- **Evidence:** The formal expansion is **Max Signal Processing**. The coincidence with Miller Smith Puckette is real and repeatedly noted.
- **Assessment:** **Wordplay/folk etymology**, not established naming history.

### “Pure Data is merely free Max”

- **Story:** Pd is a clone of Max made only to avoid payment.
- **Evidence against:** Pd deliberately changed data structures, audio processing, source distribution, stability, and other design decisions. It shares the Max paradigm and some interoperability but is a separate implementation.
- **Assessment:** **Misleading reduction.**

### “Pd means Public Domain”

- **Story:** Pure Data’s abbreviation secretly means Public Domain.
- **Evidence:** Puckette wrote that “one meaning” of Pd was Public Domain, connecting the name to his reaction against proprietary control. Pd is nevertheless not literally unrestricted public-domain code; it is distributed under an open-source license.
- **Assessment:** **Documented authorial wordplay, often misunderstood literally.**

### Solitary-genius reputation

Puckette is frequently framed as “the man behind” Max and Pd. His own histories complicate that framing by naming Vercoe, Mathews, Steiger, Hale, Manoury, Zicarelli, Déchelle, Francis, Foley, Dannenberg, and extensive user communities. The solitary-inventor image has promotional force but is historically incomplete.

## Legacy and Reputation

Puckette’s principal historical importance lies in joining several previously separate practices:

- Music-N-style unit generators;
- real-time concurrent scheduling;
- graphical patching;
- interaction with live performers;
- portable software synthesis;
- open-source community development.

Max became a commercial platform used in composition, sound installation, performance, audiovisual art, interface design, robotics, and education. Pd made a related paradigm freely modifiable and embeddable, contributing to open-source audio, installations, games, mobile systems, and custom instruments.

His influence is partly indirect. Many artists never collaborated with him but work in environments that descend from his designs. Node-and-wire creative programming is now common enough that the Max interface can appear inevitable; Puckette’s own historical writing emphasizes that it was assembled from older ideas under particular hardware and artistic constraints.

The 2023 Venice Biennale Silver Lion formalized his standing not simply as a software engineer but as a figure whose programming, performance, and collaborations enabled major contemporary works.

His later preservation work addresses a liability created by the same technological culture: electronic works may become unperformable when their dependencies vanish. PDRP and Reality Check attempt to preserve the relationships and practices defining a piece rather than freezing one obsolete machine.

## Compact Chronology

- **1959, 26 March:** reportedly born in Chattanooga, Tennessee.
- **1975:** attends Sewanee Academy; wins IMO gold medal in Bulgaria.
- **1976:** wins IMO silver medal in Lienz, Austria.
- **1979:** begins computer-music work under Barry Vercoe at MIT; becomes Putnam Fellow.
- **1980:** receives MIT B.S. in Mathematics; begins experiments in real-time computer music.
- **1981:** first ICMC publications with Vercoe and John Stautner.
- **Circa 1982:** begins Music 500; becomes MIT research specialist.
- **1983–1984:** meets Max Mathews during Mathews’s MIT visit.
- **1985:** Synthetic Performer paper; MIT Media Lab opens.
- **1986:** Harvard Ph.D.; dissertation on Shannon entropy; becomes MIT Media Lab research scientist.
- **1987:** joins IRCAM research staff; begins graphical patching interface.
- **1988:** publishes “The Patcher”; Max takes recognizable graphical form; work with Manoury on *Pluton*.
- **1989:** writes Max 2.0 reference manual.
- **1990:** Opcode releases commercial Max; software receives *Keyboard* innovation award.
- **1991:** publishes foundational Max and FTS articles; co-authors Max development manual with Zicarelli.
- **1993:** becomes head of IRCAM Real-Time Applications Group.
- **1994:** leaves IRCAM; joins UC San Diego; begins the work that becomes Pure Data.
- **1995:** publishes nonlinear formant-synthesis work; French patent listed.
- **1996:** early Pd publications; US Patent 5,524,173; becomes UCSD professor.
- **1997:** Pd publicly documented; *Lemma 1* launches Global Visual Music performance work.
- **1998:** Pd audio tools contribute to the technical lineage of MSP.
- **1999:** distributed *Lemma 2* performance.
- **2000:** becomes associate director of UCSD CRCA.
- **2001–2002:** launches public-domain live-electronics realization work and PDRP; publishes “Max at Seventeen.”
- **2004:** first international Pd Convention; publishes “Who Owns Our Software?”; begins performing with Convolution Brothers.
- **2007:** publishes *The Theory and Technique of Electronic Music*.
- **2008:** receives SEAMUS Lifetime Achievement Award.
- **2011:** CRCA closes; receives honorary doctorate from Université de Mons.
- **2012:** receives honorary degree from Bath Spa University.
- **2013:** creates *Lipsody*.
- **2014–2015:** publishes critical reflections on software/users, real-time processes, stochastic sequences, and sampling.
- **2016:** *Illud Etiam* and *Four Sound Portraits*; performs with Jaime Oliver at Pd Convention.
- **2019:** begins funded project with Roger Reynolds; publishes with Kerry Hagan.
- **2020:** develops pandemic-era network-performance work including Quacktrip; performs remotely with Irwin.
- **2021:** Puckette–Irwin IRCAM Forum presentation; publishes on Reality Check.
- **2022:** publishes quaternion-phase oscillator research.
- **2023:** retires from regular UCSD appointment; becomes professor emeritus; receives Venice Biennale Silver Lion.
- **2024:** publishes Reality Check preservation framework.
- **2025:** publishes histories and preservation studies of Manoury’s *Jupiter* and a large electronic realization.
- **2026:** living; remains professionally active and institutionally associated with UCSD and IRCAM.
