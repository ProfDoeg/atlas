# Margery Kempe: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

Margery Kempe’s life is unusually vivid but unevenly documented. The principal source is *The Book of Margery Kempe*, a retrospective spiritual life dictated in the 1430s. Its surviving manuscript is not Margery’s original dictation but a copy made around 1445–1450. The work openly states that events were not written in chronological order and that Margery had forgotten some dates and sequences.

This dossier therefore uses the following labels:

- **Documented fact:** supported by an independent contemporary record or surviving physical manuscript.
- **Reported fact:** asserted in *The Book* or another source but not independently corroborated.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** a modern inference from documentary, textual, or contextual evidence.
- **Disputed interpretation:** a substantive disagreement among scholars.
- **Visionary claim:** a supernatural experience reported by Margery; historically documented as testimony, not independently verifiable as a supernatural event.
- **Hostile allegation:** an accusation made against her by contemporaries within her narrative.
- **Later legend or reception claim:** a post-medieval representation whose historical accuracy requires separate assessment.

The documentary imbalance is fundamental: male Brunham and Kempe relatives appear in Lynn records, but almost all details of Margery’s pregnancies, businesses, marriage, pilgrimages, examinations, visions, illnesses, and personal relationships survive only through her Book. The two probable guild entries of 1437–1439 are the only generally accepted external records that may name Margery herself. [Mapping Margery Kempe: documentary introduction](https://college.holycross.edu/projects/kempe/text/intro.html)

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Usual name:** Margery Kempe.
- **Birth name:** Margery Brunham.
- **Middle English forms:** Margery, Margerie; Brunham also appears in variant forms such as Burnham.
- **Self-designation in the Book:** ordinarily “this creature,” rather than “I.” This is a devotional and narrative convention, not evidence that someone else was necessarily the work’s principal author.
- **Later descriptions:** mystic, visionary, pilgrim, vowess, spiritual autobiographer, lay religious woman.
- **Historically misleading title:** “ancress of Lynn.” Wynkyn de Worde’s approximately 1501 printed extracts and Henry Pepwell’s 1521 edition helped recast her as an anchoress. The complete Book shows that she was a mobile married laywoman, not an enclosed anchoress.
- **Birth:** approximately 1373, at Bishop’s Lynn, Norfolk, England, now King’s Lynn. No birth or baptismal record is known.
- **Death:** in or after 1438; possibly after 1439. The exact date, place, and cause are unknown.
- **Religion:** Latin Christian/Catholic. Her stated sacramental beliefs were orthodox by contemporary standards, although authorities repeatedly suspected or accused her of Lollardy or unauthorized preaching.
- **Language and literacy:** she spoke English and encountered Latin and other languages during travel. Her capacity to read or write remains disputed. The Book represents her as dependent on readers and scribes; that does not establish absolute illiteracy in every practical sense.
- **Principal residence:** Bishop’s Lynn.
- **Known or reported occupations:** household manager; brewer; operator of a horse-powered grain mill; pilgrim; devotional speaker and counselor; narrator and directing participant in the production of her Book.
- **Principal surviving work:** *The Book of Margery Kempe*, dictated in stages and substantially completed in 1436–1438.

## Family and Ancestry

### Brunham family

**Documented fact:** Margery belonged to the commercially and politically prominent Brunham family of Bishop’s Lynn.

Her father, **John Brunham senior**, was a merchant and civic officeholder. Lynn records identify him as:

- mayor in 1370, 1377, 1378, 1385, and 1391;
- member of Parliament on six occasions between 1364 and 1384;
- chamberlain;
- coroner;
- justice of the peace;
- alderman of the wealthy Holy Trinity Guild.

His municipal position placed Margery’s childhood within Lynn’s urban mercantile elite rather than among the rural poor. The town was a major East Anglian port connected to the Great Ouse, the Wash, London, the Low Countries, Baltic commerce, and the Hanseatic world. [Charity Scott Stokes, “The Family Background”](https://college.holycross.edu/projects/kempe/text/familybg.html)

**Documented fact:** A **Ralph de Brunham**, probably Margery’s grandfather or close ancestor, appears in Lynn’s Red Register by 1320 and had entered the governing community by about 1340.

**Probable relationship:** **John Brunham junior** was Margery’s brother. A **Robert Brunham**, MP for Lynn in 1402 and 1417, was a kinsman and may also have been her brother. The precise Brunham genealogy is not completely recoverable.

Margery’s mother is not named in the Book or securely identified in surviving records. Her absence is notable because Margery names her father, husband, children, confessors, and numerous spiritual associates. Nothing reliable is known of her maternal ancestry.

### Marriage

**Reported fact:** At “twenty years of age or somewhat more,” approximately 1393, Margery Brunham married **John Kempe** of Lynn.

John had civic and property interests, although he did not equal John Brunham senior’s political stature. He became a Lynn burgess and held a town office in 1394. The Book depicts differences in wealth and status between the Brunhams and Kempes as important to Margery’s early marital pride.

No marriage contract survives. The marriage remained legally valid throughout John’s life even after the couple agreed to sexual abstinence.

### Children

**Reported fact:** Margery bore fourteen children. The number comes from her Book; most are unnamed and cannot be traced individually. Their birth dates, sex distribution, survival rates, marriages, and later lives are largely unknown.

The first child was probably a son named **John Kempe**, born around 1393–1394. Anthony Bale and Sebastian Sobecki connect him with the Book’s unnamed first scribe:

- A 1431 letter prepared for a John Kempe in Danzig/Gdańsk identifies him as travelling to Lynn because of his father’s illness.
- The Book says its first scribe was an Englishman who had lived overseas in the German-speaking or Low Countries region.
- It describes the scribe as someone whose death left the initial text difficult to decipher.

This makes the son’s identification as the first scribe plausible, but not conclusively proven. [Sebastian Sobecki, “The writyng of this tretys”](https://research.rug.nl/en/publications/the-writyng-of-this-tretys-margery-kempes-son-and-the-authorship-/)

**Reported fact:** This son returned from continental Europe with his wife around 1431. The Book says that he had lived dissolutely, became ill, interpreted his condition as divine punishment for criticizing his mother, repented, and later died. These moral and causal elements belong to Margery’s retrospective spiritual interpretation.

**Reported fact:** The son’s widow—Margery’s unnamed daughter-in-law—later wished to return to her family in Prussia. Margery accompanied her on the journey beginning in 1433. The daughter-in-law’s identity and ultimate fate remain unknown.

No evidence supports confident reconstruction of Margery’s other thirteen children. Claims that all died young or that she abandoned them go beyond the surviving evidence.

## Childhood and Early Life, c. 1373–1393

Margery grew up in Bishop’s Lynn during a period of expanding commerce, municipal faction, religious patronage, and ecclesiastical–civic conflict. Lynn belonged jurisdictionally to the bishop of Norwich and possessed numerous churches, religious houses, hospitals, guilds, and shrines.

No formal record of Margery’s schooling survives. As a prosperous merchant’s daughter she could have acquired:

- household accounting and commercial knowledge;
- familiarity with contracts, measures, brewing, food production, and servants;
- oral knowledge of scripture, sermons, saints’ lives, and liturgy;
- limited vernacular reading or writing.

These are contextual possibilities, not documented elements of a curriculum.

Her Book’s later responsiveness to sermons and devotional books shows sophisticated religious memory. Works read aloud to her included or were said to include:

- Bridget of Sweden’s *Revelations*;
- Richard Rolle’s *Incendium amoris* or *Fire of Love*;
- Walter Hilton’s *Scale of Perfection*;
- the *Pricke of Love*, then attributed to Bonaventure;
- biblical passages with patristic exposition;
- saints’ lives and Passion meditations.

Whether she could recognize, read, or write individual words is unresolved. Rebecca Krug and other scholars have questioned the older assumption that “illiterate” meant wholly unable to engage with texts. The Book nevertheless makes oral performance, memory, dictation, and clerical mediation central to its production.

## Marriage, Childbirth, Illness, and Conversion, c. 1393–1394

### First pregnancy and psychological crisis

**Reported fact:** Soon after marriage Margery became pregnant. Following a difficult birth she feared death and attempted to confess a long-concealed sin. The confessor rebuked her before she completed the confession.

She then experienced an illness lasting roughly eight months. Her own account includes:

- despair over damnation;
- terrifying sights and sounds;
- demonic figures urging her to renounce faith and family;
- biting and scratching herself;
- physically violent or uncontrolled behavior;
- the need for restraint and constant supervision;
- social withdrawal and loss of ordinary judgment.

**Visionary claim:** Christ appeared at her bedside, asked why she had forsaken him, and restored her reason. She thereafter resumed household life, though her full devotional transformation developed gradually.

### Retrospective diagnosis

Modern writers have variously called this episode:

- postpartum depression;
- puerperal or postpartum psychosis;
- hysteria;
- bipolar or manic-depressive illness;
- dissociative crisis;
- culturally organized religious experience.

No retrospective diagnosis can be clinically verified. The account was dictated approximately forty years later, arranged in a spiritual conversion narrative, and transmitted through scribes.

Phyllis Freeman, Carley Rees Bogarad, and Diane Sholomskas argued that “postpartum psychosis” alone does not encompass the later cycles of elevated and depressed behavior described in the Book; they proposed that medieval categories and recurrent mania–melancholia patterns must also be considered. This remains an interpretive model, not a diagnosis established from clinical evidence. [Freeman, Bogarad, and Sholomskas](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0957154X9000100202)

## Business Activity and Finances, c. 1390s–early 1400s

### Brewery

**Reported fact:** Margery operated a brewery for three or four years. She says it became one of the leading brewing operations in Lynn and that she employed servants.

Brewing was a commercially important field in which medieval urban women frequently worked. Margery’s ability to enter it was consistent with her family background and marriage within Lynn’s burgess class.

The business failed. According to her Book, batches of ale stopped fermenting properly, employees left, and townspeople attributed the failure to divine punishment for her pride. Margery herself retrospectively interpreted it as God’s correction.

No surviving account book establishes the brewery’s capitalization, revenue, property, debts, or legal structure.

### Horse mill

**Reported fact:** She next established a horse-powered grain mill. The horses repeatedly refused to pull the machinery, and the enterprise failed.

Her account again converts commercial failure into providential narrative: God frustrated an occupation undertaken from pride and desire for status. Mechanical malfunction, animal handling, competition, financing, or managerial problems cannot be independently evaluated.

### Clothing, status, and debt

The Book presents early Margery as socially ambitious. She wore elaborate clothing, hoods, gold piping, and other marks of prosperity. Later she contrasted this display with white clothing, poverty, and penitential dependence.

After returning from her first continental pilgrimage in 1415, she was reportedly ill and in debt. Her journeys required continual negotiation of fares, gifts, food, lodging, alms, and credit. At different times she received assistance from priests, merchants, fellow pilgrims, and strangers.

No reliable total for her property or wealth survives. She was neither consistently destitute nor independently wealthy throughout adulthood. Her resources fluctuated, and her natal family’s prestige did not protect her from debt or dependence.

## Religious and Intellectual Development, c. 1400–1413

### Turn from commerce toward devotion

Margery increasingly practiced:

- prolonged prayer;
- fasting and dietary restrictions;
- frequent confession and communion;
- meditation on Christ’s Passion;
- charitable visits;
- efforts to stop sexual relations with her husband;
- public religious conversation;
- pilgrimage.

**Visionary claims:** She reported interior speeches from Christ, the Virgin Mary, apostles, saints, angels, and at times the souls of the dead. She also described demonic temptations, sensory experiences, prophetic knowledge, and scenes in which she imaginatively entered biblical history.

### Sexual temptation and ascetic negotiation

The Book does not present a smooth progress toward sanctity. It recounts an intense sexual temptation involving an unnamed man. Margery says she once offered herself to him after earlier rejecting him; he then refused and humiliated her. She understood the episode as exposure of spiritual pride.

This story has generated conflicting readings:

- a confession of actual attempted adultery;
- a stylized temptation narrative;
- evidence of marital and sexual frustration;
- a deliberate display of fallibility that strengthens the Book’s penitential structure.

There is no independent record of an affair or prosecution for adultery.

### Chaste marriage

Margery repeatedly asked John to live chastely. He initially refused and invoked marital rights. In a frequently discussed negotiation, John asked what she would do if he were in danger and she had to choose between helping him and attending religious devotions. She answered that she would assist him.

Around 23 June 1413 the couple agreed to abstain from sexual relations. John reportedly required Margery to:

- pay his debts;
- eat and drink with him on Fridays rather than maintain her preferred fast;
- consent to the arrangement as his wife.

They later sought ecclesiastical recognition from **Philip Repyngdon**, bishop of Lincoln. Repyngdon received them but directed the larger matter to Archbishop Thomas Arundel. The Book reports no formal transformation of the marriage into religious vows recognized throughout the Church.

### Confessors and validators

Important early supporters included:

- **Robert Spryngolde**, Margery’s parish priest at St Margaret’s, Lynn;
- an unnamed Dominican anchorite at Lynn;
- **Master Alan of Lynn**, Carmelite theologian and writer;
- **Richard Caister**, vicar of St Stephen’s, Norwich;
- **William Southfield/Sowthfeld**, Carmelite friar;
- **Julian of Norwich**, anchoress and author of the *Revelations of Divine Love*.

Caister, Southfield, and Julian reportedly listened to Margery’s revelations and advised her that experiences tending toward charity, humility, and obedience could be accepted as divine. Caister remained an important confessor until his death in March 1420. [Marriage, confessors, and Norwich network](https://college.holycross.edu/projects/kempe/text/marriage.html)

## Chronological Life History

## 1413: Lincoln, London, and authorization

Margery and John travelled to Lincoln to seek episcopal recognition of their chaste marriage. Bishop Philip Repyngdon treated her favorably but did not grant all she sought.

They then travelled to Lambeth Palace. The Book says Margery rebuked members of Archbishop **Thomas Arundel’s** household for swearing. The courtiers compared her to a Lynn priest burned for Lollardy in 1401, indicating the danger of vernacular religious speech under Arundel’s anti-Lollard regime.

Arundel reportedly interviewed her in his garden and issued letters allowing her:

- to choose her own confessor;
- to receive communion weekly, subject to clerical discretion.

The document itself is not known to survive. Its existence is a reported fact from the Book.

Margery also travelled to Norwich, where she consulted Richard Caister, William Southfield, and Julian of Norwich.

## 1413–1415: Jerusalem, Italy, and first major continental pilgrimage

Margery departed Lynn through Norwich and Great Yarmouth, crossed to **Zierikzee**, and travelled toward **Constance**, **Bologna**, and **Venice**. From Venice she sailed in a pilgrim galley to the eastern Mediterranean.

### Conflict with the pilgrimage company

Her companions objected to:

- her continual religious conversation;
- her crying and roaring;
- her attempts to correct conduct;
- her distinctive clothing;
- the social disruption created by her claimed revelations.

They sometimes excluded her from meals, refused companionship, or threatened to abandon her. A sympathetic, physically disabled man—apparently the “broken-backed man” earlier predicted by the Lynn anchorite—assisted her.

The Book is strongly shaped to contrast persecuting companions with providential helpers. The companions’ own accounts do not survive.

### Holy Land

In 1414 she reached **Jaffa** and travelled by donkey through the Holy Land. Sites reported include:

- Jerusalem;
- the Church of the Holy Sepulchre;
- Calvary/Golgotha;
- Bethlehem;
- the Mount of Olives;
- the River Jordan;
- other locations associated with Christ’s life and Passion.

At Calvary she experienced the first overwhelming episode of the loud sobbing or “roaring” that became her public signature. These attacks could include falling, bodily convulsion, pallor, and inability to speak normally.

**Visionary claim:** She directly witnessed the Passion in imaginative contemplation and responded as though present at Christ’s crucifixion.

The itinerary is consistent with established late-medieval pilgrimage practice, but no independent passenger list or local record confirms her presence.

### Italy

Returning through Venice, she visited:

- Assisi;
- Rome;
- major Roman basilicas and shrines.

She stayed in or was associated with the Hospital of St Thomas of Canterbury in Rome. She met English, German, Italian, and other pilgrims and clerics.

A key associate was **Margaret Florentine**, now plausibly identified by Anthony Bale and Daniela Giosuè as **Margherita degli Alberti**, a Florentine religious woman. This is a scholarly identification based on archival and contextual evidence, not an absolutely certain equation.

**Visionary claim:** In Rome, Christ contracted a mystical marriage with Margery, telling her to take him as husband. The episode supplied a spiritual alternative to ordinary marital identity while leaving her legal marriage intact.

She also reported visions at the tomb of St Jerome and during Mass.

### Return, 1415

Margery left Rome, passed again through Constance, followed the Rhine route to Middelburg, crossed the North Sea after storms and delays, and probably landed at Great Yarmouth. She reached Norwich in May 1415, saw Richard Caister, and received white clothing.

Her insistence on wearing white was controversial because white suggested virginity or formally consecrated religious status. Margery was a married mother of fourteen without an established office as nun or anchoress.

## 1415–1417: Lynn, illness, devotional reading, and preparation for Spain

After returning to Lynn, she was ill during the winter and burdened by debt.

The Dominican anchorite who had supported her died, probably around this period. Another priest read devotional and theological books aloud to her. Her Book names Bridget of Sweden, Walter Hilton, Richard Rolle, and the *Pricke of Love* among important textual influences.

Master Alan of Lynn reportedly supported her for approximately twenty years, although ecclesiastical superiors sometimes ordered him to avoid her. His support gave her access to an unusually learned clerical network.

## 1417: Santiago de Compostela

Margery travelled to Bristol and waited approximately six weeks for passage. She came under suspicion there, apparently because public religious speech by a laywoman could be associated with Lollardy.

She sailed to **Santiago de Compostela**, stayed briefly, visited the shrine of St James, and returned to England. The pilgrimage was much shorter than her Holy Land journey.

## 1417: Arrests and examinations in England

The latter part of 1417 contains the most concentrated sequence of legal and ecclesiastical danger in the Book. England was tense over Lollardy and the flight, recapture, and execution of Sir John Oldcastle.

Margery reports being detained, questioned, or threatened at Bristol, Leicester, York, Cawood, Beverley, and elsewhere. No independent transcripts survive.

### Leicester

The mayor of Leicester accused her in abusive terms of being a false strumpet, a lying Lollard, and a corrupter of women. An official or steward also behaved threateningly toward her. She was confined and interrogated.

The authorities challenged her right to speak about scripture and religion. Margery denied that she preached in the formal sense. She maintained that ordinary Christian conversation about God was permitted by the Gospel.

She was asked to recite the Articles of Faith and respond to sacramental questions. Her answers, as recorded in her Book, affirmed:

- the Eucharist;
- confession;
- baptism;
- Church authority;
- pilgrimage and religious images.

These positions distinguish her from central Lollard doctrines. Her criticism of immoral priests and swearing nevertheless overlapped with common reformist themes.

### York and Cawood

In the province of York she was examined before Archbishop **Henry Bowet** or his officers. She was challenged over her white clothing, crying, travels, and religious speech.

The Book says she told a moral tale about a priest and a bear, criticized clerical misconduct, and successfully defended her orthodoxy. Officials required her to obtain documentary protection from Canterbury rather than condemning her.

### Beverley and the Duke of Bedford’s officials

Margery says agents connected with **John, Duke of Bedford**, suspected her of being **Joan of Arc**, or of being associated with enemy forces. This should not be presented as a formal identification supported by state records. It is a reported episode reflecting wartime fear, surveillance of mobile women, and the political charge attached to female visionary authority.

### Outcome

She was not convicted of heresy. No surviving record shows indictment, formal penance, imprisonment after sentence, or ecclesiastical condemnation.

The absence of conviction does not prove every examination occurred exactly as narrated. Conversely, the lack of register entries does not disprove them: bishops’ clerks selected which proceedings to record. [Charity Scott Stokes, “Investigations”](https://college.holycross.edu/projects/kempe/text/invest.html)

## 1417–1418: English pilgrimage circuit

After returning from Spain, Margery travelled through a large circuit including:

- Hailes Abbey;
- Leicester;
- Melton Mowbray;
- York;
- Bridlington;
- Hull;
- Hessle;
- Beverley;
- Lincoln;
- West Lynn;
- London;
- Ely;
- Bishop’s Lynn.

At Bridlington she visited sites associated with **John of Bridlington**, canonized in 1401. Her mobility repeatedly placed her outside ordinary household supervision and exposed her to questioning.

## c. 1418–1420: London, Norwich, and clerical networks

Margery made repeated visits to London, Norwich, Ely, and religious houses.

She prayed at Richard Caister’s tomb after his death in March 1420. Caister acquired a local devotional reputation and was credited with the lyric “Jesu, Lord, that madest me.”

The Book also records meetings with senior clerics and theologians, including **Thomas Netter of Walden**, Carmelite anti-Lollard polemicist. Some clerics tested her claims; others accepted her as orthodox after hearing her profession of faith.

## 1420s: Ministry, tears, caregiving, and local conflict

The chronology becomes less precise because Book I is thematic and retrospective.

Margery:

- continued to travel between Lynn, Norwich, London, and regional shrines;
- sought frequent communion;
- gave spiritual advice to laypeople and clerics;
- prayed for the sick and dead;
- reported knowledge of the salvation or damnation of particular souls;
- became famous or notorious for involuntary crying;
- encountered mockery, clerical restrictions, and occasional support.

### Public crying

Her tears were not quiet. The Book describes roaring, screaming, collapsing, and bodily reactions triggered by:

- crucifixes;
- images of the Virgin and Child;
- sermons;
- childbirth or maternal scenes;
- wounded people or animals;
- references to Christ’s Passion.

Supporters interpreted the tears as a divine “gift of compunction.” Detractors regarded them as performance, disorder, hypocrisy, attention-seeking, or demonic deception.

No neutral clinical observer recorded the episodes. Their frequency and presentation are filtered through a text designed to establish spiritual authority.

### Care of John Kempe

As John aged, he suffered a serious accident or fall and increasing incapacity, possibly including cognitive decline. Margery returned to nurse him.

This episode fulfilled the earlier marital test in which she had promised to place his bodily need before her own devotions. The Book represents caregiving as part of her penitential vocation rather than escape from marriage.

John probably died around 1431. No securely identified death or burial record is known.

## 1431: Son’s return and deaths

A letter associated with Danzig indicates that John Kempe the younger returned to Lynn in 1431 because of his father’s illness. The Book states that the younger John and his wife visited Margery and that he died after conversion and reconciliation.

The sequence and exact dates of father’s and son’s deaths remain uncertain, but both probably died around 1431.

## 1433–1434: Prussia, Baltic Germany, and Aachen

Book II narrates Margery’s final major recorded journey.

In April 1433, when she was approximately sixty, she accompanied her widowed daughter-in-law toward Prussia. Her route included:

- Norwich;
- Ipswich;
- a North Sea passage;
- **Bergen**, Norway;
- **Danzig/Gdańsk**;
- **Stralsund**;
- **Wilsnack**;
- **Aachen**;
- Calais;
- Dover;
- Canterbury;
- London;
- Syon Abbey;
- Lynn.

The exact route is reconstructed from a sometimes compressed narrative.

### Bergen and Danzig

Travel was disrupted by weather, unfamiliar languages, unreliable companions, and dependence on merchants and sailors. At Danzig, Margery reached the mercantile world in which her son had lived and left her daughter-in-law with family or associates.

### Wilsnack

She travelled to the Holy Blood shrine at Wilsnack, one of northern Europe’s most controversial Eucharistic pilgrimage centers. The shrine rested on claims that consecrated hosts had survived a church fire and bled.

Margery was lame or seriously ill and was conveyed in a cart. Armed unrest in the region frightened her.

Her participation shows robust adherence to Eucharistic cult and pilgrimage, making the recurring Lollard accusations against her doctrinally incongruous.

### Aachen and return

Near Aachen she suffered from fleas while companions stripped to remove them. The anecdote preserves both the physical indignity of pilgrimage and Margery’s continuing concern with social decorum.

She crossed from Calais to Dover, remained well while other passengers were seasick, visited Canterbury and London, and went to Syon Abbey before returning to Lynn.

## 1430s: Production of the Book

### First dictation

The Proem says clerics had long urged Margery to have her experiences written. Approximately twenty years after her conversion, she dictated to an Englishman who had lived in continental Europe.

The first version was difficult or impossible for others to read. The scribe died before the project could be satisfactorily completed.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** Sebastian Sobecki argues that this first scribe was Margery’s son John. The Danzig letter strengthens but does not conclusively prove the identification.

### Priest-scribe and revision

A Lynn priest who had known Margery for many years initially declined or delayed. He attempted to work from the first manuscript but complained that its writing was unintelligible. After further hesitation and an episode of impaired eyesight, he resumed.

The substantial redictation or rewriting began on **23 July 1436**. Book II was begun on **28 April 1438**. The priest states that some of Book II was written directly “after her own tongue.”

**Scholarly reconstruction:** Robert Spryngolde is a strong candidate for this priest-scribe because documentary research shows close connections between him and the Brunham–Kempe family. The identification is probable, not certain.

### Narrative authorship

The authorship question has several defensible formulations:

- Margery is the originating narrator and experiential authority.
- The priest organized, translated speech into written prose, and may have edited.
- The surviving copyist introduced further linguistic and visual framing.
- Marginal annotators shaped the manuscript’s reception.

Lynn Staley influentially distinguished “Margery,” the character within the narrative, from “Kempe,” the authorial intelligence constructing that character. Other scholars emphasize collaborative authorship and resist treating dictated life writing as either purely Margery’s autonomous composition or merely a cleric’s production.

### Surviving manuscript

**Documented fact:** British Library **Additional MS 61823** is the unique complete witness.

It was copied around 1445–1450 on paper, probably in Norfolk. It includes:

- the Book on folios 1r–123r;
- red initials, rubrics, underlining, and devotional drawings;
- marginal annotations by several hands;
- a late-medieval or early-modern recipe for medicinal sweets or *dragges*;
- a bound-in 1440 letter from papal diplomat Peter de Monte to William Buggy of Soham.

The copyist signed **Salthows**. Anthony Bale has identified him, through palaeographical and archival research, as probably **Richard Salthouse**, a monk of Norwich Cathedral Priory. The British Library catalogue remains appropriately cautious.

The manuscript later belonged to the Carthusian house at **Mount Grace Priory**, Yorkshire. Its annotations show that at least some late-medieval readers treated Margery as a valid contemplative authority. [British Library manuscript catalogue](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/032-001962059)

## 1437–1439: Last documentary traces

Lynn Holy Trinity Guild accounts record payments by **John Assheden** for the admission of a **Margery Kempe** in 1437–1438 and again in 1438–1439.

**Probable documented identification:** Most scholars consider this likely to be the author, but the record does not contain enough biographical detail to make the identification absolute.

Admission would mark integration into the prestigious guild once associated with her father. Why Assheden paid, and whether he was a friend, relative, executor, or benefactor, is unknown.

After this entry Margery disappears from recoverable records. She may have died in 1439 or later. The frequently printed terminal date “1439” is an inference, not a documented death date.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Commercial enterprises

- **Brewery, Bishop’s Lynn:** operated for three or four years; reportedly prosperous before failure.
- **Horse mill:** short-lived grain-milling enterprise; reportedly failed when the horses would not operate the machinery.
- **Household economy:** fourteen pregnancies, servants, clothing expenditure, debt payment, and care of an incapacitated husband indicate substantial domestic financial management.
- **Pilgrimage finance:** relied on personal resources, credit, gifts, alms, fare negotiations, and aid from merchants and clergy.

No surviving records reveal corporate partnerships, formal investments, patents, or an estate inventory.

### Institutions connected to her

- St Margaret’s Church, Bishop’s Lynn, her parish church;
- Holy Trinity Guild of Lynn, probable member in 1438;
- Carmelite, Dominican, Franciscan, Benedictine, and Carthusian networks;
- Norwich Cathedral and St Stephen’s Church, Norwich;
- Lambeth Palace;
- diocesan administrations of Norwich, Lincoln, Canterbury, and York;
- Hospital of St Thomas of Canterbury, Rome;
- Syon Abbey;
- Mount Grace Priory, later owner and reader of her manuscript.

## Political Context and Activity

Margery held no political office and is not known to have participated in organized political movements.

Her life nevertheless intersected with:

- Lynn’s merchant oligarchy through her father;
- tensions between Lynn’s burgesses and episcopal lordship;
- Archbishop Arundel’s anti-Lollard constitutions;
- the suppression of vernacular religious dissent;
- Oldcastle’s rebellion and execution;
- the Hundred Years’ War;
- suspicion of mobile, unattached, or publicly speaking women;
- ecclesiastical conflicts over who could teach, preach, interpret scripture, wear religious clothing, and claim revelation.

Her confrontation with officials was religiously and politically charged, but the surviving evidence does not establish membership in a dissident party or Lollard network.

## Religious, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

Margery’s spirituality combined several established late-medieval traditions.

### Affective Passion devotion

She imaginatively entered scenes of Christ’s birth, ministry, Passion, and death. Physical crying made internal compassion publicly visible.

### Marian and maternal identification

As a mother of fourteen, she repeatedly imagined:

- assisting the Virgin after childbirth;
- caring for the infant Jesus;
- preparing food or comfort for Mary;
- grieving with Mary at the Crucifixion.

Maternal experience became a source of spiritual authority rather than merely an obstacle to devotion.

### Eucharistic devotion

She desired frequent communion and reported visions surrounding the consecrated host. Her pilgrimage to Wilsnack reinforces her commitment to orthodox Eucharistic devotion.

### Mystical marriage

Christ’s reported declaration that he was her husband transformed conventional bridal mysticism into the experience of a married laywoman.

### Orthodoxy and criticism

Her sacramental theology was orthodox as represented in the Book. Her disputes concerned:

- her right to speak publicly about God;
- direct visionary authority;
- criticism of sinful clergy;
- conspicuous dress and behavior;
- her refusal to fit established categories of wife, widow, nun, anchoress, or beguine.

### Textual influences

Her Book explicitly or implicitly draws on:

- Bridget of Sweden;
- Richard Rolle;
- Walter Hilton;
- Pseudo-Bonaventure;
- Passion meditations;
- lives of Catherine of Siena, Marie d’Oignies, Mary Magdalene, and other holy women;
- Julian of Norwich.

The degree of direct textual borrowing is debated because Margery normally heard books read rather than citing written copies herself.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### John Kempe, husband

A relationship containing sexuality, bargaining, conflict, companionship, separation, debt, and eventual caregiving. The Book does not support a simple portrait of either uninterrupted oppression or idealized support.

### John Kempe, probable son

Merchant or apprentice resident in Danzig; returned in 1431; possible first scribe. His criticism, illness, repentance, and death are narrated in providential terms.

### Julian of Norwich

Margery says she spent several days consulting Julian. Julian tested her revelations according to whether they produced love of God and neighbor and reportedly encouraged obedience to divine prompting. No independent record of the meeting survives, but it is one of the most significant reported encounters between medieval English women writers.

### Richard Caister

Confessor and validator in Norwich until his death in 1420. He accompanied Margery when she was summoned before the bishop of Norwich.

### William Southfield

Carmelite adviser who reportedly accepted the possible divine origin of her experiences.

### Master Alan of Lynn

Carmelite doctor of theology and prolific writer. He reportedly supported Margery for about twenty years and supplied intellectual validation, although ordered at times to distance himself.

### Robert Spryngolde

Parish priest and probable long-term confessor. Documentary connections to her family make him a leading candidate for the priest-scribe.

### Thomas Arundel

Archbishop of Canterbury. According to the Book, he examined her, accepted her orthodoxy, and gave limited written privileges.

### Philip Repyngdon

Bishop of Lincoln. Received Margery and John’s request concerning chastity and later reportedly supplied assistance or permission during her legal difficulties.

### Henry Bowet

Archbishop of York. Examined Margery or oversaw her examination; did not convict her but demanded authorization from Canterbury.

### Margaret Florentine

Companion and supporter in Rome, plausibly Margherita degli Alberti. She provided practical and emotional support during Margery’s Italian residence.

### Pilgrimage companions

A shifting network of priests, married couples, merchants, sailors, guides, and strangers. The Book divides them rhetorically into persecutors and providential helpers, although several relationships changed over time.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Contested Material

## Lollardy and heresy

**Hostile allegation:** Officials and townspeople repeatedly called Margery a Lollard or heretic.

**Evidence against formal Lollardy:**

- she affirmed transubstantiation and the sacraments;
- practiced confession;
- sought episcopal approval;
- venerated images, relics, saints, and pilgrimage shrines;
- sought indulgences and frequent communion;
- accepted priestly and episcopal authority.

**Evidence producing suspicion:**

- she spoke publicly about scripture;
- criticized immoral priests;
- used English religious discourse outside a cloister;
- emphasized direct divine instruction;
- travelled without stable male supervision;
- wore white as though possessing recognized religious status.

**Finding:** suspicion and examination are central and plausible; formal Lollard affiliation, charge, conviction, or punishment is not established.

## Unauthorized preaching

**Hostile allegation:** She preached despite prohibitions on women and unlicensed lay instruction.

Margery answered that she did not mount a pulpit or expound scripture formally; she only spoke of God in conversation. Whether contemporaries accepted that distinction depended on jurisdiction and circumstance.

**Finding:** she engaged in sustained public religious speech. Whether that legally constituted preaching was contested at the time and remains a question of definition.

## Sexual misconduct

Accusers called her a whore or strumpet. The Book also records sexual temptation and her offer to an unnamed man.

**Finding:** the insults are documented within her narrative as hostile allegations. No independent evidence shows prostitution, an adulterous relationship, or a sexual prosecution.

## Fraud, performance, or fabricated sanctity

Contemporaries accused her of feigning tears or performing holiness. Modern skeptical readings likewise ask whether the crying generated attention and authority.

Evidence available:

- Margery says she sometimes wanted the crying to stop because it provoked humiliation and travel difficulties.
- The Book repeatedly turns opposition into confirmation of election, creating an obvious self-validating structure.
- Some clerics accepted her after examination; others rejected or restricted her.
- No independent method can determine whether particular episodes were voluntary, involuntary, psychologically produced, or consciously staged.

**Finding:** fraudulent performance was alleged but never proven. The Book is deliberate self-fashioning; literary construction does not by itself prove that its underlying experiences were invented.

## Mental illness

Retrospective medical descriptions range from postpartum psychosis to bipolar disorder, hysteria, depression, dissociation, temporal-lobe phenomena, and non-pathological culturally mediated mysticism.

**Finding:** the first post-childbirth episode includes behavior compatible with severe psychiatric illness. Diagnosis across six centuries from a devotional text is unresolved. Later visions and crying cannot automatically be equated with the initial crisis.

## Marital and maternal neglect

Some modern criticism portrays her pilgrimages and celibacy as abandonment of husband and children.

Evidence is incomplete:

- she bore fourteen children and travelled extensively;
- the Book says almost nothing about arrangements for most children;
- John sometimes travelled with her;
- she later nursed him after disability;
- adult children and household or kin networks may have provided care, but this is not documented.

**Finding:** long absences are reported; “abandonment” is an interpretation not demonstrable from surviving household evidence.

## Pride and social antagonism

Margery admits early pride in family position, fashion, and business success. Even after conversion, the narrative sometimes displays satisfaction when critics suffer illness, seasickness, embarrassment, or reversal.

This has led scholars to read the Book as simultaneously penitential and self-assertive. The tension is textual fact, not proof that the entire narrative is fraudulent.

## Legal status

She was arrested or detained several times according to the Book, but:

- no known indictment survives;
- no conviction survives;
- she was not executed, permanently imprisoned, or formally declared heretical;
- “trial for heresy” is convenient shorthand but can overstate the procedural status of several examinations.

## Dedicated adversarial search finding

Searches combining Margery Kempe’s name with “controversy,” “scandal,” “allegations,” “lawsuit,” “fraud,” “accusations,” “conspiracy,” and “criticism,” including material indexed in 2024–2026, produced no credible evidence of a newly discovered criminal case, fraud, sexual scandal, conspiracy, or suppressed prosecution.

Recent contested scholarship continues to concern:

- retrospective psychiatric diagnosis;
- the accuracy and literary shaping of the Book;
- whether she was literate;
- whether her speech constituted preaching;
- her orthodoxy in relation to Lollardy;
- whether her son and Robert Spryngolde were her scribes;
- the identity of Salthows;
- the ethics of interpreting pilgrimage as maternal abandonment;
- whether “first autobiography in English” is anachronistic.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### “The first autobiography in English”

**Story:** Margery wrote the first autobiography in English.

**Origin and circulation:** The description became widespread after the complete manuscript’s rediscovery and 1940 edition.

**Evidence for:** The Book narrates an individual life retrospectively, foregrounds memory and personal experience, and is earlier than most comparable English life writing.

**Evidence against or qualification:** It was dictated collaboratively, uses third person, follows saint’s-life and spiritual-biography conventions, and is not ordered as a modern autobiography. Earlier English autobiographical passages and continental women’s visionary lives complicate priority claims.

**Reputational effect:** It transformed Margery from an obscure devotional figure into a canonical literary author.

### “She wrote the manuscript herself”

**Story:** Margery personally penned the surviving book.

**Evidence:** The Book explicitly describes dictation and multiple scribes. The surviving manuscript is a later copy signed Salthows.

**Finding:** false if “wrote” means physically inscribed the extant manuscript; defensible if “author” includes oral composition, direction, revision, and control of narrated experience.

### “Margery was an anchoress”

**Story:** She lived enclosed as “ancress of Lynn.”

**Appearance:** Wynkyn de Worde’s extracts, about 1501, and Henry Pepwell’s 1521 collection transmitted a contemplative, domesticated version of her.

**Evidence against:** The complete Book documents marriage, children, commerce, repeated travel, and extensive public interaction.

**Reputational effect:** Early print stripped away much of the disruptive mobility and controversy, making her resemble more conventional enclosed mystics.

### “She was a Lollard”

**Story:** Margery secretly belonged to the Lollard movement.

**Circulation:** Contemporary accusers used the label; later criticism examined her overlap with dissenting discourse.

**Evidence against:** her Eucharistic, sacramental, relic, image, pilgrimage, and clerical practices are emphatically non-Lollard.

**Finding:** contemporary allegation and important political context, not established affiliation.

### “She met Joan of Arc”

No evidence supports a meeting. The Book’s reported episode says officials suspected Margery of being Joan or connected her with the dangerous image of a female visionary. Margery and Joan were not documented in the same place, and Margery’s 1417 examinations predate Joan’s public military career if that dating is correct. Any literal identification is chronologically untenable.

### “She was a medieval feminist”

**Later interpretive claim:** Margery has been cast as a feminist pioneer resisting patriarchy.

**Evidence for the interpretation:** she negotiated sexual abstinence, travelled extensively, defended women’s religious speech, constructed authority through her own experience, and directed a major life-writing project.

**Qualification:** she did not articulate a general program of equal social or political rights and understood authority in Catholic, hierarchical, and visionary terms.

**Reputational effect:** feminist scholarship was central to her modern canonization, but the label can conceal her theological aims.

### “She was mad”

**Hostile and medicalizing narrative:** Both medieval detractors and some modern critics have treated her conduct as insanity.

**Evidence:** a severe postpartum crisis, hallucination-like experiences, bodily episodes, despair, and recurrent voices.

**Counterevidence:** sustained travel, commercial ventures, complex legal self-defense, management of clerical relationships, memory, caregiving, and supervision of a book project.

**Finding:** she experienced at least one severe mental crisis by her own account. A global characterization of her entire adult life as madness is not supported.

### “Her Book was lost for five hundred years”

The complete text effectively disappeared from public knowledge, but it was not literally absent from material existence. The manuscript was preserved, read, and annotated at Mount Grace and later entered private ownership. Extracts remained in print from 1501 and 1521.

The memorable “lost for five hundred years” formulation describes loss to scholarship and public reading, not uninterrupted physical disappearance.

### Rediscovery in a cupboard

**Later reception story:** The manuscript was found by chance in a cupboard or while searching for a ping-pong ball at the Butler-Bowdon country house.

**Documented core:** The Butler-Bowdon family possessed the manuscript; it was brought to scholarly attention in the early 1930s and identified by Hope Emily Allen in 1934.

**Legendary embellishment:** Details involving a cupboard, accidental household discovery, and a ping-pong ball occur in retellings with varying formulations.

**Reputational effect:** The chance-discovery story became part of Margery’s modern mythology, paralleling the Book’s own pattern of providential rescue.

## Works by Margery Kempe

## The Book of Margery Kempe

- **Original Middle English title:** The manuscript begins, “Here begynnyth a schort tretys and a comfortabyl for synful wrecchys…” The standard title derives from early print references to “the boke of Margerie Kempe of Lynn.”
- **Form:** dictated spiritual life, pilgrimage narrative, visionary record, household history, defense against heresy, and collaborative book project.
- **Composition:** first version probably early 1430s; substantial rewriting beginning 23 July 1436; Book II beginning 28 April 1438.
- **Authorship:** genuinely based on Margery’s oral testimony and direction; mediated by at least two scribes and a later copyist. The precise division of composition, editing, and arrangement is disputed.
- **Structure:** two books; Book I has 89 chapters in modern editions and Book II has 10.
- **Original manuscript:** lost.
- **Unique complete witness:** British Library Add MS 61823, copied about 1445–1450.
- **Early extracts:** Wynkyn de Worde, *Here begynneth a shorte treatyse of contemplacyon taught by our lorde Jhesu cryste, or taken out of the boke of Margerie Kempe of Lynn*, approximately 1501.
- **Second early extract publication:** Henry Pepwell, “A Short Treatyse of Contemplation…,” in *The Cell of Self-Knowledge*, 1521.
- **Status:** authentic attributed work with collaborative scribal mediation; not ghostwritten in the modern commercial sense.

No other independent book, letter, sermon, diary, artwork, or composition by Margery is securely known.

## Death and Final Status

Margery’s death is unrecorded.

The strongest terminal evidence is:

- Book II began in April 1438.
- A Margery Kempe was probably admitted to Lynn’s Holy Trinity Guild in 1437–1438.
- Another guild-account reference occurs in 1438–1439.

She was therefore alive in 1438 and possibly 1439. Claims that she died precisely in 1438, 1439, or 1440 are conventions or estimates.

No reliable evidence identifies:

- her place of death;
- cause of death;
- grave;
- will;
- estate inventory;
- funeral arrangements;
- memorial monument.

She was approximately sixty-five or older at the last trace.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Fifteenth-century reception

The surviving manuscript reached Mount Grace Priory and was annotated by Carthusian readers. Marginal notes emphasizing divine love, compunction, persecution, and spiritual testing show that some near-contemporary readers treated Margery seriously.

### Early print domestication

Wynkyn de Worde’s approximately 1501 extracts and Henry Pepwell’s 1521 printing preserved only selected contemplative passages. Calling her an anchoress muted:

- marriage and motherhood;
- failed businesses;
- sexual temptation;
- pilgrimage conflict;
- examinations;
- bodily crying;
- challenges to clerical control.

### Disappearance and rediscovery

The complete Book ceased to circulate widely. The Butler-Bowdon manuscript was recognized by Hope Emily Allen in 1934. W. Butler-Bowdon published a modern version in 1936; Meech and Allen’s 1940 Early English Text Society edition established the text for scholarship.

The British Library purchased the manuscript in June 1980.

### Modern reinterpretations

Successive scholarly phases have treated Margery as:

- a medieval mystic;
- a psychiatric case;
- a comic or grotesque figure;
- an autobiographer;
- a female author negotiating scribal control;
- a dissident without being a doctrinal heretic;
- a social critic;
- a merchant-class laywoman;
- a pilgrim within transnational networks;
- a practitioner of affective and embodied devotion;
- a constructed literary persona;
- an example of collaborative oral-textual authorship.

The older caricature of a hysterical, ridiculous woman has not vanished, but manuscript, gender, pilgrimage, medical-humanities, and archival scholarship has made it increasingly difficult to reduce the Book to either pathology or transparent sainthood.

### Institutional commemoration

King’s Lynn Minster, formerly St Margaret’s, commemorates Margery and contains a modern sculpture representing episodes from her life. The town has increasingly incorporated her into its historical identity.

She is remembered in parts of the Anglican Communion, although she was never formally canonized by the medieval Catholic Church. “Saint Margery” is therefore a devotional or scholarly formulation, not the result of a historical canonization process.

## Atlas Connections

Only source-supported connections are included. Mere contemporaneity has been excluded.

### Julian of Norwich

- **Nature:** reported personal meeting and spiritual consultation.
- **Date and place:** probably 1413, at Julian’s anchorhold by St Julian’s Church, Norwich.
- **Evidence:** Margery’s Book says she spent several days with “Dame Julian,” described her revelations, and received counsel concerning discernment, obedience, love, and the testing of spirits.
- **Label:** **Reported fact**. The Book is the sole narrative source; no independent entry by Julian confirms the meeting.

### Jesus of Nazareth

- **Nature:** central devotional subject and claimed visionary interlocutor; Margery presented Christ as savior, teacher, intimate companion, and mystical husband.
- **Dates and places:** visionary experiences reported from c. 1394 through the 1430s in Lynn, Jerusalem, Rome, and numerous churches.
- **Evidence:** pervasive throughout the Book.
- **Label:** **Visionary claim** as to direct speech and apparitions; **documented intellectual/religious influence** as to the content of her narrated spirituality.

### Mary Magdalene

- **Nature:** saintly model within Margery’s affective and penitential imagination.
- **Date and place:** recurrent devotional references rather than a historical meeting.
- **Evidence:** the Book invokes biblical and hagiographic women, including Mary Magdalene, as models of penitence, intimacy with Christ, and grief.
- **Label:** **Documented religious and literary influence**, not a personal historical connection.

### Catherine of Alexandria

- **Nature:** hagiographic model for a woman publicly defending faith before hostile male authorities.
- **Date and place:** literary-devotional influence; no historical meeting possible.
- **Evidence:** scholars have compared Margery’s examinations with St Catherine’s debates, and the Book’s cultural framework assumes familiarity with her cult.
- **Label:** **Documented cultural influence / scholarly interpretation**.

### Francis of Assisi

- **Nature:** Margery visited Assisi and Franciscan sacred sites during her Italian pilgrimage.
- **Date and place:** 1414–1415, Assisi.
- **Evidence:** reported itinerary in the Book.
- **Label:** **Reported devotional-geographical connection**, not a personal meeting.

No documented direct connection was found with Joan of Arc. The Duke of Bedford episode has sometimes encouraged juxtaposition, but the Book reports only that Margery was suspected or rhetorically associated with a dangerous female visionary. No meeting, correspondence, or collaboration is supported.

No further genuine intersection with the supplied atlas roster was established from the consulted evidence. The roster includes numerous biblical, legendary, and religious figures who could be associated typologically with medieval Christianity, but typological resemblance alone was not counted as a connection.
