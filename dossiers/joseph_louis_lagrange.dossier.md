# Joseph-Louis Lagrange: Research Dossier

**Prepared:** 24 August 2026  
**Scope:** Full-life chronological dossier, with documentary history separated from later anecdote and myth.  
**Name requested:** `Joseph-Louis_Lagrange_research_dossier.md`

## Research note and evidentiary conventions

This dossier rests chiefly on: Jean-Baptiste-Joseph Delambre’s memorial notice, read to the Institut after Lagrange’s death and reprinted in the collected *Œuvres*; Lagrange’s own works and correspondence; institutional records of the Turin Academy, French scientific institutions, and the Prussian Academy tradition; and modern studies by George Sarton, René Taton, Luigi Pepe, Bruno Belhoste, Sandro Caparrini, and Silvia Mazzone and Clara Roero. Delambre knew Lagrange and had access to colleagues and documents, but his text is an official éloge, not a neutral modern biography. Later character stories frequently derive from Delambre, Biot, Poisson, Chaptal, Fourier, Cournot, or nineteenth-century compilations; where the transmission is unclear, this is stated.

Labels used below:

- **Documented:** supported by primary/contemporaneous evidence or convergent high-quality scholarship.
- **Probable:** strongly supported, but the surviving record is incomplete.
- **Disputed/uncertain:** credible sources conflict or the primary record has not been located.
- **Late anecdote/legend:** a story repeated after Lagrange’s lifetime without secure contemporary documentation.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal line

Lagrange’s father was **Giuseppe Francesco Lodovico Lagrangia** (also rendered Giuseppe Francesco Ludovico), a Turin official and doctor of law. Sources describe him as treasurer connected with the Office of Public Works and Fortifications and/or with the Sardinian military chest. These descriptions may refer to connected fiscal duties rather than wholly different posts.

The family claimed French paternal origin. A great-grandfather, commonly described as a cavalry officer from Touraine serving Louis XIV, transferred to the service of the Savoyard ruler and settled in Turin after marrying into the Conti family. Later genealogical retellings sometimes make this descent grander—linking the family to Roman nobility or even Descartes. The broad French-to-Piedmont migration is well attested in biographical tradition; the more illustrious collateral connections are insufficiently documented and should be treated as family genealogy or status narrative.

### Maternal line

His mother was **Teresa Grosso** (also Gros), the daughter or only child of a physician from **Cambiano**, near Turin. Italian scholarship emphasizes that Lagrange was overwhelmingly Piedmontese by recent ancestry despite the French paternal great-grandfather. National claims that he was simply “French by blood” or simply “Italian” flatten an eighteenth-century Savoyard identity that predated modern national unification.

### Siblings and inheritance

He was the **eldest of eleven children**. Traditional biographies state that only Joseph-Louis and one younger brother survived childhood; exact names and dates of all siblings are not consistently supplied in accessible sources. Archival research published by George Sarton and René Taton records that Lagrange left no descendants, while later Turin Lagranges descended from his younger brother ([Sarton–Taton–Beaujouan, “Documents nouveaux concernant Lagrange”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/rhs_0048-7996_1950_num_3_2_2791)).

### Family finances

His father possessed a respectable office and some wealth but lost much of the family property in unsuccessful speculation or “hazardous enterprises.” The precise ventures, sums, creditors, and timing are not established in the sources consulted. A famous counterfactual attributed to Lagrange says that, had he inherited wealth, he might never have discovered his mathematical vocation. The ruin itself is broadly attested; the neat moral—poverty creating genius—is a biographical anecdote, discussed below.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1736–1754

Lagrange grew up in Turin, a court and military-administrative capital with strong technical institutions. He was educated at the **University of Turin**. His father initially directed him toward law. Lagrange later recalled that he had little early taste for mathematics and was awakened by reading a paper of **Edmond Halley** on the superiority of algebraic analysis over Greek geometrical methods. Delambre reports this story from Lagrange’s own conversation at the Bureau des longitudes, giving it better provenance than many childhood legends, although the exact Halley paper has been debated.

He learned mathematics largely by independent reading rather than through a single great master. **Giovanni Battista Beccaria**, professor of experimental physics at Turin, is often named as a teacher or formative institutional figure. Lagrange read Newton, Leibniz, the Bernoullis, Euler, d’Alembert, and Clairaut. His early ambition led him into a notable error: he initially believed he had found a new method, then discovered that it was already known through Leibniz and Johann Bernoulli. This embarrassment redirected him toward more original research.

On **23 July 1754**, aged eighteen, he sent or published a letter in Italian to **Giulio Carlo Fagnano** under the name *Luigi De la Grange Tournier*. Sarton’s archival study treats this as his first publication. Older narratives sometimes say he became professor at the Turin artillery school at sixteen; Delambre and later retellings repeat a very early appointment, while modern chronologies tend to place it in **1755**, when he was nineteen. Without the original appointment instrument, “sixteen” should be treated as a doubtful compressed tradition rather than a firm date.

## Education and Formation

Lagrange did not follow the later doctorate–advisor model. No dissertation supervisor comparable to a modern Ph.D. adviser is documented. His formation was instead a combination of:

- university education in Turin, initially oriented to law;
- Beccaria’s scientific environment;
- self-teaching through the printed work of Euler and others;
- direct correspondence with **Leonhard Euler**, beginning in 1755;
- intellectual patronage and strategic advice from **Jean le Rond d’Alembert**;
- collaborative institution-building with young Turin scholars.

In 1755 Lagrange sent Euler his new analytic method for variational problems. The [Euler Archive’s correspondence guide](https://eulerarchive.maa.org/correspondence/correspondents/Lagrange.html) confirms the exchange. Euler had already derived the differential equation for the elementary variational problem in his 1744 *Methodus inveniendi*; Lagrange’s decisive contribution was a systematic variation method that dispensed with much of Euler’s geometrical machinery. Lagrange himself later wrote that Euler had found the equation first. The modern name **Euler–Lagrange equation** appropriately captures a layered priority, not a single moment of invention.

Euler behaved generously: he delayed or framed his own publication so that the younger man would receive credit and proposed him for the Berlin Academy. Lagrange was elected a foreign member on **2 September 1756**, aged twenty.

## Early Career in Turin, 1755–1766

### Artillery school and teaching

Lagrange became professor of mathematics at Turin’s **Royal Artillery School** (usually dated 1755). He taught mechanics and mathematics to military students. The appointment supplied institutional standing but modest compensation. The assertion that he was wholly uncredentialed should be qualified: he lacked a conventional advanced mathematical degree, but he was embedded in a technically sophisticated state institution.

### The *Miscellanea Taurinensia* and future Academy

With colleagues and former pupils, including **Giovanni Francesco Cigna** and **Giuseppe Angelo Saluzzo di Monesiglio**, Lagrange helped form a private learned society in 1757. It developed into the Royal Academy of Sciences of Turin. Its journal, the *Miscellanea philosophico-mathematica Societatis privatae Taurinensis*—generally cited as the *Miscellanea Taurinensia*—became the vehicle for substantial early papers. The [Turin Academy record](https://www.accademiadellescienze.it/accademia/soci/Giuseppe-Luigi-Lagrange-Tournier) dates him as a resident national member from 1757.

His early memoirs treated maxima and minima, the calculus of variations, vibrating strings, sound, differential equations, and mechanics. In the vibrating-string controversy he entered a live dispute involving d’Alembert, Euler, Daniel Bernoulli, and others over admissible functions and physical representation. There is no evidence of personal enmity equivalent to the intellectual disagreement.

### Paris visit and Academy prizes

In **1763** Lagrange traveled from Turin to Paris, his first major journey outside Piedmont. He met d’Alembert, Clairaut, Condorcet, and other academicians. The journey made his reputation tangible within the European Republic of Letters.

The Paris Académie royale des sciences awarded him its **1764 prize** for a memoir on the Moon’s libration. He won again for work on the satellites of Jupiter (competition associated with 1766). Later prizes followed for the three-body problem (1772), lunar secular acceleration (1774), and cometary perturbations (1778; chronology and publication years in prize memoirs sometimes differ by a year between competition, decision, and printing).

### Recruitment to Berlin

Euler, Maupertuis, and d’Alembert recognized Lagrange’s exceptional ability. He declined early invitations to Berlin, at one point writing that Berlin would not suit him while Euler remained there. This is sometimes interpreted as fear of rivalry; it can equally be read as reluctance to displace or compete with the senior mathematician who had supported him. When Euler left Berlin for St Petersburg, **Frederick II of Prussia** invited Lagrange to direct the mathematical class. The famous wording—“the greatest king in Europe” wished to have “the greatest mathematician in Europe” at his court—is transmitted through biographical and correspondence traditions, but versions vary.

Lagrange left Turin in 1766. He traveled through Paris and reached Berlin in late October, beginning a twenty-one-year residence.

## Chronological Life History: Berlin, 1766–1787

### Appointment and court-academic setting

Lagrange succeeded Euler as director of mathematics at the **Royal Prussian Academy of Sciences**. He was salaried by Frederick’s academy and enjoyed high prestige, but the role belonged to an absolutist court institution: research priorities, prizes, pensions, and appointments depended on royal policy and academy politics.

His arrival disappointed **Jean de Castillon**, an older academy mathematician who believed the directorship should have gone to him. This was an appointment rivalry, not evidence of a lasting feud. Lagrange developed close relations with **Johann Heinrich Lambert** and **Johann III Bernoulli**. He continued intensive correspondence with d’Alembert, Euler, Condorcet, and Turin colleagues.

### First marriage

On **6 September 1767** (some secondary accounts give only September), Lagrange married his cousin **Vittoria Conti**, whom he had known in his father’s household. A 1769 letter to d’Alembert calls her a good housekeeper “without pretensions.” The phrase reflects Lagrange’s own domestic ideal and the gendered language of his milieu, not independent evidence about her abilities or personality.

They had no children. In the same correspondence Lagrange said he did not want children. Vittoria suffered prolonged ill health. Lagrange nursed her devotedly; she died in **1783**. Correspondence supports his grief and the severe depression or melancholy that followed.

### Work regimen, health, and depression

Frederick II valued strict routine and reportedly counseled Lagrange to regulate his life. Lagrange scheduled work carefully, planned papers mentally, and was famous for clean manuscripts with few corrections. Biographers describe recurring digestive complaints, nervous exhaustion, and periods of illness. Retrospective diagnoses are unsafe; the contemporary vocabulary is melancholy, low spirits, fatigue, and poor health.

After Vittoria’s death he experienced an especially profound collapse. Later accounts say that in Paris he left a copy of his newly printed *Mécanique analytique* unopened for two years. This may dramatize a real depressive period; exact duration and behavior derive from memorial biography rather than a clinical record.

### Scientific production

The Berlin years were his most productive. His principal lines included:

1. **Calculus of variations and mechanics.** Lagrange joined the principle of virtual velocities/work with d’Alembert’s dynamical principle and variational analysis. The mature result was a general, coordinate-based mechanics.
2. **Celestial mechanics.** He studied the Moon, Jupiter’s satellites, planetary perturbations, cometary orbits, and the three-body problem. His 1772 memoir identified the equilateral-triangle solutions now associated with the L4 and L5 equilibrium points. Euler had earlier found the collinear family; the modern five “Lagrange points” therefore compress a joint history. NASA’s concise historical explanation links the points to Lagrange’s 1772 memoir ([NASA, “What Is a Lagrange Point?”](https://science.nasa.gov/resource/what-is-a-lagrange-point/)).
3. **Number theory.** He proved the four-square theorem (every positive integer is a sum of four squares), supplied work on Pell-type equations and continued fractions, proved Wilson’s theorem, and developed reduction and classification of binary quadratic forms.
4. **Algebra and equations.** His 1770–1771 memoirs on algebraic equations analyzed permutations of roots and resolvents. They did not create group theory, but later algebra—from Ruffini and Abel to Galois—built on the structural questions he exposed.
5. **Probability, interpolation, and numerical analysis.** He worked on interpolation formulas, combining observations, and numerical equations.
6. **Fluid mechanics and differential equations.** His papers addressed fluid pressure, sound, and integration methods.

### *Mécanique analytique*

Lagrange composed his synthesis in Berlin. The first edition, **Paris, 1788**, appeared through Desaint after his move. Its program was radical in style: mechanics would become analysis, with no diagrams needed. Using generalized coordinates and virtual work/d’Alembert’s principle, it provided a unified method for statics and dynamics of particles, rigid bodies, and fluids. The modern compact equations

\[
\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q_i}-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}=0
\]

are later standardized notation, not a verbatim formula from the 1788 typography. “Lagrangian” as the function \(L=T-V\), the Lagrange equations, multipliers, and generalized coordinates are parts of a later vocabulary built from his methods.

### Departure from Berlin

Frederick II died on **17 August 1786**. The new court climate, Lagrange’s widowerhood and health, institutional disputes, and attractive foreign offers altered his position. Spain, Naples, and Italian states sought him. Louis XVI’s government offered a pension, Academy membership, and lodging in the Louvre complex. Lagrange obtained honorable release and left Berlin in 1787. René Taton’s documentary study, [“Le départ de Lagrange de Berlin et son installation à Paris en 1787”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/rhs_0151-4105_1988_num_41_1_4088), shows that the move was negotiated through diplomatic and academic networks, not a flight or exile.

## Chronological Life History: Paris and Revolution, 1787–1799

### Royal reception and the Louvre

Lagrange arrived in Paris in **May 1787**. He became a pensioned *vétéran associé* of the Académie royale des sciences and received an apartment connected with the Louvre. His *Mécanique analytique* appeared the next year.

The early Paris years were scientifically quiet and psychologically difficult. He attended salons and scientific gatherings but produced less original work. The Revolution of 1789 initially aroused intense curiosity, then alarm.

### Second marriage

Lagrange married **Renée-Françoise-Adélaïde Le Monnier** (1767–1833), daughter of astronomer **Pierre-Charles Le Monnier**, on **31 May 1792**; the marriage contract is dated 3 June in some accounts, probably reflecting separate civil/notarial acts. She was about twenty-five and he fifty-six. The royal family signed or approved the contract shortly before the monarchy’s fall.

Later gossip turned this into a caricature of an old man marrying a child. Taton’s archival investigation established her birth around 1767 and thereby corrected the insinuation. The age difference was large—about thirty-one years—but she was an adult. Contemporary accounts portray the marriage as affectionate. There were no children. After Lagrange’s death, Adélaïde remarried Jacques-Louis-César Hüe; she died in 1833.

### Foreign status and the Terror

Lagrange was a Sardinian/Prussian-associated foreign savant in revolutionary Paris. General laws and decrees against foreigners made his position dangerous. He was repeatedly granted specific exemption because of his scientific value. Statements that “Lavoisier saved him” or that a single official personally prevented expulsion oversimplify a sequence of institutional interventions.

Lagrange remained in Paris during the Terror. He was not imprisoned, indicted, tried, or charged with a crime. He held no operational role in political violence. He witnessed the execution of colleagues, including **Antoine Lavoisier** on 8 May 1794. The famous observation attributed to Lagrange—that it took only a moment to sever Lavoisier’s head but perhaps a century to produce another like it—is plausible and widely transmitted, but its exact first documentary appearance and wording require caution.

### Metric system

The Constituent Assembly and successor governments recruited leading scientists to create uniform weights and measures. Lagrange served on the commission with Borda, Condorcet, Lavoisier, Laplace, Monge, and others. He favored decimal division in the final system, although his École normale lectures included an intellectually playful discussion of base eleven. He worked on the mathematical foundations and calculations; he was not the sole “inventor of the metric system.”

Political purges removed several commission members, including Lavoisier and, for a time, Lagrange. The project continued through reorganized bodies. The 1795 law established the metric system, and in 1799 international delegates deposited definitive platinum standards. A translated documentary collection reproduces the revolutionary legislation and agency duties ([George Mason University, “Weights and Measures”](https://revolution.chnm.org/d/463)).

### New educational institutions

The suppression of old academies in 1793 destroyed Lagrange’s royal institutional base but revolutionary schools transformed him from court academician into public professor.

- **École centrale des travaux publics / École Polytechnique:** appointed professor of analysis at its opening in 1794. His courses fed into *Théorie des fonctions analytiques*, *Résolution des équations numériques*, and *Leçons sur le calcul des fonctions*.
- **École normale de l’an III:** professor in 1795 during its brief initial session. Lectures were stenographically recorded and published.
- **Institut national:** elected to the mathematical class when the Institut replaced the abolished academies in 1795.
- **Bureau des longitudes:** founding member in 1795, concerned with astronomy, geodesy, navigation, and standards.

Bruno Belhoste’s study, [“When an academician becomes professor”](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40329-014-0048-y), emphasizes that the Revolution relaunched Lagrange’s work by forcing a court mathematician into teaching and public scientific administration.

### Teaching style and Fourier

Lagrange’s lectures were admired by expert colleagues for structure and generality but were difficult for many students. **Joseph Fourier**, an École normale student in 1795, described his weak voice, strong Italian accent, and cool delivery; students gave him little response while professors appreciated him. This is contemporary eyewitness criticism, not later hostility. Fourier absorbed Lagrange’s analytic culture and briefly succeeded him in an analysis chair. Their connection was principally teacher–student and institutional; no evidence shows a close private friendship or feud.

### Analytical program

Lagrange sought to found calculus algebraically on power-series expansions, avoiding infinitesimals, fluxions, limits, and geometrical intuition. *Théorie des fonctions analytiques* (1797) embodies this program. It was historically powerful but not fully rigorous: smooth functions need not equal their Taylor series, and the generality-of-algebra principle could conceal convergence assumptions. G. Ferraro’s study, [“Lagrange’s Theory of Analytical Functions and his Ideal of Purity of Method”](https://hal.science/hal-00614606v1/document), reconstructs the program without projecting modern definitions backward.

## Chronological Life History: Consulate and Empire, 1799–1813

### Senator and dignitary

After the coup of 18 Brumaire, Bonaparte named Lagrange to the **Sénat conservateur** in December 1799, alongside a small number of eminent savants. The office carried salary, status, and a role within the constitutional machinery. Lagrange’s political activity was largely institutional and ceremonial; no independent legislative program, party organization, intelligence role, or participation in repression has been found.

As senator he belonged to a body that ratified the consolidation of Bonaparte’s personal rule and the Empire. This is documented political association, even if Lagrange was not a principal architect. Cournot later recalled him teasing Laplace about wearing senatorial costume—a glimpse of irony toward official display, not evidence of opposition.

### Honors under Napoleon

- member of the Légion d’honneur (1803/04 in staged creation lists; sources vary on exact grade chronology);
- **Grand Officer of the Légion d’honneur**, 1808;
- **Count of the Empire**, letters patent 1808;
- **Grand Cross of the Imperial Order of the Reunion**, awarded immediately before death in April 1813.

Napoleon admired mathematical science and used savants as administrators, teachers, senators, and symbols of enlightened imperial rule. Lagrange accepted the honors and income. There is no evidence that he advised on military campaigns, secret intelligence, or coercive policy.

### Property and finances

Lagrange’s early family wealth had largely disappeared. His adult income came from salaried academic chairs, royal/imperial pensions, prizes, and the Senate. In Berlin he received an Academy salary from Frederick II; in Paris Louis XVI guaranteed a pension and lodging; revolutionary appointments and later senatorial income restored security.

He commissioned or occupied an Empire-style residence commonly associated with **128 or 134 rue du Faubourg-Saint-Honoré**. Street renumbering and later property history explain some address variation; popular sites should not be used to infer the original purchase price or net worth. No complete probate inventory, salary ledger, investment portfolio, business partnership, patent income, debts, or estate valuation was located in the sources consulted. There is no documented commercial company or speculative business operated by Lagrange.

### Relations with Laplace, Monge, Poisson, and Gauss

**Pierre-Simon Laplace** was colleague, fellow metric commissioner, Bureau member, senator, and scientific peer. Their celestial-mechanics programs overlapped and sometimes differed in method and priority, but “rival” is too simple. Laplace built on and reformulated Lagrange’s perturbation theory; Lagrange responded to later stability results.

**Gaspard Monge** was a revolutionary organizer and colleague at the Polytechnique and Senate. Monge was politically more active and more closely involved with Bonaparte’s expeditions.

**Siméon-Denis Poisson** learned within the Polytechnique environment and extended Lagrange’s celestial mechanics. In 1806 Poisson’s result on orbital stability prompted Lagrange, then seventy, to return to the subject; Lagrange published a renewed variation-of-constants treatment in 1808.

**Carl Friedrich Gauss** was not Lagrange’s student and no sustained personal correspondence between them is established here. The connection is intellectual. Gauss’s *Disquisitiones arithmeticae* (1801) systematized number theory developed by Fermat, Euler, Lagrange, and Legendre, especially quadratic forms, while often correcting, completing, or superseding predecessors. Lagrange’s reduction theory and arithmetic results formed part of the platform from which Gauss advanced.

### Final revision and illness

Lagrange spent his last years revising major treatises. The second edition of *Mécanique analytique* began appearing in 1811; he completed the first volume and much of the second, which was finished/published posthumously in 1815–1816 through editorial work. A revised *Théorie des fonctions analytiques* appeared in 1813.

Accounts describe increasing weakness and an illness in early 1813. Delambre’s notice records his calm discussion of approaching death, but accessible sources do not provide a secure modern diagnosis. Claims of a particular cancer or organ disease should not be asserted without a medical record. He died at home in Paris on **10 April 1813**. Napoleon conferred the Grand Cross of the Order of the Reunion only days before his death. Funeral honors culminated in burial at the Panthéon.

### Technologies and “inventions”

Lagrange did not patent machines. His technologies were mathematical methods: the calculus of variations, multiplier method, generalized coordinates, analytical mechanics, interpolation, perturbation methods, and algorithms for numerical equations. “Lagrange points” became practical locations for twentieth- and twenty-first-century spacecraft, but that application is posthumous and should not be described as an eighteenth-century aerospace invention.

## Political, Religious, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

### Political position

No coherent corpus of political writing by Lagrange is known. His career passed through four regimes—Savoyard monarchy, Prussian monarchy, French constitutional/absolute monarchy, Revolution/Republic, and Napoleonic Consulate/Empire—without documented partisan activism. He accepted patronage from rulers and served revolutionary and imperial institutions.

This pattern can be described factually as adaptation by a transnational savant. It does not by itself establish opportunism, royalism, republicanism, Bonapartism, or political indifference. During the Terror he expressed fear privately and mourned Lavoisier; under Napoleon he accepted a Senate seat, title, orders, and salary.

### Religion

He was baptized and raised Roman Catholic in Turin. Later claims that he was an agnostic or atheist are common but poorly sourced. He produced no known theological or anti-religious treatise. Delambre’s death narrative emphasizes philosophical composure rather than sacramental practice. The safest conclusion is that his mature private belief is not securely documented.

### Intellectual style

Lagrange pursued **analytic unification**: reduce physical problems to general symbolic formulas, minimize special constructions, and separate method from diagram. The famous preface to *Mécanique analytique* announces that the reader will find no figures. In calculus foundations he sought finite algebraic analysis rather than metaphysically suspect infinitesimals. His program combined Enlightenment confidence in rational order with an unusually austere mathematical taste.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

### No documented criminal or corruption case

No lawsuit, criminal investigation, indictment, conviction, fraud proceeding, corruption inquiry, money-laundering allegation, intelligence connection, or abuse accusation involving Lagrange was found. Absence from the consulted record is not proof that no private dispute ever existed, but sensational criminal categories are not relevant to the documented life.

### Priority disputes and misattribution

1. **Euler–Lagrange equation.** Euler published the core differential equation first; Lagrange created a powerful general variation method. Claims that either man simply “stole” the result are unsupported. Their correspondence shows recognition and mutual influence.
2. **Lagrange points.** Lagrange found the triangular equilibrium solutions; Euler found collinear solutions. Calling all five points solely Lagrange’s is conventional nomenclature, not a precise priority statement.
3. **Least action, virtual work, and d’Alembert’s principle.** Lagrange synthesized methods with roots in Maupertuis, Euler, d’Alembert, and earlier mechanics. *Mécanique analytique* is a transformation and unification, not creation ex nihilo.
4. **Algebraic solution of equations.** Lagrange’s permutation/resolvent analysis was foundational but did not solve the general quintic or formulate modern group theory. Later accounts sometimes retroactively make him “inventor of group theory.”

### Scientific limitations and failures

- His algebraic foundation for calculus assumed power-series representability too freely; modern analysis rejects it as a universal foundation.
- He did not solve the general three-body problem; his special solutions and perturbative methods were major partial advances.
- His teaching delivery did not engage many novice students, despite expert admiration.
- Periods of depression and ill health reduced output after 1783 and in early Paris years.
- Some celestial stability conclusions were limited-order perturbative results, not global proofs of Solar System stability.

### Political complicity question

Lagrange was a salaried senator and count under Napoleon. The Senate legitimized authoritarian constitutional changes and imperial rule. That institutional fact should neither be hidden nor inflated into personal responsibility for campaigns or repression for which no evidence connects him. He appears to have been a scientific dignitary rather than an executive policymaker.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. Napoleon’s “lofty pyramid”

**Story:** Napoleon supposedly said, “Lagrange is the lofty pyramid of the mathematical sciences” (*Lagrange est la haute pyramide des sciences mathématiques*).

**Appearance/circulation:** The saying is prominent in E. T. Bell’s *Men of Mathematics* (1937), which uses “The Lofty Pyramid” as its Lagrange chapter title. Modern quotation sites repeat Bell ([example attribution chain](https://libquotes.com/napoleon-i-of-france/quote/lbp3k1y)).

**Evidence:** Napoleon demonstrably honored Lagrange and valued savants. However, no contemporaneous speech, letter, *Moniteur* report, memoir with a precise occasion, or archival document containing the sentence was located. Bell is more than a century late and is known for dramatized mathematical biography.

**Assessment:** **Plausible sentiment, unverified wording; late attribution.** It should not be quoted as securely authenticated.

**Reputational effect:** It crystallized the image of Lagrange as the monumental summit of eighteenth-century mathematics and tied his memory to Napoleonic meritocratic patronage.

### 2. “Greatest king” summons “greatest mathematician”

**Story:** Frederick II invited Lagrange so that Europe’s greatest king would possess Europe’s greatest mathematician.

**Evidence:** Frederick did recruit him after Euler’s departure, with d’Alembert mediating; correspondence confirms the courtship. The polished superlative wording varies across biographies.

**Assessment:** **Core event documented; epigrammatic wording may be polished.**

### 3. His father’s ruin made him a mathematician

**Story:** Lagrange said that if his family had remained rich, he would never have known mathematics.

**Evidence:** The father’s financial losses are well attested. The counterfactual is transmitted by Delambre/Chaptal tradition and fits Lagrange’s late recollection.

**Assessment:** **Probable remembered remark, impossible counterfactual.**

**Reputational effect:** Became a moral tale of adversity generating genius.

### 4. Professor at sixteen

**Story:** He became professor of mathematics at Turin’s artillery school at age sixteen.

**Evidence:** Delambre and older notices give exceptionally early ages; modern chronologies usually date the appointment to 1755, age nineteen. Sarton noted editorial debate over the claim.

**Assessment:** **Appointment documented; age sixteen disputed and probably too early.**

### 5. The unopened *Mécanique analytique*

**Story:** A copy of the book he had labored over for decades lay unopened on his desk for two years.

**Evidence:** Later memorial biographies use it to illustrate severe melancholy after Berlin and Vittoria’s death. No diary entry or dated inventory proves exactly two years.

**Assessment:** **Anecdotal expression of a documented depressive period.**

### 6. Old man and “very young girl”

**Story:** Elderly Lagrange married an extremely young girl in 1792.

**Origin/circulation:** Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century gossip exaggerated the age gap. Sarton flagged the “médisances”; Taton checked civil/genealogical records.

**Evidence:** Adélaïde was born in 1767 and was about twenty-five; Lagrange was fifty-six.

**Assessment:** **Large age gap documented; child-bride insinuation false.**

**Reputational effect:** Fed portrayals of Lagrange as emotionally dependent or rejuvenated by a youthful caretaker; archival work corrected the caricature.

### 7. Lavoisier’s severed head and the century

**Story:** After Lavoisier’s execution, Lagrange said, “It took them only an instant to cut off that head, and a hundred years may not produce another like it.”

**Evidence:** The execution and Lagrange’s association with Lavoisier are documented. Exact wording is widely repeated but primary provenance is uncertain in the accessible record.

**Assessment:** **Plausible contemporary sentiment; quotation wording not fully authenticated.**

### 8. “No diagrams” as anti-geometry dogma

**Story:** Lagrange banished geometry from mechanics because he despised figures.

**Evidence:** The *Mécanique analytique* preface explicitly celebrates a method requiring no figures. Yet he used geometrical applications and did not deny geometry’s validity.

**Assessment:** **Documented program turned into exaggerated personality myth.**

### 9. Agnostic/atheist Lagrange

**Story:** He was an avowed agnostic or atheist.

**Evidence:** Baptism and Catholic upbringing are documented; mature belief is not. No manifesto or clear declaration was located.

**Assessment:** **Unresolved; categorical labels exceed evidence.**

## Works by Joseph-Louis Lagrange

### Editorial caution

Lagrange’s output consists primarily of memoirs in academy journals, prize essays, textbooks derived from lectures, and correspondence. The definitive nineteenth-century *Œuvres de Lagrange*, edited by **Joseph-Alfred Serret** and later **Gaston Darboux**, contains fourteen volumes (Paris: Gauthier-Villars, 1867–1892). It is a **posthumous collected edition**, not a work authored as fourteen volumes by Lagrange. Volumes XIII–XIV contain correspondence and editorial material. Gallica provides scans, including the [Delambre notice and collected volumes](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/bpt6k229949x/f80).

### Principal separately published works and courses

1. **“Lettera … al signor Giulio Carlo da Fagnano”** (1754). First mathematical publication, in Italian, signed Luigi De la Grange Tournier; an early infinite-series/differential-calculus paper. Some of its purported novelty was soon found to overlap earlier work.
2. **Early Turin memoirs in the *Miscellanea Taurinensia*** (1759–1766), including work on maxima/minima, calculus of variations, sound and vibrating strings, differential equations, and mechanics.
3. **“Recherches sur la méthode de maximis et minimis”** (1760s; memoir publication chronology varies). Developed variational methods.
4. **“Recherches sur la libration de la Lune”** (prize memoir, 1764). Paris Academy prize.
5. **“Recherches sur les inégalités des satellites de Jupiter…”** (1766). Prize-winning celestial mechanics.
6. **“Solution d’un problème d’arithmétique”** / four-square work (1770). Proof that every positive integer is a sum of four squares.
7. **“Réflexions sur la résolution algébrique des équations”** (1770–1771). Permutations of roots, resolvents, and why lower-degree solution methods work.
8. **“Suite des réflexions sur la résolution algébrique des équations”** (1771).
9. **“Essai sur le problème des trois corps”** (1772). Prize memoir; includes equilateral special solutions associated with L4/L5.
10. **“Sur l’équation séculaire de la Lune”** and related lunar memoirs (1773–1774).
11. **“Recherches d’arithmétique”** (1773–1775 and related installments). Binary quadratic forms and number theory.
12. **Memoirs on cometary perturbations** (prize 1778; publication around 1780).
13. **“Mémoire sur la théorie des variations des éléments des planètes…”** (1781 and related later papers). Variation of orbital elements/constants.
14. **“Mémoire sur la théorie du mouvement des fluides”** and related fluid papers (1780s).
15. **_*Mécanique analytique*_** (Paris: Veuve Desaint, 1788). Genuine monograph. Second, substantially revised edition: vol. I, 1811; vol. II completed/published posthumously, 1815–1816. Later editions incorporate notes by Bertrand and Darboux.
16. **_*Théorie des fonctions analytiques, contenant les principes du calcul différentiel… réduits à l’analyse algébrique des quantités finies*_** (Paris: Imprimerie de la République, 1797; enlarged/revised edition 1813). Genuine treatise derived from teaching.
17. **_*Traité de la résolution des équations numériques de tous les degrés*_** (1798; enlarged edition 1808). Genuine treatise; numerical roots, continued fractions, algebraic-equation notes.
18. **_*Leçons sur le calcul des fonctions*_** (1801; revised 1806). Genuine published lectures/treatise.
19. **École normale lectures**, in *Séances des Écoles normales, recueillies par des sténographes, et revues par les professeurs* (1795). Stenographically recorded and reviewed; genuine lectures, not a conventional manuscript book.
20. **“Discours sur l’objet de la théorie des fonctions analytiques”** (1799), *Journal de l’École Polytechnique*. Programmatic statement.

### Collected works, volume guide

The fourteen-volume edition is the most practical complete bibliography:

- **Vols. I–VII (1867–1877):** memoirs arranged broadly by original venue/chronology, including Turin, Berlin, and Paris academy papers in analysis, mechanics, astronomy, algebra, number theory, and probability.
- **Vol. VIII (1879):** *Traité de la résolution des équations numériques de tous les degrés*, based on the 1808 edition, with notes on algebraic equations.
- **Vol. IX (1881):** *Théorie des fonctions analytiques*, based on the 1813 edition.
- **Vol. X (1884):** *Leçons sur le calcul des fonctions*, based on the 1806 edition.
- **Vols. XI–XII (1888–1889):** *Mécanique analytique*, based on the revised edition, with later editorial notes.
- **Vol. XIII (1882):** correspondence between Lagrange and d’Alembert, edited/annotated by Ludovic Lalanne.
- **Vol. XIV (1892):** additional correspondence and materials, including letters involving Condorcet and other savants; editorial rather than a single authored work.

### Correspondence editions

- *Correspondance de Lagrange et d’Alembert*, in *Œuvres*, vol. XIII: genuine letters, posthumously edited.
- *Lettres inédites de Joseph-Louis Lagrange à Léonard Euler*, ed. Baldassarre Boncompagni (1877): genuine correspondence, posthumously edited.
- Letters to/from Condorcet, Laplace, Frederick II, Talleyrand, Montucla, Turin colleagues, and family are dispersed through *Œuvres*, academy archives, and scholarly articles. Attribution depends on manuscript/editorial provenance; none is “ghostwritten.”

### What he did not author

- Modern books titled *Lagrangian Mechanics* are not his works merely because they use his name.
- The modern five-point L1–L5 notation is not found in his 1772 form.
- *Œuvres de Lagrange* is editorially assembled; Serret and Darboux supplied organization and notes.
- English *Lectures on Elementary Mathematics* (1898, trans. Thomas J. McCormack) is a translation/adaptation of the École normale lectures, not an original English book by Lagrange.

## Books and Major Works About Lagrange

### Contemporary and near-contemporary

1. **Jean-Baptiste-Joseph Delambre**, “Notice sur la vie et les ouvrages de M. le comte J.-L. Lagrange” (read 1813; printed in *Mémoires de l’Institut*, 1816; reprinted in *Œuvres*, vol. I). Foundational official memorial, based on acquaintance and testimony; laudatory genre.

16. **G. Ferraro**, “Lagrange’s Theory of Analytical Functions and his Ideal of Purity of Method” (2012). Power series, foundations, and analytic style.
17. Standard histories of mechanics by **René Dugas**, **Clifford Truesdell**, and later historians treat *Mécanique analytique* and its predecessors; their interpretive frameworks differ.
18. Histories of number theory and algebra by **André Weil**, **David Cox**, and others situate Lagrange between Euler/Legendre and Gauss.
19. Editions and scholarship of d’Alembert’s correspondence place Lagrange within the Republic of Letters; the [Académie des sciences d’Alembert edition portal](https://dalembert.academie-sciences.fr/liens.php) links the correspondence resources.

## Death, Funeral, and Burial

Lagrange died in Paris on **10 April 1813** after a final illness whose precise diagnosis remains undocumented in the consulted evidence. He was seventy-seven. His wife Adélaïde survived him. Delambre presented the official scientific memorial. The Empire staged honors appropriate to a senator, count, grand officer, and grand cross.

The Panthéon inscription identifies him as senator, count of the Empire, grand officer of the Legion of Honour, grand cross of the Order of the Reunion, member of the Institut and Bureau des longitudes, born Turin 25 January 1736, died Paris 10 April 1813. The burial joined scientific achievement to the state’s monumental politics.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Mathematics and physics

Lagrange’s name became embedded in the working language of science:

- Lagrangian mechanics and the Lagrangian function;
- Euler–Lagrange equations;
- Lagrange multipliers;
- Lagrange interpolation polynomial;
- Lagrange’s theorem in group theory (a later group-theoretic framing of a result in permutation theory);
- Lagrange’s four-square theorem;
- Lagrange remainder and mean-value forms in calculus;
- Lagrange identity and resolvents;
- Lagrange planetary equations;
- Lagrange points L1–L5;
- Lagrange inversion theorem and other named formulas.

Not every eponym corresponds to wording or notation used by Lagrange. Eponyms consolidate later traditions and can obscure Euler, d’Alembert, Legendre, Laplace, and others.

### Mechanics from Hamilton to modern field theory

His generalized-coordinate and variational formulation made mechanics portable. **William Rowan Hamilton** reformulated dynamics through Hamilton’s principle and canonical equations; Jacobi, Poisson, and later mathematicians extended the structure. Modern classical field theory, relativity, quantum field theory, control theory, and engineering use Lagrangian formalisms far beyond Lagrange’s original particle and continuum mechanics.

### Astronomy and spaceflight

The triangular solutions of 1772 became locations for Trojan asteroids and stable/quasi-stable configurations. Space observatories and missions exploit neighborhoods of Sun–Earth or Earth–Moon Lagrange points. This practical prestige reshaped public memory: Lagrange is now often introduced through spacecraft “parking places,” although he studied mathematical celestial mechanics, not mission design.

### Turin, France, and competing national canons

Italian histories remember **Giuseppe Luigi Lagrange**, the Turin-born founder of the local academy; French histories remember **Joseph-Louis Lagrange**, professor, senator, count, and Panthéon figure. Both claims reflect real phases. Treating nationality as an exclusive ownership contest imports nineteenth-century nation-states into a Savoyard, Prussian, and French transnational career.

### Memorials

- Name among the **72 scientists and engineers inscribed on the Eiffel Tower**.
- **Rue Lagrange** in Paris and Via Lagrange in Turin.
- Lunar crater **Lagrange**.
- Asteroid **1006 Lagrangea**.
- Educational institutions, prizes, laboratories, and mathematical societies use his name.
- Tomb/memorial in the Panthéon.

### Reinterpretation

Nineteenth-century memorials constructed the modest, austere genius who converted mechanics into pure analysis. Bell’s twentieth-century popular biography monumentalized him as Napoleon’s “lofty pyramid.” Modern scholarship has shifted attention to correspondence, patronage, academy politics, teaching, metrology, family documentation, and the revolutionary transformation of scientific careers. It also corrects priority myths and recognizes limitations in his power-series foundation of calculus without diminishing its historical importance.
