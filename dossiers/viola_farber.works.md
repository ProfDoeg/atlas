### Dance films and works made for television

- **1977:** *Brazos River*. A dance-on-camera Gesamtkunstwerk: Farber supplied the choreography, David Tudor the music, Robert Rauschenberg the costumes and visual environment, and Dan Parr directed the film at the Fort Worth Art Museum. It is therefore not solely Farber’s object, although the dance component is hers. [1][7][9]
- **1985:** *January*. A dance made for television and the Viola Farber Dance Company’s final performance, directed by Kevin Crooks for Television South West, with music by Gordon Jones and costumes by Maria Liljefors. Its status is simultaneously dance, screen work, and terminal company event. [1][7]

### Site-specific manifestations and incompletely identified projects

- **1974:** *Site-specific dance at the New York Botanical Garden*. A dance manifestation made for the garden in the Bronx; the consulted repertory does not preserve a fixed title, so the site description should not be mistaken for one. [1]
- **1974:** *Site-specific dance in the Staten Island Ferry waiting room*. A dance manifestation made for a transit space; no fixed title is documented in the consulted sources. [1]
- **1966:** *Surf Zone*. A choreographic work named and dated by the *International Encyclopedia of Dance* synopsis but absent from the later repertory chronology; its company, cast, and relationship to Farber’s other early dances remain unresolved. [2]
- **1966–1967:** *Here They Come Again*. A dance documented by performer Jeff Slayton’s recollection of appearing in it at Adelphi University; it is absent from the repertory chronology and may have been a student or institutional work rather than a stable repertory piece. [12]
- **1969:** *Three Duets*. A choreographic work named by the *International Encyclopedia of Dance* synopsis but absent under that collective title from the repertory chronology; it may refer to a program or grouping of separately titled duets rather than one independent obra. [2]
- **Date unresolved:** *Trio*. A title attributed to Farber in a historical survey of modern dance, but absent from the principal repertory chronology; without firmer documentation it cannot safely be assigned a date or treated as a distinct completed work. [13]
