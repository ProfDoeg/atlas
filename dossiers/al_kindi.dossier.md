# Abu Yusuf Ya'qub ibn Ishaq al-Kindi: Research Dossier

## Scope and Evidentiary Note

Abū Yūsuf Yaʿqūb ibn Isḥāq al-Kindī lived in ninth-century Abbasid Iraq. The surviving evidence is exceptionally uneven:

- His own treatises establish his intellectual positions, patrons, dedications, and participation in the Graeco-Arabic translation movement.
- The earliest extensive bibliography is Ibn al-Nadīm’s *Kitāb al-Fihrist*, compiled approximately a century after al-Kindī’s death.
- Most narrative biography comes from considerably later writers, especially Ibn Juljul, al-Qifṭī, Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa, Ṣāʿid al-Andalusī, al-Shahrastānī, and al-Bayhaqī.
- Almost no contemporary documentation survives concerning his childhood, private household, income, marriage, children, or exact offices.
- The familiar dates “801–873” are conventional reconstructions, not securely documented birth and death dates.
- Modern sources frequently repeat institutional claims—especially that al-Kindī formally worked at the “House of Wisdom”—more confidently than the surviving medieval evidence warrants.

Evidentiary labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by al-Kindī’s surviving writings, manuscript evidence, or securely identifiable contemporary translation activity.
- **Reported fact:** stated in medieval biographical or bibliographical sources but not independently contemporary.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** an inference made by modern specialists from texts, dedications, vocabulary, manuscript relationships, or historical context.
- **Disputed claim:** supported by competing interpretations or uncertain attribution.
- **Legend or anecdote:** a narratively elaborate later report that cannot presently be corroborated.
- **Misattribution:** a work, story, quotation, or identity wrongly joined to this al-Kindī.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name:** Abū Yūsuf Yaʿqūb ibn Isḥāq ibn al-Ṣabbāḥ ibn ʿImrān ibn Ismāʿīl ibn Muḥammad ibn al-Ashʿath al-Kindī.

A much longer genealogy, preserved by Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa, extends through the South Arabian tribe of Kinda to Qaḥṭān. Its near ancestry is historically plausible; its remote reaches belong to the conventions of medieval Arab genealogical preservation.

**Arabic:** أبو يوسف يعقوب بن إسحاق بن الصباح الكندي

**Common shortened form:** Yaʿqūb ibn Isḥāq al-Kindī.

**Latinized names:** Alkindus; Alkindi; Alchindus; Jacobus Alkindus; Jacob Al-Kindi.

**Kunya:** Abū Yūsuf, “father of Yūsuf.” This normally implies a son named Yūsuf, but no independently documented son or descendant is known. It could also have functioned conventionally.

**Nisba:** *al-Kindī*, indicating affiliation with the Arab tribe of Kinda.

**Principal title:** *Faylasūf al-ʿArab*, “the Philosopher of the Arabs.” Later authors used the title partly because al-Kindī was an Arab tribal aristocrat, unlike several later major philosophers writing in Arabic.

**Professions and activities:** philosopher, mathematical scientist, astrologer, astronomer, physician and pharmacological writer, music theorist, specialist in optics, meteorology and mineralogy, court tutor, author, editor or reviser of translations, and organizer or patron-intermediary within a translation circle.

**Religion:** Muslim. His surviving writings repeatedly identify God as creator, use Qurʾanic language, discuss prophecy, and attempt to coordinate philosophy with Islamic revelation. The more specific classification of his theology—Muʿtazilite, Muʿtazilite-influenced, early Islamic philosopher, or an independent philosophical theologian—is disputed.

**Birth:** probably around 800 CE; frequently given as approximately 801. No contemporary source supplies an exact year or day.

**Birthplace:** disputed between Kufa and Basra.

- **Reported fact:** his father was Abbasid governor of Kufa, making Kufa a plausible birthplace.
- **Reported fact:** Ibn Juljul, quoted by Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa, calls him a Basran and says his estate was near Basra.
- **Modern reconstruction:** many references select Kufa; Peter Adamson and some other specialists state Basra. The evidence does not settle the question.

**Death:** certainly after 866 according to the modern reconstruction adopted by the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*; conventionally dated to approximately 870 or 873. Dates encountered in the literature include 252 AH/866–867, 256 AH/869–870, and 259–260 AH/872–874.

**Probable place of death:** Baghdad, although the accessible medieval report describing his final illness does not securely establish the location.

## Family and Ancestry

### Kinda and the al-Ashʿath lineage

Al-Kindī belonged to a politically prominent branch of Kinda, a South Arabian tribal confederation with a long pre-Islamic and early Islamic history.

Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa gives the descent:

> Yaʿqūb ibn Isḥāq ibn al-Ṣabbāḥ ibn ʿImrān ibn Ismāʿīl ibn Muḥammad ibn al-Ashʿath ibn Qays ibn Maʿdī Karib …

The genealogy continues through chiefs associated with Hadramawt, Yamāma, Bahrain, and the wider Kindite lineage.

### Father

**Isḥāq ibn al-Ṣabbāḥ** is reported to have governed Kufa for the Abbasid caliphs al-Mahdī and Hārūn al-Rashīd. A member of the family, identified in historical reconstructions as Isḥāq ibn al-Ṣabbāḥ ibn ʿImrān, held office around 778–781 and briefly again under Hārūn al-Rashīd.

This made al-Kindī the son of a provincial ruling household rather than an impoverished independent scholar. His ancestry and access to estates help explain—but do not fully document—his entry into elite Abbasid patronage.

### Ancestors

The celebrated ancestor **al-Ashʿath ibn Qays al-Kindī** was a Kindite chief and companion of the Prophet Muḥammad. Medieval Muslim traditions concerning al-Ashʿath are politically divided: some emphasize his chiefly status and companionship; hostile traditions accuse him of opportunism, apostasy during the Ridda wars, or damaging intervention at Ṣiffīn. Those controversies concern al-Kindī’s remote ancestor, not the philosopher’s conduct.

### Mother, siblings, marriage, and children

No reliable source consulted names al-Kindī’s mother, siblings, wife, partner, or children.

The kunya Abū Yūsuf may indicate a son named Yūsuf, but this remains **unverified**. No securely traced philosophical or political dynasty descended from him.

### Property and inherited position

Ibn Juljul’s account says that al-Kindī possessed or resided upon an estate near Basra. This is a **reported fact**, not a surviving property record. It is nevertheless consistent with his aristocratic lineage, extensive private library, and ability to sponsor or mediate scholarly work.

## Childhood and Early Life

Nothing approaching a childhood narrative survives.

Al-Kindī was probably born around the turn of the ninth century, during the reign of Hārūn al-Rashīd. His father’s governorship placed the family in the politically charged environment of Kufa, while the reported Basran estate connects him to Basra’s commercial and intellectual world.

There is no documentary evidence for:

- the age at which his father died;
- the identity of his guardian;
- childhood illnesses or accidents;
- Quranic schooling;
- youthful travel;
- early employment;
- the size or disposition of his inheritance.

Claims that he mastered Greek and Syriac in childhood are common in popular biographies but are not securely documented. His writings demonstrate command of Arabic scholarly prose and deep use of Arabic translations from Greek; they do not prove that he personally read Greek fluently. Specialists generally portray him as supervising, revising, or conceptually guiding translations made by multilingual Christian scholars.

## Education and Formation

### Basra and Baghdad

Ibn Juljul’s report says that al-Kindī settled in Basra, where his estate was located, and then moved to Baghdad, “where he was educated.”

The sequence **Basra → Baghdad** is therefore a plausible reconstruction. Kufa may have preceded Basra if it was his birthplace or childhood residence.

### Teachers

No dependable conventional teacher list survives. In particular, the evidence does not justify naming Aristotle, Plato, or other Greeks as “teachers” except metaphorically.

His formation can instead be reconstructed through texts:

- Aristotle’s logical, psychological, physical, and metaphysical writings;
- late-antique Aristotelian commentators, especially Alexander of Aphrodisias and John Philoponus;
- Neoplatonic material derived from Plotinus and Proclus;
- Plato, primarily through summaries, doxographies, and the *Timaeus* tradition;
- Euclid in geometry and optics;
- Ptolemy in astronomy, astrology, geography, and optics;
- Nicomachus of Gerasa in arithmetic and musical theory;
- Galenic medical and philosophical traditions;
- earlier Arabic, Persian, and Indian mathematical and astronomical learning;
- Islamic *kalām*, particularly debates about divine unity, creation, atoms, infinity, and attributes.

### Mathematical curriculum

Al-Kindī argued that philosophy required mathematical preparation. Arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, and music—the classical quadrivium—were not peripheral interests but part of his conception of demonstrative knowledge.

### Islamic theology and possible Muʿtazilism

Al-Kindī’s insistence that the world is created and temporally finite, his language of divine unity, and his interest in proving creation from non-being overlap with Muʿtazilite theology.

The degree of affiliation is disputed:

- Some scholars describe him as strongly Muʿtazilite-influenced.
- Others note that no source securely makes him a member of a named Muʿtazilite school.
- His method and technical vocabulary belong increasingly to *falsafa*, the Arabic philosophical tradition, rather than simply to *kalām*.

## Early Career and Abbasid Context

### Baghdad’s translation movement

Al-Kindī entered Baghdad’s intellectual world during or before the caliphate of al-Maʾmūn (813–833). This was the mature phase of a translation movement that rendered Greek philosophical, medical, mathematical, and scientific texts into Arabic, sometimes through Syriac.

**Documented or strongly reconstructed role:** al-Kindī led, coordinated, or intellectually shaped a recognizable group of translators and revisers now called the “Kindī circle.”

The circle produced translations or adaptations of works connected with:

- Aristotle;
- Alexander of Aphrodisias;
- Plotinus;
- Proclus;
- Euclid;
- Ptolemy;
- other Greek mathematical and scientific authors.

Named translators associated with this textual environment include **ʿAbd al-Masīḥ ibn ʿAbd Allāh ibn Nāʿima al-Ḥimṣī**, translator of the Arabic Plotinus materials, and **Eustathius**, translator of Aristotle’s *Metaphysics* for al-Kindī. The exact membership and patronage structure varied from project to project.

### “House of Wisdom”

Popular accounts frequently say that al-Kindī was appointed to the Bayt al-Ḥikma, or House of Wisdom, and commanded its translation department.

That institutional formulation is **disputed**. Baghdad unquestionably contained caliphal libraries, translators, patrons, copyists, and scientific projects. However, modern research has questioned the picture of the Bayt al-Ḥikma as a single academy resembling a modern university. Al-Kindī’s court patronage and translation activity are well supported; a formal job title at a centralized academy is not.

### Al-Maʾmūn

Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa reports that al-Kindī enjoyed high standing under al-Maʾmūn. At least one work is addressed to the caliph, supporting the conclusion that al-Kindī had obtained court access by 833.

His exact relationship to al-Maʾmūn’s *miḥna*, the state-enforced doctrine concerning the created Qurʾan, is unknown. No surviving record makes al-Kindī an interrogator, victim, jurist, or administrative officer in the inquisition.

## Chronological Life History

### Circa 800–813: aristocratic Iraqi origins

Al-Kindī was born into the Kindite al-Ashʿath family, probably in Kufa or Basra. His father’s governorship connected the household to Abbasid administration.

### Circa 810s–820s: Basra and Baghdad

The most plausible geographical reconstruction places him at the family’s Basran property and then in Baghdad for advanced education. No exact migration date is known.

He acquired expertise in Arabic literary culture, logic, arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, medicine, music, and philosophical theology. Whether he personally traveled outside Iraq is unknown.

### Before 833: entrance into court scholarship

By the end of al-Maʾmūn’s reign, al-Kindī was sufficiently established to address writing to the caliph. Later reports call him highly regarded at court.

This period probably saw the development of his translator network and his effort to create a precise Arabic philosophical terminology.

### 833–842: high point under al-Muʿtaṣim

Al-Kindī’s best-documented patronage belongs to the reign of **al-Muʿtaṣim**.

He dedicated *On First Philosophy* to the caliph. Only the first major part survives. It defines philosophy as knowledge of the truth and “first philosophy” as knowledge of the First Truth, God.

Al-Kindī also became tutor to the caliph’s son **Aḥmad ibn al-Muʿtaṣim**. Numerous treatises are addressed to Aḥmad. Subjects include cosmology, meteorology, mathematics, astrology, psychology, and other sciences.

His role was not confined to translating. He:

- commissioned or received translations;
- corrected terminology;
- synthesized Aristotle with late Platonism;
- formulated Arabic philosophical vocabulary;
- defended the acquisition of knowledge from foreign peoples;
- applied mathematical reasoning to medicine, optics, music, and natural philosophy.

### 842–847: al-Wāthiq’s reign

Specific biographical evidence is thin. Modern summaries sometimes say al-Kindī remained favored under al-Wāthiq, but surviving dedications do not furnish a detailed court biography.

The absence of evidence should not be converted into either continued power or disgrace.

### 847–861: al-Mutawakkil and reported fall from favor

Al-Mutawakkil reversed important features of the theological policy of his predecessors and shifted court alliances. Al-Kindī’s position appears to have weakened.

The main story comes from Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa, writing approximately four centuries later:

1. The mathematician-engineers **Muḥammad and Aḥmad, sons of Mūsā ibn Shākir**, allegedly plotted against al-Kindī.
2. They persuaded al-Mutawakkil to have him beaten and to confiscate his library.
3. The books were stored separately as the *Kindiyya*, “the Kindian library.”
4. The Banū Mūsā later employed an engineer whose canal design failed because its intake was constructed at the wrong level.
5. They sought help from **Sanad ibn ʿAlī**.
6. Sanad allegedly demanded return of al-Kindī’s library as his condition for rescuing their hydraulic project and protecting them from the caliph’s anger.

**Evidentiary assessment:** the rivalry and confiscation are plausible in the competitive Abbasid patronage system, and modern specialists often accept a historical core. The detailed canal-and-rescue plot is an uncorroborated later anecdote. The motive—jealousy, political realignment, religious reaction, competition for patronage, or some combination—cannot be established.

### After 861

No secure sequence of events survives. Al-Kindī may have continued writing privately after the recovery of his library. There is no evidence that he was formally exiled from Baghdad, imprisoned for a long term, or restored to high office.

### After 866 to early 870s: final years

Modern specialists infer that he remained alive after 866. The traditional death years range from 866–867 to 873–874.

Al-Qifṭī transmits, through a report attributed to the astrologer **Abū Maʿshar al-Balkhī**, an account of his final illness:

- phlegm or swelling accumulated in his knee;
- he drank aged wine as treatment;
- he repented and substituted honey-water or honey juice;
- the condition spread;
- severe pain passed through his nerves into his head and brain;
- he died.

This is a **reported medical narrative**, not a contemporary clinical record. In modern terms it does not permit a reliable diagnosis. Proposed readings might include an infection, inflammatory joint disease, or systemic complication, but these would be speculation.

## Residences, Migrations, and Travel

Documented or reported locations are limited to Iraq:

- **Kufa:** father’s governorship; probable birthplace in one major tradition.
- **Basra:** called a Basran by Ibn Juljul; reported location of his estate; possible birthplace or early residence.
- **Baghdad:** education, translation activity, court service, teaching, writing, and probable death.

No reliable evidence establishes personal journeys to Greece, Byzantium, Persia, India, Egypt, Syria, Arabia, or al-Andalus. His knowledge of Greek, Persian, and Indian sciences arrived principally through texts and Baghdad’s multilingual scholarly networks.

Claims that he traveled throughout the “Islamic world” are unsupported generalizations.

## Professions, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Court scholar and tutor

Al-Kindī’s clearest occupational relationship was with the Abbasid court, especially al-Muʿtaṣim and Prince Aḥmad. Precise salary, title, appointment deed, and duration are unknown.

### Translation management

The Kindī circle was an intellectual and patronage network, not a documented incorporated institution. Al-Kindī appears to have:

- obtained Greek material;
- commissioned translations;
- mediated between patrons and translators;
- revised or polished Arabic;
- supplied conceptual terminology;
- used the translations as foundations for original treatises.

He should not simply be called “a translator.” Some medieval accounts say he translated many philosophical books, but the strongest modern reconstruction makes him primarily a commissioner, editor, interpreter, and author who relied on translators.

### Private library

The reported confiscation implies a large private collection. No inventory, acquisition record, valuation, or surviving ownership mark securely reconstructs its contents. Its reported designation as the *Kindiyya* suggests that it was recognizable as a personal scholarly library.

### Estates and wealth

His family held provincial office, and Ibn Juljul mentions a Basran estate. Later anecdotes portray him as living comfortably and maintaining an unusual garden or animals. Such details remain late reports.

No evidence was found for:

- commercial companies;
- loans or banking partnerships;
- patents, in the modern legal sense;
- investment contracts;
- tax records;
- probate inventory;
- documented real-estate transactions;
- litigation concerning his property.

### Cryptanalysis

The treatise *Risāla fī istikhrāj al-muʿammā* describes methods for deciphering encrypted writing, including comparing letter frequencies. It is regularly described as the earliest surviving systematic exposition of frequency analysis.

Its historical importance lies in treating language statistically:

1. count letters in a sufficiently long sample of known language;
2. rank their frequency;
3. count symbols in the ciphertext;
4. compare distributions;
5. use linguistic structure and combinations to refine substitutions.

This was analysis, not a patented invention. The exact date is unknown, and the work survives through a later manuscript tradition.

### Optics

In *On Perspectives* or *De aspectibus*, surviving principally in Latin, al-Kindī used Euclidean geometry to analyze visual rays, perspective, shadows, reflection, and related optical questions. He rejected important aspects of Aristotle’s account of sight and adopted an extramission model in which rays proceed from the eye.

His work on burning mirrors and focusing devices belongs to the ancient tradition of Euclid, Ptolemy, Anthemius, and stories attached to Archimedes.

### Pharmacology and medicine

*On Knowledge of the Powers of Compound Drugs*, known in Latin as *De gradibus*, attempted to mathematize the strengths of compound medicines. Al-Kindī used a numerical or geometric progression to relate degrees of heat, cold, moisture, and dryness.

The system was ingenious but controversial. Medieval Latin physicians struggled with its assumptions, and later pharmacologists did not retain it as an experimentally validated dosing law. It remains an early major effort to express pharmacological potency quantitatively.

### Perfume and chemical technology

The work conventionally called *Kitāb Kīmiyāʾ al-ʿiṭr wa-l-taṣʿīdāt* contains recipes and procedures for perfumes, aromatic oils, waters, distillation, and related preparations.

Al-Kindī also wrote against claims that base metals could genuinely be transformed into precious metals. This opposition to alchemical transmutation coexisted with practical interest in laboratory recipes, metallurgy, dyes, glass, and manufactured substances.

### Music

Al-Kindī treated music as mathematical science. His surviving music materials discuss:

- ratios and intervals;
- strings and frets of the ʿūd;
- tuning;
- rhythm;
- melodic composition;
- the formation of musicians;
- psychological and ethical effects of sound;
- cosmological correspondences involving strings, elements, colors, and scents.

Manuscript transmission is problematic. A London manuscript copied in 1662 derived from a 1224 exemplar that its own copyist characterized as defective. Some features—such as a five-string ʿūd—may be later interpolation; other witnesses describe four strings.

### Astronomy, astrology, and weather prediction

Al-Kindī did not draw a modern boundary between astronomy and astrology. His works addressed:

- planetary configurations;
- celestial causation;
- eclipses;
- weather;
- tides;
- comets;
- atmospheric color;
- instruments;
- determination of distances;
- historical and political prediction.

He conceived heavenly bodies as secondary causes operating under divine providence. His astrology was therefore integrated with, rather than opposed to, his monotheism.

### Political prediction

*Risāla fī mulk al-ʿArab wa-kammiyyatihi*—“Epistle on the Dominion of the Arabs and Its Duration”—used astrological and letter-based calculations to estimate the duration of Arab rule.

A manuscript copy in the British Library derives from an exemplar dated December 1136. The survival of the work does not establish that its prediction successfully identified later events; interpretations are vulnerable to textual corruption and retrospective reading.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Philosophy as foreign but legitimate knowledge

At the opening of *On First Philosophy*, al-Kindī argues that truth should be accepted regardless of the nationality or distance of those who discovered it. This was an explicit justification for appropriating Greek learning in Arabic and Islamic culture.

### God and creation

His metaphysics identifies God as:

- the First Truth;
- the True One;
- the cause of unity in everything else;
- transcendent beyond ordinary predicates;
- creator of the finite cosmos.

Al-Kindī opposed Aristotle’s eternity of the world. He offered arguments against an actually infinite body, time, or causal sequence. Creation was not merely rearrangement of eternal matter but a bringing of being from non-being.

### Philosophy and revelation

He did not treat philosophy as a rival religion. Prophetic knowledge, on his account, could be immediate and divinely given, whereas philosophy required extended human study. He interpreted Qurʾanic passages philosophically, including the verse about stars and trees prostrating themselves.

### Soul and intellect

In *On the Intellect*, al-Kindī distinguished forms or stages of intellect, including an intellect always in actuality and human intellect moving from potentiality to actuality. This small treatise became important for al-Fārābī, Avicenna, Averroes, and Latin scholastic discussions.

His writings portray the rational soul as incorporeal and capable of purification from bodily attachments.

### Ethics

*On Dispelling Sorrows* presents grief as arising from attachment to mutable possessions or conditions. The work combines Platonic, Stoic, Socratic, and Islamic elements. It offers exercises, anecdotes, and philosophical arguments rather than a full political-ethical system.

### Natural causation

Al-Kindī sought mathematical order in nature. Celestial motion, rays, elemental processes, and quantified qualities formed parts of a causally connected cosmos.

### Terminology

*On Definitions* helped stabilize Arabic equivalents for Greek philosophical terms. Its exact authorship is debated; current scholarship regards it as at least a production of the Kindī circle and possibly a layered compilation.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Abbasid patrons

- **Al-Maʾmūn:** reported patron or court connection; one work addressed to him.
- **Al-Muʿtaṣim:** best-documented caliphal patron; dedicatee of *On First Philosophy*.
- **Aḥmad ibn al-Muʿtaṣim:** pupil and frequent dedicatee.
- **Al-Wāthiq:** possible continuation of court standing, poorly documented.
- **Al-Mutawakkil:** ruler under whom al-Kindī reportedly suffered beating and library confiscation.

### Translators and collaborators

- **ʿAbd al-Masīḥ ibn ʿAbd Allāh ibn Nāʿima al-Ḥimṣī:** translator associated with the Arabic Plotinus corpus produced for al-Kindī.
- **Eustathius:** translated Aristotle’s *Metaphysics* for al-Kindī.
- **Unidentified Christian translators and revisers:** members of the broader circle.
- **Hunayn ibn Isḥāq:** contemporary leader of another translation network. Their circles overlapped intellectually, but a close personal partnership should not be assumed without specific evidence.

### Rivals and rescuers in later narrative

- **Muḥammad ibn Mūsā and Aḥmad ibn Mūsā:** alleged architects of his disgrace.
- **Sanad ibn ʿAlī:** allegedly secured the return of al-Kindī’s library.
- **Abū Maʿshar al-Balkhī:** later represented as al-Kindī’s associate and as transmitter of his death story. Other anecdotes make Abū Maʿshar initially hostile to mathematics or philosophy until al-Kindī redirected him, but details vary.

### Intellectual sources

Aristotle, Plato, Plotinus, Proclus, Alexander of Aphrodisias, Philoponus, Euclid, Ptolemy, Nicomachus, and Galen were textual rather than personal relationships.

## Significant Successes and Failures

### Successes

- Established one of the first sustained programs of original philosophical composition in Arabic.
- Helped create Arabic philosophical vocabulary.
- Connected Abbasid patrons with translators.
- Produced a corpus approaching 241–281 listed titles, depending on the medieval catalogue.
- Applied mathematics across optics, pharmacology, music, astronomy, and natural philosophy.
- Wrote the earliest surviving systematic account of cryptanalytic frequency analysis.
- Achieved wide Latin reception through *De intellectu*, *De aspectibus*, *De gradibus*, astrological works, and other translations.
- Became the conventional “Philosopher of the Arabs.”

### Failures or limitations

- Most of his corpus disappeared.
- *On First Philosophy* survives only partially.
- His optical theory retained the erroneous extramission theory of vision.
- His pharmacological quantification was not an empirically established dosage system.
- His astrology and universal ray theories did not survive as modern sciences.
- His attempt to reconcile Plato and Aristotle depended partly on works misattributed in late antiquity, especially the *Theology of Aristotle*, which is based on Plotinus.
- His court position apparently collapsed or weakened late in life.
- Later philosophers, especially al-Fārābī and Avicenna, overshadowed his system.

## Controversies, Accusations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### Reported beating and confiscation

**Status:** late reported fact with an uncertain historical core.

Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa states that al-Mutawakkil ordered al-Kindī beaten and seized his library after manipulation by the Banū Mūsā. There is no surviving court order, confiscation inventory, or contemporary eyewitness account.

### Religious persecution

The claim that al-Kindī was punished simply “for philosophy” or “as a heretic” is **unproven**. Al-Mutawakkil’s reign did bring religious and political realignment, but the narrative source foregrounds personal-professional rivalry. Neither explanation can be established exclusively.

### Accusations of deficient religion or *zandaqa*

Later Arabic polemical writing sometimes treats philosophers as heterodox and reports that al-Kindī’s religion was questioned. The surviving works do not support describing him as an atheist. They explicitly affirm God, creation, revelation, prophecy, and Islamically framed providence.

Whether particular doctrines were incompatible with later Sunni theology is a theological judgment, not evidence that he secretly rejected religion.

### Astrology and magic

Al-Kindī openly defended astrology as a causal science. Later Christian and Muslim readers could classify astral determinism, talismanic operations, or ray theory as illicit magic.

The Latin *De radiis* became particularly controversial. Giles of Rome included supposed Kindian doctrines among philosophical errors. Modern scholarship, however, has mounted a substantial case that *De radiis* in its surviving form was composed by a Latin author around 1250–1270 rather than translated from al-Kindī. It therefore cannot safely be treated as evidence for al-Kindī’s personal magical practice.

### Alchemy

Al-Kindī wrote practical works involving perfumes and chemical processes but rejected metallic transmutation. Accounts calling him a successful maker of artificial gold confuse practical chemistry with a doctrine he opposed.

### Plagiarism or mere translation

Some critics, medieval and modern, have characterized al-Kindī as primarily a compiler or translator of Greek philosophy.

Evidence shows both heavy dependence and genuine transformation:

- *Sayings of Socrates* may largely reproduce Greek source material.
- Several treatises paraphrase or rearrange Greek arguments.
- *On First Philosophy*, *On the Intellect*, the pharmacological writings, and his integrations of Qurʾanic material nevertheless display independent selection and synthesis.
- His own Greek proficiency is unproved; the attribution of numerous translations directly to him may exaggerate his linguistic role.

### Financial misconduct, criminality, and litigation

The adversarial search found no evidence that al-Kindī was accused of fraud, embezzlement, corruption, money laundering, conventional theft, violent crime, or commercial deception. No lawsuit, formal charge, trial, or conviction involving him is known.

The closest financial-political questions concern:

- elite patronage;
- possible inherited estates;
- ownership and confiscation of his library;
- competition for paid court commissions.

No amounts or contracts survive.

### Results of the dedicated adversarial pass

Searches in English and Arabic combining his name with scandal, allegations, fraud, lawsuit, conspiracy, criticism, heresy, and accusation produced four relevant classes of material:

1. the Banū Mūsā library-confiscation story;
2. later accusations of religious heterodoxy;
3. disputes about authorship of *De radiis*;
4. confusion with the Christian *Apology of al-Kindī*.

Searches for 2024–2026 controversies chiefly returned unrelated modern institutions named Al-Kindi, including a French school. None concerns the ninth-century philosopher.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The first Muslim philosopher”

**Story:** al-Kindī was the first philosopher in Islam.

**Appearance and circulation:** Ibn Juljul says that before him there had been no philosopher in Islam; later historians repeated the formula.

**Evidence:** he is the earliest major surviving author to present himself systematically as a philosopher in Arabic. Earlier Muslims nevertheless engaged in logic, theology, medicine, natural science, and Greek learning.

**Assessment:** meaningful as a title of precedence, not a literal claim that no Muslim had philosophized before him.

### “The only Arab philosopher”

**Story:** he was uniquely Arab among Islamic philosophers.

**Evidence:** his Kindite descent is well supported. Later major figures often had Persian, Central Asian, or other backgrounds.

**Assessment:** rhetorical honorific, not an accurate statement that no other Arab ever practiced philosophy.

### The jealous Banū Mūsā

**Story:** two Banū Mūsā brothers destroyed his standing, had him beaten, and took his books out of jealousy.

**Source:** Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa.

**Evidence for:** competitive Abbasid patronage and similar stories of the brothers scheming against Sanad ibn ʿAlī make a historical conflict plausible.

**Evidence against or limiting:** the report is late; motives are narratively simplified; no contemporary corroboration survives.

**Reputational effect:** al-Kindī became a symbol of the vulnerable scholar persecuted by jealous courtiers or religious reaction.

### The canal and return of the library

**Story:** an engineering blunder forced the Banū Mūsā to accept Sanad ibn ʿAlī’s condition that al-Kindī’s books be restored.

**Assessment:** memorable late anecdote, unverified. It may preserve a real restoration of property, but its dramatic mechanism cannot be checked.

### Death caused by repentance from wine

**Story:** wine was treating the swelling in al-Kindī’s knee, but after repenting he changed to honey-water and died when the illness spread to his brain.

**Source:** al-Qifṭī, reportedly from Abū Maʿshar.

**Assessment:** late medical-moral anecdote. It may preserve knowledge of a final painful illness, but its physiology and moral structure should not be treated as clinical documentation.

### Master of every language

**Story:** al-Kindī personally translated Greek and Syriac texts because he mastered both languages.

**Assessment:** unverified. His circle certainly handled Greek and Syriac materials, but the clearest evidence names other translators and places al-Kindī in a commissioning, editing, and philosophical role.

### Founder or director of the House of Wisdom

**Story:** al-Kindī was formally appointed director of a Baghdad academy called the House of Wisdom.

**Assessment:** modern institutional myth or overstatement. His court-based scholarly network is documented; the precise office and academy-like organization are not.

### Inventor of frequency analysis

**Story:** al-Kindī invented frequency analysis from nothing.

**Evidence:** his is the earliest surviving systematic explanation presently known.

**Qualification:** lost earlier work may have existed, and his method arose from Arabic philology, scribal practice, and cipher traditions. “Earliest surviving systematic author” is more defensible than absolute inventor.

### Treatment of a paralyzed boy with music

Stories circulated that al-Kindī used music to revive or treat a paralyzed or dying youth after physicians had failed.

**Assessment:** medical-musical legend illustrating the belief in music’s power over body and soul. No contemporary clinical evidence verifies the event.

### “Al-Kindī’s Apology”

The *Apology of al-Kindī* is an Arabic Christian polemic framed as correspondence between the Muslim ʿAbd Allāh al-Hāshimī and Christian ʿAbd al-Masīḥ ibn Isḥāq al-Kindī.

**Misattribution:** the Christian author is not Abū Yūsuf Yaʿqūb ibn Isḥāq al-Kindī. “Al-Kindī” is a tribal designation shared by multiple people.

The work’s date, composite character, and authorial identity remain disputed. It later became important in Latin Christian polemic against Islam and was translated under Peter the Venerable’s patronage. Connecting its attacks on Islam to the Muslim philosopher is false.

### Portraits

No authenticated contemporary portrait survives. Modern paintings, stamps, statues, and internet portraits are imaginative commemorations rather than likeness evidence.

## Works by Al-Kindī

### Bibliographical scale

The corpus cannot be reduced to one uncontested number:

- Ibn al-Nadīm lists approximately **241** works in seventeen subject divisions, while omitting several logical works mentioned elsewhere in his own book.
- Al-Qifṭī lists approximately **224**.
- Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa names approximately **281**.
- Modern catalogues have proposed totals around 239, 260, 270, or 272.
- Some titles may be alternate names, sections of larger works, revisions, or misattributions.
- Most works are lost.

Dates below are approximate unless a dedication supplies chronological context.

### Extant or substantially preserved philosophical works

1. **Kitāb fī al-falsafa al-ūlā** — *On First Philosophy*; dedicated to al-Muʿtaṣim, probably 833–842. Only the first part survives.
2. **Risāla fī al-ʿaql** — *On the Intellect*; Arabic and Latin transmission.
3. **Risāla fī ḥudūd al-ashyāʾ wa-rusūmihā** — *On the Definitions and Descriptions of Things*; attribution probably to al-Kindī or his circle, with possible textual strata.
4. **Risāla fī al-qawl fī al-nafs** — *Discourse on the Soul*.
5. **Kalām mukhtaṣar wajīz fī al-nafs** — *Concise and Brief Statement on the Soul*.
6. **Risāla fī annahu tūjadu jawāhir lā ajsām** — *That There Are Incorporeal Substances*.
7. **Risāla fī māhiyyat al-nawm wa-l-ruʾyā** — *On the Quiddity of Sleep and Dreams*.
8. **Risāla fī al-tadhakkur** — *On Recollection*.
9. **Risāla fī al-fāʿil al-ḥaqq al-awwal al-tāmm** — *On the True, First, Complete Agent*.
10. **Texts against the infinity or eternity of the world**, including arguments that body, motion, and time cannot be actually infinite.
11. **Risāla fī ibṭāl qawl man zaʿama anna al-kammiyya lā nihāya lahā** — against the claim that magnitude can be infinite.
12. **Risāla fī al-ḥīla li-dafʿ al-aḥzān** — *On the Art of Dispelling Sorrows*.
13. **Alfāẓ Suqrāṭ** or collections of **Sayings of Socrates**; probably heavily derived from Greek gnomic sources.
14. **Risāla fī kammiyyat kutub Arisṭūṭālīs wa-mā yuḥtāju ilayhi fī taḥṣīl al-falsafa** — *On the Quantity of Aristotle’s Books and What Is Required for the Attainment of Philosophy*.
15. **Risāla fī al-jawāhir al-khamsa** — *On the Five Essences*.
16. **Against the Trinity** — known through Christian refutation and reconstructed fragments; directed against Christian Trinitarian doctrine.
17. **Two texts on color** — dealing with why things appear colored.

### Cosmology and natural philosophy

18. **Risāla fī al-ʿilla al-fāʿila al-qarība li-l-kawn wa-l-fasād** — *On the Proximate Agent Cause of Generation and Corruption*.
19. **Risāla fī sujūd al-jirm al-aqṣā** — *On the Prostration of the Outermost Body/Sphere*.
20. **Risāla fī māhiyyat al-falak** — *On the Nature of the Celestial Sphere*.
21. **Risāla fī anna al-ʿālam kullahu kurī al-shakl** — on the spherical form of the cosmos.
22. **Risāla fī al-ibāna ʿan al-ʿilla al-qarība li-kawn al-maṭar** — on the proximate cause of rain.
23. **Risāla fī al-ʿilla allatī lahā yabruḍ aʿlā al-jaww** — *Why the Higher Atmosphere Is Cold*.
24. Works on snow, hail, thunder, lightning, winds, mist, and atmospheric phenomena.
25. Works on tides and the relation of seas to celestial motion.
26. Works on earthquakes.
27. Works on comets and celestial signs.
28. **Why the ancients associated the five geometrical solids with the elements**.

### Mathematics, geometry, and instruments

29. Numerous treatises on Indian arithmetic, numerical properties, fractions, multiplication, ratios, and harmony.
30. Works on Euclidean geometry, lines, surfaces, solids, spheres, and measurement.
31. Treatises on determining distances, including celestial or terrestrial distances.
32. Works on sundials and timekeeping.
33. **Risāla fī ʿamal al-sāʿāt ʿalā ṣafīḥa…** — on constructing a sundial on a plane parallel to the horizon.
34. A correction of a proposition attributed to Hypsicles; authorship has also been assigned to Qusṭā ibn Lūqā and remains disputed.
35. Works on the astrolabe and other astronomical instruments.

### Optics and rays

36. **Fī ikhtilāf al-manāẓir / De aspectibus / De causis diversitatum aspectus** — *On Perspectives* or *On the Causes of Differences in Vision*; Arabic original lost or fragmentary, Latin version extant.
37. **Fī al-āla al-muḥriqa** — on burning instruments, mirrors, or optical focusing; reconstructed from manuscript material.
38. Works on reflection, mirrors, shadows, and geometrical optics.
39. **De radiis stellarum / Theorica artium magicarum** — traditionally attributed *On Rays*. Survives in Latin; authenticity seriously disputed, with a modern argument for thirteenth-century Latin composition.

### Medicine and pharmacology

40. **Fī maʿrifat quwā al-adwiya al-murakkaba / De gradibus** — *On Knowledge of the Powers of Compound Drugs*.
41. Works on simple and compound medicines.
42. Treatises on fevers, leprosy, rabies, poisons, and bodily symptoms.
43. Works on medical regimen and preservation of health.
44. A treatise on the use of music in healing is listed or reported, though stories of individual cures are legendary.
45. Medical aphorisms and short practical epistles.

### Cryptography and language

46. **Risāla fī istikhrāj al-muʿammā** — *Epistle on Deciphering Encrypted Messages*; systematic cryptanalysis using letter frequency, linguistic patterns, and cipher classification.
47. Other listed works on encrypted correspondence and signs may overlap with this text or be lost.

### Music

Medieval catalogues attribute roughly a dozen or more music works to al-Kindī; four principal textual witnesses preserve portions of the corpus.

48. **Al-Risāla al-kubrā fī al-taʾlīf** — *Great Epistle on Composition*.
49. **Risāla fī khubr taʾlīf al-alḥān** — on the composition of melodies.
50. **Risāla fī ajzāʾ khabariyya fī al-mūsīqā** — on elements or information concerning music.
51. Treatise on the strings of the ʿūd, tuning, fingering, and proportions.
52. Works on rhythm (*īqāʿ*).
53. Works on melodic composition and the requirements of musicianship.
54. Works associating music with cosmology, colors, scents, seasons, and effects on the soul.

The exact division and titles vary among catalogues and manuscripts. Later interpolation is demonstrable or suspected in portions of the London witness.

### Astronomy and astrology

55. **Kitāb fī al-ṣināʿa al-ʿuẓmā** — a major work on Ptolemaic astronomy/astrology, partly extant.
56. Works on planetary conjunctions.
57. Works on interrogational astrology.
58. Works on annual revolutions and political prognostication.
59. Works on eclipses, stellar positions, and celestial judgments.
60. **Risāla fī mulk al-ʿArab wa-kammiyyatihi** — *On the Dominion of the Arabs and Its Duration*.
61. **Forty Chapters** or *The Forty Chapters*, an astrological work transmitted in Arabic and Latin traditions.
62. Works on weather forecasting through celestial configurations.

### Perfume, technology, minerals, and practical arts

63. **Kitāb Kīmiyāʾ al-ʿiṭr wa-l-taṣʿīdāt** — *Book of the Chemistry of Perfume and Distillations*.
64. Works on varieties and manufacture of perfume.
65. Treatises on swords, types of iron, and methods intended to prevent blades from breaking or becoming blunt.
66. Works on glass and coloring glass.
67. Works on dyes, removing stains, and manufactured substances.
68. Works concerning gems, minerals, and the properties of stones.
69. Treatises rejecting the artificial transmutation of base metals into gold and silver.
70. A work on making food from substances other than its customary ingredients is listed in later bibliographical tradition.

### Political and administrative works

71. Works categorized by Ibn al-Nadīm under *siyāsa*, encompassing ethics, governance, self-management, and courtly conduct.
72. Epistles to princes on intellectual and practical subjects.
73. Works on royal or dynastic duration, partly astrological rather than administrative.

### Lost works

The majority of the catalogue—logic, dialectic, arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, meteorology, medicine, political ethics, psychology, metallurgy, and miscellaneous sciences—is known only by title. A title in Ibn al-Nadīm is evidence that a work was attributed to al-Kindī by the tenth century, not proof that the complete work circulated exactly as described or that every attribution was genuine.

### Sympathetic, hostile, and superseded treatments

Older histories often celebrated al-Kindī as an uncomplicated scientific “genius,” director of the House of Wisdom, universal linguist, and inventor of numerous modern disciplines. Such works are important for reception history but frequently collapse manuscript evidence, late anecdote, and modern terminology.

Later theological polemics sometimes grouped him among dangerous philosophers or presumed unbelievers. Those classifications reflect later doctrinal disputes and do not override the explicitly monotheistic and Islamic content of his surviving texts.

## Films, Documentaries, Interviews, and Archival Material

No major historically authoritative feature film or dedicated scholarly documentary about al-Kindī was identified in the consulted sources. Modern educational videos frequently repeat the conventional 801–873 biography but rarely distinguish manuscript evidence from later anecdotes.

Important archival and manuscript resources include:

- **Süleymaniye Library, Istanbul, Ayasofya 4832:** the crucial codex preserving much of the philosophical corpus.
- **British Library oriental manuscripts:** manuscripts of music and astrology, including the copy of *On the Dominion of the Arabs and Its Duration*.
- **British Library Or. 2361:** a Mughal musical miscellany containing the only surviving copy of one al-Kindī music treatise; copied in 1662 from an earlier defective witness.
- **Bodleian Library, Oxford:** Latin and Arabic witnesses to scientific and musical works.
- **Staatsbibliothek zu Berlin:** musical manuscript material.
- **Manisa manuscript collection:** another witness to al-Kindī’s musical corpus.
- **Bibliothèque nationale de France:** Latin manuscripts of *De gradibus* and other Kindian material.
- **Max Planck Institute for the History of Science, Islamic Scientific Manuscripts Initiative:** authority record and work catalogue.
- **Qatar Digital Library/British Library:** digitized astrological manuscript material.
- **Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa scholarly digital edition, National Library of Medicine:** Arabic text and English translation of the principal medical-biographical notice.

Because al-Kindī died more than a millennium before recording technology, there are no authentic interviews, voice recordings, or contemporary moving images.

## Death and Burial

The exact date, place, and burial site remain uncertain.

The most defensible formulation is:

> Al-Kindī died in Baghdad or elsewhere in Abbasid Iraq sometime after 866, probably around 870–873.

The conventional 873 date should not be presented as exact. No reliable source consulted identifies his grave or documents a continuing shrine cult.

The report of knee swelling, attempted wine treatment, repentance, honey-water, systemic pain, and death comes through al-Qifṭī from a narrative attributed to Abū Maʿshar. It is the only substantial death story but cannot establish a modern cause of death.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Arabic-Islamic philosophy

Al-Kindī established an Arabic philosophical program before al-Fārābī and Avicenna. His major contributions were not a single closed “system” but:

- making Greek philosophy usable in Arabic;
- developing terminology;
- integrating mathematics with philosophy;
- treating Islamic doctrines through philosophical demonstration;
- establishing questions later philosophers reformulated.

His taxonomy of intellect contributed to a long Arabic and Latin debate. His arguments about divine unity, creation, soul, and causation were absorbed, revised, or rejected by later philosophers and theologians.

### Loss and rediscovery

Most of his works disappeared. His direct philosophical influence was therefore less visible than his original productivity suggests.

A decisive modern event was the identification by Hellmut Ritter, with Martin Plessner’s assistance, of approximately thirty-four Kindian works or texts in Istanbul libraries in 1932. The Ayasofya manuscript enabled the editions of Abū Rīda and subsequent international scholarship.

The atlas journey title, “The Philosopher of the Arabs: Kufa, Baghdad, and the Treatise That Waited Eleven Hundred Years,” corresponds closely to this history: a corpus formed in ninth-century Iraq, largely lost from ordinary circulation, then recovered from a unique or nearly unique Istanbul manuscript tradition in the twentieth century.

### Latin Europe

From the twelfth century onward, Latin translations circulated under forms of his name such as Alkindus and Jacobus Alkindus.

His works affected:

- mathematical optics;
- theories of visual rays;
- astrology;
- pharmacology;
- classifications of intellect;
- discussions of magic and astral causation.

Roger Bacon directly cited “Jacob Alkindi.” The optical tradition also passed through Robert Grosseteste, Witelo, and John Pecham. *De gradibus* entered Latin medical study and debate, including at Montpellier.

### Cryptographic reputation

Modern histories of computing and cryptology celebrate al-Kindī for the earliest surviving systematic presentation of frequency analysis. The description is justified when carefully worded; claims that he invented every principle of cryptanalysis are stronger than the evidence.

### Music

He is among the earliest Arabic music theorists whose texts survive. His synthesis of Greek ratio theory, the practical ʿūd, rhythm, composition, psychology, and cosmology helped establish music as a mathematical-philosophical science in Arabic.

### Modern commemoration

Schools, hospitals, streets, research initiatives, prizes, stamps, and public institutions have been named after him. These commemorations often use invented portraits and streamlined scientific titles.

A modern school or institution named Al-Kindi is not biographically connected to the philosopher. Recent controversies involving the Groupe scolaire Al-Kindi in France concern a twenty-first-century institution, not the historical individual.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted evidence are included. Shared historical period or geography alone is not treated as a connection.

### Aristotle — documented intellectual connection

Al-Kindī surveyed Aristotle’s corpus in *On the Quantity of Aristotle’s Books*, used Aristotle’s *Metaphysics*, *Categories*, *De anima*, and *Parva naturalia*, and relied on Aristotelian commentators. He nevertheless rejected Aristotle’s eternal cosmos and sometimes rejected specific scientific doctrines, including his account of vision.

**Dates and places:** textual reception in Baghdad, principally early to mid-ninth century.

**Label:** documented intellectual influence and critical engagement; no personal meeting was possible.

### Plato — documented intellectual connection

Al-Kindī knew Platonic material, especially the *Timaeus* tradition, summaries of the *Symposium*, doctrines about the soul, and late-antique efforts to harmonize Plato and Aristotle. His access was often indirect or epitomized rather than through complete dialogues.

**Dates and places:** Baghdad, ninth century.

**Label:** documented textual influence; the exact form of several Platonic sources is reconstructed.

### Archimedes of Syracuse — reported/textual scientific connection

Al-Kindī’s work on burning mirrors participated in a mathematical-optical tradition associated with Archimedes, Euclid, Ptolemy, and Anthemius. The famous story that Archimedes burned Roman ships with mirrors is itself disputed and should not be transferred into al-Kindī’s biography.

**Label:** documented shared textual-scientific tradition; precise direct dependence on an authentic Archimedean text is uncertain.

### Al-Khalil ibn Ahmad al-Farahidi — possible intellectual precedent

Al-Khalīl’s lost works on cryptographic messages, combinations, rhythm, and music are frequently identified as precedents for al-Kindī’s statistical and musical work. No personal meeting is possible on the usual dates, and no consulted source establishes direct study under al-Khalīl.

**Label:** scholarly reconstruction of intellectual precedent, not a documented personal relationship.

### Muhammad ibn Abdullah — documented religious and ancestral-context connection

Al-Kindī’s writings cite and philosophically interpret the Qurʾan and explain prophetic knowledge within an Islamic framework. His ancestor al-Ashʿath ibn Qays was a companion of Muḥammad according to Islamic biographical tradition.

**Label:** documented religious-intellectual influence; reported ancestral association. No personal meeting was possible.

### Roger Bacon — documented posthumous reception

Roger Bacon cited Jacob/Alkindus and drew directly or indirectly upon Kindian optics and ray theory. Modern textual work finds direct borrowing from al-Kindī’s optics in Bacon.

**Dates and places:** thirteenth-century Latin Europe, long after al-Kindī’s death.

**Label:** documented posthumous textual influence.

### Albertus Magnus — documented broader Latin reception, exact dependence variable

Al-Kindī’s translated works circulated in the Arabic-Latin philosophical and scientific corpus used by thirteenth-century scholars including Albertus Magnus. The general reception is documented, but individual doctrinal dependence must be assessed work by work.

**Label:** documented textual environment; specific influence less precisely established than for Roger Bacon.

### Nicholas of Cusa / Johannes Cusanus — misattributed-name connection only

Nicholas of Cusa used the Christian *Apology of al-Kindī* in anti-Islamic polemic. That work was not written by Abū Yūsuf Yaʿqūb ibn Isḥāq al-Kindī.

**Label:** documented reception of a different “al-Kindī”; no genuine connection to the philosopher.

### Maimonides — no direct connection established

Maimonides inherited an Arabic philosophical world partly made possible by the translation tradition in which al-Kindī participated. The consulted sources did not establish a specific personal, textual, or explicitly acknowledged Kindian influence strong enough for a direct atlas connection.

**Label:** absence of documented direct connection.

### Ahmad al-Majriti and Maslama ibn Ahmad al-Majriti — no direct connection established

Later Andalusian scientific and occult traditions operated in fields al-Kindī had treated, but no direct documented relationship or explicit influence was established in the sources consulted.

**Label:** absence of documented direct connection.

### Other roster members

No other atlas-roster intersection was securely established. Mere participation in philosophy, mathematics, optics, music, astrology, Islam, or the Abbasid world was not counted as a connection.
