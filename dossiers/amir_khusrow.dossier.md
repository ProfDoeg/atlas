# Amir Khusrow Dehlavi: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier reconstructs the life and reception of Amir Khusrow from his datable Persian writings, manuscript catalogues, medieval chronicles as represented in modern critical scholarship, academic biographies, institutional collections, and documented later traditions.

Evidence labels used here:

- **Documented fact:** supported by Khusrow’s own datable writing, a near-contemporary source, manuscript evidence, or strong scholarly consensus.
- **Reported fact:** found in later biographical or historical sources but not independently established.
- **Disputed claim:** competing scholarly accounts or unresolved textual evidence.
- **Traditional attribution:** accepted in devotional, literary, or musical tradition without secure contemporary documentation.
- **Legend or hagiography:** a culturally important story whose historical basis is uncertain.
- **Modern polemical claim:** a recent political or ideological interpretation requiring separation from the underlying medieval evidence.

Dates are given in Common Era form, with Hijri dates where they materially clarify disagreement.

## Source Base and Problems of Reconstruction

Khusrow is unusually well documented for a medieval South Asian poet because his five Persian divans have autobiographical prefaces and his narrative works frequently name rulers, patrons, campaigns, and dates. His works nevertheless present several difficulties:

1. He normally wrote from within structures of royal patronage. His descriptions of rulers and victories are therefore not neutral administrative records.
2. Panegyric convention encouraged exaggeration, cosmic imagery, suppression of royal failures, and celebration of conquest.
3. Later tazkiras, Sufi hagiographies, musical genealogies, and oral repertoires expanded his biography.
4. The secure Persian corpus is much easier to establish than the Hindavi material performed under his name.
5. Many musical inventions were first credited to him centuries after his death.
6. “Amir Khusrow” can be confused with later musicians bearing similar names, particularly an eighteenth-century musician sometimes called Khusrau Khan.
7. Exact Gregorian equivalents vary because medieval events were dated in the Islamic calendar and modern conversions differ.

The surviving evidence is much stronger for his public literary career than for his wife, household, private finances, health, and ordinary daily life.

## Family and Ancestry

### Father

**Documented or strongly supported fact:** Khusrow’s father was Amir Sayf al-Din Mahmud, a Turkic military officer who entered the Delhi Sultanate’s service. Modern scholarship generally connects him with the Hazara-yi Lachin or Lāchīn group of Transoxiana.

The precise meaning of “Lachin,” the family’s exact tribal genealogy, and its relationship to Qara Khitai political formations are inconsistently described. Popular biographies sometimes turn the label into a highly specific tribal pedigree that the contemporary documentation does not sustain.

Sayf al-Din appears to have migrated to northern India amid the displacement caused by Mongol expansion in Central Asia. The often-repeated itinerary—Kesh or Shahr-i Sabz, then Balkh, then India—is **reported fact**, not securely recoverable in every detail. He arrived under Sultan Shams al-Din Iltutmish, whose reign lasted from 1211 to 1236.

Sayf al-Din received a position or land assignment in the sultanate. Claims that he was specifically granted Patiyali in 1230 are repeated but inadequately documented. He died around 658 AH/1260, when Khusrow was approximately seven or eight.

### Mother and maternal family

Khusrow’s mother was Indian-born. Later sources call her Daulat Naz or Bibi Daulat Naz. She was the daughter of Imad al-Mulk, also called Rawat Arz in some accounts, an Indian convert to Islam and prominent official connected to Sultan Balban’s establishment.

The precise pre-conversion caste or Rajput identity sometimes assigned to this family is a **reported or disputed claim**. It rests principally on later genealogical writing.

Imad al-Mulk’s Delhi household became central to Khusrow’s upbringing after his father’s death. It gave him access to aristocratic society, literary education, state officials, and Sufi circles.

### Siblings

Later biographies say Sayf al-Din and Daulat Naz had three sons and one daughter. Names and life histories vary. An elder son is sometimes identified as Izz al-Din Ali Shah and another as Hisam al-Din or Hisham al-Din Ahmad. The evidence for a fully reconstructed sibling group is weak.

### Marriage and children

Khusrow evidently married, but no securely documented name for his wife has emerged. She is absent from the autobiographical passages normally used to reconstruct his life.

Later biographical compilations identify several sons:

- Khizr, reportedly mentioned in *Majnun o Layla*.
- Muhammad and Haji, said to have died during Khusrow’s lifetime.
- Malik Ahmad, described as a poet associated with Firuz Shah Tughluq.
- Some sources instead speak of a son Malik Muhammad.

These details are **reported facts with unstable nomenclature**. It is possible that variants conflate names or honorifics. No dependable reconstruction of daughters, descendants, marital property, or domestic arrangements is presently available.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1253–1273

### Birthplace dispute

The conventional account places Khusrow’s birth at Patiyali in 651 AH/1253–54. Encyclopaedia Iranica and much modern scholarship repeat this location.

A competing Delhi hypothesis has been advanced because:

- he consistently used “Dehlavi”;
- he wrote of Delhi with intimate attachment;
- his maternal family lived there;
- some biographers find aspects of the Patiyali tradition chronologically or geographically unconvincing.

The nisba “Dehlavi,” however, can indicate residence and literary identity rather than literal birthplace. It does not by itself disprove Patiyali.

Claims that he was born in Balkh, Kabul, or elsewhere in Central Asia appear to transpose his father’s origins onto him. The strongest presently available formulation is: **probably born at Patiyali in 1253–54, raised substantially in Delhi; exact birthplace not demonstrable beyond dispute.**

### Education

Khusrow’s early education took place within the elite Muslim culture of Delhi. It likely included:

- Qur’anic and religious instruction;
- Persian and Arabic grammar;
- calligraphy;
- rhetoric, prosody, and letter writing;
- canonical Persian poetry;
- familiarity with court protocol;
- exposure to northern Indian vernacular language and music.

Later narratives say he was sent to a calligrapher but wrote poems instead of concentrating on penmanship. This is a conventional prodigy story and should be treated as **literary biography**, not independently documented incident.

He began composing verse in childhood. His first divan’s autobiographical preface presents poetic precocity as a central element of his identity.

### Literary teachers and formative poets

Khusrow named or was associated with Shihab al-Din Mahmera Badauni as a master in poetry. His formative Persian models included:

- Sana’i;
- Khaqani;
- Anvari;
- Sa‘di;
- Nizami Ganjavi;
- earlier Arabic and Persian rhetorical traditions.

Nizami was decisive for his narrative poetry, although Khusrow did not merely translate him. His *Khamsa* deliberately answered Nizami’s five-poem model while altering its settings, emphasis, rhetoric, and political meanings.

After his father’s death, his maternal grandfather Imad al-Mulk reportedly encouraged his education and poetry. Imad al-Mulk died around 1272–73, removing the principal household patron of Khusrow’s youth.

## Education and Spiritual Formation

### Association with Nizamuddin Auliya

Khusrow became attached to the Chishti master Nizamuddin Auliya no later than the early 1270s; 671 AH/1272–73 is frequently given. The relationship lasted for more than fifty years.

Its precise institutional form is debated. Devotional tradition presents Khusrow as Nizamuddin’s pre-eminent disciple and beloved spiritual son. His poetry and the double dedications of his *Khamsa* securely demonstrate profound devotion. Whether he underwent every formal stage later Chishti hagiography assigns to him is less certain.

The relationship joined two social worlds:

- Nizamuddin’s khanqah, which cultivated spiritual authority and a degree of distance from rulers;
- the sultanate courts, where Khusrow obtained income, status, protection, and commissions.

Khusrow did not withdraw from government service. His life therefore cannot be reduced either to courtly opportunism or to renunciant Sufism. He remained simultaneously a professional panegyrist and a devotee of a saint who was frequently wary of royal power.

### Sufism in the writings

The lyrical poetry contains the conventional Persian vocabulary of love, absence, annihilation, wine, the beloved, and spiritual longing. Such language can support mystical readings but is not always exclusively mystical.

His court chronicles are much less visibly Sufi. Annemarie Schimmel observed that, outside poems directly connected to Nizamuddin, an explicitly Sufi character can be difficult to detect. Later performance traditions have foregrounded the mystical dimension more strongly than the complete written corpus necessarily does.

## Early Career, approximately 1273–1287

### Service under Malik Chajju

After Imad al-Mulk’s death, Khusrow entered the circle of Ala al-Din Kishlu Khan, better known as Malik Chajju, a nephew or relative of Sultan Ghiyas al-Din Balban. The appointment is generally placed around 1273.

Later biographies describe a rupture: Khusrow received conspicuous favor from another prince at a gathering, provoking Malik Chajju’s jealousy. He then left or fled the household. The basic change of patron is likely; the dramatic scene is not independently established.

### Bughra Khan

Khusrow next served Nasir al-Din Bughra Khan, Balban’s son. He accompanied Bughra Khan eastward, reportedly to Bengal or toward Lakhnauti, during the campaigns and political adjustments associated with Balban’s suppression of Tughril’s rebellion.

This was one of Khusrow’s first major movements beyond Delhi and exposed him to Bengal and Awadh. He did not remain permanently in Bengal.

### Prince Muhammad at Multan

Khusrow then entered the court of Balban’s eldest son, Prince Muhammad, known posthumously as Khan-i Shahid, governor of the vulnerable northwestern frontier at Multan.

Multan’s court attracted émigré intellectuals and poets. Amir Hasan Sijzi Dehlavi, later famous as Hasan Dehlavi, belonged to the same cultural circle and became an important friend and poetic contemporary. Prince Muhammad reportedly invited Sa‘di of Shiraz to Multan, but the elderly poet declined; this is a widely repeated story rather than a firmly verified personal link between Sa‘di and Khusrow.

### Mongol attack and captivity

In 1285, Mongol forces attacked the frontier. Prince Muhammad was killed. Khusrow was captured and carried away by the attackers.

His eventual escape or release is a documented element of the standard biography, though the details differ. One colorful account says his captor drank excessively, became incapacitated, or died, permitting Khusrow to escape. That mechanism is **later report or legend**.

Khusrow wrote an elegy for Prince Muhammad. Later writers say its recital caused the aged Balban to weep so intensely that the grief hastened his death. The elegy exists as part of Khusrow’s response to the disaster; the effect on Balban is **courtly legend**.

The episode establishes that Khusrow was more than a sedentary palace writer: he was physically present in a dangerous frontier environment and endured captivity. No reliable medical description of resulting injuries or psychological trauma survives.

## Chronological Life History

### 1286–1289: Awadh and return to Delhi

After the Multan disaster, Khusrow associated with Amir Ali Hatim Khan, governor of Awadh. He spent roughly two years there before returning to Delhi.

Balban died in 1287. The succession brought Mu‘izz al-Din Kaiqubad, Bughra Khan’s son, to the throne. The estranged Kaiqubad and Bughra Khan met near the Sarju in Awadh. Khusrow transformed the reconciliation into *Qiran al-sa‘dayn* (“The Conjunction of the Two Auspicious Stars”), completed around 1289.

The poem is both literary work and political document. It describes royal spectacle, Delhi, social groups, entertainments, architecture, and the ideological presentation of father-son reconciliation. It also helped establish Khusrow as a major historical poet.

### 1290–1296: Jalal al-Din Khalji

The Mamluk or Balbanid order collapsed in 1290, and Jalal al-Din Firuz Khalji founded a new dynasty. Khusrow adapted to the new regime and became a favored poet.

He commemorated four victories in *Miftah al-futuh* (“Key to the Victories”), incorporated into the third divan. The work praised Jalal al-Din’s military and political success.

His service across a dynastic overthrow illustrates the professional structure of medieval court poetry. A poet’s livelihood and literary production depended on successive rulers and grandees; loyalty was personal and patronal but rarely equivalent to modern permanent civil service.

### 1296–1316: Ala al-Din Khalji

Jalal al-Din was assassinated in 1296 by his nephew and son-in-law Ala al-Din Khalji. Khusrow subsequently served the assassin’s regime and produced the largest part of his major corpus under it.

This period included:

- repeated Mongol attacks on northern India;
- Ala al-Din’s military expansion into Gujarat, Rajasthan, Malwa, and the Deccan;
- strict market, revenue, and aristocratic-control measures;
- court factionalism;
- the prominence of generals such as Ulugh Khan and Malik Kafur.

Khusrow’s income and position are not recoverable as a modern salary history. He obtained royal rewards and patronage, but surviving evidence does not permit a reliable net-worth estimate, property inventory, investment history, or business structure. Statements that a particular poem earned an immense quantity of gold belong to the rhetoric of royal generosity unless independently confirmed.

#### The *Khamsa*, 1298–1302

At the height of his career, Khusrow composed a five-part answer to Nizami Ganjavi’s *Khamsa*. Sunil Sharma’s study concludes that this project was apparently undertaken from Khusrow’s own literary ambition rather than as a direct commission. Each poem was dedicated both to Nizamuddin Auliya and to Ala al-Din Khalji, a striking joining of spiritual and political patronage.

The sequence comprises:

1. *Matla‘ al-anwar* — didactic and ethical.
2. *Shirin o Khusraw* — romance.
3. *Majnun o Layla* — tragic love narrative.
4. *A’ina-yi Sikandari* — Alexander romance.
5. *Hasht Bihisht* — tales structured around Bahram Gur and the eight paradises.

It imitated Nizami’s architecture while competing with him in linguistic virtuosity and adapting the narratives for an Indo-Persian audience.

#### *Khaza’in al-futuh*

Around 1311 Khusrow completed *Khaza’in al-futuh* (“Treasures of Victory”), also called *Tarikh-i ‘Ala’i*. It describes Ala al-Din’s reign and conquests in elaborate Persian prose.

It is indispensable but partisan. It celebrates victories, royal order, mosque construction, idol destruction, submission of enemies, and the expansion of Islam. It does not offer an even account of Ala al-Din’s accession or the regime’s coercive policies.

#### Deval Rani and Khizr Khan

Khusrow composed *Duval Rani Khizr Khan*, also called *‘Ashiqa*, about the romance between Khizr Khan, Ala al-Din’s son, and Deval Rani, daughter of the defeated Vaghela ruler of Gujarat.

The work blends history, dynastic romance, captivity, and literary construction. After Khizr Khan was blinded and killed amid succession struggles, Khusrow expanded or revised the narrative. It preserves historical material but cannot be read as a literal transcript of the couple’s private life.

### 1316–1320: Mubarak Shah and the Khalji collapse

Ala al-Din died in 1316. After violent succession struggles, Qutb al-Din Mubarak Shah ruled. Khusrow retained favor and composed *Nuh Sipihr* (“Nine Heavens”) in 1318.

*Nuh Sipihr* is central to modern debates about Khusrow’s relationship to India. It praises:

- India’s climate and natural wealth;
- Indian learning, mathematics, languages, stories, and music;
- the intellectual capacities of Indians;
- the poet’s homeland and attachment to Delhi.

It also employs the religious vocabulary and hierarchies of a medieval Muslim court writer. Modern attempts to make it proof either of fully modern secular nationalism or of simple religious hostility flatten a complex panegyric.

Mubarak Shah was murdered in 1320 in the conspiracy that brought Khusrau Khan to power. Khusrow’s position during Khusrau Khan’s very short reign is poorly documented. The similarity between the ruler’s name and the poet’s has generated occasional confusion.

### 1320–1325: Ghiyas al-Din Tughluq and Muhammad bin Tughluq

Ghazi Malik defeated Khusrau Khan and became Sultan Ghiyas al-Din Tughluq. Khusrow again transferred his literary service to the victorious regime.

He composed *Tughluq-nama*, celebrating Ghiyas al-Din’s rise and victory. The work is both an important narrative of the 1320 revolution and a legitimating poem for the new dynasty.

Khusrow accompanied or was connected with Ghiyas al-Din’s eastern campaign in Bengal. While he was away, Nizamuddin Auliya died in Delhi in April 1325.

Later tradition says Khusrow returned dressed in black, fell at the grave, scattered or gave away his property, and recited:

> The fair one lies upon the bed, her hair spread across her face;  
> Come, Khusrow, let us go home, for night has fallen everywhere.

The Hindavi verse is among the most famous texts linked to him. The emotional association with Nizamuddin’s death is traditional; the exact wording and immediate occasion are not established through a contemporary autograph.

Ghiyas al-Din died in 1325 when a pavilion collapsed at Afghanpur after his return from Bengal. Medieval sources disagreed about whether the accident had been arranged by his son, the future Muhammad bin Tughluq. Khusrow did not live long enough to provide a developed account of that reign.

He briefly enjoyed or acknowledged Muhammad bin Tughluq’s accession. Poems in his final divan address the new ruler.

## Death and Burial

Khusrow died in Delhi in 725 AH/1325, only months after Nizamuddin Auliya.

A frequently given date is 18 Shawwal 725 AH, converted in modern devotional sources as 27 September 1325. Other references place the death in October. The Hijri date and Gregorian conversion should therefore be cited together rather than treating a single modern conversion as absolutely certain.

No reliable source gives a specific illness, injury, poisoning, addiction, or other medical cause of death. Hagiography interprets his death as the result of overwhelming grief after Nizamuddin’s death. This is spiritually meaningful within the tradition but not a documented medical diagnosis.

He was buried immediately south of Nizamuddin’s grave. The present tomb is not simply the original 1325 construction. The enclosure and building accumulated in stages. Encyclopaedia Iranica dates completion of the tomb in its present general form to 1014 AH/1605. Conservation documentation describes:

- an enclosure approximately 28 by 20 feet;
- red-sandstone lattice screens associated with Humayun’s period;
- a marble-paved court;
- an oblong marble tomb chamber;
- twelve supporting pillars;
- later roofing and architectural additions.

The grave remains a major pilgrimage and qawwali site. Conservation work documented in 2023–24 included repair of damaged stonework and lattice screens, architectural assessment, and planning for more appropriate lighting and shelter.

## Professions, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Court service

Khusrow was attached, successively, to elite households or courts associated with:

- Malik Chajju;
- Bughra Khan;
- Prince Muhammad at Multan;
- Hatim Khan in Awadh;
- Sultan Kaiqubad;
- Jalal al-Din Khalji;
- Ala al-Din Khalji;
- Qutb al-Din Mubarak Shah;
- briefly the political order following Mubarak’s death;
- Ghiyas al-Din Tughluq;
- the opening of Muhammad bin Tughluq’s reign.

The later formula that he served “seven sultans” depends on who is counted and whether short or indirect attachments are included. His career crossed at least three dynastic political orders: late Mamluk/Balbanid, Khalji, and early Tughluq.

### Literary projects

His principal self-directed and patron-related projects were:

- five chronological divans;
- a Persian *Khamsa* responding to Nizami;
- historical masnavis on current political events;
- ornate historical prose;
- a five-part manual and display of epistolary prose;
- verse in or incorporating Hindavi;
- poems intended or later adapted for musical performance.

### Finances

Khusrow belonged to patronage networks rather than a modern commercial literary market. His economic support probably consisted of gifts, stipends, offices, robes of honor, and access to aristocratic households.

There is no dependable evidence for:

- independent businesses;
- commercial investments;
- patents or patent-like privileges;
- banking relationships;
- a documented portfolio of real estate;
- a calculable fortune;
- litigation over his estate.

Later stories that he gave away everything after Nizamuddin’s death are hagiographic and cannot substitute for an estate record.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Political writing

Khusrow’s political writing developed from personal panegyric into historical narrative. His innovations included treating recent sultanate events as subjects for Persian masnavi and ornate prose.

His work presents kingship through:

- divine favor;
- victory;
- justice and order;
- spectacle;
- conquest over rebels and non-Muslim rulers;
- architectural and ceremonial display;
- the ruler’s position in Persianate universal history.

He did not formulate a modern program of constitutional politics or national sovereignty.

### India and Persianate identity

Khusrow called himself an Indian Turk and described India as birthplace, abode, and homeland. His identity joined:

- Turkic paternal ancestry;
- Indian maternal ancestry;
- Delhi residence;
- Persian literary culture;
- Sunni Islam;
- Chishti devotion;
- service to Indo-Muslim rulers;
- sustained contact with Indian languages, stories, festivals, and music.

Modern labels such as “Indian nationalist,” “secularist,” “communalist,” or “inventor of composite culture” are retrospective. Parts of his work can be selected to support each political narrative, but none maps neatly onto his thirteenth- and fourteenth-century categories.

### Language

His secure literary monument is overwhelmingly Persian. He proudly demonstrated his ability in complex Persian rhetoric and described Persian literary models extending across Iran and Central Asia.

He also explicitly knew and used Hindavi. Secure mixed-language verses and wordplay exist, but the complete Hindavi divan sometimes claimed for him has not survived. The large popular corpus of songs, riddles, *do-sukhane*, and playful verses under his name accumulated through oral and manuscript transmission.

Calling him “father of Urdu” or “father of Hindi” is a later honorific formulation, not a philologically precise description. Neither standardized modern Urdu nor standardized modern Hindi existed in his lifetime.

### Music

Khusrow demonstrably cared about music, wrote singable poetry, knew musical culture, and became foundational to hereditary qawwal traditions.

The historical minimum is substantial:

- Persian and attributed Hindavi texts under his name became central to Chishti musical assemblies;
- he described Indian music admiringly;
- later Mughal writers remembered him as a major musical authority;
- his ghazals and devotional texts remain in living performance.

Claims that he personally invented the modern sitar, tabla, khayal, tarana, qawwali, and numerous ragas are not supported by surviving works from his lifetime. His own writings, despite their self-conscious discussion of literary invention, do not claim these inventions.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Nizamuddin Auliya

Spiritual master, devotional focus, and the most important non-royal relationship of Khusrow’s adult life. Their graves became physically and ritually linked.

### Amir Hasan Sijzi Dehlavi

Friend, poetic contemporary, and fellow member of Prince Muhammad’s Multan circle. Hasan became known especially for Persian ghazals and for recording Nizamuddin’s conversations in *Fawa’id al-fu’ad*.

### Shihab al-Din Mahmera Badauni

Reported teacher or guide in poetic technique.

### Imad al-Mulk

Maternal grandfather, guardian, elite official, and early patron.

### Prince Muhammad Khan-i Shahid

Military prince and patron at Multan. His death produced one of Khusrow’s most important personal elegies.

### Bughra Khan and Kaiqubad

Successive patrons whose political reconciliation supplied the subject of *Qiran al-sa‘dayn*.

### Jalal al-Din and Ala al-Din Khalji

Successive ruler-patrons. Ala al-Din obtained power through Jalal al-Din’s assassination; Khusrow served both.

### Deval Rani and Khizr Khan

Historical royal figures whose relationship Khusrow converted into a dynastic romance. His knowledge may have included court information, but his narrative is literary rather than a private documentary archive.

### Nizami Ganjavi

Not a personal acquaintance—Nizami died before Khusrow’s birth—but the central literary predecessor against whom Khusrow defined his narrative ambitions.

### Zia al-Din Barani

Younger contemporary and historian of the Delhi Sultanate. Both belonged to the broader Delhi intellectual environment. Claims of a particularly intimate friendship should be made cautiously unless tied to a specific source.

## Works by Amir Khusrow

Dates are approximate because composition, revision, and compilation were sometimes separate events.

### Five divans: genuine core corpus

1. **Tuhfat al-sighar** (“Gift of Youth”), compiled around 1272–73.  
   Poetry of his youth, generally through about age nineteen. The prose preface contains autobiographical material.

2. **Wast al-hayat** (“Middle of Life”), compiled around 1284–85.  
   Poems from the next phase of his career.

3. **Ghurrat al-kamal** (“Prime/Rising of Perfection”), compiled around 1293–94.  
   Includes a particularly important autobiographical preface and *Miftah al-futuh*.

4. **Baqiya-yi naqiya** (“The Pure Remainder”), compiled around 1316.

5. **Nihayat al-kamal** (“The Utmost/Perfection’s End”), compiled near the end of his life, approximately 1325.  
   Includes poems connected with the final Khalji and early Tughluq transitions.

Later publishers often combined these as a *Kulliyat* or “complete works.” Such editions are posthumous compilations, not a separate book authored at one sitting.

### The *Khamsa*: genuine

Composed approximately 1298–1302:

1. **Matla‘ al-anwar** (“Rising Place of Lights”).
2. **Shirin o Khusraw**.
3. **Majnun o Layla**.
4. **A’ina-yi Sikandari** (“Alexander’s Mirror”).
5. **Hasht Bihisht** (“Eight Paradises”).

The project is a deliberate answer to Nizami’s *Khamsa*. Later manuscripts were extensively illustrated in Timurid, Safavid, and Mughal workshops.

### Historical masnavis and associated verse: genuine

- **Qiran al-sa‘dayn** (“Conjunction of the Two Auspicious Stars”), 1289: meeting of Bughra Khan and Kaiqubad.
- **Miftah al-futuh** (“Key to the Victories”), approximately 1291: four victories of Jalal al-Din Khalji; transmitted within *Ghurrat al-kamal*.
- **Duval Rani Khizr Khan**, also called **‘Ashiqa**, initial form approximately 1315, with revision or enlargement: romance and political tragedy of Deval Rani and Khizr Khan.
- **Nuh Sipihr** (“Nine Heavens”), 1318: nine sections in nine meters, written under Mubarak Shah; major source for Khusrow’s description of India.
- **Tughluq-nama**, approximately 1320–21: rise of Ghiyas al-Din Tughluq and defeat of Khusrau Khan.

### Prose works: genuine or substantially accepted

- **Khaza’in al-futuh** (“Treasures of Victory”), also **Tarikh-i ‘Ala’i**, approximately 1311: ornate prose chronicle of Ala al-Din Khalji’s victories.
- **I‘jaz-i Khusrawi**, also **Rasa’il al-i‘jaz** (“The Miracle of Khusrow”): five-part collection or treatise on epistolography, rhetoric, and prose style. Initial material was collected by approximately 1292; the larger project was completed around 1319.

### Attributed collection of Nizamuddin’s sayings

- **Afdal al-fawa’id**, dated around 1319: sayings of Nizamuddin Auliya allegedly recorded by Khusrow.  
  **Status:** disputed attribution. It has been accepted in parts of the tradition, but scholars have questioned whether Khusrow compiled it in its surviving form.

### Hindavi and mixed-language corpus

The following categories circulate under Khusrow’s name:

- mixed Persian-Hindavi ghazals, especially *Zi-hal-i miskin makun taghaf ul*;
- devotional songs including *Chhap tilak sab chini*;
- *Aaj rang hai*;
- the verse beginning *Gori sowe sej par*;
- Basant songs;
- riddles or *paheliyan*;
- *mukarniyan*;
- *do-sukhane*;
- playful verses and songs associated with marriage and seasonal festivals;
- qawwali pieces such as *Man kunto maula* in performance lineages.

**Status:** mixed. A small vernacular or mixed-language nucleus is plausible and in some cases strongly traditional, but much of the corpus lacks manuscripts close to Khusrow’s lifetime. Some riddles mention objects such as hookahs or guns that did not exist in his historical environment, proving later composition for at least those items.

### *Khaliq Bari*

A rhymed Persian-Arabic-Hindavi vocabulary widely attributed to Khusrow.

**Status:** disputed and probably later in its received form. Its manuscript history and language do not securely place the complete text in the early fourteenth century. It may preserve older material or a Khusrow-associated tradition without being wholly his work.

### Lost or uncertain works

Khusrow claimed an enormous poetic output, figures varying in later reports from hundreds of thousands of verses upward. No surviving corpus reaches those totals. Some poetry was lost; other numerical claims are rhetorical.

Works or titles found only in later lists should not automatically be counted as independent genuine books. Titles can also represent alternate names, subdivisions, or posthumous editorial compilations.

## Controversies, Accusations, and Contested Questions

### Court opportunism and changing allegiance

**Documented fact:** Khusrow served successive and sometimes mutually hostile regimes. He praised Jalal al-Din Khalji and subsequently Ala al-Din, who had Jalal al-Din killed. He later praised Mubarak Shah and then the Tughluq conqueror of Mubarak’s successor.

**Interpretation:** Modern writers have called this opportunism, political flexibility, or normal professional conduct for a court poet. There is no evidence of a criminal proceeding or contemporary prosecution against him for disloyalty.

The conventional defense that “all medieval poets did this” is contextual rather than dispositive. The documentary fact remains that his public writing repeatedly legitimated the ruler who controlled Delhi.

### Celebration of conquest and religious violence

**Documented textual fact:** In *Khaza’in al-futuh*, Khusrow celebrates Ala al-Din’s conquests with Islamic victory language. Passages describe temples and idols destroyed, the triumph of mosques and the call to prayer, defeated non-Muslims, severed heads, and bloodshed. The Somnath passage imagines the temple bowing toward Mecca and employs the Qur’anic story of Abraham breaking idols. Accounts of southern campaigning use similarly triumphalist imagery.

These passages cannot be dismissed simply because they are now circulated polemically. They belong to an authentic or generally accepted work.

Necessary distinctions:

- The text documents Khusrow’s celebratory representation of campaigns.
- It does not prove that every numerical, architectural, or battlefield detail is literal.
- Court panegyric can exaggerate violence and victory.
- Khusrow was not the commander of the Gujarat or Deccan armies.
- No strong evidence establishes that he personally destroyed a temple or personally killed captives.
- His literary celebration made him part of the ideological apparatus of conquest, even where personal operational responsibility is absent.

### Claims of personal atrocities

Recent posts accuse Khusrow personally of rape, enslavement, abduction, temple destruction, or mass killing.

**Status: unverified or false attribution unless tied to a genuine passage.** No credible primary documentation found in this research shows Khusrow personally committing rape or directing a massacre. Quotations stating that “a Turk can buy, rape, abduct, sell and kill any infidel Hindu” circulate online without a reliable work, manuscript location, or critical-text citation and should not be represented as an authenticated quotation.

This does not negate the genuine violent rhetoric of *Khaza’in al-futuh*. It distinguishes the authenticated court text from fabricated or unsourced first-person accusations.

### Suppression and distortion in the chronicles

Historians have criticized *Khaza’in al-futuh* for:

- omitting or minimizing Ala al-Din’s murder of Jalal al-Din;
- idealizing Ala al-Din;
- subordinating social costs to victory rhetoric;
- providing little balanced account of failed campaigns or opposition;
- using elaborate prose that complicates factual extraction.

The work remains valuable precisely because it is close to the court, but proximity also created dependence.

### Hindu-Muslim “unity” versus religious triumphalism

Two modern selective readings compete:

1. **Syncretic-national reading:** emphasizes his Indian mother, praise of India, Hindavi poetry, Chishti affiliation, participation in Basant traditions, and place in shared musical culture.
2. **Hostile-polemical reading:** emphasizes his court service, Islamic supremacy language, praise of temple destruction, and association with conquest.

Both draw on real aspects of the corpus, but both become misleading when presented as the whole record. A poet could admire Indian music, language, science, and homeland while also employing religiously triumphalist language about a Muslim ruler’s wars.

### Authorship of Hindavi songs

Many popular songs have no securely dated manuscript from Khusrow’s lifetime. Oral transmission can preserve old material, but it also permits revision, linguistic modernization, and fresh attribution.

The absence of early manuscripts is evidence of uncertainty, not proof that Khusrow wrote no Hindavi. His own references and mixed-language skill make some vernacular composition highly plausible.

### “Father of Urdu”

**Modern honorific, not literal documented office.** Khusrow contributed to the prestige of Indian vernacular expression in a Persian literary environment. The later language called Urdu developed through a long, regionally diverse history. It cannot be assigned a single inventor.

### Plagiarism or imitation of Nizami

Khusrow openly structured his *Khamsa* as a response to Nizami. Within Persian literary culture, composing a *nazira* or answer in an established form was competitive emulation, not concealed modern plagiarism. Critics may judge parts of his sequence inferior or overly ornate, but he did not hide the model.

### Lawsuits, fraud, corruption, and criminal proceedings

No documented lawsuit, fraud prosecution, corruption investigation, criminal indictment, conviction, or money-laundering allegation exists for Khusrow in the modern juridical sense.

No surviving documentation indicates intelligence service work. Like other courtiers, he may have obtained political information through court networks; that does not establish an intelligence role.

## Dedicated Adversarial Search: Findings

Searches combining his name with “controversy,” “scandal,” “allegations,” “lawsuit,” “fraud,” “accusations,” “conspiracy,” “criticism,” temple destruction, violence, and authorship—also constrained to material appearing in 2024–26—produced four substantive clusters.

### 1. Religious-violence controversy

This is the major evidence-based negative issue. Recent circulation focuses on passages from *Khaza’in al-futuh* concerning Gujarat, Somnath, and southern campaigns. The underlying primary text exists; its panegyrical genre and Khusrow’s non-command role must also be stated.

### 2. Fabricated or decontextualized quotations

Recent political posts circulate statements about a supposed right of Turks to rape, abduct, sell, or kill Hindus. No reliable textual location was found. These remain **unverified claims**, distinct from genuine violent passages.

### 3. Musical and linguistic appropriation disputes

Recent arguments dispute whether Khusrow “invented” Indian instruments or simply received credit for pre-existing Indian culture. The evidence supports neither a universal inventor claim nor the proposition that all associated innovations are demonstrably ancient and unchanged. The historical development of tabla, sitar, khayal, qawwali, and tarana occurred over centuries.

### 4. Interpretation of *Chhap tilak*

Some modern polemics interpret the removal of the *tilak* in love as a program for erasing Hindu identity or conversion. Within the poem’s received Sufi performance context, the language represents transformation by the beloved or spiritual master. Because the poem’s authorship and early text are themselves not securely established, sweeping claims about Khusrow’s personal conversion program exceed the evidence.

No credible new lawsuit, criminal discovery, financial scandal, or archival revelation concerning Khusrow appeared in the most recent two-year search period.

## Myths, Legends, Hagiography, and Folklore

### Inventor of the sitar

**Story:** Khusrow invented the sitar by combining or modifying Persian and Indian lutes, sometimes deriving its name from Persian *seh-tar*, “three strings.”

**Appearance and circulation:** Later musical histories, hereditary musician narratives, popular biographies, textbooks, and twentieth-century cultural writing.

**Evidence:** No surviving work by Khusrow claims this invention. Secure documentation for the modern sitar belongs to a much later period. Confusion with an eighteenth-century musician named Amir Khusrau or Khusrau Khan may have contributed.

**Effect:** The story made Khusrow the personification of Indo-Persian musical synthesis.

### Inventor of the tabla

**Story:** He cut a pakhawaj or mridang in half, producing the two tabla drums.

**Evidence against:** The physical and historical development of tabla is later and more complicated. The cut-drum explanation is organologically implausible, and no contemporary source records it.

**Status:** legend.

### Inventor of qawwali

**Story:** Khusrow created qawwali by combining Persian, Arabic, Turkic, and Indian music for Nizamuddin Auliya’s assemblies.

**Evidence:** Chishti devotional audition predates him, and qawwali as a named, stable modern genre developed gradually. Khusrow-associated verses and hereditary qawwal traditions are nevertheless central to its repertoire.

**Status:** traditional attribution expressing foundational influence, not a verifiable single act of invention.

### Qawwal Bachche lineage

**Story:** Khusrow trained a group of young singers, the Qawwal Bachche, from whom hereditary Delhi qawwals descend.

**Evidence:** Maintained in musician genealogies and shrine tradition; difficult to establish from fourteenth-century documents.

**Effect:** Provides professional and spiritual lineage for qawwal families.

### Khayal, tarana, and invented ragas

Later works credit him with khayal, tarana, *qaul*, and numerous ragas or melodic combinations.

Khusrow may have participated in processes of musical synthesis, but lists of specific inventions appear in later sources. Nonsense-syllable tarana texts are particularly associated with him in performance memory; the precise fourteenth-century form cannot be reconstructed.

### Basant at Nizamuddin

**Story:** Seeing Hindu women dressed in yellow for Basant, Khusrow dressed in yellow and carried mustard flowers to cheer Nizamuddin, who was grieving. The saint smiled, and Basant became a dargah custom.

**Evidence:** Living shrine tradition, songs, and later retellings; no contemporary record establishes the initiating incident.

**Effect:** The story became a powerful emblem of shared seasonal culture and the localization of Chishti practice in India.

### First meeting with Nizamuddin

**Story:** Khusrow tested the saint by composing inward verses or waiting outside; Nizamuddin miraculously perceived his thoughts and answered them.

**Status:** hagiographic initiation narrative.

### Death from grief

**Story:** After Nizamuddin’s death, Khusrow abandoned worldly possessions, remained at the grave, and died of grief within months.

**Documented core:** he died soon after Nizamuddin and was buried beside him.

**Unverified elements:** total divestment, continuous vigil, and grief as medical cause of death.

### “Parrot of India”

The parrot signifies eloquence, sweetness of speech, mimicry transformed into mastery, and India’s literary voice. The sobriquet helped canonize Khusrow throughout the Persianate world.

### Portraits of Khusrow

Later Persian and Mughal miniatures sometimes label a figure as Khusrow or show him with disciples. These are retrospective literary images, not authenticated contemporary portraits.

## Significant Successes and Limitations

### Successes

- Established an enduring Indo-Persian literary canon.
- Produced one of the most extensive surviving bodies of Persian verse from medieval India.
- Adapted the Persian ghazal and narrative masnavi to an Indian courtly setting.
- Developed historical masnavi as a vehicle for recent sultanate events.
- Created an ambitious answer to Nizami’s *Khamsa*.
- Recorded descriptions of Delhi, regional languages, customs, festivals, and music.
- Became central to Chishti devotional performance.
- Remained influential in Iran, Central Asia, Afghanistan, India, Pakistan, and Bangladesh.
- Inspired major manuscript-painting projects, particularly under the Mughals.

### Failures or limitations visible in the record

- His court histories are not balanced political histories.
- Patronal praise led him to pass rapidly over assassinations and coercion unfavorable to the reigning monarch.
- His ornate “powdered” prose can obscure events.
- Not all works claimed under his name survived.
- The Hindavi corpus was not securely preserved in authorial manuscripts.
- Musical invention traditions cannot be confirmed from his own testimony.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Fourteenth to fifteenth centuries

Khusrow’s Persian poetry circulated rapidly. Later Persian poets in India treated him as a canonical predecessor. His historical works became sources for the Khalji and Tughluq periods.

His grave’s placement beside Nizamuddin integrated him permanently into Chishti sacred geography.

### Timurid, Safavid, and Mughal worlds

His *Khamsa* became a prestigious manuscript subject. Illustrated copies demonstrate that his narratives circulated well beyond Delhi.

Mughal atelier manuscripts placed him beside Nizami and Jami within a transregional Persian canon. Akbar-period illustration translated his stories into sixteenth-century Indian courtly visual culture.

### Persian literary assessment

Khusrow was read across the Persianate world, though Iranian critics sometimes judged his language excessively intricate or his *Khamsa* less artistically unified than Nizami’s. The stylistic density called “powdered” or highly worked style could be admired as virtuosity or criticized as artificiality.

### South Asian devotional reception

Qawwali performance made the name “Khusrow” familiar to audiences unable to read medieval Persian. Songs under his name remain customary at Sufi shrines and in concert, film, television, and popular recording.

The performance corpus is historically layered: canonical attribution reflects devotional authority, not necessarily authorial proof for every lyric.

### Colonial and nationalist reinterpretation

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century scholars recovered and edited his Persian historical works. Nationalist and cultural-integration narratives elevated him as:

- first major Hindustani poet;
- father of Urdu or Hindi;
- founder of Hindustani classical music;
- architect of Hindu-Muslim synthesis;
- patriotic poet of India.

Some of these formulations preserve genuine dimensions of his work but turn gradual cultural processes into single-person invention stories.

### Postcolonial contestation

Khusrow has become a contested emblem:

- pluralist narratives emphasize Basant, vernacular poetry, mixed ancestry, Indian patriotism, and qawwali;
- Islamist devotional narratives emphasize his sainthood and obedience to Nizamuddin;
- Persian literary histories emphasize his place in classical Persian rather than modern Hindi-Urdu;
- Hindu-nationalist criticism emphasizes his praise of conquest and temple destruction;
- musicologists question invention myths;
- philologists question large portions of the Hindavi corpus.

The archive supports a multilingual Indo-Persian court poet and Chishti devotee whose work contains both intense attachment to India and explicit medieval Islamic victory rhetoric.

### Modern commemoration

India, Pakistan, and the Soviet Union organized congresses in 1975 under the rubric of his “700th anniversary,” although the number was not an exact anniversary of either birth or death.

The Humayun’s Tomb World Heritage Site Museum, opened in 2024, includes Khusrow among figures associated with the Nizamuddin cultural landscape. Conservation of his tomb continues under the Nizamuddin Urban Renewal Initiative.

| Mughal period | Tomb repeatedly rebuilt or embellished; major illustrated *Khamsa* manuscripts produced. |
| 1605 | Present general form of the tomb conventionally dated as complete. |
| 1870s–1930s | Major printed Persian editions and English translations appear. |
| 1927 | Mohammad Habib publishes *Hazrat Amir Khusrau of Delhi*. |
| 1935 | Wahid Mirza publishes the foundational modern biography *The Life and Works of Amir Khusrau*. |
| 1975 | India, Pakistan, and the USSR organize “700th anniversary” commemorations. |
| 1988 | Rajbans Khanna’s sixteen-part *Amir Khusrau* television serial airs on Doordarshan. |
| 2005 | Sunil Sharma publishes *Amir Khusraw: The Poet of Sultans and Sufis*. |
| 2011 | Losensky and Sharma publish *In the Bazaar of Love*. |
| 2023–24 | Conservation assessment and repairs documented at Khusrow’s tomb. |
| 2024 | Humayun’s Tomb World Heritage Site Museum opens with interpretation of the wider Nizamuddin landscape, including Khusrow. |
| Present | His Persian poetry remains studied internationally; attributed Hindavi songs remain central to qawwali and South Asian popular music; authorship and invention claims remain contested. |
