# Christina Georgina Rossetti: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

This dossier reconstructs Christina Georgina Rossetti’s life from published writings, surviving correspondence, manuscript catalogues, family records, contemporary biography, later scholarship, institutional histories, and reception studies.

Evidence labels used:

- **Documented fact:** supported by primary records, correspondence, manuscripts, publication records, or multiple independent scholarly sources.
- **Reported fact:** asserted by a contemporary or biographer but not independently established here.
- **Allegation or theory:** an interpretive or biographical claim for which conclusive documentary evidence is absent.
- **Rumor or myth:** a story circulated without adequate supporting evidence.
- **Unresolved:** surviving evidence permits competing reconstructions.

Rossetti’s brother William Michael edited her posthumous writings and became a principal custodian of the family record. He preserved much, but reportedly destroyed or withheld some private material. Consequently, the surviving archive is extensive but not neutral or complete.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal family

Christina’s father was **Gabriele Pasquale Giuseppe Rossetti** (1783–1854), born at Vasto in Abruzzo, then in the Kingdom of Naples. He was the son of Nicola Rossetti, described in family sources as a blacksmith.

Gabriele became a poet, librettist, Dante scholar, and political activist. He supported constitutional and nationalist causes during the upheavals surrounding the Neapolitan revolution of 1820–21. After the Bourbon restoration, he escaped first to Malta and then, in 1824, to England. His London home remained connected to Italian émigré and patriotic networks. In 1831 he became professor of Italian language and literature at the newly established King’s College London.

His increasingly elaborate interpretation of Dante treated the poet as a bearer of concealed religious and political meanings. Christina inherited from him Italian literary culture, knowledge of Dante and Petrarch, habits of allegorical reading, and the experience of belonging to an exiled family. She did not adopt his anticlerical or heterodox conclusions.

### Maternal family

Christina’s mother was **Frances Mary Lavinia Polidori Rossetti** (1800–1886), born in London. Frances was trained as a governess and later taught to sustain the family during its financial decline. She supervised the education of Maria and Christina.

Frances’s father, **Gaetano Polidori** (1763–1853), came from Bientina near Pisa. He studied law, served as secretary to the dramatist Vittorio Alfieri, worked as a translator and teacher, and established a private press. He printed Christina’s first collection, *Verses*, in 1847.

Frances’s mother, **Anna Maria Pierce Polidori**, was English and Anglican. The maternal household combined Italian learning with a strict English Protestant religious tradition.

Christina’s maternal uncle **John William Polidori** (1795–1821), physician to Lord Byron and author of *The Vampyre*, died before Christina’s birth but formed part of the family’s literary inheritance.

### Parents’ marriage

Gabriele Rossetti and Frances Polidori married in 1826. Their household was bilingual: contemporary and family accounts describe the children as speaking Italian with their father and English with their mother.

### Siblings

1. **Maria Francesca Rossetti** (1827–1876)

   Writer, teacher, Dante scholar, and author of *A Shadow of Dante* (1871). Maria worked as a governess when the family needed income. In 1873 she joined the Anglican Society of All Saints Sisters of the Poor. She died in 1876. Christina’s relationship with Maria was intellectually and religiously important, although Maria’s decision to enter a sisterhood removed her from the shared household.

2. **Gabriel Charles Dante Rossetti**, generally known as **Dante Gabriel Rossetti** (1828–1882)

   Poet, painter, translator, and founding member of the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood. Christina called him Gabriel. He drew and painted her, advised on her manuscripts, altered or suggested titles, and illustrated *Goblin Market and Other Poems* and *The Prince’s Progress and Other Poems*. Their relationship combined affection, mutual artistic influence, disagreement, and periods of strain, especially during his later illness and dependence on chloral.

3. **William Michael Rossetti** (1829–1919)

   Civil servant, critic, editor, and member of the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood. William provided substantial financial support to the household, acted as Christina’s literary adviser, edited her posthumous poetry and family letters, and wrote the principal early memoir of her. His documentary work is indispensable, but his selection and destruction of papers also shaped the surviving record.

### Household structure and finances

The family initially possessed the security provided by Gabriele’s King’s College salary and private teaching. His failing eyesight, bronchial and physical illness, and mental or neurological deterioration ended much of his earning capacity in the 1840s. Frances taught; Maria became a governess; William entered the Excise Office; Dante Gabriel attempted to establish himself as an artist; Christina and her mother tried running schools.

There is no evidence that Christina controlled businesses, investments, patents, or a significant independent fortune. Her income came from writing, family resources, and William’s assistance. William later stated that Christina recognized this support as a debt and made testamentary provision to repay him. Charles Cayley also left manuscripts and personal property to her.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1830–1842

Christina was born at 38 Charlotte Street on 5 December 1830, the fourth child born to the Rossettis in under four years.

The Charlotte Street household received Italian exiles, writers, musicians, and political visitors. It exposed the children to discussion of Dante, Italian nationalism, English literature, religion, and contemporary politics. The neighborhood itself was socially mixed rather than genteel; William later described it as dingy and periodically disreputable.

Christina was educated at home. Frances taught religion, languages, history, literature, and household subjects. The children read or heard the Bible, Dante, Shakespeare, Scott, Byron, Dickens, fairy tales, *The Arabian Nights*, and Italian literature.

Family recollections describe Christina as intelligent, quick-tempered, imaginative, and competitive. Stories that she cut herself with scissors after being reprimanded, or that she threw violent childhood tantrums, originate in family and biographical recollection. They are **reported facts**, not independently documented incidents.

Her earliest remembered verse, preserved by William, began “Cecilia never went to school / Without her gladiator.” Before she could write fluently, she reportedly dictated an unfinished tale about a dervish named Hassan.

On 27 April 1842, aged eleven, she wrote “To My Mother on the Anniversary of Her Birth.” From 1842 she habitually dated and copied poems into notebooks, creating an unusually traceable record of composition.

## Education and Formation

### Home education

Christina did not attend a conventional school or university. Her mother provided essentially all of her formal education. The absence of institutional schooling restricted her access to credentials but did not produce intellectual isolation: the household contained books, multilingual conversation, artists, political refugees, and scholars.

### Family literary activity

The Rossetti children produced manuscript family periodicals:

- *The Hodge-Podge; or, Weekly Efforts*, begun 20 May 1843.
- *The Illustrated Scrapbook*.

These included stories, poems, drawings, and jokes and formed an early collaborative workshop.

### Religious formation

The women of the Rossetti household began within Evangelical Anglicanism. In the 1840s they became associated with the Oxford Movement’s London expression, particularly **Christ Church, Albany Street**.

Christina worshipped there for more than twenty years from about 1843. Clerical influences included:

- William Dodsworth;
- William James Early Bennett;
- Henry William Burrows;
- Edward Bouverie Pusey;
- Richard Frederick Littledale, later an important spiritual adviser.

Her annotated and illustrated copy of John Keble’s *The Christian Year* documents close reading of Tractarian poetry. She also read John Henry Newman and Isaac Williams.

Her mature religion remained Anglican. Her objection to Roman Catholicism was decisive in her engagement to James Collinson; her objection to Charles Cayley’s lack of orthodox belief was decisive in refusing marriage to him.

### Literary and artistic influences

Documented or strongly evidenced influences included:

- the Bible and Anglican liturgy;
- Dante Alighieri;
- Petrarch;
- John Keble and Tractarian poetics;
- Shakespeare;
- traditional ballad and nursery-rhyme forms;
- Elizabeth Barrett Browning;
- Dante Gabriel Rossetti and Pre-Raphaelite aesthetics;
- Romantic poetry and, more indirectly, William Blake;
- hagiography, natural history, devotional calendars, and medieval allegory.

Her later reading was extensive. She used the British Museum Reading Room, attended lectures on Dante at University College London in 1878–80, and read periodicals including *The Athenaeum*, *Blackwood’s Magazine*, *The Edinburgh Review*, *Macmillan’s Magazine*, and *The Saturday Review*.

## Adolescence, Family Crisis, and Early Writing, 1843–1847

In 1843 Gabriele’s health deteriorated seriously. His eyesight failed; he became increasingly unable to teach; and the household’s financial security weakened.

Around 1845 Christina experienced a major physical and psychological illness. Sources describe exhaustion, depression, palpitations, fainting, religious anxiety, and periods in which she was regarded as mentally unwell. Diagnosis is unresolved. Contemporary physicians reportedly used terms such as “nervous” illness, angina, tuberculosis, or religious mania. Retrospective diagnoses are speculative.

One later annotation claimed that a physician thought the adolescent Christina was “more or less out of her mind” and suffering a kind of religious mania. This is a **reported retrospective statement**, not a surviving clinical record. Modern writers have proposed depression, anxiety disorder, psychosomatic illness, or endocrine disease, but none can be established for this episode.

In 1847 Gaetano Polidori privately printed *Verses by Christina G. Rossetti*. It was distributed chiefly within the family circle and did not constitute a commercial debut. The book nevertheless established an authorial identity and preserved her juvenilia.

---

## Pre-Raphaelite Period and James Collinson, 1848–1852

### The Pre-Raphaelite circle

Dante Gabriel, William Michael, William Holman Hunt, John Everett Millais, James Collinson, Frederic George Stephens, and Thomas Woolner founded the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood in 1848.

Christina was not a formal “Brother,” but she participated in its literary environment. She was read, criticized, published, and represented within the circle. Her exclusion from membership reflected the Brotherhood’s male institutional structure rather than absence from its activity.

She modelled for Dante Gabriel, most prominently as the Virgin in:

- *The Girlhood of Mary Virgin*;
- *Ecce Ancilla Domini!*.

The latter represents the Annunciation with Christina as Mary and William Michael contributing to the figure of Gabriel. Christina found prolonged modelling tiring and did not become a professional model.

### James Collinson

**Documented fact:** Christina became engaged to Pre-Raphaelite painter James Collinson in 1848.

Collinson had converted from Anglicanism to Roman Catholicism. Christina initially rejected him on religious grounds; he returned to Anglicanism, after which she accepted him. In 1850 he returned to Catholicism and prepared for religious training. Christina ended the engagement or accepted its termination because she would not marry a Roman Catholic.

The engagement was emotionally consequential. It is reasonable to connect some contemporary poems with the relationship, but assigning particular poems as direct autobiography is often interpretive rather than documentary. “When I am dead, my dearest,” written in 1848, has frequently been read in that context.

### *The Germ* and Ellen Alleyn

In 1850 Christina published seven poems under the pseudonym **Ellen Alleyn** in *The Germ*, the Brotherhood’s short-lived journal:

- “Dream Land”;
- “An End”;
- “Song” (“Oh roses for the flush of youth”);
- “A Pause of Thought”;
- “A Testimony”;
- “Repining”;
- “Sweet Death.”

The pseudonym provided some separation from her family name while she entered print through her brothers’ circle.

### Early fiction

Around 1850 she wrote *Maude: A Story for Girls*, a novella about female authorship, religious self-scrutiny, vanity, and rivalry. It was not published until 1897 and should be distinguished from her lifetime publications.

In 1851–52 she contributed Italian poems and prose to the privately printed *The Bouquet from Marylebone Gardens*, including “Versi,” “L’Incognita,” and the unfinished epistolary fiction “Corrispondenza Famigliare.”

## Schools, Poverty, Frome, and the Death of Gabriele, 1851–1854

In 1851 the family left Charlotte Street for Camden Town. Christina and Frances attempted to operate a small day school. It failed to provide a durable solution.

In March 1853 mother and daughter moved to Fromefield, near Frome in Somerset, to establish another school. The venture lasted until February 1854. This was Christina’s only sustained residence outside London. She disliked the teaching enterprise and feared the governess’s dependence and social ambiguity.

The family then returned to London, living at Albany Street.

Gabriele Rossetti died on 26 April 1854. Christina’s religious and poetic preoccupation with death long preceded this loss, but her father’s decline and death deepened a family culture shaped by illness, economic insecurity, and mourning.

During the Crimean War Christina offered to join Florence Nightingale’s nursing enterprise but was not accepted. Her aunt Eliza Polidori did go to Scutari. Christina temporarily undertook some of Eliza’s parish visiting among poor and sick residents.

She submitted work to *Blackwood’s* and *Fraser’s* in 1854 without success. In a letter to William Edmondstoune Aytoun she insisted that poetry was for her “not a mechanism, but an impulse and a reality.” The failed submissions show that her later career was not an automatic consequence of family connections.

## Establishing a Career, 1855–1861

Christina continued to compose, date, copy, and revise poetry. Seventeen principal manuscript notebooks cover much of 1842–66. Six now catalogued as British Library Ashley MS 1364 contain extensive revisions by Christina and editorial marks by William and Dante Gabriel.

She wrote biographical entries on Italian writers and other subjects for the *Imperial Dictionary of Universal Biography* between 1857 and 1863. Her subjects included Dante, Petrarch, and other Italian cultural figures. In her Petrarch entry she repeated a family tradition that the Rossettis descended from Petrarch’s Laura. That genealogical claim is **unverified family lore**.

### St Mary Magdalene Penitentiary

In early 1859 Christina began serving as an associate at the **St Mary Magdalene Penitentiary**, also called the London Diocesan Penitentiary, at Highgate. It housed women described by Victorian reformers as “fallen,” including former prostitutes.

As “Sister Christina,” she wore a black uniform and veil and participated in religious instruction and institutional work. Sources commonly give her active association as 1859–70, although the frequency of attendance varied.

The institution combined welfare, discipline, domestic training, and a theology of repentance. It should not be romanticized as purely emancipatory: Victorian penitentiaries subjected residents to surveillance and moral regulation. There is nevertheless evidence that Rossetti regarded the women as persons capable of restoration rather than irredeemable social outcasts.

The connection between this work and *Goblin Market* is plausible but unresolved. Rossetti dated composition of the poem to 27 April 1859, near the beginning of her Highgate service. Some scholars interpret Laura’s compulsive consumption and Lizzie’s rescue as reflecting prostitution, addiction, sacramental redemption, or women’s mutual aid. Rossetti publicly described the work as a fairy tale and denied that it had a single elaborate allegorical scheme.

### John Brett

Painter John Brett drew or began a portrait of Christina around 1857–58. Later biographers proposed that Brett sought marriage and that “No, Thank You, John” answers him.

- **Documented:** Brett knew the Rossetti family and portrayed Christina.
- **Reported:** Violet Hunt claimed he proposed.
- **Unresolved:** no surviving proposal or explicit identification of Brett as the poem’s “John” establishes the story.

## *Goblin Market* and Literary Recognition, 1862–1865

Macmillan published *Goblin Market and Other Poems* in 1862, with designs by Dante Gabriel Rossetti. Its major contents included:

- “Goblin Market”;
- “In the Round Tower at Jhansi”;
- “Remember”;
- “After Death”;
- “Cousin Kate”;
- “A Birthday”;
- “An Apple-Gathering”;
- “The Convent Threshold”;
- “Up-Hill”;
- “When I am dead, my dearest.”

The volume established Christina as a major poet. Reviewers recognized technical control, intensity, compression, and unusual metrical effects. The title poem’s apparent simplicity, sensuous language, violence, commerce, and sisterly redemption created interpretive instability from the beginning.

John Ruskin, reading the work before publication through Dante Gabriel, praised its beauty and power but criticized its irregular metre and regarded parts as commercially unpublishable. Macmillan published it despite that judgment.

Dante Gabriel’s illustrations linked the volume materially to Pre-Raphaelitism. Christina’s poems, however, were not subsidiary captions to her brother’s art. Her treatments of women’s speech, renunciation, appetite, and devotional time often differed from the eroticized female imagery of male Pre-Raphaelite painting.

The book was a critical success but did not make her wealthy. Poetry generated limited revenue, and she continued within a family economy supported substantially by William.

## Charles Bagot Cayley and *The Prince’s Progress*, 1862–1869

### Charles Bagot Cayley

**Charles Bagot Cayley** (1823–1883) was a linguist, translator, Dante scholar, and former pupil of Gabriele Rossetti. He and Christina developed a close, enduring relationship in the early 1860s.

Cayley proposed marriage, probably in 1866. Christina refused because she concluded that he was not an orthodox Christian. William later wrote that she loved Cayley deeply and constantly. Their friendship continued, including visits, gifts, literary discussion, and games.

The relationship is documented; the exact emotional history can only partly be recovered. Later readings identify Cayley as the unnamed beloved behind “Monna Innominata.” William called that sequence intensely personal, but it remains a literary construction rather than a transparent diary.

### Second major volume

*The Prince’s Progress and Other Poems* appeared in 1866, again with designs by Dante Gabriel. It contained:

- “The Prince’s Progress”;
- “The Milking-Maid”;
- “Jessie Cameron”;
- “The Poor Ghost”;
- “Twice”;
- “A Royal Princess”;
- “The Lowest Room”;
- revised and collected shorter poems.

“The Prince’s Progress” reverses rescue-romance expectations: the prince delays through indulgence and reaches the bride only after her death. The poem has been read as criticism of male procrastination, failed courtship, and social structures that require women to wait.

In 1867 Rossetti published “Dante, an English Classic,” supporting Charles Cayley’s terza-rima translation of *The Divine Comedy*.

### Family strain

Dante Gabriel’s marriage to Elizabeth Siddal in 1860, Siddal’s death from an overdose of laudanum in 1862, and his later relationships complicated the family network. Christina did not leave a full public account of Siddal’s death or Gabriel’s sexual life.

The famous exhumation of Siddal’s grave in 1869, undertaken to recover the manuscript poems Dante Gabriel had buried with her, affected the family grave in which Christina would later be interred. There is no evidence that Christina directed the exhumation.

## Prose, Children’s Writing, and Devotional Publication, 1870–1876

### *Commonplace and Other Short Stories* (1870)

This collection brought together fiction addressing family duty, unequal affection, women’s restricted choices, conversion, and renunciation. Its contents included:

- “Commonplace”;
- “The Lost Titian”;
- “Vanna’s Twins”;
- “The Waves of This Troublesome World”;
- “Nick”;
- “Hero”;
- “A Safe Investment.”

The prose fiction never attained the standing of her poetry but is important evidence of her social observation and religious ethics.

### *Sing-Song* (1872)

*Sing-Song: A Nursery Rhyme Book*, illustrated by Arthur Hughes, appeared in 1872. Its rhymes concern infancy, play, poverty, hunger, disability, death, animals, and family affection. Their compact verbal music later attracted numerous composers.

A revised and enlarged edition appeared in 1893.

### Graves’ disease

In the early 1870s Rossetti became gravely ill and was diagnosed in 1872 with **Graves’ disease**, an autoimmune form of hyperthyroidism. Symptoms described in correspondence and biography included weakness, heart disturbance, swelling, altered appearance, and protruding eyes. She came close to death and endured long periods of incapacity.

Some earlier writers loosely called her illnesses angina or tuberculosis. Graves’ disease is the best-documented diagnosis for this period but does not necessarily explain her adolescent psychological crisis.

### *Annus Domini* and *Speaking Likenesses*

In 1874 she published:

- *Annus Domini: A Prayer for Each Day of the Year, Founded on a Text of Holy Scripture*, edited or prepared with clerical assistance from Henry W. Burrows;
- *Speaking Likenesses*, illustrated by Arthur Hughes.

*Speaking Likenesses* presents three girls entering disturbing fantasy worlds. Contemporary reviewers found it strange, violent, and less congenial than Lewis Carroll’s Alice books, to which it was inevitably compared.

### *Goblin Market, The Prince’s Progress, and Other Poems*

Macmillan consolidated her two principal poetry volumes in 1875. Later impressions and revisions followed, helping sustain her readership.

### Death of Maria

Maria Rossetti died in 1876. Christina had already lost her father and now lost her only sister, closest female intellectual companion, and fellow devotional writer.

In 1876 Christina and Frances established themselves at **30 Torrington Square**, Bloomsbury, Christina’s final long-term home.

## Scholarship, Activism, and Mature Poetry, 1877–1881

### Research and lectures

From 1878 to 1880 Rossetti attended lectures on Dante at University College London. She researched at the British Museum and contemplated or began several scholarly projects:

- a life of Ann Radcliffe, abandoned because she could not obtain adequate materials;
- possible biographies of Adelaide Anne Procter and Elizabeth Barrett Browning;
- work tracing Dante, Petrarch, and Boccaccio allusions for Alexander B. Grosart’s edition of Edmund Spenser, from which illness forced her to withdraw.

### Anti-vivisection activity

Rossetti became an active opponent of vivisection, influenced in part by her friend Caroline Gemmer and by her theology of creation.

**Documented activities include:**

- membership or cooperation with the Victoria Street Society;
- reading anti-vivisection publications;
- soliciting signatures for petitions and memorials;
- supporting meetings and fundraising bazaars;
- providing autographed copies of “A Word for the Dumb” for sale;
- asking acquaintances, including Frederic Shields, to sign petitions;
- protesting the proposed licensing of an institute connected with Pasteurian treatment;
- reportedly offering the Society for Promoting Christian Knowledge £20 to withdraw a publication she considered supportive of vivisection.

This was organized political-philanthropic work, not merely poetic sentiment.

### Protection of women and children

Rossetti supported efforts to protect girls from sexual exploitation, including the campaign associated with raising the age of consent. Her Highgate work also placed her within Victorian networks addressing prostitution. She did not, however, develop a systematic public political program.

### Women’s suffrage

Her position was conflicted but ultimately anti-suffrage in organized terms.

In correspondence with the suffragist poet Augusta Webster, Rossetti grounded her hesitation in Christian ideas of male headship. She nevertheless argued that if women’s interests could not otherwise be protected, female members of Parliament might be “right and reasonable,” and she specifically objected to excluding married women.

In 1889 she signed the women’s anti-suffrage appeal. Thus:

- **Documented:** she declined to endorse parliamentary suffrage and signed an anti-suffrage declaration.
- **Documented:** she also articulated arguments for women’s representation, criticized sexual double standards, and supported protective reforms.
- Labels such as “feminist” or “anti-feminist” describe later interpretive frameworks rather than a simple self-identification.

### *Seek and Find* (1879)

*Seek and Find: A Double Series of Short Studies of the Benedicite* combined devotional exposition, biblical interpretation, natural history, and moral reflection.

### *A Pageant and Other Poems* (1881)

This was her last major new lifetime poetry collection. It included:

- “The Months: A Pageant”;
- “Monna Innominata: A Sonnet of Sonnets”;
- “Later Life”;
- “The Thread of Life”;
- “Mirage”;
- “An Old-World Thicket”;
- “A Ballad of Boding”;
- “Passing Away”;
- “A Helpmeet for Him.”

“Monna Innominata” gives speech to the conventionally silent woman addressed by Dante or Petrarch. Its prose introduction invokes Dante’s Beatrice, Petrarch’s Laura, and Elizabeth Barrett Browning. The sequence is both a formal intervention in the sonnet tradition and, according to William, an intensely personal work.

### *Called to Be Saints* (1881)

*Called to Be Saints: The Minor Festivals Devotionally Studied* combines saints’ lives, scriptural exposition, natural imagery, and moral instruction.

---

## Dante Gabriel’s Final Illness and Death, 1881–1882

Dante Gabriel suffered depression, paranoia, deteriorating health, and dependence on chloral hydrate. Christina, Frances, William, and friends attempted to manage his illness. Christina’s relations with him included anxiety, religious disagreement, and continuing loyalty.

He died at Birchington-on-Sea, Kent, on 9 April 1882. Christina attended the funeral there. He was not buried in the Highgate family grave.

His death ended her longest artistic collaboration. She preserved memories of him but did not write a comprehensive public biography.

---

## Final Prose Period, 1882–1891

After 1882 Rossetti’s major publications were predominantly devotional prose interspersed with poetry.

### *Letter and Spirit* (1883)

*Letter and Spirit: Notes on the Commandments* examined the Decalogue and Christian conduct. It reveals both adherence to orthodox hierarchy and close attention to ethical intention.

### “Dante: The Poet Illustrated out of the Poem” (1884)

Published in *The Century*, this article used *The Divine Comedy* to reconstruct Dante’s character. It was the product of lifelong family scholarship and her University College lectures.

### *Time Flies* (1885)

*Time Flies: A Reading Diary* arranged prose meditations and poems by calendar date. It addresses mortality, seasons, animals, theology, memory, and ecological interdependence. “A Word for the Dumb,” associated with her anti-vivisection work, appeared in revised form.

### Deaths of Cayley and Frances

Charles Cayley died in 1883. He left Christina manuscripts and treasured possessions. She continued to oversee or assist with aspects of his literary remains.

Frances Rossetti died on 8 April 1886. Christina had lived with her mother for most of her life. Frances’s death left her at Torrington Square with attendants, visiting relatives, correspondents, and friends but without her lifelong domestic companion.

### *The Face of the Deep*

Rossetti worked for years on a commentary on Revelation. *The Face of the Deep: A Devotional Commentary on the Apocalypse* appeared in 1892. It combines prose exposition, prayers, and verse, and is her most substantial theological work.

### Literary standing

By the late nineteenth century she was widely regarded as one of the leading living English poets. After Elizabeth Barrett Browning’s death, reviewers sometimes identified Rossetti as her principal female successor.

After Alfred Tennyson died in 1892, newspapers and commentators mentioned Rossetti as a possible poet laureate. There was no formal appointment process producing a charge or rejection, and the laureateship went to Alfred Austin in 1896. Claims that Rossetti was officially offered or formally denied the office are unsupported.

---

## Final Illness, Death, Estate, and Burial, 1892–1895

In 1892 Rossetti was diagnosed with breast cancer. A mastectomy was performed at her Torrington Square home. The operation initially appeared successful, but the cancer recurred in 1893.

She endured increasing pain and disability while revising devotional verse. *Verses* appeared through the Society for Promoting Christian Knowledge in 1893, gathering and restructuring her religious poetry. The enlarged edition of *Sing-Song* also appeared that year.

Christina died at 30 Torrington Square on 29 December 1894, aged sixty-four.

Her funeral service was held at Christ Church, Woburn Square. She was buried on 2 January 1895 in the Rossetti family grave in the west side of Highgate Cemetery. The grave also contained Gabriele and Frances Rossetti and Elizabeth Siddal; William Michael was later buried there.

The tomb is now Grade II listed by Historic England.

No evidence indicates suicide, poisoning, criminal conduct, or medical malpractice. The documented cause was recurrent cancer.

## Residences, Travel, and Geographical Movements

Rossetti was overwhelmingly London-based. Claims that she “never travelled” are exaggerations, but she did not undertake the continental journeys common among affluent literary contemporaries.

Principal locations:

- **1830–early 1850s:** 38 and subsequently 50 Charlotte Street, Portland Place district, London.
- **1851–52:** Camden Town, London.
- **March 1853–February 1854:** Fromefield near Frome, Somerset, while attempting to run a school.
- **1854 onward:** Albany Street and related central London addresses.
- **Later addresses:** Euston Square and Endsleigh Gardens appear in correspondence and family chronology.
- **1876–1894:** 30 Torrington Square, Bloomsbury.
- **Highgate:** regular religious and charitable work at St Mary Magdalene Penitentiary; eventual burial.
- **Sussex and Kent:** holidays, convalescent visits, and family travel, including Brighton, Hastings, Bognor/Aldwick, and Dante Gabriel’s funeral at Birchington.
- **Holmer Green, Buckinghamshire:** childhood and family visits to the Polidori household.
- **Scotland:** visits associated with Dante Gabriel’s circle are reported in family chronologies, but Christina never established residence there.
- **Italy:** despite her Italian identity and scholarship, she did not make a documented adult journey to Italy.

There was no exile, imprisonment, or forced migration in her own life; exile belonged to her father’s and grandfather’s histories.

## Employment, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Employment and institutional relationships

- Home teaching and attempted school operation with Frances Rossetti.
- Contributor to the *Imperial Dictionary of Universal Biography*.
- Author for Macmillan.
- Author for Routledge.
- Author for the Society for Promoting Christian Knowledge.
- Contributor to *The Germ*, *The Athenaeum*, *Macmillan’s Magazine*, *The Century*, and religious or charitable publications.
- Associate at St Mary Magdalene Penitentiary.
- Participant in anti-vivisection organizations and petition campaigns.
- British Museum reader and independent literary researcher.
- Student-auditor at Dante lectures at University College London.

### Publishing arrangements

Her brothers, especially Dante Gabriel, helped her negotiate manuscript selection, design, and publication. Macmillan was her chief commercial poetry publisher. Dante Gabriel’s designs materially shaped her first two major volumes, but Christina retained control over wording and resisted some editorial interventions.

### Money and property

She was neither destitute throughout adulthood nor independently wealthy. Family illness placed the household under genuine economic pressure in the 1840s and 1850s. William Michael’s civil-service income later furnished stability.

Her books produced royalties, but Victorian poetry did not normally yield large fortunes. No substantial speculative investments, corporate holdings, patents, or business partnerships are documented.

She lived within rented or family-managed London households rather than building a property empire. Her will reportedly recognized debts of support to William.

## Religious, Philosophical, Political, and Artistic Development

### Religion

Rossetti’s religion developed from family Evangelicalism toward disciplined High Anglicanism. Its recurrent concepts included:

- original sin and personal unworthiness;
- incarnation and sacrament;
- renunciation;
- judgment and resurrection;
- the instability of worldly beauty;
- divine love as the completion or correction of human love;
- saints as ethical exemplars;
- creation as a community extending beyond humanity.

Her devotional writing was not a late retreat from “real” poetry. Religious poems occur from childhood onward, while sensuousness, desire, humor, and social observation persist inside the devotional work.

### Sexuality and renunciation

Rossetti did not leave an explicit sexual autobiography. Her poetry addresses female desire, bodily appetite, marriage, celibacy, rejection, and same-sex bonds between women.

Claims that she was lesbian, asexual, sexually repressed, or secretly sexually active are modern hypotheses. None is established by direct evidence. “Goblin Market” and “Monna Innominata” support queer and erotic readings as texts, but textual possibility does not determine biography.

### Gender

She accepted some Anglican teachings of male authority and refused organized suffrage. She also:

- criticized men who seduced and abandoned women;
- represented women as desiring subjects;
- emphasized women’s rescue of women;
- criticized the male artist’s repeated idealization of one female model in “In an Artist’s Studio”;
- supported protection of women and children;
- imagined spiritual equality beyond earthly gender hierarchy.

Her position is therefore internally complex rather than reducible to a modern political label.

### Social reform

Documented concerns included:

- prostitution and rehabilitation;
- protection of children and young women;
- opposition to slavery;
- opposition to cruelty to animals, hunting, and vivisection;
- parish assistance to poor and sick people.

### Pre-Raphaelitism

Rossetti shared the early movement’s medieval subjects, detailed natural imagery, symbolic realism, and concern with relations among word, image, and design. She was never a sworn member and developed an independent poetic style.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Frances Rossetti

Mother, teacher, religious guide, housemate, and primary emotional attachment. Christina dedicated poems to her and cared for her into old age.

### Maria Rossetti

Sister, educator, Dante scholar, and religious companion. Maria’s institutional vocation provided a model of women’s religious work that Christina approached but did not formally join.

### Dante Gabriel Rossetti

Brother, illustrator, editor, portraitist, artistic interlocutor, and source of emotional crisis. Their manuscripts document reciprocal criticism rather than one-way influence.

### William Michael Rossetti

Brother, financial supporter, editor, biographer, and archival custodian. His posthumous control helped establish Christina’s canon while limiting access to parts of her private history.

### James Collinson

Fiancé, 1848–50. The engagement ended over his return to Roman Catholicism.

### Charles Bagot Cayley

Close friend, suitor, Dante translator, and probable major emotional attachment. Christina refused marriage because of incompatible belief but maintained the relationship.

### John Brett

Pre-Raphaelite painter and possible rejected suitor. The proposal is reported but unproven.

### Henry William Burrows and Richard Frederick Littledale

Anglican clergy and spiritual advisers. Burrows connected Rossetti to charitable work and edited or assisted with *Annus Domini*.

### Arthur Hughes

Illustrator of *Sing-Song* and *Speaking Likenesses*.

### Alexander Macmillan and Frederick Startridge Ellis

Publishers and professional correspondents. Their exchanges document editing, illustration, pricing, and the practical construction of Rossetti’s books.

### Augusta Webster

Poet and suffrage advocate. Their correspondence records mutual literary regard and disagreement over votes for women.

### Caroline Gemmer

Friend and poet associated with animal-welfare advocacy; an influence on Rossetti’s anti-vivisection activity.

Artist, religious correspondent, and recipient of her appeals for anti-vivisection signatures.

### Lewis Carroll / Charles Lutwidge Dodgson

Friend of the family, photographer of Frances and Christina, correspondent, and admirer. Their surviving correspondence extends from 1882 to 1894. *Speaking Likenesses* was compared with Carroll’s Alice books, though it was not a collaboration.

Painter, poet, and member of the extended Pre-Raphaelite network. A later biography alleged a secret love relationship; the evidence is addressed below.

## Controversies, Allegations, and Contested Questions

### Alleged sexual abuse by Gabriele Rossetti

**Status: allegation or theory; unverified.**

Jan Marsh proposed that Christina may have experienced an attempted incestuous assault by her invalid father during adolescence. The theory draws on:

- her psychological and physical collapse;
- her proximity to her dependent father;
- recurring images of predatory fathers, bodily revulsion, secrets, guilt, and threatened women;
- later descriptions of religious anxiety.

No surviving letter, diary, witness statement, medical record, or direct autobiographical declaration reports sexual abuse. Marsh presented the argument as conjectural and impossible to prove.

The claim became prominent because it offered a unifying explanation for illness, renunciation, and imagery. Critics object that it converts literary symbols into biographical evidence and risks pathologizing religious poetry. The correct evidentiary status remains **unverified biographical hypothesis**.

### Alleged affair or concealed love for William Bell Scott

**Status: theory rejected by much later scholarship.**

Lona Mosk Packer’s 1963 biography argued that Rossetti’s mature love poetry encoded a long, clandestine attachment to the married painter William Bell Scott.

Evidence offered included poem dating, emotional language, meetings within the Rossetti circle, and Packer’s interpretation of gaps in the record.

Objections include:

- no surviving declaration by Christina or Scott;
- disputed chronology of the poems;
- alternative association with Collinson, Cayley, or deliberately non-autobiographical speakers;
- the speculative conversion of poetic voice into confession.

Georgina Battiscombe and later reviewers rejected the theory as inadequately supported. It remains historically important as a chapter in Rossetti biography, not an established affair.

### John Brett as the “John” of “No, Thank You, John”

**Status: unresolved attribution.**

The poem is a firm rejection of a persistent suitor. Brett knew and portrayed Rossetti, and Violet Hunt later claimed he proposed. No documentary identification survives. The title may use a generic name.

### Sexual meanings in *Goblin Market*

**Status: critical interpretation, not allegation about conduct.**

Major readings include:

- Christian temptation and redemption;
- Eucharistic sacrifice;
- prostitution and rehabilitation;
- addiction and recovery;
- commodity exchange and capitalist appetite;
- female sexuality;
- lesbian or queer desire;
- sisterhood and maternal transmission;
- children’s fairy tale;
- critique of male predation.

The erotic vocabulary is textual fact. Rossetti’s Highgate work supplies relevant social context. No single interpretation has documentary priority, and Rossetti resisted a comprehensive allegorical explanation.

### Alleged antisemitic coding of the goblins

**Status: disputed critical theory.**

Some critics argue that the goblin merchants reproduce nineteenth-century antisemitic stereotypes linking supposedly foreign male traders, bodily grotesquerie, sexual threat, and commerce. The poem does not identify the goblins as Jews, and its illustrations and text permit other folkloric, animal, economic, and demonic genealogies.

The issue concerns the cultural vocabulary available to a Victorian writer, not proof of organized antisemitic activity by Rossetti. No evidence found in this research establishes that she intended a representation of Jews.

### Political conservatism and feminism

**Documented conflict:** Rossetti rejected organized women’s suffrage and signed the 1889 anti-suffrage appeal while also articulating conditional support for women MPs and criticizing women’s political exclusion.

Later hagiographic accounts sometimes suppress the anti-suffrage record; some feminist accounts minimize her acceptance of male ecclesiastical hierarchy. Conversely, portrayals of her as simply reactionary neglect her reform work and gender-critical writing.

### Treatment of “fallen women”

Rossetti’s work at Highgate has been represented both as feminist solidarity and as participation in coercive Victorian moral discipline.

Both contexts matter. She worked directly with marginalized women and wrote against sexual double standards; she also participated in an institution organized around repentance, domestic labor, controlled residence, and religious supervision. Surviving sources do not justify depicting her either as a modern social worker or as an individual abuser.

### Control and destruction of papers

**Reported fact:** William Michael destroyed or withheld some of Christina’s diary material and correspondence after her death, partly to protect privacy and family reputation.

The exact quantity and content cannot be reconstructed. Biographical silence should therefore not automatically be treated as proof that nothing occurred, but neither does destruction license unsupported claims.

### Criminality, litigation, and corruption audit

The dedicated adversarial search combined Rossetti’s name with controversy, scandal, allegations, lawsuit, fraud, accusations, conspiracy, criticism, sexual abuse, financial misconduct, and recent-year terms.

No credible evidence was found that Christina Rossetti:

- committed or was charged with a crime;
- participated in fraud or corruption;
- engaged in money laundering;
- was sued in a significant reported case;
- held intelligence connections;
- participated in political violence;
- was investigated by government authorities;
- abused charitable residents;
- misappropriated funds.

The major negative or contested material consists instead of speculative sexual biography, disagreements about gender politics, institutional criticism of penitentiary work, and disputed cultural meanings in her poetry.

No significant new documentary scandal concerning Rossetti was identified in material published during 2024–26. Recent publications chiefly continue literary, ecological, gender, religious, and reception studies.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Biographical Mythmaking

### “The secluded, sexless saint”

**Story:** Rossetti renounced all ordinary life, lived as a near-nun, and wrote only sorrowful religious verse.

**Origins and circulation:** Early obituary reminiscences emphasized saintliness; William’s protective memoir and Mackenzie Bell’s authorized biography reinforced reserve and devotion.

**Evidence:** She was devout, unmarried, often ill, and increasingly homebound. Against the caricature, her letters show humor, literary business, friendships, political petitions, games, travel, and sustained interest in periodicals and public events.

**Effect:** The image preserved her devotional reputation but encouraged twentieth-century critics to treat her art as merely repressed autobiography.

### “Three broken engagements”

**Story:** Rossetti successively rejected Collinson, Brett, and Cayley.

**Evidence:** Collinson’s engagement and Cayley’s proposal are documented. Brett’s proposal is uncertain. Calling all three formal engagements overstates the evidence.

### “She wrote *Goblin Market* for prostitutes at Highgate”

**Story:** Rossetti composed or read the poem specifically for residents of the penitentiary.

**Evidence:** Its composition date coincides with the beginning of her work there, and prostitution is a defensible context. Direct documentation that it was written for or first read to the residents is lacking.

### “Laura was rescued from drug addiction”

This modern analogy sometimes connects the goblin fruit with laudanum and Elizabeth Siddal. Siddal died in 1862, whereas Rossetti dated *Goblin Market* to 1859. The poem therefore was not originally written in response to Siddal’s death. Addiction remains a later interpretive analogy.

### “Rossetti was denied the laureateship because she was a woman”

She was discussed as a possible successor to Tennyson, but no formal offer, official shortlist, or documented rejection has been established. The story symbolizes women’s exclusion from literary office but should not be narrated as a documented appointment dispute.

### Petrarchan ancestry

Rossetti wrote that she descended from Laura, Petrarch’s celebrated beloved. This appears as family tradition or literary self-positioning, not verified genealogy.

### The grave shared with Elizabeth Siddal

Christina was buried in the family grave containing Siddal. The sensational nocturnal exhumation story concerns Dante Gabriel’s recovery of manuscripts in 1869, not Christina’s burial. Later retellings sometimes blur the chronology or imply that Christina was involved.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### 1894–1920s

Obituaries and reminiscences emphasized religious purity, gentleness, suffering, and lyric gift. William’s editions expanded access to unpublished poetry while establishing an authorized family narrative.

Her devotional poetry and children’s verse remained popular. Musical settings carried her words beyond literary readership.

### Modernist decline and partial survival

The rise of Modernism diminished the reputation of many Victorian devotional poets. Rossetti remained in anthologies through short lyrics such as “Remember,” “Song,” and “Up-Hill,” but was sometimes classified as a minor poet, children’s writer, or melancholy “poetess.”

Virginia Woolf’s reassessment recognized the conflict between sensuous poetic perception and the command that beauty be renounced.

### Mid-century psychobiography

Biographers and critics increasingly treated the poems as evidence of frustrated love, repression, or psychological division. Packer’s William Bell Scott theory exemplified the search for a concealed romantic key.

### Feminist recovery

From the 1970s onward, “Goblin Market,” “In an Artist’s Studio,” “Monna Innominata,” “Cousin Kate,” and “The Convent Threshold” became central to feminist studies of:

- female authorship;
- the male gaze;
- sisterhood;
- sexual commerce;
- women’s desire;
- revision of male literary traditions.

This recovery substantially restored Rossetti to the Victorian canon.

### Religious and theological reconsideration

Later scholars argued that her Anglo-Catholic theology should not be reduced to neurosis or patriarchal repression. Her devotional prose is now studied as lay theology, biblical hermeneutics, feminist theology, and sacramental poetics.

### Ecology and animal ethics

Her close observation of plants, animals, seasons, and biblical creation has generated ecological readings. Anti-vivisection letters and “A Word for the Dumb” establish a practical as well as literary animal ethic.

### Children’s literature

*Sing-Song* remains important for its musical compression and refusal to exclude poverty, disability, bereavement, or death from children’s experience. *Speaking Likenesses* has been reconsidered as deliberately unsettling rather than merely a failed imitation of Lewis Carroll.

### Canonical status

Rossetti is now normally treated as a major Victorian poet rather than only the sister of Dante Gabriel. “Goblin Market” remains her most analyzed work, while the complete poems, prose fiction, and devotional writings receive increasing attention.

Her influence or importance has been discussed in relation to Gerard Manley Hopkins, Ford Madox Ford, Virginia Woolf, Elizabeth Jennings, Philip Larkin, and later women poets. Direct influence varies by author and should not always be inferred from resemblance.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are included. Mere contemporaneity, common residence in London, or burial in the same cemetery is excluded.

### Dante Gabriel Rossetti

**Evidentiary label: documented fact.**

Christina’s elder brother, portraitist, editor, illustrator, and literary collaborator. They shared the family household and the Pre-Raphaelite scene from the 1840s. Christina published in *The Germ*; Dante Gabriel designed *Goblin Market and Other Poems* and *The Prince’s Progress*. His annotations survive in her poetry notebooks. Christina modelled for his Marian paintings. She remained involved during his final illness and attended his 1882 funeral at Birchington.

### Dante Alighieri

**Evidentiary label: documented intellectual influence; no personal meeting possible.**

Dante was central to the Rossetti family’s scholarly identity. Christina read and wrote about *The Divine Comedy*, attended University College lectures, published “Dante, an English Classic” and “Dante: The Poet Illustrated out of the Poem,” and built “Monna Innominata” partly from Dantean literary conventions.

### John Ruskin

**Evidentiary label: documented professional and social connection.**

Ruskin belonged to the broader Pre-Raphaelite network and evaluated Christina’s manuscript before *Goblin Market* appeared. In his letter of 24 January 1861 to Dante Gabriel, he praised her “beauty and power” but criticized her irregular metre and deemed the work commercially unsuitable in its existing form. Christina also encountered Ruskin through family and artistic networks. No close independent friendship comparable to Ruskin’s relationships with the male painters is established.

### William Holman Hunt

**Evidentiary label: documented shared artistic and family network.**

Hunt co-founded the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood with Dante Gabriel and Millais and was closely acquainted with the Rossetti family. Christina participated in the movement’s literary circle and published in its journal. Hunt later wrote about the family environment. She was not his formal collaborator or student.

### Sir John Everett Millais

**Evidentiary label: documented shared Pre-Raphaelite network.**

Millais co-founded the Brotherhood with Christina’s brothers and Hunt. Christina’s contributions to *The Germ* and presence in the early circle placed them in the same documented artistic network. Evidence consulted does not establish a close bilateral friendship.

### William Morris

**Evidentiary label: reported literary acquaintance.**

Rossetti and Morris belonged to overlapping later Pre-Raphaelite literary networks. Scholarship describes them as only slightly or barely acquainted while identifying a literary dialogue between “Monna Innominata” and Morris’s “The Defence of Guenevere.” This is a supported shared-scene and textual connection, not evidence of close collaboration.

### Jane Morris

**Evidentiary label: reported shared family-artistic network.**

Jane Morris was central to Dante Gabriel’s later circle and therefore crossed the extended Rossetti network. The consulted evidence does not demonstrate a substantial independent friendship with Christina; the connection should not be overstated.

### Charles John Huffam Dickens

**Evidentiary label: documented indirect shared controversy.**

Dickens publicly attacked early Pre-Raphaelite art, particularly Millais’s *Christ in the House of His Parents*, at the period when Christina was publishing in *The Germ* and modelling for Dante Gabriel’s religious paintings. This places them on opposing sides of the same public artistic controversy, but no personal meeting or correspondence has been established.

### William Blake

**Evidentiary label: documented family and artistic influence; degree of direct influence unresolved.**

The Rossetti family participated in the nineteenth-century recovery of Blake: William Michael and Dante Gabriel handled and wrote about Blake material, and Blake’s compressed visionary and devotional modes formed part of the family’s artistic environment. Critical studies discuss affinities with Christina. No direct personal connection was possible, and particular poem-to-poem influence requires case-specific proof.

### Jesus of Nazareth

**Evidentiary label: documented religious and textual influence, not a historical personal meeting.**

Christina’s faith, poetry, charitable work, prayers, and biblical commentaries were explicitly Christ-centered. This is an acknowledged intellectual and religious connection within the atlas’s broad definition of documented influence, not a claim of historical encounter.

### William Butler Yeats

**Evidentiary label: documented posthumous reception connection.**

Yeats admired and anthologized Victorian and Pre-Raphaelite poetry and participated in the afterlife of the Rossetti tradition. No meeting is possible to establish from the consulted record as Rossetti died in 1894 and their direct contact is not documented here; the connection is one of reception rather than personal association.

### Other roster names

No reliable evidence was found for a personal meeting, correspondence, collaboration, family tie, patronage, rivalry, or explicitly acknowledged influence linking Christina Rossetti with the great majority of the 896-name atlas roster. Shared Victorian nationality, London residence, Christian subject matter, Highgate burial, Pre-Raphaelite interest, or later comparison alone was not treated as a connection.

## Chronology

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 5 Dec. 1830 | Born at 38 Charlotte Street, London |
| 1831 | Gabriele Rossetti appointed professor of Italian at King’s College London |
| 1842 | Writes “To My Mother on the Anniversary of Her Birth”; begins sustained dated composition |
| 20 May 1843 | Rossetti siblings begin *The Hodge-Podge* |
| c. 1843 | Begins worshipping at Christ Church, Albany Street |
| 1843–45 | Father’s health and eyesight collapse; family financial crisis deepens |
| c. 1845–47 | Serious adolescent physical and psychological illness |
| 1847 | Gaetano Polidori privately prints *Verses* |
| 1848 | Engaged to James Collinson; Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood founded |
| 1848–50 | Models for Dante Gabriel’s early religious paintings |
| 1850 | Publishes as Ellen Alleyn in *The Germ*; engagement to Collinson ends; writes *Maude* around this period |
| 1851 | Family moves to Camden Town; Christina and Frances attempt a day school |
| 1851–52 | Work appears in *The Bouquet from Marylebone Gardens* |
| Mar. 1853–Feb. 1854 | Lives near Frome, Somerset, during second school venture |
| 26 Apr. 1854 | Gabriele Rossetti dies |
| 1854 | Rejected for Crimean nursing service; performs parish relief work |
| 1857–63 | Contributes to *Imperial Dictionary of Universal Biography* |
| 1858 | John Brett portrait period; alleged proposal remains unproven |
| Early 1859 | Begins work at St Mary Magdalene Penitentiary |
| 27 Apr. 1859 | Dated composition of *Goblin Market* |
| 1860 | Writes “No, Thank You, John” |
| 1862 | *Goblin Market and Other Poems* published |
| c. 1862–66 | Relationship with Charles Cayley becomes a central emotional attachment |
| 1866 | *The Prince’s Progress and Other Poems*; refuses Cayley’s proposal |
| 1867 | Publishes “Dante, an English Classic” |
| 1870 | *Commonplace and Other Short Stories* |
| 1871 | Maria publishes *A Shadow of Dante* |
| 1872 | *Sing-Song*; Christina diagnosed with Graves’ disease |
| 1873 | Maria enters All Saints sisterhood |
| 1874 | *Annus Domini* and *Speaking Likenesses* |
| 1875 | Consolidated *Goblin Market, The Prince’s Progress, and Other Poems* |
| 1876 | Maria dies; Christina and Frances settle at 30 Torrington Square |
| 1878–80 | Attends Dante lectures at University College London |
| 1878–90s | Sustained anti-vivisection petitioning and fundraising |
| 1879 | *Seek and Find* |
| 1881 | *A Pageant and Other Poems* and *Called to Be Saints* |
| 9 Apr. 1882 | Dante Gabriel dies at Birchington |
| 1883 | *Letter and Spirit*; Charles Cayley dies |
| Feb. 1884 | Publishes “Dante: The Poet Illustrated out of the Poem” |
| 1885 | *Time Flies* |
| 8 Apr. 1886 | Frances Rossetti dies |
| 1889 | Signs women’s anti-suffrage appeal |
| 1890–91 | Revised editions of *Poems* |
| 1892 | *The Face of the Deep*; diagnosed with breast cancer and undergoes mastectomy |
| 1893 | Cancer recurs; *Verses* and enlarged *Sing-Song* published |
| 29 Dec. 1894 | Dies at 30 Torrington Square |
| 2 Jan. 1895 | Buried at Highgate Cemetery |
| 1896 | William Michael publishes *New Poems* |
| 1897 | *Maude* published posthumously |
| 1904 | William publishes *The Poetical Works* with memoir |
| 1908 | William publishes *The Family Letters* |
| 1979–90 | Crump’s three-volume variorum *Complete Poems* published |
| 1990s onward | Major feminist, theological, bibliographical, and ecological reassessment |
| 2006 | Digital edition of Harrison’s four-volume *Letters* established by University of Virginia Press |
