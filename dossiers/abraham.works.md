## Works by Abraham

### Altars and ritual assemblages

- **Undated; first in the Genesis sequence:** Altar at Shechem, by the terebinth of Moreh. An altar attributed to Abram after the divine appearance; no physical remains or independent date can be connected securely with it ([Genesis 12:6–7](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.12.6-7)).

- **Undated; second in the Genesis sequence:** Altar between Bethel and Ai. A cultic structure beside Abram’s pitched tent, revisited in Genesis 13:3–4; the narrative calls it an altar he had made, but supplies neither dimensions nor materials ([Genesis 12:8](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.12.8), [13:3–4](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.13.3-4)).

- **Undated; third in the Genesis sequence:** Altar at the terebinths of Mamre near Hebron. A cultic structure built after Abram settled there; its identification with any surviving archaeological feature is unsupported by the text ([Genesis 13:18](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.13.18)).

- **Undated; late in the Genesis sequence:** Altar and arranged wood at the binding of Isaac. This is a temporary ritual assemblage rather than simply a building: Abraham constructs an altar, arranges its fuel, binds Isaac, and places him upon it, but the intended sacrifice is stopped ([Genesis 22:9–13](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.22.9-13)). Its traditional location in “the land of Moriah” is not independently identifiable from the narrative.

### Encampments

- **Undated:** Tent installation between Bethel and Ai. Genesis says Abram pitched his tent there; this is a mobile habitation and spatial installation, not a permanent architectural work, and the same location later serves as a point of return ([Genesis 12:8](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.12.8), [13:3](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.13.3)).

- **Undated:** Encampment at the terebinths of Mamre. Genesis describes Abram moving or pitching his tent before settling at Mamre and building an altar; it resists treatment as a discrete surviving work because neither the tent nor the duration and boundaries of the camp are specified ([Genesis 13:18](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.13.18)).

### Wells and cultivated landscape

- **Undated:** Well at Beer-sheba. Abraham claims to have dug this well, although Genesis 26 attributes the wells from “the days of Abraham” to his servants; its making is therefore an Abrahamic household project rather than securely his individual labor ([Genesis 21:25–31](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.21.25-31), [26:15–18](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.26.15-18)). The narrative gives no uncontested individual name for this particular well: *Beer-sheba* names the place and is explained through overlapping traditions about an oath and seven ewe-lambs.

- **Undated:** Wells dug in Abraham’s lifetime. Genesis 26 remembers a plurality of wells made by Abraham’s servants, subsequently stopped by the Philistines and reopened and renamed by Isaac; their original number, locations, and Abrahamic names are not preserved ([Genesis 26:15–18](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.26.15-18)). This is a collective household infrastructure project, not a catalogue of individually recoverable works.

- **Undated:** Tamarisk at Beer-sheba. A planted tree or grove associated with Abraham’s invocation of the deity; translations differ between a singular tamarisk and a more general planting, so its exact botanical and spatial form remains uncertain ([Genesis 21:33](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.21.33)).

### Food and hospitality

- **Undated:** Meal for the three visitors at Mamre. A collaborative hospitality production directed by Abraham: Sarah makes cakes, a servant prepares the calf Abraham selects, and Abraham supplies and serves curds, milk, and meat ([Genesis 18:1–8](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.18.1-8)). It should not be reduced to Abraham’s sole handiwork.

- **Undated:** Feast for Isaac’s weaning. Genesis attributes a “great feast” to Abraham but gives neither a menu nor a fixed ceremonial title; it is an event made for an occasion rather than a durable object ([Genesis 21:8](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.21.8)).

### Covenants, settlements, and performative acts

- **Undated:** Covenant with Abimelech at Beer-sheba. A negotiated agreement enacted through exchanged livestock, seven ewe-lambs set apart as testimony concerning the well, and mutual oaths ([Genesis 21:22–32](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.21.22-32)). It is closest to a manifesto in the vocabulary proposed here—a performative arrangement that creates obligations—but it is an oral and ritual act, not a text authored by Abraham.

- **Undated:** Household circumcision under the covenant. Abraham circumcises himself, Ishmael, and every male of his household on the same day in obedience to the divine protocol of Genesis 17 ([Genesis 17:9–14](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.17.9-14), [17:23–27](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.17.23-27)). These bodily modifications and the instituted household practice resist being called “works”; their inclusion records Abraham’s attributed agency without treating human bodies as art objects.

- **Undated:** Naming of the binding site as “YHWH-yireh.” A place-making utterance following the interrupted sacrifice, conventionally rendered “The LORD will provide” or “The LORD will see”; the Hebrew permits more than one translation ([Genesis 22:14](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.22.14)). This is a performative toponym rather than a material construction.

- **Undated:** Burial holding at Machpelah. Abraham does not build the cave or field: he makes them into a legally held ancestral burial property through a witnessed purchase from Ephron and then buries Sarah there ([Genesis 23:3–20](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.23.3-20)). It is therefore an acquired and socially constituted place, not an authored structure.

### Attributed progeny

- **Undated:** Ishmael. Genesis attributes Ishmael’s begetting to Abram and Hagar, while emphasizing Sarah’s role in arranging the union; a person is a creation in the biological sense but emphatically not an *obra* or possession ([Genesis 16:1–16](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.16.1-16)).

- **Undated:** Isaac. The narrative attributes Isaac to Abraham and Sarah while presenting his conception and birth as fulfillment of divine action; he is consequently a manifestation within the story, not a “work” made by Abraham alone ([Genesis 17:15–21](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.17.15-21), [21:1–7](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.21.1-7)).

- **Undated:** Zimran, Jokshan, Medan, Midian, Ishbak, and Shuah. Genesis lists these sons of Abraham and Keturah genealogically but supplies no individual birth dates or accounts of their making; as persons and lineage ancestors, they resist classification as works ([Genesis 25:1–4](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.25.1-4)).

### Sources

- [Genesis 11:26–25:18, Sefaria Library](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.11.26-25.18), Hebrew text with English translation. This Abraham cycle is the primary surviving catalogue of things and acts attributed to Abraham.

- [Genesis 26, Sefaria Library](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.26), especially verses 15–18, preserving the retrospective attribution of Abraham-era wells to his servants.

- Ronald Hendel, [*Remembering Abraham: Culture, Memory, and History in the Hebrew Bible*](https://academic.oup.com/book/10720) (Oxford University Press, 2005). Hendel treats the patriarchal narratives as a composite literary memory containing historical tradition, folklore, cultural self-definition, and multiple source layers; accordingly, the entries above report Genesis’s attributions rather than presenting the proposed c. 1813–1638 BC lifespan or any individual work-date as historically established.

- Ronald Hendel, [“Remembering Abraham”](https://academic.oup.com/book/10720/chapter/158789824), in the same volume. This study stresses the plurality and contradictions produced by the Abraham tradition’s complex literary history.

- Tremper Longman III, [“Book of Genesis,” *Oxford Bibliographies in Biblical Studies*](https://academic.oup.com/reference/62341/reference-article-abstract/554100455) (2010). Its survey describes Genesis as material shaped by multiple editors from oral traditions, folklore, genealogies, and ancestral narratives, and identifies the formation and historicity of those narratives as disputed.

- John J. Collins, [“Did It All Happen?”](https://academic.oup.com/book/26408/chapter-abstract/194773399), in *Introducing the Old Testament* (Fortress Press, 2014). Used for the methodological caution against converting Genesis’s internal chronology into invented absolute dates.
