# Pantaleon of Nicomedia: Research Dossier

## Scope and Evidentiary Method

Pantaleon—more commonly Panteleimon in Eastern Christianity—is an early-fourth-century martyr whose cult is considerably better documented than his biography.

The surviving evidence falls into three categories:

1. **Late-antique cult evidence:** calendars, martyrologies, references to shrines, and Procopius’s sixth-century record of Justinianic building activity. These support the existence of an established martyr-cult associated with Nicomedia.
2. **Hagiographic narratives:** Greek passions and their Latin, Coptic, Syriac, Arabic, Armenian, Georgian, and Slavonic descendants. These provide nearly all purported biographical detail but are later than the alleged events and extensively shaped by literary convention.
3. **Medieval and modern devotional tradition:** relic histories, miracle collections, liturgical texts, icons, churches, patronages, and local folklore.

No securely contemporary document naming Pantaleon has been identified. Eusebius and Lactantius describe the Diocletianic persecution at Nicomedia but do not name him. Consequently, the dossier distinguishes the plausible historical core from the later narrative.

### The name problem

The passion makes the two names part of the story. He is supposedly born **Pantoleon** and receives **Panteleimon** from a heavenly voice immediately before death because he will show mercy to everyone who invokes him. This is almost certainly an **etiological legend**: a story constructed to explain an already existing name and the saint’s healing function.

The early forms may also represent linguistic variation rather than a historical change of name. Medieval writers and modern churches commonly use the forms interchangeably.

## Historical Setting: Nicomedia and the Great Persecution

Nicomedia was Diocletian’s principal eastern residence and an administrative center of the tetrarchic Roman Empire. The Great Persecution began there in February 303:

- An imperial edict ordered Christian churches demolished, scriptures surrendered, and Christians deprived of legal privileges.
- The principal Nicomedian church was destroyed.
- Subsequent edicts ordered clergy imprisoned and subjected Christians to sacrifice tests.
- Fires in the imperial palace intensified official suspicion and repression.
- Eusebius reports executions and severe punishments at Nicomedia but does not include Pantaleon among his named victims.

This setting makes the execution of a Christian at Nicomedia in 303–305 historically plausible. It does not authenticate the individual incidents in Pantaleon’s passion.

Hagiographies frequently assign the persecution to **Maximian**. Some sources mean Galerius—whose full imperial name included *Maximianus*—while others appear to mean Diocletian’s western colleague Maximian. Later retellings alternately name Diocletian, Maximian, Maximinus, or Galerius. The instability is evidence against treating the courtroom narrative as a reliable transcript.

## Family and Ancestry

Everything in this section comes from hagiographic tradition.

### Parents

- **Father:** Eustorgius/Eustorgios, described as a wealthy, prominent, noble, or senatorial pagan of Nicomedia.
- **Mother:** Eubula/Euboule, described as a Christian who tried to educate her son in Christianity.
- **Religious division:** The parents’ opposing religions form the narrative background to Pantoleon’s lapse and reconversion.
- **Mother’s death:** Eubula reportedly died while he was still young, after which his father directed his pagan education.
- **Father’s conversion:** After witnessing Pantoleon restore a blind man’s sight through Christ, Eustorgius supposedly accepted baptism and died shortly afterward.

No inscription, civil record, contemporary martyr act, or independent late-antique source confirms either parent. Eubula later entered saint calendars in her own right, sometimes commemorated on 30 March, but her identity remains dependent upon her son’s legend.

### Siblings, spouse, and descendants

No tradition reliably records siblings, marriage, sexual partners, or children. The passions portray Pantaleon as an unmarried young man. Claims of noble or senatorial ancestry cannot be verified.

### Property and inheritance

The passion says Pantaleon inherited his father’s wealth and:

- emancipated enslaved people;
- distributed money to the poor;
- treated patients without payment;
- visited prisoners and Christians in hiding.

These actions establish him literarily as an “unmercenary healer.” No documentary evidence survives for his property, inheritance, dependents, or charitable expenditures.

---

## Childhood and Early Life

### Hagiographic account

Pantoleon was supposedly born into a wealthy Nicomedian household. His Christian mother instructed him in the faith, but her early death interrupted that formation. His pagan father then sent him to a non-Christian school.

Some modern devotional works assign 275 as his birth year. That date apparently results from calculating a conventional age for a martyr allegedly killed around 303–305; it does not appear in contemporary documentation.

### Historical assessment

No event from his childhood is independently recoverable. The mixed-faith household, death of the Christian mother, pagan schooling, and youthful apostasy form a recognizable conversion narrative. They may preserve family memory, but present evidence cannot distinguish memory from literary construction.

---

## Education and Formation

### Secular education

The passions say Pantoleon completed a classical or pagan education and then studied medicine under **Euphrosynus/Euphrosynos**, characterized as Nicomedia’s leading physician.

Euphrosynus reportedly introduced the gifted student at court, where the emperor intended to make him a palace physician. Versions differ over whether he served Diocletian, Maximian, Galerius, Maximinus, or an emperor described ambiguously as “Maximianus.”

**Evidentiary status:** hagiographic tradition. Neither Euphrosynus nor Pantoleon’s court appointment is independently documented.

### Christian mentor: Hermolaus

A Christian priest named **Hermolaus** supposedly lived in hiding with **Hermippus** and **Hermocrates** after a massacre of Nicomedian Christians. Watching Pantoleon walk past his refuge, Hermolaus invited him inside and contrasted the limited art of Hippocrates, Galen, and Asclepius with Christ’s power over body and soul.

Pantoleon continued visiting him. Hermolaus became his catechist and eventually baptized him.

The tradition’s opposition between paid pagan medicine and miraculous Christian healing is a standard feature of literature about the physician-saints Cosmas and Damian and other *anargyroi*. This does not prove that the persons are fictional, but it shows that their biographies were reshaped around a recognized saintly type.

### Formative influences

Within the tradition, his principal influences were:

- his mother’s Christianity;
- Greek and Roman medical training;
- Hermolaus’s catechesis;
- biblical healing narratives;
- the example of persecuted Christians;
- a miracle involving a dead child and snake.

No medical writings, prescriptions, instruments, case notes, or institutional records survive under Pantaleon’s name.

## Chronological Life History

## c. 275–290s: Birth and upbringing

**Traditional account:** Pantoleon was born at Nicomedia to Eustorgius and Eubula. His mother gave him an initial Christian formation but died during his childhood. His father raised him as a pagan.

**Historical assessment:** birthplace and association with Nicomedia are consistent across the cult, but exact date, parents, wealth, and childhood religion are unverified.

## Late 290s–early 300s: Medical training

Pantoleon supposedly studied with Euphrosynus and became so proficient that he attracted imperial attention. Later texts turn this into a formal post as physician to the emperor.

The story is possible in general terms: Nicomedia had an imperial court and required medical practitioners. No contemporary source establishes that Pantoleon belonged to it.

## c. 303: Encounter with Hermolaus

After the outbreak of persecution, Hermolaus, Hermippus, and Hermocrates allegedly survived a mass killing and lived clandestinely. Hermolaus recognized Pantoleon’s promise, instructed him, and argued that Christ was the supreme healer.

The claim that 20,000 Christians were burned in a Nicomedian church is part of later ecclesiastical tradition and should not be treated as a secure numerical report. Contemporary sources confirm persecution and executions but not that figure.

## c. 303–305: Snake-and-child miracle and baptism

The passion says Pantoleon found a child killed by a venomous snake. He prayed that Christ would revive the child and kill the snake, promising to accept baptism if the prayer succeeded. Both events occurred, after which Hermolaus baptized him.

**Status:** miracle legend. It supplies a dramatic experimental conversion: Pantoleon tests the efficacy of Christian power and moves from medical knowledge to miraculous healing.

## Conversion of his father

Pantoleon destroyed household idols or argued repeatedly against them. He then encountered a blind man whom ordinary physicians had failed to cure. Pantoleon invoked Christ and restored the man’s sight. Eustorgius and the healed man accepted baptism.

After Eustorgius’s death, Pantoleon inherited the estate, freed enslaved people, distributed his property, aided prisoners, and practiced medicine without fees.

**Status:** hagiographic claim. The healed blind man later becomes a witness in the trial narrative.

## Medical practice and conflict with other physicians

The passions say patients abandoned other physicians for Pantoleon because he healed freely and successfully. Envious colleagues denounced him to the emperor as a Christian.

This episode has several functions:

- explaining his arrest;
- depicting economic conflict between fee-taking physicians and the unmercenary saint;
- identifying professional jealousy as an agent of persecution;
- asserting Christian healing’s superiority to pagan medicine.

No names are preserved for the rival doctors. Their denunciation is undocumented outside the passion.

## Arrest and healing contest

Pantoleon was allegedly brought before the emperor. He openly confessed Christianity and proposed a test involving an incurably ill, paralyzed, or bedridden man:

1. pagan priests invoked their gods without effect;
2. Pantoleon prayed in Christ’s name;
3. the patient stood and walked;
4. some witnesses converted;
5. pagan priests demanded punishment.

The healed blind man was also examined and refused to deny what had happened. In some versions he too was executed.

**Status:** miracle and trial legend, not a surviving judicial record.

## Tortures

The passion accumulates ordeals in escalating sequence. Variants include:

- suspension on a rack;
- tearing with iron claws;
- burning with torches or heated plates;
- immersion in boiling lead;
- casting into the sea with a stone tied to him;
- exposure to wild animals;
- binding to a wheel fitted with blades;
- attempted burning in a furnace or pyre;
- scourging and imprisonment.

Christ appears repeatedly—sometimes in Hermolaus’s form—to heal or comfort him. The lead becomes cool, the sea releases him, beasts become docile, the wheel breaks, and the fire fails.

**Historical assessment:** these are conventional late-antique and medieval passion motifs. The precise sequence is not credible as documentary reportage. The shorter Roman martyrological notice retains burning with torches and death by sword but does not validate the fuller narrative.

## Arrest of Hermolaus, Hermippus, and Hermocrates

When asked who instructed him, Pantoleon allegedly identified Hermolaus. Imperial agents arrested the priest and his companions. They refused sacrifice and were beheaded after their steadfastness caused idols to fall during an earthquake.

**Status:** hagiographic claim. The three men possess their own cult and feast on 26 July, but their biographies are entwined with Pantaleon’s passion.

## Final sentence and execution

Pantoleon was condemned to death by the sword and taken outside Nicomedia. He was tied to an olive tree. The first executioner’s sword supposedly softened like wax or bent against his neck. The executioners fell at his feet, converted, and begged forgiveness.

Pantoleon prayed for everyone who would invoke him. A heavenly voice granted the request, changed his name to Panteleimon, and summoned him. He then ordered the reluctant executioners to complete their task.

At the beheading:

- blood and milk flowed from the wound;
- the olive tree immediately flowered and produced fruit;
- soldiers tried to burn the body, but fire would not consume it;
- Christians recovered and buried it.

The Latin passion places the grave approximately one stadium outside Nicomedia on the property of a scholasticus named **Adamantius**.

**Historical core:** death by execution at Nicomedia during the persecution of c. 303–305 is plausible.

**Legendary elaboration:** the failed sword, heavenly renaming, milk, fruiting olive, incorrupt body, and Adamantius episode belong to the passion tradition and lack contemporary corroboration.

## Death

### Date

The liturgical date is **27 July**, although some early Western martyrological traditions used 28 July. The year varies:

- 303;
- 304;
- 305.

The evidence does not permit a precise choice. “c. 303–305” is the safest formulation.

### Executing authority

Competing versions name:

- Diocletian;
- Maximian;
- Galerius, whose imperial titulature included Maximianus;
- Maximinus.

Because Nicomedia was Diocletian’s eastern residence and Galerius was active in the eastern tetrarchy, either could have entered an authentic local memory. The surviving passion is too late and unstable to identify the presiding ruler.

### Cause

Beheading is the consistent traditional cause of death. Even this is transmitted through martyrological and hagiographic sources rather than a contemporary execution record.

### Burial

The passion places his burial outside Nicomedia on Adamantius’s estate. No securely identified tomb or archaeological inscription has authenticated that location.

## Historical Attestation and Source Criticism

### Martyrologium Hieronymianum

The *Martyrologium Hieronymianum*, compiled in late antiquity and transmitted through early-medieval manuscripts, commemorates a Pantaleon at Nicomedia around 27–28 July. Its entry is much earlier than the surviving elaborate lives.

This establishes an early feast and location, not the full biography.

### Theodoret of Cyrrhus

Pantaleon has sometimes been identified among martyrs whose cult was known to Theodoret in the fifth century. Modern reference works caution that his name is absent from the most trustworthy manuscripts of *Graecarum affectionum curatio*. The Theodoret citation should therefore be labeled **textually disputed**, not decisive proof.

### Procopius of Caesarea

Procopius’s *Buildings* records Justinianic construction or restoration connected with Saint Pantaleon, including a shrine at Nicomedia and institutions elsewhere dedicated to him. This is strong evidence that the cult was geographically established by the sixth century, approximately 250 years after the supposed martyrdom.

Procopius does not supply a contemporary biography or verify the passion’s miracles.

### Greek passion tradition

The principal pre-Metaphrastic passion belongs to BHG 1412z–1413h and survives in approximately fifty Greek manuscripts and multiple recensions. Modern textual scholarship dates the developed tradition broadly to the fifth–sixth centuries or later, rather than to 303–305.

Symeon Metaphrastes produced or transmitted a tenth-century redaction, BHG 1414. Additional Greek encomia and miracle narratives carry BHG numbers through approximately 1418c.

### Oriental and Latin traditions

The dossier spread into:

- Latin: BHL 6429–6442 and related entries;
- Coptic;
- Syriac;
- Arabic;
- Armenian;
- Georgian;
- Church Slavonic.

Variation between these traditions—including rulers’ names, torture sequences, supporting martyrs, and miracle details—documents the expansion of the legend.

### Minimum historical reconstruction

The narrowest responsible reconstruction is:

- A Christian martyr named Pantaleon/Panteleimon was associated with Nicomedia.
- His commemoration fell on or near 27 July.
- His cult existed by late antiquity and was firmly established by the sixth century.
- He came to be remembered as a physician and healer.
- He was probably linked retrospectively or genuinely to the Diocletianic persecution.

Nothing more specific can presently be established with equivalent confidence.

## Profession, Institutions, Technologies, and Financial Interests

### Medicine

Pantaleon is consistently represented as a physician. He became one of the principal Christian physician-saints and a member of the Eastern group called the Holy Unmercenary Healers.

No evidence identifies:

- his medical school;
- an actual court salary;
- medical techniques;
- drugs or surgical procedures;
- professional guild membership;
- patients;
- licenses;
- surviving instruments;
- writings or inventions.

His iconographic pharmaceutical box, medicine spoon, spatula, or scalpel expresses his saintly category rather than proving possession of a particular artifact.

### Charitable economic model

The hagiography contrasts two medical economies:

- rivals who accept fees and protect professional income;
- Pantaleon, who heals without payment and distributes inherited wealth.

The alleged denunciation by jealous physicians may reflect real tensions around charity and patronage, but it is principally a literary explanation of persecution.

### Businesses, patents, and investments

None are documented. Claims about inherited property and enslaved household members occur only in the passion.

## Religious and Intellectual Development

The hagiographic progression is:

1. childhood Christian instruction;
2. loss of his mother and drift toward paganism;
3. classical and medical education;
4. encounter with Hermolaus;
5. testing Christ’s power through a miracle;
6. baptism;
7. integration of medicine with Christian charity;
8. public confession;
9. martyrdom;
10. posthumous role as heavenly physician.

The narrative does not reject medicine absolutely. Rather, it subordinates human medical skill to divine healing and condemns medicine motivated by profit or pagan cult. Later Orthodox devotion invokes Panteleimon alongside physicians and in services for the sick.

No philosophical, theological, or medical treatise by Pantaleon survives.

## Political Activity, Imprisonment, and State Violence

Pantaleon is not credited with a political program, office, faction, or conspiracy. His refusal to sacrifice would nevertheless have been treated as defiance of imperial religious policy.

The passion reports:

- denunciation to the emperor;
- arrest;
- interrogation;
- imprisonment;
- repeated torture;
- execution;
- execution of associates;
- attempted destruction of his remains.

These episodes reflect the known coercive environment of the Great Persecution, but no trial transcript, warrant, sentence, or administrative dossier survives. The speeches attributed to Pantaleon and the emperor are literary compositions.

## Controversies, Accusations, and Adversarial Findings

### Historical reliability of the passion

**Critical finding:** The detailed life is contained in late, miracle-saturated texts and is not contemporary testimony.

The principal problems are:

- no mention by Eusebius despite his extensive account of Nicomedian persecution;
- uncertainty over the emperor’s identity;
- formulaic tortures shared with other martyr legends;
- professional-jealousy motifs shared with physician-saint literature;
- an etiological explanation of the name Panteleimon;
- miraculous survivals repeated until voluntary death;
- multiple linguistic recensions with changing details.

This does not disprove that a martyr underlay the cult. It prevents the passion from being used as straightforward biography.

### Alleged denunciation by rival physicians

**Label:** hagiographic allegation.

The passions accuse unnamed physicians of reporting Pantaleon because his free cures deprived them of patients and income. There is no independent evidence, named defendant, investigation, or judicial finding. The episode may encode criticism of commercial medicine.

### Identification of the persecuting emperor

**Label:** unresolved textual contradiction.

Sources variously name Maximian, Diocletian, Galerius/Maximianus, or Maximinus. This is not a minor spelling issue: Maximian ordinarily ruled in the West, whereas Diocletian and Galerius were associated with Nicomedia. Some confusion may arise from Galerius’s imperial name, but the exact authority cannot be recovered.

### Relic multiplication and incompatible bodily claims

Relics identified as Pantaleon’s have been claimed at or associated with:

- Nicomedia;
- Constantinople;
- the Monastery of Saint Panteleimon on Mount Athos, which claims his head;
- Ravello;
- Madrid;
- Cologne;
- Lyon;
- Paris/Saint-Denis;
- Venice;
- Rome;
- Bari;
- Naples;
- Gorno Nerezi;
- monasteries in Russia, the Balkans, Armenia, and elsewhere.

These claims generally lack continuous provenance back to fourth-century Nicomedia. Multiple churches claim substantial portions, and popular lists sometimes assign the same category of relic—especially the head—to different locations.

**Finding:** veneration and custody are documented at many institutions; biological identity is not established.

### Liquefying-blood claims at Ravello and Madrid

Ampoules in Ravello and the Royal Monastery of the Incarnation in Madrid are said to contain Pantaleon’s blood. The Madrid material reportedly becomes fluid around 26–27 July.

The reported Madrid provenance begins much later than the martyr:

- tradition connects the ampoule to Ravello or Naples;
- it was reportedly brought to Spain by the Count of Miranda, a viceroy of Naples;
- his daughter, Sor Aldonza del Santísimo Sacramento, placed it in the Madrid convent in the seventeenth century.

**Documented facts:**

- the Madrid monastery possesses and displays an ampoule of reddish material as the saint’s blood;
- observers have reported changes in consistency;
- public exhibitions and donations have occurred;
- the Vatican has not defined the phenomenon as a miracle.

**Unresolved claim:**

- the material has not undergone a rigorous, independently published modern analysis establishing that it is human blood, much less fourth-century blood belonging to Pantaleon.
- A Spanish hematologist quoted by *El País* noted that non-invasive spectroscopy could provide limited information without opening the container. Custodians declined on preservation or religious grounds.
- Temperature-dependent waxes, fats, gels, or thixotropic mixtures are possible general explanations for liquefying relics, but no specific explanation can be demonstrated without controlled examination of this ampoule.

**Rumor and propaganda:** Failure of liquefaction has sometimes been treated as an omen of war or disaster; alleged extraordinary liquefactions were retrospectively connected with political violence in Spain. These are devotional or folkloric interpretations, not validated predictions.

### Fraud, crime, lawsuits, and scandal search

A dedicated search combining the saint’s names with *controversy, scandal, allegation, accusation, lawsuit, fraud, conspiracy,* and *criticism*, including 2024–2026 material, found:

- no lawsuit, criminal charge, financial scandal, or credible allegation concerning the historical person;
- no contemporary evidence that Pantaleon committed fraud or violence;
- skepticism focused chiefly on the late passion and unauthenticated relics;
- occasional popular allegations that blood relics are artificial, but no published forensic finding establishing fraud in the Pantaleon ampoules;
- no credible modern conspiracy narrative specific to Pantaleon comparable to political conspiracy movements.

The proper finding is therefore not that fraud has been proved, but that relic authenticity and supernatural interpretations remain **unverified**.

## Myths, Legends, Folklore, and Disputed Narratives

### The dead child and snake

**Story:** Pantoleon revives a child killed by a snake and kills the snake through prayer.

**Appearance and circulation:** central in the Greek passion and later Orthodox lives; frequently depicted in illustrated menologia.

**Evidence:** no external corroboration.

**Effect:** presents conversion as a test of Christ’s healing power and explains Pantaleon’s transition from ordinary physician to wonderworker.

### Healing of blindness

**Story:** Pantoleon restores a blind man’s sight, causing his father and patient to convert.

**Evidence:** hagiographic only.

**Effect:** establishes biblical continuity with Christ’s healings and provides a witness for the later trial.

### Contest between gods

**Story:** pagan priests and Pantoleon compete to heal a paralytic before the emperor; only Christian prayer succeeds.

**Evidence:** hagiographic only; resembles biblical and saintly miracle contests.

**Effect:** transforms a private medical practitioner into a public confessor.

### Miraculous immunity to torture

**Story:** fire, boiling metal, sea, wheel, sword, and beasts cannot harm him.

**Evidence:** literary miracle sequence; no contemporary confirmation.

**Effect:** makes Pantaleon a “great martyr,” visually supplies cycles for manuscripts and icons, and dramatizes imperial impotence.

### Christ appearing as Hermolaus

**Story:** Christ assumes the appearance of Pantaleon’s mentor during torture.

**Evidence:** vision legend.

**Effect:** merges earthly teacher and divine physician and reassures devotees that Christ participates in the martyr’s suffering.

### Heavenly renaming

**Story:** a voice changes Pantoleon into Panteleimon, “all-merciful.”

**Evidence:** etiological legend.

**Effect:** fixes his identity as universal healer and intercessor.

### Milk from the wound

**Story:** milk, or blood mixed with milk, flows from his severed neck.

**Evidence:** miracle legend.

**Symbolism:** purity, incorruptibility, Eucharistic and baptismal associations, and spiritual nourishment.

### The flowering olive tree

**Story:** the olive tree to which he is tied instantly flowers and fruits.

**Evidence:** miracle legend.

**Effect:** links his death to healing oil, fertility, peace, resurrection, and relic oil.

### Unburned body

**Story:** imperial agents cannot burn the corpse, enabling Christians to bury it.

**Evidence:** passion motif.

**Effect:** establishes a recoverable body and therefore the premise for the later relic cult.

### Omens from the blood relic

**Story:** ordinary annual liquefaction marks the feast; failure or untimely liquefaction predicts disaster.

**Evidence:** observation and interpretation are separate. The ampoules exist; supernatural causation and predictive value are unverified.

**Effect:** turns Pantaleon’s cult into a recurring public spectacle and associates his bodily relic with communal danger.

### Protection of soldiers

Eastern tradition derives military patronage partly from *Pantoleon*, “lion in everything,” and partly from soldiers’ need for healing. Russian tradition credits his intercession with:

- Prince Izyaslav’s survival in battle in 1151;
- Russian naval victories over Sweden at Gangut/Hanko in 1714 and Grengam in 1720, both connected calendrically with his feast.

These are posthumous devotional interpretations, not lifetime relationships.

## Relics, Shrines, and Geographical Expansion of the Cult

### Nicomedia

The earliest geographic anchor is Nicomedia. Procopius reports Justinianic restoration or construction associated with Pantaleon there, confirming a major sanctuary by the sixth century.

### Constantinople

Byzantine sources associate the saint with churches, relics, imperial ceremony, healing oil, and a reputed head relic. The *Book of Ceremonies* reportedly records imperial visits to a church containing the head.

The movement of relics from Nicomedia to Constantinople is traditional but not documented by a surviving fourth-century translation record.

### Jerusalem and the Jordan region

Procopius and later traditions associate Justinianic religious foundations with Pantaleon in Palestine. These indicate cult diffusion, not travel by the living saint.

### Mount Athos

The Russian Monastery of Saint Panteleimon became one of the most important centers of his cult and claims possession of his head. Its medieval institutional history begins many centuries after his death. The monastery’s name and relic claim had enormous importance for Russian pilgrimage and devotional publishing.

### Cologne

Saint Pantaleon’s church in Cologne became a major Western center of the cult. Byzantine cultural connections, especially under Empress Theophanu, helped foster devotion to eastern saints. The church is among the oldest major Western sanctuaries dedicated to Pantaleon.

### Gorno Nerezi

The Church of Saint Panteleimon at Gorno Nerezi, near Skopje, was founded in 1164. Its celebrated Komnenian frescoes are landmarks of Byzantine monumental painting. The building demonstrates the saint’s importance as a patron; it contains no biographical evidence about him.

### Ravello and Madrid

Ravello venerates Pantaleon as patron and guards an ampoule called his blood. Madrid’s related relic became the center of an annual liquefaction observance.

### Broader distribution

Churches and monasteries dedicated to him appear throughout Greece, Cyprus, Italy, France, Germany, Spain, Portugal, the Balkans, Georgia, Armenia, Russia, Ukraine, Romania, the Middle East, and immigrant communities in the Americas and Australia.

## Iconography and Symbolic Associations

Pantaleon is usually shown as:

- a youthful, beardless physician;
- wearing a tunic, mantle, and sometimes a physician’s stole;
- holding a medicine box;
- carrying a spoon, spatula, scalpel, or narrow cross;
- occasionally holding an olive branch and martyr’s palm;
- surrounded by narrative scenes of healing and torture.

Western art may give him a nail or hands nailed to his head, reflecting one version of his torment. As one of the Fourteen Holy Helpers, he appears in collective plague-era altarpieces and pilgrimage churches.

Principal symbolic associations include:

- medicine and pharmacy;
- charity without payment;
- healing oil;
- the olive tree;
- blood and milk;
- protection during illness and battle;
- the Christian victory over pagan gods and coercive empire.

---

## Honors, Patronages, and Liturgical Status

Pantaleon is:

- a **Great Martyr** in Byzantine usage;
- one of the **Holy Unmercenary Physicians**;
- one of the **Fourteen Holy Helpers** in late-medieval Western devotion;
- invoked in Orthodox prayers for the sick, blessings of water, and services of anointing;
- patron of physicians, surgeons, pharmacists, nurses, midwives, patients, and numerous hospitals;
- patron of Ravello and many towns, churches, monasteries, medical societies, and charitable institutions;
- regarded in some Orthodox traditions as a protector of soldiers.

No historical process of canonization survives. His cult predates centralized papal canonization procedures.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Late antiquity

By the fifth–sixth centuries Pantaleon had become an important physician-martyr. Shrines attributed to Justinianic patronage indicate imperial recognition and geographic reach.

### Byzantium

His feast entered the Constantinopolitan calendar. Lives, encomia, hymns, icons, relics, and healing practices proliferated. Symeon Metaphrastes’s redaction helped stabilize the mature narrative.

### Medieval West

Translated Latin passions made “Pantaleon” widely known. He became patron of physicians and midwives and joined the Fourteen Holy Helpers during the later Middle Ages, when epidemic disease intensified collective saintly patronage.

### Slavic and Russian worlds

The cult became especially prominent through Mount Athos and East Slavic devotion. The saint’s name became a monastic and baptismal name. Military patronage developed alongside healing patronage.

### Modern medicine and popular religion

Hospitals, clinics, pharmacies, medical associations, and charitable foundations bear his name. The saint remains a devotional figure for illness, while historians use his dossier to study:

- transformation of martyr memory;
- Christian appropriation of medical imagery;
- the *anargyros* ideal;
- manuscript transmission across languages;
- relic circulation;
- relations between empirical medicine and miraculous healing;
- construction of saintly biographies from cultic names and functions.

### Historical reinterpretation

Older confessional biographies often repeated the passion as literal history. Modern hagiographic criticism generally treats most detail as fabulous or typological while allowing a historical martyr behind the cult. The result is not a choice between “everything happened” and “the saint never existed”; the evidence supports an early cult but only a minimal biography.

## Works by or Attributed to Pantaleon

### Authentic works

No authentic book, medical treatise, letter, sermon, prescription, speech, diary, inscription, or other composition by Pantaleon survives.

The courtroom speeches and prayers placed in his mouth were composed by hagiographers.

### Works attributed to him in narrative form

- Final prayer for those who invoke him — contained in the passions; not an independently transmitted autograph.
- Confessions and disputations before the emperor — literary speeches within the passions.
- Medical teachings — none survive, despite later descriptions of him as a learned physician.

### Works about him sometimes mistaken for works by him

- Anonymous Greek pre-Metaphrastic *Passio*—BHG 1412z–1413h.
- Symeon Metaphrastes, *Passio metaphrastica*—BHG 1414.
- Encomium attributed to Niketas the Paphlagonian—BHG 1416.
- Other anonymous encomia, miracles, hymns, canons, and synaxarial notices—approximately BHG 1415–1418c.
- Latin passions—BHL 6429–6442 and related records.
- Coptic, Arabic, Syriac, Armenian, Georgian, and Slavonic versions.
- Anonymous *Akathist to the Great Martyr and Healer Panteleimon*.
- Supplicatory canons and liturgical offices in Greek and Slavonic traditions.
