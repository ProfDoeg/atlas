# Allen Welsh Dulles: Research Dossier

## Scope, method, and evidentiary terms

This dossier reconstructs the life of Allen Welsh Dulles from archival finding aids, declassified intelligence records, Foreign Relations of the United States documents, congressional investigations, oral history, contemporaneous records, and major biographies. Institutional accounts issued by the CIA are used for chronology and documentation but are not treated as disinterested evaluations.

The surviving record is incomplete. Princeton University states that CIA officials screened the Dulles papers before their transfer; later releases restored some separated documents, but gaps remain. Many covert operations were compartmented, incompletely recorded, deliberately concealed, or documented in records subsequently destroyed.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by primary documents, official records, or convergent high-quality sources.
- **Reported fact:** stated in credible memoirs, interviews, or secondary scholarship but not independently established here from primary records.
- **Allegation or theory:** an evidentiary claim advanced by a named source but not proved.
- **Rumor or myth:** a culturally significant story lacking reliable substantiation.
- **Unresolved question:** the surviving evidence permits competing interpretations.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Allen Welsh Dulles.
- **Common form:** Allen W. Dulles; Allen Dulles.
- **Initials:** AWD in memoranda and government records.
- **OSS identification:** frequently associated with agent or source-handling designation **110**, although this was an operational identifier rather than an everyday pseudonym.
- **Titles and offices:** American diplomat; lawyer; Office of Strategic Services station chief in Bern; CIA Deputy Director for Plans; Deputy Director of Central Intelligence; fifth Director of Central Intelligence; member of the President’s Commission on the Assassination of President Kennedy.
- **Political affiliation:** Republican.
- **Birth:** 7 April 1893, Watertown, Jefferson County, New York.
- **Death:** 29 January 1969 is the generally accepted date. A CIA-produced biographical booklet gives 28 January, apparently an error or reflection of late-night reporting. He died at Georgetown University Hospital in Washington, D.C.
- **Cause of death:** complications of influenza and pneumonia are the commonly reported causes.
- **Burial:** Green Mount Cemetery, Baltimore, Maryland.
- **Nationality:** United States.
- **Religion and upbringing:** raised in a Presbyterian clerical family; his adult public thought retained a Protestant, anti-totalitarian vocabulary, although he was less doctrinally religious than his brother John Foster Dulles.
- **Physical condition:** one biographical reference reports that he was born with a clubfoot and experienced lifelong difficulty walking. This detail is less consistently documented than his other vital information.
- **Principal residences and bases:** Watertown, New York; Auburn, New York; Princeton, New Jersey; India; Vienna; Bern; Paris; Prague; Berlin; Constantinople/Istanbul; Washington, D.C.; New York City; Bern again during the Second World War; Washington and Georgetown in later life.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Allen Macy Dulles** (1854–1930), his father, was a Presbyterian minister and theological teacher associated with Auburn Theological Seminary. The paternal family combined Scottish-Irish Presbyterian traditions with missionary work and higher education.

**Edith Foster Dulles** (1865–1946), his mother, was the daughter of diplomat John Watson Foster. She had grown up partly in diplomatic surroundings abroad. Her family furnished Allen with an unusually direct introduction to foreign affairs.

### Grandparents and diplomatic ancestry

His maternal grandfather, **John Watson Foster** (1836–1917), served as a Union officer, diplomat, minister to Mexico, Russia, and Spain, and United States secretary of state under President Benjamin Harrison. Foster became an adviser to governments and corporations after leaving office. Allen spent time at his grandfather’s Washington home and listened to discussions of diplomacy and war.

His paternal grandfather, **John Welsh Dulles**, was a Presbyterian missionary in India. Allen’s middle name honored him.

His aunt **Eleanor Foster Lansing** married **Robert Lansing**, counselor of the State Department and secretary of state under Woodrow Wilson. Lansing occupied that office when Allen entered the diplomatic service. Family connections plainly provided access and social capital; Dulles nevertheless took and passed the diplomatic examination.

### Siblings

The Dulleses had five children. The best-known were:

- **John Foster Dulles** (1888–1959), Allen’s elder brother, Sullivan & Cromwell partner, Republican foreign-policy adviser, and secretary of state under Dwight Eisenhower.
- **Eleanor Lansing Dulles** (1895–1996), economist, State Department official, and specialist in postwar Berlin, sometimes called the “Mother of Berlin.”
- Sisters **Margaret** and **Natalie** are much less prominent in the public record.

The Allen–Foster relationship was central to both careers. Foster was more austere, overtly religious, and legalistic; Allen was more socially flexible, conversational, and comfortable with clandestine relationships. During the Eisenhower administration they occupied the State Department and CIA simultaneously. That proximity expedited policy coordination but also concentrated influence within one family.

### Marriage, partners, and children

On 16 October 1920, Dulles married **Martha Clover Todd**, generally known as **Clover Dulles** (1894–1974). She was the daughter of Columbia University professor Henry Alfred Todd. The marriage endured legally until Allen’s death.

They had three children:

- **Clover Todd “Toddy” Dulles**, later Clover Jebsen.
- **Joan Dulles**, who married Austrian publisher and resistance veteran Fritz Molden in 1948. They divorced in 1954; contemporary reporting said she alleged cruelty.
- **Allen Macy Dulles Jr.** (1930–2020), who served in the Korean War and suffered a severe brain injury from shell fragments in 1952. He lived with lasting cognitive and physical disabilities and underwent prolonged institutional and family care. Correspondence about him forms a poignant part of the Princeton papers.

### Extramarital relationships

**Reported fact:** Dulles conducted numerous extramarital affairs. His sister Eleanor was quoted as estimating “at least a hundred,” a number that should be understood as recollection or family hyperbole rather than a documented count.

The best-documented relationship was with **Mary Bancroft**, an American writer and OSS worker in Switzerland. Bancroft’s memoir describes their professional and sexual relationship. Dulles assigned her to assist **Hans Bernd Gisevius**, a German conspirator and intelligence contact associated with the 20 July 1944 plot against Hitler. Bancroft later became close to Clover Dulles, who reportedly knew about the affair.

He was also romantically linked to **Wally Toscanini Castelbarco**, daughter of conductor Arturo Toscanini, and to other women in American and European social circles. Biographers differ over how much these relationships affected operations. There is ample evidence of repeated infidelity; claims that every relationship was an intelligence instrument are interpretive rather than proved.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1893–1910

Dulles was born into a family where theology, diplomacy, imperial rivalry, and international law were ordinary dinner-table subjects. His father’s clerical appointments took the family from Watertown to Auburn, New York. The children were educated for periods by tutors rather than entirely through public schools.

A famous family story concerns the Boer War. At about eight or nine years old, Allen listened to arguments among relatives and wrote a short paper favoring the Boers. His family privately printed it in 1902 as *The Boer War*. Princeton’s archival catalogue confirms the existence of the monograph and related correspondence.

The episode later became part of the Dulles family’s self-presentation: a precocious child already engaged in foreign policy. The physical pamphlet is documented; claims that it became a local “bestseller” are family legend rather than a measurable publishing fact.

Summers with John Watson Foster exposed him to visiting political and diplomatic figures. This was not formal apprenticeship, but it normalized elite public service and the use of private networks in international affairs.

---

## Education and Formation, 1910–1916

Dulles entered Princeton University in 1910. He participated in the American Whig-Cliosophic Society and studied politics, philosophy, and history. He received a B.A. in 1914. Sources sometimes say 1915 because he returned for graduate work; the more precise institutional chronology is B.A. 1914 and M.A. 1916.

After undergraduate study he traveled to India and taught at a mission-connected school. The journey continued a family association with India and gave him his first prolonged experience outside Europe and North America.

He returned to Princeton and completed an M.A. in international law in 1916. His intellectual formation combined:

- Presbyterian internationalism;
- his grandfather’s diplomatic realism;
- Princeton’s public-service culture;
- international law;
- personal observation abroad;
- the belief that informal elite contacts could obtain results that bureaucratic channels could not.

He later received an LL.B. from George Washington University in 1926 while serving in Washington.

## Early Diplomatic Career, 1916–1926

### Vienna and Bern

Dulles passed the diplomatic examination and entered the United States Diplomatic Service in 1916. His first assignment was as third secretary at the embassy in Vienna, then capital of Austria-Hungary.

When the United States severed relations with Austria-Hungary during the First World War, he moved to neutral Switzerland. In Bern he performed both diplomatic reporting and elementary intelligence work: monitoring émigrés, economic conditions, enemy contacts, and political movements.

### The Lenin telephone-call story

**Reported fact with embellished variants:** Dulles later said that in April 1917 the American legation received a call from Vladimir Lenin, then preparing to leave Switzerland for Russia. Dulles said he declined or deferred the meeting.

In the most colorful later version, he chose a social engagement with young women over Lenin. That embellishment appears in biography and anecdote, not in a contemporaneous record. Lenin’s exact purpose is unknown, and no evidence establishes that a meeting would have altered events. The story became a parable about missed intelligence opportunities and about Dulles’s appetite for women.

### First World War intelligence and influenza

In Bern, Dulles developed political contacts and learned the value of neutral territory as an intelligence marketplace. He contracted influenza during the 1918–1919 pandemic but recovered.

### Paris Peace Conference

In December 1918 he joined the American Commission to Negotiate Peace in Paris. His brother Foster also served in the American delegation, Foster in financial and reparations work. Allen handled political and territorial questions and observed the mixture of public diplomacy, expert committees, and private bargaining that shaped the settlement.

### Prague, Berlin, and Constantinople

His subsequent assignments included:

- Prague, 1919, during the formation of Czechoslovakia;
- Berlin, from October 1919;
- Washington assignments;
- the American High Commission in Constantinople, 1920–1922;
- chief of the State Department’s Division of Near Eastern Affairs, 1922–1926.

In Constantinople he dealt with the collapse of the Ottoman order, refugee issues, competing nationalisms, and the postwar settlement.

### The *Protocols of the Elders of Zion*

**Documented/report-supported fact:** while in Constantinople, Dulles assisted the investigation that traced *The Protocols of the Elders of Zion* to earlier political satire and helped establish it as a forgery. He urged a more public American repudiation of the antisemitic text; the State Department did not mount the campaign he wanted. This episode complicates later portrayals that reduce his worldview solely to financial or ethnic stereotypes.

### “Political Reporting,” 1925

A surviving 1925 paper, “On Political Reporting,” shows Dulles thinking systematically about diplomatic observation. He emphasized character, personal contact, political forces beyond formal government, concise reporting, and the need to separate evidence from rumor. It anticipated his later philosophy of human intelligence.

### Departure from government

Dulles left the State Department in 1926. A contemporaneous account quoted him explaining that a diplomatic career increasingly required private means to supplement government salary. He had also completed his law degree and was attracted by the greater income and freedom of New York practice.

## Lawyer, Internationalist, and Policy Entrepreneur, 1926–1941

### Sullivan & Cromwell

Dulles joined **Sullivan & Cromwell**, where John Foster Dulles was already a leading partner. Allen became a partner in 1928. His value lay less in courtroom advocacy than in international contacts, government knowledge, negotiation, and political-risk analysis.

The firm represented banks, corporations, bondholders, and foreign governments. Its work placed both brothers at the junction of law, finance, diplomacy, and international reconstruction.

### European and German business

Sullivan & Cromwell handled American investments and debt arrangements involving Germany and other European states. Its clients and matters included banking, chemicals, mining, utilities, bonds, and industrial combinations. Critical histories emphasize the firm’s representation of German interests and corporations that continued operating under Hitler.

The evidence requires distinctions:

- **Documented fact:** the firm facilitated American financial and industrial business with Weimar Germany and represented clients with German interests.
- **Documented fact:** some client companies later accommodated or participated in the Nazi economy.
- **Reported fact:** a 1935 internal revolt or pressure by partners contributed to the firm reducing German ties.
- **Not established:** that Allen Dulles personally financed Hitler’s political rise or was himself a Nazi supporter.
- **Relevant counterevidence:** he assisted German anti-Nazis and some Jewish clients or refugees, collected evidence later used at Nuremberg, and opposed American isolation from the European war.
- **Unresolved issue:** attorney-client secrecy and incomplete corporate files make it difficult to assign individual responsibility for every transaction.

### Diplomatic and arms-control assignments

Though in private practice, Dulles continued accepting public assignments:

- legal adviser to the American delegation at the 1927 Geneva Naval Conference;
- delegate or adviser on arms traffic and disarmament;
- American representative at League of Nations-related conferences in the early 1930s.

### Council on Foreign Relations

Dulles became deeply involved in the **Council on Foreign Relations** (CFR), contributing to study groups and *Foreign Affairs*. With Hamilton Fish Armstrong, the journal’s editor, he developed a close professional friendship.

He participated in CFR’s War and Peace Studies, which prepared confidential policy studies for the Roosevelt administration. The project was funded by the Rockefeller Foundation. Its existence supports the historical importance of private policy networks, but not claims that CFR secretly controlled the United States government.

Dulles later served as CFR president, 1946–1950.

### Political candidacy

He made an unsuccessful Republican run for Congress from New York in 1938. Electoral politics did not suit his strengths; he thereafter exercised influence through law, advisory bodies, intelligence, and executive appointments.

### Neutrality debate

Dulles and Armstrong published:

- *Can We Be Neutral?* (1936);
- *Can America Stay Neutral?* (1939).

They argued that traditional neutrality was increasingly impossible in an interdependent world. Dulles moved from conventional Republican internationalism toward intervention against the Axis. His outlook combined anti-Nazism, support for American power, and hostility to Soviet communism.

## Office of Strategic Services and the Second World War, 1941–1945

### Recruitment and New York

William J. Donovan drew Dulles into the Coordinator of Information and then the OSS. Dulles initially helped organize intelligence work in New York, operating near British intelligence offices at Rockefeller Center. In November 1942 he traveled through Vichy France into Switzerland.

### Bern station

As OSS chief in Bern, Dulles used Switzerland’s diplomatic, refugee, commercial, and banking networks to collect intelligence from inside Axis Europe. His townhouse at Herrengasse 23 became a conspicuous contact point. The fact that his role was widely suspected did not prevent sources from approaching him.

His station dealt with:

- German resistance figures;
- diplomats and industrialists;
- refugees;
- couriers;
- Vatican and church networks;
- Italian anti-Fascists;
- military officers;
- Swiss intermediaries;
- intelligence services of Allied and neutral states.

### Fritz Kolbe

German Foreign Office official **Fritz Kolbe**, cryptonym **George Wood**, brought Dulles approximately 1,600 documents between 1943 and 1945. They included German diplomatic cables, military information, intelligence on Axis relations, and indications concerning weapons and strategic plans.

British intelligence initially distrusted Kolbe; Dulles accepted him. Kolbe became one of the most important human sources of the war. Dulles later called him the war’s best spy.

### German resistance and 20 July plot

Dulles maintained contacts with **Hans Bernd Gisevius**, **Adam von Trott zu Solz**, and networks around the German military and civilian opposition. He transmitted information about anti-Hitler plans to Washington.

The relationship had limits. Allied governments would not negotiate a separate political peace with German conspirators, and Dulles could offer neither recognition nor guarantees. After the failed 20 July 1944 attempt, many contacts were executed.

Dulles later emphasized the resistance’s breadth and moral seriousness in *Germany’s Underground*. Critics argue that he presented conservative and military conspirators more favorably than their earlier records warranted. The book remains both testimony and advocacy.

### Carl Jung

Dulles consulted Swiss psychiatrist **Carl Gustav Jung** about Hitler and German political psychology. In a later CBS interview Dulles said he visited Jung “very often” and valued his estimates of whether Hitler would retreat to an Alpine redoubt or commit suicide. Jung therefore functioned as an informal psychological consultant, although popular claims that he was a formal CIA “agent” oversimplify a looser OSS relationship.

### Ciano diaries and Nuremberg evidence

Dulles’s networks obtained and preserved material from the diaries of Galeazzo Ciano, Mussolini’s foreign minister. Portions were used in the case against Joachim von Ribbentrop at Nuremberg. This is documented evidence that Dulles aided war-crimes prosecution.

### Operation Sunrise / Crossword

Beginning in early 1945, Dulles and intermediaries negotiated with SS General **Karl Wolff** and German representatives for the surrender of Axis forces in northern Italy. The negotiations, called Operation Sunrise by the Americans and Crossword by the British, produced a surrender effective 2 May 1945, several days before the general German capitulation.

The operation generated disputes:

- The Soviets objected to secret Anglo-American dealings with German officers and suspected a separate peace.
- Washington and London restricted Soviet participation.
- Dulles believed speed could save lives and prevent destruction in northern Italy.
- Wolff sought favorable treatment and political rehabilitation.
- The surrender enhanced Dulles’s reputation as a negotiator and spymaster.

### Karl Wolff and selective prosecution

**Documented controversy:** Wolff had been Himmler’s chief of personal staff and was implicated in SS administration and deportations. Dulles and associates portrayed him as instrumental in the surrender and useful to the postwar settlement. Wolff avoided immediate prosecution at Nuremberg, although a German court later convicted him in 1964 for complicity in the deportation and murder of Jews.

Scholars characterize Dulles’s assistance as an instance of selective prosecution: he helped secure evidence against some Nazis while supporting leniency or protection for useful contacts. The evidence supports criticism of his intervention for Wolff; it does not support the generalized claim that Dulles protected every Nazi official later employed by the West.

## Postwar Transition, 1945–1950

The OSS was dissolved in September 1945. Dulles returned to Sullivan & Cromwell but remained an intelligence adviser.

### Intelligence reorganization

He participated in the debate over a permanent peacetime intelligence service. In 1948–1949 he served with William H. Jackson and Mathias Correa on the survey group that produced the **Dulles–Jackson–Correa Report**. It criticized CIA coordination and argued for stronger central direction, clandestine capacity, and estimative work.

Dulles did not single-handedly create the CIA; the National Security Act of 1947 emerged from military, diplomatic, presidential, and congressional processes. He was, however, one of the most influential advocates of the operationally capable agency that developed afterward.

### CFR presidency

As CFR president from 1946 to 1950, Dulles bridged Wall Street, academia, media, diplomacy, and intelligence. Critics later treated this network as proof of a hidden government. The documented reality is substantial elite overlap and privileged access; a unitary secret command structure is not established.

### Former German intelligence personnel

American intelligence adopted the **Gehlen Organization**, assembled around former Wehrmacht intelligence chief Reinhard Gehlen, to collect information on the Soviet bloc. The organization employed former Wehrmacht, SS, and SD personnel, some with criminal or compromised histories.

Dulles favored using anti-Soviet intelligence assets, but the initial arrangements were made through the U.S. Army before his CIA directorship. Under his leadership the CIA deepened the relationship. National Archives historians conclude that Western agencies sometimes protected actual or suspected war criminals and that operational utility frequently displaced accountability.

## CIA Leadership, 1951–1961

### Appointments

- **4 January 1951:** Deputy Director for Plans, responsible for covert operations.
- **23 August 1951:** Deputy Director of Central Intelligence.
- **9–26 February 1953:** acting DCI.
- **26 February 1953:** sworn in as Director of Central Intelligence after Senate confirmation.
- **29 November 1961:** left office.

He was the first civilian DCI and remains the longest-serving person to hold the office in its CIA-era form.

### Management style

Dulles preferred oral briefings, informal contacts, small operational circles, and broad presidential authorization. He was accessible socially but sometimes inattentive to administration, documentation, counterintelligence, and internal control.

His admirers described a genial professional who recruited talent and protected initiative. Critics described an establishment operator who blurred intelligence collection, covert action, personal networks, and policy advocacy. Both descriptions have documentary support.

### Institutional construction

During his tenure:

- the CIA expanded worldwide stations and liaison relationships;
- covert action became a regular instrument of U.S. foreign policy;
- the Directorate of Plans grew in size and prestige;
- National Intelligence Estimates became central presidential products;
- technical intelligence received major investment;
- construction began on the CIA headquarters at Langley;
- counterintelligence under James Angleton acquired considerable autonomy.

## Major Operations and Projects

### Iran, 1953 — TPAJAX

The Eisenhower administration, the CIA, and British intelligence sought the removal of Iranian prime minister **Mohammad Mosaddegh** after the nationalization of the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company and an escalating political crisis.

Allen Dulles approved CIA participation; his brother John Foster directed State Department policy; **Kermit Roosevelt Jr.** led the CIA operation in Iran. The operation used propaganda, political payments, military contacts, street mobilization, and support for a royal decree dismissing Mosaddegh.

After an initial failure, forces supporting Shah **Mohammad Reza Pahlavi** prevailed on 19 August 1953. Mosaddegh was arrested and later tried; the shah’s power expanded.

Evidentiary status:

- **Documented fact:** CIA and British covert action contributed materially to Mosaddegh’s overthrow.
- **Documented fact:** Dulles approved and supervised the CIA role.
- **Not established:** that CIA action alone created the coup without Iranian political, clerical, military, and social participants.
- **Long-term consequence:** the operation became a symbol of foreign intervention and contributed to later Iranian hostility toward the United States.
- **Financial conflict allegation:** critics note that Sullivan & Cromwell represented international business interests. No documentary evidence reviewed here proves that Allen undertook the coup for a personal financial payoff.

### Guatemala, 1954 — PBSUCCESS

President Jacobo Árbenz’s agrarian reform expropriated unused land belonging in part to the United Fruit Company. The administration viewed Árbenz’s government through Cold War concerns about the Guatemalan Communist Party.

On 9 December 1953, Dulles signed approval for **PBSUCCESS** and authorized up to **$3 million**. CIA documents describe propaganda, political pressure, inducement of defections, support for Carlos Castillo Armas, a clandestine radio station, and limited air attacks.

A 28 June 1954 telephone memorandum records Allen telling Foster that bombing had been “excellent and effective” and discussing efforts to shape the successor regime. Árbenz resigned on 27 June.

Evidentiary status:

- **Documented fact:** Dulles approved and managed a covert operation intended to remove the Guatemalan government.
- **Documented fact:** United Fruit had extensive interests and lobbied in Washington.
- **Documented fact:** several administration figures had professional or social connections to the company or its advisers.
- **Unresolved/conflict allegation:** the documentary record establishes convergence between Cold War policy and corporate interest; it does not establish that Dulles personally held a controlling United Fruit investment or ordered the coup solely for private profit.
- **Consequence:** Castillo Armas dismantled reforms and repressed opponents. The coup contributed to political instability preceding Guatemala’s long civil war.

### Eastern Europe, Albania, Ukraine, and Tibet

The CIA sponsored émigré infiltration, propaganda, guerrilla support, and resistance networks against communist governments. Soviet and allied security services penetrated several operations. The Albanian insertions were catastrophic, with agents captured or killed.

Dulles supported these activities as pressure on the Soviet bloc. Failures demonstrated the danger of relying on exile organizations, compromised communications, and overoptimistic assessments.

The CIA also developed support for Tibetan resistance after the Chinese occupation. Programs expanded during the Dulles era, including training and material aid. They did not reverse Chinese control.

### Italy and electoral intervention

CIA political action supported anti-communist parties, publications, unions, and civic organizations, beginning before Dulles became director and continuing under him. Funding was sometimes passed through intermediaries to conceal American sponsorship.

### Indonesia, 1957–1958

The CIA supported regional rebels opposing President Sukarno, supplying arms, aircraft, and contract pilots. The operation became publicly embarrassing when American pilot Allen Pope was shot down and captured. The rebellion failed. Dulles’s CIA then shifted toward cultivating the Indonesian army.

### Congo and Patrice Lumumba, 1960–1961

The Congo crisis followed independence from Belgium. Prime Minister **Patrice Lumumba** sought Soviet assistance after Western governments failed to meet his demands.

The Church Committee found “solid evidence” of a CIA plot to assassinate Lumumba. On 26 August 1960, Dulles sent instructions treating Lumumba’s removal as an “urgent and prime objective.” CIA scientist Sidney Gottlieb delivered biological poison to the station; station chief Larry Devlin did not use it.

Lumumba was later transferred to Katangan custody and murdered on 17 January 1961 by Congolese and Katangan personnel with Belgian involvement.

Required distinctions:

- **Documented fact:** Dulles authorized a CIA operation aimed at Lumumba’s political removal and an assassination capability was prepared.
- **Documented fact:** CIA officers did not administer the poison.
- **Congressional inference:** the Church Committee considered it likely that Dulles interpreted President Eisenhower’s hostility as assassination authority.
- **Not proved:** a clear written Eisenhower assassination order.
- **Not established:** direct CIA participation in the physical killing on 17 January.
- **Historical responsibility question:** American covert action helped isolate Lumumba, but the causal and legal relationship between the failed CIA assassination effort and the eventual Belgian-Congolese murder remains distinct.

### Assassination plots against Fidel Castro

Under Eisenhower and continuing into the Kennedy administration, the CIA developed plots against **Fidel Castro**, including contacts with organized-crime figures and attempts to obtain poison.

The Church Committee documented CIA–Mafia activity involving figures such as Johnny Roselli, Sam Giancana, and Santo Trafficante. Richard Bissell and Sheffield Edwards were principal officials; Dulles was director.

- **Documented fact:** assassination schemes existed during Dulles’s tenure.
- **Unresolved question:** surviving evidence did not show that Dulles was briefed on every operational detail.
- **Institutional responsibility:** as DCI he authorized the anti-Castro covert program and bore command responsibility even where compartmentation obscured particular methods.
- **No conviction or charge:** Dulles was never criminally charged over these plots.

### MKULTRA

On 13 April 1953, Dulles approved **MKULTRA**, a program for research into drugs, interrogation, behavior modification, and perceived communist “brainwashing.”

Projects involved LSD and other substances, hypnosis, sensory deprivation, academic and medical research, and covert administration of drugs to subjects who did not give informed consent. Some research passed through foundations or institutions unaware of the ultimate sponsor.

- **Documented fact:** Dulles approved the program.
- **Documented fact:** serious abuses and nonconsensual experimentation occurred.
- **Documentary limitation:** Richard Helms ordered most MKULTRA records destroyed in 1973; financial records that survived by accident supported the 1977 Senate investigation.
- **Death of Frank Olson:** Olson died after CIA personnel covertly gave him LSD in 1953. Whether he died by suicide, accidental fall, or homicide remains disputed. No evidence establishes that Dulles personally ordered Olson’s death.

### U-2 and technical intelligence

Dulles approved and defended development of the high-altitude **U-2 reconnaissance aircraft**, built by Lockheed’s Skunk Works under Clarence “Kelly” Johnson with major involvement by Richard Bissell and scientific advisers.

U-2 photography significantly improved knowledge of Soviet strategic capabilities and helped rebut exaggerated “bomber gap” estimates. On 1 May 1960, the Soviets shot down Francis Gary Powers. Eisenhower initially approved a cover story, then accepted responsibility after Powers and wreckage were displayed. The incident damaged the Paris summit.

This was simultaneously a technical intelligence success and a diplomatic failure.

### Guatemala as model and the Bay of Pigs

The apparent success of PBSUCCESS encouraged confidence in deniable paramilitary operations. Cuba proved less susceptible to the same model.

The Eisenhower administration authorized planning to overthrow Castro. Kennedy inherited the operation and approved a modified landing by Cuban exiles at the Bay of Pigs in April 1961.

The invasion failed within days. Important causes identified by official inquiries and scholarship include:

- unrealistic assumptions about a popular uprising;
- inadequate secrecy;
- divided authority;
- poor contingency planning;
- relocation of the landing site;
- insufficient or curtailed air support;
- CIA pressure for approval;
- failure to ensure that Kennedy understood operational assumptions;
- Kennedy’s refusal to escalate into an overt American invasion.

Dulles was away from Washington at a Puerto Rico engagement during the invasion’s opening, an absence widely criticized.

Responsibility was shared, but Dulles’s agency designed and advocated the operation. The failure ended his directorship.

## Relationship with Presidents

### Harry S. Truman

Truman appointed Dulles to senior CIA office but did not share his later enthusiasm for an expansive covert-action agency. In December 1963 Truman publicly argued that the CIA had strayed from its intelligence mission.

Dulles visited Truman and sought to limit the damage from that article. Claims that he successfully coerced or silenced Truman are not established.

### Dwight D. Eisenhower

Dulles had regular access to Eisenhower and operated within a system of National Security Council and “Special Group” authorization. Eisenhower preferred covert operations that reduced the need for overt war. Dulles was powerful, but the description of him as wholly “rogue” understates presidential approval of major programs.

Eisenhower awarded Dulles the **National Security Medal** in 1959.

### John F. Kennedy

President-elect Kennedy asked Dulles to remain DCI. Their relationship deteriorated after the Bay of Pigs. Kennedy accepted responsibility publicly but commissioned the Taylor inquiry and reorganized intelligence oversight.

Kennedy replaced Dulles with John McCone. The formal end date was 29 November 1961. Accounts often say Kennedy “fired” him; more precisely, Kennedy arranged his departure and publicly honored him at the new CIA headquarters.

The frequently quoted claim that Kennedy vowed to “splinter the CIA into a thousand pieces and scatter it into the winds” is not found in a contemporaneous presidential document. It derives from a later anonymous recollection published by *The New York Times* and should be treated as **reported but unverified wording**.

## Retirement and Warren Commission, 1961–1969

### Writing and public appearances

Dulles remained an active commentator, speaker, author, and informal adviser. He appeared on television and defended professional intelligence as a necessary component of modern government.

### Warren Commission

After Kennedy’s assassination on 22 November 1963, President Lyndon Johnson appointed Dulles to the Warren Commission. Dulles attended more sessions than many other members and supplied knowledge of intelligence procedure.

His appointment created a structural conflict: the commission needed to investigate possible intelligence connections involving Lee Harvey Oswald and Cuba, while Dulles had led the CIA during relevant operations.

The commission concluded that Oswald acted alone and found no evidence of a domestic or foreign conspiracy.

Later congressional review established important deficiencies:

- the CIA withheld details of its plots to assassinate Castro;
- the commission did not receive a complete account of covert operations that could have supplied motives for retaliation;
- Dulles did not volunteer all he knew about agency capabilities and assassination activity;
- intelligence-agency performance impaired the investigation.

These findings support criticism of process and disclosure. They do **not** establish that Dulles participated in Kennedy’s assassination.

### Jim Garrison subpoena

In February 1968, New Orleans district attorney **Jim Garrison** subpoenaed Dulles to appear before a grand jury investigating Kennedy’s assassination. Garrison alleged that elements of the CIA were responsible and claimed Dulles might know whether Oswald had worked for the agency.

This was an investigative subpoena, not an indictment or finding. Garrison’s prosecution of Clay Shaw ended in acquittal. The subpoena therefore documents suspicion, not culpability.

### Final illness and death

Dulles experienced declining health in the late 1960s. He died at Georgetown University Hospital in January 1969 following influenza and pneumonia. He was 75.

His funeral and burial joined members of the family at Green Mount Cemetery. Clover survived him until 1974.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Sullivan & Cromwell

Dulles’s main private employer was Sullivan & Cromwell, 1926–1942 and again after 1945. He earned a prosperous professional income and moved within wealthy New York and Washington circles.

Attorney-client confidentiality prevents a complete reconstruction of his portfolio. Assertions that he personally owned decisive stakes in every company represented by the firm confuse legal representation with beneficial ownership.

### Council on Foreign Relations

Roles included member, study-group participant, author, director, and president. CFR supplied a venue in which officials, financiers, scholars, lawyers, and journalists exchanged policy ideas.

### Intelligence institutions

- Coordinator of Information.
- Office of Strategic Services.
- Central Intelligence Group and CIA advisory work.
- Central Intelligence Agency.
- Intelligence survey group associated with the Dulles–Jackson–Correa Report.
- President’s Commission on the Assassination of President Kennedy.

### Major technologies and programs

- U-2 reconnaissance aircraft.
- Overhead photography and photo interpretation.
- Communications and covert broadcasting.
- MKULTRA behavioral research.
- Paramilitary air and maritime capabilities.
- The Langley CIA headquarters complex.

Dulles held no important personal patents documented in the sources reviewed.

### Wealth and property

He lived comfortably through law-partnership income, family advantages, writing, and investments. The public record does not permit a reliable final net-worth estimate.

No criminal case established that he embezzled CIA money, laundered money, or received personal kickbacks from a coup. Accusations of “corruption” usually refer to conflicts between his private-law networks and public decisions, secrecy, favoritism toward former clients or associates, and the use of public power to advance aligned corporate and geopolitical interests.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

Dulles’s worldview developed through several overlapping stages:

1. **Presbyterian internationalism:** history was understood as moral struggle, but practical compromise was legitimate.
2. **Diplomatic professionalism:** personal reporting and private contact were often more valuable than formal communiqués.
3. **Interwar internationalism:** economic interdependence made strict neutrality unrealistic.
4. **Anti-Nazism:** he supported resistance networks and American intervention while retaining contacts with conservative German elites.
5. **Cold War anti-communism:** Soviet power was treated as a global, conspiratorial, and ideological threat requiring clandestine counteraction.
6. **Operational pragmatism:** morally compromised assets could be used if they supplied intelligence or fought a more urgent enemy.
7. **Institutional secrecy:** effective intelligence required concealment from adversaries and often from the public; critics argue that this habit also weakened democratic accountability.

He was not a systematic philosopher. *The Craft of Intelligence* presented his mature institutional outlook: intelligence as a permanent governmental profession involving collection, analysis, counterintelligence, and covert action.

## Controversies, Abuses, Investigations, and Evidentiary Findings

### Covert regime change

**Documented fact:** Dulles approved or managed interventions against foreign governments, including Iran and Guatemala, and attempted interventions in Cuba and Indonesia.

No court adjudicated their legality as personal crimes by Dulles. The operations were authorized within the executive branch, often under deliberately broad or deniable findings. Their compatibility with sovereignty, domestic authorization, and international law remains the subject of historical and legal dispute.

### Assassination policy

**Documented fact:** CIA assassination capabilities and plots operated during his directorship, most clearly concerning Lumumba and Castro.

**Unresolved question:** how much explicit presidential authority Dulles received and how completely subordinates briefed him on each operational device.

### Human experimentation

**Documented fact:** he approved MKULTRA. Nonconsensual dosing and concealed institutional sponsorship occurred. Program secrecy prevented meaningful consent and oversight.

### Nazi and war-criminal connections

**Documented fact:** American intelligence, including the CIA under Dulles, employed former Nazi intelligence personnel and protected some compromised assets.

**Documented fact:** Dulles supported favorable treatment for Karl Wolff after Operation Sunrise.

**Documented fact pointing the other way:** Dulles collected intelligence from anti-Nazi Germans, helped preserve Nuremberg evidence, and assisted some endangered people.

**Conclusion of record:** his policy was selective and utilitarian, not uniformly pro-Nazi or uniformly prosecutorial.

### Corporate conflicts

**Documented fact:** Dulles had represented international corporate interests and later made policy affecting the same regions and economic sectors.

**Allegation:** critics contend Iran and Guatemala were undertaken primarily for oil and United Fruit interests.

**Evidence assessment:** commercial interests were significant, particularly in the background to both crises, but national-security documents also show genuine anti-communist assessments. Personal enrichment by Dulles has not been established.

### Intelligence failures

Major failures during or arising from his tenure included:

- penetrated Albanian and Eastern European operations;
- faulty estimates surrounding popular support in Cuba;
- failure to anticipate or control the Bay of Pigs outcome;
- weaknesses exposed by the U-2 shootdown;
- excessive confidence after Iran and Guatemala;
- insufficient administrative and counterintelligence control.

Successes included the Kolbe network, Operation Sunrise, U-2 development and collection, improved estimates of Soviet capability, and construction of a permanent professional service.

### Warren Commission disclosure

**Documented fact:** the CIA did not give the Warren Commission a complete account of anti-Castro assassination plots.

**Documented fact:** Dulles knew more about CIA clandestine methods than other commissioners and did not remedy every omission.

**Not established:** that this withholding proves he organized or knew of a plot to murder Kennedy.

### Criminal and civil status

Dulles was not indicted or convicted of fraud, corruption, murder, conspiracy, money laundering, or another criminal offense. Later investigations criticized programs he authorized but did not produce criminal charges against him.

## Myths, Legends, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The man who refused to meet Lenin”

- **Story:** Lenin telephoned the American legation in Bern, but Dulles declined a meeting for a romantic engagement.
- **Appearance:** Dulles’s later reminiscence and subsequent biographies.
- **Evidence:** a call or proposed contact is plausible and was asserted by Dulles; the romantic detail lacks contemporaneous corroboration.
- **Effect:** it became a story about historical contingency, Dulles’s womanizing, and the danger of dismissing an unknown source.

### Child foreign-policy prodigy

- **Story:** his Boer War pamphlet was a Washington bestseller.
- **Evidence:** the 1902 pamphlet is documented; bestseller language is unverified family lore.
- **Effect:** reinforced the dynastic image that diplomacy was his natural vocation.

### “America’s master spy”

- **Story:** Dulles personally masterminded an unmatched sequence of intelligence victories.
- **Circulation:** CIA commemoration, obituaries, friendly biographies, and his own books.
- **Evidence:** Bern and the U-2 were substantial achievements; many successes were collective and several covert operations failed disastrously.
- **Effect:** supplied the CIA with a founding hero and later supplied critics with a personification of unaccountable power.

### Dulles as autonomous ruler of a “secret government”

- **Story:** he and a Wall Street–CFR network controlled American foreign policy independently of elected officials.
- **Evidence for:** extraordinary elite overlap, secrecy, informal access, incomplete presidential knowledge, and Dulles’s ability to frame operational choices.
- **Evidence against:** major operations received presidential or NSC authorization; Kennedy removed him; interagency and congressional conflicts were real.
- **Assessment:** metaphorically influential, but a literal unified secret state is not demonstrated.

### Dulles ordered Kennedy’s assassination

- **Story:** after Kennedy removed him, Dulles used CIA and anti-Castro or organized-crime networks to arrange the assassination and then controlled the Warren Commission cover-up.
- **Circulation:** assassination literature, David Talbot’s *The Devil’s Chessboard*, online conspiracy communities, and interpretations of released JFK records.
- **Evidence cited:** motive after dismissal; CIA assassination capacity; Dulles’s Warren Commission role; nondisclosure of Castro plots; his presence at the CIA’s Camp Peary facility during the assassination weekend; alleged relationships among former operatives.
- **Evidence against:** no authenticated operational order, financial trail, confession, communications chain, or witness evidence proves his participation. Neither the Warren Commission nor later congressional investigations found such proof.
- **Assessment:** **allegation or theory, not established fact**. The proven concealment of CIA operations and deficiencies in the original investigation sustain suspicion but do not establish Dulles’s guilt.

### Majestic-12 and UFO narratives

- **Story:** Dulles ran or obstructed an extraterrestrial-recovery organization called MJ-12 and defied Kennedy over UFO disclosure.
- **Evidence:** “Majestic-12” documents used to support the claim have serious authenticity and provenance problems; calendar coincidences do not authenticate them.
- **Assessment:** **rumor or myth**.
- **Effect:** merged Dulles’s documented secrecy with modern UFO folklore.

### The “Dulles Plan” to destroy Russia

A purported secret speech or plan attributed to Dulles circulates widely in Russia and other post-Soviet states. It allegedly proposes corrupting Soviet culture, youth, art, and morality from within.

- **Origin:** phrases closely resemble dialogue in Anatoly Ivanov’s Soviet novel *The Eternal Call* and themes in earlier Soviet literature.
- **Evidence:** no authentic English-language Dulles document containing the text has been produced.
- **Assessment:** **false attribution / political myth**.
- **Effect:** the text became an influential explanation of post-Soviet social change and a propaganda symbol of Western cultural subversion.

### “Dulles protected all the Nazis”

- **Story:** Dulles personally rescued the Nazi intelligence system and every major criminal later used by the United States.
- **Evidence:** he did favor utility over prosecution in specific cases, particularly Wolff, and led an agency that relied upon Gehlen’s compromised organization.
- **Counterevidence:** recruitment decisions spanned the Army, CIA, State Department, and other Allied services; Dulles also aided anti-Nazi resistance and Nuremberg prosecutions.
- **Assessment:** an overgeneralization built around a documented underlying policy.

## Works by Allen Welsh Dulles

Authorship should be treated cautiously. Dulles used research assistants, editors, collaborators, and, according to biographical accounts, ghostwriters. His papers contain drafts and correspondence permitting study of composition.

### Books and pamphlets

1. **The Boer War** (1902).  
   Juvenile privately printed monograph. Genuine authorship, though produced and circulated by his family.

2. **Can We Be Neutral?** (New York: Harper & Brothers, 1936), with Hamilton Fish Armstrong.  
   Genuine co-authorship; analysis of American neutrality.

3. **Can America Stay Neutral?** (New York: Harper & Brothers, 1939), with Hamilton Fish Armstrong.  
   Revised and expanded intervention-era argument.

4. **Germany’s Underground** (New York: Macmillan, 1947).  
   Account of German resistance to Hitler based on wartime contacts and postwar research. Genuine work with extensive assistance from sources and editors.

5. **The Craft of Intelligence** (New York: Harper & Row, 1963).  
   Published under Dulles’s sole name. Archival drafts confirm his participation, but biographical accounts say assistants or ghostwriters performed substantial drafting. Best classified as authorized and directed by Dulles, with the exact division of labor incompletely transparent.

6. **The Secret Surrender** (New York: Harper & Row, 1966).  
   Dulles’s account of Operation Sunrise, prepared with substantial contribution from Gero von Schulze-Gaevernitz and editorial assistance. German edition: *Operation Sunrise: Die geheime Geschichte des Kriegsendes in Italien*.

7. **Great True Spy Stories** (New York: Harper & Row, 1968).  
   Edited anthology rather than wholly authored work.

### Unpublished book projects

Princeton identifies drafts of:

- **The Marshall Plan**, circa 1948; never published in Dulles’s lifetime.
- **Communist Subversion**, drafted chiefly in 1962; never published.

A later work titled *The Marshall Plan*, edited or co-attributed with Michael Wala and published in 1993, is a posthumous scholarly edition rather than a new book completed by Dulles in that year.

### Posthumous collections

- **From Hitler’s Doorstep: The Wartime Intelligence Reports of Allen Dulles, 1942–1945**, edited by Neal H. Petersen (Pennsylvania State University Press, 1996).  
  Authentic wartime reports, selected and edited posthumously.

- Later reissues of *The Craft of Intelligence*, *Germany’s Underground*, and *The Secret Surrender* frequently add promotional subtitles not present in the original editions.

### Articles, speeches, and testimony

Dulles produced numerous:

- diplomatic memoranda and political reports, 1916–1926;
- CFR papers and *Foreign Affairs* contributions;
- speeches on neutrality, intelligence, communism, and national security;
- OSS reports from Bern;
- CIA congressional testimony and internal memoranda;
- television and radio interviews;
- Warren Commission memoranda and meeting interventions.

A complete item-level bibliography would require working through the Princeton papers’ extensive “Writings,” “Speeches,” and “Digital Files” series. Important individually identifiable texts include:

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Dulles’s reputation passed through several phases.

### 1940s–1960s: heroic professional

Public accounts stressed Bern, German resistance, Operation Sunrise, and the creation of a capable peacetime intelligence service. CIA secrecy concealed most failed or abusive programs.

### 1970s: congressional exposure

Watergate-era investigations revealed assassination plots, domestic surveillance, mail-opening programs, and MKULTRA. The Church Committee transformed Dulles from institutional hero into a symbol of inadequate oversight.

### 1990s–2000s: declassification

Iran, Guatemala, Nazi-related files, Bay of Pigs histories, and OSS records allowed more exact reconstruction. The picture became neither simple heroism nor total autonomy: presidents authorized major interventions, but secrecy, compartmentation, and institutional advocacy distorted decision-making.

### 2010s–2020s: renewed critical and conspiratorial interest

Books by Kinzer and Talbot popularized Dulles as a central figure in an American “deep state.” The 2017–2025 JFK releases renewed attention to CIA monitoring of Oswald, Mexico City reporting, George Joannides, and agency nondisclosure.

The 2025 releases added historical texture and newly unredacted copies of older material, including press and investigative records about Dulles. They did not, in the material reviewed here, produce documentary proof that he ordered Kennedy’s assassination.

### Institutional legacy

Dulles helped establish enduring patterns:

- worldwide liaison networks;
- covert action as a routine policy instrument;
- close relations between intelligence, law, finance, academia, foundations, and journalism;
- reliance on deniability;
- strong investment in technical collection;
- tolerance of ethically compromised assets;
- recurring tension between operational secrecy and democratic oversight.

His name remains attached simultaneously to intelligence professionalism, Cold War intervention, technological innovation, regime change, human experimentation, selective treatment of war criminals, and the mythology of secret government.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the reviewed record are included. Mere shared membership in a period or institution is excluded.

- **John Foster Dulles — documented fact.** Elder brother, law-firm colleague at Sullivan & Cromwell, political ally, and secretary of state while Allen directed the CIA. They worked within the same administration from 1953 until Foster’s illness and death in 1959, including policy on Iran, Guatemala, Indochina, and the Soviet bloc.

- **John Fitzgerald Kennedy — documented fact.** Kennedy retained Dulles as DCI in 1961, worked with him during planning and execution of the Bay of Pigs invasion, and arranged his departure in November 1961. Dulles later served on the commission investigating Kennedy’s assassination.

- **Mohammad Mosaddegh — documented fact, adversarial connection.** As DCI, Dulles approved and supervised the CIA component of the 1953 operation that overthrew the Iranian prime minister.

- **Mohammad Reza Shah Pahlavi — documented fact.** The CIA operation approved by Dulles restored and strengthened the shah’s authority in August 1953. Their connection was governmental and operational rather than a close personal relationship established here.

- **Fidel Alejandro Castro Ruz — documented fact, adversarial connection.** Dulles directed the CIA while it organized the Bay of Pigs project and assassination-related operations against Castro’s government.

- **Ernesto “Che” Guevara — documented fact, indirect adversarial connection.** Guevara was a senior Cuban revolutionary leader while Dulles’s CIA planned the overthrow of Castro’s government. No personal meeting or correspondence was found; the connection is institutional and operational.

- **Patrice Émery Lumumba — documented fact, adversarial connection.** Dulles authorized political-removal measures and treated elimination of Lumumba as an urgent CIA objective during the Congo crisis. A CIA assassination capability was prepared but was not used in Lumumba’s eventual killing.

- **Carl Gustav Jung — documented fact.** Dulles met Jung repeatedly in Switzerland during the Second World War and consulted him on Hitler’s psychology and likely behavior.

- **Ian Lancaster Fleming — reported/documented fact.** Dulles met the James Bond author at a 1959 dinner arranged through British intelligence contacts. They maintained friendly contact; Dulles showed interest in Fleming’s fictional gadgets and encouraged CIA technical personnel to consider similar devices.

- **David Rockefeller — documented institutional and reported personal connection.** Both were prominent in Council on Foreign Relations circles. Contemporary FBI material lists them among CFR officers or leaders; later accounts describe Rockefeller as Dulles’s friend and confidant. Claims that this relationship constituted a private chain of command over government are not established.

- **William Egan Colby — documented institutional connection.** Colby was an OSS veteran and CIA officer in the organization Dulles led. He belonged to the cohort of former OSS personnel who carried the Donovan–Dulles covert-action model into the postwar CIA. No close personal friendship is established here.

- **William Joseph Casey — documented institutional lineage; limited direct connection.** Casey served in OSS London while Dulles ran Bern. Both later became DCIs and drew on OSS experience. The reviewed sources do not establish a sustained personal partnership between them.

- **George Herbert Walker Bush — absence is a finding.** Bush became DCI in 1976, seven years after Dulles’s death. Although both belonged to overlapping Republican and intelligence milieus, no meaningful personal meeting or collaboration was established in the reviewed sources.

- **J. Robert Oppenheimer — absence is a finding.** Both participated in mid-century national-security circles, but no sufficiently documented personal connection was located for inclusion.

- **David Ben-Gurion, Golda Meir, Chaim Weizmann, and Benjamin Netanyahu — absence is a finding.** Dulles dealt institutionally with Middle Eastern intelligence and diplomacy, but the reviewed record did not establish a significant personal relationship with these listed individuals.
