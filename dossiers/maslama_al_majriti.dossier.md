# Maslama ibn Ahmad al-Majriti: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

Abū al-Qāsim Maslama ibn Aḥmad al-Majrīṭī is historically attested, but the surviving biographical record is sparse. Most information comes from:

- Ṣāʿid al-Andalusī’s *Ṭabaqāt al-umam* (“Categories of Nations”), composed approximately half a century after Maslama’s death;
- the later biographical compilation of Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa, which preserves Ṣāʿid’s account;
- surviving Arabic and Latin astronomical texts and manuscripts;
- modern analysis of Maslama’s scientific school and textual transmission.

The principal historiographical problem is contamination of his biography by material belonging to another Andalusi scholar, Maslama ibn Qāsim al-Qurṭubī (d. 353 AH/964 CE). The latter is now generally identified as the author of the alchemical *Rutbat al-ḥakīm* and magical-astrological *Ghāyat al-ḥakīm*, later known in Latin as the *Picatrix*. Older reference works, manuscripts, popular histories, auction catalogues, and websites routinely assign those works—and the journeys and doctrines associated with them—to Maslama ibn Aḥmad al-Majrīṭī.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** directly supported by an early text, manuscript, dated observation, or institutional record.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a premodern or modern source but not independently demonstrable.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** inference accepted or proposed in specialist scholarship.
- **Disputed attribution:** transmitted attribution rejected or questioned by modern specialists.
- **Legend or myth:** a story without adequate evidence in the medieval record.

### Name and origin

**Documented linguistic fact:** *al-Majrīṭī* means “the man from Majrīṭ,” the Arabic name of Madrid. This strongly supports Madrid as his origin.

**Contradictory report:** the version of Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa’s biography published in *A Literary History of Medicine* says that he “was born in Cordova.” This conflicts with the nisba and with modern specialist biographies, which identify Madrid as his birthplace and say that he moved to Córdoba while young. The contradiction may reflect loose usage, textual corruption, or confusion between birthplace and principal residence. No contemporary birth notice survives.

The often-repeated year 950 is therefore an approximation, not an exact documented birth date.

## Family and Ancestry

The secure patronymic is **ibn Aḥmad**, “son of Aḥmad.” Some later catalogues and reference sources expand the genealogy to Maslama ibn Aḥmad ibn Qāsim ibn ʿAbd Allāh, but this fuller chain is not consistent across the evidence consulted.

No reliable source located for this dossier identifies:

- his mother;
- his father’s occupation or social position;
- siblings;
- a spouse or partner;
- securely attested sons or daughters;
- landed property or an inherited estate.

### “Fátima de Madrid,” alleged daughter

**Legend or modern literary construction:** twentieth- and twenty-first-century sources describe a daughter named Fátima de Madrid or Fāṭima al-Majrīṭiyya. She allegedly assisted Maslama with astronomical tables, corrected Ptolemy, wrote *Correcciones de Fátima*, composed zījes, mastered several languages, and co-authored a treatise on the astrolabe preserved at El Escorial.

The evidentiary history is adverse to the story:

1. No medieval biographical dictionary or known scientific manuscript securely identifies such a daughter.
2. The familiar biographical notice appears in volume 23 of the 1924 *Enciclopedia Universal Ilustrada Europeo-Americana*.
3. The supposed *Correcciones de Fátima* have not been found.
4. No corresponding astrolabe manuscript by her has been identified at El Escorial.
5. Manuela Marín and other historians regard her as historically nonexistent.
6. An earlier romantic story, “Fátima la astrónoma,” published by Francisco Fernández y González in *La Ilustración Artística* in 1886, may represent part of the literary prehistory of the legend, but it is not documentary evidence.

**Finding:** Maslama’s parenthood is unknown. “Fátima de Madrid” should not be entered as a documented child.

## Childhood and Early Life

Nothing reliable is known about Maslama’s childhood, household, economic circumstances, or initial literacy.

If Madrid was his birthplace, he grew up in a relatively young Umayyad frontier settlement rather than in the caliphal capital. Madrid had been fortified under the Umayyads and lay within the central frontier region of al-Andalus. His later nisba preserved that geographic identity even after his move to Córdoba.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** he went to Córdoba as a young man. Córdoba was then the political capital and the principal Andalusi center of court culture, libraries, law, medicine, astronomy, and mathematical education.

There is no evidence that Maslama traveled to the Islamic East. Reports that he journeyed through the Middle East, reached Ḥarrān, contacted the Brethren of Purity, or brought their epistles to Iberia belong chiefly to the biographies of:

- Maslama ibn Qāsim al-Qurṭubī; or
- Maslama ibn Aḥmad’s student al-Kirmānī.

## Education and Formation

### Teachers

The *Biographical Encyclopedia of Astronomers* identifies two teachers:

- **ʿAbd al-Ghāfir ibn Muḥammad al-Faraḍī** — associated with inheritance calculation and author of a work on *farāʾiḍ*, the mathematical division of estates under Islamic law.
- **ʿAlī ibn Muḥammad ibn Abī ʿĪsā al-Anṣārī** — reported to have possessed astronomical knowledge.

The first relationship explains Maslama’s designation **al-Faraḍī**; the second represents the astronomical component of his formation.

An older Dictionary of Scientific Biography account says that, as a young man in Córdoba, he studied with a geometrician named ʿAbd al-Ghāfir ibn Muḥammad. Modern scholarship describes Maslama’s distinctive contribution as joining two formerly separate mathematical traditions:

1. practical calculation associated with inheritance, taxation, surveying, and commerce;
2. the mathematical sciences associated with astronomy, geometry, and natural philosophy.

### Textual influences

His demonstrated intellectual sources included:

- Ptolemy’s *Almagest*;
- Ptolemy’s *Planisphaerium* in an earlier Arabic translation;
- al-Khwārizmī’s *Zīj al-Sindhind*;
- al-Battānī’s astronomical tables;
- Menelaus’s theorem as transmitted through Arabic mathematical astronomy.

There is no sound evidence that Maslama knew Greek. The older claim that he translated the *Planisphaerium* directly from Greek has been superseded by manuscript research indicating that he revised and annotated an existing eastern Arabic translation.

---

## Early Career

Maslama was active under Caliph al-Ḥakam II, who ruled from 961 to 976. A later biographical report simply says he “lived in the time of al-Ḥakam.” He continued into the reign of Hishām II and the period of domination by the ʿĀmirid chamberlain al-Manṣūr.

No formal court appointment survives. Juan Vernet cautiously suggested that Maslama may have been associated with the Hellenizing scholars patronized by the Umayyad court and may have acted as a court astrologer. Those are plausible reconstructions, not documented offices.

Maslama’s exact sources of income are unknown. His writings on commercial calculation and his reputation as an astrologer suggest possible paid teaching, consultation, or horoscope practice, but no salary, patronage contract, endowment, property register, or client list has been found.

## Chronological Life History

### Circa 950: birth and origin

Maslama was probably born at Majrīṭ, although the exact year and place remain uncertain. The standard circa-950 date appears to be an inference from his period of mature activity, teachers, and death rather than a recorded birth date.

### Youth and movement to Córdoba

He reportedly settled in Córdoba while young and remained there for the balance of his known career. No intermediate residences are securely attested.

### Before 978–979: formation of an astronomical program

Maslama studied Ptolemaic and eastern Islamic astronomy and began adapting imported astronomical material for Andalusi conditions. He attracted students and became what Ṣāʿid al-Andalusī later called the leading Andalusi mathematician and astronomer of his generation.

### 367 AH/978 CE: astrolabe-star table

A surviving extra chapter attached to Maslama’s notes on Ptolemy’s *Planisphaerium* contains a fixed-star table dated to 367 AH/978 CE and attributes the underlying observations to Maslama.

The text treats:

- dividing the zodiac on the plane;
- projecting the horizon;
- locating stars on the astrolabe’s rete;
- mathematically constructing an astrolabe plate.

The surviving Arabic witness is Paris, Bibliothèque nationale de France, MS arabe 4821, copied in 544 AH/1149 CE. The material also entered the Latin tradition.

### 979: observation of Regulus

According to al-Zarqālī, Maslama observed Regulus in 979 and obtained an ecliptic longitude of approximately 135°40′. Comparing its position with the Ptolemaic star catalogue yielded a precessional displacement of 13°10′.

This is the clearest dated scientific activity in his life. Modern scholarship identifies him as the first known Andalusi scholar to conduct his own astronomical observations.

### Late tenth century: revision of al-Khwārizmī

Maslama, with contributions from his student Ibn al-Ṣaffār, adapted al-Khwārizmī’s *Zīj al-Sindhind* to Córdoba.

The revision:

- replaced Persian chronological material with the Islamic lunar calendar;
- recomputed planetary mean motions from the epoch of the Hijra;
- adopted Córdoba’s coordinates for locally dependent tables;
- incorporated the Hispanic era;
- modified tables for chronology, conjunctions, oppositions, and lunar-crescent visibility;
- included tables relevant to astrological houses and the projection of rays;
- preserved sine and cotangent tables;
- simplified some computational procedures.

The shift in geographical longitude also preserved an early Andalusi correction to the exaggerated Ptolemaic length of the Mediterranean.

Ṣāʿid nevertheless criticized Maslama for retaining errors in al-Khwārizmī’s work without identifying them. Thus the medieval judgment is mixed: Maslama was praised for transforming the tables but criticized for insufficient correction of his source.

The original Arabic recension is substantially lost. Its importance is known largely through a Latin translation made by Adelard of Bath in the twelfth century and through associated manuscript traditions.

### Late tenth century: teaching school

Maslama established the first clearly recognizable school of mathematical astronomy in al-Andalus. His pupils and later members of the network included:

- Abū al-Qāsim Aṣbagh ibn Muḥammad, known as **Ibn al-Samḥ**;
- Abū al-Qāsim Aḥmad ibn ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿUmar, **Ibn al-Ṣaffār**;
- Abū al-Ḥasan ʿAlī ibn Sulaymān **al-Zahrāwī**;
- Abū al-Ḥakam ʿAmr ibn ʿAbd al-Raḥmān **al-Kirmānī**;
- Abū Muslim ʿUmar ibn Aḥmad **ibn Khaldūn**;
- Ibn Bargūth;
- other figures linked through two or three generations of instruction.

Ṣāʿid claimed that no other Andalusi scholar had left so many excellent students.

The pupils developed different specialties:

- Ibn al-Samḥ wrote on arithmetic, Euclidean geometry, astronomy, medicine, and the astrolabe.
- Ibn al-Ṣaffār composed a concise astrolabe manual and astronomical tables, and later moved to Dénia during the civil war.
- Al-Zahrāwī applied demonstrative mathematics to commercial calculation.
- Al-Kirmānī became an eminent geometer and physician, traveled to the East as far as Ḥarrān, and reportedly brought the *Epistles of the Brethren of Purity* to al-Andalus.
- Ibn Khaldūn of Seville cultivated geometry, astrology, medicine, and philosophy.

### Literary recollection in Ibn Ḥazm

Ibn Ḥazm’s *Ṭawq al-ḥamāma* (*The Ring of the Dove*) preserves a story transmitted by the stationer Abū Dulaf, who said he had heard it from “the philosopher Maslama ibn Aḥmad, better known as al-Majrīṭī.”

The anecdote concerns Muqaddam ibn al-Aṣfar’s attachment to ʿAjib, a page of the vizier Abū ʿUmar Aḥmad ibn Muḥammad ibn Ḥudayr, and locates the story near a Córdoba mosque. The passage supplies little direct biography, but it shows Maslama remembered as a transmitter of urban social anecdote and called a philosopher outside strictly astronomical literature.

### 1006–1007: great conjunction and political prediction

Maslama reportedly interpreted the Saturn–Jupiter conjunction of 1006/07 as forecasting:

- dynastic change;
- ruin;
- slaughter;
- famine.

This belongs to judicial or historical astrology, not to modern physical astronomy. Córdoba entered its destructive *fitna* in 1009, shortly after Maslama’s death, and the Umayyad caliphate eventually collapsed in 1031. Later remembrance could therefore present the prediction as fulfilled.

The surviving report does not establish when it was written down, its exact wording, whether Maslama addressed a patron, or whether the prophecy was modified after the crisis began.

### 398 AH/1007–1008: death

Ṣāʿid’s report, preserved by Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa, places Maslama’s death in 398 AH and before the civil war. Depending on calendar conversion and the intended part of the Islamic year, this corresponds to 1007–1008.

Other scholarship reports **395 AH/1004 or shortly afterward**. The standard modern date is 1007, and Córdoba is usually given as the place of death. No account of his final illness, burial, funeral, or cause of death survives.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

Modern corporate categories do not apply. No company, commercial partnership, investment portfolio, patent, or identifiable property holding is recorded.

### Institutions and networks

- **Córdoba’s scholarly community:** his principal working environment.
- **Umayyad court culture:** probable context, but no surviving appointment document.
- **His teaching circle:** his most securely reconstructable institution, though it was not a university or formally incorporated academy.
- **Astrological practice:** reported profession; clientele and remuneration unknown.

### Technologies and technical practices

Maslama did not “invent” the astrolabe, astronomical tables, triangulation, or stereographic projection. His documented contribution was adaptation and improvement:

- stereographic procedures for constructing astrolabe components;
- placement of fixed stars on an astrolabe rete;
- adaptation of eastern tables to Córdoba;
- chronological conversion;
- mathematical and astrological tabulation.

Claims that he introduced surveying or triangulation to Europe are difficult to document in that broad form and should be treated as popular extrapolations from his mathematical writings.

### Commercial arithmetic

Ṣāʿid credits him with *Kitāb al-Muʿāmalāt*, a work on practical or commercial arithmetic. The genre covered applications such as sales, taxation, estate division, land measurement, and related calculations. The work is lost.

A conjecture associates it—or a related work by one of his pupils—with the Latin *Liber mahameleth*. This identification is possible but unproved.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Political setting

Maslama’s career crossed three phases:

1. the learned court of al-Ḥakam II;
2. the nominal caliphate of Hishām II and ʿĀmirid political control;
3. the immediate prelude to the Córdoba civil war.

No evidence identifies him as a minister, partisan, rebel, jurist in office, or participant in warfare. His conjunction prediction concerned dynastic politics, but political prophecy does not itself establish political membership.

### Religion and law

The titles *al-Faraḍī* and his work in inheritance arithmetic imply training in a legally grounded Islamic mathematical discipline. No source consulted gives his school of law, theological sect, devotional practice, or relationship with religious authorities.

### Intellectual profile

The securely documented Maslama was primarily a mathematical astronomer and teacher. He combined:

- observation;
- calculation;
- textual recension;
- practical arithmetic;
- astrology;
- instrument-related geometry.

Descriptions of him as an Ismāʿīlī propagandist, Bāṭinī initiate, Hermetic magician, or alchemist derive mainly from pseudo-Majrīṭī works and confusion with Maslama ibn Qāsim al-Qurṭubī.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Teachers

- **ʿAbd al-Ghāfir ibn Muḥammad al-Faraḍī — reported teacher.** Connected Maslama with inheritance arithmetic.
- **ʿAlī ibn Muḥammad ibn Abī ʿĪsā al-Anṣārī — reported teacher.** Associated with astronomy.

### Students and collaborators

- **Ibn al-Ṣaffār — documented student and probable collaborator.** Participated in the Andalusi recension of al-Khwārizmī’s tables. He later taught in Córdoba and moved to Dénia after the civil war began.
- **Ibn al-Samḥ — documented student.** Major mathematician, astronomer, and astrolabe writer.
- **Al-Zahrāwī — documented student or associate.** Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa says he learned much of his mathematics from Maslama and remained associated with him for a time.
- **Al-Kirmānī — reported student.** Geometer and physician whose own eastern journey was later transferred incorrectly to his teacher.
- **Abū Muslim ʿUmar ibn Aḥmad ibn Khaldūn — documented student.** A Sevillian scholar of geometry, astrology, medicine, and philosophical disciplines.
- **Ibn Bargūth — reported member of the school.**

### Intellectual predecessors

- **Ptolemy:** major textual authority, studied critically but not personally connected.
- **Al-Khwārizmī:** source of the zīj that Maslama revised.
- **Al-Battānī:** source of planetary-equation material summarized by Maslama.
- **Menelaus:** theorem used in Maslama’s spherical astronomy.

### Patrons

The Umayyad court is the likely institutional background, but no particular caliph, chamberlain, or official is securely documented as Maslama’s personal patron.

### Rivals or critics

No personal enemy is known. Ṣāʿid al-Andalusī, writing later, criticized Maslama’s retention of erroneous passages from al-Khwārizmī while simultaneously ranking him as the leading Andalusi mathematician of his age.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, or Financial Questions

### Results of the dedicated adversarial search

Searches in English, Spanish, and Arabic combining Maslama’s name with terms for controversy, scandal, accusation, lawsuit, fraud, conspiracy, and criticism—including material published or indexed in 2024–2026—found no credible evidence of:

- prosecution;
- imprisonment;
- lawsuit;
- embezzlement;
- financial fraud;
- political violence;
- corruption;
- espionage;
- abuse;
- criminal accusation.

This absence is unsurprising for a tenth-century scholar whose basic domestic biography is unknown.

The significant negative or contested material is historiographical:

1. **Ṣāʿid’s technical criticism:** Maslama transmitted errors from al-Khwārizmī without flagging them.
2. **Astrological prophecy:** he reportedly predicted ruin, slaughter, and famine from a planetary conjunction.
3. **False or disputed authorship:** magic, talismanic rituals, alchemy, occult philosophy, and alleged Ismāʿīlī propaganda were attached to his name for centuries.
4. **Modern exaggeration:** popular accounts credit him with discoveries or inventions that are not supported by the surviving texts.

### No evidentiary basis for suppressive or criminal narratives

No medieval source found alleges sorcery proceedings, heresy prosecution, poisoning, fraud, court intrigue, or persecution. Although magical literature later circulated under his name, no evidence shows that Maslama ibn Aḥmad was investigated or punished for it.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. Author of the *Picatrix*

**Story:** Maslama al-Majrīṭī composed *Ghāyat al-ḥakīm wa-aḥaqq al-natījatayn bi-l-taqdīm*, later translated into Castilian and Latin and known as the *Picatrix*.

**Circulation:** the attribution became widespread in later Arabic manuscript culture. The alchemist al-Jildakī and Ibn Khaldūn helped establish it; Latin and modern European scholarship then treated “Pseudo-Majrīṭī” as the author.

**Evidence against:**

- The *Ghāya* says that its author completed the preceding *Rutba* in 343 AH/954 CE and the *Ghāya* in 348 AH/959 CE.
- If Maslama ibn Aḥmad was born around 950, that chronology is impossible.
- Early biographical notices of Maslama list mathematical and astronomical works but not the *Rutba* or *Ghāya*.
- Maribel Fierro identified Maslama ibn Qāsim al-Qurṭubī (d. 964) as the stronger candidate.
- Godefroid de Callataÿ and Sébastien Moureau supplied additional manuscript evidence supporting that separation.

**Present status:** **disputed attribution, generally rejected for Maslama ibn Aḥmad.** Specialist scholarship commonly assigns both works to Maslama ibn Qāsim.

**Effect on reputation:** the attribution turned an observational astronomer and mathematician into one of medieval Europe’s best-known supposed occult authors. It remains dominant in popular occult publishing and is still repeated in catalogues and online biographies.

### 2. Author of the *Rutbat al-ḥakīm* and discoverer of conservation of mass

**Story:** Maslama wrote an alchemical work describing heating mercury in a sealed vessel and thereby anticipated conservation of mass.

**Evidence:** the experiment appears in the *Rutbat al-ḥakīm*, historically attributed to al-Majrīṭī.

**Problem:** if the modern attribution to Maslama ibn Qāsim is correct, the experiment cannot be counted among Maslama ibn Aḥmad’s achievements. The language “discovered the law of conservation of mass” is also a modern retrospective interpretation rather than a medieval scientific formulation.

**Present status:** **misattribution to the subject; broader priority claim overstated.**

### 3. Introduction of the *Epistles of the Brethren of Purity*

**Story:** Maslama traveled east, obtained the *Rasāʾil Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ*, brought them to Iberia, and perhaps wrote or edited their comprehensive epistle.

**Competing evidence:**

- Ṣāʿid explicitly says that Maslama’s pupil **al-Kirmānī** traveled to Ḥarrān and introduced the epistles into al-Andalus.
- Research on Maslama ibn Qāsim also associates that different Maslama with an eastern *riḥla* and possible transmission of the corpus.
- Later manuscripts created a philosophical “ladder” joining the *Rasāʾil*, *Rutba*, and *Ghāya*, then assigned the whole construction to the famous astronomer.

**Present status:** **disputed and probably false for Maslama ibn Aḥmad.**

### 4. Direct translator from Greek

**Story:** Maslama knew Greek and translated Ptolemy’s *Planisphaerium*.

**Evidence against:** philological analysis indicates that he revised, commented upon, and expanded an existing eastern Arabic translation.

**Present status:** **older scholarly claim superseded.**

### 5. “The Euclid of Spain”

This title is frequent in modern popular biographies. The early source consulted calls him the foremost mathematician and astronomer of his time in al-Andalus but does not use the exact epithet. It should be treated as a later honorific paraphrase unless an earlier source is produced.

### 6. “Fátima de Madrid”

As detailed above, this is a modern legend unsupported by medieval evidence. Its endurance illustrates how an encyclopedia entry, literary imagery, educational calendars, and internet repetition can manufacture a family biography.

### 7. Observation of the 1004 eclipse and SN 1006

Popular Spanish biographies sometimes say Maslama observed the solar eclipse of 1004 and the supernova of 1006.

The specialist sources consulted securely attest his Regulus observation and his interest in the 1006/07 Saturn–Jupiter conjunction. They do not provide equally firm manuscript evidence that he personally observed the 1004 eclipse or SN 1006. These claims remain **unverified** in this dossier.

## Works by the Person

Because titles vary by manuscript and later cataloguing practice, this list separates secure, reported, lost, derivative, and falsely attributed works.

### Genuine or strongly supported

#### 1. Recension of al-Khwārizmī’s *Zīj al-Sindhind*

- **Arabic descriptive title:** revision or adaptation of زيـج السندهند by Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī.
- **Date:** late tenth century.
- **Authorship:** Maslama, with probable work by Ibn al-Ṣaffār.
- **Status:** original Arabic recension largely lost; preserved substantially through Adelard of Bath’s twelfth-century Latin translation.
- **Contents:** chronology, planetary mean motions, conjunctions, oppositions, crescent visibility, trigonometric and astrological tables, Córdoba coordinates.
- **Importance:** one of the principal channels by which early Islamic astronomical tables entered Latin Europe.

#### 2. *Taʿālīq li-Maslama b. Aḥmad al-Andalusī ʿalā kitāb Baṭlamiyūs fī tasṭīḥ basīṭ al-kura*

تعاليق لمسلمة بن أحمد الأندلسي على كتاب بطلميوس في تسطيح بسيط الكرة

- **English:** Notes by Maslama ibn Aḥmad al-Andalusī on Ptolemy’s book on the projection of the sphere.
- **Date:** before his death; associated material dated 367 AH/978 CE.
- **Status:** surviving Arabic manuscript tradition; also transmitted in Latin.
- **Function:** revision and commentary on Ptolemy’s *Planisphaerium*.

#### 3. *Faṣl laysa min al-kitāb min kalām Maslama b. Aḥmad*

فصل ليس من الكتاب من كلام مسلمة بن أحمد

- **English:** A chapter not belonging to the book, from the words of Maslama ibn Aḥmad.
- **Date:** fixed-star table dated 367 AH/978 CE.
- **Status:** survives in Paris, BnF arabe 4821, fols. 76r–81v.
- **Contents:** zodiac and horizon projection, location of stars on the astrolabe rete, astrolabe-plate construction, star table.
- **Edition:** Vernet and Catalá, 1965; corrections and study by Kunitzsch and Lorch, 1994.

### Early-reported but lost

#### 4. *Kitāb al-Muʿāmalāt*

كتاب المعاملات

- **English:** Book of Applied or Commercial Arithmetic.
- **Date:** before 398 AH/1007–08.
- **Source:** listed by Ṣāʿid and Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa.
- **Status:** lost.
- **Probable subjects:** commercial calculation and other practical arithmetic; possible relationship to *Liber mahameleth* remains conjectural.

#### 5. *Ikhtiṣār taʿdīl al-kawākib min zīj al-Battānī*

اختصار تعديل الكواكب من زيج البتاني

- **English:** Abridgment of the planetary equations from al-Battānī’s zīj.
- **Status:** lost.
- **Source:** listed by Ṣāʿid and Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa.

#### 6. Notes on Menelaus’s theorem

- **Reported form:** notes or a mathematical treatment related to *al-shakl al-qaṭṭāʿ*, the transversal theorem used in spherical astronomy.
- **Status:** fragmentary or known through related texts and later analysis.
- **Attribution:** accepted as related to Maslama’s mathematical activity, though exact boundaries of the work are uncertain.

### Disputed or rejected attributions

#### 7. *Rutbat al-ḥakīm* — رتبة الحكيم

- **English:** The Rank/Step of the Sage.
- **Traditional attribution:** Maslama al-Majrīṭī.
- **Current attribution:** generally Maslama ibn Qāsim al-Qurṭubī.

## Death, Burial, and Final Status

Maslama died before the Córdoba *fitna*, most probably in 398 AH/1007–08 CE. The alternative date 395 AH/1004–05 appears in parts of the manuscript and reference tradition.

No reliable evidence identifies:

- his burial place;

His students survived into the taifa period and transmitted the school beyond Córdoba, especially toward Granada, Dénia, Zaragoza, Seville, and Toledo.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Andalusi scientific school

Maslama’s most direct legacy was pedagogical. His students carried arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, astrology, medicine, and instrument-making through the political fragmentation that followed Córdoba’s collapse.

The school helped establish the technical environment from which later Andalusi astronomy emerged. It preceded and indirectly prepared the work of al-Zarqālī and the Toledan astronomical tradition.

### Latin transmission

The al-Khwārizmī–Maslama tables entered Latin through Adelard of Bath. Maslama’s recension of the *Planisphaerium* was translated by Hermann of Carinthia or Dalmatia in 1143 and circulated with its additional material.

His astrolabe-related procedures influenced later Latin and Alphonsine writings. This influence is textual and computational; it does not demonstrate personal contact with Christian scholars, most of whom lived after his death.

### Occult afterlife

The erroneous attachment of the *Picatrix* gave “al-Majrīṭī” an afterlife far larger than that of many medieval astronomers. Renaissance and later readers associated the name with:

- astral magic;
- talismans;
- planetary spirits;
- Hermetic philosophy;
- ritual substances;
- alchemy;
- occult cosmology.

This reputation is historically important even though the central authorship claim is probably false.

### Modern Madrid commemoration

In May 2019 Madrid’s municipal government formally changed “Plaza de Maslama” to **Plaza de Maslama Al-Mayriti**, in the Hispanoamérica neighborhood of Chamartín. The act entered the amended name in the city’s official street register.

### Astronomical namesake

The exoplanet **υ Andromedae d** received the approved name **Majriti** through the International Astronomical Union’s 2019 NameExoWorlds campaign. The IAU report states that the name honors Abū al-Qāsim al-Qurṭubī al-Majrīṭī. The wording illustrates continuing instability in his extended name but clearly intends the Madrid astronomer.

### Modern popular image

Maslama is presented variously as:

- the first famous scientist born in Madrid;
- “the Euclid of Spain”;
- founder of the Andalusi mathematical school;
- a proto-chemist;
- an occult master;
- father and collaborator of a woman astronomer.

Only the first three have substantial grounding, and even “Euclid of Spain” is a later honorific rather than a securely attested medieval title.
