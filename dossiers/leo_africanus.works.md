## Works by Leo Africanus

Leo Africanus produced a handful of minor scholarly pieces in Rome and Bologna, but one book accounts for essentially his entire reputation. He did not write it as a free author pursuing a project of his own choosing: he wrote it as a converted former captive, under papal patronage, years after he had last set foot in most of the places it describes.

### Composition in Rome

Leo, born al-Hasan ibn Muhammad al-Wazzan in Granada or shortly after his family's exile from it, had traveled widely across Morocco and the Maghreb, and probably into the western Sudan and Egypt, as a young diplomatic agent for the Wattasid court of Fez. Returning from a mission in the eastern Mediterranean in 1518, he was captured by Christian corsairs and presented to Pope Leo X in Rome, where he was baptized in 1520 and took the name by which he is now known. Over the following years, working from memory rather than from any library of Arabic sources he could carry with him, he composed a description of Africa's geography, peoples, cities, and customs in Italian, the language of the court and patrons who now supported him. He completed the manuscript in Rome on 10 March 1526. It survives as a single manuscript, now held at the Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale di Roma, long thought lost until an Italian scholar identified it there in 1931.

The book moves region by region: the kingdom of Marrakesh, the kingdom of Fez, the central Maghreb around Tlemcen, the coastal strip from Tunis to Tripoli, the Sahara, the kingdoms of the western and central Sudan, and Egypt, closing with notes on rivers, animals, and plants. Leo's firsthand knowledge is strongest for Morocco and weakest for the deep interior of West Africa and the regions toward the Nile, where his account mixes personal recollection with secondhand report and occasional error, most famously in his description of Timbuktu's wealth and learning, which did more than any other single text to fix Europe's image of that city.

### Ramusio's printing and its distortions

Leo never published the book himself, and he had likely returned to North Africa, and probably to Islam, by the time it reached print. The Venetian editor Giovanni Battista Ramusio obtained a copy and printed a reworked Italian version in 1550, as part of his great compilation of travel writing, Delle navigationi et viaggi. Ramusio openly revised wording, structure, and emphasis, and comparison with the surviving 1526 manuscript shows real differences between what Leo wrote and what Europe actually read for the next four and a half centuries. From Ramusio's printed text rather than the manuscript came a French translation in 1556, a Latin translation the same year, and, in 1600, John Pory's English version, which was translated from the Latin rather than directly from Italian and compounded the earlier layer of change. Alexis Epaulard's influential French edition of 1956 still relied mainly on Ramusio, checked only in places against the manuscript. It was not until 2023 that Leo's original 1526 text appeared in English translation for the first time, prepared by Anthony Ossa-Richardson and Richard Oosterhoff, allowing readers to compare his own wording directly against the version Ramusio had shaped for print.

### Minor surviving work

A few smaller pieces survive from his Italian years: a 1521 Arabic rendering of the Pauline Epistles made for a Roman patron, a partial Arabic-Hebrew-Latin medical word list from around 1524 produced with the physician Jacob Mantino, and a short collection of brief biographies of Muslim and Jewish scholars, printed only long after his death, in 1664. None rivals the African geography in length or influence.
