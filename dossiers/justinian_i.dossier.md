# Justinian I: Research Dossier

## Scope and source method

This dossier reconstructs the life and reign of Justinian I from contemporary and near-contemporary narrative sources, Justinianic legislation and conciliar documents, material and architectural evidence, and modern historical and legal scholarship. The principal narrative witnesses are Procopius of Caesarea (*Wars*, *Buildings*, and the posthumously circulated *Secret History* or *Anecdota*), John Malalas, John the Lydian, John of Ephesus, Evagrius Scholasticus, Agathias, Marcellinus Comes, Victor of Tunnuna, Zacharias/Pseudo-Zacharias, Jordanes, Corippus, and later chroniclers. These sources do not offer a single stable portrait.

Procopius's *Wars* is a classicizing contemporary history, often critical but constrained by publication during the regime. *Buildings* is an imperial panegyric that transfers to Justinian credit for works begun by predecessors and suppresses failures. The *Secret History*, probably composed about 550 but unpublished during the principals' lives, combines genuine inside information, senatorial and administrative grievances, sexual invective, numerically impossible accusations, and supernatural demonology. Its Procopian authorship is now generally accepted; the truth of each assertion must be tested separately. John the Lydian writes as an official injured by administrative change. Ecclesiastical writers judge the emperor through rival doctrinal commitments. Justinian's laws are primary evidence for enacted policy and official ideology, but a law's repetition can indicate failure of enforcement rather than success.

Dates below follow the conventional chronology. Justinian's birth year remains uncertain (usually 482; 483 is also given), and 11 May is traditional rather than securely contemporary.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Birth name:** Petrus Sabbatius (Greek forms include Petros Sabbatios).
- **Adoptive/dynastic name:** Iustinianus/Justinianus, indicating adoption by his maternal uncle, the future emperor Justin I.
- **Attested full name:** *Flavius Petrus Sabbatius Iustinianus*, preserved on the consular diptychs of 521. By this period *Flavius* often functioned as an honorific status-name rather than an inherited personal name.
- **Imperial titulature:** Variants include *Imperator Caesar Flavius Iustinianus ... pius felix inclitus victor ac triumphator semper Augustus*. Victory titles accumulated in legal prefaces and ceremonial usage: Alamannicus, Gothicus, Francicus, Germanicus, Anticus, Alanicus, Vandalicus, Africanus.
- **Common later names:** Justinian I; Justinian the Great; Emperor Justinian; in Orthodox tradition, Saint Justinian the Great. He called himself Roman emperor; “Byzantine emperor” is a modern historiographical label.
- **Birth:** probably 482, sometimes 483; the traditional 11 May is not securely established.
- **Birthplace:** Tauresium in the civil diocese of Dacia, near Scupi (modern Skopje). Its exact archaeological identification remains debated. Justiniana Prima, founded or enlarged by Justinian near his home region, is generally associated with Caričin Grad in modern Serbia; it should not simply be equated with Tauresium.
- **Death:** night of 13/14 November 565, normally dated 14 November, in the Great Palace at Constantinople; about eighty-two or eighty-three.
- **Cause:** apparently natural death in bed. No contemporary evidence establishes poisoning or murder.
- **Burial:** imperial mausoleum adjoining the Church of the Holy Apostles, Constantinople. The tomb was later opened and despoiled, especially during the Latin sack of 1204; the church was demolished after the Ottoman conquest.
- **Reign:** Caesar and co-emperor from 1 April 527; sole emperor from Justin I's death on 1 August 527 until 14 November 565.
- **Predecessor:** Justin I, maternal uncle and adoptive father.
- **Successor:** Justin II, son of Justinian's sister Vigilantia.
- **Religion:** Chalcedonian/Nicene Christianity, with sustained attempts to formulate a Cyrillian interpretation capable of recovering anti-Chalcedonian Christians. His alleged final adoption of Aphthartodocetism rests principally on Evagrius and is discussed below.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and origins

Justinian's mother was **Vigilantia**, sister of Justin I. His father is conventionally called **Sabbatius**, inferred from Justinian's birth name. Almost nothing securely documented survives about Sabbatius's life, occupation, ancestry, or death. Procopius describes Justin's family as poor Illyrian peasants. The family came from the Latin-speaking Balkan interior; modern formulations such as “Illyro-Roman” or “Thraco-Roman” are reasonable geographical-linguistic descriptions but cannot establish a modern ethnicity.

The sixteenth- and seventeenth-century story that Justinian was a Slav originally named **Upravda**, son of Istok and Bigleniza, derives from a purported biography attributed to the abbot Theophilus and publicized by Niccolò Alamanni. The document is now treated as spurious. The contemporary name Petrus Sabbatius, Latin as Justinian's stated ancestral language, and the chronology of Slavic settlement weigh against the legend.

### Uncle, adoption, siblings, and dynastic kin

- **Justin I** (emperor 518–527), Vigilantia's brother, migrated from the Balkans, rose through the army, and commanded the Excubitors before his unexpected accession. He brought or summoned his nephew to Constantinople, adopted him, educated him, and promoted him. Later hostile writers sometimes represent the old, relatively uneducated Justin as Justinian's puppet. Justinian was influential, but he held no imperial title until 527 and Justin retained independent advisers and decisions.
- **Vigilantia**, Justinian's sister (often distinguished in modern writing as Vigilantia the Younger), married Dulcissimus or an otherwise obscure husband in differing reconstructions. Her children included **Justin II**, **Marcellus**, and **Praeiecta**. Justin II became emperor and married Theodora's niece Sophia.
- **Germanus**, usually identified as Justinian's cousin, became a senior general and potential successor. His children included Justin, Justinian, and Justina. His second marriage to the Ostrogothic princess Matasuntha in 550 may have offered a Romano-Gothic dynastic settlement; he died before taking command in Italy.
- **Boraides** and **Justus**, brothers of Germanus in the usual reconstruction, also held office. The precise extended genealogy is incomplete.

### Marriage and children

Justinian married **Theodora**, probably in 525. A law of Justin I (Codex 5.4.23), issued after Empress Euphemia's death, enabled repentant actresses restored by imperial grant to marry men of senatorial rank; it cleared the legal barrier to their union. Theodora was made Augusta when Justinian became co-emperor in 527 and remained his closest political partner until her death on 28 June 548.

No child of Justinian and Theodora is securely known. Procopius says Theodora had a daughter from before the marriage and tells a sensational, weakly corroborated story about a son named John who appeared at court and disappeared. Syriac tradition says Theodora sought Saint Sabas's prayers for a child and was refused because of her non-Chalcedonian sympathies. These stories do not establish that Justinian fathered any child. He died childless.

## Childhood and Early Life

No contemporary narrative gives a detailed childhood. Justinian was born in or near Tauresium into the provincial family from which Justin had already departed for military service. He probably grew up first in the Latin-speaking Balkans and was brought to Constantinople while young, but the date is unknown. Claims that he was a swineherd or an illiterate peasant commonly conflate him with hostile portrayals of Justin's rural origin.

His later legislation calls Latin the ancestral language, while his theological activity and later Novels demonstrate command of the Greek administrative and intellectual world. Contemporary physical descriptions, especially John Malalas's, make him short or moderate in stature, fair or ruddy, round-faced, curly-haired, attractive, with a receding hairline and later graying hair and beard. Procopius says he resembled the emperor Domitian; this comparison also activates a literary model of the tyrant.

## Education and Formation

Justinian's formal teachers are not known. Justin arranged an education in Constantinople appropriate to an imperial protégé. His later work shows sustained formation in Roman history, administrative practice, law, and Christian theology. It is unsafe to infer that he personally mastered every technical detail of the Digest; Tribonian and the commissions supplied the juristic expertise. His repeated theological interventions, however, show more than ceremonial interest.

His principal formative structures were:

- the late Roman army and palace guard through Justin;
- Constantinople's court, senate, circus factions, and imperial bureaucracy;
- the Chalcedonian settlement and the unresolved Acacian and anti-Chalcedonian schisms;
- the Roman ideal of a universal emperor who legislates, conquers, builds, and defines orthodoxy;
- collaboration with specialists: Tribonian in law; John the Cappadocian and Peter Barsymes in finance; Belisarius and Narses in war; Anthemius and Isidore in architecture; bishops and monks in doctrine.

Procopius depicts him as an ascetic worker who slept little, ate sparingly, fasted, and attended personally to an enormous flow of petitions and policy. The hostile sobriquet “the emperor who never sleeps” nevertheless agrees with the observable volume of legislation and intervention.

## Early Career, 500s–527

The early career is only intermittently visible. Justinian served as a **candidatus**, one of the ceremonial palace guards. A late tradition says the Excubitors proposed him for the throne at Anastasius I's death in July 518 and he refused; modern historians generally reject this as retrojection. Justin, commander of the Excubitors, instead secured the throne amid palace payments and factional maneuvering.

Under Justin I, Justinian advanced rapidly:

- He helped restore communion with Rome and end the Acacian Schism in 519, corresponding with papal authorities and supporting the Chalcedonian line.
- He became *comes domesticorum* and then **magister militum praesentalis**, following the murder of the powerful general Vitalian in 520. Justinian was suspected by contemporaries or later writers of involvement, but there is no judicial finding or decisive evidence.
- He was consul in 521. His inaugural games were extraordinarily expensive and politically conspicuous; later sources report mass animal spectacles and lavish distributions. The display established him publicly as the regime's heir-apparent.
- He held the rank of patrician and the title *nobilissimus*.
- He favored the Blue circus faction, whose militants Procopius accuses him of protecting. The scale and direction of his personal responsibility remain disputed.
- He met and married Theodora after legal impediments were removed, probably in 525.
- Justin, seriously ill, made him Caesar and Augustus/co-emperor on 1 April 527. Justin died on 1 August, leaving Justinian sole emperor.

## Chronological Life History

### 527–531: accession, law, eastern war, and coercive unity

Justinian and Theodora were crowned together, and her name, image, audiences, patronage, personnel network, and correspondence gave her a power unusual even for an Augusta. She was not legally a second emperor, but contemporaries treated her intercession and hostility as politically consequential.

The Persian war inherited from Justin I continued. Belisarius, with Procopius as legal adviser, defeated a Sasanian army at Dara in 530 but was defeated at Callinicum in 531. Justinian also reorganized alliances with the Ghassanid Arab ruler al-Harith ibn Jabalah, whom he elevated as a counterweight to the Sasanian-aligned Lakhmids.

Domestically, Justinian appointed a ten-man commission under **John of Cappadocia** to consolidate imperial constitutions. The first *Codex Iustinianus* took effect in 529. In December 530 the constitution *Deo auctore* ordered **Tribonian** to assemble a commission to excerpt and harmonize classical jurisprudence.

The regime intensified inherited anti-heterodox legislation. Pagans were excluded from teaching and public careers unless baptized; Manichaeans faced death; heretical and non-Christian communities suffered disabilities in office, testimony, inheritance, worship, and property. The philosophical school associated with Damascius in Athens ceased operation around 529. The common phrase “Justinian closed Plato's Academy” is imprecise: Plato's original Academy had no continuous institutional existence, and the exact relation between the law and the Athenian philosophers' departure is debated. Damascius, Simplicius, and colleagues traveled to the Sasanian court and later returned under safeguards in the peace settlement.

In 529–531 a major **Samaritan revolt** in Palestine, associated with the leader Julian ben Sabar, attacked Christians and imperial authority. Imperial and allied Ghassanid forces suppressed it with killing, enslavement, confiscation, and forced conversion. Contemporary death totals are inconsistent and inflated, but mass violence and demographic-economic damage are secure.

### 532: Endless Peace and the Nika insurrection

After Shah Kavadh I died, Justinian and Khosrow I concluded the so-called **Endless Peace** in 532. Rome paid 11,000 pounds of gold. It was a strategic purchase of freedom in the east, not tribute-free peace, and it ended when Khosrow invaded in 540.

In January, arrests after Blue-Green street violence, grievance against the prefect John the Cappadocian, quaestor Tribonian, and urban prefect Eudaemon, heavy fiscal enforcement, and hostility toward autocratic government fused into the **Nika revolt**. On 13 January factions acclaimed “Nika” (“Conquer”) and attacked public buildings. Justinian dismissed the three ministers but failed to restore control. Fires destroyed the old Hagia Sophia, Hagia Eirene, the senate house, baths, and palace approaches. Senators connected to Anastasius's dynasty became focal points; the crowd acclaimed Hypatius emperor.

Procopius says Justinian prepared to flee and Theodora stopped him with a speech culminating in the idea that imperial purple is a noble burial shroud. The council, proposed flight, and Theodora's intervention are plausible; the polished speech is Procopius's literary composition, not a transcript. Narses used money and factional ties to detach Blue supporters. Belisarius and Mundus then entered the Hippodrome with troops. Procopius gives about 30,000 dead; other ancient figures differ. The killing of a trapped crowd is secure, but the exact total is not. Hypatius and his brother Pompeius were executed and their property confiscated, though some property was later restored to their families.

The massacre ended the most serious internal threat to Justinian. Tribonian and John soon returned to office. The destruction also cleared space for the regime's reconstruction of the ceremonial center.

### 532–534: Hagia Sophia and the Corpus Juris Civilis

On 23 February 532 work began on the new **Hagia Sophia**. Justinian selected the mathematician/engineer **Anthemius of Tralles** and geometer **Isidore of Miletus**, not conventional master builders. Tens of thousands of workers, imported colored stones and columns, imperial finance, and novel combinations of central dome, pendentives, semi-domes, basilical space, light, and liturgical choreography produced the church in five years and ten months. Justinian and Patriarch Menas dedicated it on 27 December 537. The saying “Solomon, I have surpassed you” is a later reported imperial exclamation, not independently recorded verbatim at the ceremony.

The legal project proceeded simultaneously:

- **Codex Iustinianus**, first edition promulgated 7 April 529; now lost except for fragments and later references.
- **Digesta/Pandectae**, ordered 15 December 530, completed in three years, promulgated 16 December 533 and effective 30 December. Fifty books excerpted and reconciled writings of classical Roman jurists. Justinian forbade incompatible commentary and gave the compilation statutory force.
- **Institutiones**, elementary textbook prepared principally by Tribonian, Theophilus, and Dorotheus; promulgated 21 November and effective 30 December 533, with force of law.
- **Codex repetitae praelectionis**, revised Code promulgated 16 November and effective 29 December 534, replacing the 529 Code.
- **Novellae constitutiones**, new enactments after 534, mostly in Greek. No official complete collection was made under Justinian; the later Corpus includes private transmissions such as the *Epitome Juliani*, *Authenticum*, and Greek collections.

“Corpus Juris Civilis” is a medieval/early-modern collective title, not Justinian's own title. The emperor initiated, authorized, legislated, and ideologically framed the project; the technical compilation was collective. Tribonian was indispensable. Claims that Justinian personally wrote the Digest or “invented Roman law” are false.

### 533–534: conquest of Vandal Africa

Justinian used the Vandal king Gelimer's overthrow of the pro-Roman Hilderic as a cause for intervention. Despite memories of failed fifth-century expeditions, he dispatched Belisarius with roughly 15,000 fighting men and a large fleet in 533. Victories at Ad Decimum and Tricamarum brought Carthage and the Vandal kingdom under imperial control. Gelimer surrendered in 534. Belisarius celebrated a triumph in Constantinople; Justinian assumed *Vandalicus* and *Africanus* and displayed spoils associated with the Temple at Jerusalem.

Annexation did not produce immediate peace. Military mutinies, Berber/Moorish wars, religious property disputes, and unpopular taxation persisted until John Troglita's victories in the later 540s. Africa nevertheless proved the most durable of Justinian's western reconquests.

### 535–540: first Gothic War and the high imperial program

The killing of the Ostrogothic regent **Amalasuntha** by Theodahad gave Justinian a cause for war. Procopius's *Wars* makes Theodahad responsible; the *Secret History* alleges Theodora secretly ordered the murder through the envoy Peter because she feared Amalasuntha as a rival. No independent evidence proves Theodora's order.

Belisarius took Sicily in 535, crossed to Italy in 536, captured Naples after entering through an aqueduct, and entered Rome on 9 December. The sack at Naples and subsequent reprisals were real consequences of imperial conquest. He defended Rome through the Gothic siege of 537–538. Command conflict between Belisarius and Narses contributed to failures, including the inability to save Milan, destroyed in 539 with mass killing and enslavement; Procopius's figure of 300,000 adult male deaths is impossible.

In 540 Belisarius entered Ravenna after feigning acceptance of a Gothic offer to make him western emperor. Whether Justinian viewed this stratagem as dangerous disloyalty is uncertain. Belisarius returned with King Witigis and the Gothic treasure, but without the conventional triumph he had received after Africa.

Justinian founded or elevated **Justiniana Prima** near his homeland as an administrative and ecclesiastical center and pursued a vast building program: fortifications from the Danube and Balkans to Armenia, Mesopotamia, Africa, and the Black Sea; churches, monasteries, bridges, cisterns, aqueducts, roads, hospitals, baths, granaries, and restored cities. Procopius catalogues hundreds in *Buildings*, but sometimes credits Justinian with earlier construction or offers idealized descriptions.

### 540–545: renewed Persian war, catastrophe, and plague

Khosrow I broke the Endless Peace in 540, crossed Syria, extorted cities, captured and burned **Antioch**, and deported inhabitants to a new settlement near Ctesiphon deliberately styled “Khosrow's Better Antioch.” War spread to Lazica after the Lazi, resentful of Roman administration and monopoly practices, invited Persian intervention. Belisarius campaigned in the east with limited results. A fifty-year peace would not come until 562.

In 541–542 epidemic disease arrived through Egypt and reached Constantinople. Procopius, John of Ephesus, and Evagrius independently describe buboes, fever, delirium, enormous mortality, disrupted burial, labor scarcity, and social breakdown. Ancient daily totals and modern empire-wide estimates vary radically. Ancient-DNA studies have identified *Yersinia pestis* in sixth-century graves, but historians still dispute total demographic and fiscal impact.

Justinian contracted the disease and recovered. During his incapacitation, talk about succession implicated generals; Theodora acted against Buzes and other perceived threats. The regime organized corpse removal and burial, but legislation also tried to restrain rising wages and prices. Recurrent waves followed for generations.

The Persian invasion, plague, and renewed Gothic resistance under Totila overwhelmed the earlier appearance of effortless expansion. In 545 Justinian agreed to a five-year truce in the east, excluding Lazica, with another substantial payment.

### 541–548: fiscal strain, Theodora's network, and the second Italian crisis

The fall of John the Cappadocian in 541 followed a palace trap organized through Antonina and Theodora. John was stripped of office, wealth, and rank and later accused in a homicide affair. The episode reveals factional prosecution and confiscation; Procopius's details require caution.

**Peter Barsymes**, a former banker or money changer, succeeded in fiscal leadership and twice served as praetorian prefect. Procopius accuses him of debasing or manipulating supplies, monopolies, office-selling, and personal enrichment. Some fiscal measures are corroborated by administrative stress; the biographical caricature is hostile evidence, not a conviction record.

Totila reunited much of Italy. Belisarius returned in 544 but lacked troops and money, and Rome fell in 546, was recovered in 547, and fell again in 550. Italy's civilians endured siege, famine, enslavement, massacre, requisition, taxation, and destruction by all armies. The long war rather than a single side's policy ruined cities and agrarian systems.

Theodora died in 548, probably after a prolonged illness often identified as cancer, though the diagnosis is not secure. Justinian never remarried. He commemorated her in legislation, buildings, and ceremony and retained her allies, including her niece Sophia.

### 548–554: doctrinal struggle and final western victories

Theodora had protected an anti-Chalcedonian clerical network while Justinian officially enforced Chalcedon. Their apparently opposing patronage may have been personal conviction, coordinated imperial containment, or both. It helped produce a durable Syriac Miaphysite hierarchy under Jacob Baradaeus, contrary to Justinian's goal of institutional unity.

Seeking reconciliation, Justinian condemned the **Three Chapters**—Theodore of Mopsuestia, certain anti-Cyrilline writings of Theodoret, and the letter attributed to Ibas. Western bishops feared that this compromised Chalcedon. Pope **Vigilius**, brought from Rome to Constantinople under imperial pressure, alternately condemned and defended the Chapters. He suffered detention, threats, and violence around sanctuary disputes. The Second Council of Constantinople met 5 May–2 June 553 without his attendance and condemned the Chapters. His name was removed from the diptychs; after the council he accepted its doctrinal result. The coercive process produced schisms in northern Italy lasting decades.

In Italy, Germanus died in 550 before taking command. Justinian finally gave **Narses** adequate resources. Narses defeated and killed Totila at Taginae/Busta Gallorum in 552 and defeated Teia at Mons Lactarius later that year. Frankish and Alamannic invaders were defeated at the Volturnus/Casilinum in 554. The Pragmatic Sanction of 554 reorganized Italy, restored some senatorial property, confirmed selected acts of Gothic kings, and extended imperial administration and taxation. Resistance at Verona and Brixia continued until 561/562.

At the same time, Justinian intervened in a Visigothic civil war. Forces traditionally associated with the elderly Liberius seized a coastal zone in southern Hispania from 552, creating the province of **Spania**. Command details are debated. The enclave lasted into the early seventh century.

### 555–565: earthquakes, defense, final peace, conspiracy, and death

Earthquakes damaged Constantinople in 553 and severely in December 557. Hagia Sophia's first dome collapsed on 7 May 558, destroying liturgical furnishings. Justinian commissioned **Isidore the Younger** to rebuild the dome higher and lighter; the church was rededicated on 24 December 562, celebrated in Paul the Silentiary's *Ekphrasis*.

In 559 Kutrigur forces under Zabergan crossed the frozen Danube and approached Constantinople through earthquake-damaged defenses. The elderly Belisarius organized veterans, guards, and civilians and repelled the immediate threat at Melantias. Justinian then appeared ceremonially in Thrace; no evidence shows him personally commanding a pitched battle. He used diplomacy and Utigur rivalry to break the invasion.

The **fifty-year peace** with Khosrow I in 562 settled Lazica in Roman hands while Justinian accepted annual payments to Persia, formally expressed as defense subsidies. The treaty brought an expensive but meaningful eastern stabilization.

In 562 a palace conspiracy involving bankers or businessmen was uncovered. Under interrogation conspirators implicated Belisarius. A senatorial court found him guilty or accepted the accusation; he lost access and honors and was confined to his palace. Justinian restored him in 563. The later story that the emperor blinded the general and reduced him to begging—“give an obol to Belisarius”—is medieval legend without contemporary support.

In 564/565 Justinian allegedly adopted **Aphthartodocetism**, the teaching associated with Julian of Halicarnassus that Christ's body was incorruptible from the Incarnation, and ordered bishops to assent. Evagrius says Patriarch Eutychius was deposed in January 565 and Patriarch Anastasius of Antioch prepared resistance. The edict itself is not preserved; other near-contemporary evidence is thin. The policy is therefore a documented ancient report, not an extant decree whose exact doctrine can be examined.

Justinian died without publicly indisputable succession arrangements. The chamberlain Callinicus told Justin II that the dying emperor had designated him; this private report cannot be verified. Justin, supported by Sophia, Patriarch John Scholasticus, and the Excubitors, reached the palace first and was acclaimed. A rival cousin, Justin son of Germanus, was later murdered under Justin II.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

Modern categories such as companies, private investment portfolios, patents, or personal corporate ownership do not fit a sixth-century emperor. Justinian controlled the fisc, imperial estates, monopolies, appointments, taxation, confiscations, tribute, military expenditure, and ceremonial distributions as institutions of office. Separating “personal wealth” from state and crown property is usually impossible.

### Administration and finance

- **Praetorian prefecture:** John the Cappadocian reorganized provincial administration, reduced or combined offices, accelerated fiscal collection, and attacked some bureaucratic privileges. Novels 8 and 17 denounced sale of office and ordered governors to remain accessible, while appointment fees and patronage persisted.
- **Taxation:** wars, diplomacy, fortification, building, and plague recovery demanded exceptional revenue. Procopius and John the Lydian report arrears collection, reassessment, monopoly profits, confiscation, and pressure on curiales and landowners. Specific universal claims—such as Procopius's enormous casualty and confiscation totals—cannot be accepted literally.
- **Coinage:** the gold solidus remained stable. Copper coin reform under Anastasius continued; large folles carried clear denominations and regnal dating. Later weight reductions and fiscal manipulation occurred in the context of war and plague.
- **Office and justice:** Procopius alleges systematic sale of offices and judgments by Leo, Tribonian, Peter Barsymes, Justinian, and Theodora. Laws repeatedly condemn these practices. Individual bribery allegations are often unadjudicated; the survival and repetition of prohibitions show that venality was a structural problem.
- **Consulship:** Justinian ended the regular annual non-imperial consulship after 541, absorbing its ceremonial functions into imperial rule.

### Silk and industrial knowledge

Procopius reports that monks who had learned sericulture in a land called Serinda explained the process to Justinian and returned with silkworm eggs concealed in hollow canes, enabling production within the empire, probably in the 550s. The account is contemporary but geographically vague; “smuggling the secret from China” is a later simplification. State-regulated silk workshops and monopolies already existed for imported raw silk. Domestic sericulture reduced but did not end eastern dependence.

### Architecture and infrastructure

Principal projects included Hagia Sophia; Hagia Eirene; the Church of the Holy Apostles; Saints Sergius and Bacchus; reconstruction or patronage at Saint Catherine's monastery on Sinai; San Vitale and Sant'Apollinare in Classe at Ravenna within the imperial political-artistic system; Justiniana Prima; the Basilica Cistern; the Sangarius bridge; Dara's works; frontier forts; water systems; hospitals; hospices; and monasteries. Attribution varies: Procopius sometimes assigns earlier works to Justinian, and imperial “building” could mean commissioning, financing, repairing, or rhetorically claiming local work.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

Justinian's political language was not a later nationalist “Byzantinism.” He governed the **Roman Empire**, understood as Christian, universal, legally ordered, and providential. The often-used phrase *renovatio imperii* accurately describes restoration but is not securely his formal programmatic slogan. His western wars were opportunistic interventions justified by dynastic legitimacy and the recovery of Roman provinces.

His constitutional practice concentrated legislative, judicial, military, and ecclesiastical initiative in the emperor. The Institutes state that what pleases the prince has force of law, while imperial prefaces define rule as service to God, justice, and subjects. Petitioning and exhaustive legislation coexisted with executions, confiscation, coerced doctrine, and massacre.

Religiously he moved through several strategies:

1. Under Justin I, firm Chalcedonian reunion with Rome and removal of anti-Chalcedonian bishops.
2. In the 530s, conferences and “neo-Chalcedonian” formulas emphasizing Cyril's unity of Christ; deposition of Anthimus in 536 under papal pressure.
3. Protection of Chalcedonian institutions alongside Theodora's protection of Miaphysites.
4. Condemnation of Origenist propositions and then the Three Chapters, culminating in 553.
5. The reported final Aphthartodocetist turn, whose exact legal text is lost.

Policy toward Jews, Samaritans, pagans, Manichaeans, Arians, Montanists, and dissident Christians included graded civil disabilities, worship restrictions, confiscation, destruction or conversion of sanctuaries, forced baptism, exile, and capital punishment. Some laws preserved Jewish communal practice or intervened in synagogue liturgy; others subordinated it and authorized Greek scripture readings while restricting rabbinic interpretation. Enforcement varied by region and official.

Legislation also altered family and sexual law. It increased penalties for rape and abduction, protected some dowry and inheritance claims, improved routes from prostitution, regulated pimps, protected certain children born outside marriage, and gave slaves additional procedural routes to freedom. At the same time it restricted divorce, reaffirmed patriarchal household structures and slavery, criminalized same-sex sexual acts under older imperial provisions, and coupled sexual offenses with public punishment. Attributing all protective laws personally to Theodora is not demonstrable; attributing them solely to Justinian obscures court collaboration and juristic drafting.

## Controversies, Allegations, Political Violence, and Abuses

### Documented state actions

- The Hippodrome suppression in 532 killed a very large number of trapped insurgents and bystanders; ancient estimates reach 30,000 but are not verifiable.
- Hypatius and Pompeius were executed without a modern trial process; property was confiscated.
- Religious uniformity was enforced through legal disabilities, confiscation, closure/destruction of worship spaces, exile, forced baptism, and executions, with particularly severe violence against Samaritans and Manichaeans.
- Naples was sacked after capture in 536. The imperial-Gothic war inflicted repeated massacres, starvation, deportation, enslavement, and fiscal extraction across Italy.
- Pope Vigilius was removed from Rome by imperial agents, detained in Constantinople, subjected to coercion and sanctuary violence, and pressured to reverse positions.
- Officials and rivals, including John the Cappadocian and Belisarius, suffered politically shaped proceedings, confiscation or detention.
- Imperial administration used monopolies, compulsory requisition, arrears collection, and office-purchase structures despite statutes condemning abuse.

### Allegations requiring qualification

- **Murder of Vitalian (520):** Justinian benefited and was suspected; no surviving proof establishes that he ordered it.
- **Murder of Amalasuntha (535):** Theodahad's responsibility is secure. Theodora's secret order appears only in Procopius's hostile version and remains unverified.
- **Systematic sale of justice:** Procopius provides detailed allegations and laws show the structural problem; no surviving audit permits quantification or assigns every act personally to the imperial couple.
- **Confiscation and fabricated charges:** multiple sources document confiscatory government. The *Secret History* converts the pattern into a total explanation of the reign and must be checked case by case.
- **Sexual allegations about Theodora:** her theatrical background and an earlier sexual life receive partial independent support; Procopius's pornographic episodes employ ancient invective and cannot be treated as courtroom testimony.
- **“One trillion” or population-destroying death totals:** Procopius's arithmetic in *Secret History* is rhetorical and impossible.
- **Demonhood, headlessness, nocturnal transformations:** supernatural hostile mythology, not factual evidence.
- **Fraud, money laundering, modern corporate crime:** these are anachronistic legal categories. The relevant ancient practices are bribery, peculation, monopoly manipulation, sale of office, false accusation, confiscation, and diversion of public money. No surviving proceeding convicted Justinian personally of them.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### The sleepless emperor

**Story:** Justinian scarcely slept, fasted severely, worked through the night, and handled every affair himself. **Appearance:** Procopius, including hostile and laudatory works. **Circulation:** court and later historians. **Evidence:** the extraordinary output of laws and interventions supports relentless work, not literal sleeplessness. **Effect:** simultaneously the image of ascetic Christian stewardship and intrusive micromanagement.

### Justinian as a headless demon

**Story:** Palace attendants saw his face vanish or his head leave his body; a monk recognized the “Lord of Demons” on the throne; his mother allegedly conceived him by a demon. **Appearance:** Procopius, *Secret History*, especially chapter 12 and the climactic demon language of chapter 18. **Circulator:** an elite insider writing a clandestine indictment around 550. **Evidence:** none beyond reported visions and rumor; the motifs belong to ancient tyrant, Antichrist, and demonic-possession literature. **Effect:** made Justinian's fiscal and military catastrophes cosmological evil and supplied the most durable hostile image of the emperor.

### “The purple is the noblest shroud”

**Story:** During Nika, when Justinian planned flight, Theodora refused and declared imperial purple a fine burial shroud. **Appearance:** Procopius, *Wars* 1.24, in a crafted speech. **Evidence:** ancient historians conventionally composed speeches; the substance may preserve a real intervention, the wording is not stenographic. **Effect:** established Theodora as the regime's braver political will and Justinian as either prudently deliberative or cowardly.

### “Solomon, I have surpassed you”

**Story:** At Hagia Sophia's dedication Justinian compared the church with Solomon's Temple. **Appearance:** later Byzantine narrative tradition, often linked to the *Diegesis* of Hagia Sophia; it is not a secure contemporary quotation. **Evidence:** consistent with imperial building ideology but unattested as verbatim speech in Procopius, Paul the Silentiary, or the earliest record. **Effect:** became the epigram of Justinianic sacred kingship and architectural ambition.

### Upravda, the Slav emperor

**Story:** Justinian's original name was Upravda (“justice”), his relatives bore Slavic names, and he was Slavic. **Appearance:** a supposed life by “Theophilus,” promoted by Niccolò Alamanni in the seventeenth century and repeated in nationalist historiography. **Evidence:** the text is spurious; the contemporary name is Petrus Sabbatius and Latin his ancestral tongue. Modern ethnic labels remain inappropriate to the evidence. **Effect:** incorporated Justinian into competing Balkan national genealogies.

### Belisarius blinded and begging

**Story:** A jealous Justinian blinded his greatest general, who begged with the phrase “Give an obol to Belisarius.” **Appearance:** medieval tradition, elaborated in literature and art. **Evidence:** contemporary sources report accusation, disgrace, house detention, and restoration—not blinding or beggary. **Effect:** a major European exemplum of Fortune, royal ingratitude, and fallen greatness.

### Theodora alone saved women

**Story:** Theodora personally authored a coherent proto-feminist code abolishing trafficking and giving women modern equality. **Appearance:** modern popular biography. **Evidence:** Justinianic legislation did strengthen certain protections involving rape, abduction, dowry, prostitution, and inheritance, and Theodora plausibly influenced some measures. The laws were issued by Justinian through juristic institutions, retained patriarchy and slavery, and sometimes reduced women's divorce options. **Effect:** useful counter-myth to Procopian sexual invective, but it exaggerates both individual authorship and scope.

### The “last Roman” and restored empire

**Story:** Justinian was the last truly Roman emperor and restored the entire Roman world. **Appearance:** western historiography and imperial nostalgia. **Evidence:** he was unquestionably a Roman emperor and recovered Africa, Italy, and part of Spain, but never reconquered Gaul, Britain, most of Spain, or former northern provinces; Italy began fragmenting three years after his death. **Effect:** frames the reign as either climax or magnificent anachronism.

### Saint and lawgiver

Orthodox tradition commemorates Justinian and Theodora as saints, commonly on 14 November. Liturgical memory foregrounds orthodoxy, churches, and pious law. Dante placed Justinian in Mercury in *Paradiso* VI, where he narrates Roman providential history and law reform. Renaissance jurists and imperial rulers made the lawgiver the ancestor of civil law and centralized sovereignty.

## Works by or Issued Under Justinian's Name

Authorship requires distinctions. Imperial constitutions speak in Justinian's first person and carry his legal authority, but were drafted by quaestors, commissions, secretaries, and policy advisers. The legal corpus is therefore **issued and authorized by Justinian**, not individually written word-for-word by him. His dogmatic treatises may represent closer personal composition, still with clerical assistance.

### Legislation and legal compilations

1. **Codex Iustinianus**, first edition (529), lost except for survivals; commission chaired initially by John of Cappadocia.
2. **Constitutio Deo auctore** (15 December 530), instructions to Tribonian for compiling the Digest.
3. **Fifty Decisions / Quinquaginta decisiones** (530–532), constitutions resolving juristic disputes, absorbed into the revised Code.
4. **Institutiones Iustiniani** (promulgated 21 November 533; effective 30 December), prepared by Tribonian, Theophilus, and Dorotheus; textbook and statute.
5. **Digesta seu Pandectae** (promulgated 16 December 533; effective 30 December), fifty-book commission compilation of classical jurists.
6. **Constitutio Tanta/Δέδωκεν** (16 December 533), promulgating the Digest in Latin and Greek forms.
7. **Codex Iustinianus repetitae praelectionis** (534), twelve books, replacing the 529 edition.
8. **Novellae constitutiones** (535–565), individual post-Code laws, mostly Greek. Modern standard collections include 168 Novels, of which roughly 159 belong to Justinian, plus thirteen Edicts; counts vary by transmission and attribution. There was no complete official Justinianic edition.
9. **Pragmatic Sanction pro petitione Vigilii** (554), settlement for Italy, issued in Justinian's name; the role implied for Vigilius by the title is debated in detail.

### Theological and ecclesiastical texts

- Letters and edicts embedded in Codex book 1 and conciliar acts, including professions on Trinity, Incarnation, and councils.
- **Letter to the Monks of Alexandria Against the Monophysites**, in modern editions of his dogmatic writings.
- **Letter/Treatise on the Three Chapters**, connected with the 551 confession and conciliar campaign.
- **Edict on the True Faith / Confession of Faith**, a major Christological text.
- **Edict against Origen** (543), with attached anathemas; its exact relation to the 553 council's fifteen anti-Origenist anathemas is debated.
- **Ὁ Μονογενὴς Υἱὸς / Ho Monogenēs Huios** (“Only-Begotten Son and Immortal Word of God”), a liturgical hymn traditionally attributed to Justinian. Authorship is disputed; Severus of Antioch and other origins are also proposed. Justinian at least promoted its liturgical use in the imperial church.
- **Aphthartodocetist edict** (564/565): reported by Evagrius but not preserved; content and even the nature of the document cannot be verified directly.

Major textual repositories and editions include Mommsen and Krüger's *Corpus Iuris Civilis* for Digest/Institutes/Code; Schoell and Kroll for Novels; Fred H. Blume's English *Annotated Justinian Code* and Novels; Bruce Frier et al., *The Codex of Justinian: A New Annotated Translation*; Alan Watson's English *Digest*; David J. D. Miller and Peter Sarris, *The Novels of Justinian: A Complete Annotated English Translation*; Eduard Schwartz, *Drei dogmatische Schriften Iustinians*; Kenneth Paul Wesche, *On the Person of Christ*; and *Patrologia Graeca* 86.

## Illness, Personal Habits, and Death

Procopius and John of Ephesus report that Justinian became gravely ill during the plague at Constantinople in 542 and recovered. No lasting impairment is securely described. Ancient accounts stress fasting, minimal sleep, and continuous work; they do not document addiction. Interpretations of paranoia, compulsiveness, narcissism, depression, or other modern diagnoses are retrospective and cannot be clinically established.

Theodora's death in 548 was a major personal and political break. Justinian outlived her by seventeen years, increasing his attention to theology while continuing war, diplomacy, law, and construction. Earthquakes and the collapse of Hagia Sophia prompted ritual mourning and almsgiving. His final death in 565 appears natural. Corippus presents ceremonial public grief and the transfer of sovereignty; this is accession panegyric, not an opinion poll.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Law

The Justinianic corpus preserved a large, selectively edited portion of classical Roman jurisprudence that would otherwise be lost. The Digest was little used in much of the early medieval Latin West but was studied intensively at Bologna from the late eleventh century. Glossators, canonists, universities, royal courts, and early-modern jurists turned it into the *ius commune*. Its categories passed into continental European and Latin American civil codes and influenced international, canon, Scots, South African, Louisiana, and Quebec law. This influence is mediated, not a direct survival of every Justinianic rule.

### Empire and war

At Justinian's death Roman rule embraced the Mediterranean more fully than at any time since the fifth century: Africa, Italy, Dalmatia, and southern Spanish enclaves had been recovered. The price included decades of war, tribute, taxation, ruined Italian communities, and exposed Balkan sectors. The Lombards entered Italy in 568, but imperial Africa survived until the Arab conquest and Byzantine rule endured in parts of Italy for centuries. The balance sheet differs depending on time scale and region.

### Architecture and image

Hagia Sophia remained the supreme monument of the reign: cathedral, Latin church, mosque, museum, and mosque again. San Vitale's mosaic fixes Justinian's visual archetype—haloed emperor carrying the Eucharistic offering, flanked by clergy, officials, and soldiers, although he probably never visited Ravenna. The image is a claim of presence and hierarchy, not a portrait of an actual ceremony.

### Church

The Second Council of Constantinople became the fifth ecumenical council in Chalcedonian traditions. Justinian's Cyrillian Chalcedonian synthesis affected later theology, but coercion over the Three Chapters deepened western division and failed to reintegrate the emerging Coptic and Syriac Orthodox churches. Orthodox sainthood preserves the builder, lawgiver, and defender of faith; non-Chalcedonian memory often preserves the persecutor, even while honoring Theodora.

### Historical reinterpretation

- Procopius supplied both the “great builder” and “demon tyrant.”
- Medieval jurists privileged the legislator; Byzantine chroniclers and hagiographers the Christian emperor.
- Dante made him the voice of Roman providential history.
- Gibbon emphasized autocracy, theological quarrel, and costly conquest.
- nineteenth- and early twentieth-century scholarship often described a “reconquest” and Roman restoration; Balkan national traditions competed over ancestry.
- recent work stresses institutional teams, provincial experience, environmental shock, gendered invective, coerced religious uniformity, and uncertainty over plague scale.

No single epithet—“Great,” “last Roman,” “saint,” “tyrant,” or “demon”—contains the documentary record.
