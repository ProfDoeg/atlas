# William Butler Yeats: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier concerns William Butler Yeats (1865–1939), Irish poet, dramatist, essayist, folklorist, theatrical director, occultist, and senator.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary records, manuscripts, correspondence, official proceedings, or convergent archival evidence.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a generally reliable secondary source but not independently verified here from the underlying document.
- **Disputed interpretation:** the underlying evidence is accepted, but its meaning remains contested.
- **Allegation or theory:** a claim advanced without conclusive proof.
- **Rumor or myth:** folklore, retrospective anecdote, symbolic narrative, or demonstrably uncertain story.

The principal archival base includes the National Library of Ireland’s Yeats collections, the Yeats family and Abbey Theatre archives, parliamentary records, published correspondence, and modern scholarship. The National Library holds the largest collection of Yeats books, manuscripts, and personal objects, including school reports, occult papers, manuscript poems, the Nobel medal, and marital correspondence.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name:** William Butler Yeats.

**Common forms:** W. B. Yeats; William B. Yeats; Willie Yeats among family and intimates.

**Occult name:** *Daemon est Deus Inversus*, commonly abbreviated D.E.D.I., the magical motto he adopted in the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn. The Latin can be rendered “the daemon/devil is God inverted.”

**Titles and offices:**

- Senator of the Irish Free State, 1922–1928.
- Nobel laureate in literature, 1923.
- Honorary doctorates included degrees from Trinity College Dublin and Queen’s University Belfast.
- Co-founder, director, and principal institutional spokesman of the Irish Literary Theatre, Irish National Theatre Society, and Abbey Theatre.

**Birth:** 13 June 1865, at Georgeville, 23 Sandymount Avenue, Sandymount, County Dublin, then in the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland.

**Death:** 28 January 1939, Hôtel Idéal Séjour, Roquebrune-Cap-Martin, Alpes-Maritimes, France, aged seventy-three.

**Religion and worldview:** Born into the Protestant Ascendancy milieu and baptized in the Church of Ireland. He did not become an orthodox Christian. His mature thought combined Irish myth, Platonism and Neoplatonism, Blakean symbolism, folklore, spiritualism, ceremonial magic, astrology, cyclic theories of history, and an aristocratic theory of culture.

**Primary occupations:** Poet, playwright, prose writer, critic, editor, folklorist, public lecturer, theatrical administrator, and politician.

**Languages:** He wrote chiefly in English. Although central to the Irish cultural revival, he never acquired fluent Irish.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal line

Yeats’s father, **John Butler Yeats** (1839–1922), trained as a barrister but abandoned law for painting. An intellectually combative skeptic and gifted conversationalist, he resisted dogma, disliked fixed systems, and subjected his son’s beliefs to prolonged scrutiny. The father’s uncertain income and refusal of a conventional profession produced chronic financial instability.

The Yeats family belonged to the Anglo-Irish Protestant population. Its ancestry included Church of Ireland clergy, merchants, landholders, and administrators. Yeats emphasized an ancestral connection to **Jervis Yeats**, a Williamite soldier and linen merchant, and to the Butler family through his paternal grandmother. His great-grandfather, the Rev. John Yeats, had been rector at Drumcliff, County Sligo.

The poet sometimes treated this ancestry as evidence of an old, cultivated, governing class. His stylized image of an “aristocratic” Yeats lineage was partly genealogy and partly self-construction: the immediate family was cultured but frequently short of money and did not possess the secure landed wealth his rhetoric could imply.

### Maternal line

His mother, **Susan Mary Pollexfen Yeats** (1841–1900), came from a prosperous merchant and shipping family of Sligo. Her father, **William Pollexfen**, operated commercial interests involving shipping and milling. The Pollexfens supplied Yeats with a more tangible experience of property, enterprise, ships, servants, and a rooted west-Irish family world than did his father’s precarious artistic household.

Susan was reserved, strongly attached to Sligo, and interested in local tales and supernatural traditions. Yeats associated her family with the sea, weather, practical commerce, emotional reticence, and psychic sensitivity.

### Siblings

The surviving Yeats children were:

- **William Butler Yeats** (1865–1939).
- **Susan Mary “Lily” Yeats** (1866–1949), embroiderer and a principal figure in the Dun Emer and Cuala craft enterprises.
- **Elizabeth Corbet “Lolly” Yeats** (1868–1940), printer and manager of the Cuala Press.
- **John Butler “Jack B.” Yeats** (1871–1957), painter and illustrator.

Two other children died in infancy. The four surviving siblings formed an unusually productive artistic family, but money, management, temperament, and William’s intervention in the sisters’ press repeatedly generated friction.

### Marriage and children

On 20 October 1917, Yeats married **Georgie Hyde-Lees** (1892–1968), thereafter known publicly as **George Yeats**. He was fifty-two and she was twenty-five. George had independent knowledge of occultism and was not merely a passive secretary.

Their children were:

- **Anne Butler Yeats** (1919–2001), painter, stage designer, and custodian of family archives.
- **Michael Butler Yeats** (1921–2007), barrister and politician, later a member of the Irish Senate.

The National Library’s marital archive contains hundreds of surviving letters between William and George from 1917–1938. They document affection, household affairs, politics, publications, the Abbey Theatre, children, separations, and Yeats’s extramarital relationships.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1865–1880

Yeats’s infancy was divided between Dublin and Sligo. In 1867 the family moved to London so that John Butler Yeats could pursue art. William nevertheless spent extended periods with the Pollexfens in Sligo, especially at Merville and other family houses.

Sligo became the imaginative geography of his life: Ben Bulben, Knocknarea, Rosses Point, Lough Gill, the Garavogue, Glencar, and stories of fairies, apparitions, heroic Ireland, and displaced Gaelic families. His later presentation of Sligo as an enchanted origin was selective, but it rested on real childhood residence and inherited local narratives.

The family lived at various London addresses, including in Bedford Park, the late-Victorian artistic suburb associated with aestheticism. William attended the Godolphin School in Hammersmith from 1877. He was not an outstanding conventional student. His difficulties with spelling, formal examinations, and scientific or mathematical subjects coexisted with an intense visual imagination and memory for rhythm and story.

In 1880 the family returned to Ireland for financial reasons and lived at Howth, County Dublin. The sea, cliffs, fishing community, and nearby islands entered his early imaginative world.

## Education and Formation, 1881–1889

From 1881 to 1883 Yeats attended Erasmus Smith High School in Dublin. He subsequently studied art at the Metropolitan School of Art and attended the Royal Hibernian Academy schools. He initially expected to become a painter.

Important formative associates included:

- **George William Russell (“Æ”)**, poet, artist, mystic, and later editor.
- **George Pollexfen**, his maternal uncle, who shared interests in astrology and psychic phenomena.
- **John O’Leary**, the returned Fenian whose literary nationalism deeply affected Yeats.
- **Standish James O’Grady**, whose retelling of Irish heroic material helped establish the cultural possibilities of mythic nationalism.
- **Douglas Hyde**, scholar and language revivalist.
- **Katharine Tynan**, poet and early literary friend.

In 1885 Yeats’s first identified published poems, “Song of the Fairies” and “Voices,” appeared in the *Dublin University Review*. He participated in the Dublin Hermetic Society and began a lifelong attempt to combine poetry with occult discipline.

His early influences included Percy Bysshe Shelley, Edmund Spenser, William Blake, the Pre-Raphaelites, Irish folklore, Hindu and Theosophical material, and French Symbolism. His early verse favored mist, twilight, islands, dream landscapes, legendary heroes, and romantic escape. This style was later criticized as vague “Celtic Twilight” decoration, including by younger Irish modernists; Yeats himself substantially hardened and simplified his diction.

In 1887 the family again moved to London. Yeats entered literary clubs, met writers and editors, and helped found the Rhymers’ Club. This placed him among Ernest Rhys, Lionel Johnson, Arthur Symons, Ernest Dowson, and other fin-de-siècle poets.

## Maud Gonne and Revolutionary Nationalism, 1889–1903

Yeats met **Maud Gonne** on 30 January 1889 at the Yeats family home in Bedford Park. The encounter became the central emotional episode of his adult mythology. Gonne was tall, charismatic, politically militant, and active in Irish nationalist agitation.

Yeats proposed marriage to her repeatedly, usually counted as four formal proposals, all refused. Their relationship included political cooperation, occult experiments, intense correspondence, and what they described as a “spiritual marriage.” It was not simply a one-sided sequence in which Gonne coldly rejected him: Deirdre Toomey and other scholars have argued from correspondence that Yeats sometimes helped preserve the relationship’s unattainability when Gonne moved toward greater intimacy. The emotional structure remains a **disputed interpretation**, not a settled psychological diagnosis.

Unknown to Yeats at first, Gonne maintained a relationship with French Boulangist politician and journalist **Lucien Millevoye**, with whom she had two children. Their son Georges died in early childhood; their daughter was **Iseult Gonne**.

Gonne helped draw Yeats into public nationalist activity, but their politics diverged. She embraced demonstrations and revolutionary methods; he preferred cultural transformation, symbolic action, elite leadership, and at various periods constitutional or non-party politics. He joined the Irish Republican Brotherhood in the 1890s but did not become a conspiratorial organizer or participant in armed operations.

Early publications included:

- *Mosada* (1886), a verse drama.
- *The Wanderings of Oisin and Other Poems* (1889).
- *The Countess Kathleen and Various Legends and Lyrics* (1892).
- *The Celtic Twilight* (1893).
- *The Land of Heart’s Desire* (1894).
- *Poems* (1895).
- *The Secret Rose* (1897).
- *The Wind Among the Reeds* (1899).

His encounter with **Lady Augusta Gregory** in 1896 was decisive. Her residence, Coole Park in County Galway, became a place of writing, conversation, folklore collection, and institutional planning. Gregory supplied money, local knowledge, political realism, hospitality, translations and retellings, and sustained administrative labor.

In 1899 Yeats, Gregory, and Edward Martyn established the **Irish Literary Theatre**. Productions included Yeats’s *The Countess Cathleen* and Martyn’s *The Heather Field*. Some Catholic and nationalist critics attacked *The Countess Cathleen* for its story of an Irish noblewoman selling her soul to save starving tenants; objections included its treatment of religion and the appearance that Irish souls were commodities for foreign demons.

Gonne played the title role in Yeats’s *Cathleen ni Houlihan* in Dublin in 1902. The play converted Ireland into an old woman transformed into a queen through young men’s sacrificial death. It became a powerful nationalist symbol. Yeats later worried about art’s relation to political violence, asking in “The Man and the Echo” whether one of his plays had “sent out certain men the English shot.” No direct causal line between the play and the Easter Rising can be proved.

Gonne married **Major John MacBride** in Paris in 1903. Yeats was devastated. MacBride and Gonne’s marriage deteriorated amid accusations about drinking, violence, sexual misconduct, and treatment of Iseult. Their separation and custody litigation produced competing allegations. Yeats supported Gonne. A French court granted separation rather than the annulment or complete victory she sought. MacBride was later executed for participation in the 1916 Rising.

## The Golden Dawn, Theosophy, and Occult Practice

Yeats encountered **Helena Petrovna Blavatsky** and participated in the Theosophical Society milieu in London, but he was dissatisfied with its doctrinal authority and its restrictions on magical experimentation. He joined the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn in 1890.

The Golden Dawn supplied a structured system of initiation, Kabbalah, Tarot, astrology, divine names, elemental symbolism, visionary practice, ritual drama, and magical correspondence. Yeats treated magic seriously as a method of training imagination and exploring a shared symbolic mind.

He participated in internal struggles that divided the order. The celebrated “Battle of Blythe Road,” in which Yeats and associates opposed **Aleister Crowley’s** attempted access to the London temple, has acquired comic-occult mythology. The underlying organizational dispute and physical confrontation are documented; embroidered versions involving costume, curses, or Yeats personally throwing Crowley down stairs should be treated as **legendary elaboration** unless tied to a particular witness.

Yeats remained connected to successor organizations, including Stella Matutina, for many years. Occultism was not a youthful eccentricity abandoned by the mature poet. It informed *The Shadowy Waters*, *Rosa Alchemica*, *Per Amica Silentia Lunae*, *A Vision*, the later plays, his theories of mask and anti-self, and the gyres of history.

His manuscripts record séances, rituals, astrological charts, automatic writing, and efforts to identify communicating spirits. These documents establish that Yeats and George practiced such techniques. They do not establish that supernatural entities objectively communicated with them.

## Theatre, Patronage, and Institutional Conflict, 1902–1914

In 1902 Yeats, Gregory, George Russell, and the Fay brothers helped establish the **Irish National Dramatic Society**, soon reorganized as the Irish National Theatre Society. The first productions included Yeats’s *Cathleen ni Houlihan* and *The Hour-Glass*.

English heiress **Annie Horniman**, whom Yeats knew through occult circles, financed the conversion of a Dublin building into the **Abbey Theatre**, which opened on 27 December 1904. The enterprise depended on patronage, unpaid or underpaid work, touring income, and constant negotiation among writers, actors, directors, and benefactors.

Important theatre collaborators and antagonists included:

- Lady Gregory, co-director, playwright, and administrator.
- **John Millington Synge**, playwright whose work Yeats defended.
- William and Frank Fay, actor-directors.
- Annie Horniman, patron whose money came with conditions.
- George Moore and Edward Martyn, collaborators who also quarreled with Yeats.
- Lennox Robinson, actor-manager and playwright.
- Seán O’Casey, whose later break with the Abbey became a major controversy.

### The *Playboy* riots

Synge’s *The Playboy of the Western World* opened at the Abbey on 26 January 1907. Nationalist and moral objections—particularly to representations of rural violence, sexuality, and Irish womanhood—produced organized disruption. Yeats returned from Scotland and addressed the audience, defending artistic freedom. Police attended subsequent performances.

The claim that the audience uniformly rejected the play is misleading: protesters, supporters, ordinary patrons, press campaigns, and political organizations all participated in the event. The riots helped create both the Abbey’s heroic mythology of artistic independence and hostile accounts of an Anglo-Irish elite insulting Catholic Ireland.

### Censorship and Horniman

In 1909 Dublin Castle attempted to prevent the Abbey from presenting George Bernard Shaw’s *The Shewing-Up of Blanco Posnet*, which the Lord Chamberlain had banned in Britain. Yeats and Gregory insisted that the English theatrical censor lacked jurisdiction in Ireland and staged it despite threats to the theatre’s patent.

Relations with Horniman deteriorated over politics, management, finances, touring, and the theatre’s decision to remain open after the death of Edward VII. The rupture removed an important source of private funding. Accounts describing Yeats as either an ungrateful artist or Horniman as a controlling English patron simplify a documented conflict involving governance as well as personality.

The Abbey toured the United States in 1911–1912. Performances of *The Playboy* encountered demonstrations and, in Philadelphia, legal action under local obscenity provisions. The company’s American reception expanded its reputation and box-office possibilities.

## Poetic and Intellectual Transformation, 1903–1916

Collections including *In the Seven Woods* (1903), *The Green Helmet and Other Poems* (1910), and *Responsibilities* (1914) record a movement away from luxuriant fin-de-siècle diction toward harder syntax, argumentative speech, public invective, sexual candor, and political meditation.

Yeats’s artistic theories included:

- The **mask** or anti-self: creative identity achieved through confrontation with one’s ordinary nature.
- The distinction between passive personality and deliberately made form.
- History as recurring conflict rather than linear progress.
- Art as a product of discipline, inherited symbol, and tension between opposites.
- Suspicion of middle-class commercial culture and mass democratic opinion.
- Idealization of aristocratic houses as centers of cultivated continuity.

From 1913 to 1916 he spent winters at Stone Cottage in Sussex with **Ezra Pound**, who served as secretary, challenged Yeats’s diction, introduced him to East Asian literary materials, and helped move his poetry toward compression. Yeats, in turn, gave Pound access to a major elder poet’s networks and models. Their politics later overlapped in hostility to liberal modernity but did not become identical.

Yeats wrote the introduction to Rabindranath Tagore’s English *Gitanjali* in September 1912 and promoted the poems in London. His advocacy materially assisted Tagore’s English-language reception before Tagore received the 1913 Nobel Prize.

## Easter 1916 and Political Reassessment

Yeats was in England during the Easter Rising. Several people he knew were executed afterward, including John MacBride. “Easter, 1916” records his ambivalence toward the insurgents, acknowledging personal dislike and political doubt while recognizing their transformation within public memory.

The phrase “a terrible beauty is born” became one of the principal verbal memorials of the Rising. It should not be read as simple endorsement. The poem preserves contrary propositions: admiration, dread, uncertainty about necessity, and anxiety over fanaticism.

Yeats’s nationalism was never stable:

- He supported Irish cultural autonomy and independence.
- He was intermittently associated with revolutionary nationalists.
- He distrusted mass politics, egalitarianism, and doctrinaire republicanism.
- He opposed some British state actions while maintaining extensive English friendships and publishing relationships.
- During the Civil War he supported the legitimacy of the Free State against anti-Treaty forces.
- In the 1930s he briefly welcomed corporatist and Blueshirt politics.

## Marriage, Automatic Writing, and *A Vision*, 1917–1925

After MacBride’s execution, Yeats again proposed to Maud Gonne in 1916 and was refused. He then proposed to Gonne’s daughter **Iseult**, who was in her early twenties and whom he had known since childhood. Iseult refused. The proposal is documented and remains one of the most criticized episodes in his personal history because of the age difference and his prior quasi-familial relationship to her.

He married George Hyde-Lees in October 1917. During an uneasy honeymoon, George began automatic writing. Over several years she produced scripts attributed to communicators or “instructors.” Yeats questioned, organized, and expanded this material; the couple later used dream and sleep communications.

The resulting symbolic system classified personality and history through twenty-eight lunar phases, opposing gyres, incarnations, cycles, and historical epochs. It became *A Vision*, privately issued in 1925 and radically revised in 1937.

**Documented fact:** George generated much of the raw automatic script and participated intellectually in the system.

**Disputed authorship question:** The title page names Yeats, and he performed the large-scale literary and philosophical construction, but modern scholarship commonly treats George as an indispensable collaborator or uncredited co-creator. Calling *A Vision* solely a transcript dictated by spirits erases the couple’s extensive revision and organization; calling it Yeats’s unaided invention erases George’s contribution.

Yeats purchased the ruined Norman tower at Ballylee, County Galway, in 1917 for £35. Restored as **Thoor Ballylee**, it became a residence and governing symbol in his later poetry. Flooding, remoteness, repairs, and political violence made it difficult as a permanent home.

Anne was born in 1919 and Michael in 1921. The family also lived in Oxford, Dublin, London, and later at Riversdale in Rathfarnham.

## Civil War, Senate, and Nobel Prize, 1922–1928

John Butler Yeats died in New York in February 1922 after living there from 1907. His son had helped support him, and the father’s long American residence and debts complicated family finances.

In December 1922 Yeats was appointed to the first Senate of the Irish Free State. He served two terms through 1928.

His Senate activity included:

- Opposition to legislation that would constitutionally prohibit divorce.
- Arguments for civil liberty and minority rights.
- Opposition to narrow clerical control of public culture.
- Work concerning education, museums, language, cultural institutions, and the arts.
- Membership of the committee advising on the new Irish coinage.
- Defense of the historic Protestant contribution to Ireland.

In the divorce debate, Yeats warned that Catholic majoritarian legislation could alienate Protestants and convert the new state into a confessional polity. His speech became his best-known parliamentary intervention.

His minority-rights position coexisted with cultural elitism. He did not advocate modern egalitarian democracy. His ideal polity often depended on educated minorities, hereditary cultivation, hierarchy, and artistic authority.

During the Irish Civil War, armed men came to Ballylee, and violence occurred around the family’s Galway world. “Meditations in Time of Civil War” registered the conflict through house, inheritance, barricade, hatred, and isolation.

### Nobel Prize

The Swedish Academy awarded Yeats the 1923 Nobel Prize in Literature “for his always inspired poetry, which in a highly artistic form gives expression to the spirit of a whole nation.” He received the prize in Stockholm in December.

Yeats presented the award as recognition for the Irish dramatic and literary movement rather than for himself alone. It substantially increased his international reputation, sales, and financial security. He spent part of the prize money on family obligations and domestic security. Exact allocations differ in retrospective accounts and should not be treated as fully established without household ledgers.

His major late collections included:

- *The Wild Swans at Coole* (1917; enlarged 1919).
- *Michael Robartes and the Dancer* (1921).
- *The Tower* (1928).
- *The Winding Stair and Other Poems* (1933).

Contrary to the usual pattern of a prize crowning a completed career, much of the work now considered his greatest was written after the Nobel award.

## Later Theatre Conflicts and the O’Casey Break

The Abbey received an Irish state subsidy from 1925. This stabilized the institution but raised continuing questions about national representation and artistic independence.

Seán O’Casey’s *The Plough and the Stars* produced disturbances in 1926. Yeats famously compared them with the *Playboy* riots and rebuked the audience for again disgracing itself. The Abbey later rejected O’Casey’s antiwar play *The Silver Tassie*. O’Casey left Ireland and accused the Abbey’s directors of conservatism and failure of judgment.

**Documented fact:** Yeats opposed the play and the Abbey rejected it.

**Disputed interpretation:** Defenders cite theatrical structure and production difficulty; critics identify aesthetic rigidity, political discomfort, and a damaging failure to support a major dramatist. The rejection became part of the hostile mythology of Yeats as an authoritarian gatekeeper.

## Finances, Property, Institutions, and Business Relationships

Yeats did not found a conventional commercial corporation, invent technology, or hold patents. His economic history centered on publishing, patronage, theatre, inherited family obligations, property, public subsidy, and literary income.

### Principal financial structures

- **Family finances:** His father’s abandonment of law created prolonged insecurity. Yeats contributed to family support after achieving regular publishing income.
- **Publishing:** Income came from books, periodicals, lectures, theatre, collected editions, and later royalties enhanced by the Nobel Prize.
- **Civil List pension:** Yeats obtained a British Civil List pension in the 1890s, with support from literary patrons and advocates. The pension helped stabilize his career; accepting it did not end his nationalism.
- **Abbey Theatre:** Initially depended heavily on Annie Horniman’s capital and later on tours, receipts, and Irish state subsidy.
- **Dun Emer/Cuala Press:** Lily and Elizabeth Yeats participated in Evelyn Gleeson’s Dun Emer Guild before establishing Cuala Press. Elizabeth managed printing; Lily managed embroidery. William supplied texts, editorial direction, prestige, and interference. The press issued finely made limited editions by Yeats and other revival writers but repeatedly faced cash-flow and governance problems.
- **Thoor Ballylee:** Purchased in 1917 and restored at continuing expense; more important symbolically than as a profitable property.
- **82 Merrion Square, Dublin:** Major Dublin residence during his public career.
- **Riversdale, Rathfarnham:** Later family home.
- **Nobel money and increased royalties:** These brought comparative comfort, though Yeats continued to manage obligations to relatives and literary enterprises.

No credible evidence located in the dedicated adversarial search indicates fraud, embezzlement, money laundering, corrupt enrichment, undisclosed corporate ownership, or criminal financial activity by Yeats.

## Later Sexual Life, Health, and the Steinach Operation

Yeats’s health was recurrently fragile. Biographical sources describe respiratory illness, periods of exhaustion, fever, blood-pressure and circulatory problems, and declining health in the 1930s. Exact retrospective diagnoses vary.

In 1934 Australian-born sexologist **Norman Haire** performed a Steinach operation on Yeats. The procedure was a vasectomy intended under then-current theory to produce rejuvenation by redirecting sexual energy. Modern medicine does not accept the claimed rejuvenating mechanism.

Yeats described a “second puberty” and entered relationships or affairs with younger women, including **Margot Ruddock** and **Ethel Mannin**; he also formed a close late relationship with **Edith Shackleton Heald**. The marital correspondence shows that George knew of at least some of these involvements and negotiated rather than simply remaining ignorant.

The affairs affected his poetry, including the “Crazy Jane” sequence and late poems preoccupied with age, bodily desire, impotence, rage, and creative renewal.

**Documented fact:** The operation, renewed sexual activity, and several relationships are established by medical history, diaries, and correspondence.

**Disputed interpretation:** Biographers variously characterize George’s response as consent, tolerance, strategic accommodation, pain concealed by partnership, or a mixture. The evidence does not justify reducing the marriage either to uncomplicated liberation or simple deception.

## Fascism, Corporatism, Eugenics, and Antisemitism

This is the principal political controversy surrounding late Yeats.

### Blueshirts and corporatism

In 1933–1934 Yeats expressed enthusiasm for General **Eoin O’Duffy’s Army Comrades Association**, commonly called the Blueshirts, and wrote marching songs for the movement. He was attracted to uniforms, ceremony, discipline, anti-communism, corporatist organization, heroic leadership, and the prospect of ending parliamentary faction.

The Blueshirts’ precise classification remains debated by historians: fascist, proto-fascist, quasi-fascist, authoritarian conservative, or a heterogeneous pro-Treaty defensive movement. Yeats’s own attraction is nevertheless documented and should not be erased by disputes over taxonomy.

His enthusiasm was brief. He did not become a party administrator or advocate sustained street violence, and he withdrew as the movement fragmented. Scholarship on his stays in Fascist Italy between 1928 and 1934 finds that he did not follow Pound into complete allegiance to Mussolini; exposure to the regime contributed to withdrawal from overt fascist political structures, although authoritarian and “aesthetic fascist” elements remained in his thought.

### Eugenics

Yeats accepted hereditarian and eugenic ideas circulating among early twentieth-century intellectuals. His language about breeding, degeneration, selected families, and superior cultural stocks appears in letters, essays, and poems.

**Documented fact:** He used eugenic and hereditary categories and imagined cultural renewal through selected lineages.

**Necessary distinction:** This did not amount to a scientific research program, statute, forced-sterilization campaign, or administrative participation in racial persecution. It remains part of the intellectual prehistory shared with coercive eugenics and cannot be reduced to harmless metaphor.

### Antisemitism

Some of Yeats’s private and political language drew upon antisemitic stereotypes, including associations among Jews, finance, modernity, urbanism, and cultural decline. Scholarship differs over pervasiveness and weight.

**Documented fact:** Antisemitic phrases and stereotypes occur in his record, and his authoritarian politics brought him near movements in which antisemitism operated.

**Disputed interpretation:** He did not construct a systematic racial doctrine comparable to Ezra Pound’s, did not broadcast for Nazi Germany, and did not publicly campaign for anti-Jewish laws. Describing him as entirely free of antisemitism is untenable; equating his record without qualification to Pound’s wartime propaganda is also inaccurate.

### Nazi laws

Claims that Yeats straightforwardly “approved Nazi racial laws” circulate in polemical summaries. The evidentiary issue is more specific: private correspondence and political commentary show interest in authoritarian social organization and remarks interpretable as accepting aspects of German policy. No evidence consulted establishes that he endorsed the Holocaust, which had not yet begun before his death, or that he possessed later knowledge of its machinery.

This should be labeled **documented ideological susceptibility with disputed extent**, rather than either exoneration or retrospective conviction for crimes he did not administer.

## Other Controversies and Criticism

### Cultural nationalism and essentialism

Yeats helped create an image of Ireland as rural, heroic, spiritually ancient, anti-commercial, and culturally unified. Critics argue that this aesthetic marginalized urban life, labor politics, material poverty, Irish-language complexity, and forms of Irishness outside his symbolic program.

The underlying acts—selective folklore collection, mythological drama, and programmatic cultural statements—are documented. Whether they constituted recovery, invention, appropriation, essentialism, or all four is interpretive.

### Protestant ascendancy and aristocratic politics

He increasingly identified artistic cultivation with great houses and inherited class. Critics read this as nostalgia for the Protestant Ascendancy and hostility to democracy. Yeats simultaneously attacked British colonial complacency, defended an independent Ireland, and warned against Catholic domination. His politics cannot be mapped neatly onto unionism versus republicanism.

### Gender and muses

Maud Gonne, Lady Gregory, George Yeats, Olivia Shakespear, Florence Farr, Dorothy Wellesley, Margot Ruddock, and others supplied political knowledge, editing, hospitality, occult collaboration, performance, patronage, and literary stimulation. Earlier biographical conventions often reduced them to “muses.”

Contemporary criticism asks how Yeats converted living women into symbolic roles—Helen, Cathleen, Leda, dancer, moon phase, beloved, crone—and how this could subordinate their agency. Conversely, surviving correspondence shows women negotiating, refusing, mocking, correcting, and redirecting him.

### Appropriation and co-authorship

Lady Gregory’s folklore and language, George Yeats’s automatic scripts, and the labor of Lily and Elizabeth at Cuala complicate the single-author model. There is no established case of legal plagiarism or fraudulent attribution, but modern editors more explicitly acknowledge collaborative production.

### Gatekeeping

Yeats’s authority at the Abbey enabled major work but also allowed him to decide what counted as national drama. The rejection of O’Casey’s *The Silver Tassie* is the clearest adverse case. His relations with actors, the Fay brothers, Horniman, George Moore, and others repeatedly deteriorated over control.

### Revolutionary violence

Yeats was neither convicted nor formally charged for political violence. He briefly belonged to the Irish Republican Brotherhood and associated with militants, but no reliable evidence reviewed here shows him planning bombings, assassinations, or armed rebellion.

### Lawsuits and criminal proceedings

The relevant legal episodes chiefly concerned:

- Maud Gonne and John MacBride’s marital and custody litigation, in which Yeats was an ally and interested witness rather than the accused.
- Threatened or actual proceedings involving Abbey productions and licensing.
- The Philadelphia obscenity action surrounding the Abbey company’s performance of *The Playboy*.

No evidence was located of a personal criminal conviction, fraud prosecution, bankruptcy fraud, or corruption investigation against Yeats.

### Dedicated recent adversarial-search result

Searches combining Yeats’s name with *controversy, scandal, allegations, lawsuit, fraud, accusations, conspiracy,* and *criticism*, including results published or indexed in 2024–2026, principally returned renewed discussions of:

- Fascism, Blueshirts, eugenics, and antisemitism.
- His proposal to Iseult Gonne.
- Extramarital sexual relationships and the Steinach operation.
- The contested identity of the remains at Drumcliff.
- The supernatural claims surrounding automatic writing.
- Reassessment of women’s intellectual labor in his career.

No newly documented criminal, financial, or sexual-abuse prosecution emerged from that pass. Recent popular treatments of the “spirits” behind *A Vision* frequently narrate the communications as supernatural fact; the archives prove the scripts and the couple’s belief or experiment, not an external paranormal cause.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### John Butler Yeats

Father, critic, financial dependent, and intellectual antagonist. His skepticism pressured William to articulate rather than merely inherit beliefs. Their correspondence documents affection, frustration, money, aesthetics, and divergent temperaments.

### Susan Pollexfen Yeats

Mother and link to Sligo, maritime commerce, local memory, and supernatural tradition. Her reserve encouraged Yeats’s later reconstruction of an enigmatic maternal inheritance.

### Lily and Elizabeth Yeats

Sisters and collaborators in applied arts and Cuala Press. Their work materially produced the “Yeats” book as an aesthetic object. Relations with William included loyalty, dependence, resentment, and management disputes.

### Jack B. Yeats

Brother and major painter. Their careers developed independently; each contributed to the international idea of modern Irish art.

### Maud Gonne

Political collaborator, occult companion, rejected suitor’s beloved, correspondent, performer, and principal poetic figure. The relationship lasted, with interruptions and conflict, from 1889 until Yeats’s death.

### George Yeats

Wife, household manager, occult collaborator, editor, archivist, correspondent, and mother of his children. Her automatic scripts supplied the generative archive of *A Vision*. Later scholarship has substantially increased recognition of her intellectual agency.

### Lady Augusta Gregory

Closest sustained institutional collaborator. Coole Park furnished refuge and working environment. She collected folklore, wrote plays, negotiated conflicts, managed the Abbey, and shared the construction of the Revival.

### John Millington Synge

Playwright and ally. Yeats helped recruit and promote him, defended *The Playboy*, and memorialized him after his death in 1909.

### Ezra Pound

Secretary, younger modernist, critic, and political parallel. Pound helped revise Yeats’s style; Yeats remained more institutionally Irish and less wholly committed to fascism than Pound.

### Olivia Shakespear

Novelist, lover in the 1890s, lifelong friend, correspondent, and source of emotional and literary support. Through her family Yeats later knew Dorothy Shakespear and Pound.

### Florence Farr

Actor, Golden Dawn member, musical collaborator, and intimate friend. She performed Yeats’s verse with psaltery accompaniment and helped shape his experiments with spoken rhythm.

### Annie Horniman

Occult associate and decisive Abbey patron; later opponent in disputes about control, finance, politics, and management.

### George William Russell

Early mystical companion and cultural-revival colleague; also an editorial and intellectual counterweight.

### John O’Leary

Fenian mentor who turned Yeats toward Irish literary nationalism while modeling political dignity and personal austerity.

### Douglas Hyde

Language revivalist and associate in the early cultural movement. Yeats supported Irish literary recovery but differed from Hyde’s language-centered program.

### Seán O’Casey

Major Abbey playwright whose rejection became a defining artistic rupture.

### Dorothy Wellesley

Poet, editor, friend, and late intimate correspondent. Yeats stayed at Penns in the 1930s and collaborated with her on the *Broadsides*.

### Edith Shackleton Heald

Journalist and companion during Yeats’s final years. He stayed at her house, Riversdale near Steyning, and traveled with her and others in southern France.

## Residences, Journeys, and Geographical Movements

### Ireland

- Sandymount, Dublin: birthplace.
- Sligo town, Rosses Point, and Pollexfen family properties: recurrent childhood residence and lifelong imaginative homeland.
- Howth: family residence after the 1880 return.
- Dublin: education, occult societies, nationalist politics, Abbey Theatre, Senate, and Merrion Square residence.
- Coole Park and Galway: sustained work with Lady Gregory.
- Thoor Ballylee: tower residence from 1917, especially during summers.
- Riversdale, Rathfarnham: later family home.
- Drumcliff: intended and official final resting place after 1948.

### Britain

- London from childhood and again after 1887: schooling, publishing, clubs, Golden Dawn, theatre negotiations, and much of his professional network.
- Bedford Park: formative artistic-family residence.
- Oxford: family residence at 4 Broad Street in the early 1920s.
- Stone Cottage, Sussex: winter collaboration with Pound.
- Steyning and Wellingborough-related circles: late friendships and sexual-reform contacts.

### Continental Europe

Yeats traveled repeatedly in France and Italy. Paris was associated with Maud Gonne, nationalist negotiations, theatre contacts, and literary society. In the late 1920s and 1930s he visited Rapallo and other Italian locations, sometimes in Pound’s orbit, while observing Fascist Italy.

He spent final winters on the French Riviera for health reasons, dying at Roquebrune-Cap-Martin.

### Scandinavia and North America

He traveled to Sweden to receive the Nobel Prize in 1923. He also undertook lecture and theatrical tours connected to the Abbey and his literary career in the United States. American patrons and collectors, notably John Quinn, were important to Irish modernist networks.

He was not formally exiled or imprisoned.

## Illness, Psychological Crises, and Accidents

No evidence establishes a formal psychiatric diagnosis. Biographers record:

- Intense depressive or agitated periods associated with rejection by Gonne.
- Episodes of exhaustion during theatrical, political, and occult crises.
- Recurrent physical illness and declining stamina.
- Anxiety about impotence and aging, culminating in the Steinach operation.
- Cardiovascular and respiratory deterioration late in life.

Descriptions of him as “mad,” delusional, or psychotic are generally polemical unless they refer narrowly to his literal belief in spirits. Occult belief was organized and sustained within a social movement; it is not itself sufficient evidence for a clinical diagnosis.

No major addiction is documented. He drank socially, but the evidence reviewed does not support classifying him as alcohol-dependent. No consequential accident or battle injury is central to his life.

## Death and Burial

Yeats’s health worsened during the late 1930s. He traveled to the south of France with George and companions, including Edith Shackleton Heald. He died on 28 January 1939 at Roquebrune-Cap-Martin. Reported medical descriptions include heart failure associated with chronic illness; surviving summaries are not uniform enough to assign a more exact modern diagnosis without the death certificate and clinical record.

He was buried on 30 January in the local cemetery. Because war and French cemetery procedures intervened, the remains were later transferred to an ossuary.

In 1948 the Irish government arranged repatriation. A coffin presented as containing Yeats’s remains traveled from France to Ireland and was ceremonially reinterred at Drumcliff on 17 September 1948.

### The disputed remains

**Official narrative:** Yeats’s remains were identified, returned, and buried under Ben Bulben in accordance with his expressed wish.

**Documented contrary evidence:** French diplomatic papers reported that the authentic complete remains could not be securely recovered after commingling in an ossuary. One official proposed reconstructing a skeleton with the deceased’s approximate physical characteristics.

**Strongest conclusion:** The Drumcliff coffin may contain some of Yeats’s remains, mixed remains, another person’s remains, or an assembled skeleton. The documentary record does not support certainty that the entire skeleton is Yeats’s.

The epitaph, taken from “Under Ben Bulben,” reads:

> Cast a cold eye  
> On life, on death.  
> Horseman, pass by!

The uncertain corpse has intensified rather than diminished the symbolic force of the grave.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The national poet of Ireland”

**Story:** Yeats spoke for a spiritually unified Irish nation.

**Origin and circulation:** The literary revival, the Nobel citation, school curricula, state commemoration, and the poet’s own self-presentation.

**Evidence:** He profoundly affected Irish literary institutions and political language. Ireland, however, was divided by language, class, religion, region, gender, and constitutional allegiance.

**Effect:** The designation secured canonical status while generating critiques of his Anglo-Irish, Protestant, and elitist construction of Ireland.

### Maud Gonne as inaccessible muse

**Story:** Gonne continuously refused Yeats, and his frustrated devotion created his love poetry.

**Evidence:** Repeated proposals and refusals are documented. Correspondence complicates the simple story: intimacy and collaboration continued, and Yeats may at times have preserved the unattainable arrangement.

**Effect:** Gonne was long remembered chiefly through Yeats rather than as a revolutionary organizer with an independent life.

### “Did that play of mine send out certain men…?”

**Story:** *Cathleen ni Houlihan* caused young men to join the 1916 Rising.

**Evidence:** The play supplied sacrificial nationalist imagery and was culturally influential. Individual revolutionary motivation was multiple, and no documentary proof makes the play a sufficient cause.

**Classification:** Self-questioning myth with a real cultural foundation.

### The supernatural dictation of *A Vision*

**Story:** Spirits supplied Yeats and George with a complete cosmic system.

**Evidence for:** Thousands of pages of automatic script, diaries, charts, and their testimony.

**Evidence against or limiting:** The scripts were produced through George’s hand, questioning, selection, revision, and literary construction. No independent evidence verifies spirit agency.

**Effect:** The narrative contributed to Yeats’s reputation as mage, charlatan, visionary, or occult philosopher, depending on the audience.

### The magical battle with Crowley

**Story:** Yeats defeated or physically expelled Aleister Crowley from the Golden Dawn temple in a sorcerers’ battle.

**Evidence:** A genuine factional dispute and physical prevention of Crowley’s entry occurred. Colorful descriptions involving Highland dress, magical weapons, and stairway combat differ by teller.

**Classification:** Documented organizational struggle surrounded by occult folklore.

### The “Celtic Twilight”

**Story:** Yeats recovered an unbroken ancient folk spirituality.

**Evidence:** He and collaborators collected genuine oral narratives. Collection, translation, literary reshaping, class mediation, and occult theory altered the material.

**Effect:** The phrase became both the name of a revival and a hostile shorthand for sentimental pseudo-Celticism.

### Fascist Yeats versus democratic Yeats

Two opposed myths circulate:

1. Yeats was simply a fascist whose poetry encodes a coherent totalitarian program.
2. His Blueshirt episode was meaningless costume-play unrelated to serious conviction.

The evidence supports neither simplification. He expressed genuine authoritarian, corporatist, eugenic, and anti-democratic ideas; he also defended minority rights, divorce, artistic freedom, and institutional pluralism and did not remain an organized fascist.

### The authentic Drumcliff body

The state funeral created a national relic. French documents render complete authenticity improbable. The disputed body now functions as a modern relic legend: a possibly composite corpse beneath an epitaph commanding detachment from life and death.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Yeats’s canonical standing expanded after death. He came to occupy several partially incompatible roles:

- National poet of an independent Ireland.
- Anglo-Irish critic of both British rule and Catholic-nationalist conformity.
- Co-founder of a national theatre.
- Late modernist whose major achievement continued into old age.
- Occult philosopher and ceremonial magician.
- Political reactionary and intermittent fascist sympathizer.
- Defender of artistic liberty, divorce rights, and minority culture.
- Architect of myths later challenged by postcolonial, feminist, materialist, and Irish-language scholarship.

His influence is visible in:

- Irish poets including Louis MacNeice, Patrick Kavanagh, Austin Clarke, John Montague, Seamus Heaney, Derek Mahon, Eavan Boland, and Paul Muldoon, often through resistance as much as imitation.
- Modernist poetry through Pound, Eliot, Auden, and later symbolic and mythic poetics.
- Postcolonial debates over cultural revival, invented tradition, and national theatre.
- Occult and esoteric modernism.
- Political rhetoric: “terrible beauty,” “the centre cannot hold,” and “slouching towards Bethlehem” have become detached from their poems and repeatedly applied to crises.
- Music, including settings by classical, folk, and popular composers.
- Visual art, performance, dance, and Noh-influenced experimental theatre.

Later scholarship has particularly altered three inherited pictures:

1. **George Yeats** is now treated as a collaborator rather than eccentric wife or medium.
2. **Maud Gonne** is studied as an autonomous political actor rather than merely the rejecting muse.
3. Yeats’s **authoritarian, fascist, eugenic, and antisemitic language** is treated as part of the evidentiary record rather than an embarrassing detachable episode.

His reputation remains unusually capable of containing contradiction because the archive documents rather than resolves it: nationalist and cosmopolitan, institutional builder and quarrelsome controller, defender of minority liberty and opponent of egalitarian democracy, bodily skeptic and magical believer, family man and serial lover, myth-maker and relentless reviser of his own myths.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted evidence are included. The presence of both names in a historical period is not sufficient.

- **William Blake — documented fact.** Yeats regarded Blake as a central precursor. With Edwin Ellis he edited and interpreted *The Works of William Blake* (1893), helping restore attention to the prophetic books. Blakean contraries, symbolic systems, and visionary art shaped Yeats’s concepts of mask and imagination.

- **Helena Petrovna Blavatsky — documented fact.** Yeats met Blavatsky and participated in the London Theosophical milieu in the late 1880s. He later moved toward the Golden Dawn because ceremonial experiment offered what he believed Theosophy’s increasingly doctrinal organization did not.

- **Annie Besant — reported fact.** Besant belonged to the Theosophical network through which Yeats moved. Both participated in late-Victorian occult and Indian-religious discussion, although the sources consulted here do not establish a comparably close personal relationship.

- **Arthur Edward Waite — documented fact.** Waite and Yeats belonged to the Golden Dawn world and its successor disputes. Their approaches to occult tradition differed: Waite emphasized Christian mysticism and textual esotericism; Yeats emphasized magical imagination, symbolism, and visionary practice.

- **Pamela Colman Smith — reported fact.** Smith worked in the overlapping Golden Dawn and Irish theatrical networks. She contributed visual and performance work to circles involving Yeats and later created the Rider–Waite Tarot images. The consulted sources support a shared network, not a close sustained friendship.

- **James Augustine Aloysius Joyce — documented fact.** Joyce arranged a meeting with Yeats in Dublin in 1902 and sharply criticized the older poet, according to later accounts. Yeats nevertheless recognized Joyce’s talent and later assisted him through introductions and efforts to obtain support. Their relationship combined generational challenge, admiration, and divergent models of Irish authorship.

- **Rabindranath Tagore — documented fact.** Yeats encountered Tagore’s English translations in London in 1912, promoted them, and wrote the introduction to *Gitanjali: Song Offerings*. His support was important to Tagore’s early English reception before the 1913 Nobel Prize.

- **Oscar Fingal O’Flahertie Wills Wilde — reported fact / documented influence.** Wilde belonged to the Anglo-Irish and aesthetic London immediately preceding Yeats’s ascendancy. Yeats knew Wilde and his family’s cultural reputation, and Wilde formed part of the environment against which Yeats defined Irish literary identity. The evidence consulted does not support describing them as close collaborators.

- **Christina Georgina Rossetti and Dante Gabriel Rossetti — documented influence.** Pre-Raphaelite poetry and art strongly affected Yeats’s early style. The connection is artistic influence rather than a documented personal relationship with either Rossetti.

- **William Morris — documented influence and shared milieu.** Yeats admired Morris’s poetry, romance, socialism-inflected arts movement, and attempt to unite craft with cultural transformation. Morris’s book arts also formed part of the background to Dun Emer and Cuala Press. Their degree of direct personal contact was limited.

- **Friedrich Nietzsche — documented intellectual influence.** Yeats read Nietzsche and absorbed themes of aristocratic self-creation, recurrence, tragic conflict, and opposition to herd morality. His use was selective rather than doctrinal.

- **Benito Mussolini — documented political context; no close personal connection established.** Yeats observed Fascist Italy during visits from 1928–1934 and initially found corporatist order attractive. Scholarship distinguishes this from Pound’s direct devotion to Mussolini. No reliable source consulted establishes a substantive personal Yeats–Mussolini relationship.

- **Sigmund Freud — documented intellectual contrast, no personal meeting established.** Yeats’s automatic writing and symbolic psychology developed during the rise of psychoanalysis, but he preferred occult and collective-symbolic explanations. The sources reviewed do not document a personal meeting.

- **Carl Gustav Jung — intellectual parallel only; no direct connection established.** Later commentators compare Yeats’s archetypal and cyclic symbolism with Jung’s psychology. No consulted evidence establishes correspondence, collaboration, or acknowledged direct influence.

- **G. I. Gurdjieff — no documented connection found.** Both are associated retrospectively with twentieth-century esotericism, but no supported meeting or correspondence was located.

- **René Guénon, Rudolf Steiner, Mircea Eliade, Julius Evola, and Miguel Serrano — no documented personal connection found in the consulted sources.** Later esoteric or traditionalist readers may place them in adjacent genealogies, but adjacency is not biographical contact.

## Chronology

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 13 June 1865 | Born at Sandymount, County Dublin. |
| 1867 | Family moves to London; recurrent childhood stays in Sligo follow. |
| 1877–1880 | Attends Godolphin School, Hammersmith. |
| 1880 | Family returns to Ireland and settles at Howth. |
| 1881–1883 | Attends Erasmus Smith High School, Dublin. |
| 1884–1886 | Studies art; meets George Russell; joins hermetic study circles. |
| 1885 | First identified poems published in the *Dublin University Review*. |
| 1886 | Publishes *Mosada*. |
| 1887 | Returns with family to London; enters metropolitan literary networks. |
| 1888 | Edits *Fairy and Folk Tales of the Irish Peasantry*. |
| 30 January 1889 | Meets Maud Gonne. |
| 1889 | Publishes *The Wanderings of Oisin and Other Poems*. |
| 1890 | Joins the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn. |
| 1891 | Publishes *John Sherman and Dhoya*. |
| 1892 | Publishes *The Countess Kathleen and Various Legends and Lyrics*. |
| 1893 | Publishes *The Celtic Twilight* and, with Edwin Ellis, the Blake edition. |
| 1894 | *The Land of Heart’s Desire* produced. |
| 1896 | Begins decisive association with Lady Gregory and Coole Park. |
| 1897 | Publishes *The Secret Rose*. |
| 1899 | Irish Literary Theatre established; *The Countess Cathleen* staged. |
| 1900 | Golden Dawn factional struggle, later mythologized as a magical battle with Crowley. |
| 1902 | *Cathleen ni Houlihan* staged with Maud Gonne; Yeats meets James Joyce. |
| 1903 | Gonne marries John MacBride; Yeats publishes *In the Seven Woods*. |
| 27 December 1904 | Abbey Theatre opens in Dublin. |
| January 1907 | *Playboy of the Western World* riots; Yeats publicly defends Synge. |
| 1909 | Synge dies; Abbey challenges attempted censorship of Shaw’s *Blanco Posnet*. |
| 1911–1912 | Abbey tours North America amid demonstrations and legal controversy. |
| September 1912 | Yeats writes introduction to Tagore’s *Gitanjali*. |
| 1913–1916 | Winters with Ezra Pound at Stone Cottage, Sussex. |
| 1914 | Publishes *Responsibilities*. |
| April–May 1916 | Easter Rising and executions; Yeats writes “Easter, 1916.” |
| 1916 | Proposes again to Gonne and then to Iseult; both refuse. |
| 20 October 1917 | Marries Georgie Hyde-Lees; automatic writing begins. |
| 1917 | Purchases Thoor Ballylee; publishes *The Wild Swans at Coole*. |
| 1919 | Daughter Anne born. |
| 1921 | Son Michael born; publishes *Michael Robartes and the Dancer*. |
| February 1922 | John Butler Yeats dies in New York. |
| December 1922 | Appointed to first Senate of Irish Free State. |
| 1923 | Wins Nobel Prize in Literature and receives it in Stockholm. |
| 1925 | First edition of *A Vision*; Abbey begins receiving state subsidy. |
| 1926 | *The Plough and the Stars* disturbances; Yeats rebukes rioters. |
| 1928 | Publishes *The Tower*; Senate service ends. |
| 1928–1934 | Repeated visits to Italy during Fascist consolidation. |
| 1929–1933 | Publishes versions of *The Winding Stair*. |
| 1933–1934 | Brief involvement with Blueshirt/corporatist politics. |
| 1934 | Undergoes Steinach operation performed by Norman Haire. |
| 1935–1938 | Late relationships, renewed poetry, plays, and editorial projects. |
| 1936 | Edits *The Oxford Book of Modern Verse*. |
| 1937 | Revised *A Vision* published. |
| 1938 | Publishes *New Poems*; writes “Under Ben Bulben” and late plays. |
| 28 January 1939 | Dies at Roquebrune-Cap-Martin, France. |
| 30 January 1939 | Buried in Roquebrune cemetery. |
| 17 September 1948 | Coffin officially identified as Yeats’s reinterred at Drumcliff, County Sligo. |
| 2015 | Publication of French diplomatic evidence renews and substantially strengthens doubts about the identity of the Drumcliff remains. |
