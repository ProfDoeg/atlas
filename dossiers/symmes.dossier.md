# John Cleves Symmes Jr.: Research Dossier

## Scope and Evidentiary Note

This dossier concerns Captain John Cleves Symmes Jr. (1780–1829), the American army officer, trader, lecturer, and proponent of a hollow, inhabitable Earth—not his uncle and namesake Judge John Cleves Symmes (1742–1814).

The surviving record is uneven. Symmes left scattered circulars, newspaper articles, petitions, letters, and lecture notices rather than a completed treatise or autobiography. The fullest near-contemporary account was written by his supporter James McBride in 1826. A larger compilation was published by Symmes’s son Americus in 1878. Both preserve valuable evidence but are partisan defenses of Symmes and require comparison with military, legislative, genealogical, archival, and newspaper records.

Evidentiary labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a primary document, official record, physical artifact, or strong independent archival catalog.
- **Reported fact:** stated in a near-contemporary or later biographical source but not independently established here.
- **Disputed claim:** supported by competing accounts or uncertain attribution.
- **Allegation or theory:** a claim advanced without adequate proof.
- **Rumor or myth:** a culturally consequential story for which reliable evidence is absent or contrary.
- **Inference:** a reasoned conclusion not expressly stated by the source.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** John Cleves Symmes Jr.
- **Common forms:** John Cleves Symmes; John C. Symmes; Jno. Cleves Symmes; Captain Symmes; Capt. John Cleves Symmes.
- **“Junior”:** Used to distinguish him from his prominent uncle, Judge John Cleves Symmes. It did not mean that the judge was his father.
- **Epithets applied later:** “the Hollow-Earth theorist,” “the man with holes at the poles,” and, in sympathetic writing, an American philosophical or geographical pioneer.
- **Birth:** Most modern authorities give **5 November 1780**, in **Sussex County, New Jersey**. The family genealogy gives 5 November **1779**. James McBride, writing in 1826, called him approximately forty-six and a native of New Jersey, which is compatible with either late 1779 or 1780. The 1780 date is now conventional, but the year is not wholly free from documentary disagreement.
- **Death:** Either **28 or 29 May 1829**, near or in Hamilton, Butler County, Ohio. Most biographical references use 28 May; the Hamilton historical marker and some local accounts use 29 May.
- **Burial:** The old Hamilton burying ground, now Symmes Park, Hamilton, Ohio.
- **Occupations:** United States Army officer; frontier post adjutant; military supplier and licensed trader; lecturer; writer on natural philosophy and polar geography; unsuccessful promoter of a government-financed polar expedition.
- **Military rank:** Captain, United States Infantry.
- **Known portrait:** Pencil portrait by John James Audubon, dated 1820, now cataloged through the Smithsonian National Portrait Gallery and owned by the New-York Historical Society.
- **No substantiated pseudonym:** “Captain Adam Seaborn,” author of *Symzonia* (1820), was long attributed to Symmes, but the identification is disputed and probably incorrect.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal ancestry

The 1826 McBride biography identifies Symmes as the son of **Timothy Symmes of New Jersey**, grandson of another **Timothy Symmes**, and great-grandson of the Reverend **Thomas Symmes of Bradford**, a 1698 Harvard graduate.

His paternal grandfather Timothy reportedly had two sons:

1. **Judge John Cleves Symmes** (1742–1814), the land speculator, Continental Congress delegate, New Jersey judge, and pioneer associated with the “Symmes Purchase” between the Great and Little Miami rivers.
2. **Timothy Symmes**, father of the hollow-Earth theorist.

Thus Captain Symmes was the judge’s **nephew**, not his son. Confusion between the two men remains common in local histories and online summaries.

### Parents

- **Father:** Timothy Symmes.
- **Mother:** The family genealogy identifies her as **Mercy Harker**.
- Information about their household, economic circumstances, and Captain Symmes’s relationship with either parent is sparse.

### Uncle and extended family

Judge John Cleves Symmes was the father of **Anna Tuthill Symmes**, wife of President **William Henry Harrison**. Captain Symmes was therefore Anna Harrison’s first cousin and Harrison’s cousin by marriage.

The judge reportedly gave his nephew a valuable section of land near Hamilton. Captain Symmes moved onto this property in 1824. This family transfer materially affected his final residence but did not make him prosperous; accounts of his estate describe it as insolvent or heavily encumbered at his death.

### Marriage

On **25 December 1808**, at **Fort Adams**, then in Mississippi Territory rather than the later state of Louisiana, Symmes married **Mary Anne Pelletier Lockwood**, usually styled Mary Anne Lockwood, a widow.

She reportedly had **six children from her previous marriage**. Symmes accepted responsibility for them, which explains the striking dedication of his first circular “to my wife and her ten children” in 1818: the number combined stepchildren with the children then born to the Symmes marriage.

### Children

The surviving genealogies and secondary biographies are not fully consistent about the number, order, or life spans of the children. Named children attributed to John and Mary Anne include:

- **Louisiana Symmes**, born about 1810.
- **Americus Vespucius Symmes**, born at or near Belle Fontaine on 2 November 1811; physician, editor of his father’s papers, and principal custodian of the Symmes legacy.
- **William H. H. Symmes**, born about 1813; evidently named for William Henry Harrison.
- **Elizabeth Symmes**, born about 1814.
- **John Cleves Symmes III**, born about 1824.
- Other children may have died young or remain imperfectly documented in readily accessible records.

Americus later stated or was reported to have assumed responsibility, at seventeen, for an indebted estate, his widowed mother, and three younger siblings. This corroborates the family’s poor financial position when Captain Symmes died.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1780–1802

Little primary evidence survives for Symmes’s childhood. McBride says that he received what was then considered a **“common English education”**, meaning elementary instruction in reading, writing, arithmetic, and practical subjects rather than a classical or collegiate curriculum.

There is no evidence that he attended Harvard, West Point, or another college. Later confusion with a younger namesake and descendant has produced occasional erroneous claims of West Point education.

McBride portrayed him as largely self-educated through access to “tolerably well selected libraries” and an intense appetite for knowledge. The books that demonstrably or reportedly entered his later arguments included:

- accounts of Arctic and Antarctic voyages;
- Alexander Mackenzie’s travels;
- Samuel Hearne’s northern travels;
- works associated with James Cook, John Ross, and polar navigation;
- Erasmus Darwin’s natural philosophy;
- elementary astronomy, geography, magnetism, mechanics, and geology;
- discussions of meteors, refraction, animal migration, earthquakes, and planetary appearance;
- religious natural philosophy.

Some scholars propose that he encountered Edmond Halley’s seventeenth-century hollow-Earth hypothesis indirectly through Cotton Mather’s *The Christian Philosopher*. This is a plausible route of transmission, not a documented reading record.

## Military Career, 1802–1815

### Entry and early promotion

Symmes entered the United States Army as an **ensign in 1802**, at about twenty-one or twenty-two. He subsequently became a lieutenant and then captain in the First United States Infantry.

His career occurred in the small frontier army created before the War of 1812. Surviving material places him at or in relation to western posts including:

- Fort Adams;
- posts in Arkansas or the lower Mississippi region;
- Fort Belle Fontaine, near St. Louis;
- the upper Mississippi and Missouri frontier.

A surviving archival transcript identifies **John Cleves Symmes as acting adjutant at Belle Fontaine on 26 June 1810**, submitting a morning report to Lieutenant Colonel Daniel Bissell. This is direct evidence of his administrative as well as company duties.

### Duel with Lieutenant Marshall, 1807

Later biographical accounts report that in **1807**, Symmes fought a pistol duel with a Lieutenant Marshall. Symmes was wounded in the wrist and Marshall in the thigh; they afterward became friends.

- **Status:** Reported fact.
- **Uncertainties:** The immediate cause, exact location, Marshall’s full identity, and any disciplinary consequences have not been established from a court-martial or official file.
- The episode is consistent with the honor culture of the early United States officer corps but should not be embellished beyond the surviving account.

### Marriage and frontier household

His 1808 marriage at Fort Adams placed Symmes at a southern frontier installation. His growing mixed household later lived around Belle Fontaine and St. Louis. Americus was born at Belle Fontaine in 1811.

### War of 1812

For much of the early war, Symmes’s regiment remained in the western territories. In spring 1814, it was ordered east and north into the Canadian theater.

#### Battle of Lundy’s Lane / Bridgewater, 25 July 1814

Symmes fought at the Battle of Lundy’s Lane, also called Bridgewater. McBride claimed:

- Symmes was the senior captain of his regiment;
- his company fired approximately seventy rounds per man;
- it repelled three bayonet charges;
- the regiment’s conduct was later misrepresented as retreat or failure.

These particulars came from an advocate of Symmes and need to be distinguished from the basic, documented fact of his participation. McBride also said that Symmes later obtained acknowledgments vindicating his regiment and communicated material to the New-York Historical Society and newspapers.

#### Fort Erie, August–September 1814

During the American sortie from Fort Erie on **17 September 1814**, Symmes and his command reportedly participated in the capture of British Battery No. 2. McBride credited Symmes personally with spiking its cannon and claimed that official honor went to others.

- **Participation in the campaign:** Documented fact.
- **Personal spiking of the guns and denial of proper credit:** Strong near-contemporary reported claim, but presented by a partisan source.
- **Reputational consequence:** This became part of the family’s image of Symmes as an overlooked soldier before he became an overlooked theorist.

### Discharge

Symmes remained in the army until after the war and was **honorably discharged on 15 June 1815** during the postwar reduction.

No evidence located for this dossier establishes a court-martial, criminal conviction, dismissal for misconduct, or dishonorable military action.

## Postwar Business and Financial Failure, 1815–1819

After leaving the army, Symmes became a military supplier and frontier trader. McBride says he spent about three years furnishing supplies to troops along the upper Mississippi.

He also reportedly obtained a license to trade with the **Meskwaki/Fox people**. The documentary record consulted here does not establish the precise capital structure, partners, inventory, revenue, or terms of the license.

The enterprise failed or generated little profit. McBride inferred this from Symmes’s later poverty; family accounts expressly describe the venture as unsuccessful.

Factors that may have affected the business included:

- long and expensive supply lines;
- dependence on military purchasing;
- credit risk;
- the postwar contraction of army expenditure;
- the difficulty of frontier transport and trade.

These are contextual possibilities, not documented causes in Symmes’s particular accounts.

Symmes’s first circular later stated that his “pecuniary concerns” had been neglected while he pursued his theory. By 1826 McBride described him as laboring under “great pecuniary embarrassments.” At death, his estate was encumbered with debt.

There is no evidence in the consulted record of fraud, corrupt contracting, embezzlement, money laundering, or a bankruptcy adjudication. The record supports commercial failure and indebtedness, not financial crime.

## Development of the Hollow-Earth Theory

### Intellectual background

Symmes was not the first writer to imagine a hollow Earth. Earlier models were advanced by or associated with:

- **Edmond Halley**, who proposed concentric shells to explain magnetic variation;
- **Cotton Mather**, who transmitted Halley’s theory in popular religious-natural-philosophical writing;
- **Leonhard Euler**, to whom a one-shell hollow globe with a central light was later attributed, though the exact history of that attribution requires care;
- assorted early-modern speculative cosmologies.

Symmes’s distinctive contribution was not merely hollowness. He proposed:

1. Earth consisted initially of an outer shell and several nested concentric spheres.
2. Each sphere was open at both poles.
3. The openings were thousands of miles wide and curved gradually inward, rather than forming vertical pits.
4. The inner surfaces were habitable.
5. Light and warmth could reach or be generated within the concavities.
6. Comparable construction characterized planets and other “orbicular bodies.”
7. Polar expeditions could physically pass through the openings.
8. Animal migrations, warm polar currents, unusual refraction, compass variation, meteors, and supposed open polar seas were evidence for the system.

Symmes later simplified the model, apparently reducing the multiple shells to one principal hollow shell.

### Claimed mechanism

The theory depended on a speculative physics of matter. Symmes argued that rotating matter would accumulate into hollow spherical shells, leaving internal “mid-plane spaces.” He treated the polar openings as consequences of centrifugal action and the Earth’s rotational formation.

His evidence was analogical rather than experimental. He reinterpreted reports of:

- birds and mammals moving northward in winter;
- driftwood and comparatively warm currents in Arctic seas;
- mirages and looming;
- auroral or celestial illumination;
- irregular magnetic behavior;
- polar voyages encountering temporarily open water;
- apparent rings or spots on other planets.

Modern geophysics, seismology, gravimetry, geomagnetism, satellite observation, and direct polar exploration contradict the Symmes model. Historically, however, he formulated it when both polar regions remained poorly mapped and terrestrial physics was still developing.

### Religion and natural theology

Symmes’s system was not presented as atheistic. McBride’s exposition explicitly assumed a creator and argued that hollow, multiply habitable worlds would demonstrate divine economy, wisdom, and benevolence. The interior’s additional living space was treated as evidence of providential efficiency.

No evidence located here identifies Symmes with a particular sect, church office, occult society, or esoteric order. Later occult and conspiracy traditions appropriated hollow-Earth imagery, but that does not establish Symmes as an occultist.

## Public Campaign, 1818–1823

### Circular No. 1, 10 April 1818

From St. Louis on **10 April 1818**, Symmes issued his famous address “To All the World”:

> “I declare the earth is hollow and habitable within; containing a number of solid concentrick spheres, one within the other, and that it is open at the poles 12 or 16 degrees.”

He pledged his life to the claim and offered to lead an expedition if supported.

The circular reportedly had a print run of **500 copies**, sent to learned institutions, towns, newspapers, societies, and prominent persons in the United States and Europe. At least two textual states or versions appear to have circulated.

He requested one hundred well-equipped companions to begin from Siberia with reindeer and sleighs, cross the frozen sea, pass north of latitude 82°, and return the following spring. He expected a “warm and rich land” containing vegetation, animals, and perhaps humans.

The circular named three proposed “protectors”:

- Samuel Latham Mitchill;
- Sir Humphry Davy;
- Alexander von Humboldt.

This was Symmes’s unilateral designation. It is not evidence that any of them accepted patronage or endorsed his theory.

Some accounts say Symmes enclosed a certificate of sanity with the circular. The story is plausible and widely repeated, but the exact surviving certificate and its chain of custody were not established in the consulted sources.

### Reception

The dominant initial reaction was ridicule. Editors treated “Symmes’ Hole” as a comic subject; critics described the circular as evidence of madness.

McBride reports that:

- Count Volney laid the circular before the French Academy of Sciences;
- the Academy considered it unworthy of attention;
- the *London Morning Chronicle* doubted assurances that Symmes was sane.

These are near-contemporary reports, but the dossier has not independently located minutes of the French Academy documenting its disposition.

The ridicule created a durable public persona: Symmes became simultaneously a crank, curiosity, frontier philosopher, and symbol of unorthodox speculation.

### Subsequent circulars and papers

McBride’s 1826 bibliography describes:

- a second 1818 number arguing geometrically that matter forms concentric spheres;
- a third on geological principles;
- No. 4 on the formation of the Allegheny Mountains;
- No. 5 claiming discovery of open poles;
- No. 6, Cincinnati, January 1819, listing propositions for later treatment;
- No. 7, **“Arctic Memoir,”** Cincinnati, February 1819;
- **“Light Between the Spheres,”** Cincinnati, August 1819, published in the *National Intelligencer*;
- numerous subsequent newspaper pieces.

Not all survive in complete original copies. Modern transcriptions gather part of this dispersed corpus, but a definitive critical edition remains lacking.

### First lectures

Symmes began public lecturing at Cincinnati in **1820**, subsequently appearing in:

- Lexington, Kentucky;
- Frankfort, Kentucky;
- Hamilton, Ohio;
- Zanesville, Ohio;
- later cities during broader eastern tours.

He used a wooden globe cut or constructed to display curving polar openings, the “icy hoop” or verge, winding meridians, and a proposed route inward.

McBride, who attended lectures, conceded that Symmes had:

- no classical education;
- limited formal philosophical training;
- a hesitant, effortful delivery;
- a somewhat nasal voice;
- arguments arranged confusingly and expressed in homely language.

McBride nevertheless reported attentive audiences and considered the underlying case persuasive.

### Physical description

McBride described the forty-six-year-old Symmes as:

- of middle height;
- reasonably proportioned;
- round-headed;
- small, oval-faced;
- bright blue-eyed;
- frequently abstracted in appearance;
- plain and unaffected in manner.

This is the closest surviving near-contemporary verbal portrait.

## Congressional and State Expedition Campaigns

### Congressional petition of 1822

Symmes petitioned Congress for a national expedition to establish whether the globe had a habitable concavity. He requested two vessels of approximately **250–300 tons** and whatever further aid the government considered necessary.

Senator **Richard Mentor Johnson** of Kentucky presented the petition on **7 March 1822**. A motion to refer it to the Committee on Foreign Relations failed; the Senate laid it on the table by twenty-five votes.

### Renewed petitions, 1823

In December 1823, Symmes forwarded similar petitions to both houses. They produced no expedition.

### Ohio General Assembly, 1824

In January 1824, he asked Ohio’s legislature to:

- approve or recognize his theory;
- recommend him to Congress;
- support an exploratory outfit.

Representative **Micajah T. Williams** presented the memorial. Further consideration was indefinitely postponed.

### Proposed expedition design

McBride’s exposition recommended:

- two vessels with enlarged crews and extra boats;
- portable boats for crossing ice or land;
- steam propulsion where possible;
- sailing rig and coal reserved for the ice zone;
- a smaller steam launch for rivers or shallow coasts;
- entry by Spitsbergen, Bering Strait, or the Antarctic;
- attention to “winding meridians” near the polar verge.

He estimated a major northern expedition at **$100,000–$200,000**, with a southern whaling-style venture costing perhaps one-fifth as much. These were McBride’s estimates and were never enacted appropriations.

### The John Quincy Adams–Andrew Jackson story

A later popular narrative says President **John Quincy Adams** intended to finance a Symmes expedition, but lost the 1828 election, after which **Andrew Jackson** canceled it.

- **Status:** Rumor or myth in this form.
- Symmes’s petitions were repeatedly rejected or tabled.
- Jeremiah N. Reynolds later detached polar exploration from the hollow-Earth thesis and won meaningful political support for a government exploring expedition.
- That later movement contributed to the institutional history culminating in the United States Exploring Expedition of 1838–1842.
- Evidence consulted here does not establish that Adams approved an official mission to locate a “Symmes Hole,” nor that Jackson personally canceled such an authorized mission.
- The myth compresses Symmes’s campaign, Reynolds’s later lobbying, presidential politics, and the eventual Exploring Expedition into one dramatic story.

## James McBride and Jeremiah N. Reynolds

### James McBride

James McBride of Hamilton became Symmes’s principal intellectual defender. His relationship with Symmes included:

- attending and reporting lectures;
- collecting newspaper clippings and notices;
- interpreting Symmes’s scattered writings;
- presenting the theory in a systematic book;
- advocating a national expedition.

In 1826, McBride anonymously published *Symmes’s Theory of Concentric Spheres; Demonstrating That the Earth Is Hollow, Habitable Within, and Widely Open About the Poles*. Although based heavily on Symmes’s ideas and writings, it was McBride’s composition. He told Symmes in the prefatory letter that he had not seen all Symmes’s publications and had not submitted the manuscript for revision.

McBride’s scrapbook of articles and lecture material survives digitally through the Biodiversity Heritage Library/Internet Archive ecosystem and is an important primary-source collection.

### Jeremiah N. Reynolds

**Jeremiah N. Reynolds** encountered or joined Symmes’s campaign in the 1820s and became its most effective lecturer. Their association included a joint or coordinated eastern lecture tour beginning around **1824–1825**.

Reynolds was a more accomplished public speaker. He gradually emphasized conventional polar geography, natural history, commerce, sealing, navigation, and national prestige rather than access to an inhabited inner Earth.

The relationship thus moved from collaboration to intellectual divergence:

- Symmes remained committed to the polar openings.
- Reynolds converted the audience and political momentum into a broader campaign for South Seas and Antarctic exploration.
- Reynolds eventually participated in a privately organized southern voyage and helped build support for a federal exploring expedition.
- Symmes’s theory indirectly entered American exploration policy through Reynolds, but the later government expedition was not a hollow-Earth mission.

## Later Years, Illness, and Death, 1824–1829

### Move to Ohio

In **1824**, Symmes moved from Newport, Kentucky, to land near Hamilton, Ohio, reportedly a section granted to him by his uncle. He continued writing, modeling, lecturing, and promoting exploration.

### Eastern lecture travel

His lecture campaign extended into eastern cities. Exact itineraries vary among sources, and a complete city-by-city schedule would require systematic reconstruction from newspapers. The tour was physically and financially taxing.

### Health decline

Local history reports that Symmes’s health deteriorated during or after the lecture tours. The nature of his illness is not securely diagnosed in the accessible record.

He reportedly returned to New Jersey in an attempt to recuperate, then came back to Hamilton in **January 1829** when the trip failed to restore his health.

No reliable evidence establishes:

- alcoholism or drug addiction;
- a diagnosed psychiatric disorder;
- institutionalization;
- self-harm;
- poisoning;
- violence causing his death.

Contemporaries frequently called him insane because of his theory. McBride expressly defended his sanity. Those polemics are not medical diagnoses.

### Death

Symmes died at his Butler County home on **28 or 29 May 1829**, aged forty-eight or forty-nine depending on the disputed birth year and death date.

The precise medical cause has not been established in the sources consulted. “Declining health” or an unspecified illness is the strongest defensible formulation.

He was reportedly buried the next day with military honors.

### Estate

His death left:

- a widow;
- dependent children;
- an estate burdened by debt;
- scattered manuscripts and published fragments;
- no completed authoritative book explaining his system.

Americus, only seventeen, reportedly took responsibility for the household and estate. This family tradition is consistent with the documented financial embarrassment described before Symmes’s death.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Institutions and organizations

- United States Army, First Infantry.
- Fort Belle Fontaine and other frontier military establishments.
- United States Congress, as petitioner.
- Ohio General Assembly, as petitioner.
- New-York Historical Society, reportedly recipient of material concerning his regiment.
- Scientific and learned institutions that received Circular No. 1.
- French Academy of Sciences, reportedly presented with the circular through Count Volney.
- Newspapers including the *National Intelligencer* and numerous regional papers.

### Businesses

- Postwar military supply contracting on the upper Mississippi.
- Licensed trade with the Fox/Meskwaki.
- Paid public lectures, although receipts and fee arrangements are incompletely documented.
- Publication and distribution of circulars and articles.

### Technology and material models

The most important physical technology associated with Symmes was his sectional **wooden hollow-Earth globe**, used in lectures. It displayed:

- polar apertures;
- internal curvature;
- the verge of the opening;
- the path a traveler or ship should follow.

No patent issued to Captain John Cleves Symmes for the hollow globe has been established. Claims that “John Cleves Symmes Jr.” patented an improved gun concern a later namesake and should not be transferred to the theorist without patent-level identification.

### Property

- A valuable section near Hamilton reportedly conveyed by Judge Symmes.
- The property did not prevent insolvency.
- The exact deed, acreage retained at death, mortgages, liens, and probate inventory were not reconstructed from primary county records for this dossier.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Politics

Symmes pursued public patronage but is not known to have held elective office or developed a systematic party ideology. His appeals reflected early national assumptions that government might promote:

- geographical discovery;
- commerce;
- scientific prestige;
- territorial knowledge;
- national competition.

The rhetoric was patriotic and expansionary. The imagined inner world was described as land that could be explored, commercially assessed, and potentially incorporated into national projects. This aspect connects the theory to the geography and colonizing assumptions of the early American republic.

### Natural philosophy

His intellectual development joined:

- mechanical explanations of cosmic formation;
- natural theology;
- empirical reports from travelers;
- distrust of settled scientific authority;
- analogy between Earth, planets, meteors, and rings;
- the belief that apparent polar anomalies indicated hidden geography.

His method repeatedly transformed absence of knowledge into positive evidence: because no expedition had reached the poles, he treated reports of open water, animals, driftwood, or magnetic instability as corroboration of the openings.

### Change within the theory

The earliest circular proposed several concentric spheres. By later lecture activity, he apparently emphasized a single main hollow shell. This simplification may have answered objections about gravity, illumination, and complexity, but Symmes left no definitive final synthesis.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Mary Anne Pelletier Lockwood Symmes

Wife, household partner, and recipient—with her children—of the 1818 dedication. The practical burdens imposed on her by Symmes’s failed commerce, travel, and theoretical campaign are evident indirectly from the family’s indebted circumstances.

### Americus Vespucius Symmes

Eldest surviving son; physician; family provider; memorialist; editor. Americus preserved papers, erected the grave monument, and published the 1878 compilation that became a foundational source for later accounts.

### Judge John Cleves Symmes

Uncle, namesake, and reported donor of the Hamilton-area land. The uncle’s prominence both assisted Captain Symmes and caused lasting biographical confusion.

### James McBride

Follower, systematizer, defender, and biographer. McBride gave Symmes’s unsystematic material its most coherent contemporary form.

### Jeremiah N. Reynolds

Lecturing collaborator and successor in the exploration movement. Reynolds’s eventual abandonment or deemphasis of the hollow-Earth premise marks both Symmes’s greatest institutional influence and the failure of his specific theory.

### John James Audubon

Audubon drew Symmes in 1820. The portrait establishes a documented encounter or sitting, but the surviving catalog entry does not demonstrate ideological agreement or a sustained friendship.

### Richard Mentor Johnson and Micajah T. Williams

Political intermediaries who formally presented Symmes’s petitions. Presentation does not by itself demonstrate endorsement.

### Samuel Latham Mitchill, Humphry Davy, and Alexander von Humboldt

Named by Symmes as his desired “protectors.” No acceptance or sponsorship has been established. Mitchill’s interest in unusual scientific proposals may have made the choice intelligible; the designation nonetheless remained aspirational.

### Critics and newspapers

Symmes’s principal “enemies” were not identifiable personal rivals so much as skeptical editors, scientific institutions, and public ridicule. “Symmes’ Hole” became a stock joke used in political satire and commentary.

## Controversies, Allegations, and Unresolved Questions

### Was Symmes mentally ill?

- **Documented fact:** Newspapers and critics characterized the theory as madness.
- **Documented fact:** Symmes or his defenders sought to assure recipients of his sanity.
- **Documented fact:** He sustained an army career, marriage, contracting business, complex lecture campaign, and extended written argument.
- **Unresolved question:** No competent clinical evidence survives from which to diagnose his mental state.
- Treating the theory as scientifically false does not establish psychiatric illness.

### Was he denied military credit?

McBride alleged that Symmes’s role at Fort Erie and his regiment’s conduct at Lundy’s Lane were misreported and that rewards went to others. Symmes reportedly gathered testimony vindicating the unit.

- **Status:** Reported grievance with some claimed corroboration.
- A full determination would require comparing official reports, unit returns, officers’ correspondence, and the New-York Historical Society submission.

### Did he misuse public or private money?

No evidence located indicates fraud or corruption. He sought public financing openly through petitions and failed in private business. Debt and promotional zeal are documented; financial crime is not.

### Did Congress authorize his expedition?

No. Congress received petitions and declined to advance them.

### Did he actually write *Symzonia*?

Probably not, but the question remains formally disputed. See below.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Symmes’ Hole”

**Story:** Vast openings at the poles lead through a gentle curvature into a warm, inhabited inner world.

**Origin:** Symmes’s own circulars and lectures beginning in 1818.

**Circulation:** Newspapers, lecture notices, satire, *Symzonia*, McBride’s book, family compilations, later fiction, occult literature, pulp fiction, UFO lore, and Internet conspiracy culture.

**Evidence:** The historical evidence firmly establishes that Symmes believed and promoted the proposition. Exploration and modern Earth science establish that the proposed planetary openings do not exist.

**Reputational effect:** “Symmes’ Hole” made him nationally recognizable but converted a complex speculative system into a comic catchphrase.

### Certificate of sanity

**Story:** Symmes mailed his first circular together with a physician’s certificate attesting that he was sane.

**Appearance and circulation:** Repeated in biographical and hollow-Earth literature.

**Evidence:** Contemporary concern over his sanity is well documented; however, the original certificate and exact distribution have not been firmly established in the consulted record.

**Status:** Reported fact requiring archival confirmation.

### The government nearly sent Symmes into the Earth

**Story:** John Quincy Adams approved an expedition, but Andrew Jackson canceled it.

**Evidence against:** The documented Symmes petitions were tabled or indefinitely postponed. Later support centered increasingly on Reynolds’s conventional exploration program. The eventual federal Exploring Expedition occurred years after Symmes’s death and had no mission to enter a polar opening.

**Status:** Rumor or myth produced by conflation.

### Symmes wrote *Symzonia*

**Story:** Symmes used “Captain Adam Seaborn” as a pseudonym for the 1820 hollow-Earth novel *Symzonia; A Voyage of Discovery*.

**Early and later circulation:** Bibliographers and twentieth-century editors repeatedly assigned the novel to Symmes. J. O. Bailey’s 1965 edition accepted the attribution.

**Evidence for:**

- the novel openly invokes Symmes’s theory;
- it appeared during his publicity campaign;
- its author concealed his identity;
- it vividly dramatizes a polar entrance.

**Evidence against:**

- the book’s satirical character does not read straightforwardly as Symmesian advocacy;
- its geography and southern route diverge from aspects of Symmes’s preferred expedition;
- stylistic and historical work has suggested **Nathaniel Ames**;
- reference works now describe authorship as unresolved or likely not Symmes.

**Status:** Disputed attribution; it must not be listed as genuine Symmes authorship.

### The grave marks a real portal

Roadside folklore has jokingly suggested that the hollow globe in Symmes Park marks an actual entrance or “forgotten Symmes Hole.”

- **Evidence:** None.
- **Status:** Modern local folklore.
- **Effect:** It sustains tourism and the symbolic identity of Hamilton’s monument.

### Founder of hollow-Earth theory

Symmes is often called the inventor or “father” of hollow-Earth theory.

- **Against literal priority:** Halley and other writers preceded him.
- **Defensible meaning:** Symmes originated the best-known nineteenth-century American version involving navigable polar openings and a public expedition campaign.

## Works by John Cleves Symmes Jr.

### Authenticated or substantially authenticated writings

1. **“No. 1 Circular” / “To All the World”**  
   St. Louis, 10 April 1818. Privately printed circular. At least two textual states may exist. Genuine authorship.

2. **Second circular or “Memoir 2”**  
   St. Louis, 1818. Argument from geometry and the behavior of rotating matter. Genuine, though surviving texts and titles vary.

3. **Third circular/memoir**  
   St. Louis, 1818. Geological principles. Genuine but bibliographically unstable.

4. **Circular or memoir No. 4**  
   1818 or early 1819. On the original formation of the Allegheny Mountains. Reported by McBride, who had not seen it.

5. **Circular or memoir No. 5**  
   1818 or early 1819. Claiming discovery of open poles. Reported by McBride.

6. **No. 6**  
   Cincinnati, January 1819. Prospectus or list of propositions intended for future treatment.

7. **“Arctic Memoir,” No. 7**  
   Cincinnati, February 1819.

8. **“Light Between the Spheres”**  
   Cincinnati, August 1819; published in the *National Intelligencer*.

9. **Petition to the United States Congress**  
   Presented 7 March 1822 by Senator Richard M. Johnson. Genuine petition; the exact original text should be consulted in legislative records where available.

10. **Renewed petitions to both houses of Congress**  
    December 1823.

11. **Memorial to the Ohio General Assembly**  
    January 1824; presented by Micajah T. Williams.

12. **Numerous newspaper essays, letters, replies, and lecture notices, 1818–1828**  
    Dispersed across American newspapers. A complete authoritative bibliography remains to be compiled from the press record.

13. **Military correspondence and reports**  
    Includes the 26 June 1810 Belle Fontaine morning report signed or submitted by Symmes as acting adjutant, and reported wartime letters in archival collections.

### Works based on Symmes but not written wholly by him

1. **James McBride, *Symmes’s Theory of Concentric Spheres; Demonstrating That the Earth Is Hollow, Habitable Within, and Widely Open About the Poles***  
   Cincinnati: Morgan, Lodge and Fisher, 1826. Published anonymously as “By a Citizen of the United States.” McBride was the author; Symmes was the principal intellectual source and addressee.

2. **Americus Symmes, ed., *The Symmes Theory of Concentric Spheres, Demonstrating That the Earth Is Hollow, Habitable Within, and Widely Open About the Poles***  
   Louisville: Bradley and Gilbert, 1878. Posthumous compilation from Captain Symmes’s writings, edited by his son. Valuable but not a book prepared for publication by Captain Symmes himself.

### Disputed attribution

1. **Captain Adam Seaborn, *Symzonia; A Voyage of Discovery***  
   New York: J. Seymour, 1820. Sometimes attributed to Symmes; other candidates include Nathaniel Ames and Washington Irving. Current evidence does not justify treating it as an authenticated Symmes work.

### Uncompleted or lost projects

Circular No. 1 claimed that Symmes had ready for press a treatise on the “principles of matter” explaining his cosmology and Erasmus Darwin’s “Golden Secret.” No completed publication under that title is known.

He later referred to additional projected memoirs, including one on the source and production of animal heat. Their final manuscript status is uncertain.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Family commemoration

Americus Symmes became the chief architect of his father’s posthumous reputation. He:

- stabilized or supported the indebted family;
- preserved writings;
- published the 1878 compilation;
- obtained permission in **1873** to erect the Hamilton monument.

The monument consists of a stone pedestal topped by a carved globe with a large polar opening. It reproduces the central Symmesian image more effectively than a conventional statue would have.

The old burying ground was later converted to public use. Many remains were moved when Greenwood Cemetery opened, but Symmes’s grave remained. The site became **Symmes Park**.

A restoration commissioned by Historic Hamilton reportedly cost **$16,000** and was completed in 1991.

### Scientific standing

Symmes’s model is scientifically untenable. His significance lies instead in:

- the history of speculative geology and geography;
- the public culture of science in the early republic;
- the relationship between lectures, newspapers, models, and state patronage;
- the history of American polar exploration;
- the formation of hollow-world fiction;
- the boundary between scientific conjecture and fringe theory.

### Exploration legacy

Symmes failed to obtain an expedition and never traveled to either polar region. Reynolds, however, converted part of the movement into practical agitation for Antarctic and Pacific exploration. Symmes therefore had an indirect institutional legacy even though the hollow-Earth objective was discarded.

### Literary legacy

Symmes supplied a highly visual narrative structure:

1. approach an unknown pole;
2. cross an apparently icy barrier;
3. encounter warmer seas;
4. pass unknowingly over a curved verge;
5. discover an inhabited inner world.

This structure entered American and international speculative fiction. *Symzonia* was the earliest substantial fictional response, followed by connections—some direct, some mediated—to Poe, Verne, later hollow-world romances, pulp fiction, occultism, and UFO mythology.

### Local and popular legacy

Hamilton treats the monument as both historical artifact and local curiosity. Modern stories often detach Symmes from his military and commercial life and reduce him to a single eccentric proposition. Conversely, family and regional memorial writing sometimes elevates him into a persecuted visionary. Both are selective traditions.

## Atlas Connections

Only documented or source-supported intersections with names on the supplied atlas roster are included. Mere contemporaneity has been excluded.

### Alexander von Humboldt

- **Nature:** Symmes named Humboldt as one of three desired “protectors” in Circular No. 1.
- **Date and place:** Circular issued at St. Louis, 10 April 1818; distributed internationally.
- **Label:** **Documented fact** that Symmes invoked Humboldt; **no evidence found** that Humboldt accepted, corresponded with Symmes, or endorsed the theory.

### William Henry Harrison

- **Nature:** Family connection. Harrison married Anna Tuthill Symmes, daughter of Judge John Cleves Symmes; Captain Symmes was Anna’s first cousin and therefore Harrison’s cousin by marriage. One of Captain Symmes’s sons was named William H. H. Symmes.
- **Dates and places:** Family relationship existed from before Captain Symmes’s adult life; centered in the Ohio–Indiana–Kentucky family network.
- **Label:** **Documented fact** for the kinship; no source consulted establishes Harrison as a patron or supporter of the hollow-Earth campaign.

### John James Audubon

- **Nature:** Artist and sitter; Audubon made a pencil portrait of Symmes.
- **Date and place:** 1820; exact sitting place not specified in the Smithsonian catalog.
- **Label:** **Documented fact** through the surviving portrait and institutional catalog. No sustained friendship or scientific collaboration was established.

### Edgar Allan Poe

- **Nature:** Indirect intellectual and literary connection through Jeremiah N. Reynolds, polar-expedition discourse, and Symmesian literature.
- **Dates and places:** Poe reviewed Reynolds’s exploration address in 1837 and used Reynolds material in *The Narrative of Arthur Gordon Pym* (1838), after Symmes’s death.
- **Label:** **Documented fact** that Poe used Reynolds’s exploration writing; **reported scholarly influence** for the broader Symmes–*Symzonia*–Poe relationship. No meeting or correspondence with Symmes was possible or found.

### Jules Verne

- **Nature:** Later literary relation through nineteenth-century subterranean and polar-entry traditions.
- **Dates and places:** *Voyage au centre de la Terre*, France, 1864, thirty-five years after Symmes’s death.
- **Label:** **Reported intellectual influence / unresolved degree of directness**. Symmes is part of the historical background to hollow-world fiction, but no consulted source establishes personal contact or an explicit acknowledgment by Verne.

### William R. Bradshaw

- **Nature:** Literary successor. Bradshaw’s *The Goddess of Atvatabar* (1892) used polar access to an inhabited inner world.
- **Date and place:** United States, 1892.
- **Label:** **Reported intellectual/literary influence** through the established Symmesian hollow-world tradition; no personal contact.

### Edmond Halley

Halley is central to Symmes’s intellectual ancestry, but he does **not** appear under that exact name in the supplied atlas roster. He is therefore noted in the main dossier but not counted as an atlas connection.

### Andrew Jackson

- **Nature:** Appears in the later myth that Jackson canceled an Adams-authorized Symmes expedition.
- **Date and place:** United States presidential transition, 1828–1829.
- **Label:** **Rumor or myth**. No documentary evidence consulted establishes a meeting, correspondence, or personal action by Jackson against Symmes.

### Remaining roster

No documented personal, familial, institutional, or explicitly acknowledged intellectual crossing was found between Symmes and the other rostered lives. Given the size of the roster and the fragmented Symmes archive, this is a finding limited to the sources consulted, not proof that every possible archival contact is impossible.
