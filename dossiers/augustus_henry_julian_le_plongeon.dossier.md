# Augustus Henry Julian Le Plongeon: Research Dossier

**Prepared:** 20 August 2026  
**Subject:** Augustus Henry Julian (also Julius) Le Plongeon, photographer, surveyor, medical practitioner, antiquarian, excavator, author, and theorist of ancient American civilization  
**Scope:** Life history, documentary record, work, networks, controversies, myths, bibliography, archives, and legacy

## Research and Evidence Note

This dossier is a reconstruction from the Augustus and Alice Dixon Le Plongeon papers at the Getty Research Institute; the papers and publications of Lawrence G. Desmond; contemporary publications by Augustus and Alice Le Plongeon and Stephen Salisbury Jr.; institutional records; later archaeological studies; and surviving editions and catalogs of Le Plongeon's writings. The principal archive occupies 63 boxes (about 39.4 linear feet) and includes correspondence, diaries, manuscripts, photographs, plans, personal documents, and papers of the couple's heirs.

The record has unusual evidentiary problems:

- Le Plongeon repeatedly converted archaeological images into an elaborate narrative of Queen Móo, Prince Coh, Atlantis/Mu, Egypt, and prehistoric Freemasonry. That narrative is documented as his belief and publication, but it is not accepted as a decipherment of Maya history.
- Alice Dixon Le Plongeon's 1909 memorial is indispensable but hagiographic. Several of its claims—especially that Augustus attended the École Polytechnique and helped found the California Academy of Sciences—are unsupported or contradicted by institutional evidence.
- Alice's Yucatán “diary” survives as a later compilation from notes, letters, and recollection, not as an untouched day-by-day field journal. It remains a major first-person source, but exact dialogue and chronology require caution.
- Older sources commonly give 1825 as the birth year. Archive-based work by Desmond gives 4 May 1826 and reports that the 1826 date appears in family documentation. This dossier uses 1826 as the best-supported date while preserving the disagreement.
- “Dr.,” “M.D.,” and occasionally “LL.D.” appear with his name. No securely identified medical-school or university degree has been found in the consulted records. His medical practice is documented; the route by which he acquired credentials is not.

Labels used below are literal: **documented** means supported by a primary or institutional record; **reported** means preserved in later testimony or biography but not independently established; **claim** means an assertion by an interested party; **disputed** means materially challenged by another source; and **myth/theory** means a narrative whose historical circulation is documented but whose asserted ancient events are not established.

## Family and Ancestry

Alice Le Plongeon's memorial identifies Augustus as the eldest child of **François-Guillaume Le Plongeon**, a French naval officer described as a commodore and chevalier of the Legion of Honour, and **Frances Le Gros du Roche**. The memorial gives the maternal family as Le Gros du Roche. The father's exact rank and full service history were not independently reconstructed from French naval records for this dossier. Modern archival summaries repeat that the family was French and resident on Jersey.

No dependable list of Augustus's siblings was located in the consulted material. The statement that he was the eldest child comes from Alice's memorial. Claims of aristocratic or exceptionally distinguished ancestry in later romantic sketches should be treated as embellishment unless supported by genealogical records.

His wife, **Alice Dixon**, was born in London on 12 December 1851. She was one of nine children of **Henry Dixon**, a copperplate printer and photographer, and **Sophia Cook**. Photography was therefore a family craft before her partnership with Augustus. Alice became far more than an assistant: she operated cameras and the wet-plate process, kept field records, copied murals, wrote articles, gave lectures, handled a rifle during dangerous travel, and co-developed or at least vigorously promoted the couple's historical theories. The Victorian convention of publishing many results under Augustus's name obscured the degree of her contribution.

The couple had no documented children. Their intimate late-life circle included **Henry Field Blackwell** and **Maude Alice Kane Blackwell**. After Alice's death the Blackwells inherited the papers and attempted to publish and sell or place them. Their stewardship became a major part of the posthumous mythology.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1826–1848

Very little securely documented material survives about Le Plongeon's childhood. He was born on Jersey, a British Crown dependency with strong French cultural and linguistic connections. His adult fluency in French, English, and Spanish, later work with Yucatec Maya, and early employment teaching mathematics, drawing, and languages indicate broad education, but the names of childhood schools and teachers have not been established.

Alice later asserted that he studied at the **École Polytechnique in Paris**. Earlier biographies repeated this. Lawrence Desmond checked the school's records and reported no evidence that Le Plongeon attended or graduated. The safest conclusion is that a Polytechnique education is **unverified and probably erroneous**, not a documented credential.

The origin of his medical title is similarly unclear. He later practiced medicine, advertised or operated electro-hydropathic treatment, and signed work as M.D.; the Getty finding aid suggests that he may have apprenticed in medicine in California. No diploma or licensing record was identified. “LL.D.” in the memorial is also unsupported by a named granting institution.

## Education and Formation

Le Plongeon's formation was practical, itinerant, and multilingual rather than securely institutional:

- **Mathematics and drawing** supported his reported teaching in Valparaíso and his surveying and city-planning work in California.
- **Photography** became his most durable expertise. He mastered portrait work and the wet-collodion glass-negative process, later adapting it to hot, humid, remote ruins.
- A frequently repeated account says that in England he observed new photographic techniques and persuaded **William Henry Fox Talbot** to teach him a paper-negative process. The Getty biography presents this as reported, not conclusively documented. Direct apprenticeship to Talbot should therefore remain qualified.
- His archaeological imagination drew heavily on **John Lloyd Stephens and Frederick Catherwood**, whose books made Maya monuments famous, and on **Charles-Étienne Brasseur de Bourbourg**, whose diffusionist writings and mistaken reading of the Troano manuscript helped produce the idea of a lost land called Mu.
- His Peruvian field travel, the 1868 earthquake, and study of ancient sites moved him toward a New World origin theory for civilization.
- His later Masonic, spiritualist, and Theosophical milieu supplied analogies through which he interpreted architectural and ritual imagery. He was a Freemason; surviving certificates document lodge affiliations and authority to represent a Lima lodge. Direct personal membership in the Theosophical Society has not been established, although Alice lectured at a Blavatsky Lodge in 1890 and Augustus's posthumous work appeared in the Theosophical journal *The Word*.

## Early Career and Global Movements, c. 1848–1872

### Chile and Valparaíso

According to Alice's memorial and later archive-based biographies, Le Plongeon sailed toward South America in the late 1840s, was shipwrecked off Chile, and settled for a time in **Valparaíso**. He reportedly taught mathematics, drawing, and languages at a college. No ship manifest, wreck report, or college appointment was identified, so the episode is **reported**, though consistent across biographical accounts.

### California Gold Rush, surveying, and Marysville

News of the California Gold Rush drew him north. By about 1849–1850 he was in California. He worked as a surveyor and is credited with preparing the plan or laying out streets for **Marysville** in the Sacramento Valley, usually dated 1850 or 1851. He was paid partly in land deeds. Their sale provided capital for later travel. The archival papers include land and financial documentation from this phase.

He returned to California after a period of travel and by the mid-1850s operated a photographic portrait studio on **Clay Street, San Francisco**. Desmond places the studio in 1855. He joined the **California Academy of Sciences** in 1856. Alice's later claim that he was one of the Academy's founders is inconsistent with the Academy's own history: it was founded on 4 April 1853 by seven named men, before Le Plongeon's recorded membership. Membership is documented; founding status is not.

### England, the Caribbean, Mexico, the Pacific, Australia, and China

After selling California land, Le Plongeon traveled to England and then through a wide geographic circuit reported to include **St. Thomas in the Danish West Indies, Mexico, Australia, China, and Pacific islands**. Exact dates, route, and duration are not fully recoverable. Some biographies connect the English trip to the 1851 Great Exhibition and to Fox Talbot; this dossier treats the Talbot instruction story as reported rather than established.

### San Francisco photography and medicine

Back in San Francisco, he combined photographic work with medical practice. The precise nature of his medical training is unknown. By the 1860s he consistently used “Doctor.” His later Lima practice employed **electro-hydropathy**—electrical stimulation and water treatments then circulating in alternative and reform medicine.

### Peru, 1862–1870

In 1862 Le Plongeon moved to **Lima**, where he ran a photographic studio and an electro-hydropathic clinic. An 1864 grant or license associated with his hydroelectric baths survives in the archive. His Masonic certificates from Lima, 1862–1870, are also preserved.

Over roughly eight years he traveled through Peru and photographed antiquities, landscapes, people, and ruins, including **Tiwanaku/Tiahuanaco** in present-day Bolivia. He applied wet-collodion photography outside the studio, carrying glass plates, chemicals, water, a darkroom, and heavy camera equipment. These years made him an experienced archaeological photographer before he entered Yucatán.

He studied earthquakes and observed the destructive **1868 Arica earthquake** and its effects. The archive includes his notes on seismology, interviews or observations, and a philanthropic society concerned with earthquake victims. He also wrote on the temperature of the earth's interior.

Le Plongeon became embroiled in public religious controversy. He attacked Jesuit influence and answered the Franciscan Pedro Gual's defense of Catholic teaching in two Spanish-language books, *La religión de Jesús comparada con las enseñanzas de la Iglesia* (1867) and *Los Jesuitas y el Perú* (1869). The argument was rationalist and anticlerical rather than an archaeological dispute.

The relationship with American diplomat and antiquarian **Ephraim George Squier** became contentious. Le Plongeon supplied or shipped Peruvian glass negatives through Squier in 1863. He later accused Squier of publishing images without proper credit and repeatedly tried to recover the negatives. The archive preserves the dispute, but no court adjudication has been found. It is best described as a **documented authorship/property accusation by Le Plongeon**, unresolved in law.

He sought institutional support and at one point hoped Squier might help him obtain a diplomatic or official post in Lima. That ambition did not produce a lasting appointment.

### California, New York, and London, 1870–1873

Le Plongeon returned to California around 1870 and lectured at the California Academy of Sciences on Peruvian antiquities. An unpublished manuscript, “A Sketch of the Ancient Inhabitants of Peru and Their Civilization,” is associated with these lectures. He then worked between **New York and London**, lecturing, seeking patrons, studying Spanish manuscripts, and trying to monetize items acquired in Peru. He offered paintings attributed to **Murillo** and **Juan del Castillo** for sale; later correspondence shows that these paintings remained potential sources of expedition finance.

In London he met **Alice Dixon**, twenty-five years his junior and already trained in photography through her father. The couple met again in New York and married there in 1873. Alice joined the project as technical collaborator, writer, logistical partner, and eventually a public lecturer in her own right.

Le Plongeon published *Manual de fotografía* in New York in 1873. The Spanish-language handbook consolidated his practical knowledge immediately before the Yucatán expedition.

## Chronological Life History: Yucatán and British Honduras, 1873–1885

### 1873: Voyage, yellow fever, and the Mérida arrest

The Le Plongeons left New York aboard the *Cuba* on **28 July 1873**, reached Havana on 3 August, and arrived at Progreso on 7 August. In Mérida, Alice contracted yellow fever. Augustus nursed her while the couple established contacts among local intellectuals and officials and began learning Yucatec Maya.

Within weeks Augustus became involved in a violent confrontation arising from a dispute over a rented house. According to Alice's later-compiled diary, a lawyer, **Escalante**, insulted him. Augustus sought protection from a judge, returned home for a revolver, slapped Escalante twice, and confronted the lawyer when Escalante drew a sword cane. Augustus displayed the revolver with its muzzle down and then surrendered himself. He was held incommunicado in the **Castillo de San Benito**, later moved to a hospital, and detained for about fifteen days. The proceeding was dismissed in October. This account is detailed but derives principally from Alice; no complete court file was consulted. It is a **reported arrest and detention**, not a conviction.

### 1874: Izamal, public-health work, and itinerant photography

The couple photographed in **Progreso** and **Izamal**. At the great Kinich Kak Moo mound in Izamal, Augustus explored narrow interior spaces and acquired a local reputation as a magician or “wizard.” The story appears through Alice's narrative and later retellings; it records local perception rather than a supernatural event.

During a smallpox epidemic in September–October 1874, Governor **Liborio Irigoyen** reportedly asked Augustus to vaccinate inhabitants. He undertook the work without adequate public funds. This episode fits his medical practice but is known chiefly from the expedition record.

General **Guillermo Palomino** became an essential political and military patron. He supplied servants, introductions, and, later, armed protection. On 14 November 1874 the couple left Mérida for eastern Yucatán. They traveled by *volán coche*, opened temporary portrait studios to earn cash, and endured heat, illness, insects, snakes, shortages of food, and the logistical demands of wet-plate chemistry.

### 1875: Valladolid, the Caste War frontier, and Chichén Itzá

By May 1875 they reached **Valladolid**. The Caste War was still active. Chichén Itzá lay close to the “Line of the East,” beyond which forces associated with Chan Santa Cruz operated. The devastated village of **Pisté** and military escorts made the war a constant part of fieldwork. Two military companies accompanied or protected the expedition on the approach to Chichén Itzá.

At **Chichén Itzá**, Augustus and Alice cleared vegetation, measured buildings, photographed façades, copied inscriptions and murals, made molds, and conducted excavations with local Maya laborers. Their overlapping façade photographs, elevated camera positions, stereographs, and processing in darkened rooms within ruins were technically advanced for the period. Alice shared both camera and documentation work.

In November they excavated the **Platform of the Eagles and Jaguars** and uncovered a reclining stone figure. Augustus first called it **Chaacmol**; Stephen Salisbury's publications helped stabilize the spelling **Chacmool**, later **Chac Mool**. Le Plongeon believed the figure represented a murdered historical prince named Coh, whose story he claimed to read in Chichén's imagery. Modern archaeology retains the type-name *chacmool* for a class of reclining offering figures but does not accept Le Plongeon's derivation or biography of Prince Coh.

To move the heavy sculpture, the party built a road and cart. Because the political situation and export permission were uncertain, they concealed it near Pisté.

### 1876: Export petition, revolution, Uxmal, and the Chacmool loss

On **27 January 1876**, from Mérida, Le Plongeon petitioned President **Sebastián Lerdo de Tejada**. He identified himself as a U.S. citizen and sought authority to display the Chacmool and other antiquities at the Philadelphia Centennial Exhibition, an appointment as Mexican commissioner, and armed protection. The memorial was printed in Yucatán's official press in April.

Revolution and political disorder interrupted the work; his local laborers were disarmed. Mexican authorities refused to let him export the Chacmool. Photographs and smaller materials intended for Philadelphia were delayed; Salisbury ultimately bought at least part of that material.

From April through August 1876, the couple worked at **Uxmal**, living in a room of the Governor's Palace. They systematically photographed architecture, made molds, and began a measured survey later completed in 1881. Their high scaffolds and heavy glass-plate apparatus created obvious risks. The photographs now preserve details subsequently lost or damaged.

The Mexican government's decision to take possession of the Chacmool became Le Plongeon's largest material and psychological setback. He described it as theft or robbery and assigned the antiquities a value of thousands of pounds. There is no evidence that a court recognized his private ownership. The episode is a conflict between an excavator's asserted property rights and Mexico's control of antiquities, not a proven criminal taking by either party.

### 1877: State recovery of the Chacmool, public spectacle, and Cozumel

In February 1877 a Mexican commission with soldiers and roughly 150 Maya workers recovered the hidden statue. It entered Mérida on **1 March 1877** amid public ceremony. Stephen Salisbury recorded a local story that workers heard the statue call at night, “**Conex! Conex!**”—“Let us go!” The talking-statue motif is folklore attached to the recovery, not evidence about the object.

The Le Plongeons traveled through **Isla Mujeres**, **Cancún**, and **Cozumel**. Augustus reached Cozumel in early February 1877 and spent several months recording structures and antiquities. Their work extended to **Aké** and **Mayapán** at different points in the expedition.

### 1877–1879: British Honduras/Belize

The couple spent nearly two years in **British Honduras**, principally Belize, supporting themselves through portrait photography while writing and seeking purchasers for images. Augustus offered a set of about seventy Belize views at ten dollars each; only a fraction is now identified. Letters to Salisbury reveal persistent cash shortage and requests to sell London property or the Spanish paintings. These are not signs of hidden wealth: the expedition depended on studio income, sales, patrons, and intermittent institutional support.

### 1880: Fundraising in New York and negotiations with Porfirio Díaz

On **25 April 1880** Augustus sailed to New York to solicit patrons, sell photographs and molds, and lecture. In Mexico City he met **Porfirio Díaz**. According to the expedition record, Díaz offered a permissive arrangement under which underground finds might remain with the excavators if a representative share went to the national museum. Alice was not included in the meeting. The precise legal force and duration of this understanding are unclear.

Le Plongeon experimented with making light-weight molds and casts, including use of powdered paper supplied by publisher and patron **Pierre Lorillard**. He proposed a reconstruction or model of a Maya temple for an exposition in New Orleans. Smithsonian Secretary **Spencer F. Baird** supported aspects of his documentation, but the temple project collapsed when fire-insurance requirements made it impracticable.

### 1881: Mayapán and Uxmal

Le Plongeon published *Mayapan and Maya Inscriptions* through the American Antiquarian Society. He returned to Uxmal, completed survey work, and continued molds and photography. His investigations were real field operations, but his interpretive framework had by then hardened: he treated resemblance as proof of migration, assigned enormous ages to monuments, and read architectural iconography as a continuous dynastic narrative.

### 1882: Institutional rupture and museum disputes

In January *Scientific American* publicized his claim that Maya civilization had seeded Chaldea and Egypt. His relationship with the **American Antiquarian Society** deteriorated as editors criticized his arguments. He resigned his membership in July 1882; he had been elected in April 1878.

At the **Metropolitan Museum of Art**, director **Luigi Palma di Cesnola** initially requested casts but later canceled or mishandled arrangements. The couple alleged that some casts were damaged. The affair added to their conviction that institutions exploited or obstructed them, but the consulted record does not establish fraud by the museum.

He returned to Yucatán in April 1882 and spent more than a year in Mérida writing and preparing another season.

### 1883–1884: Final Chichén season and the Platform of Venus

In November 1883 the Le Plongeons began a second major season at Chichén Itzá under armed protection arranged through Palomino. They excavated the **Platform of Venus**, which they called the “Mausoleum of the High Priest Cay.” For this excavation they recorded artifact positions, architectural plans, sections, color, and the stages of excavation in photographs. Whatever the errors of interpretation, the field documentation was unusually systematic for its date.

They also traced or photographed murals, including imagery that became raw material for the Queen Móo narrative. Alice described nights of armed vigilance and once manned defenses at Tizimín; their dog **Trinity** appears in expedition accounts. The war zone was not literary scenery but a real operational risk.

Alice published reports in outlets including *Scientific American*. By 1884–1885 the couple concluded the long Yucatán phase and moved permanently to **Brooklyn**.

## Later Life in Brooklyn, 1885–1908

### Publishing and lecturing

The final twenty-three years were devoted to arranging photographs, giving lectures, corresponding with patrons and scholars, defending the couple's theories, and publishing increasingly synthetic works.

*Sacred Mysteries among the Mayas and the Quiches, 11,500 Years Ago* appeared in 1886. It argued that Maya and Quiché rites were ancestral to the mysteries of Egypt, Greece, Chaldea, and India and to Freemasonry. Its Masonic audience provided an intellectual community that mainstream Americanist institutions increasingly denied him.

*Queen Móo and the Egyptian Sphinx* appeared in London in 1896, with a revised/self-published New York edition in 1900. A donation from **Phoebe Apperson Hearst** helped finance publication. The book presented the full Queen Móo–Prince Coh narrative, transatlantic migrations, Mu/Atlantis, Egyptian derivations, linguistic comparisons, and photographic plates.

Alice wrote articles, lectured, and published *Queen Móo's Talisman: The Fall of the Maya Empire* in 1902 as an epic poem dedicated to Augustus. She developed a dramatic version, *The Fall of Maya*. Her works recast the couple's reconstruction in imaginative rather than conventionally archaeological form.

### Finances and property

No evidence was found of a corporation, major investment enterprise, patent portfolio, or permanent academic salary. Le Plongeon's finances were a patchwork:

- proceeds from California land;
- portrait studios and medical practice;
- sale of photographs, antiquities, casts, and paintings;
- lecture fees and article payments;
- assistance from Stephen Salisbury Jr., Pierre Lorillard, Phoebe Hearst, and institutional contacts;
- book sales and royalties;
- efforts to obtain museum purchases and government commissions.

The Chacmool seizure, failed Centennial display, canceled cast sales, New Orleans insurance obstacle, inability to secure a teaching post, and the U.S. depression beginning in 1893 repeatedly damaged the couple's position. Correspondence includes direct statements that they needed money. *Sacred Mysteries* supplied income until its stock sold out; *Queen Móo* required outside support and self-publication. Assertions that Le Plongeon financed everything from Marysville land profits oversimplify decades of precarious fundraising.

### Conflict with professional archaeology

His opponents included **Daniel Garrison Brinton**, **Samuel Haven**, and **Philipp J. J. Valentini**, all of whom rejected his chronologies, readings, or Old World diffusion claims. Le Plongeon issued challenges and polemics; a proposed public “scientific duel” with Brinton, printed in the *Brooklyn Daily Eagle* in 1894, was ignored.

The conflict was not simply “professional versus amateur.” Many nineteenth-century Americanists lacked modern archaeological training. Le Plongeon possessed exceptional field-photographic competence and gathered original data. The deeper break lay in method: he assigned narrative identities to figures without a valid decipherment, treated visual or lexical resemblance as genealogical proof, and did not revise central conclusions when evidence and colleagues contradicted them.

His edited illustrations require caution. R. Tripp Evans and other scholars have noted that some published images were redrawn, composited, or enhanced to make motifs easier to compare. Such interventions were not always transparent to readers. This is a criticism of documentary practice; the consulted sources do not establish a criminal fraud prosecution.

### Religious and esoteric milieu

Le Plongeon's archive contains Masonic certificates from the 1860s, including authority to represent a Lima lodge in California. *Sacred Mysteries* explicitly addressed Masonic origins. His later circle overlapped spiritualist and Theosophical readerships. The archive preserves a manuscript, “Experiences of Augustus Le Plongeon,” concerning psychic experiences.

Claims that he and Alice formally belonged to a Theosophical organization, or that they explicitly announced themselves as literal reincarnations of Prince Coh and Queen Móo, are less secure than later retellings suggest. Desmond found no firm evidence of a direct personal relationship between Augustus and the Theosophical Society. Alice's 1890 lecture to a Blavatsky Lodge and the publication of posthumous manuscripts in *The Word* document proximity and reception, not necessarily formal membership or a specific reincarnation declaration.

### Final illness and death

Le Plongeon's health declined during 1908. Alice's memorial says an illness culminated in his death but does not name a diagnosis. He died in Brooklyn on **13 December 1908**. No reliable evidence of addiction, psychiatric hospitalization, suicide, violence causing death, or a specific terminal disease was found.

Desmond reported, from a memorial formerly held by the Long Island Historical Society, that the body was cremated at **Fresh Pond Crematory** and the ashes scattered at sea. Because the cited memorial could not later be located, those disposition details are best treated as reported, not independently verified here.

Alice survived him by eighteen months. She died on **8 June 1910**, aged fifty-eight, from breast cancer. The Blackwells inherited the papers.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Photography

Le Plongeon's principal technology was the **wet-collodion glass-plate negative process**, supplemented by stereoscopic cameras, panoramic/overlapping sequences, elevated tripods or scaffolds, printmaking, tracings, and molds. In the field each plate had to be sensitized, exposed, and developed while wet. The couple converted rooms in ruins into darkrooms and transported fragile glass and chemicals through tropical terrain.

Their surviving output is variously counted because “image” may mean a unique negative, print, stereoview, slide, or duplicate. Desmond cataloged **1,034 Yucatán and Belize photographs** in one major survey; broader institutional counts identify more than **2,400 photographic objects** associated with the couple. The collection includes architecture, inscriptions, sculpture, excavation stages, landscapes, towns, ethnographic portraits, and studio work.

### Surveying, planning, and architectural recording

His earliest major project was the plan of Marysville. In Yucatán, he and Alice applied survey methods to façade sequences, maps, ground plans, elevations, and cross-sections. Their Uxmal and Platform of Venus records are the strongest evidence for his genuine archaeological contribution.

### Medical and electro-hydropathic practice

Le Plongeon operated a Lima clinic using hydrotherapy and electricity and undertook vaccination work during the 1874 Yucatán smallpox epidemic. The scientific efficacy of all electro-hydropathic treatments cannot be inferred from the record. No patent for the apparatus has been identified.

### Casts, molds, and proposed displays

The couple made molds of Maya reliefs and sought to sell casts to museums. A group of about 264 papier-mâché molds has been associated with the American Museum of Natural History, but Desmond was unable to locate the full group. A proposed New Orleans temple reconstruction failed because of insurance costs. Metropolitan Museum negotiations failed. These projects show an attempted business model—reproductions and museum display—that never became financially stable.

### Organizational affiliations

- California Academy of Sciences: member from 1856; not securely a founder.
- San Francisco Microscopical Society: claimed affiliation in the 1876 Mexican memorial.
- New York Philological Society: claimed affiliation in the same memorial.
- Mexican Geographical and Statistical Society: claimed corresponding membership.
- American Antiquarian Society: elected April 1878; resigned July 1882 after editorial conflict.
- Freemasonry: documented by certificates; exact complete lodge history and rank remain incompletely reconstructed.
- No university faculty appointment, permanent museum curatorship, government archaeological office, or continuing diplomatic post has been found.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Politics and states

Le Plongeon was not a party politician. His political activity was operational and petitionary:

- He negotiated with Lerdo de Tejada's government over excavation, export, protection, and the Philadelphia Centennial.
- He obtained or sought protection from Yucatecan governors and military officers during the Caste War.
- He met Porfirio Díaz and sought favorable excavation terms.
- His 1876 memorial asserted U.S. citizenship, partly to frame a transnational claim.
- He publicly opposed Jesuit and Catholic ecclesiastical authority in Peru.

The expedition depended on armed state power and local Maya labor while operating in a civil war. Alice later wrote sympathetically about Maya grievances and at times considered the rebellion justified. Evidence for Augustus's own political position is less explicit.

### Intellectual trajectory

1. **Practical science and technology:** mathematics, surveying, photography, medicine, and seismology.
2. **American antiquarianism:** direct recording of Peru and Maya monuments.
3. **American primacy:** conviction that advanced civilization began in the Americas rather than arriving from the Old World.
4. **Diffusionism:** claims that Maya migrants founded or transformed Atlantis/Mu, Egypt, Chaldea, India, and other civilizations.
5. **Esoteric synthesis:** identification of Maya rites as sources of ancient mysteries and Freemasonry.
6. **Dynastic romance:** Queen Móo, Prince Coh, Prince Aac, and their supposed migration to Egypt.

This trajectory mixed anti-Eurocentric reversal—the Americas as source rather than recipient—with a method that imposed Old World and Masonic categories on Maya material.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### 1. Unverified academic and medical credentials

**Claim:** École Polytechnique education; M.D. and LL.D. credentials.  
**Evidence:** Alice's memorial and titles on publications. Desmond found no Polytechnique enrollment or graduation record. No degree-granting medical or law institution was identified.  
**Status:** Polytechnique claim unverified and likely false; medical practice documented but academic credential unresolved; LL.D. unexplained.

### 2. 1873 Mérida assault/arrest

**Claim/event:** He slapped attorney Escalante and displayed a revolver during a sword-cane confrontation.  
**Evidence:** Alice's expedition diary narrative; detention at Castillo de San Benito and later hospital, about fifteen days.  
**Outcome:** Case reportedly dismissed in October 1873.  
**Status:** Strong first-person report, but no court file reviewed and no conviction established.

### 3. Squier negative and attribution dispute

**Claim:** E. G. Squier retained or used Le Plongeon's Peruvian negatives and published images without proper attribution.  
**Evidence:** Surviving correspondence and repeated recovery efforts.  
**Status:** Documented accusation and property dispute; not adjudicated in the consulted record.

### 4. Chacmool ownership/export dispute

**Claim:** Le Plongeon owned the excavated statue and Mexico “robbed” him by taking it.  
**Evidence:** His 1876 memorial, state refusal of export, concealment of the statue, and government recovery in 1877.  
**Status:** The recovery by Mexican authorities is documented; his moral and financial language was his claim. No court finding of ownership or theft has been found.

### 5. Treatment of antiquities

Le Plongeon sought to export major objects and sell smaller materials, photographs, molds, and casts. Such practice was common among nineteenth-century antiquarians but collided with growing Mexican state control of heritage. The record shows dispute and removal attempts; it does not show a criminal smuggling conviction.

### 6. Image alteration and tendentious reconstruction

Later scholars have shown that published illustrations could be redrawn, enhanced, or composited, sometimes serving arguments about cross-cultural resemblance. The underlying field photographs often remain valuable and can be compared against the printed plates. **Status:** documented methodological criticism; not the subject of a known fraud prosecution.

### 7. Institutional conflict

Le Plongeon resigned from the American Antiquarian Society after criticism; disputed cast arrangements with the Metropolitan Museum; and accused scholars of suppression or appropriation. Institutions in turn regarded his deductions as speculative. These were scholarly and financial disputes, not criminal investigations.

### 8. No evidence located for other requested categories

No reliable evidence was found of money laundering, organized corruption, intelligence-service employment, political violence committed by Le Plongeon, imprisonment beyond the 1873 detention, addiction, a major civil judgment, bankruptcy adjudication, or a criminal conviction. Absence from the consulted record is not proof that no minor proceeding existed.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### Queen Móo, Prince Coh, and Prince Aac

1. **Story:** Queen Móo, daughter of King Canchi and Queen Zoc, chooses her brother Coh; their brother Aac kills Coh from jealousy; Móo flees Mayab, reaches the vanished land of Mu/Atlantis, proceeds to Egypt, becomes the prototype of Isis, and commemorates Coh/Osiris in the Sphinx.
2. **Appearance and circulation:** Developed from the 1870s and fully published in *Sacred Mysteries* (1886), *Queen Móo and the Egyptian Sphinx* (1896/1900), and Alice's *Queen Móo's Talisman* (1902). Masonic, Theosophical, occult, lost-continent, and popular-archaeology audiences circulated it.
3. **Evidence offered:** Chichén murals and reliefs, the reclining statue, architectural comparisons, word resemblance, a jade cylinder, and Le Plongeon's claimed readings of Maya inscriptions.
4. **Evidence against:** His glyph readings do not correspond to modern Maya epigraphy; the assigned personal identities and chronology are not supported; resemblance is not proof of migration; Maya and Egyptian chronologies do not fit his story.
5. **Reputational effect:** The romance made him famous beyond archaeology but helped discredit him among professionalizing Americanists. It continues to overshadow his photography.

### The Chacmool as Prince Coh

1. **Story:** The excavated reclining figure was Prince Coh or his effigy, associated with his tomb and sacrificed heart.
2. **Appearance:** Le Plongeon's 1876 reports and Salisbury's 1877 publication; elaborated in later books.
3. **Evidence:** Location of discovery and Le Plongeon's reading of surrounding images.
4. **Against:** *Chacmool* is now a type-name for reclining offering figures found in several Mesoamerican contexts. The “Prince Coh” identity and proposed Yucatec etymology are rejected.
5. **Effect:** Ironically, Le Plongeon's invented name became standard archaeological vocabulary even as its explanation was discarded.

### The talking statue: “Conex! Conex!”

1. **Story:** Maya workers recovering the Chacmool heard it call “Let us go!” at night.
2. **Appearance:** Reported by Stephen Salisbury in the contemporary account of the 1877 recovery.
3. **Circulators:** Workers, local observers, and Salisbury's publication.
4. **Evidence:** Testimony/folklore only; no evidentiary basis for a speaking object.
5. **Effect:** It dramatized the statue's journey and the local appropriation of an archaeological event into folklore.

### Augustus as “wizard” at Izamal

1. **Story:** His entry into narrow or hidden spaces and unfamiliar equipment made local people regard him as a magician.
2. **Appearance:** Alice's expedition narrative and later biographies.
3. **Evidence:** First-person report of local reaction; wording may be literary.
4. **Effect:** Fed the explorer-magician image later attached to him.

### Mu and Atlantis

1. **Story:** A lost Atlantic land, Mu, transmitted civilization between Maya America and Egypt; the Maya were ancestral to much of the ancient Old World.
2. **Appearance:** Built on Brasseur de Bourbourg's 1869–1870 publication of the Troano manuscript and developed by Le Plongeon from the 1870s onward.
3. **Evidence offered:** A mistaken codex reading, claimed cognates, symbolic and architectural similarities, and catastrophist traditions.
4. **Against:** The “Mu” reading was erroneous; no geological or archaeological evidence supports Le Plongeon's continent or migration scheme.
5. **Effect:** Influenced later lost-continent literature. **James Churchward** relocated Mu to the Pacific and built a different system around supposed Naacal tablets. Influence is plausible; claims that Alice simply handed Augustus's papers to Churchward conflict with the documented Blackwell inheritance and should not be repeated as fact.

### Maya origins of Freemasonry

1. **Story:** Maya initiatory rites and architecture preserved mysteries ancestral to Freemasonry, Egypt, Greece, Chaldea, and India.
2. **Appearance:** Especially *Sacred Mysteries* (1886).
3. **Circulators:** Le Plongeon, Masonic readers, occult and Theosophical publishing networks.
4. **Against:** No accepted textual or archaeological chain connects Classic/Postclassic Maya institutions to modern Freemasonry.
5. **Effect:** Secured an audience after archaeological institutions rejected his larger chronology.

### Alice and Augustus as reincarnations of Móo and Coh

1. **Story:** The couple believed themselves literal reincarnations of the royal pair; Alice wore “Queen Móo's talisman” as proof or token.
2. **Appearance:** Common in later secondary and popular accounts, especially those emphasizing “psychic archaeology.”
3. **Evidence:** Alice wore the excavated jade ornament and wrote imaginative work in Móo's voice/world; Augustus recorded psychic experiences; the couple intensely identified with the narrative.
4. **Against/limits:** In the consulted primary texts, no unambiguous joint declaration of literal reincarnation was located. Alice's poem says Augustus's works inspired it. Identification, performance, spiritual belief, and literal past-life claim have often been collapsed into one assertion.
5. **Effect:** Strengthened the image of the couple as occult fantasists and further displaced attention from Alice's technical labor and the field archive.

### Hidden Maya codices at Uxmal and Chichén Itzá

1. **Story:** The Le Plongeons found ancient Maya manuscripts in the Akab Dzib at Chichén Itzá and the Pyramid of the Magician/Adivino at Uxmal, reburied them, and told Maude Blackwell the secret on Alice's deathbed.
2. **Appearance:** Maude Blackwell told Frans Blom and Sylvanus Morley this in 1931 while trying to place the papers.
3. **Evidence:** Blackwell's letters and interview document that she made the claim.
4. **Against:** A 1907 letter from Augustus to Charles Pickering Bowditch says he believed manuscripts might exist and would search if financed—strong evidence that he had not already found the alleged cache. Desmond concluded the codex story was hearsay or contrivance.
5. **Effect:** Created a treasure-secret around the papers, complicated negotiations with scholars, and remains attractive to conspiracy literature.

### Founder of the California Academy of Sciences

1. **Story:** Alice's memorial called Augustus a founder.
2. **Evidence:** He was a member by 1856.
3. **Against:** The Academy identifies seven founders at its 4 April 1853 establishment; Le Plongeon is not among them.
4. **Status/effect:** Hagiographic inflation of a real affiliation.

### Reception and interpretive categories

Later writing tends to fall into four groups:

- **Institutional archaeological histories**, which reject the diffusionist chronology but credit photography and early excavation.
- **Occult, Masonic, Atlantis, and Mu literature**, which preserves or adapts his speculative system.
- **Histories of photography**, which emphasize technique, visual archives, and Alice's collaboration.
- **Gender and intellectual-history studies**, which examine how Alice's work was absorbed into Augustus's authorship and how romantic narrative shaped supposedly scientific images.

## Death, Estate, and Posthumous Publication

After Augustus's death in 1908, Alice continued to organize and defend the work until her death in 1910. Henry and Maude Blackwell inherited the estate. Maude arranged publication of *The Origin of the Egyptians* and “The Pyramid of Xochicalco” in *The Word* in 1913–1914. She tried to interest Frans Blom and Sylvanus Morley in the collection, but reacted strongly to criticism of Le Plongeon's diffusionism.

Financial hardship after Henry Blackwell's death contributed to efforts to sell the archive. The **Philosophical Research Society**, associated with Manly P. Hall, acquired a large portion in 1931. The subsequent concentration and cataloging of the papers at the Getty Research Institute transformed Le Plongeon from a caricature recoverable mainly through polemics into a documentable historical actor.

The hidden-codices story arose during these posthumous negotiations. It should be understood as part of the archive's sales and reputation history, not evidence of an extant codex cache.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Archaeological record

Le Plongeon's central historical system—Maya origin of Egyptian civilization, Queen Móo as Isis, Coh as Osiris, Mu/Atlantis migrations, an 11,500-year chronology, and Maya origins of Freemasonry—is rejected by modern archaeology and epigraphy. Rejection rests on incompatible chronology, invalid linguistic and glyph readings, lack of a transmission chain, and misuse of resemblance.

His documentary work has a different standing. Augustus and Alice were among the first systematic photographers and excavators at Chichén Itzá and Uxmal. Their images capture monuments before later damage, reconstruction, removal, and environmental erosion. Overlapping façade sequences, stereographs, excavation photographs, mural copies, and measured drawings remain useful evidence.

### The word “chacmool”

His longest-lived archaeological coinage is *chacmool*. The name's supposed ancient etymology and identity with Prince Coh are unsupported, yet archaeologists retained the label for a recognizable Mesoamerican sculptural form. The survival of the term is a case in which nomenclature outlived theory.

### Professionalization and exclusion

Le Plongeon's career marks the transition from gentleman-antiquarian exploration to professional archaeology. His rejection was not caused by lack of field experience; it followed repeated insistence on untestable or refuted conclusions and escalating conflict with editors and institutions. Later retellings turned him into a stock “crank,” obscuring how much early archaeological practice—including collection, removal, patronage, and dramatic reconstruction—was shared by his more successful contemporaries.

### Alice's recovered authorship

Modern scholarship has restored Alice Dixon Le Plongeon as co-producer of the visual and textual archive. Some photographs traditionally credited to Augustus were made by Alice; their notebooks and published arguments emerged from constant collaboration. Attribution remains difficult because nineteenth-century publication conventions, later cataloging, and Maude Blackwell's editing frequently merged “we” into a single male author.

### Lost continents and popular culture

Le Plongeon helped transfer “Mu” from Brasseur's erroneous codex reading into anglophone lost-continent literature. Theosophical, Masonic, New Age, and pseudoarchaeological writers reused elements of his system; Churchward's Pacific Mu was different but belongs to the later tradition his work helped normalize. Queen Móo persists in occult history, fiction, internet lore, and popular video.

### Archival reinterpretation

The major late-twentieth- and twenty-first-century reassessment, led by Lawrence Desmond and Phyllis Messenger, separates three things that earlier judgments collapsed:

1. technically important photography and field documentation;
2. historically significant but invalid diffusionist interpretation;
3. a collaborative marriage whose gendered authorship was misdescribed.

This separation neither rehabilitates the ancient-history claims nor dismisses the archive because those claims failed.

[Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/57458/57458-h/57458-h.htm)

### Helena Petrovna Blavatsky

- **[T3]** **Documented fact.** The written engagement was reciprocal but not evidence of correspondence or a personal meeting. Blavatsky cited Le Plongeon and *Sacred Mysteries* in *The Secret Doctrine* (1888), accepting claimed Maya–Egyptian ritual similarities while disputing his direction of cultural transmission. Le Plongeon subsequently cited Blavatsky’s *Secret Doctrine* in *Queen Móo and the Egyptian Sphinx* (1896), alleging that her Book of Dzyan cosmology corresponded to Maya teaching. Alice’s 1890 Blavatsky Lodge lecture confirms proximity to Blavatsky’s institutional milieu but does not establish that Augustus met her. [Blavatsky’s discussion](https://st-helens-spiritualist-church.co.uk/books/Helena%20Petrovna%20Blavatsky/The%20Secret%20Doctrine/The%20Secret%20Doctrine%20Vol%20II%20%20Blavatsky%20H.P.%20.pdf), [Le Plongeon’s discussion](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/03/Queen_M%C3%B3o_and_the_Egyptian_sphinx_-_by_Augustus_Le_Plongeon_%28IA_cu31924083722037%29.pdf), [Desmond archival study](https://archaeoplanet.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/of-facts-and-hearsay-bringing-augustus-le-plongeon-into-focus-1988.pdf)

### Christopher Columbus

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact.** In the preface to *Sacred Mysteries* (1886), Le Plongeon discussed Columbus—under the Spanish form “Cristobal Colon”—and his 1498 sighting and navigation of the northern South American coast. [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/57458/57458-h/57458-h.htm)

### El Inca Garcilaso de la Vega

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact.** Le Plongeon cited Garcilaso de la Vega as an authority on ancient Peru in *Sacred Mysteries* (1886), incorporating his account into a comparison of American and Old World religious traditions. [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/57458/57458-h/57458-h.htm)

### Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact; underlying claim: reported fact repeated by Le Plongeon.** In *Sacred Mysteries* (1886), Le Plongeon described Leibniz as a member of a secret learned society from which he supposedly received otherwise unavailable instruction; the book’s index rendered his name “Leibnitz.” [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/57458/57458-h/57458-h.htm)

### Herodotus

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact.** Le Plongeon repeatedly quoted and discussed Herodotus in *Sacred Mysteries* (1886), especially on Egyptian secrecy, initiation, the Thesmophoria, and alleged transmission of Egyptian rites to Greece. These passages formed part of Le Plongeon’s speculative Maya-origin argument. [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/57458/57458-h/57458-h.htm)

### Hypatia

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact.** In the index and historical discussion of *Sacred Mysteries* (1886), Le Plongeon identified Bishop Cyril as Hypatia’s murderer while describing Christian persecution of adherents of older religions. [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/57458/57458-h/57458-h.htm)

### Jesus of Nazareth

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact.** Jesus was the subject of Le Plongeon’s Spanish-language polemic *La religión de Jesús comparada con las enseñanzas de la Iglesia* (Boston, 1867). Le Plongeon also invoked advice attributed to Jesus in Mark 6:11 in the preface to *Sacred Mysteries* (1886). [1886 primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/57458/57458-h/57458-h.htm), [Getty papers finding aid](https://www.getty.edu/research/collections/static/pdf/2004.M.18.pdf)

### Jacques de Molay

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact; underlying genealogy: theory.** *Sacred Mysteries* (1886) recounts Molay’s 1308 arrest and 1314 execution and alleges that his death, Templar succession, and commemorative rites contributed to the emergence of Scottish Rite Freemasonry. [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/57458/57458-h/57458-h.htm)

### Moses

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact; underlying conclusion: theory.** In *Sacred Mysteries* (1886), Le Plongeon discussed the traditional attribution of the Pentateuch to Moses and endorsed the view that it was composed after the Hebrew monarchy’s foundation, within his larger argument that biblical traditions ultimately derived from earlier civilizations. [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/57458/57458-h/57458-h.htm)

### Origen of Alexandria

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact.** Le Plongeon cited “Origenes” in *Sacred Mysteries* (1886) for an account of Mithraic priests imitating the movements of celestial bodies and assuming the names and dress of constellations. [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/57458/57458-h/57458-h.htm)

### Plato

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact; underlying use: theory.** Plato was a major ancient authority in Le Plongeon’s published system. In *Sacred Mysteries* (1886) and *Queen Móo and the Egyptian Sphinx* (1896), Le Plongeon treated Plato’s Atlantis account as substantially historical and claimed that Maya records independently confirmed it. [1886 primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/57458/57458-h/57458-h.htm), [1896 primary text](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/03/Queen_M%C3%B3o_and_the_Egyptian_sphinx_-_by_Augustus_Le_Plongeon_%28IA_cu31924083722037%29.pdf)

### Roger Bacon

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact.** In *Sacred Mysteries* (1886), Le Plongeon quoted Roger Bacon on withholding philosophical discoveries from the unworthy and discussed Bacon’s learning and imprisonment as part of a history of esoteric secrecy. [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/57458/57458-h/57458-h.htm)

### King Solomon

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact; underlying genealogy: theory.** *Sacred Mysteries* (1886), subtitled *Freemasonry in Times Anterior to the Temple of Solomon*, discussed the theory that Masonry originated in the building of Solomon’s Temple, then rejected that chronology in favor of Le Plongeon’s claimed prehistoric Maya origin. [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/57458/57458-h/57458-h.htm)

### Zoroaster

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact; underlying account: theory.** In *Sacred Mysteries* (1886), Le Plongeon described Zoroaster as founder of the Mithraic mysteries, discussed the cavern allegedly selected for their celebration, and used the account in his proposed genealogy of ancient initiation and Freemasonry. [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/57458/57458-h/57458-h.htm)

No documented personal meeting, friendship, shared-site succession, or prophecy/hyperstition connection was established with any other roster member. Mere chronological overlap, shared esoteric interests, or later participation in the broader Atlantis/Mu tradition has therefore been omitted.
