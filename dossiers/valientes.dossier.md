# Eustaquia de Souza & Ana de Urinza: Research Dossier

## Scope and Evidentiary Warning

Eustaquia de Souza and Ana de Urinza are known almost entirely from a single narrative written decades after their supposed deaths: Bartolomé Arzáns de Orsúa y Vela’s *Historia de la Villa Imperial de Potosí*. Arzáns, born about 1674–1676, placed their adventures in 1653 and the following eight years. He therefore could not personally have witnessed the earliest events.

Arzáns claimed to rely on:

- an eyewitness history by “Don Juan Pasquier”;
- three elderly people who had known the women;
- Diego Melgarejo, their adolescent companion in Potosí;
- the formerly enslaved servant who accompanied them;
- Don Juan de Itulaín, who reportedly knew them in Lima and later commissioned their portraits.

The central source problem is that no manuscript of Pasquier’s supposed *History of Potosí* has been found, and no independently verified documentary record has yet been located for Eustaquia, Ana, the homicides attributed to them, their travels, their wills, or their portraits. Scholars describe Arzáns’s chronicle as occupying a fluid territory between history, oral tradition, moral exemplum, novella, and fiction. Mónica Navia Antezana expressly classifies the pair’s story as fictional or undocumented, while allowing that it may preserve a distant memory of real events. Modern editions similarly warn that the episode is highly novelized. [Navia Antezana, “Las damas travestidas de Charcas”](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/354848880_Las_damas_travestidas_de_Charcas_The_transvestite_ladies_of_Charcas); [*The Bolivia Reader*](https://dokumen.pub/the-bolivia-reader-history-culture-politics-9780822371618.html)

Accordingly, the labels used below are:

- **Documented fact:** supported independently of Arzáns by surviving contemporary documentation.
- **Chronicle report:** stated by Arzáns but not independently verified.
- **Reported fact:** a later scholarly or popular source’s restatement of the chronicle.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** an analyst’s reading of the narrative rather than a biographical fact.
- **Modern fiction or legend:** a later invention, embellishment, or adaptation not present in the foundational account.
- **Unresolved:** possible but presently unverified.

For the women themselves, almost every seventeenth-century biographical detail must be labeled **chronicle report**, not documented fact.

## Basic Identifying Information

### Eustaquia

- **Principal name:** Doña Eustaquia de Souza.
- **Variants:** Eustaquia Souza; Eustaquia de Sousa; Eustaquia de Sonza; Eustaquia de Sonza/Sonza; “Kiki” de Sonza in Melissa Grey’s modern novel.
- **Title:** “Doña,” indicating the noble or genteel status ascribed to her by Arzáns.
- **Collective epithets:** “the valiant ladies of Potosí,” “Peruvian ladies of Potosí,” “warrior maidens,” “valiant maidens,” and, in modern popular retellings, “teen vigilantes.”
- **Birth:** approximately 1639 or 1640, inferred from the assertion that she was in her fourteenth year in 1653. No baptismal record has been identified.
- **Birthplace:** the Imperial Villa of Potosí, Charcas, Viceroyalty of Peru, now Bolivia — **chronicle report**.
- **Death:** approximately 1661, four months after Ana’s death — **chronicle report**.
- **Place of death:** La Plata, now Sucre, Bolivia — **chronicle report**.
- **Reported cause:** an “affection of the chest,” intensified or accompanied by grief for Ana — **chronicle report**.
- **Occupation:** no formal occupation is documented. Arzáns represents her as a noble daughter, armed traveler, musician, singer, horsewoman, participant in bullfighting culture, and prospective nun.
- **Historical status:** uncertain. Her existence has not been independently established.

### Ana

- **Principal name:** Doña Ana de Urinza.
- **Expanded modern form:** Ana Lezama de Urinza, combining her mother’s surname with her father’s.
- **Variants:** Ana Urinza; Ana de Lezama; Ana Lezama de Urinza.
- **Title:** “Doña.”
- **Collective epithets:** the same as Eustaquia’s.
- **Birth:** approximately 1639 or 1640, inferred from the 1653 age statement. No baptismal record has been identified.
- **Birthplace:** Potosí — **chronicle report**.
- **Death:** approximately 1661 — **chronicle report**.
- **Place of death:** La Plata — **chronicle report**.
- **Reported cause:** a prolonged ailment associated by Arzáns with a fall from a horse during a Lima bullfight, together with unspecified “other causes” about which he professed uncertainty.
- **Occupation:** no formal occupation is documented. Arzáns presents her as a noble orphan, armed traveler, horsewoman, and prospective nun.
- **Historical status:** uncertain; no independent contemporary record presently verifies her existence.

### Political and Geographical Terminology

The women are called “Peruvian” because Potosí and La Plata belonged to the Viceroyalty of Peru during the supposed events. They were not residents of the modern Republic of Peru or citizens of an independent Bolivia, neither of which then existed. “Charcas,” “Upper Peru,” and present-day Bolivia are appropriate geographical or historiographical descriptions, but not citizenship labels they would themselves have used.

## Family and Ancestry

### Eustaquia’s Family

According to Arzáns:

- **Father:** Captain Antonio de Souza, a Portuguese nobleman resident in Potosí.
- **Mother:** Doña Leonor de Meneses, a native of Potosí.
- **Brother:** Don Juan de Souza, older than Eustaquia.
- **Residence:** a house near Munaypata in Potosí.
- **Social position:** wealthy, noble, and sufficiently established to possess costly clothing, jewelry, gold, silver, numerous weapons, private servants, and a domestic oratory.

Antonio’s military title, Portuguese ancestry, fortune, and precise activities are not independently documented. No mining enterprise, encomienda, office, commercial firm, landholding, or Portuguese mercantile connection can presently be assigned to him securely.

### Ana’s Family

According to Arzáns:

- **Father:** Don Pedro Urinza, described as an Andalusian gentleman.
- **Mother:** Doña Plácida Lezama.
- **Siblings:** none; Ana was reportedly their only child.
- **Residence:** the same Munaypata neighborhood as the Souza family.
- **Childhood bereavement:** Plácida died when Ana was very young; Pedro died within two years.
- **Guardianship:** Pedro reportedly entrusted Ana to Antonio de Souza. Ana was brought up beside Eustaquia from about age four.

This is guardianship in Arzáns’s account, not necessarily legal adoption. Modern retellings often say Ana was “adopted,” but no adoption instrument or guardianship record has been found.

### Household Dependents and Slavery

Arzáns repeatedly mentions an unnamed Black woman enslaved in Antonio de Souza’s household. She was central to the young women’s survival:

- she guarded the door during their first nocturnal excursion;
- knew their concealed identities;
- helped them escape confinement by securing a rope to their bedroom window;
- carried messages while disguised in Indigenous clothing;
- arranged transport through her son;
- accompanied or supported their journeys;
- was reportedly emancipated in their final disposition of property.

Her omission from the women’s celebrated name-pair obscures that their mobility depended partly on the labor, secrecy, and risk-taking of an enslaved woman. Arzáns never supplies her name. Her son, also unnamed, lived in the Mataca valley and procured four equipped mules. The chronicle calls him “the Negro” and later the women’s servant. His exact legal status—free, enslaved, or conditionally dependent—is unclear.

## Childhood and Early Life

### Around 1639–1643

**Chronicle report:** Eustaquia and Ana were born in Potosí at approximately the same time. After Ana lost both parents, she entered the Souza household when both girls were about four.

Their childhood unfolded in a wealthy but unusually enclosed domestic setting. Arzáns says they were raised “with such circumspection” that few people in the neighborhood knew they existed. The house contained an oratory where they heard Mass, and they supposedly attended church only about ten times a year.

The statement serves several literary purposes:

- it establishes their presumed sexual and social seclusion;
- explains why they could later move through Potosí without recognition;
- intensifies the contrast between confinement and urban adventure;
- supports Arzáns’s concluding insistence on their virginity.

No source records ordinary childhood activities, tutors, literacy training, languages, needlework, household management, or formal religious instruction.

### Exposure to Weapons

Eustaquia’s elder brother Juan received instruction from a fencing master and practiced with edged weapons and firearms kept in the family house. Eustaquia and Ana watched. Arzáns says they regretted not being male because male status would have permitted them to handle the weapons openly.

When adults were absent, they reportedly practiced shooting and using other arms. This is the only training described. There is no record of a named teacher instructing the girls directly.

### Death of Juan de Souza

When Eustaquia and Ana were about thirteen, Juan supposedly drank cold water while overheated during a fencing lesson and died within twenty-four hours, aged fifteen.

This explanation reflects an early-modern medical belief about sudden cooling after exertion. It is not a medically verifiable cause of death. No burial or parish record has been located.

### Death of Leonor de Meneses

Approximately fifteen months after Juan’s death, Leonor reportedly died. Eustaquia and Ana were then left in Antonio’s sole guardianship. No cause or exact date is given.

## Education and Formation

No school, convent, tutor, or formal curriculum is named. Their identifiable formation in Arzáns consists of:

- Catholic observance through the household oratory;
- elite domestic upbringing;
- observation and clandestine imitation of Juan’s weapons practice;
- access to expensive arms, armor, textiles, horses, jewelry, and cash;
- stories supplied by a young male servant about public festivals, plays, bullfights, dances, and street life;
- musical skill: Eustaquia is described as able to tune a guitar and sing skillfully;
- practical literacy, implied when they sent Diego Melgarejo a written note;
- familiarity with elite manners sufficient to pass as young noblemen in La Plata and Lima.

The chronicle does not explain how they learned horsemanship, bullfighting, travel logistics, estate management, or the sustained male social presentation attributed to them.

## Chronological Life History

## 1653: First Nocturnal Excursion

Arzáns dates the decisive episode to 1653, when both were in their fourteenth year.

Prompted by accounts of Potosí’s fiestas, theater, bullfights, dances, and other public amusements, they resolved to see their native city at night dressed as men. Their elaborate equipment included:

- fine linen underclothes;
- doublets;
- fine mail shirts;
- scarlet brocade tunics and hose;
- white shoes;
- Castilian buckskin jackets;
- short scarlet overskirts;
- white beaver hats;
- scarlet capes with gold embroidery;
- swords;
- stout bucklers;
- one pistol each.

The luxury and complexity of this costume are central to Arzáns’s presentation. They were not dressed as impoverished laborers or anonymous soldiers but as conspicuous young gentlemen.

The unnamed enslaved woman waited at the house door. In the street, the pair encountered Diego Melgarejo, a young man sent to buy bread. He reportedly came from a noble Andalusian family, had been orphaned in Mexico, and had moved to Potosí, where Juan Bravo sheltered him.

Eustaquia and Ana initially covered their faces but disclosed to Diego their names, sex, and reason for wearing men’s clothes. He agreed to guide them through the city.

### Encounter at the Plaza de San Lorenzo

At the Plaza de San Lorenzo, servants and enslaved men belonging to chief magistrate Luis Pimentel allegedly surrounded the richly dressed youths intending to rob them. Arzáns portrays Pimentel’s patrol as habitually exploiting laborers and Indigenous residents under cover of law enforcement.

Eustaquia warned the group’s leader and then shot him in the chest when he raised his sword. He fell and was said to have died. Ana fired but missed. The remaining men fled.

The pair returned home without Antonio discovering their absence. The next day, President Nestares and Pimentel reportedly initiated a search for the killer, but no suspect was found.

**Evidentiary status:** allegation within a literary chronicle. No court, municipal, notarial, or ecclesiastical record of the killing has been identified. Modern accounts that describe the women as organized crime-fighters or vigilantes substantially expand this incident.

## 1653: Second Excursion and the San Juan’s Eve Fight

A few days later, the women asked Diego to accompany them on San Juan’s Eve. He initially refused. They sent him a note calling him a woman and coward, after which he agreed.

Their second outfit included:

- Damascus-steel cutlasses;
- heavy shields;
- pistols;
- black hats;
- scarlet capes embroidered in silver;
- white hose;
- black shoes;
- silver-cloth tunics;
- blue brocade doublets;
- protective jackets and mail shirts.

They also brought a guitar. At the Plaza del Gato, Eustaquia played and sang. A crowd gathered, believing the performers to be young men but expressing surprise at their voices.

Later, on Calle de la Pelota, they rested beneath a balcony. Four young men accused them of compromising the honor of women living in the house by loitering outside. An exchange of insults escalated into armed combat.

Ana’s pistol shot missed. Eustaquia’s pistol failed to fire. Both drew cutlasses. During the fight:

- Ana was pierced through her shield, clothing, jacket, and mail, receiving a wound beneath the left breast;
- she also suffered a thigh wound;
- Eustaquia protected Ana while she was down;
- Ana rose and wounded the man who had struck her;
- Eustaquia struck another opponent in the head;
- Ensign Mendoza, a native of Madrid, was left dead with a severe head wound;
- an Andalusian named Rufino was seriously wounded;
- Eustaquia sustained wounds to her back, arm, and foot.

Eustaquia reportedly called Ana “Don Juan” during their escape, the only masculine alias explicitly supplied in the foundational account.

They fled past the cemetery of San Francisco, stopped under a bridge to inspect and bind one another’s wounds, and continued toward the Ribera refinery district. Eustaquia and Diego eventually carried Ana.

**Evidentiary status:** allegation within Arzáns’s story. No record corroborates Mendoza’s death, Rufino’s injury, or a related criminal inquiry.

## Concealment and Recovery

The women concealed their wounds from Antonio by claiming another illness. They refused examination by a male physician on grounds of modesty. A neighboring Sevillian woman, Doña Paula, secretly treated them and allowed Antonio to believe they suffered from a nonviolent ailment.

They remained bedridden for almost two months and reportedly recovered completely.

Doña Paula is an important but poorly documented member of their network. Her surname, marital status, occupation, and exact residence are not given.

## Discovery, Beating, and Confinement

Three months later, the women planned another excursion without Diego. Antonio found them armed and dressed as men.

Arzáns says Antonio nearly fired pistols at them, beat them severely, confined them in their bedroom, and intended further punishment. The narrative describes him as violent-tempered and portrays the confinement as potentially life-threatening.

**Status:** chronicle allegation. It is not a documented criminal complaint or court finding.

## Escape from the Souza House

The enslaved servant secured a rope to the bedroom window. Eustaquia and Ana descended to a stable roof and then reached the street around seven in the evening. On this occasion they initially wore short women’s skirts and carried no weapons.

Doña Paula sheltered them. The servant, disguised in Indigenous clothing, contacted Diego and arranged a message to her son in the Mataca valley. Using money supplied by the women, Diego purchased two sets of men’s clothing and two swords. The servant’s son arrived approximately a week later with four saddled mules.

The group left for La Plata at night:

- Eustaquia and Ana dressed as men;
- the Black servant dressed as an Indigenous person;
- her son accompanied them;
- Doña Paula remained in Potosí.

This episode reveals a layered use of clothing, racial categorization, and social status. Eustaquia and Ana’s noble male disguise increased mobility and prestige, while the servant’s Indigenous disguise served concealment and courier work.

## La Plata

Arzáns says the women were received favorably in La Plata because of their beauty and prudent conduct, although observers regarded them as young men.

No host, lodging, government contact, or local record is named. After approximately one month they joined a merchant traveling to Lima. The merchant is unnamed.

The enslaved woman remained in La Plata in the household of an unnamed canon, now wearing her ordinary clothing. Her son accompanied Eustaquia and Ana as their servant.

## Lima and Other Travels

The pair arrived in Lima carrying jewelry and “a large quantity of gold and silver” taken from Antonio’s house. Their wealth and dress reportedly allowed them to enter elite male society and attract numerous would-be friends.

The chronicle says:

- they lived in Lima for about two years;
- society believed they were noble young men;
- they preserved the disguise successfully;
- they participated in fiestas and bullfights;
- they traveled to Trujillo and other unspecified cities;
- they eventually returned to Lima;
- Ana suffered a fall from a horse during a Lima bullfight.

No exact itinerary, dates, inns, patrons, merchants, official licenses, or witnesses are documented. The phrase “other cities” cannot be reconstructed further from the presently located sources.

### Juan de Itulaín

Arzáns later claimed to have met Don Juan de Itulaín in Chayanta. Itulaín supposedly knew Eustaquia and Ana in Lima while believing them to be men. He later learned their sex after traveling to La Plata.

This reported acquaintance is important because Arzáns uses it to bridge his narrative with a physical-object claim: the alleged portraits.

## Death of Antonio de Souza and Inheritance

While in Lima, the women learned that Antonio had died “of grief.” His will reportedly provided that Eustaquia would inherit his large fortune if she returned unmarried. If married, she would lose it.

The condition may reflect early-modern concerns about marriage, patrimonial control, and a husband’s access to property. However:

- no will has been identified;
- the estate’s size is unspecified;
- no executor, notary, property, mining share, house inventory, or probate proceeding is named;
- “died of grief” is a literary and emotional explanation, not a medical diagnosis.

Five years after leaving Potosí, at about twenty years old, Eustaquia and Ana returned not to Potosí but to La Plata. Before entering the city they changed into richly decorated women’s clothing. Eustaquia then took possession of the inheritance.

The chronology implies a return around 1658–1659, but Arzáns’s age statements are not exact enough to determine a single year.

## Proposed Entry into Santa Monica

The two women reportedly arranged to become “brides of Christ” in the convent of Santa Monica.

The plan did not occur because Ana became seriously ill. No profession, dowry agreement, convent register, or ecclesiastical correspondence has been located. It is not clear whether they were postulants, merely discussing admission, or had completed formal arrangements.

## Portraits

After learning that his Lima companions were women, Juan de Itulaín reportedly asked them to put on their preserved men’s clothes. Although Ana was ill, both complied, and he commissioned portraits bearing the inscriptions:

- “The valiant Doña Eustaquia de Souza”
- “The valiant Doña Ana de Urinza, Peruvian ladies of Potosí”

Arzáns says he later saw the portraits in Itulaín’s La Plata house while traveling from Chayanta. He claimed their beauty exceeded his descriptive powers.

This passage is internally problematic. Arzáns was apparently born about 1674–1676, whereas the women supposedly died around 1661. He therefore could not have seen portraits in their lifetime, although he could theoretically have viewed surviving paintings years later. The chronicle provides no painter, date, dimensions, medium, subsequent owner, or present location. No authenticated portrait is known.

Modern illustrated representations should not be mistaken for the missing seventeenth-century works.

## Deaths and Final Disposition

After living in La Plata for “a little more than two years,” Ana reportedly died of her prolonged ailment. Arzáns links it to her horse fall in Lima but also mentions unspecified causes he declined to repeat because he was uncertain.

Eustaquia died four months later from a chest ailment while grieving for her “beloved companion.”

At death, both reportedly declared themselves virgins. Arzáns insists that they had resisted many temptations during their years in male society. This declaration is part of the Catholic moral architecture of the story and not independently verifiable medical or sexual evidence.

Their property was reportedly distributed as follows:

- most of the estate to the poor;
- freedom for the unnamed enslaved woman;
- enough money for her and her son to live and return to Potosí;
- 1,000 pesos to Diego Melgarejo;
- their men’s clothes to Diego.

No wills, charitable accounts, manumission deed, receipt, or estate inventory has been found.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

No company, formal profession, invention, patent, mining concession, or commercial enterprise is securely associated with either woman.

### Household Wealth

The narrative nevertheless implies extensive elite wealth:

- a substantial house near Munaypata;
- a private oratory;
- enslaved labor;
- numerous firearms and bladed weapons;
- multiple mail shirts;
- imported or expensive textiles;
- beaver hats;
- jewelry;
- liquid gold and silver;
- access to horses and long-distance mule transport;
- an inheritance described as large.

The source never quantifies Antonio’s fortune or identifies its origin. Given Potosí’s silver economy, mining or trade is conceivable but cannot be inferred as fact.

### Property Taken During the Escape

Arzáns says the women took jewelry, gold, and silver from Antonio’s house. In modern legal language this could be characterized as unauthorized removal or theft, but the narrative later treats Eustaquia as Antonio’s heir. There is no recorded accusation, charge, lawsuit, restitution demand, or estate accounting.

### The 1,000-Peso Gift

The bequest to Diego was substantial, but its modern value cannot be calculated responsibly without knowing denomination, silver content, local prices, and purchasing context. It demonstrates the scale of fortune Arzáns wished his readers to imagine, not a verified financial transaction.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Political Activity

Neither woman is reported to have held office, joined a political faction, petitioned the Crown, or participated in a rebellion. Their conflicts occurred within a violent colonial city and involved servants of a magistrate, but Arzáns does not portray them as political insurgents.

Modern claims that they “defied an empire” or “cleaned Peru from crime” are interpretive promotional language, not descriptions supported by the foundational narrative.

### Religion

Their reported life remained framed by Catholic practice:

- childhood Mass in a domestic oratory;
- references to divine protection;
- intended entry into Santa Monica;
- final declarations of virginity;
- charitable disposition of wealth;
- emancipation and support of a servant;
- Arzáns’s claim that they embraced perpetual chastity.

Because Arzáns wrote moralized Catholic history, these elements may represent authorial shaping as much as preserved biography.

### Gender Presentation

The pair reportedly adopted male dress first for a nocturnal adventure and later for travel and sustained public life. Their presentation gave access to:

- streets at night;
- weapons;
- travel without male guardians;
- elite friendships;
- fiestas;
- bullfights;
- social recognition as young noblemen.

Their masculine names are mostly unknown. Only Eustaquia’s use of “Don Juan” for Ana during the second escape is recorded.

Mónica Navia Antezana interprets the episode primarily as colonial cross-dressing or *cambio de hábito*. She cautions that the narrative does not by itself establish lesbian sexuality. The women ultimately resume women’s clothing, seek convent entry, and are repeatedly described by Arzáns within a feminine and virginal framework. [“Las damas travestidas de Charcas”](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/354848880_Las_damas_travestidas_de_Charcas_The_transvestite_ladies_of_Charcas)

### Music and Performance

Eustaquia’s guitar playing and singing are among the few artistic skills assigned to either woman. The San Juan’s Eve audience’s fascination with their allegedly masculine appearance and “sweet” voices heightened the unstable gender perception on which the episode depends.

No composition, lyric, instrument, or performance record survives.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Each Other

Arzáns uses kinship and affective language:

- they were raised together from about age four;
- Ana called the Souza parents her own;
- Eustaquia called Ana “sister” during combat;
- they dressed, fought, traveled, and planned convent life together;
- they dressed one another’s wounds;
- Eustaquia died while grieving for her “beloved companion.”

**Documentary limit:** The chronicle does not explicitly describe a sexual relationship. Their joint declaration of virginity is central to Arzáns’s ending.

**Modern interpretation:** Numerous twenty-first-century works label them lovers, lesbian partners, or a sapphic couple. That reading is possible as a reception history and literary interpretation, but it is not a documented seventeenth-century fact.

### Captain Antonio de Souza

Father and guardian; source of household wealth and weapons; later antagonist after discovering the disguises. His reported beating and confinement of the women prompted their escape.

### Don Juan de Souza

Eustaquia’s brother and indirect weapons mentor. His fencing practice stimulated their interest in arms. His early death removed the male heir and eventually left Eustaquia as Antonio’s reported conditional heir.

### The Unnamed Enslaved Black Woman

Confidante, logistical organizer, rescuer, courier, and travel companion. She accepted considerable risk while facilitating the escape. According to the chronicle, the women later freed and financially supported her.

### The Servant’s Son

Transport organizer and servant during the journey to La Plata and Lima. No name is supplied.

### Diego Melgarejo

Guide, confidant, reluctant accomplice, purchaser of clothing and weapons, intermediary with the enslaved servant, and purported later oral informant. The women reportedly left him 1,000 pesos and their masculine wardrobe.

### Doña Paula

A Sevillian neighbor who secretly treated their wounds and sheltered them after their escape. She is one of the few adult women in the account who exercised independent agency on their behalf.

### Juan Bravo

Householder who reportedly took Diego into his home. He does not otherwise enter the women’s story.

### The Unnamed Merchant

Provided the social framework for travel from La Plata to Lima. No direct relationship beyond the journey is described.

### The Unnamed Canon

Hosted the Black servant in La Plata while the women traveled onward. His knowledge of their history is unclear.

### Don Juan de Itulaín

Lima acquaintance and alleged patron of their portraits. Arzáns claimed to have interviewed him and seen the paintings.

### Luis Pimentel and President Nestares

Pimentel was the chief magistrate whose servants allegedly tried to rob the women. Nestares and Pimentel supposedly investigated the first killing. Neither is shown learning their identities.

## Controversies, Allegations, Crimes, and Unresolved Questions

### Two Alleged Killings

Arzáns attributes two deaths to the pair:

1. Eustaquia shot a servant of Pimentel in the chest during an attempted robbery.
2. Ensign Mendoza died from a head wound during the San Juan’s Eve street fight, apparently inflicted by Eustaquia.

These are **chronicle allegations**, not established criminal findings. No victim’s full identity, inquest, prosecution, indictment, trial, or judgment has been located.

### Injuries to Others

Ana allegedly wounded the man who stabbed her. Eustaquia allegedly gravely wounded Rufino and struck another opponent. Again, these claims are unverified.

### Removal of Family Wealth

The women reportedly carried off jewels, gold, and silver from Antonio’s house. Arzáns neither condemns this as theft nor records a legal response. The episode remains an unresolved financial allegation within the story.

### Cross-Dressing

No trial or ecclesiastical proceeding against them is recorded. Navia contrasts their story with colonial cases in which gender disguise generated an archival proceeding. Their successful concealment means that, within Arzáns’s narrative, authorities never obtained the knowledge necessary to charge them.

### Violence by Antonio

Antonio’s beating and threatened further punishment are allegations made by Arzáns. No independent witness statement or complaint survives.

### Ownership of Enslaved People

The Souza household’s enslavement of the unnamed Black woman is stated directly in the chronicle. If the characters were historical, this would place their wealth and freedom of movement within colonial slaveholding society. The eventual manumission is likewise only a chronicle report.

### Sexuality

The description of Eustaquia and Ana as lesbian lovers is widespread in current popular culture, Wikidata, reviews, and fiction. It is not stated in Arzáns.

Evidence invoked in favor of the interpretation includes:

- lifelong intimate companionship;
- shared bedchamber or household;
- mutual wound care;
- joint travel;
- a joint plan for convent life;
- Eustaquia’s death while grieving for her “beloved companion.”

Evidence limiting the claim includes:

- “sister” was Arzáns’s explicit relational term;
- the chronicle repeatedly insists they remained virgins;
- no letter, testimony, legal proceeding, or self-description records sexual desire between them;
- much of the narrative may itself be fictional.

The appropriate classification is **modern interpretation or legend**, not documented fact.

### Historical Existence

No independent record presently establishes the women. Relevant problems include:

- Arzáns wrote roughly fifty years after the events;
- his cited Pasquier text has not been found;
- documents associated with a historical Juan Pasquier have not been connected to the historian-poet Arzáns describes;
- several supposed historians used by Arzáns may be literary inventions or composites;
- no wills, baptismal entries, death records, criminal records, travel licenses, convent files, or portraits have surfaced;
- the narrative exhibits Cervantine construction and moralized symmetry.

Scholars do not unanimously declare every element invented. Some suggest that a real episode may lie behind the elaboration. The present conclusion must remain **unresolved, with substantial grounds for treating the surviving account as literary legend rather than verified biography**. ## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### The “Teen Vigilantes Who Cleaned Potosí of Crime”

**Story:** Eustaquia and Ana patrolled Potosí at night, protected the weak, and systematically fought criminals.

**Appearance and circulation:** Twenty-first-century popular histories, entertainment sites, illustrated anthologies, and marketing for historical fiction.

**Evidence:** Arzáns relates two excursions and two fights. In the first, the women defend themselves against alleged robbers; in the second, they escalate a confrontation about household honor. He does not describe a sustained campaign of policing or crime-fighting.

**Assessment:** Modern heroic expansion.

**Effect on reputation:** Recast the pair from adventurous noble girls involved in lethal street violence into proto-superheroes, sometimes compared with Batman and Robin.

### The Lesbian-Lovers Narrative

**Story:** The women were openly or secretly lovers.

**Appearance and circulation:** Modern online profiles, Jason Porath’s illustrated popular history, Mackenzi Lee’s anthology, Melissa Grey’s young-adult novel, book reviews, and user-generated databases.

**Evidence:** Intense companionship and shared life, but no explicit sexual statement in the principal source. Arzáns’s virginity claim does not resolve emotional or romantic attachment, but neither does it document one.

**Assessment:** Plausible literary interpretation; historically unverified.

**Effect:** Made the pair prominent in queer historical memory and inspired explicitly sapphic fiction.

### Ana as a Street Orphan or Brothel-Raised Girl

**Story:** Ana grew up poor, on the streets, or in a brothel before joining Eustaquia’s household.

**Origin:** Modern popular retellings and Melissa Grey’s novel.

**Evidence against:** Arzáns calls Ana the only daughter of an Andalusian gentleman and Doña Plácida Lezama, living in the same neighborhood as the Souzas. She was orphaned young but placed directly with Antonio de Souza.

**Assessment:** Modern invention.

### Eustaquia as a Baron’s Daughter or Fiancée of the Viceroy’s Son

**Story:** Eustaquia was a baroness or heiress engaged to Sebastián, son of the Viceroy of Peru.

**Origin:** Melissa Grey’s *Valiant Ladies*.

**Evidence:** None in Arzáns. Antonio is called a noble Portuguese captain, not a baron. No engagement is mentioned.

**Assessment:** Historical fiction.

### Murder-Mystery Investigation

**Story:** The women investigated the murder of Eustaquia’s brother through brothels and aristocratic institutions.

**Origin:** *Valiant Ladies*.

**Evidence against:** In Arzáns, Juan died suddenly after drinking water during fencing practice; no murder investigation occurs.

**Assessment:** Historical fiction.

### “Potosí, Peru” Versus Bolivia

Modern summaries frequently place them in “Potosí, Peru.” This is defensible only in the colonial administrative sense of the Viceroyalty of Peru. The city lies in present-day Bolivia. Calling them simply Peruvian or Bolivian without explaining the temporal distinction can mislead.

### Authentic Portraits

The story says portraits once existed. No authenticated paintings have been located. Internet illustrations are modern works and cannot be used as evidence of their appearance.

### Catalina de Erauso as Direct Inspiration

Sherry Velasco and other scholars note strong affinities with Catalina de Erauso, the “Lieutenant Nun,” whose celebrated history circulated before Arzáns wrote. Erauso traveled through Charcas and Potosí dressed as a man.

The parallels include:

- elite or respectable female origin;
- male dress;
- weapons and lethal violence;
- travel through colonial Spanish America;
- repeated assertions of virginity;
- masculine social identity;
- a portrait tradition.

There is no evidence that Eustaquia or Ana personally met Erauso. The supported claim is literary influence on Arzáns or on the tradition from which he wrote, not personal influence on the women. [Sherry Velasco, *The Lieutenant Nun*](https://transreads.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/2022-01-13_61e08051370c0_TheLieutenantNunTransgenderismLesbianDesireandCatalinadeErausobySherryVelascoz-lib.org_.pdf)

## Works by the Persons

No work authored, co-authored, edited, dictated, or securely attributed to Eustaquia or Ana is known.

Possible textual or artistic traces within Arzáns’s narrative are:

- **A note to Diego Melgarejo**, allegedly written jointly or on their behalf, mocking him as a woman and coward. The text does not survive independently.
- **Songs performed by Eustaquia**, selected and sung by her at the Plaza del Gato. No titles or lyrics are preserved, and Arzáns does not say she composed them.
- **Alleged portraits**, commissioned by Juan de Itulaín rather than created by the women.
- **Reported deathbed declarations**, asserting virginity. These were recorded only through Arzáns’s narration.
- **Possible wills or estate instructions**, implied by the distribution of wealth, manumission, and gifts. No testamentary document has been found.

They therefore have no authentic personal bibliography in the ordinary sense.

## Books and Major Works About the Persons

### Foundational Source

- Bartolomé Arzáns de Orsúa y Vela, *Historia de la Villa Imperial de Potosí, riquezas incomparables de su famoso cerro, grandezas de su magnánima población, sus guerras civiles y casos memorables*. Begun around 1705 and unfinished at Arzáns’s death in 1736; completed in part by his son. The Eustaquia-and-Ana episode is assigned to 1653. Critical edition by Lewis Hanke and Gunnar Mendoza, 3 volumes, Brown University Press, 1965.
- Bartolomé Arzáns de Orsúa y Vela, *Relatos de la Villa Imperial de Potosí*, selection, introduction, and chronology by Leonardo García Pabón, La Paz: Plural, 2000. Includes “Las aventuras de las doncellas guerreras.”
- Sinclair Thomson et al., eds., *The Bolivia Reader: History, Culture, Politics*, Durham: Duke University Press, 2018. Includes an English translation titled “The Adventures of the Warrior Maidens,” with editorial warning that Arzáns’s annals inhabit a blurred domain between history and fiction.

### Scholarly Studies

- Mónica Navia Antezana, “Las damas travestidas de Charcas,” *Estudios Bolivianos*, no. 32, 2020. Compares colonial cases of women’s cross-dressing; identifies the Eustaquia-and-Ana story as fictional or undocumented and examines clothing, gender, race, and political visibility.
- Ana María Diez de Medina, *La representación de las mujeres en la Historia de la Villa Imperial de Potosí de Bartolomé Arzáns de Orsúa y Vela*, master’s thesis, Universidad Andina Simón Bolívar, 2020. Analyzes Arzáns’s women characters and the literary construction of Eustaquia and Ana.
- Sherry Velasco, *The Lieutenant Nun: Transgenderism, Lesbian Desire, and Catalina de Erauso*, University of Texas Press, 2000. Treats the “Warrior Maidens” as an Arzáns-created colonial story possibly inspired by Erauso and discusses its sexual and institutional ambiguities.
- Gina Herrmann, study cited by Velasco concerning the “Warrior Maidens” and Arzáns’s cultural, economic, racial, sexual, and institutional ambivalence. The exact standalone bibliographic record was not securely recoverable from the accessible materials and should not be supplied conjecturally.
- Mariano Baptista Gumucio, *El mundo desde Potosí: vida y reflexiones de Bartolomé Arzáns de Orsúa y Vela (1676–1736)*. Useful for Arzáns’s biography and the uncertain status of Pasquier and the other alleged predecessor-historians.
- Blanca Wiethüchter et al., *Hacia una historia crítica de la literatura en Bolivia*. Discusses Arzáns’s use of oral tradition, archival materials, literary technique, and purported authors whose existence or writings remain doubtful.

### Popular Histories and Anthologies

- Jason Porath, *Rejected Princesses: Tales of History’s Boldest Heroines, Hellions, and Heretics*, New York: Dey Street Books, 2016. Includes “The Valiant Ladies of Potosí.” This is an illustrated popular retelling, not independent evidence.
- Mackenzi Lee, *Bygone Badass Broads: 52 Forgotten Women Who Changed the World*, New York: Abrams Image, 2018, pp. 38–40. Includes Ana and Eustaquia in a popular-history format.
- Ben Thompson, online profile “Ana Lezama de Urinza,” *Badass of the Week*. A deliberately hyperbolic retelling that supplies numerous unsupported details and should be read as modern mythmaking.
- Cultura Colectiva, “The Valiant Ladies of Potosí: The Teen Vigilantes That Cleaned Peru from Crime.” Popular retelling responsible for several claims later incorporated into Wikidata.

### Historical Fiction

- Melissa Grey, *Valiant Ladies*, Feiwel & Friends, 2022. Young-adult historical fantasy/mystery featuring Ana Lezama de Urinza and Eustaquia “Kiki” de Sonza as lovers and vigilantes.
- Melissa Grey, *Las heroínas*, Spanish translation, Puck/Ediciones Urano, 2024, ISBN 978-84-19252-87-6.

Grey’s plot, including Ana’s brothel upbringing, Eustaquia’s engagement, her brother’s murder, aristocratic conspiracies, and an organized vigilante mission, is fiction rather than evidence about seventeenth-century lives.

## Death and Present Status

If Arzáns’s chronology is accepted internally:

- Ana died in La Plata about 1661, aged roughly twenty-one or twenty-two.
- Eustaquia died there four months later at approximately the same age.
- Neither married.
- Both reportedly intended to enter the convent of Santa Monica.
- Both declared themselves virgins.
- Their remaining property was distributed to the poor and close dependents.

None of those end-of-life details has been independently documented. Their graves and burial church are unknown.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Eighteenth to Twentieth Centuries

Their reputation depended on the circulation of Arzáns’s chronicle. Because the complete critical edition did not appear until 1965, their reach was initially narrower than that of Catalina de Erauso.

Within Arzáns scholarship, they became representative of:

- the chronicle’s mixture of history and novella;
- the violence of colonial Potosí;
- women’s use of male clothing to enter public space;
- elite honor culture;
- the interaction of gender, race, and social rank;
- Arzáns’s habit of citing possibly fictitious predecessor-historians.

### Twenty-First-Century Recovery

Popular anthologies and online media transformed them into:

- lesbian heroines;
- teen vigilantes;
- defenders of Potosí’s poor;
- Latin American counterparts to comic-book superheroes;
- icons of gender nonconformity;
- protagonists of young-adult romantic adventure.

This recovery greatly expanded awareness of their names but also introduced invented details that now circulate as biography.

### Queer and Transgender Historiography

Modern readers variously interpret the pair as lesbians, gender-nonconforming women, temporary cross-dressers, or figures whose narrative destabilizes fixed gender categories.

The surviving account does not establish how either person privately understood sex, gender, or desire. Applying a precise modern identity is therefore interpretive. What the story unmistakably preserves is a cultural imagination of two female-assigned colonial subjects obtaining masculine mobility and authority through clothing, weapons, wealth, voice, and companionship.

### Historiographical Significance

Even if entirely fictional, the story remains historically informative about:

- eighteenth-century memory of seventeenth-century Potosí;
- acceptable and unacceptable forms of female violence;
- elite fascination with disguised women;
- Catholic narrative strategies that reconcile transgression with chastity;
- the racialized division of risk and labor;
- the literary relationship between New World chronicles and Spanish adventure literature;
- later construction of feminist and queer historical icons.
