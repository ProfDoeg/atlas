# Joaquín Trincado Mateo — Research Dossier

**Research status:** 20 August 2026  
**Life:** 19 August 1866–6 December 1935  
**Principal locations:** Cintruénigo, Navarra; Bilbao; Madrid and Valencia; Buenos Aires; Santiago del Estero; Salta  
**Principal institution:** Escuela Magnético-Espiritual de la Comuna Universal (EMECU), founded in Buenos Aires on 20 September 1911

## Method and Evidentiary Note

Joaquín Trincado left an unusually difficult biographical record. The surviving narrative is dominated by three kinds of material:

1. his own books, in which autobiography, doctrinal argument, spirit communication, prophecy, and sacred history are interwoven;
2. institutional biographies produced by the EMECU and its successor bodies, generally devotional and often dependent on Trincado's own testimony;
3. later studies of the EMECU, Sandino, or Latin American esotericism, which usually discuss only a part of his life.

No independently edited autobiography, complete scholarly edition, comprehensive correspondence, civil-register dossier, immigration file, university record, patent file, probate inventory, or medical/death certificate was located in the sources consulted. Assertions about his Belgian engineering degree, patents, fortune, Masonic rank, journey to Jerusalem, encounter with surviving Essene sages, mediumistic powers, previous incarnations, and posthumous communications must therefore be kept separate from the better-documented chronology.

The most detailed biographical reconstruction is José Rafael Mendoza Vallejo's *Joaquín Trincado: Algo de su vida y de su obra* (Caracas: Cromotip, 2001). Mendoza was an EMECU adherent and Mason who interviewed family members and visited Trincado-related sites in Spain and Argentina. His work is invaluable but is neither disinterested nor fully documented to modern scholarly standards. Alejandra G. Galicia Martínez's 2024 study in *REHMLAC+* is the strongest recent academic synthesis of Trincado's ideas and explicitly warns that the biographical literature is largely apologetic and hagiographic.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Joaquín Trincado Mateo. A 2024 academic study gives the expanded form **Joaquín Trincado Matheo Alfaro y Ayala**, reflecting both parental lines and an older spelling of *Mateo*. Institutional texts alternate among *Mateo*, *Matheo*, and, less carefully, other variants.
- **Epithets and offices used within the movement:** *El Maestro*, *Maestro Juez*, *Maestro Legislador*, founder and Director General of the EMECU. These are institutional or religious titles, not state or university appointments.
- **Birth:** 19 August 1866, reportedly within the first half-hour after 1:00 a.m., at Cintruénigo, Navarra, Spain.
- **Death:** 6 December 1935, Buenos Aires, Argentina, aged 69.
- **Nationality:** Spanish; an Argentine naturalization has not been documented.
- **Occupations:** agricultural laborer, mason/building worker, carpenter, village teacher, electrician, electrical contractor or merchant, inventor (claimed), writer, editor, lecturer, spiritist organizer, communal-colony founder, and political-utopian propagandist.
- **Languages:** he wrote and organized almost entirely in Spanish. The movement says he opposed translation of his doctrine because he considered Spanish uniquely suited to it; English and other translations appeared later.
- **Core doctrine:** *Filosofía Austera Racional*, also called *Espiritismo Luz y Verdad*, *Espiritismo Racionalista Comunista*, or trincadismo.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and siblings

Institutional and family-derived sources identify his parents as **Ignacio Trincado Alfaro** and **Romualda Mateo Ayala/de Ayala**. One abbreviated biography calls her Romualda Mateo de Alfaro, apparently using her married name. They reportedly married on 13 April 1856. Joaquín was the fourth child. The parents are consistently described as poor or modest agricultural workers.

Mendoza Vallejo connects the surname to an old Cintruénigo line and notes that Juan, Bertol, and Pedro Trincado proved hidalguía before Navarrese tribunals in 1523. No documentary chain from those men to Joaquín was supplied, so this is a possible surname history, not a demonstrated personal pedigree. Describing Trincado simply as “Basque,” common in EMECU and Sandino literature, is imprecise: he was born in Navarra, in a culturally mixed Navarrese/Ebro setting. The label may express a broad regional identity rather than a documented ethnic genealogy.

### First marriage and Spanish family

On 24 April 1888, aged 21, he married **María Dolores Agramonte y Navascués**. Their documented or consistently reported children were:

- **Demetrio Jesús Trincado Agramonte**, born 1890;
- **Máximo Trincado Agramonte**, born 1892;
- **Ramón Trincado Agramonte**, born 1894; reportedly killed on the Alicante front during the Spanish Civil War at about 42;
- **Ángel**, born 1896 and dead at about one year;
- **María**, born 1898 and dead at about two years.

The first two children were reportedly born at the family's residence at Cantón de la Villa no. 3 in Cintruénigo; Ramón and Ángel at Dos de Mayo no. 11/15, fourth floor, Bilbao; and María at Arréchega no. 6, fourth floor, Bilbao.

Trincado left Spain alone for Argentina in 1903, leaving María Dolores and their surviving minor sons in Bilbao. A hostile Sandino biography calls this “abandonment”; Mendoza's reconstruction presents it as emigration undertaken while the family remained behind. In February 1909 María Dolores and sons Demetrio Jesús, Máximo, and Ramón sailed from Santurce/Bilbao on the *Paraná* to Buenos Aires. Trincado received them and they lived on Calle Cangallo. On 19 December 1909 María Dolores and the sons returned to Spain. The separation became permanent. The sources consulted do not document a civil or ecclesiastical divorce, annulment, or Trincado's subsequent legal marital status.

### Mariana/Marciana Palacios

**Mariana Palacios**, also called **Marciana**, appears as a speaking or possession medium in Trincado's early Buenos Aires circle and as one of three women who, in Mendoza Vallejo's account, marked distinct phases of his adult life. Later family lore contains mutually inconsistent stories: that she knew him in Spain and traveled with him; that they met aboard the *Berenguer el Grande*; or that they met at the Sociedad Constancia in Buenos Aires. None is independently established.

Mendoza Vallejo says Trincado had or acknowledged nine children in total: Demetrio Jesús, Máximo, Ramón, Ángel, María, **Javier**, Juan Donato, Joaquina Ana, and Ignacio Francisco. The five children of María Dolores and three children of Mercedes account for eight; Javier appears to belong to the intervening relationship, but the excerpted evidence does not securely establish his mother, birth date, or legal status.

### Argentine marriage/union and children

Trincado formed a household with **María Mercedes Riglos Cosis**, sometimes rendered Mercedes Riglos Negrete or María Mercedes Riglos Cosis de Trincado. EMECU sources call her his wife, “Hermana,” “Maestra,” and principal collaborator. Their children were:

- **Juan Donato Trincado Riglos**, later Director General and “Maestro Nato”; died 1992;
- **Joaquina Ana Trincado Riglos**;
- **Ignacio Francisco Trincado Riglos**.

The existence and date of a legal marriage to Mercedes have not been independently verified. The absence of documentation dissolving the Spanish marriage makes the exact civil status unresolved. This is a documentary gap, not evidence by itself of bigamy.

After Trincado's death Mercedes took control of the central organization. The institutional biography gives her death as 1967, although some later movement material says 1965. Juan Donato then headed the hereditary succession; after his death in 1992, **Víctor Rolando Trincado** became the principal successor recognized by the Buenos Aires-centered lineage.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1866–1888

Movement biographies say Trincado was premature, physically frail, and raised amid economic hardship. He wrote in *Profilaxis de la vida* that he had performed hard agricultural labor from the age of seven. He probably received elementary instruction in Cintruénigo while helping in the fields.

At about 17, he entered the former Cistercian Monastery of Santa María de Veruela near Vera de Moncayo, Zaragoza, then associated in the biographical tradition with Jesuit formation. The sources disagree whether he remained eighteen months or two years. He left around 1885 without ordination or vows. Later institutional biography interprets his departure as the first stage of his rejection of Catholic authority.

Back in Cintruénigo he reportedly resumed farm work, practiced masonry and carpentry, and taught at night in response to local illiteracy. The writing desk and chair he used in Buenos Aires were later said to have been made by him, a detail offered as evidence of his manual skill.

## Education and Technical Formation

Two incompatible educational narratives circulate:

- **Institutional-degree version:** after the Jesuit novitiate he studied for eight years at several European universities, graduated as an electrical engineer or *perito electricista no. 22* at Liège and/or Brussels, and participated in early generating-plant installations.
- **Apprentice/autodidact version:** after moving to Bilbao as a construction worker, a Belgian engineer employed on an electrical project recognized his aptitude, lent him technical books, and helped him train himself as an electrician.

No named diploma, matriculation record, thesis, faculty, graduation date, or archive reference was found. “University of Liège and Brussels” is itself too vague to be a normal credential citation. The safest conclusion is that Trincado became a technically competent working electrician; the formal engineering degree and numbered qualification remain unverified institutional claims.

His formation also included the liberal, republican, anticlerical, socialist, anarchist, freethinking, Masonic, and Kardecian currents circulating in Spain and immigrant Buenos Aires. A direct relationship with Francisco Ferrer Guardia or the Escuela Moderna is sometimes inferred from similarities in rationalist pedagogy, but the 2024 academic study says this connection is not fully documented.

## Early Career in Spain, 1888–1903

After marrying María Dolores, Trincado reportedly obtained a village teaching position. In 1892 he moved to industrializing Bilbao, initially working as a mason on buildings connected with an electric company. He learned electrical work, established a workshop and retail outlet for electrical materials in or near the Plaza de Santiago, and took contracts in Bilbao and elsewhere in Vizcaya.

Mendoza Vallejo's reconstruction says he lived almost a decade in Bilbao and was sufficiently prosperous to support his family. It also portrays him as talented, socially expansive, and “muy mujeriego,” a judgment attributed to his son Demetrio Jesús. Family tensions were reportedly recurrent and sometimes mediated by Jesuit acquaintances.

Later biography credits Trincado with overseeing illumination for the coronation of Alfonso XIII in Madrid in May 1902 and then managing work for the company **Saltos y Villora** on a hydroelectric installation in Valencia. These precise claims are not accompanied in the accessible sources by payroll, company, engineering, or royal-house records. They should be treated as plausible but unverified.

From Bilbao he may have traveled in France in 1898–1899; a letter found in Mexico has been interpreted as evidence of a visit to Venice. Broader claims that he spent eight years traveling through European universities are not presently substantiated.

## Migration to Argentina, 1903

Most sources place his departure and arrival in **November 1903**, at age 37. EMECU tradition names the Spanish cargo steamer **Berenguer el Grande** and describes a near-wreck in heavy seas near Santa Catarina, Brazil. The vessel itself existed, but no passenger list or immigration record consulted here independently verifies Trincado's passage. Colonia Jaime's current official biography gives 1904, a minority date likely arising from later recollection.

His motives are variously presented as the search for economic opportunity, a spiritual compulsion to enter the “New World,” escape from domestic complications, or preparation for a universal mission. Only emigration and settlement in Buenos Aires are well supported.

In Buenos Aires he resumed electrical work and commerce. Later accounts place his business or residence at **Bartolomé Mitre 3458**. He reportedly alternated between prosperity and hardship.

## Electrical Business, Patents, Fortune, and Bankruptcy

EMECU biography says Trincado invented and patented electrical devices, especially a system of domestic electric heating, accumulated “an appreciable fortune,” and lost it in litigation or commercial struggle defending his patent. IAPSOP summarizes the tradition as a financial collapse caused by defense of an electrical home-heating patent. Trincado himself referred in *Conócete a ti mismo* to a pamphlet on “La calefacción eléctrica” and claimed the work had spread through the world.

No patent number, jurisdiction, filing date, assignee, specification, adverse claimant, court caption, or judgment appears in the consulted accounts. Targeted searches of indexed patent sites did not produce a patent under his name. That does not prove no filing existed—Spanish and Argentine historical records may be incomplete or poorly indexed—but the invention, patent ownership, infringement, and litigation stories remain **unverified claims**.

Likewise, no balance sheet, bankruptcy petition, creditor list, property record, or probate inventory was found. His temporary prosperity, collapse around 1909, and later financial strain are repeated across friendly and hostile sources and are probably substantially true; the amounts and causes are not established.

## Crisis, Spiritism, and the Oratorio, 1908–1911

Around 1908–1909 Trincado experienced financial ruin, isolation, illness, and what later authors describe as depression. His own *Buscando a Dios* says, “Sufro la soledad más espantosa y la pobreza en todo su horror.” Several biographies say he contemplated suicide. This is a self-reported or family/institutional account, not a clinical diagnosis.

He entered the **Sociedad Espiritista Constancia**, founded in Buenos Aires in 1877 and one of the oldest Argentine Kardecian organizations. Accounts describe séances involving levitation, object movement, materialization, trance speech, and somnambulism. Trincado rejected what he called its “phenomenism,” religious admixture, hierarchy, mysticism, and insufficient controls. Around 1910 he withdrew with several sympathizers, declaring that he would found a school beginning “where Jesus stopped.”

In 1910 he opened an oratory in his home. Named collaborators included:

- **Mariana/Marciana Palacios**, speaking or possession medium;
- **Pedro Portillo**, trance/speaking medium, later styled the “Voz Universal”;
- **José González**, clairvoyant, later styled the “Ojo del Creador.”

Trincado claimed automatic or mechanical writing, clairvoyance, clairaudience (*autófono*), conscious telepathy, and out-of-body “doubling.” Texts produced in the circle were presented as communications from “superior spirits” or *hermanos del Espacio*. Historically, the séances, named circle, and resulting documents belong to the movement's record; the supernatural causation is a religious claim not independently demonstrable.

From 1909 to 1911 he wrote *Buscando a Dios y asiento del Dios Amor*, his first major doctrinal-autobiographical work. It accepted Allan Kardec as a preparer but argued that Kardec had not completed or securely “set” the foundation of true Spiritism.

Later biography says a severe “gangrenous” illness confined him for six months and reached a crisis on 19 September 1911, fulfilling a prediction that illness at 45 would end his ordinary mission and allow him to “be reborn.” No medical diagnosis or treatment record was located. His recovery immediately before the institutional founding became part of his sacred biography.

## Foundation of the EMECU, 20 September 1911

On **20 September 1911**, Trincado formally founded the **Escuela Magnético-Espiritual de la Comuna Universal** in Buenos Aires. The date was later linked to several symbolic chronologies:

- the anniversary of the capture of Rome in 1870 and the end of papal temporal power, associated by Trincado with Garibaldi and anticlerical liberation;
- an alleged astronomical perihelion of Earth, which is factually incorrect for September;
- completion of the 3,600-year interval in a supposed secret Testament of Abraham;
- the beginning of a “New Era” and the “Government of Spiritism” for Earth.

The EMECU began as a study group of Constancia dissidents. Its formal legal/institutional development took years; Argentine authorities approved statutes and regulations in January 1919. The organization was hierarchical, with Trincado as founder/director, regional and local *cátedras*, and local heads called *celadores*.

Its fixed proclamation was:

> El Universo solidarizado.  
> El Mundo todo comunizado.  
> La Ley es una. La substancia una.  
> Uno es el principio. Uno es el fin.  
> Todo es Magnetismo Espiritual.

Its recurring maxims included “Conócete a ti mismo,” “Ama a tu hermano,” “Por el fruto conocerás el árbol,” and “Siempre más allá.”

## The Jerusalem/Essene Narrative

### What the story says

Trincado was said to have traveled through Bethlehem to “the Orient of Jerusalem,” used secret signs identifying him to guardians of a surviving Essene Kabbalistic school, examined ancient archives, original documents, parchment books, and mummies, and received authority from aged Kabbalists to reveal truths preserved since Moses founded the “Cábala Mayor” 3,600 years earlier. The EMECU was therefore not merely a new association but the direct public continuation of Moses' secret school.

### When and where it appears

The narrative is embedded in Trincado's own *Buscando a Dios* and repeated in the 1972 *Voz Informativa* biography and later EMECU materials. Dates conflict sharply: some say 1903, others 1912, after the EMECU had already been founded.

### Who circulated it

Trincado, his immediate institution, successor cátedras, and devotional biographers.

### Evidence

No passport, itinerary, ship record, hotel record, letter from Jerusalem, named Jewish or Christian interlocutor, archive inventory, photograph, or independent contemporary report was found. The alleged institution combines historically distinct terms—Essenes, Kabbalah, Moses, Jerusalem, “sexagenarian sages”—in a lineage not recognized by standard histories of Second Temple Judaism or Kabbalah. The chronology conflict and absence of identifiable custodians make the story historically unverified.

### Reputational function

The journey authorized Trincado independently of Kardec, the Catholic Church, universities, and existing spiritist federations. It converted personal revelation into institutional succession and made the EMECU the public culmination of an ancient hidden chain.

## Doctrinal and Intellectual Development

### Spirit, matter, and “Eloí”

Trincado treated spirit as the active, responsible, evolving principle; body and soul were temporary instruments. “Eloí” was the universal creative center or Father, but he resisted conventional theism and rejected the anthropomorphic God of institutional religion. Electricity was “fuerza omnipotente y madre de todo lo creado”; magnetism resulted from universal motion. He presented electricity, magnetism, life, and spirit as aspects of a continuous lawful cosmos.

### Relation to Kardec

He adopted reincarnation, spirit communication, inhabited worlds, moral laws, and the claim that spiritism could be investigated scientifically. He simultaneously argued that Kardec's revelation was incomplete and later distorted. The EMECU offered itself as the final rational, anti-dogmatic correction.

### Anti-religious position

Trincado was not an atheist in the ordinary sense. He invoked a creator and revelation but denounced organized religions—especially Catholicism and Christianity—as engines of ignorance, hierarchy, war, sexual repression, and material exploitation. His writing repeatedly attacks papal authority, celibacy, virgin-birth doctrine, sacramental control, and the divinity of Jesus. He reconstructed Jesus as a human teacher and revolutionary prophet of love and freedom.

### Comunalism, socialism, and politics

The “Comuna Universal” abolished private parcels and national boundaries in the final ideal: work, production, and benefits were to be common. Trincado used *socialism* and *communism* in a metaphysical rather than Marxist-materialist sense. Individual moral transformation, family, disciplined hierarchy, and spiritual rank remained essential. His system thus combined economic communalism and anti-capitalism with a teleological, ordered, and strongly supervised society.

He identified the Castilian Comuneros and Juan de Padilla as ancestors of communism; read the French Revolution as another stage; and treated Latin American revolutions as providential vehicles. In *La Revolución de México y el crimen de Norteamérica* he supported Pancho Villa, portrayed Villa as a reincarnation of Padilla, and regarded armed revolution as just when the majority imposed communal law on defenders of private property. He distinguished “revolution,” which he considered principled and regenerative, from “war,” which he associated with conquest and domination.

The book's treatment of revolutionary violence is consequential: Trincado rationalized actions attributed to Villa, including the Columbus raid, as expressions of a higher communal law. This is a documented political doctrine, not evidence that Trincado himself committed violence.

### Hispanism and colonial hierarchy

Trincado projected a transnational Spanish-speaking unity and treated the conquest-era movement of Europeans to America as part of providential human fusion. He interpreted Hernán Cortés as bringing Padilla's “seed of the commune” to Mexico and privileged Villa while largely omitting Zapata and Indigenous communal agency. Galicia Martínez describes this framework as Hispanophile, Eurocentric, colonialist, and proto-imperial. Those are the scholar's analytical classifications; the underlying documentary facts are Trincado's Spanish-centered sacred history and his positive use of conquest as a stage in universal integration.

### Race and eugenic social engineering

Short biographies usually omit the explicit racial program in *Conócete a ti mismo*. Trincado at times affirmed the common humanity, intelligence, dignity, and equal bodily organization of Black, white, “yellow,” and “copper-colored” peoples and condemned historical prejudice. He also advanced a hierarchy of bodily “beauty,” described Black people as generally strong but “poco ilustrado,” said “el negro no lo quiere ser,” and proposed controlled intermarriage intended to make the “white or Adamic race” prevail within two generations. He recommended removing young Black women from their region, pairing them with white men, pairing young Black men with older white women, regulating climate and diet, and even declaring a region uninhabitable if offspring remained Black.

These passages are primary-source evidence of racialist and eugenic thinking, including coercive reproductive implications, even though Trincado framed the project as unification, education, beauty, and the eventual abolition of racial distinctions. There is no evidence in the consulted sources that an EMECU colony implemented this racial breeding program.

### Women, sex, and family

Trincado argued that spirit has no sex, condemned women's legal and educational subordination, supported women's participation in plebiscites, called women at least half of society and “society entire” through motherhood, and demanded their education and freedom. At the same time, he essentialized women as mothers, emotional repositories, and companions of male workers, treated heterosexual procreation as universal law, condemned celibacy, and developed speculative physiological claims about sexuality. His practical personal history—permanent separation from his first wife, a relationship with Mariana Palacios, and a second Argentine household—also sits uneasily beside his idealized family doctrine, though the surviving evidence does not permit a full reconstruction of consent, legal status, or household finances.

### Cosmology and sacred history

His books describe vast prehistory, multiple inhabited worlds, migrations of spirits from Neptune, Venus, and other planets, the Atlantis catastrophe, humans emerging from cinchona-tree forms, and chains of reincarnation connecting biblical, classical, and modern figures. One scheme has the same missionary spirit successively inhabiting Seth, Jacob, Moses, Confucius, Socrates, and James; another links Isaiah to Jesus and Ignatius of Loyola to Garibaldi. Trincado claimed to be one of twenty-nine planetary missionary spirits.

These narratives are religious mythology and revealed cosmology, not accepted scientific or historical findings. They were nevertheless organizationally important: they placed current adherents, political leaders, and Trincado himself inside a single cosmic genealogy.

## Institutional Expansion and Publishing

The EMECU expanded through *cátedras*, books, circulars, lectures, correspondence, and affiliated periodicals. Counts vary by date and source:

- 172 cátedras in some Sandino-era summaries;
- 180 or 184 in institutional biographies;
- 185 branches and three colonies at Trincado's death in one biography;
- 194 across the continent, 94 in Mexico, in the UNAM reconstruction;
- a movement claim of about 50,000 adherents in approximately 185 branches, preserved by IAPSOP.

The differing counts probably reflect dates, recognition disputes, and promotional rounding. Presence is documented or repeatedly reported in Argentina, Mexico, Cuba, Nicaragua, Venezuela, Puerto Rico, Uruguay, Guatemala, Honduras, El Salvador, the Dominican Republic, the United States, Spain, and elsewhere.

**Ediciones Voz Informativa** published Trincado's books in Buenos Aires; a parallel or affiliated Mexican imprint was operating by the late 1920s. Mexico's regional cátedra issued *El Heraldo del Espiritismo* from January 1923.

The EMECU's official Buenos Aires periodical, **La Balanza**, began in January 1933 as a fortnightly. IAPSOP preserves a major digitized run and reports that some affiliated journals claimed circulations around 4,000. An Argentine official-periodical record identifies Mercedes Riglos Cosis de Trincado as proprietor of *La Balanza* nos. 200–201 in May 1941, demonstrating continuity after Joaquín's death.

## Political and International Organizations

### Unión Hispano-Américo-Oceánica (UHAO)

On 12 October 1921 Trincado promoted the **Unión Hispano-Américo-Oceánica**, also rendered Unión Hispano América Oceánica. Its emblem combined a seven-color rainbow flag with the oak of Guernica. It sought political, moral, and cultural unity among Spanish-speaking and “oceanic” peoples through a *Referéndum Básico*. Movement accounts say several governments received or accepted its principles, but no treaty, ratification, or official multilateral organization was located.

### Orden del Templo Azul Racionalista (OTAR)

Around 1925 he founded the **Orden del Templo Azul Racionalista**, intended to organize scientists and intellectuals. He also promoted a teachers' organization or “Círculo Defensor del Maestro,” designed as secular, rational education by and for teachers. Detailed membership and financial records were not found.

### Freemasonry

Trincado is repeatedly described as a Freemason who reached high degrees, and the University of Salta museum history places him among Buenos Aires freethinkers and Francophone/Franco-Masonic networks. Sandino literature also treats their connection partly as Masonic. No lodge name, initiation date, degree certificate, grand-lodge register, or lodge correspondence was located. Masonic affiliation is probable but not independently documented in detail; claims that he reached the “highest degrees” remain institutional assertions.

## Augusto César Sandino

Sandino encountered the EMECU in Mexico, especially Mérida/Progreso, around 1929, read Trincado's works, attended séances, and embraced reincarnation and “rationalist communism.” Evidence summarized by Wenceslao Amezcua and other Sandino researchers indicates that:

- Sandino and Trincado communicated by letters and apparently never met in person;
- Trincado appointed or recognized Sandino as an EMECU *celador* in Nicaragua;
- Sandino established a cátedra in Las Segovias;
- he changed or supplemented “Patria y Libertad” with the EMECU motto “Siempre más allá”;
- in 1932 he created a Río Coco mutual-aid/fraternal community and displayed the seven-color EMECU flag;
- he requested a teacher from the Mexican EMECU to instruct peasants;
- he reportedly styled Trincado “Venerable Patriarch” and issued him a credential as Sandino's representative in Argentina.

Trincado's actual effect on Sandino is disputed in scope, not existence. Hostile biographers make trincadismo the key to Sandino's last political phase; other scholars treat it as one strand within his anti-imperialism, Masonic contacts, theosophical and Indigenous imagery, and revolutionary organization. Sandino's own letters and practices establish meaningful engagement, but they do not show that every political act after 1929 was dictated by Trincado.

Trincado was deeply affected by Sandino's assassination in February 1934. Movement biography says he regarded Sandino as both disciple and political martyr.

## Communal Colonies and Material Projects

### Colonia Jaime, Santiago del Estero

Trincado first visited Santiago del Estero around 1930 and returned while land was acquired and the community organized. The best-supported foundation date is **25 July 1932**, despite a common 1931 date in derivative summaries. The colony, in Robles department near National Route 34 km 711, was named **Colonia Jaime** and was founded under the principles of *Espiritismo Luz y Verdad*.

The property is variously measured at approximately **600–607 hectares**. Its defining arrangement was common ownership/use without internal family parcels; private space was limited largely to sleeping rooms. Work, production, vehicles, machinery, and income were pooled and administered through a communal council. The colony survived Trincado and remains active. A 2022 study counted 91 residents in 23 family groups, with dairy, pigs, poultry, apiculture, horticulture, fodder, a biodigester, workshops, communal vehicles, and retail outlets. This is the most durable material realization of Trincado's social program.

### Colonia Los Franciscos, Salta

The **Colonia Comunal Los Franciscos** was founded on **31 December 1934** in Anta department near El Quebrachal, Salta. It was also EMECU Cátedra no. 175. Former owner **Eulalia Gimeno de Álvarez** served as internal director/administrator, while Trincado was designated founder-owner in the moral, spiritual, and external sense. The colony's rules provided for common work and common usufruct. It continued until approximately 1966.

The Museo Histórico “Prof. Eduardo Ashur” of the Universidad Nacional de Salta holds a substantial archive: an album of 118 photographs, loose prints and negatives, birth, marriage, labor, resident, visitor, accounting, donation, investment, construction, and meeting books, correspondence, seals, a transformer, and other objects. This is a major underused primary collection.

### Other colony claims

Derivative biographies name a **Colonia Los Libertadores** and sometimes speak of three colonies existing at his death. The accessible sources did not establish its location, date, title history, or relationship to Los Franciscos; it should not be conflated with the two documented colonies without further archival work.

## Later Life, Illness, and Death

From the mid-1920s to 1935, Trincado's Buenos Aires home also served as the Central Cátedra and administrative headquarters. He wrote, edited, lectured, answered correspondence, authorized branches, managed disputes, and supervised colonies. Movement accounts say he worked eighteen to twenty hours a day; this is plausible as a description of intensity but not independently timed.

He admired the singer Carlos Gardel and reportedly said after Gardel's June 1935 death, “Ese hombre pone el alma en lo que canta.”

In the last month of his life Trincado, already ill, reportedly traveled thousands of kilometers by Argentine rail to advise communal colonies. His children remembered him at his desk with his legs wrapped in blankets while handling the affairs of roughly 180 cátedras. The sources describe a progressive disease that “mined” his body but do not name it. One summary attributes his decline generally to overwork. No reliable cause of death, physician's report, hospital record, or death certificate was located.

He died in Buenos Aires on 6 December 1935. No documented burial place, funeral account, estate valuation, or will was found in the consulted material.

## Controversies, Allegations, and Unresolved Questions

### Documented or strongly supported

- He permanently separated from his Spanish wife and surviving sons after their short 1909 reunion and formed later relationships and an Argentine family.
- His business failed around 1909; the exact legal mechanism and losses are unknown.
- He rejected Constancia and built a rival, tightly hierarchical organization.
- His political writing defended revolutionary violence under specified “communal” conditions and sacralized Pancho Villa.
- His anthropology included a coercive racial-eugenic program favoring eventual whitening, notwithstanding universalist statements elsewhere.
- He made sweeping scientific, historical, archaeological, medical, and cosmological claims unsupported by conventional evidence.
- His institutional succession became hereditary through his widow and son.

### Repeated claims lacking independent documentation

- formal electrical-engineering graduation at Liège/Brussels and the credential “no. 22”;
- supervision of Alfonso XIII's coronation illumination;
- management of a Valencian hydroelectric plant;
- patents for electric heating or other inventions, and patent litigation causing bankruptcy;
- great personal wealth or a precisely measurable fortune;
- the highest Masonic degrees;
- a historical journey to and initiation by a surviving Essene-Kabbalistic school;
- supernatural mediumship, telepathy, automatic dictation, and fulfilled prophecies;
- governmental acceptance of the UHAO referendum;
- 18–20-hour daily writing as a measured fact.

### Allegation of abandonment

Alejandro Bolaños Geyer's hostile biography of Sandino says Trincado “abandoned” his wife and two small children in Bilbao in November 1903. The family reconstruction indicates three surviving sons by then and shows that he received them all in Buenos Aires in February 1909 before their return that December. “Abandonment” is therefore an interpretation of the separation, not a complete factual description. The economic support he provided between 1903 and 1909 and after 1909 is not known.

### Fraud, crimes, or criminal proceedings

No evidence was found that Trincado was investigated, indicted, convicted, or imprisoned for fraud, theft, sexual crime, political violence, or financial crime. His teachings were denounced by religious and rival spiritist opponents as superstition, imposture, materialism, atheism, or corruption; those are polemical accusations, not court findings.

Posthumous EMECU bodies became involved in civil and criminal litigation in Venezuela over corporate identity, authority, premises, and related institutional disputes. These cases show fragmentation of the legacy but do not establish wrongdoing by Trincado, who had been dead for decades.

## Myths, Legends, Propaganda, and Self-Created Sacred History

### The predestined birth and “Maestro Nato”

Movement narrative treats his birth time, poverty, prematurity, manual labor, Jesuit training, bankruptcy, suicidal crisis, gangrenous illness, and recovery as ordered trials producing the appointed teacher. This hagiographic structure appears in EMECU biographies and turns misfortune into proof of mission. Birth and hardship are plausible; predestination is a doctrinal interpretation.

### The electrical prophet

The story presents the working electrician who discovered that electricity was the material face of spirit, invented electric heating before others, lost his fortune defending the invention, and transformed ruin into universal science. His electrical occupation and writing on heating are supported; priority, patents, theft, and global adoption are not documented.

### The hidden Essene succession

The Jerusalem story gave the EMECU an ancient Mosaic charter and authority superior to churches, Kardecian societies, and universities. It remains one of the central legitimating myths of the movement.

### The Judgment of Majorities, 5 April 1912

EMECU tradition says that a cosmic “Juicio de Mayorías” or final judgment occurred on 5 April 1912, after which the spiritual government of Earth entered a new stage. Trincado was styled judge or principal earthly agent. This event is attested as an internal revelatory claim, not as an independently observable judicial or astronomical event.

### Reincarnational chains

Trincado's sacred histories identify modern figures as reincarnations of biblical, classical, or revolutionary predecessors—Padilla/Villa, Loyola/Garibaldi, and others—and place Trincado among a small corps of planetary missionaries. These stories sacralized political alliances and made current history the last act of a cosmic drama.

### Prophecy of geological catastrophe and the Electromagno

He predicted cataclysmic terrestrial transformation: sinking continents and islands, new lands, a second moon or changed heavens, and a post-catastrophe communal order. The **Electromagno** was presented as a future superior energy capable of replacing ordinary electricity and transforming medicine and material life. No operational device, engineering specification, or successful demonstration was located. The prophecies remain active in some successor literature and online media.

### Posthumous communications

After his death the Buenos Aires leadership announced that Trincado's spirit was tired and would rest. In 1938 the Mexican regional cátedra reported that his spirit instead appeared there because spirits in Buenos Aires were “ofuscados.” Between 1939 and 1940 the Mexican group attributed corrections of the *Código de Amor Universal* to his posthumous dictation. This became a charter for Mexico's break with Mercedes Riglos's central authority. The communications are documented as claims circulating inside the movement; supernatural authorship is unverified.

## Works by Joaquín Trincado

### Bibliographical cautions

The corpus has never been fully catalogued. Counts of **40, 42, and 46** works coexist. “Work” may mean a book, multi-volume series, pamphlet, periodical contribution, lecture cycle, spirit communication, regulation, or an announced manuscript. The familiar statement “42 works, 14 printed, 32 unpublished” is mathematically inconsistent unless volumes, pamphlets, lost works, and revised editions are counted differently. A 2024 scholar reports a corpus of about 46 books still not academically systematized.

Original editions were commonly printed by **Voz Informativa** in Buenos Aires and Mexico. Later cátedras silently modernized spelling, combined texts, issued electronic transcriptions, or assigned Trincado's name to selections. Dates below distinguish composition, first known publication, revision, and posthumous compilation wherever possible.

### Major works published during his life

1. **Buscando a Dios y asiento del Dios Amor** — composed approximately 1909–1911. Autobiographical crisis narrative and doctrinal foundation. Its exact first-edition date is inconsistently reported; bibliographies associate the text with 1911, while later combined editions appeared in 1931 and afterward.
2. **Discurso del obispo Strossmayer** — published in the Trincado corpus with his prologue and anti-papal commentary. The central text purports to be a speech by Bishop Josip Juraj Strossmayer at Vatican I in 1870 and is not an original speech authored by Trincado. Its historical authenticity and textual transmission require separate critical study.
3. **La Revolución de México y el crimen de Norteamérica: Juicio biológico, etno-ético y fisiológico** — written in 1914; contains letters dated April and July 1914 and commentary on the Mexican Revolution. Trincado said he lacked funds for publication and rejected a publisher's rights demand; the first public edition date remains unclear, and modern editions are posthumous.
4. **El magnetismo en su origen: Método Supremo. Manual de educación magnética** — 1st ed. August 1916 (movement claim: 6,000 copies); 2nd enlarged ed. September 1919 (2,000 copies); 3rd greatly enlarged ed. November 1928; 4th photo-engraved ed. April 1935.
5. **El Primer Rayo de Luz: Juicio crítico a la controversia católico-anarquista** — one academic bibliography dates an initial form to 1917; the surviving edition states 1st ed. June 1922 and 2nd ed. February 1932. The date discrepancy may reflect an earlier pamphlet or later retitling.
6. **El espiritismo en su asiento** — 1st ed. August 1919; corrected and enlarged 2nd ed. April 1929.
7. **Filosofía Austera Racional** — 1st ed. 1920. Trincado was revising it at death; a corrected second edition was issued posthumously by his children several decades later. Some editions run to two volumes.
8. **El espiritismo estudiado, o Política del Creador y gobierno del espiritismo: fundamento de la Filosofía Austera Racional** — Buenos Aires, Voz Informativa, January 1922; catalogued at 416 pages in one edition.
9. **Los cinco amores: Ética y Sociología. Suplemento a la Filosofía Austera Racional** — Buenos Aires, January 1922. The five domains are commonly rendered love of flesh/family, family, homeland, humanity, and the Creator, though headings vary by edition.
10. **Profilaxis de la vida: Los 24 ancianos** — published in 1922 according to the original bibliographical citation; some later lists associate it with 1931. Pedagogy, health, sexuality, family, and communal life.
11. **Conócete a ti mismo: Fisiología, Fisiognosía y Etnología del Universo** — written July 1913–March 1914; 1st ed. June 1924; corrected 2nd ed. February 1931. One of his broadest works and the principal source for his anthropology and racial program.
12. **Alfaquí Vademécum: El Maestro va conmigo** — Buenos Aires, June 1924; a compact dialogue/prontuario of the creed.
13. **Los extremos se tocan: Epílogo de la guerra y prólogo de la paz** — written across 1914–1929; published 1929. Historical-political and antiwar/communal synthesis.
14. **Código de Amor Universal para el régimen de la Comuna de Amor y Ley**, book/tome I — rubricated 20 September 1911 and signed 5 April 1912 according to later editions; published by 1934 in the standard bibliography. Institutional legal-moral code.
15. **Filosofía Enciclopédica Universal: Voz del Espiritismo**, tomes I–II — portions circulated or were published in the Trincado period; exact first-edition dates were not established. Later institutional accounts describe tomes III–XII as unpublished or fragmentarily released.
16. **Cuestionario espírita-racionalista de la Escuela Magnético-Espiritual de la Comuna Universal** — catechetical manual “for sworn and presented children”; first date not established.
17. **La Balanza** — editor/director and frequent contributor from January 1933 to death; the periodical also printed statutes, regulations, circulars, articles, movement news, and doctrinal judgments.
18. **Statutes, Reglamento Interno, circulars, orders, proclamations, lecture texts, UHAO referendum, colony rules, and correspondence** — institutional writings dispersed across periodicals, archives, and successor compilations; a complete critical inventory does not exist.

### Posthumously published or edited from manuscripts

1. **Código de Amor Universal**, book/tome II — attributed to Trincado, edited by Juan D. Trincado Riglos, Buenos Aires, 1975. The edition says it was rubricated in 1911 and signed in 1912; its publication and editorial form are posthumous.
2. **Vida de María / Historia verdadera de María de Nazareth / La verdadera vida de María de Jericó** — a Trincado manuscript issued posthumously; a 2003 electronic edition was authorized by Víctor Rolando Trincado. It presents Mary as a natural, prolific mother rather than a perpetual virgin.
3. **Jesús hombre y no Dios** — a posthumous selection from *Buscando a Dios*, *Filosofía Austera Racional*, *Los extremos se tocan*, and *Enciclopedia del Séptimo Día*; electronic edition, Monterrey, 2004. It should not be cited as an independently composed lifetime book without qualification.
4. **Filosofía Enciclopédica Universal**, later tomes/fragments — institutionally attributed, incompletely and irregularly published.
5. **Enciclopedia del Séptimo Día**, including a surviving/posted part 25 of tome III dated April 1913, “Estudio crítico del mundo” — manuscript or fragmentary posthumous release.

### Announced, lost, disputed, or incompletely available works

- **El Libro Rojo / La justicia en acción**, several tomes;
- **Renovación de la faz de la Tierra**, several tomes;
- **Manual del Electromagno**;
- **Anatomía política, etapa III**;
- **Después del Juicio**, several tomes;
- **El Juicio Final de la Tierra**, several tomes;
- additional tomes of **Filosofía Enciclopédica Universal** and **Enciclopedia del Séptimo Día**;
- an **Autobiografía**, announced in *La Balanza* but never published.

Mendoza Vallejo reported that Elena Prados de Trincado and Joaquina Ana Trincado Riglos personally confirmed to him in Buenos Aires on 31 July 1963 that an autobiography had existed. He also reported that by 1993 members meeting at Colonia Jaime acknowledged that the oft-repeated cache of thirty-two unpublished works was no longer in the Trincado Riglos family's possession. Whether manuscripts were lost, destroyed, divided, misidentified, privately retained, or absorbed into later editions remains unresolved.

## Posthumous Succession, Schisms, and Legal Legacy

Mercedes Riglos Cosis de Trincado assumed the central directorship in Buenos Aires. The succession then passed to Juan Donato Trincado Riglos and later to Víctor Rolando Trincado. This hereditary model preserved textual custody and institutional continuity but also made authority dependent on family lineage.

The most important early schism occurred in **Mexico in 1938**. A Mexican cátedra claimed posthumous communications from Trincado criticizing the Buenos Aires spiritual leadership and authorizing corrections to the *Código de Amor Universal*. The rebellious Mexican regional body separated from Mercedes's authority. A loyalist faction created a new regional cátedra and, with a 19 March 1942 authorization from Mercedes, a new journal, *Justicia*. The older *Heraldo del Espiritismo* faction appears to have survived longer. The dispute combined institutional governance with competing claims to authentic speech from Trincado's spirit.

Later EMECU organizations in Venezuela litigated over corporate status, authority, property, and representation. Venezuelan court records from 2001–2015 refer to Trincado, Mercedes, and Juan Donato in reciting the organization's chain of authority. These are posthumous institutional conflicts rather than cases against Trincado.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Trincado's legacy exists in several partially separate fields:

- **religious founder:** followers regard him as the final rational systematizer of spiritism, “Maestro Juez,” and revealer of a Mosaic-Essene doctrine;
- **Latin American esotericism:** scholars increasingly treat the EMECU as a major transnational occult movement rather than a marginal sect;
- **political religion:** his synthesis of spiritism, anti-clericalism, Hispanism, communal property, disciplined hierarchy, and revolution is a distinctive form of early twentieth-century Latin American utopianism;
- **Sandino studies:** his correspondence and institutional network are essential to understanding Sandino's late spiritual vocabulary, communal experiment, rainbow flag, and “Siempre más allá” motto;
- **communal practice:** Colonia Jaime is the clearest living institutional legacy, demonstrating that his program generated a durable agricultural common-property community;
- **print culture:** *Voz Informativa*, *La Balanza*, *El Heraldo del Espiritismo*, *Justicia*, and local cátedra journals created a continental Spanish-language counterpublic;
- **contested archive:** lost manuscripts, divergent editions, posthumous dictations, family control, and schisms make authorship and doctrine unstable;
- **critical reinterpretation:** recent scholarship has moved beyond devotional biography to examine colonial Hispanism, revolutionary violence, hierarchy, gender, and racial eugenics in the corpus.

No state award, honorary degree, official scientific prize, or formal public appointment was documented. His honors were internal titles and the loyalty of adherents. Conversely, his obscurity in standard histories is not evidence of negligible influence: the number of cátedras, Mexican and Caribbean print record, Sandino connection, and survival of Colonia Jaime show substantial reach.

## Atlas Connections

### Abraham

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Trincado repeatedly wrote about Abraham in the *Código de Amor Universal*, presenting an alleged “Testament of Abraham” as the foundation of a secret initiatory succession transmitted through Jacob and Moses and supposedly fulfilled by the EMECU. The fact that Trincado published this account is documented; the testament, transmission, and fulfillment are **mythic or religious claims**, unsupported by accepted historical evidence. [Primary text: *Código de Amor Universal*, tome I](https://emedelacu.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/CodigodeAmorUniversalTomoI-1.pdf)

### Moses

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Trincado made Moses central to his sacred history, describing him as founder of an ancient esoteric school and custodian of Abraham’s testament. He identified the EMECU, founded in Buenos Aires in 1911, as the reopening or public continuation of this supposed Mosaic-Essene school. Trincado’s authorship of these statements is documented; the lineage itself is **myth** rather than established history. [Primary text: *Código de Amor Universal*, tome I](https://emedelacu.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/CodigodeAmorUniversalTomoI-1.pdf)

### Jesus of Nazareth

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Across *Buscando a Dios*, *Código de Amor Universal*, and other works written between approximately 1909 and 1935, Trincado reconstructed Jesus as a natural-born human teacher of love and liberty, denied that Jesus was God, and distinguished the historical Jesus from “Christ,” which he called a later religious fiction. The writings are documented; Trincado’s purported biographical details, spirit identities, and reincarnational history of Jesus are **religious allegations or myths**. [Primary text: *Código de Amor Universal*, tome I](https://emedelacu.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/CodigodeAmorUniversalTomoI-1.pdf)

### Paul the Apostle

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Trincado discussed Paul while constructing his revisionist history of early Christianity, including an appeal to the Epistle to the Galatians as evidence that James was Jesus’s brother. This constitutes textual engagement with Paul and Pauline writings, not evidence of any historical or supernatural contact. [Primary text: *Código de Amor Universal*, tome I](https://emedelacu.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/CodigodeAmorUniversalTomoI-1.pdf)

### Hernán Cortés

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Trincado devoted a chapter of *La Revolución de México y el crimen de Norteamérica*, written in 1914, to Mexico through the age of Cortés. He acknowledged Cortés’s military ability and interpreted the Spanish victory over Moctezuma as an instrument of a metaphysical law driving the eventual unification of humanity. This interpretation is documented in Trincado’s text; its providential mechanism is an **allegation or theory**, not a historical finding. [Primary text: *La Revolución de México y el crimen de Norteamérica*](https://www.coloniajaime.org/documentos/Libros%20emecu/La%20Revolucion%20de%20Mexico.pdf)

### Moctezuma II

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** In the same 1914 work, Trincado portrayed Moctezuma as a prestigious ruler presiding over a comparatively advanced collective or communal order, then used the encounter between Moctezuma and Cortés to support his metaphysical interpretation of conquest and universal integration. Trincado’s discussion is documented; the claim that an “eternal law” directed both rulers is an **allegation or theory**. [Primary text: *La Revolución de México y el crimen de Norteamérica*](https://www.coloniajaime.org/documentos/Libros%20emecu/La%20Revolucion%20de%20Mexico.pdf)

### Christopher Columbus

- **[T1-]** **Reported fact:** Trincado wrote about Columbus’s identity and voyage, advancing the theory that Columbus came from Galicia—specifically the Pontevedra area—was probably Jewish, and possessed prior knowledge of Caribbean lands from an earlier storm-driven Atlantic crossing. Later authors explicitly attribute this argument to Trincado, but the accessible citations do not establish it as accepted history; the biographical and pre-discovery claims remain an **allegation or theory**. [Account attributing the argument to Trincado](https://www.kienyke.com/kien-fue/cristobal-colon-el-ladron-que-descubrio-america)

### Giuseppe Garibaldi

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Trincado wrote about Garibaldi as an anticlerical liberator and treated the EMECU’s foundation on 20 September 1911 as a continuation of the struggle symbolized by the capture of Rome on 20 September 1870. His reincarnational identification of Garibaldi with Ignatius of Loyola belongs to Trincado’s **religious mythology**, not documented biography. [Alejandra G. Galicia Martínez, “La Comuna Universal”](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/9293415.pdf)

### Carlos Gardel

- **[T3]** **Reported fact:** A movement-derived biography says that Trincado heard Gardel’s performances on Argentine radio, came to admire him despite initially disliking tango, and remarked that Gardel “puts his soul into what he sings.” This supports one-sided reception of Gardel’s performance, probably in Buenos Aires before Gardel’s death on 24 June 1935; it does **not** document a meeting, friendship, correspondence, or attendance at a live performance.
