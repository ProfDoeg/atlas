# Publius Aelius Hadrianus: Research Dossier

## Scope, Identification, and Source Problems

This dossier concerns the Roman emperor commonly called **Hadrian**, who reigned from 117 to 138 CE.

The reconstruction is unusually dependent on evidence of different kinds and dates:

- **Contemporary or near-contemporary evidence:** inscriptions, coins, papyri, military diplomas, buildings, legal rulings, letters, and administrative documents.
- **Literary witnesses:** Cassius Dio’s *Roman History*, written roughly a century after Hadrian; Fronto; Pausanias; Arrian; Justin Martyr; Eusebius; and later epitomators.
- **The *Historia Augusta*, Life of Hadrian:** probably composed in the late fourth century, although presented under fictitious earlier authorship. It preserves valuable material, some apparently derived from Hadrian’s lost autobiography and earlier imperial biographies, but also scandal, inventions, forged documents, literary jokes, and hostile senatorial traditions.
- **Rabbinic and Christian traditions:** indispensable for the Bar Kokhba revolt and Hadrian’s later reputation, but often preserved substantially later than the events and shaped by religious commemoration.
- **Archaeology and numismatics:** essential for reconstructing Hadrian’s journeys, provincial patronage, portraiture, frontier policies, Aelia Capitolina, Antinous’s cult, and the chronology of imperial titles.

Statements below are labeled when their evidentiary status is materially disputed.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal line

Hadrian’s father was **Publius Aelius Hadrianus Afer**, a Roman senator and former praetor. The family belonged to the municipal aristocracy of Italica. Its ancestors were said to have migrated from **Hadria in Picenum** to Hispania during the Roman Republic, traditionally in the period of the Scipios.

The surname **Afer**, “the African,” may indicate an African connection or service, but its precise origin is uncertain.

Hadrian’s paternal family was related to the family of the future emperor **Trajan**. Ancient and modern genealogical reconstructions differ over the exact degree of kinship; Hadrian is conventionally described as Trajan’s first cousin once removed or a close younger kinsman.

### Maternal line

His mother was **Domitia Paulina**, usually called Paulina Major by modern scholars to distinguish her from Hadrian’s sister. She came from **Gades**, modern Cádiz, another wealthy Baetican city.

Her family background connected Hadrian to the Hispano-Roman elite of southern Spain. The evidence does not permit a complete reconstruction of her ancestry.

### Sibling

Hadrian had an elder sister:

- **Aelia Domitia Paulina**, commonly called Paulina Minor.
- She married **Lucius Julius Ursus Servianus**, a senator, three-time consul, and major figure in Hadrian’s family network.
- Their daughter, Julia Paulina, married Gnaeus Pedanius Fuscus Salinator.
- Their grandson **Gnaeus Pedanius Fuscus Salinator** was eventually treated as a potential rival in the succession and executed with Servianus in 136.

### Guardianship

Hadrian’s father died when Hadrian was approximately ten, conventionally in 85 or 86. Guardianship passed to:

- **Trajan**, then an ascending senatorial and military figure.
- **Publius Acilius Attianus**, a Roman knight and family associate from Italica.

Attianus later became praetorian prefect and played an important role in Hadrian’s accession. Hadrian removed him from effective power after the controversial executions of four former consuls in 118.

### Marriage

Around 100 CE Hadrian married **Vibia Sabina**, daughter of Lucius Vibius Sabinus and **Salonia Matidia**, and grandniece of Trajan.

The marriage tied Hadrian directly to the imperial household. Plotina, Trajan’s wife, and Matidia appear to have promoted it.

**Documented fact:** the marriage produced no securely attested children.

**Reported hostile tradition:** the *Historia Augusta* describes the marriage as unhappy and says Hadrian declared Sabina’s difficult character would have justified divorce had he been a private citizen. The same work accuses Hadrian of relationships with married women and alleges that Sabina prevented pregnancy to avoid bearing a child resembling him. These are late, hostile, and uncorroborated claims.

Sabina traveled with Hadrian on at least part of the great eastern journey of 128–132 and was present in Egypt. She died in approximately 136 or 137 and was deified.

## Childhood and Early Life, 76–93

Hadrian was born into a wealthy senatorial family with estates and enduring municipal ties in Baetica. Whether born in Rome or Italica, he spent part of his youth in Spain.

Ancient tradition says that he became so devoted to Greek language and culture that he received the nickname **Graeculus**. His later life confirms unusually deep Greek interests: he wrote in Greek, participated in Greek civic and religious institutions, patronized Athens, underwent initiation at Eleusis, cultivated philosophers and sophists, and presented himself visually in a Greek-inflected style.

After his father’s death, Hadrian came under the supervision of Trajan and Attianus. At approximately fourteen or fifteen he went to Italica and developed an enthusiasm for hunting. The *Historia Augusta* says Trajan recalled him to Rome because the hunting had become excessive. The basic episode is plausible, though its moralizing presentation may belong to biographical convention.

His formal preparation was that of a Roman senator’s son: rhetoric, literature, law, public ceremony, and military apprenticeship. No complete list of teachers survives.

## Education and Intellectual Formation

Hadrian was highly literate in Latin and Greek. His known or reported interests included:

- Greek poetry and rhetoric.
- Roman antiquarian literature.
- Architecture and engineering.
- Geometry and artistic design.
- Astronomy, astrology, and divination.
- Medicine.
- Hunting.
- Music and performance.
- Legal administration.
- Epicurean, Stoic, and other philosophical schools.

The *Historia Augusta* says he preferred **Cato to Cicero**, **Ennius to Virgil**, and the obscure Greek poet **Antimachus to Homer**. These preferences may reflect genuine archaizing taste, deliberate contrarianism, or the biographer’s caricature of an emperor who enjoyed correcting experts.

Hadrian reportedly attended or encountered leading intellectuals including:

- **Epictetus**, the Stoic philosopher, whom later tradition associates with Hadrian’s visit to Nicopolis.
- **Favorinus of Arelate**, sophist and philosopher.
- **Dionysius of Miletus**, sophist.
- **Polemon of Laodicea**, sophist and civic notable.
- **Heliodorus**, rhetorician.
- **Fronto**, rhetorician and later tutor of Marcus Aurelius.
- **Arrian of Nicomedia**, historian, administrator, and military writer.
- **Phlegon of Tralles**, imperial freedman, chronicler, and literary associate.

A well-known anecdote says Favorinus yielded to Hadrian during a linguistic disagreement. When friends protested, Favorinus replied that it was prudent to consider a man commanding thirty legions the better scholar. The story captures the unequal relationship between imperial patronage and intellectual independence; its exact historicity cannot be established.

## Early Public and Military Career

The chronology depends principally on the *Historia Augusta*, inscriptions, and modern reconstruction.

### Approximately 93–95

Hadrian began the preliminary offices of a senatorial career:

- *Decemvir stlitibus iudicandis*, one of the minor magistracies in the *vigintivirate*.
- Prefect of the Latin Festival.
- *Sevir turmae equitum Romanorum*, an honorary role in the annual parade of Roman equestrians.

### Military tribunates

He held an unusually extensive series of military tribunates:

- **Legio II Adiutrix**, stationed in Lower Moesia.
- **Legio V Macedonica**, then in the Danubian region.
- **Legio XXII Primigenia**, in Upper Germany.

In 97, after Nerva adopted Trajan, Hadrian traveled to inform or congratulate Trajan. In January 98 he hurried to bring Trajan news of Nerva’s death. The *Historia Augusta* alleges that Servianus tried to delay him by disabling his carriage and that Hadrian continued on foot. This colorful detail is unverified.

### Under Trajan

Hadrian advanced through the senatorial career:

- **Quaestor**, conventionally dated to 101. He was reportedly selected to read Trajan’s speeches to the Senate.
- He participated in Trajan’s **First Dacian War**, 101–102.
- *Ab actis senatus*, responsible for senatorial records.
- **Tribune of the plebs**, 105.
- **Praetor**, 106.
- Legionary commander of **Legio I Minervia** during the Second Dacian War, 105–106.
- Governor or imperial legate in **Lower Pannonia**, approximately 107–108.
- **Suffect consul**, 108.
- **Archon of Athens**, 112, demonstrating his established ties to the city before becoming emperor.
- Member of Trajan’s eastern expedition against Parthia.
- Governor of **Syria**, 117, placing him close to the eastern armies when Trajan became mortally ill.

Trajan awarded him military distinctions during the Dacian campaigns. Hadrian’s service brought him into contact with the army, provincial administration, and frontier politics that later defined his reign.

## Accession Crisis, 117–118

Trajan died at **Selinus in Cilicia** on 8 August 117 while returning from the east.

### Adoption

**Official version:** Trajan adopted Hadrian shortly before death. Plotina informed Hadrian of the adoption; news of Trajan’s death followed.

**Ancient allegation:** Cassius Dio reports that his father, Apronianus, had learned that Trajan’s death was concealed for several days so that the adoption could be announced first. The *Historia Augusta* preserves allegations that Plotina’s faction forged the adoption, or that someone impersonated the dying Trajan’s voice.

**Assessment:** Hadrian’s adoption is an official and legal fact in the succession settlement. Whether Trajan personally completed it while conscious remains unresolved. The timing, Plotina’s decisive support, and Hadrian’s control of the Syrian army generated suspicion. There is no primary evidence that Hadrian murdered or poisoned Trajan.

The eastern army acclaimed Hadrian emperor on **11 August 117**. He wrote to the Senate apologizing for accepting acclamation before senatorial authorization. The Senate subsequently recognized him.

Hadrian abandoned most of Trajan’s recent Mesopotamian conquests and restored a client kingdom in Armenia. He treated the Euphrates, rather than an indefinitely expanding eastern frontier, as the practical boundary. This was a strategic reversal rather than complete pacifism: Hadrian retained military readiness and employed overwhelming force when revolts threatened imperial rule.

### The four consulars

In 118, four prominent former consuls were killed:

- **Aulus Cornelius Palma Frontonianus**
- **Lucius Publilius Celsus**
- **Gaius Avidius Nigrinus**
- **Lusius Quietus**

The government alleged a conspiracy against Hadrian. Some were killed in Italy and Quietus in the east.

**Documented fact:** four senior men associated with Trajan’s regime were eliminated without a normal, publicly demonstrated trial.

**Official claim:** they had plotted against Hadrian.

**Unresolved question:** whether there was a genuine coordinated conspiracy.

**Hadrian’s position:** he denied ordering the killings and blamed the Senate or his praetorian prefect Attianus.

**Political result:** the deaths stained relations with the Senate. Hadrian removed Attianus from command and elevated him into senatorial rank, an honorable-looking means of neutralizing him. He reportedly promised not to execute senators without senatorial judgment, a promise his later treatment of Servianus and Fuscus placed in question.

## Chronological Life History as Emperor

## 117–121: Consolidation and Retrenchment

Hadrian returned west through the Danubian provinces rather than immediately entering Rome. He managed unrest involving the Roxolani and reorganized frontier arrangements.

He reached Rome in July 118. Early measures included:

- Cancellation of large tax arrears owed to the imperial treasury, publicly dramatized by burning records.
- Gifts to soldiers and distributions to civilians.
- Expansion or adjustment of alimentary support for children.
- Games and public spectacles.
- Honors for Trajan and the imperial women.
- Efforts to reassure the Senate after the four executions.

The emperor spent several years consolidating the administration. He rarely accepted repeated consulships after his third consulship in 119, but accumulated tribunician and religious authority.

Matidia, Trajan’s niece and Sabina’s mother, died in 119 and was deified. Hadrian delivered an encomium for her.

He promoted a political presentation based on stability, provincial integration, military discipline, and direct imperial inspection.

---

## 121–125: First Great Imperial Journey

Hadrian left Rome in 121. His movements are reconstructed from literary references, inscriptions, coinage, and civic building programs; exact itineraries remain disputed.

### Gaul and Germany

He traveled through Gaul and the Rhine frontier. He inspected troops, reduced luxury, enforced training, and reportedly shared ordinary soldiers’ conditions.

Ancient accounts emphasize:

- Walking or riding with troops.
- Eating military rations.
- Inspecting weapons and fortifications.
- Removing ornamental buildings that impaired discipline.
- Requiring officers and soldiers to maintain physical fitness.

These descriptions are partly imperial exempla, but African inscriptions preserving his detailed addresses to troops confirm close attention to maneuvers and unit performance.

### Britain, 122

Hadrian visited Britain in 122. The best-known result was the frontier subsequently called **Hadrian’s Wall**, extending approximately 73 Roman miles across northern Britain.

Its purposes included:

- Regulating movement.
- Enabling surveillance and customs control.
- Marking the operational limit of directly occupied territory.
- Supporting troops and communications.
- Separating the province from unconquered communities to the north.

It was not simply an impermeable defensive barrier. Gates, milecastles, forts, and roads made it a controlled military zone.

### Hispania

Hadrian wintered at **Tarraco** in Hispania. According to the *Historia Augusta*, a slave attacked him with a sword; Hadrian restrained the man and, after determining that he was mentally ill, handed him over for treatment rather than punishment. The episode cannot be independently confirmed.

He maintained ties with Italica and sponsored major construction there, including the enlarged urban quarter conventionally called the *nova urbs*. Whether this proves birth at Italica is disputed.

### Mauretania and Africa

He crossed into Mauretania to address disturbances and then proceeded eastward. His African frontier policy included roads, forts, and controlled barriers, though no single African structure corresponds exactly to the British wall.

### The East

Hadrian moved through:

- Asia Minor
- Bithynia
- Pontus
- Cappadocia
- Syria
- Antioch
- Palmyra and neighboring regions, depending on itinerary reconstruction

He met the Parthian king **Osroes I**, returning a daughter captured by Trajan and negotiating over a disputed royal throne. He avoided renewed large-scale war.

### Bithynia and Antinous

Antinous came from **Bithynium-Claudiopolis**, modern Bolu. No contemporary narrative records when he entered Hadrian’s household. A meeting during Hadrian’s Bithynian visit around 123–124 is a plausible modern reconstruction, not a documented date.

Antinous was probably born around 110–112. His legal status—freeborn youth, household dependent, page, or slave—is not securely known.

### Greece and Athens, 124–125

Hadrian’s association with Athens was central to his imperial identity. He:

- Received civic honors.
- Participated in Greek religious life.
- Was initiated at the **Eleusinian Mysteries**, probably first in 124 and possibly into the higher grade during the later journey.
- Continued or planned completion of the Temple of Olympian Zeus.
- Supported libraries, aqueducts, temples, gymnasia, and festivals.
- Promoted Athens as the center of a new association of Greek cities.

He returned to Rome in 125.

## 125–128: Italy, Administration, and the Villa at Tibur

Hadrian spent much of this interval in Italy.

### Villa Adriana

His enormous residential complex at **Tibur**, modern Tivoli, was developed throughout the reign. It combined:

- Palatial residential quarters.
- Administrative and ceremonial spaces.
- Baths.
- Gardens and pools.
- Libraries.
- Theaters.
- Artificial landscapes.
- Architectural references to regions and monuments encountered during travel.

Ancient tradition says the villa reproduced famous places Hadrian had visited, including the Lyceum, Academy, Canopus, and Vale of Tempe. Modern archaeology cautions against treating every building name as a literal miniature of a foreign original.

The complex was an imperial center of government as well as a retreat. Its innovative domes, curves, concrete structures, and manipulation of water and space reflect Hadrianic architectural experimentation, though Hadrian’s precise role as designer cannot be separated from that of professional architects.

### Legal and administrative reform

Hadrian professionalized imperial government:

- The imperial council became a more regular body of salaried advisers.
- Equestrians increasingly replaced freedmen in senior secretarial and administrative offices.
- Jurists, including **Publius Salvius Julianus**, received prominent roles.
- The praetorian edict was consolidated in the form conventionally called the **Edictum Perpetuum**. Later tradition attributes the revision to Julianus acting under Hadrian, although the exact form and scope are uncertain because evidence is late and sparse.
- Financial advocates defended the imperial treasury.
- The state assumed more responsibility for the imperial postal system.
- Judicial districts and officials were introduced in Italy, provoking senatorial resentment because Italy had traditionally been distinguished from the provinces.

### Slavery and social hierarchy

Hadrianic legislation reportedly:

- Restricted masters’ power to kill slaves without judicial proceedings.
- Prohibited selling slaves to pimps or gladiatorial trainers contrary to conditions of sale.
- Modified collective punishment of an entire slave household after a master’s murder.
- Penalized extreme mistreatment.

These measures limited particular abuses but did not question slavery itself. Hadrian’s court, estates, and governmental system continued to depend on enslaved and freed labor.

The period also contributed to sharper legal distinctions between socially privileged **honestiores** and lower-status **humiliores**, whose punishments and protections increasingly differed.

### Wealth and property

No reliable figure survives for Hadrian’s personal wealth. As emperor, he controlled:

- The imperial fiscus.
- Vast imperial estates.
- Palaces and villas.
- Revenue from provinces, mines, customs, taxation, and confiscations.
- Patronage budgets used for urban construction, military pay, civic gifts, alimentary schemes, and disaster relief.

The distinction between imperial office, dynastic property, and personal possession was not equivalent to a modern public/private division.

## 128–132: Second Great Journey

### Africa, 128

Hadrian inspected the African army at **Lambaesis**. Surviving inscriptions record remarkably detailed speeches praising individual units’ exercises and officers. These are among the strongest contemporary witnesses to his military activity.

He accepted the title **pater patriae** around this period after earlier refusals; a military diploma dated to 128 supplies important epigraphic evidence for the title’s adoption.

### Athens and the Panhellenion

Hadrian returned to Athens, where he promoted the **Panhellenion**, a league of Greek cities centered on shared Hellenic identity and imperial loyalty.

Membership required cities to present claims of Greek ancestry. The institution combined:

- Religious festivals.
- Elite competition.
- Civic diplomacy.
- Imperial patronage.
- A politically managed definition of Hellenism.

Hadrian completed or dedicated the vast **Temple of Olympian Zeus** in 131/132, centuries after construction began. He was honored as **Olympios** and associated with Zeus in civic cult without simply being identified as the god.

### Asia Minor and Syria

The court traveled through Asia Minor, Cilicia, Syria, Phoenicia, Arabia, and Judaea. Hadrian founded, renamed, or favored numerous cities; the proliferation of places called **Hadrianopolis**, **Hadriane**, or related names complicates exact attribution.

### Jerusalem and Aelia Capitolina

Hadrian decided to establish the Roman colony **Aelia Capitolina** on or near the ruins of Jerusalem. “Aelia” derived from his nomen; “Capitolina” invoked Jupiter Capitolinus.

**Documented fact:** the colony and its pagan civic identity were central to the conflict that followed.

**Disputed chronology:** Cassius Dio connects the decision with the outbreak of revolt; Eusebius presents the transformation as an aftermath. Coins and archaeological evidence support substantial Hadrianic planning and possibly foundation activity before the war ended, but the exact sequence remains debated.

### Egypt, 130

Hadrian’s party visited:

- Pelusium.
- Alexandria.
- The tomb or memorial traditions of Pompey.
- The tomb of Alexander the Great.
- The Nile Valley.
- Thebes and the Colossi of Memnon.

The court included Sabina, Antinous, and intellectual and administrative companions.

Hadrian’s Alexandrian relations were difficult. Later traditions portray mutual mockery between the emperor and the city’s population. A letter attributed to Hadrian describing Egyptians as volatile, mercenary, and religiously confused survives in the *Historia Augusta*. Its present form is generally treated as a late literary pastiche rather than a secure verbatim Hadrianic document.

### The lion hunt

The court poet **Pancrates** celebrated a lion hunt in which Hadrian and Antinous participated. The surviving poetic tradition says Hadrian saved Antinous from the lion. While an imperial hunt is plausible and connected with contemporary court poetry, later romantic elaboration cannot be treated as documentary fact.

### Death of Antinous

Antinous drowned in the Nile in **October 130**, traditionally on 24 October, near the place where Hadrian founded **Antinoöpolis**.

Competing accounts:

1. **Accidental drowning — documented official account:** Hadrian’s own reported version was that Antinous fell into the Nile.
2. **Voluntary ritual sacrifice — ancient allegation or theory:** Cassius Dio reports a belief that Antinous sacrificed himself because divination demanded a voluntary life to prolong Hadrian’s.
3. **Suicide — later theory:** sometimes linked to Antinous aging out of the socially approved role of an *eromenos*, emotional distress, or court pressures. No direct evidence survives.
4. **Murder — rumor:** proposed intermittently in modern popular accounts, sometimes involving Sabina or political enemies. No ancient evidence establishes murder.
5. **Imperially arranged sacrifice — hostile theory:** Hadrian’s occult interests have prompted speculation that he caused the death. No surviving document or contemporary testimony proves this.

The death’s circumstances are unresolved. Drowning is certain; intention is not.

Hadrian’s response was extraordinary:

- Foundation of **Antinoöpolis**.
- Creation or encouragement of Antinous’s cult.
- Statues and portraits across the empire.
- Identification of Antinous with Osiris in Egypt and with Dionysus, Hermes, Apollo, and other gods elsewhere.
- Games and priesthoods.
- Association with a newly identified star or constellation.
- Coins issued by eastern cities.

Egyptian belief held that those drowned in the Nile could become assimilated to Osiris, providing a local religious framework. Hadrian’s patronage transformed the commemoration into an empire-wide phenomenon.

The Senate apparently did not confer standard Roman imperial apotheosis on Antinous. His divinity developed through Egyptian custom, civic cults, and Hadrianic patronage.

## 132–136: Bar Kokhba Revolt

A massive rebellion erupted in Judaea under **Simon ben Kosiba**, remembered as Bar Kokhba.

### Causes

No surviving continuous contemporary history explains the revolt. Modern reconstructions consider several accumulated causes:

- Roman occupation and military presence.
- Economic and landholding pressures.
- Memories of the revolts of 66–73 and 115–117.
- Messianic and national expectations.
- Foundation of Aelia Capitolina.
- A possible ban on circumcision.
- Wider conflict over Roman cultural and religious transformation.

**Cassius Dio’s version:** the construction of Aelia Capitolina and a temple of Jupiter on the site associated with the Jewish Temple provoked the revolt.

**Historia Augusta version:** Jews rebelled because Hadrian prohibited genital mutilation, conventionally interpreted as circumcision.

**Scholarly dispute:** Hadrianic legislation may originally have targeted castration, especially forced castration of slaves, rather than Jewish circumcision specifically. Antoninus Pius later allowed Jews to circumcise their own sons while prohibiting the circumcision of outsiders. Whether Hadrian’s restriction preceded the revolt, helped cause it, or formed part of post-revolt repression cannot be conclusively established.

### Course

The rebels:

- Captured territory in Judaea.
- Established an administration.
- Issued coins overstruck with Jewish symbols and slogans.
- Maintained contracts and military communications documented in desert papyri.
- Used caves, tunnels, and fortified positions.
- Recognized Simon ben Kosiba as **nasi**, “prince,” of Israel.

Rome brought major forces under **Sextus Julius Severus**, recalled from Britain. Other commanders and detachments were drawn from multiple provinces. Hadrian’s personal presence in the theater of war is uncertain; evidence places him in the broader region earlier, while he appears to have been in Rome during at least the final phase.

Roman forces avoided a single decisive battle and reduced fortified positions systematically. **Betar** fell in 135 or possibly 136, and Bar Kokhba was killed.

### Casualties and destruction

Cassius Dio reports:

- Fifty major strongholds captured.
- 985 villages destroyed.
- 580,000 Jewish combatants killed.
- Uncounted deaths from famine, disease, and fire.
- Severe Roman losses.

Ancient numerical totals are often rhetorical and cannot be independently verified. Nevertheless, archaeological destruction, refuge-cave evidence, settlement contraction, enslavement, and the scale of Roman deployment confirm catastrophic devastation in core areas of Judaea. Recent archaeological scholarship has argued that Dio’s figures should not be dismissed automatically, though their exact accuracy remains unknowable.

### Aftermath

Measures associated with the aftermath included:

- Consolidation of Aelia Capitolina as a Roman colony.
- Exclusion of Jews from Jerusalem, with later traditions allowing entry only on specified occasions.
- Religious repression remembered in rabbinic accounts, including bans on teaching Torah, ordination, circumcision, or public observance. The exact content and duration of individual measures are difficult to establish.
- Execution of Jewish religious leaders in martyr traditions.
- Enslavement and displacement.
- Strengthening of the Roman military presence.
- Renaming or administrative reorganization of Judaea as **Syria Palaestina**, though the precise purpose and chronology are debated.

### “Genocide” and ethnic erasure

Some modern scholars characterize the destruction of Jewish settlement in central Judaea as genocidal; others avoid applying the modern legal category to ancient imperial warfare.

What is documented is mass killing, enslavement, destruction of settlements, religious repression, demographic displacement, and deliberate monumental Romanization of Jerusalem. Evidence does not establish a general empire-wide policy to kill every Jew. Jewish communities continued in Galilee, the diaspora, and elsewhere.

The popular claim that Hadrian renamed Judaea solely to erase the Jewish people from history is plausible as an interpretation of the punitive reorganization but is not stated in a surviving Hadrianic decree. “Palestine” and related geographical names predated Hadrian.

## 136–138: Succession Crisis, Illness, and Death

### First designated heir: Lucius Aelius Caesar

In 136 Hadrian adopted **Lucius Ceionius Commodus**, renamed **Lucius Aelius Caesar**.

The choice surprised contemporaries. Aelius lacked exceptional military experience and was in poor health. Ancient sources alleged that Hadrian was sexually attached to him or that Aelius was an illegitimate son; neither claim is proven.

Hadrian gave him tribunician authority and sent him to govern the Pannonian provinces. Aelius died on **1 January 138**, reportedly after hemorrhaging.

### Servianus and Fuscus

Hadrian’s elderly brother-in-law **Servianus**, approximately ninety, and Servianus’s grandson **Pedanius Fuscus** were forced to die or executed in 136.

**Official or implied charge:** Fuscus entertained ambitions for the throne, possibly with Servianus’s support.

**Documented fact:** both were eliminated during succession planning.

**Ancient hostile tradition:** Servianus cursed Hadrian before death, praying that the emperor would long for death but be unable to die.

**Assessment:** the proceedings appear politically motivated, but surviving evidence cannot determine whether Fuscus actively conspired. The deaths intensified senatorial hostility.

### Final adoption settlement

After Aelius’s death, Hadrian adopted **Titus Aurelius Fulvus Boionius Arrius Antoninus**, the future Antoninus Pius.

Hadrian required Antoninus in turn to adopt:

- **Marcus Annius Verus**, the future Marcus Aurelius.
- **Lucius Ceionius Commodus**, son of Aelius and future emperor Lucius Verus.

This constructed the succession for two generations.

### Illness

Hadrian developed a severe chronic illness generally interpreted as **congestive heart failure with dropsy/edema**, possibly involving hypertension and arteriosclerotic disease. Ancient descriptions mention swelling, bleeding, difficulty breathing, and progressive incapacity.

No retrospective diagnosis is certain.

### Suicide attempts and assisted death requests

Cassius Dio and the *Historia Augusta* report that Hadrian repeatedly sought death:

- He asked a servant or huntsman, Mastor, to kill him.
- He requested poison from a physician, who allegedly killed himself rather than comply.
- He attempted or considered stabbing himself.
- Antoninus restrained access to weapons and watched over him.

The precise episodes may be dramatized, but multiple traditions agree that prolonged illness produced a crisis in which Hadrian wanted to die.

Hadrian reportedly stopped following his prescribed diet, moved to Baiae, and died on **10 July 138**.

The famous poem beginning *Animula vagula blandula* is presented by the *Historia Augusta* as his address to his departing soul:

> *Animula vagula blandula,*  
> *hospes comesque corporis…*

The wording may be authentic, but its attribution depends on the late biography. The often repeated story that it was inscribed on Hadrian’s tomb is unsupported.

## Death, Burial, and Deification

Hadrian died at Baiae. His body was initially buried near Puteoli because the Mausoleum of Hadrian was unfinished or unavailable. His remains were later transferred to the mausoleum in Rome, now the core of **Castel Sant’Angelo**.

The Senate initially resisted granting him divine honors. Hostility arose from:

- The executions of senators.
- His reduction of senatorial authority.
- Administrative centralization.
- His severe conduct during his final illness and succession crisis.

Antoninus threatened, according to tradition, to refuse the imperial position unless Hadrian was deified. The Senate consented. Antoninus’s advocacy contributed to the honorific **Pius**, though ancient sources offer several explanations for that title.

A temple of the deified Hadrian was built in Rome. Its surviving columns form part of the modern Piazza di Pietra complex.

## Government, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

## Imperial administration

Hadrian’s government emphasized direct monarchical administration:

- Professional imperial secretariats.
- Greater employment of equestrians.
- Permanent legal advisers.
- Regular inspection of provincial governors.
- Direct receipt of petitions.
- Greater uniformity between Italy and the provinces.
- Increased imperial involvement in municipal finances.

This did not create a modern civil service, but it further reduced the practical independence of Republican magistracies and senatorial administration.

## Military system and frontiers

Major policies included:

- Withdrawal from most of Trajan’s Mesopotamian annexations.
- Reinforcement of the Rhine and Danube systems.
- Hadrian’s Wall in Britain.
- Frontier barriers and roads in Germany and North Africa.
- Regular troop exercises.
- Standardized discipline and reduced luxury.
- Continued recruitment of non-Roman and irregular auxiliary formations.

Hadrian did not abolish offensive warfare. His strategy combined negotiated boundaries with punitive campaigns and readiness to concentrate large forces, most notably in Judaea.

## Major architectural and urban projects

Projects attributed wholly or partly to Hadrian include:

### Rome

- Rebuilding of the **Pantheon**, while retaining Agrippa’s inscription. Debate continues over the design’s precise chronology and Hadrian’s personal architectural input.
- **Temple of Venus and Roma**, one of Rome’s largest temples.
- **Mausoleum of Hadrian**.
- Restoration of the Basilica of Neptune.
- Bridges, baths, aqueduct work, roads, and shrines.
- Completion or restoration of projects associated with Trajan.

### Tivoli

- **Villa Adriana**, the most extensive surviving Hadrianic palatial complex.

### Athens

- Completion of the Temple of Olympian Zeus.
- Library of Hadrian.
- Aqueduct.
- Pantheon and other sanctuaries.
- Arch of Hadrian, erected by Athenians or through civic-imperial cooperation.
- Panhellenion and associated sanctuary.

### Egypt

- **Antinoöpolis**, founded after Antinous’s death.

### Jerusalem

- **Aelia Capitolina**, a Roman colony whose construction and religious program became inseparable from the Bar Kokhba conflict.

### Italica

- Major expansion including monumental residences, baths, streets, and the Traianeum.

## Architecture controversy: Apollodorus of Damascus

Cassius Dio says the architect **Apollodorus of Damascus** mocked Hadrian’s architectural pretensions, telling him to “go draw pumpkins,” apparently referring to dome designs. After Hadrian became emperor, he allegedly exiled and then executed Apollodorus after the architect criticized the Temple of Venus and Roma.

**Status:** ancient allegation in Cassius Dio, written a century later.

**Evidence against certainty:** Apollodorus’s final years are otherwise poorly documented; the anecdote follows a recognizable pattern about a tyrannical ruler punishing frank speech. It may preserve a real professional conflict, but execution by Hadrian is not independently established.

## Coinage and economic messaging

Hadrianic coinage advertised:

- Military discipline.
- Provincial personifications.
- Imperial journeys.
- *Restitutor*—“restorer”—titles for provinces.
- Concord, justice, liberality, and security.
- Antinous, mainly through eastern civic issues rather than Rome’s standard imperial mint.

The “travel series” coins are a major source for imperial self-presentation, though they do not independently prove the date of every journey.

## Political, Religious, Philosophical, and Artistic Development

## Political outlook

Hadrian retained the autocratic substance of the principate while reducing its dependence on expansionist military prestige. His political program stressed:

- The emperor’s personal accessibility.
- Provincial civic participation.
- Defensible frontiers.
- Administrative expertise.
- Urban benefaction.
- Imperial adjudication.
- Cultural integration through Greek and Roman traditions.

He could be conciliatory toward cities and soldiers while ruthless toward perceived dynastic threats and rebellion.

## Greek identity and the Panhellenion

Hadrian was not simply a Roman ruler who privately enjoyed Greece. He used Hellenism as imperial policy:

- Athens became a symbolic cultural capital.
- Greek cities competed for recognized ancestry.
- Festivals and priesthoods tied local elites to the emperor.
- Greek architecture and religious titles reinforced imperial legitimacy.

His “Panhellenic” policy privileged civic elites able to articulate acceptable Greek pedigrees; it was not an egalitarian cultural union.

## Roman religion

Hadrian served as *pontifex maximus* and participated in traditional Roman cults. He accepted provincial religious honors and was associated with Zeus Olympios and other divinities within conventions of ruler cult.

He showed particular interest in:

- Eleusinian initiation.
- Egyptian religion.
- Oracles.
- Astrology and divination.
- Local cults.
- The creation or sponsorship of Antinous’s cult.

## Christians

A rescript addressed to **Gaius Minicius Fundanus**, proconsul of Asia, is preserved by Justin Martyr and Eusebius. It requires formal accusations and proof rather than mob denunciation and threatens punishment for malicious accusers.

**Status:** probably based on a genuine Hadrianic rescript, though textual transmission and interpretation are debated.

It was not a declaration of religious freedom. Christians could still be prosecuted when formally accused under existing procedures.

Later Christian writers associated Hadrian with apologetic petitions by **Quadratus** and **Aristides**, but exact dates and direct effects are uncertain.

A different “Hadrianic letter” claiming that Egyptian worshipers of Serapis were also Christians appears in the *Historia Augusta*. Its present wording and context are not reliable evidence for Hadrian’s understanding of Christianity.

## Judaism and Samaritan relations

Hadrian’s early policy toward Jewish communities may initially have been conventional or conciliatory. Later Jewish tradition sometimes says he considered permitting restoration of the Jerusalem Temple before reversing himself; this narrative is not securely contemporary.

After the Bar Kokhba revolt, his name became associated with severe religious persecution and martyrdom. Some measures may be historical, but rabbinic narratives often compress several Roman officials and episodes into a theological memory of “Hadrianic persecution.”

## Artistic culture

Hadrian’s reign produced a distinct court style:

- Bearded imperial portraiture.
- Classicizing Greek sculpture.
- Idealized Antinous portraits.
- Experimental concrete architecture.
- Egyptianizing imagery.
- Provincial personifications.
- An extensive circulation of imperial portrait types.

His personal participation in painting, sculpture, music, or architectural design is repeatedly asserted by literary sources but cannot be quantified.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Trajan

Guardian, kinsman, commander, emperor, and official adoptive father. Hadrian owed his career to Trajan’s regime but repudiated its newest eastern conquests. Whether Trajan voluntarily chose him at the last moment is unresolved.

### Pompeia Plotina

Trajan’s wife and Hadrian’s decisive patron. Ancient sources credit her with promoting his marriage and succession. Allegations that she forged the adoption remain unproved.

### Publius Acilius Attianus

Guardian and praetorian prefect. He helped secure Hadrian’s rule and was implicated in the four consular executions. Hadrian subsequently removed him from operational power.

### Vibia Sabina

Wife and empress. The marriage anchored Hadrian in Trajan’s dynasty. It was childless. Hostile accounts of mutual hatred and Sabina’s reproductive choices are unverified.

### Antinous

Companion and probable sexual partner. Ancient sources characterize him as Hadrian’s beloved; the relationship fits Greek pederastic language, but Antinous’s legal status, age at first contact, degree of agency, and private experience are unknown.

Modern labels such as “gay couple,” “bisexual emperor,” “boyfriend,” and “sexual-abuse victim” illuminate some dimensions but can also impose modern categories. The power imbalance was extreme: Hadrian was emperor and roughly thirty-four years older.

### Arrian

Greek intellectual, historian, military commander, and provincial governor. Hadrian advanced him through imperial office; Arrian’s *Ektaxis kata Alanon* reflects his command in Cappadocia during the reign.

### Favorinus

Philosopher and sophist within Hadrian’s intellectual orbit. Anecdotes portray a mixture of patronage, rivalry, and cautious submission.

### Herodes Atticus

Wealthy Athenian benefactor who rose under Hadrian and the Antonines. His family participated in the networks surrounding the Panhellenion and Athenian monumental culture.

### Polemon of Laodicea

Prestigious sophist patronized by Hadrian. He participated in the ceremonial dedication of the Olympieion at Athens.

### Salvius Julianus

Jurist associated with compilation or stabilization of the praetorian edict and the professionalization of imperial legal counsel.

### Sextus Julius Severus

General entrusted with suppressing the Bar Kokhba revolt.

### Lucius Aelius Caesar

First adopted heir, who died before Hadrian.

### Antoninus Pius

Final adopted son and successor. He cared for or controlled the gravely ill Hadrian, implemented the dynastic adoption settlement, and secured Hadrian’s deification.

### Servianus and Pedanius Fuscus

Brother-in-law and grandnephew. Once members of the dynastic circle, they became perceived succession threats and were killed in 136.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, and Abuses

No modern lawsuits, corporate fraud proceedings, patents, or financial investigations apply to a second-century emperor. The relevant controversies concern succession, political killings, sexual power, imperial violence, religious repression, and ancient allegations.

### Questionable succession

- **Documented:** Hadrian was officially adopted and acclaimed.
- **Ancient allegation:** Plotina arranged or forged the adoption after Trajan’s death.
- **Unresolved:** Trajan’s conscious personal authorization.
- **No evidence:** that Hadrian murdered Trajan.

### Executions of the four consulars

- **Documented:** four senior Trajanic senators were killed in 118.
- **Official allegation:** conspiracy.
- **Unresolved:** the existence and scope of the plot.
- **Political consequence:** lasting senatorial mistrust.

### Servianus and Fuscus

- **Documented:** killed or forced to suicide in 136.
- **Official implication:** succession conspiracy.
- **Unresolved:** whether Fuscus did more than appear to be a plausible dynastic candidate.

### Bar Kokhba repression

- **Documented:** overwhelming military suppression, settlement destruction, enslavement, displacement, and restrictive reorganization.
- **Reported numbers:** Dio’s 580,000 Jewish war dead and 985 destroyed villages.
- **Scholarly characterization:** genocide is argued by some modern historians but is not a neutral or universally accepted classification.
- **Unresolved:** exact mortality, the full legal program, and Hadrian’s personal direction of individual atrocities.

### Circumcision prohibition

- **Late source claim:** the *Historia Augusta* says it caused the revolt.
- **Competing evidence:** Roman legislation also targeted castration and involuntary genital mutilation.
- **Unresolved:** whether a specifically anti-Jewish ban preceded the revolt.

### Sexual relationships and coercion

The *Historia Augusta* accuses Hadrian of pursuing married women and young men. Antinous’s relationship with him is historically accepted as intimate, probably erotic.

Because Hadrian possessed absolute power and Antinous was young and of uncertain status, meaningful equality cannot be assumed. Conversely, no surviving testimony records Antinous alleging coercion. Descriptions of the relationship as consensual romance or documented sexual assault both go beyond the surviving evidence.

### Apollodorus’s alleged execution

Preserved by Cassius Dio but uncorroborated. It may record a genuine clash or function as a tyranny anecdote.

### Jealousy toward intellectuals

Ancient biographies portray Hadrian as intensely competitive, humiliating scholars and punishing criticism. His patronage of intellectuals is well documented; individual stories of spite must be evaluated separately.

### Animal spectacles

Hadrian sponsored hunting and arena spectacles involving the killing of large numbers of animals. The *Historia Augusta* reports birthday games with one hundred lions and one hundred lionesses. This is a literary claim consistent with imperial spectacle culture but not independently confirmed in all details.

### “Damnatio memoriae”

The Senate’s reluctance to deify Hadrian is documented in literary tradition. Claims that it formally voted a complete *damnatio memoriae* are less secure. His monuments, inscriptions, succession, and cult were preserved, and Antoninus obtained deification.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## “Hadrian was Spanish”

The story describes Hadrian as an emperor born at Italica and therefore “Spanish.”

- **Ancient basis:** Dio’s epitome gives Italica; Hadrian’s family unquestionably came from Italica.
- **Counterevidence:** the *Historia Augusta* says Rome, and major modern reconstructions favor Rome.
- **Reputational effect:** Spain and especially Andalusia have incorporated Hadrian into regional heritage alongside Trajan.
- **Status:** ancestry and childhood links are documented; birthplace remains disputed.

## Plotina used an impersonator

The story says Trajan was already dead and someone imitated his failing voice to declare Hadrian adopted.

- **Appearance:** preserved in the *Historia Augusta* and related succession rumor.
- **Circulators:** hostile senatorial or court traditions.
- **Evidence for:** suspicious chronology and Plotina’s clear political interest.
- **Evidence against:** no contemporary document proves impersonation; imperial-woman intrigue is a recurring literary trope.
- **Status:** rumor.

## The “pumpkins” insult

Apollodorus supposedly told Hadrian to stop interfering with real architects and “go draw pumpkins.”

- **Appearance:** Cassius Dio.
- **Meaning:** probably mockery of Hadrian’s domed architectural forms.
- **Outcome in the story:** exile and execution.
- **Status:** memorable but uncorroborated hostile anecdote.

## Antinous sacrificed himself for Hadrian

The story says diviners demanded a voluntary death to prolong Hadrian’s life and Antinous entered the Nile.

- **Appearance:** Cassius Dio reports the belief.
- **Context:** Hadrian’s illness, astrology, Egyptian religious associations, and beliefs about substitutionary life.
- **Evidence for:** ancient circulation close enough to be known to Dio; unusual cultic response.
- **Evidence against:** no eyewitness narrative, ritual text, or physical evidence; an accidental drowning required no supernatural explanation.
- **Effect:** transformed Antinous into a model of sacrificial devotion in ancient cult and later literature.
- **Status:** ancient theory or religious legend.

## Sabina killed Antinous

Later stories make the empress jealous and responsible for Antinous’s death.

- **Evidence:** none in surviving ancient accounts.
- **Function:** imports a romantic triangle into a poorly documented court relationship.
- **Status:** unsupported rumor.

## Hadrian murdered Antinous

Some hostile and modern sensational accounts imply that the emperor arranged a magical sacrifice.

- **Evidence:** none establishing Hadrian’s agency.
- **Status:** conspiracy theory.

## Antinous became a new god by imperial command alone

- **Truth within the story:** Hadrian’s patronage was indispensable.
- **Complication:** Egyptian religious custom, Greek civic initiative, local priesthoods, games, and decentralized cult formation also mattered.
- **Status:** oversimplification of a documented cult.

## Hadrian rebuilt only to create peace

Later “good emperor” narratives emphasize walls, law, travel, and prosperity.

- **Evidence for:** strategic retrenchment, long periods without expansionist war, administrative reform, urban patronage.
- **Counterevidence:** political executions and catastrophic repression in Judaea.
- **Status:** selective political mythology.

## Hadrian as persecutor in Jewish memory

Rabbinic tradition associates “Hadrian the wicked” with decrees, martyrdoms, Torah persecution, and the destruction after Bar Kokhba.

- **Historical core:** war, devastation, enslavement, exclusion from Jerusalem, and religious restrictions.
- **Transmission issue:** many narratives were edited generations later and may telescope different officials, dates, and punishments.
- **Effect:** Hadrian became a durable symbol of imperial persecution.
- **Status:** mixture of documented historical memory, hagiography, and later narrative elaboration.

## Hadrian as proto-Christian tolerationist

Christian tradition sometimes presents the Fundanus rescript as protection for Christians.

- **Historical core:** insistence on formal accusation and proof.
- **Limitation:** Christianity was not legalized as a protected religion; prosecution remained possible.
- **Status:** later apologetic amplification of a probably genuine procedural ruling.

## The deathbed poem as tomb inscription

The poem *Animula vagula blandula* is frequently said to have been engraved above Hadrian’s tomb.

- **Evidence:** the poem is attributed to him by the *Historia Augusta*.
- **Against:** no dependable ancient evidence places it on the tomb.
- **Status:** modern legend attached to a possibly genuine poem.

## “The Five Good Emperors”

Hadrian became the third member of the modern canonical sequence Nerva–Trajan–Hadrian–Antoninus Pius–Marcus Aurelius.

- **Origin and development:** shaped especially by early-modern and Enlightenment historiography, including Machiavelli and Edward Gibbon.
- **Effect:** established a default favorable reputation centered on adoptive succession and imperial stability.
- **Problem:** Hadrian’s adoption was disputed; his rule included dynastic executions and the Bar Kokhba catastrophe.
- **Status:** influential historiographical schema, not an ancient official category.

## Works by or Attributed to Hadrian

Most of Hadrian’s writings are lost.

### Genuine or probably genuine surviving material

1. **“Animula vagula blandula”**  
   Latin poem, conventionally dated to 138. Preserved in the *Historia Augusta*. Probably authentic but not contemporary in manuscript transmission.

2. **Reply to Florus, “Ego nolo Florus esse”**  
   Short Latin comic poem answering Florus’s *Ego nolo Caesar esse*. Preserved in the *Historia Augusta*. Attribution plausible but dependent on that source.

3. **Greek epigrams**  
   Several poems in the *Greek Anthology* have been attributed to Hadrian. Individual attributions require textual scrutiny; not all are equally secure.

4. **Imperial speeches and addresses preserved epigraphically**  
   Particularly the addresses to troops at Lambaesis in 128. These are official texts and may reflect secretarial composition as well as Hadrian’s words.

5. **Rescripts, letters, and legal decisions**  
   Hadrian issued a large body of imperial correspondence. Much survives only as excerpts or citations in juristic, epigraphic, papyrological, or Christian collections. Authorship is institutional: emperors approved documents often drafted by secretaries and jurists.

6. **Rescript to Gaius Minicius Fundanus**  
   Traditionally dated approximately 124–125, preserved by Justin Martyr and Eusebius. Probably genuine in substance; textual form debated.

7. **Fragmentary letter to Antoninus Pius**  
   A papyrus school exercise preserves a text interpreted as part of Hadrian’s autobiographical or final correspondence. Attribution and relationship to the lost autobiography remain debated.

### Lost works reported by ancient sources

1. **Autobiography or “books of his life”**  
   Original title unknown; composed before his death. The *Historia Augusta* says Hadrian gave the work to learned freedmen to publish under their names. It may underlie parts of the surviving biography.

2. **Works published under Phlegon’s name**  
   The *Historia Augusta* says books attributed to **Phlegon of Tralles** were actually Hadrian’s. This is an ancient attribution, not an established bibliographical fact.

3. **Catacannae**  
   Obscure poems modeled on Antimachus. Lost.

4. **Greek and Latin poems**  
   Ancient sources say he wrote extensively in both languages. Most are lost.

5. **Speeches**  
   Hadrian delivered senatorial, funerary, military, civic, and ceremonial speeches, including the encomium for Matidia. Most survive only through references, fragments, or inscriptions.

6. **Technical or artistic works**  
   Literary sources say he practiced architecture, painting, and other arts, but no surviving building plan or artwork can be securely identified as personally executed by him.

### Spurious or doubtful material

1. **Letter on Egyptian religion preserved in the *Historia Augusta***  
   Commonly treated as a late pastiche with, at most, uncertain historical elements.

2. **Numerous sayings and letters in the *Historia Augusta***  
   Must be treated individually; the collection contains invented documents.

3. **Later occult, magical, or astrological texts attached to Hadrian’s name**  
   No substantial such corpus is securely Hadrianic.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Immediate Roman reputation

Hadrian left a stable succession but a hostile Senate. Antoninus Pius preserved his policies selectively and secured his deification.

His administrative system, juristic reforms, equestrian bureaucracy, and planned succession influenced the Antonine state.

### Architectural legacy

The Pantheon, Hadrian’s Wall, Villa Adriana, the Temple of Olympian Zeus, the Temple of Venus and Roma, and the Mausoleum of Hadrian remain among the most visible monuments associated with any Roman emperor.

The mausoleum became:

- A late Roman fortress.
- A medieval stronghold.
- A papal castle.
- The modern Castel Sant’Angelo.

### Legal legacy

Hadrianic jurists and the stabilized praetorian edict became part of the long development leading to Justinian’s sixth-century codification. Claims that Hadrian personally “codified Roman law” exaggerate the evidence; his reign nevertheless marks an important stage in professional juristic administration.

### Jewish memory

Hadrian became one of the most negatively remembered Roman rulers in Jewish tradition because of:

- The Bar Kokhba catastrophe.
- Destruction of Judaean communities.
- Religious repression.
- Aelia Capitolina.
- Exclusion from Jerusalem.
- Martyr traditions.

This memory was historically grounded but also developed through liturgy, rabbinic narrative, and collective commemoration.

### Christian memory

Christian writers presented a mixed image:

- Recipient of apologetic petitions.
- Author of a comparatively procedural rescript.
- Pagan ruler associated with Antinous’s cult.
- Emperor whose transformation of Jerusalem helped separate its gentile Christian community from Jewish institutions.

### Antinous and queer reception

From the Renaissance onward, Antinous portraits became central to European ideals of male beauty. Hadrian and Antinous were subsequently used to discuss:

- Greek love.
- Homosexual identity.
- Pederasty.
- Imperial power.
- Mourning.
- Sacrificial devotion.
- Censorship and coded queer history.

Nineteenth-century writers often romanticized the relationship while minimizing the age and power disparity. Later queer scholarship reclaimed it as a prominent same-sex relationship while also examining colonialism, slavery, and coercive hierarchy.

### Modern political symbolism

Hadrian’s Wall has been invoked in debates about:

- Borders.
- Immigration.
- Defensive strategy.
- Imperial overstretch.
- British and European identity.

These analogies frequently oversimplify a frontier that functioned through controlled movement and economic interaction rather than total separation.

### Historiographical reinterpretation

Hadrian’s reputation has moved among several models:

- The “good emperor.”
- The peaceful consolidator after Trajan.
- The cultured philhellene.
- The traveling administrator.
- The architect-emperor.
- The autocratic bureaucrat.
- The persecutor of Judaea.
- The mourning lover.
- The insecure ruler who destroyed rivals.

Current scholarship generally treats these not as mutually exclusive portraits but as products of different evidence and constituencies.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the supplied roster and consulted evidence are included. Mere chronological proximity has been excluded.

### Alexander the Great — documented fact

Hadrian visited **Alexandria in 130 CE** and is reported to have visited or paid respects at Alexander’s tomb. Alexander also functioned as an enduring model of mobile, Greek-oriented universal monarchy, though direct proof of Hadrian consciously imitating every aspect of Alexander is lacking.

**Nature:** visit to tomb; documented cultural and imperial reference.  
**Date/place:** 130 CE, Alexandria, Egypt.  
**Evidence label:** documented or strongly reported ancient fact.

### Homer — reported intellectual relationship

The *Historia Augusta* says Hadrian preferred Antimachus to Homer. The claim depicts Hadrian as an intellectually competitive archaizer with deliberately unconventional literary judgments.

**Nature:** literary evaluation and rejection of conventional ranking.  
**Date/place:** undated; associated with Hadrian’s intellectual life.  
**Evidence label:** reported fact from a late and problematic biography.

### Jesus of Nazareth — no direct personal connection

Hadrian lived after Jesus’s death. His rescript concerning Christian accusations and the apologetic activity of Christians during his reign affected communities identifying themselves with Jesus, but no meeting, correspondence, family relationship, or historically documented direct influence connects the two persons.

**Nature:** indirect relationship through Hadrianic Christian policy only.  
**Evidence label:** absence of direct connection.

### Publius Aelius Hadrianus

The subject is himself included in the atlas roster; this is an identity match, not an interpersonal connection.

No other roster member was found to have a securely documented personal meeting, correspondence, family tie, patronage relationship, rivalry, or explicit acknowledged influence involving Hadrian. Trajan, Plotina, Sabina, Antinous, Epictetus, Favorinus, Arrian, Salvius Julianus, Bar Kokhba, Antoninus Pius, and Marcus Aurelius are central connections but do not appear as named entries in the supplied roster.
