## Works by Pamela Colman Smith

### Folklore, fiction, poetry, and essays

- **1896:** *Two Negro Stories from Jamaica*. Two Jamaican folktales collected and retold by Smith for the *Journal of American Folk-Lore*; the historical title is retained bibliographically, although its terminology is now offensive.
- **1899:** *Annancy Stories*. A collection of Jamaican Anancy tales gathered, retold, and illustrated by Smith—simultaneously folklore, literary adaptation, performance repertory, and artist’s book.
- **1899:** *The Golden Vanity and the Green Bed*. A hand-coloured visual treatment of two traditional ballads, issued in the United States by Doubleday & McClure; it is sometimes catalogued as *Fair Vanity*, while the British issue appeared in 1903 under the fuller title. These are editions of substantially the same obra, not separate authored narratives.
- **1899:** *Widdicombe Fair*. An illustrated ballad portfolio containing thirteen mounted colour plates and the traditional words and music; Smith made its visual sequence, not the song.
- **1903–1904:** Smith’s writings in *The Green Sheaf*. Poems, tales, editorial matter, advertisements, ornaments, and the magazine’s recurring manifesto form part of her larger periodical Gesamtkunstwerk; a fully itemized contents-level bibliography is supplied by the digital edition rather than repeated here.
- **1905:** *Chim-Chim: Folk Stories from Jamaica*. A second collection of Jamaican tales written and illustrated by Smith and hand-coloured by the Green Sheaf operation; like *Annancy Stories*, it derives from oral tradition and therefore resists the fiction/nonfiction divide.
- **1907:** *A Protest Against Fear*. A short declaration in *The Craftsman* urging artistic independence. Because it seeks to change artists’ conduct rather than merely discuss it, it is properly a manifesto.
- **1908:** *Should the Art Student Think?* An illustrated essay in *The Craftsman* on observation, imagination, theatre, and resistance to mechanical commercial illustration; portions function as an artistic protocol.
- **1912:** *Susan and the Mermaid*. A fantasy story written and illustrated by Smith for the December issue of *The Delineator*; it is a magazine work, not a separately published book.
- **Undated, probably early twentieth century:** *Little Charles*. An illustrated children’s-book project rejected by publishers. Portions appeared in *A Broad Sheet* and *The Green Sheaf*, but the intended book remained unfinished; the project, rather than a completed volume, is the obra.
- **After 1914:** Unpublished later writings and illustrations. Smith reportedly continued making work after her commercial career declined, but no complete, securely titled corpus survives; these remnants resist item-by-item treatment as finished works.

### Periodicals and publishing Gesamtkunstwerke

- **1901:** Contributions to *The Kensington: A Magazine of Art, Literature and the Drama*. Smith supplied illustrations and participated in the magazine’s theatrical milieu; the seven-issue periodical was collaborative, so it is not wholly her work.
- **1901–1908:** Contributions to *The Irish Homestead* and *A Celtic Christmas*. These included illustrations and a female personification of the Celtic spirit superimposed on a map of Ireland, reused as the Christmas supplement’s cover from 1904 through 1908.
- **1902–1903:** *A Broad Sheet*. Thirteen monthly, single-sheet issues co-edited and illustrated by Smith and Jack B. Yeats, combining traditional verse, new writing, typography, and hand-coloured pictures. It is a collaborative periodical Gesamtkunstwerk rather than thirteen books by either editor.
- **1903:** *Prospectus to The Green Sheaf*. An illustrated subscription prospectus that announces and defines the projected magazine; as both advertisement and statement of purpose, it is a project-document and manifesto.
- **1903–1904:** *The Green Sheaf*. Thirteen issues edited, published, designed, and extensively illustrated by Smith, while carrying work by numerous collaborators. Its integration of literature, illustration, hand colouring, typography, commerce, and a recurring declaration of purpose makes it her clearest Gesamtkunstwerk.
- **1903–1904:** *The Green Sheaf* manifesto. Printed in the magazine’s back matter and later prominently on its covers, this text declares what the periodical will bring together; it acts institutionally and is therefore a manifesto, not merely editorial description.
- **1903–1904:** Illustrated advertisements and commercial designs for *The Green Sheaf*. Smith made advertisements for her own prints, storytelling, press, and School of Hand-Colouring and for such clients as Alice Quinn’s sweets; these are functional graphic objects whose status as autonomous artworks is doubtful.
- **1904–1906:** *Green Sheaf Press*. A publishing project operated by Smith with “Mrs. Fortescue,” probably Ethel P. F. Fryer-Fortescue, producing roughly ten hand-finished books by Smith and other writers. The press itself—a feminist commercial and aesthetic intention—is an obra, although Smith did not author every book it issued and may not personally have coloured every copy.
- **1903–1906:** *Green Sheaf School of Hand-Colouring*. A workshop and teaching enterprise providing colouring, decorative printing, and design services. It is better classified as a pedagogical and production project than as a discrete artwork.

### Illustrated books, portfolios, and printed objects

- **1898:** *The Illustrated Verses of William Butler Yeats*. Smith supplied the triptych frontispiece *The Land of Heart’s Desire*; the poems and book as a whole are Yeats’s, while the triptych is Smith’s contribution.
- **1898:** *The Land of Heart’s Desire*. A three-panel hand-coloured print based on Yeats’s play, also used as the frontispiece above; the print and its appearance in the book are manifestations of one design.
- **1899:** *In Chimney Corners: Merry Tales of Irish Folk Lore*. Seumas MacManus’s story collection with illustrations by Smith; she made its visual programme, not its prose.
- **1899:** *Sir Henry Irving and Miss Ellen Terry in Robespierre, The Merchant of Venice, The Bells, Nance Oldfield, The Amber Heart, Waterloo, etc.* A commemorative portfolio or souvenir booklet written by Bram Stoker and drawn by Smith, recording theatrical roles through hand-coloured images.
- **1899:** *Shakespeare’s Heroines*. A calendar for 1899 designed and illustrated by Smith, accompanied by a separate advertising poster. It is a dated functional object as well as a sequence of theatrical character designs.
- **1899:** *Trelawny of the “Wells”*. A set of colour prints derived from Arthur Wing Pinero’s theatrical work; it is a print portfolio rather than Smith’s play.
- **1900:** *William Gillette as Sherlock Holmes*. A theatrical souvenir with Smith’s images of Gillette in his stage role; the Holmes character and drama are not her creations.
- **1905:** Alix Egerton, *The Book of Hours*. A Green Sheaf Press book for which Smith made the frontispiece and participated as publisher/designer; Egerton authored the text.
- **1906:** Smara Khamara, *In the Valley of Stars There Is a Tower of Silence: A Persian Tragedy*. A Green Sheaf Press drama with a frontispiece by Smith; her role was visual and publishing-related, not dramatic authorship.
- **1906:** Laurence Alma-Tadema, *Tales from My Garden*. A Green Sheaf Press collection of three fairy tales illustrated and produced under Smith’s press project; bibliographies sometimes overstate Smith’s role by treating her as co-author.
- **1909:** *Rider–Waite–Smith Tarot*. Seventy-eight published tarot-card designs commissioned and conceptually directed by A. E. Waite but drawn by Smith, with fully pictorial scenes on the numbered Minor Arcana as well as the trumps. Smith’s surviving letter calls the commission “80 designs,” creating an unresolved discrepancy with the seventy-eight-card deck—possibly including the card back and title design.
- **1910:** A. E. Waite, *The Key to the Tarot*. A guide incorporating reduced reproductions of Smith’s card designs; it is a textual work by Waite and a secondary manifestation of her tarot imagery.
- **1911:** A. E. Waite, *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot*. Waite’s expanded manual illustrated with Smith’s tarot designs; the book is not authored by Smith, and its images do not constitute a second tarot obra.
- **1911:** Bram Stoker, *The Lair of the White Worm*. A novel first issued with six colour plates by Smith. Later abridgments and reprints often omit them and should not be counted as new Smith works.
- **1913:** *Blue Beard*. An anonymously retold tale issued with Smith’s illustrations; it is an illustrated book and adaptation of inherited material, not an original Smith narrative.
- **1913:** Ellen Terry, *The Russian Ballet*. Terry’s essays on the Ballets Russes with Smith’s colour images of performers, costumes, and stage action; text and pictures constitute a collaboration rather than sole authorship.
- **1914:** Eunice Fuller, *The Book of Friendly Giants*. A children’s story collection illustrated by Smith; Fuller made the stories and Smith their visual world.
- **1924:** *The Townchild’s Alphabet*. An illustrated alphabet book attributed to Smith’s visual authorship. The surviving bibliographical record is sparse, so its precise division of textual and pictorial responsibility should not be inferred.
- **1925:** Edith Lyttelton, *The Sinclair Family*. A novel illustrated by Smith and among her latest securely documented published commissions; Smith did not author the narrative.

### Drawings, watercolours, and visionary “music pictures”

- **1896–1897:** Early watercolours, Christmas cards, lampshades, sketches, and decorative objects. These constitute a documented body of student and early professional work, but many objects are lost, privately held, or untitled; treating them as a recoverable list of finished works would create false completeness.
- **1897–1899:** Shakespearean and theatrical character prints. Smith exhibited and sold hand-coloured images through New York’s Macbeth Gallery; some later entered calendars and theatrical portfolios, making it uncertain whether surviving impressions are independent works or manifestations of reused designs.
- **c. 1903:** *The Wave*. A watercolour, ink, and graphite image of anthropomorphic waves; the Whitney Museum dates it circa 1903, so a more exact year should not be forced.
- **1903:** *The Lament of the Lyceum Rat*. A headpiece for Mary Brown’s poem in *The Green Sheaf*, combining portraiture, theatrical memory, decoration, and satire.
- **1903:** *Deirdre*. A hand-coloured illustration for A.E.’s three-act drama, issued as a supplement to *The Green Sheaf* no. 7; it belongs both to the periodical and to the drama’s illustrated manifestation.
- **1904:** *The Fountain of Faithful Lovers*. An illustration published in *The Green Sheaf* no. 10; it is a periodical image, not an independently issued book.
- **1907:** *The Blue Cat*. A Symbolist watercolour associated with the period of Smith’s Stieglitz exhibitions; surviving reproductions and archival descriptions establish the title and date.
- **1907:** *Overture: “Egmont,” Beethoven*. A watercolour made in response to Beethoven’s music. It is neither an illustration of a literary scene nor a notation of the music, but a visual manifestation of Smith’s listening.
- **1907–1909:** The “music pictures” and visionary watercolours. Smith exhibited seventy-two Symbolist watercolours at Alfred Stieglitz’s Little Galleries of the Photo-Secession in 1907 and returned in 1908 and 1909; many were untitled or are now dispersed, so this body of work cannot honestly be converted into a complete title list.
- **1907:** Stieglitz portfolio after Smith’s drawings. Alfred Stieglitz made a portfolio of twenty-two platinum photographs of her works. The pictured designs are Smith’s, but the photographs are Stieglitz’s manifestations and should not be assigned to her without qualification.

### Theatre, performance, and embodied works

- **Before 1900:** Smith’s miniature theatre and toy-theatre plays. She constructed a theatre, figures, scenery, and performances during her Jamaican and New York years; titles and dates are incompletely recorded, and the ephemeral project survives more clearly than any individual production.
- **c. 1899–1900:** Designs for the Lyceum Theatre circle. Smith made costume and stage designs associated with Henry Irving, Ellen Terry, and Bram Stoker. The surviving evidence does not justify assigning every Lyceum production to her, and these collaborative designs resist treatment as autonomous authored works.
- **1902:** Designs for early Irish Literary Theatre performances. Smith supplied theatrical visual work in W. B. Yeats’s circle; documentation is fragmentary, so the body of work is safer than invented production-by-production attributions.
- **1904:** *Where There Is Nothing*. Smith and Edith Craig collaborated on scenery for a production of Yeats’s play. The stage environment was ephemeral and collaborative: Smith did not make the dramatic text.
- **1903–c. 1914:** *Gelukiezanger*. Smith’s storytelling persona and advertised performance practice for Jamaican Anancy tales. It is an embodied, repeatable creation rather than a fixed text, and its reliance on culturally marked voice and costume is central to, and contested in, modern scholarship.
- **1900s–1910s:** Storytelling performances of Jamaican folklore. Smith performed in Britain and the United States, adapting timing, voice, gesture, and story selection to each audience; individual performances generally lack stable titles and cannot be counted as editions of the printed collections.
- **1911 onward:** Designs associated with Edith Craig and the Pioneer Players. Smith contributed scenery, costumes, programmes, or related visual material to this suffrage-theatre network, but surviving attributions are incomplete and should not be silently converted into a definitive production list.

### Suffrage, wartime, and commercial graphics

- **c. 1909–1910:** *A Bird in the Hand*. A poster and postcard produced in the Suffrage Atelier context. Its graphic argument makes it visual propaganda—an image that acts politically, close to a pictorial manifesto.
- **1909–1914:** Suffrage Atelier posters, postcards, programmes, and banners. Smith is documented as a contributor, but much of the Atelier’s output was anonymous or collectively printed; attribution of every unsigned object to her would be unsafe.
- **First World War:** Bulldog-benefit poster. A commercial-charitable poster encouraging the purchase of a bulldog to raise funds for soldiers; it is applied graphic work rather than an autonomous painting.
- **Undated:** Bookplates, cards, menus, ball programmes, signs, banners, and textile designs. Smith advertised and executed these objects for clients and supplied designs to the Dun Emer Guild. Most were functional, collaborative, ephemeral, or anonymous and therefore resist conventional work-listing.
