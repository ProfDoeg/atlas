# Sabino Arana: Research Dossier

**Sabino Policarpo Arana Goiri (1865–1903)**  
**Founder of the first durable Basque nationalist party; co-designer of the ikurriña; political journalist, polemicist, amateur philologist, publisher, and mythic founder of modern Basque nationalism**

*Prepared 31 August 2026. This dossier distinguishes contemporary documentation, later recollection, historiographical interpretation, and political mythology. Dates and spellings retain historical forms when they matter.*

The common formula that Arana “invented Basque nationalism” is accurate only in a restricted sense. Basque fuerism, Carlist defense of regional law, Romantic historical legend, linguistic revival, and projects of Basque unity all preceded him. What Arana created was a new, explicitly anti-Spanish and initially separatist doctrine, an organizational network, a durable party, and a repertoire of names, press organs, symbols, rituals, and myths that converted earlier materials into modern mass politics.

The equally common formula that he invented “half the Basque vocabulary from a prison cell” is a compressed legend. He coined or promoted a very large number of neologisms, but this was the product of nearly twenty years of work in Abando, Barcelona, Bilbao, and Sukarrieta. His prison writing included poems, correspondence, political documents, and stages of the party anthem; it did not account for anything like half of Basque vocabulary.

## Family and Ancestry

Sabino was the eighth and youngest child of **Santiago José Ramón Arana Ansótegui (1823–1883)** and **Josefa Pascuala Leonarda Goiri Acha/Atxa**. The family was prosperous, intensely Catholic, and Carlist. Santiago was a shipbuilder and owner of a shipyard on the Bilbao estuary, had served as mayor of Abando, and helped finance or prepare the Carlist rising of 1872. The family house, built in the 1850s, marked comfortable local standing rather than industrial-magnate wealth.

The best-documented siblings in Sabino's political and personal life were:

- **Luis Arana Goiri (1862–1951)**, architect by training, political co-founder, collaborator in the design of the ikurriña, and the person whom Sabino credited with converting him from Carlism to a specifically Biscayan national doctrine. Luis later led factions of the PNV and defended a more rigid form of Sabinian orthodoxy.
- **Paulina Arana Goiri**, a principal family chronicler whose recollections preserve details of Sabino's schooling, imprisonment, illness, and death. Because her testimony also helped build the founder's posthumous cult, it must be used as both family evidence and memorial literature.
- Other siblings included Lucila, Francisca, and Juan Balbino; the surviving public record is much thinner for them. Online genealogical trees are inconsistent and should not substitute for parish and family archives.

The Aranas belonged to a local Catholic-Carlist bourgeois/shipbuilding milieu. Descriptions of them as *jauntxo* or petty-noble should not be read as a title of nobility. The decisive family experience was defeat in the Third Carlist War. Santiago's implication in the rising forced exile; defeat and the 1876 abolition of the remaining foral order damaged both his politics and the family's fortunes. Later nationalist biography said he died “of grief” over the fueros. That is memorial language, not a medical diagnosis. He died in 1883 after a period described in later accounts as depression or collapse.

### Marriage and household

Arana met **Nicolasa/Nikole Achica-Allende**, born in 1873 at the Abiña farmhouse in the Pedernales/Busturia area, during his recurring stays for health. She was from a poor rural family and had little formal education. Their courtship produced an extensive correspondence. Before marriage he arranged instruction for her with Carmelite nuns. His letters combine affection with explicit demands for religious formation, obedience, and wifely submission.

They married privately on **2 February 1900** in the hermitage of San Antonio de Abiña. Some party associates and members of his family disapproved of the match on class and genealogical grounds. Arana himself had promoted ancestry rules for political membership and initially investigated her surnames; this tension between doctrine and marriage became a recurrent hostile story about him. The secrecy also reflected his wish to avoid crowds curious to see him marry an *aldeana*.

The couple honeymooned at Lourdes and lived mainly in rented accommodations in Sukarrieta. In August 1900 Nicolasa miscarried; no other child is documented. Arana died after less than four years of marriage. Nicolasa later remarried in 1915, to the merchant-marine captain Eugenio Alegría Bilbao. Later nationalist memory often marginalized her, while recent works have restored her correspondence and independent biography.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1865–1882

### Abando and war exile

Arana was born into Abando while the old municipal world was being absorbed by industrial Bilbao. The juxtaposition later became central to his imagination: a Catholic, rural-foral Biscay placed against mines, factories, urban liberalism, Spanish administration, and migrant labor.

In 1871 he began at the municipal school. His father's involvement in the Carlist conspiracy drove part of the family across the border in 1873. They moved through places in the French Basque Country including Bayonne, Hendaye, Saint-Jean-de-Luz, and Guéthary/Getaria. Sources disagree on the exact sequence and on whether he attended ordinary French schools continuously. He studied at the Collège Saint-Louis-de-Gonzague in Bayonne during 1873–1874 and was also taught privately, including by an Álava Carlist officer. Later accounts say Santiago avoided Jacobin French schools; another explanation is that the family concealed the children's status as political exiles when they re-entered Spanish schooling.

The family returned after the Carlist defeat in 1876. Sabino passed an entrance examination at Hondarribia/Fuenterrabía and became a boarder at the Jesuit Colegio de Nuestra Señora de la Antigua in Orduña. The school reinforced Catholic discipline and Carlist-foralist formation. He completed the baccalaureate in 1881 with strong marks, reportedly taking final examinations while bedridden.

### Illness and conversion narrative

At sixteen he developed a grave pulmonary illness commonly described as tuberculosis. During prolonged convalescence at the family home, he read and studied. This sickbed became the first station of his later hagiography.

The canonical conversion story dates to Easter Sunday, **9 April 1882**. Sabino, then a Carlist, and Luis discussed whether loyalty to a Spanish dynastic claimant could restore Biscayan freedom. Luis argued that Carlism remained Spanish and that Biscay was a nation in its own right. Sabino later represented the exchange as a decisive awakening followed by study of Basque language, law, and history.

The conversation is supported by family and autobiographical testimony; its exact Easter dating is less secure. Historian José Luis de la Granja showed that the fully developed Easter chronology and “resurrection” symbolism were systematized for the first Aberri Eguna in 1932. Thus:

- **Documented core:** Luis decisively influenced Sabino's break with Carlism around 1882.
- **Uncertain detail:** the precise date and dramatic garden setting depend on later recollection.
- **Posthumous myth:** Easter converted the awakening into a national Passion-and-Resurrection calendar.

## Education and Formation, 1882–1892

### Barcelona years

Santiago died in 1883. Pascuala moved with several children to Barcelona. Sabino enrolled in Law and in Philosophy and Letters at the University of Barcelona, reportedly to satisfy his mother, while his own interests leaned toward natural science, linguistics, and history. Luis studied architecture. Sabino attended irregularly, undertook independent philological work, and never took a degree. Pascuala died in Barcelona in 1888; Sabino and Paulina then returned to Biscay.

His formative reading included the Basque apologetic and philological tradition, especially **Pablo Pedro Astarloa**, whose speculative etymology influenced him. He also read fuerist and historical-legendary literature. He did not simply receive a developed nationalist doctrine from a master. His project emerged by recombining Catholic integralism, Carlist anti-liberalism, foral sovereignty, Romantic nation theory, racial anthropology, linguistic purism, and the shock of industrial transformation.

### Early philology and journalism

Arana began drafting an orthographic essay around 1884. In 1886 he entered public controversy with Miguel de Unamuno over Basque origins and spelling. His first known printed article, **“¿Basco o Vasco?”**, appeared in the *Revista de Vizcaya* on 15 July 1886. *Etimologías euskéricas* followed in 1887 and *Pliegos histórico-políticos* in Barcelona in 1888.

Recent research identifies a neglected **El Euskaro phase (1888–1890)**: before the overtly separatist public campaign, Arana experimented with cultural and linguistic revival through a projected or partially produced publication. This complicates the later story of an instantaneous 1882 conversion followed by a straight line to the PNV.

In 1892 he gathered four historical-political narratives as **_Bizkaya por su independencia. Cuatro glorias patrias_**. The first edition, printed by Sebastián de Amorrortu, reportedly ran to 2,000 copies. Its episodes—the battles of Arrigorriaga/Padura (888), Gordexola and Otxandio (1355), and Mungia (1470)—served as evidence of continuous Biscayan resistance.

Modern historical work does not accept these narratives as literal reconstructions. They mixed chronicle, legend, inference, and invented continuity. Their significance lies in political mythmaking: Arana converted scattered legendary battles into a usable national epic.

## Early Career and Organized Nationalism, 1893–1895

### Larrazabal, 3 June 1893

Seventeen guests associated with the Euskalerria milieu honored Arana at a dinner in the Larrazabal farmhouse in Begoña. His after-dinner address, later known as the **Discurso or Juramento de Larrazabal**, publicly narrated his Carlist youth, conversion, historical studies, and vow to work until death for Biscayan independence. It is often treated as the public birth certificate of Aranist nationalism.

At this stage his primary political subject was **Bizkaya**, not yet a centralized seven-province Basque state. His larger solution was confederal: each historical territory could join or leave freely.

### *Bizkaitarra*

Five days later, on 8 June 1893, he issued the first *Bizkaitarra* as a broadsheet. It evolved into a periodical and produced 32 numbers through 5 September 1895, with an estimated circulation near 1,500. Arana was proprietor, director, chief writer, and polemical voice.

Its language was deliberately incendiary. It attacked liberals, Spanish nationalism, Carlists who accepted Spanish statehood, the industrial oligarchy, socialists, and immigrant workers called **maketos**. Arana later wrote that the severity was strategic: shock was needed to awaken a society that had forgotten its patria. That later explanation documents his propaganda method; it does not erase the content he chose.

### Gamazada and movement symbols

In February 1894 Sabino, Luis, and companions traveled to Castejón to support Navarrese resistance to Finance Minister Germán Gamazo's fiscal project. Their banner read, in substance, “God and Old Law—Biscay greets Navarre.” This was an early public bridge from Biscayan separatism toward solidarity among the southern Basque territories.

On **14 July 1894**, the brothers opened **Euskeldun Batzokija**, a nationalist club at 22 Calle Correo, Bilbao. Ciriaco de Iturri, the oldest member and a former Carlist officer, raised the new flag at six in the evening.

The flag now called the **ikurriña** was jointly designed by Sabino and Luis for Biscay, not initially for all Basque territories:

- red field: Biscay;
- green saltire: the oak of Gernika and the old law/foral freedom;
- white upright cross: God, placed over the law in the visual hierarchy of JEL.

Its resemblance to the Union Jack is evident and has generated claims of direct copying. The brothers' own explanation derived the design from the Biscayan arms and **Jaun-Goikua eta Lagi-Zaŕa**. British influence is plausible but no consulted primary document proves simple copying. The flag's first public function and stated symbolism are documented; the exact visual genealogy remains inferential.

The club required Catholicism and Basque ancestry. Early rules are usually described as demanding four Basque surnames; later practice became less restrictive. Its statutes, social life, ritual, and executive committee provided the template for later *batzokis*.

### Founding the PNV

On **31 July 1895**, feast of St Ignatius, the first **Bizkai Buru Batzar** was constituted. Sabino was president; the executive included Luis Arana, Fabián de Ispizua, Elías de Lekue, Juan de Aranburuzabala, Ciriaco de Llodio, and Salvador de Etxeita. This is the organizational founding date of **Euzko Alderdi Jeltzalea–Partido Nacionalista Vasco (EAJ-PNV)**.

“Jeltzale” meant adherent of **JEL**, the initials of *Jaun-Goikua eta Lagi-Zaŕa* (“God and the Old Law”). The party was small, semi-clandestine, and initially Biscay-centered. Arana's later reputation can obscure how fragile it was: a network of family, printers, clubs, correspondents, and several dozen core activists rather than a mass party.

## Imprisonment, Prosecutions, and Publishing, 1893–1898

### The first prosecution cycle

*Bizkaitarra* was denounced repeatedly—eight denunciations and seven proceedings according to archival research. Important cases included:

- No. 2 (19 August 1893) and No. 6 (28 February 1894): proceedings later covered by press amnesty or dismissed.
- No. 14 (31 August 1894), containing **“Un fino maketófilo”**: physician and former councilor Filomeno Soltura sued for insult. On 10 August 1895 Arana received one month and eleven days' arrest, a 125-peseta fine, costs, and accessory penalties.
- No. 25 (12 May 1895), with **“Ellos y nosotros”** and **“La bandera española”**: prosecution.
- Supplement no. 4, with **“Abolición y reconquista”** and **“Vengan escobas”**: prosecution; a jury later found him not guilty.
- No. 32 (5 September 1895), including **“La invasión maketa en Guipúzcoa”**: civil and military proceedings.

Arana entered Larrinaga prison on **28 August 1895**. His original sentence ended 8 October, but accumulated cases and proceedings against the club kept him confined until **13 January 1896**. Authorities closed *Bizkaitarra* and sealed Euskeldun Batzokija on 13 September 1895; its 110 members were processed and directors imprisoned.

His lawyer was **Daniel de Irujo Urra**, later father of nationalist statesman Manuel de Irujo. The defense had to confront a legal anomaly: penal provisions framed for colonial separatism had not clearly contemplated a separatist movement within peninsular Spain.

### What he did in prison

The 1895–1896 confinement helped create his martyr image. He wrote articles, letters, poems, and a draft of the text later associated with **“Gora ta Gora” / _Euzko Abendaren Ereserkia_**. A final or substantially revised version dates from his 1902 imprisonment. The tune was an older dance/salute melody; Cleto de Zabala later arranged it. Thus Arana wrote words for an existing musical basis; he did not compose the inherited melody.

Prison was not the birthplace of his basic nationalist vocabulary. Terms and linguistic projects appeared before, between, and after the imprisonments. The “prison-cell lexicon” story fuses real prison writing with his larger reputation as a word-maker.

### Publishing house and linguistic program

After release he developed **Bizkaya'ren Edestija ta Izkerea Pizkundia** (“Renaissance of Biscay's History and Language”), an editorial imprint/enterprise. Its publications included:

- *Lecciones de ortografía del euskera bizkaino* (1896);
- *Egutegi Bizkaitarra* (calendar work, issued 1896–1897 for subsequent years);
- *Lenengo Egutegi Bizkaitarra*;
- *Umiaren lenengo aizkidia* (a primer for children, 1897).

In 1897 he published **_El Partido Carlista y los Fueros Vasco-Nabarros_**, a sustained attack on the Spanish dynastic framework of Carlism. It attracted converts from Carlist and integrist circles. He then founded *Baserritarra* (2 May–29 August 1897), producing 18 numbers and scores of his own articles before suppression and financial difficulty ended it.

## Electoral Politics, Alliances, and Public Office, 1898–1902

### The 1898 realignment

Industrialist **Ramón de la Sota y Llano** led the *euskalerriaco* current: wealthy, foralist, commercially powerful, and initially more autonomist than separatist. In 1898 Arana admitted Sota and associates into the nationalist movement. Their money, press capacity, and social networks transformed the party's prospects; their pragmatism also moderated and internally complicated Aranist doctrine.

Arana was elected **provincial deputy for Bilbao in September 1898**, serving to 1902. He was often isolated in the Diputación. His interventions dealt with Basque-language support, administrative questions, interprovincial solidarity, and protection of native Biscayans. He publicly accused other deputies of gaining office by illicit methods and faced institutional hostility. He declined some official allowances, while his personal publishing ventures consumed family money.

This was not a conventional career ascent. Electoral participation required accepting institutions whose sovereignty he denied. Historians generally date a second, more pragmatic stage from this campaign: separatist ends persisted, but legal elections, alliances, and incremental institutions became legitimate instruments.

### Newspapers and finances

On 4 June 1899 Arana and allies launched the daily **_El Correo Vasco_**. It survived 103 numbers, closing 15 September. Sabino and Luis spent heavily and worked nights writing and correcting proofs. The venture demonstrates both organizational stamina and weak business foundations.

The 1899 municipal elections returned five nationalist councilors in Bilbao, five in Bermeo, and representatives in Arteaga and Mundaka; Mundaka obtained the first nationalist mayor. On 15 May the government suspended constitutional guarantees in Biscay and closed nationalist centers and publications under anti-separatist measures. They reopened when guarantees returned in April 1900.

The movement created the nominally apolitical **Centro Vasco** in 1899 as a broader social venue. It was later attacked and sacked in May 1902.

Arana's personal finances were not those of a magnate. Family capital funded publications, and the alliance with Sota-connected industrial wealth gave the movement resources it had lacked. By 1900 Sabino and Nikole rented their home because they could not afford the planned house. No evidence in the consulted record suggests patents, speculative investments, secret accounts, fraud, corruption, or money laundering.

### Culture, youth, and international contacts

In 1901 Arana launched the cultural review **_Euzkadi_** and helped establish **Euzko Gaztedia**, an early nationalist youth organization. On 16 September he served as joint vice-president of the orthographic congress at Hendaye. Participants included Arturo Campión, Resurrección María de Azkue, Luis Arana, Gracian Adéma, Martin Guilbeau, and other Basque scholars.

He invited intellectual opponents as well as adherents. The congress exposed a real disagreement. Arana sought unified spelling but did not want a single supradialectal standard to erase provincial varieties; Azkue and Campión favored different orthographic and literary solutions.

## The 1902 Telegram, Second Imprisonment, and “Spanish Evolution”

### Telegram to Theodore Roosevelt

After the formal establishment of the Cuban republic, Arana attempted on **24 May 1902** to send President Theodore Roosevelt a telegram in the PNV's name. It congratulated the United States for Cuba's independence and added that, if Europe imitated it, the ancient Basque nation would also be free. The authorities intercepted the message.

He was arrested on **30 May 1902** and charged under the amended Article 248 of the Penal Code with an attack on the integrity of the Spanish nation, framed as rebellion. The prosecutor sought **eight years and one day** in prison, accessories, and costs. Daniel de Irujo again led the defense, with Teodoro de Aguirre involved in procedural matters. More than 9,000 signatures reportedly supported release on health grounds; the courts rejected the requests.

Arana was **acquitted by a jury on 8 November 1902** and released. The prosecutor announced an appeal. He temporarily crossed into France using the name **Sylvain de Arbeste**. This was evasion of renewed prosecution after acquittal, not a conviction or a permanent exile.

### “Grave y trascendental” and the proposed league

From prison, on **22 June 1902**, *La Patria* published **“Grave y trascendental.”** Arana proposed dissolving or superseding the openly nationalist party and creating a legal organization that would seek “the most radical autonomy possible within the unity of the Spanish state.” The projected name commonly given is **Liga de Vascos Españolistas**.

The proposal was real, not a later forgery. Correspondence with Luis de Eleizalde shows Arana asking trusted followers to enter the new party and describing the change as an immense sacrifice for the patria. A committee collected adhesions from May 1902 to June 1903. But the project never became an effective party.

Competing interpretations remain:

1. **Sincere ideological conversion:** prison, electoral repression, and demographic pessimism convinced Arana that separatism was impossible and regional autonomy within Spain was necessary.
2. **Strategic camouflage:** the legal party would preserve organization and culture under repression while independence remained the private end.
3. **A tactical but genuine new stage:** he meant the autonomist program seriously as the feasible politics of the moment, without psychologically reconciling himself to Spanish identity.
4. **Health or prison-pressure explanation:** illness and confinement distorted judgment. This is possible but insufficient; related ideas preceded the arrest, and his letters show deliberate planning.

Evidence cuts both ways. He publicly specified radical autonomy inside Spain and asked allies to make the sacrifice. Yet *La Patria* continued nationalist writing, he remained involved in nationalist candidates, and in September 1903 he selected the clearly independentist **Ángel Zabala Ozámiz (“Kondaño”)** as successor. His early death prevented a settled program. Claims that he “became Spanish with all his soul” and claims that the evolution was pure deception both exceed the surviving evidence.

## Final Illness and Death, 1903

In 1903 Arana returned to Sukarrieta and resumed work despite worsening disease. In March, under the pseudonym Jelalde, he published **_Libe_**, a four-act historical musical drama based on the fifteenth-century battle of Mungia. The patriotic heroine was intended as an exemplary type.

Physician **Carlos de Iruarrizaga** informed him of the diagnosis of Addison's disease in August 1903. Symptoms worsened sharply in late October. His illness involved weakness, digestive distress, and the pigmentation associated with Addison's. Earlier tuberculosis and recurrent respiratory and digestive problems had already reduced his health.

On 30 September he delegated or transferred movement leadership to Ángel Zabala. Family testimony describes prolonged confession and final religious observance. He died in rented accommodation at Sukarrieta on **25 November 1903**.

The claim that he “contracted Addison's disease in prison” became part of the martyr narrative. Imprisonment in the poorly ventilated Larrinaga jail could have aggravated his health, but Addison's disease is adrenal insufficiency and the record does not establish prison as its cause. Contemporary diagnosis, chronology, and later political commemoration must be kept separate.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Linguistic Development

### From fuerist Carlism to separatism

Arana's family Carlism taught him Catholic counterrevolution and defense of the fueros but placed them inside a Spanish dynastic struggle. His break consisted in declaring that no Spanish king or party could restore what was not Spain's to give. He treated the law of 25 October 1839—not only the final 1876 abolition—as the decisive act by which an independent constitutional order had been subordinated.

### JEL: God above the Old Law

His maxim **Jaun-Goikua eta Lagi-Zaŕa** established a hierarchy:

1. Catholic truth and divine law;
2. the historic law/sovereignty of each Basque territory;
3. confederation by free will.

He opposed liberalism, secular republicanism, socialism, anarchism, and the secular state. At the same time, he advocated institutional separation in which clergy should not function as ordinary party militants, harmony between church and state, and state subordination to Catholic moral law. His controversies with non-nationalist clergy and the bishop of Vitoria show that Catholic identity did not mean obedience to every ecclesiastical political intervention.

### Race, ancestry, language, and immigration

Arana defined nation primarily through descent or “race,” recognizable through surnames, not through individual choice or language acquisition. In the period's vocabulary, *raza* could mean lineage, people, or biological type; in his work it carried all three and repeatedly supported claims of inherited moral difference.

He depicted Spaniards and migrant workers as **maketos**, associated them with irreligion, low wages, crime, socialism, sexual disorder, and cultural contamination, and contrasted them with idealized Catholic Biscayans. This is documented ethnic hierarchy and exclusion, not merely an accusation invented by later opponents. His early membership restrictions sought ancestry proof; his writings sometimes preferred a Spanish-speaking person of Basque descent over a Basque-speaking immigrant.

Context explains the target without nullifying the content. Biscay's population was being transformed by mines, steel, and mass migration after the foral abolition. Racial typology, imperial hierarchy, anti-Semitic *limpieza de sangre* traditions, and national essentialism were widespread in European and Spanish thought. Arana used those materials to construct a specifically anti-Spanish racial nationalism.

His doctrine also contains tensions:

- he wanted Basque restored in all public functions but sometimes argued it should not be taught to outsiders, because linguistic separation protected ancestry;
- his 1899 *Protohistoria de la nación vasca deducida del euskera* inferred common descent through language, complicating an absolute blood/language separation;
- he condemned slavery and wrote against white cruelty to colonized peoples;
- he praised the United States and Britain when their imperial behavior served his contrast with Spain.

His anti-colonialism was therefore selective. He treated Biscay as colonized, supported Cuban emancipation, and defended Africans in some texts, while praising “gentler” Anglo-American power elsewhere.

### Women, sex, and domestic order

Arana's writings and letters state that women were intellectually and morally inferior and should be obedient wives and mothers. These were not merely hostile paraphrases; his correspondence documents them. His courtship of Nikole combined tenderness, pedagogy, control, and patriarchal Catholic expectations. His marriage across class lines, and his grief after miscarriage, do not cancel the general doctrine.

Later accounts sometimes portray the marriage as proof that he transcended ancestry prejudice, and hostile accounts portray the genealogical inquiry as hypocrisy. Both facts matter: he investigated lineage in a culture he had helped politicize, faced internal class prejudice, and nevertheless married her.

### Capital and labor

He attacked industrial capitalism for uprooting traditional society and exploiting workers. His anti-capitalism was not socialist: he rejected class struggle, materialism, internationalism, and anticlerical labor movements. The later Christian-national union Solidaridad de Obreros Vascos/ELA (founded after his death) developed from this unresolved problem—how to organize workers outside Spanish socialist institutions.

### Linguistic work and neologisms

Arana's linguistic program included:

- a phonemic orthography for Biscayan Basque;
- purging Romance loans and replacing them with native-root neologisms;
- extending Basque into politics, administration, education, calendars, journalism, and modern life;
- provincial literary norms rather than one uniform spoken/literary Basque;
- systematic Basque personal names.

Notable creations or promotions include **Euzkadi** (later *Euskadi*), **aberri** (fatherland), **abertzale** (patriot/nationalist), **ikurriña/ikurrina** (flag), and a wide family of political and institutional terms. Some derivations were fanciful. *Aberri*, for example, rested on an inferred root *aba* (“father”) extracted from kinship words. Yet *aberri* and *abertzale* became ordinary modern Basque.

His posthumous **_Deun-Ixendegi Euzkotarra_**, completed by Luis de Eleizalde and published in 1910, contained roughly 960 baptismal names. Some were older Basque forms; many were reconstructions or inventions. Widely surviving examples include **Iñaki, Kepa, Nekane, Garbiñe, Josune, Karmele,** and **Julen**. Authorship must therefore be divided: Arana initiated the system and supplied much material; Eleizalde completed and edited the published volume.

Modern Euskara Batua, standardized from 1968, is not “Arana's invented language.” It drew on dialects and a long literary tradition. Some Aranist spelling devices, including special marked *ĺ* and *ŕ*, were rejected. His broader insistence that Basque could serve modern public life, however, influenced later standardization and vocabulary.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, and Financial Questions

### Documented legal record

- Repeated press denunciations from 1893 onward.
- **1895 conviction** for minor insults to Filomeno Soltura: one month and eleven days, 125-peseta fine, costs.
- Additional 1895–1896 prosecutions for separatist and inflammatory press writings; several ended in acquittal or dismissal.
- Club closure and proceedings against 110 members of Euskeldun Batzokija.
- **1902 rebellion charge** for the Roosevelt telegram; prosecutor requested eight years and one day; **jury acquittal on 8 November 1902**.
- Flight under an alias after the prosecutor announced appeal.

Calling him a “convicted traitor” is inaccurate: the serious 1902 integrity/rebellion case ended in acquittal. Calling all imprisonment arbitrary also overstates the record: the first term included a valid contemporary conviction for insult, followed by preventive detention in other political cases.

### Hate rhetoric and exclusion

Arana's descriptions of Spaniards and immigrant workers included racialized contempt, collective moral accusation, and demands for separation. These are primary-source facts. Whether the best analytical label is racism, ethnic nationalism, xenophobia, or nineteenth-century racialism is a secondary debate; denying the exclusionary content is not sustainable.

### Historical falsification

His historical works presented legendary or weakly sourced battles as evidence of continuous national independence. Modern scholars describe this as mythic history or invented national history. There is no evidence that he forged archival documents. The controversy concerns credulous selection, speculative etymology, and political narration—not documentary counterfeiting.

### Political violence and ETA

Arana used metaphors of reconquest, invasion, and war, but did not create an armed organization and is not documented as ordering assaults or assassinations. ETA, founded in 1959, inherited a Basque national frame and some Aranist symbols while rejecting his clericalism and transforming the ideology through revolutionary, anti-colonial, socialist, and Third World currents.

The statement that Arana “made ETA possible” is a political genealogy, not a direct causal fact. The defensible formulation is that ETA emerged within a nationalist field whose language of separate Basque nationhood Arana had helped establish; more than half a century, dictatorship, war, ideological transformation, and new organizations separated them.

### Finances

His family wealth and Sota alliance funded political work. Newspapers failed or were suppressed, and he spent heavily on print. The available record describes rental housing and restricted means by 1900. No documented fraud, embezzlement, corruption, money laundering, or hidden commercial interest was found.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. “He invented Basque nationalism alone”

**Story:** A solitary founder created nation, party, flag, language, and consciousness.  
**Appearance/circulation:** Disciple biographies, anniversaries, party iconography, and hostile accounts that require a single originator.  
**Evidence:** He was uniquely consequential in synthesizing separatist doctrine and building the PNV. But Luis Arana co-designed symbols and supplied the conversion argument; earlier fuerists, writers, philologists, Carlists, and euskalerriacos supplied material; printers and financiers made organization possible.  
**Effect:** Concentrated a collective transition into one life and made attacks or reverence toward the movement possible through one face.

### 2. Easter 1882 as a national revelation

**Story:** On Easter Sunday Sabino experienced a conversion through Luis, and Basque national consciousness was resurrected.  
**Appearance/circulation:** Family memory; codified at the first Aberri Eguna, Bilbao, 27 March 1932.  
**Evidence:** A decisive fraternal discussion around 1882 is credible. Exact dating and liturgical staging were sharpened posthumously.  
**Effect:** Joined political time to Christian sacred time and made every Easter a repeat of the founder's awakening.

### 3. “He invented half the Basque language in jail”

**Story:** Arana fabricated a vast artificial language during imprisonment.  
**Appearance/circulation:** Anti-nationalist polemic, popular summaries, and compressed social-media biography.  
**Evidence:** He did coin hundreds of forms and promoted extreme purism. Work began by 1884 and continued through publications, correspondence, calendars, and the Hendaye congress. Prison is specifically tied to poems and stages of the anthem, not the whole lexicon. Basque itself is ancient and extensively documented before him.  
**Effect:** For admirers, it magnifies creative resistance; for opponents, it depicts Basque public language as fraudulent.

### 4. The ikurriña as a copied Union Jack

**Story:** The brothers copied Britain's flag, sometimes because of admiration for British power.  
**Appearance/circulation:** Vexillological discussion and hostile political commentary.  
**Evidence:** Visual similarity is undeniable. Their documented explanation maps the flag onto Biscay's red field, Gernika's green oak/old law, and the white Christian cross. No consulted document proves a one-source copy.  
**Effect:** Challenges claims of purely indigenous symbolism while reinforcing the flag's modern, designed character.

### 5. The prisoner-martyr killed by Spain

**Story:** Persecution and Larrinaga prison gave Arana the disease that killed him.  
**Appearance/circulation:** Obituaries, disciple biographies, commemorations; comparisons to Christ and a martyr.  
**Evidence:** He was repeatedly prosecuted and spent about four months in prison in 1895–1896 and more than five months in 1902. His health was poor before either imprisonment, beginning with serious illness in 1881. Addison's disease was diagnosed in 1903; causation by prison is not established.  
**Effect:** Turned legal repression into redemptive suffering and justified a quasi-sacred founder cult.

### 6. Complete Spanish conversion in 1902

**Story:** The separatist abandoned nationalism and became an autonomist loyal to Spain.  
**Appearance/circulation:** Contemporary Spanish press, later anti-nationalist polemic, and literal readings of “Grave y trascendental.”  
**Evidence:** The autonomist proposal and correspondence are genuine. Contrary evidence includes continuing nationalist activity and appointment of an independentist successor.  
**Effect:** Serves critics as a deathbed recantation and defenders as either sacrifice or camouflage. The unresolved ambiguity became part of his afterlife.

### 7. Sabino as Basque Christ or providential messiah

**Story:** Providence sent him to redeem a dying people; prison was Passion, death was sacrifice, and nationalism was resurrection.  
**Appearance/circulation:** Engracio de Aranzadi and other disciples after 1903; intensified in the 1932 Aberri Eguna and 1965 centenary.  
**Evidence:** This is hagiographic interpretation, not a claim Arana was literally divine. His own providential language and religious self-discipline made the analogy available.  
**Effect:** Stabilized party loyalty, ritualized biography, and marginalized inconvenient details such as the Spanish evolution and Nikole's later life.

### 8. Direct fatherhood of ETA

**Story:** Arana's doctrine leads in a straight line to ETA terrorism.  
**Appearance/circulation:** Contemporary street-name disputes and Spanish party polemic.  
**Evidence:** Ideological lineage exists at the level of nationhood, symbols, and independence. Direct organizational, strategic, or personal continuity does not.  
**Effect:** Makes commemoration of a nineteenth-century founder part of present memory politics.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Immediate succession and party development

Ángel Zabala succeeded Arana. The PNV did not implement the projected Spanish-minded league. It continued to oscillate between Sabinian independence, autonomist practice, the influence of Sota's wealthy euskalerriacos, and later social and democratic currents. Luis Arana periodically led orthodox factions and contested pragmatic adaptation.

Arana's enduring institutional creations include:

- the PNV, one of Spain's oldest surviving parties;
- the *batzoki* as social-political form;
- JEL and the term *jeltzale*;
- the ikurriña, official flag of the Basque autonomous community and broader informal Basque symbol;
- *Euzkadi/Euskadi* as a modern country name;
- a political lexicon still used across ideological camps;
- the anthem text and the melody's nationalist institutionalization;
- a model of language revival tied to publishing, schools, personal names, and public administration.

### Founder cult

Disciples rapidly sacralized him. Obituaries and biographies called him providential, messianic, apostolic, martyr, or redeemer. A proposed island mausoleum was compared to Rome or Mecca; it failed. An Egyptian-style obelisk proposal also failed. The modest grave remained the actual site.

The cult reached a major public form in the **first Aberri Eguna on 27 March 1932**, organized in Bilbao for the fiftieth anniversary of the 1882 awakening and ending at the restored/inaugurated Sabin Etxea. Easter joined Christ's resurrection, Sabino's conversion, and the nation's rebirth. Later celebrations moved to Donostia (1933), Vitoria (1934), and Pamplona (1935).

### War, dictatorship, and archive

Arana's writings were microfilmed in 1936 to protect them as war approached. Under Franco, the ikurriña and nationalist commemoration were prohibited, while exile communities in France and the Americas preserved the cult and publications. Sabindiar Batza, founded in Bayonne in 1950, collected and published the *Obras completas* in 1965, printed in Buenos Aires. Sendoa issued a three-volume edition in 1980.

### Symbols beyond doctrine

The ikurriña became official under the Basque government in 1936, was banned under Franco, legalized in 1977, and recognized in the 1979 Statute. The Basque Parliament adopted the anthem melody without official lyrics in 1983, partly because Arana's words were denominational and partisan. The symbols therefore outgrew the exact doctrine of their maker.

Personal names may be his most intimate legacy. People with no commitment to racial nationalism routinely bear forms systematized by Arana and Eleizalde. Likewise *abertzale* now describes political positions ranging from Christian democracy to revolutionary leftism.

### Historical reinterpretation

The major interpretive phases are:

1. **1903–1936:** disciple hagiography, party consolidation, Christic founder myth.
2. **Franco era/exile:** preservation, canonization, publication of writings as an act of national survival.
3. **Transition and early autonomy:** recovery of symbols; expansion of social-scientific research by Solozábal, Corcuera, Larronde, Elorza, and others.
4. **ETA-era polemics:** competing portrayals as democratic national founder, racist precursor, anti-colonial prophet, or remote father of violence.
5. **Twenty-first-century archival revision:** stronger attention to Nikole, unpublished letters, myth construction, language competence, early intellectual phases, race, gender, and the genuine ambiguity of 1902.

The PNV retains Arana as founder while no longer enforcing his ancestry criteria, racial hierarchy, confessional state, or original separatist program as a complete package. Critics use his words on *maketos*, women, and racial purity in public debates over statues and street names. Admirers emphasize cultural revival, imprisonment, anti-slavery writing, political organization, and the creation of durable symbols. Both selections are grounded in real parts of the archive; neither is the whole record.
