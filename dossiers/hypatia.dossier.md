# Hypatia of Alexandria: Research Dossier

## Scope and Evidentiary Limits

Hypatia left no surviving autobiography, diary, correspondence, inscription, securely identified portrait, or independently transmitted philosophical treatise. Nearly everything reconstructable about her private life comes from:

1. Letters written to her or referring to her by her student Synesius of Cyrene, chiefly in the 390s and early fifth century.
2. Socrates Scholasticus’s *Ecclesiastical History*, written approximately twenty-five years after her death.
3. The sixth-century pagan philosopher Damascius’s *Life of Isidore* or *Philosophical History*, surviving in fragments and later excerpts.
4. The tenth-century Byzantine *Suda*, whose Hypatia entry combines material associated with Hesychius of Miletus and Damascius.
5. The seventh-century *Chronicle* of John, bishop of Nikiu, preserved through a complicated Coptic-Arabic-Ethiopic textual transmission.
6. Titles, subscriptions, and textual features in mathematical and astronomical manuscripts associated with her father, Theon of Alexandria.
7. Later literary, ecclesiastical, philosophical, political, and artistic receptions.

The evidentiary labels used below are:

- **Documented fact:** directly attested by a contemporary or near-contemporary text, manuscript subscription, law, or multiple substantially independent early witnesses.
- **Reported fact:** stated by an ancient source but not independently verifiable.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** a modern inference from the surviving evidence.
- **Disputed claim:** supported by some scholars but rejected or qualified by others.
- **Allegation:** an accusation made in an identifiable historical conflict.
- **Rumor or myth:** a story lacking adequate historical support but significant in Hypatia’s reception.

Dates before her death are frequently approximate.

## Family and Ancestry

### Theon of Alexandria

**Documented fact:** Hypatia was the daughter of Theon of Alexandria. A subscription to Book III of Theon’s commentary on Ptolemy’s *Almagest* calls her “my daughter Hypatia, the philosopher.”

Theon was an Alexandrian mathematician, astronomer, commentator, teacher, and calculator of astronomical tables. He produced or transmitted commentaries on:

- Euclid’s *Elements*;
- Ptolemy’s *Almagest*;
- Ptolemy’s *Handy Tables*;
- other mathematical or astronomical materials known only incompletely.

Theon observed eclipses in 364 CE, establishing that he was professionally active by that date. This provides context, but not a reliable date for Hypatia’s birth.

The *Suda* calls Theon a man of the Mouseion. Whether this meant membership in a surviving formal institution continuous with the Hellenistic Museum or was a conventional designation for an Alexandrian scholar is uncertain. There is no evidence that Hypatia worked in the ancient Great Library or that the classical Library survived intact into her lifetime.

### Mother, ancestry, and household

No source names Hypatia’s mother, maternal ancestry, grandparents, or broader kin group. Nothing reliable establishes Egyptian, Greek, mixed, aristocratic, or priestly ancestry beyond her association with Alexandria’s Greek-language scholarly culture.

Her father’s ability to sustain advanced mathematical work and educate a daughter suggests a literate scholarly household with access to manuscripts and elite intellectual networks. Its exact wealth and legal status are unknown.

### Possible sibling: Epiphanius

Theon dedicated parts of his work to a person named Epiphanius, addressing him as “my dear son” or similar language. Two interpretations remain possible:

- **Disputed claim:** Epiphanius was Theon’s biological son and Hypatia’s brother.
- **Alternative reconstruction:** “Son” was pedagogical language for a student.

No independent source identifies Epiphanius as Hypatia’s brother.

### Marriage and children

No contemporary or near-contemporary evidence gives Hypatia a spouse, partner, or child.

The *Suda* contains a statement making her “wife of Isidore the philosopher.” This cannot refer to the sixth-century Isidore of Alexandria, who lived long after her death. It may result from textual confusion over a Greek word capable of meaning “woman” or “wife,” contamination between biographical entries, or identification with an otherwise unknown Isidore.

**Conclusion:** Hypatia’s marriage to Isidore is a textual error or unresolved corruption, not an established relationship.

Damascius portrays her as remaining a virgin. This accords with his representation of her philosophical self-control but cannot be independently verified.

## Childhood and Early Life

No ancient source describes Hypatia’s birth, childhood home, mother, childhood illnesses, play, or elementary education.

### Birth-date problem

Suggested dates include:

- approximately 350–355;
- approximately 360;
- approximately 370–375.

The later dating was once inferred partly from the assumption that she was young when teaching Synesius. Maria Dzielska and Michael Deakin argued in different ways for an earlier birth, noting her already-established authority when Synesius studied with her and Damascius’s description of her maturity at death. Robert Penella and others caution that neither extreme can be demonstrated.

**Strongest conclusion:** Hypatia was born sometime in the middle decades of the fourth century, probably in Alexandria. Her exact year and age at death are unknown.

Statements that she was exactly forty-five, sixty, or a particular age when murdered are modern reconstructions, not documentary facts.

### Probable education

**Scholarly reconstruction:** Theon was almost certainly an important teacher in her mathematical and astronomical formation. Their manuscript association and common fields make this considerably more than a generic supposition, although no narrative source describes lessons between them.

Her curriculum probably included:

- Euclidean geometry;
- arithmetic and number theory;
- Apollonian conic sections;
- Ptolemaic astronomy;
- astronomical tables and computational procedures;
- philosophical reading of Plato and Aristotle;
- later Platonist metaphysics;
- rhetorical exposition and public teaching.

There is no reliable evidence that Theon subjected her to a documented physical-training regimen, horseback-riding program, or idealized “perfect human” education. Such details occur largely in modern popular biographies.

### Alleged education in Athens

Some later accounts say Hypatia traveled to or studied at Athens. No early source establishes this.

Damascius compares her favorably with his own Alexandrian and Athenian philosophical world, but comparison is not proof of travel. She could acquire Platonist learning in Alexandria.

**Status:** unverified tradition.

## Education and Intellectual Formation

## Mathematics and astronomy

Theon’s school worked in an Alexandrian tradition in which editing, explaining, correcting, and teaching authoritative texts were major intellectual activities. The modern division between “original research” and “mere commentary” does not map cleanly onto this environment. Preparing a usable text could require reconstruction of corrupt passages, new demonstrations, numerical checking, pedagogical rearrangement, and astronomical calculation.

Hypatia’s named subjects connect her to:

- Diophantus of Alexandria’s *Arithmetica*;
- Apollonius of Perga’s *Conics*;
- Claudius Ptolemy’s astronomy;
- the mathematical tradition transmitted by Theon.

### Platonism

Socrates says she succeeded to or represented “the school of Plato and Plotinus.” Damascius describes her as wearing the philosopher’s mantle, publicly explaining Plato and Aristotle, and attracting hearers.

Her precise school affiliation is disputed:

- **Plotinian-Porphyrian reconstruction:** Hypatia represented a contemplative, intellectually oriented Platonism centered on ascent through reason and purification.
- **Iamblichean reconstruction:** Like much later antique Platonism, her circle may also have accepted ritual, theurgy, or religious practices associated with Iamblichus.
- **Cautious conclusion:** The evidence securely establishes Platonism but does not permit a detailed reconstruction of her metaphysics, theology, ritual practice, or commentary sequence.

She is often called a “Neoplatonist,” a useful modern classification but not a name she is known to have used for herself.

### Teachers besides Theon

No other teacher is securely named. Claims that she studied under Plutarch of Athens, Syrianus, or other Athenian philosophers are chronological or speculative reconstructions without direct attestation.

## Early Career

By the time Synesius of Cyrene studied with her—probably in the 390s—Hypatia had become the center of an influential intellectual circle in Alexandria.

Her school was probably not a modern public university with a fixed institutional charter. It may have consisted of private or semi-public teaching supported by fees, patronage, family resources, and elite networks. The exact location is unknown.

Damascius describes her as going through the city and publicly interpreting Plato or Aristotle to those wishing to hear her. This suggests unusual civic visibility. Socrates says she appeared in public before magistrates and was respected for her dignity and self-possession.

No surviving payroll, appointment document, school roll, property deed, or government office identifies her employer.

## Chronological Life History

## Mid-fourth century: birth and family setting

Hypatia was probably born in Alexandria while Theon’s astronomical career was active. Alexandria was a major Mediterranean metropolis under Roman rule, with Greek, Egyptian, Jewish, and Christian communities and recurring struggles over urban authority.

The city retained important mathematical, medical, rhetorical, and philosophical traditions. Christianity had become increasingly powerful but was internally divided; traditional religious cults and philosophical schools remained active.

## Approximately 380s–390s: advanced formation and emergence as teacher

No dated event survives from these decades. By the 390s, however, Hypatia must have completed an advanced education and established a reputation sufficient to attract students from wealthy provincial families.

## Approximately 390s: Synesius and the student circle

Synesius of Cyrene, a landowner and intellectual from Cyrenaica who later became bishop of Ptolemais, studied with Hypatia. His surviving correspondence provides the closest evidence for her living intellectual network.

He addresses her as:

- mother;
- sister;
- teacher;
- benefactor;
- “most revered philosopher.”

These formulations indicate reverence, pedagogical kinship, and continuing friendship. They do not imply biological kinship.

Members or associates of the circle named in Synesius’s letters include:

- Synesius;
- his brother Euoptius;
- Herculianus;
- Olympius;
- Ision;
- possibly Theotecnus, Athanasius, Gaius, and other correspondents whose exact relationship to Hypatia is less certain.

Synesius warned Herculianus not to disclose doctrines that ought to remain private, indicating some graded or confidential philosophical teaching. This does not prove a secret cult or conspiracy.

The circle crossed religious boundaries. Synesius eventually became a Christian bishop; other students were Christians or imperial officials. Hypatia’s teaching was therefore not simply an anti-Christian political organization.

## 397–400: Synesius in Constantinople and the astrolabe

While pursuing an embassy in Constantinople, Synesius composed a work accompanying a silver planispheric astrolabe. He credited Hypatia, his “most revered teacher,” with contributing to its theoretical preparation.

**Documented fact:** Hypatia participated intellectually in astrolabe-related work.

**Myth correction:** She did not invent the astrolabe. Forms of the instrument and its mathematical theory predated her.

## Early fifth century: established Alexandrian authority

Socrates reports that Hypatia surpassed the philosophers of her time, had inherited or represented the Platonic school, and attracted people from many places.

Damascius portrays her house as a destination before which crowds, people, and horses gathered. He attributes this to her fame and teaching. His presentation is admiring but written about a century later and partly designed to contrast an exemplary pagan philosopher with Cyril.

Her reputation extended into political society. Socrates says she frequently met public officials and was not embarrassed to appear among men, who admired her for dignity and virtue.

## Scientific consultation: Synesius’s hydroscope

In a letter written during severe personal distress, Synesius asked Hypatia to have a “hydroscope” cast in brass. His description is of a graduated floating instrument used to compare the weight or specific gravity of liquids—a hydrometer or densimeter.

**Documented fact:** Synesius trusted her to commission, supervise, obtain, or understand the construction of such an instrument.

**Myth correction:** The letter does not prove that Hypatia invented the hydrometer. Comparable principles and instruments were older.

## 402–413: continuing correspondence and patronage requests

Synesius’s letters show him appealing to Hypatia not only about instruments and philosophy but also about people and political assistance. He asked her to support associates or advance requests through influential contacts.

This indicates that her network included people capable of affecting civic or provincial decisions. It does not establish a formal office.

In later letters, Synesius describes the deaths of his children and the collapse of his fortunes. He addresses Hypatia as one of the few people whose regard still mattered to him. The exact date of his final letter to her is disputed, but the correspondence probably ends before his own death, usually placed around 413 or 414.

## 391 and the Serapeum

In 391, during Theophilus’s episcopate, imperial policy and local violence culminated in the destruction or conversion of the Serapeum after conflict between pagans and Christians.

No ancient source places Hypatia inside the Serapeum, identifies her as a combatant, or says she tried to save its books. Socrates’s narrative of Hypatia’s death does not connect her to the event.

The film *Agora* and some modern retellings merge the Serapeum crisis with her biography. That is dramatic reconstruction, not documented life history.

The Serapeum’s destruction was also not the destruction of an intact “Great Library” personally overseen by Hypatia. The fate of Alexandria’s successive libraries was a long and complex process.

## 412: Cyril becomes bishop of Alexandria

After the death of Theophilus in 412, Cyril became bishop following a contested succession. Socrates reports disorder and rivalry surrounding the election.

Cyril rapidly became a major urban and ecclesiastical power. The prefect Orestes represented imperial civil authority. Their conflict involved jurisdiction, public order, religious factions, and control of Alexandrian political life.

## 414–415: conflict among Cyril, Orestes, Jewish Alexandrians, monks, and church personnel

According to Socrates:

1. Cyril’s supporter Hierax attended a public performance or assembly and was suspected of provoking disorder.
2. Orestes had Hierax publicly tortured.
3. Violence followed between Christians and Jews.
4. After an alleged nighttime massacre of Christians, Cyril seized synagogues and expelled or displaced Jewish residents.
5. Orestes complained to the emperor.
6. Approximately five hundred monks from Nitria entered Alexandria and confronted Orestes.
7. A monk named Ammonius struck the prefect with a stone.
8. Orestes’s attendants fled, but Alexandrians rescued him; Ammonius was tortured and died.
9. Cyril initially honored Ammonius as a martyr, then allowed the matter to subside when many Christians rejected that characterization.

This sequence created the political environment in which Hypatia was attacked.

Details differ among sources, and the extent of Cyril’s expulsion of Alexandrian Jews is disputed. Socrates is closer to events than John of Nikiu but wrote with his own ecclesiastical and political commitments.

## Hypatia and Orestes

Socrates says Hypatia had frequent contact with Orestes. He does not describe them as lovers, spouses, or co-conspirators. Her position as an esteemed adviser and civic intellectual sufficiently explains the contact.

A rumor circulated that Hypatia prevented Orestes from reconciling with Cyril. Socrates presents this as the belief that brought accusations against her, not as an established fact.

John of Nikiu later claimed she used “satanic wiles,” magic, astrolabes, and musical instruments to beguile people and that Orestes honored her excessively.

Damascius says Cyril became envious after seeing the crowd at Hypatia’s house and planned her murder. This is the earliest surviving text explicitly placing personal responsibility on Cyril, but it was composed approximately a century later by a pagan author hostile to Cyril’s religious politics.

### Evidentiary assessment of Cyril’s responsibility

- **Documented fact:** Hypatia was murdered by Christians in the setting of Cyril’s conflict with Orestes.
- **Documented fact:** Peter, identified as a reader, led the crowd in Socrates’s account. A reader was a minor church functionary.
- **Reported fact:** the accusation that Hypatia obstructed reconciliation circulated among church-associated people.
- **Disputed claim:** Cyril personally ordered or planned the murder.
- **Evidence for responsibility:** Damascius’s explicit accusation; Peter’s ecclesiastical status; the political benefit to Cyril’s side; the wider activities of Cyril’s militant supporters.
- **Evidence against treating an order as proven:** Socrates does not say Cyril ordered it; no order, trial finding, confession, or contemporary documentary accusation survives.
- **Reasonable conclusion:** direct command is unresolved. Political and institutional responsibility remains a historical question distinct from proof of an explicit assassination order.

## March 415: abduction and murder

Socrates gives the most important near-contemporary account. During Lent in March, a crowd led by Peter the reader intercepted Hypatia while she was traveling, dragged her from her carriage or vehicle, and took her to a building called the Caesareum.

They stripped her and killed her with *ostraka*. The Greek word can mean pieces of pottery, roof tiles, shells, or other sharp fragments. Later accounts and translations variously describe:

- stoning with tiles;
- beating;
- cutting or scraping her flesh with potsherds;
- flaying.

Her body was then dismembered. The remains were carried to a place called Cinaron and burned.

Socrates states that the killing brought disgrace upon Cyril and the Alexandrian church, because murder and violence were alien to professed Christianity.

Damascius gives a more graphic account in which the crowd cuts her apart and disposes of her remains. John of Nikiu says believers seized her, dragged her through the streets, killed her, and burned her body, presenting the killers approvingly as defenders of Christianity.

### Date

March 415 is now most widely accepted. Older literature sometimes gives 414, partly because of chronological uncertainty in the source tradition. A date in 416 is less likely because imperial legislation commonly connected with the aftermath dates from September 416.

### Site

The Caesareum had originated as a Roman imperial cult complex and had become a church. Its exact archaeological relationship to later Alexandrian structures remains uncertain. Cinaron’s location is not securely identified.

### Age

Unknown. Depending on the birth chronology, she may have been in her forties, fifties, or sixties.

### Medical details

No source permits a clinical account of wounds or duration of survival. Claims that she was alive during every stage of dismemberment, that her eyes were gouged out, or that oyster shells were used are expansions or particular translations, not uniformly attested facts.

## Death, Investigation, and Immediate Aftermath

No surviving record documents the conviction or punishment of Peter or another individual killer.

An imperial law of 29 September 416 restricted Alexandria’s *parabalani* or *parabolani*, church-associated attendants of the sick who also functioned as a formidable urban body. It:

- limited their number to 500;
- placed recruitment under the prefect’s supervision;
- restricted attendance at public spectacles, courts, and council meetings;
- sought to reduce ecclesiastical deployment of organized crowds in civic affairs.

Restrictions were partly relaxed in 418, when their authorized number rose to 600 and episcopal influence recovered.

Many scholars connect the 416 law with the crisis following Hypatia’s murder. The law does not name Hypatia or identify her killers as *parabalani*. Socrates calls the killers a crowd and names Peter, not the *parabalani*. Their specific participation is therefore probable to some historians but not proven by the principal narrative source.

Orestes disappears from the surviving narrative record soon afterward. Whether he resigned, was recalled, transferred, dismissed, or died is unknown.

Cyril retained office until 444 and became one of the most consequential theologians and church politicians of late antiquity. Hypatia’s death did not end Alexandrian philosophy, mathematics, pagan life, or classical scholarship, though it damaged the city’s reputation and removed a major intellectual and political figure.

## Employment, Institution, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Teaching establishment

Hypatia appears to have directed a private or semi-public philosophical circle in Alexandria. Sources do not establish:

- a formal title such as university president;
- appointment as head of the Mouseion;
- control of the Library of Alexandria;
- government salary;
- tuition rates;
- school ownership;
- number of students;
- exact teaching premises.

### Public advisory role

Her students and contacts entered imperial, municipal, and ecclesiastical careers. Synesius appealed to her influence. Socrates describes her access to magistrates.

She may consequently be understood as an elite civic adviser or broker, but she held no documented magistracy.

### Instruments and technology

Hypatia was associated with:

- mathematical design or theory for a planispheric astrolabe;
- procurement or construction of a hydrometer/densimeter;
- astronomical computation;
- textual editing and mathematical demonstration.

There are no patents; patent systems in the modern sense did not exist in her setting.

Claims that she invented the astrolabe, hydrometer, laboratory distillation apparatus, or a new model of the universe are unsupported.

### Wealth and property

No evidence survives for:

- landholdings;
- urban real estate;
- slaves or household labor;
- inherited estate;
- student fees;
- investments;
- debts;
- business partnerships;
- testament or heirs.

Her public carriage, educated household, manuscript access, and elite students suggest material resources, but their extent and ownership cannot be reconstructed.

## Religious, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

### Religious identity

Socrates and John of Nikiu identify Hypatia with the non-Christian philosophical or pagan world. Damascius treats her as an exemplary Hellenic philosopher.

No source records:

- Christian baptism;
- conversion;
- participation in Christian worship;
- membership in a named temple priesthood;
- sacrifice;
- initiation into a mystery cult;
- personal creed.

Her continued public pagan identity after imperial restrictions on traditional cult was possible because philosophical teaching and private religious adherence were not identical with public sacrifice.

### Relationship with Christians

Her circle included Christians, most prominently Synesius. His elevation as bishop indicates that study under Hypatia did not automatically exclude ecclesiastical advancement.

There is no evidence that Hypatia persecuted Christians, tried to restore pagan state power, or led an anti-Christian underground.

### Magic and astrology

John of Nikiu accuses her of magic and satanic deception. Within polemical Christian writing, astronomical instruments and pagan philosophy could be redescribed as sorcery.

No independent evidence shows criminal magic, poisoning, curses, necromancy, or demonic ritual.

Ancient astronomy and astrology overlapped conceptually and computationally. Hypatia’s work with astronomical tables does not by itself establish that she practiced personal horoscope astrology.

### Philosophical teaching

Damascius says she explained Plato and Aristotle publicly. Synesius’s works display themes compatible with later Platonism:

- the hierarchy of being;
- purification of the soul;
- ascent toward divine intellect;
- providence;
- dreams;
- the relation between philosophy and civic life.

It is hazardous to assign every position in Synesius to Hypatia. His thought also reflects Christianity, rhetoric, personal development, and other reading.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Theon of Alexandria

**Nature:** father, probable teacher, mathematical collaborator or senior colleague.

**Evidence:** manuscript subscription and later biographical tradition.

**Unresolved questions:** extent of collaboration; whether Hypatia succeeded him institutionally; date of his death.

### Synesius of Cyrene

**Nature:** student, friend, correspondent, philosophical dependent, and occasional requester of patronage.

**Places:** Alexandria; correspondence linking Alexandria, Cyrene, Ptolemais, and Constantinople.

**Dates:** chiefly the 390s through early fifth century.

Synesius provides the most personal evidence for Hypatia. His language depicts a relationship of intellectual reverence and durable affection.

### Herculianus

**Nature:** fellow member of the student circle and recipient of Synesius’s warning against disclosing philosophical teachings.

Whether Herculianus corresponded directly with Hypatia is unknown.

### Olympius

**Nature:** friend or associate in Synesius’s network, apparently connected with Hypatia’s circle.

Precise identity and career remain disputed.

### Euoptius

**Nature:** Synesius’s brother and later bishop; associated with the broader intellectual network. Direct study under Hypatia is plausible but not established as securely as Synesius’s.

### Orestes

**Nature:** prefect of Egypt, political associate or adviser’s contact.

No evidence establishes romance. His reliance upon or respect for Hypatia became the basis of the rumor that she blocked reconciliation with Cyril.

### Cyril of Alexandria

**Nature:** rival center of Alexandrian authority; no documented meeting or correspondence.

Their relationship is reconstructed through civic conflict and accounts of her death. Damascius alleges Cyril’s jealousy and planning; Socrates supplies no direct personal encounter.

### Peter the reader

**Nature:** named leader of the crowd that murdered Hypatia.

Nothing else about him is securely known. Attempts to identify him with other historical Peters are speculative.

### Theophilus of Alexandria

**Nature:** Cyril’s predecessor and uncle; no documented personal relationship with Hypatia.

Hypatia’s mature career overlapped his episcopate and the destruction of the Serapeum, but no source records direct interaction.

## Successes, Failures, Offices, and Honors

### Documented or strongly supported successes

- Achieved exceptional public recognition as a woman teaching advanced philosophy and mathematics.
- Attracted students from outside Alexandria.
- Maintained a network spanning civic, imperial, landowning, and Christian ecclesiastical elites.
- Participated in the transmission or editing of major mathematical and astronomical texts.
- Was consulted on scientific instruments and patronage.
- Acquired sufficient civic standing to appear before magistrates without the social marginalization ordinarily imposed on women in formal public life.

### Failures or losses

- None of her independent works survives intact.
- Her political influence did not protect her during the Orestes–Cyril struggle.
- No surviving prosecution brought her murderers to documented justice.
- Her thought can no longer be reconstructed in systematic form.

### Offices and honors

No government office, priesthood, endowed chair, formal school presidency, or ancient award is securely documented.

“The philosopher” was a status designation reflecting recognized expertise, not necessarily an imperial appointment.

## Controversies, Accusations, and Unresolved Questions

## Did Hypatia practice magic?

**Allegation:** John of Nikiu says she practiced magic and deceived Orestes and the population through satanic arts.

**Evidence for:** only hostile, much later ecclesiastical testimony.

**Evidence against treating it as fact:** Socrates, a Christian and much nearer contemporary, praises her learning and moral conduct and reports the rumor about political obstruction without alleging sorcery.

**Assessment:** polemical allegation, not documented criminal activity.

## Did she prevent reconciliation between Orestes and Cyril?

**Contemporary rumor reported by Socrates:** Christians believed her influence blocked reconciliation.

No letter, meeting record, or testimony from Orestes substantiates it.

**Assessment:** historically consequential rumor; underlying conduct unresolved.

## Did Cyril order the murder?

Damascius says Cyril’s envy led him to plan it. Socrates does not allege an order, while nevertheless connecting the crime to Cyril’s political conflict and saying it disgraced his church.

**Assessment:** disputed historical claim. Direct command is unproven; the killers emerged from a mobilized Christian political environment linked institutionally to Cyril’s church.

## Were the killers *parabalani*?

Frequently asserted in modern histories and dramatizations.

**Evidence:** their known connection to Alexandrian church power and the restrictive imperial law of 416.

**Problem:** Socrates does not use that designation for Hypatia’s killers.

**Assessment:** plausible but unproven identification.

## Was her death a result of opposition to science?

Her learning, pagan status, civic authority, and political relationship with Orestes all mattered. The immediate surviving explanation is political jealousy and the rumor that she frustrated reconciliation.

There is no evidence that she was killed for discovering heliocentrism, elliptical orbits, or a particular theorem.

**Assessment:** presenting the murder exclusively as “religion versus science” compresses a civic, religious, gendered, and political conflict into a modern category.

## Was she the last ancient scientist or philosopher?

No. Philosophical and mathematical work continued in Alexandria, Athens, Constantinople, and elsewhere. Later Alexandrian teachers included Hierocles, Ammonius son of Hermias, Olympiodorus, and John Philoponus.

**Assessment:** symbolic myth, historically false as a literal claim.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## The beautiful young pagan martyr

### Story

Hypatia was a young, extraordinarily beautiful virgin whose physical attractiveness intensified Christian hatred.

### Origin and circulation

Damascius praises her beauty, wisdom, eloquence, and chastity. Later European literature amplified these features. Charles Kingsley’s 1853 novel made her an eroticized, tragic pagan heroine.

### Evidence

Damascius wrote about a century after her death. No contemporary portrait or physical description survives.

### Reputation effect

Her beauty became central to paintings, novels, theatrical adaptations, and popular biographies, frequently eclipsing her age uncertainty and technical work.

## The rejected suitor and menstrual cloth

### Story

A student fell in love with her. To cure him, Hypatia displayed a cloth stained with menstrual blood and told him that this—not ideal beauty—was what he loved.

### Source

Damascius’s *Life of Isidore*, preserved through the *Suda*. The same tradition notes another version involving music and dismisses it.

### Evidence

Single late anecdotal tradition, shaped by philosophical ideals of chastity, bodily disgust, and mastery over passion.

### Reputation effect

Used variously to portray Hypatia as ascetic, anti-erotic, medically frank, misogynistically represented, or radically independent. It cannot be verified biographically.

## Wife of Isidore

### Story

Hypatia married Isidore the philosopher.

### Source

A corrupted or confused *Suda* notice.

### Evidence against

The famous Isidore of Alexandria lived generations later; the notice conflicts with the virginity tradition.

### Assessment

Textual error or unidentified namesake, not reliable biography.

## Inventor of the astrolabe

### Story

Hypatia invented the astrolabe.

### Evidence

Synesius credits her contribution to an astrolabe-related work. Astrolabe theory and precursors existed centuries earlier.

### Assessment

False as a claim of first invention; documented as scientific involvement.

## Inventor of the hydrometer

### Story

Hypatia invented the hydrometer.

### Evidence

Synesius asked her to have one made and supplied a description. The physical principle was older.

### Assessment

Unsupported invention claim; documented technical consultation.

## Discoverer of heliocentrism and elliptical orbits

### Story

Hypatia rejected Ptolemy, revived Aristarchus’s heliocentrism, and discovered that planets move in ellipses.

### Circulation

Most influentially dramatized in Alejandro Amenábar’s *Agora*.

### Evidence

No ancient source or mathematical manuscript attributes these discoveries to her. Elliptical planetary orbits are associated with Johannes Kepler in the seventeenth century.

### Reputation effect

Made Hypatia legible as a modern experimental scientist but obscured her documented role in editing and teaching Ptolemaic mathematical astronomy.

## Defender or librarian of the Great Library

### Story

Hypatia directed or defended the Library of Alexandria and died during or because of its destruction.

### Evidence

No source connects her institutionally to the Great Library or places her death during its destruction. The Serapeum crisis occurred in 391; she died in 415.

### Assessment

Chronological and institutional conflation.

## Hypatia as Catherine of Alexandria

### Story or theory

The Christian virgin martyr Catherine of Alexandria may be a transformed or inverted memory of Hypatia: learned Alexandrian virgin, philosopher, disputant with men, and martyr.

### Evidence

Catherine’s legend appears substantially later and contains motifs common to hagiography. No early source explicitly identifies Catherine with Hypatia.

### Assessment

Modern comparative theory, not a demonstrated line of transmission.

### Reputation effect

The comparison exposes the possibility that late antique stories of learned female sanctity borrowed or shared narrative patterns, but it cannot establish that Catherine was created directly from Hypatia.

## Cyril as proven personal assassin

### Story

Cyril personally issued a documented order naming Hypatia for execution.

### Evidence

No order survives. Damascius alleges planning; Socrates supplies political context but not an instruction.

### Assessment

Possible historical responsibility has often been converted into a more precise documentary claim than the evidence supports.

## Hypatia as anti-religious rationalist

### Story

Hypatia was an atheist or secular modern scientist opposed to all religion.

### Evidence

Ancient Platonism was metaphysical and religious. Sources treat her as a Hellenic or pagan philosopher, not an atheist.

### Assessment

Modern ideological appropriation.

## Hypatia as witch or demonic corrupter

### Story

She used satanic magic to control Orestes and oppose the church.

### Source and circulators

John of Nikiu and later hostile religious retellings.

### Evidence

No independent corroboration.

### Reputation effect

Provided theological justification for treating her killers as defenders of Christian order.

## Works by Hypatia

No securely independent manuscript survives under Hypatia’s sole name. Titles are chiefly preserved in the *Suda* and inferred through Theon’s manuscripts.

## 1. Commentary on Diophantus’s *Arithmetica*

**Original description:** εἰς Διόφαντον τὸν ἀστρονόμον κανόνα, or a nearby reading in the problematic *Suda* syntax; generally understood to include a commentary on Diophantus.

**Probable date:** late fourth or early fifth century.

**Extent:** sometimes described as covering thirteen books, corresponding to the ancient extent attributed to the *Arithmetica*.

**Survival:** lost as an independently attributed work.

**Possible traces:** Some scholars, including Paul Tannery, Thomas Heath, Jacques Sesiano, and Michael Deakin, have proposed that problems, interpolations, or the relatively elementary shape of parts of the surviving Greek and Arabic Diophantine traditions preserve material from Hypatia’s edition.

**Status:** genuine ancient attribution; identification of surviving fragments is disputed.

## 2. Commentary on Apollonius of Perga’s *Conics*

**Original title:** not preserved securely; conventionally *Commentary on the Conics of Apollonius*.

**Probable date:** late fourth or early fifth century.

**Subject:** conic sections—ellipse, parabola, hyperbola, and associated geometric propositions.

**Survival:** lost.

**Significance:** Ability to edit or comment on Apollonius implies advanced command of difficult Greek geometry. No theorem can be securely assigned to Hypatia.

## 3. *Astronomical Canon*

**Greek:** Ἀστρονομικὸς κανών or a related expression inferred from the *Suda*.

Interpretations include:

- an original set of astronomical tables;
- an edition or commentary on Ptolemy’s *Handy Tables*;
- a reference grammatically linked with her Diophantus commentary rather than a separate work.

**Status:** ancient attribution, exact identity disputed.

## 4. Edition associated with Book III of Ptolemy’s *Almagest*

A manuscript heading is commonly translated approximately:

> Commentary by Theon of Alexandria on Book III of Ptolemy’s *Mathematical Syntaxis*, the edition having been revised/prepared by my daughter Hypatia, the philosopher.

Older scholarship interpreted this as Hypatia revising Theon’s commentary. Alan Cameron and others argue that she edited the underlying text of Ptolemy used by Theon.

**Survival:** material survives within the manuscript tradition, but the boundary between Ptolemy, Theon, copyists, and Hypatia cannot be completely isolated.

**Status:** strongest direct material evidence for her scholarly work.

## 5. Possible contribution to Theon’s edition of Euclid’s *Elements*

Theon’s recension strongly influenced the medieval and early modern Euclidean text.

No surviving subscription names Hypatia in the *Elements*. Her contribution is sometimes inferred from collaboration with her father.

**Status:** scholarly possibility, not documented authorship.

## 6. Proposed Archimedean editorial material

Wilbur Knorr and other historians of mathematics have explored whether material associated with Archimedes’ *Measurement of a Circle*, isoperimetric figures, or later medieval transmission may reflect Hypatia’s editorial activity.

**Status:** specialist hypothesis; not an anciently named work.

## 7. Letters

No letter written by Hypatia survives. Letters addressed to her by Synesius survive, but must not be catalogued as her writings.

## 8. Philosophical commentaries or lectures

Damascius reports that she publicly explained Plato and Aristotle. No written philosophical commentary is securely attributed to her.

Claims that she wrote a systematic Neoplatonic treatise are unsupported.

## Ancient Sources and Testimonia

### Synesius of Cyrene

Relevant items include:

- Letter 10;
- Letter 15, requesting the hydroscope;
- Letter 16;
- Letter 33;
- Letter 81;
- Letter 124;
- Letter 154;
- *On an Astrolabe*, especially the dedication crediting Hypatia’s contribution.

These are contemporary evidence, though dating and numbering vary between editions.

### Socrates Scholasticus

*Historia Ecclesiastica* VII.15, written approximately 439–440.

This is the principal near-contemporary narrative of her reputation, political position, and murder.

### Damascius

*Life of Isidore* or *Philosophical History*, written in the early sixth century and surviving fragmentarily in Photius and the *Suda*.

Provides material on her appearance, public teaching, chastity, civic standing, Cyril’s alleged envy, and death.

### *Suda*

Entry upsilon 166, “Hypatia.”

A tenth-century compilation drawing upon earlier sources, conventionally including Hesychius of Miletus and Damascius. Valuable but textually composite.

### John of Nikiu

*Chronicle* 84.87–103 in common numbering.

Written in the seventh century and surviving through later translations. It preserves an explicitly hostile Christian interpretation of Hypatia as a magician and approves her removal.

### Philostorgius

His lost ecclesiastical history survives in epitome and fragments. He reportedly treated Hypatia’s death, but the surviving evidence is much thinner than Socrates’s account.

### Imperial legislation

*Codex Theodosianus* 16.2.42–43, concerning Alexandrian *parabalani*, dated 416 and 418. The laws do not name Hypatia but illuminate the institutional response to Alexandrian crowd politics.

## Literary and Artistic Works About Hypatia

### Early modern polemic

- John Toland, *Hypatia: Or, the History of a Most Beautiful, Most Virtuous, Most Learned, and Every Way Accomplish’d Lady* (1720). Anti-clerical and anti-Catholic polemic using her murder against ecclesiastical authority.
- Thomas Lewis, *The History of Hypatia, a Most Impudent School-Mistress of Alexandria* (1721). Christian rebuttal to Toland, hostile to Hypatia.
- Voltaire discussed Hypatia as a victim of fanaticism and clerical power.
- Edward Gibbon incorporated her death into *The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire*, making it emblematic of Christian violence against classical civilization.

### Nineteenth-century fiction and visual culture

- Charles Kingsley, *Hypatia; or, New Foes with an Old Face* (1853). Highly influential historical novel. It Christianizes, romanticizes, and ultimately converts its fictionalized Hypatia before death.
- Charles William Mitchell, *Hypatia* (1885), painting based substantially on Kingsley’s eroticized literary image.
- Julia Margaret Cameron produced photographic imagery associated with Kingsley’s Hypatia.
- Numerous Victorian stage, salon, and illustrated adaptations transformed her into a symbol in debates over religion, empire, sexuality, and women’s education.

### Twentieth- and twenty-first-century literature

- Marcel Proust referred to Hypatia in literary-cultural contexts.
- Arnulf Zitelmann, *Hypatia* (historical novel).
- Ki Longfellow, *Flow Down Like Silver: Hypatia of Alexandria* (2009).
- Faith L. Justice, *Selene of Alexandria* (2009), a fictional world centered on Hypatia.
- Brian Trent, *Remembering Hypatia* and related speculative treatments.
- Umberto Eco and other modern writers have invoked her as a figure of lost knowledge, though not always as a principal character.

- Director: Alejandro Amenábar.
- Screenplay: Amenábar and Mateo Gil.
- Hypatia portrayed by Rachel Weisz.
- Production: Spanish, English-language historical drama.

### Historically grounded elements

- Hypatia’s Alexandrian teaching.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Late antiquity and Byzantium

Socrates made Hypatia an example of philosophical distinction and her murder an institutional disgrace. Damascius made her an exemplary pagan philosopher destroyed by Christian jealousy. John of Nikiu made the same event a victory over demonic pagan influence.

The *Suda* preserved both admiring biography and textual confusion, ensuring her survival in Byzantine learned memory.

### Early modern period

Toland, Lewis, Voltaire, and Gibbon converted Hypatia into a contested emblem:

- victim of clerical fanaticism;
- dangerous pagan teacher;
- embodiment of classical reason;
- weapon in Protestant–Catholic and deist–Christian controversies.

These authors often intensified the ancient evidence to serve current polemics.

### Nineteenth century

Kingsley’s novel made her internationally recognizable. Victorian culture reworked her as:

- eroticized pagan beauty;
- tragic learned woman;
- failed philosopher;
- prospective Christian convert;
- symbol in disputes over women’s education and public authority.

### Feminist and scientific commemoration

From the nineteenth century onward, and especially during the twentieth-century recovery of women’s intellectual history, Hypatia became an emblem of women excluded from or punished within male institutions.

Commemorations include:

- the feminist philosophy journal *Hypatia*, founded in the twentieth century;
- university programs and organizations bearing her name;
- schools, streets, lecture series, and awards;
- astronomical and scientific naming;
- the exoplanet Iota Draconis b receiving the approved proper name Hypatia;
- the lunar crater Hypatia;
- asteroid 238 Hypatia;
- the Hypatia Catalog of nearby stellar abundances;
- software, computing, mathematics, and educational projects.

These honorific uses document her modern symbolic importance but do not supply evidence about her ancient biography.

### Historical significance

Her importance rests on several independently supported features:

- she was a professionally recognized female philosopher in a social environment where such visibility was exceptional;
- she belonged to the working transmission of Greek mathematical astronomy;
- her students linked philosophical education with imperial and Christian careers;
- she acted within Alexandrian civic networks;
- her murder reveals the interaction of episcopal organization, imperial administration, crowd violence, religious faction, gender, and elite patronage;
- competing accounts of her life became records of later conflicts as much as records of Hypatia herself.
